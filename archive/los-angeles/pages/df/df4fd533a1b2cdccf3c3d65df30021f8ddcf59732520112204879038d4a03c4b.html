Case Number: 21STCV03737    Hearing Date: July 13, 2026    Dept: 415

    TENTATIVE RULING

 

DEPARTMENT

415

HEARING DATE

July 13, 2026

CASE NUMBER

21STCV03737, related to 21STCV02841

MOTION

Motion to Quash Subpoena

MOVING PARTY

Defendant, Cross-Complainant, and Cross-Defendant Paragon Resources Incorporated

OPPOSING PARTIES

Defendant and Cross-Defendant Julian Ledesma

 

MOTION

              Defendant, Cross-Complainant, and Cross-Defendant Paragon Resources Incorporated (“Paragon”) moves to quash a deposition notice Defendant and Cross-Defendant Julian Ledesma (“Ledesma”) served regarding the deposition of Paragon’s person most qualified to testify on specified topics.  Ledesma opposes the motion.

ANALYSIS

Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.420 provides, “The court, for good cause shown, may make any order that justice requires to protect any . . . deponent . . . from unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression, or undue burden and expense . . . .” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.420.)

Paragon argues the deposition notice does not adequately specify the matters on which Paragon’s person most qualified must testify.  If a deponent is a corporation or other entity, the deposition notice must describe with “reasonable particularity” the matters on which questions will be asked so that the deponent can designate the “most qualified” persons to testify on its behalf.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.230.)  Paragon contends categories Nos. 7, 8, 10, 11, 15, 16, and 17 are not reasonably particularized categories for which Paragon can designate a witness to testify.  Paragon is incorrect. 

Matters must be reasonably particularized from the point of view of the party on whom the demand for discovery is made.  (Calcor Space Facility, Inc. v. Superior Court (1997) 53 Cal.App.4th 216, 222.)  The matters are readily understandable from Paragon’s perspective.  As such, the matters are reasonably particularized.  While Paragon complains these matters could also constitute document demands, Paragon cites no authority for the proposition matters are not reasonably particularized if those matters could also constitute document demands.  Certainly, a witness may testify about matters for which relevant documents might also exist. Moreover, Ledesma has conceded he is not seeking production of documents through the deposition notice, but rather testimony, which in part, may concern the existence of documents.

The Court also notes Paragon’s argument the deposition notice may call for testimony which is irrelevant or privileged is premature.  The Court cannot limit the deposition notice, which calls for discoverable information, based on the parties’ speculation in this regard.  In any event, Paragon will not be foreclosed from asserting any appropriate objections at the deposition.

Paragon correctly notes the deposition notice does not contain document demands.  As such, any references to electronically-stored materials are irrelevant.  As noted above and conceded by Ledesma, the deposition notice does not compel Paragon to produce documents or electronically-stored materials. 

Accordingly, the motion to quash is denied, and Paragon is to produce a person or persons most qualified  to testify regarding the topics in the deposition notice within 30 days of the notice of the Court’s order.[1]

Under the particular circumstances of this matter, the Curt does not find the imposition of sanctions is warranted.  Therefore, the sanctions request is denied. 

Paragon is to give notice of the Court’s ruling and to file proof of service of same.


[1]To the extent Paragon contends discovery is closed and precludes the taking of the deposition at issue,  such contention is wholly inappropriate, as the record reflects this discovery was initiated well before any purported discovery cut-off date.


Case Number: 24STCV33489    Hearing Date: July 13, 2026    Dept: 415

   TENTATIVE RULING

 

DEPARTMENT

415

HEARING DATE

July 13, 2026

CASE NUMBER

24STCV33489

MOTIONS

Demurrer to First Amended Complaint

MOVING PARTY

Defendant City of Los Angeles

OPPOSING PARTIES

Plaintiffs Amani McGee and Markesha Cummings

 

MOTION

Defendant City of Los Angeles (“Defendant”) demurs to the first amended complaint of Plaintiffs Amani McGee and Markesha Cummings (“Plaintiffs”).  Plaintiffs oppose the demurrer. 

ANALYSIS

“It is black letter law that a demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the allegations in a complaint.” (Lewis v. Safeway, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 385, 388.) In ruling on a demurrer, the court must “liberally construe[]” the allegations of the complaint. (Code Civ. Proc., § 452.) “This rule of liberal construction means that the reviewing court draws inferences favorable to the plaintiff, not the defendant.” (Perez v. Golden Empire Transit Dist. (2012) 209 Cal.App.4th 1228, 1238.)

Plaintiffs sued Defendant based on the death of Decedent Miah Ladell Banks (“Decedent”).  Plaintiffs allege Decedent attended a gathering at a warehouse at which someone shot Decedent; Defendant’s police department personnel prevented emergency medical personnel from assisting Decedent; and Decedent died as a result.  (First Amended Complaint, ¶¶ 12-13.)  

Defendant is a governmental entity.  Government Code section 815, subdivision (a) provides “[a] public entity is not liable for an injury, whether such injury arises out of an act or omission of the public entity or a public employee or any other person,” except as provided by statute.  (Hoff v. Vacaville Unified School Dist. (1998) 19 Cal.4th 925, 932.)  “[D]irect tort liability of public entities must be based on a specific statute declaring them to be liable, or at least creating some specific duty of care, and not on the general tort provisions of Civil Code section 1714.  Otherwise, the general rule of immunity for public entities would be largely eroded by the routine application of general tort principles.”  (Eastburn v. Regional Fire Protection Authority (2003) 31 Cal.4th 1175, 1183.) Moreover, to state a claim against a public entity, “every fact essential to the existence of statutory liability must be pleaded with particularity, including the existence of a statutory duty.”  (Searcy v. Hemet Unified School Dist. (1986) 177 Cal.App.3d 792, 802.) 

In the third cause of action in their complaint, Plaintiffs allege Defendant is liable pursuant to Government Code sections 815.6, 820, and 820.8.  (First Amended Complaint, ¶ 33.)  However, pursuant to Government Code section 845, Defendant cannot be liable for failing to provide sufficient police protection service.  (Gov. Code, § 845.)  The immunity set forth in that provision shields Defendant from claims based on its failure to rescue shooting victims.  (See, e.g., Lopez v. City of San Diego (1987) 190 Cal.App.3d 678, 681 [in the context of a mass shooting at a restaurant, Court of Appeal held “[i]n the usual situations involving the performance of police duties, [a special relationship between the police and victims] has been held to depend on representations or conduct by the police which cause the victim(s) to detrimentally rely on the police such that the risk of harm as the result of police negligence is something more than that to which the victim was already exposed”].)  This immunity extends to paramedics and other rescue personnel as well as to police officers.  (Zepeda v. City of Los Angeles (1990) 223 Cal.App.3d 232, 235-236.) 

In opposition, Plaintiffs rely on Williams v. State of California (1983) 34 Cal.3d 18, which is not on point.  In that case, the California Supreme Court held when a police officer voluntarily assumes a protective duty and undertakes action, he or she must exercise due care not to increase the risk to which the citizen is exposed, or to induce the citizen to rely on other protective measures. (Id. at p. 24.)  In the instant case, however, Plaintiffs’ claims are not premised on Defendant’s voluntary assumption of a duty to protect Decedent.  Indeed, Plaintiffs allege Defendant failed to permit Decedent to receive medical assistance.  (First Amended Complaint, ¶¶ 12, 33.)  They do not allege Defendants, through the actions or conduct of its personnel, voluntarily assumed a special duty to Decedent or otherwise induced reliance on a promise, express or implied. Accordingly, Government Code section 845 would appear to bar Plaintiffs’ claims.

In addition, pursuant to Health and Safety Code section 1799.106, firefighters, law enforcement officers, emergency medical technicians, registered nurses, and the agencies hiring them are not liable for damages resulting from emergency treatment rendered at the scene of an emergency or during an emergency transport, unless they acted in a grossly negligent manner or without good faith.  Likewise, emergency rescue personnel who are employees of a federal, state, local, or private firefighting agency are not liable unless they act in bad faith or in a grossly negligent manner.  (Health & Safety Code, § 1799.107.)  Thus, if Plaintiffs contend rescue personnel provided incorrect or inappropriate treatment for Decedent, Plaintiffs must allege facts which establish such personnel were grossly negligent or acted in bad faith.

Defendant also argues Plaintiffs failed to exhaust their administrative remedies.  Pursuant to the Government Claims Act, a party with a claim for damages against a public entity must first file the claim directly with that entity.  The party may file a lawsuit only if the public entity denies or rejects the claim.  (Gov. Code §§ 905, 945.4; City of Ontario v. Superior Court (1993) 12 Cal.App.4th 894.)  This claims presentation requirement provides the public entity with an opportunity to evaluate the claim and decide whether to pay on it.  (Roberts v. County of Los Angeles (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 474.)  Failure to allege facts demonstrating compliance with the claims presentation requirement subjects the complaint to a general demurrer.  (State of Calif. v. Superior Court (2004) 32 Cal.4th 1234, 1239.) 

In this regard, Plaintiffs allege they filed a claim with Defendant.  (First Amended Complaint, ¶ 10.)  The claim is on behalf of Plaintiffs.  (First Amended Complaint, Exhibit A, p. 1.)  For pleading purposes, Plaintiffs’ allegations suffice.  If Defendant contends Plaintiffs did not file this claim, Defendant must conduct the appropriate discovery and raise that argument through an evidentiary motion.

The Court has already provided Plaintiffs with one opportunity to amend the complaint to state claims against Defendant.  As explained above, Plaintiffs have not yet sufficiently done so.  However, in an abundance of caution, the Court will provide Plaintiffs with another opportunity to amend to cure the deficiencies the Court has noted to date.  (See City of Stockton v. Superior Court (2007) 42 Cal.4th 730, 747 [further amendment permitted when operative complaint “does not on its face foreclose any reasonable possibility of amendment”].)

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Defendant’s demurrer to Plaintiffs’ first amended complaint is sustained with leave to amend.  Plaintiffs are to file a second amended complaint within 20 days of notice of this order.

Defendant is ordered to provide notice of the Court’s ruling and to file a proof of service of same.
