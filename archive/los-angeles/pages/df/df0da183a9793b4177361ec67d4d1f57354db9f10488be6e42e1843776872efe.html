MOTION

Petition to Compel Arbitration

MOVING PARTY

Defendants Tzur Gabi, D.D.S.; Gabi Dental Corp. d/b/a Primary Integrative Dentistry and Dentalogics by Dr. Gabi

OPPOSING PARTY

Plaintiff Martha Beatriz Ticas

 

MOTION

 

This case arises from allegations of dental malpractice. 

 

On June 5, 2026, Plaintiff Martha Beatriz Ticas (“Plaintiff”) filed suit against Defendants Tzur Gabi, D.D.S.; Gabi Dental Corp. d/b/a Primary Integrative Dentistry and Dentalogics by Dr. Gabi (“Defendants”) alleging two causes of action for (1) professional negligence (dental malpractice) and (2) fraudulent concealment.

 

Defendants now petition to compel Plaintiff’s claims to arbitration.  Plaintiff opposes the petition.

 

ANALYSIS

 

1.     MOTION TO COMPEL ARBITRATION – LEGAL STANDARDS

 

            “[T]he advantages of arbitration include a presumptively less costly, more expeditious manner of resolving disputes.  It follows a party to a valid arbitration agreement has a contractual right to have its dispute with another party to the contract resolved quickly and inexpensively.”  (Henry v. Alcove Investment, Inc. (1991) 233 Cal.App.3d 94, 99–100 [cleaned up].)  Thus, “on petition of a party to an arbitration agreement alleging the existence of a written agreement to arbitrate a controversy and that a party to the agreement refuses to arbitrate that controversy, the court shall order the petitioner and the respondent to arbitrate the controversy if it determines that an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2; see also

EFund Capital Partners v. Pless (2007) 150 Cal.App.4th 1311, 1320 [the language in section 1281.2 compelling arbitration is mandatory].) The right to compel arbitration exists unless the court finds that the right has been waived by a party’s conduct, other grounds exist for revocation of the agreement, or where a pending court action arising out of the same transaction creates the possibility of conflicting rulings on a common issue of law or fact.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2, subds. (a)-(c).)   

 

            “On a petition to compel arbitration, the trial court must first determine whether an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists.  Because the existence of the agreement is a statutory prerequisite to granting the petition, the petitioner bears the burden of proving its existence by a preponderance of the evidence.  The party seeking arbitration can meet its initial burden by attaching to the petition a copy of the arbitration agreement purporting to bear the respondent's signature.”  (Bannister v. Marinidence Opco, LLC (2021) 64 Cal.App.5th 541, 543-544 [cleaned up].)  The party seeking to compel arbitration must also “plead and prove a prior demand for arbitration and a refusal to arbitrate under the agreement.”  (Mansouri v. Superior Court (2010) 181 Cal.App.4th 633, 640-641.) 

 

            And while the moving party on a motion to compel arbitration “bears the burden of proving the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by a preponderance of the evidence, [a] party opposing the petition bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence any fact necessary to its defense.  The trial court sits as the trier of fact, weighing all the affidavits, declarations, and other documentary evidence, and any oral testimony the court may receive at its discretion, to reach a final determination.”  (Ruiz v. Moss Bros. Auto Group, Inc. (2014) 232 Cal.App.4th 836, 842 [cleaned up]; see also Pinnacle Museum Tower Assn. v. Pinnacle Market Development (US), LLC (2012) 55 Cal.4th 223, 236 [“The party seeking arbitration bears the burden of proving the existence of an arbitration agreement, and the party opposing arbitration bears the burden of proving any defense, such as unconscionability”].) 

 

2.     ENFORCEABLE ARBITRATION AGREEMENTS

 

Petitioner neither alleges, nor offers evidence showing, Respondent has improperly denied Petitioner’s demand for arbitration pursuant to an arbitration agreement with Respondent.  But the opposing party’s refusal to arbitrate may be demonstrated by its filing of a lawsuit rather than commencing arbitration proceedings, as required by the parties’ agreement.  (See Espejo v. Southern California Permanente Medical Group (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 1047, 1060 [moving party meets initial burden by showing agreement exists, and need only establish its validity on challenge by opponent].) 

 

In support of the petition, Defendants advance the “Treatment & Arbitration Agreement” Plaintiff signed on September 30, 2024, which provides as follows:

 

It is understood that any dispute as to medical malpractice, that is as to whether any dental services rendered were unnecessary or unauthorized or improperly, negligently. or incompetently rendered will be determined by submission to arbitration as provided by California law and not by a lawsuit or resort to court process except as California law provides for judicial review of arbitration. Both parties to this contract, by entering into it, are giving up their constitutional right to have any such dispute in a court of law before a jury, and instead are accepting the arbitration. Within 15 days after PATIENT or ATTENDING DENTIST shall give notice to the other demanding arbitration of such controversy shall appoint a licensed dentist as an arbitrator and give such appointment to the other. Within a reasonable time. the two arbitrators shall select another licensed dentist as the third arbitrator. The arbitrator shall hold a hearing within a reasonable time. All notices or other papers required to be served will he by United State mail. The arbitration shall be conducted in accordance with and governed by the provision of Title 9 of the California Code of Civil Procedure.

 

NOTICE: BY SIGNING THIS CONTRACT YOU ARE AGREEING TO HAVE ANY ISSUES OF DENTAL MALPRACTICE DECIDED BY NEUTRAL ARBITRATION AND YOU ARE GIVING UP YOUR RIGHT TO A JURY OR COURT TRIAL.

 

(Ex. A to Isfeld Decl.)

 

            Plaintiff does not deny that this agreement exists, but rather argues that it is unenforceable because it does not include the mandatory disclosures of Code of Civil Procedure section 1295, subdivision (b) in bold red type.

 

            Code of Civil procedure section 1295, subdivision (b) provides:

 

(b) Immediately before the signature line provided for the individual contracting for the medical services must appear the following in at least 10-point bold red type:

 

“NOTICE: BY SIGNING THIS CONTRACT YOU ARE AGREEING TO HAVE ANY ISSUE OF MEDICAL MALPRACTICE DECIDED BY NEUTRAL ARBITRATION AND YOU ARE GIVING UP YOUR RIGHT TO A JURY OR COURT TRIAL. SEE ARTICLE 1 OF THIS CONTRACT.”

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 1295, subd. (b).)

 

            The disclosure language appears directly above the signature line, but it is neither bolded nor in red type.

 

            In support, Plaintiff cites to Rosenfield v. Superior Court (1983) 143 Cal.App.3d 198, 200, which held, “the failure of a health care contract to contain the advisements specified in subdivisions (a) and (b) of section 1295 automatically renders unenforceable the arbitration provisions of such contract[.]”  The Court of Appeal further explained:   “The very requirement that jury-waiver advisements be included in “all” medical service arbitration contracts in specified language, and be in prominent locations in 10-point bold red type, evidences an implicit legislative determination that they are critical to safeguard against patients unknowingly waiving their constitutional right to jury trial.”  (Id. at p. 202.)

 

            Similarly, in Bolanos v. Khalatian (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 1586, 1589, fn. 3, the Court of Appeal explained:   “The quoted language contained in Article 1 and the warning in 10-point bold red type are required for medical malpractice arbitration agreements.”  (Emphasis added.)

Thus, here, because the required language does not appear in bolded type or red type, as required by the statute, the arbitration provision is unenforceable.

CONCLUSION

 

            Finding the arbitration provision unenforceable because the required disclosure language is neither bolded nor in red type, as required by Code of Civil Procedure section 1295, subdivision (b), the Court denies Defendants’ Petition to Compel Arbitration. 

 

Defendants shall provide notice of the Court’s ruling and file the notice with a proof of service forthwith.

Case Number: 26SMCV02319    Hearing Date: August 5, 2026    Dept: 207

TENTATIVE RULING

 

DEPARTMENT

207

HEARING DATE

August 5, 2026

CASE NUMBER

26SMCV02319

MOTION

Motion for Attorneys’ Fees

MOVING PARTY

Plaintiff Christopher Ho

OPPOSING PARTY

Defendant Tesla Motors, Inc.

 

MOTION

 

This case arises from allegations that manufacturer Defendant Tesla Motors, Inc. (“Defendant” or “Tesla”) sold to Plaintiff Christopher Ho (“Plaintiff”) a defective 2024 Tesla Cybertruck and failed to repair the defects or replace the vehicle in violation of the Song-Beverly Act.

 

On April 27, 2026, Plaintiff filed suit against Defendant, alleging three causes of action under the Song-Beverly Act for (1) breach of implied warranty; (2) breach of express warranty; and (3) breach of section 1793.2.

 

Plaintiff filed a Notice of Settlement of Entire Case on July 6, 2026. 

 

            Plaintiff now moves to recover reasonable costs, attorneys’ fees, and expenses pursuant to the Song-Beverly Act, in the amount of $7,307.60, composed of $3,697.50 incurred in attorneys’ fees, a 1.1 multiplier enhancement of $369.75; $740.35 in costs and expenses; and $2,500 in attorneys’ fees incurred in connection with the instant motion.  Defendant opposes the motion and Plaintiff replies. 

 

ANALYSIS

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5, which outlines recoverable costs to a prevailing party under Code of Civil Procedure section 1032, permits the recovery of attorneys’ fees when authorized by contract, statute or law.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (a)(10).) 

 

Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d) provides: “If the buyer prevails in an action under this section, the buyer shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.” 

 

Defendant does not contest that Plaintiff is entitled to recover reasonable attorneys’ fees, costs, and expenses under the Song-Beverly Act.  Instead, Defendant argues that the requested fees are excessive; fees for the instant motion are not authorized by the settlement agreement; counsel’s billing rate is inflated; and a multiplier is unwarranted. 

 

I.                Reasonableness of Fees

 

Plaintiff’s counsel seeks $3,697.50 in attorneys’ fees actually incurred, representing 8.7 hours of time at billed at a rate of $425 per hour.  (Barahmand Decl. ¶ 5 and Ex. B.) 

 

Defendant argues this is excessive in a case where the only work done was to file a stock complaint, prematurely propound template discovery, and accept a repurchase offer one month after the Complaint was filed.  (Kuredjian Decl. ¶ 11.)

 

Specifically, Defendant points out that the complaint filed in this matter is nearly identical to a complaint filed in another matter in 2024, and therefore should not have taken 1.2 hours to prepare.  (Compare Kuredjian Decl. ¶ 3 Ex. B1 with Ex. B2.)

 

The Court agrees with Defendant that only 30 minutes is reasonable to make the changes to the template complaint and that a fee cut of $510, representing 1.2 hours, is warranted.

 

Defendant also challenges 0.1 of 0.2 hours claimed for counsel’s time spent to “draft, file, and serve Plaintiff’s Notice of Settlement” on the ground that filing is clerical work, not recoverable at the attorney fee rate.  The Court finds this time negligible and that reductions are not warranted.

 

Defendant also argues that the settlement agreement excludes attorneys’ fees incurred in connection with the instant motion.  The agreement provides:

 

In addition to the above, Tesla shall pay Releasing Party’s attorney’s fees, costs and expenses to date that have been reasonably incurred in an amount to be determined by way of a noticed single noticed motion, unless the Parties can agree on the amount of fees to be paid absent such a motions….

 

(Kuredjian Decl. ¶ 10 and Ex. C, emphasis added.)

 

            In reply, Plaintiff asserts that attorneys’ fees incurred in connection with the fee motion itself are recoverable.  On this point, the Court agrees with Plaintiff.  Although the settlement agreement expressly provides that Tesla shall pay attorneys’ fees, costs, and expenses occurred to date, it does not otherwise indicate that Plaintiff agrees to waive fees incurred in connection with the fee motion, which are otherwise recoverable by operation of law. 

 

            Notwithstanding, the Court finds $2,500 excessive and reduces the amount sought by $800.

 

            In total, the Court reduces the Attorneys’ fees requested by $1,310.

 

II.             Billing Rate

 

In support of the $425 billing rate, Plaintiff advances the Declaration of Navid Barahmand, which provides:

 

11. I am aware of the hourly rates of other attorneys in California who work in the area of lemon law and consumer law. An hourly rate of $425.00 for myself is consistent with other attorneys who litigate consumer matters and who have my level of experience. I am counsel of record and lead counsel for Plaintiff. I routinely charge that rate, and that rate is routinely paid in most consumer claims that I have resolved. Notably, Plaintiff’s retainer agreement provides Plaintiff’s counsel bills at an hourly rate of $425.00. I believe that these rates are reasonable and customary charges. Additionally, I have litigated Song-Beverly Act cases at my own firm for over 4 years.

 

(Barahmand Decl. ¶ 11.)

 

            In opposition, Defendant advances the competing attorney Declaration of Aram Kuredjian, which provides:

 

13. It is common for Defense firms like mine to typically charge approximately $200- $275/hour in warranty defense litigation. This includes seasoned practitioners with as much as thirty years of trial experience

 

14. On July 21,2026, I navigated to the website for the State Bar of California at calbar.ca.gov and entered Navid Barahmand in the space provided to “Look Up a Lawyer”. One result was returned and it indicated that Navid Barahmand with Bar No. 340934 was admitted to the state bar on December 1, 2021.

 

15. Attached as Exhibit D is a true and correct copy of the ruling in Berumen v MBUSA, Los Angeles Superior Court Case No. 22CHCV01007, where the Court reduced the rates for the partner and senior counsel who billed on the case to $400.00 per hour.

 

16. Attached as Exhibit E is a true and correct copy of the tentative ruling and minute order adopting the tentative ruling on Plaintiff’s Motion for Attorneys’ Fees in Fernando Cervantes v. Tesla Motors, Inc., where the Court reduced all of another plaintiff’s counsel’s hourly rates to $250 per hour.

 

17. Attached as Exhibit F1 is a true and correct copy of the tentative ruling on Plaintiff’s Motion for Attorneys’ Fees in the Zeledon v. MBUSA matter, where the Court awarded $12,655.03 out of the $29,430.03 requested by Plaintiff’s Counsel and reduced Plaintiff’s counsel’s hourly rates to $395.00 per hour. Attached as Exhibit F2 is a true and correct copy of the court’s order adopting the tentative ruling Zeledon.

 

18. Attached hereto as Exhibit G is the Order on Plaintiffs’ Motion for Attorneys’ Fees in Arias v. Ford Motor Company, No. 5:18-cv-01928-PSG-SP, (C.D. Cal. Jan. 27, 2020) (ECF Dkt 50). In this case, the Court found that “counsel’s requested rate of between $350 and $650 for partners and between $250 and $450 for associates outpaces the prevailing rate for similar work” and reduced the rates for partners to $325 and reduced the rates of associates to $225.

 

19. Attached hereto as Exhibit H is the Order on Plaintiffs’ Motion for Attorneys’ Fees in Di Gioia v. Ford Motor Company, No. 5:18-cv-01724-JGB-KK, (2020 WL 1955311) (C.D. Cal. Apr. 1, 2020) (ECF Dkt 56). In this case, the Court adjusted the rate for partners to $325, and the rate for associates to $225.

 

20. Attached hereto as Exhibit I is the Order on Plaintiffs’ Motion for Attorneys’ Fees in Poore et al v Hyundai Motor America Orange County Sup. Crt. case No. 30-2022-01253785-CUBC-CJC. Hon. Lee Gabriel reduced the rates of Plaintiff’s counsel rates from $350-$550 per hour to a blended rate of $400 per hour.

 

(Kuredjian Decl. ¶¶ 13-20.)

 

            With the exception of the last case, Ex. I, all of the other cases were decided between the years 2020 and 2023.  Ex. I was decided in 2024, and determined that $400 was a reasonable blended rate.  When adjusted for inflation, the Court finds Plaintiff’s requested billing rate in line with the cases cited. 

 

            Therefore, the Court does not reduce Plaintiff’s counsel’s requested hourly billing rate.

 

III.           Multiplier

 

Factors to be considered in applying a multiplier include “the quality of the representation, the novelty and complexity of the issues, the results obtained, and the contingent risk presented.”  (Consumer Privacy Cases (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 545, 556.) 

 

Plaintiff argues that a multiplier is warranted because counsel took this case on a contingency basis.  The Court does not find that the case was taken on contingency alone to warrant a multiplier.  Rather, this was a relatively straightforward Song-Beverly Act case and Plaintiff has not demonstrated any novelty or difficulty requiring additional skill warranting a multiplier.

 

 Therefore, the Court denies Plaintiff’s requested multiplier as unwarranted.

 

IV.           Costs

 

Plaintiff seeks $740.35 in costs, comprised of filing and service fees.  (See Appendix 1 to Barahmand Decl.)  Filing and service fees are expressly recoverable, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5, subdivision (b).  Defendant does not challenge the requested costs.

 

Therefore, the Court awards Plaintiff’s requested costs.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

 

            Therefore, the Court grants in part Plaintiff’s request for attorneys’ fees and costs in the total amount of $5,627.85, representing $4,887.50 in attorneys’ fees and $740.35 in costs.

 

            Further, the Court will enter the proposed Order lodged on July 7, 2026 in conformity with the ruling.

 

            Plaintiff shall provide notice of the Court’s ruling and file the notice with a proof of service forthwith.
