Case Number: 23STCV19072    Hearing Date: August 13, 2026    Dept: 510

No. 11 - PlumbingAndFire, Inc. v. Nehorai Construction Inc., et al.

Nissim Kanizo’s Motion for Leave to Intervene and Set Aside Default and Vacate any Judgment Obtained by Plaintiff

Non-party Nissim Kanizo (“Kanizo”) moves for leave to intervene in this action between Plaintiff PlumbingAndFire, Inc. and Defendants Nehorai Construction Inc. and Ilan Kenig. Kanizo also moves to set aside and vacate the default judgment entered against Defendants on December 13, 2024.

Request for Judicial Notice

Plaintiff requests the Court take judicial notice of two documents associated with Kanizo’s contractor’s license.  The Court declines to take judicial notice of these documents, as they were irrelevant in deciding the instant motion.

The request for judicial notice is denied.

Evidentiary Objections

          Plaintiff makes 9 objections to Kanizo’s declaration submitted in support of his motion. Objections nos. 3-6 are sustained based on lack of foundation. Objection no. 9 is sustained based on lack of relevance. The remaining objections are overruled. Kanizo also submitted a declaration purportedly in support of his reply brief, but filed three days after he filed the reply brief and only three days before the hearing on this motion. The Court did not consider the untimely declaration.

Leave to Intervene

As an initial matter the Court notes that Kanizo did not include a proposed answer in intervention along with his motion. Code of Civil Procedure section 387(c) states “The petition shall include a copy of the proposed complaint in intervention or answer in intervention and set forth the grounds upon which intervention rests.” The failure to attach the proposed pleading alone justifies denial of the motion. (See Muller v. Robinson (1961) 193 Cal.App.2d 835, 837-838.)

Timeliness of Motion to Intervene

          Plaintiff argues Kanizo failed to timely file this motion. Intervention, whether mandatory or permissive, must be done “upon timely application.” (CCP § 387(d)(1) & (2).) To determine timeliness, the court must examine “the totality of the circumstances” with a “focus on three primary factors: (1) the stage of the proceeding at which an applicant seeks to intervene; (2) the prejudice to other parties; and (3) the reason for the delay.” (Crestwood Behavioral Health, Inc. v. Lacy (2021) 70 Cal.App.5th 560, 574.) “The determination of the timeliness of intervention is committed to the discretion of the trial court.” (Lofton v. Wells Fargo Home Mortgage (2018) 27 Cal.App.5th 1001, 1012.)

          First, default judgment was entered 20 months ago on December 13, 2024 for $34,346.15. Plaintiff argues that Kanizo learned of the judgment in March 2025 at the latest, because he called Plaintiff’s counsel on March 25, 2025, offering to pay the amount due on the judgment. (See Apanian Decl., ¶ 8.) This suggests that he was aware of his license suspension at that time, or else he would have no incentive to pay the judgment. Kanizo does not dispute this in reply. Kanizo did not file this motion until January 27, 2026.[1] Thus, Kanizo attempts to intervene well into the post-judgment and enforcement stage of proceedings.

          Second, Plaintiff shows prejudice resulting from Kanizo’s delay. Plaintiff explains that collection efforts on the judgment have been stalled because of the delay. In addition, because Plaintiff received a judgment in this case, it dismissed its companion mechanic’s lien suit against Defendants. Now the mechanic’s lien claim is time barred under the statute of limitations, and Plaintiff could not recover under that claim.

          Third, Kanizo does not provide a good reason for his delay. He argues in reply that he tried to resolve the matter without litigation, and “was attempting to determine the correct legal procedure to protect my license and my livelihood because I was not originally a party to this lawsuit.” (Kanizo Reply Decl., ¶ 22.) He argues that he filed this motion once it became clear that the CSLB would continue the suspension of his license in September 2025. (See id., ¶¶ 20, 23.) However, Kanizo does not explain why he could not have filed this motion before engaging in informal settlement discussions, nor why he waited an additional four months to file this motion after he realized the suspension would continue.

          The motion is untimely.

          Even if the Court had granted the motion, it is unclear what role Kanizo anticipates playing in this case. His dispute is with the California Contractors State License Board, which is apparently putting at risk his contractor’s license due to his long-ago affiliation with Defendant Nehorai. That dispute is not the subject of this litigation, and cannot be resolved merely by adding Kanizo as a party to this case.

Set Aside or Vacate Default Judgment

          To the extent that Kanizo requests the Court to set aside the default judgment under CCP § 473(d), the Court declines to do so. The default judgment is not void on its face. While Kanizo argues that the proof of service of the complaint on Defendants is defective, he provides no admissible evidence showing the addresses identified on the proof of service were incorrect. Even so, Kanizo also has no standing to raise this issue. (See CCP § 473(d) [“The court may, upon motion of the injured party, or its own motion, correct clerical mistakes in its judgment or orders as entered.”] [emphasis added].)

Disposition

          Non-party Nissim Kanizo’s motion for leave to intervene and set aside default and vacate any judgment obtained by Plaintiff is denied. 


[1] The Court also notes that after Kanizo filed the instant motion, he continued the hearing twice—once from March 30, 2026 to May 28, 2026 and another time from May 28, 2026 to October 29, 2026. In July 2026, the Court granted Plaintiff’s motion to advance the hearing to prevent further delay.

 


Case Number: 24STCV04924    Hearing Date: August 13, 2026    Dept: 510

No. 12 - Seong Ho Cho v. Keith Ferrazzi, et al.

          Defendant KV Construction 1, Inc.’s Motion for Determination of Good Faith Settlement

Defendant KV Construction 1, Inc. moves for an order determining the good faith of its settlement with Plaintiff Seong Ho Cho under Code of Civil Procedure section 877.6, subdivision (a).

When only some of multiple defendants alleged to be jointly liable settle a case, the settling parties may apply to the court for a determination of good faith settlement.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 877.6(a).) A determination of good faith “shall bar any other joint tortfeasor or co-obligor from any further claims against the settling tortfeasor or co-obligor for equitable comparative contribution, or partial or comparative indemnity, based on comparative negligence or comparative fault.” (Id., subd. (c).)        

Any opposing party bears “the burden below to show the settlement amount was ‘so far “out of the ballpark” in relation to’ the [various] factors that the settlement was inconsistent with the equitable objectives of section 877.6.” (Cahill v. San Diego Gas & Electric Co. (2011) 194 Cal.App.4th 939, 967; accord Code Civ. Proc., § 877.6(d).)

After considering all relevant factors, the Court finds the settlement between Defendant KV Construction 1, Inc. and Plaintiff was made in good faith. In making such a determination, the Court must consider various factors stated in Tech-Bilt, Inc. v. Woodward-Clyde & Associates (1985) 38 Cal.3d 488, 499-500. “ ‘Good faith may be found only if there has been no collusion between the settling parties and where the settlement amount appears to be within the “reasonable range” of the settling party’s proportionate share of comparative liability for a plaintiff’s injuries.’ ” (Long Beach Memorial Medical Center v. Superior Court (2009) 172 Cal.App.4th 865, 872.) 

Defendant KV Construction 1, Inc. settled with Plaintiff for $312,000 and agreed that a designated witness from Defendant will be available to testify at trial on at least 48 hours’ notice. (Motion, at p. 1; Bednar Decl., ¶ 8.) Defendant states that the settlement “is not the product of collusion nor is it for the purpose of prejudicing or harming any other defendant or cross-defendant.” (Bednar Decl., ¶ 7.) After considering all relevant factors, the Court finds Defendant presents sufficient evidence showing this settlement is within the reasonable range of its potential share of liability.    

No other party has asserted a lack of good faith or presented any evidence on that issue. “The party asserting the lack of good faith shall have the burden of proof on that issue.” (CCP § 877.6(d).)

Disposition

Defendant KV Construction 1, Inc.’s motion for determination of good faith settlement is granted. Under Code of Civil Procedure section 877.6, the Court hereby finds the settlement between Defendant KV Construction 1, Inc. and Plaintiff Seong Ho Cho was made in good faith.
