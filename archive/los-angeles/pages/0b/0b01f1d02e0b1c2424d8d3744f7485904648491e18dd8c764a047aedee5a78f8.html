21STCV21246 S.C. v. County of Los Angeles, et al.

Thursday, September 10, 2026

FINAL [MODIFIED] ORDER GRANTING IN PART DEFENDANT LOS ANGELES UNIFIED SCHOOL DISTRICT’S MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES TO REQUESTS FOR ADMISSION FROM PLAINTIFF  [Res. No. -5269]

 

                                                                                         I.         BACKGROUND

      The complaint alleges that Defendants, various public entities including moving party Los Angeles Unified School District (“Defendant”), discriminated against Plaintiff over the course of years by failing to provide for her special needs as a disabled individual while she was in the entities’ care as a foster child, patient, or – for the moving Defendant – a student. Plaintiff asserts independent claims for negligence against each of the four entity defendants and a single claim for violation of Education Code sections 200 and 220.

                                                                                          II.        ARGUMENTS

A.     Motion filed May 22, 2026.

      Plaintiff served vagueness objections to RFAs that are not vague, and in any case did not attempt to respond to the extent possible.

B.     Opposition filed August 27, 2026.

       The RFAs are unmanageably vague, and Defendant did not meet and confer as required.

C.     Reply filed September 2, 2026.

      Meeting and conference requirements are relaxed for such a straightforward dispute. Other parties have responded to the same RFAs to the extent possible even where they asserted vagueness objections.

                                                                                        III.       LEGAL STANDARD

      The party to whom requests for admission have been directed shall respond in writing, under oath, separately to each request. (§ 2033.210(a).) The response must either admit or deny the matter, or state that a reasonable inquiry was made and the party lacks sufficient information to admit the matter. (§ 2033.220.) If the response is an admission, the admission may “[a]dmit so much of the matter involved in the request as is true, either as expressed in the request itself or as reasonably and clearly qualified by the responding party.” (§ 2033.220(b)(1).)

      If the responding party makes an objection that the propounding party considers to be “without merit or too general,” or an answer which the propounding party finds evasive or incomplete, the propounding party may move for an order compelling further response. (§ 2033.290 (a)(2).) The motion must be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration and a separate statement or concise outline. (§ 2033.290(b).)

 

                                                                                                IV.       DISCUSSION

      Meeting and Conference

      As an initial matter: the Court finds Defendant’s efforts to meet and confer by letter sufficient, although barely. Defendant is admonished to make better efforts to meet and confer before future motions.

      Requests in Dispute

      The following eight RFAs are in dispute:

1.

ADMIT that YOU did not agree to have S.C. placed at the Texas Hill Country School.

2.

ADMIT YOU told the IEP Team at the April 2020 IEP Team meeting words to the effect that wherever Plaintiff S.C is placed, S.C. needed to be in a locked facility.

3.

ADMIT YOU were informed that the Texas Hill Country School was not a locked facility.

4.

ADMIT that the staff of the County of Los Angeles contacted many locked facilities to find a placement for Plaintiff S.C.

5.

ADMIT that between 2017 and 2020, no locked facility agreed to accept Plaintiff S.C.

6.

ADMIT that Plaintiff S.C. was rejected for placement at Devereux Texas and Devereux Florda.

7.

ADMIT that you were informed in IEP meetings that many facilities had refused to accept Plaintiff S.C.

8.

ADMIT that between the day that Plaintiff S.C. was placed in College Hospital in 2017 and the day the Judge Rothenberg Center agreed to accept S.C., no other locked facility agreed to accept S.C.

      In response to each of these RFAs Plaintiff objected on various grounds, including vagueness and the assumption of the truth or existence of a disputed fact, and stated that Plaintiff lacked sufficient information to admit or deny.

 

      Requests Nos. 1-3 and 7

      For RFAs 1-3 and 7 in particular, Plaintiff objected the the term “YOU” is ambiguous and rendered the Requests compound because the Requests defined the term to include “Plaintiff S.C. or the approved Guardian for Plaintiff S.C., attorneys for Plaintiff, or any other person acting on behalf of Plaintiff S.C. or who has acted on behalf of Plaintiff S.C. in this litigation.”

      The Court disagrees that the term “YOU” is ambiguous or renders the requests compound. It reasonably refers to “S.C. or anyone acting on S.C.’s behalf”, as indicated by the language “any other person acting on behalf of Plaintiff S.C.”

      With this in mind, Plaintiff’s responses to Requests Nos. 1, 3, and 7 are insufficient. Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.220 requires Plaintiff to admit or deny to the extent possible. If Plaintiff is aware of anyone acting on S.C.’s behalf either (1) agreeing to have S.C. placed or (3) being informed that the specified school was not a locked facility, S.C. must admit as much. Otherwise, Plaintiff may deny for lack of information.

      As to RFA No. 2, however, the Court agrees the request is fatally vague or ambiguous. The term “words to the effect of” is too subjective to be susceptible to admission. In fact, even an unqualified admission would not serve the purpose of the discovery mechanism; it would simply raise new disputes on the definition of Defendant’s chosen term, rather than clarifying or narrowing matters for settlement or trial.

      Request No. 4

      Plaintiff’s response to No. 4 complies with the Code, and Plaintiff’s vagueness objection is sustained. Plaintiff cannot reasonably be asked to admit what County staff did or did not do, and the term “many locked facilities” is ambiguous.

      Requests Nos. 5 and 8

      Plaintiff’s responses to Requests Nos. 5 and 8 are insufficient for similar reasons to Requests Nos. 1, 3, and 7. Plaintiff must admit whether the statements are true based on the information available to Plaintiff at this time. For agreements by locked facilities not within Plaintiff’s knowledge, Plaintiff may refuse to admit or deny.

      Request No. 6

      Plaintiff’s vagueness objection to Request No. 6 is sustained. The term “rejected for placement” is not defined in the Requests, and the Request is not confined to any particular time period. As with RFA No. 2, even an unqualified admission would open new disputes over what amounts to a “rejection for placement.”

      Sanctions

      No sanctions are requested or awarded.

 

                                                                                           V.        CONCLUSION

      Based on the foregoing, Defendant’s motion is GRANTED IN PART and DENIED IN PART.

      Plaintiff is ordered to provide supplemental responses to Requests for Admission Nos. 1, 3, 5, 7, and 8 within 30 days, admitting or denying the specified matters to the extent possible based on information currently available to Plaintiff.

      As to Requests for Admission Nos. 2, 4, and 6, the motion is denied.


Case Number: 25STCV00311    Hearing Date: September 10, 2026    Dept: 414

25STCV00311 Coalition for Responsible Equitable Economic Development Los Angeles v. Chinatown Station Owner LLC.

Thursday, September 10, 2026

TENTATIVE ORDER GRANTING IN PART, DENYING IN PART, PLAINTIFF’S MOTION TO SEAL  [Res. No. -9395]

 

                                                                                         I.         BACKGROUND

      The first amended complaint alleges Defendant applied for permits for the College Station Project (“Project”) to develop 725 residential units and ground floor commercial uses. (FAC ¶ 10.) Plaintiff submitted comments on the Environmental Impact Report for the Project and filed appeals challenging the City’s determinations and recommendations. (FAC ¶ 11.)

      Defendant then entered into a Confidential Environmental Settlement Agreement and Mutual Special Release for the College Station Project (“Agreement”) with Plaintiff on March 21, 2019, whereby Defendant agreed to fund the work of a non-profit corporation (“Corporation”) to be formed by Plaintiff with permanent staff who would work with disadvantaged and underserved communities in Los Angeles to address the impacts of ongoing development. (FAC ¶ 12.) As part of the consideration given to Defendant under the Agreement, Plaintiff agreed to withdraw its appeals of the Project approvals. The Agreement settled claims related to the development of the Project. (FAC ¶ 2.)

      Defendant allegedly breached the Agreement by failing to pay $830,800 to the Corporation after the Project received final approval pursuant to the terms of the Agreement. (FAC ¶ 15.) Plaintiff alleges a claim for breach of contract and specific performance pursuant to Civil Code sections 3300 and 3384 requiring Defendant’s payment to the Corporation of $830,800.

      Plaintiff moves to seal Exhibits 30 through 48 and Exhibits 55 through 57 (collectively, “Exhibits”) of Defendant’s Exhibits in Support of Defendant’s Opposition to Plaintiff’s Motion for Summary Judgment.[1]

      The Court entered judgment for Plaintiff in this case on July 14, 2026.

      On August 5, 2026, Defendant filed a Notice of Appeal, which was placed in default on August 21, 2026, pursuant to California Rules of Court, rule 8.140(a), for failing to designate what documents and oral proceedings, if any, are to be included in the record that will be sent to the Court of Appeal, pursuant to rule 8.121.

 

                                                                                         II.        LEGAL STANDARD

      Unless confidentiality is required by law, court records are presumed to be open to the public, pursuant to a potent “open court” policy bolstered by the First Amendment of the U.S. Constitution and favoring the public nature of court proceedings. (Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 2.550, subd. (c); see NBC Subsidiary (KNBC-TV), Inc. v. Superior Court (1999) 20 Cal.4th 1178, 1199-1200.) Consequently, pleadings, motions, discovery documents, and other papers may not be filed under seal merely by stipulation of the parties; filing under seal requires a court order. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.551, subd. (a); see H.B. Fuller Co. v. Doe (2007) 151 Cal.App.4th 879, 888.)  

      In order to issue a sealing order, the Court must expressly find that: (1) there exists an overriding interest that overcomes the right of public access to the record; (2) the overriding interest supports sealing the record; (3) a substantial probability exists that the overriding interest will be prejudiced if the record is not sealed; (4) the proposed sealing is narrowly tailored; and (5) no less restrictive means exist to achieve protection of the overriding interest. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.550, subd. (d)(1)-(5); see McGuan v. Endovascular Techs., Inc. (2010) 182 Cal.App.4th 974, 988.) The constitutional policy favoring disclosure must be balanced against other factors, e.g., privacy rights. (See People v. Jackson (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1009, 1026-27.) The Court acts within its discretion in making factual determinations on a motion to seal. (Universal City Studios, Inc. v. Superior Court (2003) 110 Cal.App.4th 1273, 1285.)  

      A sealing order must be sought by means of a motion (or application) and accompanied by a memorandum of points and authorities, as well as evidence and testimony containing facts sufficient to justify the mandatory findings required to support a sealing order. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.550, subd. (d) and 2.551, subd. (b).) The proponent of the sealing order must also conditionally lodge the unredacted matter to be sealed with the court. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.551, subd. (b)(4).) 

     

                                                                                                III.       DISCUSSION

      Plaintiff moves to permanently seal the Exhibits, which were previously lodged under seal, because: (1) Exhibits 30, 37, 38, 43, 45, and 48 (hereinafter, “Settlement Agreements”) “each consist of the complete text of settlement agreements between CREED LA and developers resolving concerns regarding the environmental impacts of various proposed development projects;” (2) Exhibits 31-36, 39-42, and 46-47 “each consist of notices of settlement sent to city or county agencies (hereinafter, ‘Notices of Settlement’) informing them that CREED LA has entered into environmental settlement agreements with a developer addressing environmental concerns that CREED LA raised in its administrative appeals of CEQA approvals;” and (3) Exhibits 55-57 (hereinafter, “Modrzejewski Deposition and Exhibits”) contain Modrzejewski’s—the executive director of CREED LA— explanation of “CREED LA’s strategies in opposing development projects and how CREED LA achieved particular settlements.” (Mot., at pp. 3-4.)

      First, Plaintiff argues that it has an overriding interest in maintaining the confidentiality of the Exhibits because these “documents reveal CREED LA’s internal strategies and the terms and conditions that developers have been willing to agree to in previous negotiations.” (Mot., at p. 5.) The Settlement Agreements are allegedly “not public record,” and Plaintiff and its counsel maintain that they are “proprietary in nature.” (Carmichael Decl., ¶¶ 5-7 [“These agreements are not individually readily accessible to the public nor are they available to the public in an aggregate grouping. These settlement agreements are between private parties and are not public records; only partial summaries of these agreements are provided to cities or the County of Los Angeles when withdrawing appeals pursuant to these agreements.”].) As to the Notices of Settlement, counsel states that they are considered “proprietary trade secret for the same reasons” as the Settlement Agreements, and if made public, Plaintiff “would be severely prejudiced because it would put CREED LA at a disadvantage in future settlement agreement negotiations. Developers would use information about CREED LA’s past settlements to establish the starting position of settlement negotiations, giving them an unfair advantage in the negotiation process.” (Id., ¶¶ 9-11.) Lastly, as to the Modrzejewski Deposition and Exhibits, counsel argues that “Mr. Modrzejewski testified regarding several sensitive and confidential topics, including the methods that CREED LA uses when opposing development projects; and Mr. Modrzejewski was shown copies of the prior CREED LA settlements provided to Defendants in discovery and was asked questions about their content…[which] could allow developers to attempt to aggregate these settlements in a manner that would provide an advantage in future negotiations of environmental settlements.” (Id., ¶¶ 15-16.)

      Second, Plaintiff argues that it will be prejudiced if the Exhibits are made public because the documents shed light on their internal strategies that can be leveraged in subsequent settlement negotiations by developers to gain an unfair advantage. (Mot., at p. 5; Carmichael Decl., ¶¶ 7, 11, 16 [“CREED LA would be severely prejudiced because CREED LA would be at a disadvantage when entering into settlement negotiations to resolve claims over future projects. Developers would use information about CREED LA’s other settlements to set the starting point for negotiations, giving them an advantage over CREED LA in the settlement negotiation process.”].)

      Third, Plaintiff argues that the proposed sealing is narrowly tailored because Plaintiff has only selected “a small subset of [Defendant’s exhibits] as sufficiently sensitive to meet the sealing requirements…[and] include[s] only those materials that must be kept confidential to avoid a substantial probability of prejudice to CREED LA.” (Mot., at p. 6.) Due to the confidential and sensitive information in the Exhibits, Plaintiff argues that “there is no less restrictive means of maintain[ing] their confidentiality than by sealing in full.” (Id.)

      In opposition, Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s request is not narrowly tailored and Plaintiff has not adequately established that sealing all of the lodged Exhibits is the least restrictive means possible. (Opp., at p. 2.)

      The Court finds that Plaintiff sufficiently established that the Settlement Agreements and the Modrzejewski Deposition and Exhibits are allegedly “not public record,” that they are “proprietary in nature,” that Plaintiff would be severely prejudiced if the documents are not sealed because the documents contain internal strategies that can be leveraged in subsequent settlement negotiations by developers to gain an unfair advantage, and that the request is narrowly tailored. (Carmichael Decl., ¶¶ 5-7, 15-16.) Defendant, also, does not oppose the motion to seal specifically as to the Settlement Agreements. As to the Modrzejewski Deposition and Exhibits, specifically, while Counsel acknowledges that the testimony includes both confidential and non-confidential information, he states that the two “are inextricable intertwined” and thus “selective redaction [is] burdensome and/or impractical.” (Id., ¶ 16.) In addition, counsel states that both parties, due to the intertwined nature, “agreed to designate the entire deposition” as confidential; Defendant does not dispute this assertion in its opposition. (Id.)

      The Court, however, finds that Plaintiff has failed to sufficiently plead it had an overriding interest in sealing the Settlement Notices. Plaintiff’s counsel states that the Settlement Notices are “a matter of public record and do not contain the full settlement agreements.” (Id., ¶ 10.) The issue turns on the compilation of said Notices. The Court finds Plaintiff’s argument that a compilation of said Notices would be prejudicial to be unpersuasive.  Accordingly, the motion is granted in part as to the Settlement Agreements and Modrzejewski Deposition and Exhibits and denied in part as to the Settlement Notices.

                                                                                         IV.       CONCLUSION

      Based on the foregoing, Plaintiff’s motion to seal is granted in part as to the Settlement Agreements and Modrzejewski Deposition and Exhibits and denied in part as to the Settlement Notices.


[1] The Court notes that Defendant filed an untimely opposition on September 3, 2026. The hearing for this motion is set for September 10, 2026; thus, Defendant’s opposition was due by August 27, 2026.

25STCV12848 Ronald E. Wilson, et al. v. American Honda Motor Co., Inc.

Thursday, September 10, 2026

TENTATIVE ORDER GRANTING IN PART AND DENYING IN PART DEFENDANT AMERICAN HONDA MOTOR CO., INC.’S MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT OR, IN THE ALTERNATIVE, SUMMARY ADJUDICATION  [Res. No. -7756]

 

                                                                                         I.         BACKGROUND

Plaintiffs Ronald Wilson and Tracy Smith-Wilson (collectively, “Plaintiffs”) allege that the 2019 Acura TLX (“Acura”) they purchased under a warranty contract from Defendant American Honda Motor Co., Inc. (“Honda”) suffered from defects and nonconformities to warranty, including but not limited to transmission, engine, and electrical defects.

On May 1, 2025, Plaintiffs filed a complaint against Honda and Does 1 through 10, inclusive, alleging the following causes of action: (1) violation of subdivision (d) of Civil Code section 1793.2; (2) violation of subdivision (b) of Civil Code section 1793.2; (3) violation of subdivision (a)(3) of Civil Code section 1793.2; (4) breach of the implied warranty of merchantability; and (5) fraudulent inducement - concealment.

Honda moves for summary judgment on the Complaint’s five causes of action or, in the alternative, summary adjudication on the grounds that Plaintiffs cannot establish any genuine issue of material fact and cannot prevail as a matter of law.

                                                                                          II.        JUDICIAL NOTICE

A.     Honda’s Request

      Honda seeks judicial notice of the Complaint and attached Exhibit A in this case. The request is denied as moot because the Court does not need to take judicial notice of filings on the instant docket.

B.     Plaintiffs’ Request

      Plaintiffs ask the Court to take judicial notice of Exhibits A-D & F, which include bulletins, ServiceNews Articles, and dealer messages, available on the website of the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (“NHTSA”).  (Request for Judicial Notice, pgs., 2-3; Evid. C. §§ 452, 453.)

      The Court grants Plaintiffs’ request for judicial notice as to the Exhibits from the NHTSA website, but only as to the existence of the documents, not as to the truth of any hearsay statements asserted therein.

                                                                             III.       EVIDENTIARY OBJECTIONS

“In granting or denying a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication, the court need rule only on those objections to evidence that it deems material to its disposition of the motion.  Objections to evidence that are not ruled on for purposes of the motion shall be preserved for appellate review.”  (Code Civ. Proc., §¿437c, subd. (q).) 

Honda’s Objections

1.     Declaration of Shannon Duane: Sustained – None | Overruled: No. 1-3

2.     Declaration of Ronald Wilson: Sustained – Nos. 1, 3-6, 7 (in part on hearsay grounds as to the dealerships’ statements only), 9 (in part on hearsay grounds as to the dealerships’ statements only), and 10 | Overruled – 2 & 8

3.     Declaration of Tracy Smith-Wilson: Sustained – none | Overruled: Nos. 1-5

                                                                                        IV.       LEGAL STANDARD

      The purpose of a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication “is to provide courts with a mechanism to cut through the parties’ pleadings in order to determine whether, despite their allegations, trial is in fact necessary to resolve their dispute.” (Aguilar¿v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) “Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (c), requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.)¿ 

      In ruling on a motion for summary judgment, the Court must consider all of the evidence and the reasonable inferences drawn from that evidence, viewing both in the light most favorable to the opposing party. As the Supreme Court explained, the court “must consider all of the evidence and all of the inferences reasonably drawn therefrom [citation] and must view such evidence [citations] and such inferences [citations] in the light most favorable to the opposing party.” (Aguilar, supra, at pp. 844–845 [quotation marks omitted].)

      The moving party bears the initial burden of making a prima facie showing that no triable issue of material fact exists. (Scalf v. D. B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1519.) A defendant satisfies this burden by showing that one or more elements of a cause of action cannot be established or that a complete defense exists. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).)            Once the defendant makes that showing, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to demonstrate a triable issue of material fact. The plaintiff may not rely upon the allegations or denials of the pleadings but instead must “set forth the specific facts showing that a triable issue of material fact exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.” (Ibid.)

      To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence. (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.) If the plaintiff cannot make that showing, summary judgment should be granted. (Avivi v. Centro Medico Urgente Medical Center (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 463, 467.)

      A motion for summary judgment ordinarily must be heard “no later than 30 days before the date of trial, unless the court for good cause orders otherwise.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (a)(3).)[1].)

      The motion is being heard on September 10, 2026, with trial set for September 28, 2026, less than 30 days later. The Court previously denied Honda’s ex parte application to advance the hearing or, alternatively, to continue the trial. (Minute Order, July 16, 2026.) Plaintiffs have not objected to the timing of the hearing. Under these circumstances, the Court finds good cause to hear the motion within 30 days of trial.

 

                                                                                               V.        DISCUSSION[1]

 

A. Statute of Limitations

      Honda first argues that Plaintiffs’ Song-Beverly and fraud claims are barred by the applicable statutes of limitations. The Court addresses the Song-Beverly claims and fraud claim separately because they are governed by different accrual rules.

 

 

1. Song-Beverly Causes of Action

      The Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act does not contain its own statute of limitations. An action for breach of warranty under the Act is therefore governed by the four-year limitations period of Commercial Code section 2725. (Mexia v. Rinker Boat Co., Inc. (2009) 174 Cal.App.4th 1297, 1305; Com. Code, § 2725, subd. (1).) Ordinarily, a breach of warranty occurs upon tender of delivery. Where, however, a warranty explicitly extends to future performance and discovery of the breach necessarily awaits that performance, the cause of action accrues when the breach is or should have been discovered. (Com. Code, § 2725, subd. (2).)

      A manufacturer's promise to repair defects occurring during a specified future period constitutes an express warranty extending to future performance. (Krieger v. Nick Alexander Imports, Inc. (1991) 234 Cal.App.3d 205, 217.) For such a warranty, the limitations period runs from the time the buyer reasonably concludes that the warrantor will not repair the vehicle. (Id. at pp. 218–219.) On summary judgment, a defendant asserting the statute of limitations must negate the applicability of this discovery-based accrual rule. (Id. at p. 216.)

      An implied warranty is different. It arises by operation of law and does not promise future performance. (Cardinal Health 301, Inc. v. Tyco Electronics Corp. (2008) 169 Cal.App.4th 116, 135.) In the case of a latent defect, the implied warranty is breached by the existence of the defect, not its later discovery. (Mexia, supra, 174 Cal.App.4th at p. 1305.) Civil Code section 1791.1, subdivision (c), defines the duration of the implied warranty; it does not transform that warranty into one extending to future performance.

      Plaintiff Tracy Smith-Wilson purchased the new 2019 Acura on August 24, 2018, from Valencia Acura and is the sole buyer identified on the sales contract. (Honda’s UMF No. 1.) The Acura came with a four-year/50,000-mile new-vehicle limited warranty and a six-year/70,000-mile powertrain warranty expressly covering the transmission. (Honda’s UMF No. 2.) Mr. Wilson testified that the Acura began exhibiting hesitation and harsh shifting within months of purchase and that Plaintiffs sought repairs for transmission problems even before the first documented repair order dated August 22, 2019. (Honda’s UMF Nos. 3–4; RW Depo., 21:14–23:7, 31:10–15, 32:15–33:16.)

      On August 22, 2019, Mr. Wilson brought the Acura to Valencia Acura with transmission complaints, but the technician found no problem. (Honda’s UMF Nos. 5–6; Evans Decl., ¶¶ 3, 5, Exs. A–B; RW Depo., 31:16–32:9.) On September 9, 2019, Mr. Wilson reported to Acura Client Relations that the vehicle was jerking when shifting, appeared to downshift on its own, and had exhibited the problem “from the beginning.” (Honda’s UMF No. 8; Pacheco Decl., ¶ 4, Ex. 1.) The dealership nevertheless reported that the vehicle was operating as designed. (Honda’s UMF No. 9; Pacheco Decl., ¶ 4, Ex. 1.) In October 2019, Mr. Wilson again complained that the vehicle was revving and would not shift and stated that he was prepared to pursue “all possible channels” to resolve the problem. (Honda’s UMF No. 11; Pacheco Decl., ¶ 4, Ex. 1.) A second dealership thereafter test-drove the Acura but found no fault codes. (Honda’s UMF Nos. 12–15; Ladouceur Decl., ¶ 3, Ex. A; RW Depo., 34:23–36:14.)

      The repair efforts did not end in 2019. On May 7, 2022, Plaintiffs again presented the Acura for the transmission complaint. The dealership performed a TCM software update and idle-learn procedure when the vehicle had approximately 38,820 miles. (Honda’s UMF Nos. 20–22; Evans Decl., ¶ 5, Ex. B; RW Depo., 44:3–50:20.) Mr. Wilson testified that the transmission problem persisted after that visit and that he thereafter stopped returning to the dealership because it continued giving him the “same old excuses.” (Honda’s UMF Nos. 23–24; RW Depo., 46:8–12, 47:5–48:13.)

 

a. Express Warranty.

      This evidence does not establish as a matter of law that Plaintiffs reasonably concluded more than four years before filing suit that Honda would not repair the vehicle. Although Plaintiffs were unquestionably aware of transmission problems by 2019, awareness of a defect is not necessarily the accrual point for a warranty expressly promising future repairs. Under Krieger, the relevant inquiry is when Plaintiffs reasonably concluded that Honda would not repair the vehicle. (Krieger, supra, 234 Cal.App.3d at pp. 218–219.)

      Honda’s own evidence shows that Plaintiffs continued seeking warranty repairs and that Honda continued attempting repairs through May 7, 2022. A reasonable trier of fact could therefore conclude that Plaintiffs did not reasonably determine that Honda would not repair the transmission until that final repair attempt failed. If the claim accrued on May 7, 2022, the four-year limitations period would not expire until May 7, 2026. Plaintiffs filed this action on May 1, 2025. Because the evidence does not compel an accrual date more than four years before the Complaint was filed, Honda has not established its statute of limitations defense to the express warranty claims as a matter of law. The motion for summary adjudication of the first cause of action on statute of limitations grounds is therefore DENIED.

 

b. Implied Warranty.

      The fourth cause of action stands differently because the implied warranty does not extend to future performance. Plaintiffs purchased the Acura on August 24, 2018. (Honda’s UMF No. 1.) Under Commercial Code section 2725, the four-year limitations period ordinarily ran from tender of delivery and expired on August 24, 2022. Even assuming, in Plaintiffs’ favor, that the period did not begin until August 24, 2019—the end of the one-year implied warranty period—the limitations period expired on August 24, 2023. Plaintiffs did not file the Complaint until May 1, 2025.

      Plaintiffs argue that Honda has not established when they discovered or reasonably should have discovered the latent defect. (Opp., p. 15.) That argument conflates the accrual rules governing express and implied warranties. Unlike an express warranty extending to future performance, an implied warranty is breached by the defective condition of the goods, and discovery of a latent defect does not postpone accrual under Commercial Code section 2725. (Mexia, supra, 174 Cal.App.4th at p. 1305.)

      Plaintiffs also allege generally that equitable tolling, delayed discovery, equitable estoppel, the repair rule, and class-action tolling apply. (Compl., ¶ 23.) They do not, however, identify evidence or develop an argument establishing that any of those doctrines renders the implied warranty claim timely. The undisputed dates therefore establish that the fourth cause of action was filed outside the applicable four-year limitations period. Accordingly, the motion for summary adjudication as to the fourth cause of action is GRANTED.

 

B. Second Cause of Action: Section 1793.2, subdivision (b)

      Plaintiffs’ second cause of action alleges that Honda violated Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (b), which requires a manufacturer to commence necessary warranty repairs within a reasonable time and, absent a written agreement otherwise, to service or repair the goods to conform to the applicable warranties within 30 days. (Civ. Code, § 1793.2, subd. (b).)

Honda argues that there is no evidence that any repair of the Acura exceeded 30 days. (Mot., p. 10.) In discovery, Honda asked Plaintiffs to identify the total number of days they were unable to use the Acura because it was being repaired. (Honda’s UMF Nos. 27–28; Chong Decl., Ex. C.)            Plaintiffs objected and referred generally to the Complaint and the Acura’s repair history. (Ibid.) Honda also requested documents concerning the number of days the vehicle was unavailable while being serviced or repaired. Plaintiffs again asserted objections and stated that they would produce responsive, nonprivileged documents in their possession. (Honda’s UMF Nos. 27–28; Chong Decl., Ex. D.)

      The repair records likewise do not identify a repair exceeding the statutory period. It is undisputed that the Acura was at the repair facility for only one day on August 22, 2019, October 24, 2019, November 4, 2019, and May 7, 2022. (Honda’s UMF Nos. 6, 15, 18, 21.) Honda’s exhibits document the remaining presentations, each of which also lasted only one day. (P.’s UMF No. 10; Evans Decl., Ex. B; Ladouceur Decl., Ex. A.) Honda has therefore presented evidence establishing that none of the identified repair visits exceeded 30 days.

Plaintiffs respond that Honda’s separate statement addresses only six of the seventeen presentations reflected in Honda’s own exhibits and argue that the duration and number of repair attempts therefore cannot be resolved on summary adjudication. (Opp., p. 12.) The argument does not create a triable issue. The remaining presentations appear in Honda’s exhibits, and those records likewise reflect one-day visits. (P.’s UMF No. 10; Evans Decl., Ex. B; Ladouceur Decl., Ex. A.) Plaintiffs identify no evidence of any presentation during which the Acura remained unavailable for repair for more than 30 days.

      Plaintiffs separately contend that the May 7, 2022, repair may not actually have been completed. According to Plaintiffs, the technician recommended a TCM software update, while the repair order reflects only that an idle-learn procedure was performed. Plaintiffs argue that a jury could therefore find that the prescribed repair was never completed and that the vehicle’s same-day return did not satisfy section 1793.2, subdivision (b). (Opp., p. 12.) But Plaintiffs identify no affirmative evidence establishing that the recommended repair was not performed or that the Acura remained in Honda’s possession, or otherwise unavailable to Plaintiffs for repair, beyond the one-day period reflected in the repair record. Their contention that the repair may have been incomplete does not, without supporting evidence, establish a triable issue as to whether Honda violated the 30-day requirement asserted as the basis for this cause of action.

      Accordingly, Honda has established that the identified repairs did not exceed the statutory period, and Plaintiffs have not produced evidence creating a triable issue on that point. The motion for summary adjudication as to the second cause of action is GRANTED.

     

C. Third Cause of Action: Section 1793.2, subdivision (a)(3)

      Plaintiffs’ third cause of action alleges that Honda violated Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (a)(3), which requires a manufacturer that has made an express warranty to “[m]ake available to authorized service and repair facilities sufficient service literature and replacement parts to effect repairs during the express warranty period.” (Civ. Code, § 1793.2, subd. (a)(3).)

      Honda argues that Plaintiffs have no evidence identifying any service literature or replacement parts that Honda failed to make available or any repair that could not be effected because of such a failure. (Mot., pp. 10–11.) In response to Special Interrogatory No. 35, which asked Plaintiffs to state all facts supporting this cause of action, Plaintiffs asserted objections and referred generally to the Complaint and the Acura’s repair history. (Honda’s UMF Nos. 31–32; Chong Decl., Ex. C.) Similarly, when asked to produce documents supporting the claim, Plaintiffs asserted objections and stated that they would produce responsive, nonprivileged documents in their possession. (Honda’s UMF Nos. 31–32; Chong Decl., Ex. D.) Neither response identifies particular service literature or replacement parts that Honda failed to make available to its authorized repair facilities.

      Plaintiffs contend that Honda’s own repair records nevertheless create a triable issue. They identify two instances in which a dealership did not have a particular part immediately in stock. On October 24, 2019, Mr. Wilson presented the Acura for repair and returned on November 4, 2019, for installation of a driver-side rear door inner-handle pocket trim piece that was not then in stock. (Opp., p. 13; Ladouceur Decl., ¶ 3, Ex. A.) On June 23, 2022, a dealership identified a leaking evaporative-emissions purge valve, ordered the part, and installed it when Mr. Wilson returned four days later on June 27. (Opp., p. 13; Evans Decl., ¶ 5, Ex. B.)

      These circumstances do not create a triable issue under section 1793.2, subdivision (a)(3). Neither part concerns the transmission defect upon which Plaintiffs predicate their claims. More fundamentally, the fact that a dealership did not have a particular replacement part physically in stock on the day a vehicle was presented does not, without more, establish that the manufacturer failed to “make available” sufficient replacement parts to effect warranty repairs. In both instances identified by Plaintiffs, the necessary part was obtained, and the repair was subsequently performed. Plaintiffs identify no evidence that Honda failed to supply a requested part, that a part was unavailable from Honda, or that an authorized repair facility was unable to effect a repair because Honda had failed to make sufficient replacement parts available.

      Plaintiffs also rely on Safety Recall 24-002 and note that the recall procedure was performed on the Acura on October 21, 2024. (Opp., p. 13; Duane Decl., ¶ 14, Ex. J; Evans Decl., ¶ 5, Ex. B.) That evidence likewise does not establish a violation of subdivision (a)(3). Plaintiffs identify no evidence that an authorized repair facility lacked the service literature or replacement parts necessary to perform the recall procedure. To the contrary, Plaintiffs acknowledge that the procedure was performed on their Acura.

Thus, the evidence identified by Plaintiffs does not establish that Honda failed to make sufficient service literature or replacement parts available to its authorized repair facilities. At most, it establishes that two particular parts were not physically present at a dealership when the vehicle was initially presented and had to be ordered. Plaintiffs cite no authority construing section 1793.2, subdivision (a)(3), as requiring every authorized repair facility to maintain every potentially necessary replacement part in its on-site inventory at all times. Accordingly, Plaintiffs have not demonstrated a triable issue of material fact as to whether Honda violated Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (a)(3). The motion for summary adjudication as to the third cause of action is GRANTED.

 

D. Fifth Cause of Action: Fraudulent Inducement – Concealment

      Plaintiffs’ fifth cause of action alleges Fraudulent Inducement – Concealment against Honda. “The required elements for fraudulent concealment are (1) concealment or suppression of a material fact; (2) by a defendant with a duty to disclose the fact to the plaintiff; (3) the defendant intended to defraud the plaintiff by intentionally concealing or suppressing the fact; (4) the plaintiff was unaware of the fact and would not have acted as he or she did if he or she had known of the concealed or suppressed fact; and (5) plaintiff sustained damage as a result of the concealment or suppression of the fact.” (Hambrick v. Healthcare Partners Medical Group, Inc. (2015) 238 Cal.App.4th 124, 162.)

      Honda argues that Plaintiffs’ fraudulent concealment claim fails as a matter of law because: (1) Plaintiffs have no evidence of concealment; (2) Honda had no duty to disclose additional information to Plaintiffs; (3) any affirmative misrepresentation theory fails; and (4) Rattagan v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1, bars the claim under the economic loss doctrine. (Mot., at pp. 13-20.)

1.     Honda’s Knowledge of Alleged Defect

      An essential element of intentional concealment includes the duty to disclose, which must be based upon a transaction, or a special relationship, between plaintiff and defendant.  (Bigler-Engler v. Breg, Inc. (2017) 7 Cal.App.5th 276, 311.) “There are ‘four circumstances in which nondisclosure or concealment may constitute actionable fraud: (1) when the defendant is in a fiduciary relationship with the plaintiff; (2) when the defendant had exclusive knowledge of material facts not known to the plaintiff; (3) when the defendant actively conceals a material fact from the plaintiff; and (4) when the defendant makes partial representations but also suppresses some material facts.’”  (Ibid.)  “[O]ther than the first instance, in which there must be a fiduciary relationship between the parties, ‘the other three circumstances in which nondisclosure may be actionable presuppose[ ] the existence of some other relationship between the plaintiff and defendant in which a duty to disclose can arise... “[W]here material facts are known to one party and not to the other, failure to disclose them is not actionable fraud unless there is some relationship between the parties which gives rise to a duty to disclose such known facts.”  [Citation.]’  [Citation.]”  (Hoffman v. 162 North Wolfe LLC (2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 1178, 1187 (Hoffman).)  “Such a transaction must necessarily arise from direct dealings between the plaintiff and the defendant; it cannot arise between the defendant and the public at large.”  (Bigler-Engler, supra, 7 Cal.App.5th at p. 312.)

      Plaintiffs allege as follows. Honda knew that the 9-speed transmission in the Acura was defective but failed to disclose this fact prior to and at the time of the sale. (Compl., ¶ 47.) These defects include: “(1) hesitation or delayed acceleration, (2) harsh or hard shifting, (3) jerking, (4) shuddering, or juddering; (5) surging and/or inability to control the vehicle's speed, acceleration, or deceleration, (6) symptoms requiring reprogramming of the transmission control module ("TCM") and/or powertrain control module ("PCM"), (7) failure or replacement of the transmission ("Transmission Defect").” (Id., ¶ 48.) Honda knew of said defects through “pre-production and post-production testing data; early consumer complaints about the Transmission Defect made directly to Defendant HONDA and its network of dealers; aggregate warranty data compiled from Defendant HONDA's network of dealers; testing conducted by Defendant HONDA in response to these complaints; as well as warranty repair and part replacements data received by Defendant HONDA from Defendant HONDA's network of dealers.” (Id., ¶ 49.) On information and belief, Plaintiffs claim that Honda knew of the transmission defect and its safety risks but failed to disclose them.  (Id., ¶ 51.) Had Plaintiffs known, they would not have purchased the Subject Vehicle. (Id.)

      Honda puts forth the following interrogatory responses in support of its argument that Plaintiffs have put no admissible evidence into the record to support their concealment theory: Special Interrogatory No. 23 seeks “all facts that support [Plaintiffs’] claim for fraudulent concealment; No. 24 asks Plaintiffs to identify all documents supporting said claim; No. 27 asks them to state all facts supporting their claim that an individual agent of Honda engaged in fraudulent conduct in relation to their purchase of the Acura; No. 28 asks them to state all facts supporting their claim that Honda concealed any information from Plaintiffs’ at or before the time they bought the Acura; No. 29 asks them to specifically describe what they contend Honda should have disclosed; and No. 30 asks them to state the amount of all damages they claim Honda is responsible for in relation to the fraud cause of action. (Honda’s UMF Nos. 36-37; Chong Decl., ¶ 3, Ex. A.) In response, “Plaintiffs identified no information or documents showing AHM had knowledge of any alleged transmission defect before they bought the Acura TLX, much less that it concealed any defect.” (Id.) Plaintiffs do not dispute this fact.

      The Court finds that Honda failed to meet its burden of showing that Plaintiffs cannot establish that Honda had prior knowledge of the defect.  Honda argues that the Plaintiffs’ discovery responses were deficient, which on its own is an insufficient argument.  To show that the challenged cause of action cannot be established, a defendant must also introduce evidence demonstrating that the “plaintiff not only does not have but cannot reasonably expect to obtain a prima facie case.  (Addy v. Bliss & Glennon (1996) 44 Cal.App.4th 205, 214 (Addy) (emphasis added).)  Honda fails to present any evidence showing that the Plaintiffs cannot reasonably expect to obtain a prima facie case for fraudulent inducement.

      In response, Plaintiffs produced the NHTSA documents noted above, that suggest that Honda knew of related transmission issues years before and after the Acura’s purchase date. (Duane Decl., Exs. A-D, F.) The Court finds that these documents are material to the instant case because: (1) they involve the same defect alleged, the vehicle’s transmission; (2) they were in the possession of Honda (contain Honda’s Copyright Registration Notice); and (3) the age of the documents suggest that Honda had notice of the defects before the vehicle in question in this lawsuit was manufactured or sold.

      The Court finds that this material difference in fact speaks directly to the first element of the fraudulent inducement claim.  Because these purported deficiencies were known to Honda well before the Acura was manufactured, it remains disputed as to whether Honda concealed or suppressed the deficiency.  (See Bank of America Corp, et al., v. Superior Court (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 862, 870).

2.      Duty to Disclose

      Honda next argues that Plaintiffs have failed to provide evidence of a relationship with Honda. (Mot., at pp. 14-15.) Honda points to Bjoin v. J-M Mfg. Co. (2025) 113 Cal.App.5th 884, 901-902, in which the Court of Appeal found that direct dealings between the parties are necessary to create a duty to disclose. (Id.)

      There is no universal duty to disclose.  For a defendant to be under a duty to disclose a material fact to the plaintiff, they must have had a relationship.  There are two relationships between parties that can support a duty of disclosure: a fiduciary relationship or a relationship that “c[a]me into being as the result of some sort of transaction between the parties” — more succinctly, a “transaction-based relationship.”  (LiMandri v. Judkins (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 326, 336-337 (LiMandri); Hoffman v. 162 North Wolfe LLC (2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 1178, 1192 (Hoffman).)  There are ‘four circumstances in which nondisclosure or concealment may constitute actionable fraud: (1) when the defendant is in a fiduciary relationship with the plaintiff; (2) when the defendant had exclusive knowledge of material facts not known to the plaintiff; (3) when the defendant actively conceals a material fact from the plaintiff; and (4) when the defendant makes partial representations but also suppresses some material facts.’ (LiMandri, supra, 52 Cal.App.4th at pg. 336.)

      Honda contends that Plaintiffs did not engage in a direct transaction with it because they purchased the Acura from an independently owned and operated dealership, Valencia Acura. (Mot., at p. 14; Honda’s UMF No. 1.)

      The Parties do not dispute that Plaintiffs purchased the Acura from Valencia Acura. (Honda’s UMF No. 1.) Honda submits the Retail Installment Sale Contract (“RISC”). (Evans Decl., Ex. A.) The Court notes that in the RISC, Plaintiff Tracy M. Smith-Wilson is named as the “Buyer” and the “Seller-Creditor” is listed as Valencia Acura. (Id.) Thus, the uncontested record shows that the Plaintiffs bought the Acura from Valencia Acura, not directly from Honda.

      Honda carries its burden to establish that there was no fiduciary or transactional relationship between the parties that gave rise to a duty to disclose facts as Honda is the manufacturer while Plaintiffs only entered into a contract with a third-party retailer. (See Hoffman, supra, 228 Cal.App.4th at 1187.)

      In opposition, Plaintiffs must allege that Honda knew of the alleged defect before the sale or lease of the vehicle, and that the defendant was unable or unwilling to fix it.  (Santana v. FCA (2020) 56 Cal.App.5th 334) [reversing a judgment for plaintiff since the record showed no substantial evidence that the defendant was aware of the alleged defect and thus could not have concealed it from plaintiff at the time of sale.) 

       Plaintiffs rely on Dhital v. Nissan North America, Inc. (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 828, in opposition to support their assertion that “[a] manufacturer that issues a written warranty to the consumer, authorizes dealerships to sell and service its vehicles on its behalf, and exercises control over warranty repairs stands in a relationship sufficient to support a duty to disclose material facts about the product.” (Opp., at p. 16.)

      A cursory review shows that there are several distinctions between this matter and the facts assumed in Dhital. Dhital found that the plaintiffs’ statutory warranty claims under the Song-Beverly Act were the equivalent of contract claims for the purposes of determining whether the economic loss rule applies; This did not, however, establish a duty to disclose. (Dhital, 84 Cal. App. 5th at 838, fn. 3.) Only later in its decision did the Dhital court state that the plaintiffs’ allegations were sufficient to overcome the defendant's argument that there was no buyer-seller relationship giving rise to a duty to disclose. (Id. at 844.)

      The most important considerations are that the instant case is at the Summary Judgment/Adjudication phase, not the pleading phase, and, as noted above, the record is replete with facts, some of which are uncontested: The Parties do not dispute that Plaintiffs purchased the Acura from Valencia Acura. (Honda’s UMF No. 1.) Honda submits the Retail Installment Sale Contract (“RISC”). (Evans Decl., Ex. A.) The Court notes that in the RISC, Plaintiff Tracy M. Smith-Wilson is named as the “Buyer” and the “Seller-Creditor” is listed as Valencia Acura. (Id.) Thus, the uncontested record shows that the Plaintiffs bought the Acura from Valencia Acura, not directly from Honda.

      Plaintiffs fail to present any additional facts concerning the relationship between Honda and themselves. Plaintiffs reference to marketing and advertisements are unpersuasive. (See Bjoin v. J-M Mfg. Co., 113 Cal.App.5th 884, 902 [“Such a transaction must necessarily arise from direct dealings between the plaintiff and the defendant; it cannot arise between the defendant and the public at large.’ (internal citation omitted)”].) The Court of Appeals in Bjoin specifically rejected the argument that a manufacturer’s advertising or profits from downstream sales create a fiduciary and/or transaction relationship. (Ibid.) As to the phone call and communications between Plaintiff Ronald Wilson and Acura Client Relations in September & October 2019, Plaintiff fails to offer evidence that the couple communications established a fiduciary relationship. (P.s’ Response to UMF Nos. 8-11.)

      Accordingly, the Court finds that Plaintiffs have failed to meet their burden to establish that Honda owed them a duty to disclose the alleged defects at the time of purchase. The motion for summary adjudication as to the fifth cause of action is GRANTED. The Court, however, considers the merits of Honda’s other arguments, as well.

3.     Affirmative Misrepresentation

      Honda argues that Plaintiffs’ Complaint fails to allege Honda made any affirmative misrepresentations to Plaintiffs; thus, summary adjudication is warranted. (Mot., at p. 15.)

      The Court finds this argument to be unpersuasive and irrelevant because Plaintiffs’ claim is for fraudulent inducement – concealment, not fraudulent misrepresentation. Plaintiffs have not put forth an affirmative misrepresentation theory or claim.

4.     Economic Loss Rule under Rattagan v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1

      Honda argues that the Supreme Court’s holding in Rattagan independently bars Plaintiffs’ fraud claim, because “the elements of the claim cannot be established independent of the parties’ contractual rights and obligations and because the alleged tortious conduct did not expose Plaintiff to a risk of harm beyond the reasonable contemplation of the parties when they entered into the contract.” (Rattagan, supra, 17 Cal.5th at 1; Mot., at pp. 16-20.)

      “In general, there is no recovery in tort for negligently inflicted ‘purely economic losses,’ meaning financial harm unaccompanied by physical or property damage.” (Rattagan, supra, 17 Cal.5th at p. 20.) The Rattagan court set forth a two-part inquiry as to whether the economic loss rule bars contractual fraud causes of action:(1) “A plaintiff must first demonstrate the defendant’s injury-causing conduct violated a duty that is independent of the duties and rights assumed by the parties when they entered the contract. … [and (2)] the defendant’s conduct must have caused injury to persons or property that was not reasonably contemplated by the parties when the contract was formed.” (Id. at pp. 20–21.) 

      Under the Economic Loss Rule, fraudulent concealment, occurring in the course of a contractual relationship, may be conduct independent of the contractual rights, where the risk of harm went beyond the reasonable contemplation of the parties.  (Rattagan, supra, 17 Cal.5th at p. 13.)

      Nevertheless, there is a core distinction between a tort committed during an ongoing contractual relationship (which may, or may not, be barred by the economic loss rule) and a tort committed in the formation of a contract.  When one party commits fraud during contract formation, the injured party may recover in both tort (for the fraudulent inducement) and in contract (by affirming the contract.  (Harris v. Atlantic Richfield (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 70, 77 (Harris).) Allowing recovery in tort for fraudulent inducement to enter a contract does not interfere with parties’ freedom to bargain over economic risk.  The fundamental nature of a fraudulent inducement claim is that the parties were not able to properly bargain over economic risk because an initial transaction was induced by fraud.  (See Id.) 

      As to the first Rattagan requirement, Honda argues that the basis of Plaintiffs’ claim was pre-contemplated under the express warranty at issue; thus, “[b]ecause Plaintiff[s] cannot prove any sort of fraud without showing a warranty breach, the claims are not independent.” (Mot., at p. 19.) In addition, “Plaintiffs did not suffer any injury unless they received a vehicle that was both defective and not reparable under the warranty.” (Id.) Therefore, Plaintiffs’ fraud claim cannot satisfy the first prong.

      As to the second requirement, Honda argues that its warranty expressly disclosed the risk of harm at issue – a defective vehicle. (Mot., at pp. 19-20.)

      Based on the foregoing and following, the Court finds that Honda has met its initial burden to establish a defense to Plaintiffs’ fifth cause of action. Plaintiffs’ fraud allegations pertain to the allegedly defective 9-speed transmission that was contemplated under the power-train warranty at issue. (Compl., ¶¶ 46-57.) In addition, Plaintiffs’ alleged damages are purely monetary: “Plaintiffs were harmed by purchasing a vehicle that Plaintiffs would not have leased and/or purchased had Plaintiffs known the true facts about the Transmission Defect.” (Compl., ¶ 57.) Plaintiffs do not allege physical injuries due to the alleged defects. The burden thus shifts to Plaintiffs to produce substantial responsive evidence to show that a triable issue of material fact remains.

      In opposition, Plaintiffs primarily rely on the holding in Dhital v. Nissan North America, Inc. (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 828, to support their contention that “the economic loss rule does not bar a claim for fraudulent inducement by concealment in the sale of a defective vehicle.” (Opp., at p. 18.) Plaintiffs argue that Rattagan does not reach the claims here because the alleged concealment occurred before the contract’s formation and the injury is “that Plaintiffs would not have purchased the vehicle at all had they known what AHM knew.” (Id.)

      In Dhital, the Court of Appeal found that the plaintiffs’ fraudulent inducement claim was not barred by the economic loss rule, because the plaintiffs sufficiently alleged fraudulent conduct that was independent of the defendant vehicle manufacturer’s alleged warranty breaches. (84 Cal.App.5th at 843.) The Court of Appeal, however, expressly left open the possibility that, based “on the evidentiary record developed at summary judgment or trial, a fraudulent inducement claim could be found not to be independent of a plaintiff’s contract or warranty claims.” (Dhital, supra, 84 Cal.App.5th at 842, fn.5 [The Supreme Court dismissed review and remanded after deciding Rattagan].) Notably, the Court of Appeal concluded that the allegations were sufficient in Dhital because: "Plaintiffs alleged that they bought the car from a Nissan dealership, that Nissan backed the car with an express warranty, and that Nissan's authorized dealerships are its agents for purposes of the sale of Nissan vehicles to consumers." (Dhital, 84 Cal.App.5th at 844.) Here, while Plaintiffs allege that they purchased the Acura from Honda’s authorized dealership, they do not allege, as in Dhital, that the dealer was Honda’s authorized agent. (Compl., ¶¶ 16, 49, Ex. A.)

      Plaintiffs failed to offer evidence of direct dealings with Honda and did not allege harm independent of their disappointed expectations concerning the Acura’s condition and warranty performance. Plaintiffs have not alleged personal injury or damage to property other than the Acura. Plaintiffs only seek economic losses associated with the allegedly defective Acura. Based on the foregoing, the Court finds that Plaintiffs fail to offer competent evidence of independent pre-sale fraud; thus, Plaintiffs have failed to meet their responsive evidentiary burden to show that a triable issue remains as to whether the fifth cause of action is barred by the economic loss rule. 

      Accordingly, the Court finds that the fifth cause of action is independently barred by the economic loss rule under Rattagan. 

 

E. Continuance

      Plaintiffs request a continuance to permit them to complete the deposition of Honda’s PMQ. “In seeking a continuance of a summary judgment motion, a plaintiff has essentially two options.” (Hamilton v. Orange County Sheriff's Dept. (2017) 8 Cal.App.5th 759, 764.) First, a plaintiff can seek a mandatory continuance under Code of Civil Procedure Section 437c subdivision (h), which states: “If it appears from the affidavits submitted in opposition to a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication,¿or both,¿that facts essential to justify opposition may exist but cannot, for reasons stated,¿be presented, the court shall deny the motion, order a continuance to permit affidavits to be obtained or discovery to be had, or make any other order as may be just. The application to continue the motion to obtain necessary discovery may also be made by ex parte motion at any time on or before the date the opposition response to the motion is due.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (h).) Second District precedent holds, “A declaration in support of a request for continuance under section 437c, subdivision (h) must show: ‘(1) the facts to be obtained are essential to opposing the motion; (2) there is reason to believe such facts may exist; and (3) the reasons why additional time is needed to obtain these facts. [Citations.]’” (Cooksey v. Alexakis (2004) 123 Cal.App.4th 246, 254.) 

      Second, “[w]here a plaintiff cannot make the showing required under section 437c, subdivision (h), a plaintiff may seek a continuance under the ordinary discretionary standard applied to requests for a continuance” which “requires a showing of good cause.”  (Hamilton, supra, 8 Cal.App.5th at p. 765.) “‘[I]n deciding whether to continue a summary judgment to permit additional discovery courts consider various factors, including (1) how long the case has been pending; (2) how long the requesting party had to oppose the motion; (3) whether the continuance motion could have been made earlier; (4) the proximity of the trial date or the 30-day discovery cutoff before trial; (5) any prior continuances for the same reason; and (6) the question whether the evidence sought is truly essential to the motion.’ [citation.]” (Ibid.)

      Plaintiffs have not filed any affidavit alongside their opposition, nor have they included a signed declaration that includes an amended notice of deposition for Honda’s PMQ. Plaintiffs have not put forth any argument in support of continuance; the request is merely thrown in at the end of the opposition’s conclusion. (Opp., at p. 20.) Plaintiffs’ counsel does not mention the continuance in her declaration either.

      Plaintiffs fail to present sufficient evidence to show they are entitled to a continuance of this motion. Plaintiffs therefore fail to establish why they believe additional facts exist to support their opposition. The request for a continuance is DENIED. 

                                                                                         VI.       CONCLUSION

      Based on the foregoing, Honda’s motion for summary judgment is DENIED. Honda’s motion for summary adjudication as to the second, third, fourth, and fifth causes of action is GRANTED. Honda’s motion for summary adjudication as to the first cause of action is DENIED.

      Honda is ordered to give notice.   


[1] In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that Honda’s motion is procedurally defective because it only addresses the transmission defects and not the engine and electrical defects. The Court finds Plaintiffs’ argument unpersuasive. The engine and electrical defects are only mentioned a single time, in paragraph 11 of the Complaint, in a general, kitchen-sink sentence. The pleadings “delimit the scope of the issues,” so Honda needed only to negate the theories actually and adequately framed by the Complaint. (Lee v. Bank of Am., 27 Cal.App.4th 197, 216.) Thus, Plaintiffs cannot defeat the motion by resting on defects (i.e. the engine and electrical defects) pleaded too thinly to raise.

25STCV31737 Kevin Ward v. Southern California Gas Company
Thursday, September 10, 2026
TENTATIVE ORDER GRANTING WITH LEAVE TO AMEND DEFENDANT SOUTHERN CALIFORNIA GAS COMPANY’S MOTION TO STRIKE PLAINTIFF’S REQUESTS FOR PUNITIVE DAMAGES  [Res. No. -5018]

 

                                                                                         I.         BACKGROUND

      The first amended complaint alleges that Defendant, Southern California Gas Company (Defendant), negligently performed maintenance near Plaintiff’s property in a manner that that caused soil erosion and flooding on Plaintiff’s property. As alleged, Defendant was aware of the impact on Plaintiff’s property from at least February 2023 and repeatedly claimed it intended to remedy the issue, but as of April 2026 no final design plan had been completed or confirmed. Plaintiff asserts claims for negligence, trespass, private nuisance, public nuisance, inverse condemnation, and declaratory and injunctive relief regarding the use of an easement.

                                                                                          II.        ARGUMENTS

A.     Motion filed May 26, 2026.

      Plaintiff’s complaint does not contain factual allegations demonstrating oppression, fraud, or malice, nor has he alleged facts sufficient to ascribe the necessary state of mind to a corporate defendant.

B.     Opposition filed August 27, 2026.

      Defendant made “institutional decisions” that disregard Plaintiff’s property rights in a manner that amounts to malice or oppression.

C.     Reply filed Septmber 1, 2026.

      Plaintiff’s opposition fails to identify facts in his pleading sufficient to demonstrate malice or oppression, and his “institutional decision” argument is unsupported by law.

 

                                                                                       III.       LEGAL STANDARDS

      “The court may, upon a motion made pursuant to Section 435 [notice of motion to strike whole or part of complaint], or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper: (a) Strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading.” (Id. § 436 (a).) Irrelevant matters include immaterial allegations that are not essential to the claim or those not pertinent to or supported by an otherwise sufficient claim. (Id., § 431.10.) The court may also “[s]trike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court.” (Id. § 436 (b).)           A motion to strike is also the proper procedure to attack a purportedly improper remedy such as unjustified punitive damages. (Venice Town Council, Inc. v. City of Los Angeles (1996) 47 Cal.App.4th 1547, 1561-1562.) A complaint including a request for punitive damages must also include allegations showing that the plaintiff is entitled to such an award. (Clauson v. Superior Court (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1253, 1255.)

 

                                                                                                IV.       DISCUSSION

      The motion is granted. Plaintiff’s complaint fails to identify decision-makers within Defendant’s management structure that had advance knowledge sufficient to act with malice, etc., and he fails to plead facts showing Defendant ratified an employee’s torts after the fact. To the extent Plaintiff alleges Defendant has dragged its feet after the alleged nuisance was brought to Defendant’s attention, Plaintiff fails to allege specific facts showing malice, etc. sufficient for punitive damages.

 

                                                                                          V.        CONCLUSION

      Based on the foregoing, Defendant’s motion is GRANTED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND. Plaintiff is ordered to file an amended pleading within 30 days or to stand on his operative pleading subject to this order.


Case Number: 25STCV35554    Hearing Date: September 10, 2026    Dept: 414

25STCV35554 Michele Nasatir v. David Arakelyan, et al.

Thursday, September 10, 2026

TENTATIVE ORDER SUSTAINING DEFENDANT OSCAR CASTILLO’S DEMURRER TO THE FIRST AMENDED COMPLAINT [Res. No. -4388]

 

I. BACKGROUND

          The first amended complaint alleges that Defendant David Arakelyan (“Arakelyan”) fraudulently obtained Plaintiff’s quarterly tax payment check for $118,600.00 sometime between September 1 to 10, 2024. Arakelyan then “washed” the check, altering the payee and making the check payable to “David Arakelian (Arakelyan) – Od Exotic Rental [sic] LLC.” Arakelyan and Defendant Oscar Castillo (“Castillo”) are members of Od Exotic Rentals LLC. Plaintiff alleges 4 causes of action for conversion, fraudulent misrepresentation, violation of Penal Code section 496, and negligence.

 

II. LEGAL STANDARD

               Where pleadings are defective, a party may raise the defect by demurrer. (Coyne v. Krempels (1950) 36 Cal.2d 257, 262.) A demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the pleading; the Court accepts properly pleaded material facts as true, but not contentions, deductions, or conclusions of fact or law. (CCP, § 430.30(a); Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318; Daar v. Yellow Cab Co. (1967) 67 Cal.2d 695, 713.) The Court does not determine whether the plaintiff can ultimately prove properly pleaded allegations. (Fisher v. San Pedro Peninsula Hospital (1989) 214 Cal.App.3d 590, 604.)

          Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.)¿The burden is on the complainant to show the Court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Ibid.)

               Allegations on information and belief are permitted as to matters outside a plaintiff’s personal knowledge where the plaintiff has information leading to a belief that the allegations are true. But the label “information and belief” does not permit a plaintiff simply to plead the missing element of a cause of action. A plaintiff may plead matters not within his or her personal knowledge on information and belief, and ordinarily need plead ultimate rather than evidentiary facts. But an allegation made on information and belief must rest upon some information that leads the plaintiff to believe the allegation is true; a bare assertion of the ultimate fact, without any alleged basis for that belief, does not itself supply the missing factual predicate. (Doe v. City of Los Angeles (2007) 42 Cal.4th 531, 550-551 & fn. 5; Pridonoff v. Balokovich (1951) 36 Cal.2d 788, 792.)

III. DISCUSSION

          Castillo demurs to Plaintiff’s first and third causes of action on the grounds that the FAC fails to sufficiently plead a claim against Castillo.

A. First Cause of Action: Conversion

          Conversion is the wrongful exercise of dominion over the property of another.  “The elements of a conversion claim are: (1) the plaintiff’s ownership or right to possession of the property; (2) the defendant’s conversion by a wrongful act or disposition of property rights; and (3) damages.” (Lee v. Hanley (2015) 61 Cal.4th 1225, 1240.) “It is not necessary that there be a manual taking of the property; it is only necessary to show an assumption of control or ownership over the property, or that the alleged converter has applied the property to¿his own use.”¿(Farmers Ins. Exchange v. Zerin¿(1997) 53 Cal.App.4th 445, 452.)¿Conversion is a strict liability tort; thus,¿intent is not a necessary¿component. (See¿Burlesci¿v. Petersen¿(1998) 68¿Cal.App.4th 1062, 1066 [“[A]ction rests neither in the knowledge nor the¿intent¿of the defendant. . . .¿Therefore, questions of the defendant's good faith, lack of knowledge, and motive are ordinarily immaterial.”].)¿Money can be the subject¿of an action for conversion if¿there is¿a specific¿sum¿capable of identification. (See¿Farmers Ins. Exchange v. Zerin¿(1997) 53 Cal.App.4th 445, 452.) 

               Castillo argues that the FAC does not allege facts showing that he personally took the check or actually exercised dominion over the $118,600.00. He emphasizes that the new allegation that he was an authorized signatory establishes only authority to access the LLC account, not that he knew of this deposit or exercised control over these particular funds. (Mot., at pp. 6-9.) He further argues that personal liability cannot rest merely on LLC membership or account authority. Plaintiff responds that the allegations of signatory authority, control over the company’s finances, and the suspicious nature of the check permit an inference of Castillo’s participation and knowledge. (Opp., at pp. 5-9.)

               The FAC alleges that Plaintiff mailed a check for $118,600.00 to the United States Treasury, with “2024 Form 1041-ES” on the memo line; that Arakelyan stole and washed the check; and that the altered check was deposited into a BOA account associated with Od Exotic. (FAC ¶¶ 10, 12-13, 17-18.) Arakelyan and Castillo allegedly were the only authorized signatories on that account and therefore had authority to access, withdraw, transfer, and direct the disposition of funds deposited into it. (FAC ¶¶ 18-19.) The FAC then alleges that Castillo was “not a passive or nominal participant,” exercised control over the company’s finances and banking activities, “knew or had reason to know” the $118,600.00 was not derived from a legitimate transaction, and “authorized, ratified, or permitted” its retention and use. (FAC ¶ 29.) It further alleges that Castillo “knowingly retained, controlled, and exercised dominion” over Plaintiff’s funds. (FAC ¶ 30.)

               Those latter allegations state the required conclusions, but the FAC does not plead the facts that support them as to Castillo. The distinction is important on demurrer. The Court accepts properly pleaded facts, not a conclusion merely because it is introduced on “information and belief” or expressed in the language of an element. (Doe v. City of Los Angeles, supra, 42 Cal.4th at pp. 550-551 & fn. 5.) The FAC identifies facts supporting Arakelyan’s participation: he allegedly stole and altered the check and caused it to be deposited. By contrast, as to Castillo, the pleaded facts establish only his status as a member or principal of Od Exotic and his authority as one of two signatories on the company account. The FAC does not allege that Castillo saw or endorsed the check, knew when or by whom it was deposited, withdrew or transferred any portion of the proceeds, directed their use, received a distribution, concealed the funds, refused a demand for their return, or otherwise took an identified act with respect to the $118,600.00. Authority to exercise control is not itself an allegation that control was actually exercised.

               Corporations Code section 17703.04 confirms the same distinction. An LLC’s liabilities do not become the liabilities of a member or manager merely because of that status, although a member remains liable for his or her own participation in tortious conduct. (Corp. Code, § 17703.04, subds. (a)(2), (c); People v. Pacific Landmark, LLC (2005) 129 Cal.App.4th 1203, 1216.) Thus, the issue is not whether Castillo theoretically could have controlled the account, but whether facts are alleged showing his own participation in the asserted conversion. In Holistic Supplements, LLC v. Stark (2021) 61 Cal.App.5th 530, 544, the individual defendants were alleged to have personally participated in the conduct constituting conversion. Here, the FAC supplies no comparable factual allegations as to Castillo.

               Plaintiff’s contrary argument effectively asks the Court to infer actual dominion from the combination of Castillo’s status, signatory authority, and the fact that the funds entered the LLC account. That inference does not follow from the facts pleaded. Two people having authority over an account does not mean that both knew of, much less exercised dominion over, every deposit into it. Nor does the allegation that no legitimate transaction justified the payment establish what Castillo personally knew or did. Without facts connecting Castillo to the particular deposit or its subsequent disposition, the allegations that he “authorized,” “ratified,” “retained,” or “controlled” the funds remain unsupported conclusions. The first cause of action therefore does not sufficiently allege Castillo’s personal participation in the conversion.

               The problem here is not that Plaintiff pleads on information and belief, nor that Plaintiff fails to plead evidentiary detail. The problem is that the pleaded facts do not provide a factual basis from which Castillo’s knowledge or personal exercise of dominion may reasonably be inferred. The FAC alleges that Castillo was a member of the LLC and an authorized signatory with access to the account, and then alleges that he “knew,” “authorized,” “ratified,” and “knowingly retained” the funds. But it alleges no facts showing that Castillo saw the check, knew of the deposit, communicated with Arakelyan about it, directed any transfer or use of the funds, received any portion of them, or otherwise personally participated in their disposition. Pleading those ultimate conclusions on information and belief does not cure the absence of alleged facts supplying a basis for the inference. (Doe v. City of Los Angeles, supra, 42 Cal.4th at pp. 550-551 & fn. 5; Pridonoff v. Balokovich, supra, 36 Cal.2d at p. 792.)Accordingly, the Court sustains Castillo’s demurrer to the first cause of action with leave to amend.

 

B. Third Cause of Action: Violation of Penal Code section 496

          “Every person who buys or receives any property that has been stolen or that has been obtained in any manner constituting theft or extortion, knowing the property to be so stolen or obtained, or who conceals, sells, withholds, or aids in concealing, selling, or withholding any property from the owner, knowing the property to be so stolen or obtained, shall be punished…” (Pen. Code, § 496 subd. (a).) “To prove theft, a plaintiff must establish criminal intent on the part of the defendant beyond “mere proof of nonperformance or actual falsity.” [Citation.] This requirement prevents “‘[o]rdinary commercial defaults’” from being transformed into a theft.” (Siry Investment, L.P. v. Farkhondehpour (2022) 13 Cal.5th 333, 361–362.)

               Castillo argues that the section 496 claim suffers from the same defect and an additional one: the FAC does not plead facts showing that he actually knew the funds were stolen. (Mot., at pp. 10-11.) Plaintiff responds that knowledge may be inferred circumstantially from Castillo’s role in the company, his signatory authority, the absence of a legitimate transaction, and the facial irregularities on the washed check. (Opp., at pp. 7-9.)

               The FAC alleges that Arakelyan stole and washed the check and rerouted the proceeds by depositing the check into Od Exotic’s BOA account. (FAC ¶¶ 40-41.) It then alleges that Castillo, as a principal and authorized signatory, “received and exercised dominion and control” over the stolen funds and “directly participated in and controlled” their disposition and use. (FAC ¶ 42.) As to knowledge, the FAC alleges that Castillo “knew or had reason to know” the deposit was derived from theft because he was one of only two persons operating and controlling the business, no legitimate transaction justified the payment, and the altered payee was inconsistent with the tax-payment notation on the check. (FAC ¶ 43.)

               These allegations do not sufficiently plead actual knowledge. Penal Code section 496 requires knowledge that the property was stolen or obtained by theft; constructive notice, negligence, or facts showing only that a person “should have known” are not substitutes for that element. Plaintiff is correct that actual knowledge may be established by circumstantial evidence. But at the pleading stage there still must be facts from which the inference of Castillo’s actual knowledge reasonably arises. The FAC alleges none showing that Castillo ever saw the washed check or its memo line, learned of the deposit, reviewed the account activity, discussed the funds with Arakelyan, participated in a withdrawal or transfer, or otherwise encountered circumstances from which his actual knowledge of the theft can be inferred. The asserted irregularities on the face of the check cannot support an inference of Castillo’s knowledge absent a factual allegation that he saw or was otherwise made aware of the check.

               The information-and-belief allegations do not cure that omission. The FAC does not identify information upon which Plaintiff’s belief in Castillo’s knowledge is founded beyond his status, account authority, and the absence of a legitimate transaction. Those circumstances may support suspicion or a theory that Castillo should have discovered the deposit, but they do not supply facts showing actual knowledge. Nor may Arakelyan’s alleged knowledge simply be imputed to Castillo because both were members, principals, or signatories of the same LLC. The FAC alleges no agency, communication, participation, or other factual basis from which Arakelyan’s knowledge of his own alleged theft can be attributed to Castillo personally. The section 496 claim therefore rests on speculation rather than pleaded facts supporting the required knowledge and personal receipt, withholding, concealment, or control of stolen property. Accordingly, the Court sustains Castillo’s demurrer to the third cause of action with leave to amend.

CONCLUSION

          Based on the foregoing, Castillo’s demurrer as to the first and third causes of action in the FAC is SUSTAINED with 30 days leave to amend.

 

 


Case Number: 26STCV15700    Hearing Date: September 10, 2026    Dept: 414

26STCV15700 Rommel Herrera Ramirez v. Essex Property Trust, Inc., et al.

Thursday, September 10, 2026

TENTATIVE ORDER SUSTAINING IN PART AND GRANTING IN PART DEFENDANT ESSEX PROPERTY TRUST, INC.’S DEMURRER TO AND MOTION TO STRIKE PORTIONS OF PLAINTIFF’S SECOND AMENDED COMPLAINT  [Res. No. -3910]

 

                                                                                         I.         BACKGROUND

      The second amended complaint alleges that demurring/moving Defendant, Essex Property Trust, Inc., and its co-defendants illegally evicted Plaintiff from his residential rental unit in violation of various tenant protection and anti-discrimination laws. Plaintiff asserts the following claims:

                        1. Wrongful Eviction

                        2. Violation of L.A. Just Cause Ordinance

                        3. Defective Notice to Pay or Quit

                        4. Forcible Entry/Detainer

                        5. Violation of Civil Code section 789.3

                        6. Source-of-Income Discrimination (Government Code)

                        7. Source-of-Income Discrimination (L.A. Municipal Code)

                        8. Bane Act (Civ. Code, § 52.1)

                        9. Conversion

                        10. Breach of Quiet Enjoyment

                        11. Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress

                        12. Rosenthal FDCPA Violation

      Defendant Essex Property Trust, Inc., Plaintiff’s former landlord, demurs to all causes of action in Plaintiff’s complaint and moves to strike several paragraphs of Plaintiff’s prayer for relief.

       

                                                                                       II.        LEGAL STANDARDS

      Where pleadings are defective, a party may raise the defect by way of a demurrer. (Coyne v. Krempels (1950) 36 Cal.2d 257, 262.) A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint alleges facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. (Cal. Code Civ. Proc. § 430.10; Young v. Gannon (2002) 97 Cal.App.4th 209, 220.) When considering a demurrer, a court reads the allegations stated in the challenged pleading liberally and in context, and “treat[s] the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Serrano v. Priest (1971) 5 Cal.3d 584, 591.) Put differently: for purposes of demurrer, the court treats all facts alleged – but only the facts alleged – in the complaint as true. (Picton v. Anderson Union High School District (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 726, 732.) “The only issue involved in a demurrer hearing is whether the complaint, as it stands, unconnected with extraneous matters, states a cause of action.” (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)

      “The court may, upon a motion made pursuant to Section 435 [notice of motion to strike whole or part of complaint], or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper: (a) Strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading.” (Id. § 436 (a).) Irrelevant matters include immaterial allegations that are not essential to the claim or those not pertinent to or supported by an otherwise sufficient claim. (Id., § 431.10.) The court may also “[s]trike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court.” (Id. § 436 (b).)

 

                                                                                                III.       DISCUSSION

      A. First Cause of Action for Wrongful Eviction

      “ ‘[A] person in peaceable possession of real property may recover, in an action sounding in tort, damages for injuries to his person and goods caused by the forcible entry of one who is, or claims to be, the lawful owner or possessor ... .’ [Citations.]”  (Spinks v. Equity Residential Briarwood Apartments (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1004, 1039.)

      As alleged, Defendant filed an unlawful detainer action against Plaintiff in May 2025. (SAC, ¶ 30.) The parties signed a stipulated judgment for possession on November 4, 2025. (Id., ¶ 41.) However, the stipulated judgment (1) “struck through” a “pre-printed waiver of remaining claims” and (2) “expressly provided ... : ‘Plaintiff may bring premature lockout claims in the event of a forcible lockout.’ ” (Id., ¶¶ 41-42.)

      According to the SAC, on March 9, 2026, Defendant’s agent “request[ed] that Plaintiff schedule a ‘pre move out inspection’ of the Premises.” (Id., ¶ 43.) Later that morning, “Plaintiff returned to the Premises to find the lockout already in progress”, with no Sheriff present, and without having received five days’ notice of the lockout pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 715.010. (Id., ¶¶ 43-45.)

      Plaintiff alleges Defendant ousted him from the property while he was still in lawful possession, without proper use of legal process, and in violation of the terms of the parties’ stipulated unlawful detainer judgment. This is sufficient to state a claim for wrongful eviction. (See Zimmerman v. Stotter (1984) 160 Cal.App.3d 1067, 1074-1075 [wrongful eviction plaintiff did not litigate “the right [to possession], but the abuse of that right”]; cf. Needelman v. DeWolf Realty Co., Inc. (2015) 239 Cal.App.4th 750, 758-761 [wrongful eviction unavailable because of waiver in stipulated unlawful detainer judgment[.)

      The demurrer to the first cause of action is overruled.

 

B. Second and Third Causes of Action for Violation of Just Cause Ordinance and Defective Notice to Pay or Quit

     

      As to the third cause of action, Plaintiff’s alleged violations of the unlawful detainer statutes do not themselves create an independent cause of action for damages. Those alleged defects concern the validity of the underlying unlawful detainer proceedings and could have been raised as defenses in that action. Plaintiff may not recast alleged procedural defects in the unlawful detainer proceeding as a separate statutory damages claim. Accordingly, the demurrer to the third cause of action is sustained without leave to amend.

      The Court reaches a different conclusion as to the second cause of action, which alleges violations of the Los Angeles Just Cause for Eviction Ordinance (JCO). Unlike provisions that merely supply defenses to an unlawful detainer action, the JCO expressly creates a private civil remedy. LAMC section 165.07 provides that, in addition to permitting a tenant to raise a landlord’s noncompliance as an affirmative defense in an action for possession, “any landlord who violates this article shall be liable in a civil action to the affected tenant.” The ordinance further authorizes compensatory damages, civil penalties, attorney fees and costs, and other appropriate relief.

      Plaintiff alleges specific violations of duties imposed by the JCO. Among other things, he alleges that Defendant failed to provide him with the required written notice of JCO protections and failed to post the required notification in an accessible common area, as required by LAMC section 165.05(A). Plaintiff further alleges that Defendant failed to file the termination notice with the Los Angeles Housing Department as required by section 165.05(B)(5). These are affirmative requirements imposed by the JCO, and section 165.07 expressly provides a civil remedy to an affected tenant for a landlord’s violation of the article.

That some of these alleged violations may also have provided defenses in the underlying unlawful detainer proceeding does not, standing alone, eliminate the separate civil remedy expressly created by section 165.07. To the extent Plaintiff seeks to relitigate the validity of the stipulated judgment itself or matters necessarily resolved by that judgment, different preclusion or collateral-attack principles may apply. But Plaintiff’s allegations of independent violations of the JCO are not subject to demurrer merely because those violations could also have been raised in the unlawful detainer proceeding. Accordingly, the demurrer to the second cause of action is overruled.

     

C. Fourth Cause of Action for Forcible Entry/Detainer

      An action for forcible detainer is governed by Code of Civil Procedure section 1160, which provides in relevant part:

      “(a) Every person is guilty of a forcible detainer who either:

            (1) By force, or by menaces and threats of violence, unlawfully holds and keeps the possession of any real property, whether the same was acquired peaceably or otherwise.

            (2) Who ... during the absence of the occupant of any lands, unlawfully enters upon real property, and who, after demand made for the surrender thereof, for the period of five days, refuses to surrender the same to such former occupant.”

      The SAC does not clearly allege that Defendant presently and unlawfully holds possession of the property against Plaintiff’s current right to possession. To the contrary, Plaintiff repeatedly refers to a stipulated judgment pursuant to which he appears to have surrendered his right to possession. That judgment, however, is not attached to or incorporated into the SAC. Nor does it appear to have been included as Exhibit I to either the original or first amended complaint, as the SAC alleges.

      The distinction is material. A forcible detainer claim concerns Defendant’s unlawful withholding of possession from a plaintiff entitled to possession. If Plaintiff no longer claims a present right to possession and instead seeks damages based upon a completed dispossession or eviction, the allegations do not state a distinct claim for forcible detainer and instead appear to overlap with Plaintiff’s wrongful eviction claim. The SAC does not presently allege facts sufficient to establish the former. Accordingly, the demurrer to the fourth cause of action is sustained with leave to amend. 

 

D. Fifth Cause of Action for Violation of Civil Code section 789.3

      Plaintiff adequately alleges a claim for violation of Civil Code section 789.3, which provides in relevant part:

      “[A] landlord shall not, with intent to terminate the occupancy under any lease or other tenancy or estate at will, however created, of property used by a tenant as his or her residence, willfully: (1) Prevent the tenant from gaining reasonable access to the property by changing the locks or using a bootlock or by any other similar method or device[.]”

      Plaintiff alleges that Defendant, his landlord, changed the locks to the property with the intent to terminate Plaintiff’s occupancy. Plaintiff further alleges that, at the time the locks were changed, he retained a right to occupy the premises pursuant to a written agreement, which he identifies as the stipulated judgment. Although the precise nature of Plaintiff’s continued right of occupancy is not entirely clear from the SAC, section 789.3 is not limited to occupancy under a lease, but applies to “any lease or other tenancy or estate at will, however created.” Plaintiff’s allegations are therefore sufficient at the pleading stage to state a claim under section 789.3. The demurrer to the fifth cause of action is overruled.

     

      E. Sixth and Seventh Causes of Action for Source-of-Income Discrimination

      In support of his discrimination claims, Plaintiff contends only that Defendant first accepted, then refused income-based rental assistance. This is insufficient to show the discriminatory intent necessary for Plaintiff’s sixth or seventh causes of action. Plaintiff cites a settlement with the California Civil Rights Department in which Defendants admitted no liability. (See Compl., Exh. A, ¶ 3.)  This is insufficient. Plaintiff does not allege facts sufficient to state a claim for discrimination. The demurrer to the sixth and seventh causes of action is sustained with leave to amend.

     

F. Eighth Cause of Action for Violation of the Bane Act (Civ. Code, § 52.1)

      Civil Code section 52.1 authorizes an individual whose exercise or enjoyment of rights secured by federal or state law has been interfered with, or attempted to be interfered with, to bring a civil action for damages. (Civ. Code, § 52.1(c).) To state a claim, however, the alleged interference must be accomplished “by threats, intimidation, or coercion.” (Id., subd. (b).)

Plaintiff alleges that Defendant interfered with his possessory and tenancy rights by unlawfully changing the locks and excluding him from his unit. The Bane Act, however, “requires a showing of threatening conduct independent from the alleged interference or violation of a civil right.” (Animal Protection & Rescue League, Inc. v. County of Riverside (2025) 111 Cal.App.5th 914, 922, quoting Doe v. State of California (2017) 8 Cal.App.5th 832, 842-843.) Here, the alleged lockout constitutes the asserted violation of Plaintiff’s rights; the SAC does not allege separate threats, intimidation, or coercion used to accomplish or further that violation. The alleged lockout itself therefore cannot satisfy both elements of the claim.  Accordingly, the demurrer to the eighth cause of action is sustained with leave to amend. 

 

G. Ninth Cause of Action for Conversion

      A cause of action for conversion requires allegations of “(1) the plaintiff’s ownership or right to possession of personal property; (2) defendant’s disposition of the property inconsistent with plaintiff’s rights; and (3) resulting damages.” (Fremont Indemnity Co. v. Fremont General Corp. (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 97, 119.)

      Plaintiff adequately alleges each element. He alleges that his personal property remained inside the unit when Defendant unlawfully took possession of the premises and, in doing so, also took possession and control of Plaintiff’s personal property. These allegations sufficiently plead Plaintiff’s right to possession of the personal property, Defendant’s exercise of control over it inconsistent with Plaintiff’s rights and resulting damages. The demurrer to the ninth cause of action is overruled.   

 

H. Tenth Cause of Action for Breach of the Covenant of Quiet Enjoyment

      It is unclear whether Plaintiff may state a claim based on the covenant of quiet enjoyment where his right to possession depended on a stipulated judgment that is not included with his complaint, and not necessarily on the underlying lease. His tenth cause of action also arguably duplicates his claims for wrongful eviction and illegal lockout (his first and fifth). The demurrer to the tenth cause of action is sustained with leave to amend.

 

I. Eleventh Cause of Action for Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress

      Plaintiff has stated a claim for intentional infliction of emotional distress. He alleges Defendant illegally ejected him from his home without proper notice while aware that Plaintiff suffered from an illness that required a consistent treatment regimen and was assisted by at least one emotional support animal. (See SAC, ¶¶ 52-58.) The Court finds this sufficient to describe intentional extreme, outrageous conduct perpetrated with knowledge it would cause Plaintiff extreme distress. (See Stoiber v. Honeychuck (1980) 101 Cal.App.3d 903, 921 [elements].) The demurrer to the eleventh cause of action is overruled.

 

J. Twelfth Cause of Action for Unfair Debt Collection Practices

      Under the Rosenthal Act, a “debt collector” is a person who, in the ordinary course of business, regularly engages in “debt collection,” meaning acts or practices undertaken in connection with the collection of consumer debts. A “consumer debt,” in turn, must arise from a “consumer credit transaction”—a transaction in which a natural person acquires property, services, or money on credit primarily for personal, family, or household purposes. (Davidson v. Seterus, Inc. (2018) 21 Cal.App.5th 283, 295.)

      Plaintiff alleges that Defendant maintains a unit that, in the ordinary course of its business, collects debts owed by former tenants. Those allegations, however, do not establish that the particular debt Defendant allegedly sought to collect from Plaintiff constitutes a consumer debt within the meaning of the statute. Although Plaintiff appears to allege that he owed money under the stipulated judgment, he does not allege facts showing that this obligation arose from property, services, or money extended to him on credit by Defendant as part of a consumer credit transaction. The mere existence of a monetary obligation arising from the stipulated judgment does not, without more, satisfy that requirement. Plaintiff therefore fails to allege a consumer debt subject to the Rosenthal Act or, correspondingly, the FDCPA. The demurrer to the twelfth cause of action is sustained with leave to amend.  

 

K. Motion to Strike

      Defendant’s motion to strike is DENIED AS MOOT for paragraph G of Plaintiff’s prayer for relief, which follows from a cause of action to which the demurrer has been sustained.

      Defendant’s motion to strike is DENIED for paragraph H of Plaintiff’s prayer for relief. Plaintiff has alleged malice or oppression sufficient to sustain a claim for punitive damages based on Defendant’s malicious disregard of his rights as a tenant, repeatedly ratified after the illegal lockout by Defendant’s failure to restore Plaintiff to possession or allow him to promptly retrieve his belongings.

      Defendant’s motion to strike is GRANTED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND for paragraphs L and M of Plaintiff’s prayer for relief. Particularly given the breadth of the Court’s ruling on Defendant’s demurrer, the basis for Plaintiff’s broad requested relief is unclear.

      Defendant’s motion to strike is DENIED for paragraph 46 of Plaintiff’s complaint. The allegation is related to several of Plaintiff’s claims that survive demurrer and it is not improper for inclusion in his pleading.

 

                                                                                         IV.       CONCLUSION

      Defendant’s demurrer is OVERRULED as to Plaintiff’s first, second, fifth, ninth, and eleventh causes of action. Defendant’s demurrer is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND as to Plaintiff’s fourth, sixth, seventh, eighth, tenth, and twelfth causes of action. Defendant’s demurrer is SUSTAINED WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND as to Plaintiff’s third causes of action.

      Defendant’s motion to strike is DENIED as to paragraph 46 of Plaintiff’s SAC and paragraph H of his prayer for relief. Defendant’s motion to strike is DENIED AS MOOT as to paragraph G of Plaintiff’s prayer for relief. Defendant’s motion is GRANTED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND as to paragraphs L and M of Plaintiff’s prayer for relief.

      Plaintiff is ordered to file a third amended complaint within 30 days of this Order. The Court notes that exhibits, to the limited extent they are necessary or appropriate, may not be included by reference to prior iterations of Plaintiff’s filings.
