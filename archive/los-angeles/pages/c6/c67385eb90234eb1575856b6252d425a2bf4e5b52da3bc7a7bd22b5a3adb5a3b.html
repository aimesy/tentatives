DEPARTMENT 400 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 24STCV07875    Hearing Date: July 23, 2026    Dept: 400

MOTION TO QUASH

 

            The Court tenders the following tentative decision in the matter O.A., et al. v. Jessica Devita, Los Angeles County Superior Court case number 24STCV07875, set for hearing on July 7, 2026. O.A., J.A., and M.A. (collectively, Plaintiffs) move for an order quashing third party subpoenas for the production of business records served on the following parties by Jessica Devita (Defendant) pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 1985.3 and 1987.1: (1) Santa Monica Malibu Unified School District (SMMUSD); (2) Fusion Academy (Fusion); (3) Dr. Anshu Batra; (4) Joseph Taravella, Ph.D.; (5) Dr. Richard Boles; (7) Dr. Lev Gottlieb; (8) UCLA Health Systems – M.R.; (9) UCLA Health Systems – Billing; (10) UCLA Health Systems – Radiology; (11) Dr. Manny Tau, Psy. D.; (12) Westside Regional Center (WRC); (13) Beyond Karate (BK); (14) Lagerlof, LLP; (15) Denis A. Perez, LCSW; (16) Sinclair Broadcasting Group C/O Ct Corp. (Sinclair); and (17) Univision Emerging Networks, LLC C/O CT Corp. (Univision). Defendant opposes the motion. The motion is granted in part.

 

A.    Legal Standard

 

“If a subpoena requires the attendance of a witness or the production of books, documents, electronically stored information, or other things . . . at the taking of a deposition, the court, upon motion reasonably made by any person described in subdivision (b) . . . may make an order quashing the subpoena entirely, modifying it, or directing compliance with it upon those terms or conditions as the court shall declare, including protective orders. In addition, the court may make any other order as may be appropriate to protect the person from unreasonable or oppressive demands, including unreasonable violations of the right of privacy of the person.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.1, subd. (a).) Any party to the action may make such a motion. (Id., § 1987.1, subd. (b)(1).) “Any consumer whose personal records are sought by a subpoena duces tecum and who is a party to the civil action in which this subpoena duces tecum is served may, prior to the date for production, bring a motion under Section 1987.1 to quash or modify the subpoena duces tecum. Notice of the bringing of that motion shall be given to the witness and deposition officer at least five days prior to production.” (Id., § 1985.3, subd. (g).)

 

“For discovery purposes, information is relevant if it ‘might reasonably assist a party in evaluating the case, preparing for trial, or facilitating settlement.’ [Citation]. Admissibility is not the test and information, unless privileged, is discoverable if it might reasonably lead to admissible evidence. [Citation] These rules are applied liberally in favor of discovery.” (Gonzales v. Superior Court (1995) 33 Cal.App.4th 1539, 1546.) “[A] party seeking to compel production of records from a nonparty must articulate specific facts justifying the discovery sought; it may not rely on mere generalities.” (County of Los Angeles v. Superior Court (2021) 65 Cal.App.5th 621, 639 (Los Angeles).) There is a “more limited scope of discovery available from nonparties.” (Ibid.)

 

“In making an order pursuant to motion made under subdivision (c) of Section 1987 or under Section 1987.1, the court may in its discretion award the amount of the reasonable expenses incurred in making or opposing the motion, including reasonable attorney’s fees, if the court finds the motion was made or opposed in bad faith or without substantial justification or that one or more of the requirements of the subpoena was oppressive.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.2, subd. (a).)

 

B.     Discussion

 

Plaintiffs move to quash or reduce the scope of deposition subpoenas served on various third parties by Defendant pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1985.3, subdivision (g). These subpoenas seek educational and medical records related to O.A., medical records related to J.A., and employment records related to M.A. The Court will address each category of subpoenas in turn.

 

First, Defendant has subpoenaed SMMUSD and Fusion for all education records related to O.A.’s time as a student. These include his academic record, special education records, records related to his disability, attendance records, documents related to any investigation of O.A.’s conduct, documents in his disciplinary file, documents related to his appeal of his suspension, and documents related to hearings, meetings, and phone calls. (Exs. 1, 2.) Defendant argues that these records are relevant because Plaintiffs have put O.A.’s school history and conduct at issue by alleging that he was well behaved.

 

Plaintiffs object to this request on the grounds that O.A.’s privacy rights are implicated. (Cal. Const., Art. 1.) A party asserting a privacy right must “establish a legally protected privacy interest, an objectively reasonable expectation of privacy in the given circumstances, and a threatened intrusion that is serious.” (Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 552 (Williams).) The party seeking information may then “raise in response whatever legitimate and important countervailing interests disclosure serves, while the party seeking protection may identify feasible alternatives that serve the same interests or protective measures that would diminish the loss of privacy.” (Ibid.) The Court then balances those competing considerations. (Ibid.)

 

Here, O.A.’s privacy rights are implicated by Defendant’s desired intrusion into his educational records. Defendant seeks to demonstrate that Defendant did not know her statements were false or defamatory based on evidence of O.A.’s reputation at school. But information about O.A.’s reputation at school more generally will not shed light on what Defendant knew about O.A. at the time she made her statements. Thus, the only relevant records are those relating to Plaintiff’s interactions with M.S. specifically, as such evidence will help determine the truthfulness of Defendant’s publications. Accordingly, the Court modifies the SMMUSD subpoena to only seek items 6 and 14 related to O.A.’s interactions with M.S. (Ex. 1.) It additionally modifies the Fusion subpoena to only seek items 7, 15, and 16 related to O.A.’s interactions with M.S. and his tuition payments, which he has put at issue by claiming them as damages.

 

Second, Defendant has subpoenaed (1) Dr. Anshu Batra; (2) Dr. Joseph Taravella, Ph.D.; (3) Dr. Ruth Ziemba; (4) Dr. Richard Boles; (5) UCLA Health Systems – M.R.; (6) UCLA Health Systems Billing; and (7) UCLA Health Systems Radiology for O.A.’s medical records. These subpoenas seek all medical records, correspondences, test results, and billing documents from the various healthcare providers. (Exs. 3–6, 8–10.) Defendant argues that these records are relevant because Plaintiffs have alleged that they treated O.A. and provide a basis for his medical damages. Once again, O.A.’s right to privacy is invoked because of the sensitive nature of these medical records. The Court agrees with Defendant that an important countervailing interest exists here, where Plaintiffs have put these records at issue by claiming damages. The Court also notes that the federal court in the concurrent action allowed for discovery of the same documents spanning from 2018 to the present. Although the federal court records are so heavily redacted as to be almost meaningless, the Court accepts the representation that these same documents were ordered produced in that case.  If they have already been produced then any privacy concerns are lessened substantially.  The Court finds the federal court conclusion reasonable, and modifies the present subpoena to apply to all documents listed in the relevant subpoenas but spanning from 2018 rather than 2016 to the present.

 

Third, Plaintiffs move to quash the subpoenas of Dr. Gottlieb and Dr. Tau, as they seek premature expert discovery. (Exs. 7, 11.) Indeed, these are Plaintiffs’ retained experts. And expert witness information is properly obtained via Code of Civil Procedure section 2034.210. Thus, the present subpoenas are premature and quashed.

 

Fourth, Defendant has subpoenaed WRC seeking all documents related to services it provided O.A. (Ex. 12.) Defendant notes that Plaintiff has used WRC to demonstrate O.A.’s cognitive, developmental, and physical condition. However, the records at issue are private documents related to the provision of disability services. Defendant may still obtain some information based on O.A.’s alleged damages arising out of him no longer being able to attend WRC. Thus, consistent with the federal court, this Court finds the subpoena proper. Thus, Plaintiff’s motion to quash with respect to WRC is denied.

 

Fifth, Defendant has subpoenaed BK seeking his employment records and records related to his behavior while attending classes there. Similarly to the SMMUSD and Fusion subpoenas, O.A.’s behavior and reputation prior to Defendant’s statements is not relevant to show what she knew about him. But Plaintiff’s employment records are still properly sought based on his damages stemming from loss of employment at BK. Thus, the subpoena is modified to only encompass request 1. (Ex. 13.)

 

Sixth, Defendant’s subpoena to Lagarlof, LLP for O.A.’s conservatorship records is improper, as such records are confidential. Defendant makes no argument in support of this subpoena, and it is quashed. (Ex. 14.)

 

Seventh, Defendant’s subpoena to Denis A. Perez, LCSW is improper because J.A. has not put his medical files at issue. (Ex. 15.) He is only seeking “garden variety” emotional distress damages (which the Court understands means general emotional distress but not evidence of treatment or diagnosis by medical doctors or other professionals). and will not offer evidence from any physicians at trial. Thus, the documents sought by Defendant are not relevant. Accordingly, the subpoena is quashed.

 

Eighth, Defendant seeks the employment records of M.A. from Sinclair and Univision. (Exs. 16, 17.) While individuals have a protected privacy interest in their employment records, here M.A. has placed his at issue by claiming damages for lost bonuses, poor performance reviews, and lost pay. (See Board of Trustees of Leland Stanford Jr. University v. Superior Court (Dong) (1981) 119 Cal.App.3d 516, 528–530.) Thus, the subpoenas are proper and the motion to quash is denied.

 

Conclusion:


            The Court (1) modifies the SMMUSD subpoena to only include requests 6 and 14; (2) modifies the Fusion subpoena to only include items 7, 15, and 16; (3) modifies subpoenas 3–6 and 8–10 to apply to the period from 2018 to the present; (4) quashes subpoenas 7, 11, 14, and 15; (5) denies Plaintiffs’ motion to quash with respect to subpoenas 12, 16, and 17; and (6) modifies subpoena 13 to only encompass request 1.



ADDITIONAL MOTIONS

 

            The Court tenders the following tentative decision in the matter O. A., et al. v. Jessica DeVita, Los Angeles County Superior Court case number 24STCV07875, set for hearing on July 23, 2026. O.A., J.A., and M.A. (collectively, Plaintiffs) move for a protective order for the deposition of O.A. by Jessica DeVita (Defendant). Plaintiffs also move to seal additional records submitted in opposition to the continued motion for summary judgment. Separately, Defendant moves for a protective order canceling the deposition of M.S. The Court grants all motions.

 

A.    Plaintiffs’ motion to seal is granted.

 

Plaintiffs move for the sealing of certain additional documents filed in support of their further opposition to Defendant’s motion for summary judgment. Pursuant to California Rule of Court, rule 2.550(d), the court may order that a record be sealed if it finds facts that establish the following: (1) There exists an overriding interest that overcomes the right of public access to the record; (2) The overriding interest supports sealing the record; (3) A substantial probability exists that the overriding interest will be prejudiced if the record is not sealed; (4) The proposed sealing is narrowly tailored; and (5) No less restrictive means exist to achieve the overriding interest. The burden of showing there are less restrictive means of achieving the overriding interest lies with one who is seeking disclosure of the allegedly confidential information. (NBC Subsidiary (KNBC-TV), Inc. v. Superior Court (1999) 20 Cal.4th 1178, 1224.) 

 

A “reasoned decision about sealing or unsealing records cannot be made without . . . (1) identifying the specific information claimed to be entitled to such treatment; (2) identifying the nature of the harm threatened by disclosure; and (3) identifying and accounting for countervailing considerations. The burden of presenting information sufficient to accomplish the first two steps is logically placed on the party seeking the sealing of the documents, who is presumptively in the best position to know what disclosures will harm and how.” (H.B. Fuller Co. v. Doe (2007) 151 Cal.App.4th 879, 894.)

 

Plaintiffs filed the present motion to seal to protect certain materials from the public record, including records of juvenile court proceedings. There is an overriding interest in safeguarding the confidentiality of a minor’s records to prevent collateral consequences. (T.N.G. v. Superior Court (1971) 4 Cal.3d 767, 776.) This interest supports sealing the designated materials. There is a substantial probability that disclosure of the records in question would prejudice Plaintiffs, as they contain identifying information about a minor and their sensitive juvenile court records. The sealing request is narrowly tailored and applies to only specific documents containing the relevant information. No less restrictive means exist of protecting the information. Thus, Plaintiffs have satisfied every requirement of rule 2.550(d). Accordingly, Plaintiffs’ unopposed motion to seal is granted.

 

B.     Plaintiffs’ motion for a protective order is granted.

Plaintiffs seek a protective order providing O.A. certain medical accommodations for his upcoming deposition, including the following: (1) that he may select the location; (2) that he may be accompanied by his parents, physicians, and counsel; (3) that counsel will avoid repetitive questions if he cannot remember; (4) that the deposition may not exceed 4 hours with breaks that may be taken as needed; and (5) that only one attorney may question O.A. and may not raise their voice or agitate him.

Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.060 provides that “[t]he court, for good cause shown, may make any order that justice requires to protect any party or other person from unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression, or undue burden and expense.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.060, subd. (a).) The motion “shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.” (Ibid.)  Similarly, the responding party may move for protective orders regarding interrogatories and requests for admission. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.090, 2033.080.)

The protective order may include one or more of the following directions: (1) that all or some items or categories in the demand need not be produced at all; (2) that the time to respond to a set of demands be extended; (3) that the place of production be changed; (4) that the inspection, copying, testing, or sampling be made under specified terms and conditions; (5) that a trade secret or other confidential information not be disclosed, or disclosed only in a certain way to certain people; or (6) that the items produced be sealed and only opened after a court order. (Id., § 2031.060, subd. (b).)

Code of Civil Procedure section 2017.020, subd. (a), provides that “[t]he court shall limit the scope of discovery if it determines that the burden, expense, intrusiveness of that discovery clearly outweighs the likelihood that the information sought will lead to the discovery of admissible evidence.” Additionally, the court shall restrict the frequency or extent of the use of a discovery method if it determines either of the following: 

1.       The discovery sought is unreasonably cumulative or duplicative, or is obtainable from some other source that is more convenient, less burdensome, or less expensive.¿ 

2.       The selected method of discovery is unduly burdensome or expensive, taking into account the needs of the case, the amount in controversy, and the importance of the issues at stake in the litigation.¿¿ 

(Code Civ. Proc.,¿§ 2019.030, subd. (a).)

Here, O.A. suffers from a rare genetic medical condition that makes participation in legal proceedings potentially damaging. This creates a greater risk that the taking of his deposition will result in unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression. Indeed, the Court finds that given O.A.’s condition, a protective order is proper. They have demonstrated good cause in that the lack of the suggested protections could prove harmful to O.A. Defendant argues against the application of the fourth protection, which would limit the deposition to 4 hours. With the breaks also allowed for under that proposed protection, Defendant’s time for deposing O.A. would be severely limited. The same protection for O.A. can be achieved by splitting the deposition into two sessions on separate days, each not exceeding 4 hours. Defendant also argues that the third restriction would limit the scope of her questioning, but that provision would only limit the use of repetitive questions. Thus, the Court finds the proposed protective order warranted and proper but amends the fourth protection to allow for 2 separate sessions not exceeding 4 hours with breaks. Accordingly, Plaintiffs’ motion for a protective order is granted with that edit to the 4th protection.

 

C.     Defendant’s motion for a protective order is granted.

 

Defendant seeks a protective order postponing the deposition of M.S. until proper medical accommodations are provided. M.S. suffers from autism and is intellectually disabled. Thus, similarly to O.A., a deposition will be potentially harmful to him. Defendant seeks protections similar to those afforded O.A. under the Plaintiffs’ protective order. In the interest of fairness, the Court grants those exact same protections in the deposition of M.S. However, the Court does not make any particular order as to when the depositions must take place in relation to one another. Accordingly, Defendant’s motion is granted in part: M.S.’s deposition will go forward, but the same protections applied to the deposition of O.A. as discussed above will be applied to the taking of M.S.’s deposition as well.

 

Conclusion:

 

            The motions are granted in part. Plaintiffs’ requested documents are sealed. With respect to the depositions of O.A. and M.S., the following restrictions apply: (1) the deponent may select the location; (2) he may be accompanied by his parents, physicians, and counsel; (3) deposing counsel will avoid repetitive questions if the deponent cannot remember; (4) the deposition may not exceed the usual 7 hours with breaks that may be taken as needed, but must be split over two sessions neither of which may exceed 4 hours; and (5) only one attorney may question the deponent and may not raise their voice or agitate him.

Case Number: 24STCV20645    Hearing Date: July 23, 2026    Dept: 400

MOTION TO BIFURCATE

 

            The Court tenders the following tentative decision in the matter Eleyna Guidry v. Delta Air Lines, Inc., et al., Los Angeles County Superior Court case number 24STCV20645, set for hearing on July 23, 2026. Delta Air Lines, Inc. and Wade Hasegawa (collectively, Defendants) move the Court for an order bifurcating the issues of liability and punitive damages at trial. Eleyna Guidry (Plaintiff) opposes bifurcation. The motion is granted.

 

A.    Legal Standard


“The court, in furtherance of convenience or to avoid prejudice, or when separate trials would be conducive to expedition and economy, may order a separate trial of any cause of action, including a cause of action asserted in a cross-complaint or of any separate issue or of any number of causes of action or issues, preserving the right of trial by jury required by the Constitution or a statute of this state or of the United States.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1048, subd. (b).)

 

“The court may, when the convenience of witnesses, the ends of justice, or the economy and efficiency of handling the litigation would be promoted thereby, on motion of a party, after notice and hearing, make an order, no later than the close of pretrial conference in cases in which such pretrial conference in cases in which such pretrial conference is to be held, or, in other cases, no later than 30 days before the trial date, that the trial of any issue or any part thereof shall precede the trial of any other issue or any party thereof in the case.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 598.) “Where trial of the issue of liability as to all causes of action precedes the trial of other issues or parts thereof and the decision of the court or jury is in favor of the allegedly liable party, judgment in favor of that party shall be entered and no trial of other issues in the action against that party will be had.” (Ibid.) If the decision of the Court or jury on the issue of liability is against the party allegedly liable, the trial of the other issues shall be had before the same or another jury as ordered by the court. (Ibid.)

 

            In determining whether to order separate trials, courts consider a number of factors including judicial economy, the danger of unnecessary jury confusion if separate trials are not held, and potential prejudice. (Grappo v. Coventry Fin. Corp. (1991) 235 Cal.App.3d 496, 504.) Granting or denying of a motion for separate trials lies within the trial court's sound discretion and is subject to reversal on appeal only for clear abuse. (Ibid.) Bifurcation frequently occurs when the determination of one issue, such as an affirmative defense, renders other issues moot. (See, e.g., Buran Equipment v. H& C Investment Co. (1983) 142 Cal.App.3d 338, 343–344.) Trial courts also often bifurcate equitable and legal issues, resolving the issues of equity first in order to promote judicial economy. (Darbun Enters, Inc. v. San Fernando Community Hosp. (2015) 239 Cal.App.4th 399, 408–409.)

 

B.     Discussion

 

Defendants argue that bifurcation is warranted in the present matter with respect to the punitive damages issue. “The court shall, on application of any defendant, preclude the admission of evidence of that defendant’s profits or financial condition until after the trier of fact returns a verdict for plaintiff awarding actual damages and finds that a defendant is guilty of malice, oppression, or fraud in accordance with Section 3294.” (Civ. Code, § 3295, subd. (d).) Here, bifurcation of the punitive damages issue would avoid prejudice to Defendants by excluding evidence of profits or financial condition from the liability phase and support judicial economy by potentially eliminating discussion of such evidence if the jury returns a defense verdict. Indeed, the Court lacks discretion to deny bifurcation pursuant to section 3295. And the bifurcation requested by Defendant is only with respect to the punitive damages phase, not bifurcation of damages in its entirety. Thus, Plaintiff is still entitled to introduce evidence of damages and is only prohibited from introducing evidence of the kind discussed in section 3295 until the punitive damages phase of the trial, should one be necessary. Accordingly, Defendants’ motion is granted.

 

Conclusion:


            The Court grants the motion.

Case Number: 24STCV32278    Hearing Date: July 23, 2026    Dept: 400

MOTION FOR SUMMARY ADJUDICATION

 

The Court tenders the following tentative decision in the matter Arturo Herrera, Jr., et al. v. Ford Motor Company, et al., Los Angeles County Superior Court case number 24STCV32278, set for hearing on July 23, 2026. Ford Motor Company (Defendant) moves for summary adjudication as to Arturo Herrera, Jr. and Norayda Herrera’s (Plaintiffs) fifth and sixth causes of action. Plaintiffs oppose summary adjudication. The motion is granted.


A.    Legal Standard


The function of a motion for summary judgment or adjudication is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party can show evidentiary support for a claim or defense and if not to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) Code of Civil Procedure Section 437c, subdivision (c) “requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.)

 

As to each claim as framed by the complaint, a moving defendant “has met that party’s burden of showing that a cause of action has no merit if the party has shown that one or more elements of the cause of action, even if not separately pleaded, cannot be established, or that there is a complete defense to the cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) Courts “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.” (Dore v. Arnold Worldwide, Inc. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 384, 389 (Dore).) Once the defendant has met that burden, the burden shifts to the opposing plaintiff “to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence. (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

 

B.     Discussion

 

Defendant moves for summary adjudication as to Plaintiffs’ fifth cause of action for negligent repair and sixth cause of action for fraudulent inducement of contract through concealment. The Court will address each cause of action in turn.

 

Defendant is entitled to summary adjudication as to Plaintiffs’ fifth cause of action for negligent repair.

 

Defendant argues that it is entitled to summary adjudication as to Plaintiffs’ fifth cause of action because it is barred by the economic loss rule. Under the economic loss rule, tort recovery for breach of a contract duty is generally barred. (Rattagan v. Uber Technologies Inc. (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1, 20 (Rattagan).) A plaintiff may still bring a tort action if they “demonstrate the defendant’s injury-causing conduct violated a duty that is independent of the duties and rights assumed by the parties when they entered the contract” and that “defendant’s conduct . . . caused injury to persons or property that was not reasonably contemplated by the parties when the contract was formed.” (Id. at pp. 20–21.)

 

Here, Plaintiffs’ negligent repair cause of action is essentially a common law negligence claim against the defendant which failed to repair Plaintiffs’ vehicle. (Compl. ¶¶ 60–62.) Plaintiffs allege that their negligent repair cause of action arises out of Defendants’ warranty obligations based on the written warranty issued with the subject vehicle. (Id. ¶ 11.) Plaintiffs further allege a duty by Defendants to repair the subject vehicle in accordance with industry standards. (Id. ¶ 60.) But Plaintiffs have not presented evidence showing any duty separate from the duties arising out of the warranty agreement. (Plaintiffs’ Separate Statement (PSS) ¶¶ 21–22.) Thus, the economic loss rule applies, and Defendant has met its burden of showing that Plaintiffs cannot prove their fifth cause of action as a matter of law.

 

Plaintiffs cite no evidence raising any triable issue of material fact. Indeed, they merely refer to the lack of any evidence as disputed without citing any materials in the record. (Ibid.) Rather, Plaintiffs argue that damage to the entire subject vehicle from a single defective component allows for liability under a negligent repair theory pursuant to Jimenez v. Superior Court (2002) 29 Cal.4th 473, 483–484. But such a theory requires evidence that Defendant damaged the relevant component while repairing it, whereas here Plaintiffs instead provide evidence that the component was damaged or defective at the time of sale. Thus, the economic loss rule applies and Plaintiffs have not presented any triable issues of material fact to defeat its application. Accordingly, Defendant’s motion for summary adjudication is granted as to Plaintiffs’ fifth cause of action.

 

Defendant is entitled to summary adjudication as to Plaintiffs’ sixth cause of action for fraudulent inducement via concealment.

 

            Defendant argues that it is entitled to summary adjudication as to Plaintiffs’ sixth cause of action for fraudulent inducement of contract through concealment. “The required elements for fraudulent concealment are (1) concealment or suppression of a material fact; (2) by a defendant with a duty to disclose the fact; (3) the defendant intended to defraud the plaintiff by intentionally concealing or suppressing the fact; (4) the plaintiff was unaware of the fact and would have acted differently if the concealed or suppressed fact was known; and (5) plaintiff sustained damage as a result of the concealment or suppression of the material fact.” (Rattagan, supra, 17 Cal.5th at p. 40.) A duty to disclose a material fact arises in multiple situations: (1) if it is imposed by statute; (2) if the defendant is acting as the plaintiff’s fiduciary or is in some other confidential relationship; (3) if the material facts are only known or accessible to the defendant and they know that plaintiff may not easily discover them; (4) if the defendant discloses some facts but fails to disclose others; or (5) if the defendant actively conceals material facts from the plaintiff. (Ibid.) Situations 3, 4, and 5 “presuppose a preexisting relationship between the parties,” and a transaction creating such a relationship must “arise from direct dealings between the plaintiff and the defendant; it cannot arise between the defendant and the public at large.” (Id. at pp. 40–41.)

 

            Specifically, Defendant argues that Plaintiffs’ claim is barred by the economic loss rule and that Plaintiff has not presented evidence creating a duty to disclose or showing that Defendant was aware of the relevant defect when it sold the vehicle. First, with respect to the economic loss rule, a plaintiff may still bring a tort action if they “demonstrate the defendant’s injury-causing conduct violated a duty that is independent of the duties and rights assumed by the parties when they entered the contract” and that “defendant’s conduct . . . caused injury to persons or property that was not reasonably contemplated by the parties when the contract was formed.” (Rattagan, supra, 17 Cal.5th at pp. 20–21.) However, as noted by the Court in Rattagan, fraudulent inducement “is not a context where the ‘traditional separation of tort and contract law’ obtains.” (Id. at p. 41.) Thus, Plaintiffs’ claim is not barred by the economic loss rule.

 

            Next, Defendant argues that it is entitled to summary adjudication because Plaintiffs did not produce evidence of a sufficient relationship between the parties required under Rattagan. Where no fiduciary or confidential relationship exists between the parties, a plaintiff must show a relationship through some transaction. (Bigler-Engler v. Breg, Inc. (2017) 7 Cal.App.5th 276, 310–311.) Having satisfied its initial burden, Plaintiffs must demonstrate the existence of some triable issue of material fact. Here, Plaintiffs present evidence that they purchased the subject vehicle with Defendant’s express written warranties. (Compl., Ex. A.) Parties entering into a contractual agreement are parties to a transaction, “from which a duty to disclose facts material to the transaction arises under certain circumstances.” (Rattagan, supra, 17 Cal.5th at p. 41; see Dhital v. Nissan North America (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 828, 844 [holding that a car manufacturer’s express warranty created a transactional relationship requiring the disclosure of known defects].) Thus, Plaintiffs have demonstrated a triable issue of material fact as to whether Defendant owed a duty to disclose based on the warranty agreement under Rattagan even if Defendant did not directly sell Plaintiffs the subject vehicle.

 

            Finally, Defendant contends that Plaintiffs cannot show it was aware of the defect in the subject vehicle at the time of sale or that it was unwilling or unable to fix the defect. If Plaintiffs lacked such evidence, they would be unable to show that Defendant concealed or suppressed a material fact. Thus, Defendant has met its initial burden once again of demonstrating that Plaintiffs’ sixth cause of action lacks merit. Plaintiffs point to exhibits in their request for judicial notice showing that Defendant was in fact aware of the relevant transmission defect. (RJN, Exs. 1–18.) But these exhibits are not properly subject to judicial notice as facts not reasonably subject to dispute; rather, they constitute internal documents of Defendant that should instead be offered into evidence. In fact, Plaintiffs do not point to any admissible evidence establishing a triable issue of material fact with respect to Defendant’s knowledge of the relevant defect. Accordingly, Plaintiffs have failed to meet their burden and summary adjudication is granted with respect to their sixth cause of action as well.

 

Conclusion:

Defendant’s motion for summary adjudication is granted.


Case Number: 25STCV11123    Hearing Date: July 23, 2026    Dept: 400

MOTION FOR TEMINATING SANCTIONS

 

The Court tenders the following tentative decision in the matter Dustin Clemons v. Pinkerton Consulting & Investigations, Inc., et al., Los Angeles County Superior Court case number 25STCV11123, set for hearing on July 23, 2026. Sequoia Consultants, Inc. (Defendant) moves for an order imposing terminating sanctions against Dustin Clemons (Plaintiff). Plaintiff opposes the imposition of terminating sanctions. Defendant’s motion for sanctions is granted in part: the Court imposes evidentiary sanctions.


A.    Legal Standard

 

Misuses of the discovery process justify the imposition of sanctions. (Id., § 2030.030.) Disobeying a court order to provide discovery constitutes misuse of the discovery process. (Id., § 2023.010, subd. (g).) Specifically, if a party fails to obey an order compelling a response to an inspection demand or interrogatories, the Court may impose “an issue sanction, an evidence sanction, or a terminating sanction.” (Id., §§ 2030.290, subd. (c), 2031.320, subd. (c).) A court imposing an issue sanction may order that “designated facts shall be taken as established in the action in accordance with the claim of the party adversely affected by the misuse of the discovery process.” (Id., § 2030.030, subd. (b).) An evidence sanction may prohibit the sanctioned party from introducing designated matters into evidence. (Id., § 2030.030, subd. (c).) In imposing a terminating sanction, a court may order one of the following: (1) the striking of pleadings or parts of pleadings; (2) the staying of further proceedings by the offending party; (3) the dismissal of the action; or (4) a judgment by default against the offending party. (Id., § 2030.030, subd. (d).)

 

B.     Discussion

 

Defendant requests monetary, evidentiary, issue, and terminating sanctions for repeated abuses of the discovery process. Specifically, Defendant seeks an order striking Plaintiff’s complaint in its entirety with prejudice and awarding Defendant reasonable attorney’s fees and costs. “[T]erminating sanctions are to be used sparingly, only when the trial court concludes that lesser sanctions would not bring about the compliance of the offending party.” (R.S. Creative, Inc. v. Creative Cotton, Ltd. (1999) 75 Cal.App.4th 486, 496.)

 

Here, Defendant initially served Plaintiff with form interrogatories, special interrogatories, requests for production, and requests for admission on July 30, 2025. (Debus Decl. ¶ 2.) After Plaintiff failed to provide timely responses, the Court granted Defendant’s motions to compel responses on October 23, 2025. (Ibid.) Plaintiff then served incomplete responses on November 17. (Id. ¶ 3.) After meet and confer efforts and multiple extensions, Plaintiff failed to provide further supplemental responses as promised. (Id. ¶¶ 3–4.) Defendant then filed motions to compel further responses, which the Court granted on January 29, 2026. (Id. ¶ 4.) Plaintiff’s subsequent writ concerning the Court’s orders to compel further responses was denied. (Id. ¶ 5.) Plaintiff served further responses on May 1, 2026, but continued to withhold documents he promised to produce. (Id. ¶ 6.) Plaintiff has failed to pay the ordered monetary sanctions. (Ibid.)

 

The Court finds that Plaintiff’s actions in failing to provide discovery and pay sanctions constitute failure to obey Court orders to provide discovery. Thus, Plaintiff has engaged in misuse of the discovery process, and sanctions are warranted. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.010, subd. (g).) Monetary sanctions are inadequate, given that they have failed to force compliance on Plaintiff’s part and have not even been paid when imposed. But terminating and issue sanctions are too harsh. The Court generally seeks to impose sanctions on an incrementally increasing scale of harshness, and here evidentiary sanctions are most appropriate. Thus, the Court orders that Plaintiff will be prohibited from introducing evidence in support of his claims which has not already been produced or is produced within 15days of this Order, as described in Defendant’s notice of motion under points (B)(i)–(xiii). The Court does not impose issue or terminating sanctions, but may consider them if there is a misuse of the discovery process.

 

C.     Conclusion

 

The Court grants Defendant’s motion in part.
