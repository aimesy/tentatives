DEPARTMENT 400 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 23STCV20885    Hearing Date: July 20, 2026    Dept: 400

MOTIONS

 

            The Court tenders the following tentative decision in the matter Michael Washington v. Stewart Manhattan Investments Inc., et al., Los Angeles County Superior Court case number 23STCV20885, set for hearing on July 20, 2026. Simeon Rainier Stewart (Defendant) moves for orders compelling further responses to the following discovery requests propounded on Miachel Washington (Plaintiff): (1) form interrogatories, set one (FROGs); (2) requests for production of documents, set one (RFPs); and (3) special interrogatories, set one (SPROGs). Defendant also requests a total $29,700 in monetary sanctions along with evidence and issue sanctions. Plaintiff opposes the motions and argues that sanctions are unwarranted. Defendant’s motions to compel further responses are granted in part. The Court awards $2,700 in sanctions.


A.    Legal Standard

 

A party may move for an order compelling further responses to a request for production of documents if the propounding party deems that responses are incomplete, evasive, or contain meritless objections. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (a).) A motion to compel further responses to requests for production “shall set forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought by the inspection demand.” (Id., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(1).) A party may also move for an order compelling further responses to interrogatories if the propounding party deems that responses are incomplete, evasive, contain meritless objections, or improperly exercise the option to produce documents under section 2030.230. (Id., § 2030.300, subd. (a).)

 

“For discovery purposes, information is relevant if it ‘might reasonably assist a party in evaluating the case, preparing for trial, or facilitating settlement.’ [Citation]. Admissibility is not the test and information, unless privileged, is discoverable if it might reasonably lead to admissible evidence. [Citation] These rules are applied liberally in favor of discovery.” (Gonzales v. Superior Court (1995) 33 Cal.App.4th 1539, 1546.) “If the court determines that the answer or production sought is subject to discovery, it shall order that the answer be given, or the production be made on the resumption of the deposition.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.480, subd. (i).)

 

B.     Discussion

 

Defendant propounded the relevant discovery on November 13, 2024. (Mills Decl. ¶ 2.) Plaintiff served initial unverified responses consisting of only objections on December 17 and 24, 2024. (Id. ¶ 3.) After the parties met and conferred, Plaintiff served supplemental responses in March 2025. (Id. ¶ 5.) The parties then met and conferred further, with Plaintiff providing additional supplemental responses in August 2025. (Id. ¶¶ 6–8.) Plaintiff sent more responses in November of that year. (Id. ¶¶ 11–12.) On February 10, 2026, the Court granted motions by Defendant to compel further responses to SPROGs and RFPs, instructing Plaintiff to: (1) remove introductory general objections; (2) eliminate or elaborate as to boilerplate objections in specific responses; (3) log documents withheld for privilege; (4) instruct as to how Defendant might prepare compilations; (5) provide further information regarding any confidentiality objections; (6) complete responses which previously provided only an example; (7) comply with section 2031.230; and (8) remove use of the phrase “subject to and without waiving the objections.” (Minute Order, February 10, 2026.) The parties met and conferred further, and Defendant filed the present motions to compel further responses on May 22, 2026, and June 15, 2026.

 

Defendant seeks further responses to FROGs 9.1, 9.2, 15.1, and 17.1. Defendant also seeks further verified responses to the same SPROGs and RFPs at issue in the Court’s previous February 10, 2026, order. Defendant requests issue, evidence, and monetary sanctions. The Court will address each discovery matter in turn, before turning to which sanctions are appropriate.

 

Plaintiff must provide further responses to FROGs 9.1, 9.2, 15.1, and 17.1.

 

Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s responses to FROGs 9.1, 9.2, 15.1, and 17.1 are deficient. Specifically, Defendant notes that the responses contain general objections disapproved of by the Court in its prior order, generic blanket objections, are qualified with phrases limiting the reach of the responses and are evasive and incomplete. Plaintiff contends that he has provided further responses since the filing of Defendant’s motion that are in compliance with the Court’s prior order, rendering Defendant’s motion moot. However, the motion is not rendered moot by Plaintiff’s late amended responses, as the responses were deficient when the motion to compel was filed because they contained improper boilerplate objections. Additionally, Defendant details in his separate statement how Plaintiff’s answers remain ambiguous or evasive. Finally, Plaintiff argues that Defendant failed to sufficiently meet and confer prior to filing the present motion. But the parties discussed the FROGs throughout May after Plaintiff offered to produce amended responses. (Mills Decl. ¶¶ 29–30.) Thus, Defendant’s motion to compel further responses to FROGs is proper. Accordingly, the Court grants Defendant’s motion.

 

Plaintiff must provide further responses to SPROGs 1–3, but not SPROGs 4, 5, 8, 10–15, 17, 18, 20, 21, 23–28, and 36.

 

Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s responses to SPROGs 1–3 4, 5, 8, 10–15, 17, 18, 20, 21, 23–28, and 36 are inadequate. Specifically, Defendant suggests that Plaintiff continues to evade answering the requests at issue and asserts invalid confidentiality objections. While the Court did not foreclose confidentiality objections provided that Plaintiff explained their basis, the Court specifically instructed Plaintiff to at least identify the parties to confidential agreements so that Defendant may test the confidentiality claim. Yet in SPROGs 1–3, Plaintiff continues to refuse to identify the parties to some of the allegedly confidential agreements. Meanwhile, Plaintiff’s responses to SPROGs 4, 5, 8, 10, 12, 17, 20, 23, 25, and 36 properly removed objections as instructed in the Court’s prior order and provided non-evasive responses; thus, further responses are not required. And Plaintiff properly invokes section 2030.230 with respect to SPROGs 11, 13–15, 18, 21, 24, and 26–28 by referring to the relevant documents. Thus, Plaintiff has not complied with the Court’s prior order and further responses are required with respect to SPROGs 1–3 only.

 

Plaintiff must provide further responses to RFPs 1, 2, 18–23 but not RFPs 3–13 and 15–17.


Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s responses to RFPs 1–13, 15–23 are inadequate. Specifically, Defendant suggests that Plaintiff continues to evade answering the requests at issue and asserts invalid confidentiality objections. While the Court did not foreclose confidentiality objections entirely as discussed above, the Court specifically instructed Plaintiff to at least identify the parties to confidential agreements so that Defendant may test the confidentiality claim. Yet in RFPs 1, 2, and 23 Plaintiff continues to refuse to identify the parties to some of the allegedly confidential agreements. With respect to RFPs 3, 4, 6–13, and 15–17 Defendant argues that Plaintiff has not produced responsive documents, but a proper motion to force production must be brought pursuant to section 2031.320. Meanwhile, Plaintiff’s response to RFP 5 properly removed objections as instructed in the Court’s prior order and provided a non-evasive response; thus, further responses are not required. Separately, the Court overrules Plaintiff’s objection to RFPs 18–22 (except as to tax returns) on privacy grounds despite his privacy interest in financial matters and other companies’ privacy interests in their documents given the countervailing interests at stake in terms of determining the details of Plaintiff’s business dealings in this matter. (Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 552.) Thus, Plaintiff has not complied with the Court’s prior order and further responses are required with respect to RFPs 1, 2, 18–23 only.

 

Defendant is awarded monetary sanctions only.

 

Sanctions may be awarded where a party unsuccessfully opposes a motion to compel further responses, provided that the party does so without substantial justification. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300, 2031.310.) Here, Plaintiff opposed Defendant’s motions regarding SPROGs and RFPs with substantial justification, as further responses were not warranted in some instances as discussed above. However, Plaintiff lacked substantial justification to oppose Defendant’s motion with respect to FROGs. Thus, sanctions are warranted pursuant to section 2030.300, subdivision (d). Counsel testifies to an hourly rate of $450, which the Court credits. (Mills Decl. ¶ 35.) However, given the mere moderate complexity of the motion to compel further responses to FROGs at issue, the Court only credits counsel with 4 hours for drafting the motion and 1 hour for reviewing Plaintiff’s opposition and drafting a reply. Finally, the Court credits 1 hour for appearing at the hearing. Accordingly, the Court awards Defendant sanctions in the amount of $2,700 ([$450 per hour] x [6 hours] = $2,700).

 

C.     Conclusion


            The Court grants Defendant’s motions in part. Plaintiff must provide further responses to FROGs 9.1, 9.2, 15.1, and 17.1, SPROGs 1–3, and RFPs 1, 2, and 18–23. Defendant is awarded sanctions in the amount of $2,700.

 


Case Number: 24STCV10288    Hearing Date: July 20, 2026    Dept: 400

MOTION TO COMPEL ARBITRATION

 

The Court tenders the following tentative decision in the matter James Moultrie v. Nazareth Haysbert, Los Angeles County Superior Court case number 24STCV10288, set for hearing on July 20, 2026. Nazareth Haysbert (Defendant) moves for an order compelling James Moultrie (Plaintiff) to submit to binding arbitration and staying these proceedings in the interim. Plaintiff opposes the motion. The motion is granted.

 

A.    Legal Standard

 

“On petition of a party to an arbitration agreement alleging the existence of a written agreement to arbitrate a controversy and that a party thereto refuses to arbitrate such controversy, the court shall order the petitioner and the respondent to arbitrate the controversy if it determines that an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists, unless it determines that: (a) The right to compel arbitration has been waived by the petitioner; or (b) Grounds exist for the revocation of the agreement.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 1281.2, subds. (a), (b).)

 

A proceeding to compel arbitration is in essence a suit in equity to compel specific performance of a contract. (Freeman v. State Farm Mutual Auto Insurance Co. (1975) 14 Cal.3d 473, 479.) Such enforcement may be sought by a party to the arbitration agreement. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1280, subd. (e)(1).) A motion to compel arbitration requires the facts to be proven by affidavit or declaration and documentary evidence with oral testimony taken only in the court’s discretion. (Code Civ. Proc., §1290.2; Rosenthal v. Great Western Fin. Securities Corp. (1996) 14 Cal.4th 394, 413–414.) The motion must set forth the provisions of the written agreement and the arbitration clause verbatim, or such provisions must be attached and incorporated by reference. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1330; see Condee v. Longwood Mgmt. Corp. (2001) 88 Cal.App.4th 215, 218.) 

 

            Once the moving party alleges that an arbitration agreement exists, the burden shifts to the responding party to prove the falsity of the purported agreement, and no evidence or authentication is required to find the arbitration agreement exists. (See Condee, supra, 88 Cal.App.4th at p. 219.) However, if the existence of the agreement is challenged, “[movant] bears the burden of proving [the arbitration agreement’s] existence by a preponderance of the evidence.” (Rosenthal, supra, p. 413; see also Espejo v. Southern California Permanente Medical Group (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 1047, 1058–1060.)


B.     Discussion

 

Defendant moves to compel Plaintiff to submit to binding arbitration pursuant to a partnership agreement between the parties. While the agreement contains an arbitration clause and Plaintiff does not contest that he signed the agreement, he maintains that it does not control because the agreement expired. Importantly, unless a party challenges the arbitration clause itself, “the issue of the contract’s validity is considered by the arbitrator in the first instance.” (Buckeye Check Cashing, Inc. v. Cardegna (2006) 546 U.S. 440, 446; see also Sandquist v. Lebo Automotive, Inc. (2016) 1 Cal.5th 233, 252 (Sandquist).) Thus, the Court must first examine the agreement as a whole and determine whether Plaintiff challenges the applicability of the entire agreement or the arbitration clause specifically.

 

The parties entered the partnership agreement (Agreement) on January 28, 2015. (Haysbert Decl., Ex. A.) Section 2 of the Agreement states that it shall begin on November 7, 2014, and continue “until automatically terminating on December 31, 2016.” (Ibid.) It further states that the terms and conditions of the Agreement “shall be continuously renewable for another year up to fifteen (15) days before the termination date with the unanimous consent of all partners.” (Ibid.) An arbitration clause is contained in section 26, stating that “In the event a dispute arises out of or in connection with this Agreement . . . If mediation is not successful in resolving the entire dispute or is unavailable, any outstanding issues will be submitted to final and binding arbitration.” (Ibid.)

 

Plaintiff’s claims in the present action arise out of the terms of the Agreement and whether it expired. (FAC ¶¶ 8–10.) The parties were unable to resolve the dispute over the Agreement’s potential expiration via friendly consultation or mediation as instructed under the Agreement. (Id. ¶¶ 34–35, Ex. 1.) Thus, the arbitration provision applies to Plaintiff’s claims. Plaintiff argues that the Agreement expired and that the arbitration provision thus carries no legal effect — that there is no enforceable agreement to arbitrate. In essence, Plaintiff challenges the enforceability of the entire Agreement. But, as discussed in Sandquist, “even a challenge to the enforceability of the entire contract in which an arbitration clause appears is presumptively for the arbitrator, not the court.” (Sandquist, supra, 1 Cal.5th at p. 252.) Because the parties agreed to settle disputes under the Agreement via arbitration, it is up to the arbitrator to determine whether the agreement expired or was renewed. Thus, Defendant has met its burden of demonstrating the existence of a valid agreement to arbitrate.

 

Plaintiff further argues that his claims are not subject to arbitration because they involve conduct after the Agreement’s expiration. But once again, this presupposes that the Agreement did indeed expire at the end of 2016. Whether or not the Agreement expired is a question for the arbitrator to resolve. Thus, Plaintiff has not presented a valid argument as to why the arbitration clause should not be enforced. Accordingly, Defendant’s motion to compel arbitration is granted. These proceedings are stayed pending the outcome of arbitration pursuant to section 1281.4.

 

C.     Conclusion


Defendant’s motion to compel arbitration and stay these proceedings is granted.

Case Number: 24STCV10326    Hearing Date: July 20, 2026    Dept: 400

MOTION TO CONTINUE TRIAL

 

The Court tenders the following tentative decision in the matter Tina Mei Ting Loo, et al. v. SC Group 856 S. Wilton, LLC, Los Angeles County Superior Court case number 24STCV10326, set for hearing on July 20, 2026. Pascual Montalvo, Alcal Specialty Contracting, Inc., West Coast Hot Mop, Inc., and Art Deck, Inc. (collectively, Defendants) move to continue the trial currently set for October 19, 2026, to a date in April 2027 or later. Tina Mei Ting Loo, et al. (collectively, Plaintiffs) oppose the motion. The motion is granted.

 

A.    Legal Standard

 

Continuances of trials are disfavored. (Rules of Court, rule 3.1332(c).) A party seeking a continuance of the date set for trial must make the request for a continuance by a noticed motion or an ex parte application as soon as reasonably practical once the necessity for the continuance is discovered. (Id., rule 3.1332(b).) The request for continuance may be granted on an affirmative showing of good cause. (Id., rule 3.1332(c).) “A party’s excused inability to obtain essential testimony, documents, or other material evidence despite diligent efforts” constitutes good cause for continuance. (Id., rule 3.1332(c)(6).)

 

Factors to be considered in determining whether good cause exists include: the proximity of the trial date; whether there was any previous continuance, extension of time, or delay of trial due to any party; the length of the continuance requested; the availability of alternative means to address the problem that gave rise to the motion or application for a continuance; the prejudice that parties or witnesses will suffer as a result of the continuance; whether trial counsel is engaged in another trial; whether the interests of justice are best served by a continuance; and any other fact or circumstance relevant to a fair determination of the motion or application. (Id., rule 3.1332(d).)

 

B.     Discussion

 

Defendants argue that good cause exists for continuance because of the addition of themselves as new parties. (Rules of Court, rule 3.1332(c)(5).) Good cause for continuance exists if “(A) The new party has not had a reasonable opportunity to conduct discovery and prepare for trial; or (B) The other parties have not had a reasonable opportunity to conduct discovery and prepare for trial in regard to the new party’s involvement in the case.” (Ibid.) Here, the present action was first filed on April 24, 2024. (Pritchard Decl. ¶ 4.) Plaintiffs amended their complaint to include Defendants on November 14, 2025, and all Defendants were served by the end of that month. (Id. ¶¶ 5–6.) Defendants filed responsive pleadings by March 2026. (Id. ¶ 7.) Expert designations are due at the end of August 2026. (Id. ¶ 9.) Defendants contend that they cannot conduct sufficient discovery to prepare their defense based on Plaintiffs’ cost of repair estimate by the current trial date. (Id. ¶ 10.) Yet Defendants received the estimate on February 23, 2026. (Libertino Decl. ¶ 5.) Additionally, Defendants originally requested that the parties stipulate to a trial continuance on April 10, 2026, which Plaintiffs declined. (Ibid.)

 

In balancing the factors outlined in rule 3.1332(d), the Court finds it proper to continue trial. Some factors weigh against continuance. Trial is set for October 2026, and thus imminent. (Rules of Court, rule 3.1332(d)(1).) And Plaintiffs oppose continuance having refused to stipulate, citing prejudice they will face by having potential relief delayed. (Id., rules 3.1332(d)(5), (9).) But the vast majority of the relevant factors weigh in favor. There has not been any previous continuance. (Id., rule 3.1332(d)(2).) The length of the requested continuance is relatively brief, at less than a year. (Rules of Court, rule 3.1332(d)(3).) Their do not appear to be alternative means to address the issue of Defendants obtaining necessary discovery to prepare their case. (Id., rule 3.1332(d)(4).) And Defendants have shown that they too will suffer prejudice if the relief is not granted, as they will lack the chance to complete discovery. (Id., rule 3.1332(d)(5).) The Court finds that the interests of justice are best served by continuing trial and allowing Defendants to conduct discovery. The Court does not find that Defendants unreasonably delayed these proceedings by waiting until a month offering the stipulated continuance to file the present motion. Accordingly, Defendants’ motion is granted.

 

Conclusion:


            The Court grants the motion.


Case Number: 26STCV04261    Hearing Date: July 20, 2026    Dept: 400

MOTION TO COMPEL

 

            The Court tenders the following tentative decision in the matter Marta Cabrera Cabrera v. FCA US, LLC, et al., Los Angeles County Superior Court case number 26STCV04261, set for hearing on July 20, 2026. Marta Cabrera Cabrera (Plaintiff) moves for an order compelling FCA US, LLC (Defendant) to comply with Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26 by producing certain documents. Plaintiff also seeks the imposition of sanctions pursuant to that section. Defendant opposes the motion. The motion is granted in part. The Court awards Plaintiff $2,500 in sanctions.

 

A.    Legal Standard

 

In civil actions seeking restitution or the replacement of a motor vehicle pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 871.20, within 60 days of the filing of an answer or other responsive pleading, the “parties shall, without awaiting a discovery request, provide to all other parties an initial disclosure and documents pursuant to subdivisions (f), (g), and (h).” (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26, subd. (b).) Plaintiffs must provide certain documents and information pursuant to subdivisions (f) and (g), and defendants must provide the documents outlined in subdivision (h). Sanctions of $1,500 and $2,500 are required against a plaintiff or defendant, respectively, that fails to comply with section 871.26, subdivision (b), absent a showing of good cause. (Id. § 871.26, subd. (j)(1).)

 

B.     Discussion

 

Plaintiffs move for an order compelling Defendant to produce all discovery materials required as initial disclosures under Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26. Specifically, Plaintiffs argue that Defendant has not provided verified responses and has not produced all materials listed within section 871.26, subdivision (h), subparagraphs 6–10, 12, and 13. Plaintiffs also seek sanctions for Defendant’s failure to produce these materials. The Court will address each issue in turn.

 

Defendant need not provide verifications.

 

First, Plaintiff argues that Defendant’s responses are deficient because it has not provided verifications. Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26 does not include a verification requirement. Instead, Plaintiff points to the verification requirement set out in section 2016.090. That section provides for certain initial disclosures required in all civil actions. (Id., § 2016.090, subd. (a)(1).) It further states that “disclosures under this section shall be verified either in a written declaration by the party or the party’s authorized representative, or signed by the party’s counsel.” (Id., § 2016.090, subd. (a)(5).) This requirement is limited to section 2016.090 by subdivision (a)(5). Thus, the provision does not require verifications for other initial disclosures, such as those required under section 871.26. Plaintiff’s citation to the legislative history surrounding Assembly Bill 1755, the legislation which produced the Song-Beverly initial disclosure regime, is irrelevant. Even if such sources constituted binding authority, the specific report to which Plaintiff refers does not even discuss a verification requirement. Thus, this argument is without merit.

 

Defendant has not provided all initial disclosures as required by statute.

Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s motion should be denied because Defendant has provided the required initial disclosures. However, Defendant provides no testimony supporting this assertion. Defendant represents that it provided “repair records, warranty claims records, the repair order detail report, vehicle information records, sales documents, service contract reports, Customer Assistance Inquiry records, the Monroney label, TSBs, and recall-related materials, among other documents.” Subdivision (h)(10) requires production of published information service bulletins for vehicles of the same year, make, and model of the subject vehicle and reasonably related to its nonconformities, which Defendant has not provided. Subdivision (h)(12) requires production of warranty policies and procedure manuals, which Defendant has not provided. And subdivision (h)(13) requires production of service manuals for the subject vehicle and reasonably related to its nonconformities, which Defendant has not provided. Thus, Defendant has not provided all initial disclosures as required under section 871.26, subdivision (h).

 

Sanctions are warranted under section 871.26.

 

Plaintiff argues for the imposition of sanctions. Unless the defendant failing to comply with section 871.26 shows good cause, a court shall impose a $2,500 sanction against the defense attorney if the defendant fails to comply with document production requirements. (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26, subd. (j)(1).) Here, Defendant does not present a justification for failing to comply with the initial disclosure requirements besides disagreement over their scope. Accordingly, the Court imposes $2,500 in sanctions on Defendant’s counsel.

 

C.     Conclusion


The Court grants Plaintiff’s motion in part. Defendant is ordered to provide initial disclosures as required under section 871.26, subdivisions (h)(10), (12), (13). The Court awards $2,500 in sanctions.
