Background

 

On January 3, 2025, Karen Shainsky (“Shainsky”) filed a petition to confirm arbitration award against Farmers Insurance Exchange (“Farmers”) under lead case number 25STCP00038, and on March 18, 2025, the Court confirmed the award acknowledging that the award did not address coverage issues and that any issues could be resolved in a subsequent lawsuit. On April 11, 2025, the Court entered judgment.

 

On May 9, 2025, Farmers filed a Complaint under case number 25VECV02593 against Shainsky for declaratory relief seeking a judicial declaration of rights, duties and obligations under the Policy (“Declaratory Relief Action”). Shainsky filed a Cross-Complaint for breach of contract, breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, and unfair business practices (“Breach Cross-Complaint”) against Farmers.

 

On June 24, 2025, in the lead case, the Court granted Farmers’s motion for a new trial and vacated the April 11, 2025 Judgment; the Court corrected the Judgment to reflect that the Appraisal Award evaluated the replacement cost value as $672,662.19 and the actual cash value as $631,984.03. The motion to vacate the judgment was denied as moot. The amended Judgment was entered on July 7, 2025.

 

On June 10, 2026, Shainsky filed her Motion for Summary Judgment. On August 25, 2026, Farmers filed its Opposition. On September 4, 2026, Shainsky filed her Reply.

 

LEGAL STANDARD – SUMMARY JUDGMENT

 

The function of a motion for summary judgment is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party cannot show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co.¿(2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) Code of Civil Procedure section 437c(c) “requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to¿any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.”¿ (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.)¿ “The function of the pleadings in a motion for summary judgment is to delimit the scope of the issues; the function of the affidavits or declarations is to disclose whether there is any triable issue of fact within the issues delimited by the pleadings.”¿ (Juge¿v. County of Sacramento¿(1993) 12 Cal.App.4th 59, 67 [citing¿FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima¿(1991) 231 Cal. App. 3d 367, 381-382].)¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿

 

As to each claim as framed by the complaint, the party moving for summary judgment must satisfy the initial burden of proof by presenting facts to negate an essential element, or to establish a defense. (Code Civ.¿Proc., § 437c(p)(2);¿Scalf¿v. D. B. Log Homes, Inc.¿(2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1520.) Courts “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.” (Dore v. Arnold Worldwide, Inc.¿(2006) 39 Cal.4th 384, 389.)¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿

 

Once the defendant has met that burden, the burden shifts to the opposing party to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to that cause of action or a defense thereto. To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence. (Sangster v.¿Paetkau¿(1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

DISCUSSION

 

A.    Request for Summary Adjudication

 

While the Notice to the Motion states that Shainsky is moving in the alternative for summary adjudication of issues, this is the only reference to adjudication in both the Motion and Reply. (Mot., at p. 2.) The Separate Statement identifies no causes of action, affirmative defenses, claim for damages or issues of duty that Shainsky is asking the Court to summarily adjudicate.  (Code Civ. Proc. § 437c(f)(1); Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1350(b).)  The Motion’s caption and conclusion only reference a Motion for Summary Judgment.  For these reasons, because Shainsky has failed to comply with the statute and rules for presenting a motion for summary adjudication, the Court denies summary adjudication and analyzes below Shainsky’s request as a Motion for Summary Judgment only.

 

B.    Res Judicata & Collateral Estoppel

 

Shainsky argues that the appraisal award issued on November 16, 2024, and entered by this Court on July 7, 2025, is binding on the parties and operates as a final judgment on the value of Shainsky’s loss. (Mot., at pp. 9-10.) Shainsky further argues that the doctrines of res judicata and collateral estoppel “prohibit Farmers from using this action to collaterally attack the confirmed judgment.” (Mot., at p. 10.)

 

The doctrine of res judicata applies when the issues decided in the prior adjudication are identical with those presented in the later action; there was a final judgment on the merits in the prior action; and the party against whom the plea is raised was a party in privity with a party in the prior adjudication. (Citizens for Open Access to Sand and Tide, Inc. v. Seadrift Ass’n (1998) 60 Cal.App.4th 1053, 1065.)  

 

“Res judicata describes the preclusive effect of a final judgment on the merits. Res judicata, or claim preclusion, prevents relitigation of the same cause of action in a second suit between the same parties or parties in privity with them. Collateral estoppel, or issue preclusion, precludes relitigation of issues argued and decided in prior proceedings. Under the doctrine of res judicata, if plaintiff prevails in an action, the cause is merged into the judgment and may not be asserted in a subsequent lawsuit; a judgment for the defendant serves as a bar to further litigation of the same cause of action. [¶] A clear and predictable res judicata doctrine promotes judicial economy. Under this doctrine, all claims based on the same cause of action must be decided in a single suit; if not brought initially, they may not be raised at a later date. Res judicata precludes piecemeal litigation by splitting a single cause of action or relitigation of the same cause of action in a different legal theory or for different relief.” (Mycogen Corp. v. Monsanto Co. (2002) 28 Cal.4th 888, 897.)  

 

The doctrine of collateral estoppel, also known as “[i]ssue preclusion, prevents relitigation of issues argued and decided in prior proceedings. The threshold requirements for issue preclusion are: (1) the issue is identical to that decided in the former proceeding, (2) the issue was actually litigated in the former proceeding, (3) the issue was necessarily decided in the former proceeding, (4) the decision in the former proceeding is final and on the merits, and (5) preclusion is sought against a person who was a party or in privity with a party to the former proceeding.” (Castillo v. City of Los Angeles (2001) 92 Cal.App.4th 477, 481.)  

  

Collateral estoppel applies to material issues well pleaded in the Complaint in the former action. It does not apply to immaterial allegations or issues not raised in the pleadings. (Burtnett v. King (1949) 33 Cal.2d 805, 810.) 

 

It is undisputed that on January 7, 2024, Shainsky discovered water in the kitchen of her home located on Aleman Drive in Tarzana, California, due to a failed refrigerator supply line. (UMF No. 1; Shainsky Decl., ¶ 2.) Farmers had previously issued Homeowners Policy No. 31747-80-50 for the property, which included “Coverage A (Dwelling) of $707,000, Coverage C (Personal Property) of $282,800, and Coverage D (Loss of Use / Additional Living Expense) of $141,400.” (UMF No. 2; Shainsky Decl., ¶ 2.) On November 16, 2024, the appraisal panel that Shainsky had invoked issued a Declaration of Appraisal Panel Award, signed by her appraiser and the neutral umpire, valuing the total loss at “$672,662.19 (replacement cost) / $631,984.03 (actual cash value), including: Dwelling Repairs ($406,781.61 RCV / $366,103.45 ACV); Building Code Upgrades ($13,592.25); Packout, Packback & Storage ($45,288.33); and Monthly Rental Cost ($207,000).” (UMF Nos. 10-11; Shainsky Decl., ¶¶ 6-7.) The award was made “without consideration of any coverage issues, policy limits, deductible amounts, prior payments by the Insurer, non-covered items, or other provisions of the governing insurance policy that might affect the existence and/or amount of the Insurer's liability, if any.” (UMF No. 12; Shainsky Decl., ¶ 7; Comp., Ex. 3.)

 

On January 3, 2025, Shainsky filed a Petition to Confirm the Appraisal Award in Los Angeles Superior Court case no. 25STCP00038; on April 11, 2025, judgment on the confirmed appraisal award was initially entered, specifying, however, that “[n]othing herein is intended to preclude separate legal proceedings on issues unrelated to the appraisal process. … The issue of whether the loss is covered under the policy is a separate, legal issue that must be resolved outside the appraisal process.” (UMF Nos. 13-14; S.’s RJN, Exs. 1-3.) On June 24, 2025, the Court granted Farmers’ motion for new trial and vacated the April 11 Order, correcting the Judgment “to reflect that the Appraisal Award evaluated the replacement cost value as $672,662.19 and the actual cash value as $631,984.03.” (UMF No. 16; S.’s RJN, Ex. 4.) Farmers did not seek to vacate the appraisal award under Code of Civil Procedure section 1286.2 and did not appeal the judgment. (UMF No. 17; Shainsky Decl., ¶ 8.)

 

Shainsky argues that Farmers’ “complain[t]s that the appraisal did not conform to technical policy requirements” and its request to “declare the appraisal award ‘unenforceable’ based on alleged misconduct by the umpire” and abuse of power allegations are barred based on the Court’s prior Judgment on June 24, 2025, in LASC, case no. 25STCP00038. (Mot., at pp. 9-10.) The amount of loss, including the ALE component, was “squarely at issue” in the prior proceeding and are thus barred by res judicata. (Mot., at pp. 10-11.) Shainsky concedes, however, that “some coverage issues remain open” based on said prior order but contends that the current Complaint “goes far beyond coverage disputes.” (Mot., at p. 11.)

 

Even if the Court were to find that Farmers may not prevail on some of the issues as to which it is seeking declaratory relief, Shainsky has failed to show that she is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.  Under Insurance Code section 2071, an appraiser’s role is constrained to that of “appraising ‘the loss, stating separately actual cash value and loss to each item. …’ Appraisers have no power to interpret the insurance contract or the governing statutes.” (Kirkwood v. California State Auto. Assn. Inter-Ins. Bureau (2011) 193 Cal.App.4th 49, 58-59.) An appraiser’s authority is limited only to the factual question of actual cash value or amount of loss of a given item. (Ibid.) As the Court highlighted in the Amended Judgment filed on July 7, 2025, “the Appraisal Award evaluated the replacement cost value as $672,662.19 and the actual cash value as $631,984.03;” this finding does not stretch to coverage, policy limits, and the insured’s compliance with the conditions. (UMF No. 16; S.’s RJN, Ex. 4.) Thus, Farmers’ claims as to coverage, the ALE limit, non-covered items and the award, Shainsky’s cooperation and honesty during the investigation, the joint-obligation and void-policy provisions, and/or the collectability of the judgment are not barred because they have not been actually litigated or necessarily decided. (Compl., ¶ 35.)

 

For the reasons explained above, the Court finds that Farmers’ claims are not completely barred under the doctrines of res judicata and/or collateral estoppel.

C.    Fraud or Material Misrepresentation

 

Shainsky argues that the Complaint’s reliance on the policy’s Misrepresentation, Concealment, or Fraud Provision as a basis to deny coverage fails as a matter of law, because “the argument fails on the undisputed facts.” (Mot., at pp. 12-13.) Specifically, Shainsky argues that “[t]here is no legal bar to renting a property from a spouse,” and that her husband’s failure to provide his private banking record cannot be attributed to her. (Mot., at p. 13.) Lastly, Shainsky contends that the confirmed appraisal award undermines the fraud theory. (Id.)

 

As an initial matter, the Court finds Shainsky’s last argument regarding the appraisal award to be unpersuasive because as the Court analyzed above, appraisal fixes only the amount of loss and does not speak to coverage or liability; Shainsky’s own moving papers acknowledge this standard.

 

“In order for a policy to be voided under a fraud and concealment clause, the insured's misrepresentation must: (1) relate to a material matter; (2) be knowingly and wilfully [sic] made with knowledge of falsity; and (3) be made with the intent to defraud or deceive.” (Chierfue Her v. State Farm Ins. Co. (2015) 92 F. Supp. 3d 957, 969.)

 

A misrepresentation is material if it relates to the insurer's ‘investigation to determine its obligations under the policy.’ [internal citation omitted] If an insured's misrepresentation ‘concerns a subject reasonably relevant to the insurer's investigation, and if a reasonable insurer would attach importance to the fact misrepresented, then it is material.’ [internal citation omitted] Materiality is generally a mixed question of law and fact, but may be decided as a matter of law if reasonable minds could not disagree on the materiality of the misrepresentation. [internal citation omitted] The issues of whether a statement was knowingly and willfully made with knowledge of falsity, and whether the statements were made with the intent to defraud, are both questions of fact. [internal citation omitted] However, the intent to defraud is necessarily implied ‘when the misrepresentation is material and the insured wilfully [sic] makes it with knowledge of its falsity.’ [internal citation omitted].” (Ibid.)

 

Here, Shainsky fails to carry her burden. Her argument regarding whether renting from a spouse is lawful misframes the issue. The question here, instead, turns on whether Shainsky concealed, omitted, or misrepresented a material fact about the arrangement in presenting the claim and/or during the investigation. Shainsky does not dispute in reply “the fact that [she] did not volunteer, unprompted, that her landlord was her husband before Farmers’ April 1, 2024, recorded interview.” (Reply at p. 6.) Instead, Shainsky impermissibly attempts to shift the burden to Farmers and unpersuasively hinges her argument primarily on “intent.” Shainsky does not address whether “a reasonable insurer would attach importance to the fact misrepresented” – i.e. whether she is an owner of the Studio City property and if her husband was her landlord. (Chierfue Her, supra, 92 F. Supp. 3d at 969.) Thus, Shainsky fails to establish that “reasonable minds could not disagree on the materiality of the misrepresentation.” (Ibid.)

 

Shainsky’s second argument is also unpersuasive. While her husband is a non-insured third party, this point does not resolve the case on summary judgment because, as found in the previous paragraph, Shainsky failed to overcome the burden as to her own statutory duty. (Mot., Ex. 1.) Farmers alleges that Shainsky misrepresented or concealed that she was married to her “landlord” and/or her ownership stake in the Studio City property she was leasing during the investigation. Thus, the “husband argument” is not enough to support summary judgment.  

 

D.    ALE

 

Shainsky argues that Farmers’ contention “that the ALE appraisal award of $207,000 exceeds the policy's ALE limit of $141,400 and is therefore unenforceable,” is impermissible. (Mot., at p. 13.) Specifically, Shainsky contends that this claim “conflates” the valuation and coverage/limits issue. (Id.)

 

In the Complaint, Farmers seeks a judicial declaration “[t]hat the maximum additional living expenses recoverable under the policy is $141,400, irrespective of the appraisal award.” (Compl., ¶ 35.) In the general allegations, Farmers alleges that “the appraisal award amount was $207,000 -- far in excess of the policy’s ALE limit of $141,000 [sic].” (Id., ¶ 28.) Shainsky misrepresents Farmers’ “contention;” based on a plain reading of the Complaint’s allegations, Farmers seems to argue and focus on the coverage/limits issue rather than the valuation. Farmers does not allege the valuation is “unenforceable” based on the ALE finding – unenforceability is alleged only once in the Complaint, as to the arbitrators’ alleged excess power, the umpire’s misconduct, and/or the undue procurement of the award. (Id., ¶ 35.)

 

Based on the foregoing, the Court finds that this point is not in contention. And as analyzed in Section A, the coverage/limits issue is still ripe.

 

Conclusion

 

As explained above, defendant Karen Shainsky has failed to carry the burden required to obtain summary judgment under section 437c(c) of the Code of Civil Procedure – that is, to establish that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to a judgment as a matter of law.  Her Motion for Summary Judgment is DENIED.

Background

 

On April 2, 2026, Plaintiff Rosette Aquino (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Defendants JetSelect, LLC dba Vista America, Stephen Barnhouse, and Does 1 through 10, inclusive, alleging causes of action for (1) Sex/Gender Discrimination and Harassment in Violation of FEHA; (2) Assault; (3) Battery; (4) Negligent Supervision; and (5) Negligent Retention. The action arises from alleged assault, battery, and harassment committed by Defendant Stephen Barnhouse (“Barnhouse”), who is employed as a Pilot by Defendant JetSelect, LLC (“JetSelect”), during a commercial flight on October 21, 2024, during which Plaintiff worked as a Cabin Hostess.

 

On May 11, 2026, Barnhouse’s counsel removed this action to the United States District Court for the Central District of California. On May 28, 2026, the action was remanded back to this Court per a joint stipulation.

LEGAL STANDARD – MOTION TO QUASH SERVICE

 

A defendant may move to quash service of summons on the ground of lack of jurisdiction of the court over them. (Code Civ. Proc., § 418.10, subd. (a)(1).) The court may dismiss without prejudice the complaint in whole, or as to that defendant, when dismissal is made pursuant to section 418.10. (Code Civ. Proc., § 581, subd. (h).)¿ 

 

"A court of this state may exercise jurisdiction on any basis not inconsistent with the Constitution of this state or of the United States." (Code Civ. Proc., § 410.10.) "The Due Process Clause protects an individual's liberty interest in not being subject to the binding judgments of a forum with which he has established no meaningful 'contacts, ties, or relations."' (Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz (1985) 471 U.S. 462, 471 – 472.) A state court may not exercise personal jurisdiction over a party under circumstances that would offend "traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice." (Asahi Metal Industry Co., Ltd., v. Superior Court of California, Solano County (1987) 480 U.S. 102, 113.)¿ 

 

When a defendant moves to quash service of process on jurisdictional grounds, the plaintiff has the initial burden of demonstrating facts justifying the exercise of jurisdiction. (Jayone Foods, Inc. v. Aekyung Industrial Co. Ltd. (2019) 31 Cal.App.5th 543, 553.) Once facts showing minimum contacts with the forum state are established, the defendant has the burden to demonstrate the exercise of jurisdiction would be unreasonable. (Ibid.) "The plaintiff must provide specific evidentiary facts, through affidavits and other authenticated documents, sufficient to allow the court to independently conclude whether jurisdiction is appropriate. [Citation.] The plaintiff cannot rely on allegations in an unverified complaint or vague and conclusory assertions of ultimate facts. [Citation.]" (Strasner v. Touchstone Wireless Repair & Logistics, LP (2016) 5 Cal.App.5th 215, 222.)¿ 

 

A defendant is subject to a state's general¿jurisdiction¿if its contacts "are so continuous¿and systematic as to render [it] essentially at home in the forum State." (Daimler AG v. Bauman¿(2014) 571 U.S. 117, 127.) A nonresident defendant may be subject to specific¿jurisdiction¿in the forum "if the defendant has purposefully availed himself or herself of forum benefits [citation], and the 'controversy is related to or "arises out of' a defendant's contacts with the forum.' [Citations.]" (Vons Companies, Inc. v.¿Seabest¿Foods, Inc.¿(1996) 14 Cal.4th 434, 446.) This test does not require a "causal relationship between the defendant's in-state activity and the litigation." (Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial District Court¿(2021)¿592 U.S. 351,¿362) The "arise out of" standard "asks about causation," but "relate to" does not.¿(Ibid.)¿"[W]hen a corporation has 'continuously and deliberately exploited [a State's] market, it must reasonably anticipate being¿haled¿into [that State's] court[s]'¿to defend actions 'based on' products causing injury there."¿(Id.¿at p.¿364.)

DISCUSSION

 

A.    General Jurisdiction

 

General jurisdiction is not at issue here. General jurisdiction exists where the activities of a non-resident defendant are so “extensive or wide-ranging” or “substantial … continuous and systematic” to establish a constitutionally sufficient relationship to warrant jurisdiction for all causes of action asserted against it.¿ (Cornelison¿v. Chaney¿(1976) 16 Cal.3d 143, 147.)

Plaintiff does not dispute that the Court lacks general jurisdiction over Barnhouse and only asserts arguments as to specific jurisdiction in her opposition. Accordingly, the Court analyzes whether Plaintiff presents sufficient evidence that Barnhouse is subject to specific jurisdiction in California.

B.    Specific Jurisdiction

 

A court may exercise specific jurisdiction over a defendant when three elements are satisfied: “(1) ‘the defendant has purposefully availed himself or herself of forum benefits’ [citation]; (2) ‘the controversy is related to or “arises out of” [the] defendant's contacts with the forum’ [citation]; and (3) “‘“the assertion of personal jurisdiction would comport with ‘fair play and substantial justice’” [citation.]”  (Pavlovich v. Superior Court (2002) 29 Cal.4th 262, 269.)

 

Here, Barnhouse argues that there is no factual basis to establish specific jurisdiction because Barnhouse has not purposefully availed himself of California’s benefits and the controversies at issue are not related to Barnhouse’s forum-related contacts. (Motion at pp. 6-8 [“Plaintiff’s theory improperly conflates JetSelect’s contacts with those of Barnhouse. The Complaint relies on JetSelect’s California operations to establish jurisdiction (Compl. ¶¶ 2(c), 4), but jurisdiction must be assessed as to each defendant individually. Plaintiff further alleges that Barnhouse availed himself of California by working on flights departing from the state (Compl. ¶ 6), yet also admits he acted solely within the course and scope of his employment (Compl. ¶¶ 1, 13). These allegations confirm that any contacts are derivative of his employment—not the result of deliberate, personal forum-directed conduct. … the alleged conduct occurred within a transitory, federally regulated aviation context, not from Barnhouse invoking the benefits or protections of California law or targeting the forum. (Barnhouse Decl. ¶¶ 10-14) This attenuated connection is insufficient to satisfy the “arising out of” requirement. See Vons, supra, 14 Cal.4th at 452.”].)

 

1.     Purposeful Availment

 

Purposeful availment “focuses on the defendant's intentionality. This prong is satisfied when the defendant purposefully and voluntarily directs his activities toward the forum so that he should expect, by virtue of the benefit he receives, to be subject to the court's jurisdiction based on his contacts with the forum.” (Pavlovich, supra, 29 Cal.4th at p. 269.)

 

Plaintiff argues that Barnhouse is subject to specific jurisdiction because the claims arise from an intentional tort he committed while physically present in California. (Opp., at p. 6.) Plaintiff relies on Taylor-Rush v. Multitech Corp. (1990) 217 Cal.App.3d 103, 113, and Calder v. Jones (1984) 465 U.S. 783, in support. Plaintiff further argues that Barnhouse cannot “shield” himself from jurisdiction by arguing that his conduct is derivative of his employment. (Opp., at pp. 7-8.) Lastly, Plaintiff argues that Barnhouse’s relevant contacts with California were not incidental or “transitory,” and the real issue “is whether California may adjudicate claims arising from intentionally tortious conduct this pilot engaged in while here.” (Opp., at p. 9.)

 

Plaintiff relies solely on Barnhouse’s and her own Declarations as evidentiary support.

 

Barnhouse states in his declaration that he was employed by JetSelect at the time of the incident and occasionally operated flights originating from and/or landing in California (like LAX). (Barnhouse Decl., ¶¶ 7-8.) He did not personally select California as a business destination but instead was directed by JetSelect’s operational needs and assignments. (Id., ¶ 9.) He states that the alleged incident arises solely out of his duties as prescribed in the course and scope of his employment and his presence in California’s airspace was “transitory in nature and incidental to interstate aviation operations.” (Id., ¶¶ 14-15.)

 

Plaintiff states the following in her declaration: On October 21, 2024, she was assigned to work as a Cabin Hostess for a flight from LAX to Moffett Federal Airfield in Mountain View, California; Barnhouse was the Captain and Pilot in Command. (Aquino Decl., ¶ 3.) Fifteen minutes after departure, while the plane was in California’s airspace, Barnhouse approached Plaintiff “and forcefully grabbed [her] right hand and wrist.” (Id., ¶¶ 4-5.) Plaintiff attempted to remove her hand, but Barnhouse maintained his hold and “flung” her hand, resulting in the parts of the table at which she was seated to break. (Id., ¶ 6.) She reported the incident both during the flight and once it landed. (Id., ¶¶ 8-9.) As a Cabin Hostess, Plaintiff worked through and interacted with JetSelect’s fixed-base operator location at Van Nuys Airport; she also worked with Barnhouse on multiple flights, including the October 21 flight. (Id., ¶¶ 13-17.)

 

Martensen v. Koch, 942 F.Supp.2d 983 (N.D. Cal. 2013) is applicable and persuasive here. In Martensen, the plaintiff alleged that defendant falsely imprisoned him through his agents’ actions, which included ordering him onto a flight from Denver to Oakland, CA on a private plane allegedly owned and/or controlled by defendant. (Id. at 995.) The court found that “Plaintiff's allegations that Defendant committed the tort of false imprisonment within the forum satisfy the prima facie requirement for personal jurisdiction.” (Ibid.) The court also noted that because the alleged tortious conduct occurred in California, the Calder effects test is not relevant. (Id. at 994; Calder v. Jones (1984) 465 U.S. 783.)

 

Based on the analysis in Martensen, Plaintiff has established sufficient evidence of Barnhouse’s purposeful availment of California. Barnhouse’s argument that his presence in California was employer directed and thus not purposeful is unpersuasive.  Purposeful availment hinges on the defendant’s intentional conduct. Barnhouse does not dispute that the alleged incident occurred in California airspace on a flight that departed from a California airport and landed in another California airport. Accordingly, the alleged intentional torts committed by Barnhouse while in California airspace satisfy the purposeful availment prong.

 

2.     Relatedness

 

The relatedness inquiry is not strictly causal and instead focuses on whether “the claim bears a substantial connection to the nonresident's forum contacts.” (Vons Companies, Inc., supra, 14 Cal.4th at 452.)

 

Plaintiff argues in opposition that her claims arise directly from the alleged assault and battery committed by Barnhouse. (Opp., at pp. 10-11 [“The argument artificially separates Barnhouse’s California contact from the conduct giving rise to this action. They are the same occurrence. Barnhouse was physically present in California, serving as Captain of a flight originating at LAX and traveling to another California airport, when he left the flight deck, entered Plaintiff’s workspace, grabbed her hand and wrist, and forcefully caused her hand to strike and break part of the table. (Aquino Decl., ¶¶ 3-6.) Plaintiff’s claims against Barnhouse arise directly from that conduct.”].) Plaintiff further argues that Barnhouse’s authorities are not applicable here because her argument supporting jurisdiction relies on Barnhouse's intentional conduct while present in California airspace, not “because Barnhouse landed an aircraft here or happened to be physically present here.” (Opp., at p. 11.)

 

Plaintiff relies on the same evidence as above: On October 21, 2024, she was assigned to work as a Cabin Hostess for a flight from LAX to Moffett Federal Airfield in Mountain View, California; Barnhouse was the Captain and Pilot in Command. (Aquino Decl., ¶ 3.) Fifteen minutes after departure, while the plane was in California’s airspace, Barnhouse approached Plaintiff “and forcefully grabbed [her] right hand and wrist.” (Id., ¶¶ 4-5.) Plaintiff attempted to remove her hand, but Barnhouse maintained his hold and “flung” her hand, resulting in the parts of the table she was seated at to break. (Id., ¶ 6.) She reported the incident both during the flight and once it landed. (Id., ¶¶ 8-9.) As a Cabin Hostess, Plaintiff worked through and interacted with JetSelect’s fixed-base operator location at Van Nuys Airport; she also worked with Barnhouse on multiple flights, including the October 21st flight. (Id., ¶¶ 13-17.) Barnhouse also acknowledges that the alleged incident arose while present in California’s airspace, even though he argues said contact was “transitory in nature and incidental to interstate aviation operations.” (Barnhouse Decl., ¶¶ 14-15.)

 

Plaintiff alleges that the intentional torts were committed in the California airspace by Barnhouse; the claims arise out of this very (direct) conduct. (Compl., ¶¶ 33-43.) Based on the foregoing, the Court finds that Plaintiff has satisfied her burden. (See Martensen, supra, 942 F.Supp.2d at 996 [“Because Plaintiff has adequately alleged that Defendant committed the tort of false imprisonment within the forum, the Court accordingly finds that Plaintiff has made a prima facie showing of minimum contacts needed to establish personal jurisdiction.”].)

 

3.     Fair Play and Substantial Justice

 

“In making this determination [as to whether the assertion of¿jurisdiction¿would comport with fair play and substantial justice], the court must consider the burden on the defendant, the interests of the forum State, and the plaintiff's interest in obtaining relief. It must also weigh in its determination the interstate judicial system's interest in obtaining the most efficient resolution of controversies; and the shared interest of the several States in furthering fundamental substantive social policies.”¿(Snowney¿v. Harrah’s Entertainment, Inc. (2005) 35 Cal.4th 1054, 1070.)

 

Barnhouse argues that this Court’s exercise of jurisdiction would not be reasonable over him because: (1) he would be unfairly prejudiced as a Florida resident; (2) California lacks an interest in this case because both Barnhouse and Plaintiff are Florida residents; (3) Plaintiff, as mentioned, is not a California resident and she is “attempting to improperly use California’s more stringent and robust laws to her advantage;” and (4) “the extent to which Plaintiff’s causes of action ar[i]se out of Barnhouse’s local activities … is extremely attenuated.” (Motion at pp. 9-10.)

 

Plaintiff argues in opposition that Barnhouse’s reliance on his out-of-state status is irrelevant because this fact does not inherently make jurisdiction unreasonable and because Barnhouse’s work had him traveling to California. (Opp., at p. 12.) In addition, the assault and incident report occurred in California and Plaintiff was treated in a California hospital for her alleged injuries. (Id.)  Plaintiff contends that the exercise of jurisdiction is reasonable because the action is already pending in this Court for the same issue and California is directly connected to the intentional torts alleged. (Id)

 

The Court finds that factors weigh in favor of litigation in California. Plaintiff alleges that she was assaulted in California. California has an interest in resolving this dispute in California because Plaintiff sustained her injuries in California. Additionally, other Defendants to this action are properly sued in California. The interstate judicial system has an interest in allowing this matter to stay in California because it is most efficient to resolve Plaintiff’s dispute with Barnhouse in California together with the other Defendants.[1]
