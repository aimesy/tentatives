Case Number: 24VECV00339    Hearing Date: September 3, 2026    Dept: B

Tentative Ruling

King v. Seamster, Case no. 24VECV00339

Hearing date September 3, 2026

Proposed Intervenor Geico’s Motion for Leave to Intervene

Plaintiff King sues defendant Seamster for personal injury arising out of a motor vehicle accident. Defendant’s insurer, Geico General Insurance Company, moves for leave to intervene. Plaintiff filed opposition 3 days late; the court will consider the arguments on the merits, as Geico filed a substantive reply.

Per Code Civ. Proc. §387(d)(1): “The court shall, upon timely application, permit a nonparty to intervene in the action or proceeding if …: (A) A provision of law confers an unconditional right to intervene; (B) The person seeking intervention claims an interest relating to the property or transaction that is the subject of the action and that person is so situated that the disposition of the action may impair or impede that person's ability to protect that interest, unless that person's interest is adequately represented by one or more of  the  existing parties.”

Intervention is permitted when: (1) proper procedures have been followed; (2) the nonparty has a direct and immediate interest in the action; (3) intervention will not enlarge the issues; and (4) the reasons for the intervention outweigh any opposition by the parties presently in the action. Siena Court Homeowners Ass’n v. Green Valley Corp. (2008) 164 Cal.App.4th 1416, 1428.  Code Civ. Proc. §387 should be liberally construed in favor of intervention. Simpson Redwood Co. v. State of California (1987) 196 Cal.App.3d 1192, 1200.

Because a liability insurer agrees to pay any judgment obtained against its insured (Ins. Code §11580(b)(2)), it has the right to intervene where an insured is barred from defending itself. In such cases, intervention is necessary to protect the insurer’s own interest because it may be obligated to pay any judgment rendered against its insured. See Reliance Ins. Co. v. Sup. Ct. (2000) 84 Cal.App.4th 383, 387.

Geico is defendant’s insurer. Decl. Rabbani, para. 2. Geico has an undisputable direct interest in this litigation, in which defendant refused to cooperate or participate in the defense. Id. at paras. 5, 8-9.

Plaintiff argues Geico’s motion is untimely, as trial is 2 months away. Plaintiff argues Geico should have been aware of defendant’s lack of cooperation sooner, as defendant demonstrated an intent not to defend as early as 10/3/25. See Decl. Navarro, para. 2. Plaintiff argues allowing intervention on the eve of trial will cause undue prejudice after plaintiff expended “months attempting to obtain discovery from Defendant.” Opp., 8:3-4.

In determining whether intervention is timely, courts focus on the date the person attempting to intervene should have been aware his interests would no longer be protected adequately by the parties, not the date the person learned of the litigation. Defense counsel Rabbani states prior defense counsel was unable to contact defendant. Decl. Rabbani, para. 3. Current defense counsel associated into this matter in January 2026 and began attempting to contact defendant. Id. at paras. 4-5. Current counsel became certain of defendant’s intention not to cooperate when defendant refused to speak to Geico after being located and refused to cooperate with police. Id. at paras. 5, 8.

Geico does not seek a trial continuance and does not intend to raise any defenses not previously asserted. Decl. Rabbani, para. 9. The risk of prejudice to plaintiff is minimal. Geico timely moved to intervene after becoming certain defendant would not participate and has a clear, indisputable interest in this litigation. Intervention is appropriate. GRANTED; Geico to file its proposed answer-in-intervention within 2 court days.


Case Number: 24VECV05588    Hearing Date: September 3, 2026    Dept: B

Tentative Ruling

Mohammadi v. Farmers Insurance Exchange, Case no. 24VECV05588

Hearing date September 3, 2026

Plaintiff Mohammadi’s Motion to Compel Further Responses

Plaintiff Mohammadi sues defendant Farmers Insurance Exchange for breach of contract, breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, violations of Bus. & Prof. §17200, et seq. and derivative causes of action, alleging he was injured in a motor vehicle incident, and defendant insurer failed to pay benefits.

Plaintiff moves to compel further responses to special interrogatories, set one, and for monetary sanctions. Defendant opposes. The parties were unable to resolve the dispute at IDC. 7/23/26 Min. Order.

Per Code Civ. Proc. §2031.310(a) “demanding party may move for an order compelling further response to the demand if the demanding party deems that . . . (1) A statement of compliance with the demand is incomplete. (2) A representation of inability to comply is inadequate, incomplete, or evasive...”

Plaintiff served 70 special interrogatories, set one. Plaintiff asserts defendant served baseless objections and evasive responses to nos. 1, 5, 8, 15, 26, 27, 30, 33, 36, 39, 40, 48, 49, 51, 52, 62, 63, 65, 67, and 68. See Decl. Jimenez, exh. B. Plaintiff asserts defendant improperly exercised the “Document Production Option” regarding responses nos. 1, 5, 8, 15, 27, 30, 33, 36, 39, 40, 48, 49, 51, 52, 62, 63, and 65. Id. Plaintiff argues defendant improperly asserts attorney-client and work product privilege objections. Plaintiff further argues defendant improperly refers to documents not sufficiently specified to allow plaintiff to locate and identify the responses. See Code Civ. Proc. §2030.230.

Defendant argues its responses contain reference to Bates stamp ranges, allowing plaintiff to locate the responses. Defendant argues its privilege and work product doctrine objections were proper and stands on the objections. Defendant asserts it offered to produce responses previously withheld on the basis of privacy if plaintiff enters into a protective order, and plaintiff failed to respond. See Decl. McKeon, exh. F. Defendant asserts it agreed to serve further responses to SROG no. 67. Opp., 5:15-18.

Per Code Civ. Proc. §2030.230: “[i]f the answer to an interrogatory would necessitate the preparation or the making of a compilation, abstract, audit, or summary of or from the documents of the party to whom the interrogatory is directed, and if the burden or expense of preparing or making it would be substantially the same for the party propounding the interrogatory as for the responding party, it is a sufficient answer to that interrogatory to refer to this section and to specify the writings from which the answer may be derived or ascertained.” Providing responses consisting of Bates stamp ranges is consistent with Deyo v. Kilbourne (1978) 84 Cal.App.3d 771, 783–784. The responses to nos. 1, 5, 8, 15, 27, 30, 33, 36, 39-40, 48-49, 51-52, 62-63 and 65 are sufficient.

To the extent responses were withheld exclusively on privacy grounds, production is warranted pursuant to protective order. Counsel to jointly draft a proposed protective order, to be filed by plaintiff’s counsel within 10 days of hearing. Counsel may wish to use the model protective order available on the court website. Subsequent to, and contingent on, the protective order being filed, defendant is to serve further responses to nos. 26 and 68 within 30 days.

To the extent responses were withheld on privilege grounds, defendant to provide a privilege log, stating the portions of each interrogatory being objected to and providing code-compliant responses to all remaining portions of the interrogatory. Code Civ. Proc. §2030.240. Responses within 30 days of hearing.

Defendant admitted further responses to no. 67 are warranted, and the court ordered further responses to nos. 26 and 68, subject to protective order. Monetary sanctions are mandatory per Code Civ. Proc. §2030.300(d). As defense counsel was responsive and participated in meet-and-confer efforts and IDC, there is no basis to sanction defense counsel. Sanctions against defendant only.

Plaintiff requests a reasonable hourly rate of $350/hour. Decl. Jimenez, para. 7. Plaintiff requests 4 hours for motion drafting, 1 hour for opposition/reply and 1 hour for hearing (6 hours x $350/hour). Id. These hours are reasonable and granted, plus $60 in filing fees.

GRANTED. Responses as above and sanctions payable by defendant only within 30 days.

Tentative Ruling

Bibicoff v. Ford Motor Company, Case no. 25VECV05502

Hearing date September 3, 2026

Plaintiff Bibicoff’s Motions to Compel Further Discovery Responses

Plaintiff Bibicoff sues defendants Ford Motor Company and Knight Sunrise Fontana LLC, alleging violations of the Song-Beverly Act. Plaintiff moves to compel further responses to RFPS, set one, and SROGs, set one, and for sanctions on both motions. Ford opposes.

Per Code Civ. Proc. §2031.310(a) “the demanding party may move for an order compelling further response to the demand if the demanding party deems that . . . (1) A statement of compliance with the demand is incomplete. (2) A representation of inability to comply is inadequate, incomplete, or evasive...”

Per plaintiff’s 8/27/26 reply, the remaining issues were plaintiff’s RFPs relating to “RAV Policies and Procedures for the years 2022 through 2025” and “Ford’s Customer Relationship Center (“CRC”) materials for those same years.” Per a supplemental filing on 8/31/26, those documents were produced. See 8/31/26 Decl. Perez, paras. 5-6. The only remaining issue is sanctions.

Ford only served full responses after plaintiff filed the motion to compel further, demonstrating necessity of the motion. Sanctions are mandatory. Code Civ. Proc. 2031.310. As Ford’s counsel was responsive in serving responses, regardless of the alleged sufficiency, and in meet-and-confer efforts (see Decl. Leite, exh. C), sanctions are imposed against Ford only.

Plaintiff requests a reasonable hourly rate of $350/hour. Decl. Leite, para. 9. Plaintiff requests 2.6 hours for motion drafting, 2 hours for opposition/reply and 2 hours for hearing. Id. The court awards 2.6 hours for motion drafting, 2 hours for opposition review and reply drafting, and 1 hour for hearing, plus $60 in filing fees. Id. at para. 10. The court awards $2,020 against Ford only on the motion to compel further responses to RFPs, set one. GRANTED as to plaintiff’s motion to compel further responses to RFPs, set one, regarding sanctions only.

Plaintiff separately moves to compel further responses to SROGs, set one, nos. 10, 26, 47, 49-58. Plaintiff asserts Ford served objection-only responses and refused to serve supplemental responses despite meet-and-confer efforts. See Decl. Leite, para. 4-7, exhs. B-E. Plaintiff argues the SROGs seek information regarding training Ford provided from 2023 to present regarding evaluation of pre-litigation repurchase requests and the facts supporting the contention that the Subject Vehicle does not qualify for repurchase or replacement under Song-Beverly. Plaintiff argues this information is relevant.

Ford stands on its objections, arguing the SROGs are overly broad and seek irrelevant information. Ford argues there is no basis to produce responses regarding pre-litigation repurchase request training policies, as they have no direct relation to the subject vehicle. Ford argues no. 47 in particular seeks privileged information not subject to production, information regarding the process and mechanisms by which Ford met its discovery obligations in this case.

Ford produced information on its training policies, subject to a protective order, via the CRC documents above. There is no basis to compel further responses to nos. 10 and 26. Similarly, the SROGs seeking information regarding Ford’s contentions as to why the subject vehicle was not purchasable are sufficiently answered by Ford’s earlier responses to discovery. See 8/24/26 Sep. Statement; see also 8/31/26 Decl. Perez, paras. 5-6.

SROGs nos. 47, 49-58 improperly seek irrelevant discovery-on-discovery as to record retention technologies, policies and personnel. Plaintiff is not entitled to Ford’s internal litigation processes, including the manner in which Ford conducts discovery. While plaintiff is entitled to broad information regarding general records and retention policies (see Kwan v. Mercedes-Benz of North America (1994) 23 Cal.App.4th 174, 186) plaintiff is not entitled to information such as individuals responsible for operation, maintenance, expansion and upkeep of internal databases.

Relevance is a threshold issue for discovery. National Steel Products Co. v. Sup. Ct. (1985) 164 Cal.App.3d 476, 492-93. In a Song Beverly case, plaintiff’s discovery must relate to claims he is required to prove, including: (1) purchase/lease of a new motor vehicle manufactured by Ford; (2) Ford provided a written warranty; (3) the vehicle had a defect was covered by the warranty that substantially impaired its use, value, or safety; (4) plaintiff delivered the vehicle to Ford or authorized repair facility; (5) Ford or authorized repair facility failed to repair the vehicle to match the written warranty after a reasonable number of opportunities to do so; and (6) Ford did not promptly replace or buy back the vehicle. See CACI 3201. Ford’s internal records-keeping processes is not at issue; discovery is not relevant nor reasonably calculated to produce admissible evidence. There is no basis to compel further responses to SROGs nos. 47-49-58. DENIED. No sanctions in relation to plaintiff’s motion to compel further responses to SROGs, set one.


Case Number: 26VECV03729    Hearing Date: September 3, 2026    Dept: B

Tentative Ruling

Maradiaga v. 15116 Parthenia Street, Case no. 26VECV03729

Hearing date September 3, 2026

Defendant 15116’s Demurrer and Motion to Strike Complaint

Plaintiff Maradiaga sues defendant 15116 Parthenia Street, Inc. for violations of Los Angeles Municipal Code (LAMC) §§151.04 and 151.10, as well as declaratory relief. Defendant demurs to both and moves to strike the first four paragraphs. Plaintiff opposes. 

Defendant 15116’s Demurrer to the Complaint 

“The function of a demurrer is to test the sufficiency of the complaint as a matter of law.” Holiday Matinee, Inc. v. Rambus, Inc. (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 1413, 1420. A complaint “is sufficient if it alleges ultimate rather than evidentiary facts.” Doe v. City of Los Angeles (2007) 42 Cal.4th 531, 550, but plaintiff must allege essential facts “with reasonable precision and with particularity sufficient to acquaint [the] defendant with the nature, source and extent” of the plaintiff’s claim. Doheny Park Terrace Homeowners Ass’n., Inc. v. Truck Ins. Exchange (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 1076, 1099. 

Defendant demurs to the first cause of action for violations of LAMC §§151.04 and 151.10 as uncertain. “[S]tatutory causes of action must be pleaded with particularity.” Covenant Care, Inc. v. Superior Ct. (1985) 32 Cal.4th 771, 790. The first cause of action identifies 13 potential bases for the alleged violations. See Compl., para. 16. The complaint alleges defendants “demanded, accepted, or retained excess rent by one or more” of the 13 listed violations. Id. at para. 16.  

Plaintiff argues the particular means by which defendant illegally or impermissibly elicited or increased rent can be ascertained by documents in defendant’s possession, permitting plaintiff to plead with less particularity. Burks v. Poppy Construction Co. (1962) 57 Cal.2d 463, 474 (holding “less particularity is required where the defendant may be assumed to possess knowledge of the facts at least equal, if not superior, to that possessed by the plaintiff.”). This is unavailing.

While plaintiff need not plead exactly how a defendant allegedly engaged in a specific alleged violation, plaintiff cannot simply list 13 potential violations and assert defendant engaged in “one or more.” Compl., para. 16. California pleading law requires a complaint be pled with sufficient particularity that a defendant knows exactly what plaintiff is alleging. Doheny Park Terrace Homeowners Ass’n., Inc., supra. Plaintiff must allege with particularity which of violation(s) defendant committed. SUSTAINED with 15 days leave to amend.  

Defendant demurs to the declaratory relief claim as superfluous. "[A]n action in declaratory relief will not lie to determine an issue which can be determined in the underlying … action. 'The object of the statute is to afford a new form of relief where needed and not to furnish a litigant with a second cause of action for the determination of identical issues.'" California. Ins. Guaranty Assoc. v. Superior Court (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 1617, 1623. Defendant argues adjudication of the first cause of action would result in the same relief requested in the second, i.e., a judicial determination of the rent owed by plaintiff or creditable by defendant.  

Plaintiff argues her tenancy and rent obligations are ongoing, and the complaint prays for additional relief in the second cause of action not requested under the first. See Compl., prayers 1(a)-(g), 2(a)-(i). Where a relationship between the parties continues, a suit on the matured claim is not an adequate substitute for a declaration that will “guide their future conduct.” Warren v. Kaiser Foundation Health Plan, Inc. (1975) 47 Cal.App.3d 678, 683-684. 

The ongoing nature of plaintiff’s tenancy, coupled with the prayer for judicial determination of future rent and rent increases, distinguishes the second cause of action from the first. The second cause of action is not superfluous. OVERRULED. 

Defendant 15116’s Motion to Strike 

Defendant moves to strike the first four paragraphs of the complaint as irrelevant and inflammatory. As the court sustained the demurrer to the first cause of action with leave to amend, the motion to strike is MOOT.

The first four paragraphs contain general allegations regarding the state of the rental and housing situation in Los Angeles, not particularized to this case. Unnecessarily inflammatory and conclusory language has no place in a pleading.
