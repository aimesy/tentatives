Case Number: 24SMCV03288    Hearing Date: June 29, 2026    Dept: I

This is an application to continue the trial.  The court’s first question is whether this is an application by all parties, or only all defendants.  The court will assume the former. 

 

The basis is the recent death of lead counsel for one of the defendants.  There are other reasons as well.  The trial is currently set for the end of August. 

 

The court warned the parties at the CMC that the trial would not be continued absent truly unforeseen and unforeseeable circumstances.  But lead counsel’s death—as well as the death of another lead counsel for a different defendant—would qualify.  Accordingly, and assuming that this is a joint request by all parties to the litigation, the court is inclined to GRANT the request and VACATE the FSC and trial dates.  The court will hold a TSC at 8:30 am on August 24, 2026.  The parties should meet and confer prior to that time to see if there are blocks of time that will work for them.  The parties should also do their best to consult with the new parties in the case and to give notice to those parties of the TSC.  The court will inquire only to ensure that the request is by all parties, and not just the defense.

 


Case Number: 24SMCV03557    Hearing Date: June 29, 2026    Dept: I

This is a motion for judgment on the pleadings by plaintiff.  Plaintiff, a member of the HOA, claims that there has been a failure of governance.  He contends that the HOA has 532 units but that there is a history of neglect.  He further alleges that management has stolen $1.6 million.  Plaintiff notes that a verified answer was filed but that defendants claimed to lack sufficient information to admit or deny the allegations 94 times and that in other instances, defendant contended that it did not have to admit or deny an allegation.  Plaintiff also states that defendant spoliated evidence and that in a prior case, the HOA was found to have violated its governing documents.  Because of this, plaintiff argues that the court should deem all of the allegations in the complaint to be admitted, and therefore, he concludes, he wins on the pleadings.  Defendant contends that the argument lacks merit.  What the defense argues is that this is plaintiff’s attempt to relitigate a case he already lost and that the real motive here is to forestall foreclosure of a lien against him because he is delinquent in paying his HOA dues.  As to affirmative defenses (upon which plaintiff also seeks judgment), the defense argues that the pleading requirements are not as stringent as they are for a complaint, and that the answer pleads enough.  Finally, defendant contends that there is no “sham pleading” problem in the answer.  In reply, plaintiff contends that this case is different than the earlier case, and that service by defendant has been defective.  Plaintiff argues that in the prior litigation, the trial court held that there was no claim preclusion, and that ought to bind defendant here and bar that affirmative defense.  Plaintiff also reiterates that the lack of information and belief is bad faith.

 

The way to attack the “information and belief” issue is through demurrer, not an MJOP.  While the two motions are similar, the office of the MJOP is more merits based and not technical.  And even were plaintiff correct as to the “information and belief” point, the result would not be a judgment or to deem the allegations admitted; it would be to give defendant leave to amend.  The same is true of the claims that the affirmative defenses are not pled with sufficient particularity.  The court also notes that it will not consider new arguments raised for the first time in reply.

 

The motion is DENIED.

 

The matter is also hear on plaintiff’s ex parte application to file an overlength brief.  Although the court rarely does so, the court will GRANT the request to file an overlength brief in support of the preliminary injunction motion.  The brief may not exceed a total of 17.5 pages, excluding the cover page, tables, and proof of service.  Plaintiff has stated that he intends to use normal margins and font and spacing (24 lines/page), and it is on that basis the court is making its ruling.  The opposition may also be 17.5 pages in length.  The reply will be the normal 10 pages.  The court will not hear argument on this matter today.


Case Number: 26SMCV01759    Hearing Date: June 29, 2026    Dept: I

In this case, plaintiff alleges that defendants negligently delivered their daughter causing the daughter to suffer permanent injury.  The mother, Diana, also claims that defendants’ actions caused injuries to her.  (The court uses first names due to the common surname to avoid confusion.  No disrespect is intended.)  And the father, Joseph, alleges that defendants were negligent as to him as well.  Plaintiffs also sue the UC, which is the ultimately governing organization of the facility where the conduct occurred.  Defendants demur to parts of the complaint.  As to Diana’s claim, defendants contend that this is really a claim for medical malpractice, so the additional claim for negligence fails.  As to Joseph, defendants state that they owed him no duty directly because he was not a patient, and that there is no “bystander liability” as to him because defendants acts were not such that would give rise to liability in that an ordinary person would not perceive the alleged negligent act.  Defendants also state that the loss of consortium causes of action fail.  Finally, the UC contends that there is no basis for corporate liability here under a negligent hiring or retention theory.  Plaintiffs oppose.

 

The court turns first to the NIED claim.  The court tends to agree with plaintiffs here.  The court’s understanding is that both parents were in the room when the events in question occurred.  They were therefore in a position to observe what happened.  The court agrees with the defense that it is not enough that they saw a traumatic event that was allegedly mishandled.  The court agrees that under Bird v. Saenz (2002) 28 Cal.4th 910 and Ochoa v. Superior Court (1985) 39 Cal.3d 159, more is needed than that.  The court also agrees, at least for pleading purposes, with the defense’s characterization that the act has to be so bad that it is obvious to a layperson.  In other words, there are difficult deliveries all the time; that is not enough for NIED.  And a difficult delivery might be mishandled; but that, too, is not enough for NIED.  In order to state the tort, the doctors’ actions must be such that the plaintiff knows that it is malpractice while it is going on.  For example, seeing a newborn in obvious distress while the doctor is in the room watching the World Cup rather than attending to the newborn could do it.  The problem is that the court here does not know enough of the specifics to know whether the actions were obvious malpractice or merely bad judgments that are only obvious with hindsight (or not bad judgments at all but rather a bad situation being handled as well as possible).  Because the court cannot tell from the pleadings, the court cannot rule out NIED for either Diana or Joseph.  Put another way, even if defendants did everything right, there would be emotional distress at the situation—just no liability.  But it is also true that if defendants’ conduct was so obviously improper that even a layperson would know that the malpractice is causing the severe injuries that the child suffered, then there is emotional distress and (at least potentially) NIED.  Because the court cannot tell from the pleadings, the demurrer must be OVERRULED.  The same is true for loss of consortium.  The theory there is that both parents are so devastated that each is incapable of providing comfort and companionship to the other.  That essentially stands or falls (at least in part) with the NIED and also with the evidence to be adduced.  The demurrer is OVERRULED as to that theory as well.  Finally, the UC’s argument that it did nothing wrong in hiring or supervising the medical staff cannot be adjudicated this way.  The court has no idea whether there were previous instances of obvious malpractice that went ignored or whether this is the first time—or even if there was any malpractice at all.  The UC’s demurrer is also OVERRULED.  The court notes defendants’ position that Diana’s claim is really just one for medical malpractice and so the NIED cause of action is superfluous.  There is some appeal to that.  Diana was a patient and to the extent that her providers owed her a duty of care as such (and they did), the failure to meet the standard of care is medical malpractice.  The issue is whether there are separate damages that Diana can seek for a breach of the duty of care that was not to her, but rather only to the child.  It might well be that the answer is “no” and that the only duty is the professional one, but the court is not sure that there was not a separate duty owed to the child, the breach of which—independent of any breach of the standard of care regarding Diana’s treatment—could lead to damages.  (By way of example, actions taken with regard to the child after the delivery might be one for which Diana cannot claim malpractice as to her.)

 

The bottom line is that these arguments cannot be resolved on the pleadings.  They must be resolved on summary adjudication or trial.  The demurrer is OVERRULED.  Defendants have 30 days to answer.


Case Number: 26SMCV02545    Hearing Date: June 29, 2026    Dept: I

This is a UD case where (as far as the court knows) possession remains at issue.  The court set the trial date very recently—June 16, 2026.  That was at plaintiff’s request.  The court warned the parties, though, especially plaintiff, that it expected full compliance with the court’s FSC and trial rules.  To date, the court has received no FSC materials other than two motions in limine by plaintiff.  Given that, it does not appear that the matter is ready for trial.  The court will inquire, though.  That could be because possession is no longer at issue.  If that is the reason, it is a very good one.  If it is because the parties are not ready, the court is not in a position to go forward with the trial because the court does not have the necessary trial materials, which likely means that the trial date will have to be vacated and we will need to have a different discussion.  The court will inquire.  The matter remains one where possession is at issue, the likely result will be that the trial date will be vacated and the court will hold a TSC sometime in the next two weeks to re-set the trial date.
