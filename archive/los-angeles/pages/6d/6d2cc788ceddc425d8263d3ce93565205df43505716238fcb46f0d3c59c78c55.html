25STCV11728

DEBORAH CHRISTENSEN, et al. vs GENERAL MOTORS, LLC.

September 23, 2026

8:30 AM

 

 

 

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: PLAINTIFFS’ MOTION FOR RELIEF FROM DISMISSAL PURSUANT TO CODE OF CIVIL PROCEDURE SECTION 473(b) [RE: DEFENDANT GENERAL MOTORS LLC’S DEMURRER TO PLAINTIFFS’ FIRST AMENDED COMPLAINT.]

 

RULING:

The Motion for Relief is granted.

The Court vacates and sets aside the Judgment filed February 5, 2026.

Plaintiff to give notice.

 

I.                    BACKGROUND

On April 22, 2025, DEBORAH CHRISTENSEN and WAYNE CHRISTENSEN III (Plaintiffs) filed a Complaint against GENERAL MOTORS, LLC (Defendant), alleging Causes of Action under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act (Civ. Code §§ 1790-1795.8), and one claim for Fraud and Concealment.

Plaintiffs allege purchasing a 2018 Chevrolet Traverse that Defendant manufactured, which had and developed defects, including engine, transmission, and electrical. Plaintiffs further allege that Defendant failed to repair the vehicle within a reasonable number of attempts and refused to promptly replace it or to make restitution.  Plaintiffs also allege the Fifth Cause of Action for Fraudulent Inducement – Concealment, regarding Defendant and its agents actively concealing and not disclosing the engine defect to Plaintiffs at the time of purchase and after.

On September 30, 2025, Defendant had filed an unopposed Demurrer to the FAC, based on the Statute of Limitations, Economic Loss Rule and pleading fraud elements.

On February 5, 2026, the Court filed the Judgment.

On August 4, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the Motion for Relief from Dismissal Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure Section 473(b), based upon an attorney declaration admitting fault in failing to oppose the Demurrer.

Defendant filed an Opposition, based on several grounds addressed below.

 

II.                 LEGAL STANDARD

 

As to mandatory relief, judges must vacate dismissals, default entries, and default judgments “whenever (1) an application is made no more than six months after entry of judgment, (2) the application is accompanied by an attorney's sworn affidavit attesting to his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect, and (3) the attorney's mistake, inadvertence, surprise or neglect in fact caused the dismissal or entry of default.” (Benedict v. Danner Press (2001) 87 Cal.App.4th 923, 927 [citing Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b)]. Accord, Jimenez v. Chavez (2023) 97 Cal.App.5th 50, 57.)

For discretionary relief, a motion pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 473 must be made “‘within a reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months, after the judgment, dismissal, order, or proceeding was taken….’” (Zamora v. Clayborn Contracting Group, Inc. (2002) 28 Cal.4th 249, 258 [quoting Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b)].) Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b) provides: “The court may, upon any terms as may be just, relieve a party or his or her legal representative from a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against him or her through his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect....”

Orders denying relief under Code of Civil Procedure section 473 would be scrutinized on appeal more carefully than orders granting and enabling a resolution on the merits. (E.g., Henderson v. Pacific Gas and Elec. Co. (2010) 187 Cal.App.4th 215, 230.)

 

III.              ANALYSIS

Plaintiffs request relief from the Judgment, and consideration of their Opposition attached to the Motion as Exhibit A, which request they base upon counsel’s declared admission of fault. In their Opposition to the Demurrer, Plaintiffs advocate overruling or alternatively leave to amend.

Defendant opposes relief, contending that (1) mandatory relief is unavailable where dismissal was on the merits, (2) discretionary relief is unavailable for inexcusable neglect, (3) six months’ delay to file the Motion after the Judgment filing was unreasonable, and (4) a condition of counsel paying attorneys’ fees and costs is mandatory. However, the opposing filings contain no evidence of any amount of attorneys’ fees or costs to support any monetary award as a condition for mandatory relief.

To qualify for mandatory relief, an affidavit from an attorney must be a straightforward admission of fault. (State Farm Fire & Casualty Co. v. Pietak (2001) 90 Cal.App.4th 600, 609- 10.) “‘In order to qualify for [discretionary] relief under section 473, the moving party … must submit affidavits or testimony demonstrating a reasonable cause for the default.’” (Huh v. Wang (2007) 158 Cal.App.4th 1406, 1419.) Where a ruling was the procedural equivalent of a default, not involving court consideration of factors, and resulted from counsel’s admitted inexcusable conduct of failing to file an opposition, then the mandatory relief provision, based on attorney fault, applies. (Younessi v. Woolf (2016) 244 Cal.App.4th 1137, 1148.) Whenever relief is based upon an attorney’s affidavit of fault, the court must order the attorney to pay reasonable legal fees and costs of the opposing side and may order penalties of up to $1,000. (Solv-All v. Superior Court (2005) 131 Cal.App.4th 1003, 1009, fn.7 [citing Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subds. (b) and (c)].)

Here, Plaintiffs’ counsel states a straightforward admission of fault at paragraph 6 of the Declaration attached to the Motion.

Further, the Court finds that the prior Demurrer ruling was not based upon the merits, but upon the lack of any Opposition. Any statement orally is not part of the Demurrer ruling. (See Opposition, 6:27 (“court informed Plaintiffs’ counsel that even without any written opposition, it had considered the merits….”).) A judge’s order made orally but not filed in writing or entered in the minutes, is ineffective. (Collins v. Hertz Corp. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 64, 77-78 [“‘An oral . . . opinion by a trial judge, discussing and purporting to decide the issues, . . . is merely an informal statement….’”]; In re Marriage of Drake (1997) 53 Cal.App.4th 1139, 1170; Code Civ. Proc., § 1003.) Furthermore, an Order referencing demurrer page numbers satisfies a minimal statutory standard for statements of decision. Specifically, as to demurrer rulings, statements of decision may consist of references to appropriate page numbers and paragraphs, which requirement opposing parties may waive. Code Civ. Proc., §472d.

Because the Motion fully qualifies for mandatory relief, the Court need not reach the requirements of an alternative ground of discretionary relief.

 

IV.              CONCLUSION

The Court grants relief from the Judgment for the reasons set forth herein.

26STCV00981

SRS DISTRIBUTION, INC., A DELAWARE CORPORATION vs ABI CONSTRUCTION INC., A CALIFORNIA CORPORATION, et al.

September 23, 2026

8:30 AM

 

 

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: PLAINTIFF’S MOTION TO COMPEL DEFENDANT ABI CONSTRUCTION INC.’S RESPONSE TO REQUESTS FOR PRODUCTION OF DOCUMENTS (SET ONE) BY PLAINTIFF; REQUEST FOR MONETARY SANCTIONS. PLAINTIFF’S MOTION TO COMPEL DEFENDANT SHLOMY ABITAN’S RESPONSE TO REQUESTS FOR PRODUCTION OF DOCUMENTS (SET ONE) BY PLAINTIFF; REQUEST FOR MONETARY SANCTIONS. PLAINTIFF’S MOTION FOR AN ORDER THAT MATTERS FOR ADMISSION BE DEEMED ADMITTED AGAINST DEFENDANT ABI CONSTRUCTION INC.; REQUEST FOR MONETARY SANCTIONS. PLAINTIFF’S MOTION FOR AN ORDER THAT MATTERS FOR ADMISSION BE DEEMED ADMITTED AGAINST DEFENDANT SHLOMY ABITAN; REQUEST FOR MONETARY SANCTIONS.

 

RULING:

The Court grants all four unopposed Motions, as prayed.

The Court will sign and file the proposed Orders received on August 14, 2026.

Plaintiff to give notice.

 

I.                    BACKGROUND

On January 12, 2026, SRS DISTRIBUTION, INC. (Plaintiff) filed a Complaint against ABI CONSTRUCTION INC.; SHLOMY ABITAN; and THE WESTERN SURETY COMPANY (Defendants), listing Causes of Action for:

(1) BREACH OF CONTRACT;

(2) ON COMMON COUNTS; 

(3) BREACH OF PERSONAL GUARANTY; and

(4) ENFORCEMENT OF CLAIM ON CONTRACTOR’S LICENSE BOND.

Plaintiff alleges that it supplied materials for Defendants’ business, but they breached a written contract, by failing to pay the sum of $365,461.53.

On August 14, 2026, Plaintiff filed four discovery Motions to compel initial document responses, and to deem requests admitted, on grounds that Defendants ABI CONSTRUCTION INC. and SHLOMY ABITAN failed to serve any responses to Requests for Production of Documents and Requests for Admissions. Plaintiff also requests monetary sanctions against each Defendant ($985.00 per motion).

Defendants have filed no Opposition to these matters.

 

II.                 LEGAL STANDARD

A motion to compel initial discovery responses need only show that discovery was properly served on the opposing party, the time to respond expired, and no response was served. (Leach v. Superior Court (1980) 111 Cal.App.3d 902, 905-906; Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.290 [compelling initial interrogatory responses], 2031.300 [compelling initial document requests].) A motion to compel initial discovery responses need not show good cause, meeting and conferring, or timely filing, and need not be accompanied by a separate statement. (Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pac. Healthcare Consultants (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, 404.)

Monetary discovery sanctions are mandatory as to parties losing discovery motions, unless courts find substantial justification or other injustice. (Deck v. Devs. Inv. Co. (2023) 89 Cal.App.5th 808, 830.) “‘[T]he phrase “substantial justification” has been understood to mean that a justification is clearly reasonable because it is well grounded in both law and fact.’” (Kwan Software Engineering, Inc. v. Hennings (2020) 58 Cal.App.5th 57, 75.) “If the party seeking a monetary sanction meets its burden of proof, the burden shifts to the opposing party attempting to avoid a monetary sanction to show that it acted with ‘substantial justification.’” (Doe v. U.S. Swimming, Inc. (2011) 200 Cal.App.4th 1424, 1435.) Courts may determine substantial justification based on the “whole picture” of the discovery dispute, including where parties partially prevailed as to some discovery items in dispute, or where discovery concessions occurred only after improperly asserted objections. (In re Marriage of Moore (2024) 102 Cal.App.5th 1275, 1297.)

 

III.              ANALYSIS

Plaintiff reports that Defendants failed to serve discovery responses, and Plaintiff provided a meet and confer letter about overdue responses, giving an extension of time to respond.

The failure to file a proper and timely opposition in trial court creates a waiver of the issues on any appeal. (Bell v. Am. Title Ins. Co. (1991) 226 Cal.App.3d 1589, 1602; Cabrini Villas Homeowners Assn. v. Haghverdian (2003) 111 Cal.App.4th 683, 693 [appellate court will not consider any erroneous rulings where an objection could have been made].) “A failure to oppose a motion may be deemed a consent to the granting of the motion.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 8.54(c).)

The burden of justifying any failure to respond to discovery requests remains at all times with the responding party. (E.g., Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 541.)

Here, Defendants filed no Opposition to address the burden to show any justification in avoidance of sanctions.

 

IV.              CONCLUSION

Therefore, the Court grants all four Motions, as prayed.

26STCV15328

UNDERDOG AMERICA LLC vs HARBOR PROPERTY MANAGEMENT, INC., et al.

September 23, 2026

8:30 AM

 

 

 

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: PLAINTIFF’S DEMURRER TO DEFENDANTS’ ANSWER TO PLAINTIFF’S COMPLAINT.

 

RULING:

The Court sustains the Demurrer, with 20 days’ leave to amend.

Plaintiff to give notice.

 

I.                    BACKGROUND

On May 13, 2026, UNDERDOG AMERICA LLC, (Plaintiff) filed a Complaint against  HARBOR PROPERTY MANAGEMENT, INC. and SCOTT COLETTE (Defendants), listing Causes of Action for:

1. FRAUD IN THE INDUCEMENT

2. FRAUDULENT MISREPRESENTATION

3. NEGLIGENT MISREPRESENTATION

4. BREACH OF CONTRACT

5. BREACH OF FIDUCIARY DUTY

6. NEGLIGENCE

7. INTERFERENCE WITH PROSPECTIVE ECONOMIC ADVANTAGE

8. VIOLATION OF BUSINESS & PROFESSIONS CODE § 17200, ET SEQ.

9. VIOLATION OF PENAL CODE § 496(C)

10. DECLARATORY RELIEF

11. CONVERSION.

Plaintiff alleges that Defendants negligently managed Plaintiff’s apartment complex property at 1115 East 75th Street, Los Angeles, including by (1) not timely maintaining and leasing vacant units, (2) not properly handling tenant matters and financial obligations, (3) not properly communicating with the Los Angeles County Development Authority, and (4) not assisting in an orderly transition to new management.

On August 10, 2026, Plaintiff filed the Demurrer to Defendants’ Answer, essentially on grounds that it does not state facts sufficient to constitute an Affirmative Defense, with certainty.

Defendants have filed an Opposition, asserting some uncommon defense positions regarding answer sufficiency. Alternatively, Defendants request leave to amend.

 

II.                 LEGAL STANDARD

A general demurrer may be made on the ground that an answer does not state facts sufficient to constitute a defense. (Timberidge Enterprises, Inc. v. City of Santa Rosa (1978) 86 Cal.App.3d 873, 880.) “Generally speaking, the determination whether an answer states a defense is governed by the same principles which are applicable in determining if a complaint states a cause of action.” (South Shore Land Co. v. Petersen (1964) 226 Cal.App.2d 725, 732.)

Affirmative defenses are based upon facts demonstrating that no cause of action exists, notwithstanding the truth of the complaint’s allegations. (Walsh v. W. Valley Mission Cmty. College Dist. (1998) 66 Cal.App.4th 1532, 1542, fn.3.) Facts tending to disprove complainants' allegations constitute a traverse that may be stated as general denials, but facts tending to support a defense independently of them constitute new matter that must be specially pleaded as affirmative defenses. (Walsh v. W. Valley Mission Cmty. College Dist. (1998) 66 Cal.App.4th 1532, 1546; State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. v. Superior Court (1991) 228 Cal.App.3d 721, 725.)

 

III.              ANALYSIS

Plaintiff contends that the First-through-Nineteenth Affirmative Defenses in Defendants’ Answer to the Complaint, fail to state facts sufficient to constitute valid defenses with certainty. The Reply adds that Plaintiff does not presumptively know, and uncertainty exists as to what Cause of Action each Affirmative Defense relates to (citing, Code Civ. Proc., § 431.30, subd. (g)).

Defendants have filed an Opposition, principally contending that the general denial portions of the Answer are not Affirmative Defenses, the Complaint supplies facts, Plaintiff presumptively knows details with certainty, and the Demurrer fails to specifically address each Affirmative Defense.

An answer must aver facts as carefully as is required for complaints. (FPI Dev., Inc. v. Nakashima (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 367, 384.) “Affirmative defenses must not be pled as ‘terse legal conclusions,’…” but rather as facts averred in as much detail as is required in complaints. (In re Quantification Settlement Agreement Cases (2011) 201 Cal.App.4th 758, 813.) Affirmative defenses consisting of legal conclusions cannot overcome a demurrer or motion for judgment on the pleadings. (Westly v. Board of Administration (2003) 105 Cal.App.4th 1095, 1117 [insufficient answer allegations that: “‘Plaintiff's claims are barred in whole or in part by the doctrine of waiver,’” and “‘Plaintiff's causes of action, or some of them, are barred by the doctrine of laches because plaintiff unreasonably delayed bringing suit, causing defendants to reasonably rely on the status quo.’”].)

A reading of Defendants’ Answer readily reveals that it unsatisfactorily contains conclusions as to elements of various affirmative defenses, without alleging any ultimate facts as to any of the elements.

 

IV.              CONCLUSION

Courts generally allow at least one time to amend a pleading, after sustaining a demurrer, even without any request for leave to amend. (McDonald v. Superior Court (1986) 180 Cal.App.3d 297, 303.)

Therefore, the Court sustains the Demurrer, with 20 days’ leave to amend.

 


Case Number: 26STCV16988    Hearing Date: September 23, 2026    Dept: 311

SUPERIOR COURT OF CALIFORNIA, COUNTY OF LOS ANGELES

26STCV16988

RANDALL RENICK, et al. vs SUNDANCE GROUP, LLC, et al.           

September 23, 2026

8:30 AM

 

 

 

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: DEFENDANTS' DEMURRER TO PLAINTIFFS’ COMPLAINT FOR DAMAGES.

 

RULING:

The Court overrules the Demurrer.

Twenty days to answer.

Plaintiff to give notice.

 

I.                    BACKGROUND

On May 28, 2026, RANDALL RENICK and CANDY RENICK (Plaintiffs) filed a Complaint against SUNDANCE GROUP, LLC, and TERRENCE W. CHOW and CATALINA H. CHOW, individually and as TRUSTEES of THE CHOW LIVING TRUST DATED MARCH 13, 2019 (Defendants), listing Causes of Action for:

1. VIOLATION OF UNFAIR COMPETITION LAW, BUS. & PROF. CODE §§ 17200, ET SEQ.

2. VIOLATION OF THE CITY OF LOS ANGELES PRICE GOUGING LAW, L.A.M.C. § 47.12.

Plaintiffs allege that the landlord Defendants engaged in price gouging to exploit the January 2025 wildfires, by charging a displaced family inflated rent for a home at 3731 Toland Way, Los Angeles, with a lease term from January 26, 2025 through July 31, 2025. Further, Plaintiffs allege that the price gouging restrictions were effective starting January 7, 2025, pursuant to the following authorities: Governor’s State of Emergency pursuant to Government Code Section 8625; Los Angeles County Board of Supervisors’ Local Emergency; and Mayor’s Local Emergency pursuant to Los Angeles Administrative Code Section 8.27. (Complaint, ¶¶ 71-73.)

On August 11, 2026, Defendants filed the Demurrer to the Complaint, on grounds that Code of Civil Procedure section 396, based upon the Emergency Declaration, unconstitutionally did not provide fair notice of when rent control limits were invoked, or provide for a fair return financially. (Request for Judicial Notice, Exhibit 1.) Additionally, Defendants contend that LAMC section 47.12 did not regulate housing rents until after the parties entered into the lease but Defendants never increased that rent thereafter. Also, Defendants argue unconstitutionality based on uncertain law meaning, and wrongful taking by rent restricting.

Plaintiffs’ Opposition counters each of Defendants’ arguments and alternatively requests leave to amend.

 

II.                 LEGAL STANDARD

Demurrers are to be sustained where a pleading fails to plead adequately any essential element of the cause of action. (Cantu v. Resolution Trust Corp. (1992) 4 Cal.App.4th 857, 879-880.) A complaint does not necessarily need to be a model of pleading but needs sufficient factual allegations. (E.g., State v. The Superior Court (2001) 87 Cal.App.4th 1409, 1411.)

Plaintiffs are not required to address and anticipate affirmative defenses in their pleadings, unless already revealed. (E.g., Gentry v. eBay, Inc. (2002) 99 Cal.App.4th 816, 825.) “‘[A] demurrer based on an affirmative defense will be sustained only where the face of the complaint discloses that the action is necessarily barred by the defense.’” (McKenney v. Purepac Pharmaceutical Co. (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 72, 78-79.)

Issues of legislative interpretation involve pure questions of law that reviewing courts independently review de novo, without any deference to trial courts. (E.g., Wolf v. CDS Devco (2010) 185 Cal.App.4th 903, 913; Regents of Univ. of Cal. v. Superior Court (1999) 20 Cal.4th 509, 531.)

“Evidence Code section 456 provides the trial court must indicate for the record if it denies a request for judicial notice.” (Aaronoff v. Martinez-Senftner (2006) 136 Cal.App.4th 910, 919 [“The record contains no indication the request for judicial notice was denied. We conclude the trial court took judicial notice of the requested matter, particularly in light of its decision.”].)

 

III.              ANALYSIS

Defendants summarize the Demurrer bases, as follows:

The application of Penal Code § 396 fails because the emergency declaration on which Plaintiffs rely did not give fair notice that section 396's rent control limits had been invoked, the Complaint alleges no rental-price increase by Defendants, and because imposing liability would impose a confiscatory ceiling without any procedure available to assure a fair return. The LAMC section 47.12 claim independently fails because the ordinance did not regulate housing rents until after the parties fixed the rent in a written lease, and Defendants never increased that rent after the amendment took effect.

(Demurrer, 2:10-15.)

Plaintiffs’ Opposition primarily contends (1) Section 396, subdivision (e) applies beyond just increasing landlords’ previously charged rent, (2) Defendants had the information to understand the applicable law, (3) no unconstitutional taking is revealed, and (4) retroactive application is not present.

The parties’ briefing, and the Court’s independent research, indicates that there is very little governing law on point regarding price gouging during emergencies. “A legal proposition asserted without apposite authority necessarily fails.” (People v. Taylor (2004) 119 Cal.App.4th 628, 643.)

 

A.    Legislative and Ordinance Interpretation

Legislative interpretation begins by examination of the language of the statute, giving the words their ordinary meaning and considering them in the context of the statutory framework. (Barnes v. Dept. of Corrections (1999) 74 Cal.App.4th 126, 131.)

 “The court will apply common sense to the language at hand and interpret the statute to make it workable and reasonable.” (Gattuso v. Harte-Hanks Shoppers, Inc. (2007) 42 Cal.4th 554, 567.) Courts interpret ambiguous statutes in a manner that promotes their general purpose, and avoids “unreasonable, impractical, or arbitrary results.” (Catholic Mut. Relief Soc'y v. Superior Court (2007) 42 Cal.4th 358, 372.) “[I]f the clear meaning of the statutory language is not evident after attempting to ascertain its ordinary meaning or its meaning as derived from legislative intent, we will ‘apply reason, practicality, and common sense to the language at hand.’” (Sisemore v. Master Financial, Inc. (2007) 151 Cal.App.4th 1386, 1411.)

“‘If the language permits more than one reasonable interpretation,… the court looks “to a variety of extrinsic aids, including the ostensible objects to be achieved, the evils to be remedied, the legislative history, public policy, contemporaneous administrative construction, and the statutory scheme of which the statute is a part.”’” (S.B. Beach Properties v. Berti (2006) 39 Cal.4th 374, 379.) When legislation is ambiguous, courts consider, among other things, the objects, remedies, history, public policy, contemporaneous administrative construction, and the statutory scheme. (Nolan v. City of Anaheim (2004) 33 Cal.4th 335, 340.)

“The legislature is … presumed to have enacted legislation with existing law in mind.” (Harris v. Verizon Communications (2006) 141 Cal.App.4th 573, 585, disapproved on other grounds by Azure Ltd. v. I-Flow Corp. (2009) 46 Cal.4th 1323, 1336.) “'It is a cardinal rule of statutory construction that statutes relating to the same subject matter must be read together and reconciled whenever possible.’” (Womack v. San Francisco Community College Dist. (2007) 147 Cal.App. 854, 860-861.)

“The administrative construction of a statute is entitled to weight unless a contrary legislative purpose is apparent.” (Goehring v. Chapman University (2004) 121 Cal.App.4th 353, 383.)

“Courts interpret ordinances in the same way as they construe statutes.” (Stolman v. City of Los Angeles (2003) 114 Cal.App.4th 916, 928. Accord, H.N. & Frances C. Berger Found. v. City of Escondido (2005) 127 Cal.App.4th 1, 12 [“The construction of an ordinance is a pure question of law for the court.”].)

“Section 396, subdivision (e), provides in relevant part: ‘Upon the proclamation of a state of emergency declared by the President of the United States or the Governor, or upon the declaration of a local emergency by an official, board, or other governing body vested with authority to make that declaration in any city, county, or city and county, and for a period of 30 days following that proclamation or declaration, or any period the proclamation or declaration is extended by the applicable authority, it is unlawful for any person, business, or other entity, to increase the rental price, as defined in paragraph (11) of subdivision (j), advertised, offered, or charged for housing, to an existing or prospective tenant, by more than 10 percent.... This subdivision does not authorize a landlord to charge a price greater than the amount authorized by a local rent control ordinance.’ ” (W. Manufactured Hous. Communities Assn. v. City of Santa Rosa (2026) 119 Cal.App.5th 1275, 1280, 1287 [“Legislature expressly did impose the 10-percent limit on mobilehome spaces subject to rent control—both those already rented at the time of the declaration of emergency and those rented afterwards.”].)

“It is the intent of the Legislature in enacting this act to protect citizens from excessive and unjustified increases in the prices charged during or shortly after a declared state of emergency or local emergency for goods and services that are vital and necessary for the health, safety, and welfare of consumers, whether those goods and services are offered or sold in person, in stores, or online. Further, it is the intent of the Legislature that this section be liberally construed so that its beneficial purposes may be served.” (Cal. Penal Code § 396, subd. (a).)

Plaintiffs reason that, “Penal Code section 396(e) prohibited Defendants from advertising, offering, or charging more than ten percent above the rental price subparagraph (11)(B) defines for this never-rented, furnished house.” (Opposition, 4:18-20.) The Court concurs that the written intent of the section is to preclude rent amounts that are increased above statutorily permitted sums, not just increases from previously charged rent. Because Legislative intent is to prevent unjustified prices during or after emergencies, it would be ineffective to allow landlords to initially invite or enter into a lease for rent already increased to take advantage of emergency conditions, whether before renting, or during monthly rent charges. Alternatively, assuming that successive rent amounts must be the type of increase, Plaintiffs allege that Defendants increased the advertised price up to the lease amount. (Opposition, 9:7-9:27.)

As for LAMC section 47.12, Plaintiffs maintain that it provides for a rent ceiling for units not rented in the year preceding an emergency, including units initially rented after an emergency is declared. (Opposition, 7:6-16.) The Court concurs with that interpretation also. Similar price-gouging provisions should be construed harmoniously towards regulating emergency-based price gouging, without a large loophole that would allow initially charging increased rent to take advantage of emergencies or perpetuating those increases in monthly rental payments.

Further, administrative handling indicates that the above interpretations are correct, since allegedly the Los Angeles Housing Department and City Attorney had issued advisories to Defendants expressing similar meanings. (Opposition, 8:16-9:6.)

 

B.     Constitutionality

First, a statute is unconstitutionally vague and cannot be upheld where it forbids or requires an act in terms such that people of common intelligence must “‘guess at its meaning and differ as to its application.’” (Ortiz v. Lyon Management Group, Inc. (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 604, 612, disapproved on other grounds by Connor v. First Student, Inc. (2018) 5 Cal.5th 1026, 1038.)

However, that ascertaining statutory meaning involves some effort, may be permissible. “[T]he due process clauses of the California and federal Constitutions require civil as well as criminal statutes to be sufficiently clear to provide a standard for uniform application…. A statute will be upheld if its terms may be made reasonably certain by reference to its legislative history or purpose.” (In re Marriage of Walton (1972) 28 Cal.App.3d 108, 116.)

Procedurally, because constitutionality is not a claim element, but a defense, Plaintiff has no obligation to plead around it. Alternatively, Defendants reportedly had access to various information sources that would reasonably make certain the statutory requirements. (E.g., Opposition, p. 10.)

Second, “[c]ompensation need not be paid unless the ordinance or regulation fails to serve an important governmental purpose or ‘goes too far’ as applied to the specific property that is the object of the litigation. [Citation.] The impact of a law or regulation on the owner's right to use or develop the property cannot be assessed until an administrative agency applies the ordinance or regulation to the property and a final administrative decision has been reached with regard to the availability of a variance or other means by which to exempt the property from the challenged restriction.” (Hensler v. City of Glendale (1994) 8 Cal.4th 1, 12.) “Controls in the landlord-tenant area are legitimate exercises of the police power if they are reasonably calculated to eliminate excessive rents and at the same time provide landlords with a just and reasonable return on their property. The fact that rent control allows tenants to reap economic benefit the owners would otherwise enjoy does not mean it is an unconstitutional taking if the owners are not denied a fair and reasonable return.1” (29 Cal. Jur. 3d Eminent Domain § 350.)

Again, Plaintiffs are not required to anticipate and to negate defenses that are not claim elements. Additionally, the Complaint and Judicial Notice are lacking in information for enabling a determination of an unconstitutional taking, including the importance of the governmental purpose to prevent price gouging during emergencies, and the amount of a just and reasonable return for the residential rental.

 

C.     Retroactivity

“In construing statutes, there is a presumption against retroactive application unless the Legislature plainly has directed otherwise ….” (Bank of America, N.A. v. Roberts (2013) 217 Cal.App.4th 1386, 1394.)

The Complaint does not seek to apply LAMC section 47.12 to rent collected before the Ordinance became effective on February 26, 2025, but only to initially excessive rent perpetuated as monthly payments, thereafter. (Opposition, 13:8-26.) Beyond initial rent payments, Section 47.12, subdivision (e), broadly includes rent “advertised, offered, or charged to, or accepted from, an existing or prospective tenant.”

 

IV.              CONCLUSION

The Court overrules the Demurrer, for reasons stated herein.

Finally, the Court sustains Plaintiffs’ objection, filed September 10, 2026, to Judicial Notice of Exhibit 4. Judicial notice requests may be denied based upon lack of relevance. (Kashian v. Harriman (2002) 98 Cal.App.4th 892, 901, fn.3; Ragland v. U.S. Bank National Assn. (2012) 209 Cal.App.4th 182, 194; Barratt Amer., Inc. v. City of San Diego (2004) 117 Cal.App.4th 809, 812, fn. 2 [court denied judicial notice of city resolutions as being irrelevant to question involved in demurrer].)

 


Case Number: 26STCV17285    Hearing Date: September 23, 2026    Dept: 311

SUPERIOR COURT OF CALIFORNIA, COUNTY OF LOS ANGELES

Civil Division

Central District, Stanley Mosk Courthouse, Department 311

Tentative Ruling

26STCV17285

RODNEY ROUZAN vs FELL LAW, P.C.

September 23, 2026

8:30 AM

 

 

 

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: PLAINTIFF’S MOTION FOR LEAVE TO FILE SECOND AMENDED COMPLAINT.

 

RULING:

The Court grants the Motion.

The proposed, corrected Second Amended Complaint may be served and filed as a separate document, on or before October 1, 2026.

Plaintiff to give notice.

 

I.                    BACKGROUND

On August 21, 2026, RODNEY ROUZAN (Plaintiff) filed a Motion for Leave to File a Second Amended Complaint (SAC), against FELL LAW, P.C. and BIBIANNE FELL (Defendants), listing Causes of Action for:

1.      PROFESSIONAL NEGLIGENCE / LEGAL MALPRACTICE;

2.      BREACH OF FIDUCIARY DUTY;

3.      BREACH OF WRITTEN CONTRACT;

4.      CONVERSION / FAILURE TO RETURN CLIENT PROPERTY;

5.      CONSTRUCTIVE FRAUD / CONCEALMENT; and

6.      DECLARATORY RELIEF.

(Motion for Leave, Exhibit 1.)

Plaintiff alleges that Defendants committed Legal Malpractice in representing him in the underlying case, Rouzan v. City of Los Angeles, et al., Los Angeles Superior Court Case number 24STCV03054, including by not presenting a timely Government Claim regarding wrongful employment termination of Plaintiff, a Los Angeles Airport Police officer, done in retaliation for whistleblowing.

On September 9, 2026, Plaintiff filed some corrections to the Motion and proposed SAC.

On September 10, 2026, Defendants filed their Opposition, on bases including a need for a finalized SAC, inexcusable delay, a changed litigation strategy, the effect of verified allegations, and pending-demurrer avoidance.

 

II.                 LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (a)(1) allows courts “in furtherance of justice” to “allow a party to amend any pleading ... in any ... respect”, which is reviewed for abuse of discretion and requires courts to consider factors including the conduct of moving parties and belated presentations of amendments without a showing of excuse. (Emerald Bay Cmty. Assn. v. Golden Eagle Ins. Corp. (2005) 130 Cal.App.4th 1078, 1097.)

Where a pleading contradicts, or omits facts pled in, prior complaints, judges have discretion to take judicial notice of the earlier allegations and disregard inconsistent ones, absent the pleader’s satisfactory explanation. (State of Cal. ex rel. Metz v. CCC Information Services, Inc. (2007) 149 Cal.App.4th 402, 412; Holland v. Morse Diesel Internat. (2001) 86 Cal.App.4th 1443, 1447; Berman v. Bromberg (1997) 56 Cal.App.4th 936, 946 [perceiving no inconsistency or sham pleading].) The rule that pleaders cannot without explanation omit allegations, “does not mean that every word dropped in a pleading becomes a binding admission by which the pleader is forever bound.” (Schmidlin v. City of Palo Alto (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 728, 755.) Courts may overlook harmless procedural errors made in the documents or proceedings that do not affect substantial rights of parties. (Code Civ. Proc., § 475; Morgan v. AT & T Wireless Services, Inc. (2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 1235, 1252; Reedy v. Bussell (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 1272, 1289 [“‘Procedural defects which do not affect the substantial rights of the parties do not constitute reversible error….’”]; McAllister v. County of Monterey (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 253, 283 [court is bound to ignore any “ ‘defect … in the pleadings or proceedings which, in the opinion of said court, does not affect the substantial rights of the parties.’ ” ].)

­
 
“The trial court has discretion to permit or deny the amendment of the complaint, but instances justifying the court's denial of leave to amend are rare.” (Armenta ex rel. City of Burbank v. Mueller Co. (2006) 142 Cal.App.4th 636, 642.)

The principles that self-represented litigants are entitled to no greater rights than represented litigants, and trial courts must expeditiously process civil cases, “must yield to the even greater principles of providing in propria persona litigants with meaningful access to the courts and of deciding bona fide civil actions on their merits.” (Apollo v. Gyaami (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 1468, 1487.) In light of increasing numbers of self-represented litigants, there is a growing trend in our courts to be cognizant of their ability to access the courts, in conducting court business and developing rules and procedures. (Garcia v. Santana (2009) 174 Cal.App.4th 464, 473, fn.6.) Judges who preside over cases with self-represented litigants must manage the courtroom in a manner that provides all litigants an opportunity to have their matters adjudicated fairly. (Code of Judicial Ethics, Canon 3B(8).)

 

III.              ANALYSIS

Plaintiff explains that the proposed SAC is to respond to objections that Defendants raised recently during meeting and conferring.

Defendants advocate denying the Motion, on bases including:

1.      The proposed SAC has not been finalized according to the applicable rule. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1324).

2.      Defendants should not have to reconstruct the proposed complaint from comparisons, a location table, errata, and future pleading.

3.      Some facts were not actually discovered in August 2026, but before. For example, Plaintiff only vaguely states that new section 1017 allegations arose from “ ‘facts developed in the writ proceeding’ ” and Defendants’ June 2025 correspondence.

4.      Plaintiff should clearly explain materially changing damages theory allegations.

5.      Reframing the pleading may be Plaintiff’s attempt to answer Defendants’ causation and damages objections.

6.      There is apparent, erroneous AI-assisted drafting and citation verification with the Motion.

7.      Plaintiff’s self-represented status does not exempt him from applicable procedural rules.

8.      Any ruling should preserve Defendants’ right to demur and move to strike on the merits.

 

Importantly, a newly raised argument or court ruling may be justification for delay in moving for leave to amend. (Cf. Foundation for Taxpayer and Consumer Rights v. Nextel Communications, Inc. (2006) 143 Cal.App.4th 131, 136 [“The argument fails because the FTCR had no reason to amend its complaint until Nextel sought to apply Proposition 64 to this case.”].)

“ ‘ “[I]t is irrelevant that new legal theories are introduced as long as the proposed amendments 'relate to the same general set of facts….” ’ ” (Atkinson v. Elk Corp. (2003) 109 Cal.App.4th 739, 761.)

In considering whether there was unjustified delay as to a motion for leave to file an amended pleading, the court should consider whether the party, “was not diligent in offering the amendment after knowledge of the facts….” (Solit v. Tokai Bank (1999) 68 Cal.App.4th 1435, 1448.) Absent some kind of disadvantage from delay in seeking to amend a complaint, such as faded memories or lost evidence, delay causing the need to defend, in and of itself, is not a valid reason to deny amendment. (Jo Redland Trust, U.A.D. 4-6-05 v. CIT Bank, N.A. (2023) 92 Cal.App.5th 142, 168.)

Here, Plaintiff justifies the proposed SAC’s new theories and other changes, based upon learning defense positions during relatively recent meeting and conferring. And Defendants have confirmed that occurrence, by a declaration stating: “After reviewing the First Amended Complaint (“FAC”), on July 24, 2026, I sent Plaintiff a detailed meet-and-confer letter identifying Defendants’ anticipated grounds for demurrer and motion to strike….” (Opposition, p. 19 (Joshua D. Cahn Declaration), ¶ 2.)

 

IV.              CONCLUSION

Therefore, the Court grants the Motion.

Additionally, should Plaintiff file the SAC, the Court requests Defendants consider promptly taking off calendar the Demurrer and Motion to Strike scheduled for hearing on October 15, 2026. Judges disregard previously filed complaints after an amended pleading has been filed. (Berman v. Bromberg (1997) 56 Cal.App.4th 936, 945-946.) An amended complaint that makes substantive changes supersedes the original. (E.g., Carrasco v. Craft (1985) 164 Cal.App.3d 796, 808-809.)

SUPERIOR COURT OF CALIFORNIA, COUNTY OF LOS ANGELES

Civil Division

Central District, Stanley Mosk Courthouse, Department 311

Tentative Ruling

26STCV17549

ARACELI RAMIREZ vs FASHION NOVA, LLC, et al.

September 23, 2026

8:30 AM

 

 

 

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: DEFENDANT FASHION NOVA, LLC’S MOTION TO COMPEL ARBITRATION.

 

RULING:

The Court denies the Motion.

Plaintiff to give notice.

 

I.                    BACKGROUND

On June 1, 2026, ARACELI RAMIREZ (Plaintiff) filed a Complaint against FASHION NOVA, LLC and SARAH HAN (Defendants), listing Causes of Action for:

(1) ASSOCIATIONAL DISABILITY DISCRIMINATION IN VIOLATION OF FEHA;

 (2) RETALIATION IN VIOLATION OF FEHA; 

(3) FAILURE TO ENGAGE IN THE INTERACTIVE PROCESS IN VIOLATION OF FEHA; 

(4) FAILURE TO PROVIDE REASONABLE ACCOMMODATION IN VIOLATION OF FEHA;

 (5) RETALIATION FOR REQUESTING ACCOMMODATION IN VIOLATION OF FEHA;

 (6) FAILURE TO PREVENT DISCRIMINATION AND RETALIATION IN VIOLATION OF FEHA;

 (7) VIOLATION OF CFRA RIGHTS;

 (8) CFRA RETALIATION;

 (9) WRONGFUL TERMINATION IN VIOLATION OF PUBLIC POLICY;

(10) INTENTIONAL INFLICTION OF EMOTIONAL DISTRESS;

(11) FAILURE TO PRODUCE PERSONNEL RECORDS (CAL. LAB. CODE § 1198.5); and,

(12) FAILURE TO PRODUCE PAYROLL RECORDS (CAL. LAB. CODE § 226).

Plaintiff alleges that Defendants wrongfully terminated her employment as Production Manager, two days after she requested protected medical leave to care for her ill mother.

On August 24, 2026, FASHION NOVA, LLC (Defendant) filed the Motion to Compel Arbitration, on grounds that she electronically signed a Mutual Agreement to Arbitrate Employment-Related Disputes.

Plaintiff’s Opposition raises several issues, including electronic signature authentication, unconscionability, agreement scope, and co-Defendant as nonsignatory.

 

II.                 LEGAL STANDARD

Federal law applies to arbitration provisions in contracts involving interstate commerce. (Higgins v. Superior Court (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 1238, 1247.) “Even when the Federal Arbitration Act applies, state law governs such matters as who is bound by and who may enforce an arbitration agreement.” (Thomas v. Westlake (2012) 204 Cal.App.4th 605, 614, fn. 7.)

Parties petitioning to compel arbitration have the burden to prove the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by a preponderance of evidence, and parties opposing have the burden to prove by a preponderance of the evidence any fact necessary to a defense. (Gonzalez v. Nowhere Beverly Hills LLC (2024) 107 Cal.App.5th 111, 121.)

 

III.              EVIDENTIARY OBJECTIONS

As to motions to compel arbitration, where parties objected to evidence, and the court does not rule on them, then appellate courts will presume that the evidence was admitted. (Valentine v. Plum Healthcare Grp., LLC (2019) 37 Cal.App.5th 1076, 1089.)

Where the facts stated in a declaration do not show personal knowledge, a bare statement that the affiant has personal knowledge, and can testify, is incompetent proof. (Snider v. Snider (1962) 200 Cal.App.2d 741, 754.)

Courts have broad discretion in determining whether witnesses are qualified to testify concerning “‘the identity and mode of preparation’” of business records, to permit a determination that ‘[t]he sources of information and method and time of preparation were such as to indicate its trustworthiness.’” (Sierra Managed Asset Plan, LLC v. Hale (2015) 240 Cal.App.4th Supp. 1, 9 [declaration and testimony that an assignee received business records falls short of necessary foundation]. Accord, Grail Semiconductor, Inc. v. Mitsubishi Electric & Electronics USA, Inc. (2014) 225 Cal.App.4th 786, 798.) To establish admissibility, based on the business records exception to the hearsay rule, a witness having personal knowledge of the transaction or event, need not testify, but instead any qualified witness familiar with the procedures followed, may testify. (Jazayeri v. Mao (2009) 174 Cal.App.4th 301, 322.) “‘The witness need not have been present at every transaction to establish the business records exception; he or she need only be familiar with the procedures followed ….’” (Midland Funding LLC v. Romero (2016) 5 Cal.App.5th Supp. 1, 8.)

The Court sustains all eight evidentiary objections Plaintiff filed on September 10, 2026, and further addresses witness competency below.

 

IV.              JUDICIAL NOTICE

Materials prepared by private parties that are on file with governmental agencies, such as the Secretary of State, are not official records of which judicial notice may be taken. (People v. Thacker (1985) 175 Cal.App.3d 594, 598-599; Stevens v. Superior Court (1999) 75 Cal.App.4th 594, 608.)

Given the above-referenced law, the Court denies Defendant’s request to take judicial notice of Limited Liability Company Articles of Organization filed with the Secretary of State on December 31, 2020, by Fashion Nova, LLC.

As a result, there remains no basis to support a determination that the differently named entities are the same contracting employer.

 

V.                ANALYSIS

Defendant states that, on December 14, 2018, the Arbitration Agreement was sent to Plaintiff’s personal email address and signed shortly thereafter using a computer system. Defendant’s Reply counters the opposing arguments.

Plaintiff raises issues including the following:

1.      Plaintiff does not recall seeing or signing any emailed arbitration agreement, and could not find any by searching emails.

2.      Defendant failed to evidence the agreement with any competent authentication.

3.      No arbitration agreement was in the personnel file that Defendant produced in response to Plaintiff’s statutory demand.

4.      The arbitrator delegation clause does not override the Court’s determination of assent, and is unconscionable.

5.      The arbitration agreement shows Fashion Nova, Inc. as signatory, not Defendant Fashion Nova, LLP.

6.      Defendant never explained arbitration aspects.

7.      No arbitration rules were incorporated.

8.      There are a third party and a possibility of conflicting rulings.

9.      The eleventh and twelfth causes of action are based upon post-employment conduct and hence outside the scope of arbitration.

10.  The attorneys’ fees provision violates FEHA law.

11.  Severance is inappropriate where the central purpose is tainted with illegality.

12.  Alternatively, the Court should allow discovery followed by an evidentiary hearing.

Parties seeking to compel arbitration meet their initial burden simply by reciting the terms of the governing provision, or by attaching a copy of the provisions, unless there is a dispute over authenticity that is beyond merely contesting the preliminary showing. (Sprunk v. Prisma LLC (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 785, 793; Ruiz v. Moss Bros. Auto Group, Inc. (2014) 232 Cal.App.4th 836, 846 [“In the face of … failure to recall signing … had the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence that the electronic signature was authentic….”]. See also Iyere v. Wise Auto Grp. (2023) 87 Cal.App.5th 747, 758 [“If a party confronted with his or her handwritten signature on an arbitration agreement is unable to allege that the signature is inauthentic or forged, the fact that that person does not recall signing the agreement neither creates a factual dispute as to the signature's authenticity nor affords an independent basis to find that a contract was not formed.”]; Espejo v. So. Cal. Permanente Med. Group (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 1047, 1060 [“defendants … met their initial burden by attaching to their petition a copy of the purported arbitration agreement bearing … electronic signature. Once … challenged …, defendants were then required to establish by a preponderance of the evidence that the signature was authentic.”].)

The business records exception to the hearsay rule differs from authentication of contracts or other writings that instead involve the “ ‘introduction of evidence sufficient to sustain a finding that it is the writing that the proponent of the evidence claims it is.’ ” (Vargas v. Gallizzi (2023) 96 Cal.App.5th 362, 371–372, fns. 5, 6.) Hence, Defendant cannot rely solely upon business records to show contracting.

“Evidence Code section 1417, states, ‘The genuineness of handwriting, or the lack thereof, may be proved by a comparison made by the trier of fact with handwriting (a) which the court finds was admitted or treated as genuine by the party against whom the evidence is offered or (b) otherwise proved to be genuine to the satisfaction of the court.’” (People v. Rodriguez (2005) 133 Cal.App.4th 545, 552.) Comparisons of signatures or other handwriting need not be made by a witness or an expert, but instead the trial judge or other trier of fact may make the comparison. (Devereaux v. Frazier Mountain Park & Fisheries Co. (1967) 248 Cal.App.2d 323, 330.) An agreement’s signature can be shown by having a judge compare a genuine signature to that on an agreement, or by a declaration of a custodian of records. (Gamboa v. Ne. Cmty. Clinic (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 158, 170 [proponent of arbitration failed to file any such authenticating proof, including because agreement’s signature and declaration signature had some dissimilarities].)

“Civil Code section 1633.9 addresses how a proponent of an electronic signature may authenticate .... 'The act of the person may be shown in any manner, including a showing of the efficacy of any security procedure applied to determine the person to which the electronic record or electronic signature was attributable.’” (Ruiz v. Moss Bros. Auto Group, Inc. (2014) 232 Cal.App.4th 836, 843 Accord, Espejo v. S. California Permanente Med. Grp. (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 1047, 1062 [factual details to properly authenticated electronic signature, such as using security, passwords, time and IP address]; J.B.B. Investment Partners, Ltd. v. Fair (2014) 232 Cal.App.4th 974, 989 [insufficient proof to show party printed name at the end of e-mail with any intent to formalize electronic transaction].) For showing an electronic signature, a declaration did not detail the security precautions as to use of the username and password; the arbitration agreement time or IP address, creation within the computer system. (See Garcia v. Stoneledge Furniture LLC (2024) 102 Cal.App.5th 41, 54.) Electronic signatures are attributable to a person if done by the person’s act, which may be shown in any manner, including the efficacy of a security procedure. (Perez v. Kaiser Found. Health Plan, Inc. (2023) 91 Cal.App.5th 645, 652 [regarding enforceable arbitration agreement].)

Here, Defendant properly filed the Arbitration Agreement with the Motion (Rosley Garcia Declaration, filed 8-24-26, ¶¶ 5, 6; and Exhibit A). However, because Plaintiff declared a lack of recollection of encountering the electronic agreement, Defendant has incurred the burden of proving by a preponderance of evidence that the electronic signature was authentic.

In that regard, the Reply Declaration of Rosely Garcia, including paragraph 2, shows that she became employed by Fashion Nova, LLC in November 2024, which means that she would have no way of knowing the procedures followed and the technology systems’ features when Plaintiff began employment in 2018 with Fashion Nova, Inc. Instead, the declarant relies upon inherited business records from years past, without personal knowledge of the procedures used back then to create business records or computer records. Further, the reported computer system authentication, based upon Plaintiff’s email address, lacks trustworthiness as addressed in published opinions cited above, such as details about security precautions to exclude input of employer agents, usernames, and passwords. The Reply, at page 4, acknowledges further details involved in such opinions addressing electronic signatures.

While the signatures made available for comparison, including one on Plaintiff’s own Declaration, do appear to be very similar, the Court finds that the unusually minimal security precautions that Defendant describes do not suffice to identify Plaintiff, or to rule out the possibility of electronic signature forgery or technological error.

Further, there is no Declaration by anyone technologically knowledgeable about the computer systems “Greenhouse” and “Hellosign,” as they were at the time of use in 2018. (Cf. Fabian v. Renovate Am., Inc. (2019) 42 Cal.App.5th 1062, 1069 [“did not provide any evidence from or about DocuSign in its petition,…”].)

Because the Court does not find sufficient proof of Plaintiff’s assent to the Arbitration Agreement, it need not further address the unconscionability issues raised. However, the Court will briefly note that (1) employers’ failing to explain can constitute procedural unconscionability (Gentry v. Superior Court (2007) 42 Cal.4th 443, 470; Htay Htay Chin v. Advanced Fresh Concepts Franchise Corp. (2011) 194 Cal.App.4th 704, 710), (2) agreements need not incorporate arbitration rules because arbitration is automatically controlled by the California Arbitration Act (Cruise v. Kroger Co. (2015) 233 Cal.App.4th 390, 399-400), (3) even where valid delegation clauses clearly and unmistakably provide arbitrators have authority to resolve all disputes the issue of parties’ entering into an agreement is for courts to decide (Mendoza v. Trans Valley Transp. (2022) 75 Cal.App.5th 748, 776), and (4) unconscionable provisions may be severed, and the remainder of the arbitration agreement enforced (Fittante v. Palm Springs Motors, Inc. (2003) 105 Cal.App.4th 708, 726).

Similarly, the Court need not address whether the scope of the arbitration agreement covers all claims but opines that all Plaintiff’s claims fall within the scope because all are related to employment, whether during employment or after termination. (See generally California Correctional Peace Officers Ass'n v. State (2006) 142 Cal.App.4th 198, 205.)

Next, the Court need not reach the issue of SARAH HAN (co-Defendant) inconsistently proceeding in court during Defendant’s arbitration, since co-Defendant theoretically could move to compel arbitration after service of summons, and she appears to be covered by the agreement listing managers, supervisors, and other categories of nonsignatories, as beneficiaries. (See generally Laswell v. AG Seal Beach, LLC (2010) 189 Cal.App.4th 1399, 1405.)

Lastly, attorneys’ fees provisions, contrary to FEHA, may be severed and not enforced. (E.g., Ramirez v. Charter Commc'ns, Inc. (2024) 16 Cal.5th 478, 541.)

 

VI.              CONCLUSION

Therefore, the Court denies the Motion, based upon the issues of objectionable evidence and judicial notice, and insufficient proof of Plaintiff’s electronic assent.

Finally, the Court determines that Plaintiff’s alternative requests for discovery and a hearing regarding arbitration, are moot, after the ruling denying arbitration, but are authorized possibilities. (See generally Bouton v. USAA Cas. Ins. Co. (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 412, 427–428; Warfield, v. Summerville Senior Living, Inc. (2007) 158 Cal.App.4th 443, 446.)

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: APPLICATION OF EVAN E. WRIGHT TO APPEAR PRO HAC VICE AS COUNSEL FOR PLAINTIFFS.

 

RULING:

 

The Application for counsel to appear pro hac vice in this case is granted.

 

The Court sets a non-appearance case review for October 2, 2027, at 8:30 a.m., in Department 311, regarding payment of any pro hac vice renewal fees per Government Code section 70617, subdivision (e)(2).

 

Plaintiffs to give notice.

 

 

ANALYSIS

 

 

By the Application filed August 17, 2026, an attorney residing in Kentucky, requests leave to appear pro hac vice for Plaintiffs, on grounds that it satisfies the requirements of California Rules of Court, rule 9.40.

 

There is no opposition, as is usual for these applications.

 

A judge’s determination of whether to grant an application to appear pro hac vice is evaluated under the abuse-of-discretion standard. (E.g., United States Golf Ass'n v. Arroyo Software Corp. (1999) 69 Cal.App.4th 607, 624.) There is no statement of the limit of court discretion in ruling on applications to appear pro hac vice in civil cases. (Sheller v. Superior Court (2008) 158 Cal.App.4th 1697, 1713.)

 

The Court finds that the Application sufficiently complies with California Rules of Court, rule 9.40, including the applicant’s declaration addressing the rule elements.

 

Additionally, no reason appears to deny the Application, including because of the absence of any opposition.

Accordingly, the Court grants the Application.
