DEPARTMENT 406 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 19STCV05324    Hearing Date: July 17, 2026    Dept: 406

 

CENTRAVEL, INC.,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

FERDINAND LIPANA, et al.,

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  19STCV05324

  Hearing Date:  July 17, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

defendants’ motion for preliminary injunction

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On February 15, 2019, Plaintiff Centravel, Inc. filed this action against Ferdinand Lipana, Bernadine Lipana, Lawrence Lipana, Lancelle Lipana, and Sandrine Lipana. Lawrence, Lancelle, and Sadrine Lipana were subsequently dismissed from the lawsuit.

            On June 23, 2026, Defendants filed the instant motion for preliminary injunction. Plaintiff filed an opposition on July 6, 2026. Defendants filed a reply on July 9, 2026.

LEGAL STANDARD

“The general purpose of a preliminary injunction is to preserve the status quo pending a determination on the merits of the action.” (Jamison v. Department of Transportation (2016) 4 Cal.App.5th 356, 361.) A trial court deciding whether to issue an injunction weighs two interrelated factors—the likelihood the moving party will prevail on the merits at trial and the relative balance of interim harms that are likely to result from the granting or denial of preliminary injunctive relief. (White v. Davis (2003) 30 Cal.4th 528, 554; Cohen v. Board of Supervisors (1985) 40 Cal.3d 277, 286.) “Generally, the ruling on an application for a preliminary injunction rests in the sound discretion of the trial court.” (Cohen, supra, 40 Cal.3d at p. 286.)

DISCUSSION

I. Preliminary Injunction is Inapplicable

As a threshold matter, the purpose of a preliminary injunction is to preserve the status quo pending resolution of the merits of an action. In this case, there is no basis for a preliminary injunction because judgment has already been entered. The test for a preliminary injunction is inapplicable to the present situation. For example, there is no point in analyzing whether Defendants are likely to prevail on the merits, because Defendants have already lost.

II. The Issue is Moot

            Moreover, Plaintiff has assigned its interest in the judgment to its parent company, Hispanic Express, Inc. (Ponce-Gomez Decl., Ex. 9, 10.) Therefore, Defendants’ arguments regarding Plaintiff’s standing are moot.

III. Defendants Have No Probability of Prevailing

            Even if the Court were to apply the test for preliminary injunction, Defendants would have no probability of prevailing. The Court has stated multiple times that Plaintiff retains standing to pursue the action and enforce judgment despite being dissolved. The law is clear that “[a] corporation which is dissolved nevertheless continues to exist for the purpose of winding up its affairs, prosecuting and defending actions by or against it and enabling it to collect and discharge obligations, dispose of and convey its property and collect and divide its assets, but not for the purpose of continuing business except so far as necessary for the winding up thereof.” (Corp. Code, § 2010(a).)

            Defendants cite no authority abrogating this principle. First, Defendants cite to Civil Code section 2355(b), which provides that an agency is terminated upon “[t]he extinction of its subject.” Defendants argue that because Plaintiff is a dissolved corporation, there is no valid agency relationship between Plaintiff and its counsel, Eanet PC. However, to the extent agency principles apply, Plaintiff would be the principal, not the subject. The subject of the agency relationship—legal representation—continues to exist and has not been extinguished under section 2355(b). Section 2355 does not state that an agency is terminated upon dissolution of the principal, nor does it otherwise abrogate Corporations Code section 2010(a).

            Second, Defendants argue that Eanet PC is “entirely clientless” under Corporations Code section 2001. (Opp. at pp. 3-4.) Section 2001 sets forth “[t]he powers and duties of the directors (or other persons appointed by the court pursuant to Section 1805) and officers after commencement of a dissolution proceeding.” The statute does not govern agency relationships, does not abrogate section 2010(a), and does not preclude attorneys from representing a dissolved corporation in a civil action. Thus, the statute is inapposite. Defendants cite no evidence for their accusation that Eanet PC is “act[ing] as its own principal to expand collection tracks to fund private firm operating accounts.” (Opp. at p. 4.) It is routine for a law firm to receive money owed to its client who is a judgment creditor and then distribute the funds to the client. 

            Lastly, Defendants cite Limon v. Circle K Stores Inc. (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 671 for the proposition that “a party completely lacks standing in California courts if they do not hold an active, current beneficial interest under substantive law.” (Opp. at p. 4.) The court in Limon reiterated the undisputed rule that “[t]o have standing, a party must be beneficially interested in the controversy; that is, he or she must have ‘some special interest to be served or some particular right to be preserved or protected over and above the interest held in common with the public at large.’” (Limon, supra, 84 Cal.App.5th at p. 697.)

Under Limon, Centravel is the real party in interest in this case. Ferdinand embezzled millions of dollars from Centravel, and Centravel sued to recover it. This makes Centravel “beneficially interested in the controversy.” (See Limon, supra, 84 Cal.App.5th at p. 697.) Centravel, as the victim of the theft, holds a “special interest . . . over and above the interest held in common with the public at large.” (Ibid.) Thus, Centravel has standing under Limon. That case does not abrogate Corporations Code section 2010(a) or otherwise suggest that a dissolved corporation loses standing to enforce a judgment.

In sum, each argument made in the motion is meritless. Defendants raise additional arguments and law in the reply, which the Court is not required to consider. (See Tellez v. Rich Voss Trucking, Inc. (2015) 240 Cal.App.4th 1052, 1066.) In any event, none of the new authorities raised in the reply abrogates Corporations Code section 2010(a) or the principle that a dissolved corporation continues to exist for purposes of enforcing its debts. (See Corp. Code, §§ 1900-1905; Catalina Investments, Inc. v. Jones (2002) 98 Cal.App.4th 1.)

CONCLUSION

            Defendants’ motion for preliminary injunction is DENIED.

 


Case Number: 22STCV23458    Hearing Date: July 17, 2026    Dept: 406

 

COSTTREE HOLDINGS, LLC,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

ECIVIS, INC., et al.,

                        Defendants.

 

 

           AND RELATED MATTERS.

 

  Case No.:  22STCV23458

  Hearing Date:  July 17, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

defendant gty technology holdings inc.’s motion to compel further responses

 

 

I. BACKGROUND

a. The Underlying Complaint

On July 20, 2022, Plaintiff CostTree Holding, LLC (CostTree) filed this action against Defendants eCivis, Inc. (eCivis), GTY Technology Holdings, Inc. (GTY), and James Ha (Ha). On March 15, 2024, Plaintiffs CostTree and Capital Accounting Partners, LLC (CAP) filed the operative Second Amended Complaint (SAC), alleging (1) breach of the covenant of good faith and fair dealing, (2) fraud, (3) negligent misrepresentation, (4) civil conspiracy, (5) breach of contract, and (6) intentional interference with contractual relations.    

            The SAC alleges that CostTree agreed to sell its assets to eCivis through an Asset Purchase Agreement (APA) with eCivis. One of these assets was a certain program developed by CostTree, called Allocate. In exchange for the sale, the APA provided that CostTree would make earn-out payments from Allocate until a certain date and that eCivis would assume certain liabilities of CostTree, including a License Agreement with CAP. eCivis has allegedly failed to adequately promote sales or renewals of Allocate and has not assumed any of CostTree’s liabilities.

eCivis allegedly acted to minimize CostTree’s earn-out payments from Allocate until CostTree’s right to earn-out payments expired under the APA. Meanwhile, Ha, the CEO of eCivis, sought to maximize his own earn-out payments from eCivis’s programs under an agreement with GTY, which acquired eCivis and its programs. Despite increased resources from the GTY acquisition to market eCivis’s programs, eCivis allegedly refused to market or sell Allocate until CostTree’s right to earn-out payments expired. This allegedly allowed eCivis to effectively acquire CostTree’s assets for little to no consideration. Defendants allegedly concocted this scheme to remove a competitor from the market and maximize their own profits.

Defendants also allegedly terminated the Licensing Agreement with CAP without following the procedures for termination outlined in the Licensing Agreement. Defendants allegedly cut off CAP’s access to Allocate, preventing CAP from servicing its clientele for nearly a month. Defendants allegedly did this as retaliation for CostTree filing this action, as Nicolie Wickwire (Wickwire) is the CEO of both CostTree and CAP.

Ha has since been dismissed.  

b. Wickwire’s Related Action

            On October 6, 2023, Wickwire filed the action Nicolie Wickwire v. eCivis, Inc., et al. (23CMCV01613), alleging (1) breach of the covenant of good faith and fair dealing, (2) constructive discharge in violation of public policy, (3) defamation, and (4) declaratory relief. The Wickwire action was consolidated with this action, with this action as the lead case. However, Wickwire subsequently dismissed her complaint with prejudice. 

c. The Cross-Complaint

            On April 29, 2024, eCivis and GTY (dba Euna Solutions) cross-complained against CostTree, CAP, Wickwire, and Stephanie Ratajczak (Ratajczak). eCivis and GTY filed the operative Third Amended Cross-Complaint (TACC) on September 15, 2025, alleging (1) conversion, (2) intentional interference with prospective economic advantage, (3) breach of employment agreement, (4) breach of the APA, (5) breach of the CAP Licensing Agreement, (6) fraud, and (7) civil conspiracy. 

            The TACC alleges that Allocate underperformed as compared to Wickwire’s promises, despite assistance from eCivis and GTY. Upon Wickwire’s job title change, she allegedly refused to sign the new employment agreement and directed her subordinate, Ratajczak, to not sign her agreement either. Ratajczak was allegedly working for CAP at the same time as working for eCivis. Wickwire, Ratajczak, and CAP allegedly sold Allocate services to third party customers who should have contracted with eCivis and GTY. Wickwire, Ratajczak, and CAP allegedly allowed third parties to access and use Allocate without a proper license and without paying fees to eCivis and GTY. These actions allegedly violated the APA, Licensing Agreement, and employment agreements.    

When Wickwire and Ratajczak simultaneously resigned, they allegedly left eCivis and GTY without any knowledgeable employees to manage Allocate. Wickwire allegedly failed to train any other employees on Allocate as was her duty prior to resigning, leaving Allocate with little value and thereby damaging eCivis and GTY. Since their resignations, Wickwire and Ratajczak have allegedly continued to impermissibly access or grant unpermitted access to Allocate beyond the limited access permitted for customers of CAP.

d. The Instant Motion

            On June 11, 2026, GTY filed the instant motion to compel CAP’s further responses to Requests for Production, Set One. CAP filed an opposition on July 6, 2026. GTY filed a reply on July 10, 2026.

 

 

II. LEGAL STANDARD

Upon receiving responses to its discovery requests, the propounding party may move for an order compelling further responses if the responses are incomplete or evasive, or objections are without merit or too general. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300(a), 2031.310(a), 2033.290(a).)

III. DISCUSSION

            a. Statement of Compliance

            A statement of compliance “shall state that the production . . . will be allowed either in whole or in part, and that all documents or things in the demanded category that are in the possession, custody, or control of that party and to which no objection is being made will be included in the production.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.220.)

            CAP’s statement of compliance states: “The production, inspection, copying, testing, or sampling, and related activity demanded will be allowed either in whole, and all documents or things in the demanded category that is in the possession, custody, or control of that party and to which no objection is being made will be included in the production.”

            This response precisely tracks the Code and is therefore proper. GTY argues that the response is incomplete because CAP states it will produce documents “to which no objection is being made” but does not actually raise any objections. This language tracks the Code. To the extent CAP raises no objections, that means the production will be allowed in whole, and nothing will be withheld under an objection.

            GTY also contends that CAP has failed to produce any documents. To the extent CAP fails to abide by its statements of compliance, GTY may file a motion under section 2031.320. This has no bearing on the sufficiency of the written responses.

            Lastly, GTY argues that CAP’s responses fail to provide bates numbers in violation of section 2031.280(a): “Any documents or category of documents produced in response to a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling shall be identified with the specific request number to which the documents respond.” This is a labeling requirement imposed on the production itself. It has no bearing on the sufficiency of the written responses.

            Therefore, the motion is DENIED as to RFP Nos. 19-21, 23, 25-26, 32-33, 44-45, 48, 50-51, 53-55, 58-72, 81-82, 87, 96, 115, and 98[2d].

            b. CAP’s Possession of Documents

            In response to the remaining requests, CAP responded with the following: “Capital Accounting Partners is unable to comply with this demand because the records requested have never been in its possession, custody, or control. If they exist, the records are in possession of Nicolie Wickwire or CostTree Holdings, LLC.” 

            CAP argues that it cannot produce documents belonging to CostTree, even though Wickwire is the CEO of both CAP and CostTree, because Wickwire owes CostTree a fiduciary duty not to disclose CostTree’s information without consent. However, Wickwire is the sole principal of both CAP and CostTree. No one else’s consent or control is implicated. Wickwire cannot breach fiduciary duties to herself.

Moreover, the RFPs do not ask for CostTree’s documents; they ask for documents supporting CAP’s contentions in the SAC. Any responsive document that CAP intends to use to support its contentions is necessarily a document in its possession, custody, or control. CAP cannot have it both ways—it cannot have control over documents for purposes of prosecuting its claims but disavow control for purposes of discovery.

Therefore, the motion is GRANTED as to RFP Nos. 10-18, 35-37, and 116-117.

IV. CONCLUSION

            Defendant GTY’s motion to compel further responses is GRANTED in part as set forth above. Sanctions are denied as the parties acted with substantial justification.

 


Case Number: 24STCV05932    Hearing Date: July 17, 2026    Dept: 406

 

5379 WHITTIER BOULEVARD LLC,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

YOLANDA PADILLA, et al.,

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  24STCV05932

  Hearing Date:  July 17, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

cross-complainants’ motion to permit discovery of financial condition

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On March 8, 2024, Plaintiff 5379 Whittier Boulevard LLC (Whittier) filed this action against Defendants Yolanda Padilla (Padilla) and Jonathan Flores (Flores), asserting causes of action for (1) trespass, (2) negligent interference with prospective economic advantage, (3) intentional interference with prospective economic advantage, and (4) declaratory and injunctive relief.

            Whittier owns a property adjoining that of Padilla and Flores. The dispute arises from Whittier’s attempt to conduct renovations on a part of its property close to the boundary line between the two properties. After Padilla and Flores declined Whittier access to their property to conduct the renovations, Whittier began the renovations supposedly without encroaching onto the other property. However, Padilla and Flores allegedly engaged in a campaign of harassment to prevent the renovations from proceeding, based on the false assertion that the work encroached onto their property. This allegedly included trespassing onto Whittier’s property and engaging in physical violence.

            On April 3, 2024, Padilla and Flores filed a cross-complaint against Whittier, Logan Altman, Duval Campbell, Armed Elite Services, Raul Alvarez Ramirez, and Caribe Construction. Padilla and Flores filed the operative First Amended Cross-Complaint on February 10, 2026. The FACC adds Tekoa E. Burdine as a cross-defendant. The FACC asserts claims for (1) assault and battery, (2) terrorist threats, (3) trespass, (4) intentional infliction of emotional distress, (5) negligent infliction of emotional distress, and (6) negligent retention. Padilla and Flores allege that Whittier’s renovations encroached onto their property and that the construction workers refused to leave. Whittier hired a security guard, Campbell, who allegedly helped the workers trespass onto the other property and allegedly assaulted Padilla when she attempted to stop him.

            On June 5, 2026, Padilla and Flores filed the instant motion to permit discovery into Cross-Defendant Logan Altman’s financial condition.

LEGAL STANDARD

            “No pretrial discovery by the plaintiff shall be permitted with respect to the [defendant’s financial condition] unless the court enters an order permitting such discovery pursuant to this subdivision.” (Civ. Code, § 3295(c).) “Upon motion by the plaintiff supported by appropriate affidavits and after a hearing, if the court deems a hearing to be necessary, the court may at any time enter an order permitting the discovery otherwise prohibited by this subdivision if the court finds, on the basis of the supporting and opposing affidavits presented, that the plaintiff has established that there is a substantial probability that the plaintiff will prevail on the claim pursuant to Section 3294.” (Ibid.)

            “In this context, a ‘substantial probability’ of prevailing on a claim for punitive damages means that it is ‘very likely’ that the plaintiff will prevail on such a claim or there is ‘a strong likelihood’ that the plaintiff will prevail on such a claim.” (Kerner v. Superior Court (2012) 206 Cal.App.4th 84, 120.) “The purpose of this requirement is to protect defendants' financial privacy and prevent defendants from being pressured into settling nonmeritorious cases in order to avoid disclosure of their financial information.” (Ibid.)

DISCUSSION

            Altman is the alleged owner of the Whittier property. (FACC ¶ 16.) Padilla and Flores seek punitive damages against Altman for his alleged role in trespassing onto their property, for hiring an armed guard to escalate the situation, and for purportedly demonstrating a disregard for their wellbeing.

            a. Trespass

            Padilla and Flores begin their argument by pointing out that Altman knew of the encroachment problem and intentionally trespassed onto their property. (Mtn. 4:17-7:26.) However, the mere commission of a tort such as trespass is insufficient to justify punitive damages. None of the arguments in the motion or the cited evidence indicates malice, oppression, or fraud. At most, Altman was aware of the property lines and disregarded them to proceed with the construction. This is a plain tort with no aggravating circumstances warranting punitive damages. Thus, the alleged intentional trespass does not support a “substantial probability” or “strong likelihood” of succeeding on the claim for punitive damages. (See Kerner, supra, 206 Cal.App.4th at p. 120.)

            b. Armed Security

            Next, Padilla and Flores argue that Altman intentionally hired armed security to escalate a volatile situation. (Mtn. 7:26-8:15.) However, the cited evidence does not demonstrate any malice, oppression, or fraud. Altman’s testimony shows that he was aware of an altercation in which the police were called. Thus, Altman testified that he “call[ed] Armed Elite Services to request their guard at the site to protect the workers.” (Mtn. 8:1-6.) This is not egregious or aggravating. Hiring security after learning of a serious confrontation is arguably a reasonable response. Even the motion acknowledges that Altman “understood at that moment that the situation was sufficiently contentious that armed professional security was needed before he sent workers back the next day.” (Mtn. 8:4-6.)

            Padilla and Flores argue that Altman “did not try to resolve the dispute through discussion, seek a court order, or pause the project.” (Mtn. 8:6-11.) Instead, he hired armed security, which Padilla and Flores claim “was a choice made in conscious disregard of the homeowners' rights.” (Ibid.) However, the fact that Altman did not take a more prudent approach to the situation does not mean he engaged in malice, oppression, or fraud. There is nothing particularly egregious about hiring security after learning of an altercation involving the police. The fact that the guard may have subsequently harmed Padilla or Flores does not prove that Altman acted with malice, oppression, or fraud in hiring security. There is no evidence that Altman hired security with the intent to harm. Even the testimony cited in the motion shows that Altman was concerned for the workers and had no reason to assume that Campbell would act inappropriately. (See Mtn. 10:27-11:3.)

            Therefore, the fact that Altman hired Armed Elite Services does not support a “substantial probability” or “strong likelihood” of succeeding on the claim for punitive damages. (See Kerner, supra, 206 Cal.App.4th at p. 120.)

            c. Concern for Padilla

            The motion argues that Altman demonstrated a disregard for Padilla’s wellbeing: “When Altman arrived at the property after the February 22 incident, he did not ask about Ms. Padilla. He did not ask whether she had been hurt. He was asked at deposition whether, when he arrived and saw that Campbell was armed, he criticized Campbell or told him he should not have been armed. His answer was ‘No.’ His explanation: ‘When I arrived on the site, I was mostly concerned for his well-being because he was visibly bleeding and injured from being attacked by the neighbor. So I was concerned about his injuries and I was surprised that he didn't press charges.’ (Altman Depo., Vol. I, p. 78.).” (Mtn. 8:20-26.)

            This testimony does not demonstrate malice, oppression, or fraud. To the contrary, Altman testified that he was concerned about Campbell, the guard, because Campbell was visibly injured. The fact that Altman did not express equal concern for Padilla, the other person allegedly injured in the altercation, does not mean Altman acted with malice, oppression, or fraud. There is no evidence that Altman was aware of Padilla’s alleged injuries at the time, intended for Padilla to be harmed, prevented Padilla from obtaining medical treatment, or otherwise caused Padilla’s alleged injuries. Padilla and Flores merely take issue with Altman’s subjective reaction after the incident had already occurred. This is not sufficient to establish a “substantial probability” or “strong likelihood” of succeeding on the claim for punitive damages. (See Kerner, supra, 206 Cal.App.4th at p. 120.)     

            d. Condemning Campbell’s Actions

            Padilla and Flores further take issue with the fact that Altman did not sufficiently condemn Campbell’s statements and actions, and even suggested that Campbell should have pressed charges against Padilla. (Mtn. 9:7-10:24.) As with above, Altman’s subjective reaction after the fact does not show that he engaged in malice, oppression, or fraud. As the motion acknowledges, Altman testified that he witnessed Campbell’s injuries. The motion also acknowledges that Altman testified he was unaware Campbell had thrown something at Padilla: “He answered that he had not known about the wood-throwing at the time.” (Mtn. 9:23-25.) Thus, it was arguably reasonable for Altman to believe that Padilla or Flores were in the wrong. The evidence does not show that Altman confirmed Campbell had engaged in wrongdoing and then ratified or encouraged it.

            Moreover, Altman did testify that he would have preferred if Campbell had not been involved in the situation, and testified that he does not “condone throwing boards at each other.” (Plntf.’s Ex. K at 88:4-11, 185:10-12.) This is the opposite of approving or ratifying Campbell’s alleged actions. Altman is not guilty of malice, oppression, or fraud merely because he did not condemn Campbell to the degree that Padilla and Flores would prefer. This is not sufficient to establish a “substantial probability” or “strong likelihood” of succeeding on the claim for punitive damages. (See Kerner, supra, 206 Cal.App.4th at p. 120.)     

             In sum, Padilla and Flores have failed to meet the showing required under Code of Civil Procedure section 3295. Therefore, their request to conduct financial discovery must be denied.

CONCLUSION

            Cross-Complainants Yolanda Padilla’s and Jonathan Flores’ motion to compel financial discovery is DENIED.

 

 


Case Number: 25STCV04083    Hearing Date: July 17, 2026    Dept: 406

 

JOSE A. MONROY FERREYRA,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

BMW OF NORTH AMERICA, LLC,

                        Defendant.

 

  Case No.:  25STCV04083

  Hearing Date:  July 17, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

plaintiff’s motion to reopen discovery

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On February 13, 2025, Plaintiff Jose A. Monroy Ferreyra filed this action against Defendant BMW of North America, LLC, asserting violations of the Song-Beverly Act.

            On May 19, 2026, the Court granted Plaintiff’s ex parte application to continue trial, continuing the trial to October 1, 2026. However, the Court held that “[d]iscovery is cut off.”

            On June 17, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion to reopen discovery. Defendant filed an opposition on July 6, 2026. Plaintiff filed reply on July 8, 2026.  

DISCUSSION

“On motion of any party, the court may grant leave to complete discovery proceedings, or to have a motion concerning discovery heard, closer to the initial trial date, or to reopen discovery after a new trial date has been set.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2024.050(a).) The Court must consider the following factors: (1) the necessity and the reasons for the discovery; (2) the diligence or lack of diligence of the party seeking the discovery; (3) the likelihood that permitting the discovery or hearing the discovery motion will prevent the case from going to trial on the date set, or otherwise interfere with the trial calendar, or result in prejudice to any other party; and (4) the length of time that has elapsed between any date previously set, and the date presently set, for the trial of the action. (Id., subd. (b).)

DISCUSSION

            Plaintiff seeks to reopen discovery to permit the deposition of Defendant’s PMK and to conduct expert discovery.

            The Court finds that the deposition of Defendant’s PMK is necessary for determining “Defendant’s failure to repurchase the subject vehicle and on whether that failure was willful for purposes of a civil penalty under the Song-Beverly Act.” (Enav Decl. ¶ 11.) Plaintiff acted diligently in attempting to obtain the PMK’s deposition. Plaintiff noticed the deposition in January 2026, but Defendant failed to produce the PMK. (Enav Reply Decl. ¶¶ 3-4.) Permitting a single PMK deposition will not delay the trial date or cause prejudice. The length of time between the prior June 2026 trial date and the current October 2026 trial date is small. Therefore, discovery is reopened for the limited purpose of deposing Defendant’s PMK.

            However, Plaintiff fails to identify any expert discovery that needs to be completed and simply requests reopening expert discovery in general. The Court does not find sufficient cause to do so. Moreover, Plaintiff does not explain why he could not complete expert discovery before the cutoff. Plaintiff presents no evidence of any attempts to conduct expert discovery before the cutoff. Therefore, expert discovery remains cut off.

CONCLUSION

            Plaintiff’s motion to reopen discovery is GRANTED as to the PMK deposition only.


Case Number: 25STCV37142    Hearing Date: July 17, 2026    Dept: 406

 

CLAUDIA HUG,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

GROWTH CENTERS OF AMERICA, et al.,

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  25STCV37142

  Hearing Date:  July 17, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

plaintiff’s motions to compel further responses (CRS# 6156, 7579)

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On December 19, 2025, Plaintiff Claudia Hug filed this action for employment discrimination against Defendants Growth Centers of America; Growth Management, Inc.; Joy Lewis; and John Lewis.

            On May 21 and 22, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant two motions to compel Defendant Growth Management, Inc.’s further responses to form interrogatories and requests for production. Defendant filed oppositions on July 2, 2026. Plaintiff filed replies on July 10, 2026.

LEGAL STANDARD

Upon receiving responses to its discovery requests, the propounding party may move for an order compelling further responses if the responses are incomplete or evasive, or objections are without merit or too general. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300(a), 2031.310(a), 2033.290(a).)

A party “is entitled to demand answers to its interrogatories, as a matter of right . . . [and] the burden of justifying any objection and failure to respond remains at all times with the party resisting an interrogatory.” (Williams v. Sup. Ct. (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 541.) By contrast, the party seeking production of documents bears the initial burden of showing good cause through a fact-specific showing of relevance. (Kirkland v. Superior Court (2002) 95 Cal.App.4th 92, 98.) Once this showing is made, the burden shifts to the responding party to justify any objections. (Ibid.)

DISCUSSION

I. Requests for Production (CRS# 6156)

            a. Termination of Employment (RFP Nos. 41, 42, 48)

            RFP Nos. 41, 42, and 48 seek documents pertaining to the end of Plaintiff’s employment, including Plaintiff’s complaints, discipline, or other reasons for termination. There is good cause for the information, as Plaintiff alleges that she was wrongfully terminated.

Defendant objected to the requests as overbroad and stated that it would only respond to the requests, if at all, consistent with the Court’s prior order on Defendant’s motion for protective order. However, the requests pertain specifically to Plaintiff and are therefore not overbroad. The Court’s prior order did not pertain to these particular requests and therefore has no bearing here.

The motion is GRANTED as to RFP Nos. 41, 42, and 48.

b. Complaints and Investigations (RFP Nos. 29, 30, 33, 35, 36)

RFP Nos. 29, 30, 33, and 36 seek documents pertaining to any reports regarding the mistreatment of Plaintiff and any actions taken by Defendant as a result of such reports. There is good cause for such information because Plaintiff alleges that she engaged in protected activity, that Defendant failed to address her complaints, and that Defendant retaliated against her. Defendant’s objections regarding the breadth of the requests and the Court’s prior order are without merit for the reasons explained above.

RFP No. 35 seeks any electronic mail sent by or to Plaintiff evidencing any complaints or concerns by any person regarding any inappropriate conduct at the worksite. This is overbroad. Not every email sent to Plaintiff, or every complaint made by anyone, is relevant just because it mentions misconduct. To the extent Plaintiff seeks to prove she engaged in protected activity, the request is limited to emails sent by Plaintiff reporting misconduct.

The motion is GRANTED as to RFP Nos. 29, 30, 33, 35, and 36. RFP No. 35 is limited to emails sent by Plaintiff reporting misconduct.

c. Medical Condition and Accommodation (RFP Nos. 112-126, 128, 129)

            RFP Nos. 112-126 seek documents pertaining to Plaintiff’s medical condition, or pertaining to Plaintiff’s requests for accommodation or medical leave. There is good cause for such information because Plaintiff alleges that Defendant discriminated against her and failed to accommodate her. Defendant’s overbreadth and privacy objections are without merit, as the requests pertain solely to Plaintiff.

            The motion is GRANTED as to RFP Nos. 112-126, 128, and 129.

            d. Sexual Harassment Training (RFP Nos. 130, 131, 136)

            RFP Nos. 130, 131, and 136 seek documents pertaining to sexual harassment training received by Defendants John and Joy Lewis. There is good cause for the information because Plaintiff accuses the named defendants of harassment. Training is relevant to Defendant’s obligation to prevent harassment under FEHA. Defendant’s overbreadth and privacy objections are without merit.

            The motion is GRANTED as to RFP Nos. 130, 131, and 136.

            e. Demotion (RFP Nos. 132, 133)

            RFP Nos. 132 and 133 seek documents pertaining to Defendant’s decision to cut Plaintiff’s hours. There is good cause for such information because Plaintiff alleges that Defendant discriminated and retaliated against her. Cutting hours is a potential adverse employment action. Defendant’s overbreadth and privacy objections are without merit.

            The motion is GRANTED as to RFP Nos. 132 and 133.

            f. Remaining Requests

            Plaintiff’s motion makes no attempt to justify the remaining RFPs. These RFPs are also facially improper because they seek overbroad information on issues unrelated to Plaintiff’s claims. For example, RFP No. 73 seeks documents evidencing any complaint by anyone regarding staffing problems at all three facilities for a three-year period. But Plaintiff is not suing for the underlying violations. The lawsuit only pertains to Plaintiff’s own experience. Plaintiff only worked as a personal assistant to John and Joy Lewis for five months. Thus, Plaintiff has failed to demonstrate good cause for the remaining requests.

            g. Statement of Compliance

            A statement of compliance “shall state that the production . . . will be allowed either in whole or in part, and that all documents or things in the demanded category that are in the possession, custody, or control of that party and to which no objection is being made will be included in the production.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.220.)

            Defendant opposes the motion partly on the grounds that it produced responsive documents. However, that does not cure the deficient written responses. Defendant objected and unequivocally stated that it would not produce. Therefore, to the extent Defendant intends to produce documents, it shall provide a statement of compliance.

            If Defendant is unable to comply, then it must provide a statement of non-compliance as follows:

 

A representation of inability to comply with the particular demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling shall affirm that a diligent search and a reasonable inquiry has been made in an effort to comply with that demand. This statement shall also specify whether the inability to comply is because the particular item or category has never existed, has been destroyed, has been lost, misplaced, or stolen, or has never been, or is no longer, in the possession, custody, or control of the responding party. The statement shall set forth the name and address of any natural person or organization known or believed by that party to have possession, custody, or control of that item or category of item.

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.230.)

            Lastly, Defendant shall provide a privilege log for any documents withheld based on a claim of privilege. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.240(c).)

II. Form Interrogatories (CRS# 7579)

            a. FROG No. 207.2

            FROG No. 207.2 asks if Plaintiff complained to Defendant about any of the unlawful conduct alleged in the pleadings, and if so, to provide specific information on each such complaint.

            Defendant’s objections are without merit. This is a standard form interrogatory routinely used in employment cases. Apart from the objections, Defendant responded: “Defendant denies that Plaintiff made any formal complaint of unlawful conduct of the type alleged in the Complaint. To the extent Plaintiff communicated any general workplace concerns, such communications did not constitute formal complaints of unlawful conduct and did not trigger any formal investigative obligation.”

            This is an evasive response. The FROG is not limited to “formal” complaints or complaints that “trigger investigative obligations.” If Plaintiff made any complaint about the unlawful conduct alleged in the complaint, then Defendant must provide the information requested in FROG No. 207.2.

            The motion is GRANTED as to FROG No. 207.2.

            b. FROG No. 211.1

            FROG No. 211.1 asks Defendant to identify and provide information for each benefit to which Plaintiff would have been entitled from the date of her termination to the present. Defendant responded that discovery was ongoing and reserved the right to supplement. This is a valid response because information on Plaintiff’s benefits is reasonably subject to further investigation before it can be ascertained.

            The motion is DENIED as to FROG No. 211.1.

            c. FROG No. 214.1

            FROG No. 214.1 seeks information on any insurance policy covering the claims at issue. Defendant’s objections are without merit. Insurance information is expressly discoverable. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2017.210.) Apart from the objections, Defendant responded: “Defendant will produce information regarding any applicable insurance policies, if any, consistent with Code of Civil Procedure section 2017.210 and the Court's rulings.”

            The response is incomplete. Defendant cannot merely affirm its intent to produce information in compliance with the law. Defendant must actually provide the information in response to FROG No. 214.1. That is what the law requires.

            The motion is GRANTED as to FROG No. 214.1.

            d. FROG No. 216.1

            FROG No. 216.1 asks for information on Defendant’s denials and affirmative defenses. Defendant’s objections are without merit as this is a standard form interrogatory, and Defendant is required to produce information on its own assertions.

Apart from the objections, Defendant responded: “no special or affirmative defenses have been asserted. Defendant filed a demurrer and motion to strike which are currently pending. Defendant reserves the right to supplement this response if and when any such defenses are asserted.” Defendant’s demurrer and motion to strike have since been heard, and Defendant filed an answer on May 19, 2026. Therefore, Defendant must respond to FROG No. 216.1.

The motion is GRANTED as to FROG No. 216.1.

CONCLUSION

            Plaintiff’s motions to compel further responses are GRANTED in part as set forth above. Defendant Growth Management, Inc. shall provide further responses within 20 days of today. The Court sanctions Defendant and its counsel, jointly and severally, in the amount of $1,000, to be paid within 30 days of today.
