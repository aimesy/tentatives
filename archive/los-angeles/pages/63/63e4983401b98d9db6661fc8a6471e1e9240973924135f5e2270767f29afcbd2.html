Tentative Ruling

 

Judge Nicholas F. Daum, Department 507

 

 

HEARING DATE:  June 23, 2026                 TRIAL DATE: Mar. 30, 2027

                                                

CASE:                    Liubou Partnaya v. Vladislav Chizhevsky et al.

 

CASE NO.:             24STCV33256

 

MOTION FOR ISSUE, EVIDENTIARY AND MONETARY SANCTIONS

 

MOVING PARTY:          Plaintiff Liubou Partnaya

 

RESPONDING PARTY(S): Defendants Vladislav Chizhevsky, Felix Chizhevsky and Yelena Kamenir

 

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

Plaintiff’s motion to impose issue, evidentiary and monetary sanctions based on an alleged spoliation of evidence is DENIED.

 

Moving Party to give notice.

 

DISCUSSION:

 

          Plaintiff seeks evidentiary, issue, or monetary sanctions based on what Plaintiff claims was deliberate spoliation of evidence.  The motion must be denied.  Plaintiff has not shown that any evidence was deliberately spoiled or not produced.

 

          Plaintiff’s main concern is with a cellular phone held by Defendant Felix Chizhevsky, apparently used to send text messages with the Plaintiff (the Plaintiff produced, separately, several such messages).  The record evidence indicates that before Felix Chizhevsky was a party to this action (and before a separate subpoena was served upon him) he exchanged an older cellular phone for a newer one, after copying the information from the old phone to a new one.  That is not deliberate destruction of evidence.  Mr. Chizhevsky was not a party to the action at this time he exchanged the phone.  Nor is there evidence indicating that he knew or should have known of some broader duty to preserve evidence at that time (e.g., no duty to preserve evidence letter had been sent).  Nothing in the record indicates that the phone was exchanged for any reason other than obtaining a new phone.  Nor is there any specific evidence of any particularly relevant evidence that was not produced.  Notably, Plaintiff both has and has produced her own text messages with Mr. Chizhevsky.

 

          In these circumstances, the Court cannot award sanctions.  It certainly cannot impose issue or evidentiary sanctions.  Such sanctions are for egregious and intentional violations, generally following a violation of a court order.  Here, the Court sees no violation at all.

 

          Plaintiff and Defendants makes reference to a few other documents that she claims should have been produced beyond the phone, but, because of the manner in which the motion has been presented to the Court, the Court cannot tell what documents are at issue.   The moving and reply papers also contain a host of other merits arguments, all of which appear entirely irrelevant to the sanctions motion at issue.  Based on this record, the Court certainly cannot award monetary, issue or evidentiary sanctions.

 

          Accordingly, the motion is denied, as are all accompanying requests for sanctions.

 

CONCLUSION:

 

Plaintiff’s motion to impose issue, evidentiary and monetary sanctions based on an alleged spoliation of evidence is DENIED.

 

Moving Party to give notice.

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Case Number: 25STCV09520    Hearing Date: June 23, 2026    Dept: 507

Tentative Ruling

 

Judge Nicholas F. Daum, Department 507

 

 

HEARING DATE:  June 23, 2026                 TRIAL DATE: Nov. 17, 2026

                                                

CASE:                    Willis Oliver v. BMW of North America, LLC

 

CASE NO.:             25STCV09520

 

MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER DISCOVERY RESPONSES TO DEFENDANT’S REQUEST FOR PRODUCTION, SET ONE

 

MOVING PARTY:          Defendant BMW of North America, LLC

 

RESPONDING PARTY(S): Plaintiff Willis Oliver

 

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

BMW’s motion to compel further responses to requests for production is DENIED.

 

Plaintiff is ordered to provide copies of the documents identified in the Court’s order of September 8, 2025 within 10 days of this order.

 

Moving Party to give notice.

 

DISCUSSION:

 

          On September 8, 2025, this Court ordered the parties to comply with its standard discovery order for pre-2025 lemon law cases.  Counsel for BMW was present when that order issued.  Notice of the order was provided to BMW.  That order specifically provides that “[a]ny additional requests for documents may only be propounded by stipulation and/or court order (via motion upon showing of good cause.”  (Order at p. 14.)  Nonetheless, BMW served additional discovery requests seeking documents, as to which it now seeks to compel responses or further responses.

 

          The motion to compel must be denied.  BMW served the requests at issue in violation of the Court’s order.  Nonetheless, it appears (from the moving and opposition papers) that the Plaintiff has also failed to comply with the Court’s order of September 8, 2025, which required the Plaintiff to produce certain documents within 30 days of that order.  Accordingly, the Court orders Plaintiff to provide the documents identified in the Court’s order of September 8, 2025 within 10 days of this order.  If these documents were already produced, nothing more need be done.

 

          All requests for sanctions are denied.

 

 

CONCLUSION:

 

BMW’s motion to compel further responses to requests for production is DENIED.

 

Plaintiff is ordered to provide copies of the documents identified in the Court’s order of September 8, 2025 within 10 days of this order.

 

Moving Party to give notice.

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Case Number: 25STCV15914    Hearing Date: June 23, 2026    Dept: 507

Tentative Ruling

 

Judge Nicholas F. Daum, Department 507

 

 

HEARING DATE:  June 23, 2026                 TRIAL DATE: April 6, 2027

                                                

CASE:                    Skye Wright v. PIH Health Physicians, et al.

 

CASE NO.:             25STCV15914 

 

MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT

 

MOVING PARTY:          Defendant PIH Health Good Samaritan Hospital (“Hospital”)

 

RESPONDING PARTY(S): Plaintiff Skye Wright (Non-opposition)

 

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

Hospital’s Motion for Summary Judgment is GRANTED.

 

Moving Party to give notice.

 

BACKGROUND

         

          This is a medical malpractice action. Wright alleges that the defendants were negligent in the provision of medical care, causing the loss of her unborn baby.

 

DISCUSSION:

 

          Hospital moves for summary judgment. The hospital has presented expert testimony establishing that the Hospital staff complied with the standard of care, and that the physicians were not agents of the Hospital. Wright does not oppose the motion, and has submitted a statement affirmatively indicating that there is no opposition. The Court therefore grants summary judgment as to the Hospital.

 

Legal Standard

 

The function of a motion for summary judgment or adjudication is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party can show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and, if not, to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) Code of Civil Procedure Section 437c(c) “requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.”  (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.)  “The function of the pleadings in a motion for summary judgment is to delimit the scope of the issues; the function of the affidavits or declarations is to disclose whether there is any triable issue of fact within the issues delimited by the pleadings.” (Juge v. County of Sacramento (1993) 12 Cal.App.4th 59, 67, citing FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima (1991) 231 Cal. App. 3d 367, 381-82.)

 

As to each claim as framed by the complaint, the defendant moving for summary judgment must satisfy the initial burden of proof by presenting facts to negate an essential element, or to establish a defense. (Code Civ Proc. § 437c(p)(2); Scalf v. D. B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1520.) Courts “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.” (Dore v. Arnold Worldwide, Inc. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 384, 389.) The lack of opposition by a plaintiff is not grounds to grant a motion for summary judgment if a defendant cannot meet their initial burden of proof. (See Thatcher v. Lucy Stores, Inc. (2000) 79 Cal.App.4th 1081, 1087.)

 

          Once the defendant has met that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to that cause of action or a defense thereto. To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence. (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

 

Professional Negligence

 

          The Hospital moves for summary judgment. The undisputed evidence establishes that the attending physicians and nursing staff complied with the standard of care and did not cause the loss of Wright’s baby. Because Wright does not oppose the motion, the Court finds that the Hospital is entitled to summary judgment on this basis.

 

The elements of a cause of action for medical malpractice are: (1) a duty to use such skill, prudence, and diligence as other members of the profession commonly possess and exercise; (2) a breach of the duty; (3) a proximate causal connection between the negligent conduct and the injury; and (4) resulting loss or damage.” (Lattimore v. Dickey (2015) 239 Cal.App.4th 959, 968.) In a medical negligence action, the standard of care is a matter that must be established by expert testimony. (See Howard v. Owens Corning (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 621, 632-33.) The causal connection between the negligent conduct and the injury is similarly a matter of expert testimony, and that testimony must establish that the injury was more likely than not the result of medical negligence. (Jones v. Ortho Pharmaceutical Corp. (1985) 163 Cal.App.3d 396, 402-403.)

 

          According to the undisputed evidence, Wright presented to the Hospital on June 30, 2024 with complaints of cramping, abdominal pain, and pink discharge. (Separate Statement of Undisputed Material Facts No. 1, 13.) Hospital nursing staff attempted to take the heart rate of Wright’s fetus, but were unable to do so. (SSUMF Nos. 2-3, 14-15.) Dr. Varun Khetan attempted a bedside ultrasound, but also could not obtain the fetus’s heart rate. (SSUMF No. 4, 16.) Dr. Khetan suggested a formal ultrasound, and Wright agreed. (Id.) The ultrasound confirmed the loss of Wright’s baby, and Dr. Khetan discussed a plan of care with Wright. (SSUMF Nos. 5-6, 17-18.) Wright agreed to vaginal delivery, and her baby was delivered stillborn at 4:40 AM on July 1. (SSUMF No. 7, 19.) The Hospital presents a declaration from Joseph Ouzonian, M.D., a board-certified OBGYN with extensive experience in clinical practice. (Declaration of Joseph Ouzonian ISO Mot. ¶ 2.) Dr. Ouzonian states that, based on his review of the records, his training, and his experience, the residents, nurses, and nonphysician staff all acted within the standard of care in treating Wright. (SSUMF Nos. 9-12.) Dr. Ouzonian concurs with the Forensic and Surgical Pathologist’s conclusion that Wright lost her baby due to complications from pre-eclampsia, and that the baby had passed before Wright came to the hospital on June 30. (SSUMF No. 21.)

 

          The Hospital has presented substantial evidence tending to prove that it did not breach the standard of care and that no act or omission was responsible for the death of Wright’s baby. Because Wright formally does not oppose this motion, the Court finds that the Hospital has carried its burden and that there is no triable issue of material fact on this point.

 

Ostensible Agency

 

          The Hospital also argues that the physician defendants were neither the actual nor ostensible agents of the Hospital, and thus the Hospital cannot be liable for any act or omission by the doctors.

 

          A principal is liable for the acts of an ostensible agent when the principal “intentionally, or by want of ordinary care, causes a third person to believe another to be his agent who is not really employed by him.” (Civ. Code § 2300; see also § 2317.) A principal is bound by the acts of an ostensible agent “to those persons only who have in good faith, and without want of ordinary care, incurred a liability or parted with value, upon the faith thereof.” (Civ. Code § 2334.) As applied to hospitals, the first element is satisfied when a hospital holds itself out to the public as a provider of care, and reliance exists when the plaintiff “looks to” the hospital, rather than an individual physician, for services. (Mejia v. Community Hospital of San Bernardino (2002) 99 Cal.App.4th 1448, 1453-54.) Put differently, “unless the patient had some reason to know of the true relationship between the hospital and the physician—i.e., because the hospital gave the patient actual notice or because the patient was treated by his or her personal physician—ostensible agency is readily inferred.” (Id. at 1454-55.)

 

Here, the Hospital states that, upon her admission on June 30, Wright signed a Conditions of Admissions document which stated that physicians were not agents or employees of the Hospital. (SSUMF NO. 23.) The Hospital has thus presented evidence that Wright was given actual notice of the true relationship between the Hospital and the physicians. Because Wright has declined to oppose the motion, the Court finds that there is no triable issue of fact on this point.

 

Because there is no dispute of material fact regarding the Hospital staff’s compliance with the standard of care, the cause of death of Wright’s child, and the agency relationship between the physicians and the Hospital, the Hospital is entitled to summary judgment.

 

CONCLUSION:

 

          Accordingly, Hospital’s Motion for Summary Judgment is GRANTED.

 

Moving Party to give notice.

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.
