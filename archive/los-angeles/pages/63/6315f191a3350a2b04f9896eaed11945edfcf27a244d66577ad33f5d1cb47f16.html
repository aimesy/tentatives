SANCTIONS

 

MOVING PARTY: Defendant, County of Los Angeles

RESPONDING PARTY: Plaintiff, Kourtney Kuder

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Motion for Terminating Sanctions, or Alternatively, Issue, Evidentiary, or Monetary Sanctions

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

Plaintiff Kourtney Kuder was terminated as a probationary firefighter with Defendant County of Los Angeles. On May 1, 2023, Plaintiff sued Defendant under the Fair Employment and Housing Act, alleging discrimination and harassment based on her sex.   

 

RULING: Denied.

Defendant moves for terminating sanctions, or alternatively, issue, evidentiary, or monetary sanctions against Plaintiff for alleged spoliation of evidence after Plaintiff’s phone was “destroyed” in a boating incident. Defendant claims that Plaintiff failed to preserve evidence of text messages between her and colleagues, which Plaintiff relies on in support of her claims to show false rumors were being spread about her. Plaintiff opposes the motion, denying any intentional spoliation. Defendant’s motion is denied.

 

A trial court has inherent power to “control the litigation before it.” (New Albertsons, Inc. v. Superior Court (2008) 168 Cal.App.4th 1403, 1431.) When faced with litigation abuse, a court has discretion to preclude evidence to ensure a fair trial (Peat, Marwick, Mitchell & Co. v. Superior Court (1988) 200 Cal.App.3d 272, 288) and to dismiss an action if no other remedy could ensure a fair trial. (Stephen Slesinger, Inc. v. Walt Disney Co. (2007) 155 Cal.App.4th 736, 764.) Such remedies are available when a party intentionally destroys evidence. (Williams v. Russ (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 1215, 1223.)

 

“Spoliation of evidence means the destruction or significant alteration of evidence or the failure to preserve evidence for another’s use in pending or future litigation.” (Williams, supra, 167 Cal.App.4th at p. 1223.) “[A] party moving for discovery sanctions based on the spoliation of evidence must make an initial prima facie showing that the responding party in fact destroyed evidence that had a substantial probability of damaging the moving party’s ability to establish an essential element of his claim or defense.” (Id. at p. 1227.)

 

Neither terminating sanctions nor issue, evidentiary, or monetary sanctions are warranted. The evidence presented does not show that Plaintiff’s failure to preserve the relevant data on her phone was intentional. Plaintiff stated in her deposition that she had moved to Tennessee, that her old phone stopped working on her way to work, and that while in Tennessee she moved to a new home. [Seemen Decl. ¶ 2, Ex. A: Plaintiff’s Depo. pp. 17:12-15, 123:20-124:4. 125:5-126:13.] She explained that she currently could not locate her phone and that it may be at her previous home in Tennessee. [Id. at pp. 125:21-126:4.] She explained her inattention to the whereabouts of her phone was due to her belief that she had already produced the relevant information through her discovery production. [Id. at pp. 123:20-124:4.] These facts do not indicate intentional spoliation, but rather the inadvertent loss of a damaged phone during subsequent moves at a time Plaintiff believed (mistakenly) that the phone served no purpose. [Cf. Williams, supra, 167 Cal.App.4th at p. 1224 [intentional spoliation where party failed to pay storage fees to prevent sale and destruction of files despite repeated warnings].) The absence of intentional conduct is further buttressed by the fact that Plaintiff attempted to comply with Defendant’s discovery requests by providing screenshots of the texts and creating spreadsheets of the messages through the “iMazing” software program. [Sheldon Decl. ¶¶ 9, 12, Exs. 5-6, 9; Seemen Decl. ¶ 2, Ex. A: Plaintiff’s Depo. pp. 107:8-108:2.] The record thus provides no basis for imposing sanctions against Plaintiff. Defendant’s motion is therefore denied.

 

The Court’s denial of the sanctions request does not preclude Defendant from later challenging the introduction of the text messages during trial or requesting an inference under Evidence Code section 413 on a more developed record demonstrating intentional spoliation. (New Albertsons, Inc., supra, 168 Cal.App.4th at p. 1434.)

 

Defendant to give notice.

LEAVE TO AMEND

 

MOVING PARTY: Plaintiff, Kelly Soojin Kim

RESPONDING PARTY: Defendant, Jason Bin Lee

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Motion for Leave to File First Amended Complaint

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

Plaintiff Kelly Soojin Kim filed a verified complaint against Defendant Jason Bin Lee for breach of contract, breach of fiduciary duty, fraud, and unfair competition. Kelly Kim alleges that Lee approached her and her husband, Brandon Kim, in early 2021 to establish a business relationship, whereby Lee would work with Kelly Kim at Star Night, an existing establishment owned and operated by Kelly Kim. Kelly Kim claims that Lee proposed the joint venture to improve revenue at Star Night given Lee’s prior operation of a similar restaurant business, OB Bear, which was lost to a fire. Lee purportedly represented that his secret recipes and business knowledge could increase Star Night’s sales to at least $400,000 per month.

 

In March 2021, Kelly Kim and Lee entered into a shareholder agreement. The agreement provides Kelly Kim a 51% ownership share of B&K Enterprise, Inc., a corporation previously incorporated by her. Lee is granted the remaining 49% in exchange for his secret recipes and “business management know how.” The agreement articulates certain responsibilities between the owners based on either the first or second floor location within the premises and also provides Lee with the right to open additional OB Bear restaurants outside California without Kelly Kim’s consent.

 

The parties’ working relationship was unsuccessful, with accusations of stolen property and improper operational management practices. Lee subsequently requested cessation from the partnership. Kelly Kim alleges that Lee’s intent was to either take over Star Night or ruin it so it could not compete with the new business Lee wanted to establish once he received fire insurance proceeds. She also contends that Lee failed to provide the secret recipes in violation of the shareholder agreement and that the agreement fails for lack of consideration. Kelly Kim therefore denies any enforceable shareholder agreement entitling Lee to rights as a shareholder.

 

On June 26, 2023, Lee answered the complaint and filed a cross-complaint. On May 28, 2024, Lee filed a Verified Second Amended Cross-Complaint asserting 18 causes of action. Lee alleges multiple violations of the shareholder agreement in the form of interference with his operation of the first floor, unauthorized and unpaid dividends, and Kelly Kim’s unilateral dissolution of B&K. He also alleges an unpaid $150,000 loan and mismanagement of corporate assets.

 

RULING: Granted in part.

Kelly Kim moves for leave to file a First Amended Complaint recharacterizing the parties’ agreement as an oral partnership in Star Night and adding a corresponding cause of action for breach of oral partnership agreement and wrongful dissociation. She also seeks to amend her fraud claim and add a UCL claim based on Lee’s collection of insurance proceeds from the loss of OB Bear. The basis for the amendment is Lee’s October 8, 2025 deposition testimony concerning the parties’ relationship and Lee’s compensation arrangement. Lee opposes the motion, arguing that Kim’s 8-month delay in seeking amendment since his deposition prejudices him because it will necessitate additional discovery and further delay trial. Kelly Kim’s motion is granted in part, and she is granted leave to add factual allegations concerning an oral partnership agreement and the corresponding cause of action for breach of oral partnership agreement and wrongful dissociation.   

 

A motion for leave to amend must comply with the requirements set forth in California Rules of Court, rule 3.1324, which states as follows:

 

(a)        Contents of motion

A motion to amend a pleading before trial must:

(1)               Include a copy of the proposed amendment or amended pleading, which must be serially numbered to differentiate it from previous pleadings or amendments;

(2)               State what allegations in the previous pleading are proposed to be deleted, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the deleted allegations are located; and

(3)               State what allegations are proposed to be added to the previous pleading, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the additional allegations are located.

 

(b)        Supporting declaration

A separate declaration must accompany the motion and must specify:

(1)        The effect of the amendment;

(2)        Why the amendment is necessary and proper;

(3)        When the facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered; and

(4)               The reasons why the request for amendment was not made

earlier.

 

Dilatory delays and prejudice to the opposing parties is a valid ground for denial. (Hirsa v. Superior Court (1981) 118 Cal.App.3d 486, 490.) Prejudice exists where the amendment would require delaying the trial, resulting in loss of critical evidence, or added costs of preparation such as an increased burden of discovery. (Magpali v. Farmers Group, Inc. (1996) 48 Cal.App.4th 471, 486-88.)

 

Leave to amend is generally liberally granted. (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (a); Mesler v. Bragg Management Co. (1985) 39 Cal.3d 290, 296.) The Court will not generally consider the validity of the proposed amended pleading in ruling on a motion for leave, instead deferring such determinations for a demurrer or motion to strike, unless the proposed amendment fails to state a valid claim as a matter of law. (Kittredge Sports Co. v. Superior Court (1989) 213 Cal. App.3d 1045, 1048; California Casualty Gen. Ins. Co. v. Superior Court (1985) 173 Cal.App.3d 274, 280-81 disapproved of on other grounds by Kransco v. American Empire Surplus Lines Ins. Co. (2000) 23 Cal.4th 390.)

 

Kelly Kim is granted leave to add factual allegations concerning the alleged oral partnership agreement and claim for breach of the agreement and wrongful dissociation. These amendments are based on the same set of previously alleged facts regarding the structure of the parties’ relationship, which have been the subject of litigation and discovery. Contrary to Lee’s claim, Kelly Kim has not affirmatively denied the existence of an oral partnership agreement via discovery or otherwise. [See Garcia Decl. ¶ 8, Exs. 3, 7-8.] The additional cause of action is also not facially barred by the statute of limitations because the alleged dissociation occurred in June 2021, the original complaint was filed in May 2023, and the cause of action is based on the same facts asserted in the initial complaint. (Hirsa, supra, 118 Cal.App.3d at p. 489.) There is also no concern regarding impingement of Lee’s summary judgment rights because he has been a party since inception of the litigation and has had an opportunity to test the same facts underlying Kelly Kim’s existing causes of action. (Cf. Polibrid Coatings, Inc. v. Superior Court (2003) 112 Cal.App.4th 920, 923-24 [belatedly-added defendant should be given “at least enough time for that party to reasonably complete discovery and bring a summary judgment motion”].) Kelly Kim is thus granted leave to add the oral partnership allegations and related cause of action.

 

Leave is not granted, however, to add the new fraud allegations and fraud and UCL causes of action. Up until now, Kelly Kim’s fraud theory has rested on Lee’s purported intent to take over or destroy Star Night. This is very different from the proposed amendments which shift the factual basis to Lee’s intent to leave Star Night upon receipt of insurance proceeds. Kelly Kim also has not previously claimed a purported scheme by Lee to use the partnership to conceal income from his insurer. Allowing these amendments would prejudice Lee at this stage of the litigation as he has not had the opportunity to explore and test these facts through discovery. Kelly Kim is thus not granted leave to add the fraud allegations and related fraud and UCL claims.

 

Kelly Kim’s motion is thus granted in part. Kelly Kim to file a First Amended Complaint in accordance with this order within 10 days.

 

Kelly Kim to give notice.

LEAVE TO AMEND

RESPONDING PARTY: Cross-Complainant, Jason Bin Lee

Motion for Leave to File First Amended Answer to Verified Second Amended Cross-Complaint

RULING: Granted in part.

Cross-Defendants Kelly Soojin Kim, Brandon Kim, and B&K Enterprise, Inc. move for leave to file a First Amended Answer to Cross-Complainant Jason Bin Lee’s Verified Second Amended Cross-Complaint. They seek to add affirmative defenses for failure of consideration, rescission, lack of mutual assent, and selective claim of shareholder benefits without corresponding obligations. The basis for the amendments is the Court’s denial of their summary adjudication motion and developments in discovery. Lee opposes the motion, arguing prejudice due to the timing of the motion and claiming that the rescission and selective claim of shareholder benefits defenses improperly seek affirmative relief. Cross-Defendants’ motion is granted in part, and they are granted leave to add the defenses for failure of consideration and rescission.

 

A motion for leave to amend must comply with the requirements set forth in California Rules of Court, rule 3.1324, which states as follows:

 

(a)        Contents of motion

A motion to amend a pleading before trial must:

(1)               Include a copy of the proposed amendment or amended pleading, which must be serially numbered to differentiate it from previous pleadings or amendments;

(2)               State what allegations in the previous pleading are proposed to be deleted, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the deleted allegations are located; and

(3)               State what allegations are proposed to be added to the previous pleading, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the additional allegations are located.

 

(b)        Supporting declaration

A separate declaration must accompany the motion and must specify:

(1)        The effect of the amendment;

(2)        Why the amendment is necessary and proper;

(3)        When the facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered; and

(4)               The reasons why the request for amendment was not made

earlier.

 

Dilatory delays and prejudice to the opposing parties is a valid ground for denial. (Hirsa v. Superior Court (1981) 118 Cal.App.3d 486, 490.) Prejudice exists where the amendment would require delaying the trial, resulting in loss of critical evidence, or added costs of preparation such as an increased burden of discovery. (Magpali v. Farmers Group, Inc. (1996) 48 Cal.App.4th 471, 486-88.)

 

Leave to amend is generally liberally granted. (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (a); Mesler v. Bragg Management Co. (1985) 39 Cal.3d 290, 296.) The Court will not generally consider the validity of the proposed amended pleading in ruling on a motion for leave, instead deferring such determinations for a demurrer or motion to strike, unless the proposed amendment fails to state a valid claim as a matter of law. (Kittredge Sports Co. v. Superior Court (1989) 213 Cal. App.3d 1045, 1048; California Casualty Gen. Ins. Co. v. Superior Court (1985) 173 Cal.App.3d 274, 280-81 disapproved of on other grounds by Kransco v. American Empire Surplus Lines Ins. Co. (2000) 23 Cal.4th 390.)

 

Cross-Defendants are granted leave to add the affirmative defenses for failure of consideration and rescission. Both defenses are based on Lee’s purported failure to provide Cross-Defendants with the OB Bear recipes, a fact in issue since inception of the litigation and subject to discovery. Lee’s challenge to the rescission defense is not proper for consideration on the instant motion and there is no concern of impingement of his right to bring a summary adjudication motion as he has been a party for the entirety of the case and has had the opportunity to test the facts underlying the basis for rescission. (Cf. Polibrid Coatings, Inc. v. Superior Court (2003) 112 Cal.App.4th 920, 923-24 [belatedly-added defendant should be given “at least enough time for that party to reasonably complete discovery and bring a summary judgment motion”].) However, leave is not granted to add the defenses for lack of mutual assent and selective claim of shareholder benefits because both defenses are premised on facts that Lee has not been able to test and explore through discovery. The mutual assent defense is based on misunderstandings due to a lack of translation of the written shareholder agreement and Lee’s intent to leave the business after receiving insurance proceeds. The selective claim of shareholder benefits defense seeks an accounting of the liabilities incurred during the period of Lee’s claimed ownership. Lee has not had the opportunity to conduct discovery on these issues. Cross-Defendants are therefore only granted leave to add the defenses for failure of consideration and rescission.

 

Cross-Defendants’ motion is thus granted in part. Cross-Defendants to file a First Amended Answer in accordance with this order within 10 days.

DEMURRER

 

MOVING PARTY: Defendant, VII Commercial Corp.

RESPONDING PARTY: Plaintiff, C7 Endeavors, LLC

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Demurrer to First through Sixth Causes of Action in Complaint

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

The instant dispute centers around Plaintiff C7 Endeavors, LLC’s lease of property owned by Defendant Dunn Investment Properties, Inc. (Dunn) and managed by Defendant VII Commercial Corp. (VII) Plaintiff was in discussions to become a franchisee of D-Bat Academies, Inc. to operate an indoor baseball training facility. On or about January 12, 2024, Plaintiff toured the property with representatives from Dunn and VII. Plaintiff alleges that during the tour, it identified a list of issues with the property including a roof leak and other concerns relating to HVAC, electrical wiring, faulty plumbing, mold, and cracks in the concrete flooring. Plaintiff claims that Defendants’ agent, Greg Offsay, confirmed that Dunn “has the funds available and is willing to do the work to address the condition concerns that you presented in your lease comments.” Defendants then sent Plaintiff a “CAPEX Project List” identifying the agreed-upon work that needed to be done. Plaintiff alleges that based on these representations, it entered into a lease agreement with Dunn. VII was a signatory as Dunn’s broker.

 

Plaintiff alleges that an inspection after the lease’s commencement date revealed that most identified issues were not resolved, previous representations regarding completed work were false, and work actually completed was done in a shoddy manner. Plaintiff eventually elected to terminate the lease and vacated the premises. On July 7, 2025, Plaintiff filed a complaint for: (1) Breach of Contract; (2) Breach of the Covenant of Good Faith and Dealing; (3) Fraud – Intentional Misrepresentation; (4) Fraud – Negligent Misrepresentation; (5) Fraud – False Promise; (6) Constructive Eviction; and (7) Declaratory Relief.

 

RULING: Sustained.

VII demurs to the first through sixth causes of action in Plaintiff’s complaint, arguing that Plaintiff fails to sufficiently state its claims. VII further contends that the fraud claims fail for uncertainty. Plaintiff opposes the demurrer, maintaining the sufficiency of its fraud-based causes of action. VII’s demurrer is sustained without leave to amend as to the first, second, and sixth contract causes of action, but sustained with leave to amend as to the fraud claims.

 

A demurrer is an objection to a pleading, the grounds for which are apparent from either the face of the complaint or a matter of which the court may take judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.30, subd. (a); see also Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) The purpose of a demurrer is to challenge the sufficiency of a pleading “by raising questions of law.” (Postley v. Harvey (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 280, 286.) “In the construction of a pleading, for the purpose of determining its effect, its allegations must be liberally construed, with a view to substantial justice between the parties.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 452.) The court “treat[s] the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law . . . .” (Berkley v. Dowds (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 518, 525.) In applying these standards, the court liberally construes the complaint to determine whether a cause of action has been stated. (Picton v. Anderson Union High School Dist. (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 726, 733.) “If the complaint states a cause of action under any theory, regardless of the title under which the factual basis for relief is stated, that aspect of the complaint is good against a demurrer.” (Quelimane Co. v. Stewart Title Guaranty Co. (1998) 19 Cal.4th 26, 38.)

 

“A demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Khoury v. Maly’s of Cal., Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616; Williams v. Beechnut Nutrition Corp. (1986) 185 Cal.App.3d 135, 139, fn. 2 [“[U]nder our liberal pleading rules, where the complaint contains substantive factual allegations sufficiently apprising defendant of the issues it is being asked to meet, a demurrer for uncertainty should be overruled or plaintiff given leave to amend”].)

 

First, Second, and Sixth Causes of Action: Breach of Contract, Breach of the Covenant of Good Faith and Dealing, and Constructive Eviction

VII’s demurrer to Plaintiff’s contract-based claims is sustained without leave to amend. VII argues these causes of action fail because it was not in contractual privity with Plaintiff. “[A]n agent cannot be held liable for breach of a duty which flows from a contract to which he is not party.” (Filippo Industries, Inc. v. Sun Ins. Co. of N.Y. (1999) 74 Cal.App.4th 1429, 1443.) It follows that agents are not liable for claims derivative of the contract, such as the duty of good faith and fair dealing. (Id. at pp. 1442-43.) Here, VII is not a party to the lease because the lease defines Plaintiff and Dunn as parties and VII is designated only as “Lessor’s Brokerage Firm.” [Compl. ¶ 22, Ex. 1, p. 1.] Plaintiff presents no argument on this point in opposition. VII’s demurrer to these claims is therefore sustained without leave to amend.

 

Third through Fifth Causes of Action: Intentional Misrepresentation, Negligent Misrepresentation, and False Promise

VII argues that Plaintiff’s fraud-based causes of action fail because they are not pled with requisite particularity.

 

“The elements of fraud, which give rise to the tort action for deceit, are (a) misrepresentation (false representation, concealment, or nondisclosure); (b) knowledge of falsity (or ‘scienter’); (c) intent to defraud, i.e., to induce reliance; (d) justifiable reliance; and (e) resulting damage.” (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 638.) Fraud must be pled with specificity; conclusory allegations are insufficient. (Id. at p. 645.) This requires “pleading facts which show how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were tendered.” (Ibid.) These strict pleading requirements apply to negligent misrepresentation claims. (Daniels v. Select Portfolio Servicing, Inc. (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 1150, 1166-67.)

 

VII’s demurrer to Plaintiff’s fraud claims is sustained with leave to amend. While Plaintiff identifies specific representations made, these representations are attributed to VII and Dunn collectively as “Defendants.” [See, e.g., Compl. ¶¶ 18-19.] It thus cannot be ascertained whether the representations were made on VII’s behalf. Because there is a reasonable possibility that Plaintiff can amend its complaint to identify each alleged representation with each defendant, it is granted leave to amend.

 

Plaintiff is granted 30 days’ leave to amend. Plaintiff may only add facts in support of the successfully challenged fraud-based claims. (Harris v. Wachovia Mortgage, FSB (2010) 185 Cal.App.4th 1018, 1023.) If Plaintiff declines or fails to file an amended complaint, VII shall answer the operative complaint within 10 days of the lapsed deadline. Any new causes of action outside the scope of the order may be addressed via a motion to strike.

 

VII to give notice.

DEMURRER

 

MOVING PARTY: Defendant, Dunn Investment Properties, Inc. 

RESPONDING PARTY: Plaintiff, C7 Endeavors, LLC

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Demurrer to Complaint

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

The instant dispute centers around Plaintiff C7 Endeavors, LLC’s lease of property owned by Defendant Dunn Investment Properties, Inc. (Dunn) and managed by Defendant VII Commercial Corp. (VII) Plaintiff was in discussions to become a franchisee of D-Bat Academies, Inc. to operate an indoor baseball training facility. On or about January 12, 2024, Plaintiff toured the property with representatives from Dunn and VII. Plaintiff alleges that during the tour, it identified a list of issues with the property including a roof leak and other concerns relating to HVAC, electrical wiring, faulty plumbing, mold, and cracks in the concrete flooring. Plaintiff claims that Defendants’ agent, Greg Offsay, confirmed that Dunn “has the funds available and is willing to do the work to address the condition concerns that you presented in your lease comments.” Defendants then sent Plaintiff a “CAPEX Project List” identifying the agreed-upon work that needed to be done. Plaintiff alleges that based on these representations, it entered into a lease agreement with Dunn. VII was a signatory as Dunn’s broker.

 

Plaintiff alleges that an inspection after the lease’s commencement date revealed that most identified issues were not resolved, previous representations regarding completed work were false, and work actually completed was done in a shoddy manner. Plaintiff eventually elected to terminate the lease and vacated the premises. On July 7, 2025, Plaintiff filed a complaint for: (1) Breach of Contract; (2) Breach of the Covenant of Good Faith and Dealing; (3) Fraud – Intentional Misrepresentation; (4) Fraud – Negligent Misrepresentation; (5) Fraud – False Promise; (6) Constructive Eviction; and (7) Declaratory Relief.

 

RULING: Sustained in part.

Dunn demurs to each cause of action in Plaintiff’s complaint, arguing that Plaintiff fails to sufficiently state its claims. Plaintiff opposes the demurrer, maintaining the sufficiency of the allegations in support of its claims. Dunn’s demurrer is sustained with leave to amend as to Plaintiff’s fraud-based claims. The demurrer is overruled as to the remaining causes of action.

 

A demurrer is an objection to a pleading, the grounds for which are apparent from either the face of the complaint or a matter of which the court may take judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.30, subd. (a); see also Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) The purpose of a demurrer is to challenge the sufficiency of a pleading “by raising questions of law.” (Postley v. Harvey (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 280, 286.) “In the construction of a pleading, for the purpose of determining its effect, its allegations must be liberally construed, with a view to substantial justice between the parties.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 452.) The court “treat[s] the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law . . . .” (Berkley v. Dowds (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 518, 525.) In applying these standards, the court liberally construes the complaint to determine whether a cause of action has been stated. (Picton v. Anderson Union High School Dist. (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 726, 733.) “If the complaint states a cause of action under any theory, regardless of the title under which the factual basis for relief is stated, that aspect of the complaint is good against a demurrer.” (Quelimane Co. v. Stewart Title Guaranty Co. (1998) 19 Cal.4th 26, 38.)

 

First Cause of Action: Breach of Contract

Dunn first argues that Plaintiff’s breach of contract cause of action fails because Plaintiff’s claimed damages are precluded by the lease agreement.

 

The elements of a breach of contract cause of action are: “(1) the existence of the contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance, (3) defendant’s breach, and (4) the resulting damages to the plaintiff.” (D’Arrigo Bros. of Cal. v. United Farmworkers of America (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 790, 800.)

 

Plaintiff’s alleged damages are not precluded by the lease agreement. Among other damages, Plaintiff alleges “expended Rent despite the Premises not being delivered.” [Compl. ¶ 60.] To the extent section 9.6 of the lease is applicable to preexisting damage on the property, it does not preclude recovery of the expended rent. Although that provision limits rent abatement to “the proceeds received from [Dunn’s] Rental Value insurance,” this merely limits the amount Plaintiff may recover rather than totally barring recovery. [Id. ¶ 22, Ex. 1, p. 8.] Thus, Plaintiff does not fail to plead damages.     

 

Dunn also argues that the breach of contract claim fails because Plaintiff does not adequately allege breach. It points to the notice and warranty provisions in the lease as well as its limits on modification to the premises.

 

Plaintiff has sufficiently alleged Dunn’s breach of the lease agreement. Plaintiff alleges Dunn breached the lease agreement by failing to comply with its maintenance and repair obligations under section 2.2. [Compl. ¶¶ 22, 54-56, Ex. 1, p. 2.] Plaintiff reported the issues to Dunn within the 30-day warranty period because it emailed Dunn its inspection findings on June 28, 2024, 28 days after the May 31, 2024 commencement date. [Id. ¶¶ 22, 35-37, Ex. 1, p. 2.] Dunn failed to remedy the issues “within a reasonable time,” as contemplated by section 13.6(a) of the lease, because Plaintiff alleges the necessary repairs were never made. [Id. ¶¶ 22, 34-44, Ex. 1, p. 11.] And any limit on improvements absent Dunn’s written approval is inapplicable given that Plaintiff alleges the issues on the property prevented it from building at all. [Id. ¶¶ 22, 46, Ex. 1: Addendum ¶ 53.] Plaintiff has thus sufficiently alleged Dunn’s breach and the demurrer to this claim is overruled.

 

Second Cause of Action: Breach of the Covenant of Good Faith and Dealing

Dunn’s demurrer to Plaintiff’s cause of action for breach of the covenant of good faith and fair dealing is overruled. Dunn’s demurrer to this claim rests on Plaintiff’s inability to allege breach of the lease and recoverable damages. As explained above, Plaintiff has sufficiently alleged breach and damages and thus the demurrer to this claim is overruled.

 

Third through Fifth Causes of Action: Intentional Misrepresentation, Negligent Misrepresentation, and False Promise

Dunn contends that Plaintiff’s fraud-based claims fail because it does not allege its claims with requisite particularity.

 

“The elements of fraud, which give rise to the tort action for deceit, are (a) misrepresentation (false representation, concealment, or nondisclosure); (b) knowledge of falsity (or ‘scienter’); (c) intent to defraud, i.e., to induce reliance; (d) justifiable reliance; and (e) resulting damage.” (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 638.) Fraud must be pled with specificity; conclusory allegations are insufficient. (Id. at p. 645.) This requires “pleading facts which show how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were tendered.” (Ibid.) These strict pleading requirements apply to negligent misrepresentation claims. (Daniels v. Select Portfolio Servicing, Inc. (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 1150, 1166-67.)

 

Dunn’s demurrer to Plaintiff’s fraud claims is sustained with leave to amend. While Plaintiff identifies specific representations made, these representations are attributed to VII and Dunn collectively as “Defendants.” [See, e.g., Compl. ¶¶ 18-19.] It thus cannot be ascertained whether the representations were made on Dunn’s behalf. Because there is a reasonable possibility that Plaintiff can amend its complaint to identify each alleged representation with each defendant, it is granted leave to amend.

 

Sixth Cause of Action: Constructive Eviction

Dunn argues that Plaintiff’s constructive eviction cause of action fails because the lease precludes Plaintiff’s ability to assert this claim. It further contends that the claim is insufficiently pled because Plaintiff did not provide notice within the warranty period and no facts indicate an interference with Plaintiff’s possession.

 

“A constructive eviction occurs when the acts or omissions . . . of a landlord, or any disturbance or interference with the tenant’s possession by the landlord, renders the premises, or a substantial portion thereof, unfit for the purposes for which they were leased, or which has the effect of depriving the tenant for a substantial period of time of the beneficial enjoyment or use of the premises.” (Stoiber v. Honeychuck (1980) 101 Cal.App.3d 903, 925-26.) “Failure to repair and keep the premises in a condition suitable for the purposes for which they were leased has been held to constitute eviction.” (Id. at p. 926.)

 

Dunn’s demurrer to Plaintiff’s constructive eviction claim is overruled. No provision of the lease limits Plaintiff’s ability to assert this claim and, as explained above, Plaintiff provided notice of the property issues within the warranty period. Plaintiff has also alleged interference with its possession of the property because it was unable to proceed with its buildout due to Dunn’s alleged failure to repair the identified issues. [Compl. ¶¶ 102-05.] The demurrer to this claim is thus overruled.

 

Seventh Cause of Action: Declaratory Relief

Dunn’s demurrer to Plaintiff’s claim for declaratory relief is overruled. Dunn argues this claim fails because any remedy Plaintiff seeks would be addressed by the breach of contract cause of action. While it is true that where a plaintiff has a “fully matured cause of action for money” declaratory relief is unavailable (Jolley v. Chase Home Finance, LLC (2013) 213 Cal.App.4th 872, 909-10), Plaintiff’s breach of contract action does not provide an adequate remedy for the relief it seeks here. Plaintiff alleges a current dispute whether the lease was terminated under section 3.3, thus suspending its rent obligations. [Compl. ¶¶ 110-12.] Under section 3.3, Dunn cannot be liable for failure to deliver exclusive possession of the premises; only Plaintiff’s rent obligations are suspended. [Id. ¶ 22, Ex. 1, p. 3.] Thus, the breach of contract claim will not remedy this issue and the demurrer to this claim is overruled.

 

Plaintiff is granted 30 days’ leave to amend. Plaintiff may only add facts in support of the successfully challenged fraud-based claims. (Harris v. Wachovia Mortgage, FSB (2010) 185 Cal.App.4th 1018, 1023.) If Plaintiff declines or fails to file an amended complaint, Dunn shall answer the operative complaint within 10 days of the lapsed deadline. Any new causes of action outside the scope of the order may be addressed via a motion to strike.
