Tentative Ruling

Patricia Valencia v. Flow Health Services, LLC, et al., Case No. 23SMCV03060

Plaintiff’s Motion for Leave to Amend to File First Amended Complaint

Hearing Date: September 28, 2026

Background

On July 7, 2023, Plaintiff Patricia Valencia (“Valencia”) sued her former employer and supervisor, Defendants Flow Health Services LLC and Adonis Salazar (“Salazar”), for harassment under FEHA and intentional infliction of emotional distress. Valencia alleges that Defendants retaliated after she reported that Salazar made inappropriate advances at work and touched her without her consent. (Complaint, ¶ 14, 19, 22.)

On January 7, 2026, Valencia filed this Motion to Compel Salazar’s Deposition. Salazar has not filed opposition. On April 27, 2026, Valencia filed a notice of non-opposition. On May 1, 2026, Salazar filed a declaration in response to Valencia’s Motion to Compel.

On January 20, 2026, Flow Health Services filed a notice of Stay of Proceedings pending Bankruptcy.

On April 1, 2026, Plaintiff amended the complaint to name Flow Health Holdings, LLC as a Doe defendant.

On April 15, 2026, Plaintiff amended the complaint to name Flow Health Holdings, LLC and Alex Meshkin as Doe defendants.

On April 15, 2026, Flow Health Laboratories filed a notice of Stay of Proceedings pending bankruptcy.

On August 21, 2026, Flow Health Holdings filed a demurrer to the complaint.

On September 1, 2026, Plaintiff filed this Motion for Leave to Amend. On September 15, 2026, Flow Health Holdings filed opposition. On September 18, 2026, Plaintiff replied.

Arguments

Plaintiff seeks leave to amend to add additional Doe allegations identifying Flow Health Holdings, LLC, Flow Health Laboratories, LLC, Alex Meshkin, and Signal Diagnostics, LLC; to add and expand alter-ego, agency, successor, and fraudulent-transfer allegations; and to add a cause of action for fraudulent conveyance in violation of the California Uniform Voidable Transactions Act, Civil Code section 3439 et seq. (Motion for leave to amend, p. 3.)

In opposition to this motion, Flow Health Holdings argues that the Plaintiff’s declaration attached to the motion for leave to amend does not satisfy the requirements of Rule 3.1323(a) because it does not explain the delay in amending the complaint or explain changes to the complaint. (Opposition, pp. 5-6.) Flow Health Holdings also argues that the proposed first amended complaint’s FEHA claims may be deficient because the proposed FAC does not attach documents it purports to attach. (Id. at p.7.)

In reply, Plaintiff attaches a supplemental declaration explaining that the Flow Health Services’ January 2026 bankruptcy prompted her to begin investigating related entities and assets and add them to this action. Plaintiff argues that these facts are newly discovered and support the motion for leave to amend.

Motion for Leave to Amend

The trial court has discretion to allow amendments to pleadings in the furtherance of justice. (Code Civ. Proc. § 473, subd. (a)(1).) “Any judge, at any time before or after commencement of trial, in the furtherance of justice, and upon such terms as may be proper, may allow the amendment of any pleading.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 576.) “There is a policy of great liberality in permitting amendments to the pleadings at any stage of the proceeding.” (Berman v. Bromberg (1997) 56 Cal.App.4th 936, 945 [citations omitted].) “An application to amend a pleading is addressed to the trial judge’s sound discretion.”  (Ibid. [citations omitted].) A judge may deny a motion for leave to amend where a moving party has been dilatory in seeking leave to amend and such delay has prejudiced the opposing party. (Hirsa v. Superior Court (1981) 118 Cal.App.3d 486, 490 (“Hirsa”).) “It is difficult to understand how [a named defendant] can be prejudiced by amendment to add an additional theory of liability against it.” (Ibid.) 

“[I]t is an abuse of discretion to deny leave to amend where the opposing party [is] not misled or prejudiced by the amendment.” (Kittredge Sports Co. v. Superior Court (1989) 213 Cal.App.3d 1045, 1048 (“Kittredge”).) Prejudice exists where the amendment would require delaying the trial, resulting in loss of critical evidence, or added costs of preparation. (Solit v. Tokai Bank, Ltd. New York Branch (1999) 68 Cal.App.4th 1435, 1448 (“Solit”).)  

A motion for leave to amend must include a separate declaration specifying: “(1) The effect of the amendment; (2) Why the amendment is necessary and proper; (3) When the facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered; and (4) The reasons why the request for amendment was not made earlier.” (Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1324(b).) A motion for leave to amend must “[i]nclude a copy of the proposed amendment or amended pleading, which must be serially numbered to differentiate it from previous pleadings or amendments.” (Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1324(a)(1).) Moreover, a motion to amend must “[s]tate what allegations in the previous pleading are proposed” to be deleted or added, if any, and must set forth the page, paragraph, and line number of such allegations. (Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1324(a)(2)-(3).)  

Discussion

First, this action is not stayed against Flow Health Holdings because Flow Health Services and Flow Health Laboratories’ notices of deposition only stayed this action against those entities. A bankruptcy stay does not automatically stay proceedings against co-defendants not in bankruptcy. (Higgins v. Superior Court (2017) 15 Cal.App.5th 973, 981, fn. 14.)

Normally, the Court does not consider evidence submitted in reply. Here, however, Plaintiff’s initial motion for leave to amend described the circumstances that lead to this motion for leave to amend. The supplemental declaration provides additional details but does not materially change Plaintiff’s argument. With the additional information provided in the supplemental declaration, the motion satisfies Rule 3.1324’s requirements.

Plaintiff discovered the newly-named defendants and the new claim for fraud after Flow Health Services entered bankruptcy. Plaintiff promptly added the new defendants to this action. Plaintiff now seeks to elaborate on her allegations against the new defendants. Flow Health Holdings makes no argument that the amendment will prejudice it or that this motion was brought with undue delay. Additionally, this case is not yet at issue and is partially stayed. There is no prejudice to Defendants.

Flow Health Holdings also argues that the proposed FAC fails to state claims against it. The sufficiency of the FAC’s claims are issues better addressed on a demurrer.  

Conclusion

Plaintiff’s Motion for Leave to Amend is GRANTED.


Case Number: 25SMCV01569    Hearing Date: September 28, 2026    Dept: P

Tentative Ruling

Arian Eskandari v. General Motors, LLC Case No. 25SMCV01569

Plaintiff’s Motion for Attorneys’ Fees

Hearing Date: September 28, 2026

Background

This is a lemon law case. On March 21, 2023, Plaintiff Arian Eskandari sued Defendant General Motors, LLC for breach of implied and express warranties under the Song-Beverly Act. Eskandari alleged that GM failed to repair or replace his new 2023 Cadillac Escalade ESV. (Complaint, ¶ 6.)

On April 30, 2025, GM answered.

On October 31, 2025, Eskandari filed a notice of settlement of entire case.

On January 8, 2026, the Court dismissed this case.

On May 21, 2026, Eskandari filed a Motion for Attorneys’ Fees. On September 21, 2026, Eskandari filed a notice of non-opposition to the motion for attorneys’ fees.

Eskandari seeks $23,832.59 in attorneys’ fees and costs, consisting of (1) $21,290 in attorneys’ fees; (2) $1,041.59 in costs; and (3) $1,500 for reviewing opposition, preparing a reply, and attending the hearing. (Motion for Attorneys’ Fees, pp. 5-6.) Eskandari argues that he is entitled to fees under the Song-Beverly Act and under the § 998 compromise settlement agreement, and that the fees he requests are reasonable.

Motion for Attorneys’ Fees

 A prevailing buyer in an action for damages and other legal remedies should receive full compensation of reasonable sums expended in pursuit of their claims against manufacturers who failed to comply with the Song-Beverly Act. (Civ. Code § 1794(d).)

“A trial court assessing attorney fees begins with a touchstone or lodestar figure, based on the ‘careful compilation of the time spent and reasonable hourly compensation of each attorney ... involved in the presentation of the case.” (Christian Research Institute v. Alnor (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 1315, 1321.) “The reasonableness of attorney fees is within the discretion of the trial court, to be determined from a consideration of such factors as the nature of the litigation, the complexity of the issues, the experience and expertise of counsel and the amount of time involved. The court may also consider whether the amount requested is based upon unnecessary or duplicative work.” (Wilkerson v. Sullivan (2002) 99 Cal.App.4th 443, 448.) 

 

Analysis

Eskandari is entitled to recover attorneys’ fees as the prevailing party in an action under the Song-Beverly Act and pursuant to the terms of the § 998 compromise. (Civ. Code § 1794(d); Ryu Decl., Exhibit 6, ¶ 2.)  

a.      Lodestar Analysis

Eskandari requests fees for two senior litigation attorneys, Mr. Ryu and Ms. Shumake, at $500 per hour and $475 per hour respectively; one junior associate, Ms. Souto, at $350 per hour; one foreign-licensed attorney, Mr. Singla, at $400 per hour; three paralegals, Ms. Basilio, Ms. Carolyne Castillo and Ms. Martiza Castillo at $250, $200, and $125 per hour; a Case Resolution Manager, Ms. Pastor, at $200 per hour; and six legal assistants at $125.00 per hour. (Ryu Decl., ¶¶ 45-58.) Generally, staffing a case as simple as this one with fourteen individuals would be excessive. However, Eskandari’s records show that different attorneys, paralegals, and legal assistants worked on this matter sequentially, so multiple individuals were not billing for the same work at the same time. For example, Mr. Ryu and Ms. Shumake were the primary attorneys in this matter at the beginning. However, Mr. Ryu was later supported by Ms. Suoto and then by Mr. Singla. At any given time, there were only two attorneys working on this matter. The support staff’s entries are also not duplicative and show that different paralegals and legal assistants worked on the case at different times. Absent opposition, and following a careful review of the record, the Court finds Plaintiff’s staffing reasonable.

Eskandari’s counsel’s rates are also reasonable. Rates of $500, $475, $350, and $400 per hour are reasonable for attorneys of Mr. Ryu, Ms. Shumake, Ms. Suoto, and Mr. Singla’s experience and education working on a lemon law matter.

Following a review of Eskandari’s billing records, and absent opposition identifying unreasonable or duplicative billing, the Court finds Eskandari’s attormeys’ time billed on this matter to be reasonable.

Eskandari also requests costs of $1,041.59 (Ryu Decl., Exhibit 8.) Following a review of Eskandari’s records, the Court finds those costs reasonable.

Finally, Eskandari requests $1,500 for time spent reviewing opposition, preparing a reply, and appearing at the hearing on this motion. Since this motion is unopposed, the Court will only grant one hour of Mr. Ryu’s time for appearing at the hearing.  

Conclusion

Eskandari’s Motion for Attorneys’ Fees is GRANTED in the amount of $22,831.59 ($21,290 + $1,041.59 + $500).


Case Number: 26SMCV02381    Hearing Date: September 28, 2026    Dept: P

Tentative Ruling

Danny Suleminian v. PGB Holdings, LLC, et al., Case No. 26SMCV02381

PGB Holdings and Peter Pooya Bakhtiari’s Motion to Set Aside Default

Hearing Date: September 28, 2026

Background

Plaintiff Danny Suleminian sues Defendants PGB Holdings, Peter Pooya Bakhtiari, Sassan Construction, Inc., and Sassan Salehipour for negligence, private nuisance, trespass, and strict liability arising out of Defendants’ construction work on Suleminian’s neighbor’s property. (Complaint, ¶ 23.) Suleminian alleges that Defendants’ work caused structural damage to his property, including cracks in perimeter walls, stucco, and fencing. (Id. at ¶ 24.)

On April 28, 2026, Suleminian filed the complaint.

On August 6, 2026, Suleminian filed proof of service on all defendants and entered default judgment against all defendants.

On August 28, 2026, PGB Holdings and Peter Pooya Bakhtiari (collectively “PGB”) moved to set aside default.

PGB moves to set aside default based on excusable mistake or surprise. PGB argues that it received the complaint and summons on May 7, 2026, but was not able to obtain sufficient information to determine if service was code compliant from PGB or Suleminian. PGB argues that it delayed filing a response in case it needed to move to quash service, but received no response from Suleminian for months, until Suleminian suddenly filed proof of service and entered default on August 6, 2026. Defendants submit their proposed demurrer and motion to strike concurrently with this motion.

Legal Standard- Motion to Set Aside Default

CCP section 473(b) provides, in relevant part, that “the court shall, whenever an application for relief is made no more than six months after entry of judgment, is in proper form, and is accompanied by an attorney’s sworn affidavit attesting to his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect, vacate any (1) resulting default entered by the clerk against his or her client, and which will result in entry of a default judgment, or (2) resulting default judgment or dismissal entered against his or her client, unless the court finds that the default or dismissal was not in fact caused by the attorney’s mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect.”  (Id.)

Discussion

  1. Timeliness

PGB brought this motion on August 28, 2026, less than six months after Suleminian entered default on August 6, 2026. This motion is timely.

  1. Surprise

PGB submits evidence that it diligently contacted Suleminian’s counsel to obtain proof of service between the time it received the complaint and the time Suleminian filed the proof of service and entry of default with the Court. (Su Decl., ¶ 2.) PGB’s evidence shows that it did not make a general appearance because it believed service was improper. (Id. at ¶ 6.) The fact that Suleminian filed the proof of service and entered default simultaneously supports PGB’s contention that it was surprised by the entry of default, especially since it had been in communication with staff at Suleminian’s attorney’s office. (See Su Decl., Exhibit 1.)

This evidence is sufficient to show that PGB’s failure to respond timely was due to surprise. Suleminian’s entry of default is set aside.

Conclusion

PGB’s Motion to Set Aside Default is GRANTED.

PGB is to file a responsive pleading within ten (10) days.
