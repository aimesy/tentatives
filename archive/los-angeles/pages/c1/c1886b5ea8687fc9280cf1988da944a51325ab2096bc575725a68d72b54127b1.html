Case Number: 24STCV08682    Hearing Date: September 22, 2026    Dept: 414

24STCV08682 1500 W Highland, LLC, et al v. Oregon Trail Corporation, et al.

Tuesday, September 22, 2026

 

[TENTATIVE] ORDER SUSTAINING DEMURRER TO SECOND AMENDED COMPLAINT AND GRANTING THE SEPARATELY FILED MOTION TO STRIKE (3264)

 

I.          BACKGROUND

       The second amended complaint alleges that Defendant, Oregon Trail Corporation (“OTC”), through its agents, owners, and employees, Eric Mark Sr., Eric Mark, Jr., and Christopher Mark (“Mark Defendants”) (collectively, “Defendants”) issued three commercial loans to the Borrower LLCs, (1500 W. Highland, LLC and 15918 Merrill, LLC), secured by real property. Plaintiff, Jose Gonzalez, allegedly signed personal guaranties for each loan.

       Plaintiffs allege Defendants charged usurious interest rates and inflated origination fees. Plaintiffs allege claims for (1) contractual unconscionability, (2) unfair competition, (3) negligence per se, (4) violation of DRE Licensing Act, (5) failure to disclose, and (6) usury.

       On March 18, 2025, Plaintiffs voluntarily dismissed the LLC Borrowers leaving Jose Gonzalez, the alleged guarantor of some of the loans, as the only Plaintiff.

       On April 23, 2026, the Court granted the Mark Defendants and OTC’s motion for judgment on the pleadings. Plaintiffs filed a second amended complaint on May 26, 2026. The Mark Defendants and OTC (“Defendants”) demur to the First Cause of Action for Contractual Unconscionability, Second Cause of Action for Unfair Competition, Third Cause of Action for Negligence Per Se, Fifth Cause of Action for Failure to Disclose, and Sixth Cause of Action for Usury on grounds Plaintiff did not cure the defects identified by the Court when it granted Defendants’ motion for judgment on the pleadings.

       Defendants’ notice incorrectly identifies the Fifth Cause of Action for Failure to Disclose as the fourth cause of action and the Sixth Cause of Action for Usury as the fifth cause of action. The memorandum, however, correctly identifies and addresses those claims as the Fifth and Sixth Causes of Action and does not separately challenge the Fourth Cause of Action for Violation of the DRE Licensing Act. Although the reply addresses Plaintiff’s allegations concerning an allegedly unlicensed broker, origination fees, and violations of the Real Estate Law, those arguments do not expand the causes of action placed at issue by the demurrer. The Court therefore construes the demurrer as directed to the First, Second, Third, Fifth, and Sixth Causes of Action only. The Fourth Cause of Action is not before the Court on this demurrer.

II.         LEGAL STANDARDS

       A demurrer tests the sufficiency of a complaint as a matter of law and raises only questions of law. (Schmidt v. Foundation Health (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 1702, 1706.) In testing the complaint’s sufficiency, the court must assume the truth of the properly pleaded factual allegations as well as facts that can be reasonably inferred from those expressly pleaded facts. The court may also consider matters properly subject to judicial notice. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

       The court may not consider contentions, deductions, or conclusions of fact or law. (Moore v. Conliffe (1994) 7 Cal.4th 634, 638.) Plaintiff is required to allege facts sufficient to establish every element of each cause of action. (Rakestraw v. California Physicians Service (2000) 81 Cal.App.4th 39, 43.) Where the complaint fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action, courts should sustain the demurrer. Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10(e); Zelig v. County of Los Angeles (2002) 27 Cal.4th 1112, 1126.)

       Sufficient facts are the essential facts of the case stated, "with reasonable precision and with particularity that is sufficiently specific to acquaint the defendant with the nature, source, and extent of his cause of action.” (Gressley v. Williams (1961) 193 Cal.App.2d 636, 643-644.)  Whether the Plaintiff will be able to prove the pleaded facts is irrelevant. (Stevens v. Superior Court (1986) 180 Cal.App.3d 605, 609–610.)

       A demurrer may also be sustained if a complaint is “uncertain.” Uncertainty exists where a complaint’s factual allegations are so confusing, they do not sufficiently apprise a defendant of the issues it is being asked to meet. (Williams v. Beechnut Nutrition Corp. (1986) 185 Cal.App.3d 135, 139, fn. 2; Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10(f).)

       A pleading is required to assert general allegations of ultimate fact. Evidentiary facts are not required. (Quelimane Co. v. Stewart Title Guaranty Co. (1998) 19 Cal. 4th 26, 47; Lim v. The.TV Corp. Internat. (2002) 99 Cal. App. 4th 684, 690.)

III.        DISCUSSION

A.      Judicial Notice

       The court grants Defendants’ request for judicial notice of public records showing that the parcels of property that served as security for each of the loans, have been foreclosed upon and sold or reconveyed. (Evid. Code §452(c); Fontenot v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 256, 264 ["courts have taken judicial notice of the existence and recordation of real property records, including deeds of trust, when the authenticity of the documents is not challenged."].) Defendants argue that without an obligation, there is no viable claim.  

B.      Contractual Unconscionability

       Defendants argue that the borrowers on the loans for three parcels of real property securing the notes were the now dismissed LLCs. Gonzalez guaranteed those notes. The parcels of property at issue have been foreclosed upon via full credit bid, which extinguishes the loans and guarantees. This undermines the claim for rescission of the loan contracts.

       Plaintiff argues that the loan at issue is the mortgage secured by Mr. Gonzalez’s primary residence at 9365 Gallatin Road. He is the record title holder and personal borrower on the note and Deed of Trust and asserts usurious interest and unconscionability in reference to the personal mortgage. He contends this is his personal loan.

       However, the second amended complaint alleges that the loan at issue is the commercial loan taken out by Mark Defendants on a Florence Avenue home in Bell owned by Gonzalez. The loan required Gonzalez to sign under penalty of perjury that the Bell California home was not his principal residence, but rather Gonzalez’s true and principal residence was located at Gallatin Road in Downey. (SAC ¶ 14.) These allegations contradict Plaintiff’s opposition that the loan at issue is a personal mortgage secured by the Gallatin residence.

       Once a full credit bid in an amount equal to the unpaid principal and interest of the mortgage debt, with costs and fees is successfully made, resulting in acquisition of the property, the borrower “is released from further obligations under the defaulted note." (Kalnoki v. First American Trustee Servicing Solutions, LLC (2017) 8 Cal.App.5th 23, 45.)

       Gonzalez alleges he entered into a series of loans with OTC “directly and indirectly through LLCs he owned” and which loans he signed “as their manager.” (SAC ¶ 5.)  

       The defect remains that the borrowers were the now dismissed LLCs. A nonjudicial foreclosure sale constitutes a “final adjudication of the rights of the borrower and lender." (Kalnoki v. First American Trustee Servicing Solutions, LLC (2017) 8 Cal.App.5th 23, 49.) Even if Gonzalez was a borrower, the sale finally adjudicated his rights as borrower.

       The amendments to the complaint do not clarify what is at issue. Again, the loan at issue is the loan on the Bell property located at Florence Ave, which Plaintiff declared was not his principal residence, not a personal mortgage on his Gallatin home. (SAC ¶ 14.)  The second amended complaint seeks rescission of that loan because Gonzalez speaks and reads Spanish which impaired his ability to understand the documents provided to him. (SAC ¶ 5.)

       Plaintiff does not address the issue raised by Defendants that once the deed is reconveyed, as Defendants observe, there is no basis for rescission as the reconveyance eliminates that claim. Defendants contend that unconscionability-based rescission requires a live contract to unwind.

       Plaintiff cites De La Torre v. CashCall, Inc. (2018) 5 Cal.5th 966, which generally provides for a cause of action for unconscionability arising from excessive interest rates. (Id. at 990.) Plaintiff has not adequately addressed the issue of whether he is entitled to rescind a promissory note that has been reconveyed following a sale; instead, Plaintiff argues Defendants are addressing loans that are not at issue.

       As De La Torre explains, unconscionability requires a showing of facts showing oppression or surprise (procedural unconscionability) and facts showing that the contract was overly harsh or one-sided (substantive unconscionability.) (De La Torre at 982.)

       Plaintiff alleges he speaks and reads Spanish, that it would be obvious to Defendants that his ability to understand would be impaired, and the loan documents were presented as contracts of adhesion. (SAC ¶ 5.) None of these conclusory facts support the claim that the loan documents were unconscionable, procedurally or substantively. The defect remains.

C.      Unfair Competition 17200

       This claim alleges that Defendants’ conduct with respect to the loan at issue was usurious. (SAC ¶ 27.)

       Defendants contend correctly that this cause of action must be alleged with particularity. As defined by statute, “unfair competition” includes “any unlawful, unfair or fraudulent business act or practice.” (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 17200). Its purpose is to protect both consumers and competitors by promoting fair competition in commercial markets for goods and services.” (Gutierrez v. Carmax Auto Superstores California (2018) 19 Cal.App.5th 1234, 1265). A plaintiff alleging unfair business practices under the UCL must state with “reasonable particularity” the facts supporting the statutory elements of the violation. (Khoury v. Maly's of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 619 [Where Plaintiff did not identify the statutory scheme which was allegedly violated and the particular facts supporting the violation].

       Plaintiff argues he has alleged injury in fact, that the unlawful encumbrance of his primary residence, payment of usurious fees and the wrongful threat of foreclosure provide injury in fact. However, the alleged loan at issue was made on the Bell property, owned by Gonzales, but was not his principal residence. (SAC ¶ 14.)

       The claim remains fatally defective.

D.   Negligence Per Se

       This claim is based on an alleged breach of a fiduciary duty owed by Defendants by allowing unlicensed people to broker loans through a company. (SAC ¶ 32.) A negligence claim requires factual allegations showing that defendant owed plaintiff a duty of care, breach of that duty, causation and damages. (Merrill v. Navegar, Inc. (2001) 26 Cal.4th 465, 477; Jones v. Grewe (1987) 189 Cal. App. 3d 950, 954.) The absence of these allegations renders a complaint fatally defective and is properly challenged by demurrer. (Id.); (Hegyes v. Unjian Enterprises, Inc. (1991) 234 Cal.App.3d 1103.)  In addition to the lack of facts, inclusion of property that has been subsequently sold renders the operative pleading fatally uncertain.

       The pleading must allege facts supporting each element of a negligence claim  which are: “the defendant's duty to conform to a certain standard of care for the protection of others against unreasonable risks; (2) the defendant's breach of that duty; (3) a reasonably close connection between the defendant's conduct and the resulting injuries, otherwise known as proximate causation; and (4) actual loss suffered by the plaintiff. " ( E.I. v. El Segundo Unified School Dist. (2025) 111 Cal.App.5th 1267, 1285.) The cause of action is defective as alleged.   

       Plaintiff alleges that Eric Mark, Sr. through OTC broke the loan on the Bell property in December 2021. (SAC ¶ 21.) Plaintiff alleges that Sr. had a broker’s license but that it was revoked. The Department of Real Estate Records attached to the complaint refer to Eric Mark without distinguishing between Jr. and Sr. The pleading represents that Exhibit 1 is the revocation of Eric Mark Sr. “aka Jr.” Is Eric Mark one person or two different people?

       The operative pleading is incomprehensible and not supported by the exhibits attached to the complaint.  Exhibits attached to the complaint that contradict allegations take precedence. (Mead v. Sanwa Bank California (1998) 61 Cal.App.4th 561, 567–568 [“If the facts appearing in the attached exhibit contradict those expressly pleaded, those in the exhibit are given precedence.”].)

D.     Failure to Disclose

       This claim asserts one that Defendants intentionally failed to provide the proper disclosures on “the above loans” and loans disguised as commercial loans in order to avoid proper disclosure. (SAC ¶ 38.) Plaintiff “relied that Defendants were properly licensed.” (SAC ¶ 39.) Plaintiff relied “that” all requirements for the residential loan had been followed and disclosed. (SAC ¶ 39.)

       The claim remains fatally uncertain as to what loan is really at issue, brokered by which defendant (Sr. or Jr.) or what tort claim Plaintiff is attempting to allege, facts showing how Defendants owed a duty to disclose, and the claim lacks any specific identification of the “proper disclosures” at issue. 

E.      Usury

       This claim generally alleges that Defendants charged rates in excess of the required disclosures, or in some cases with no disclosure. The SAC is fatally uncertain as to what loan(s) are implicated and by which Mark defendant or which Mark defendant’s license was revoked and when.

       The loan alleged to be at issue was a commercial loan on the Bell property which Plaintiff declared was not his primary residence. The accusations attached to the second amended complaint concern Christopher Mark, who did not broker the Bell property loan.

       The claim remains fatally defective.   

F.    Motion to strike

       Defendants separately move to strike allegations claiming punitive damages. (SAC ¶ 41, 48, and the prayer for punitive damages. A plaintiff may recover on a claim for exemplary damages where the defendant is guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice. (Civ. Code, § 3294 subd. (a).) The predicate acts to support the claim must be intended to cause injury or must constitute “malicious” or “oppressive” conduct as defined by statute.

       “Malice” is defined as “conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.” (Civ. Code, § 3294 subd. (c)(1); College Hospital Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 725 ["malice involves awareness of dangerous consequences and a willful and deliberate failure to avoid them"].)

       "Oppression" is defined as “despicable conduct that subjects a person subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person's rights.”].) In other words, absent an intent to injure the plaintiff, the conduct must be “despicable” defined as “base, vile, or contemptible.” (College Hospital Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 724.)

       None of the claims at issue survive demurrer. Therefore, there are no facts to support the predicate acts of malice, fraud, or oppression to support recovery of punitive damages.

IV.        CONCLUSION

       Based on the foregoing, Defendants’ demurrer to the First, Second, Third, Fifth, and Sixth Causes of Action is SUSTAINED. Defendants did not demur to the Fourth Cause of Action for Violation of the DRE Licensing Act, which remains operative. The motion to strike is GRANTED. Plaintiff was previously given leave to amend, and none of the defects in the challenged causes of action were cured. Accordingly, leave to amend as to the First, Second, Third, Fifth, and Sixth Causes of Action is DENIED.

Case Number: 24STCV30256    Hearing Date: September 22, 2026    Dept: 414

24STCV30256 Enedino Espinoza v. Target Corporation, et al.

Tuesday, September 22, 2026

 

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING IN PART AND DENYING IN PART MOTION FOR JUDGMENT ON THE PLEADING BY DEFENDANTS, WATERMARK SECURITY GROUP, INC.; JAMES MCBRIDE; AND ADRIAN MOODY, TO PLAINTIFF’S COMPLAINT (RES. NO. 7358)

 

          I.          BACKGROUND

      The operative pleading is the first amended complaint filed on September 17, 2025, wherein Plaintiff, brings claims against Defendants as a consumer of services and products of Defendant Target Corporation (“Target”) who contracted with Plaintiff’s employee, Watermark Security Group, Inc. (“Watermark”). Plaintiff alleges he suffered injury on November 15, 2022, while providing security services at Target. A homeless man, David Franklin, attacked two customers with a butcher knife before being shot by the security guard (“the Franklin assault.”)

      Watermark allegedly discriminated and retaliated against Plaintiff and ultimately terminated Plaintiff’s employment. Defendant, Adrian Moody, was Watermark’s manager, while Defendant, James McBride, owned and managed Watermark.

      Plaintiff also alleges that he was a defendant in two underlying cases referred to as the “Buddenberg” and “Molina/Song” cases, wherein Defendants breached their duty to defend and indemnify Plaintiff, in two cases brought against Target and Watermark by Plaintiffs’ Molina and Song.

      Additionally, Plaintiff asserts claims as a consumer of Target’s services and products based on injuries sustained when he was subjected to an unsafe and hostile environment.

      On January 2, 2026, after taking the matter under submission, the Court sustained Target’s demurrer to the first amended complaint and granted the motion to strike without leave to amend. The Court ordered Target’s dismissal with prejudice on February 6, 2026.

II.         ARGUMENTS

      Watermark, McBride, and Moody (collectively “Watermark”) challenge all 18 causes of action first on grounds that some claims arising from the Molina/Song and Buddenburg actions are barred by claim preclusion since Watermark was a party to both actions and the cases were dismissed. Watermark independently challenges all 18 causes of action for failure to state a claim.

      Plaintiff argues the motion is untimely because it was filed after the FSC and trial dates. Defendant obtained ex parte relief on July 7, 2026, and the covering judge vacated the FSC and trial dates. Plaintiff renews his objections to that procedure and requests that the July 7 order be vacated.  Plaintiff argues that claim preclusion does not bar any of his claims, and each of the 18 causes of action are adequately alleged.

 

III.        LEGAL STANDARDS

      A motion for judgment on the pleadings performs the same function as a general demurrer and attacks only defects disclosed on the face of the pleadings or by matters subject to judicial notice. (Burnett v. Chimney Sweep (2004) 123 Cal.App.4th 1057, 1065 [“a motion for judgment on the pleadings, like a demurrer, raises only questions of law ... .”].)

      For purposes of this motion, all properly alleged material facts are deemed true as well as all facts that may be implied or inferred from those expressly alleged. (Fire Ins. Exchange v. Superior Court (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 446, 452.)

 

IV.        DISCUSSION

A.   Timeliness of the Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings

      Plaintiff is correct that a statutory motion for judgment on the pleadings must be made 30 days before the initial trial date. (Cal. Code Civ. Procedure § § 438.)  As Defendant’s reply makes clear, however, a non-statutory motion may be brought at any time, before trial, or even during trial. (Stoops v. Abbassi (2002) 100 Cal.App.4th 644, 650; Saltarelli & Steponovich v. Douglas (1995) 40 Cal.App.4th 1, 5 [a nonstatutory motion may be granted when the complaint fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action."].)

      Accordingly, this motion is properly before the Court. The order granting Defendants’ ex parte  relief on July 7, 2026, is not relevant to the timing of this motion.

 

B.   Effect of the Molina/Song Judgment

      In its primary aspect, res judicata, or “claim preclusion” prevents relitigation of the same cause of action in a second suit between the same parties or their privities where the claim was finally litigated. (DKN Holdings LLC v. Faerber (2015) 61 Cal.4th 813, 824.) Under the res judicata doctrine, if a plaintiff prevails in an action, the cause is merged into the judgment and may not be asserted in a subsequent lawsuit; a judgment for the defendant serves as a bar to further litigation of the same cause of action." (Mycogen Corp. v. Monsanto Co. (2002) 28 Cal.4th 888, 896–897.)

      Res judicata would bar Molina/Song’s claims in a subsequent action against Watermark, but not Plaintiff’s claims here.  Res judicata does not apply because Plaintiff was not a party in the Molina/Song actions. He did not assert the claims alleged here in the Molina/Song actions.

 

C.   Indemnity Claim Based on Molina/Song

      Although the Molina/Song judgment does not preclude Plaintiff’s claims, the third cause of action separately fails to state a claim for equitable indemnity. Plaintiff alleges Defendants were required to defend and indemnify him in Molina/Song and that he incurred costs, expenses, and attorney’s fees. (FAC ¶ 71.) But Plaintiff also alleges he was not a named defendant; he incurred costs only because he attended a deposition. (FAC 6:4.)

No claims were alleged against Plaintiff in Molina/Song that required defense or indemnification. Labor Code section 2802 applies to expenditures incurred as a consequence of an employee’s discharge of duties or obedience to the employer’s directions. Plaintiff’s participation as a deposition witness was not in discharge of his security-guard duties.

 

D.   Claim Preclusion Based on Espinoza I

      Watermark next argues that Plaintiff’s claims are barred because he could have asserted these claims in Case No. 23STCV19129 (Espinoza I) which arose from the same Franklin attack. The third amended complaint in Espinoza I alleged claims against Defendants, Target Corporation and Universal Protection Service, LP among others for intentional infliction of emotional distress, negligence, and assault. The Espinoza I TAC asserts Plaintiff was an employee of Watermark Security Group, “which is not a party to this complaint at the present time.” (Espinoza I TAC ¶ 1.) Watermark’s reply argues it does not need to have been a named Defendant in Espinoza I for claim preclusion to apply. [1] because of privity and the primary rights doctrine.

      Plaintiff alleged employment violations of FEHA against BOTH Target and Watermark in the 13th through 16th causes of action alleging FEHA and Labor Code violations for discrimination, harassment. Plaintiff alleges both Watermark and Target refused to permit Plaintiff to continue working at Target and discriminated against him. (FAC ¶ 127.)

      The Court granted Target’s motion with respect to these claims because they were based on the same facts, namely the Franklin attack and Target’s refusal to hire Plaintiff subsequent to those attacks. The FAC establishes that the FEHA claim against Target grew out of Target’s alleged treatment of Plaintiff as a result of the alleged disabilities caused by the attack.

      Watermark contends it was in privity with Target for purposes of Plaintiff’s specific FEHA and Labor Code claims asserted here in Espinoza II. “Privity” according to Defendant’s case authority is explained as follows:

"As applied to questions of preclusion, privity requires the sharing of ‘an identity or community of interest,” with ‘adequate representation’ of that interest in the first suit, and circumstances such that the nonparty ‘should reasonably have expected to be bound’ by the first suit. [Citation.] A nonparty alleged to be in privity must have an interest so similar to the party's interest that the party acted as the nonparty's ‘virtual representative’ in the first action.’ (Castillo v. Glenair, Inc. (2018) 23 Cal.App.5th 262, 276–277, 232 Cal.Rptr.3d 844.) ‘Put another way, privity, ‘as used in the context of res judicata or collateral estoppel, does not embrace relationships between persons or entities, but rather it deals with a person's relationship to the subject matter of the litigation.” (Atwell v. City of Rohnert Park (2018) 27 Cal.App.5th 692, 703.)

     

       A cause of action is comprised of a primary right, a corresponding duty, and a wrongful act in breach of that duty. (Franceschi v. Franchise Tax Bd. (2016) 1 Cal.App.5th 247, 258 ["The most salient characteristic of a primary right is that it is indivisible: the violation of a single primary right gives rise to but a single cause of action.”].) One injury gives rise to only one claim for relief, although there may be many forms of relief. (Id.) In other words, ‘[e]ven where there are multiple legal theories upon which recovery might be predicated, one injury gives rise to only one claim for relief.’” (Id.)

Watermark has established privity with Target as to the claims arising from the Franklin attack. Watermark and Target share the same interest in defeating claims based on the attack and the alleged refusal to employ Plaintiff because of resulting disabilities. Those interests are sufficiently aligned that Target acted as Watermark’s virtual representative in Espinoza I. (Atwell at 703.) Because the claims seek redress for the same alleged injury, they implicate the same primary right.

      Accordingly, the following causes of action assert the same primary right and are subject to claim preclusion. Although pleaded under different legal theories, each seeks redress for the same alleged injury: Plaintiff’s exposure to, and resulting injury from, the attack:

      6th Cause of Action – Negligence. Plaintiff alleges that Defendants failed to protect Plaintiff and thereby allowed Plaintiff to be victimized by the attack.

      7th Cause of Action – Assault. This claim arises directly from the attack and seeks recovery for the resulting injury.

      8th Cause of Action – Violation of Civil Code section 52.1. Plaintiff alleges that Defendants violated Plaintiff’s constitutional rights by permitting dangerous conditions on the premises and allowing Plaintiff to be victimized and threatened with unreasonable and unconsented-to force. (FAC ¶¶ 87, 98.)

      13th, 14th, and 15th Causes of Action – FEHA Claims. These claims allege discrimination and harassment arising from the same underlying events and seek recovery for injuries associated with the attack.

      16th Cause of Action – Workplace Dangers. This claim rests on allegations that Defendants exposed Plaintiff to workplace dangers arising from the attack.

The remaining causes of action assert different primary rights and are not barred by claim preclusion. Their sufficiency is addressed below.

E.   Sufficiency of the Individual Causes of Action

1. First Cause of Action – Indemnity in Buddenburg.  Denied.

      As a threshold matter, this argument relates to indemnity in the Buddenberg action, which is the subject of the first cause of action for indemnity (not the third cause of action which relates to Molina/Song). Plaintiff alleges that Watermark was obligated to defend and indemnify Plaintiff and failed to do so in violation of Labor Code § 2802. The Buddenberg plaintiff filed an action against Espinoza and others for physically attacking and assaulting Buddenberg on October 18, 2022. (RJN, Ex. 4, ¶2, ¶ 6.)

      Plaintiff filed a first amended cross-complaint against Target in the Buddenberg action alleging total and equitable indemnity as a first cause of action. (RJN, Ex. 4.) Espinoza alleges that if he is found to have contributed to Buddenberg’s injuries, then Cross-Defendants are liable in proportion to their liability. (RJN, Ex. 4 ¶ 43.) As cross-complainant, Plaintiff alleges that Target and others should be required to defend, indemnify, and hold Plaintiff harmless. (Id. at ¶ 44.)

      Watermark argues Plaintiff did not allege a factual basis for a duty owed to indemnify Plaintiff and Plaintiff does not allege damages with specificity. The Buddenberg action settled.

Labor Code section 2802 requires an employer to indemnify an employee for necessary expenditures or losses incurred in direct consequence of the discharge of the employee’s duties or obedience to the employer’s directions. Buddenberg alleged that Espinoza, acting as a security guard, assaulted him on October 18, 2022. Plaintiff therefore alleges that Watermark was required to defend, indemnify, and hold him harmless from the resulting claims and defense costs. (FAC ¶ 62.)

      The claim is adequately alleged. Defendant’s contention that it did not have a duty to file the cross-complaint on Espinoza’s behalf in Buddenberg is a legal conclusion.

2.   Second Cause of Action – Interference with Prospective Economic Advantage. GRANTED.

      To prevail on a claim for intentional interference with prospective economic advantage, plaintiff must prove "(1) the existence, between the plaintiff and some third party, of an economic relationship that contains the probability of future economic benefit to the plaintiff; (2) the defendant's knowledge of the relationship; (3) intentionally wrongful acts designed to disrupt the relationship; (4) actual disruption of the relationship; and (5) economic harm proximately caused by the defendant's action.” (Roy Allan Slurry Seal, Inc. v. American Asphalt South, Inc. (2017) 2 Cal.5th 505, 512.) Additionally, the plaintiff must show that that defendant’s conduct was “wrongful by some legal measure other than the fact of interference itself.” (Ixchel Pharma, LLC v. Biogen, Inc. (2020) 266 Cal.Rptr.3d 665.) An act is independently wrongful if it is unlawful, ie., "it is proscribed by some constitutional, statutory, regulatory, common law, or other determinable legal standard.” (Id. at 1142.)

      Plaintiff argues that the independently unlawful conduct was Watermark’s demand that Plaintiff waive his right to recover defense costs and damages incurred in Buddenberg. Plaintiff argues the interference with Plaintiff’s ability to obtain reimbursement and recovery

in connection with the Buddenberg litigation and Cross-Complaint and alleges that Defendants

conditioned settlement on dismissal of Plaintiff’s Cross-Complaint. This is not a relationship with a third person. What Plaintiff appears to be alleging is that Defendant interfered with his statutory right to indemnification under the Labor Code. The alleged facts do not support the claim.

3.   Third Cause of Action – Equitable Indemnity in Molina/Song. GRANTED.

For the reasons stated in Section IV.C, the third cause of action fails to state a claim for equitable indemnity.

4.   Fourth Cause of Action – Violation of Labor Code Section 1102.5. DENIED.

      This code section bars retaliation against an employee for disclosing information that an employer violated a state or federal statute. (Lab. Code, § 1102.5.) This is known as the so-called whistleblower statute that prohibits an employer’s retaliation for disclosing information to parties "if the employee has reasonable cause to believe that the information discloses a violation of state or federal statute, or a violation of or noncompliance with a local, state, or federal rule or regulation, regardless of whether disclosing the information is part of the employee's job duties.” (§ 1102.5, subd. (b).) Subdivision (c) prohibits retaliation against an employee “for refusing to participate in an activity that would result in a violation of state or federal statute, or a violation of or noncompliance with a local, state, or federal rule or regulation.” (Nejadian v. County of Los Angeles (2019) 40 Cal.App.5th 703, 718.)

      Defendant contends Plaintiff must specifically allege that statutes, rules, or regulations allegedly violated. There is no legal basis cited for this requirement. Plaintiff alleges that he testified truthfully in Molina/Song about Defendants’ misconduct and provided a declaration that was used in the cases that Defendants were responsible for dangerous conduct that deprived him of workplace safety. (FAC ¶ 128.R.) Plaintiff subsequently alleges he was terminated after Defendant received that information.

      This claim is adequately alleged.

5.   Fifth Cause of Action – Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress. DENIED.

      To prevail on this claim, plaintiff must allege facts showing (1) extreme and outrageous conduct with the intention of causing, or reckless disregard of the probability of causing emotional distress, and (2) plaintiff suffered severe or extreme emotional distress as a proximate result. (Christensen v. Superior Court (1991) 54 Cal.3d 868, 903.)

      Conduct is “extreme and outrageous” where a defendant (1) abuses a relation or position which gives him power to damage the plaintiff’s interest; (2) knows the plaintiff is susceptible to injuries through mental distress; or (3) acts intentionally or unreasonably with the recognition that the acts are likely to result in illness through mental distress.” (Agarwal v. Johnson (1979) 25 Cal.3d 932, 946.) If the court concludes that reasonable minds may differ as to whether the conduct rises to the level of “extreme and outrageous conduct,” then “it is for the jury, subject to the control for the court, to determine whether, in the particular case, the conduct has been sufficiently extreme and outrageous to result in liability.” (Alcorn v. Anbro Engineering, Inc. (1970) 2 Cal.3d 493, 499.)  

      To the extent bases this claim on emotional distress suffered arising from the whistle-blower claim, the allegations are sufficient.

6.   Sixth Through Eighth Causes of Action. GRANTED.

As discussed above, the sixth through eighth causes of action are barred by claim preclusion because they seek redress for the same primary right adjudicated in Espinoza I. Defendant also asserts that these claims are barred by workers’ compensation exclusivity. Plaintiff invokes the fraudulent-concealment exception under Labor Code section 3602(b)(2). The Court need not resolve that additional issue because the claims are independently barred by claim preclusion.

7.   Ninth Cause of Action – Violation of Business and Professions Code Section 17200. DENIED.

      Defendants argue this is uncertain because Plaintiff did not specify which prong of what statute Plaintiff relies upon to support the “unlawful conduct.”

      As defined by statute, “unfair competition” includes “any unlawful, unfair or fraudulent business act or practice.” (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 17200). Its purpose is to protect both consumers and competitors by promoting fair competition in commercial markets for goods and services.” (Gutierrez v. Carmax Auto Superstores California (2018) 19 Cal.App.5th 1234, 1265).

The UCL is stated in the disjunctive; a plaintiff need allege only an unlawful, unfair, or fraudulent practice. A practice may be unfair or deceptive even if it is not independently unlawful. (Cel-Tech Communications, Inc. v. Los Angeles Cellular Telephone Co. (1999) 20 Cal.4th 163, 180.) Section 17200 also borrows violations of other laws and treats them as unlawful practices, while permitting a practice to be deemed unfair even if no other law specifically proscribes it. (Id. at 180–181.)

      Plaintiff has alleged violations under the Labor Code that survive the motion and support the “unlawful” practice required in a UCL claim.  

8.   Tenth Cause of Action – Breach of Express and Implied Contract. DENIED.

      Defendant argues that the allegation that “Defendants expressly promised Plaintiff’s employment would continue as long as he performed satisfactorily and obeyed all reasonable and lawful directions, rules and regulations of his employment” is devoid of specificity to support the existence of a contractual misunderstanding regarding defense, indemnity or any other specific contractual term.

      However, Plaintiff alleges that an oral contract existed pursuant to which Plaintiff agreed to work for Defendant WATERMARK and Defendant WATERMARK agreed to treat Plaintiff fairly, to provide a safe workplace and not to terminate him without good cause.” (FAC ¶ 105.) Defendant ignores these allegations and requests specificity with regard to any agreement to indemnify, which is not implicated in this cause of action.

9.   Eleventh Cause of Action – Breach of the Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing. GRANTED.

      Defendant argues that this “rehashes” the same verbal contract alleged in the 10th cause of action. Defendant contends this is superfluous as it seeks damages and other relief already claimed in a companion contract cause of action.

      The prerequisite for any action for breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing is the existence of a contractual relationship between the parties, since the covenant is an implied term in the contract." (Smith v. City and County of San Francisco (1990) 225 Cal.App.3d 38, 49.)  To prevail on a claim for breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, the plaintiff must first establish “the existence of a contractual relationship between the parties, since the covenant is an implied term in the contract. Secondly, benefits due under the contract must have been withheld, and the reason for withholding benefits must have been unreasonable or without proper cause. (Love v. Fire Ins. Exchange (1990) 221 Cal.App.3d 1136, 1151.)

      This claim is not “superfluous” of the contract claim because it seeks tort damages for the alleged unreasonable withholding of contract benefits. However, Defendant is correct that tort damages in a non-insurance contract are not recoverable. (Cates Construction, Inc. v. Talbot Partners (1999) 21 Cal.4th 28, 43–44 ("In the insurance policy setting, an insured may recover damages not otherwise available in a contract action, such as emotional distress damages resulting from the insurer's bad faith conduct. … "At present, this court recognizes only one exception to that general rule: tort remedies are available for a breach of the covenant in cases involving insurance policies."].)

10. Twelfth Cause of Action – Wrongful Termination in Violation of Public Policy. DENIED.

      This claim asserts multiple bases. Defendant essentially argues that Plaintiff did not allege the statute which supports the policy allegedly violated. To allege a claim for wrongful termination in violation of public policy, Plaintiff must allege facts to support the following elements: (1) the employee engaged in a protected activity; (2) the employer subjected the employee to an adverse employment action, and (3) a causal link existed between the protected activity and the employer's action. (Loggins v. Kaiser Permanente Internat. (2007) 151 Cal.App.4th 1102, 1109.) The type of public policy at issue "must involve a matter that affects society at large rather than a purely personal or proprietary interest of the plaintiff or employer and must be not only ‘fundamental’ and ‘substantial,’ but also ‘well established’ at the time of the discharge." (Stevenson v. Superior Court (1997) 16 Cal.4th 880, 889.) There are four categories of protected employee conduct: "(1) refusing to violate a statute [citations]; (2) performing a statutory obligation [citation]; (3) exercising a statutory right or privilege [citation]; and (4) reporting an alleged violation of a statute of public importance [citations].” (Id.) The public policy supporting a wrongful discharge claim must be supported by either constitutional or statutory provisions, and it must inure to the public’s benefit. (Id. at 890.)

      This claim arises in part based on Defendant’s firing of Plaintiff because he gave testimony in Molina/Song and is proper to that extent. Demurrer does not lie to a part of a cause of action. (Kong v. City of Hawaiian Gardens Redevelopment Agency (2002) 108 Cal.App.4th 1028, 1047 ["a demurrer cannot rightfully be sustained to part of a cause of action or to a particular type of damage or remedy.”].)

11. Thirteenth Through Sixteenth Causes of Action – FEHA Disability Discrimination. GRANTED.

      The Court previously discussed why these claims are precluded by the primary rights doctrine and privity.

12. Seventeenth Cause of Action – Violation of Labor Code Section 232.5. DENIED.

      This statute prohibits an employer to restrict an employee from disclosing information about the employer’s working conditions. Defendant argues that Plaintiff failed to identify what third party Plaintiff made any disclosure to. Plaintiff allegedly testified in the Molina/Song action.  This claim is adequately supported. Plaintiff alleges he was retaliated against by termination from employment for giving this testimony.

13. Eighteenth Cause of Action – Violation of Labor Code Section 1198.5. DENIED.

      This claim asserts Defendant failed to give Plaintiff his personnel and wage records in violation of the Labor Code. Defendant argues that Plaintiff did not plead statutory requirements that he submitted a request that was either: (i) written and submitted by the current or former employee or his or her representative; or (ii) written and submitted by the current or former employee or his or her representative by completing an employer-provided form. The statute does not mention “employer provided forms.” The statute only requires a written request and does not specific what particular forms are required.

V.   CONCLUSION

      Based on the foregoing, Defendants’ motion for judgment on the pleadings is GRANTED IN PART and DENIED IN PART. The motion is GRANTED as to the 6th, 7th, 8th, 13th, 14th, 15th, and 16th causes of action on grounds of claim preclusion, privity, and the primary-right doctrine. The motion is also GRANTED for failure to state a claim as to the 2nd cause of action for interference with prospective economic advantage; 3rd cause of action for equitable indemnity arising from Molina/Song; and 11th cause of action for breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing. In all other respects, including as to the 1st, 4th, 5th, 9th, 10th, 12th, 17th, and 18th causes of action, the motion is DENIED.

24STCV33906 Yolanda Islas v. General Motors, LLC

Tuesday, September 22, 2026

 

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING PLAINTIFF’S MOTION FOR ATTORNEY’S FEES (7041)

 

I.            BACKGROUND

       The complaint alleges that Defendant breached an express warranty issued in connection with Plaintiff’s purchase of a 2020 Chevrolet Malibu. On April 10, 2026, Plaintiff filed a notice of settlement. Plaintiffs now move for attorney’s fees of $27,089 in attorney’s fees, a .3 enhancement of $8,126.70, and costs of $773.30.

       Defendant argues Plaintiff uses template complaints and discovery, and “rote” meet and confer letters. This case is identical to other Song Beverly Consumer Warranty Act cases. Plaintiff performed unnecessary and duplicative tasks. The enhancement is unjustified. Defendant contends that the fee should be reduced by at least $18,100 for a total of award of $8,989 in fees and zero in costs.

II.   LEGAL STANDARDS

       A party prevailing on a Song-Beverly claim shall be allowed to recover fees, costs, and expenses as part of the judgment. (Civ. Code, § 1794 (d).) The court applies the lodestar method to determine a reasonable fee award, which requires a calculation of the number of hours reasonably spent multiplied by a reasonable hourly rate. The latter is based on the prevailing community rate for similar work. (Graciano v. Robinson Ford Sales, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 140.)

       Defendant does not dispute that Plaintiff is the prevailing party.

III.  DISCUSSION          

BILLER

       Plaintiff’s counsel’s requested hourly rates are supported by other minute orders obtained in other cases. The Court finds that the rates are reasonable in these types of cases based on the Court’s own experience.

       The action was filed on December 20, 2024, and settled approximately one year and four months later. The parties did not engage in any discovery motions. Mr. McCabe spent approximately 4.3 hours drafting the fee motion and related cost matters. The bulk of litigation was conducted by Mr. Khoubian and a paralegal who charged $250 per hour.

       Defendant’s contention that the “flat fee” of $3,500 for various communications with the client and other matters incurred before the complaint was filed is not reasonably incurred. Block billing occurs when counsel assigns a single block of time to multiple tasks without itemizing the time attributable to each task. (Mountjoy v. Bank of America, N.A. (2016) 245 Cal.App.4th 266, 279, citing Heritage Pacific Financial, LLC v. Monroy (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 972, 1010.) Although block billing does not, standing alone, require the wholesale rejection of a fee request, the Court may reduce or exclude fees where the manner in which the time is recorded prevents the Court from determining whether the hours claimed were reasonably incurred. (Mountjoy, supra, 245 Cal.App.4th at pp. 279–281.) Here, the challenged entry combines multiple tasks into a single undifferentiated time entry without identifying the amount of time devoted to each task. The Court therefore cannot determine what portion of the claimed time was reasonably incurred for compensable work or segregate that time from work for which fees are not recoverable. Because the deficiency prevents the Court from making a reasonable allocation as to this particular entry, the Court excludes the amount claimed for the entry in its entirety.

       As Defendant argues, it accounts for 13 percent of the total fees and it is not shown to be actually incurred. Plaintiff’s counsel is entitled to reasonable compensation; however, “‘padding” in the form of inefficient or duplicative efforts is not subject to compensation." (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132.)

       Counsel incurred 7.3 hours to prepare roughly 12 meet and confer letters regarding various issues. These consist of sending, receiving and reviewing emails. The Court has discretion to reduce requested fees where name partners were doing work that could have been done by lower-billing attorneys or paralegals (Morris v. Hyundai Motor America (2019) 41 Cal.App.5th 24, 41.) Therefore, the Court applies the paralegal’s $250/hour rate x 7.3 hours a reduction of $512.50 dollars.

       Counsel argues that eight hours to review discovery is outrageous given Defendant’s discovery requests are templates. Defendant contends these are not novel issues. Counsel requests that one hour be allotted and not 8 hours. The argument has merit. The Court reduces the discovery fee request by $1,750.00.

       Defense argues that 2.4 hours to draft a full assessment of litigation trial/strategy and to finalize draft repair technology, contending they are not billable to the client, and should not be billed to GM. There is no basis for the contention that these hours are not billable. Plaintiff’s counsel is entitled to fees reasonably incurred.

       Plaintiff spent four hours to draft discovery consisting of form and special interrogatories, requests for admission and document requests, which Defendant also argues are derived from templates. The argument has merit and the Court allows 2 hours of work prepared by the paralegal and for review by Mr. Khoubian and reduces the $1,227.50 request by $500.00.

       Defendant argues 6.8 hours at $2,025 for excessive miscellaneous correspondence. The Court has reviewed the billing records and find that many of these “receipt and review” or “confirm” are duplicative, for example, confirming mediation 11 times and reviewing document production numerous times. The Court reduces the request by 3.8 hours for a total reduction of  $950.00.

       The “unspecified declaration” referred to by defense counsel was in response to the Court’s order in support of the request for discovery conference. The fee request is reasonable.

       The Court reduces the time spent to prepare this fee motion and reply to five hours (instead of 13.4 hours at Mr. McCabe’s rate of $430.00, totaling $5,762). This results in a reduction of $3,612.00.

       Defendant’s remaining proposed reductions are without merit and are denied.

       Plaintiff’s request for an enhancement is denied. The court may consider the following factors to augment or diminish the lodestar: (1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, (4) the contingent nature of the fee award. (Id. [“the court determines, retrospectively, whether the litigation involved a contingent risk or required extraordinary legal skill justifying augmentation of the unadorned lodestar in order to approximate the fair market rate for such services.”].)

       Plaintiff has not shown that case was so complex to warrant a multiplier.        

IV.  CONCLUSION

       Accordingly, the Court reduces the total fee request of $27,089 by $10,824.50 leaving a total approved fee request of $16,264.50. The Court permits costs as requested by Plaintiff of $773.30.

25STCV36998 Lang, et al v. Peterson

Tuesday, September 22, 2026

 

[TENTATIVE] ORDER ON PLAINTIFF’S MOTION TO ADOPT APPRAISED VALUE OF $759,000 UNDER THE PARTITION OF REAL PROPERTY ACT

 

I.          BACKGROUND

       The verified complaint seeks partition of a single-family residence, in which Plaintiff has a 50 percent undivided interest with Defendant as tenants-in-common. A verified appraisal was filed by the court-appointed appraiser of $759,000. No objection has been filed.

 

II.         DISCUSSION

       The Partition of Real Property Act (“PRP”) which applies to actions filed on or after January 1, 2023, "applies to real property held in tenancy in common where there is no agreement in a record binding all the cotenants which governs the partition of the property.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 874.311. At trial, the court shall determine whether the Plaintiff has the right to partition, which is a matter of right as to concurrent interests in the property unless barred by a valid waiver. (Code Civ. Proc., § 872.710.) The court “shall upon adequate proof” determine the state of the title to the property. (Code Civ. Proc., § 872.620.)

       Plaintiff owns an undivided 50 percent interest in the real property at issue. (Talkov decl., Ex. 3.) Defendant was granted a 50 percent interest of the property on May 7, 2024. (Talkov decl., Ex. 2). Plaintiff's counsel declares that an agreement governing the partition of the property was not provided. (Talkov decl., ¶ 2.)          

       Fair market value “shall” be determined by the court except as otherwise provided. (Code Civ. Proc., § 874.316.) Those exceptions are if the cotenants have agreed to value and method of valuation; or if the court determines after an evidentiary hearing "that the evidentiary value of an appraisal is outweighed by the cost of the appraisal.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 874.316.)  

III.        CONCLUSION

       The Court has appointed an appraiser, who has filed a verified appraisal. No objection has been filed. Accordingly, the Court will conduct a hearing to "determine the fair market value of the property” by considering the appraisal and any other evidence of value offered by a party. (Code Civ. Proc., § 874.316.)
