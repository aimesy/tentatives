I.                Background

 

On October 11, 2024, Plaintiffs Antony and Janice Ward filed their complaint for personal injury alleging Mr. Ward developed malignant mesothelioma from direct exposure to asbestos-contaminated talcum powder products. On November 15, 2024, Plaintiffs dismissed their claims for punitive damages against Walgreens and CVS. Ms. Ward was dismissed from this action on May 13, 2025.

On February 5, 2026, Defendant CVS Pharmacy, Inc. (“CVS” or “Defendant”) moved for summary judgment or summary adjudication, arguing that Plaintiff lacks and cannot reasonably obtain evidence that he was exposed to any asbestos-containing product manufactured, distributed by CVS.

On July 10, 2026, Plaintiff opposed, arguing his evidence raises triable issues of material fact regarding Mr. Ward’s exposure to asbestos from talc supplied by CVS.

On July 17, 2026, CVS replied, arguing Plaintiff’s evidence fails to raise a triable issue of material fact to defeat summary judgment.

CVS meets its initial burden to prove that Plaintiff’s factually devoid discovery responses support an inference that they lack and cannot reasonably obtain evidence to support his contention that Mr. Ward was exposed to an asbestos-contaminated product for which it was responsible. Plaintiff presented sufficient evidence in opposition to raise a triable issue of material fact over whether the CVS foot and body powders Mr. Ward used were contaminated with asbestos. Accordingly, the motion for summary judgment is denied.

CVS’s motion for summary adjudication fails to completely dispose of the negligence cause of action. CVS’s motion for summary adjudication is denied.

11. Declaration of Antony Ward In Opposition to Defendants’ Motions for Summary Judgment, Or, In the Alternative, Summary Adjudication in its entirety.

CVS objects to Mr. Ward’s declaration on the grounds that it lacks foundation, is irrelevant, and is prejudicial. Again, this objection fails to direct any objection to any particular statement in the declaration.

CVS also objects on the grounds that the declaration runs afoul of the rule articulated in D'Amico v. Board of Medical Examiners (1974) 11 Cal.3d 1. The D’Amico rule comes into play when a party makes statements in a declaration opposing summary judgment that contradicts earlier discovery responses or deposition testimony. When a party’s admission “is obtained not in the normal course of human activities and affairs but in the context of an established pretrial procedure whose purpose is to elicit facts,” this admission “is entitled to and should receive a kind of deference not normally accorded evidentiary allegations in affidavits.” (D’Amico, supra, 11 Cal.3d at p.23.) In other words, a party may not create an issue of fact on summary judgment by a declaration which contradicts prior deposition testimony unless the contradiction is explained. (Preach v. Monter Rainbow (1993) 12 Cal.App.4th 1441, 1451 [“In determining whether any triable issue of material fact exists, the trial court may, in its discretion, give great weight to admissions made in deposition and disregard contradictory and self-serving affidavits of the party.”]; see also Niederer v. Ferreira (1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 1485, 1502.)

Here, Mr. Ward’s declaration stating he “can unequivocally attest” that he purchased “many bottles of CVS-brand body powder products” between 2005 and 2016 does not contradict his earlier deposition testimony. (Ward Decl. at ¶3.) Mr. Ward previously testified that he used CVS foot and body powder between 1987 and 2023. (Stock Decl., Exh. A at 285:19-24, 290:2-16 [“Q: And you started using the CVS foot powder in 1987; correct? A: Yes. Q: When did you last use CVS store brand foot powder? A: 23…Q:…You also started using the CVS body powder in 1987, correct? A: That is correct. Q: Did you also last use a CVS body powder in 2023? A: That is correct.”].) Even if the later declaration describes a narrower range of time, 2005 and 2016, this statement does not contradict Mr. Ward’s earlier testimony. Accordingly, the D’Amico rule does not apply here. The objection is overruled.

III.           Discussion

 

A.    Legal Standard

 

A defendant seeking summary judgment must “conclusively negate[] a necessary element of the plaintiff’s case, or . . . demonstrate[] that under no hypothesis is there a material issue of fact that requires the process of trial.”  (Guz v. Bechtel Nat. Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 317, 334.)  To show that a plaintiff cannot establish an element of a cause of action, a defendant must make the initial showing “that the plaintiff does not possess, and cannot reasonably obtain, needed evidence.”  (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 854.)  “The defendant may, but need not, present evidence that conclusively negates an element of the plaintiff’s cause of action.  The defendant may also present evidence that the plaintiff does not possess, and cannot reasonably obtain, needed evidence – as through admissions by the plaintiff following extensive discovery to the effect that he has discovered nothing.”  (Id. at p. 855.) “ ‘If plaintiffs respond to comprehensive interrogatories seeking all known facts with boilerplate answers that restate their allegations, or simply provide laundry lists of people and/or documents, the burden of production will almost certainly be shifted to them once defendants move for summary judgment and properly present plaintiff’s factually devoid discovery responses.’ ”  (Weber v. John Crane, Inc. (2006) 143 Cal.App.4th 1433, 1440.)

B.    Causation

In asbestos litigation, a plaintiff is required to show causation. A plaintiff must show “some threshold exposure to the defendant's defective asbestos-containing products” and must further establish in “reasonable medical probability” that a particular exposure or series of exposures was a “legal cause” of his injury, i.e., a substantial factor in bringing about the injury. (Rutherford v. Owens-Illinois, Inc. (1997) 16 Cal.4th 953, 982.)  “[T]he proper analysis is to ask whether the plaintiff has proven exposure to a defendant's product, of whatever duration, so that exposure is a possible factor in causing the disease and then to evaluate whether the exposure was a substantial factor.” (Lineaweaver v. Plant Insulation Co. (1995) 31 Cal.App.4th 1409, 1416.

Where asbestos was not an intended ingredient in a talc product, plaintiffs bear the additional burden of showing that it is “more likely than not that the talc product was contaminated with asbestos during the time the plaintiff used it.” (LAOSD Asbestos Cases (2020) 44 Cal.App.5th 475, 489 [hereinafter “Gibbons”].) Evidence sufficient to support this finding includes “long term use of a talcum powder product alleged to contain asbestos by a mesothelioma sufferer who was not exposed to any other known source of asbestos above background asbestos levels that are ever-present in the environment, together with expert testimony reporting positive test results for the presence of asbestos in ore from the sources used to manufacture the product.” (Strobel v. Johnson & Johnson (2021) 70 Cal.App.5th 796, 815.) “In the absence of evidence explaining how asbestos in the source ore would have been eliminated in the process of mining and then milling talc, that is enough to support more than a mere possibility that the accused product… was a substantial factor in causing [plaintiff] to develop mesothelioma.” (Ibid.)

CVS argues that it is entitled to summary judgment because (1) Plaintiff’s factually devoid discovery responses support an inference that Plaintiff lacks and cannot reasonably obtain evidence that he was exposed to an asbestos-containing product sold by CVS and (2) Plaintiff can only speculate that his exposure to a CVS product was a substantial factor in causing his mesothelioma.

1.     Defendant’s Burden: Factually Devoid

CVS argues that Mr. Ward has no proof that he purchased asbestos-contaminated products from CVS because he failed to provide specific testimony of the quantities of products he purchased from CVS, has no documents evidencing his purchases from CVS, and had no personal knowledge of whether the products he purchased were contaminated with asbestos. (Motion at pp.14-18 [“Critically, Plaintiff’s written discovery responses fail to set forth any specific facts demonstrating that he was exposed to asbestos as a result of using Powder Products purchased from CVS…As an initial matter, none of Plaintiff’s responses provide any information specific to CVS, such as identifying any employees, specific instances where Plaintiff or any other person purchased any Powder Products from CVS, any specific stores where he allegedly purchased any Powder Product from CVS, or any company documents…Plaintiff fails to actually identify in his discovery responses any specific ‘generic’ or ‘store brand’ talcum powder products which he contends that he obtained from CVS, and likewise failed to specifically identify any such products at deposition…Nor does Plaintiff have any containers of any Powder Products allegedly purchased from CVS, to which he was purportedly exposed…With no bottles of said products, there can be no testing of products from CVS to which Plaintiff was allegedly exposed, and thus, Plaintiff lacks evidence that any of the said products from CVS contained asbestos.”].)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that they have evidence that CVS’s talc suppliers sourced their talc from mines that were contaminated with asbestos. (Opp. at pp.15-16 [“This affirmative evidence demonstrates the existence of a triable issue of fact as to whether Plaintiff was exposed to asbestos from CVS supplied talcum powder products and that such exposures were sufficient to increase his risk of developing mesothelioma. Given Plaintiff’s evidentiary showing, the Court cannot conclude that, as a matter of law, based upon the evidence before it, triable issues of material fact do not exist. Accordingly, CVS’ request for summary judgment must be denied.”].)

In reply, CVS argues that Plaintiff’s evidence fails to establish that the particular products Mr. Ward purchased from CVS was contaminated with asbestos. (Reply at p.5 [“Rather than identifying admissible evidence that the particular powder products allegedly purchased by Mr. Ward from CVS—whether CVS-branded or national-brand products—contained asbestos, Plaintiff relies on generalized expert opinions concerning historical talc suppliers, mine geology, historical testing, and other products. Those opinions do not substitute for product-specific evidence.”].)

                                                    i.     Analysis

CVS moves for summary judgment on the grounds that Plaintiff lacks and cannot reasonably obtain evidence to support their contention that Mr. Ward was exposed to asbestos by an asbestos-contaminated product for which CVS was responsible. Specifically, CVS argues that Plaintiff’s factually devoid discovery responses support an inference that they lack and cannot reasonably obtain evidence to support their claims against it. When CVS asked Plaintiff to state “all EVIDENCE that supports [his] contention that any TALCUM POWDER PRODUCT, sold by CVS and to which [he was] EXPOSED, contained asbestos,” Plaintiff responded by referring to a list of CVS’s own records. (White Decl., Exh. 12 at pp.4-20.) When CVS asked Plaintiff to “IDENTIFY all TALCUM POWDER PRODUCTS that [they] claim were obtained from CVS and EXPOSED [Mr. Ward] to asbestos,” Plaintiff responded as follows:

“Mr. Ward was an avid runner since he moved to the United States in 1987. He ran for fitness and in competition. He ran every Los Angeles Marathon starting in 2009, including the 2017 race in the pouring rain. He used foot powder products daily to prevent and cure athletes foot, and to keep his feet dry and comfortable. Mr. Ward regularly inhaled asbestos dust from these products, contributing to his development of mesothelioma. He used and purchased from Defendant CVS CO., the following brands of foot powder: Desenex, Dr. Scholls, Gold Bond and CVS’s store brands and/or generic foot powders.

Defendant was aware that the level of asbestos contamination contained in its talcum powder was hazardous to human health. The source of this information is found in the medical and scientific literature available to CVS, as well as CVS’s corporate documents, which are already in propounding Defendant’s possession. In addition, despite the knowledge demonstrated in its corporate documents and/or by its employees’ testimony, CVS failed in all respects to adequately warn Plaintiff Antony Ward or other consumers or users and/or bystanders of the significant hazards inherent with such products.”

(White Decl., Exh. 12 at pp.21-22.) The remainder of the response states that CVS knew its products contained asbestos and failed to warn the public of the risks of asbestos exposure, a decision motivated by financial interest. (Id. at p.22.) Plaintiff incorporated his answers to interrogatories 1 and 2 for the remainder of CVS’s interrogatories. (Id. at pp.23-28.) Plaintiff’s response to form interrogatory 17.1 identifies Mr. Ward, his family members, and friends, as witnesses who may have evidence to support their claims. (Id., Exh. 13 at pp.6-7.) Plaintiff also identified CVS’s person most qualified, expert witnesses, and witnesses named in Mr. Ward’s medical and employment records. (Id.)

CVS meets its initial burden to prove that Plaintiff lacks and cannot reasonably obtain evidence that Mr. Ward was exposed to an asbestos-contaminated product for which it was responsible. Because Plaintiff alleges that the body and foot powders at issue were made with talc contaminated with asbestos, Plaintiff’s discovery responses were required to set forth facts from which the court can reasonably infer that it was “more likely than not that the talc product was contaminated with asbestos during the time” that Mr. Ward used it. (Strobel, supra, 70 Cal.App.5th at p.815.)

Here, Plaintiff’s responses to an interrogatory seeking all facts supporting his contention that Mr. Ward was exposed to asbestos-contaminated talcum powder products supplied by CVS failed to state the factual basis for his contention that the body and foot powders Mr. Ward used were contaminated with asbestos. (White Decl., Exh. 12 at pp.21-22.) Although Plaintiff identified his family members and friends as witnesses with knowledge to support his claims, there is no evidence that Plaintiff’s family members would have personal knowledge of whether the foot and body powders at issue were contaminated with asbestos. (Id., Exh. 13 at pp.6-7.) The discovery record contains no evidence showing that the products Mr. Ward used were contaminated with asbestos. Plaintiff’s factually devoid discovery responses support an inference that he lacks and cannot reasonably obtain evidence to support his claims against CVS. Accordingly, CVS meets its initial burden on summary judgment. The burden shifts to Plaintiff.

Dr. Krekeler opines based on his assumptions as to the talc sources used in the CVS products at issue; his “critical review and analysis of the scientific literature, internal/historical test results, contemporary test results and information on the mining and milling processes involved”; and his “education, training and experience,” Dr. Krekeler opines that to a reasonable degree of scientific certainty that CVS and Walgreens-branded body powder products manufactured between 2005 and 2016 and CVS-branded consumer foot powder products from 2008 to 2012…more likely than not contained asbestos and consumers using such products would have been exposed to asbestos.” (Id. at ¶26.)

In the report, Dr. Krekeler notes that generally, “talc mines in Montana are located in one of three mountain ranges in the southwestern region of the state. These are the Ruby Range, the Greenhorn Range, and the Gravelly Range. Major talc mines in Montana include the Willow Creek Mine (The Greenhorn Range), the Regal Mine, The Yellowstone Mine (Gravelly Range), the functionally co-located Treasure Mine, Treasure Chest Mine, and Beaverhead Mine (Ruby Range).” (Krekeler Decl., Exh. 2, p. 75.) Dr. Krekeler relies on historical testing of a large swathe of mines in Montana and provides a chart reflecting the test results. (Id. at pp.174-207.) The test results show that each of the mines tested in each of three mountain ranges where talc is mined contained some amount of asbestos fiber. (Id.) Relying on these test results and studies conducted regarding asbestos exposure to workers in the Beaverhead, Treasure, Willow Creek, and Yellowstone mines, Dr. Krekeler opines that “given the geological properties of these materials, and the geology of” these mines, it is “more probable than not that persons who used or were exposed to talc product derived from these mines” were exposed to asbestos. (Id. at p.127.) Dr. Krekeler summarizes his opinions from his report as follows:

“1.) A mature body of scientific literature exists on talc and commensurately a mature body of literature that clearly shows the association of talc and asbestos minerals in a wide range of geologic conditions also exists. Talc deposits and associated lithologies in Montana experienced conditions that would enable the formation of asbestos and related non-asbestiform minerals. Large quantities of talc have been mined from Montana since the 1940s making Montana talc a significant source.

2.) A body of government, peer-reviewed, and company literature reviewed thus far provide context for evaluating the geology of the deposits. Companies had access to this and could have developed basic geologic models to assess that talc deposits could have had asbestos or countable particles (e.g., the IWGACP December 2021 White Paper).

3.) It is scientifically much more probable than not, that asbestos occurred in talc ores from the Beaverhead, Treasure, Regal, Willow Creek, and Yellowstone Mines as well as other talc mines in Montana. The common forms of asbestos or countable particles (e.g., IWGACP December 2021 White Paper) are, or are expected to be, chrysotile and tremolite. However, forms of asbestos or countable particles of actinolite, amosite, anthophyllite, and crocidolite asbestos could be present in localized environments in talc ore, and/or could be or likely to very likely would be, derived from some associated lithologies in and around the mines. It is scientifically much more probable than not that lithologies (e.g., amphibolites, schists, mafic dikes, iron formation) adjacent to talc units in the deposits and mines contributed countable particles (e.g., IWGACP December 2021 White Paper) to talc materials through the commonly used processes of drilling, blasting, digging, White Paper) to talc materials through the commonly used processes of drilling, blasting, digging, transporting and storing ore. Evidence exists that some companies at least partially understand asbestos contributions from adjacent lithologies (e.g., The Regal Mine).

4.) A body of testing indicates findings for asbestos in multiple mines over a period of decades.

5.) Flawed testing (e.g., powder X-ray diffraction) has been used extensively and this method of analysis does not have an adequate detection limit for the purpose of asbestos analysis. The observed records indicate that a large amount of testing was not done in a scientifically meaningful way.

6.) Based on the geological properties of these materials, It is more probable than not, that workers exposed to talc materials at and generated from Montana talc mines were exposed to asbestos.

7.) Based on the geological properties of these materials, it is more probable than not, that people or consumers exposed to talc materials at and generated from Montana talc mines were exposed to asbestos.”

(Krekeler Decl., Exh. 3, p.149.)

            Dr. Krekeler’s declaration assumes that the CVS body and foot powders contained “Microtalc BP-210 from Barretts Minerals” (Krekeler Decl. at ¶2.) Plaintiff’s evidence shows that Vi-Jon was using Microtalc BP-210 in its CVS body and foot powders by 2014. (Stock Decl., Exh. C at 262:2-22.) Dr. Krekeler’s declaration and incorporated report fail to state that Microtalc BP-210 came from an asbestos-contaminated mine. Dr. Krekeler’s report concludes that talc ores from “Beaverhead, Treasure, Regal, Willow Creek, and Yellowstone Mines as well as other talc mines in Montana” are contaminated with asbestos. (Krekeler Decl., Exh., p.2.) However, nothing in Dr. Krekeler’s 200-page report, Dr. Krekeler’s declaration, or the discovery record identifies where Barretts sourced the talc used in Microtalc BP-210. Although Dr. Krekeler alleges Barretts operates the Regal Mine, the Treasure State Mine, and the Barretts Mill in Montana, these facts are case-specific hearsay which Dr. Krekeler may not relay to the court. (See Sanchez, supra, 63 Cal.4th at pp.685-686.) CVS properly objected to Dr. Krekeler’s declaration on the grounds that it relays case-specific hearsay.

            Dr. Krekeler’s report is, however, sufficient to support an inference that the CVS brand body and foot powders Mr. Ward purchased were more likely than not contaminated with asbestos because the report shows that most of the major mines in the mountain ranges where talc is mined in Montana are contaminated with asbestos. Thus, the report supports an inference that any talc sourced from Montana is more likely than not contaminated with asbestos. This inference combined with Plaintiff’s evidence that CVS’s body and foot powders came from talc suppliers using Montana talc between 1999 and 2016 leads to an inference that the CVS body and foot powders Mr. Ward purchased at this time were also contaminated with asbestos.

            Plaintiff also argues that his expert, Mark Bailey, confirms that Mr. Ward was exposed to asbestos from the branded products he purchased at CVS. (PAMF No.2, Stock Decl., Exh. F.) Mr. Bailey testified at his deposition that he issued analytical reports of testing he performed on 11 bottles of Gold Bond brand foot powder in 2025. (Stock Decl., Exh. F at 16:21-4.) Mr. Bailey did not detect asbestos in five of the 11 bottles tested. (Id. at p.17:12-17.) Mr. Bailey did not test Desenex products. (Id. at 18:5-13.) Like Dr. Krekeler, Mr. Bailey performed testing on the Beaverhead and Regal mines in Montana. (Id. at 18:15-22, 95:8-20.) Mr. Bailey also performed testing on bottles of Dr. Scholls foot powder and found that one bottle had chrysotile asbestos fibers and three bottles had amphibole asbestos fibers out of six bottles of the foot powder. (Id. at 94:22-95:7, 98:21-99:4.)

            Mr. Bailey’s deposition and report show that he performed testing on bottles of Gold Bond and Dr. Scholls foot powder. However, there is no evidence that the bottles tested contained the same talc that Mr. Ward was exposed to when he used these products between 1987 and 2023. Plaintiff provides no evidence of where the products tested came from, what year they were made, or any other details that would support an inference that the products Mr. Ward used were more likely than not contaminated with asbestos. Although Mr. Bailey alleges that the talc used in Gold Bond, Desenex, and Dr. Scholls foot powder came from the Beaverhead and Regal mines in Montana, these are case-specific facts. As CVS points out in their objections to Dr. Haber’s declaration, Mr. Bailey may not relate case-specific facts to the court. (CVS’s objection numbers 3, 7.) Accordingly, Mr. Bailey’s report is insufficient to support an inference that it was more likely than not that the brand name talcum powder products Mr. Ward purchased from CVS were contaminated with asbestos.

            Plaintiff provides sufficient evidence to support an inference that the CVS body and foot powders Mr. Ward purchased between 1999 and 2016 were more likely than not contaminated with asbestos. Plaintiff therefore meets his burden on summary judgment. The motion for summary judgment is denied on this ground.

3.     Substantial Factor

 

CVS next argues that “even if Plaintiff could establish that the Powder Products that were allegedly purchased from a CVS store were somehow contaminated with asbestos—and CVS maintains that Plaintiff cannot—Plaintiff’s claims against CVS would still fail because he cannot demonstrate that these likely non-existent particular exposures were a substantial factor in causing his mesothelioma.” (Motion at p.20.) Specifically, CVS argues that Plaintiff’s theory that his exposure to a CVS product was a substantial factor in causing Mr. Ward’s cancer is speculative because Plaintiff assumes that “some amount of talc at some time may have contained asbestos, Plaintiff supposes that some of the talc incorporated into the Powder Products may have contained asbestos, and further speculate that some of the containers of the unknown number of these products that Plaintiff may or may not have purchased from CVS (as opposed to other sellers) contained the supposedly contaminated talc.” (Id. at p.20, emphasis in original.)

The relevant standard to determine whether Plaintiff presents sufficient evidence to create a triable issue of material fact over whether an asbestos-contaminated talc product was a substantial factor in causing his injuries is set out in Strobel. In Strobel the court held: “long term use of a talcum powder product alleged to contain asbestos by a mesothelioma sufferer who was not exposed to any other known source of asbestos above background asbestos levels that are ever-present in the environment, together with expert testimony reporting positive test results for the presence of asbestos in ore from the sources used to manufacture the product.” (Strobel, supra, 70 Cal.App.5th 796, 815.)

Here, it is undisputed that Mr. Ward used talcum powder products, including CVS body and foot powders, continuously for over three decades between 1987 and 2023. Plaintiff’s evidence that Mr. Ward purchased body and foot powders from CVS is not speculative because he provides deposition testimony and a declaration establishing that he purchased those products from CVS. (Stock Decl., Exh. A at 284:23-285:18; Ward Decl. at ¶¶2-4.) As discussed, Plaintiff’s evidence that CVS products were manufactured using asbestos-contaminated talc is not speculative because CVS’s manufacturers, Vi-Jon and Premier Brands, testified that they used talc sourced from mines in Montana. (Stock Decl., Exh. C at 104:2-18; Exh. E at 14:1-15:15, 35:12-36:24, 41:2-8.) The expert declaration and report of Dr. Krekeler show that the major mines in the regions of Montana where talc is mined are contaminated with asbestos, leading to an inference that the talc products made with ore from Montana are also contaminated with asbestos. (Krekeler Decl., Exh. 3, p.149, 174-207.) Plaintiff’s evidence meets the standard articulated in Strobel because it is undisputed that Mr. Ward used talcum powder products long term and there is expert testimony reporting positive asbestos test results for talc ores from the mines where the talc was sourced. Accordingly, Plaintiff’s evidence is sufficient to support an inference that the CVS body and foot powders Mr. Ward used was a substantial factor in causing his cancer.

As for CVS’s argument that Plaintiff cannot prove Mr. Ward’s cancer was caused by CVS products as opposed to other sellers, Plaintiff is not required to pinpoint the exact asbestos sources that caused Mr. Ward’s cancer. As the California Supreme Court explained in Rutherford v. Owens-Illinois, Inc. (1997) 16 Cal.4th 953, 975, which CVS cites, “asbestos plaintiffs… ‘are not required to identify the manufacturer of specific fibers’ that caused the cancer…Plaintiffs cannot be expected to prove the scientifically unknown details of carcinogenesis, or trace the unknowable path of a given asbestos fiber.” (Rutherford, supra, 16 Cal.4th at p.976, emphasis in original.) Rather, “plaintiffs may prove causation in asbestos-related cancer cases by demonstrating that the plaintiff's exposure to defendant's asbestos-containing product in reasonable medical probability was a substantial factor in contributing to the aggregate dose of asbestos the plaintiff or decedent inhaled or ingested, and hence to the risk of developing asbestos-related cancer, without the need to demonstrate that fibers from the defendant's particular product were the ones, or among the ones, that actually produced the malignant growth.” (Id.) Here, Plaintiff is not required to show that the CVS talcum powder products caused Mr. Ward’s cancer as opposed to other sellers. As discussed, Plaintiff’s evidence meets the standard articulated in Strobel and supports an inference that CVS’s talcum powder products were asbestos-contaminated and that they contributed to Mr. Ward’s risk of developing cancer. Accordingly, CVS’s argument is without merit and the motion for summary judgment is denied.

C.    Motion for Summary Adjudication

CVS moves for summary adjudication of the cause of action for negligence on the grounds that CVS did not have a duty to test or inspect the powder products it sold. (Motion at pp.22-23.) In opposition, Plaintiff argues that CVS mischaracterizes his negligence claim. Rather, “Plaintiff alleges that CVS sold foot and body powders containing asbestos — a known human carcinogen — and failed to exercise ordinary care with respect to a hazard it knew or should have known about, including by failing to warn users or recall/retrofit such products.” (Opp. at p.17.)

The complaint alleges that all Defendants, including CVS, “negligently and carelessly researched, tested or failed to test, warned or failed to warn, failed to recall or retrofit, failed to provide protective measures to prevent take home exposure, failed to warn of the dangers to those who come in contact with users, manufactured and/or caused to be manufactured, fabricated, designed, developed, labeled, assembled, distributed, supplied, leased, bought, offered for sale, sold, inspected, serviced, repaired, installed, demolished, contracted for installation, contracted others to install, repaired, marketed, warranted, rebranded, manufactured for others, packaged and advertised asbestos and/or products containing asbestos and/or products which created an asbestos hazard, and/or safety equipment…” (Compl. at ¶14.) As Plaintiff points out in opposition, summary adjudication is only appropriate where the motion “completely disposes of a cause of action, an affirmative defense, a claim for damages, or an issue of duty.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 437c, subd. (f)(1).) Here, CVS addresses one of Plaintiff’s theories of negligence and fails to address the others. The motion thus fails to dispose of the entire cause of action. Accordingly, the motion for summary adjudication is denied.

IV.           Conclusion

CVS meets its initial burden to prove that Plaintiff’s factually devoid discovery responses support an inference that they lack and cannot reasonably obtain evidence to support his contention that Mr. Ward was exposed to an asbestos-contaminated product for which it was responsible. Plaintiff presented sufficient evidence in opposition to raise a triable issue of material fact over whether the CVS foot and body powders Mr. Ward used were contaminated with asbestos. Accordingly, the motion for summary judgment is denied.

CVS’s motion for summary adjudication fails to completely dispose of the negligence cause of action. CVS’s motion for summary adjudication is denied.

III.           Discussion

 

A.    Legal Standard

 

A defendant seeking summary judgment must “conclusively negate[] a necessary element of the plaintiff’s case, or . . . demonstrate[] that under no hypothesis is there a material issue of fact that requires the process of trial.”  (Guz v. Bechtel Nat. Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 317, 334.)  To show that a plaintiff cannot establish an element of a cause of action, a defendant must make the initial showing “that the plaintiff does not possess, and cannot reasonably obtain, needed evidence.”  (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 854.)  “The defendant may, but need not, present evidence that conclusively negates an element of the plaintiff’s cause of action.  The defendant may also present evidence that the plaintiff does not possess, and cannot reasonably obtain, needed evidence – as through admissions by the plaintiff following extensive discovery to the effect that he has discovered nothing.”  (Id. at p. 855.) “ ‘If plaintiffs respond to comprehensive interrogatories seeking all known facts with boilerplate answers that restate their allegations, or simply provide laundry lists of people and/or documents, the burden of production will almost certainly be shifted to them once defendants move for summary judgment and properly present plaintiff’s factually devoid discovery responses.’ ”  (Weber v. John Crane, Inc. (2006) 143 Cal.App.4th 1433, 1440.)

B.    Causation

In asbestos litigation, a plaintiff is required to show causation. A plaintiff must show “some threshold exposure to the defendant's defective asbestos-containing products” and must further establish in “reasonable medical probability” that a particular exposure or series of exposures was a “legal cause” of his injury, i.e., a substantial factor in bringing about the injury. (Rutherford v. Owens-Illinois, Inc. (1997) 16 Cal.4th 953, 982.)  “[T]he proper analysis is to ask whether the plaintiff has proven exposure to a defendant's product, of whatever duration, so that exposure is a possible factor in causing the disease and then to evaluate whether the exposure was a substantial factor.” (Lineaweaver v. Plant Insulation Co. (1995) 31 Cal.App.4th 1409, 1416.

Where asbestos was not an intended ingredient in a talc product, plaintiffs bear the additional burden of showing that it is “more likely than not that the talc product was contaminated with asbestos during the time the plaintiff used it.” (LAOSD Asbestos Cases (2020) 44 Cal.App.5th 475, 489 [hereinafter “Gibbons”].) Evidence sufficient to support this finding includes “long term use of a talcum powder product alleged to contain asbestos by a mesothelioma sufferer who was not exposed to any other known source of asbestos above background asbestos levels that are ever-present in the environment, together with expert testimony reporting positive test results for the presence of asbestos in ore from the sources used to manufacture the product.” (Strobel v. Johnson & Johnson (2021) 70 Cal.App.5th 796, 815.) “In the absence of evidence explaining how asbestos in the source ore would have been eliminated in the process of mining and then milling talc, that is enough to support more than a mere possibility that the accused product… was a substantial factor in causing [plaintiff] to develop mesothelioma.” (Ibid.)

Walgreen argues that it is entitled to summary judgment because (1) Plaintiff’s factually devoid discovery responses support an inference that Plaintiff lacks and cannot reasonably obtain evidence that he was exposed to an asbestos-containing product sold by Walgreens and (2) Plaintiff can only speculate that his exposure to a Walgreen product was a substantial factor in causing his mesothelioma.

1.     Defendant’s Burden: Factually Devoid

Walgreen argues that Mr. Ward has no proof that he purchased asbestos-contaminated products from Walgreen because he failed to provide specific testimony of the quantities of products he purchased from Walgreen, has no documents evidencing his purchases from Walgreen, and had no personal knowledge of whether the products he purchased were contaminated with asbestos. (Motion at pp.14-18 [“Critically, Plaintiff’s written discovery responses fail to set forth any specific facts demonstrating that he was exposed to asbestos as a result of using Powder Products purchased from Walgreens…As an initial matter, none of Plaintiff’s responses provide any information specific to Walgreens, such as identifying any employees, specific instances where Plaintiff or any other person purchased any Powder Products from Walgreens, any specific stores where he allegedly purchased any Powder Product from Walgreens, or any company documents…Plaintiff fails to actually identify in his discovery responses any specific ‘generic’ or ‘store brand’ talcum powder products which he contends that he obtained from Walgreens, and likewise failed to specifically identify any such products at deposition…Nor does Plaintiff have any containers of any Powder Products allegedly purchased from Walgreens, to which he was purportedly exposed…With no bottles of said products, there can be no testing of products from Walgreens to which Plaintiff was allegedly exposed, and thus, Plaintiff lacks evidence that any of the said products from Walgreens contained asbestos.”].)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that they have evidence that Walgreens’s talc suppliers sourced their talc from mines that were contaminated with asbestos. (Opp. at pp.15-16 [“This affirmative evidence demonstrates the existence of a triable issue of fact as to whether Plaintiff was exposed to asbestos from Walgreens supplied talcum powder products and that such exposures were sufficient to increase his risk of developing mesothelioma. Given Plaintiff’s evidentiary showing, the Court cannot conclude that, as a matter of law, based upon the evidence before it, triable issues of material fact do not exist. Accordingly, Walgreens’ request for summary judgment must be denied.”].)

In reply, Walgreens argues that Plaintiff’s evidence fails to establish that the particular products Mr. Ward purchased from Walgreens was contaminated with asbestos. (Reply at p.5 [“Rather than identifying admissible evidence that the particular powder products allegedly purchased by Mr. Ward from Walgreens—whether Walgreens-branded or national-brand products—contained asbestos, Plaintiff relies on generalized expert opinions concerning historical talc suppliers, mine geology, historical testing, and other products. Those opinions do not substitute for product-specific evidence.”].)

                                                    i.     Analysis

Walgreens moves for summary judgment on the grounds that Plaintiff lacks and cannot reasonably obtain evidence to support their contention that Mr. Ward was exposed to asbestos by an asbestos-contaminated product for which Walgreens was responsible. Specifically, Walgreens argues that Plaintiff’s factually devoid discovery responses support an inference that they lack and cannot reasonably obtain evidence to support their claims against it. When Walgreens asked Plaintiff to state “all EVIDENCE that supports [his] contention that any TALCUM POWDER PRODUCT, sold by WALGREEN and to which [he was] EXPOSED, contained asbestos,” Plaintiff responded as follows:

“Mr. Ward was an avid runner since he moved to the United States in 1987. He ran for fitness and in competition. He ran every Los Angeles Marathon starting in 2009, including the 2017 race in the pouring rain. He used foot powder products daily to prevent and cure athletes foot, and to keep his feet dry and comfortable. Mr. Ward regularly inhaled asbestos dust from these products, contributing to his development of mesothelioma. The remainder of the response states that Walgreens knew its products contained asbestos and failed to warn the public of the risks of asbestos exposure, a decision motivated by financial interest. (Id.) Plaintiff incorporated his answers to interrogatory number 1 for the remainder of Walgreens’s interrogatories. (Id. at pp.10-17.) Plaintiff’s response to form interrogatory 17.1 identifies Mr. Ward, his family members, and friends, as witnesses who may have evidence to support their claims. (Id., Exh. 13 at pp.11-13.) Walgreens meets its initial burden to prove that Plaintiff lacks and cannot reasonably obtain evidence that Mr. Ward was exposed to an asbestos-contaminated product for which it was responsible. Because Plaintiff alleges that the body and foot powders at issue were made with talc contaminated with asbestos, Plaintiff’s discovery responses were required to set forth facts from which the court can reasonably infer that it was “more likely than not that the talc product was contaminated with asbestos during the time” that Mr. Ward used it. (Strobel, supra, 70 Cal.App.5th at p.815.)

Here, Plaintiff’s responses to an interrogatory seeking all facts supporting his contention that Mr. Ward was exposed to asbestos-contaminated talcum powder products supplied by Walgreens failed to state the factual basis for his contention that the body and foot powders Mr. Ward used were contaminated with asbestos. (White Decl., Exh. 12 at pp.4-10.) Although Plaintiff identified his family members and friends as witnesses with knowledge to support his claims, there is no evidence that Plaintiff’s family members would have personal knowledge of whether the foot and body powders at issue were contaminated with asbestos. (Id., Exh. 13 at pp.11-13.) The discovery record contains no evidence showing that the products Mr. Ward used were contaminated with asbestos. Plaintiff’s factually devoid discovery responses support an inference that he lacks and cannot reasonably obtain evidence to support his claims against Walgreens. Accordingly, Walgreens meets its initial burden on summary judgment. The burden shifts to Plaintiff.

Dr. Krekeler’s report concludes that talc ores from “Beaverhead, Treasure, Regal, Willow Creek, and Yellowstone Mines as well as other talc mines in Montana” are contaminated with asbestos. (Krekeler Decl., Exh., p.2.) However, nothing in Dr. Krekeler’s 200-page report, Dr. Krekeler’s declaration, or the discovery record identifies where Barretts sourced the talc used in Microtalc BP-210. Although Dr. Krekeler’s declaration omits his conclusions as to the Walgreens foot powder, Ms. Murray testified that Vi-Jon used the same talc in all of the talcum powder products it made for its retail customers. (Stock Decl., Exh. C at 97:1-9.) Thus, the report supports an inference that any talc sourced from Montana is more likely than not contaminated with asbestos. This inference combined with Plaintiff’s evidence that Walgreens’s body and foot powders came from talc suppliers using Montana talc between 2002 and 2016 leads to an inference that the Walgreens body and foot powders Mr. Ward purchased at this time were also contaminated with asbestos.

Mr. Bailey testified at his deposition that he issued analytical reports of testing he performed on 11 bottles of Gold Bond brand foot powder in 2025. (Stock Decl., Exh. F at 16:21-4.) Mr. Bailey did not detect asbestos in five of the 11 bottles tested. (Id. at p.17:12-17.) Mr. Bailey did not test Desenex products. (Id. at 18:5-13.) (Id. at 94:22-95:7, 98:21-99:4.)

However, there is no evidence that the bottles tested contained the same talc that Mr. Ward was exposed to when he used these products between 1987 and 2023. Plaintiff provides no evidence of where the products tested came from, what year they were made, or any other details that would support an inference that the products Mr. Ward used were more likely than not contaminated with asbestos. Although Mr. Bailey alleges that the talc used in Gold Bond, Desenex, and Dr. Scholls foot powder came from the Beaverhead and Regal mines in Montana, these are case-specific facts. As Walgreens points out in its objections to Dr. Haber’s declaration, Mr. Bailey may not relate case-specific facts to the court. (Walgreens’s objection numbers 3, 7.) Accordingly, Mr. Bailey’s report is insufficient to support an inference that it was more likely than not that the brand name talcum powder products Mr. Ward purchased from Walgreens were contaminated with asbestos.

            Plaintiff provides sufficient evidence to support an inference that the Walgreens body and foot powders Mr. Ward purchased between 2002 and 2016 were more likely than not contaminated with asbestos. Plaintiff therefore meets his burden on summary judgment. The motion for summary judgment is denied.

3.     Substantial Factor

 

Walgreens next argues that “even if Plaintiff could establish that the Powder Products that were allegedly purchased from a Walgreens store were somehow contaminated with asbestos—and Walgreens maintains that Plaintiff cannot—Plaintiff’s claims against Walgreens would still fail because he cannot demonstrate that these likely non-existent particular exposures were a substantial factor in causing his mesothelioma.” (Motion at p.20.) Specifically, Walgreens argues that Plaintiff’s theory that his exposure to a Walgreens product was a substantial factor in causing Mr. Ward’s cancer is speculative because Plaintiff assumes that “some amount of talc at some time may have contained asbestos, Plaintiff supposes that some of the talc incorporated into the Powder Products may have contained asbestos, and further speculate that some of the containers of the unknown number of these products that Plaintiff may or may not have purchased from Walgreens (as opposed to other sellers) contained the supposedly contaminated talc.” (Id. at p.20, emphasis in original.)

The relevant standard to determine whether Plaintiff presents sufficient evidence to create a triable issue of material fact over whether an asbestos-contaminated talc product was a substantial factor in causing his injuries is set out in Strobel. In Strobel the court held: “long term use of a talcum powder product alleged to contain asbestos by a mesothelioma sufferer who was not exposed to any other known source of asbestos above background asbestos levels that are ever-present in the environment, together with expert testimony reporting positive test results for the presence of asbestos in ore from the sources used to manufacture the product.” (Strobel, supra, 70 Cal.App.5th 796, 815.)

Here, it is undisputed that Mr. Ward used talcum powder products, including Walgreens body and foot powders, continuously for over three decades between 1987 and 2023. Plaintiff’s evidence that Mr. Ward purchased body and foot powders from Walgreens is not speculative because he provides deposition testimony and a declaration establishing that he purchased those products from Walgreens. (Stock Decl., Exh. A at 48:18-22; Ward Decl. at ¶¶2-4.) As discussed, Plaintiff’s evidence that Walgreens products were manufactured using asbestos-contaminated talc is not speculative because Walgreens’s manufacturer, Vi-Jon, represented that it used talc sourced from mines in Montana, among other sources. (Stock Decl., Exh. C at 104:2-18; Exh. E at 14:1-15:15, 35:12-36:24, 41:2-8.) The expert declaration and report of Dr. Krekeler show that the major mines in the regions of Montana where talc is mined are contaminated with asbestos, leading to an inference that the talc products made with ore from Montana are also contaminated with asbestos. (Krekeler Decl., Exh. 3, p.149, 174-207.) Plaintiff’s evidence meets the standard articulated in Strobel because it is undisputed that Mr. Ward used talcum powder products long term and there is expert testimony reporting positive asbestos test results for talc ores from the mines where the talc was sourced. Accordingly, Plaintiff’s evidence is sufficient to support an inference that the Walgreens body and foot powders Mr. Ward used was a substantial factor in causing his cancer.

As for Walgreens’s argument that Plaintiff cannot prove Mr. Ward’s cancer was caused by Walgreens products as opposed to other sellers, Plaintiff is not required to pinpoint the exact asbestos sources that caused Mr. Ward’s cancer. As the California Supreme Court explained in Rutherford v. Owens-Illinois, Inc. (1997) 16 Cal.4th 953, 975, which Walgreens cites, “asbestos plaintiffs… ‘are not required to identify the manufacturer of specific fibers’ that caused the cancer…Plaintiffs cannot be expected to prove the scientifically unknown details of carcinogenesis, or trace the unknowable path of a given asbestos fiber.” (Rutherford, supra, 16 Cal.4th at p.976, emphasis in original.) Rather, “plaintiffs may prove causation in asbestos-related cancer cases by demonstrating that the plaintiff's exposure to defendant's asbestos-containing product in reasonable medical probability was a substantial factor in contributing to the aggregate dose of asbestos the plaintiff or decedent inhaled or ingested, and hence to the risk of developing asbestos-related cancer, without the need to demonstrate that fibers from the defendant's particular product were the ones, or among the ones, that actually produced the malignant growth.” (Id.) Here, Plaintiff is not required to show that the Walgreens talcum powder products caused Mr. Ward’s cancer as opposed to other sellers. As discussed, Plaintiff’s evidence meets the standard articulated in Strobel and supports an inference that Walgreens’s talcum powder products were asbestos-contaminated and that they contributed to Mr. Ward’s risk of developing cancer. Accordingly, Walgreens’s argument is without merit and the motion for summary judgment is denied.

C.    Motion for Summary Adjudication

Walgreens moves for summary adjudication of the cause of action for negligence on the grounds that Walgreens did not have a duty to test or inspect the powder products it sold. (Motion at pp.22-23.) In opposition, Plaintiff argues that Walgreens mischaracterizes his negligence claim. Rather, “Plaintiff alleges that Walgreens sold foot and body powders containing asbestos — a known human carcinogen — and failed to exercise ordinary care with respect to a hazard it knew or should have known about, including by failing to warn users or recall/retrofit such products.” (Opp. at p.17.)

The complaint alleges that all Defendants, including Walgreens, “negligently and carelessly researched, tested or failed to test, warned or failed to warn, failed to recall or retrofit, failed to provide protective measures to prevent take home exposure, failed to warn of the dangers to those who come in contact with users, manufactured and/or caused to be manufactured, fabricated, designed, developed, labeled, assembled, distributed, supplied, leased, bought, offered for sale, sold, inspected, serviced, repaired, installed, demolished, contracted for installation, contracted others to install, repaired, marketed, warranted, rebranded, manufactured for others, packaged and advertised asbestos and/or products containing asbestos and/or products which created an asbestos hazard, and/or safety equipment…” (Compl. at ¶14.) As Plaintiff points out in opposition, summary adjudication is only appropriate where the motion “completely disposes of a cause of action, an affirmative defense, a claim for damages, or an issue of duty.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 437c, subd. (f)(1).) Here, Walgreens addresses one of Plaintiff’s theories of negligence and fails to address the others. The motion thus fails to dispose of the entire cause of action. Accordingly, the motion for summary adjudication is denied.

IV.           Conclusion

Walgreens meets its initial burden to prove that Plaintiff’s factually devoid discovery responses support an inference that they lack and cannot reasonably obtain evidence to support his contention that Mr. Ward was exposed to an asbestos-contaminated product for which it was responsible. Plaintiff presented sufficient evidence in opposition to raise a triable issue of material fact over whether the Walgreens foot and body powders Mr. Ward used were contaminated with asbestos. Accordingly, the motion for summary judgment is denied.

Walgreens’s motion for summary adjudication fails to completely dispose of the negligence cause of action. Walgreens’s motion for summary adjudication is denied.

I.                Background

On October 11, 2024, Plaintiffs Anthony and Janice Ward (collectively, “Plaintiffs”) filed their complaint for personal injury alleging Mr. Ward developed mesothelioma from direct exposure to asbestos-containing talc products from March 1987 through 2017. On May 21, 2025, Mr. Ward dismissed Ms. Ward as a Plaintiff and her cause of action for loss of consortium. As against Defendant Vi-Jon, LLC (“Vi-Jon” or “Defendant”), Mr. Ward alleges that he was exposed to Defendant’s asbestos-containing CVS and Walgreens store brand talc-based foot and body powder products from the late 1980’s to the early 2020’s through personal use.

On February 5, 2026, Defendant moved for summary judgment or summary adjudication, arguing that Mr. Ward’s discovery responses were factually devoid as to specific facts supporting his alleged exposure to any asbestos-containing product for which it is responsible. Defendant further argues that it is not liable for Mr. Ward’s pre-1999 or post-2016 claimed exposure, because Defendant’s predecessor purchased the alleged product from PTC and Perrigo and Defendant ceased manufacturing talc powder products in 2016. Vi-Jon also moves for summary adjudication of Mr. Ward’s claims for concealment, fraud, and punitive damages.

On July 10, 2026, Mr. Ward opposed, arguing that his affirmative evidence creates a triable issue of material fact as to whether Mr. Ward was exposed to asbestos from Vi-Jon supplied talcum powder products and to sustain his cause of action for punitive damages based on Defendant’s conscious disregard for the health and safety of its consumers. Mr. Ward only addresses the motion for summary adjudication as to Plaintiff’s punitive damages.

On July 17, 2026, Defendant replied, arguing that Mr. Ward failed to set forth evidence that any of the products Mr. Ward used was contaminated with asbestos. Defendant also argues that although Mr. Ward advances expert opinions, Dr. Krekeler’s deposition lacks foundation and is speculative and Mr. Tomaino’s deposition is incomplete, unauthenticated, and inadmissible. As to the punitive damages claim, Defendant argues that Mr. Ward fails to present evidence establishing that Defendant knew or should have known its talc products contained asbestos and cannot meet the heavy burden for imposing punitive damages.

Vi-Jon meets its burden to show that Mr. Ward lacks and cannot reasonably obtain evidence to support their claims that Mr. Ward was exposed to asbestos through an asbestos-containing product for which it is responsible. Mr. Ward, however, successfully meets his burden on summary judgment to raise a triable issue of material fact over whether Vi-Jon caused his injuries. Vi-Jon’s motion for summary judgment is denied.

Mr. Ward does not address Defendant’s motion for summary adjudication as to the claims for concealment and fraud in his opposition. In addition, Vi-Jon only requests in reply that if the motion for summary judgment is denied, that, in the alternative, the court grant its motion for summary adjudication as to punitive damages; the conclusion does not mention the claims for fraud and concealment. Mr. Ward’s complaint does not include any causes of action for fraud. Accordingly, Vi-Jon’s motion for summary adjudication of the concealment and fraud claims are denied as moot.

Vi-Jon’s motion for summary adjudication as to Mr. Ward’s claim for punitive damages is granted. Vi-Jon satisfies its initial burden to present evidence that Mr. Ward’s discovery responses pertaining to punitive damages were devoid of facts to support his contention that Vi-Jon engaged in conduct amounting to malice. Mr. Ward failed to present evidence to raise a triable issue of fact over whether Vi-Jon knew its products were contaminated with asbestos.

Vi-Jon’s motion for summary judgment is denied. The motion for summary adjudication of Mr. Ward’s claims for concealment and fraud are denied as moot. The motion for summary adjudication of Mr. Ward’s claim for punitive damages is granted.

III.           Discussion

 

A.    Legal Standard

 

A defendant seeking summary judgment must “conclusively negate[] a necessary element of the plaintiff’s case, or . . . demonstrate[] that under no hypothesis is there a material issue of fact that requires the process of trial.”  (Guz v. Bechtel Nat. Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 317, 334.)  To show that a plaintiff cannot establish an element of a cause of action, a defendant must make the initial showing “that the plaintiff does not possess, and cannot reasonably obtain, needed evidence.”  (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 854.)  “The defendant may, but need not, present evidence that conclusively negates an element of the plaintiff’s cause of action.  The defendant may also present evidence that the plaintiff does not possess, and cannot reasonably obtain, needed evidence – as through admissions by the plaintiff following extensive discovery to the effect that he has discovered nothing.”  (Id. at p. 855.) “‘If plaintiffs respond to comprehensive interrogatories seeking all known facts with boilerplate answers that restate their allegations, or simply provide laundry lists of people and/or documents, the burden of production will almost certainly be shifted to them once defendants move for summary judgment and properly present plaintiff’s factually devoid discovery responses.’”  (Weber v. John Crane, Inc. (2006) 143 Cal.App.4th 1433, 1440.)

 

The court is required to view the evidence in the light most favorable to Plaintiffs as the nonmoving party and¿“liberally construe plaintiff[s’] evidentiary submissions and strictly scrutinize defendant[’s] own evidence, in order to resolve any evidentiary doubts or ambiguities in plaintiff[’s] favor.” (McDonald v. Antelope Valley Community College Dist.¿(2008) 45 Cal.4th 88, 96–97.)  

 

B.    Causation

In asbestos litigation, a plaintiff is required to show causation. A plaintiff must show “some threshold exposure to the defendant's defective asbestos-containing products” and must further establish to a “reasonable medical probability” that a particular exposure or series of exposures was a “legal cause” of his injury, i.e., a substantial factor in bringing about the injury. (Rutherford v. Owens-Illinois, Inc. (1997) 16 Cal.4th 953, 982.) “[T]he proper analysis is to ask whether the plaintiff has proven exposure to a defendant's product, of whatever duration, so that exposure is a possible factor in causing the disease and then to evaluate whether the exposure was a substantial factor.” (Lineaweaver v. Plant Insulation Co. (1995) 31 Cal.App.4th 1409, 1416 (Lineweaver).)   

Where asbestos was not an intended ingredient in a talc product, plaintiffs bear the additional burden of showing that it is “more likely than not that the talc product was contaminated with asbestos during the time the plaintiff used it.” (LAOSD Asbestos Cases (2020) 44 Cal.App.5th 475, 489 [hereinafter “Gibbons”].) Evidence sufficient to support this finding includes “long term use of a talcum powder product alleged to contain asbestos by a mesothelioma sufferer who was not exposed to any other known source of asbestos above background asbestos levels that are ever-present in the environment, together with expert testimony reporting positive test results for the presence of asbestos in ore from the sources used to manufacture the product.” (Strobel v. Johnson & Johnson (2021) 70 Cal.App.5th 796, 815.) “In the absence of evidence explaining how asbestos in the source ore would have been eliminated in the process of mining and then milling talc, that is enough to support more than a mere possibility that the accused product… was a substantial factor in causing [plaintiff] to develop mesothelioma.” (Ibid.)

C.    Punitive Damages

When a motion for summary adjudication targets a request for punitive damages, a higher standard of proof is at play. “Although the clear and convincing evidentiary standard is a stringent one, ‘it does not impose on a plaintiff the obligation to “prove” a case for punitive damages at summary judgment [or summary adjudication.’ [Citations.]  Even so, ‘where the plaintiff’s ultimate burden of proof will be by clear and convincing evidence, the higher standard of proof must be taken into account in ruling on a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication, since if a plaintiff is to prevail on a claim for punitive damages, it will be necessary that the evidence presented meet the higher evidentiary standard.’ [Citation.]” (Butte Fire Cases (2018) 24 Cal.App.5th 1150, 1158 1159.) For a corporate defendant, the oppression, fraud or malice “must be on the part of an officer, director, or managing agent of the corporation.” (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (b).) That requirement can be satisfied “‘if the evidence permits a clear and convincing inference that within the corporate hierarchy authorized persons acted despicably in “willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.”’ [Citation.]” (Morgan v. J-M Manufacturing Company, Inc. (2021) 60 Cal.App.5th 1078, 1090.) A plaintiff also “can satisfy the ‘managing agent’ requirement ‘through evidence showing the information in the possession of the corporation and the structure of management decision making that permits an inference that the information in fact moved upward to a point where corporate policy was formulated.’  [Citation.]” (Id., at p. 1091.)

“‘[I]ntentionally marketing a defective product knowing that it might cause injury and death is ‘highly reprehensible.’ [Citation.]” (Bankhead v. ArvinMeritor, Inc. (2012) 205 Cal.App.4th 68, 85.) Punitive damages may be available when a defendant knows the dangers of asbestos, took action to protect its own employees from the hazard, knew that its products were likely to pose a danger to users, and did not warn them.  (Pfeifer v. John Crane, Inc. (2013) 220 Cal.Ap.4th 1270, 1300.) Such evidence “was sufficient to show malice, that is, despicable conduct coupled with conscious disregard for the safety of others.” (Id., at pp. 1300-1301.)

Vi-Jon moves for summary adjudication of Mr. Ward’s claim for punitive damages.[1]  Vi-Jon argues: “Plaintiff has provided no facts or evidence at all demonstrating fraud, oppression or malice on the part of Vi-Jon. Vi-Jon propounded comprehensive written discovery to Plaintiff asking him to specifically identify all facts, all documents, all witnesses, and the knowledge those witnesses may possess that support his prayer for punitive damages and Plaintiff’s responses are factually devoid and lacking any supporting evidence or documents supporting the contention that an Officer, Director, or Managing Agent of Vi-Jon acted with oppression, fraud, or malice against Plaintiff Antony Ward. (UMF No. 29) Plaintiff’s testimony and discovery responses confirm that he has no personal knowledge or information specifically related to Vi-Jon and fails to cite to any conduct or evidence that satisfies the above standard. (UMF Nos. 1-27, 29) (See, e.g., Andrews, 138 Cal.App.4th at 106-107) [granting summary judgment in an asbestos product liability lawsuit because plaintiffs’ discovery responses were “factually devoid” and therefore did not raise a triable issue of fact].) Plaintiff’s evidence similarly fails to identify any Vi-Jon officer, director, or managing agent by name or any documents showing communications with Vi-Jon’s corporate officers, directors, or managing agents. (Id.)” (Motion at pp. 26-27.)

In opposition, Mr. Ward argues that all the evidence described above “is more than sufficient to demonstrate triable issues of fact exist as to Defendant Vi-Jon’s conscious disregard for the health and safety of its consumers, including Mr. Ward.” (Opp., at p. 16.) However, Mr. Ward makes no specific argument as to whether Vi-Jon knew its products contained asbestos and continued to sell them without warnings.

In reply, Vi-Jon argues that Mr. Ward fails to present clear and convincing evidence that Vi-Jon knew or should have known its talc powder products contained asbestos: “the discovery to date in this case established that Vi-Jon (1) had a zero tolerance policy for asbestos in the talc it used for its products; (2) purchased only cosmetic grade talc, a condition of which is that the talc is asbestos-free; (3) required its supplier to test the raw talc for the presence of asbestos using state of the art equipment and testing protocols in accordance with the USP; and (4) required certifications for every lot received from its supplier that the talc was asbestos-free. There is not a single document in this case, or any testimony from any Vi-Jon employee, that establishes that Vi-Jon was ever told, or ever believed, that its talcum powder products contained asbestos.” (Reply at p. 8.) In addition, Defendant argues that Mr. Ward fails to present clear and convincing evidence that it engaged in acts rising to the level of malice, fraud, or oppression. (Reply at pp. 8-9.)

1.     Defendant’s Burden: Factually Devoid

Vi-Jon presets evidence that Mr. Ward’s discovery responses were factually devoid as to facts supporting their claim for punitive damages. Vi-Jon directed special interrogatories number 9 and request for production No. 13 to the topic of punitive damages. The responses to the interrogatory and the request for production both incorporated the responses to no. 1. As discussed, the responses to interrogatory and request for production no. 1 contained significant facts supporting Mr. Ward’s claim that he was exposed to asbestos-containing products which Defendant was responsible for. The responses, however, fail to show that Vi-Jon knew its products contained asbestos and continued to sell them without warnings. Thus, the responses are factually devoid of facts which establish that Defendant engaged in conduct amounting to malice. Vi-Jon satisfies its initial burden based on factually devoid discovery responses.

2.     Plaintiff’s burden:

In his separate statement, Mr. Ward does not dispute Vi-Jon’s claim that “Vi-Jon propounded comprehensive written discovery to Plaintiff asking him to specifically identify all facts, all documents, all witnesses, and the knowledge those witnesses may possess that support his prayer for punitive damages and Plaintiff’s responses are factually devoid and lacking any supporting evidence or documents supporting the contention that an officer, director, or managing agent of Vi-Jon acted with oppression, fraud, or malice against Plaintiff Anthony Ward.” (PSS No. 29.) Mr. Ward also points to the evidence described above, specifically the various depositions of Ms. Murray, to support his assertion, but as seen above, the evidence fails to show that Vi-Jon knew its products contained asbestos and continued to sell them without warnings. Ms. Murray continuously maintained that Vi-Jon never found asbestos in their talc products. Therefore, Mr. Ward fails to present evidence to raise a triable issue of material fact over whether Vi-Jon knew its products were contaminated with asbestos and continued to sell them without warnings.

Accordingly, Vi-Jon’s motion for summary adjudication of Mr. Ward’s claim for punitive damages is granted.  

IV.           Conclusion

Vi-Jon, LLC’s motion for summary judgment is denied. The motion for summary adjudication of Mr. Ward’s claim for punitive damages is granted.
