DEPARTMENT 408 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 24STCV17735    Hearing Date: September 17, 2026    Dept: 408

 

1.     Defendant Jonathan Everett Mills’ Motion for Order Requiring Plaintiff to Accept Electronic Service is GRANTED.

 

2.     Plaintiff Shiloh Parker’s Motion to Vacate Void Order is DENIED.

 

I.                BACKGROUND

 

On July 16, 2024, Plaintiff Shiloh Parker (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Defendants Jonathan Everett Mills (“Mills”) and Durden and Mills, P.C. (“Durden”) alleging causes of action for:

 

1.     Civil Extortion;

2.     Civil Conspiracy;

3.     Abuse of Process; and

4.     Unfair Business Practice.

 

On August 5, 2024, Plaintiff filed a First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) against Defendants Mills, Durden, Robert John Lamb (“Lamb”), Daniel Freeland (“Freeland”), and Enrique Rivera (“Rivera”) alleging causes of action for:

 

1.     Civil Extortion;

2.     Abuse of Process;

3.     Malicious Prosecution;

4.     Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress;

5.     Negligent Infliction of Emotional Distress; and

6.     Unfair Business Practice.

 

On February 27, 2024, Plaintiff filed a Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”).

 

On June 12, 2025, pursuant to an oral request by Plaintiff, the court dismissed Freeland and Rivera from Plaintiff’s SAC without prejudice.

 

On July 28, 2025, Plaintiff filed a Third Amended Complaint (“TAC”) alleging causes of action for:

 

1.     Civil Extortion;

2.     Abuse of Process;

3.     Malicious Prosecution;

4.     Unfair Business Practice; and

5.     Slander Per Se.

 

On October 13, 2025, the court denied in part Mills and Durden’s Motion to Quash Service of Summons and Complaint as to Mills and granted in part as to Durden as it had not been served with Plaintiff’s TAC; denied as moot Plaintiff’s Motion to Continue Specially Appearing Defendants’ Motion to Quash Service of Summons; and denied as moot Plaintiff’s Motion for Leave to Conduct Jurisdictional Discovery as to Specially Appearing Defendants.

 

          On January 20, 2026, the court granted Plaintiff’s Motion for Leave to File a Fourth Amended Complaint, overruled Mills’ Demurrer, and denied the Motion to Strike to Plaintiff’s TAC as moot.

 

          On January 20, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Fourth Amended Complaint (“4AC”) against Mills and Lamb (“Defendants”) alleging causes of action for:

 

1.     Civil Extortion;

2.     Abuse of Process; and

3.     Defamation/Slander Per Se.

 

On April 9, 2026, the court overruled Mills’ Demurrer and denied the Motion to Strike as to Plaintiff’s 4AC.

 

On April 17, 2026, Mills filed an answer to Plaintiff’s 4AC. On August 5, 2026, the court overruled Plaintiff’s Demurrer to Mills’ answer to Plaintiff’s 4AC.

 

On July 1, 2026, Plaintiff filed this Motion to Vacate Void Order #1. On September 2, 2026, Mills filed an opposition. On September 10, 2026, Plaintiff filed a reply.

 

On July 24, 2026, Mills filed this Motion for Order Requiring Plaintiff to Accept Electronic Service. On September 8, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition. On September 9, 2026, Mills filed a reply.

 

II.             MOTION FOR ORDER REQUIRING PLAINTIFF TO ACCEPT ELECTRONIC SERVICE

 

A.        Legal Standard

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 1010.6 provides that an unrepresented person may consent to receive electronic service. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 1010.6, subd. (a)(3)(c)(2).)   

 

B.         Discussion

 

Mills moves for an order requiring Plaintiff to accept electronic service. (Motion, at p. 3.) Mills argues that Code of Civil Procedure section 1010.6 authorizes Plaintiff to accept electronic service as licensed attorney who represents herself. (Id., at pp. 3-5.)

 

The court finds that Plaintiff is required to accept service electronically. For purposes of electronic service, a “self-represented” individual is defined as “a party or other person who is unrepresented in an action by an attorney and does not include an attorney appearing in an action who represents himself or herself.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.250, subds. (b)(10)[emphasis added].) As Plaintiff is a licensed attorney representing herself, Plaintiff is not subject to the rules governing electronic service for unrepresented persons.

 

 Moreover, the rules governing electronic service and filing “must be construed to authorize and permit filing and service by electronic means to the extent feasible.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.250, subd. (a).) In opposition, Plaintiff argues that ordering electronic service would be prejudicial as Plaintiff has no email address. (Opp., at pp. 4-6.) Although the court notes that whether Plaintiff does not possess an email address is disputed, the court notes that Plaintiff fails to demonstrate that it is not feasible to create an email address for purposes of electronic service.

 

As such, the court orders Plaintiff to accept electronic service commencing in 5 days.

 

III.          MOTION TO VACATE VOID ORDER

 

A.        Legal Standard

 

“The court may, upon motion of the injured party, or its own motion, correct clerical mistakes in its judgment or orders as entered, so as to conform to the judgment or order directed, and may, on motion of either party after notice to the other party, set aside any void judgment or order.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (d).)

 

B.         Discussion

 

Plaintiff moves to vacate portions of a court order dated June 18, 2026, on the grounds that the portion of the order relating to Mills’ motions to compel further responses do not comply with existing law, they are inconsistent with the record, they are not supported by substantial evidence, and are vague to the point of implicating Plaintiff’s due process rights. (Motion, at p. 1.)

 

To succeed on the motion to vacate, Plaintiff must show that the order at issue is void. “A court can lack fundamental authority over the subject matter, question presented, or party, making its judgment void, or it can merely act in excess of its jurisdiction or defined power, rendering the judgment voidable.” (In re Marriage of Goddard¿(2004) 33 Cal.4th 49, 56.) As an initial matter, Plaintiff has not shown that the court lacked jurisdiction to issue the June 18, 2026, order or some other deficiency rendering the order at issue void. Plaintiff’s arguments dispute the propriety of the court’s analysis and ruling as to Mills’ motions to compel further discovery. Such arguments seek that the court to reconsider its ruling, a request that is subject to the requirements prescribed under Code of Civil Procedure section 1008. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1008, subd. (a) [within ten days of service of an order, a party may move for reconsideration based on new facts, circumstances, or law].) As such, the court finds that the June 18, 2026, order is not void.

 

Accordingly, the motion is denied.

 

IV.          CONCLUSION

1.     Defendant Jonathan Everett Mills’ Motion for Order Requiring Plaintiff to Accept Electronic Service is GRANTED.

 

2.     Plaintiff Shiloh Parker’s Motion to Vacate Void Order is DENIED.


Case Number: 26STCV07891    Hearing Date: September 17, 2026    Dept: 408

Defendant Dave Michalski’s Motion to Set Aside/Vacate Default is GRANTED.

 

I.                BACKGROUND

 

On March 12, 2026, Plaintiff Susan Agostinelli (“Agostinelli”) filed a complaint against Defendant Dave Michalski (“Michalski”) alleging causes of action for:

 

1.     Breach of Implied Contract;

2.     Unjust Enrichment;

3.     Constructive Trust;

4.     Declaratory Relief;

5.     Accounting;

6.     Promissory Estoppel; and

7.     Equitable Lien.

 

On May 11, 2026, pursuant to Agostinelli’s request, default was entered against Michalski.

 

On May 15, 2026, Michalski filed an answer to Agostinelli’s complaint and a cross-complaint against Agostinelli.

 

On July 27, 2026, Agostinelli filed an answer to Michalski’s cross-complaint.

 

On July 27, 2026, Michalski filed this Motion to Set Aside/Vacate Default. On September 10, 2026, Michalski filed a Notice of Non-Opposition.

 

On July 29, 2026, the court ordered Michalski’s cross-complaint and Agostinelli’s answer to the cross-complaint stricken.

 

II.             LEGAL STANDARD

 

“The court may, upon any terms as may be just, relieve a party or his or her legal representative from a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against him or her through his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b).) “Application for this relief shall be accompanied by a copy of the answer or other pleading proposed to be filed therein, otherwise the application shall not be granted, and shall be made within a reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months, after the judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding was taken.” (Ibid.) “‘[W]hen relief under section 473¿is¿available, there is a strong¿public¿policy¿in¿favor¿of granting relief and allowing the requesting party his or her day in court…[Citation.]” (Rappleyea v. Campbell¿(1994) 8 Cal. 4th 975, 981-982.)¿ 

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 473(b) provides both discretionary and mandatory relief from orders. Discretionary relief is available under the statute as “the court may, upon any terms as may be just, relieve a party or his or her legal representative from judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against him or her through his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect.” (Code of Civ. Proc. § 473, subd. (b).) Mandatory relief is available when “accompanied by an attorney’s sworn affidavit attesting to his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect.” (Code of Civ. Proc. § 473, subd. (b).)  

 

“The terms mistake, inadvertence, surprise, and excusable neglect warranting relief under section 473(b) are defined as follows: ‘Mistake is not a ground for relief under section 473, subdivision (b), when “the court finds that the ‘mistake’ is simply the result of professional incompetence, general ignorance of the law, or unjustifiable negligence in discovering the law....” [Citation.] Further, “[t]he term ‘surprise,’ as used in section 473, refers to ‘“some condition or situation in which a party ... is unexpectedly placed to his injury, without any default or negligence of his own, which ordinary prudence could not have guarded against.”’ [Citation.]” Finally, as for inadvertence or neglect, “[t]o warrant relief under section 473 a litigant’s neglect must have been such as might have been the act of a reasonably prudent person under the same circumstances. The inadvertence contemplated by the statute does not mean mere inadvertence in the abstract. If it is wholly inexcusable it does not justify relief.’ [Citation.]” (Henderson v. Pacific Gas & Electric Co. (2010) 187 Cal.App.4th 215, 229-230.)

 

III.          DISCUSSION

 

Michalski seeks relief pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 473(d) to set aside the default entered against him on May 11, 2026. (Motion, at p. 1.) Michalski’s counsel, Megan L. Whipp (“Whipp”), declares that, on May 8, 2026, Whipp electronically submitted Michalski’s answer and cross-complaint for filing and served Agostinelli the same day. (Whipp Decl., ¶¶ 3-4, Exhs. A-B.) On May 15, 2026, Whipp received notice that the filing was rejected as Whipp mistakenly submitted the answer and cross-complaint in a single document rather than separate filings. (Id., ¶ 5, Exh. C.) Upon receiving notice, Whipp corrected the filing defect, re-submitted the same substantive pleadings in proper form, and re-served Agostinelli that same day. (Id., ¶ 8, Exhs. D-E.)

The court finds that the default entered against Michalski was due to his counsel’s mistake. The motion is also timely as it was filed within the six-month deadline. (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b).) The court further notes that Agostinelli failed to file an opposition, effectively consenting to the court granting the motion. (Cal. Rules of Court, 8.54(c) [“A failure to oppose a motion may be deemed a consent to the granting of the motion.”]; Sexton v. Superior Court (1997) 58 Cal.App.4th 1403, 1410.)

Accordingly, the motion is granted.

 

IV.          CONCLUSION

 

Defendant Dave Michalski’s Motion to Set Aside/Vacate Default is GRANTED.


Case Number: 26STCV09353    Hearing Date: September 17, 2026    Dept: 408

Defendant City of Los Angeles’ Demurrer to Plaintiff Alex Gonzalez’s First Amended Complaint is OVERRULED.

 

I.                BACKGROUND

 

On March 23, 2026, Plaintiff Alex Gonzalez (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Defendants City of Los Angeles (“City”) and Los Angeles Fire Department (“LAFD”, collectively “Defendants”) alleging causes of action for:

 

1.     Discrimination in Violation of FEHA (Gov. Code, § 12940, et seq.);

2.     Retaliation in Violation of FEHA (Gov. Code, § 12940, et seq.);

3.     Failure to Take All Reasonable Steps to Prevent Unlawful Discrimination and Retaliation (Gov. Code, § 12940(k)); and

4.     Injunctive Relief (Gov. Code, § 12940, et seq.).

 

On June 12, 2026, Plaintiff filed a First Amended Complaint (“FAC”).

 

On July 13, 2026, the City filed this Demurrer to Plaintiff’s FAC. On September 3, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition. As of September 14, 2026, no reply has been filed.

 

II.             LEGAL STANDARD

 

          “The party against whom a complaint or cross-complaint has been filed may object, by demurrer or answer as provided in Section 430.30, to the pleading on any one or more of” various grounds listed in statute. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10.)

 

          When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context. In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) “A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Ibid.) The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)

 

          Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Id.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.) However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245.)

 

III.          DISCUSSION

 

The City demurs to the four causes of action alleged in Plaintiff’s FAC pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (e), on the grounds that Plaintiff fails to state sufficient facts to constitute such causes of action.

1.     First Cause of Action – FEHA Discrimination

To establish a claim for discrimination under FEHA, a plaintiff must allege “that (1) he was a member of a protected class, (2) he was qualified for the position he sought or was performing competently in the position he held, (3) he suffered an adverse employment action, such as termination, demotion, or denial of an available job, and (4) some other circumstance suggests discriminatory motive.” (Guz v. Bechtel National Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 317, 355.) 

 

The City argues that Plaintiff fails to allege that he was performing competently in his position and instead alleges facts demonstrating the opposite. (Demurrer, at p. 4.) The City further argues that Plaintiff fails to allege any facts suggesting a discriminatory animus for the purported adverse employment action taken against him. (Id., at pp. 4-5.)

 

The FAC alleges that Plaintiff was a firefighter for the LAFD. (FAC, ¶ 14.) On August 31, 2022, Plaintiff was promoted to the rank of Fire Captain I. (Id., ¶ 17.) The same day as Plaintiff’s promotion, Captain I Brock Larue (“Larue”) assisted Plaintiff with his training as Fire Captain I. (Id., ¶ 18.) Larue allegedly made false statements about Plaintiff, accusing Plaintiff of making negative comments about the LAFD. (Ibid.) Despite Plaintiff’s colleagues’ testimony that they did not hear or remember Plaintiff making such comments, the LAFD released a negative evaluation of Plaintiff indicating that he needed an improvement in attitude and loyalty due to his negative comments towards the LAFD. (Id., ¶ 19.) On September 1, 2022, Larue reported the alleged false statements regarding Plaintiff to Battalion Chief Castillo (“Castillo”) and sent an email to Castillo containing such allegations on September 3, 2022. (Id., ¶ 20.) Larue further alleged that Plaintiff failed to “establish a supply line at a working structure fire, failed to ensure proper patient care, leaving an RA to workup an overdose patient in a volatile area, and failing to follow appropriate procedures when requesting time off.” (Ibid.) The Professional Standards Division (“PSD”) also received an anonymous complaint against Plaintiff that same day. (Ibid.)

 

On October 5, 2022, Plaintiff was transferred from C platoon to B platoon. (Id., ¶ 21.) On October 12, 2022, Plaintiff reported for his first shift under Castillo and first became aware that he was under investigation as Plaintiff did not previously know of Larue’s accusations. (Ibid.) On October 12, 2022, Plaintiff was interviewed regarding the allegations against him and stated that he did not recall making such statements. (Id., ¶ 22.) On October 25, 2022, Plaintiff was served with the anonymous complaint against him and received notice that a ten-day suspension was being proposed as a form of discipline against Plaintiff. (Id., ¶ 25.) On October 31, 2022, Plaintiff received his two-month evaluation stating that his performance was “unsatisfactory”. (Id., ¶ 26.) On November 1, 2022, Plaintiff sent a memo to Deputy Chief Phillip Fligiel (“Fliegiel”) regarding his evaluation and stated that he believed his evaluation was incorrect, unfair, and subjective. (Id., ¶ 27.) On November 8, 2022, Plaintiff had a pre-disciplinary hearing and served a three workday suspension. (Id., ¶¶ 28-30.)

 

On January 12, 2023, Plaintiff was terminated from his probationary Captain I position stating that Plaintiff failed to meet department standards and was demoted to an Engineer position. (Id., ¶ 32.) Plaintiff alleges that he was demoted without being placed on a Performance Improvement Plan (“PIP”) despite it being LAFD policy to place employees on PIPs to correct performance issues and give them an opportunity to pass their probation. (Ibid.)

 

In support of the discrimination claim, the FAC alleges that Plaintiff and Captain Patrick Turner (“Turner”), another Fire Captain on probation with Plaintiff, belonged to protected classes based on their race, were not given the opportunity to go through a PIP process to pass probation, and were subsequently demoted. (Id., ¶ 33.) In contrast, Plaintiff alleges that a third Caucasian Fire Captain on probation was placed on a PIP which allowed him to complete his probationary period and become a Fire Captain. (Id., ¶¶ 33, 39.)

 

The court finds that Plaintiff sufficiently alleges a claim for discrimination under FEHA. The FAC alleges that Plaintiff was a member of a protected class based on his Hispanic ethnicity; that Plaintiff was qualified for the position he sought; that Plaintiff suffered an adverse employment action including a failed probationary period and subsequent demotion; and alleges circumstances suggesting a discriminatory animus as Plaintiff was denied an opportunity to be placed on a PIP, along with Turner, while a Caucasian counterpart was placed on a PIP which allowed him to complete his probationary period and became a Fire Captain. (Id., ¶¶ 1, 14, 33, 39.) Such allegations are sufficient at the pleading stage.

 

Accordingly, the demurrer is overruled as to the first cause of action.

 

2.     Second Cause of Action – FEHA Retaliation

 

“To establish a prima facie case of retaliation under [FEHA], a plaintiff must show (1) he or she engaged in a ‘protected activity,’ (2) the employer subjected the employee to an adverse employment action, and (3) a causal link existed between the protected activity and the employer’s action.” (Meeks v. Autozone, Inc. (2018) 24 Cal.App.5th 855, 878-879.)

 

The City argues that Plaintiff fails to allege that he engaged in a protected activity under FEHA based on his purported complaints regarding the disciplinary actions taken against him. (Demurrer, at pp. 5-6.) The City also argues that even if Plaintiff sufficiently alleged a protected activity, Plaintiff fails to allege any adverse employment action that occurred after he filed his grievances. (Id., ¶¶ 6-7.)

 

The court finds that Plaintiff sufficiently alleges a claim for retaliation under FEHA. The FAC alleges that Plaintiff engaged in various protected activities, including that Plaintiff signed his two-month unsatisfactory evaluation with “Do Not Concur” and contacting the union about filing a grievance for his evaluation on October 31, 2022; sending a memo to Fligiel regarding his evaluation stating that it was incorrect, unfair, and subjective on November 1, 2022; and filing for a board of rights regarding Plaintiff’s proposed suspension. (FAC, ¶¶ 26-27, 29.) Such alleged protected activity occurred prior to Plaintiff being terminated from his probation period and subsequent demotion on January 12, 2023. (Id., ¶ 32.) Reading the FAC as a whole, Plaintiff’s allegations regarding his grievances demonstrate that Plaintiff attempted to oppose the discriminatory conduct taken against him constituting a protected activity under FEHA.

 

Accordingly, the demurrer is overruled as to the second cause of action.

 

3.     Third Cause of Action – Failure to Prevent Discrimination and Retaliation

 

The elements of a cause of action for failure to prevent discrimination, harassment, or retaliation are: (1) actionable discrimination, harassment, or retaliation by employees or nonemployees; (2) defendant’s legal duty of care toward plaintiff (i.e., defendant is the plaintiff’s employer); (3) breach of that duty (i.e., failure to take all reasonable steps necessary to prevent discrimination, harassment, or retaliation from occurring); (4) legal causation; and (5) damages to plaintiff. (Trujillo v. North County Transit District (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 280, 287, 289; Bradley v. Department of Corrections & Rehabilitation (2008) 158 Cal.App.4th 1612, 1630; Gov. Code, § 12940.)

 

The City argues that as Plaintiff fails to plead a cause of action for discrimination or retaliation, the derivative claim for failure to prevent also fails for the reasons stated above. (Demurrer, at pp.7-8.) The City further argues that Plaintiff failed to allege how the City failed to take steps to prevent the purported discrimination or retaliation. (Ibid.)

 

The court finds that Plaintiff sufficiently alleges a failure to prevent discrimination and retaliation as the underlying causes of action are sufficiently pled for the reasons previously stated. The court also finds that Plaintiff sufficiently alleges that the City failed to take reasonable steps to prevent discrimination and retaliation. (FAC, ¶ 59.) Moreover, the FAC alleges that “Castillo was given direction by a supervisor to include false, inappropriate critiques of Plaintiff in his evaluation for the purpose of causing Plaintiff to fail probation.” (FAC, ¶ 39.) The FAC further alleges that Castillo admitted in a lawsuit against the City that he was forced to demote both Plaintiff and Turner on the basis of their race and to not given them a PIP. (Ibid.) Such allegations demonstrate, at the pleading stage, that the City failed to prevent the alleged discrimination and retaliation against Plaintiff as they condoned such conduct. (Id., ¶¶ 45, 52.)

 

Accordingly, the demurrer is overruled as to the third cause of action.

 

4.     Fourth Cause of Action – Injunctive Relief

 

The City argues that Plaintiff’s claim for injunctive relief fails as the underlying causes of action also fail for the same reasons stated above. (Demurrer, at p. 8.)

 

The court finds that as Plaintiff’s underlying claims survive for the reasons mentioned above, the demurrer is overruled as to the fourth cause of action.

 

IV.          CONCLUSION

 

Defendant City of Los Angeles’ Demurrer to Plaintiff Alex Gonzalez’s First Amended Complaint is OVERRULED.
