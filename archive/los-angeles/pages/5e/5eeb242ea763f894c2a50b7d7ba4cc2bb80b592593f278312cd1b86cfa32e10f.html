RAUL ARMANDO RAMOS LECHUGA,

Plaintiff,

vs.

URTH CAFFE CORPORATION, et al.,

Defendants.

Case No.:

ORDER SUSTAINING DEMURRER TO FIRST AMENDED COMPLAINT

 

I.           BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Raul Armando Ramos Lechuga (“Lechuga”) was allegedly subjected to harassment and assault by his supervisor, non-party Santiago Llamas (“Llamas”), during the course of his employment with Defendant Urth Cafe Corporation (“Urth”). Lechuga reported this conduct to Defendant Jacqueline Vasquez (“Vasquez”) and Edgar Encarnacion (“Encarnacion”), who were a HR representative and district manager, respectively. Vasquez allegedly accused Lechuga of fabricating his report, leading to his eventual termination. Lechuga sues Defendants for seven causes of action, including 6 FEHA violations and intentional infliction of emotional distress.

On March 12, 2026, Defendants Encarnacion, Jilla Berkman (“Jilla”), and Shallom Berkman (“Shallom”) (collectively “Moving Defendants”) filed the instant demurrer to the entirety of the first amended complaint, on the basis FEHA claims cannot be brought against individual defendants. Lechuga filed an opposition. Moving Defendants filed a reply.

 

II.         LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10 states: “The party against whom a complaint or cross-complaint has been filed may object, by demurrer or answer as provided in Section 430.30, to the pleading on any one or more of the following grounds: (a) The court has no jurisdiction of the subject of the cause of action alleged in the pleading; (b) The person who filed the pleading does not have the legal capacity to sue; (c) There is another action pending between the same parties on the same cause of action; (d) There is a defect or misjoinder of parties; (e) The pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action; (f) The pleading is uncertain. As used in this subdivision, “uncertain” includes ambiguous and unintelligible; and (g) In an action founded upon a contract, it cannot be ascertained from the pleading whether the contract is written, is oral, or is implied by conduct.”

As a general matter, in a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice.  (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)  “A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.”  (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.)  As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations.  (Ibid.)  The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action.  (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment.  (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.)  The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully.  (Ibid.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.)  However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.”  (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245).

           

III.       DISCUSSION

Moving Defendants demurr to all causes of action asserted against Moving Defendants, listed as follows: sexual harassment, failure to prevent harassment, retaliation, failure to prevent retaliation, and aiding and abetting FEHA violations. All relevant causes of action are based upon violation of FEHA, or Government Code section 12940, et seq. Moving Defendants request the Court sustain the demurrer as a party may not sue individuals under FEHA, unless said individual committed actionable harassment.

The Court notes it will not address any potential wrongful termination claims, as the wrongful termination claim is not asserted against any party. Moving Defendants have no basis to demurrer to a cause of action not asserted against them. Similarly, the Court will not address any potential intentional infliction of emotional distress claims as that is only claimed against Vasquez, who is not a moving party for this motion.

A.         Harassment

Lechuga’s cause of action for harassment is subject to demurrer as no Moving Defendant is alleged to have sexually harassed or assaulted Lechuga.  FEHA allows an individual to be liable for harassment only if the individual employer is the party perpetrating the harassment. (Gov. Code § 12940, subd. (j)(3).)

Lechuga’s complaint alleges non-party Llamas engaged in sexual harassment, discriminatory language, unwelcome physical contact, and abusive behavior towards Lechuga. No other individual is alleged to have engaged in harassing behavior. Therefore, Lechuga’s complaint fails to allege facts sufficient to allege a cause of action for harassment against Moving Defendants. The Court sustains the demurrer to this cause of action.

 

B.          Retaliation / Failure to Prevent Retaliation / Failure to Prevent Harassment

Lechuga’s causes of action for retaliation and failure to prevent retaliation as FEHA explicitly prohibits attributing individual liability on supervisor for such claims.

FEHA explicitly holds that, only an employer may be found liable for discrimination against an employee. (Gov. Code § 12940, subd. (a).) Similarly, only an employer, labor organization, employment agency or training program, may be liable for failure to take all reasonable steps necessary to prevent discrimination and harassment. (Id. at subd. (k).)

An employer is “any person acting as an agent of an employer, directly or indirectly.” (Gov. Code § 12926, subd. (d).) The ‘agent’ language is not intended to create individual, personal liability for discrimination, but rather to ensure employers are held liable for discrimination by supervisory employees. (Janken v. GM Hughes Electronics (1996) 46 Cal. App. 4th 55, 67, 70-71.) These limitations similarly extend to causes of action for retaliation, failure to prevent retaliation, and failure to prevent harassment.  (Lloyd v. City of Los Angeles (2009) 172 Cal. App. 4th 320, 330; Jones v. Lodge at Torrey Pines Partnership (2008) 42 Cal. 4th 1158, 1173; Fiol v. Doellstedt (1996) 50 Cal. App. 4th 1318, 1326.)

Moving Defendants, as owners and supervising officers, cannot be held liable as individuals for practices such as retaliation or failing to prevent retaliatory or harassing behavior. These causes of action are without sufficient facts to support a claim and therefore are subject to demurrer.

C.          Aiding and Abetting

Lechuga's cause of action for aiding and abetting is similarly without factual basis, as the FEHA aiding and abetting subdivision was not intended to apply to individuals like Moving Defendants.

Government Code section 12940, subdivision (i) holds it is unlawful for “any person to aid, abet, incite, compel, or coerce the doing of any of the acts forbidden under this part, or to attempt to do so.” However, the section is not intended to apply to supervisory individuals: “we conclude that the Legislature did not intend to impose personal liability upon individual supervisory employees by the roundabout method of “aiding and abetting” language. If we have misperceived the Legislature's intent, the Legislature can correct our misperception by enacting statutory language clearly stating the exposure to personal liability of all individual supervisory employees in California.” (Janken supra at p. 79.) As the legislature never updated the statutory language to explicitly include supervisory employees, despite numerous opportunities, law dictates this section is not intended to apply to supervisory employees.

As all of Moving Defendants are either supervisory employees or owners, acting as supervisory employees, this cause of action has insufficient facts to support a claim and is therefore subject to demurrer.

D.         Leave to Amend

The burden is on a plaintiff to show in what manner he can amend the complaint, and how that amendment will change the legal effect of the pleading. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349; Hendy v. Losse (1991) 54 Cal.3d 723, 742.) While Lechuga requests leave to amend, Lechuga makes no argument as to how he will amend the pleading. Although Lechuga has not met his burden, the Court grants leave to amend in the interest of resolving this issue on the merits and allowing Lechuga another opportunity to present an actionable complaint.

 

IV.        CONCLUSION

Defendants Edgar Encarnacion, Jilla Berkman, and Shallom Berkman’s demurrer to the SAC is SUSTAINED, with 30 days leave to amend.

Moving Party is ordered to give notice.

RAUL ARMANDO RAMOS LECHUGA,

Case No.:

ORDER SUSTAINING DEMURRER TO FIRST AMENDED COMPLAINT

 

I.           BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Raul Armando Ramos Lechuga (“Lechuga”) was allegedly subjected to harassment and assault by his supervisor, non-party Santiago Llamas (“Llamas”), during the course of his employment with Defendant Urth Cafe Corporation (“Urth”). Lechuga reported this conduct to Defendant Jacqueline Vasquez (“Vasquez”) and Edgar Encarnacion (“Encarnacion”), who were a HR representative and district manager, respectively. Vasquez allegedly accused Lechuga of fabricating his report, leading to his eventual termination. Lechuga sues Defendants for seven causes of action, including 6 FEHA violations and intentional infliction of emotional distress (“IIED”).

On April 23, 2026, Vasquez filed the instant demurrer to the entirety of the first amended complaint, on the basis FEHA claims cannot be brought against individual defendants and the IIED does not have facts sufficient to support a cause of action. Lechuga filed an opposition. Vasquez filed a reply.

 

II.         LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10 states: “The party against whom a complaint or cross-complaint has been filed may object, by demurrer or answer as provided in Section 430.30, to the pleading on any one or more of the following grounds: (a) The court has no jurisdiction of the subject of the cause of action alleged in the pleading; (b) The person who filed the pleading does not have the legal capacity to sue; (c) There is another action pending between the same parties on the same cause of action; (d) There is a defect or misjoinder of parties; (e) The pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action; (f) The pleading is uncertain. As used in this subdivision, “uncertain” includes ambiguous and unintelligible; and (g) In an action founded upon a contract, it cannot be ascertained from the pleading whether the contract is written, is oral, or is implied by conduct.”

As a general matter, in a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice.  (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)  “A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.”  (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.)  As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations.  (Ibid.)  The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action.  (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment.  (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.)  The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully.  (Ibid.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.)  However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.”  (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245).

           

III.       DISCUSSION

Vasquez demurs to all causes of action asserted against Vasquez. The causes of action for sexual harassment, failure to prevent sexual harassment, retaliation, failure to prevent retaliation, and aiding and abetting FEHA violations are based upon violation of FEHA, or Government Code section 12940, et seq. Vasquez request the Court sustain the demurrer as a party may not sue individuals under FEHA, unless said individual committed actionable harassment.

Vasquez also demurrers to the IIED claim on the basis the alleged actions do not constitute extreme and/or outrageous conduct.

The Court notes it will not address any potential wrongful termination claims, as the wrongful termination claim is not asserted against any party.

A.         Harassment

Lechuga’s cause of action for harassment is subject to demurrer as Vasquez is not alleged to have sexually harassed or assaulted Lechuga.  FEHA allows an individual to be liable for harassment only if the individual employer is the party perpetrating the harassment. (Gov. Code § 12940, subd. (j)(3).)

Lechuga’s complaint alleges non-party Llamas engaged in sexual harassment, discriminatory language, unwelcome physical contact, and abusive behavior towards Lechuga. No other individual is alleged to have engaged in harassing behavior. Therefore, Lechuga’s complaint fails to allege facts sufficient to allege a cause of action for harassment against Vasquez. The Court sustains the demurrer to this cause of action.

 

B.          Retaliation / Failure to Prevent Retaliation / Failure to Prevent Harassment

Lechuga’s causes of action for retaliation and failure to prevent retaliation as FEHA explicitly prohibits attributing individual liability on supervisor for such claims.

FEHA explicitly holds that, only an employer may be found liable for discrimination against an employee. (Gov. Code § 12940, subd. (a).) Similarly, only an employer, labor organization, employment agency or training program, may be liable for failure to take all reasonable steps necessary to prevent discrimination and harassment. (Id. at subd. (k).)

An employer is “any person acting as an agent of an employer, directly or indirectly.” (Gov. Code § 12926, subd. (d).) The ‘agent’ language is not intended to create individual, personal liability for discrimination, but rather to ensure employers are held liable for discrimination by supervisory employees. (Janken v. GM Hughes Electronics (1996) 46 Cal. App. 4th 55, 67, 70-71.) These limitations similarly extend to causes of action for retaliation, failure to prevent retaliation, and failure to prevent harassment.  (Lloyd v. City of Los Angeles (2009) 172 Cal. App. 4th 320, 330; Jones v. Lodge at Torrey Pines Partnership (2008) 42 Cal. 4th 1158, 1173; Fiol v. Doellstedt (1996) 50 Cal. App. 4th 1318, 1326.)

Vasquez, as a supervising HR officer, cannot be held liable as individuals for practices such as retaliation or failing to prevent retaliatory or harassing behavior. These causes of action are without sufficient facts to support a claim and therefore are subject to demurrer.

C.          Aiding and Abetting

Lechuga's cause of action for aiding and abetting is similarly without factual basis, as the FEHA aiding and abetting subdivision was not intended to apply to individuals like Vasquez.

Government Code section 12940, subdivision (i) holds it is unlawful for “any person to aid, abet, incite, compel, or coerce the doing of any of the acts forbidden under this part, or to attempt to do so.” However, the section is not intended to apply to supervisory individuals: “we conclude that the Legislature did not intend to impose personal liability upon individual supervisory employees by the roundabout method of “aiding and abetting” language. If we have misperceived the Legislature's intent, the Legislature can correct our misperception by enacting statutory language clearly stating the exposure to personal liability of all individual supervisory employees in California.” (Janken supra at p. 79.) As the legislature never updated the statutory language to explicitly include supervisory employees, despite numerous opportunities, law dictates this section is not intended to apply to supervisory employees.

As Vasquez is a supervisory employee, this cause of action has insufficient facts to support a claim and is therefore subject to demurrer.

D. IIED

Vasquez next argues Lechuga's claim for IIED is subject to demur, as Vasquez's described conduct was neither extreme nor outrageous.

A cause of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress requires: (1) extreme and outrageous conduct by the defendant with the intention of causing, or reckless disregard of the probability of causing, emotional distress; (2) the plaintiff's suffering severe or extreme emotional distress; and (3) actual and proximate causation of the emotional distress by the defendant's outrageous conduct. (Hughes v. Pair (2009) 46 Cal.4th 1035, 1050-1051 [citations and quotations omitted].) A defendant’s conduct is “outrageous” when it is so extreme as to exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated in a civilized community. (Ibid.) The defendant must either intend his or her conduct to inflict injury or have engaged in it with the realization that injury will result. Liability for intentional infliction of emotional distress does not extend to mere insults, indignities, threats, annoyances, petty oppressions, or other trivialities. (Ibid.) 

Lechuga’s complaint alleges, after Lechuga reported Santiago’s misconduct to HR, Vasquez accused Lechuga of “lying and fabricating his report,” including during an in-person termination meeting with Vasquez. (FAC ¶ 25.) It also alleges that during the termination meeting, Vasquez belittled Lechuga, threatened Lechuga with insubordination, and dismissively invited additional complaints after accusing Lechuga of dishonesty. (FAC ¶ 26.)

Extreme and outrageous conduct must be more than “mere insults, indignities, threats, annoyances, petty oppressions or other trivialities’ but only to conduct so extreme and outrageous as to go beyond all possible bounds of decency, and to be regarded as atrocious, and utterly intolerable in a civilized community.” (Alcorn v. Anbro Engineering, Inc. (1970) 2 Cal.3d 493, 499.) A supervisor’s actions as relating to terminations, criticism of work practices, and negotiations are not within the realm of extreme and/or outrageous conduct, even if improper motive is alleged. (Janken supra at p. 80.)

As alleged, Vasquez acted inappropriately and dismissively within the context of her role as a supervisory employee. However, nothing meets the standard for extreme and outrageous conduct outside all bounds of decency. This cause of action also has insufficient facts to support the alleged claim and is therefore subject to demurrer.

E.          Leave to Amend

The burden is on a plaintiff to show in what manner he can amend the complaint, and how that amendment will change the legal effect of the pleading. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349; Hendy v. Losse (1991) 54 Cal.3d 723, 742.) Lechuga requests leave to amend, stating he can provide further facts in support of his allegations. The Court grants leave to amend in the interest of resolving this issue on the merits and allowing Lechuga another opportunity to present an actionable complaint.

 

IV.        CONCLUSION

Defendant Jacqueline Vasquez’s demurrer to the SAC is SUSTAINED, with 30 days leave to amend.

Moving Party is ordered to give notice.

Superior Court of California

JANE DOE,

Plaintiff,

vs.

CRISPIN GLOVER,

Defendant.

Case No.:

26STCV06161

Hearing Date:

July 20, 2026

[TENTATIVE] ORDER ON DEFENDANT’S special motion to strike (ANTI-SLAPP MOTION)

 

I.           BACKGROUND

Defendant Cripsin Glover (“Glover”) allegedly invited Plaintiff Jane Doe (“Doe”) to move into one of his homes and work as Glover’s assistant. Once Doe moved in, Doe allegedly learned Glover intended Doe to be Glover’s girlfriend and work for him for free. When Doe left the home without Glover’s consent, Glover locked Doe out and asked her to find a new place to live. Doe allegedly attempted to re-enter; in response, Glover choked Doe and filed a police report against her for trespass. Doe sues Glover for five causes of action, including malicious prosecution.

On June 3, 2026, Glover filed the instant special motion to strike (or “anti-SLAPP motion”) to the third cause of action for malicious prosecution under Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16.[1] Doe filed an opposition. Glover filed a reply.

 

II.         REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

Glover requests that the Court take judicial notice of certain documents. As to those requests, the Court GRANTS the request for judicial notice as to all documents.

 

III.       EVIDENTIARY OBJECTIONS

A special motion to strike under CCP section 425.16 uses a “summary-judgment-like procedure” and follows the same rules as CCP section 437c in that the evidence relied upon in the supporting or opposing papers must be admissible. (Sweetwater Union High Sch. Dist. v. Gilbane Bldg. Co. (2019) 6 Cal. 5th 931, 945; see also Tuchscher Dev. Enters., Inc. v. San Diego Unified Port Dist. (2003) 106 Cal. App. 4th 1219, 1237.)

“Authentication of a writing is required before it may be received in evidence.” (Evid. Code, § 1401, subd. (a).) “Authentication of a writing means (a) the introduction of evidence sufficient to sustain a finding that it is the writing that the proponent of the evidence claims it is or (b) the establishment of such facts by any other means provided by law [Citations]… .” (Jacobson v. Gourley (2000) 83 Cal. App. 4th 1331, 1334.)

 

A.    Glover’s Objections

The Court rules on Glover’s objections to Doe's evidence as follows:

1.     All objections to Doe’s declaration are OVERRULED, unless otherwise noted.

a.      SUSTAINED, as to lack of personal knowledge - ¶ 5; ¶ 8 as to “Mr. Glover’s conduct after the incident also shows he did not genuinely fear me.”; ¶ 13; ¶ 25 as to ““He knew I would not have the same support system in Los Angeles that I had in the United Kingdom. He knew I would be dependent on the housing and stability he repeatedly promised.”; ¶ 26; ¶ 28; ¶ 30; ¶ 33 as to “He knew I was dependent on the housing and work arrangement he had promised.”; ¶ 92; ¶ 98; ¶ 104; ¶ 107; ¶ 108; ¶ 109; ¶121 as to “he made no genuine effort to pursue it...”; ¶ 134.

b.     SUSTAINED, as to oral testimony to prove content of a writing - ¶ 7, as to “I got a cited dismissal after he filed a restraining order petition that he then failed to prosecute by not attending Court”; ¶ 15; ¶ 29 as to “After wrongfully evicting me, he followed up with an email still trying to get me to play his leading lady in his upcoming production.”; ¶ 101.

c.      SUSTAINED, as to argumentative - ¶ 9; ¶ 14; ¶ 37; ¶ 44; ¶ 45; ¶ 53 as to ““Mr. Glover’s declaration also falsely tries to portray me as someone who targeted him for money.”; ¶ 54; ¶ 79 as to “Mr. Glover’s declaration attempts to make himself the victim, but it was...”; ¶ 93; ¶ 96; ¶97 as to ““If Mr. Glover truly believed I was dangerous and truly needed protection from me, he would have made genuine efforts to serve me and appear in court.”; ¶ 125; ¶ 126; ¶ 131.

d.     SUSTAINED, as to lack of authentication – Ex. 7.

 

IV.        LEGAL STANDARD

“Litigation of an anti-SLAPP motion involves a two-step process. First, ‘the moving defendant bears the burden of establishing that the challenged allegations or claims ‘aris[e] from’ protected activity in which the defendant has engaged.’ [Citation.] Second, for each claim that does arise from protected activity, the plaintiff must show the claim has “at least ‘minimal merit.’ [Citation.]”  (Bonni v. St. Joseph Health System (2021) 11 Cal.5th 995, 1009, citations omitted.)  To separate communications which form the source of the dispute from those that merely provide evidence of it, courts ask whether “the same dispute would exist” if there were no pending threat of litigation.  (Gotterba v. Travolta (2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 35, 42.)

As to the second step of the inquiry, a plaintiff seeking to demonstrate the merit of the claim “may not rely solely on its complaint, even if verified; instead, its proof must be made upon competent admissible evidence.”  (Sweetwater Union High School District v. Gilbane Building Co. (2019) 6 Cal.5th 931, 940, citations omitted.)

A party opposing a special motion to strike has the burden to “state [] and substantiate [] a legally sufficient claim.”  (Navellier v. Sletten (2002) 29 Cal.4th 82, 88 & 93.)  “‘Put another way, the party “must demonstrate that the complaint is both legally sufficient and supported by a sufficient prima facie showing of facts to sustain a favorable judgment if the evidence submitted by the defendant is credited.”’ [Citations.]”  (Id. at pgs. 88-89.)  To that end, the party must present competent evidence, “that would be admissible at trial.”  (HMS Capital, Inc. v. Lawyers Title Co. (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 204, 212.)  “[D]eclarations may not be based upon ‘information and belief’ [citation]” and documents submitted without the proper foundation will not be considered.  (Id.)  The complaint, even if verified, is insufficient to carry the plaintiff’s shifted burden.  (Roberts v. Los Angeles County Bar Association (2003) 105 Cal.App.4th 604, 614; Karnazes v. Ares (2016) 244 Cal.App.4th 344, 354 [“pleadings do not constitute evidence”]; see also Burke, Anti-SLAPP Litigation (The Rutter Group, Civil Litigation Series 2018 §5:13) [“To satisfy prong two, the plaintiff must submit admissible evidence that if credited is sufficient to sustain a favorable judgment against the legal theories asserted by the defendant.”].)

“A cause of action is subject to a special motion to strike if the defendant shows that the cause of action arises from an act in furtherance of the defendant’s constitutional right of petition or free speech in connection with a public issue and the plaintiff fails to demonstrate a probability of prevailing on the claim. [Citations.]”  (Digerati Holdings, LLC v. Young Money Entertainment, LLC (2011) 194 Cal.App.4th 873, 883.) 

“An ‘act in furtherance of a person’s right of petition or free speech under the United States or California Constitution in connection with a public issue’ is defined by statute to include ‘(1) any written or oral statement or writing made before a legislative, executive, or judicial proceeding, or any other official proceeding authorized by law, (2) any written or oral statement or writing made in connection with an issue under consideration or review by a legislative, executive, or judicial body, or any other official proceeding authorized by law, (3) any written or oral statement or writing made in a place open to the public or a public forum in connection with an issue of public interest, or (4) any other conduct in furtherance of the exercise of the constitutional right of petition or the constitutional right of free speech in connection with a public issue or an issue of public interest.’  [Citation.] If the defendant shows that the cause of action arises from a statement described in clause (1) or (2) of section 425.16, subdivision (e), the defendant is not required to separately demonstrate that the statement was made in connection with a ‘public issue.’ [Citation.]”  (Id.)

“A cause of action is one ‘arising from’ protected activity within the meaning of section 425.16, subdivision (b)(1) only if the defendant’s act on which the cause of action is based was an act in furtherance of the defendant’s constitutional right of petition or free speech in connection with a public issue. [Citation.] In deciding whether the ‘arising from’ requirement is satisfied, ‘the court shall consider the pleadings, and supporting and opposing affidavits stating the facts upon which the liability or defense is based.’ [(Code Civ. Proc.. § 425.16(b)(2).)] Whether the ‘arising from’ requirement is satisfied depends on the ‘gravamen or principal thrust’ of the claim. [(Episcopal Church Cases (2009) 45 Cal.4th 467, 477, quoting Martinez v. Metabolife International, Inc. (2003) 113 Cal.App.4th 181, 193.)] A cause of action does not arise from protected activity for purposes of the anti-SLAPP statute if the protected activity is merely incidental to the cause of action. [(Martinez, 113 Cal.App.4th at pg. 188.)]”  (Digerati Holdings, LLC, 194 Cal.App.4th at pgs. 883-884.)  To show that a claim arises from protected activity under §425.16(b)(1), it is not sufficient to show that the claim “was filed after, or because of, protected activity, or when protected activity merely provides evidentiary support or context for the claim.”  (Rand Resources, LLC v. City of Carson (2019) 6 Cal.5th 610, 621.)  “Rather, the protected activity must ‘supply elements of the challenged claim.’ [Citation.]”  (Id., citations omitted.)

“At the first step, the moving defendant bears the burden of identifying all allegations of protected activity, and the claims for relief supported by them. When relief is sought based on allegations of both protected and unprotected activity, the unprotected activity is disregarded at this stage.”  (Baral v. Schnitt (2016) 1 Cal.5th 376, 396, emphasis added.)  

 

V.         DISCUSSION

Glover requests the Court strike the third cause of action for malicious prosecution as it arises from Glover’s protected exercise of free-speech rights. Pursuant to section 425.16, subdivision (b), a cause of action against a person arising from the person’s right of petition or free speech shall be subject to a special motion to strike unless the court determines the plaintiff has established there is a probability the plaintiff will prevail on the claim.

A.         Protected Activity

Glover asserts Glover’s activity as to the claim for malicious prosecution is protected, satisfying the first prong of an anti-SLAPP motion, as the claim consists of Glover making a statement to or in connection with a legal/police proceeding. Doe’s complaint alleges Glover gave a false police report and submitted a fraudulent petition for restraining order against Doe. (Complaint ¶ 1.)

 The Anti-SLAPP statute defines relevant protected activity as follows:

“(1) any written or oral statement or writing made before a legislative, executive, or judicial proceeding, or any other official proceeding authorized by law, (2) any written or oral statement or writing made in connection with an issue under consideration or review by a legislative, executive, or judicial body, or any other official proceeding authorized by law, (3) any written or oral statement or writing made in a place open to the public or a public forum in connection with an issue of public interest, or (4) any other conduct in furtherance of the exercise of the constitutional right of petition or the constitutional right of free speech in connection with a public issue or an issue of public interest.” (§ 425.16, subd. (e).)

Glover asserts the alleged activity is protected as it is both a statement made before an official proceeding authorized by law and a statement made in connection with an issue under consideration or review by an official proceeding authorized by law.

Glover first argues making a police report and seeking a domestic violence restraining order are protected activities under the anti-SLAPP statute. Generally speaking, reporting to and seeking assistance from law enforcement, and related court filings, litigation, and court communications in seeking a restraining order, are petitioning activity within the meaning of section 425.16(e). “Section 425.16 is construed broadly, to protect the right of litigants to the utmost freedom of access to the courts without the fear of being harassed subsequently by derivative tort actions.” (Rohde v. Wolf (2007) 154 Cal.App.4th 28, 35.) Courts have thus adopted “a fairly expansive view of what constitutes litigation-related activities within the scope of section 425.16.” (Optional Capital, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss, Hauer & Feld LLP (2017) 18 Cal.App.5th 95, 113.) However, there exists an exemption when the defendant concedes, or the evidence conclusively establishes, the asserted protected speech or activity was illegal as a matter of law. (Zucchet v. Galardi (2014) 229 Cal.App.4th 1466, 1478.) In such situations, a defendant is precluded from using an anti-SLAPP motion to strike the plaintiff’s action. (Ibid.)

Here, Doe alleges Glover’s activity arose out of illegal activity of filing a false criminal report with police, an act not in furtherance of his constitutional rights. However, Glover submitted a declaration attesting he “accurately reported [] facts to law enforcement.” (Mot., Glover Decl. ¶ 44.) Glover has not conceded to the police report being false, and thus not protected under section 425.16. Glover similarly did not concede any portions of the restraining order being false. Therefore, the subject activity is considered protected activity for purposes of determining the first prong of the anti-SLAPP motion, unless Doe can conclusively establish the speech or activity was illegal as a matter of law.

Glover has met his initial burden of showing the activity satisfies the first prong of section 425.16. The burden to demonstrate the probability of prevailing on these causes of action shifts to Doe.

B.          Probability of Prevailing

Under the second prong of the anti-SLAPP analysis, Doe has the shifted burden of establishing a probability of prevailing on her cause of action for malicious prosecution. (§ 425.16, subd. (b)(3).) A plaintiff “cannot simply rely on his or her pleadings, even if verified,” but must submit “competent, admissible evidence.” (Grenier v. Taylor (2015) 234 Cal.App.4th 471, 480; see also Morrow v. Los Angeles Unified School District (2007) 149 Cal.App.4th 1424, 1444.) In evaluating the parties’ submissions, the Court considers the pleadings and admissible evidence presented by both sides, without weighing credibility, and must grant the motion “if, as a matter of law, the defendant’s evidence supporting the motion defeats the plaintiff’s attempt to establish evidentiary support for the claim.” (Wilson v. Parker, Covert & Chidester (2002) 28 Cal. 4th 811, 821 [emphasis original].) The question is whether the plaintiff has presented evidence in opposition to the defendant’s motion that, if believed by the trier of fact, is sufficient to support a judgment in the plaintiff’s favor. (Zamos v. Stroud (2004) 32 Cal.4th 958, 965.)

“‘To establish a cause of action for the malicious prosecution of a civil proceeding, a plaintiff must plead and prove that the prior action (1) was commenced by or at the direction of the defendant and was pursued to a legal termination in his, plaintiff’s, favor [citations]; (2) was brought without probable cause [citations]; and (3) was initiated with malice [citations].’” (Lanz v. Goldstone (2015) 243 Cal.App.4th 441, 458 (quoting Bertero v. National General Corp. (1974) 13 Cal.3d 43, 50).)

1.          False Police Report

As noted in Glover’s moving papers, merely making ‘false’ police reports does not qualify as a prior action for purposes of malicious prosecution, nor does a warrantless arrest. (Van Audenhove v. Perry (2017) 11 Cal.App.5th 915, 922.) Doe’s complaint specifically states “[n]o charges were brought forth upon Mr. Glover’s false police report.” (Complaint ¶ 21.) There are no allegations of Doe ever being arrested, pursuant to warrant. As there is no indication of Glover’s allegedly false statement resulting in an arrest warrant or any charges against Doe, Glover’s statements to the police are not a potentially valid theory for malicious prosecution.

2.          Restraining  Order

The only remaining avenue for malicious prosecution concerns Glover allegedly filing a fraudulent petition for restraining order. It is undisputed the subject restraining order was commenced by or at the direction of Glover. It is also undisputed the restraining order was dismissed, indicating legal termination in Doe’s favor. Parties dispute whether the restraining order was brought without probable cause or initiated with malice.

Doe argues the action is brought without probable cause as Glover brought the restraining order under false allegations. “[T]he question of probable cause is whether it was objectively reasonable for the defendant . . . to suspect the plaintiff . . . had committed a crime.” (Greene v. Bank of America (2013) 216 Cal.App.4th 454, 465.) Doe submitted a declaration in support of this assertion, attesting she did not attack Glover on the day of the alleged incident and that Glover provided a false and misleading version of events, later used as a basis for the restraining order petition. (Opp., Doe Decl. ¶¶ 80, 83, 90.) Contrary to Glover’s objections, Doe’s declaration does not lack foundation or personal knowledge, nor is it otherwise subject to objection. As the Court is obligated to review a plaintiff’s evidence as if believed by a trier of fact, the Court finds Doe’s evidence sufficient to establish lack of probable cause for purposes of this motion.

Doe also argues Glover’s failure to prosecute the case demonstrates a lack of probable cause. Failure to prosecute or eventual dismissal is not indicative of whether a defendant reasonably believed there was probable cause. (Verdier v. Verdier (1957) 152 Cal.App.2d 348, 352, fn. 3.) Therefore, the Court does not consider this argument.

Doe next argues Glover acted with malice in filing the restraining order. Malice is not limited to a showing of hostility or ill will—it also includes “if the defendant had no substantial grounds for believing in the plaintiff’s guilt, but, nevertheless, instigated proceedings against the plaintiff, [as] it is logical to infer that the defendant’s motive was improper.’ ” (Greene, supra, 216 Cal.App.4th at p. 464-465, internal citation omitted.) “Malice may be inferred from want of probable cause but want of probable cause cannot be inferred from malice, but must be affirmatively shown by the plaintiff.” (Verdier, supra, 152 Cal.App.2d at p. 354.) As Doe has provided sufficient evidence, if believed by a trier of fact, to establish Glover had no probable cause to file the restraining order, and thus had no substantial grounds, Doe has also provided sufficient evidence to establish a finding of malice.

Glover argues Glover as a complete defense to any claim of malicious prosecution as he filed the petition for restraining order on the advice of legal counsel. “Good faith reliance on advice of counsel, after truthful disclosure of all the relevant facts, is a complete defense to a claim of malicious prosecution.” (Bisno v. Douglas Emmett Realty Fund 1988 (2009) 174 Cal.App.4th 1534, 1544.) Glover attested he filed his petition “on the advice of legal counsel,” but provided no detail as to what information he told to counsel. (Mot., Glover Decl. ¶ 47.) There is insufficient evidence to establish Glover truthfully disclosed all relevant facts to counsel prior to counsel advising Glover to file the restraining order. Therefore, Glover has failed to provide evidence of essential elements of this defense.

However, the Court concludes that Glover’s seeking of a domestic violence restraining order cannot be the basis for a malicious prosecution cause of action. (S.A. v. Maiden (2014) 229 Cal.App.4th 27, 38 [“We conclude both [Bidna v. Rosen (1993) 19 Cal.App.4th 27]’s holding and reasoning support our conclusion that S.A. cannot state a malicious prosecution cause of action against Maiden for N.A.’s request, filed in the family law court, for the initial DVPA restraining order”].)  In S.A., the court noted that Bidna “applied the California Supreme Court’s reasoning in Sheldon Appel to establish a ‘bright-line rule’ barring malicious prosecution causes of action arising out of family law proceedings” and concluded that it properly applied to actions under the Domestic Violence Prevention Act (DVPA). (S.A., at p. 36-38.) Courts have concluded that the same reasoning bars malicious prosecution causes of action based on civil harassment and workplace violence restraining orders. (Siam v. Kizilbash (2005) 130 Cal.App.4th 1563, 1571 [reversing trial court’s denial of anti-SLAPP motion on a malicious prosecution cause of action based on an unsuccessful civil harassment petition]; Robinzine v. Vicory (2006) 143 Cal.App.4th 1416, 1420–1421 [reversing trial court’s denial of anti-SLAPP motion on a malicious prosecution cause of action based on an unsuccessful workplace violence petition].) Accordingly, the restraining order proceedings in this case cannot be the basis for the malicious prosecution cause of action.

 

VII.     CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, Glover's special motion to strike the third cause of action for malicious prosecution in Doe's complaint is GRANTED.

Moving Party is ordered to give notice.

ORDER DENYING MOTION TO CONSOLIDATE

 

I.           BACKGROUND

Defendants allegedly executed fraudulent real estate documents, unlawfully recording a deed of trust to Plaintiff Ronald Hills’s (“Hills”) home. Defendants then improperly sold the property. Hills sued Defendant for 11 causes of action, including fraud.

This action was dismissed on June 7, 2016, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6,[1] with the Court retaining jurisdiction to enforce settlement.

On June 9, 2026, Defendants Ivan Moore and Devra Allen (collectively “Moving Defendants”) filed the instant motion to consolidate this action with Los Angeles Superior Court cases nos. 25STCV04203, 25STCV14579 and 26STCV05387. Multiple other parties filed oppositions.

 

II.         LEGAL STANDARD

California Rules of Court, rule 3.350, subdivision (a), provides:

“Requirements of motion “(1) A notice of motion to consolidate must: “(A) List all named parties in each case, the names of those who have appeared, and the names of their respective attorneys of record; “(B) Contain the captions of all the cases sought to be consolidated, with the lowest numbered case shown first; and “(C) Be filed in each case sought to be consolidated. “(2) The motion to consolidate: “(A) Is deemed a single motion for the purpose of determining the appropriate filing fee, but memorandums, declarations, and other supporting papers must be filed only in the lowest numbered case; “(B) Must be served on all attorneys of record and all nonrepresented parties in all of the cases sought to be consolidated; and “(C) Must have a proof of service filed as part of the motion.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.350, subd. (a).)

Under section 1048, when actions involved a common question or law or fact, a court may order a joint hearing or trial, or order all actions consolidated. The purpose of consolidation is to enhance trial court efficiency—e.g., to avoid unnecessary duplication of discovery, evidence, motions, and other procedures, and to avoid the substantial danger of inconsistent adjudications. (See Todd-Stenberg v. Dalkon Shield Claimants Trust (1996) 48 Cal.App.4th 976, 978-79.) In general, consolidation exists in two primary forms: (1) a complete consolidation resulting in a single action; and (2) a consolidation for pretrial and/or trial purposes, where the actions still remain effectively separate. (Committee for Responsible Planning v. City of Indian Wells (1990) 225 Cal.App.3d 191, 196.)

Los Angeles County Superior Court Local Rules rule 3.3(g) provides as follows: “(1) Cases may not be consolidated unless they are in the same department. A motion to consolidate two or more cases may be noticed and heard after the cases, initially filed in different departments, have been related into a single department, or if the cases were already assigned to that department. (2) Upon consolidation of cases, the first filed case will be the lead case, unless otherwise ordered by the court. After consolidation, all future papers to be filed in the Consolidated case must be filed only in the case Designated as the lead case.”

III.       DISCUSSION

Moving Defendants request the Court consolidated this action with Los Angeles Superior Court cases nos. 25STCV04203, 25STCV14579, and 26STCV05387.

A.         Procedural Requirements

Moving Defendants have complied with all procedural requirements for this motion, as they have filed a notice of motions in all cases requested for consolidated. Moving Defendants have also named and served all other parties who have appeared in these actions. All cases are in the same department, Stanley Mosk Dept. 730, as required under LACSC Local Rules rule 3.3(g).

The Court proceeds to review the motion on the merits.

B.          Merits of Consolidation

Moving Defendants request the Court consolidate the requested cases as the subject cases all concern the real property commonly known as 1236-1238 Redondo Blvd., Los Angeles, California 90019 (the “subject property”).

Judgment in this case was entered on August 24, 2016, with the Court restoring ownership of the subject property to Hills and retaining jurisdiction to enforce judgment pursuant to section 664.6. Moving Defendants allege that, following the Courts ruling, Hills conveyed the subject property to Allen, who hereafter conveyed her interest to Moore.

The pending cases requested for consolidation are all brought by Moving Defendants against other alleged owners of the subject property, who allegedly obtained the property via a chain of events starting with a 2021 fraudulent deed recorded by non-party Clear Recon Corp. (“CRC”) in favor of non-party Deutsche Bank National Trust Company (“Deutsche”). Deutsche subsequently deeded the property to Maria Julia Hernandez (“Hernandez”) and Carlos Ramirez (“Ramirez”), the defendants in the other cases.

The Court does not find good cause to consolidate these actions, as they are entirely unrelated outside of the fact it concerns the same subject property. This case was resolved in 2016—5 years prior to the alleged creation of the fraudulent deed. It has not been reopened on the merits. The only relevance this case poses to the currently ongoing cases is the judgment entered, which can be judicially noticed as a state court record, providing better judicial efficiency than reviving a settled case. (Evidence Code § 452, subd. (d).)

If Moving Defendants wish to consolidate the currently ongoing cases, Moving Defendants may file in the appropriate action to request such an order; the Court will review any such request on the merits.

 

IV.        CONCLUSION

Moving Defendants’ motion to consolidate is DENIED.

Opposing parties are ordered to give notice.
