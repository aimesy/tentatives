Tentative Ruling – Motion to Quash

 

 

 

I.        Background

 

On June 12, 2025, Plaintiff Annunziata Crupi (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Defendants Edward B. Moreton, Jr. (“Moreton”), Randolph M. Hammock (“Hammock”), University of Southern California (“USC”), Valter Longo (“Longo”), Sebastian Brandhorst (“Brandhorst”), Wendy Snaer (“Snaer”), Gregory Wong (“Wong”), Heather Cox (“Cox”), L-Nutra, Marta Alcumbrac (“Alcumbrac”), Howard Smith (“Smith”), Peter Walrod (“Walrod”), Candice Zee (“Zee”), Mark Schaeffer (“Schaeffer”), and Does 1 to 100 alleging twenty-three (23) causes of action: (1) civil conspiracy; (2) civil conspiracy to persecute, isolate, and defame; (3) constructive eviction; (4) violation of civil rights via affirmative acts; (5) infliction of public humiliation; (6) retaliation; (7) discrimination; (8) defamation; (9) tortious interference with contract; (10) tortious interference with prospective economic advantage; (11) invasion of privacy; (12) civil harassment; (13) abuse of process; (14) fraud in arbitration; (15) economic coercion and duress; (16) declaratory and injunctive relief; (17) loss of consortium and familial association; (18) violation of constitutional right to travel; (19) violation of right to intimate association and family life; (20) violation of right to reproductive liberty and family formation; (21) unfair business practices; (22) intentional infliction of emotional distress; and (23) negligent infliction of emotional distress.

 

On July 14, 2026, Smith filed the instant motion to quash service of summons and complaint.

 

To date, the motion remains unopposed.

 

II.     Discussion

 

Cal. Code of Civ. Proc. § 418.10(a)(1) permits quashing of service of summons and complaint when there was no valid service of summons. (See CCP § 418.10(a)(1).) CCP § 415.10 governs personal service of summons and requires personal, physical delivery of a copy of the summons and complaint to the person to be served. (See CCP § 415.10.)

 

Smith brings the instant motion on the grounds that, on July 9, 2026, Plaintiff attempted service on Smith by serving Smith’s counsel with the pleadings via electronic email. (Stone Decl. ¶ 7; Exh. 8.) Accordingly, Plaintiff did not perform service via “personal delivery” as required by CCP § 415.10.

 

III.  Conclusion

 

Based on the foregoing, specially appearing defendant Howard Smith’s motion to quash service of summons is GRANTED.

I.       BACKGROUND  

On March 3, 2026, Plaintiff Rosa Marquez (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Defendants FM Restaurants HQ LLC (“FM Restaurants”), Eduardo Munoz (“Munoz”), and Does 1 to 100 alleging seven causes of action: (1) failure to pay overtime compensation; (2) failure to pay minimum wage; (3) failure to provide meal periods; (4) failure to provide rest breaks; (5) failure to pay wages due upon termination; (6) failure to issue accurate and itemized wage statements; and (7) unlawful business practices.

On June 8, 2026, FM Restaurants filed the instant motion to compel arbitration.

On July 2, 2026, Plaintiff filed her opposition.

On July 9, 2026, FM Restaurants filed its reply.

II.          LEGAL STANDARD

In deciding a motion to compel arbitration, trial courts must first decide whether an enforceable arbitration agreement exists between the parties and then determine the second gateway issue of whether the claims are covered within the scope of the agreement. (See Omar v. Ralphs Grocery Co. (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 955, 961.)(See also Chiron Corp. v. Ortho Diagnostic Systems, Inc. (9th Cir. 2000) 207 F. 3d 1126, 1130 [“The court’s role under the [Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”)] is therefore limited to determining (1) whether a valid agreement to arbitrate exists and, if it does, (2) whether the agreement encompasses the dispute at issue.”]. “The petitioner bears the burden of proving the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by the preponderance of the evidence, and a party opposing the petition bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence any fact necessary to its defense.” (Engalla v. Permanente Medical Group, Inc. (1997) 64 Cal.Rptr.2d 903, 915-16.) “In these summary proceedings, the trial court sits as a trier of fact, weighing all the affidavits, declarations, and other documentary evidence, as well as oral testimony received at the court’s discretion, to reach a final determination.” (Id. at 916.) No jury trial is available for a petition to compel arbitration. (Ibid.)

Accordingly, under both the FAA and California Law, arbitration agreements are valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, except on such grounds that exist at law or equity for voiding a contract. (Winter v. Window Fashions Professions, Inc. (2008) 166 Cal.App.4th 943, 947.) 

 

III.      DISCUSSION

Evidentiary Objections

            As a preliminary matter, FM Restaurants’s July 9, 2026, evidentiary objection to the declaration of Rosa Marquez (“Marquez”) is sustained since Marquez declares that she is a native Spanish speaker, not fluent in English, and the declaration fails to include a certified translation. (See Marquez Decl. at ¶¶ 1 and 3-4; see also Fonseca v. Halol (C.D. Cal 2008) 568 F.Supp.2d 1110, 1125.)

 

Existence of a Valid Arbitration Agreement

FM Restaurants proves the existence of a valid arbitration agreement with Plaintiff.

Plaintiff’s claims arise from her employment with FM Restaurants and FM Restaurants’s alleged failure to pay her wages owed. (Complaint at ¶¶ 9-14.) On November 19, 2018, Plaintiff was presented with a written document entitled, in bolded and underlined text, “Agreement for Individual Arbitration of Covered Disputes and Related Provisions.” (See Franklin Decl. Exh. 1)(hereinafter the “Agreement”). The Agreement provides that the parties:

“. . . mutually agree that any and all covered disputes (as defined below) that either party

has the legal right to sue for in court shall be subject to final and binding arbitration under

the arbitration provisions set forth below and not decided by a court or jury . . . The term

‘covered disputes’ is defined to mean disputes and claims . . . arising out of or related in

any manner to your employment with [FM Restaurants] or any predecessor employer,

arising under or related to this Agreement, or arising under any federal or state statutes

and laws, municipal ordinances, executive orders, contracts, civil wrongs, or common

law . . . including but not limited to any and all types of claims regarding wages, wage

payments, wage notices, meal times, working conditions, accommodations,

discrimination, labor relations, retaliation, whistleblowing, employee benefits, expense

reimbursement, termination pay, fringe benefits, paid time off, payroll taxes . . .”


            (Franklin Decl. Exh. 1.)

In opposition, Plaintiff first argues that “Plaintiff created a factual dispute about the validity of the arbitration agreement by submitting a declaration denying that she agreed to arbitrate any of her claims with [FM Restaurants].” (Opposition at 4:25-27.) However, as discussed supra, Marquez’s declaration is inadmissible.

Next, Plaintiff argues that the Agreement is unenforceable due to unconscionability. Specifically, Plaintiff argues the contract was provided on a “take it or leave it basis.” (Opposition at 8:19-5 [citing Armendariz v. Found. Health Psychcare Servs., Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 83, 114.) “When an employee is required to consent to arbitration as a condition of employment with no opportunity to negotiate, the agreement is procedurally unconscionable.” (Armendariz, supra, 24 Cal.4th at 114-115.) As previously discussed, the Agreement is in English and Plaintiff is a native Spanish speaker. (See discussion supra at 2:24-3:4.)

In Nunez v. Cycad Mgmt. LLC (2022) 77 Cal.App.5th 276, the Court held that the employer’s failure to provide a Spanish-speaking gardener with a Spanish version of the arbitration agreement “demonstrated oppression and surprise amounting to procedural unconscionability,” specifically because the employer knew the employee was not proficient in English, did not explain the arbitration agreement, and handed the employee the agreement without allowing ample time to review, ask questions, or obtain a translation. (Nunez, supra, 77 Cal.App.5th at 284.) Similarly, in Subcontracting Concepts (CT) LLC v. De Malo (2019) 34 Cal.App.5th 201, the Court found unconscionability when the employee was not fluent enough in English to understand the arbitration agreement, was not told what arbitration meant, and was told he had to sign for employment. (Subcontracting Concepts (CT) LLC, supra, 34 Cal.App.5th at 206.) Similarly, here, Plaintiff has worked with FM Restaurants since 1999 (see Motion at 2:19-3:3) and FM Restaurants’s agents only spoke to Plaintiff in Spanish. (Ibid.) FM Restaurants was aware of Plaintiff’s inability to speak or understand English. (Ibid.) Accordingly, pursuant to Nunez and Subcontracting Concepts, the arbitration agreement is unenforceable as unconscionable.

III.    CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, the instant motion to compel arbitration is DENIED.

I.       BACKGROUND  

            On March 13, 2026, Plaintiffs Nancy Dewi (“Dewi”), Cymax Pathernia, LLC (“Cymax Pathernia”), Cymax Holding, LLC dba Cymax Homes (“Cymax Homes”)(collectively “Plaintiffs”) filed a complaint against Defendant Hertha Tan (“Defendant”) and Does 1 to 10 alleging six causes of action: (1) quiet title; (2) breach of joint venture agreement; (3) breach of fiduciary duty; (4) partition by sale; (5) appointment of partition referee; and (6) accounting.

            On June 22, 2026, Defendant filed a cross-complaint against Plaintiffs alleging eighteen causes of action: (1) financial elder abuse; (2) fraud/fraudulent inducement; (3) undue influence; (4) rescission of contract; (5) cancellation of instruments; (6) declaratory relief; (7) quiet title; (8) breach of fiduciary duty; (9) accounting; (10) conversion; (11) trespass; (12) intentional infliction of emotional distress; (13) fraudulent concealment; and (14) constructive trust.

            On June 22, 2026, Defendant also filed the instant demurrer to and motion to strike portions of Plaintiffs’ complaint.

            On July 29, 2026, Plaintiffs filed their oppositions.

            On August 5, 2026, Defendant filed her replies.

 

II.          LEGAL STANDARDS

Demurrer

            A demurrer is a pleading that may be used to test the legal sufficiency of the factual allegations in a complaint. (Cal. Code of Civ. Proc. § 430.10.) There are two types of demurrers: general demurrers and special demurrers. (See McKenney v. Purepac Pharmaceutical Co. (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 72, 77.)

            General demurrers can be used to attack pleadings for failure to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action or for lack of subject matter jurisdiction. (CCP § 430.10(e); McKenney, supra, 167 Cal.App.4th at 77.) Such demurrers can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable; evidence or extrinsic matters are not considered. (CCP §§ 430.30 and 430.70; Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318; Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) To test the sufficiency of the cause of action, the Court admits “all material facts properly pleaded” and “matters which may be judicially noticed,” but does not consider contentions, deductions, or conclusions of fact or law. [Citation].” (Blank, supra, 39 Cal.3d at 318.) The Court gives these facts “a reasonable interpretation, reading it as a whole and its parts in their context.” (Ibid.) At the pleading stage, a plaintiff need only allege ultimate facts sufficient to apprise the defendant of the factual basis for the claim against him. (Semole v. Sansoucie (1972) 28 Cal. App. 3d 714, 721.) The face of the complaint includes exhibits attached to the complaint. (Frantz v. Blackwell (1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 91, 94.) "If facts appearing in the exhibits contradict those alleged, the facts in the exhibits take precedence." (Holland v. Morse Diesel Intern., Inc. (2001) 86 Cal.App.4th 1443, 1447.)

            CCP § 430.41 requires that “[b]efore filing a demurrer pursuant to this chapter, the demurring party shall meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer.” (CCP § 430.41(a).) The parties are to meet and confer at least five days before the date the responsive pleading is due. (CCP § 430.41(a)(2).) Thereafter, the demurring party shall file and serve a declaration detailing their meet and confer efforts. (CCP § 430.41(a)(3).)

            When a demurrer is sustained, the Court determines whether there is a reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment. (Blank, supra, 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) When a plaintiff “has pleaded the general set of facts upon which his cause of action is based,” the court should give the plaintiff an opportunity to amend his complaint, since plaintiff should not “be deprived of his right to maintain his action on the ground that his pleadings were defective for lack of particulars.” (Reed v. Norman (1957) 152 Cal.App.2d 892, 900.) Generally, the court will allow leave to amend on at least the first try, unless there is absolutely no possibility of overcoming the issue. (See Angie M. v. Superior Court (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 1217, 1227 [“Denial of leave to amend constitutes an abuse of discretion unless the complaint shows on its face it is incapable of amendment. (Citation.) Liberality in permitting amendment is the rule, if a fair opportunity to correct any defect has not been given."].)

Motion to Strike

            The court may, upon a motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (Cal. Code of Civ. Proc. § 436(a).) The court may also strike all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Id. at (b).) The grounds for a motion to strike are that the pleading has irrelevant, false or improper matter, or has not been drawn or filed in conformity with laws. (Ibid.) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (CCP § 437.)

Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Vaccaro v. Kaiman (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 761, 768 [“When the defect which justifies striking a complaint is capable of cure, the court should allow leave to amend.”].) The burden is on the complainant to show the Court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Blank, supra, 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

Before filing a motion to strike, the moving party shall meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to the motion to strike. (CCP § 435.5(a).)

 

III.       DISCUSSION

Meet and Confer

            Defense counsel declares that, on June 19, 2026, they met and conferred with Plaintiffs’ counsel regarding the instant demurrer and motion to strike via telephone. (Cai Decl. at ¶ 5.) The parties were unable to reach an agreement. (Id. at ¶ 6.) Accordingly, Defendant satisfies the meet and confer requirements of CCP §§ 430.41(a) and 435.5(a).

 

Demurrer

            Defendant demurs to Plaintiffs’ first, second, third, fourth, and fifth causes of action for quiet title, breach of joint venture agreement, breach of fiduciary duty, partition by sale, and appointment of partition referee, respectively, on the grounds that each cause of action fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. (See Demurrer at p. 2.)

 

 First Cause of Action – Quiet Title

            First, Defendant argues that Plaintiffs’ first cause of action for quiet title fails because “Plaintiffs do not plead the real property interests required by the quiet title statute, the Complaint contradicts itself, the dispute is an intra-LLC governance dispute governed by the Revised Uniform Limited Liability Companies Act (‘RULLCA’), and equity bars the relief sought.” (Demurrer at 14:23-15:4.)

            To successfully plead a cause of action for quiet title, a plaintiff must allege ultimate facts providing (1) a description of the property; (2) plaintiff’s title or interest and the basis; (3) defendant’s asserting adverse claim or antagonistic property interest; (4) date as of which the determination is sought; and (5) prayer for determination of title. (See CCP § 761.020; see also Lucas v. Sweet (1956 47 Cal.2d 20, 22 [sufficient to plead that plaintiff is the owner of described property and defendant wrongfully claims interest].) There is a “general rule that a cause of action to quiet title may be stated in general terms.” (Leeper v. Beltrami (1959) 53 Cal.2d 195, 214.) For a plaintiff to show the required element of having title where there is a contract between the parties, there must be something legally wrong with the contract justifying relief or rescission. (Id. at 216.) “[A]bsent an interest in the property, a party has no standing to ask the court to quiet title in the property or to obtain damages for the cloud on title.” (Chao Fu, Inc. v. Wen Ching Chen (2012) 206 Cal.App.4th 48, 59.)

            Here, Plaintiffs allege that this action “arises out of the breach of a joint venture agreement to renovate and rent the residential real property commonly known as 15925 Parthenia Street, North Hills, California 91343 (‘Property’) . . .” (Complaint at ¶ 1.) Plaintiff Cymax Pathernia was “formed for the sole purpose of rehabilitating and renting the Property, which is the sole asset of Cymax Pathernia.” (Id. at ¶ 3.) Plaintiffs Dewi and Cymax Homes both hold a 33.3% interest in Cymax Pathernia. (Id. at ¶¶ 2 and 4.) Defendant also holds a 33.3% interest in Cymax Pathernia. (Id. at ¶ 3.) Plaintiffs allege that Defendant transferred ownership of the Property to Cymax Pathernia on April 13, 2022. (Id. at ¶ 5.) Plaintiffs further allege that the terms of the joint venture to repair and rehabilitate the Property were set forth in the Cymax Pathernia Operating Agreement (the “Operating Agreement”), attached to Plaintiffs’ complaint as Exhibit 1. (Id. at ¶ 9; see Exh. 1 [Operating Agreement].) Plaintiffs allege that they have performed all duties pursuant to the Operating Agreement since March 31, 2022. (Complaint at ¶ 11.) Plaintiffs further allege that, since March 31, 2022, Defendant “has appeared at the Property unannounced and scared the tenants; withheld tax documentation required for the short-term rental permit; demanded monthly income although Cymax Pathernia has not yet realized a profit; and gleefully stated that she will move back into the Property, in clear violation of the Operating Agreement.” (Id. at ¶ 12.) On March 6, 2026, Plaintiffs received a letter from defense counsel stating that Defendant repudiated the Operating Agreement. (Id. at ¶ 13; Exh. 2 [correspondence].) Plaintiffs allege that the repudiation letter constitutes a material breach of the Operating Agreement, thereby depriving Plaintiffs of the benefit of their bargain. (Id. at ¶ 14.) Consequently, Plaintiffs seek to quiet title against the claims of Defendant as follows: Plaintiffs Dewi and Cymax Homes have a 66.6% interest in the Property; Defendant has a 33.3% interest in the Property. (Id. at ¶ 18.)

            Defendant argues that “[Plaintiffs’] alleged interests are membership interests in Cymax Parthenia, LLC. Cymax Parthenia, LLC, in turn, allegedly holds title to the Property. Plaintiffs therefore attempt to quiet title based on LLC membership and internal ownership allegations, not direct real property interests.” (Demurrer at 15:6-24.) Defendant cites Cal. Corp. Code § 17705.1, providing that “[a] transferable interest is personal property.” (See Corp. Code § 17705.1.) Notably, in Kwok v. Transnation Title Ins. Co. (2009) 170 Cal.App.4th 1562, the court held that “as members of [an] LLC, appellants never held an ownership interest in the property to which the LLC held title.” (Kwok, supra, 170 Cal.App.4th at 1570.) “A member or assignee has no interest in specified limited liability company property.” (Denevi v. LGCC, LLC (2004) 121 Cal.App.4th 1211, 1214.)

            Finally, Defendant argues that equity bars Cymax Parthenia from quieting title against Defendant, whose property it allegedly acquired by fraud. (Demurrer at 17:24-18:15.) Notably, this argument will not be considered upon a demurrer, where a court only considers the sufficiency of the pleadings as alleged. (See Semole, supra, 28 Cal. App. 3d at 721.)

            Notwithstanding the foregoing, Defendant’s demurrer to Plaintiffs’ first cause of action for quiet title is sustained with leave to amend on the grounds that Plaintiffs fail to allege an ownership interest in the real property.

 

Second Cause of Action – Breach of Joint Venture Agreement

            Next, to successfully plead a cause of action for breach of joint venture agreement, a plaintiff must allege ultimate facts showing (1) joint interest in a common business; (2) with an understanding to share profits and losses; and (3) a right to joint control. (April Ent., Inc. v. KTTV (1983) 147 Cal.App.3d 805, 818-820.) Joint venturers have the fiduciary duty to act with the highest good faith towards each other as to affairs of the joint venture. (Pellegrini v. Weiss (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 515, 524.) “Whether a partnership or joint venture exists is primarily a factual question to be determined by the trier of fact from the evidence and inferences to be drawn therefrom.” (Filippo Industries, Inc. v. Sun Ins. Co. of N.Y. (1999) 74 Cal.App.4th 1429, 1444.)

            See discussion of Plaintiffs’ complaint supra at 6:3-7:2. Plaintiffs further allege that Defendant materially breached the joint venture agreement by sending Dewi and Cymax Homes a letter from her attorney wherein Defendant repudiated the Operating Agreement without cause and subsequently terminated the joint venture. (Complaint at ¶ 22.)

            In her demurrer, Defendant argues that the parties’ agreement is an LLC Operating Agreement, not a joint venture agreement. (Demurrer at 18:24-19:13.) Defendant cites Section 4.1 which gives Dewi, as Managing Member, “complete and exclusive authority, power, and discretion to manage and control the business, property and affairs of the Company.” (Ibid. [citing Complaint Exh. 1 [Operating Agreement] at Sec. 4.1].) Therefore, Defendant argues, there is no right of joint control pursuant to Holtz v. Untied Plumbing & Heating Co. (1957) 49 Cal.2d 501, 506-507. The Court agrees.

            Even assuming, arguendo, that a joint venture agreement existed, the terms of the Operating Agreement provide that “[a]ny member may withdraw without the unanimous approval of the Class A Members.” (See Complaint Exh. 1 [Operating Agreement] at Sec. 3.2].) Based on the foregoing, Defendant’s demurrer to Plaintiffs’ second cause of action for joint venture agreement is sustained with leave to amend.

 

Third Cause of Action – Breach of Fiduciary Duty

            Next, to successfully plead a cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty, a plaintiff must allege ultimate facts showing (1) defendant owed plaintiff a fiduciary duty; (2) defendant breached that duty; and (3) damages. (Charnay v. Cobert (2006) 145 Cal.App.4th 170, 182; Stanley v. Richmond (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 1070, 1086.) Allegations of the ultimate fact of agency sufficiently allege a fiduciary relationship without any need for “explaining how this fact of agency originated . . .” (Skopp v. Weaver (1976) 16 Cal.3d 432, 439.) However, facts giving rise to a confidential, fiduciary or trustee relationship must be pled, and a “bare allegation that defendants assumed a fiduciary relationship” is an improper conclusion. (Zumbrun v. Univ. of So. Cal (1972) 25 Cal.App.3d 1, 13.) A fiduciary relationship may be alleged when one “must either knowingly undertake to act on behalf and for the benefit of another, or must enter into a relationship which imposes that undertaking as a matter of law.” (Oakland Raiders v. National Football League (2005) 131 Cal.App.4th 621, 632.)

            See discussion of Plaintiffs’ complaint supra at 6:3-7:2. Plaintiffs also allege that Defendant breached her fiduciary duties to Plaintiffs by “repudiating and terminating the joint venture, without cause . . . thereby preventing plaintiffs from accomplishing the joint venture goals and making a profit . . . As a result of such conduct, plaintiffs have incurred substantial damages including loss of money and time, entitling plaintiffs to compensatory damages in excess of $500,000 . . .” (Complaint at ¶ 27.)

            In her demurrer, Defendant argues that no joint venture was formed, so no joint venture fiduciary duties exist. (Demurrer at 22:1-9.) Defendant further argues that the Revised Uniform Limited Liability Company Act (“RULLCA”) only imposes fiduciary duties on managers – not passive, non-managing members. (Id. at 22:11-24.) Again, as discussed supra, the Operating Agreement permits withdrawal, at any time, without the approval of Class A Members. (See Complaint Exh. 1 [Operating Agreement] at Sec. 3.2.) Accordingly, Plaintiffs fail to plead any breach of fiduciary duty.

            Defendant’s demurrer to Plaintiff’s third cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty is sustained with leave to amend.

 

Fourth and Fifth Causse of Action – Partition by Sale and Appointment of Partition Referee

            Next, to successfully plead a cause of action for partition by sale, a plaintiff must plead coownership of real property. (See CCP § 871.210(a).) As discussed supra at 7:3-13, Plaintiffs fail to plead an ownership interest in the real property. Plaintiffs’ fifth cause of action for appointment of partition referee fails for the same reason. Accordingly, Defendant’s demurrer to Plaintiffs’ fourth and fifth causes of action for partition by sale and appointment of partition referee are sustained with leave to amend.

 

Motion to Strike

            Next, Defendant moves to strike language from Plaintiffs’ complaint regarding punitive damages, attorney’s fees, and allegations that Defendant acted with oppression, fraud, or malice. (See Motion at p. 1.)

            To state a claim for punitive damages under Cal. Civ. Code § 3294, a plaintiff must allege specific facts showing that the defendant has been guilty of malice, oppression or fraud.

(Smith v. Superior Court (1992) 10 Cal. App. 4th 1033, 1042.) The basis for punitive damages must be pled with specificity; conclusory allegations devoid of any factual assertions are insufficient. (Id.) A motion to strike may lie where the facts alleged, if proven, would not support a finding that the defendant acted with malice, fraud or oppression. (Turman v. Turning Point of Central California (2010) 191 Cal. App. 4th 53, 63.)

            Here, the basis of Plaintiffs’ claims are that Defendant exercised her right to withdraw from the Operating Agreement. (See discussion of Plaintiffs’ complaint supra at 6:3-7:2.) Therefore, Plaintiffs fail to plead that Defendant acted with oppression, fraud, or malice. Defendant’s motion to strike is granted as to punitive damages and related language.

            Next, the Court addresses Defendant’s motion to strike Plaintiffs’ request for attorney’s fees. Attorney’s fees are only recoverable when authorized by contract, statute or law. (CCP § 1021; Reid v. Valley Restaurants, Inc. (1957) 48 Cal.2d 606, 610.) In opposition, Plaintiffs note that paragraph 12.7 of the Operating Agreement provides for recovery of attorney’s fees in the event of a dispute between the Company or its members. (See Complaint Exh. 1 at ¶ 12.7.) Accordingly, Defendant’s motion to strike Plaintiffs’ request for attorney’s fees is denied.

 

Leave to Amend

Generally, the court will allow leave to amend on at least the first try. (See Angie, supra, 37 Cal.App.4th at 1227 [“Denial of leave to amend constitutes an abuse of discretion unless the complaint shows on its face it is incapable of amendment. (Citation.) Liberality in permitting amendment is the rule, if a fair opportunity to correct any defect has not been given."].)

Based on the foregoing, Defendant’s demurrer to Plaintiffs’ complaint is sustained in its entirety with leave to amend.

Defendant’s motion to strike Plaintiffs’ request for punitive damages and related language is granted with leave to amend.

IV.    CONCLUSION

Defendant’s demurrer is SUSTAINED in its entirety with leave to amend.

Defendant’s motion to strike punitive damages and related language is GRANTED with leave to amend.

Defendant’s motion to strike attorney’s fees is DENIED.

Tentative Ruling - Motion to Consolidate

 

 

 

I.        Background

 

On May 29, 2026, Plaintiff Razieh Shaidi (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Defendants Ahang Mirshojae (“Ahang”), Evan Hardman (“Hardman”), Ashley Rose Sweeting (“Sweeting”), Sarallah Jawed (“Jawed”), Shawn Randolph (“Randolph”), and Does 1 through 100 alleging three causes of action: (1) intentional infliction of emotional distress; (2) wrongful death; and (3) violation of the Uniform Voidable Transactions Act. Plaintiff also added Ghazal Simorgh (“Simorgh”), Avina Mirshojae, by and through her guardian ad litem Ghazal Simorgh (“Avina”), Jadon Babak Mirshojae (“Jadon”), and Hana Parivash Mirshojae (“Hana”) as nominal defendants.

 

On June 17, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion to consolidate the instant action (hereinafter the “Shahidi Action”) with LASC Case No. 25VECV04080 Simorgh, et al. v. Mirshojae, et al. (hereinafter the “Simorgh Action”).

 

To date, the motion remains unopposed.

 

II.     Motion to Consolidate

 

Cal. Code of Civ. Proc. § 1048 provides, in pertinent part, “When actions involving a common question of law or fact are pending before the court, it may order a joint hearing or trial of any or all the matters in issue in the actions; it may order all the actions consolidated and it may make such orders concerning proceedings therein as may tend to avoid unnecessary costs or delay.” (CCP § 1048(a).)

 

A motion to consolidate must satisfy the requirements of Cal. Rules of Court Rule 3.350, which provides in relevant part that a notice of motion to consolidate must:

 

“(A) List all named parties in each case, the names of those who have appeared, and the   names of their respective attorneys of record;

(B) Contain the captions of all the cases sought to be consolidated, with the lowest            numbered case shown first; and

(C) Be filed in each case sought to be consolidated.”

 

(CRC Rule 3.350(a).)

 

III.  Discussion

 

Plaintiff brings the instant motion on the grounds that the two actions “arise from the same death, allege the same murder-for-hire scheme, name the same five defendants, and turn on the same questions of liability, causation, conspiracy, and the defendants’ conduct.” (Motion at 6:9-16.)

 

Based on the foregoing, the Court agrees that good cause exists to consolidate the two actions. However, Plaintiff failed to file the instant motion in the Simorgh Action as required by CRC Rule 3.350(a)(C). (See CRC Rule 3.350(a)(C).) Additionally, Plaintiff is not a party to the Simorgh Action and, as such, lacks standing to bring the instant motion. (See CCP § 387.)

 

IV.  Conclusion

 

Based on the foregoing, the instant motion to consolidate is DENIED without prejudice.
