BACKGROUND

On December 19, 2025, Defendant’s counsel sent an email requesting new dates for the deposition.  (Geraghty Decl., ¶ 16.)

On March 10, 11, 12, and 19, 2026, Defendant’s counsel sent multiple emails to Plaintiff’s counsel requesting Plaintiff’s availability for her deposition.  (Geraghty Decl., ¶¶ 20-23, Exhs. 3.)  No response was received from Plaintiff’s counsel until March 20, 2026.  (Geraghty Decl., ¶ 24, Exh. 4.)  Plaintiff’s counsel asserted that Plaintiff “will need her daughter’s assistance with the deposition.  The daughter will be available the week of April 13, 2026 – April 17, 2026, so we can schedule the deposition that week.”  (Geraghty Decl., ¶ 24, Exh. 4.)

On March 24, 2026, Defendant’s counsel confirmed that April 15, 2026, was an acceptable date for the deposition.  (Geraghty Decl., ¶ 24, Exh. 4.)  On the same day, Defendant’s counsel served an amended deposition notice, scheduling the deposition for April 15, 2026, at 10:00 a.m. via Zoom.  (Geraghty Decl., ¶ 25, Exh. 5.)

On April 8, 2026, Plaintiff served objections to the deposition notice, asserting that “[t]his deposition was unilaterally noticed by Defendant for a date and time on which Plaintiff and/or Plaintiff’s counsel is unavailable.”  (Geraghty Decl., ¶ 26, Exh. 6.)

On the same day, Defendant’s counsel emailed Plaintiff’s counsel, reminding them that the deposition date had been mutually agreed upon and asserting that Plaintiff was in violation of the Court’s order.  (Geraghty Decl., ¶ 27, Exh. 7.)  Defendant’s counsel requested that “Plaintiff [] withdraw this objection and [] appear for deposition on the agreed-upon date in compliance with the Court’s Order.  Absent such confirmation, Defendant will seek Court intervention, including an order compelling Plaintiff’s appearance and the imposition of appropriate sanctions.”  (Geraghty Decl., ¶ 27, Exh. 7.)  Plaintiff’s counsel did not respond to this email.  (Geraghty Decl., ¶ 27.)

On April 8, 2026, Defendant’s counsel sent a follow-up email, informing Plaintiff that a failure to attend the deposition scheduled for April 15, 2026, would result in Defendant pursuing terminating, issue, and evidentiary sanctions.  (Geraghty Decl., ¶ 28, Exh. 8.)  Once again, Plaintiff’s counsel did not respond.  (Geraghty Decl., ¶ 28.)

On April 15, 2026, Defendant’s counsel attended the deposition; however, neither Plaintiff nor her counsel was present.  (Geraghty Decl., ¶ 29.)  Consequently, Defendant took a certificate of nonappearance.  (Geraghty Decl., ¶ 29.)

LEGAL STANDARD

If a party fails to comply with a court order compelling discovery responses or attendance at a deposition, the court may impose monetary, issue, evidence, or terminating sanctions.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2025.450, subd. (h), 2030.290, subd. (c), 2031.300, subd. (c), 2033.290, subd. (e).)  Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.030 provides that, “[t]o the extent authorized by the chapter governing any particular discovery method ... , the court, after notice to any affected party, person, or attorney, and after opportunity for hearing, may impose … [monetary, issue, evidence, or terminating] sanctions against anyone engaging in conduct that is a misuse of the discovery process[.]”  Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.010 states, “Misuses of the discovery process include, but are not limited to, the following: ... (d) Failing to respond or to submit to an authorized method of discovery... (g) Disobeying a court order to provide discovery.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.010.)

Upon finding a party has engaged in a misuse of the discovery process, the trial court has discretion to impose monetary, issue, or evidentiary sanctions.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030, subds. (a)-(c).)

“The court may impose a monetary sanction ordering that one engaging in the misuse of the discovery process, or any attorney advising that conduct, or both pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney’s fees, incurred by anyone as a result of that conduct.  The court may also impose this sanction on one unsuccessfully asserting that another has engaged in the misuse of the discovery process, or on any attorney who advised that assertion, or on both.  If a monetary sanction is authorized by any provision of this title, the court shall impose that sanction unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030, subd. (a).)  Issue sanctions may designate facts that shall be taken as established in the action in accordance with the claim of the party adversely affected by a misuse of the discovery process.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030, subd. (b).)  Evidentiary sanctions may prohibit any party engaging in the misuse of the discovery process from introducing designated matters in evidence.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030, subd. (c).)

“Discovery sanctions serve to remedy the harm caused to the party suffering the discovery misconduct.  [Citation.]  Because discovery sanctions are not designed to punish, ‘ “sanctions should be tailored to serve that remedial purpose, should not put the moving party in a better position than he would otherwise have been had he obtained the requested discovery, and should be proportionate to the offending party’s misconduct.” ’ [Citation.]”  (Kwan Software Engineering, Inc. v. Hennings (2020) 58 Cal.App.5th 57, 74 (Kwan).)  Discovery sanctions are matters squarely within the trial court’s discretion and will not be returned except in instances of extreme abuse.  (Liberty Mutual Fire Ins. Co. v. LcL Administrators, Inc. (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 1093, 1102.)

“The trial court may order a terminating sanction for discovery abuse ‘after considering the totality of the circumstances: [the] conduct of the party to determine if the actions were willful; the detriment to the propounding party; and the number of formal and informal attempts to obtain the discovery.’”  (Los Defensores, Inc. v. Gomez (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 377, 390, quoting Lang v. Hochman (2000) 77 Cal.App.4th 1225, 1246.)  “Generally, ‘[a] decision to order terminating sanctions should not be made lightly. But where a violation is willful, preceded by a history of abuse, and the evidence shows that less severe sanctions would not produce compliance with the discovery rules, the trial court is justified in imposing the ultimate sanction.’”  (Los Defensores, Inc. v. Gomez, supra, 223 Cal.App.4th at p. 390, citation omitted.)

“Under this standard, trial courts have properly imposed terminating sanctions when parties have willfully disobeyed one or more discovery orders.”  (Los Defensores, Inc. v. Gomez, supra, 223 Cal.App.4th at p. 390, citing Lang v. Hochman, supra, 77 Cal.App.4th at pp. 1244-1246 [discussing cases]; see, e.g., Collisson & Kaplan v. Hartunian (1994) 21 Cal.App.4th 1611, 1617-1622 [terminating sanctions imposed after defendants failed to comply with one court order to produce discovery]; Laguna Auto Body v. Farmers Ins. Exchange (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 481, 491, disapproved on other grounds in Garcia v. McCutchen (1997) 16 Cal.4th 469, 478, fn. 4 [terminating sanctions imposed against plaintiff for failing to comply with a discovery order and for violating various discovery statutes].)

 

DISCUSSION

Defendant moves the Court to impose terminating sanctions against Plaintiff due to her failure to comply with the Court’s order dated February 23, 2026, which mandated her appearance for deposition by March 23, 2026.  (Motion, pp. 1:3-2:3.)  In the alternative, Defendant seeks evidentiary sanctions that would bar Plaintiff from testifying at trial.  (Motion, p. 2:4-8.)  Additionally, Defendant requests monetary sanctions to recover costs incurred because of Plaintiff’s noncompliance.  (Motion, p. 2:9-10.)  The Court notes that the Defendant is not moving to compel Plaintiff’s deposition, despite what the title indicates.  

Terminating Sanctions

The imposition of terminating sanctions is unwarranted at this stage.  Notwithstanding Plaintiff’s willfully failed to comply with the February 23, 2026 order, there is no documented history of abuse on the part of Plaintiff, nor is there sufficient evidence to support the notion that less severe sanctions would be ineffective in ensuring compliance with the discovery rules.  (Los Defensores, Inc. v. Gomez, supra, 223 Cal.App.4th at p. 390.)

On January 22, 2026, Defendant filed its second motion to compel the deposition of Plaintiff.  (Geraghty Decl., ¶ 17.)  On February 23, 2026, the Court granted the motion, ordering that Plaintiff attend her deposition no later than March 23, 2026.  (Geraghty Decl., ¶ 18, Exh. 1.)  On February 25, 2026, Defendant’s counsel served the Notice of Ruling concerning this order on Plaintiff’s counsel.  (Geraghty Decl., ¶ 19, Exh. 2.)

On March 10, 11, 12, and 19, 2026, Defendant’s counsel sent multiple emails to Plaintiff’s counsel requesting Plaintiff’s availability for her deposition.  (Geraghty Decl., ¶¶ 20-23, Exhs. 3.)  No response was received from Plaintiff’s counsel until March 20, 2026.  (Geraghty Decl., ¶ 24, Exh. 4.)  Plaintiff’s counsel asserted that Plaintiff “will need her daughter’s assistance with the deposition.  The daughter will be available the week of April 13, 2026 – April 17, 2026, so we can schedule the deposition that week.”  (Geraghty Decl., ¶ 24, Exh. 4.)

On March 24, 2026, Defendant’s counsel confirmed that April 15, 2026, was an acceptable date for the deposition.  (Geraghty Decl., ¶ 24, Exh. 4.)  On the same day, Defendant’s counsel served an amended deposition notice, scheduling the deposition for April 15, 2026, at 10:00 a.m. via Zoom.  (Geraghty Decl., ¶ 25, Exh. 5.)

On April 8, 2026, Plaintiff served objections to the deposition notice, asserting that “[t]his deposition was unilaterally noticed by Defendant for a date and time on which Plaintiff and/or Plaintiff’s counsel is unavailable.”  (Geraghty Decl., ¶ 26, Exh. 6.)

On the same day, Defendant’s counsel emailed Plaintiff’s counsel, reminding them that the deposition date had been mutually agreed upon and asserting that Plaintiff was in violation of the Court’s order.  (Geraghty Decl., ¶ 27, Exh. 7.)  Defendant’s counsel requested that “Plaintiff [] withdraw this objection and [] appear for deposition on the agreed-upon date in compliance with the Court’s Order.  Absent such confirmation, Defendant will seek Court intervention, including an order compelling Plaintiff’s appearance and the imposition of appropriate sanctions.”  (Geraghty Decl., ¶ 27, Exh. 7.)  Plaintiff’s counsel did not respond to this email.  (Geraghty Decl., ¶ 27.)

On April 8, 2026, Defendant’s counsel sent a follow-up email, informing Plaintiff that a failure to attend the deposition scheduled for April 15, 2026, would result in Defendant pursuing terminating, issue, and evidentiary sanctions.  (Geraghty Decl., ¶ 28, Exh. 8.)  Once again, Plaintiff’s counsel did not respond.  (Geraghty Decl., ¶ 28.)

On April 15, 2026, Defendant’s counsel attended the deposition; however, neither Plaintiff nor her counsel was present.  (Geraghty Decl., ¶ 29.)  Consequently, Defendant took a certificate of nonappearance.  (Geraghty Decl., ¶ 29.)

Plaintiff contends that her failure to comply with the Court’s order was not willful.  She explains that her absence from the deposition scheduled for April 15, 2026, was due to factors beyond her control and a mistake by her counsel, who marked that the deposition had already occurred.  (Opp., p. 3:17-23; Declaration of Paul Peterson, ¶¶ 5-6.)  Furthermore, Plaintiff asserts that she “offered dates beginning on the very day the Court’s Order issued, and, after the deadline passed, proactively offered a further specific date of July 2, 2026.”  (Opp., p. 6:13-14.)  However, the Court finds these assertions unpersuasive.

Plaintiff does not specify the circumstances that prevented her from attending the deposition, nor does she provide evidence to show that she offered alternative deposition dates beyond those submitted on March 20, 2026, which she later canceled without justification.  The evidence indicates that her counsel neglected to communicate with Defendant or to propose alternative dates after raising an inaccurate objection to the agreed-upon deposition date.  This leads the Court to conclude that Plaintiff may be unwilling to fulfill her deposition obligations.

However, this incident marks Plaintiff’s first violation of a court order.  The defendant has not provided sufficient evidence to indicate that less severe sanctions would be ineffective in ensuring compliance with discovery obligations or the Court’s order.  Consequently, while the Court recognizes that Plaintiff willfully failed to comply with its order, it does not find terminating sanctions to be warranted at this stage.  Should Plaintiff continue to willfully disregard the Court’s order to appear for her deposition, the Court will consider the imposition of terminating sanctions.

Evidentiary Sanctions

The Court finds that the imposition of evidentiary sanctions is justified.  The requested sanctions are proportionate to Plaintiff’s conduct, particularly in light of her continued refusal to comply with the Court’s order.  (Kwan, supra, 58 Cal.App.5th at p. 74.)

The record demonstrates that Defendant has been attempting to schedule Plaintiff’s deposition for nearly one year.  (Geraghty Decl., ¶ 6.)  Plaintiff’s failure to appear for her deposition has hindered Defendant’s ability to evaluate Plaintiff’s claims, prepare for trial, and engage meaningfully in settlement negotiations.  (Motion, p. 1-3.)  Moreover, the Court notes that Plaintiff’s noncompliance has forced the Court to advance and vacate the trial date, delaying the resolution of this case.  (April 30, 2026, Minute Order, p. 1.)  As such, the evidentiary sanction of precluding Plaintiff from testifying at trial is GRANTED.

 

 

 

 

Monetary Sanctions

The defendant moves the Court for monetary sanctions in the amount of $2,232.50 against Plaintiff and her counsel to cover the attorneys’ fees and costs associated with bringing this motion.  (Motion, pp. 9:18-10:10.)

Pursuant to the Defendant’s counsel, Samantha M. Geraghty declaration, counsel spent 3.4 hours drafting the motion and conferring with Plaintiff’s counsel, billing at a rate of $275.00 per hour.  (Geraghty Decl., ¶ 31.)  Geraghty also anticipates that an additional 1.5 hours will be necessary to review the opposition and prepare the reply, and an estimated 3.0 hours to attend the hearing on this motion.  (Geraghty Decl., ¶ 31.)  Furthermore, Defendant incurred $60.00 in filing fees for this motion.  (Geraghty Decl., ¶ 31.)

Monetary Sanctions are warranted based on the Plaintiff misuse of the discovery process by failing to attend her deposition which was ordered and scheduled twice, and Counsel continuously ignoring of the Defendant’s attempts to schedule the deposition.  As such, Plaintiff’s request for monetary sanctions against in the amount of $2,232. 50 is GRANTED.

LEGAL STANDARDS

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b), discretionary and mandatory relief are available to parties from a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding.

Discretionary relief is available under the statute as “the court may, upon any terms as may be just, relieve a party or his or her legal representative from judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against him or her through his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b).)  “Application for this relief shall be accompanied by a copy of the answer or other pleading proposed to be filed therein, otherwise the application shall not be granted, and shall be made within a reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months, after the judgment, dismissal, order, or proceeding was taken.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b).)

Alternatively, mandatory relief is available when “accompanied by an attorney’s sworn affidavit attesting to his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b).)  Under this statute, an application for discretionary or mandatory relief must be made no more than six months after entry of the judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding from which relief is sought.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b); English v. IKON Business Solutions, Inc. (2001) 94 Cal.App.4th 130, 143.) “‘[W]hen relief under section 473¿is¿available, there is a strong public policy in favor of granting relief and allowing the requesting party his or her day in court[.]  [Citation.]”  (Rappleyea v. Campbell¿(1994) 8 Cal.4th 975, 981-982.)

“The court may, upon motion of the injured party, or its own motion, correct clerical mistakes in its judgment or orders as entered, so as to conform to the judgment or order directed, and may, on motion of either party after notice to the other party, set aside any void judgment or order.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (d).)  “Under section 473, subdivision (d), the court may set aside a default judgment which is valid on its face, but void, as a matter of law, due to improper service.”  (Ellard v. Conway (2001) 94 Cal.App.4th 540, 544.)

DISCUSSION

Defendant moves to vacate the Court's order dated May 18, 2026, which granted Plaintiff’s Motion for Issue Sanctions, Evidentiary Sanctions, and Adverse Jury Instruction Against Defendant the Vons Companies, Inc. for the Destruction of Video Surveillance Evidence.  (Notice of Motion, pp. 1:26-2:3; Macksoud Decl., Exh. B.)  Defendant seeks to vacate the order on the following grounds: (1) the proof of service associated with Plaintiff’s sanctions motion is facially defective; (2) the email transmission dated March 12, 2026, which purportedly served the sanctions motion, was not received by Defendant’s counsel or her assistant, rendering the order voidable or void due to lack of service; (3) the order infringes upon Defendant’s due process rights; and (4) relief is warranted under Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b), due to surprise and excusable neglect.  (Notice of Motion, pp. 2:11-3:27.) 

“Section 473 empowers a trial court to set aside any judgment or order that is void as a matter of law, for example, because the court lacked subject matter jurisdiction, or because the summons and complaint were not properly served, so that the court lacked personal jurisdiction over a defendant, or otherwise because the judgment or order violated a party’s due process rights to notice and an opportunity to be heard.  (§ 473, subd. (d); see Reid v. Balter (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 1186, 1194, 18 Cal.Rptr.2d 287 [dismissal order entered without notice and opportunity to be heard was void and subject to being set aside pursuant to section 473].)”  (Brown v. Williams (2000) 78 Cal.App.4th 182, 186, fn. 4.)

Code of Civil Procedure section 1005, subdivision (b), mandates that written notice must be provided to all parties prior to a court hearing on any motion.  This requirement ensures that the opposing party is afforded a reasonable opportunity to respond.  In the absence of adequate notice or an opportunity to be heard, an order constitutes “a clear violation of [the opposing party’s] due process rights[.]”  (Reid v. Balter (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 1186, 1193.)  “An elementary and fundamental requirement of due process in any proceeding which is to be accorded finality is notice reasonably calculated, under all the circumstances, to apprise interested parties of the pendency of the action [or motion] and afford them an opportunity to present their objections.”  (Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co. (1992) 339 U.S. 306, 314.)

In this matter, Defendant was not informed of Plaintiff’s sanctions motion until 6:07 p.m. on May 18, 2026, when Plaintiff’s counsel served the Notice of Ruling on Defendant’s counsel, Kori N. Macksoud.  (Macksoud Decl., Exh. C.)  Within three minutes, Macksoud responded, stating, “We never received notice of this motion[,]” and asked, “Who did you serve it upon?”  (Macksoud Decl., ¶ 5, Exh. C.)  Within fifty minutes, Plaintiff’s counsel requested another staff member to “please send Kori the service email[,]” while asserting that “[i]t was served on Kori and Alicia.”  (Macksoud Decl., ¶ 5, Exh. C.)  However, Macksoud asserted that “[n]either of us received it.”  (Macksoud Decl., Exh. C.)

Subsequently, Plaintiff’s counsel then forwarded an email stating that it included electronic service of the motion dated March 12, 2026, at 2:02 p.m.  (Macksoud Decl., ¶ 5, Exh. C.)  Upon receipt of the forwarded email, Macksoud “examined the proof of service filed with the motion and observed that it reflects a purported service date of March 5, 2026, rather than March 12, 2026.”  (Macksoud Decl., ¶¶ 5, 10, Exhs. C, G.)  Thus, considering Plaintiff’s counsel’s admission, the proof of service contains an incorrect service date.

Alicia Gonzalez, Macksoud’s litigation assistant, conducted an email search and confirmed the absence of any relevant emails from March 12, 2026; she included a screenshot of her inbox, junk email, archive, deleted items, recoverable items, all folders, and all mailboxes as evidence.  (Macksoud Decl., ¶ 6, Exh. D; Gonzalez Decl., ¶¶ 1-2, 4, Exhs. I-J.)  Macksoud conducted a similar search and provided comparable documentation.  (Macksoud Decl., ¶ 11, Exh. C.)

On March 20, 2026, Afinety, Macksoud’s external IT provider, confirmed: “You are correct that it is not in either users mailbox, but it has been too long to find out what happened.  The quarantine in O365 is only 30 days and Mimecast only has 30 days’ worth of logs.  And according to extended message tracking it looks like I misread the report.  It never got to exchange so it more than likely got blocked in Mimecast or was just never received by Mimecast.  Sorry I saw the email, but it was from Monday not March.”  (Macksoud Decl., Exh. F.)

This evidence overwhelmingly demonstrates that Defendant did not receive notice of the motion and, therefore, was denied the opportunity to be heard.  It appears that Plaintiff either did not send the motion or that Defendant’s counsel did not receive it due to their email security system.  Notably, Plaintiff’s conditional non-opposition brief does not contest any of the evidence presented.

Accordingly, the Defendant’s motion to vacate the order is GRANTED.

Request for Expedited Briefing Schedule

Non-Opp., p. 2:7-11.)  Defendant does not oppose this request in its reply.  (Reply, pp. 3:24-4:20.)  However, this request is not feasible for the Court’s calendar.  Instead, the Plaintiff’s underlying Motion for Issue Sanctions, Evidentiary Sanctions, and Adverse Jury Instruction is reset to August 7, 2026, at 1:30pm.

The Court considers the moving papers filed on June 5, 2026, the opposition brief filed on June 29, 2026, and the reply brief filed on July 6, 2026.

BACKGROUND

Factual Background

On February 24, 2026, plaintiff Christine Marie Stewart (“Plaintiff”) filed the Complaint against defendants Hawthorne School District (“HSD”), City of Hawthorne (“the City”), and DOES 1 through 20.  The Complaint includes the following causes of action: (1) Racial/Disability Discrimination (California Gov. Code § 12941 et seq.); (2) Gender Discrimination (California Gov. Code § 12941 et seq.); (3) Retaliation in Violation of Government Code § 12940(h) & Labor Code § 1102.5; (4) Harassment Based on Race/Gender/Disability in Violation of Government Code § 12923 et seq.; (5) Failure to Engage in the Interactive Process and to Provide Reasonable Accommodations; (6) Failure to Take All Reasonable Steps Necessary to Prevent Discrimination and Harassment from Occurring (California Government Code § 12940(k)); and (7) Wrongful Termination in Violation of FEHA (Government Code § 12940(h) et seq.).

On April 22, 2026, the City’s counsel emailed Plaintiff’s counsel to assert that the City did not employ Plaintiff and that the City and HSD are separate entities.  (Declaration of Jennie M. Kelly (“Kelly Decl.”), ¶ 2, Exh. 1.)  The City’s counsel requested a conversation with Plaintiff’s counsel to discuss these matters.  (Kelly Decl., ¶ 2, Exh. 1.)  Subsequently, on April 24, 2026, the City’s counsel issued a meet-and-confer letter to Plaintiff’s counsel, aimed at addressing the alleged deficiencies.  (Kelly Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. 2.)  However, Plaintiff’s counsel did not respond to either letter.  (Kelly Decl., ¶¶ 2-3.)

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

Filed concurrently with its moving papers, the City has filed a Request for Judicial Notice (“RJN”), requesting this Court take judicial notice of:

1.      City’s Codified Ordinance, Title One, Chapter 1.08, section 1.08.010; and

2.      City’s Codified Ordinance, Title One, Chapter 2.02, section 2.02.010.

The Court GRANTS this request.  The Court takes judicial notice of these ordinances as “[r]egulations and legislative enactments issued by or under the authority of the United States or any public entity in the United States[,]” in accordance with Evidence Code section 452, subdivision (b).  However, the Court will not take judicial notice of the truth of the matters presented in these documents since they are subject to interpretation.  (See L.B. Research & Education Foundation v. UCLA Foundation (2005) 130 Cal.App.4th 171, 180, fn. 2, citing Comings v. State Bd. of Education (1972) 23 Cal.App.3d 94.)

LEGAL STANDARD

A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable.  (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)  “To survive a demurrer, the complaint need only allege facts sufficient to state a cause of action; each evidentiary fact that might eventually form part of the plaintiff’s proof need not be alleged.”  (C.A. v. William S. Hart Union High School Dist. (2012) 53 Cal.4th 861, 872.)  For the purpose of testing the sufficiency of the cause of action, the demurrer admits the truth of all material facts properly pleaded.  (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 966-967.)  A demurrer “does not admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.”  (Daar v. Yellow Cab Co. (1967) 67 Cal.2d 695, 713.)

A pleading is uncertain if it is ambiguous or unintelligible.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (f).)  A demurrer for uncertainty may lie if the failure to label the parties and claims renders the complaint so confusing that the defendant cannot tell what he or she is supposed to respond to.  (Williams v. Beechnut Nutrition Corp. (1986) 185 Cal.App.3d 135, 139, fn. 2.)  However, “[a] demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.”  (Khoury v. Maly’s of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.)

MEET AND CONFER REQUIREMENT

Before filing a demurrer, the moving party is required to meet and confer in person, by telephone, or via video conference with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to the motion, to determine whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer or motion to strike.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a).)  The parties are to meet and confer at least five days before the date the responsive pleading is due.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(2).)  Thereafter, the moving party shall file and serve a declaration detailing their meet and confer efforts.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(3).)  A determination by the court that the meet and confer process was inadequate shall not be grounds to overrule or sustain a demurrer.  (Code Civil Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(4).)

On April 22, 2026, the City’s counsel emailed Plaintiff’s counsel to assert that the City did not employ Plaintiff and that the City and HSD are separate entities.  (Kelly Decl., ¶ 2, Exh. 1.)  The City’s counsel requested a conversation with Plaintiff’s counsel to discuss these matters.  (Kelly Decl., ¶ 2, Exh. 1.)  Subsequently, on April 24, 2026, the City’s counsel issued a meet-and-confer letter to Plaintiff’s counsel, aimed at addressing the alleged deficiencies.  (Kelly Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. 2.)  However, Plaintiff’s counsel did not respond to either letter.  (Kelly Decl., ¶¶ 2-3.)

Consequently, the Court finds that the City made sufficient attempts to meet and confer with Plaintiff prior to filing this demurrer.

DISCUSSION

Employment Relationship

The City demurs to the entirety of the Complaint on the grounds that Plaintiff fails to allege an employment or applicant relationship with the City.  (Dem., pp. 8:7-9:19.) 

“[B]ecause under the Tort Claims Act all governmental tort liability is based on statute, the general rule that statutory causes of action must be pleaded with particularity is applicable.  Thus, ‘to state a cause of action against a public entity, every fact material to the existence of its statutory liability must be pleaded with particularity.’  [Citations.]”  (Lopez v. Southern Cal. Rapid Transit Dist. (1985) 40 Cal.3d 780, 795.)

“In general, FEHA is designed to prevent and deter unlawful employment practices, and to redress their adverse effects.  [Citations.]  Essential to plaintiff’s statutory claims is the existence of ‘an employment relationship.’  [Citation.]”  (Patterson v. Domino’s Pizza, LLC (2014) 60 Cal.4th 474, 599.)  “[T]he FEHA confers employee status on those individuals who have been appointed, who are hired under express or implied contract, or who serve as apprentices.”  (Estrada v. City of Los Angeles (2013) 218 Cal.App.4th 143, 149.)

First, the City specifically contends that “the Complaint does not allege that the City hired, appointed or employed Plaintiff, does not alleged that the City paid Plaintiff’s wages, salary, or benefits, that the City supervised Plaintiff’s work, controlled the terms and conditions of Plaintiff’s employment or exercised management authority over Plaintiff.”  (Dem., pp. 8:27-9:5.) 

Yet, in the Complaint, Plaintiff alleges: “At all relevant times herein, defendants HSD, [the City] and ROES 1 to 20, inclusive, and each of them, inclusive were the employer of Plaintiff and were listed as such on Plaintiffs W-2 forms and pay stubs pursuant to Government Code §12968 and Labor Code §226.”  (Compl., ¶ 7.)  Thus, Plaintiff asserts that the City was one of her employers, as it is identified on her W-2 forms and pay stubs.  

Here, the Court finds this factual assertion sufficient to establish an employer relationship at the pleading stage.

Second, the City contends that this allegation is insufficient, drawing a comparison to Shields v. County of San Diego (1984) 155 Cal.App.3d 103, 113 (Shields), in which the Court of Appeal determined that the plaintiff offered “no allegations sufficient to infer there was, in fact, an employment or independent contractor relationship between County and the other defendants.”  (Dem., p. 5:23-28.)  However, the conclusion in Shields stemmed from the plaintiff merely alleging that “Sun Harbor and Westgate were ‘independent contractors‘ of County, that all defendants were agents and employees of one another, and that County ‘caused the commencement of an enterprise‘ which harmed the plaintiff.”  (Shields, supra, 155 Cal.App.3d at p. 113.)  In contrast, Plaintiff provides a specific factual basis by stating that the City is listed as her employer on her W-2 and pay stubs, which is sufficient to infer an employment relationship.

Accordingly, the demurrer is OVERRULED on this basis.

Separate Legal Entities

Moreover, the City demurs to the entirety of the Complaint on the grounds that the City and HSD “are separate public entities with distinct legal status, governance structures, and operational responsibilities.”  (Dem., pp. 9:22-23.) 

Here, the City contends that Plaintiff must establish alter-ego liability, a joint-employer relationship between the City and HSD, or an agency theory that would render the City liable for HSD’s actions due to their status as separate legal entities.  (Dem., pp. 10:15-11:18.)  However, the City does not adequately explain why the distinct legal status of these entities should result in the demurrer being sustained for the entire Complaint, particularly when Plaintiff has sufficiently alleged that both are her employers.

Accordingly, the demurrer is OVERRULED on this basis.

Seventh Cause of Action - Wrongful Termination in Violation of FEHA (Government Code § 12940(h) et seq.)

Finally, the City demurs to the seventh cause of action for wrongful termination in violation of FEHA (Government Code § 12940(h) et seq.) on the grounds that (1) Plaintiff fails to plead any factual assertions that demonstrate she was an employee of the City and (2) the cause of action is barred on the grounds that a common law tort claim for wrongful termination cannot be asserted against a public entity.  (Dem., pp. 11:19-12:27.) 

As discussed above, the Plaintiff has met the first ground. With respect to the second ground, Government Code section 815 provides: “Except as otherwise provided by statute[,] ... [a] public entity is not liable for an injury, whether such injury arises out of an act or omission of the public entity or a public employee or any other person.”  (Gov. Code, § 815.)  “In short, sovereign immunity is the rule in California; governmental liability is limited to exceptions specifically set forth by statute.”  (Cochran v. Herzog Engraving Co. (1984) 155 Cal.App.3d 405, 409.)

Here, Plaintiff contends that “In performing the acts and omissions alleged hereinabove, the defendants, and each of them, have violated public policy and the guarantees and provisions of law embodied in California Government Code, §§12940, 12945 et seq.; California Labor Code §233; California Constitution, Article I, §8; relevant provisions of the California Fair Employment and Housing Act; and relevant provisions of the California Family Rights Act in that defendants, and each of them, have discriminated against and harassed Plaintiff on the basis of Plaintiffs race, sex, gender and/or any related characteristics.”  (Compl., ¶ 111.)  Consequently, Plaintiff is not alleging a common law wrongful termination.

In the reply brief, the City introduces a new argument asserting that Plaintiff cannot “plead a wrongful termination claim under FEHA’s retaliation cause of action or under Labor Code section 1102.5[.]”  (Reply, pp. 8:26-9:1.)  The Court observes that Plaintiff is not asserting a wrongful termination claim under Labor Code section 1102.5.  Rather, she is presenting a wrongful termination claim under Government Code sections 12940 and 12945 et seq., in addition to California Labor Code section 233.  As such, Plaintiff has met the second ground, and the demurrer is OVERRULED on this basis.

Accordingly, the City’s demurrer is OVERRULED.

ORDERS

1)      Defendant City of Hawthorne’s Demurrer is OVERRULED.

2)      The City is ordered to provide notice of this Court’s ruling.
