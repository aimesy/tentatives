STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

           

This is an action for personal injury. Plaintiff alleges that a student at his school was bullying him, and Defendant did not discipline, intervene or attempt to prevent any harassment. Plaintiff alleges that on February 9, 2024, Plaintiff was brutally attacked by the student. Plaintiff claims he suffered a concussion, stroke and traumatic brain injury as a result.

(2)  The Court has reviewed the declaration of counsel in support of its fee request. The Court finds that the declaration substantiates 30% in attorney fees.

(3)  The proposed order is not completely filled out.

Case Number: 25STCV19527    Hearing Date: July 27, 2026    Dept: 507

Tentative Ruling

 

Judge Rolf M. Treu, Department 507

 

 

HEARING DATE:     July 27, 2026                          TRIAL DATE:  Not Set

                                                          

CASE:                         Masood A. Khan v. Hyundai Motor America

 

CASE NO.:                 25STCV19527

 

 

MOTION TO COMPEL ARBITRATION

 

MOVING PARTY:               Defendant Hyundai Motor America 

 

RESPONDING PARTY:      Plaintiff Masood A. Khan

 

CASE HISTORY:

  • 6/30/2025: Complaint filed.

 

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

           

This is a lemon law action.

 

            Defendant Hyundai Motor America (Defendant) moves to compel arbitration.

 

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

Defendant’s motion to compel arbitration is DENIED.

 

            Defendant to give notice, unless waived.

 

DISCUSSION:

 

Evidentiary Objections

 

Plaintiff’s objection is OVERRULED.

 

Request for Judicial Notice

 

            Defendant seeks judicial notice of the Complaint.

 

The request is granted.

 

Legal Standard

 

Parties may be compelled to arbitrate a dispute upon a finding that: (1) there was a valid agreement to arbitrate between the parties; and (2) the agreement covers the controversy or controversies in the parties’ dispute.¿ (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2; Omar v. Ralphs Grocery Co. (2004)¿118 Cal.App.4th 955, 961.) 

 

Motion to Compel Arbitration

 

Defendant moves to compel arbitration on grounds that there exists an agreement to arbitrate in the Owner’s Handbook & Warranty Information booklet that accompanied Plaintiff’s vehicle (the “Warranty Booklet”).  

 

Defendant argues Plaintiff is bound to arbitrate her dispute with Defendant because the Warranty Booklet states that the vehicle’s warranty is subject to the terms of a binding arbitration provision: 

 

If you purchased or leased your Hyundai vehicle in the State of California, you and we, Hyundai Motor America, each agree that any claim or disputes between us (including between you and any of our affiliated companies) related to or arising out of your vehicle purchase, advertising for the vehicle, use of your vehicle, the performance of the vehicle, any service relating to the vehicle, the vehicle warranty, representations in the warranty, or the duties contemplated under the warranty, including without limitation claims related to false or misleading advertising, unfair competition, breach of contract or warranty, the failure to conform a vehicle to warranty, failure to repurchase or replace your vehicle, or claims for a refund or partial refund of your vehicle's purchase price (excluding personal injury claims), but excluding claims brought under the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act, shall be resolved by binding arbitration at either your or our election, even if the claim is initially filed in a court of law.

 

(Ameripour Decl., Exh. 2, p. 12.)¿ 

 

In opposition, Plaintiff contends that Defendant has not established, and has presented no evidence, that the warranty Booklet was ever presented to Plaintiff prior to or at the time of purchase. Plaintiff argues the warranty booklet is not a contract.

 

 Defendant fails to show that the arbitration provision contained in the Warranty Booklet constituted an offer to which Plaintiff assented, thus the statements included in the Warranty Booklet do not amount to an enforceable contract.¿ (See Leslie v. Brown Bros. Inc. (1929) 208 Cal. 606, 621 (offer cannot be turned into agreement merely because the person to whom it is made makes no reply, even where offer states that silence will be taken as consent); see also Norcia v. Samsung Telecommunications America, LLC (9th Cir. 2017) 845 F.3d 1279, 1291 (offeree, regardless of apparent manifestation of her consent, is not bound by inconspicuous contractual provisions of which she was unaware, contained in a document whose contractual nature is not obvious); see Golden Eagle Ins. Co. v. Foremost Ins. Co. (1993) 20 Cal.App.4th 1372, 1385 (silence or inaction do not constitute acceptance).)

 

Because Defendant is without evidence that the statements regarding arbitration in the Warranty Booklet amount to a contract, those statements are not grounds on which the Court will order Plaintiff to arbitrate her dispute with Defendant.¿ (See Omar, supra, Cal.App.4th at p. 961.)

 

Defendant also argues Plaintiff should be estopped from asserting that the absence of her signature on a written contract precludes the enforcement of a contract’s arbitration clause, citing Boucher v. Alliance Title Co., Inc. (2005) 127 Cal.App.4th 262, 269.¿ However, this principle only applies where there is an enforceable contract that is defective because one of the parties failed to sign.¿ Here, Defendant presents insufficient evidence that the Subject Vehicle’s warranty constitutes an enforceable contract under basic principles of contract formation.¿ 

 

The warranty agreement included in the Warranty Booklet does not constitute an enforceable contract under principles of estoppel or foundational theories of contract formation.¿

 

Defendant presents no alternative theory for compelling arbitration.  

 

 

CONCLUSION:  

 

Accordingly, Defendant’s motion to compel arbitration is DENIED.

 

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Case Number: 25STCV29434    Hearing Date: July 27, 2026    Dept: 507

Tentative Ruling

 

Judge Rolf M. Treu, Department 507

 

 

HEARING DATE:     July 27, 2026              TRIAL DATE: NOT SET

                                                          

CASE:                         Clément Souyri v. Bryan Freedman, et al.

 

CASE NO.:                 25STCV29434

 

MOTION FOR ATTORNEY FEES AND COSTS PURSUANT TO CODE OF CIVIL PROCEDURE SECTION 425.16

 

MOVING PARTY:               Defendants Bryan Freedman and Liner Freedman Taitelman + Cooley, LLP

 

RESPONDING PARTY:     Plaintiff Clément Souyri, individually and as administrator of the estate of Travis Michael Flores

 

 

CASE HISTORY:

·       10/08/25: Complaint filed.

 

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

Defendants’ Motion for Attorney Fees is GRANTED in part. The Court awards Defendants $37,496.75 in attorney fees, $1,479.30 in costs, and $17,302 in connection with this fees motion, for a total of $56,278.05.

 

            Moving Party to give notice.

 

 

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

                       

This action is brought by the estate of Travis Michael Flores against Flores’ former lawyer, Bryan Freedman.

 

In 2021 and 2022, Freedman represented Flores (now deceased) in an idea-submission and copyright case against Justin Baldoni, a well-known director. In that case, Flores claimed he had originated the idea for a film, Five Feet Apart, created and directed by Baldoni. That case settled as a “walk-away” (i.e., without payment by either side). Freedman, Flores’ former lawyer, then represented Baldoni in a very high-profile litigation involving Baldoni and movie star Blake Lively.

 

The Lively/Baldoni litigation garnered worldwide attention. Freedman was asked by the press about the older lawsuit in which he represented a client adverse to Baldoni. Freedman responded by saying:

 

In the copyright infringement case, I learned what great people Justin and Wayfarer [Baldoni’s company] were. […] I learned that they did absolutely nothing wrong and that there was no liability on their part whatsoever. As a result, the case was resolved without any liability on Justin’s or Wayfarer or any other defendant’s part. This only further confirmed to me that Justin and Wayfarer are exceedingly honorable and highly ethical.

 

(Complaint ¶ 19; Exh. B; (“Media Statement”).) According to the Complaint and evidence submitted by Flores’s estate, this Media Statement (and similar statements by Freedman to the press) brought attention to the Flores’s earlier lawsuit, damaging the reputation of the late Flores and causing the estate itself to incur legal and other fees to deal with the press attention.

 

The estate brought the instant action. The Complaint alleges causes of action for breach of contract, breach of fiduciary duty, legal malpractice, invasion of privacy, and defamation.

 

The Complaint alleges three general bases for Freedman’s liability across the five causes of action. First, the estate alleges that Freedman committed malpractice, breach of contract, and breach of fiduciary duty in the original representation when Flores was Freedman’s existing client. For example, the estate alleges that Freedman advised Flores to settle his (allegedly) meritorious case for a walk-away. Second, the estate claims that the Media Statement (and similar statements to the press) constituted a breach of Freedman’s duties as an attorney to Flores as a former client. Most significantly, the estate alleges that the Media Statement violated Freedman’s duties to his former client because it shared confidences about the representation with the press. Third, the estate claims that Freedman committed torts going beyond his duties as an attorney, specifically that he defamed Flores’ character and invaded his privacy by issuing the Media Statement and similar press statements.

 

Freedman brought an anti-SLAPP motion. The Court concluded that first, to the extent it sought to impose liability for Freedman’s actions when Flores was an existing client, this is a legal malpractice case, to which the anti-SLAPP law does not apply.

 

Second, to the extent the malpractice, breach of contract, and breach of fiduciary duty allegations were based on Freedman’s breach of duties to Flores as a former client based on actions taken in a subsequent representation (Baldoni), the Court found that the anti-SLAPP law applies to Freedman’s Media Statement in connection with his representation of Baldoni (i.e., that imposing liability based on the Media Statement meets the first step of the anti-SLAPP law.) But ruled that the anti-SLAPP law did not apply to other alleged actions. However, because the Media Statement on its face could reasonably be construed to violate the fiduciary and other duties of an attorney to a former client, and because the statement is not otherwise privileged as Freedman argued, the estate showed that the breach of contract, breach of fiduciary duty, and legal malpractice claims based on the Media Statement have sufficient merit to proceed (i.e., the second step of the anti-SLAPP law is not satisfied).

 

Third, the Court ruled that the claims for defamation and invasion of privacy cannot go forward at all. Those claims are entirely based on the Media Statement and similar statements to the press. They are thus subject to the anti-SLAPP law in their entirety. The Court also reasoned that they also lack merit in their entirety. Since Flores is deceased, his estate cannot bring a defamation or invasion of privacy cause of action on Flores’s behalf.

 

            Now, Defendants move for attorney fees in connection with the partial grant of their anti-SLAPP motion.

 

DISCUSSION:

 

Request for Judicial Notice

 

            Plaintiff seeks judicial notice of three court filings. The request is GRANTED.

 

Evidentiary Objections

 

            Defendants’ objections are overruled.

 

Motion for Attorney Fees

 

Defendants move for attorney fees and costs in the amount of $56,098.88, which they contend represents 75% of the fees incurred to litigate the Anti-SLAPP motion (reflecting their partial success on that motion), plus $17,302 in additional fees to prepare this fees motion, the reply, and to attend the hearing, and $1,479.30 in costs, for a total of $74,880.18.

 

Entitlement to Attorney Fees

 

“[A] prevailing defendant on a special motion to strike shall be entitled to recover his or her attorney’s fees and costs.”  (Code Civ. Proc. § 425.16(c).)  “A party who partially prevails on an anti-SLAPP motion must generally be considered a prevailing party unless the results of the motion were so insignificant that the party did not achieve any practical benefit from bringing the motion.”  (City of Colton v. Singletary (2012) 206 Cal.App.4th 751, 782.)  The relevant test is “whether the defendant obtained a practical benefit.”  (Id. at p. 783.) In awarding fees to a partially prevailing party, the Court is to consider “the extent to which the defendant's litigation posture was advanced by the motion, whether the same factual allegations remain to be litigated, whether discovery and motion practice have been narrowed, and the extent to which future litigation expenses and strategy were impacted by the motion. The fees awarded to a defendant who was only partially successful on an anti-SLAPP motion should be commensurate with the extent to which the motion changed the nature and character of the lawsuit in a practical way. The court should also consider any other applicable relevant factors, such as the experience and abilities of the attorney and the novelty and difficulty of the issues, to adjust the lodestar amount as appropriate.”   (Mann v. Quality Old Time Serv., Inc. (2006) 139 Cal. App. 4th 328, 344-45.)   

 

Plaintiff does not dispute that Defendants were the prevailing party. The Court finds that Defendants were the prevailing party and entitled to fees due to achieving a practical benefit. However, Plaintiff argues that Defendants’ figure of 75% of the fees incurred does not comply with the Court ordered appointment. The Court noted in its minute order granting in part the anti-SLAPP motion that the award must be limited only to the success obtained on the motion—e.g., fees and costs incurred regarding briefing on the defamation and invasion of privacy causes of action.

 

            Reasonableness of the Fees

 

In determining whether the attorneys’ fees requested under Section 425.16 are “reasonable,” the Court “may and should consider the nature of the litigation, its difficulty, the amount involved, the skill required and the skill employed in handling the litigation, the attention given, the success of the attorney’s efforts, his learning, his age, and his experience in the particular type of work demanded . . . [,] the intricacies and importance of the litigation, the labor and necessity for skilled legal training and ability in trying the cause, and the time consumed.” (Church of Scientology v. Wollersheim (1996) 42 Cal.App.4th 628, 659, disapproved on other grounds by Equilon Enters. v. Consumer Cause, Inc. (2002) 29 Cal.4th 53, 68, fn. 5, quotation marks omitted, emphasis omitted.)¿ 

 

This approach “concentrates on the practical impact of a partially successful motion on the overall litigation[.]” (Id. at p. 347.) To that end, in adjusting the lodestar for overlapping work, the court considers such factors as (1) the extent to which the defendants’ litigation posture was advanced by the anti-SLAPP motion and appeal; (2) whether the same factual allegations remain to be litigated; (3) whether discovery and motion practice have been narrowed; (4) the extent to which future litigation expenses and strategy were impacted by the motion; and (5) any other applicable relevant factors, such as the experience and abilities of the attorneys and the novelty and difficulty of the issues. (Id. at p. 345.)¿ 

 

The successful defendant is also entitled to fees incurred in filing the motion for anti-SLAPP fees, also referred to as “fees on fees.” (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1141 [in anti-SLAPP, Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16 context].) 

 

i.                 Reasonableness of Hourly Rate

 

The lodestar calculation starts “with a determination of the ‘reasonable hourly rate,’ i.e., the rate ‘prevailing in the community for similar work.’” (Marshall, supra, 54 Cal.App.5th at p. 285 [citing PLCM Group, Inc. v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095.]  As a general rule, the “relevant ‘community’ is that where the court is located.” (Id. [quoting Altavion, Inc. v. Konica Minolta Systems Laboratory, Inc. (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 26, 71.)  

 

The Court finds that the hourly rates sought by Defendants are reasonable based on fee rates in the Los Angeles County area, the Real Rate Report, and the declarations of counsel supporting those rates. (Mot., Jassy Decl., ¶¶ 5-6, 8, 11 -12; Exh. 4; Excelsior Union High Sch. Dist. of L.A. Cnty. v. Lautrup (1969) 269 Cal.App.2d 434, 448.) 

 

Defendants’ counsel seek their hourly rate pursuant to an agreement with Defendants that they would pay a flat fee of $50,000 for the anti-SLAPP motion, and if the anti-SLAPP motion was successful, counsel could pursue and retain its full hourly rate as a fee enhancement and in turn, counsel agreed to absorb the loss of its billable time if the motion was not successful. Defendants cite to Dowling v. Zimmerman (2001) 85 Cal. App. 4th 1400, 1425 in support of their position.

 

An award of fees and costs on an anti-SLAPP motion must represent the reasonable market value of the services rendered, and need not follow the actual fees incurred by the prevailing party. In Dowling v. Zimmerman, supra, 85 Cal.App.4th 1400, the prevailing party only incurred only $1,300.00 in fees on the anti-SLAPP motion, but was awarded $9,300.00 based on the court’s assessment of what was reasonable. (Id. at pp. 1425–26.) Faced with the argument that an anti-SLAPP fee award “should not exceed the amount of attorney fees the defendant actually incurred,” the court stated, “We reject those contentions.” (Id. at p. 1425.) Thus, the Court finds that Defendants may seek the reasonable market value of their services rendered.

 

ii.               Reasonableness of the Hours Expended

 

Defendants’ Reply clarifies that the total time expended on the Anti-SLAPP motion was 104.6 hours. (Reply pg. 7; Jassy Suppl. Decl., ¶ 3; Exh. 5.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that Defendants failed to properly apportion their time and that the 75% figure is arbitrary. Plaintiff contends Exhibit 2 is built from time that demonstrably went to arguments, evidence, and drafting unrelated to the ground on which the defamation and privacy claims were struck. Plaintiff also contends that the billing records are too heavily redacted to permit review.

 

The Court finds that a 50% reduction in total hours is appropriate. This is because although Defendants attacked all five causes of action, they were successful in striking only two. The Court rejected Defendants’ defenses in step two of the analysis on the three causes of action that survived and found Plaintiffs showed their claims had minimal merit. Further, as to the two causes of action Defendants prevailed on, the second step of the analysis appeared to be an easy win based on black letter law. Thus, the bulk of the work that was successful was spent on step one of the analysis in connection with the Media Statements arising from protected speech. The Court also rejected the other alleged acts that Defendants claimed were protected speech.

 

Thus, the fee award is calculated as follows:   

 

Total hours of 104.6

 

Mr. Payne (56.3 x $650 = $36,595 – 50% = $18,297.50)

Mr. Jassy (48.3 x $795 = $38,398.50 – 50% = $19,199.25)

 

Total = $37,496.75

 

Costs

 

            Defendants seek costs in the amount of $1,479.30.

 

            Plaintiff challenges $1,000 of the costs, arguing that there is an unexplained deposition services invoice, but no deposition was taken in relation to the anti-SLAPP motion. In Reply, Defendant clarifies that this is for the Court Reporter for the hearing on the anti-SLAPP motion. Plaintiff had surmised that the fee be for the Court Reporter and did not challenge it.

 

Thus, the Court awards costs in the amount of $1,479.30.

 

Fees in Connection with the Fees Motion

 

Plaintiff challenged certain fees in connection with this motion as duplicative. In Reply, Defendants have stated this was an inadvertent error and corrected the amount sought.

 

Thus, the Court awards $12,302 in fees to prepare this fees motion, and $5,000 for the reply, and to attend the hearing for a total of $17,302.

 

CONCLUSION:

 

            Accordingly, Defendants’ Motion for Attorney Fees is GRANTED in part. The Court awards Defendants $37,496.75 in attorney fees, $1,479.30 in costs, and $17,302 in connection with this fees motion, for a total of $56,278.05.

 

            Moving Party to give notice.

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Case Number: 26STCV01696    Hearing Date: July 27, 2026    Dept: 507

Tentative Ruling

 

Judge Rolf M. Treu, Department 507

 

 

HEARING DATE:       July 27, 2026                                TRIAL DATE:  None Set

                                                          

CASE:                         Karleen Cordova v. Charges Football Company, LLC, et al.

 

CASE NO.:                 26STCV01696

 

 

MOTION TO COMPEL ARBITRATION AND STAY PROCEEDINGS

 

MOVING PARTY:               Defendant Chargers Football Company, LLC

 

RESPONDING PARTY(S): Plaintiff Karleen Cordova

 

PROOF OF SERVICE:

·       Correct Address: Yes.

·       16/21 (CCP § 1005(b)): (1)-(2) OK. Served electronically on March 10, 2026, for a hearing date of October 7, 2026.

 

CASE HISTORY:

·       01/16/26: Complaint filed.

·       03/10/26: Motion to Compel Arbitration and Stay Proceedings filed.

·       07/14/26: Opposition filed.

·       07/20/26: Reply filed.

 

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

           

In this employment law action, Plaintiff Karleen Cordova (“Plaintiff”) alleges (among other things) that her former employer, Defendant Chargers Football Company, LLC (“Defendant”), discriminated and wrongfully terminated her after she became pregnant and subsequently requested accommodations to care for her disabled son.

            Defendant now moves to compel Plaintiff’s claims to arbitration pursuant to the Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”). Plaintiff opposes the motion but does not dispute that the FAA governs the agreement. 

 

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

Defendant’s motion to compel arbitration and to stay proceedings pending completion of arbitration is GRANTED.

 

Defendant to give notice. 

 

DISCUSSION:

 

Motion to Compel Arbitration and Stay Proceedings

 

Legal Standard

 

Under the FAA, “a written agreement to arbitrate ‘shall be valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, save upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract ....’ (9 U.S.C. § 2.)” (Ford Motor Warranty Cases (2025) 17 Cal.5th 1122, 1128.)

 

“On a motion to compel arbitration, the moving party bears the burden of proving the existence of an arbitration agreement, and the opposing party bears the burden of proving any defense to enforcement, such as unconscionability.” (Doss v. Tesla, Inc. (2026) 121 Cal.App.5th 81, 89.) On such motions, “‘the court must first determine whether the parties actually agreed to arbitrate the dispute. [Citations.] General principles of California contract law guide the court in making this determination.’ [Citation.]” (Ford Motor Warranty Cases, supra, 17 Cal.5th at p. 1128.) “‘[D]ue regard must be given to the federal policy favoring arbitration and, as a result, ambiguities as to the scope of the arbitration clause should be resolved in favor of arbitration. [Citations.]” (LaCour v. Marshalls of CA, LLC (2025) 117 Cal.App.5th 505, 521.)

 

Evidentiary Objections

 

The Court overrules Plaintiff’s objections (i.e., Nos. 1 to 5) to the declaration of Dayreca Sims (“Sims”), Defendant’s Director of Payroll & Benefits. Defendant did not file objections.

 

Existence of Arbitration Agreement

 

As Defendant argues in its papers, a party moving to compel arbitration “‘can meet its initial burden by attaching to the petition a copy of the arbitration agreement purporting to bear the [opposing party’s] signature.’ [Citation.] Alternatively, the moving party can meet its burden by setting forth the agreement’s provisions in the motion. [Citations.] For this step, ‘it is not necessary to follow the normal procedures of document authentication.’ [Citation.]” (Gamboa v. Northeast Community Clinic (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 158, 165 (Gamboa).)

 

The Court finds that Defendant has met its initial burden by submitting a copy of the agreement purporting to bear Plaintiff’s signature. (See Sims Decl., Exh. A – a copy of the “Arbitration Agreement and Acknowledgement”.) The agreement states in relevant part:

 

“As a condition of my employment and/or continuing employment, I agree and acknowledge through this Arbitration Agreement and Acknowledgment form (‘Arbitration Agreement’) that the Chargers Football Company, LLC (the ‘Company’) and I will utilize binding arbitration to resolve all disputes arising out of or relating to my employment with the Company. Both the Company and I mutually agree that any claim, dispute and/or controversy that either I may have against the Company (or its owners, directors, officers, managers, employees, and agents) or the Company may have against me, arising from, related to, or having any relationship or connection whatsoever with my seeking employment with, employment by, or other association with the Company, shall be submitted to and determined exclusively by binding arbitration under the Federal Arbitration Act (‘FAA’), in conformity with the procedures of the California Arbitration Act (California Code of Civil Procedure § 1280, et seq., including § 1283.05 and all of the Act’s other mandatory and permissive rights to discovery). Included within the scope of this Arbitration Agreement are all disputes, whether based in tort, contract, statute, equitable law, or otherwise.”

 

(Id. at p. 61, the first paragraph [italics added].)

 

“If the moving party meets its initial prima facie burden and the opposing party disputes the agreement, then in the second step, the opposing party bears the burden of producing evidence to challenge the authenticity of the agreement.” (Gamboa, supra, 72 Cal.App.5th at p. 165 [emphasis added].) “The opposing party can do this in several ways. For example, the opposing party may testify under oath or declare under penalty of perjury that the party never saw or does not remember seeing the agreement, or that the party never signed or does not remember signing the agreement.” (Ibid.)

 

Here, Plaintiff has failed to meet her burden by failing to submit any evidence (e.g., her affidavit) challenging the Arbitration Agreement’s authenticity. Plaintiff’s arguments in her opposition do not count as evidence. The Court also finds that the broad language of the Arbitration Agreement covers Plaintiff’s claims in this action. Plaintiff has not argued otherwise in her opposition.  

 

For those reasons, the Court finds that the parties agreed to arbitrate this dispute.

 

            Unconscionability

 

            Plaintiff argues that even if she signed the Arbitration Agreement, the agreement is unenforceable because it is unconscionable.

 

“Arbitration agreements are contracts, and thus they are subject to generally applicable contract defenses, such as unconscionability. [Citations.] ‘“A contract is unconscionable if one of the parties lacked a meaningful choice in deciding whether to agree and the contract contains terms that are unreasonably favorable to the other party.” [Citation.]’ [Citation.]” (Stoker v. Blue Origin, LLC (2026) 120 Cal.App.5th 91, 104 (Stocker).)

 

“Unconscionability has both a substantive and procedural element.” (Stoker, supra, 120 Cal.App.5th at p. 104.) “Both procedural and substantive unconscionability must be shown for the defense to be established, but they need not be present in the same degree. Instead, they are evaluated on a sliding scale. [T]he more substantively oppressive the contract term, the less evidence of procedural unconscionability is required to’ conclude that the term is unenforceable. Conversely, the more deceptive or coercive the bargaining tactics employed, the less substantive unfairness is required.” (Ibid. [internal citations and quotation marks removed].)

 

1. Procedural Unconscionability

 

“‘“The procedural element addresses the circumstances of contract negotiation and formation, focusing on oppression or surprise due to unequal bargaining power.”’ [Citation.] This element ‘is generally established by showing the agreement is a contract of adhesion, i.e., a “standardized contract which, imposed and drafted by the party of superior bargaining strength, relegates to the subscribing party only the opportunity to adhere to the contract or reject it.”’ [Citation.]” (Stoker, supra, 120 Cal.App.5th at p. 104.)

 

For example, in Stoker, the Court of Appeal held the arbitration agreement in that employment law case was procedurally unconscionable because the plaintiff, Craig Stoker, “asserted in a declaration that when he was offered the position with [the defendant employer] in August 2020, [the employer’s] recruiter, Ben Baker, said Stocker would ‘have to sign an employment agreement as a term and condition of my employment.’ Baker represented that the employment agreement ‘contained “standard terms,” that “everyone has to sign to these terms,” and “everybody always signs to these terms.”’ Stoker asserted in his declaration that he had questions about the documents he was signing, but ‘Baker did not offer to answer any questions I had,’ and ‘[t]he way I was presented the agreement, and based on our prior discussions, it seemed that the terms that were not previously negotiated were nonnegotiable.’” (Stoker, supra, 120 Cal.App.5th at p. 106.) “In support of its motion to compel arbitration, [the employer] did not present any evidence that the employee agreement was not adhesive—i.e., that Stoker had, or could have, negotiated the terms of the employee agreement. [The appellate court] therefore conclude on the basis of the undisputed evidence that the employee agreement was a contract of adhesion.” (Ibid.)

 

Unlike in Stoker, Plaintiff has not submitted any declaration or evidence to support her claim that the Arbitration Agreement is procedurally unconscionable. Also unlike the defendant in that case, in this case, Defendant’s Director of Payroll & Benefits, Sims, testifies that, based on her own experience with the onboarding process: “It is the [Defendant’s] policy to provide newly hired employees ample time to review the onboarding documents, including the Arbitration Agreement.” (Sims Decl., ¶ 3.) Plaintiff has not submitted evidence disputing those facts.

 

Therefore, the Court finds that Plaintiff has failed to establish procedural unconscionability. Notwithstanding that finding, the Court analyzes Plaintiff’s substantive unconscionability arguments. 

 

2. Substantive Unconscionability

 

“Substantive unconscionability concerns ‘the fairness of a contract’s terms. This analysis “ensures that contracts, particularly contracts of adhesion, do not impose terms that have been variously described as ‘“‘overly harsh’”’, ‘“unduly oppressive”’, ‘“so one-sided as to ‘shock the conscience’”’, or ‘unfairly one-sided’ [.] All of these formulations point to the central idea that the unconscionability doctrine is concerned not with “a simple old-fashioned bad bargain’ [citation], but with terms that are “unreasonably favorable to the more powerful party.’”’ [Citation.]” (Stoker, supra, 120 Cal.App.5th pp. 106–107 [internal citations removed].)

 

Plaintiff first argues that the Arbitration Agreement is substantively unconscionable because it lacks mutuality. She argues that the agreement requires her to arbitrate any dispute she might have against Defendant but not vice versa. (Opposition, pp. 8:20-9:14.)

 

That argument is unpersuasive. As Defendant points out, the Arbitration Agreement requires Defendant to also arbitrate “any claim, dispute and/or controversy … the Company may have against [Plaintiff], arising from, related to, or having any relationship or connection whatsoever with my seeking employment with, employment by, or other association with the Company ….”

 

Next, Plaintiff argues that the agreement is substantively unconscionable because its scope is too broad. (See Opposition, p. 11:19-24 [“Because the clause reaches any dispute bearing any relationship or connection whatsoever to Ms. Cordova’s ‘association with the Company,’ and because it never expires, it captures disputes that have nothing to do with her job. Under its terms, if years after her termination Ms. Cordova were injured as a spectator at a Chargers game, she would be forced to arbitrate even that claim”].)

 

Plaintiff also argues that the agreement is unconscionable because it is of an infinite duration, as the arbitration agreement in Cook v. University of Southern California (2024) 102 Cal.App.5th 312 (Cook).

 

“In Cook, the plaintiff (Cook) filed a lawsuit against her former employer, the University of Southern California (USC), and two coworkers alleging discrimination and harassment. (Cook, supra, 102 Cal.App.5th at pp. 316–317.)” (Ayala-Ventura v. Superior Court (2026) 119 Cal.App.5th 241, 256 (Ayala-Ventura).)

 

“Cook and USC had an arbitration agreement wherein they ‘“agree[d] to the resolution by arbitration of all claims, whether or not arising out of Employee’s University employment, remuneration or termination, that Employee may have against the University or any of its related entities, including but not limited to faculty practice plans, or its or their officers, trustees, administrators, employees or agents, in their capacity as such or otherwise; and all claims that the University may have against Employee.”’ ([Cook, supra, 102 Cal.App.5th] at p. 317.)” (Ayala-Ventura, supra, 119 Cal.App.5th at p. 256.)

 

“The Court of Appeal … found unconscionable the agreement’s requirement the plaintiff must resolve by arbitration ‘any and all claims, whether or not they arose from the employee/employer relationship.’ ([Cook, supra, 102 Cal.App.5th] at p. 324.) The agreement was further deemed unconscionable because it survived indefinitely following Cook’s termination from USC and lacked mutuality as it did not require USC’s ‘“related entities”’ to arbitrate their claims against Cook. (Id. at pp. 325–326.)” (Ayala-Ventura, supra, 119 Cal.App.5th at p. 256 [emphasis added].)

 

Plaintiff argues that the Arbitration Agreement is as problematic as the one in Cook because it requires arbitration of all claims “arising from, related to, or having any relationship or connection whatsoever with [her] seeking employment with, employment by, or other association with the Company …” (emphasis added). Plaintiff argues that the italicized phrase is so broad that if a fellow employee hit Plaintiff’s car in a parking lot, she will have to submit that claim to arbitration. (Opposition, p. 9:22-24.) In its reply, Defendant disputes Plaintiff’s interpretation of the meaning of the scope of the agreement, arguing that the scope is limited to “disputes arising out of Plaintiff’s efforts to obtain employment with Defendant and her employment relationship with Defendant, and the cessation thereof.” (Reply, p. 10:1-3.)

 

The Court agrees with Plaintiff that the Arbitration Agreement contains broad language and is not restricted to a specific time period.

 

            However, as the Court of Appeal explained in Ayala-Ventura: “the Cook court did not conclude an arbitration agreement covering all claims including those unrelated to employment is per se unconscionable as [the plaintiff in that case and Plaintiff in this case] seem[] to argue. Because ‘parties are free to contract for asymmetrical remedies and arbitration clauses of varying scope,’ the Agreement’s purportedly broad scope does not necessarily mandate a finding of unconscionability. [Citation.]” (Ayala-Ventura, supra, 119 Cal.App.5th at p. 257.) Instead, “[t]he agreement in Cook was unconscionable in part because of the multifarious ways in which a claim against USC ‘completely unrelated to [Cook’s] employment’ could arise. [Citation.] As an example, the trial court observed that if Cook were to undergo a botched surgery at USC’s hospital in 15 years, her claims would still be subject to arbitration.” (Ibid.)

 

Here, unlike the arbitration agreement in Cook, the Arbitration Agreement does not require Plaintiff to arbitrate all claims “whether or not they arose from the employee/employer relationship” such that it is possible to come up with all the multifarious ways in which a claim unrelated to Plaintiff’s employment could arise.

 

Furthermore, when parties to an arbitration agreement indicate that the agreement is ambiguous by disputing its meaning but, like here, fail to present extrinsic evidence in support of their arguments, California courts “resolve the question of ambiguity based solely on the contents of their agreement.” (Duran v. EmployBridge Holding Co. (2023) 92 Cal.App.5th 59, 66.)

 

Here, looking solely at the contents of the Arbitration Agreement, the Court finds that the Arbitration Agreement is confined to all disputes arising out of or relating to Plaintiff’s employment with Defendant. This is because, under the principle of ejusdem generis (which means “of the same kind”): “‘“Where general words follow specific words in a [contractual provision] the general words are construed to embrace only objects similar in nature to those objects enumerated by the preceding specific words. Where the opposite sequence is found, i.e., specific words following general ones, the doctrine is equally applicable, and restricts application of the general terms to things that are similar to those enumerated.”’ [Citation.]” (Mountain Air Enterprises, LLC v. Sundowner Towers, LLC (2017) 3 Cal.5th 744, 754; Nygard, Inc. v. Uusi-Kerttula (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 1027, 1045 [citations removed].) Here, the first scenario applies because the phrase at issue—“other association with the Company”— is general and follows more specific phrases, i.e., “all disputes arising out of or relating to [Plaintiff’s] employment with the Company,” and “any claim, dispute and/or controversy that either [party]… may have … arising from, related to, or having any relationship or connection whatsoever with [Plaintiff] seeking employment with, employment by … the Company.” Therefore, the phrase “other association with the Company” must be construed to embrace only similar in nature to those objects enumerated by the preceding specific words, which restrict the Agreement to claims arising out of and relating to Plaintiff’s employment with Defendant.

 

Therefore, Plaintiff’s Cook arguments are unpersuasive.

 

Lastly, Plaintiff argues that the Arbitration Agreement is substantively unconscionable because: (1) it was executed at the same time as the parties’ Confidentiality Agreement; (2) the Confidentiality Agreement is unconscionable because it requires Plaintiff, as a condition of employment, to refrain and sign a waiver preventing her from disclosing her wages in violation of her rights under Labor Code section 232; and (3) California courts have found substantive unconscionability where, like here, arbitration agreements are executed on the same day as other documents containing unconscionable terms. (Opposition, pp. 12:21-13:24; citing Silva v. Cross Country Healthcare, Inc. (2025) 111 Cal.App.5th 1311, 1322-1323 (Silva); Alberto v. Cambrian Homecare (2023) 91 Cal.App.5th 482, 490-491 (Alberto).)

 

In Alberto, “the plaintiff signed two contracts as part of the hiring process—namely, (1) an arbitration agreement, and (2) a confidentiality agreement. The arbitration agreement specified that ‘[a]ny and all claims or controversies arising out of [the employee’s] employment’ would ‘be resolved through final and binding arbitration.’ (Alberto, at p. 486, 308 Cal.Rptr.3d 230.) The confidentiality agreement obligated the employee to keep secret the employer’s ‘trade secrets,’ to ‘acknowledge’ that unauthorized disclosure ‘would cause irreparable injury’ to the employer, and to ‘consent to the order of an immediate injunction, without bond, from any court of competent jurisdiction.’ (Id. at p. 487, 308 Cal.Rptr.3d 230.)” (Silva, supra, 111 Cal.App.5th at p. 1323.)

 

“Invoking [Civil Code] section 1642, the appellate court in Alberto had ‘no difficulty concluding’ that the two agreements ‘should be read together’ because ‘both [were] separate aspects of a single primary transaction—[the employee’s] hiring’ and ‘both governed, ultimately, the same issue—how to resolve disputes arising between [the employee] and [the employer] arising from ... employment.’ ([Alberto] at pp. 490-491, 308 Cal.Rptr.3d 230.) ‘Failing to read them together,’ the court in Alberto continued, ‘artificially segments the parties’ contractual relationship’ and ‘fails to account for the overall dispute resolution process the parties agreed upon.’ (Id. at p. 491, 308 Cal.Rptr.3d 230.)” (Silva, supra, 111 Cal.App.5th at p. 1323.)

 

Here, Defendants have not addressed or disputed Plaintiff’s argument that the Confidentiality Agreement and Arbitration Agreement should be read together, and that the former violates Labor Code section 232.

 

Therefore, the Court finds that Plaintiff has established that the Arbitration Agreement is substantively unconscionable because the Confidentiality Agreement prevents her from disclosing her wages in violation of Labor Code section 232, which states: “No employer may do any of the following: ¶ (a) Require, as a condition of employment, that an employee refrain from disclosing the amount of his or her wages. ¶ (b) Require an employee to sign a waiver or other document that purports to deny the employee the right to disclose the amount of his or her wages. ¶ (c) Discharge, formally discipline, or otherwise discriminate against an employee who discloses the amount of his or her wages.”

 

However, “‘[a]n unconscionable contractual term may be severed and the resulting agreement enforced, unless the agreement is permeated by an unlawful purpose, or severance would require a court to augment the agreement with additional terms. [Citation.]’ [Citation.] Severance may be properly denied when the agreement contains more than one unconscionable provision, and ‘“there is no single provision a court can strike or restrict in order to remove the unconscionable taint from the agreement.” [Citation.]’ [Citation.]” (Cook, supra, 102 Cal.App.5th at pp. 328–329.)

 

Here, the Court can sever the unconscionable terms in the Confidentiality Agreement.

 

However, even if severance is improper as Plaintiff argues, as stated earlier in this ruling, unconscionability requires both procedural and substantive unconscionability. Here, Plaintiff has failed to establish procedural unconscionability.

 

For those reasons, the Court finds that Plaintiff has failed to establish unconscionability.

 

            Request for Stay

 

The Court grants Defendant’s request to stay this action pending arbitration. (See Ford Motor Warranty Cases, supra, 17 Cal.5th at p. 1128 [“Unless an exception applies, if the matter is arbitrable, the court ‘shall on application of one of the parties stay the trial of the action until such arbitration has been had in accordance with the terms of the agreement ....’ (9 U.S.C. § 3)”].)

 

Conclusion

 

            Defendant’s motion to compel arbitration and to stay proceedings pending completion of arbitration is GRANTED.
