BACKGROUND

This is a wage and hour class action.  On January 25, 2024, Roberto Armando Palomo (“Palomo”), individually and on behalf of all others similarly situated, filed a putative class action against Defendant Los Angeles LGBT Center (“Defendant” or “Center”) and Does 1 through 100 asserting eleven causes of action for violations of the California Labor Code and California Business and Professions Code, Case No. 24STCV02011 (“Palomo Class Action”).  On April 2, 2024, Palomo filed a First Amended Class Action Complaint (“FAC”).

On April 3, 2024, Palomo filed a separate action against Defendant under the Private Attorneys General Act (“PAGA”) for civil penalties, Case No. 24STCV08409 (“Palomo PAGA Action”).

On April 30, 2024, Palomo filed a Notice of Related Case, listing the Palomo PAGA Action.  On May 8, 2024, pursuant to the stipulation of the parties, the Court ordered the Palomo Class Action and the Palomo PAGA Action to be consolidated and designated the Palomo Class Action (24STCV02011) as the lead case.

Defendant filed an Answer to the Palomo PAGA Action on May 30, 2024, and an Answer to the Palomo Class Action on June 27, 2024.

On November 18, 2024, the parties informed the Court that the Palomo Class Action and Palomo PAGA Action were mediated together with two other cases filed against Defendant in the Los Angeles Superior Court: Kyle Neely v. Los Angeles LGBT Center (“Neely Action”) (21STCV28073) initiated on July 30, 2021, and Rebecca Reyes Lomeli, et al. v. Los Angeles LGBT Center (“Lomeli PAGA Action”) (24STCV08581) initiated on April 5, 2024.  As a result of the global mediation with Mark S. Rudy, Esq., held on September 24, 2024, a settlement of all four cases was reached.  (Declaration of Brandon M. Chang (“Chang Decl.”), ¶14.)  A copy of the fully executed Class and PAGA Settlement Agreement (“Settlement Agreement” or “Agreement”) is attached to counsel Chang’s supplemental declaration, filed on October 30, 2025, as Exhibit 1.  As part of the Settlement Agreement, the parties agreed to the filing of an amended complaint.

On October 13, 2025, the Court granted leave for the parties to file an amended complaint, pursuant to the stipulation of the parties, in order to remove Palomo as a named plaintiff, substitute Rebecca Reyes Lomeli (“Lomeli”) and Danita Goss (“Goss”) as named plaintiffs, and to effectively consolidate the Neely Action, Lomeli PAGA Action, Palomo Class Action, and Palomo PAGA Action .  On October 14, 2025, Lomeli and Goss (jointly “Plaintiffs”), individually and on behalf of all others similarly situated, filed the Operative Second Amended Class and Representative Action Complaint (“SAC”) against Defendant and Does 1 through 100 asserting the following causes of action: (1) failure to pay overtime wages; (2) failure to pay minimum wages; (3) failure to provide meal periods; (4) failure to provide rest periods; (5) waiting time penalties; (6) wage statement violations; (7) failure to timely pay wages; (8) failure to indemnify; (9) failure to pay interest on deposits; (10) unfair competition; (11) violation of Labor Code §226.8; and (12) civil penalties under PAGA for violations of Labor Code sections 210, 226.3, 558, 1174.5, 1197.1, and 2699, et seq.

Now before the Court is the Motion for Preliminary Approval of Class and Representative Action Settlement (“MPA”), filed on October 23, 2025.  On October 30, 2025, Plaintiffs filed a Notice of Errata, along with corrected versions of the Settlement Agreement, MPA, and proposed Order.

ANALYSIS OF SETTLEMENT AGREEMENT

·       Does a presumption of fairness exist?

1.      Was the settlement reached through arm’s-length bargaining?  Yes.  Around April 5, 2022, Kyle Neely and Defendant attended a mediation presided over by Mark S. Rudy, Esq., which did not result in a settlement.  (Chang Decl., ¶5.)  On September 24, 2024, Palomo, Lomeli, Goss, and Defendant participated in a second mediation presided over by Mark S. Rudy, Esq, which resulted in global settlement of all four actions.  (Id. at ¶14.)  A copy of the fully executed Class and PAGA Settlement Agreement (“Settlement Agreement” or “Agreement”) is attached to counsel Chang’s supplemental declaration, filed on October 30, 2025, as Exhibit 1.

2.      Were investigation and discovery sufficient to allow counsel and the court to act intelligently?  Yes.  Class counsel represents that, prior to mediation, Plaintiffs obtained, through informal discovery: (1) a sampling of time and payroll records for putative Class Members; (2) a class list of hire dates, termination dates, and job positions worked by putative Class Members; (3) a collective bargaining agreement ("CBA") from the relevant time period; and (4) all policy documents pertaining to Plaintiffs available to Defendant.  (Chang Decl., ¶15.)  According to Class Counsel, settlement was reached after factual and legal investigation and research; the exchange of informal discovery; negotiation regarding the scope of informal discovery; exchange of documents and information that included review of time and pay records and analysis thereof with the aid of Plaintiffs and expert consultant; analysis of shifts and Workweeks worked by Class Members in the Class Periods, analysis of the number of pay periods and number of Aggrieved Employees in the PAGA Periods for calculating PAGA penalties, number of Class Members eligible for wage statement penalties and waiting time penalties; an analysis of Defendant's financial ability to fund the settlement; an analysis of Plaintiffs' employment records; extensive correspondence and communication between counsel; a review of pleadings, evidence and rulings in similar actions litigated elsewhere in the state; a review of similar cases in the same industry elsewhere in the country; and preparation for and attendance at two full-day mediation sessions, followed by further discussions and coordination to finalize the terms and conditions of the general settlement parameters agreed to by the Parties.  (Id. at ¶25.)

Provide more information regarding the sample, such as the percentage of data reviewed.  Demonstrate that the sample is statistically reliable.

3.      Is counsel experienced in similar litigation?  Yes.  Class Counsel represent that they are experienced in class action litigation, including wage and hour class actions.  (Chang Decl., ¶¶55-58; Declaration of David D. Bibiyan, ¶¶2-15.)

4.      What percentage of the class has objected?  This cannot be determined until the fairness hearing.  (See Weil & Brown, Cal. Practice Guide: Civil Procedure Before Trial (The Rutter Group 2014) ¶ 14:139.18, [“Should the court receive objections to the proposed settlement, it will consider and either sustain or overrule them at the fairness hearing.”].)

             

              CONCLUSION:  The settlement is entitled to a presumption of fairness.

·       May conditional class certification be granted?

1.      Standards

A detailed analysis of the elements required for class certification is not required, but it is advisable to review each element when a class is being conditionally certified (Amchem Products, Inc. v. Winsor (1997) 521 U.S. 620, 622-627.)  The trial court can appropriately utilize a different standard to determine the propriety of a settlement class as opposed to a litigation class certification.  Specifically, a lesser standard of scrutiny is used for settlement cases. (Dunk v. Ford Motor Co. (1996) 48 Cal.App.4th 1794, 1807 fn. 19.) Finally, the Court is under no “ironclad requirement” to conduct an evidentiary hearing to consider whether the prerequisites for class certification have been satisfied. (Wershba v. Apple Computer, Inc. (2001) 91 Cal.App.4th 224, 240.)

2.      Analysis

a.       Numerosity.  There are approximately 1,560 Class Members.  (Chang Decl., ¶52.)  This element is met.

b.      Ascertainability.  The proposed class is defined above.  The class definition is “precise, objective and presently ascertainable.”  (Sevidal v. Target Corp. (2010) 189 Cal.App.4th 905, 919.)  Class Members are identifiable through Defendant’s records.  (Chang Decl., ¶51.)

c.       Community of interest.  “The community of interest requirement involves three factors: ‘(1) predominant common questions of law or fact; (2) class representatives with claims or defenses typical of the class; and (3) class representatives who can adequately represent the class.’”  (Linder v. Thrifty Oil Co. (2000) 23 Cal.4th 429, 435.)

As to commonality, Class Counsel contends that this litigation is brought to resolve common issues that include whether Defendant failed to pay for all hours worked; whether Defendant provided full, timely and un-interrupted meal and rest periods; whether Class Members are entitled to premium pay for incomplete, untimely or interrupted meal or rest periods, among other claims.  (Chang Decl., ¶53.)

As to typicality, Class Counsel contends that Plaintiffs’ claims are typical of those of other Class Members as Plaintiffs: (1) were employees like other Class Members; (2) complain of not being paid for all time under Defendant's control or suffered and/or permitted to work for Defendant; (3) did not receive full premium pay for meal periods that were not compliant with the Labor Code; (4) did not receive premium pay for rest periods that were not provided, among other claims.  (Chang Decl., ¶54.)

Finally, as to adequacy, Plaintiffs are represented by experienced counsel, do not have any conflicts of interest with other Class Members, and have been active in the litigation of the case.  (Chang Decl., ¶59; see Declarations of Rebecca Reyes Lomeli, Danita Goss.)

d.      Adequacy of class counsel.  As indicated above, Class Counsel have shown experience in class action litigation, including wage and hour class actions.

e.      Superiority.  Given the relatively small size of the individual claims, a class action appears to be superior to separate actions by the class members.

 

              CONCLUSION:  The class may be conditionally certified since the prerequisites of class certification have been satisfied.

BACKGROUND

 

This is a wage-and-hour representative action.

LAW

 

PAGA permits an “aggrieved employee” to recover Labor Code civil penalties on the Labor and Workforce Development Agency’s (“LWDA”) behalf, if the LWDA declines to collect the penalties itself.  (Cal. Lab. Code, § 2699, subd. (a); see also Mejia v. Merchants Building Maintenance, LLC (2019) 38 Cal.App.5th 723, 732-733.)  The California Supreme Court has distinguished between Labor Code “civil penalties” that are “intended to ‘punish the employer’ for wrongdoing, often ‘without reference to the actual damage sustained’” and “statutory damages” that “primarily seek to compensate employees for actual losses incurred” – a PAGA action can recover only the former.  (Z.B., N.A. v. Superior Court (2019) 8 Cal.5th 175, 182, 198 [holding that Labor Code section 558 “amount sufficient to recover unpaid wages” is not a “civil penalty” recoverable via PAGA].) “A PAGA action is ‘fundamentally a law enforcement action designed to protect the public and not to benefit private parties.’”  (Mejia, supra, 38 Cal.App.5th at 732; see also Iskanian v. CLS Transportation Los Angeles, LLC (2014) 59 Cal.4th 348, 381.)

 

PAGA requires a court to “review and approve any settlement of any civil action filed” under PAGA, but it does not provide review and approval standards or guidelines. (Cal. Lab. Code, § 2699, subd. (l)(2).)  The California Supreme Court has interpreted PAGA as requiring courts to ensure that “any negotiated [PAGA] resolution is fair to those affected.”  (Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 549, emphasis added).)  The parties affected by a PAGA settlement include: (1) the LWDA, who receives 65% of settlement funds and is “bound by the outcome of the proceeding to adjudicate the employee’s PAGA claim” (Mejia, supra, 38 Cal.App.5th at 732); (2) the aggrieved employees, party and non-party, who receive 35% percent of settlement funds and are, like the LWDA, bound by a PAGA action judgment; (3) plaintiffs’ counsel, who may be awarded reasonable attorney fees and costs; and (4) defendant, who pays the settlement.[1]

 

Moniz v. Adecco USA, Inc. (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 56 provides greater detail about the standard courts should apply when evaluating PAGA settlements.  The opinion adopts the “fair, reasonable, and adequate” standard used in class settlements.

The Court finds as follows.

 

Gross Settlement Amount and Net Settlement Amount

 

Plaintiff’s counsel calculated Defendant’s maximum PAGA exposure to be $658,200.00.  (See id. at ¶ 22.)  He calculated Defendant’s discounted, reasonable PAGA exposure to be $197,460.00, i.e., 30% of the maximum exposure, accounting for Defendant’s defenses, case law unfavorable to stacking, and other factors.  (See id. at ¶¶ 20, 22-25.)

 

The Court agrees that stacking is unlikely.  “Until the employer has been notified that it is violating a Labor Code provision (whether or not the [Labor] Commissioner or court chooses to impose penalties), the employer cannot be presumed to be aware that its continuing underpayment of employees is a ‘violation’ subject to penalties.”  (Bernstein v. Virgin America, Inc. (9th Cir. 2021) 3 F.4th 1127, 1144.)  There does not seem to be a prior citation by the Labor Commissioner in this case.

 

Nevertheless, the Court needs more information.  The calculation of the maximum exposure amount is based on a $100.00 penalty per pay period ($100.00 × 6,582 pay periods), yet Plaintiff alleges multiple violations (overtime violations, rest period violations, vacation pay violations, etc.).  (See Second Amended Complaint for Representative Action, ¶¶ 35-137.)  Is the $100.00 penalty meant to cover all alleged violations that each aggrieved employee allegedly experienced during each pay period?  If yes, why does Plaintiff believe it is more reasonable to use a single penalty per pay period than to do a claim-by-claim assessment?  Plaintiff’s counsel should be prepared to answer these questions.  If necessary, the Court will continue the hearing to give Plaintiff’s counsel an opportunity to submit a supplemental declaration regarding the purported reasonableness of the aggregate penalty. 

 

Whether the net settlement amount ($94,014.01) is fair, reasonable, and adequate depends on whether the gross settlement amount ends up being found fair, reasonable, and adequate.

 

Attorney Fees

 

The amount for attorney fees ($63,333.33) is fair, reasonable, and adequate.  This is a contingency case.  (See, e.g., Kim Decl., ¶ 28.)  In contingency cases in the complex courts, 33% to 35% of the gross recovery is standard.

 

Attorney Costs

 

The amount for attorney costs ($22,162.66) is fair, reasonable, and adequate.  It is under 12% of the gross settlement amount, and it is within the range that has been approved in other complex cases in Department 11.

 

The settlement agreement caps attorney costs at $25,000.00.  (See id. at Ex. 1, § 3.2.1.)  If the costs fail to reach the cap, the Court will award the incurred amount to ensure that additional settlement money goes to the LWDA and aggrieved employees.

 

Administration Costs

 

The amount for administration costs ($5,490.00) is fair, reasonable, and adequate.  Like the attorney costs, it is within the range that is typically approved in Department 11.

 

The Court will award the incurred amount if it turns out to be less than the cap.  (See id. at Ex. 1, § 3.2.2 [capping administration costs at $5,490.00].)

 

Incentive Award

 

The amount for Plaintiff’s incentive award ($5,000.00) is fair, reasonable, and adequate.  It is the Court’s standard award.

 

Releases[2]

 

The Court is concerned about overbreadth.  First, do Plaintiff and the aggrieved employees have standing to release claims on behalf of “former and present spouses, representatives, agents, heirs, administrators, successors and assigns[?]”  (Id. at Ex. 1, § 5.1.)  Second, is the definition of “Released Parties” overinclusive?[3]  At the hearing, counsel needs to address the scope of the releases.

APPLICABLE LAW

 

When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context, and “treat the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.”  (Serrano v. Priest (1971) 5 Cal.3d 584, 591.)  “A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters.  Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed.”  (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)  It is error “to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend if the plaintiff shows there is a reasonable possibility any defect identified by the defendant can be cured by amendment.”  (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 967.)

 

DISCUSSION

 

Standing

 

This issue applies to all causes of action.

 

Defendants contend Plaintiff lacks standing because she fails to allege an injury in fact.  (See Demurrer, pp. 2-5 [arguing that Plaintiff’s allegations fail to show risk of future harm, lost time and mitigation costs, anxiety and loss of privacy, increased spam, lost value of PII, and lost benefit of the bargain].)

 

Plaintiff claims the FAC alleges concrete injuries.  She insists that her allegations justify a finding of standing because she alleges that the ransomware attack “involved exfiltration of unencrypted data, not mere encryption.”  (Opposition, p. 2, bolding deleted; see also id. at p. 3 [citing cases]; cf. Reply, pp. 1-2 [asserting that “[e]xfiltration of unencrypted data is not prima facie evidence of a concrete, cognizable injury”], bolding deleted.)

 

Further, Plaintiff claims “[e]ach of [the] specific injuries is . . . adequately pleaded.”  (Opposition, p. 3, bolding deleted; see also id. at pp. 4-5; cf. Reply, pp. 2-4 [rearguing that Plaintiff’s injury allegation fail to demonstrate concrete injuries].)

 

“[A]s a general matter, to have standing to pursue a claim for damages in the courts of California, a plaintiff must be beneficially interested in the claims he is pursuing.”  (Limon v. Circle K Stores, Inc. (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 671, 700.)  “A beneficial interest means the party has a special interest over and above the interest of the public at large.”  (Muha v. Experian Information Solutions, Inc. (2024) 106 Cal.App.5th 199, 208.)  “This standard ‘is equivalent to the federal “injury in fact” test, which requires a party to prove by a preponderance of the evidence that it has suffered “an invasion of a legally protected interest that is ‘(a) concrete and particularized, and (b) actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical.’”’”  (Ibid.)  “Thus, as a general rule, a plaintiff must allege he or she suffered a concrete ‘injury,’ as that term is used in Article III standing jurisprudence, to sue in California state court.”  (Ibid.) 

 

The Court turns to the allegations.  The FAC states:

 

107. On or about September 13, 2023, cybercriminals launched an “Akira” ransomware attack against the M-Theory servers that housed data from the Windsor Healthcare facilities.

 

108. The initial intrusion vector for the ransomware attack was through vulnerabilities in the misconfigured and unmonitored Cisco Meraki firewalls that were owned, operated, and managed by IHS on behalf of the Windsor facilities.

 

109. The attackers exploited known vulnerabilities in the Meraki firewall systems to gain unauthorized access to the M-Theory servers.

 

110. These vulnerabilities in the IHS-managed firewalls would have been detected and remediated if the Vulnerability Management Service had not been terminated in February 2023, leaving the enterprise with “no visibility to vulnerabilities.”

 

111. Once inside the network, the attackers gained access to the M-Theory servers and exfiltrated massive amounts of unencrypted and non-redacted data containing the sensitive PII and PHI of patients and employees.

 

112. After exfiltrating the data, the attackers encrypted the M-Theory servers with the “.akira” extension, rendering the data inaccessible to the NewGen Defendants.

 

113. Evidence of the stolen data includes server names suggesting electronic health record (EHR) systems (“SNFV-ALLSCRIPPS”) and payroll data (“SNFV-PAYROLL”), demonstrating that highly sensitive medical and financial information was compromised.

 

114. The data compromised in the attack was stored in an unencrypted and non-redacted format, making it immediately accessible and usable by the cybercriminals.

 

115. The Data Breach was the direct result of security vulnerabilities in the firewall systems managed by IHS that would have been detected if the vulnerability scanning service maintained by M-Theory had not been terminated seven months before the attack.

 

(FAC, ¶¶ 107-115.)  These paragraphs indicate that the attackers exfiltrated, and gained access to, patients’ unencrypted and unredacted PII and PHI.

 

However, Defendants argue that exfiltration, alone, is insufficient.  They claim Plaintiff must allege that the hackers misused her PII and PHI or that her PII and PHI were intentionally targeted as part of the ransomware attack.  (See Reply, pp. 1-2 [citing Podroykin v. American Armed Forces Mutal Aid Assn. (E.D. Va. 2022) 634 F.Supp.3d 265 and Graham v. Universal Health Serv., Inc. (E.D. Pa. 2021) 539 F. Supp. 3d 481].)

 

Podroykin and Graham were decided by district courts in the Third and Fourth Circuits. 

 

The Court finds the Ninth Circuit’s decision in In re Zappos.com, Inc. (9th Cir. 2018) 888 F.3d 1020 (“In re Zappos”) more persuasive.  There, hackers breached the defendant’s online servers and “stole the names, account numbers, passwords, email addresses, billing and shipping addresses, telephone numbers, and credit and debit card information of more than 24 million [] customers.”  (In re Zappos, supra, 888 F.3d at 1023.)  The Ninth Circuit found that the plaintiffs alleged standing by alleging a substantial risk of future harm, namely, the plaintiffs alleged that the stolen information could “be used to commit identity theft, including by placing them at higher risk of ‘phishing’ and ‘pharming,’ which are ways for hackers to exploit information they already have to get even more PII.”  (Id. at 1027; see also id. at 1025-1026, 1028-1029.)

 

The FAC is similar.  Plaintiff alleges that “names, addresses, medical diagnoses, lab results, medications, treatment information, dates of birth, Social Security numbers, driver’s license numbers, insurance claims information, credit card numbers, bank account numbers, and other financial information” were taken.  (FAC, ¶ 8; see also id. at ¶ 13.)  Paragraph 228 states that Plaintiff “has experienced a marked increase in sophisticated phishing emails and spam calls that reference her personal information since the Data Breach, demonstrating that her stolen information is actively being used by cybercriminals.”  (Id. at ¶ 228; see also id. at ¶ 231 [“On information and belief, Plaintiff’s and Class members’ PII/PHI has already been published—or will be published imminently—by cybercriminals on the Dark Web.”].)

 

The demurrer is overruled.  Plaintiff’s allegations – especially the “phishing” allegation – appear analogous enough to In re Zappos to establish standing at the pleading stage.[2] 

 

Causation

 

This issue applies to all causes of action.

 

Citing Castillo v. Seagate (N.D. Cal. Sept. 14, 2016, No. 16-cv-01958-RS) 2016 WL 9280242 and Stasi v. Inmediata Health Group Corp. (S.D. Cal. 2020) 501 F.Supp.3d 898, Defendants argue that Plaintiff cannot trace her injuries to Defendants or show that Defendants caused them.  (See Demurrer, pp. 5-6; see also Reply, p. 4 [attempting to analogize Stasi].)

 

Castillo is distinguishable.  The district court found causation lacking because the plaintiff admitted that her PII “had been compromised during a previous, unrelated data breach.”  (Castillos, supra, 2016 WL 9280242, at *4.)  As Plaintiff notes, there is no equivalent admission in her case.  (See Opposition, p. 5.)

 

Stasi is distinguishable for the same reason.  The plaintiff alleged that he had also been a victim of an earlier data breach targeting a different company in 2017.  (See Stasi, supra, 501 F.Supp.3d at 916.)  In part, the district court determined that the plaintiff’s admission undermined his effort to link identity theft to the defendant’s 2019 data breach instead of the 2017 data breach.  (See ibid.)

 

The demurrer is overruled.

 

Failure to State Elements

 

Alter Ego

 

NewGen Defendants assert:

 

Plaintiff tries to summarily equate the actions of the distinct “Bold Quail” and “NewGen” entities as one. Plaintiff’s bare, conclusory allegations of alter ego are insufficient. The Complaint primarily asserts legal conclusions (e.g., Compl. ¶¶ 3, 22, 27) and otherwise lacks factual support (e.g., id. ¶ 23). “To prevail on a claim of ‘alter ego,’ the third party must show (1) there is such a unity of interest that the separate personalities of the corporations no longer exist; and (2) inequitable results will follow if the corporate separateness is respected.”  Tomaselli v. Transamerica Ins. Co., 31 Cal. Rptr. 2d 433, 443 (Cal. App. 4th Dist. 1994). Even if the contentions based on information and belief about the separate “Bold Quail” and “NewGen” entities are sufficient to satisfy the first prong (and they are not) Plaintiff never alleges an inequitable result would occur if the separateness is recognized.  

 

(Demurrer, p. 6; see also Reply, p. 4.)

 

The demurrer is overruled.  “To prevail on a claim of “alter ego,” the third party must show (1) there is such a unity of interest that the separate personalities of the corporations no longer exist; and (2) inequitable results will follow if the corporate separateness is respected.”  (Tomaselli v. Transamerica Ins. Co. (1994) 25 Cal.App.4th 1269, 1285.)  Plaintiff alleges:

 

21. At all relevant times, each of the NewGen Defendants maintained their principal place of business at the same address: 9526 West Pico Blvd., Los Angeles, CA 90035.

 

22. The NewGen Defendants operate as a single integrated enterprise with shared management, employees, and centralized control over administrative, financial, and information technology functions for a network of rehabilitation, skilled nursing, behavioral health, and assisted living facilities throughout California, including, without limitation, S&F Market Street Healthcare, LLC; Windsor Care Center National City, Inc.; Windsor Cheviot Hills, LLC; Windsor Country Drive Care Center, LLC; Windsor Court Assisted Living, LLC; Windsor Cypress Gardens Healthcare, LLC; Windsor El Camino Care Center, LLC; Windsor Elk Grove and Rehabilitation Center, LLC; Windsor Elmhaven Care Center, LLC; Windsor Gardens Convalescent Hospital, Inc.; Windsor Hampton Care Center, LLC; Windsor Hayward Estates, LLC; Windsor Monterey Care Center, LLC; Windsor Rosewood Care Center, LLC; Windsor Sacramento Estates, LLC; Windsor Skyline Care Center, LLC; Windsor Terrace Healthcare, LLC; Windsor The Ridge Rehabilitation Center, LLC; Windsor Vallejo Care Center, LLC; American River Center; Anaheim Terrace Care Center; Ballard Center; Bay Crest Care Center; Creekside Center; Devonshire Care Center; Fountain View Subacute and Nursing Center; Kingsburg Center; Linden Grove Health Care Center; Montebello Care Center; Montesano Health - Rehab Center; Orchard Park Health Care and Rehab Center; Playa Del Rey Center; Rio Hondo Subacute and Nursing Center; Saint Joseph Transitional Rehabilitation Center; The Earlwood; The Heights of Summerlin, LLC; Willows Post Acute; and Woodland Care Center.

 

23. On information and belief, the NewGen Defendants commingle funds and fail to maintain corporate formalities, treating assets and operations as belonging to a single entity rather than separate legal entities.

 

24. On July 1, 2023, the NewGen Defendants acquired the Windsor Healthcare facilities, including Windsor El Camino Care Center, LLC, from S&F Management Company, LLC.

 

25. Shortly after the acquisition, on August 23, 2023, substantially all of the Windsor facility-level entities filed voluntary Chapter 11 bankruptcy petitions, which were jointly administered under Case No. 23-11200 in the United States Bankruptcy Court for the Central District of California.

 

26. NewGen Administrative Services, LLC conducted business under the name Bold Quail Holdings, LLC, further demonstrating a unity of interest and disregard for corporate formalities among the NewGen Defendants.

 

27. Based on the foregoing, the NewGen Defendants acted as agents and alter egos of one another and the acquired Winsor entities, making them jointly and severally responsible for the security of patient data across the entire enterprise, both before and after the July 1, 2023 acquisition.

 

(FAC, ¶¶ 21-27.)  Although these paragraphs do not contain an allegation showing that “an inequitable result would occur if the separateness is recognized” (Demurrer, p. 6), Plaintiff is entitled to allege on information and belief (see, e.g., Weil & Brown, Cal. Practice Guide: Civ. Procedure Before Trial (The Rutter Group 2026) ¶ 6:121.5). The question of alter-ego liability is ultimately a fact question that must be fleshed out via discovery.  The efficient approach is to move to the discovery stage. 

 

Negligence (First Cause of Action)

 

Defendants advance two arguments in support of the demurrer to the negligence claim.  First, they contend that the economic-loss rule bars the negligence cause of action.  (See Demurrer, pp. 6-7 [citing cases]; cf. Opposition, p. 7 [“Where the parties are not in privity and no commercial transaction between them allegedly went awry, the economic loss rule does not apply.”].)  Second, as to the NewGen Defendants and IHS, they contend Plaintiff fails to allege duty, breach, and causation.  (See Demurrer, p. 7; see also Reply, p. 5.)

 

The first argument is unavailing.  The economic-loss rule forecloses “recovery of purely economic loss[,]” yet many exceptions exist.  (Gardiner v. Walmart Inc. (N.D. Cal. Mar. 5, 2021, No. 20-cv-04618-JSW) 2021 WL 2520103, at *8.)  The rule does not apply if there is “(1) personal injury, (2) physical damage to property, (3) a ‘special relationship’ existing between the parties, or (4) some other common law exception to the rule[.]”  (Ibid.)  In Stasi, “the compromised information [] include[d] medical information, the disclosure of which leads to damages that are not necessarily as ‘economic’ as those resulting from the theft of credit card information and social security numbers.”  (Stasi, supra, 501 F.Supp.3d at 913.)  “Plus,” the complaint alleged that the plaintiffs “noticed an increase in spam/phishing e-mails and/or calls . . . , which is harm that is also not necessarily ‘economic’ in nature.”  (Ibid.)  The district court found the rule inapplicable, reasoning that “the time spent responding to a data breach is a non-economic injury[.]”  (Ibid.; see also id. at 913-914 [reasoning that the plaintiffs and defendant “were not in privity of contract, there was no commercial activity between” the plaintiffs and defendant, “and the case d[id] not involve a defective product or services resulting in mere ‘disappointed expectations[]’”].)  Plaintiff alleges like allegations concerning medical information and time expended “monitoring her financial accounts, reviewing credit reports, and taking protective measures.”  (FAC, ¶ 227; see also, e.g., ¶¶ 8, 13.)  Overall, the Court agrees with Plaintiff that Stasi is more akin to Plaintiff’s case than Moore v. Centrelake Medical Group, Inc. (2022) 83 Cal.App.5th 515 is.  (See Opposition, p. 7 [distinguishing Moore]; see also Moore, supra, 83 Cal.App.5th at 534-535 [explaining that the rule “applies, inter alia, where the parties are in contractual privity and the plaintiff’s claim arises from the contract (in other words, the claim is not independent of the contract)” and noting that the plaintiff alleged “direct contractual privity” with the defendant].)

 

The second argument is unsubstantiated.  Defendants devote one paragraph to the argument, and they cite nothing.  (See Demurrer, p. 7.)

 

The demurrer to the first cause of action is overruled.

 

Negligence Per Se (Second Cause of Action)

 

Defendants argue:

 

Plaintiff cannot rely on the Federal Trade Commission Act (“FTC Act”) and the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (“HIPAA”) to support her negligence per se claim because no private right of action exists under either statute. (See Compl. ¶¶ 234-50.) Numerous courts have held that FTC Act does not provide for a private right of action and cannot serve as the basis of a negligence per se claim. . . . Because Plaintiff bases her negligence per se claim entirely on alleged violations of HIPAA and the FTC Act, the claim fails. 

 

(Demurrer, pp. 7-8 [citing cases].)

 

Plaintiff represents that she wants to utilize the California Customer Records Act (CCRA) and Confidentiality of Medical Information Act (CMIA) as predicates.  She requests leave to amend to “expressly designate” them.  (Opposition, p. 10; cf. Reply, p. 5 [opposing leave to amend].)

 

The demurrer to the second cause of action is sustained with leave to amend.  The FAC only mentions the FTC Act and HIPPA.  (See FAC, ¶¶ 233-254.)  Plaintiff concedes that the FTC Act and HIPPA cannot be used for negligence per se.  (See Opposition, p. 10 [agreeing that the FTC Act and HIPPA do not “create[] private right[s] of action”].)

 

The court grants leave to amend to base the claim on the CCRA and CMIA. The court makes not ruling on the viability of a not yet pleaded claim.

 

Breach of Implied Contract (Third Cause of Action)

 

Defendants assert that Plaintiff fails to plead formation of the implied contract and the contractual terms.  (See Demurrer, pp. 8-9 [citing cases]; see also Reply, pp. 5-6.)

 

Plaintiff points the Court to Kirsten v. California Pizza Kitchen, Inc. (C.D. Cal. July 29, 2022, No. 2:21-CV-09578-DOC-KES) 2022 WL 16894503.  (See Opposition, pp. 10-11.)  The employer “argue[d] that there was no meeting of the minds that its systems would never be breached.”  (Kirsten, supra, 2022 WL 16894503, at *5.)  The district court disagreed, finding that “the mandatory receipt of PII implies the recipient's assent to protect the PII[.]”  (Ibid.)

 

The demurrer to the third cause of action is sustained with leave to amend.  “An implied contract requires that both parties agree to its terms and have a ‘meeting of the minds,’ but the creation of an implied contract can be manifested by conduct rather than words.”  (Castillo, supra, 2016 WL 9280242, at *8.)  The trend, recognized in Kirsten, is to find an implied promise to protect the data, and implied consent, when the defendant receives the “plaintiffs’ names, Social Security Numbers and other personal information[.]”  (Kirsten, supra, 2022 WL 16894503, at *5 [citing Castillo, In re Target Corp. Customer Data Security Breach Litigation (D. Minn. 2014) 66 F.Supp.3d 1154, and In re Ambry Genetics Data Breach Litigation (C.D. Cal. 2021) 567 F.Supp.3d 1130].)  An important distinction, though, is that the Kirsten plaintiffs alleged that the defendant had a statutory obligation to “implement and maintain reasonable security procedures and practices to protect the" PII.  (Ibid.)  Plaintiff fails to highlight a statutory obligation in the FAC.  See FAC, ¶¶ 255-276.)

 

The demurrer to the cause of action for breach of implied contract is sustained with leave to amend.

 

Unfair Competition Law (UCL) (Seventh Cause of Action)

 

Defendants argue:

 

* Plaintiff must demonstrate that “she lacks an adequate remedy at law before securing equitable restitution for past harm under the UCL” (Demurrer, p. 9);

 

* “Plaintiff has not alleged that she has incurred any out-of-pocket expenses” (id. at p. 10); and

 

* “Plaintiff fails to satisfy the unlawful prong[.]”  (Ibid.; see also Reply, p. 6.)

 

The first point fails. California law allows Plaintiff to plead inconsistent theories, request inconsistent remedies, and leave election for another day.  (See, e.g., Weil & Brown, Cal. Practice Guide: Civ. Procedure Before Trial (The Rutter Group 2026) ¶ 6:249.5 [advising that a “plaintiff may proceed to trial on alternative theories with inconsistent remedies” and that an election is not required until “entry of judgment”], emphasis in original.)  Whether Plaintiff possesses an adequate remedy at law is a question for either summary judgment or trial.

 

The second point – failure to plead out of pocket expenses – is well taken.  Plaintiff alleges:              

 

Plaintiff has standing to bring this UCL claim because she lost money or property as a result of Defendants’ unfair competition. Specifically, Plaintiff lost the value of her PII/PHI, which was compromised and rendered valueless or of negative value due to Defendants’ conduct. Plaintiff also spent over 15 hours monitoring accounts and taking protective measures, representing lost time and opportunity costs. Additionally, Plaintiff did not receive the full benefit of her bargain, as she paid for medical services that were to include adequate data security but received substandard security that resulted in the Data Breach.

 

(FAC, ¶ 279.)  The allegations are conclusory and do not show that Plaintiff actually “lost money or property as a result of unfair competition[.]”  (Kwikset Corp. v. Superior Court (2011) 51 Cal.4th 310, 320-321, internal quotation mark omitted.)  The Court will give Plaintiff a chance to amend to fix this defect.

 

The third point amounts to an improper partial demurrer.  The UCL is written in the disjunctive, meaning alleging a violation of one prong is sufficient to defeat a demurrer.  Defendants do not challenge Plaintiff’s unfair-prong and fraud-prong allegations.  (See FAC, ¶¶ 290-302.)

 

The demurrer to the seventh cause of action is sustained with leave to amend.

 

California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) (Eighth Cause of Action)

 

Defendants state:

 

First, the CCPA broadly exempts health information and healthcare providers from its reach. See Cal. Civ. Code §§ 1798.145(c)(1), 1798.146(a). In particular, the CCPA states that it “shall not apply to any . . . [m]edical information governed by” the CMIA, or to any “protected health information that is collected by a covered entity . . . governed by” HIPAA. Id. §§ 1798.145(c)(1)(A), 1798.146(a)(1). “Medical information” and “protected health information” are broad terms. See id. § 56.05(i); 45 C.F.R. § 160.103. And even when other types of information may be at issue, entities “governed by” the CMIA or HIPAA is still exempt from the CCPA “to the extent” that they “maintains patient information in the same manner as medical information or protected health information.” Cal. Civ. Code §§ 1798.145(c)(1)(B), 1798.146(a)(2). These exemptions apply here. Plaintiff contends that her information was provided in relation to healthcare, and, likewise, that the CMIA and HIPAA apply. See, e.g., id. ¶¶ 160-70, 349-65. This alone defeats the CCPA claim.

 

(Demurrer, pp. 10-11.)

 

The Court disagrees.  The hackers allegedly exfiltrated more than just health information.  (See, e.g., FAC, ¶¶ 8, 13.)  How Defendants maintained the various kinds of information – whether they maintained them “in the same manner” as PHI (Demurrer, p. 11) – is a factual issue.

 

Next, Defendants state:

 

Plaintiff also does not sufficiently provide any facts to establish that any of the Defendants are a “business” within the meaning of the CCPA. The term “business” has a specific statutory definition in the CCPA, requiring that an entity pass at least one of three “thresholds.” Cal. Civ. Code § 1798.140(d)(1). Two thresholds are revenue-based, but the Complaint says nothing about the Defendants’ annual gross revenue, id. § 1798.140(d)(1)(A), or whether Defendants derive revenue “from selling or sharing consumers’ personal information,” id. § 1798.140(d)(1)(C). The remaining threshold asks whether a defendant does certain things with the personal information of enough Californians. Id. § 1798.140(d)(1)(B). Plaintiff does not plausibly allege that Defendants meet any of these threshold requirements. Additionally, “to qualify as a “business” under the CCPA, the entity must . . . determine why and how (“the purposes and means”) the PII should be processed.”  In re Accellion, Inc. Data Breach Litig. 713 F.Supp.3d 623, 640 (N.D. Cal. 2024). Here, Plaintiff does not plead any of the Defendants made such a determination. 

 

(Ibid.)

 

The Court agrees.  Plaintiff cites the definition of “Business” in Civil Code section 1798.140, subdivision (d)(1)(B):

 

(d) “Business” means:

 

(1) A sole proprietorship, partnership, limited liability company, corporation, association, or other legal entity that is organized or operated for the profit or financial benefit of its shareholders or other owners, that collects consumers' personal information, or on the behalf of which such information is collected and that alone, or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing of consumers' personal information, that does business in the State of California, and that satisfies one or more of the following thresholds:

 

* * *

 

(B) Alone or in combination, annually buys, sells, or shares the personal information of 100,000 or more consumers or households.

 

(Cal. Civ. Code § 1798.140, subd. (d)(1)(B), emphasis added; see also Opposition, p. 13.)  According to the FAC, the breach purportedly affected over 105,000 patients.  (See FAC, ¶ 1.)  This allegation fails to aver that Defendants annually “buy[], sell[], or share[]”personal information of the requisite number of consumers or households. 

 

 

Next, Defendants contend:

 

[Section] 1798.150(b) provides that private actions under this statute may be brought if “prior to initiating any action against a business for statutory damages on an individual or classwide basis, a consumer provides a business 30 days’ written notice identifying the specific provisions of this title the consumer alleges have been or are being violated.” (emphasis added). Further, if  “within the 30 days the business actually cures the noticed violation . . . no action for individual statutory damages or class-wide statutory damages may be initiated against the business.” Id. Here, Plaintiffs express allegations reveal that her own action may not satisfy these requirements. See Compl. ¶ 324 (“Pursuant to § 1798.150(b), Plaintiff will provide or has provided each Defendant with 30 days’ written notice . . .”).

 

(Demurrer, pp. 11-12, emphasis added.)

 

The argument appears to be moot.  Plaintiff declares that the statute does not impose the 30-day requirement on claims for equitable relief.  She says that, currently, she is not seeking damages under the statute.  (See Opposition, p. 14; see also Reply, pp. 6-7 [not addressing the 30-day requirement].)

 

Last, Defendants assert:

 

Regarding the Bold Quail, NewGen, and IHS Defendants, Plaintiff fails to allege a violation of the statute whatsoever.  Plaintiff cites Cal Civ. Code § 1798.150(a)(1) as to all Defendants, summarily accusing them all of failing to “implement and maintain reasonable security procedures and practices appropriate to the nature of the information” to protect personal information[.]” But the “security procedures” of the Bold Quail, NewGen and IHS Defendants are not implicated by the alleged Incident, which, is stated to have occurred on M-Theories servers. (Compl. ¶ 4). 

 

(Demurrer, p. 12.)

 

Plaintiff responds: “The operative complaint alleges that all defendants collected, maintained, and processed the personal information of over 105,425 patients and that each defendant is subject to CCPA liability on the face of those allegations. (Amend. Compl. ¶¶ 1, 6, 280-283.) The Court must accept those allegations as true at the pleading stage.”  (Opposition, p. 13.)

 

The Court agrees with Defendants.  Paragraph 4 alleges that M-Theory “design[ed], install[ed], manag[ed], and operat[ed] the IT infrastructure and servers that housed patients’ sensitive PII and PHI on behalf of the NewGen Defendants” (FAC, ¶ 4) whereas Paragraph 281 lumps all Defendants together: “Defendants violated the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA), Cal. Civ. Code § 1798.150, by failing to implement and maintain reasonable security procedures and practices appropriate to the nature of the information, resulting in unauthorized access and exfiltration, theft, or disclosure of Plaintiff’s and the Class members’ nonencrypted and nonredacted PII/PHI.”  (Id. at ¶ 281.)  The Court finds that paragraph 281 is too conclusory in light of the specificity of the location of the incident described in the Complaint at paragraph 4.  Plaintiff needs to allege more facts about NewGen Defendants and IHS to state a claim against them.

 

In summary, for these reasons, the demurrer to the eighth cause of action is sustained with leave to amend.

 

 

California Consumer Records Act (CCRA) (Ninth Cause of Action)

 

Defendants contend:

 

* Plaintiff fails to allege unreasonable delay in disclosing the data breach to patients (see Demurrer, pp. 12-13);

 

* Plaintiff does not qualify as a “Customer” under the statute (see id. at p. 13 [“‘Customer’ means an individual who provides personal information to a business for the purpose of purchasing or leasing a product or obtaining a service from the business.’ Plaintiff alleges she was a patient of a Windsor entity long before it was acquired by NewGen Defendants and ended that relationship over three years before the Incident. (Id. at ¶ 12). Plaintiff was never seeking to obtain a product or service from the Bold Quail or NewGen Defendants. Nor could she be a customer of M-Theory or IHS.”]);

 

* Plaintiff alleges inconsistent allegations against NewGen Defendants (see ibid. [“In the CCRA claim, Plaintiff attempts to allege that “[d]espite discovering the breach on September 14, 2023, the NewGen Defendants did not begin notifying affected individuals until February 23, 2024—over five months (162 days) later.” (Compl. ¶ 340). Yet, elsewhere in the Complaint, Plaintiff alleges “M-Theory refused to provide the NewGen Defendants with access to the compromised servers or share forensic findings regarding the breach,” (id. ¶ 118) and “NewGen Defendants had no way of conclusively identifying what data was stored on the impacted servers and to whom it relates.” (Id. ¶ 119). Plaintiffs cannot plausibly allege that NewGen should have sent notices out sooner while simultaneously acknowledging that NewGen was deprived of critical Incident-related information.”]); and

 

* “Plaintiff does not plausibly allege that any delay caused her harm.”  (Ibid.; see also id. at p. 14.)

 

The Court finds the second point decisive.  Plaintiffs assert that NewGen Defendants are liable as successors of the former owners of the Windsor entity, that M-Theory is liable as the owner of the servers that were hacked, and that IHS is liable as the licensor of the data.  (See Opposition, pp. 15-16.)  The singular authority that Plaintiff cites is page 920 of the Stasi opinion.  (See id. at p. 16.)  Page 920 analyzes breach-of-contract issues.  (See Stasi, supra, 501 F.Supp.3d at 920 [analyzing third-party beneficiary and damages].)  The citation fails to provide insight on applying the CCRA’s “Customer” definition to Defendants.

 

The demurrer to the ninth cause of action is sustained with leave to amend.

 

Confidentiality of Medical Information Act (CMIA) (Tenth Cause of Action)

 

Defendants contend that Plaintiff fails to allege that her medical records were “viewed by an unauthorized individual.”  (Demurrer, p. 14 [quoting Regents of University of California v. Superior Court (2013) 220 Cal.App.4th 549, 570 (“Regents”) and citing other cases]; see also Reply, pp. 7-8.)

 

The demurrer is overruled.  The California Supreme Court recently disapproved Regents and the “actual viewing” requirement.  (See J.M. v. Illuminate Education, Inc. (2026) 19 Cal.5th 705, 711, 719-723.)  All that needs to be alleged is that the data was “exposed to a significant risk of unauthorized access or use.”  (Id. at 711; see also id. at 719-723.)  The FAC goes above and beyond the standard, alleging actual viewing by cybercriminals (see FAC, ¶¶ 360, 362) and circumstances – phishing contacts – showing a substantial risk of improper usage.  (See, e.g., id. at ¶¶ 140-141, 228.)

 

Arguments advanced by Defendants M-Theory and IHS

 

No Legal Relationship

 

This issue applies to the negligence claim (first cause of action).

 

M-Theory and IHS argue that Plaintiff fails to allege that she had a special relationship with M-Theory and IHS and, thus, cannot avoid the economic-loss rule.  (See Demurrer, pp. 16-17.)

 

The demurrer is overruled.  As explained above, the economic-loss rule is inapplicable for a separate reason – “the time spent responding to a data breach is a non-economic injury[.]”  (Stasi, supra, 501 F.Supp.3d at 913; see also id. at 913-914 [reasoning that the plaintiffs and defendant “were not in privity of contract, there was no commercial activity between” the plaintiffs and defendant, “and the case d[id] not involve a defective product or services resulting in mere ‘disappointed expectations[]’”]; FAC, ¶ 227 [alleging similar allegations  about medical information and time expended “monitoring [Plaintiff’s] financial accounts, reviewing credit reports, and taking protective measures”]; id. at ¶¶ 8, 13.)  In any case, the “special relationship” test is a six-factor, fact-intensive test.  (See Opposition, p. 8.)  The inquiry cannot be resolved without discovery and evidence.   

 

California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) (Eighth Cause of Action)

 

M-Theory and IHS assert:

 

* they are not “Businesses” under the CCPA (see Demurrer, p. 18);

 

* the CCPA exempts “medical information” governed by the CMIA and “protected health information” governed by HIPPA from its reach (id. at pp. 18-19 [claiming “Plaintiff expressly alleges that the data at issue constitutes medical and health information and that the CMIA and HIPAA apply”]); and

 

* Plaintiff did not comply with the pre-suit notice requirement.  (See id. at p. 19.)

 

The first point has merit.  Plaintiff claims all Defendants “collected, maintained, and processed the personal information of over 105,425 patients[.]”  (Opposition, p. 13.)  To repeat, Plaintiff cites the definition of “Business” in Civil Code section 1798.140, subdivision (d)(1)(B):

 

(d) “Business” means:

 

(1) A sole proprietorship, partnership, limited liability company, corporation, association, or other legal entity that is organized or operated for the profit or financial benefit of its shareholders or other owners, that collects consumers' personal information, or on the behalf of which such information is collected and that alone, or jointly with others, determines the purposes and means of the processing of consumers' personal information, that does business in the State of California, and that satisfies one or more of the following thresholds:

 

* * *

 

(B) Alone or in combination, annually buys, sells, or shares the personal information of 100,000 or more consumers or households.

 

(Cal. Civ. Code § 1798.140, subd. (d)(1)(B), emphasis added; see also Opposition, p. 13.)  According to the FAC, the breach purportedly affected over 105,000 patients.  (See FAC, ¶ 1.)  What is missing is a statement stating that all Defendants “buy[], sell[], or share[]”personal information in the requisite volume on an annual basis. 

 

The second point is unconvincing.  The Court reiterates that the hackers allegedly exfiltrated some non-healthcare data.  (See, e.g., FAC, ¶¶ 8, 13.)  Evidence must be evaluated to assess whether Defendants maintained the non-healthcare data “in the same manner” as PHI.

 

The third point is moot since Plaintiff is not requesting damages.  (See Opposition, p. 14.)

 

The demurrer by M Theory and IHS to the to the eighth cause of action under the Confidential Medical Records Act is sustained with leave to amend.

 

 

Prompt Notification by M-Theory

 

M-Theory states:

 

Several of Plaintiff’s claims rest on allegations of delayed notification. Those theories do not apply to M-Theory. The Complaint itself alleges that M-Theory notified the NewGen Defendants of the ransomware incident on or about September 14, 202; the day after it was discovered. (Compl. ¶ 7.) Any delay in notifying affected individuals thereafter is attributable solely to the NewGen Defendants’ independent investigation, not to M-Theory. 

 

The Complaint concedes that M-Theory was conducting an active forensic investigation of a criminal intrusion. The delay Plaintiff complains about was a necessary consequence of the forensic process, not a breach of any duty by M-Theory. (Compl. ¶¶ 118-119.) 

 

(Demurrer, p. 19.)

 

The demurrer is overruled.  The delay issue – the reasonableness of the delay, and the extent of M-Theory’s responsibility for the delay – is a factual issue.  (See FAC, ¶¶ 117-119 [suggesting that M-Theory inhibited NewGen Defendants’ investigation, contributing to the allegedly delayed disclosure to patients].)

 

California Consumer Records Act (CCRA) (Ninth Cause of Action)

 

M-Theory and IHS contend Plaintiff was not a “Customer.”  (See Demurrer, pp. 19-20 [“Plaintiff never purchased a product from M-Theory or IHS. Plaintiff never sought or obtained a service from M-Theory or IHS. Plaintiff never had any interaction with M-Theory or IHS. She was a patient of Windsor El Camino, not a customer of an IT infrastructure services company.”]; cf. Opposition, pp. 15-16 [citing Stasi].)

 

The Court agrees.  The authority Plaintiff advances – page 920 of the Stasi decision – does not support extending the statute’s “Customer” definition to M-Theory as the owner of the servers or to IHS as the licensor of the data.

 

These defendants’ demurrer to the ninth cause of action is sustained with leave to amend.

 

Indispensable Party

 

M-Theory and IHS claim:

 

Windsor El Camino Care Center, LLC is the entity that collected Plaintiff’s personal information, served as her healthcare provider, and held the contractual relationship Plaintiff invokes. (Compl. ¶¶ 12, 136.) Windsor El Camino has since filed for bankruptcy. Any liability determination in this action, particularly on the contract, CCRA, and CMIA claims, necessarily implicates Windsor El Camino’s role, obligations, and rights. If Windsor El Camino cannot be joined because of the bankruptcy stay, this Court must determine under Code of Civil Procedure section 389(b) whether the action should proceed or be dismissed. M-Theory and IHS join in this ground under Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10(d), which provides that a demurrer lies where there is a defect or misjoinder of parties.

 

(Demurrer, p. 20.)

 

The demurrer is overruled.  M-Theory and IHS do not meet their burden as the moving parties.  Other than declaring that the Windsor entity has filed for bankruptcy, they analyze none of the factors for finding a party indispensable.  (Cf. Opposition, pp. 16-17.)

 

 

Negligence Per Se (Second Cause of Action)

 

Piggybacking on the other Defendants’ argument, M-Theory and IHS reemphasize that the FTC Act and HIPPA cannot serve as the underlying statutes because they do not provide private rights of action.  (See Demurrer, p. 20.)

 

Plaintiff seeks leave to amend to add the CCRA and CMIA as predicates.  (See Opposition, p. 10; cf. Reply, p. 5 [contesting leave to amend].)

 

The demurrer is sustained with leave to amend.

 

CMIA (Tenth Cause of Action)

 

M-Theory and IHS contend a plaintiff bringing a CMIA claim is obligated to allege that “an  unauthorized individual actually viewed her medical information.”  (Demurrer, p. 20 [citing Regents], emphasis in original.)

 

The demurrer is overruled.  As noted, the “actual viewing” requirement is not required.  (See J.M., supra, 19 Cal.5th at 711, 719-723.)
