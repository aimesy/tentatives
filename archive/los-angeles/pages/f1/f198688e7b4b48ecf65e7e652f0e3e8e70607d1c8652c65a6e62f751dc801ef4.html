ANALYSIS:

 

I.                Background

V.              Legal Standard & Discussion

 

A.    Motion to Withdraw Deemed Admissions

 

Plaintiffs move to withdraw the deemed admissions on the grounds that Plaintiffs erroneously relied on prior counsel to timely and properly engage in discovery, including RFA responses, and did not realize prior counsel failed to so do. Plaintiffs file a declaration from Ammari in support of the RFAs Motion and accompanying exhibits.

 

In Opposition, Defendants accuse Plaintiffs of bringing the RFAs Motion on false and fraudulent premises and highlight that Plaintiffs previously provided declarations earlier in the year regarding the delayed RFA responses. Defendants also contest Plaintiffs’ reliance on a declaration from former counsel filed in support of the RFA Motion’s accompanying declaration. Defendants submit a declaration from defense counsel in support of the Opposition as well as various exhibits.

 

In Reply, Plaintiffs state they do not rely on the declaration from former counsel as authenticated testimony, and Plaintiffs’ core contentions in the RFAs Motion are separate and apart from the Cho declaration as well as the objected-to portions of the Ammari declaration. The Reply emphasizes contentions raised in the RFAs Motion as well as also provides a supplemental declaration to respond to the concerns raised in the Opposition.

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.300 states in relevant part: “[t]he court may permit withdrawal or amendment of an admission only if it determines that the admission was the result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect, and that the party who obtained the admission will not be substantially prejudiced in maintaining that party’s action or defense on the merits.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.300, subd, (b), emphasis added.)  

 

Here, Plaintiffs’ Motion is supported by an affidavit of fault from Ammari explaining that Ammari provided responses to the subject RFAs on January 28, 2026, to Plaintiffs’ former counsel; however, Ammari did not know that Ammari’s RFA responses were not served. (RFAs Motion, Ammari Decl., ¶¶ 7-9.) Further, Ammari states that: “Had I known that Mr. Cho had not served the RFA responses, that he was facing serious default judgments, or that he was unable to properly manage my case, I would have immediately sought new counsel and taken steps to protect my claims.” (RFAs Motion, Ammari Decl., ¶ 15.) Finally, Ammari attests to learning of the deemed admissions when reviewing SoCal Edison’s motion for summary judgment and/or discussing the case with Plaintiffs’ new counsel and promptly tried to correct the issue through the instant RFAs Motion. (RFAs Motion, Ammari Decl., ¶¶ 17-19.) The RFAs Motion also is accompanied by an asserted declaration from Plaintiffs’ former counsel (RFAs Motion, Ammari Decl., Exh. 2.)

 

While the Court acknowledges Defendants’ arguments regarding Ammari’s past declarations filed to the Court regarding the RFAs in March 2026, the Court finds the supplemental declaration filed by Plaintiffs in response to the Opposition addresses this issue. (See Opp.; Reply, Supp. Ammari Decl.) In the supplemental declaration, Ammari declares under penalty of perjury that Ammari “did not personally understand all distinctions between draft responses, finalized responses, verifications, service, supplemental responses, and responses required by court order. [Ammari] relied on my attorney to handle those procedural requirements.” (Reply, Supp. Ammari Decl., ¶ 7.) Further, Ammari states “[t]o the extent any prior declaration or communication referred to ‘pending discovery responses,’ [Ammari] did not intend to represent that [Ammari] wanted the Requests for Admission deemed admitted, that [Ammari] agreed with the admissions, or that [Ammari] was knowingly allowing admissions to be entered against Plaintiffs. [Ammari] was relying on counsel regarding what responses remained to be finalized, verified, served, or corrected. [Ammari] understood that Mr. Cho was handling the discovery and that he would advise [Ammari] regarding what was required.” (Reply, Supp. Ammari Decl., ¶¶ 8-9.)

 

While Defendants’ arguments in the Opposition are well-taken, the Court finds Ammari’s declaration demonstrates that Plaintiff’s admissions were the result of excusable mistaken reliance on Plaintiffs’ counsel to timely and properly engage in discovery. (See New Albertsons, Inc. v. Superior Court (2008) 168 Cal.App.4th 1403, 1421.) The Court finds the cases Defendants rely on in the Opposition brief to demonstrate otherwise are factually distinguishable from the instant circumstances. Moreover, the Court notes that, in other circumstances, the Court is encouraged to grant relief to clients whose lawyers may have engaged in misconduct. (See Talbott v. Ghadimi (2025) 109 Cal.App.5th 967, 981-983.)

 

To the extent Defendants challenge the Cho declaration accompanying the RFAs Motion, the Court notes Defendants fail to provide any evidence beyond speculation to assert the Cho declaration filed in support of the RFAs Motion is fraudulent. The Court may not rely on mere speculation.

 

Further, Defendants state substantial prejudice will ensue if the Court allows the RFAs to be withdrawn as defense counsel has incurred more than $100,000 in fees and costs to bring a motion for summary judgment, motion for terminating sanctions, and a motion for sanctions that are all based, in part, on the deemed admitted RFAs. (Opp., pp. 16-17.) However, the Court notes motions for terminating sanctions and motions for sanctions incorporate renumeration; Defendants were awarded sanctions regarding the RFAs; Defendants represent that their pending motion for summary judgment does not entirely rely on the deemed admitted RFAs; and payment of certain fees and costs regarding the instant RFAs Motion appear to significantly remedy Defendants’ grievances. 

 

Therefore, the Court exercises its discretion under Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.300 to relieve Plaintiffs from the admissions. Further, Defendants shall be awarded costs, including attorney fees, in having to oppose the Motion and appear at the hearing on the Motion. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.300, subd. (c); see Rhule v. WaveFront Technology (2017) 8 Cal.App.5th 1223.)

 

The Court will decide the amount of attorney fees to be awarded to Defendants upon Defendants’ noticed motion should they choose to file one.

 

Also, the Court notes that the prior sanctions award regarding the RFAs is still in effect. (See 4/10/26 Order.)

 

B.    Motion to Set Aside Discovery Orders

 

Plaintiffs request the Court set aside the 3/5/26 and 4/10/26 discovery orders, except for the portion of the 4/10/26 order regarding the deemed admitted RFAs, under Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b).

 

Legal Standard

 

The court has broad discretion to vacate the entry of default, default judgment, or a dismissal where the moving party timely establishes a proper ground for relief. (Cruz v. Fagor America, Inc. (2007) 146 Cal.App.4th 488, 495.)  Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b) contains two distinct provisions for relief from default.  The first provision is discretionary and broad in scope, providing: “The court may, upon any terms as may be just, relieve a party or his or her legal representative from a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against him or her through his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect.  Application for this relief shall be accompanied by a copy of the answer or other pleading proposed to be filed therein, otherwise the application shall not be granted, and shall be made within a reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months, after the judgment, dismissal, order, or proceeding was taken.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b).) 

 

The second provision of Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b) is mandatory, providing: “[T]he court shall, whenever an application for relief is made no more than six months after entry of judgment, is in proper form, and is accompanied by an attorney’s sworn affidavit attesting to his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect, vacate any (1) resulting default entered by the clerk against his or her client, and which will result in entry of a default judgment, or (2) resulting default judgment or dismissal entered against his or her client, unless the court finds that the default or dismissal was not in fact caused by the attorney’s mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect. The court shall, whenever relief is granted based on an attorney’s affidavit of fault, direct the attorney to pay reasonable compensatory legal fees and costs to opposing counsel or parties. However, this section shall not lengthen the time within which an action shall be brought to trial pursuant to Section 583.310.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b).)

 

The general underlying purpose of Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b) is to promote the determination of actions on their merits. (Even Zohar Const. & Remodeling, Inc. v. Bellaire Townhouses, LLC (2015) 61 Cal.4th 830, 838-839.)  

 

Timeliness

 

As an initial matter, the Court notes the Discovery Motion was filed on August 24, 2026, which is less than six months from the date of both subject discovery orders. Thus, the Motion is timely.

 

Analysis

 

Here, the Discovery Motion is accompanied by a declaration from Ammari attesting that the prior discovery orders were issued due to Plaintiffs’ mistaken reliance on their former counsel and Plaintiff’s health-related issues at the time of the subject discovery requests and responses. (See Discovery Motion, Ammari Decl.)

 

However, the Discovery Motion and declaration fail to specify whether Plaintiffs seek relief under the discretionary or mandatory provision of Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b).

 

Further, the Discovery Motion and accompanying declaration fail to specify how Plaintiffs’ former counsel erred and/or deviated from Plaintiffs’ expectations regarding the subject discovery. Indeed, the Discovery Motion and accompanying Ammari declaration merely state that “[Ammari] understood that discovery responses were prepared in December 2025 and January 2026. [Ammari] also understood that responses were being served or had been served by Mr. Cho’s office. [Ammari] did not understand at the time that Defendants would later claim that responses were not received, that objections had been waived, or that the discovery issues would result in orders compelling further responses, deemed admissions, or sanctions.” (Discovery Motion, pp. 3-4, 7-8, Ammari Decl., ¶ 6.) Plaintiffs fail to provide any argument or evidence that Plaintiffs complied with providing fully finalized and proper responses to the subject discovery to their former counsel prior to the March and April discovery orders. The Court does not find the ambiguous references to discovery response preparation prior to the discovery orders demonstrates that Plaintiffs believed the discovery responses were completed and timely served by former counsel prior to the discovery orders.

 

Plaintiffs also fail to identify any particular mistake made by Plaintiffs’ former counsel regarding the subject discovery.

 

Further, the Court does not find that discovery responses provided after the Court’s prior orders remedies the discovery issues present at the time the Court ruled on the discovery motions.

 

Finally, the Court notes that the attorney declaration from Plaintiffs’ former counsel accompanying the Discovery Motion is admitted by Ammari to not be independently authenticated by former counsel himself. (See Discovery Motion, Ammari Decl., pp. 4-5.)

 

For all these reasons, the Court DENIES the Discovery Motion.

 

VI.            Conclusion & Order

 

For the foregoing reasons, Plaintiffs Sami Ammari and Cherry Walsh’s Motion to Withdraw Deemed Admissions Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.300 is GRANTED.

 

Plaintiffs Sami Ammari and Cherry Walsh’s Motion to Set Aside and/or for Relief from the Court’s March 5, 2026 and April 10, 2026 Discovery Orders Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 473(b) is DENIED.

ANALYSIS:

 

I.                Background

 

On May 19, 2025, Plaintiff Steven Gonzalez (“Plaintiff”) filed the instant action against Defendant General Motors LLC (“Defendant”) and Does 1-10, alleging multiple violations under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act and Violation of Business and Professions Code.

 

On February 13, 2026, Plaintiff filed a First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) against Defendant and Does 1-10, alleging violations of the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act, Breach of Express and Implied Warranties, Violation of the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act, and Violation of the Business and Professions Code.

 

On March 18, 2026, Defendant filed an Answer.

 

            On July 8, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant Motion to Determine the Amount of Reasonable Attorney’s Fees, Costs, and Expenses to be Paid by Defendant (the “Motion”).

 

            No Opposition has been filed.

 

II.              Legal Standard

 

As to Song-Beverly warranty claims, prevailing buyers are entitled to recover reasonable attorney fees and have the burden to show that the fees incurred were reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation and were reasonable in amounts. (Doppes v. Bentley Motors, Inc. (2009) 174 Cal.App.4th 967, 997-998.)

 

“[V]erified time statements of the attorneys, as officers of the court, are entitled to credence in the absence of a clear indication the records are erroneous.” (Horsford v. Board of Trustees of California State University (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 359, 396.)

 

“[I]t is inappropriate and an abuse of a trial court's discretion to tie an attorney fee award to the amount of the prevailing buyer/plaintiff's damages or recovery in a Song-Beverly Act action, or pursuant to another consumer protection statute with a mandatory fee-shifting provision.” (Warren v. Kia Motors Am., Inc. (2018) 30 Cal.App.5th 24, 37.)

Under the Song-Beverly Act, “the lodestar adjustment method of calculating attorney fees is appropriate,” and an award of attorney’s fees is “limited to actual hours both expended and determined by the court to be reasonably incurred.” (Reynolds v. Ford Motor Co. (2020) 47 Cal.App.5th 1105, 1112-1117.)

The Court’s objective is to award attorneys’ fee at the fair market value based on the particular action. (See Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132.) “The reasonable hourly rate is that prevailing in the community for similar work.” (PLCM Group v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095.) “ ‘[T]he fee setting inquiry in California ordinarily begins with the ‘lodestar,’ i.e., the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate.’ ” (Ketchum, supra, 24 Cal.4th at p. 1134.) The lodestar method is based on several factors, as relevant to each particular case: “(1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, (4) the contingent nature of the fee award.” (Id., at p. 1132.) “The ‘ “experienced trial judge is the best judge of the value of professional services rendered in his court, and while his judgment is of course subject to review, it will not be disturbed unless the appellate court is convinced that it is clearly wrong.” ’ ” (Ibid.) A negative multiplier is appropriate when duplicative work is performed. (Thayer v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2001) 92 Cal.App.4th 819, 834.)

 

For purposes of determining a reasonable hourly rate for attorneys’ fees, the “relevant ‘community’ is generally based on where the services are rendered, i.e., where the court is located.” (Tidrick v. FCA US LLC (2025) 112 Cal.App.5th 1147, 1157; see also Syers Properties III, Inc. v. Rankin (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 691, 702.)

 

Courts typically find that paralegal fees are recoverable as a component of attorney fees. (See Del Biaggio v. Bansen (2026) 121 Cal.App.5th 831, 840.)

 

III.            Discussion

 

A.    Entitlement to Fees and Costs

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 1794, subdivision (d) states: “If the buyer prevails in an action under this section, the buyer shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.”

 

Here, Plaintiff provides a copy of the Settlement Agreement between the parties that states, in pertinent part, as follows:

 

Plaintiff Steven Gonzalez has agreed to accept the total sum of $5,000.00 exclusive of all attorney’s fees, costs, and expenses to resolve the Lawsuit and all claims concerning the Vehicle (the “Settlement Payment”). GM will issue a check, in that amount, made payable to “Steven Gonzalez and LAW OFFICES OF JON JACOBS,” which will be delivered to LAW OFFICES OF JON JACOBS, within 45 days after GM’s counsel has received a copy of this Agreement signed by Plaintiff. Reasonable attorneys’ fees, costs, and expenses pursuant to subdivision (d) of Section 1794 of the Civil Code shall be determined by the court by way of a noticed motion. Defendant agrees Plaintiff is the prevailing party for purposes of said fee motion.

 

(Motion, David Decl., ¶¶ 4-5, Exh. 1, ¶ 1, emphasis added.)

 

Given the express language of the Settlement Agreement and Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d), the Court finds Plaintiff is the prevailing party in this action for purposes of attorney’s fees and costs.

 

Additionally, the Court finds Plaintiff timely moved for attorney’s fees within the allowable time from the date of settlement in this matter. (See Hatlevig v. General Motors LLC (2026) 118 Cal.App.5th 644, 648-649.)

 

B.    Amount of Fees

 

Plaintiff moves for an award of $29,624.50 in attorney’s fees and $1,342.17 in costs. (Motion, p. 7.) Plaintiff states the requested attorney’s fees reflect 26.1 hours in attorney time billed at a rate of $795.00 per hour and 19.6 hours in paralegal time billed at a rate of $250.00 per hour, plus five hours of attorney time for the instant Motion and “wrap up” fees. (See Motion, pp. 4-5, David Decl., ¶¶ 3-19.)  

 

Here, Plaintiff submits a declaration from Plaintiff’s counsel’s managing partner, Chad A. David, as well as copies of the billing records for this matter. (See Motion, David Decl., Exh. 9.) The billing records demonstrate attorney time in this matter was spent on itemized tasks such as drafting pleadings, attending mediation, talking to client, attending court hearings, conducting legal research, engaging in settlement matters, and preparing the instant Motion. (See Motion, David Decl., Exh. 9.) The billing records also demonstrate paralegal time in this matter was spent on itemized tasks such as emails, filing, reserving court hearings, invoicing, taking and updating notes, reviewing court docket, and completing various administrative tasks. (See Motion, David Decl., Exh. 9.)

 

Upon review of the Motion and submitted evidence, the Court finds the hourly rates sought by Plaintiff to be reasonable and adequately supported. (See Motion, David Decl., Exh. 9.)

 

Given the length and nature of the action, that mediation was held in the action, and the nature of the fee award, the Court finds most of the time and hours incurred by Plaintiff’s counsel to be reasonable under a lodestar method. However, many of the entries accounted for paralegal work are secretarial in nature. For example, on 7/6/2026, an entry states “Drop off UPS shipper (settlement check) to UPS store, billing for .2. This is a secretarial task. Another example, an entry on 7/6/2026, states “review & enter receipt for UPS 2nd Day Air to Steven Gonzalez.” This is a secretarial task. Another example, on 7/6/2026, “called and spoke with Steven. He confirmed the spelling of his full name and confirmed the address we have is current.” These are not paralegal tasks that would warrant billing rate of $250.  Thus, the billing will be reduced to reflect these clerical/secretarial tasks in the amount of $2,000.

 

Furthermore, the Court does not find that all of the anticipated attorney’s fees for the instant Motion were incurred given there was no opposition or reply filed. (See Motion, David Decl., ¶ 19.) The Court thus finds three hours at $795.00 per hour reasonable for the instant Motion.

 

 

 

Thus, the Motion is GRANTED as to attorney’s fees in the reduced total amount of $26,034.50, reflecting a lodestar calculation of 29.1 hours billed at $795.00 per hour and 11.6 hours billed at $250.00 per hour.

 

As to the Motion’s request for costs, the Court does not find that a memorandum of costs was separately filed in this matter. (Benson v. S. California Auto Sales, Inc. (2015) 239 Cal.App.4th 1198, 1209, fn. 8 [“Benson did not make a motion for costs under Code of Civil Procedure sections 1032 and 1033.5, which,… required filing a stand-alone memorandum of costs to begin the process. (See Cal. Rules of Court, rules 3.1700 and 3.1702)”].)

 

As such, the Court may not award costs at this time. (Ibid.) Thus, the Motion is DENIED as to costs.

 

IV.            Conclusion & Order

 

For the foregoing reasons, Plaintiff Steven Gonzalez’s Motion to Determine the Amount of Reasonable Attorney’s Fees, Costs, and Expenses to be Paid by Defendant is GRANTED IN PART AND DENIED IN PART.

 

The Motion is GRANTED as to the request for attorney’s fees in the reduced total amount of $26,034.50.

 

The Motion is DENIED as to the request for costs.

ANALYSIS:

 

I.                Background  

 

On June 9, 2026, Petitioner Datura Capital, LLC filed the instant Petition for Approval of Transfer of Structured Settlement Payment Rights (the “Petition”) against Certain Statutorily Defined Interested Parties – here, Kylie Lefefe (“Payee”).

II.              Legal Standard

 

“The California Legislature has adopted the Structured Settlement Protection Act (SSPA) (§ 10134 et seq.) to protect structured settlement payees from exploitation by factoring companies.” (RSL Funding, LLC v. Alford (2015) 239 Cal.App.4th 741, 745.) 

 

“The court-approval process requires the factoring company to file a petition in the county in which the transferor resides for approval of the transfer, attaching copies of the petition, the transfer agreement, the disclosure form, the annuity contract, any qualified assignment agreement and the structured settlement agreement, a list of the names and ages of the transferor's dependents, notice of the court hearing date, and notice of a right to respond.” (321 Henderson Receivables Origination LLC v. Sioteco (2009) 173 Cal.App.4th 1059, 1066 [citing Ins. Code, §10139.5(c)].) 

 

“The California act…governing transfers of structured settlement payment rights defines a ‘“[s]tructured settlement agreement” [as] an arrangement for periodic payment of damages established by settlement or judgment in resolution of a tort claim in which the payment of the judgment or award is paid in whole, or in part, in periodic tax-free payments rather than a lump-sum payment.’” (Matthews v. Liberty Assignment Corp. (2016) 247 Cal.App.4th 71, 78.) 

 

“Annuity issuers and structured settlement obligors are defined as ‘interested parties’ under the SSPA (§ 10134, subd. (g)), and as such, are entitled to notice of petitions to authorize transfer of payments under a structured settlement agreement.” (RSL Funding, LLC, supra, 239 Cal.App.4th at p. 745.) 

 

Insurance Code section 10137 provides: “A transfer of structured settlement payment rights is void unless a court reviews and approves the transfer and finds the following conditions are met: (a) [t]he transfer of the structured settlement payment rights is fair and reasonable and in the best interest of the payee, taking into account the welfare and support of his or her dependents [; and] (b) [t]he transfer complies with the requirements of this article, will not contravene other applicable law, and the court has reviewed and approved the transfer as provided in Section 10139.5.”  (Ins. Code, § 10137.)  The transfer agreement is effective only upon approval in a final court order.  (Ins. Code, § 10139.5(a).) 

 

Insurance Code section 10139.5(a) requires the court to make the following findings: 

 

  1. The transfer is in the best interest of the payee, taking into account the welfare and support of the payee’s dependents. 
  1. The payee has been advised in writing by the transferee to seek independent professional advice regarding the transfer and has either received that advice or knowingly waived, in writing, the opportunity to receive the advice. 
  1. The transferee has complied with the notification requirements pursuant to paragraph (2) of subdivision (f), the transferee has provided the payee with a disclosure form that complies with Section 10136, and the transfer agreement complies with Sections 10136 and 10138. 
  1. The transfer does not contravene any applicable statute or the order of any court or other government authority. 
  1. The payee understands the terms of the transfer agreement, including the terms set forth in the disclosure statement required by Section 10136. 
  1. The payee understands and does not wish to exercise the payee’s right to cancel the transfer agreement. 

 

(Ins. Code, § 10139.5(a).) 

 

In addition, pursuant to Insurance Code section 10139.5(c):  

 

Every petition for approval of a transfer of structured settlement payment rights, except as provided in subdivision (d), shall include, to the extent known after the transferee has made reasonable inquiry with the payee, all of the following: 

 

  1. The payee’s name, address, and age. 
  1. The payee’s marital status, and, if married or separated, the name of the payee’s spouse.       
  1. The names, ages, and place or places of residence of the payee’s minor children or other dependents, if any. 
  1. The amounts and sources of the payee’s monthly income and financial resources and, if presently married, the amounts and sources of the monthly income and financial resources of the payee’s spouse.  
  1.  Whether the payee is currently obligated under any child support or spousal support order, and, if so, the names, addresses, and telephone numbers of any individual, entity, or agency that is receiving child or spousal support from the payee under that order or that has jurisdiction over the order or the payments in question. 
  1. Information regarding previous transfers or attempted transfers, as described in paragraph (11), (12), or (13) of subdivision (b).  The transferee or payee may choose to provide this information by providing copies of pleadings, transaction documents, or orders involving any previous attempted or completed transfer or by providing the court a summary of available information regarding any previous transfer or attempted transfer, such as the date of the transfer or attempted transfer, the payments transferred or attempted to be transferred by the payee in the earlier transaction, the amount of money received by the payee in connection with the previous transaction, and generally the payee’s reasons for pursuing or completing a previous transaction. The transferee’s inability to provide the information required by this paragraph shall not preclude the court from approving the proposed transfer, if the court determines that the information is not available to the transferee after the transferee has made a reasonable effort to secure the information, including making an inquiry with the payee. 

 

(Ins. Code § 10139.5(c).) 

 

III.            Discussion

 

The Payee declaration states Payee agrees to transfer the following payments to Petitioner: (1) one lump sum payment of $250,000.00 due on December 18, 2031; (2) one lump sum payment of $500,000 due on December 18, 2036; and (3) one lump sum payment of $1,154,663.00 due on December 18, 2041.  (First Amended Petition, Payee Decl., ¶ 9, Exh. 2, Addendum.) (First Amended Petition, Payee Decl., ¶ 8.) Indeed, Payee further states that Payee “received the underlying settlement from hot water being spilled on [Payee] at Dennys when [Payee] was a baby. [Payee is] not affected by those injuries anymore.” (First Amended Petition, Supp. Payee Decl., ¶ 2.)

 

Additionally, the Payee provided further information regarding the prior transfer – namely: “With respect to the prior transfer, [Payee] sold 7 semi-annual payments of $15,000 due July 1, 2026, through July 1, 2029, and $100,000 due December 18, 2027. Further, Petitioner files a supplemental declaration demonstrating that the requisite notice was served by FedEx.

Based on the foregoing reasons, Petitioner Datura Capital, LLC’s First Amended Petition for Approval of Transfer of Structured Settlement Payment Rights is GRANTED.

The underlying action arises from Defendant’s towing of Plaintiff’s motorhome. (See FAC.)

 

On June 30, 2026, Defendant filed the instant Demurrer to Plaintiff’s FAC (the “Demurrer”) and Motion to Strike Plaintiff’s FAC (the “Motion”).

 

On July 15, 2026, Plaintiff filed an Opposition to the Demurrer and an Opposition to the Motion.

 

On August 24, 2026, Defendant filed a Reply to the Demurrer.

 

           As of September 11, 2026, no Reply to the Motion has been filed.

 

II.             Request for Judicial Notice

 

In Support of the Demurrer, Defendant requests the Court take judicial notice of an administrative record issued by the State of California Department of California Highway Patrol – namely, “State of California Department of California Highway Patrol VEHICLES REPORT (Form CHP 180) issued on December 22, 2025.” (Demurrer, RJN, pp. 1-2, Exh. A.)

 

After reviewing the moving papers, the Court DENIES the request as irrelevant to the Court’s ruling on the Demurrer.  

 

III.           Legal Standard & Discussion

 

A.    Demurrer

 

“As a general matter, in a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) “A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Ibid.) The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)

 

A general demurrer may be brought under Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (e) if insufficient facts are stated to support the cause of action asserted or under section 430.10, subdivision (a), where the court has no jurisdiction of the subject of the cause of action alleged in the pleading. All other grounds listed in Section 430.10, including uncertainty under subdivision (f), are special demurrers.

 

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Id.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.) However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245).

 

           Meet and Confer

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41 requires that “[b]efore filing a demurrer pursuant to this chapter, the demurring party shall meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a).) The parties are to meet and confer at least five days before the date the responsive pleading is due. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(2).) Thereafter, the demurring party shall file and serve a declaration detailing their meet and confer efforts. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(3).) 

 

The Demurrer is accompanied by a meet and confer declaration as required by Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, subdivision (a). (Demurrer, Heller Decl.) According to the declaration, defense counsel and Plaintiff, who is proceeding pro per, met and conferred telephonically on June 22, 2026, regarding the issues raised in the Demurrer but were unable to arrive at a resolution. (Demurrer, Heller Decl., ¶¶ 2-3.)

 

Thus, the Court finds the meet and confer requirement is satisfied.

 

Analysis

 

Defendant demurs solely to the second, fourth, fifth, eighth, ninth, tenth, and eleventh causes of action in the FAC.

 

Cause of Action No. 2 – Breach of Bailee Duty

 

Here, the Court is unaware of a civil cause of action for “breach of bailee duty.” However, this cause of action appears in substance to be a negligence-based cause of action that states a separate duty and separate breaches than the first cause of action for negligence. Given that the Demurrer’s sole challenge to this cause of action is that it is duplicative of the first cause of action, the Court finds this argument meritless.

 

           Thus, the Demurrer is OVERRULED as to the second cause of action.

 

Cause of Action No. 4 – Common Law Duress and Extortion

 

           Here, the FAC pleads a cause of action for common law duress and extortion but fails to plead that Defendant obtained money through duress or extortion (Fuhrman v. Cal. Satellite Sys. (1986) 179 Cal.App.3d 408, 426, overruled on other grounds by Silberg v. Anderson (1990) 50 Cal.3d 205) or that Plaintiff entered into an agreement or transaction due to duress or extortion (see Tran v. Nguyen (2003) 97 Cal.App.5th 523, 529-531). (See e.g., FAC, pp. 68-69.)

 

           Indeed, the FAC does not plead that Plaintiff paid the fees demanded by Defendant regarding the mobilehome or consented to or entered into any agreement with Defendant regarding the mobilehome.

 

           Thus, the FAC fails to plead sufficient facts to plead a cause of action for civil duress or extortion.      

 

           Thus, the Demurrer is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND as to the fourth cause of action.

 

Cause of Action No. 5 – Violation of Vehicle Code section 22851.3

 

Vehicle Code section 22851.3 does not provide for a civil cause of action.

 

Thus, the Demurrer is SUSTAINED WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND as to the fifth cause of action.

 

           Cause of Action No. 8 – Violation of Penal Code section 115

 

           Here, the FAC pleads a cause of action for Violation of Penal Code section 115, which provides for the elements of a criminal felony and/or a post-conviction civil motion. The instant Court is a civil, not criminal, court, and there are no facts in the FAC stating the code section is applicable.

 

           Thus, the Demurrer is SUSTAINED WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND as to the eighth cause of action.

          

Cause of Action No. 9 – IIED

 

“ ‘The elements of a prima facie case for the tort of intentional infliction of emotional distress are: (1) extreme and outrageous conduct by the defendant with the intention of causing, or reckless disregard of the probability of causing, emotional distress; (2) the plaintiff’s suffering severe or extreme emotional distress; and (3) actual and proximate causation of the emotional distress by the defendant’s outrageous conduct. Conduct to be outrageous must be so extreme as to exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated in a civilized community.’ ” (Wilson v. Hynek (2012) 207 Cal.App.4th 999, 1009, citation and ellipses omitted.)

 

Here, the FAC alleges Defendant tampered with evidence; fabricated holds; made coercive demands; colluded with the government; “coordinated with a DMV official to defeat Plaintiff’s regulatory complaint”; and “conditioned release of Plaintiff’s home build on waiver of damage claims while accruing illegal storage fees.” (FAC, pp. 81-82.) The FAC further states that Defendant conducted these actions while knowing “that Plaintiff was recovering from two surgical procedures to evaluate and remove potentially malignant masses.” (FAC, p. 82.) As a result of Defendant’s actions, Plaintiff “suffered severe emotional distress including anxiety, depression, insomnia, fear of cancer during active medical evaluation, feelings of hopelessness, and the psychological trauma of watching his life’s work and path to his son being destroyed in real.” (FAC, pp. 82-83.)

 

The Court finds the FAC, when viewed as a whole, alleges Plaintiff suffered severe or extreme emotional distress (Hailey v. California Physicians’ Service (2007) 158 Cal.App.4th 452, 474-77 [finding facts supporting IIED claim where plaintiff pled resulting “depression, anxiety, and physical illness,” which manifested as “vomiting, stomach cramps, and diarrhea”]), and Defendant’s conduct was so extreme as to exceed all bounds tolerated in a civilized society. (See Michaelian v. State Comp. Ins. Fund (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 1093, 1114.)

 

Thus, the Court finds the FAC pleads sufficient facts to state an IIED cause of action.

 

           Accordingly, the Demurrer is OVERRULED as to the ninth cause of action.

 

           Cause of Action No. 10 – Negligence Per Se

 

To state a claim for Negligence Per Se, the following elements must be met: (1) Defendant violated a statute, ordinance, or regulation of a public entity; (2) that violation caused an injury or death; (3) death or injury resulted from occurrence which the law was designed to prevent; and (4) person suffering the injury or death was one of the class of persons for whose protection the law was adopted. (See Urhausen v. Longs Drug Stores Cal., Inc. (2007) 155 Cal.App.4th 254, 267; Evid. Code, § 669.)

 

           Here, the FAC appears to allege a cause of action for negligence per se on page 83 although the text is embedded under the ninth cause of action subheading.

 

The FAC states facts alleging that Defendant violated Vehicle Code section 22851.3; Plaintiff suffered an actual injury as a result of Defendant’s alleged statutory violation; and Plaintiff is within the protected class of persons for those statutes and suffered an actual injury that resulted from occurrence which the law(s) were designed to prevent. (Urhausen, supra, 155 Cal.App.4th at p. 267; Evid. Code, § 669.)

 

However, as discussed ante, the FAC fails to plead a cause of action for violation of Vehicle Code section 22851.3. The tenth cause of action fails to allege facts regarding any other statutory violation. (See FAC, pp. 83-84.)

 

Thus, the Demurrer is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND as to the tenth cause of action.

 

           Cause of Action No. 11 – Civil Action for Treble Damages

          

Penal Code section 496 provides that :

 

Every person who buys or receives any property that has been stolen or that has been obtained in any manner constituting theft or extortion, knowing the property to be so stolen or obtained, or who conceals, sells, withholds, or aids in concealing, selling, or withholding any property from the owner, knowing the property to be so stolen or obtained, shall be punished by imprisonment in a county jail for not more than one year, or imprisonment pursuant to subdivision (h) of Section 1170. However, if the value of the property does not exceed nine hundred fifty dollars ($950), the offense shall be a misdemeanor….

 

(Pen. Code, § 496, subd. (a).)

 

“Although section 496 defines a criminal offense, it also provides an enhanced civil remedy in the event there has been a violation of the statute—that is, where a person has knowingly received, withheld or sold property that has been stolen or that has been obtained in any manner constituting theft. The enhanced civil remedy authorized by the statute is that any party injured by the violation of section 496 may file an action for ‘three times the amount of actual damages’ sustained, and for costs of suit and reasonable attorney fees.” (Switzer v. Wood (2019) 35 Cal.App.5th 116, 119.)

 

“[T]he Penal Code provides that persons are guilty of theft if they (1) ‘feloniously steal . . . [or] take . . . the personal property of another’ or (2) ‘fraudulently appropriate property which has been entrusted to [them].’ The first of these definitions describes theft by larceny. . . . Importantly, larceny requires an ‘intent to steal’—in other words, ‘the intent, without a good faith claim of right, to permanently deprive the owner of possession.’ A jury instruction defining theft by larceny without the requisite specific intent is erroneous as a matter of law. . . . By leaving out any description of the defendant’s mental state, the modification effectively turned theft into a strict liability offense.’ ” (Garrabrants v. Erhart (2023) 98 Cal.App.5th 486, 504, internal citations omitted.)

 

“Although we are not asked here to determine whether plaintiff would have been able to prove theft, we observe that not all commercial or consumer disputes alleging that a defendant obtained money or property through fraud, misrepresentation, or breach of a contractual promise will amount to a theft. To prove theft, a plaintiff must establish criminal intent on the part of the defendant beyond ‘mere proof of nonperformance or actual falsity.’ ” (Siry Investment, L.P. v. Farkhondehpour (2022) 13 Cal.5th 333, 361–362, internal citation omitted.)

 

           Here, the FAC appears to allege a cause of action for treble damages under Penal Code section 496(a) on page 84 although the text is embedded under the ninth cause of action subheading.

 

           However, the FAC does not allege that Defendant bought or received the subject mobilehome in any manner constituting theft or extortion. (See Pen. Code, § 496, subd. (a).) Rather, the FAC states that Defendant’s withholding of the mobilehome was unlawful. (See e.g., FAC, pp. 74-75, 84-85.) Further, the FAC fails to plead sufficient facts to demonstrate the requisite criminal intent on behalf of Defendant for this cause of action. (See Siry Investment, L.P., supra, 13 Cal.5th at pp. 361-362.)

 

           Thus, the Demurrer is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND as to the eleventh cause of action.

 

B.    Motion to Strike

 

Legal Standard

 

California law authorizes a party’s motion to strike matter from an opposing party’s pleading if it is irrelevant, false, or improper. (Code Civ. Proc. §§ 435, 436.) Motions may also target pleadings or parts of pleadings that are not filed or drawn in conformity with applicable laws, rules, or orders. (Code Civ. Proc. § 436, subd. (b).) The Code of Civil Procedure also authorizes the Court to act on its own initiative to strike matters, empowering the Court to enter orders striking matter “at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 436.)

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 435.5 requires that “[b]efore filing a motion to strike pursuant to this chapter, the moving party shall meet and confer in person or by telephone with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to the motion to strike for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that resolves the objections to be raised in the motion to strike.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 435.5, subd. (a).) (Emphasis added.)

 

Meet and Confer

 

As an initial matter, the Court finds Defendant has not satisfied the meet and confer requirement of Code of Civil Procedure section 435.5, as there is no meet and confer declaration accompanying the Motion.

 

 

Analysis

 

Turning to the merits of the Motion, the Court finds that the Motion’s request to strike matters in the fifth, eighth, tenth, and eleventh causes of action are DENIED as moot given the Court’s ruling to sustain the Demurrer as to these causes of action, discussed ante.

 

As to the Motion’s request to strike damages for the IIED cause of action on the grounds the cause of action is insufficiently pled, this request is DENIED given that the Court overruled the Demurrer as to this cause of action.

 

As to the punitive damages request, there is an unchallenged cause of action for fraud in the FAC. (FAC, pp. 59-62.) Thus, there are sufficient facts to support a punitive damages request in the FAC. The Motion’s request to strike punitive damages request from the FAC is DENIED.

 

As to the Motion’s request to strike matters regarding Defendant’s alleged suppression of evidence, the Court DENIES this request. The Court does not find these matters to be irrelevant, false, or improper in context of the FAC and given the nature of the FAC. (See Code Civ. Proc. §§ 435, 436.)

 

As to the Motion’s request to strike paragraphs 8 and 35a of the FAC on the grounds Plaintiff is improperly seeking to individually recover for the entity described/named in these paragraphs, the Motion is DENIED. The Court finds these paragraphs are merely part of the general allegations portion of the FAC and not associated with any particular cause of action.

 

As to the Motion’s request to strike reference to “gross negligence” in the first cause of action for “negligence,” the Court DENIES this request. “California does not recognize a distinct cause of action for ‘gross negligence’ independent of a statutory basis” (see Saenz v. Whitewater Voyages (1990) 226 Cal.App.3d 758, 766; CACI No. 425), and a motion to strike is not the proper vehicle to challenge the sufficiency of the facts pled to support this aspect of the negligence cause of action.

 

As to the Motion’s request to strike all economic damages requests or attorney’s fees from the FAC, the Court does not find ruling on this request to be appropriate at this time given the Court’s ruling on the Demurrer.

 

Thus, the Motion is DENIED.

 

IV.          Conclusion & Order

 

For the foregoing reasons, Defendant Towwerks LLC dba Viertel’s Towing Service’s Demurrer to Plaintiff’s First Amended Complaint is OVERRULED IN PART AND SUSTAINED IN PART.

 

The Demurrer is SUSTAINED WITH 30 DAYS’ LEAVE TO AMEND as to the fourth, eighth, tenth, and eleventh causes of action. The Demurrer is SUSTAINED WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND as to the fifth and eighth causes of action. The Demurrer is OVERRULED as to the second and ninth causes of action.

 

Defendant’s Motion to Strike Plaintiff’s First Amended Complaint is DENIED.

II.              Legal Standard

 

For out-of-state counsel to appear before a California court as counsel pro hac vice, out-of-state counsel must be (1) a member in good standing of another state bar, (2) eligible to practice in any United State court or the highest court in another jurisdiction, and (3) associated with an active member of the California Bar as attorney of record. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(a).)

 

Further, the application must state:¿ 

¿ 

(1) The applicant's residence and office address;¿ ¿ 

(2) The courts to which the applicant has been admitted to practice and the dates of admission;¿ 

(3) That the applicant is a licensee in good standing in those courts;¿ 

(4) That the applicant is not currently suspended or disbarred in any court;¿ 

(5) The title of each court and cause in which the applicant has filed an application to appear as counsel pro hac vice in this state in the preceding two years, the date of each application, and whether or not it was granted; and¿ 

(6) The name, address, and telephone number of the active licensee of the State Bar of California who is attorney of record.¿ 

¿ 

(Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(d).)¿ 

 

Out-of-state counsel must file a verified application and a proof of service, which provides that a copy of the application and a notice of hearing have been served on all parties and on the California Bar at its San Francisco office. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(c)(1).)

 

No person is eligible to appear as counsel pro hac vice if the person is: (1) A resident of the State of California; (2) Regularly employed in the State of California; or (3) Regularly engaged in substantial business, professional, or other activities in the State of California. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(a).)

 

Out-of-state counsel is also subject to the California Bar’s Rules of Professional Conduct. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(f).)

 

Finally, any application fee set by the State Bar of California must be paid by counsel pro hac vice. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(e).)
