DEPARTMENT 511 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 24STCV17069    Hearing Date: September 15, 2026    Dept: 511

Tentative Ruling

 

Judge Upinder S. Kalra, Department 511

 

HEARING DATE:   September 15, 2026                                       

 

CASE NAME:           Ernesto Martinez v. American Honda Motor Co., Inc.

 

CASE NO.:                24STCV17069

 

MOTION FOR ATTORNEYS’ FEES

 

MOVING PARTY:  Plaintiff Ernesto Martinez

 

RESPONDING PARTY(S): American Honda Motor Co., Inc.

 

REQUESTED RELIEF:

 

1.      An order awarding $30,000.81 in attorneys’ fees and costs.

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

1.      Motion for attorneys’ fees and costs is GRANTED as modified in the amount of $15,928 in fees and $594.21 in costs.

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

 

On July 10, 2024, Plaintiff Ernesto Martinez (Plaintiff) filed a Song-Beverly complaint against Defendant American Honda Motor Co., Inc. (Defendant or Honda) with causes of action for: (1) breach of the implied warranty of merchantability; (2) breach of the implied warranty of fitness; (3) sale of defective merchandise without disclosing defects; (4) breach of express warranty; (5) failure to promptly repurchase product; and (6) failure to commence repairs within a reasonable time and to complete them within 30 days.

 

Plaintiff alleges he leased a 2022 Honda Ridgeline from Honda that developed various defects during the warranty period that Honda could not fix.

 

On August 12, 2024, Honda filed an answer.

 

On September 3, 2025, Honda filed a stipulation and order to continue the post-mediation status conference which the court GRANTED.

 

On December 10, 2025, Plaintiff filed an offer to compromise and acceptance under CCP § 998.

 

On December 18, 2025, Plaintiff filed a proposed order granting offer to compromise which the court GRANTED.

 

On June 15, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion for attorneys’ fees and costs. On September 1, 2026, Honda filed an opposition.[1] On September 8, 2026, Plaintiff filed a reply.

 

LEGAL STANDARD:

 

A prevailing buyer in an action under Song-Beverly “shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees based on actual time expended, determined by the Court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.”¿ (Civ. Code, § 1794(d).) By¿permitting¿buyers who prevail under Song-Beverly to recover their attorneys’ fees, “our Legislature has provided injured consumers strong encouragement to seek legal redress in a situation in which a lawsuit might not otherwise have been economically feasible.” (Murillo v. Fleetwood Enterprises, Inc. (1998) 17 Cal.4th 985, 994.)¿¿¿¿¿¿¿ 

¿¿¿¿¿ 

The prevailing party has the burden of showing that the requested attorney fees are reasonable. (Robertson v. Fleetwood Travel Trailers of California Inc.¿(2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 785, 817.) The party seeking attorney fees “is not necessarily entitled to the compensation of the value of attorney services according to [his or her] own notion or to the full extent claimed¿. . . .”¿ (Levy v. Toyota Motor Sales, USA, Inc.¿(1992) 4 Cal.App.4th 807, 816.) If the “time expended or the monetary charge being made for the time expended are not reasonable under all circumstances, then the court must take this into account and award fees in a lesser¿amount.”¿(Nightingale v. Hyundai Motor America¿(1994) 31 Cal.App.4th 99, 104.)¿¿¿¿¿¿¿ 

¿¿¿¿¿ 

A calculation of attorneys’ fees for a Song-Beverly action begins with the “lodestar” approach, under which the Court fixes the lodestar at “the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate.” (Margolin v. Regional Planning Com.¿(1982) 134 Cal.App.3d 999, 1004-1005.) “California courts have consistently held that a computation of time spent on a case and the reasonable value of that time is fundamental to a determination of an appropriate attorneys’ fee award.” (Ibid.)¿¿¿¿¿¿ 

¿¿¿¿¿ 

“It is appropriate for a trial court to reduce a fee award based on its reasonable determination that a routine, non-complex case was overstaffed to a degree that significant inefficiencies and inflated fees resulted.”¿ (Morris v. Hyundai Motor America¿(2019) 41 Cal.App.5th 24, 39.) It is also¿appropriate to¿reduce a fee award based on “inefficient or duplicative efforts” in the billing record. (Id. at p. 38.) However, the analysis must be “reasonably specific” and cannot rely on general notions about the fairness of the fee award. (Kerkeles¿v. City of San Jose¿(2015) 243 Cal.App.4th 88, 102.) Moreover, in conducting the analysis, courts are not¿permitted¿to tie any reductions in the fee¿award¿to some proportion of the¿buyer’s damages¿recovery. (Warren v. Kia Motors America, Inc.¿(2018) 30 Cal.App.5th 24, 39.)¿¿¿¿¿¿¿ 

¿¿¿¿¿ 

The lodestar figure may also be adjusted, based on consideration of factors specific to the case,¿in order to¿fix the fee at¿the¿fair¿market value¿for the legal services provided. (Serrano v. Priest¿(1977) 20 Cal.3d 25, 49;¿PLCM Group, Inc. v. Drexler¿(2000) 22 Cal.App.4th 1084, 1095.)¿The factors considered in¿determining¿the modification of the lodestar include the nature and difficulty of the litigation, the amount of money involved, the skill¿required¿and employed to handle the case, the attention given, the success or failure,¿and other circumstances in the case. (EnPalm, LLC v.¿Teitler¿Family Trust¿(2008) 162 Cal. App. 4th 770, 774 (emphasis in original).)¿A negative modifier was¿appropriate when¿duplicative work had been performed. (Thayer v. Wells Fargo Bank,¿N.A.¿(2001) 92 Cal.App.4th 819.)¿

 

ANALYSIS:

 

  1. Prevailing Party¿ 

 

Here, Plaintiff is the prevailing party because the CCP § 998 offer he accepted on November 6, 2025 provides such.¿(Offer to Compromise, December 10, 2025.)¿ 

¿ 

  1. Lodestar Fees¿ 

 

The lodestar method looks at the time spent on a matter multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate. (Serrano, supra¿20 Cal.3d at 49).¿The two-step process begins with the lodestar method, which is the time spent on the matter multiple by the hourly rate. After the lodestar method, the second step is¿determining¿whether a multiplier should be applied. The factors that¿Courts¿look at to determine if a multiplier is reasonable are: 1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, (4) the contingent nature of the fee award.” (Ketchum v. Moses¿(2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132).¿¿¿¿¿ 

¿ 

  1. Reasonableness of Hourly Rate¿ 

 

Plaintiff seeks to recover an hourly rate of $465.00.¿ 

¿ 

Honda challenges the hourly rate as excessive and insufficiently supported. They propose a rate between $400 based on the United States Consumer Law Attorney Fee Survey report.

 

Plaintiff replies that they provided sufficient evidence supporting their requested hourly rate and reiterate that it is reasonable.

¿ 

The trial court has discretion to “determine the hourly rates that should be used in the lodestar calculus.” (Morris, supra,¿41 Cal.App.5th at p. 35.)¿It may do so¿by relying “on its own knowledge and familiarity with the legal market,” “the experience, skill, and reputation of the attorney requesting fees,” “the difficulty or complexity of the litigation to which that skill was applied,” and “affidavits from other attorneys regarding prevailing fees in the community and rate determinations in other cases.” (Id.¿at p. 41.)¿¿ 

¿ 

Here, the hourly rates requested are reasonable. Plaintiff provided evidence of Counsel’s experience. (Declaration of Raphael B. Davis (Davis Decl.) ¶¶ 19-25.)¿ Counsel also provided evidence rooting his hourly rate in his knowledge of rates charged in California for comparable matters, prior awards of his rate in separate matters, and with the Laffey Matrix. (Id. at ¶¶ 26-32.)

¿ 

Thus, the hourly rates are¿appropriate. ¿However, billing at a high rate comes with the expectation that the attorney also works in an efficient manner that reflects the premium paid for his or her services. The court considers this fact in addressing the reasonableness of the hours expended, below.  

 

¿ 

  1. Reasonableness of Hours Spent¿ 

 

Plaintiff seeks to recover a total of 52.7 hours¿on this¿matter.[2] (Davis Decl. ¶¶ 33-35.)¿ 

¿ 

Honda argues the hours spent are woefully inflated, contain nonrecoverable administrative work, and improper block billing. They propose a $12,226.50 recovery.

 

Plaintiff replies that Honda did not meet their burden to demonstrate excessive billing, the billing was reasonable, and the court should make a de minimis adjustment, if any.

¿ 

Although a verified fee bill is “prima facie evidence the costs, expenses and services listed were necessarily incurred,” (Hadley v. Krepel¿(1985) 167 Cal.App.3d 677, 682), ultimately,¿Counsel¿still has the burden to¿demonstrate¿the reasonableness of charges.¿(Mikhaeilpoor,¿supra,¿48 Cal.App.5th¿at p.¿247.)¿¿ 

¿ 

“A trial court may not rubber stamp a request for attorney¿fees, but¿must determine the number of hours reasonably expended.” (Morris, supra,¿41 Cal.App.5th at p. 38 [citing¿Donahue v. Donahue¿(2010)¿182 Cal.App.4th 259, 271.])¿In their evaluation, courts¿consider¿whether the case was overstaffed, how much time the attorney spent on¿particular claims, and whether the hours were¿reasonably expended. (Ibid.) In short, courts will not allow “padding” in the form of inefficient or duplicative efforts. (Ibid.; see also¿Ketchum v. Moses¿(2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132.)¿“Plainly, it is appropriate for a trial court to reduce a fee award based on its reasonable determination that a routine, noncomplex case was overstaffed to a degree that significant¿inefficiencies and inflated fees resulted.” (Morris, supra,¿41 Cal.App.5th at p. 39.)¿¿¿¿ 

¿ 

Here, the court has reviewed the fee bill submitted by Plaintiff’s counsel and the proposed reductions by Defendant, with an eye for excessive, duplicative, or unreasonable charges as contended by Defendant. As will be shown, Defendant’s contention that the billings are excessive, duplicative and unreasonable is well taken, in part.

 

To¿start,¿Plaintiff¿bills at counsel’s rate regardless of task type. (Davis¿Decl.,¿Exhibit¿1.)¿ Boilerplate filings serve a useful purpose. They increase productivity by allowing for simple edits to existing documents. Thus, utilizing templates is to be commended when it results in efficiency. However, if templates are employed but attorneys are still billing significant time to make minor changes, such use of legal resources is unwarranted and any such bill is unreasonable, particularly if the attorney is billing at a high hourly rate. (Mikhaeilpoor, supra, at p. 250.) Here, the court found instance where the time spent for minor cut and paste edits to templates was unreasonable. 

 

For example, Plaintiff billed 2.5 hours to prepared a template bare bones complaint and other court documents. Only paragraphs 4, 15, 17 and the prayer list any facts specific to this case. Notably, Paragraphs 15 and 17 specific fact is the date of purchase. As far as the court can determine, the other court documents are mandatory court forms and the summons on complaint.

 

The list of excessive billing continues:

13.6 hours to prepare initial discovery requests to Honda, 0.7 hours to draft verifications, 0.5 hours to review the initial discovery requests prior to service, and 7.4 hours to review Honda’s responses. (Davis Decl., Exhibit 1.) This excess hardly represents efficiency and experience. Additionally, there are a few entries for apparently clerical work, such as, reviewing conformed copies of filed documents, forwarding copies of documents to opposing counsel, and preparing documents for filing. (Ibid.)

 

In sum, this was a garden variety lemon law case with only one court appearance before resolution. Moreover, there were no contested motions filed or defended, no depositions taken and the vehicle was never even inspected by Plaintiff.

 

 Considering counsel’s professed expertise in this area, these are not novel or complex issues requiring extended time. (Morris, supra,¿41 Cal.App.5th at p. 37.)

 

Therefore, the Court finds based upon its experience and knowledge of this type of litigation, the lack of novelty and complexity of this case, and, to align with the hours that should reasonably have been incurred, a reasonable lodestar amount is $15,928. To be clear, the Court makes this finding based upon its experience and knowledge of this type of litigation, the lack of novelty and complexity of this case, and the professed specialization of Plaintiffs’ counsel.

 

 

Accordingly, the court GRANTS Plaintiffs’ motion for attorneys’ fees, as modified, for a total of $15,928.

 

  1. Costs¿ 

 

Plaintiff seeks to recover¿$594.21 in costs.¿(Davis Decl. ¶ 36; Exhibit 1.)

¿

Honda contends the costs are not recoverable because Plaintiff is not a prevailing buyer and failed to substantiate the cost requests rendering them unreasonable.[3]

¿ 

“Except as otherwise¿expressly¿provided by statute, a prevailing party is entitled as a matter of right to recover costs in any action or proceeding.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1032, subd. (b).) (Emphasis added.) Code of Civil Procedure section 1032, subdivision (a) defines a “prevailing party” as “[1] the party with a net monetary recovery, [2] a defendant in whose favor dismissal is¿entered, [3] a defendant where neither plaintiff nor defendant obtains any relief, and [4] a defendant as against those plaintiffs who do not recover any relief against that defendant.”¿¿¿¿¿¿ 

¿¿¿¿¿ 

Costs are allowable if incurred,¿whether or not¿paid. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (c)(1).) Costs must also be “reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation rather than merely convenient or beneficial to its preparation” and must be reasonable in amount. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (c)(2)-(3).)¿¿ 

¿ 

Here, the costs are warranted, in part. Next, the costs are recoverable because the parties so agreed. (Davis Decl., Exhibit 2 [Judgment Pursuant to CCP § 998 at item 2].) The costs consist of filing fees and process service fees. (Davis Decl. ¶ 36.) These costs fall within the scope of recoverable costs contemplated by statute. (CCP § 1033.5(a)(1), (a)(4).) 

 

Therefore, the court GRANTS Plaintiff’s motion for costs of $594.21.  

 

CONCLUSION:

 

            For the foregoing reasons, the Court decides the pending motion as follows:

 

1.      Motion for attorneys’ fees and costs is GRANTED as modified in the amount of $15,928 in fees and $594.21 in costs.

Moving party is to give notice.

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Case Number: 25STCV03137    Hearing Date: September 15, 2026    Dept: 511

Tentative Ruling

 

Judge Upinder S. Kalra, Department 511

 

HEARING DATE:   September 15, 2026                                       

 

CASE NAME:           CCT-Steller, LLC v. Four Light Investments, LLC, et al.

 

CASE NO.:                25STCV03137

 

DEMURRER TO CROSS-COMPLAINT WITH MOTION TO STRIKE

 

MOVING PARTY:  Plaintiff/Cross-Defendants CCT-Steller, LLC and Mishel Mikail

 

RESPONDING PARTY(S): Defendants/Cross-Complainants Four Light Investments, LLC; Jeffrey B. Light; and Francine J. Light

 

REQUESTED RELIEF:

 

1.      Demurrer to the first, second, third, fourth, and fifth causes of action for failing to state sufficient facts to constitute a cause of action;

2.      Motion to strike the fourth cause of action, the fifth cause of action, and the prayer requesting punitive damages.

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

1.      Demurrer to the first, second, and third causes of action are OVERRULED;

2.      Demurrer to the fourth cause of action is SUSTAINED with leave to amend as to Four Light and SUSTAINED without leave to amend as to Jeffrey B. Light and Francine J. Light;

3.      Demurrer to the fifth cause of action is SUSTAINED without leave to amend;

4.      Motion to strike is DENIED without prejudice as moot;

5.      Cross-Complainants to file an amended cross-complaint, if any, within 21 days of this ruling.

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

 

On February 4, 2025, Plaintiff CCT-Steller, LLC (Plaintiff) filed a Complaint against Defendants Four Light Investments, LLC, Jeffrey B. Light, and Francine J. Light (Defendants) with seven causes of action for: (1) Breach of Contract; (2) Breach of Contract; (3) Specific Performance; (4) Breach of the Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing; (5) Fraud; (6) Constructive Trust; and (7) Declaratory Relief. 

 

According to the Complaint, Plaintiff and Defendants contracted to redevelop property located at 8468 Warner Drive, Culver City, CA 90231 (the Subject Property) from a warehouse to a creative office space. Plaintiff alleges that Defendants breached the contracts, impeded their ability to perform, and wrongfully denied bargained for benefits. 

 

On June 2, 2025, Defendants filed a demurrer with motion to strike. After taking the matter under submission, the court OVERRULED the demurrers to the first, second, and fourth causes of action; SUSTAINED the demurrers to the fifth and sixth causes of action with leave to amend; SUSTAINED the demurrers to the third and seventh causes of action without leave to amend; and GRANTED the motion to strike with leave to amend. 

 

On November 20, 2025, Plaintiffs filed the operative first amended complaint (FAC).  

 

On January 23, 2026, Defendants filed a demurrer with motion to strike. After oral argument, the court OVERRULED the demurrer and DENIED the motion to strike.

 

On April 14, 2026, Defendants filed an answer and a cross-complaint against Plaintiff/Cross-Defendant CCT-Steller, LLC and Cross-Defendant Mishel Mikail (collectively Plaintiff/Cross-Defendants) with causes of action for: (1) breach of development agreement; (2) declaratory relief re: grant agreement; (3) breach of the implied duty of good faith and fair dealing; (4) fraud; and (5) financial elder abuse.

 

According to the cross-complaint, Cross-Defendants induced Cross-Complainants into a development agreement. Cross-Defendants then allegedly repeatedly breached the agreement by mishandling project funds, failing to provide required documentation, excluding one of the managers from meetings, overcharging through affiliated entities, secretly negotiating an unfavorable lease, and failing to complete improvements by the contractual deadline. Cross-Complainants further allege that Cross-Defendant Mikail made unauthorized plan changes, misrepresented the feasibility of subdividing a portion of the project, failed to obtain a required certificate of occupancy, and left substantial work defective or incomplete, all while attempting to secure a 50% membership interest under the grant agreement.

 

On June 22, 2026, Plaintiff/Cross-Defendants filed the instant demurrer with motion to strike. On September 1, 2026, Defendants/Cross-Complainants filed oppositions. On September 8, 2026, Cross-Defendants filed replies.

 

LEGAL STANDARD:

 

Meet and Confer¿ 

¿ 

Prior to filing a demurrer, the demurring party is required to satisfy their meet and confer obligations pursuant to Code of Civ. Proc. §430.41, and demonstrate that they so satisfied their meet and confer obligation by submitting a declaration pursuant to Code of Civ. Proc. §430.41(a)(2) & (3). ¿The meet and confer requirement also applies to motions to strike. (CCP § 435.5.)¿

 

Here, the parties met and conferred via telephone after Plaintiff/Cross-Defendants sent a meet and confer email. (Declaration of Nilou A. Zakariaie ¶¶ 3-4.) This requirement is therefore met. 

 

Demurrer – Sufficiency of Facts¿ 

¿ 

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action.¿(Hahn v. Mirda¿(2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context.¿In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice.¿(Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)¿“A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. …. The only issue involved in a demurrer hearing is whether the complaint, as it stands, unconnected with extraneous matters, states a cause of action.”¿(Hahn¿147 Cal.App.4th at 747.)¿¿¿ 

¿¿ 

When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context, accepting the alleged facts as true. (Nolte v. Cedars-Sinai Medical Center (2015) 236 Cal.App.4th 1401, 1406.) Courts also consider exhibits attached to the complaint and incorporated by reference. (See Frantz v. Blackwell (1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 91, 94 (Frantz).)¿¿ 

 

Motion to Strike¿ 

¿ 

The court may, upon a motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (CCP § 436(a).) The court may also strike all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Id., § 436(b).) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (Id.¿§¿437.)¿“When the defect which justifies striking a complaint is capable of cure, the court should allow leave to amend.” (Vaccaro v. Kaiman¿(1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 761, 768.)¿

 

ANALYSIS:

 

Request for Judicial Notice

 

The court GRANTS Defendants/Cross-Complainants request for judicial notice of Exhibit A [final ruling on submitted matter dated October 28, 2025] and Exhibit B [ruling on demurrer dated March 24, 2026]. (Evid. Code § 452(c), (d).)

 

Demurrer

 

First Cause of Action: Breach of Development Agreement [Four Light against all Cross-Defendants]

 

Cross-Defendants contend this claim fails to allege Cross-Complainants’ own performance or an excuse for non-performance with facts.

 

Cross-Complainants argue they sufficiently plead this claim since they may generally allege their performance or excuse for nonperformance.

 

Cross-Defendants reply that Cross-Complainants must specifically allege facts of excuse and prevention from performance since that it what they alleged – not that they performed.

 

“To establish a cause of action for breach of contract, the plaintiff must plead and prove (1) the existence of the contract, (2) the plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance, (3) the defendant’s breach, and (4) resulting damages to the plaintiff. [Citation.]” (Maxwell v. Dolezal (2014) 231 Cal.App.4th 93, 97-98.) “A written contract may be pleaded either by its terms – set out verbatim in the complaint or a copy of the contract attached to the complaint and incorporated therein by reference – or by its legal effect. [Citation.] In order to plead a contract by its legal effect, plaintiff must ‘allege the substance of its relevant terms.’” (Heritage Pacific Financial, LLC v. Monroy (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 972, 993.)¿ 

 

The only element at issue is Four Light’s performance or excuse for nonperformance. A plaintiff's performance is excused if the other party prevented it. (Civ. Code § 1511(1); City of Hollister v. Monterey Ins. Co. (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 455, 490-419.) Substantial performance may be sufficient. (Murray’s Iron Works, Inc. v. Boyce (2008) 158 Cal.App.4th 1279, 1291-1292 [discussing evidence of substantial performance of contract by general contractor.]) 

 

“Where contractual liability depends upon the satisfaction or performance of one or more conditions precedent, the allegation of such satisfaction or performance is an essential part of the cause of action.” (Careau & Co. v. Security Pacific Business Credit, Inc. (1990) 222 Cal.App.3d 1371, 1389 (Careau).) “This requirement can be satisfied by allegations in general terms.” (Ibid.) However, there are two “important caveats” to this rule. (Ibid.) The first applies “where the condition is an event, as distinguished from an act to be performed by the plaintiff” which requires “a specific allegation of the happening of the condition. . . .” (Ibid.) The second applies when “specific facts” alleged by the plaintiff “do not constitute due performance.” (Id. at p. 1389-1390.)

 

Four Light alleges it performed “in accordance with the terms and conditions of its contractual obligations under the Development Agreement, except to the extent that Four Light was excused or prevented from doing so.” (Cross-Compl. ¶ 65.) On its face, this satisfies the general pleading requirements articulated in Careau. In fact, Cross-Defendants did not put forth any conditions precedent that were supposed to occur.[1]

 

Accordingly, the court OVERRULES the demurrer to the first cause of action.

 

Second Cause of Action: Declaratory Relief re Grant Agreement [All against CCT-Steller]

 

Cross-Defendants contend this claim fails because there is no ongoing controversy – only past breaches. They add this claim is duplicative of other causes of action.

 

Cross-Complainants argue they plead a forward-looking dispute over ownership of a 50% membership interest in Four Light concerning the Grant Agreement which is not separately engaged in a breach of contract claim.  

 

Cross-Defendants reply that the Grant Agreement, on which Cross-Complainants rely, is alleged by them to already have terminated. Therefore, there is no agreement for the court to determine rights under.

 

Any person claiming rights under a contract (oral or written) or under a written instrument other than a will or trust, or with respect to property, may bring an action for a declaration of the person's rights or duties with respect to another. (CCP § 1060.) A declaratory relief complaint must specifically allege that an actual, present controversy exists, and must state the facts of the respective claims concerning the disputed subject matter. (City of Cotati v. Cashman (2002) 29 Cal.4th 69, 79; Connerly v. Schwarzenegger (2007) 146 Cal.App.4th 739, 746.) A sufficient complaint: (1) sets forth facts showing the existence of an actual controversy relating to the parties’ legal rights and duties, and (2) requests the court to adjudge these rights and duties. (Ludgate Ins. Co. v. Lockheed Martin Corp. (2000) 82 Cal.App.4th 592, 606; Qualified Patients Ass’n v. City of Anaheim (2010) 187 Cal.App.4th 734, 751; see also Travers v. Louden (1967) 254.Cal.App.2d 926, 931-32 (Travers) [commenting that declaratory relief may be appropriate for parties with a continuing relationship].) A declaratory relief claim should not be used to determine issues that are already engaged by other causes of action. (Hood v. Superior Court (1995) 33 Cal.App.4th 319, 324.)¿¿ 

 

Here, Cross-Complainants sufficiently stated a claim for declaratory relief. First, it is not duplicative of other claims because there is no breach of contract claim concerning the Grant Agreement.[2] Second, there is an ongoing controversy even if the Grant Agreement is allegedly terminated. It specifically alleges that the Grant Agreement depends on the Development Agreement and since Four Light terminated the Default Agreement, the Grant Agreement likewise has terminated. (Cross-Compl. ¶¶ 60-63, 74-75.) They allege the controversy is whether CCT-Steller is still entitled to receive membership interests. (Cross-Compl. ¶¶ 77-78.) That they terminated the Grant Agreement, or contend it is terminated, is their contradictory position. It is not an allegation of a stale dispute.

 

Accordingly, the court OVERRULES the demurrer to the second cause of action.

 

Third Cause of Action: Breach of the Implied Duty of Good Faith and Fair Dealing [Four Light against all Cross-Defendants]

 

Cross-Defendants contend this claim fails because the only alleged conduct consists of the alleged breaches rather than separate conduct that frustrated the spirit of the bargain.

 

Cross-Complainants argue that they alleged independent bad faith conduct by Cross-Defendants to frustrate the bargain’s benefits not a mere restatement of failure to perform express terms.

 

Cross-Defendants reply that there is no independent conduct aside from the breach at issue in this claim.

 

Every contract contains an implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing that neither party will do anything to interfere with the other party’s right to receive the benefits of the agreement. (Howard v. American Nat’l Fire Ins. Co. (2010) 187 Cal.App.4th 498, 528; see also Careau, supra, 222 Cal.App.3d at p. 1393.)¿

¿¿ 

The elements of a claim for breach of implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing are: (1) a contractual relationship; (2) plaintiff fulfilled their contractual obligations; (3) any conditions precedent to defendant’s performance occurred; (4) defendant unfairly interfered with plaintiff’s right to receive the benefits of the contract; and (5) plaintiff was harmed by defendant’s conduct. (Waller v. Truck Ins. Exchange, Inc. (1995) 11 Cal.4th 1, 36.)¿¿¿ 

 

Here, this claim is sufficiently stated. Notably, there are allegations of “unfair dealing rather than mistaken judgment” that, while also constituting breaches of the Development Agreement, satisfy the pleading requirements for this claim. (Careau, supra, 222 Cal.App.3d at p. 1394-1395.) For instance, Cross-Complainants allege that Cross-Defendants agreed to deal at arms-length with third parties and serve the best interests of Four Light in dealing with third parties. (Cross-Compl. ¶ 24.) However, Cross-Defendants used third parties under their ownership or control in order to overcharge Four Light and were evasive when asked to provide cost breakdowns. (Cross-Compl. ¶¶ 25-26.) As another example, Mikail allegedly negotiated a long-term lease with a third party without authority or Four Light’s knowledge or consent. (Cross-Compl. ¶¶ 27-32.) This conduct shows “a conscious and deliberate act, which unfairly frustrates the agreed common purposes and disappoints the reasonable expectations of the other party thereby depriving that party of the benefits of the agreement.” (Careau, supra, 222 Cal.App.3d at p. 1395.) This is more than a simple restatement of contract breach.

 

Accordingly, the court OVERRULES the demurrer to the third cause of action.

 

Fourth Cause of Action: Fraud [Against All]

 

Cross-Defendants contend this claim fails to state this claim with the requisite specificity. They add the claim lacks allegations showing actual and justifiable reliance or contemporaneous intent not to perform. Finally, they contend the claim is duplicative of the breach of contract claim and the individuals lack standing to bring the claim.

 

Cross-Complainants argue that they sufficiently stated this claim that Cross-Defendants induced them to enter into a contract by identifying the speaker, the recipient, the timing, the means, and the content. They also argue they plead actual and justifiable reliance as well as contemporaneous fraudulent intent. Next, Cross-Complainants argue the claim is not barred by the economic loss rule and they have standing because they committed their own capital and Cross-Defendants alleged in their underlying complaint that the Lights are alter egos of Four Light.  

 

Cross-Defendants reply that the allegations describe the relationship, not misrepresentation, the only intent not to perform comes from the subsequent breaches, the alleged promises are contract terms which is not an independent misrepresentation, and the individual Lights do not have standing.

 

“The elements of fraud, which give rise to the tort action for deceit, are (a) misrepresentation (false representation, concealment, or nondisclosure); (b) knowledge of falsity (or 'scienter'); (c) intent to defraud, i.e., to induce reliance; (d) justifiable reliance; and (e) resulting damage.” (Lazar v. Superior Court¿(1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 638.)¿¿¿ 

 

Fraud in the inducement is “a misrepresentation involving a contract in which the promisor knows what he or she is signing but consent is induced by fraud.” (McClain v. Octagon Plaza, LLC (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 784, 792 [internal quotations omitted].)

 

“In California, fraud must be pled specifically; general and conclusory allegations do not suffice…this particularity requirements necessitates pleading facts which show how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were tenders.” (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 645; Cansino v. Bank of America (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 1462, 1469; Perlas v. GMAC Mort., LLC (2010) 187 Cal.App.4th 429, 434 [requiring plaintiffs who claim fraud against a corporation to allege the names of the persons who made the misrepresentations, their authority to speak for the corporation, to whom they spoke, what they said or wrote, and when it was said or written.]) Fraud allegations need not be liberally construed, general pleading of the legal conclusion of fraud is insufficient, and every element of the cause of action for fraud must be alleged fully, factually and specifically. (Wilhelm v. Pray, Price, Williams & Russell (1986) 186 Cal. App. 3d 1324, 1331.)¿¿However, “[p]urely evidentiary matters – usually circumstantial evidence or admissions showing lack of that intention [to perform] – should not be pleaded.”[3] (Beckwith v. Dahl (2012) 205 Cal.App.4th 1039, 1060 (Beckwith).)  

 

After reviewing the cross-complaint, in full and in context, Cross-Complainants insufficiently stated a fraud claim. To start, the alleged misrepresentations are merely promises to perform under the Development Agreement. As plead, this appears as a mere restatement of the breach of contract claim. What is more, there are insufficient allegations of knowledge of falsity. (Cross-Compl. ¶ 90 [concluding no intention to perform at the time made, at the time of contracting and/or subsequent thereto]; ¶ 91 [alleging on information and belief that Cross-Defendants had already formulated a plan not to perform].) Finally, there are insufficient facts demonstrating that the individual Lights have standing. Throughout the Cross-Complaint, they allege that Four Light owns the property, Four Light entered the Development Agreement, Four Light would make payments, Four Light was coerced into making additional payments, and Cross-Defendants acted against Four Light’s best interests. (Cross-Compl. ¶¶ 10, 13, 15, 18, 19, 26.) There are no allegations as to how the Lights in their individual, rather than representative capacities as members of Four Light, were defrauded.

 

“A fraudulent representation is an antecedent statement made as an inducement to the contract, but is not a part or element of the contract.” (A.A. Baxter Corp. v. Colt Industries, Inc. (1970) 10 Cal.App.3d 133, 153-154.)

 

Accordingly, the court SUSTAINS the demurrer on the fourth cause of action.

 

Fifth Cause of Action: Financial Elder Abuse [Jeffrey & Francine Light against all Cross-Defendants]

 

Cross-Defendants contend this claim fails because the Lights lack standing since Four Light paid funds, not them. Additionally, Cross-Defendants argue this is another recasting of the breach of contract claim plead in conclusory terms (especially allegations of undue influence).

 

Cross-Complainants argue they have standing to bring this claim, the commercial setting does not immunize Cross-Defendants’ predatory conduct, and they sufficiently alleged wrongful use and undue influence.

 

Cross-Defendants reply that the Lights cannot recover for alleged property rights held by Four Light, that Four Light is not a representative under the Welfare and Institutions Code, the alleged conduct is contractual performance, and the conclusory language of wrongful use and undue influence insufficiently state the claim.

 

The necessary elements of a cause of action for financial abuse of an elder are: (1) that the defendant obtained the elder’s property; (2) that the elder was 65 years of age or older at the time of the conduct; (3) that the defendant took the property for a wrongful use, such as fraud; (4) that the elder was harmed; and (5) that the defendant’s conduct was a substantial factor in causing the elder’s harm.¿ (See Welf. & Inst. Code, § 15610.30 (a); see also Stebley v. Litton Loan Servicing, LLP (2011) 202 Cal.App.4th 522, 527-528 (Stebley).) 

 

An elder means “any person residing in this state, 65 years of age or older.” (Welf. & Inst. Code § 15610.27; Hilliard v. Harbour (2017) 12 Cal.App.5th 1006, 1011 (Hilliard).)

 

There is a “distinction between derivative and individual actions [which] applies to limited liability companies as well as corporations,” “in determining whether an induvial action as opposed to a derivative action lies a court looks at the gravamen of the wrong alleged in the pleadings,” and “a shareholder may not recover individually for injury to the corporation . . . .” (Hilliard, supra, 12 Cal.App.5th at p. 1013 [internal quotations omitted].) “[O]ne who has suffered injury both as an owner of a corporate entity and in an individual capacity is entitled to pursue remedies in both capacities.” (Ibid. [citing Denevi v. LGCC, LLC (2004) 121 Cal.App.4th 1211, 1221.)

 

In Hilliard, the Court of Appeal concluded the individual elder did not have standing to pursue a financial elder abuse claim. There, the elder owned a controlling interest in an LLC which took out loans. (Hilliard, supra, 12 Cal.App.5th at p. 1008.) The defendant banks sold the loan to a third party who commenced a civil action, and obtained a judgment in their favor, for breach of one of the agreements. (Id. at p. 1009-1010.) The Court reasoned that the conduct alleged in this action was directed to the already-existing LLC that the elder controlled but not to the elder’s separate property. (Id. at p. 1015.) Critically, the only connection was the elder’s shareholder status so it was not a personal claim. (Ibid.) The Court refused to extend a public policy protection simply because of an LLC member’s status as an elder. (Id. at p. 1015-1016.)

 

So, too, here. As discussed above, the only interests identified as harmed are Four Light’s. Four Light owns the property. (Cross-Compl. ¶ 10.) Four Light entered into the contracts. (Cross-Compl. ¶¶ 14, 15.) Four Light paid to $4 million for Improvements. (Cross-Compl. ¶ 19.) They have one allegation that they were induced to inject millions of dollars of capital into Four Light to fund the Improvements, but this allegation is qualified that is was part of the Development Agreement. (Cross-Compl. ¶ 102.) Like in Hilliard, the only identified interest the Lights have is as members of Four Light. (Cross-Compl. ¶ 10.) The purported bad acts are alleged in the context of performance (or breach thereof) of the Development Agreement: excluding Francine Light from meetings about Improvements, hostility when discussing paying bills “in complete disregard of Four Light’s contractual rights,” and failing to forward refund payments “that were for Four Light.” (Cross-Compl. ¶ 104.) In sum, these injuries are to Four Light – not to the Lights individually. As such, they lack standing to pursue this claim.

 

Accordingly, the court SUSTAINS the demurrer to the fifth cause of action.

 

Motion to Strike

 

In light of the ruling on the demurrer, the motion to strike is DENIED as moot.

 

Leave to Amend

 

Leave to amend should be liberally granted if there is a reasonable possibility an amendment could cure the defect.¿ (County of Santa Clara v. Superior Court (2022) 77 Cal.App.5th 1018,1035.)¿ The plaintiff has the burden of demonstrating that leave to amend should be granted, and that the defects can be cured by amendment. (“Plaintiff must show in what manner he can amend his complaint and how that amendment will change the legal effect of his pleading.” (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349.)

 

Here, Cross-Complainants seek leave to amend. Leave to amend is warranted, in part. The fraud claim is reasonably capable of curing as to Four Light. It is not reasonably capable of curing as to the Lights in their individual capacities. Additionally, the financial elder abuse claim is not reasonably capable of curing. Therefore, leave to amend is warranted only for Four Light as to the fraud claim.

 

Accordingly, leave to amend is GRANTED, in part.

 

CONCLUSION:

 

            For the foregoing reasons, the Court decides the pending motion as follows:

 

1.      Demurrer to the first, second, and third causes of action are OVERRULED;

2.      Demurrer to the fourth cause of action is SUSTAINED with leave to amend as to Four Light and SUSTAINED without leave to amend as to Jeffrey B. Light and Francine J. Light;

3.      Demurrer to the fifth cause of action is SUSTAINED without leave to amend;

4.      Motion to strike is DENIED without prejudice as moot;

5.      Cross-Complainants to file an amended cross-complaint, if any, within 21 days of this ruling.

Moving party is to give notice.
