LEGAL STANDARD:

 

C.C.P. § 187 states: “When jurisdiction is, by the Constitution or this Code, or by any other statute, conferred on a Court or judicial officer, all the means necessary to carry it into effect are also given; and in the exercise of this jurisdiction, if the course of proceeding be not specifically pointed out by this Code or the statute, any suitable process or mode of proceeding may be adopted which may appear most conformable to the spirit of this Code.”

 

Section 187 “grants to every court power to use all means to carry its jurisdiction into effect, even if those means are not specifically pointed out in the code. [Citation.] It is now settled that ‘... the authority of the court will be exercised to impose liability under a judgment upon the alter ego who has had control of the litigation.’ [Citation.]”  (Jack Farenbaugh & Son v. Belmont Construction, Inc. (1987) 194 Cal.App.3d 1023, 1028–1029 [citation and footnote omitted]; see Greenspan v. LADT, LLC (2010) 191 Cal.App.4th 486, 508 [“Judgments may be amended to add additional judgment debtors on the ground that a person or entity is the alter ego of the original judgment debtor….”].)  This is an equitable procedure based on the theory that the Court is not amending the judgment to add a new defendant but is merely inserting the correct name of the real defendant.  (McClellan v. Northridge Park Townhome Owners Association (2001) 89 Cal. App. 4th 746, 752 [permitting a plaintiff to add the individual who was the alter ego of a judgment debtor corporation].)  This procedure permits the Court to make the judgment “speak the truth” as to the true names of the defendant.  (Jack Farenbaugh, supra, 194 Cal.App.3d at 1029.)

 

“The ability under section 187 to amend a judgment to add a defendant, thereby imposing liability on the new defendant without trial, requires both (1) that the new party be the alter ego of the old party and (2) that the new party had controlled the litigation, thereby having had the opportunity to litigate, in order to satisfy due process concerns.” (Toho-Towa Co., Ltd. v. Morgan Creek Productions, Inc. (2013) 217 Cal.App.4th 1096, 1106.) 

 

“In California, two conditions must be met before the alter ego doctrine will be invoked. First, there must be such a unity of interest and ownership between the corporation and its equitable owner that the separate personalities of the corporation and the shareholder do not in reality exist. Second, there must be an inequitable result if the acts in question are treated as those of the corporation alone. [Citation.]” (Cam-Carson, LLC v. Carson Reclamation Authority (2022) 82 Cal.App.5th 535, 549 [internal quotation marks and citations omitted].)  “Alter ego liability is not limited to the parent-subsidiary corporate relationship; rather, under the single-enterprise rule, liability can [also] be found between sister [or affiliated] companies.”  (Id. at 550.) 

 

“[A] successor company has liability for a predecessor's actions if: (1) the successor expressly or impliedly agrees to assume the subject liabilities […], (2) the transaction amounts to a consolidation or merger of the successor and the predecessor, (3) the successor is a mere continuation of the predecessor, or (4) the transfer of assets to the successor is for the fraudulent purpose of escaping liability for the predecessor's debts.”  (CenterPoint Energy, Inc. v. Superior Court (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 1101, 1120.)

 

ANALYSIS:

 

Plaintiff seeks to amend the Judgment—originally entered against Elit Auto Group, Inc. (“EAG”) and Karlen Malkhasyan—to include non-party Elit Los Angeles (“ELA”) as the alter ego or successor in interest of EAG. 

 

Plaintiff’s counsel, Daren M. Schlecter, states that the California Secretary of State records show that EAG was registered on January 25, 2016, Karlen Malkhasyan is the owner and manager of EAG, EAG is designated as a “Retail Automotive Dealership,” and EAG is operated at 19420 Business Center Dr., Suite 100 in Northridge.  (Schlecter Decl., ¶¶6-7, Exs. A-B [EAG’s March 10, 2021 and May 18, 2022 Secretary of State filings].)  EAG is currently inactive and was suspended by the Franchise Tax Board in 2024.  (Id., ¶8, Ex. C.) 

 

Mr. Schlecter states that on April 20, 2020, ELA was created by Vahag Malkhasyan, Edgar Antonyan was identified as the CEO, its address was 19420 Business Center Dr., Suite 100 (the address later switched to 14310 Victor Blvd. in Van Nuys in 2026), and ELA was designated as a “Retail Automotive Dealership.”  (Id., ¶¶10-11, Exs. D-E [ELA’s April 2, 2020 and February 16, 2026 Secretary of State filings].)  Mr. Schlecter states that according to LinkedIn, Karlen Malkhasyan identifies himself as ELA’s CEO.  (Id., ¶¶12-13, Ex. F.) 

 

On February 26, 2026, Plaintiff’s counsel served on Zelle (a mobile application to transfer funds between bank accounts) a Subpoena for Judgment Debtor Karlen Malkhasyan’s transactional history and received responsive documents on March 16, 2026, showing transactions between Karlen Malkhasyan and ELA from 2022 to the present.  (Id., ¶¶14-17, Ex. G.)  Mr. Schlecter states that the logos for EAG and ELA are identical in color, shape, and usage to promote both auto businesses.  (Id., ¶¶18-19, Ex. H.)  (The Court notes that the EAG logo appeared to be comprised solely of a red circle with a tilted “E,” whereas the ELA logo includes the same image but with “ELIT LOS ANGELES” written to the right of the circle image.) 

 

Plaintiff argues that based on when the loan agreement was entered (March 4, 2020), ELA was formed (April 2, 2020), Defendants defaulted (June 2020), and Judgment was entered (August 25, 2021), it believes that Karlen Malkhasyan created ELA to evade payment to Plaintiff as he continues to operate a new entity.  (Schlecter Decl., ¶¶2-4, 10, 20-22.) 

 

Plaintiff argues that the Court should apply the alter ego doctrine between EAG and ELA because: (1) Karlen Malkhasyan controls both EAG and ELA; (2) the interest between EAG and ELA are identical as they have the same/similar names, are in the same industry/line of business, have the same logo, and are/were located at the same address; (3) an inequitable result would occur if ELA was not added as an alter ego because Defendants could avoid payment to Plaintiff and continue to operate their business under a new name (even if the Secretary of State documents indicate a different CEO, which Plaintiff asserts was Karlen Malkhasyan’s attempt to evade service and enforcement of the Judgment); and (4) Plaintiff did not discover the extent of the relationship between EAG and ELA until after ELA was created in April 2020 and Plaintiff began further investigation.  (Mot. at pp.6-7.)  It also argues that successor liability applies for the same reasons.  (See id. at pp.7-8.)

 

This motion was served on EAG, Karlen Malkhasyan, and ELA.  Neither Defendants nor ELA opposed the motion or provided any contrary evidence that EAG and ELA are separate entities, are not alter egos of the other, or are not successor corporations. 

 

Based on Mr. Schlecter’s declaration and the attached documentary evidence, as well as Defendants and ELA’s lack of opposition to the motion, the Court finds that Plaintiff has partially upheld its burden in showing alter ego liability and/or successor company liability.  Here, while Plaintiff has shown that an inequitable result would occur if ELA were treated as a separate corporation from Defendants, Plaintiff has not fully established that there is a unity of interest between the EAG, ELA, and Karlen Malkhasyan.  There may be some similarities between the entities and the timing of ELA’s formation may be suspect, but without more, the Court cannot ascertain whether there is such a unity of interest where there is no separation between Defendants and ELA.  Similarly, with respect to ELA being the successor in interest to EAG, Plaintiff has not shown that ELA expressly or impliedly agreed to assume EAG’s liabilities, their transactions that amounted to a consolidation or merger of the entities, or that there were indeed any transfer of assets to avoid judgment.  Thus, the motion is continued to allow Plaintiff additional time to brief the aforementioned issues and present further supporting evidence.

ANALYSIS RE MOTION FOR WAIVER/MODIFICATION OF UNDERTAKING: 

 

A.    Legal Standard

 

C.C.P. § 917.1 states: in relevant part

 

(a) Unless an undertaking is given, the perfecting of an appeal shall not stay enforcement of the judgment or order in the trial court if the judgment or order is for any of the following:

 

(1) Money or the payment of money, whether consisting of a special fund or not, and whether payable by the appellant or another party to the action.

 

(2) Costs awarded pursuant to Section 998 which otherwise would not have been awarded as costs pursuant to Section 1033.5.

 

(3) Costs awarded pursuant to Section 1141.21 which otherwise would not have been awarded as costs pursuant to Section 1033.5.

 

(b) The undertaking shall be on condition that if the judgment or order or any part of it is affirmed or the appeal is withdrawn or dismissed, the party ordered to pay shall pay the amount of the judgment or order, or the part of it as to which the judgment or order is affirmed, as entered after the receipt of the remittitur, together with any interest which may have accrued pending the appeal and entry of the remittitur, and costs which may be awarded against the appellant on appeal. This section shall not apply in cases where the money to be paid is in the actual or constructive custody of the court; and such cases shall be governed, instead, by the provisions of Section 917.2. The undertaking shall be for double the amount of the judgment or order unless given by an admitted surety insurer in which event it shall be for one and one-half times the amount of the judgment or order. The liability on the undertaking may be enforced if the party ordered to pay does not make the payment within 30 days after the filing of the remittitur from the reviewing court.

 

(C.C.P. § 917.1(a)-(b).) 

 

C.C.P. § 995.240 states: “The court may, in its discretion, waive a provision for a bond in an action or proceeding and make such orders as may be appropriate as if the bond were given, if the court determines that the principal is unable to give the bond because the principal is indigent and is unable to obtain sufficient sureties, whether personal or admitted surety insurers. In exercising its discretion the court shall take into consideration all factors it deems relevant, including but not limited to the character of the action or proceeding, the nature of the beneficiary, whether public or private, and the potential harm to the beneficiary if the provision for the bond is waived.”   

 

“If it is apparent from the plaintiff's declaration and financial information that obtaining an undertaking would be impossible, the law does not require the futile act of attempting to do so.”  (Alshafie v. Lallande (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 421, 436, fn.10.)

 

B.     Evidentiary Objections

 

With Plaintiff’s Notice of Nonexistent Case Citations, Plaintiff submitted evidentiary objections to Defendants’ reply brief and the accompanying declaration of defense counsel Noah Green.  The objections are not enumerated but the Court will consider them in consecutive order.  Objection Nos. 1, 2, and 7 are sustained as to paragraph 3, 4, and 8 and Exhibits A-C as no exhibits are attached to the declaration.  Objection Nos. 3-5 as to paragraphs 6-8 regarding meeting and confer efforts are overruled.  Objection No. 6 to Mr. Green’s declaration at paragraphs 10-11 in support of the reply arguments at 7:12-18 is sustained as his declaration does not speak to the arguments raised.  Objection Nos. 8-9 citing to Mr. Green’s declaration at paragraph 8 as referenced in the reply brief at 8:15-21 are sustained to the extent that they do not support the arguments raised in the reply brief.  With respect to arguments in the reply brief, the objections are overruled as arguments are not evidence but the Court is sustaining the citations to evidence in the reply brief as specifically cited above.

 

C.     Discussion

 

Defendants move for a waiver or modification of a C.C.P. § 917.1 undertaking, find that Defendants are indigent or unable to obtain sufficient sureties pursuant to section 995.240, and impose a stay of enforcement of Judgment pending appeal.  Defendants argue that despite their efforts to obtain funds for a surety bond securing the Judgment, they lack the financial means to do so.  Defendants calculate that the collateral required to secure a surety bond is nearly $700,000 for Zavela (based on his $466,082.20 judgment) and $625,000 for Ryde (based on its $416,082.20 judgment), as surety companies typically require 150% of the judgment to issue a bond.  (The Court notes that Defendants calculated the aforementioned judgment amounts by overlapping the joint-and-several liability portions and assigning them to each Defendant.)

 

First, in considering whether to apply section 995.240’s waiver provision, the Court assesses whether Defendants are unable to give the bond because they are indigent and unable to obtain sufficient sureties. 

 

Defendants argue that, despite best efforts, they have been unable to compile the more than $625,000 in collateral estimated to be needed to post a bond to stay enforcement of the judgment pending appeal.  They argue the undertaking requirement should be waived or modified due to financial hardship, their inability to qualify for an appeal bond, and lack of prejudice to Plaintiff.

 

The Court has reviewed Defendants’ declarations and exhibits and finds that the showing of indigency is insufficient to support granting this motion. 

 

Zavela provides his FL-150, which reflects his total monthly income of $6,000 and monthly estimated expenses of $11,323.41.  (Zavela Decl., ¶6, Ex. 1 [Income and Expense Declaration].)  He also provides a Wells Fargo letter, denying his application for a $75,000 personal loan.  (Id., ¶7, Ex. 2 [Feb. 24, 2026 Wells Fargo Letter].)  He states his primary checking account balance has about $3,000 and that the highest balance he had from January 2025 to September 2025 was $9,421.26, but typically his balance is between $2,000 to $3,500. (Id., ¶8.)  He states that he does not own any real estate or brokerage accounts.  (Id., ¶9.) 

 

Zavela’s Income and Expense Declaration (FL-150 form) is not signed or dated.  Furthermore, it is not supported by any further documentation, such as bank account records, tax returns, etc.  With respect to the February 24, 2026 letter entitled “Information regarding your rate check inquiry,” Wells Fargo states: “Thank you for requesting personalized rates and payments for a Wells Fargo Personal Loan. We are not able to provide you rate and payment estimate at this time. … You may submit another rate check inquiry after 30 days of the date of this letter.”  Zavela did not include any information regarding his $75,000 loan application nor does this letter explicitly state that Wells Fargo was in fact rejecting the loan application for the $75,000 amount (it may be that Wells Fargo would approve a lesser amount upon reapplication after 30 days).

 

Next, Ryde’s CEO, Roman Dombrovsky, states that Ryde’s Profit and Loss Statements reflected an income of $40,241.05 for 2024 and -$274,867.98 for 2025.  (Dombrovsky Decl., ¶6, Ex. A [2024 and 2025 Profit and Loss Statements.)  He states that Ryde’s only notable asset is a Bank of America checking account with an approximate balance of $70,000, but the funds are needed for Ryde’s ongoing business operations.  (Id., ¶7.)  Mr. Dombrovsky states that Ryde does not own any real estate and does not have assets in the amount of $416,082.20 (for the judgment amount).  (Id., ¶ 8-9.)  He states that Ryde will suffer prejudice if the Court does not grant this motion given Ryde’s limited assets and operating capital and that it will suffer irreparable reputational harm and would be required to curtail projects, layoff employees, suspend services, etc. (Id., ¶¶10-12.)  He contends that Plaintiff will not suffer harm if the enforcement of judgment were stayed as it will not reduce Plaintiff’s award.  (Id., ¶13.) 

 

It is unclear how the 2024 and 2025 Profit and Loss Statements were calculated and produced, who performed the calculations, whether they are accurate representations of Ryde’s finances, etc. The statements include the subheadings for “Equip Your Gym,” but do not include other information such as for Rental Gym Equipment or Gym Equipment Rental (or any other fictitious business names Ryde may have used).  Ryde too did not provide any tax returns or bank account information, including any Bank of America account statements.  At this stage, the Court cannot verify Ryde’s financial status and whether the Profit and Loss Statements capture an accurate representation of its finances.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that Defendants have consistently failed to produce financial information.  Alternatively, Plaintiff argues that the Court should defer ruling on this motion until Defendants sit for their judgment debtor examinations and produce documents.  (Opp. at p.12.) 

 

Without more, the Court finds that Defendants have not established their burden in showing that they are entitled to a discretionary waiver or modification of the undertaking requirement.  (See Cardinal Care Management, LLC v. Afable (2020) 47 Cal.App.5th 1011, 1020 [finding that the appellants’ showing of indigency was conclusory with “no specification of any detail, any facts, any reasons why, any anything,” such that it was “no abuse of discretion to deny relief from an undertaking where, as here, a litigant has made only a ‘weak and incomplete showing of indigency.’”].)  Thus, the motion may be denied on this basis. 

 

Second, the Court considers the factors, if relevant, including: the character of the action or proceeding, the nature of the beneficiary/Plaintiff (whether public or private), and the potential harm to Plaintiff if the provision for the bond is waived.  Plaintiff argues argue the Judgment arose due to Defendants’ willful misconduct through discovery abuses, misrepresentation, and contempt of Court orders.  (Opp. at pp.5-11.)  This Court and the parties are well aware of the factual and procedural background of the parties’ dispute and discovery violations.   Defendants engaged in a pattern and practice of claiming documents did not exist, discovery was complied with when in fact Plaintiff showed such representations were not accurate.   Ultimately the Defendants actions resulted in terminating sanctions.

 

Additionally, Plaintiff argues that it would be prejudiced if the bond requirement was waived.  Plaintiff points to Defendants’ conduct violating a temporary restraining order, preliminary injunction, and two discovery order.  Plaintiff also contends that Ryde and Jarvel’s corporate structure facilitates the concealment of or dissipation of assets. 

 

In light of the factors discussed above regarding Defendants’ failure to show their indigent status and Plaintiff’s claim for prejudice, the Court declines to exercise its discretion to grant this motion for waiver or modification of undertaking.

In addition, the court may make any other order as may be appropriate to protect the person from unreasonable or oppressive demands, including

 

(C.C.P. § 1987.1(a).)

 

C.C.P. § 2017.020 states:

 

(a) The court shall limit the scope of discovery if it determines that the burden, expense, or intrusiveness of that discovery clearly outweighs the likelihood that the information sought will lead to the discovery of admissible evidence. The court may make this determination pursuant to a motion for protective order by a party or other affected person. This motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.

(c)(1) Notwithstanding subdivision (b), or any other section of this title, absent exceptional circumstances, the court shall not impose sanctions on a party or any attorney of a party for failure to provide electronically stored information that has been lost, damaged, altered, or overwritten as the result of the routine, good faith operation of an electronic information system.

 

(2) This subdivision shall not be construed to alter any obligation to preserve discoverable information.

 

(C.C.P. § 2017.020.)

C.     Discussion

 

Defendants Zavela and Ryde and dismissed/non-party Jarvel Enterprises, LLC (“Jarvel”) move to: (1) quash or modify the subpoena duces tecum served on Zavela on April 10, 2026; (2) quash or modify the subpoena duces tecum served on Ryde on April 21, 2026; (3) quash the subpoena for production of business records served on Jarvel on April 24, 2026; and (4) issue a protective order pursuant to C.C.P. §§ 1987.1 and 2017.020 to protect the privacy and propriety interests of the moving parties and third parties. 

 

Defendants acknowledge Plaintiff’s right to seek information regarding assets to satisfy a judgment, but contend that the subpoenas represent an invasive “fishing expedition” that violates financial privacy and corporate separateness.  They argue that Plaintiff improperly seeks to violate the tax return privilege, targets a dismissed non-party and its financial records, seeks alter-ego discovery without any legal or factual basis to pierce the corporate veil, invades third-party privacy and trade secrets, and includes overbroad personal discovery.  They contend they offered complete, unredacted corporate and personal bank statements to facilitate asset tracing, but such offer was rejected by Plaintiff.  (Reply at p.2.) 

 

The Subpoena Duces Tecum issued on Zavela, Ryde, and Ryde’s PMK Roman Dombrovsky (“Debtor Subpoena”) seeks seven categories of documents and 22 document requests, including: (1) Bank and Financial Accounts; (2) Intercompany Transfers (Ryde/Jarvel); (3) Tax Returns; (4) Compensation and Distributions; (5) Corporate Records and Ownership (of Ryde and Jarvel); (6) Accounts Receivable and Customer Contracts (of Ryde and Jarvel); and (7) Zavela’s Personal Assets.  (Mot., Ex. A.) 

 

The Deposition Subpoena for Production of Business Records issued on Jarvel (“Debtor Subpoena”) seeks six categories of documents and 31 document requests, including: (1) Ownership and Control of Jarvel by Judgment Debtors; (2) Transfers of Funds and Assets from Ryde to Jarvel; (3) Transfer of Customers and Revenue from Ryde to Jarvel; (4) Compensation Paid to Judgment Debtors Through Jarvel; (5) Financial Records Reflecting the Ryde/Jarvel Relationship; and (6) Commingled Operations and Shared Resources with Ryde.  (Mot., Ex. B.) 

 

1.      Zavela and Ryde

 

Defendants argue that the documents sought are privileged by the tax return privilege. 

 

“Tax returns are privileged from disclosure.”  (Strawn v. Morris, Polich & Purdy, LLP (2019) 30 Cal.App.5th 1087, 1098.)  California recognizes a statutory privilege against disclosing tax returns, which is intended to encourage the voluntary filing of tax returns and truthful reporting of income.  (Li v. Yan (2016) 247 Cal.App.4th 56, 66.)  However, the privilege is not absolute and will not be upheld when: “(1) the circumstances indicate an intentional waiver of the privilege; (2) the gravamen of the lawsuit is inconsistent with the privilege; or (3) a public policy greater than that of the confidentiality of tax returns is involved.”  (Id.)  Further, with regard to corporate tax information, such documents are discoverable as business records.  (Schnabel v. Superior Court (1993) 5 Cal.4th 704, 708.)  

 

Defendants argue that they have always maintained the confidentiality of their records and never put their tax records at issue in this action.  They also argue that Defendants have already subpoenaed bank and financial statements in Categories 1 and 11, such that tax records in Category 3 are not necessary.  Though they have disclosed their income, they contend they did not waive the tax privilege and that Plaintiff may use bank statements to verify their finances. 

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that Defendants put their financial condition at issue by filing the aforementioned Motion for Waiver or Modification of Undertaking, wherein they declared and/or represented their financial condition.  Plaintiff seeks tax records and K-1 schedules to help reveal ownership interests, distributions, and income from pass-through entities that do not appear on bank statements.  Plaintiff also represents that Defendants have not produced any bank statements. 

 

As discussed above, the Court notes that Defendants’ declaration and evidence were insufficient to show its indigent status and that a greater showing with supporting documents was required.  At this stage, Defendants are arguing in blanket terms that they have little-to-no assets without any documentary support. 

 

The Court denies the motion to quash/modify the Debtor Subpoena with respect to Ryde regarding corporate tax information (Category 3) and finds that the records are discoverable. 

 

With respect to Zavela, the Court finds that under the unique circumstances of this case, including prior discovery violations, and in order to verify Zavela’s assets (as his unsupported declaration statements alone in support of the Motion for Waiver or Modification of Undertaking were insufficient), the Court will order the production of Zavela’s tax records (Category 3) as well.  However, to preserve the confidentiality of these documents, the Court will order the document be produced subject to the parties’ protective order (entered November 27, 2023).  These tax returns may be redacted to show sources of income only and the filers name.   Other personal identifying information such as social security numbers may be redacted, as well as other information which is not associated with income and income sources.

 

2.      Jarvel

 

Defendants argue that the subpoenas are improper against Jarvel because it is a dismissed non-party, Plaintiff cannot use a debtor’s exam to audit a non-party under Hooser v. Superior Court (2000) 84 Cal.App.4th 997, the Jarvel Subpoena violates the “Stranger in the Action” rule in Valley Bank of Nevada v. Superior Court (1975) 15 Cal.3d 652, and the demand for internal transfers is an improper end-run around corporate formalities. 

 

First, the Court addresses the Valley Bank of Nevada v. Superior Court (1975) 15 Cal.3d 652 case.  Defendants argue that Plaintiff improperly seeks “alter ego” discovery and third-party data to bypass corporate formalities in the Jarvel Subpoena.  Defendants rely on Valley Bank, arguing the Jarvel Subpoena violates the “Stranger to the Action” rule.

 

In Valley Bank, the real parties in interest noticed the bank’s chairman’s deposition and requested documents pertaining to loan transactions between the bank and seven named persons/corporations, and record of banking relationships with a union.   The bank objected to protect the privacy of its customers.  The Supreme Court considered under what circumstances a litigant may, through ordinary civil discovery procedures, obtain from a bank information disclosed to it in confidence by a customer.  (Valley Bank, supra, 15 Cal.3d at 654.)  The Court noted that the record supported a conclusion that the requested information was relevant to the real parties’ defense in the action.  (Id. at 655.)  The Court found that there must be a careful balance of the right of litigants to discover relevant facts with bank customers’ right to maintain reasonable privacy regarding their financial affairs.  (Id. at 657.) The Supreme Court concluded that “although such information is discoverable in a proper case, nevertheless the bank must first take reasonable steps to locate the customer, inform him of the discovery proceedings, and provide him a reasonable opportunity to interpose objections and seek appropriate protective orders.”  (Id. at 654.)  It further stated: “With respect to bank customer information, the trial court has available certain procedural devices which may be useful in fashioning an appropriate order that will, so far as possible, accommodate considerations of both disclosure and confidentiality. These include deletion of the customer's name (evidently inappropriate in the instant case), ordering that the information be sealed, to be opened only on further order of court [citation], and the holding of in camera hearings. There may well be others which ingenious courts and counsel may develop.”  (Id. at 658-659.)

 

The Court finds Valley Bank to be factually and legally distinguishable from this action.  This action does not involve a bank faced with a subpoena seeking the disclosure of bank customers’ financial records.  As such, the Supreme Court’s guidance regarding the bank’s reasonable steps does not apply to the facts of this case. 

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that this is not a situation where Jarvel is a “stranger” to the action.  (Opp. at pp.13-14.)  Plaintiff provides evidence that Ryde and Jarvel share the same ownership and management under Roman Dombrovsky and Yovahny Grazziani and same agent for service of process, Jeff Muyrong.  (Opp. at Ex. 6 [Secretary of State, Statements of Information].)  Plaintiff contends that they share addresses: Ryde is located at as well as addresses at 6017 Scott Way, Long Beach, CA 90813, while Jarvel’s address is 6017 Scott Way, Commerce, CA 90040.  (Id.) 

 

Plaintiff contends that Ryde and Jarvel do business as “Equip Your Gym” (based on Ryde’s Profit and Loss Statements) and Jarvel also does business as “Gym Equipment Rental” and “Rental Gym Equipment.”  (Weisberg Decl., ¶¶8, 13, Ex. 11.)  Mr. Dombrovsky holds himself out as the founder and CEO of Equip Your Gym and Gym Equipment Rental for wholesale and rental of gym equipment, to which Plaintiff argues shows that he engages in both Ryde’s wholesale business and Jarvel’s rental business under one identity.  (Id., Ex. 10.)  Plaintiff provides Zavela’s November 16, 2023 and April 8, 2024 declarations wherein he states that he is the current Director of Sales of Ryde since April 2023.  (Opp. at Ex. 12 [Zavela November 16, 2023 Decl., ¶¶3-4]; Ex. 13 [Zavela April 8, 2024 Decl., ¶¶3-4].)  However, Plaintiff’s counsel Michael P. Weisberg states that Jarvel (as opposed to Ryde) produced an employment agreement with Zavela.  Mr. Weisberg did not attach the document in case the “Confidential” sticker on the document was in connection with the protective order (as opposed to a pre-existing “Confidential” sticker at the time of signing the employment agreement) and states he will provide the document to the Court for an in camera inspection at the hearing.  (Weisberg Decl., ¶8.)  Plaintiff argues that the fact that Zavela himself is confused by or has contradicted who is employer is shows a lack of distinction between Ryde and Jarvel. 

 

Plaintiff also argues that Ryde and Jarvel share business interests, providing the Fullerton Marriott Hotel as an example.  Plaintiff contends that from August to September 2023, Zavela (using email address [email protected]) solicited business from Fullerton Marriott Hotel but the contract, insurance, and W-9 were in Jarvel Enterprises LLC dba Rental Gym Equipment’s name.  Exhibit 9 includes Zavela’s emails, a Certificate of Liability Insurance and W-9 issued to Jarvel Emprises dba Gym Equipment Rental, and a Gym Equipment Rental contract.   (Weisberg Decl., Ex. 9; see also Ex. 14 [Zavela’s email to insurer to change the insured from Ryde to Jarvel].) 

 

The Court does not find that Valley Bank apply in this instance and finds sufficient connection between Jarval and the judgment debtors. Nontheless, will quash in part and deny in part quashing the subpoenas directed at Jarvel. 

 

Second, Defendants rely on Hooser v. Superior Court (2000) 84 Cal.App.4th 997, disapproved by Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 557 (“To the extent prior cases require a party seeking discovery of private information to always establish a compelling interest or compelling need, without regard to the other considerations articulated in Hill v. National Collegiate Athletic Assn., supra, 7 Cal.4th 1, 26 Cal.Rptr.2d 834, 865 P.2d 633, they are disapproved.”).   In Hooser, the plaintiff sued her attorney for misappropriation of funds and obtained a judgment in her favor.  To collect on the judgment, she served the defendant with an order to appear for judgment debtor’s examination and a subpoena duces tecum, seeking the production of a list of clients, all filed and unfiled current claims/cases, all his bank statements, all bank statements for his attorney-client trust accounts.  (Hooser, supra, 84 Cal.App.4th at 1001.)  The defendant filed a motion to quash, asserting the attorney-client privilege.  The Court of Appeal found that defendant may be compelled to disclose information regarding his filed claims/cases, as well as unfiled claims/cases and prospective payors, except insofar as such information includes the identities of clients whose relationship with defendant were yet undisclosed to third parties.  (Id. at 1007-08.)  With respect to client trust account information, the Court of Appeal stated:

 

It is clear from the foregoing that [plaintiff] Ray is entitled to discover from [defendant] Hooser information regarding the funds received and held by him in trust for her. However, Hooser's other clients have a right of privacy as to their financial affairs. (Valley Bank of Nevada v. Superior Court, supra, 15 Cal.3d at p. 656.) Thus, to the extent that the requested bank statements contain information tying Hooser's other clients to funds held on their behalf, Ray's requests implicate those clients' privacy rights. Ray's right to discover such information depends on whether she can establish a compelling need for the bank statements that outweighs the other clients' privacy interests. We conclude that she has not made such a showing.

 

(Hooser, supra, 84 Cal.App.4th at 1008.) 

 

As stated above, Hooser was disapproved “[t]o the extent prior cases require a party seeking discovery of private information to always establish a compelling interest or compelling need, without regard to the other considerations articulated in Hill ….”  (Williams, supra, 3 Cal.5th at 557.)  The Williams Court summarized the Hill framework for evaluating potential invasions of privacy as follows:

 

The party asserting a privacy right must establish a legally protected privacy interest, an objectively reasonable expectation of privacy in the given circumstances, and a threatened intrusion that is serious. (Id. at pp. 35–37, 26 Cal.Rptr.2d 834, 865 P.2d 633.) The party seeking information may raise in response whatever legitimate and important countervailing interests disclosure serves, while the party seeking protection may identify feasible alternatives that serve the same interests or protective measures that would diminish the loss of privacy. A court must then balance these competing considerations. (Id. at pp. 37–40, 26 Cal.Rptr.2d 834, 865 P.2d 633.)

 

(Williams, supra, 3 Cal.5th at 552.) 

 

Relying on Hooser, Defendants argue that Plaintiff’s broad discovery rights are limited by the constitutional right of privacy and that Hooser specifically ruled that a creditor could not compel a third party to reveal private financial dealings without a specific showing of relevance.  In this instance, relevance has been shown for the reasons stated in the opposition.

 

The mere fact that Jarvel is no longer a party to this action does not shield it from subpoenas.  Plaintiff argues that under C.C.P. § 708.130, third parties may be subject to discovery.  (C.C.P. § 708.130(a) [generally stating that “witnesses” may be “required to appear and testify before the court or referee in an examination proceeding under this article in the same manner as upon the trial of an issue.”].)  Plaintiff argues that there is a compelling need to trace judgment debtor assets that may be held by a third party, particularly since Jarvel is paying expenses relating to the same space and employees shared by Ryde.  (Plaintiff clarifies in its opposition that it is not seeking to establish alter ego liability as judgment has already been entered; rather, it seeks to identify Defendants’ assets, including potentially transferred to and held by Jarvel.  [Opp. at p.15.]) 

 

However, at this point, the Court cannot ascertain what financial or business relationships Ryde has with Jarvel.  The parties indicate that Ryde conducts sales while Jarvel provides rentals.  Without more, the Court declines to compel Jarvel to produce its tax records without a greater showing from Plaintiff that such records are necessary.  Although Plaintiff claims that Ryde has or may have transferred assets to Jarvel, no evidentiary proof of this assertion has been provided.  As a result, the subpoena will only be permitted in part.  Thus, the motion to quash or modify the Debtor Subpoena seeking Jarvel’s tax records (Category 3) is granted.  However, if Plaintiff can show a compelling need for the tax records or inter-company records following alternative, less intrusive means of discovery, it may file a motion to that affect with supporting evidence.  Any subsequent order would include redaction of information not related to the inquiry at hand.

 

The motion to quash the Subpoenas as directed against Jarvel is granted in part only as to:

-          Category 2:       2.7

-          Category 3:      3.2 and 3.5

-          Category 4:      denied as to all

-          Category 5:      5.1, 5.2, and 5.3

-          Category 6:      6.2 and 6.3

LEGAL STANDARD 

 

The function of a motion for summary judgment or adjudication is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party cannot show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) CCP § 437c(c) “requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.”  (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.)

 

As to each claim as framed by the complaint, the defendant moving for summary judgment must satisfy the initial burden of proof by presenting facts to negate an essential element, or to establish a defense. (CCP § 437c(p)(2); Scalf v. D. B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1520.)  Courts “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.”  (Dore v. Arnold Worldwide, Inc. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 384, 389.) Once the defendant has met that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to that cause of action or a defense thereto.  To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence. (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

A.     Issue 1 – First Cause of Action for Negligence: Scope of Work

 

The elements of negligence are “duty, breach of duty, proximate cause, and damages.”  (Carlsen v. Koivumaki (2014) 227 Cal.App.4th 879, 892.) 

 

WSR argues that the Negligence Cause of Action fails because Plaintiff cannot establish WSR had a duty to Plaintiff or caused the alleged damages.

 

In support of Issue 1, WSR provides the following material facts: 

 

Plaintiff filed its Complaint on November 7, 2023, alleging water damage at the Subject Property on May 3, 2021.  (WSR Fact 1.)  The contract between WSR and AFCO is dated December 11, 2019.  (Id. at 2.)  On behalf of Plaintiff, AFCO, which is owned by Sam Afghani, hired WSR.  Both AFCO and Plaintiff share the same address and telephone number.  (Id. at 3.)  WSR’s scope of work was limited to roofing on the upper roof and temporary waterproofing of the fifth-floor walkway and a small section of the fourth-floor walkway. (Id. at 4.)  NOHO alleges the WSR failed to use proper waterproofing material for the second, third and fourth floors’ walk decks; failed to properly install flashings between the walk deck and the exterior stucco wall; failed to use the appropriate waterproofing membranes between the exterior walls and the roof deck; failed to comply with all applicable roofing repair building and safety protocols; failed to properly waterproof the roof.  (Id. at 5.) 

 

The Subject Property’s upper roof which WSR installed was inspected after construction and did not leak, but if there was a defect, Shawn Reeves (WSR’s owner) never received notice of a claim.  (Id. at 7.)  The roof slope was established during the framing phase, which predated the WSR installed roofing.  (Id. at 8.)  Installing gutters and downspouts was outside WSR’s scope of work.  (Id. at 9.)  Mr. Reeves states that the installation of the temporary waterproofing on the fifth-floor walkway and a section of the fourth-floor walkway was completed in January 2020, and a walkthrough occurred. (Id. at 10.)  Notably, it rained in the days preceding the walk-through and the temporary waterproofing held up. About three inches of rain water pooled on top of the walkway, but there was no water leak from the walkways or damage to the Subject Property observed by or reported to Mr. Reeves.  (Id. at 10.)  The units’ door thresholds were built two inches lower than the concrete level in the walkways. WSR did not design or construct the thresholds of the units or the level of the walkways.  (Id. at 11.)  The units’ interior, the building’s exterior walls, and installing weep screeds were not within WSR's scope of work. (Id. at 12.) 

 

First, WSR contends that Plaintiff cannot establish WSR owed it a duty with respect to the alleged defects because its scope of work under the WSR-AFCO Contract was limited to the installation of the upper roof, along with temporary waterproofing on the fifth floor and a small portion of the fourth floor;  it owed no further duties or obligations.

 

The WSR’s contract was between WSR and AFCO only.  Although Plaintiff is not a party to the contract, Plaintiff alleges in the Complaint that AFCO hired/retained WSR on behalf of Plaintiff for the waterproofing project at the Subject Premises.  (Compl., ¶11.)  The WSR-AFCO Contract states in relevant part:

 

            2. SPECIFICATIONS:

 

a) Provide and install modified granulated torch down to all flat roof areas including upper pedestrian deck @ roof level, over

b) 1-layer glass base sheet

            c) Install metal edging @ courtyard edge metal upper roof level only

d) Provide and install Presidential Shake composition shingles @ slope roofs (6” x 12” roof areas over one layer 30lb, felt

e) Seal and paint all pipes, vents, etc. to match singles

f) Clean up all related trash and debris daily

g) Permits by others

 

(WSR-AFCO Contract, § 2.)

 

WSR argues that it installed the roof on the upper portion of the Subject Property, but was not contracted to install gutters, downspouts, or to slope the roof.  WSR’s expert, Mr. Farasatpour, opines that the Subject Property damages bore no evidence that the upper roof leaked.  (Farasatpour Decl., ¶9.)  He opines that while a weep screed helps moisture escape, preventing water damage, but that such installation was outside the scope of WSR’s work.  (Id., ¶10.)  Shawn Reeves, WSR’s owner and project manager, states that WSR installed temporary waterproofing on the fifth-floor walkway and a partial section of the fourth-floor walkway, which was completed in January 2020; he states that it rained after completion (and before a walkthrough) and that three inches of rainwater pooled on the walkway without leaking or causing damage to the Subject Property.  (Reeves Decl., ¶5.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that WSR’s scope of work expanded as a result of a Change Order.  According to the WSR-AFCO Contract (which the Court notes was signed only by WSR’s owner, Shawn Reeves), modifications could be made in writing with the express approval of both parties.  (WSR-AFCO Contract at p.1)  Plaintiff provides Sam Afghani’s (AFCO’s President and CEO) declaration, wherein he states that on February 13, 2020, WSR submitted a change order relating to the work on the third floor.  (Afghani Decl., ¶¶21-24, Ex. C [Change Order].)  Change Order 20-US-3511 dated February 13, 2020, signed only by Shawn Reeves, states under “Description”:

 

            Furnish and install walk pads at 3rd floor: App torch down over one layer glass base,

            AFCO to provide angle ledge metal and 90° for roof to wall.

 

(Change Order at p.1; Pl.’s Fact 4.) 

 

By way of this evidence, Plaintiff has shown that WSR’s scope of work was not limited solely to the fourth and fifth floors, but that it required WSR to furnish services on the third-floor walk deck.   (Pl.’s Additional Material Fact [AMF] 7.)  (Plaintiff argues that this service is “unambiguously waterproofing work. Torch down application over glass base is a standard waterproofing method and was agreed to be performed by WSR under contract at the third-floor walk deck.”  [Opp. at 7:3-6.]  Neither WSR nor Plaintiff provided expert testimony regarding torch-down roofing over glass base sheets with respect to the WSR-AFCO Contract or the Change Order.  The Court also notes that neither the WSR-AFCO Contract nor the Change Order explicitly stated “waterproofing” work.  However, as the use of this method and material are not disputed by the parties, the Court accepts that this is a commonly used method of waterproofing.) 

 

In reply, WSR argues that any deviation from the work was expressly directed by AFCO and thus could not be attributed to WSR as a breach for unauthorized change, citing to Mr. Reeves’ declaration at paragraphs 4-11.  (Reply at 1:16-17.)  However, a review of Mr. Reeves’ original declaration at these paragraphs does not reflect what changes AFCO proposed and whether such modifications were in writing to approve deviations from the original WSR-AFCO Contract. 

 

Here, there is a triable issue of material fact regarding WSR’s scope of work.  While WSR offered material facts that its scope of work was limited solely to waterproofing the fourth and fifth floors only, Plaintiff has provided opposing evidence that WSR was also required to provide waterproofing services to the third floor.  As such, Plaintiff has raised a triable issue of material act regarding the issue of WSR’s scope of work, or duty. 

 

Additionally, WSR argues that its scope of work was limited to “temporary” waterproofing on the fourth and fifth-floor walkways.  (Def.’s Fact 4.)  However, as pointed out by Plaintiff, WSR has not shown where in the WSR-AFCO Contract the parties agreed that WSR’s waterproofing work would only be for “temporary” waterproofing.  (Pl.’s Fact 4; Pl.’s AMF 9-10; Afghani Decl., ¶¶16-18.)  (The Court is also unaware of whether the listed “Specifications” implicitly included temporary work, as opposed to permanent work.)  As such, WSR has not established its initial burden on this particular scope of work. 

 

Second, WSR argues that Plaintiff cannot prove causation because, in response to Special Interrogatory requests regarding causation, Plaintiff reiterated the Complaint’s allegations without supporting facts.  WSR provides Fact 6, in which it contends that when asked to provide all facts in support of its contention that WSR’s work and/or services caused or contributed to Plaintiff’s damages, Plaintiff copied and pasted the Complaint’s allegations without providing any new details.  (WSR Fact 6.)  Thus, WSR argues that Plaintiff lacks facts/evidence supporting its claims such that the Court should infer that no evidence exists.  (Mot. at p.11 [citing to Andrew v. Foster Wheeler LLC (2006) 138 Cal.App.4th 96, 102-103].)   

 

WSR’s Special Interrogatory No. 12 asks: “State all facts supporting YOUR contention that WSR’s work and/or services caused or contributed to NOHO URBAN APARTMENTS, LLC’s damages.”  (Singh Decl., Ex. E [SROG No. 12 at p. 9].)  Plaintiff responded in part, without waiving objections, with the same language alleged in the Complaint at paragraphs 11-21 regarding the First Cause of Action’s allegations (see SROG No. 12 at 9:22-13:3); paragraphs 25-26 regarding the Second Cause of Action’s allegations (see id. at 13:4-7); paragraph 29 regarding the Third Cause of Action (see id. at 13:8-12); and paragraphs 32-38 regarding the Fourth Cause of Action (see id. at 13:13-14:1). 

 

While Plaintiff’s SROG response essentially reiterates the allegations of the Complaint, the Court does not find that this alone should result in a finding to support summary judgment/adjudication in favor of WSR.  The SROG response and Complaint’s allegations allege numerous categories of WSR’s alleged failures that Plaintiff contends WSR breached its duties and thereby caused or contributed to Plaintiff’s injuries.  (See SROG No. 12 at 11:24-12:28; Compl., ¶19(a)-(m).) 

 

A defendant making a “no evidence” motion must introduce admissible evidence (by declaration or otherwise) that, “discovery was ‘sufficiently comprehensive, and plaintiffs' responses so devoid of facts, as to lead to the inference that plaintiffs could not prove [their case] upon a stringent review of the direct, circumstantial and inferential evidence.’”  (Casey v. Perini Corp. (2012) 206 Cal.App.4th 1222, 1231 [quoting Andrews v. Foster Wheeler LLC (2006) 138 Cal.App.4th 96, 107].)  In addition, a defendant making a “no evidence” motion must also establish that, by the time the case comes up for trial, the plaintiff “cannot reasonably expect to obtain” the evidence necessary to raise a triable issue of fact on the issue.  (Schieding v. Dinwiddie Construction Company (1999) 69 Cal.App.4th 64, 83; Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 854-855 [noting that a defendant seeking summary judgment “must show that the plaintiff does not possess needed evidence . . . the defendant must also show that the plaintiff cannot reasonably obtain needed evidence . . .”].)

 

The Court finds that these elements to a “no evidence” motion have not been satisfied. WSR’s counsel Princepreet Singh provides his declaration attaching various exhibits, but he makes no statements otherwise regarding the sufficiency of the discovery conducted in this action.  Additionally, the Court cannot conclude that Plaintiff lacks evidence and cannot reasonably be expected to obtain evidence necessary to raise a triable issue of material fact.  Plaintiff’s expert, James Baschnagel, opines that based on his August 13, 2023 site inspection, that WSR did not install the Duro-Last 50-mil PVC reinforced roof membrane for the flat roof areas or the CertainTeed Presidential Solaris shingles for the sloped roof portions, and that there were no Change Orders or Material Substitution approval forms authorizing deviations from the specified materials.  (Baschnagel Decl., ¶11; Pl.’s AMF 27.)  As discussed above, and based on the opposing expert declarations, there is at least a baseline showing by Plaintiff that there is conflicting evidence, which may and have resulted in triable issues of material fact.  

 

For these reasons, the motion is denied as to Issue 1 regarding the First Cause of Action for Negligence.  

 

B.     Issue 2 – Two-Year Limitations Clause

 

WSR argues that Plaintiff’s Complaint is barred by the two-year limitations period in the WSR-AFCO Contract.  (Mot. at pp.12-13.)  WSR argues that although Plaintiff is not a party to the WSR-AFCO Contract, Plaintiff is a third-party beneficiary to the Contract as the property owner, and it acknowledged in the Complaint that AFCO hired/retained WSR on its behalf. 

 

“It is a well-settled proposition of law that the parties to a contract may stipulate therein for a period of limitation, shorter than that fixed by the statute of limitations, and that such stipulation violates no principle of public policy, provided the period fixed be not so unreasonable as to show imposition or undue advantage in some way.”  (Beeson v. Schloss (1920) 183 Cal. 618, 622.) 

 

“An agent is one who represents another, called the principal, in dealings with third persons. Such representation is called agency.”  (Civ. Code, § 2295.)  “An agent represents his principal for all purposes within the scope of his actual or ostensible authority, and all the rights and liabilities which would accrue to the agent from transactions within such limit, if they had been entered into on his own account, accrue to the principal.”  (Civ. Code, § 2330.) 

 

“When plaintiff seeks to secure benefits under a contract as to which he is a third-party beneficiary, he must take that contract as he finds it. As the rules above stated make clear, the third party cannot select the parts favorable to him and reject those unfavorable to him.”  (Sanders v. American Cas. Co. of Reading, Pa. (1969) 269 Cal.App.2d 306, 310.) 

 

For Issue 2, WSR provides the following material facts.  Plaintiff filed its Complaint on November

7, 2023, alleging water damage on May 3, 2021, more than two years after the date of completion of WSR’s work.  (WSR Fact 13.)  The WSR-AFCO Contract included the following terms and conditions:

 

12. ENFORCEABILITY AGAINST TRANSFEREES & SUCCESSORS: This agreement and all of its terms shall be binding upon and enforceable against the parties hereto and also against the heir’s estates, successors in interest, assigns, and personal representatives of the parties hereto. It is further expressly agreed that this Agreement and all terms hereof shall also be binding upon and may be enforced against any person, firm, association or entity to whom Owner [AFCO Construction – Attn: Sam] transfers any part of his right, title, or interest in or to the premises upon which this Contract is to be performed.

 

…

 

18. LIMITATIONS: No action arising from or related to this contract or performance of this contract, shall be started by either party against the other more than two years from either the date of completion or the date of cessation of work under this contract. This limitation applies to all actions of any character.

 

(Id. at 14-15; Singh Decl., Ex. B [WSR-AFCO Contract].)

 

In SROG No. 1, WSR asked Plaintiff to identify all documents related to the work of any contractors it retained for work at the Subject Property from January 1, 1995, up to and including the date of its responses.  (Singh Decl., Ex. H [SROG No. 1].)  Plaintiff was defined to include, among other things, its agents and all other persons or entities representing or acting on its behalf.  Plaintiff responded by identifying the WSR-AFCO Contract.  (Id.) 

 

First, the Court notes that WSR has established its initial burden showing that Plaintiff was the intended third-party beneficiary to the WSR-AFCO Contract as the contract involved the Subject Property owned by Plaintiff and Plaintiff sued WSR for work performed pursuant to the Contract on the grounds that WSR had duties arising from the agreements between AFCO and WSR.  (See Compl., ¶¶1, 11, 13.)  WSR has shown that Plaintiff filed this action on November 7, 2023, which is over two years from WSR’s alleged May 3, 2021 completion date.  (Compl., ¶14.)  Applying the limitations period, this action would be time-barred. As WSR has established its initial burden on Issue 2, the burden shifts to Plaintiff to raise a triable issue of material fact. 

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that it was not a party to, nor a third-party beneficiary of, the WSR-AFCO Contract and therefore is not bound by the contractual limitations period.  Plaintiff relies on Mr. Afghani’s declaration.  Mr. Afghani states that the WSR-AFCO Contract identifies AFCO as the buyer/owner and not Plaintiff Noho; WSR chose to contract with AFCO; and AFCO is an independent general contractor who negotiated and executed the contract with WSR.  (Afghani Decl., ¶¶1-11.)  Plaintiff argues that shared ownership between the principals of AFCO and Plaintiff, or a shared business address, does not establish agency as a matter of law.  Plaintiff argues that its Complaint allegations that AFCO hired WSR on Plaintiff’s behalf was not an admission, but that AFCO acted as a general contractor for the benefit of its property owner.  (Opp. at pp.11-12.) 

 

Plaintiff also argues that it is not a third-party beneficiary who is bound to the limitations clause because it did not negotiate or have the opportunity to object to the Contract’s terms and it did not sue to enforce the Contract’s terms but instead sued for WSR’s negligent performance of the work that damaged Plaintiff’s property.  (Opp. at p.12.)  The arguments appear to be incongruent; negligence based on duties founded in the WSR-AFCO is dissonant to not being a third-party  beneficiary of the Contract.  Furthermore, the scope of the limitations clause was not limited to only contract claims, but stated it applied to “all actions of any character.” 

 

Finally, Plaintiff argues that even if the limitations clause applied, the Complaint is timely because the period was for two years from “either the date of completion or the date of cessation of work under this contract.”  (Opp. at pp.12-13; WSR-AFCO Contract, ¶18 [italics added].)  Plaintiff cites to the WSR-AFCO Contract, paragraph 15, which states in relevant part:

 

15. GUARANTEE AGREEMENT: Contractor agrees that following completion of work done by him on the building at the above address, he will repair free of charge any water leaks occurring in the roof area on which was performed under said Contract, provided it can be shown after investigation that said leaks resulted from faulty workmanship and provided further, that Buyer has given the Contractor written notice of the existence of such leaks. During the term of this Guarantee, notice must be given promptly upon discovery of any defect or damage. …

 

(WSR-AFCO Contract at ¶15; Pl.’s AMF 28.) 

 

Plaintiff argues that following the May 3, 2021 water intrusion, WSR received actual notice of the defects from repeated direct communications, personal site visits, water testing, and repair attempts by Mr. Reeves, but WSR attempted and failed to inspect and repair the issue.  (Pl.’s AMF 26.)  Mr. Reeves personally visited the Subject Property and sent workers to discuss and perform repairs through, at the least, January 2022.  (Id. at 20.)  Mr. Afghani states in his declaration details of  the parties’ communications and provides text messages with Mr. Reeves from May 2021 to January 2022.  (Afghani Decl., ¶¶47-71, Ex. D [Text Messages].)  Plaintiff contends that WSR did not timely and adequately repair the defective roofing and waterproofing conditions which caused or contributed to the water intrusion and because WSR did not accept responsibility for the water intrusion damage, Plaintiff and/or AFCO were required to proceed with corrective work and repairs to protect the Subject Property, mitigate damages, and restore affected units and common areas. (Pl.’s AMF 29.)

 

The text messages between Mr. Afghani and Mr. Reeves show communication through at least January 24, 2022 regarding water intrusion and potential repair methods.  Although the project’s completion date may have been May 3, 2021, the parties appear to have continued discussing the quality of and issues with the waterproofing work up until January 24, 2022.  If the January 2022 timeframe is the date the work ceased, then there is a triable issue regarding whether the Complaint was timely filed on November 7, 2023.  (At this stage, the Court is not making a definitive finding regarding the terms “completion” or “cessation of work” but instead finding that there is at least a triable issue of material fact regarding whether the limitations clause was meant to include repair work, whether the project was indeed “completed,” and whether WSR’s “cessation” of completing repair work falls under this limitations clause.) 

 

For these reasons, the motion is denied as to Issue 2 regarding the limitations clause.

 

C.     Issue 3 – Right to Repair Act

 

WSR argues that Plaintiff failed to comply with the Right to Repair Act barring its construction defect claims. 

 

Issue 3 relies on the following material facts: 

 

Plaintiff sent text messages on January 18 and 24, 2021, presumably between Sam Afghani and Shawn Reeves, but did not serve a formal Notice of Claim as required by Civ. Code § 910(a).  (WSR Fact 16.)  (The Court notes that while WSR states the texts were sent in “2021,” this appears to be a typographical error in the separate statements.) No Notice of Claim was found in WSR’s document production.  (Id. at 17.)

 

Civil Code, § 910(a) states:

 

Prior to filing an action against any party alleged to have contributed to a violation of the standards set forth in Chapter 2 (commencing with Section 896), the claimant shall initiate the following prelitigation procedures:

 

(a) The claimant or his or her legal representative shall provide written notice via certified mail, overnight mail, or personal delivery to the builder, in the manner prescribed in this section, of the claimant's claim that the construction of his or her residence violates any of the standards set forth in Chapter 2 (commencing with Section 896). That notice shall provide the claimant's name, address, and preferred method of contact, and shall state that the claimant alleges a violation pursuant to this part against the builder, and shall describe the claim in reasonable detail sufficient to determine the nature and location, to the extent known, of the claimed violation. In the case of a group of homeowners or an association, the notice may identify the claimants solely by address or other description sufficient to apprise the builder of the locations of the subject residences. That document shall have the same force and effect as a notice of commencement of a legal proceeding.

 

(Civ. Code, § 910(a); McMillin Albany LLC v. Superior Court (2018) 4 Cal.5th 241, 259 [“[C]laims seeking recovery for construction defect damages are subject to the Act's prelitigation procedures regardless of how they are pleaded….”].) 

 

“Builder” means “any entity or individual, including, but not limited to a builder, developer, general contractor, contractor, or original seller, who, at the time of sale, was also in the business of selling residential units to the public for the property that is the subject of the homeowner's claim or was in the business of building, developing, or constructing residential units for public purchase for the property that is the subject of the homeowner's claim.”  (Civ. Code, § 911(a).) 

 

WSR argues that Plaintiff’s Complaint seeks recovery for construction defects, which fall within the scope of the Right to Repair Act.  Thus, it argues that Plaintiff and/or AFCO were required to provide WSR notice under section 910(a). 

 

Other than arguing that Plaintiff’s claims include construction defect claims, WSR has not shown how the Right to Repair Act applies.  For example, Civil Code, § 896 states that “This title applies to original construction intended to be sold as an individual dwelling unit.”  (Civ. Code, § 896.) 

 

The McMillin Court discussed section 896 as follows:

 

Three aspects of this text are instructive. First, the provision applies to “any action” seeking damages for a construction defect, not just any action under the title. (§ 896.) This suggests an intent to create not merely a remedy for construction defects but the remedy. Second, “the claimant's claims or causes of action shall be limited to violation of ... the following standards, except as specifically set forth in this title.” (Ibid.) This express language of limitation means that a party seeking damages for a construction defect may sue for violation of these standards, and only violation of these standards, unless the Act provides an exception. This clause evinces a clear intent to displace, in whole or in part, existing remedies for construction defects. Third, “[t]his title applies to original construction intended to be sold as an individual dwelling unit,” but “[a]s to condominium conversions, this title does not apply to or does not supersede any other statutory or common law.” (§ 896) The Act governs claims concerning stand-alone homes; for such disputes, the Act's provisions do “supersede any other statutory or common law” except as elsewhere provided. (§ 896.)

 

(McMillin Albany LLC v. Superior Court (2018) 4 Cal.5th 241, 250.) 

 

WSR has not presented evidence that the Subject Property was an original construction as defined under section 911 to whom section 910’s notice procedures apply.  Rather, as stated by Mr. Reeves, WSR was a subcontractor for roof and waterproofing work.  (Reeves Decl., ¶3.)  Neither WSR nor Plaintiff were in the business of selling residential units (as stated by Mr. Afghani [¶84], Plaintiff rented apartment units).  The Court notes WSR did not address Issue 3 in its reply brief.

 

As WSR did not meet its initial burden on this issue, the burden does not shift to Plaintiff to raise a triable issue of material fact.  The motion is denied as to Issue 3 regarding the Right to Repair Act. 

 

D.    Issue 4 – Rental Income

 

In the alternative, WSR moves for summary adjudication arguing that Plaintiff’s $427,200 claim for lost rental income lacks merit and is void as a matter of law.  For Issue 4, WSR argues Plaintiff claims $427,200 in lost rental income for six to seven months following May 3, 2021, without providing lease agreements or consistent figures.  (WSR Fact 19.)  WSR contends that no Certificate of Occupancy (CO) or Temporary Certificate of Occupancy (TCO) were issued for the property on May 3, 2021, with the first TCO issued on October 21, 2022.  (WSR Fact 18.) 

 

In response to Form Interrogatory No. 305.1, Plain

 

Los Angeles Municipal Code (“LAMC”) § 91.109.1, entitled “Certificate Required,” states:

 

In order to safeguard life and limb, health, property and public welfare, every building or structure and every trailer park shall conform to the construction requirements for the subgroup occupancy to be housed therein, or for the use to which the building or structure or trailer park is to be put, as set forth in this article.

 

No building or structure or portion thereof and no trailer park or portion thereof shall be used or occupied until a Certificate of Occupancy has been issued thereof.

 

EXCEPTIONS:

 

1.   Unless it is specifically required by other provisions of this article, no existing building or portion thereof shall require a Certificate of Occupancy, provided: 

 

A.   The occupancy housed therein is the same for which the original building permit was issued; and

 

B.   The use of a building or portion thereof housing a Group A or E Occupancy and constructed prior to 1934 has not been discontinued for a period of more than six months.

 

2.   No structure, the architecture of which inhibits occupancy, shall require a Certificate of Occupancy.

 

(LAMC § 91.109.1.) 

 

WSR argues that the TCO is valid only for six months and that the October 21, 2022 TCO was issued more than 17 months after the alleged water leak on May 3, 2021.  (Mot. at p.16; LAMC § 91.109.5 [“A temporary Certificate of Occupancy shall be valid for a period not to exceed six months. The Department may issue additional temporary Certificates of Occupancy. After the expiration of a temporary Certificate of Occupancy, the Department shall require a Certificate of Occupancy in accordance with other provisions of this section.”].)  WSR argues that without a CO or TCO in place during the relevant period, Plaintiff’s lost rental income claim is speculative and unsupported.  WSR has established its initial burden on Issue 4.  As such, the burden shifts to Plaintiff to raise a triable issue of material fact.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff does not dispute Fact 18 that it did not have a CO or TCO on May 3, 2021, but argues instead that this does not bar Plaintiff’s damages for lost use and value to its property due to WSR’s defective work pursuant to Civil Code, § 3333.  (Pl.’s Fact 18.)  Civil Code, § 3333 states: “For the breach of an obligation not arising from contract, the measure of damages, except where otherwise expressly provided by this Code, is the amount which will compensate for all the detriment proximately caused thereby, whether it could have been anticipated or not.” 

 

With respect to the $427,000 claim, Plaintiff states it provided calculations supporting its lost rental income claim, citing to its FROG Responses.  (Pl.’s Fact 19; Jacobson Decl., ¶¶9-10, Ex. B [FROG Nos. 305 and 309 responses].)  Plaintiff’s No. 305.1 (“Do you attribute any loss of or damage to subject property to the facts on which the construction claim or the construction defect claim is based?”) responses states that Plaintiff claimed $1,671,366.14 in repair costs; $289,950 for temporary repairs; $195,000 in estimated loss of rental income for six to seven months; and approximately $33,000 in amounts paid in settlement to a unit owner.  In response to No. 309.1 (“Are there any other damages that you attribute to the construction claim or construction defect claim alleged in this action?”), Plaintiff approximated $33,000 for the settlement amount to a unit owner and $427,200 for loss of rental income. 

 

Based on Plaintiff’s FROG responses, it is unclear if Plaintiff is claiming $195,000 (for six to seven months) or $427,200 (no time period specified) in estimated loss of rental income.  Although Mr. Afghani provides accounting for repairs and refunds to unit renters in Exhibits E and F, no actual accounting is provided for estimated loss of rental income.  (Afghani Decl., ¶¶81-85, Exs. E-F.)  Plaintiff does not provide any statements on how many units were unable to be rented, what the rental costs of those units were, and the period for which the units were not able to be rented out.  Although Plaintiff relies on Civil Code, § 3333, Plaintiff has not shown how it was actually damaged by loss of rental income and has not raised a triable issue of material fact on Issue 4.  Furthermore, without being able to rent out the properties in the first place, as CO/TCO compliance was not met, Plaintiff has not shown how it would have been entitled to rental income in the first place.

 

The motion for summary adjudication on Issue 4 is granted.

 

E.     Issue 5 – Tenant Settlement

 

WSR argues that Plaintiff’s $33,000 damages for tenant settlement fails because the claim lacks merit and is void as a matter of law.  For Issue 5, WSR argues that Plaintiff alleged a $33,000 settlement with a tenant due to the May 3, 2021 incident but provides no details regarding the tenant’s lease agreement or proof of payment. (WSR Fact 20.)  WSR contends, and Plaintiff does not dispute, that no CO or TCO was issued for the property on May 3, 2021, rendering tenant occupancy illegal.  (Id. at 21; Pl.’s Fact 21.)  For the same reasons discussed above regarding Issue 4, WSR has established its initial burden on Issue 5.  The burden shifts to Plaintiff to raise a triable issue of material fact.

 

Plaintiff argues that the tenant settlement was a foreseeable and recoverable consequence of WSR’s defective work and that its FROG responses establish the existence of the amount.  (Pl.’s Fact 20.)  To support this assertion, Plaintiff cites to Mr. Jacobson’s expert declaration at paragraphs 9-10 wherein he makes opinions about WSR’s “temporary” waterproofing and that a waterproofing contractor cannot defeat causation by suggesting others may have damaged the membrane after installation.  Neither of these statements discuss the settlement. 

 

If relying on the same FROG responses as summarized in Issue 4, these only provide approximations of Plaintiff’s settlement amount to a unit owner in the amount of $33,000, but there is no definitive proof that Plaintiff paid a settlement, no checks, no settlement agreement, or any declaration statement by Mr. Afghani.  As such, Plaintiff has not raised a triable issue of material fact on this issue.

 

As such, the motion for summary adjudication on Issue 5 is granted. 

 

F.     Issue 6 – The Fourth Cause of Action for Negligent Hiring, Supervision, and Retention

 

WSR argues that Plaintiff’s Fourth Cause of Action for Negligent Hiring, Supervision, and Retention lacks evidence of unfit employees or Defendant’s knowledge of the same.  “California case law recognizes the theory that an employer can be liable to a third person for negligently hiring, supervising, or retaining an unfit employee. [Citation.] Liability is based upon the facts that the employer knew or should have known that hiring the employee created a particular risk or hazard and that particular harm materializes.”  (McKenna v. Beesley (2021) 67 Cal.App.5th 552, 566 [footnote omitted].) 

 

For Issue 6, WSR relies on Facts 22-23.  Plaintiff alleges Negligent Hiring but fails to identify specific unfit employees or provide evidence that WSR knew or should have known of their incompetence.  (WSR Fact 22.)  WSR contends that its employees were qualified, licensed where required, belonged to unions which required them to be trained in accordance with industry standards, and it has no record of any complaints or issues regarding the competence of the employees assigned to this project prior to or during the work performed at the Subject Property.  (Id. at 23.)

 

Essentially, WSR makes a “no evidence” motion with respect to the Fourth Cause of Action.  WSR again relies on SROG No. 12.  (See Singh Decl., Exs. E and M [SROG No. 12].)  However, as quoted above, this SROG only requested facts to support Plaintiff’s contention that WSR’s work/services caused or contributed to Plaintiff’s damages.  The SROG did not specifically seek information about the identity of employees, unfitness of those employees, and WSR’s knowledge thereto. 

 

Thus, for the same reasons discussed in part in Issue 1, the motion is denied as to Issue 6.

LEGAL STANDARD:

 

C.C.P. § 1008(a) states: “(a) When an application for an order has been made to a judge, or to a court, and refused in whole or in part, or granted, or granted conditionally, or on terms, any party affected by the order may, within 10 days after service upon the party of written notice of entry of the order and based upon new or different facts, circumstances, or law, make application to the same judge or court that made the order, to reconsider the matter and modify, amend, or revoke the prior order. The party making the application shall state by affidavit what application was made before, when and to what judge, what order or decisions were made, and what new or different facts, circumstances, or law are claimed to be shown.”

 

ANALYSIS:

 

A.    Relevant Background – May 22, 2026 Order

 

On May 22, 2026, the Court granted Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel LACMTA’s PMQ Deposition. LACTMA does not seek reconsideration of the PMQ order.

B.     Discussion

 

LACMTA argues that the Court’s May 22, 2026 order requires it to produce for deposition employees and retained third-party investigators who have no personal knowledge of the incident and that they mostly investigated the incident in conjunction with and under the direction of LACMTA’s attorneys in anticipation of litigation, such that compelling the depositions violates the attorney-client privilege and attorney work product doctrine. It also argues that the identities of some persons in the Order were impermissibly obtained by Woods’ intentional production of documents to Plaintiff’s counsel, which Plaintiff and Woods knew were privileged.  LACMTA claims the documents were identified in its October 21, 2025 and November 14, 2025 privilege logs, and the privileges were reasserted on April 7, 2026. 

 

The Court notes that Plaintiff filed his motion on March 2, 2026, which LACMTA opposed on May 11 and 12, 2026.  The parties’ counsel orally argued at the May 22, 2026 hearing and the Court issued its final ruling on the submitted matter after taking into consideration the parties’ written briefs and oral arguments.

 

Other than citing to section 1008(a) in the notice of motion, there is no further discussion by LACMTA regarding the requisite factors for reconsideration.  LACMTA has not shown what new or different facts, circumstances, or law apply for the Court to reconsider its prior order. 

 

First, LACMTA has not identified any new law.  Rather, it relies on existing case law regarding the attorney-client privilege and attorney work product doctrine.  Such citations do not amount to new or different law for the purposes of a section 1008 motion.  (Baldwin v. Home Sav. of Am. (1997) 59 Cal.App.4th 1192, 1196 [stating it is not “new” law for purposes of a reconsideration motion when the law existed prior to the court’s ruling on the motion at issue].) 

 

Next, LACMTA argues that it asserted certain documents were privileged in its privilege log and that the employees identified in the deposition notices and the Court’s prior order were from privileged documents.  LACMTA provides the declaration of Alberto Vasquez (Manager, General Liability Claim) in support of its Motion for Reconsideration.  Mr. Vasquez states that the remaining employees were not percipient witnesses to the incident and were only involved through LACMTA’s post-incident investigation and claims-handling process.  (Vasquez Decl., ¶6.)  LACMTA identifies: (5) Gary Ross as its accident investigator who performed work during the claims and litigation response process; (6) Manager Melissa Williams as the Bus Operator Control employee; (7) Roman Alarcon was an executive level employee; (8) Mr. DeJoseph could not be identified by LACMTA’s records; and (11)/(12) Sam Hooper and Derek Brown were third-party accident investigators for post-incident investigation of the incident—none of whom witnessed the subject incident.  (Id., ¶¶7-8, 11-15.)  LACMTA thus argues that the information sought would be subject to the attorney-client privilege.  (In Plaintiff’s opposition to the reconsideration motion, Plaintiff reiterates the reasons he seeks to depose these individuals as already argued in his prior motion to compel.  As the Court need not get into the substantive merits of the prior motion, the Court declines to summarize Plaintiff’s justification for the depositions here.) 

 

However, LACMTA was aware of these facts prior to and by the time it filed its opposition papers and the hearing on the motion to compel the employees’ depositions.  LACMTA’s motion for reconsideration repeats that it told Plaintiff multiple times that Plaintiff was using improperly produced materials to seek depositions that it had already explained were improper.  (See e.g., Mot. at pp.5-6, 9:9-15, 16:7-9.)    Thus, it is unclear why LACMTA did not raise these arguments regarding its own privilege log and attorney-client privilege/attorney work product in its opposition brief or at the hearing on the underlying motion.  “A motion for reconsideration must be based on new or different facts, circumstances or law [citation], and facts of which the party seeking reconsideration was aware at the time of the original ruling are not ‘new or different.’  [Citation.]  In addition, a party must provide a satisfactory explanation for failing to offer the evidence in the first instance.”  (In re Marriage of Herr (2009) 174 Cal.App.4th 1463, 1468.) 

 

A review of LACMTA’s opposition to Plaintiff’s motion to compel the deposition of LACMTA’s employees argued that it did not refuse to produce employees for their depositions but was engaging in a good faith effort to coordinate overlapping and overbroad discovery, some of the noticed employees were not proper deponents, Plaintiff sought the same testimony twice through employee and PMQ depositions, it already produced knowledgeable witnesses on the issues, and it sought to coordinate the deposition in light of Woods’ entry into the case.  While there was a reference to the attorney-client privilege/attorney work product doctrine as to Sam Hooper and Derek Brown in Thomas H. Citron’s declaration at paragraph 8(f), there was no further discussion on this ground in LACMTA’s opposition papers to the underlying motion.

 

Finally, in reply, LACMTA states that on June 9, 2026, Plaintiff served the Third Amended Notice, noticing only the depositions of Mr. Gomez, Lewis Simms, T. Simmon, Alfonso Munoz, and Deckar Alexander—the same five employees LACMTA already agreed to produce—and argues that the Court should therefore modify its May 22, 2026 order to narrow it to these individuals only.  However, the fact that Plaintiff served this Third Amended Notice as to these five uncontested employees does not mean that Plaintiff has waived its right to seek the depositions of the other remaining employees as stated in the Court’s May 22, 2026 order.  Rather, Plaintiff served his opposition on June 12, 2026 (after the Third Amended Notice), opposing the reconsideration motion and LACMTA’s withholding of the employee depositions.  Thus, the Court declines to construe Plaintiff’s Third Amended Notice of five of the employees as a waiver of the remaining employee depositions. 

 

For these reasons, the Motion for Reconsideration is denied.  The Court notes that producing these employees for their depositions does not mean that LACMTA and its counsel are barred from raising objections at the deposition.  If there are legitimate objections based on the attorney-client privilege and/or attorney work product doctrine, then those objections may be raised at the time of the depositions. 

 

Plaintiff requests $950 in sanctions against LACMTA and its counsel of record for misuse of the discovery process (= 3 hours for the opposition).  (Schultz Decl., ¶39.)  The Court declines to award discovery sanctions in connection with this motion for reconsideration.

LEGAL STANDARD:

 

The function of a motion for summary judgment or adjudication is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party cannot show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) CCP § 437c(c) “requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.”  (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.)

 

As to each claim as framed by the complaint, the defendant moving for summary judgment must satisfy the initial burden of proof by presenting facts to negate an essential element, or to establish a defense. (CCP § 437c(p)(2); Scalf v. D. B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1520.)  Courts “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.”  (Dore v. Arnold Worldwide, Inc. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 384, 389.) Once the defendant has met that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to that cause of action or a defense thereto.  To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence. (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

A.     First Cause of Action for Strict Liability (Issue 1); Third Cause of Action for Breach of the Implied Warranty of Merchantability (Issue 2); and Fourth Cause of Action for Breach of the Implied Warranty of Fitness for its Intended Purpose (Issue 3)

 

Defendants argue that Plaintiff cannot maintain causes of action for Strict Liability, Breach of the Implied Warranty of Merchantability, or Breach of the Implied Warranty of Fitness (Issues 1-3), relying on Mexicali Rose v. Superior Court (1992) 1 Cal.4th 617.  (See also Ford v. Miller Meat Co. (1994) 28 Cal.App.4th 1196 [re bone fragment in ground beef].) 

 

In Mexicali Rose, the plaintiff was a customer of defendants’ restaurant where he ordered a chicken enchilada, sustained throat injuries when he swallowed a one-inch chicken bone contained in the enchilada.  (Mexicali Rose, supra, 1 Cal.4th at 620.)  He brought an action for negligence, breach of implied warranty, and strict liability.  (Id.)  The Supreme Court address the question whether a restaurant keeper may be held liable for serving food containing substances natural to the product that, when consumed by the patron, cause injury.  (Id.)  The Court found that the “reasonable expectation” test applied and concluded that the plaintiff may not state a cause of action under the theories of strict liability or breach of the implied warranties of merchantability or fitness, but he may state a cause of action in negligence based on defendants' asserted failure to exercise due care in the preparation of the chicken enchilada.  (Id. at 620-621.)   The Court stated:

 

[T]he trend developing in courts recently considering the issue whether a plaintiff may recover for injuries caused by a natural or foreign substance can be summarized as follows: If the injury-producing substance is natural to the preparation of the food served, it can be said that it was reasonably expected by its very nature and the food cannot be determined to be unfit for human consumption or defective.[] Thus, a plaintiff in such a case has no cause of action in implied warranty or strict liability.[] The expectations of the consumer do not, however, negate a defendant's duty to exercise reasonable care in the preparation and service of the food. Therefore, if the presence of the natural substance is due to a defendant's failure to exercise due care in the preparation of the food, an injured plaintiff may state a cause of action in negligence. By contrast, if the substance is foreign to the food served, then a trier of fact additionally must determine whether its presence (i) could reasonably be expected by the average consumer and (ii) rendered the food unfit for human consumption or defective under the theories of the implied warranty of merchantability or strict liability.

 

As adopted in most of the preceding decisions, the “reasonable expectation” test differs from the foreign-natural rule of Mix, supra, 6 Cal.2d 674, in two important respects. First, whether bones or other injurious substances ought to be anticipated in a particular dish becomes a question for the trier of fact, unless as a matter of law the food was fit for consumption because the substance was natural to the food served. (See, e.g., Morrison's Cafeteria, supra, 431 So.2d 975, 978.) Second, and more important, this reasonable expectation test focuses not on the components of the dish, but on the final item sold to the consumer and the expectations that are engendered by the type of dish and the type of preparation used in making the dish. Thus, courts rejecting the exclusive application of the Mix foreign-natural test, which by definition bars a negligence claim, have frequently identified the failure to focus on food preparation and the consumer expectation that is created by processing or by the nature of the dish sold as the principal analytical defect of the Mix rule. (See Morrison's Cafeteria, supra, 431 So.2d at p. 978.)

            …

As noted above, we agree with defendants to the extent they reason that a restaurant patron cannot expect a chicken pie to be free of all bones. Such an expectation would be unreasonable and unrealistic to the ordinary consumer and would not conform to either federal or state health and safety standards.[] Moreover, as also discussed above, the implied warranties of merchantability and fitness are based on cases imposing liability for foreign objects in food (see, e.g., Cal. U. Com. Code, § 2314, Cal. Code Com. [see 23A West's Ann. Cal. U. Com. Code, supra, § 2314, p. 265]), and the Restatement Second of Torts, section 402A, comment h, emphasizes that the doctrine of strict liability applies in the limited situation arising when foreign objects or ingredients not characteristic of food cause harm. Under these guidelines, therefore, a reasonable plaintiff cannot expect a chicken enchilada always to be free of bones, and defendants owe no duty to provide a perfect enchilada.

 

(Mexicali Rose, supra,1 Cal.4th 630–632 [footnotes omitted].)

 

Defendants argue that based on cases like Mexicali Rose (chicken enchilada with a chicken bone) and Ford (bone fragment in ground beef), Plaintiff’s First, Third, and Fourth Causes of Action fail because the alleged defect in the candy apple was a codling moth larvae (or apple worm), which naturally occurs in apples.  Defendants rely on Dr. Crawford’s opinions identifying the pest as a codling moth larvae, that they are common and well-documented pests associated with apples, and that internal infestation is not externally detectable.  (Def.’s Facts 51-53.)  She opines that the condition described is consistent with a naturally occurring agricultural defect rather than a manufacturing-introduced hazard. 

 

While Dr. Crawford provides her opinions based on observations through photographs and video of the organism in the apple, she states: “My opinion regarding consistency with codling moth larvae is offered to a reasonable degree of scientific certainty based on visual characteristics and known host associations, but is not intended as definitive taxonomic identification.”  (Crawford Decl., ¶13.)  She provides a detailed explanation on the presence of codling moth larvae, their prevalence in apples, how they burrow in apples and are not externally visible, etc., but her statement in paragraph 13 raises a question of fact regarding whether the photographed organism is in fact and definitively a codling moth larvae.  Defendants did not offer any photographs/videos of the subject incident (which the Court recognizes was due to Plaintiff immediate disposal of the subject apple), nor did Dr. Crawford provide photographs of what a typical codling moth larvae looks like.  (The Court notes that Plaintiff provided a photograph of what she states was the worm-like organism in the apple, but the photograph itself appears to be a tan-colored smudge, such that the Court cannot ascertain based on this photograph alone whether Dr. Crawford’s opinions and identification of the matter is in fact a codling moth larvae. [Opp. Evid., Ex. 5.])

 

Ultimately Dr. Crawford only established that the insect is consistent with worm, but fails to opine whether it is also consistent with any other insect which would typically not be in apples.  Likewise Dr. Crawford’s declaration is without any discussion that even if not a Codling moth, other similar insects are routinely found in apples.  Without first establishing the nature of the worm/larvae/insect, the Court finds that Defendants have not established their initial burden in summary adjudication on the First, Third, and Fourth Causes of Action and whether Mexicali Rose applies.  Following Dr. Crawford’s uncertain identification of the organism, she opines about the nature of codling moth larvae, their inevitable presence in a certain number of apples, etc., but such opinions/facts are not relevant without first establishing what the organism is.  Only after Defendants establish that it was in fact a codling moth larvae can the Court assess whether a consumer can reasonably expect an apple to be free of codling moth larvae (as opposed to something completely foreign like a spider or some other type of pest not typically found in apples). 

 

As Defendants did not establish their initial burden in summary adjudication on Issues 1-3, the burden does not shift to Plaintiff to raise a triable issue of material fact.  The motion for summary adjudication is denied as to Issues 1-3 on the First, Third, and Fourth Causes of Action.

 

B.     Fifth Cause of Action for Negligent Misrepresentation (Issue 4)

 

The elements for negligent misrepresentation are: (1) a misrepresentation of a past or existing material fact; (2) without reasonable grounds for believing it to be true; (3) with intent to induce another's reliance on the fact misrepresented; (4) ignorance of the truth and justifiable reliance thereon by the party to whom the misrepresentation was directed; and (5) damages.  (B.L.M. v. Sabo & Deitsch (1997) 55 Cal. App. 4th 823, 834.)  Negligent misrepresentation, unlike fraud, does not require knowledge of falsity.  (Apollo Capital Fund, LLC v. Roth Capital Partners, LLC (2007) 158 Cal.App.4th 226, 243.) 

 

In the Fifth Cause of Action, Plaintiff alleges that Defendants made false, misleading, and inaccurate representations relating to the production, distribution, manufacturing, condition, sale, and representation of the caramel apple, which were intended to induce Plaintiff to enter into a transaction regarding the ability, option, or decision to purchase the caramel apple.  (Compl., ¶¶31-33.)  She allege she justifiably relied and was injured by the misrepresentation because she was Defendants’ customer and relied on their experience, skill, judgment, and due care.  (Id., ¶34.) 

 

At her deposition, Plaintiff testified:

 

Q.        Had you -- did you have a particular brand of candy apple in mind when you made that purchase on September 15th, 2024?

A.        No, I don’t –

Q.        Go ahead.

A.        -- base my fruit off brands or any labels or anything like that. I think, you know, fruit is fruit.

 

(Mot., Ex. A [Ramos Depo. at 14:25-15:6].) 

 

Q.        But the brand name didn't mean anything to you, did it?

A.        It didn't, no.

Q.        To you, essentially an apple is an apple, right?

A.        I mean, yes.

 

(Ramos Depo, pp. 16:3-7.)  She further testified:

 

Q.        Had you ever seen any advertising by Happy Apples prior to this purchase?

A.        Never before, no.

Q.        Ever seen anything in writing from the Happy Apple Company about their products?

A.        Never before, no.

 

(Ramos Depo., p. 21:3-8)

 

Defendants have established that they did not make any misrepresentations to Plaintiff or that Plaintiff relied on any misrepresentations when purchasing the caramel apple.  As such, the burden shifts to Plaintiff to raise a triable issue of material fact.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that while she did not select the product based on brand loyalty or advertising, this does not establish that she placed no reliance whatsoever on the product’s packaging, appearance, or implicit representations of fitness.  (Opp. at p.16.) 

 

Plaintiff relies on Hauter v. Zogarts (1975) 14 Cal.3d 104, which involved a golf training device sold in a container bearing the words “Completely Safe Ball Will Not Hit Player.” However, in that case, the plaintiff had read the printed instructions that accompanied the product and when swinging the club to hit the ball, the ball got caught under the cord of his club (which was part of the device), thus drawing the cord upwards and towards him on his follow-through. 

 

In contrast, Plaintiff has not provided excerpts of her deposition testimony or a declaration stating that she read the package materials, that the package stated any misrepresentation regarding the quality of the caramel apple (or its contents), and that she relied on the packaging in purchasing the product.  She argues that “if” the packaging contained quality representations, freshness claims or imagery inconsistent with the presence of live insects, then these representations could support her Negligent Misrepresentation and Express warranty claims.  (Opp. at p.16.)  However, the time for Plaintiff to raise such facts was at the time of her opposition.  As most, the Court only has a partial photograph of the caramel apple package, which merely states the “best by” date, the name of the product, a logo of a caramel apple on a stick, the weight of the product, and a notation to “Refrigerate After Purchase.”  (See Opp. Evid. at Ex. 5.)  This photograph does not provide any indication regarding the representations on quality, presence of live insects, etc.  As such, Plaintiff has not raised a triable issue of material fact on Issue 4.

 

The motion for summary adjudication is granted as to Issue 4 on the Fifth Cause of Action. 

 

C.    Sixth Cause of Action for Breach of Express Warranty (Issue 5)

 

“An express warranty is a contractual promise from the seller that the goods conform to the promise. If they do not, the buyer is entitled to recover the difference between the value of the goods accepted by the buyer and the value of the goods had they been as warranted.”  (Dagher v. Ford Motor Co. (2015) 238 Cal.App.4th 905, 928 [internal quotation marks omitted].)

 

In the Fifth Cause of Action, Plaintiff alleges that on September 15, 2024, Defendants sold Plaintiff multiple items, including the caramel apple.  (Compl., ¶37.) At the time of sale, Plaintiff asserts that she relied on Defendant’s representations that described the caramel apple was of quality, fresh, merchantable, and healthy.  (Id., ¶38.) She alleges she relied on Defendants’ express warranty, but Defendants breached the express warranty because the caramel apple was not merchantable, of quality, or healthy because it was defective. (Id., ¶¶39-40.)

 

Defendants provide Mr. Reidy’s declaration.  He states: “The Happy Apple caramel apple product is sold without any express warranty being made to distributors, retailers or consumers regarding naturally occurring substances, such as stems, seeds or insects.”  (Reidy Decl., ¶2.)  He states that HAC makes no statements in its advertising or other written materials for the caramel apple product regarding the lack of naturally occurring substances.  (Id., ¶3.)  He states: “No such warranty is offered because although the company makes efforts to limit naturally occurring contaminants such as Codling moth larvae, including following standard food safety and inspection practices, the presence of larvae cannot be eliminated in a commercial context.”  (Id., ¶4.)

 

Here, Defendants have established that they did not provide an express warranty for the caramel apple product as they did not provide any written contract/warranty/materials for the product. 

 

The burden shifts to Plaintiff to raise a triable issue of material fact.

 

Plaintiff argues that a description of the goods creates an express warranty.  She argues that the “Happy Apples” brand name and product packaging, which she contends Defendants have not produced in full, constitute an affirmation of quality or description of the good sufficient to create an express warranty.  (Opp. at p.17.)  She relies on Keith v. Buchanan (1985) 173 Cal.App.3d 13, 20, arguing that the “use of an evocative brand name associated with wholesome food, combined with packaging depicting the product as ready to eat, may itself constitute a basis-of-the-bargain representation.  (Id.) 

 

The Keith case involved a discussion of express warranties under Commercial Code, § 2313 and the Song-Beverly Act.  With respect to section 2313,

 

In deciding whether a statement made by a seller constitutes an express warranty under this provision, the court must deal with three fundamental issues. First, the court must determine whether the seller's statement constitutes an “affirmation of fact or promise” or “description of the goods” under California Uniform Commercial Code section 2313, subdivision (1)(a) or (b), or whether it is rather “merely the seller's opinion or commendation of the goods” under section 2313, subdivision (2). Second, assuming the court finds the language used susceptible to creation of a warranty, it must then be determined whether the statement was “part of the basis of the bargain.” Third, the court must determine whether the warranty was breached.

 

(Keith v. Buchanan (1985) 173 Cal.App.3d 13, 20.) 

 

The Court does not find that the discussion in Keith stands for the proposition set forth by Plaintiff in the opposition papers.  Rather, Keith states that the express warranty must be a statement that constitutes an affirmation of fact or promise or a description of goods (as opposed to an opinion) and then whether the statement was part of the basis of the bargain.

 

Here, Plaintiff has not pointed to any statement on the packaging or otherwise.  Next, Plaintiff testified that she did not rely on any brand, advertising, or written material when purchasing the caramel apple such that she has not established that any such purported statements were the basis of the bargain.  Finally, to the extent she argues that Defendants have not produced the packaging, this is not sufficient to avoid summary adjudication as Plaintiff admits that she threw away the packaging of the offending product nor has she sought a continuance pursuant to C.C.P. § 437c(h). 

 

Plaintiff has not raised a triable issue of material fact.  As such, the motion for summary adjudication is granted as to Issue 5 on the Sixth Cause of Action.

Motion TO COMPEL ARBITRATION

Los Angeles Superior Court Case # 25NNCV07856

 

MP:       Petitioner David Yurman

RP:      Respondent State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Company

Notice may be given either by email at [email protected] or by telephone at (818) 260-8412.

 

ALLEGATIONS:

 

On November 5, 2025, Claimant/Petitioner David Yurman (“Petitioner”) filed a Petition to Assign Case Number for Purpose of Filing and Hearing Discovery Motions (Insurance Code, § 11580.2(f)) against State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Company (“Respondent”). 

 

MOTION ON CALENDAR:

 

On March 20, 2026, Petitioner filed a Motion to Compel Underinsured Motorist Arbitration and Set an Arbitration Completion Date.  On March 27, 2026, Respondent filed a “Response and Opposition to the Petition.”  On June 18, 2026, Petitioner filed a reply.

 

LEGAL STANDARD:

 

C.C.P. § 1281.2(a) states in relevant part: “On petition of a party to an arbitration agreement alleging the existence of a written agreement to arbitrate a controversy and that a party to the agreement refuses to arbitrate that controversy, the court shall order the petitioner and the respondent to arbitrate the controversy if it determines that an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists….” 


“The award shall be made within the time fixed therefor by the agreement or, if not so fixed, within such time as the court orders on petition of a party to the arbitration. The parties to the arbitration may extend the time either before or after the expiration thereof. A party to the arbitration waives the objection that an award was not made within the time required unless he gives the arbitrators written notice of his objection prior to the service of a signed copy of the award on him.”  (C.C.P. § 1283.8.)  “[A]bsent an arbitration completion deadline established by agreement, section 1283.8 gives the trial court the power, on petition of a party to the arbitration, to set a date by which the arbitration proceeding must be completed and the award rendered.[] The better practice is to establish that date when the case is first ordered to arbitration. However, the court has discretion to entertain a petition and set a completion date even after the arbitration is in progress. Of course, any date chosen (whether before or after arbitration has commenced) must be reasonable in light of the circumstances presented by the particular arbitration.”  (Bosworth v. Whitmore (2006) 135 Cal.App.4th 536, 550 [footnote omitted].)

 

Insurance Code, § 11580.2 regarding uninsured motorist coverage/claims states that “[a]ny arbitration instituted pursuant to this section shall be concluded … [w]ithin five years from the institution of the arbitration proceeding.”  (Ins. Code, § 11580.2(i)(2)(A).)  

 

ANALYSIS: 

 

Petitioner seeks an order setting a firm arbitration completion date within 90 days of the hearing and the issuance of an Order to Show Cause within 30 days of the hearing to confirm the parties have secured an arbitration date within the timeframe.  Petitioner argues that an arbitration completion date is necessary because he is prejudiced and continues to suffer as a result of Respondent’s inactions and delays. 

 

By way of background, Petitioner asserts that at the time of the underlying September 17, 2024 collision, Respondent had provided Petitioner with underinsured motorist coverage for his bodily injuries.  (Burunsuzyan Decl., Ex. A [State Farm Policy].)  On June 30, 2025, Petitioner made a Demand for Underinsured Motorist Arbitration, of which Respondent confirmed receipt.  (Id., Exs. B [June 30, 2025 Demand for Arbitration Letter], C [July 28, 2025 State Farm Response Letter.)  From August 2025 to March 2026, the parties engaged in meet and confer efforts to select an arbitrator and on March 4, 2026, Respondent agreed to David M. Karen, Esq. of Judicate West.  (Id., Exs. D-G [Emails].)  However, within the next week, Respondent instructed the arbitrator’s case manager to delay scheduling, claiming via a March 13, 2026 email that it was “too early to set a date” and that additional discovery was needed.  (Id., Exs. G [Emails], H [March 6, 2026 Judicate West Email].)  The parties’ counsel discussed a mid-to-late August arbitration date, but the parties had differing views on discovery parameters.  (Id., Exs. G, I [Petitioner’s March 13, 2026 Letter], J [March 17, 2026 Judicate West Email offering August dates].)

 

In opposition, Respondent argues that the motion should be denied because it has agreed to arbitrate the dispute with the proposed arbitrator, has never refused arbitration, and there are two outstanding demands for medical examinations remaining that Petitioner refuses to attend—an independent medical exam and a second medical examination specialized in orthopedics.  Respondent argues that if this motion is granted before discovery is complete, this will either require a continuance of the arbitration or force Respondent to arbitrate without adequate discovery.  (Response at pp.2-3, ¶¶6-10; McNulty Decl., Exs. B [Demand for Physical Examination with Dr. Peter-Brian Andersson], C [Respondent’s February 2, 2026 Email requesting the IME be rescheduled due to the examining doctor’s unexpected hospitalization], D [Emails], E [Notice of Continuance of Demand for Physical Examination of Plaintiff with Dr. Andersson], F [Emails], G [Notice of Continuance of Demand for Physical Examination of Plaintiff with Dr. Andersson], H [Demand for Physical Examination of Plaintiff with Dr. Brian D. Rudin], I [Emails], J [Petitioner’s Objections to the IMEs], K [Respondent’s response letter], L [Final Notices of Demand for Physical Examination of Claimant], M-P [Emails].)  In large part, Petitioner refused to attend the IMEs or proceed with any further discovery until Respondent scheduled an arbitration hearing.  

 

Respondent relies on Spear v. California State Auto. Assn. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 1035, wherein the Supreme Court discussed that under section 1281.2, “a party to an agreement to arbitrate may not bring an action to compel specific performance of the arbitration provision until he or she can allege not only the existence of the agreement, but also that the opposing party refuses to arbitrate the controversy. Therefore, it appears that a cause of action to compel arbitration does not accrue until one party has refused to arbitrate the controversy.”  (Id. at 1041-42.)  Respondent argues that it has had good faith discussions about arbitration and discovery and that it has not refused to arbitrate the matter.  Respondent also contends that the Insurance Code provides for a five-year completion deadline, such that the 90-day deadline is improper. 

 

In reply, Petitioner argues that judicial intervention is necessary to ensure Respondent commits to arbitration instead of allowing Respondent to preserve an open-ended discovery period.  Petitioner argues that other than two IMEs, expert discoverer, and a recently noticed records deposition, Respondent has not identified additional discovery it intends to conduct and that its arguments that there “may” still be ongoing future discovery is speculative.  (Reply at p.3.) Petitioner represents that the remaining discovery matters can be completed within 90 days.  (Id. at p.4.)  He also argues that he already agreed to appear for the two IMEs on condition that Respondent agree not to engage in limitless future discovery and offered to appear for one and submit additional IMEs to the Arbitrator—which Respondent rejected.  (Id. at p.5.)

 

Based on the emails between counsel, it appears that the parties have agreed to arbitrate the matter and chose a mutually agreeable arbitrator, but the arbitration has not yet commenced.  The Court understands that the parties are at an impasse—Respondent seeks to conduct Petitioner’s IME before the arbitration begins, while Petitioner seeks an arbitration completion date before submitting to an IME. 

 

Although Respondent has not outright refused to arbitrate the matter, by its conduct with delaying the arbitration until discovery has been completed, it has in essence refused to arbitrate. Reliance on he five year rule is likewise misplaced.   Five years has never been the goal, but an outward limit tantamount to the five year rule in civil litigation encompassed in Code of Civil Procedure 583.310 (setting forth a nondiscretionary dismissal if an action is not commenced within five years).  Respondent relies on Insurance Code, § 11580.2(o), which states:

 

If an insured … has failed to submit to a medical examination arranged by the insurer within 20 days of the insurer's request, the insurer may, at any time prior to 30 days before the actual arbitration proceedings commence, request, and the insured shall furnish, wage loss information or medical treatment record releases, and the insurer may require the insured, except during periods of hospitalization, to make himself or herself available for a medical examination. The wage loss information or medical treatment record releases shall be submitted by the insured within 10 days of request and the medical examination shall be arranged by the insurer no sooner than 10 days after request, unless the insured agrees to an earlier examination date, and not later than 20 days after the request. If the insured fails to comply with the requirements of this subdivision, the actual arbitration proceedings shall be stayed for at least 30 days following compliance by the insured. The proceedings shall be scheduled as soon as practicable following expiration of the 30-day period.

 

(Ins. Code, § 11580.2(o).) 

 

The parties have engaged in conduct which has resulted in a legal stand-off.   Petitioner will not agree to an IME until an arbitration date is set and Respondent will not agree to a date unless the IME has been completed.  The Court has a responsibility to resolve this impasse, and is empowered to do so pursuant to section 1283.8, discussed below. 

 

Based on subsection 11580.2(o), Respondent is entitled to take Petitioner’s IME and Petitioner’s failure to submit within a given timeframe is also an issue.  To aid the parties in ensuring discovery is completed and the arbitration proceeds in an efficient manner, the Court will set both an IME and arbitration completion date.  (Bosworth v. Whitmore (2006) 135 Cal.App.4th 536, 550 [“[A]bsent an arbitration completion deadline established by agreement, section 1283.8 gives the trial court the power, on petition of a party to the arbitration, to set a date by which the arbitration proceeding must be completed and the award rendered.”].) 

 

While Respondent argues that it has up to five years to complete the arbitration pursuant to Insurance Code, § 11580.2(i), this subsection sets the outer limit for bringing the arbitration to its conclusion and is not meant to be the date of required completion.  (Bouton v. USAA Casualty Ins. Co. (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 412, 419 [“The purpose of section 11580.2, subdivision (f), ‘is to offer a means of resolving disputes that is more expeditious and less expensive than litigation. [Citations.] Its beneficiaries include the insurer and the insured....’”]; cf. C.C.P. § 583.310 [“An action shall be brought to trial within five years after the action is commenced against the defendant.”] and Alpha Media Resort Investment Cases (2019) 39 Cal.App.5th 1121, 1128 [stating that section 583.310 subserves the purpose of encouraging the “expeditious disposition of litigation” and “bring cases to a conclusion, to secure for plaintiffs the relief, and to defendants, the repose, to which the law entitles them, and to free the court's resources for the efficient adjudication of other claims.”].)  Respondent’s five year argument is antithetical to expeditious disposition of this matter.  Thus, the Court finds that setting an arbitration completion date does not conflict with the five-year outer limit set by Insurance Code, § 11580.2(i)(2)(A).

 

The motion is granted.  The Court will set an arbitration completion date, as well as order the parties to meet and confer regarding conducting Petitioner’s IMEs.  In setting the dates, the Court is guided by the initial Demand for Underinsured Arbitration made on June 30, 2025.
