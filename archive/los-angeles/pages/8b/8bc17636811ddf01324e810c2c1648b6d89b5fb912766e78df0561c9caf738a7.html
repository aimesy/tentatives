[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: PLAINTIFF’S MOTION FOR SANCTIONS FOR MISUSE OF THE DISCOVERY PROCESS

 

Dept. 733

8:30 a.m.

I.                   INTRODUCTION

This is a Song-Beverly action.

On March 13, 2025, Plaintiff Sulim Ji (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Volkswagen Group of America, Inc. (“Volkswagen”), Fletcher Jones Motor Cars, Inc. dba Audi Beverly Hills (“Fletcher”) (collectively, “Defendants”), alleging causes of action for (1) Breach of Express Warranty, (2) Breach of Implied Warranty, (3) Violation of Song-Beverly Act Section 1793.2(b), and (4) Negligent Repair.

            On December 3, 2025, the Court granted Plaintiff’s motion to compel further discovery responses, and ordered Volkswagen to serve verified, further code compliant responses Plaintiff's Request for Production (“RFPDs”) without objection — except attorney client privilege/work product and privacy — within twenty (20) days of the order (“December 2025 Order”).

            On March 26, 2026, the Court granted Plaintiff’s motion to compel compliance with the Court’s December Order. The Court ordered Volkswagen to provide verified, further code compliant responses Plaintiff's RFPDs without objection — except attorney client privilege/work product and privacy — within twenty (30) days of notice of this order (“March 2026 Order”).

            On April 29, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion for sanctions.

            On September 1, 2026, Volkswagen filed an opposition.

            No reply was filed.

II.                LEGAL STANDARD

If a party fails to comply with a court order compelling discovery responses or attendance at a deposition, the court may impose monetary, issue, evidence, or terminating sanctions. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450(h) (depositions); § 2030.290(c) (interrogatories); § 2031.300(c) (demands for production of documents); § 2033.290(e) (requests for admission).) Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.030 provides that, “[t]o the extent authorized by the chapter governing any particular discovery method . . . , the court, after notice to any affected party, person, or attorney, and after opportunity for hearing, may impose . . . [monetary, issue, evidence, or terminating] sanctions against anyone engaging in conduct that is a misuse of the discovery process . . . .” Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.010 provides that “[m]isuses of the discovery process include, but are not limited to, the following: . . . (d) Failing to respond or to submit to an authorized method of discovery. . . . (g) Disobeying a court order to provide discovery. . . .”

“A decision to order terminating sanctions should not be made lightly. But where a violation is willful, preceded by a history of abuse, and the evidence shows that less severe sanctions would not produce compliance with the discovery rules, the trial court is justified in imposing the ultimate sanction.”  (Mileikowsky v. Tenet Healthsystem (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 262, 279-280.)  When deciding whether to impose terminating sanctions, courts generally weigh three factors: (1) whether the party subject to the sanction acted willfully, (2) the detriment to the party seeking discovery, and (3) the number of formal and informal unsuccessful attempts to obtain discovery.  (Creed-21 v. City of Wildomar (2017) 18 Cal.App.5th 690, 702; Lang v. Hochman (2000) 77 Cal.App.4th 1225, 1246.)  Nonmonetary sanctions require evidence of willful violations of discovery orders or a history of egregious abuses of discovery.  (R.S. Creative, Inc. v. Creative Cotton, Ltd. (1999) 75 Cal.App.4th 486.)  A prerequisite to the imposition of nonmonetary sanctions is willful disobedience of a court order.  (New Albertsons, Inc. v. Superior Court (2008) 168 Cal.App.4th 1403.)  By requiring a violation of a discovery order before imposing nonmonetary sanctions, California courts can be sure that the offending party does not intend to comply with the discovery request.  (Ruvalcaba v. Government Employees Insurance Co. (1990) 222 Cal.App.3d 1579, 1581.)  “[A] terminating sanction issued solely because of a failure to pay a monetary discovery sanction is never justified.”  (Newland v. Superior Court (1995) 40 Cal.App.4th 608, 615.) 

“Under this standard, trial courts have properly imposed terminating sanctions when parties have willfully disobeyed one or more discovery orders.” (Id. at 390 [citing Lang, supra, 77 Cal.App.4th at 1244-1246 [discussing cases]; see, e.g., Collisson & Kaplan v. Hartunian (1994) 21 Cal. App. 4th 1611, 1617-1622 [terminating sanctions imposed after defendants failed to comply with one court order to produce discovery]; Laguna Auto Body v. Farmers Ins. Exchange (1991) 231 Cal. App. 3d 481, 491, disapproved on other grounds in Garcia v. McCutchen (1997) 16 Cal. 4th 469, 478, n.4 [terminating sanctions imposed against plaintiff for failing to comply with a discovery order and for violating various discovery statutes].)¿

III.             DISCUSSION

a.       Terminating Sanctions

Plaintiff moves for terminating sanctions, on the grounds that Volkswagen willfully

disobeyed the Court’s December 2025 Orde and March 2026 order.

As stated above, on December 3, 2025, the Court granted Plaintiff’s motion to compel further discovery responses, and ordered Volkswagen to serve verified, further code compliant responses Plaintiff's Request for Production (“RFPDs”) without objection — except attorney client privilege/work product and privacy — within twenty (20) days of the order. Plaintiff then asserted that Volkswagen had failed to comply with this Order.

On March 26, 2026, the Court granted Plaintiff’s motion to compel compliance and again ordered Volkswagen to serve verified, further code compliant responses Plaintiff's RFPDs without objection — except attorney client privilege/work product and privacy — within thirty (30) days of the order.

Now, Plaintiff provides that on April 28, 2026, he reached out to Volkswagen’s counsel stating that Plaintiff has not received further code compliant responses, production of documents or a privilege log. (Hozven Decl., ¶ 15.) According to Plaintiff, Volkswagen served supplemental document production but has not served further code compliant responses Plaintiff's RFPDs. (Ibid.) However, in the email correspondence between the parties provided by Plaintiff, Volkswagen counsel communicates on two separate occasions that she served the document production but was still awaiting verification for the further responses. (Id., Ex. 4.)

On May 1, 2026, Volkswagen ultimately served further responses to the RFPDs, without objections other than privilege, in compliance with the Court’s March 2026 Order. (Kim Decl., ¶ 7, Exs. A, B.)

The Court is not persuaded that terminating sanctions are appropriate at this time. Volkswagen has ultimately provided the responses and has sufficiently explained to this Court and to Plaintiff’s counsel that the delay was merely waiting on the verifications. There is simply no evidence of willful disobedience or intentional attempt to evade discovery obligations.

b.      Monetary Sanctions

Plaintiff further requests monetary sanctions in the amount of $500 per day for each day of noncompliance with the Court’s Orders, in the event Volkswagen has not served further responses by the time of the hearing on this motion. Given that Volkswagen has provided further responses in compliance with the Court’s order prior to the hearing, the Court declines to impose monetary sanctions.

IV.             CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing,

Plaintiff’s motion for sanctions is DENIED in its entirety.

I.                   INTRODUCTION

This is a Song-Beverly action.

On July 24, 2025, Plaintiff Katriena K. Dummett (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Defendants Toyota Motor Sales, U.S.A., Inc. (“Toyota”), and Chuck Patterson Toyota Scion Dodge (“Toyota Scion Dodge”) (collectively, “Defendants”), alleging causes of action for (1) Violation of Subdivision (d) of Civil Code section 1793.2, (2) Violation of Subdivision (b) of Civil Code section 1793.2, (3) Violation of Subdivision (a)(3) of Civil Code section 1793.2, (4) Breach of Implied Warranty of Merchantability, (5) Negligent Repair, and (6) Fraudulent Inducement – Concealment.

On August 17, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motions to compel initial responses to Form Interrogatories, set one (“FROGs”), Special Interrogatories set one (“SROGs”), and Requests for Production, set one (“RFPDs”) and to deem admitted Requests for Admission, set one (“RFAs”) (together, “Discovery Requests”).

On September 1 and 3, 2026, Defendant filed oppositions.

On September 8 and 10, 2026, Plaintiff filed replies.

II.                LEGAL STANDARD

1.      Interrogatories

A responding party has 30 days after service of interrogatories to serve their responses on the propounding party. (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.260(a).) However, if interrogatories are served by electronic service, a responding party has an additional two (2) court days to respond. (Id. § 1010.6(a)(3)(B).)¿ 

If the responding party fails to timely respond to the interrogatories, “The party to whom the interrogatories are directed waives any right to exercise the option to produce writings under Section 2030.230, as well as any objection to the interrogatories, including one based on privilege or on the protection for work product under Chapter 4 (commencing with Section 2018.010).” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.290(a).)¿ 

Furthermore, the party propounding the interrogatories may move for an order compelling response to the interrogatories. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.290(b), 2031.300(b).) And “[u]nlike a motion to compel further responses, a motion to compel responses is not subject to a 45-day time limit, and the propounding party does not have to demonstrate either good cause or that it satisfied a ‘meet and confer’ requirement.” (Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pacific Healthcare Consultants (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, 404.)¿¿ 

Failure to timely respond waives all objections, including privilege and work product, unless “[t]he party has subsequently served a response that is in substantial compliance” and “[t]he party’s failure to serve a timely response was the result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290 (a)(1)-(2).)

2.      Requests for Production

A party must respond to requests for production of documents within 30 days after service. (Code Civ. Proc. § 2031.260 (a).) The propounding and responding parties may agree to extend the time for response. (Id. § 2031.270(a).) If a party to whom requests for production of documents are directed does not provide timely responses, the requesting party may move for an order compelling responses to the discovery. (Id. § 2031.300 (c).) The party also waives the right to make any objections, including one based on privilege or work-product protection. (Id. § 2031.300 (a).)

There is no time limit for a motion to compel responses to requests for production of documents other than the cut-off on hearing discovery motions 15 days before trial. (Id. §§ 2024.020 (a), 2031.300.) No meet and confer efforts are required before filing a motion to compel responses to the discovery. (Id. § 2031.300; Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pacific Healthcare Consultants (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, 411.)¿¿¿

The party who fails to serve a timely response to a demand for inspection waives any objection to the demand unless the court finds that the party has subsequently served a response that is in substantial compliance or party’s failure was the result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect. (Code Civ. Proc. § 2031.300 (a)(1)-(2).)

3.      Requests for Admission

“Any party may obtain discovery . . . by a written request that any other party to the action admit the genuineness of specified documents, or the truth of specified matters of fact, opinion relating to fact, or application of law to fact. A request for admission may relate to a request matter that is in controversy between the parties.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.010.)  “Within 30 days after service of requests for admission, the party to whom the requests are directed shall serve the original of the response to them on the requesting party, and a copy of the response on all other parties who have appeared . . . .”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.250(a).)

If a party to whom request for admissions are served fails to provide a timely response, the party to whom the request was directed waives any objections, including based on privilege or the work product doctrine. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280(a).) The requesting party can move for an order that the genuineness of any documents and the truth of any matters specified in the request be deemed admitted, as well as for monetary sanctions. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280(b).) The court shall issue this order unless the party to whom the request was made serves a response in substantial compliance prior to the hearing on the motion. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280(c).)¿¿¿ 

III.             DISCUSSION

Plaintiff moves to compel Defendant to provide verified responses without objections to Plaintiff’s FROGs, SROGs, RFPDs and to deem admitted its RFAs.

Plaintiff declares that on November 4, 2025, Plaintiff served upon Defendant her Discovery Requests. (Wong Decl., ¶ 3, Exs. 1-2.)  The responses were therefore due on December 6, 2025. Plaintiff provides that as of the date of filing the motion on August 7, 2026, Defendant has not served any responses. (Id. ¶ 4.)

In opposition, Defendant asserts that due to internal oversight, Defendant failed to produce the responses within thirty (30) days of Plaintiff’s service. (Kong Decl., ¶ 4.) However, Defendant provides that on August 26, 2026, after attempting to meet and confer with Plaintiff’s counsel, Defendant provided Code-compliant responses to all Plaintiff’s Discovery Requests. (Id. ¶ 13, Ex. G.)

Given that Defendant has provided responses prior to the hearing that are in substantial compliance with the Code, the instant motions are denied as moot.

Sanctions

When a request for monetary sanctions is concurrently filed with a motion to compel responses, “The court may impose a monetary sanction ordering that one engaging in the misuse of the discovery process, or any attorney advising that conduct, or both pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney’s fees, incurred by anyone as a result of that conduct.” (Code of Civ. Proc., § 2023.030(a).) Additionally, “If a monetary sanction is authorized by any provision of this title, the court shall impose that sanction unless it finds that one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of sanction unjust.” (Ibid.)¿ 

            The Court does not find sanctions against Defendant to be appropriate. As Defendant provides, once Plaintiff served her motions upon Defendant and Defendant realized the internal oversight, on August 25, 2026, Defendant’s counsel reached out to Plaintiff’s counsel to indicate that Defendant would promptly serve its responses. (Kong Decl., ¶ 8.) Plaintiff’s counsel informed Defendant’s counsel that he still would not be withdrawing the motions. (Id. ¶ 10.) That next day, Defendant’s counsel served the responses. (Id. ¶ 13, Ex. G.) This entire course of conduct clearly indicates that a simple communication by Plaintiff’s counsel with Defendant’s counsel, after doing nothing for nine months, would have avoided all this. Thus, the Court finds that the imposition of sanctions would be unjust and, in fact, would reward the sort of gamesmanship engaged in by Plaintiff’s counsel in this case.

 

IV.             CONCLUSION

Plaintiff’s motions to compel initial responses to Requests for Production, set one, Form Interrogatories, set one and Special Interrogatories, set one and motion to deem Requests for Admission, set one admitted are DENIED as moot.

Plaintiff’s accompanying requests for sanctions are DENIED.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: DEFENDANT’S MOTION TO QUASH SERVICE OF SUMMONS AND COMPLAINT

 

Dept. 733

8:30 a.m.

September 15, 2026

 

I.                   INTRODUCTION

This is an unlawful detainer action.

On February 26, 2026, Plaintiff Marina Bogin (“Plaintiff”) filed an Unlawful Detainer complaint against Defendant Vito Teruna aka Hadrian (“Defendant”).

On March 13, 2026, specially appearing Defendant filed the instant motion to quash service of summons and complaint.

On September 1, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition.

No reply was filed.

II.                LEGAL STANDARD

“‘Service of process, under longstanding tradition in our system of justice, is fundamental to any procedural imposition on a named defendant.’ [Citation.]”  (AO Alfa-Bank v. Yakovlev (2018) 21¿Cal.App.5th 189, 202.)  “To establish personal jurisdiction, compliance with statutory procedures for service of process is essential.”  (Kremerman v. White (2021). 71 Cal.App.5th 358, 371.)  Defendant’s knowledge of the action does not dispense with statutory requirements for service of summons.  (Kappel v. Bartlett (1988) 200 Cal.App.3d 1457, 1466.) 

“A defendant, on or before the last day of his or her time to plead or within any further time that the court may for good cause allow” may move “to quash service of summons on the ground of lack of jurisdiction of the court over him or her” that results from lack of proper service.  (Code of Civ. Proc., § 418.10, subd. (a)(1)). A defendant has 30 days after the service of the summons to file a responsive pleading.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 412.20, subd. (a)(3).)  

Once a challenge to the court’s personal jurisdiction is filed, “the burden of proof is placed upon the plaintiff to establish the facts of jurisdiction by a preponderance of the evidence. This may be done through presentation of declarations, with opposing declarations received in response.” (CenterPoint Energy, Inc. v. Superior Court (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 1101, 1118 (citing Aquila, Inc. v. Superior Court (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 556, 568).) “When a defendant challenges the court’s personal jurisdiction on the ground of improper service of process ‘the burden is on the plaintiff to prove the existence of jurisdiction by proving, inter alia, the facts requisite to an effective service.’”  (Summers v. McClanahan¿(2006) 140¿Cal.App.4th 403, 413.) 

III.             DISCUSSION

Here, Defendant moves to quash service of summons and complaint, on the basis of lack of personal jurisdiction due to ineffective service pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure § 415.20.

Under California law, service of process of a summons and complaint may be made “by delivering a copy of the summons and complaint to the individual defendant personally.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 415.10; Board of Trustees of Leland Stanford Junior University v. Ham (2013) 216 Cal.App.4th 330, 336 (citing Evartt v. Superior Court (1979) 89 Cal.App.3d 795, 799) [“The preferred way to serve a defendant, of course, is by personal delivery as prescribed in section 415.10, as this is the most likely to ensure actual notice to the defendant.”].)

However, “[i]f a copy of the summons and complaint cannot with reasonable diligence be personally delivered to the person to be served, as specified in Section 416.60, 416.70, 416.80, or 416.90, a summons may be served by leaving a copy of the summons and complaint at the person’s dwelling house, usual place of abode, usual place of business, or usual mailing address other than a United States Postal Service post office box, in the presence of a competent member of the household or a person apparently in charge of his or her office, place of business, or usual mailing address other than a United States Postal Service post office box, at least 18 years of age, who shall be informed of the contents thereof, and by thereafter mailing a copy of the summons and of the complaint by first-class mail, postage prepaid to the person to be served at the place where a copy of the summons and complaint were left. Service of a summons in this manner is deemed complete on the 10th day after the mailing.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 415.20, subd. (b).) Reasonable diligence has been described as “denot[ing] a thorough, systematic investigation and inquiry conducted in good faith by the party or his agent or attorney.” (Kott v. Superior Ct. (1996) 45 Cal.App.4th 1126, 1137.)

Here, in Plaintiff’s Proof of Service of the summons and complaint filed on June 9, 2026, the process server provides that he effectuated substitute serve on February 28, 2026 at 8:00am. The process server provides that he left the documents with “JOHN DOE (CAROL) (Gender: M Age: 30 Height: S'S" Weight: 120 Race: WHITE Hair: LIGHT Other:) CO OCCUPANT” at 418 N Serrano Avenue, Los Angeles, CA 90004, which the process server provides is the “dwelling house or usual place of abode” of Defendant. In the Declaration of Reasonable Diligence, the process server attempted to serve the documents at 418 N Serrano Avenue on three separate occasions prior to service being effectuated: February 26, 2026 at 7:10pm; February 27, 2026 at 7:57am; and February 28, 2026 at 7:59am. On the third attempt at service, the process server provides that according to the co-occupant, “subject is not home.” The co-occupant was then served on February 28, 2026 at 8:00am. The process server then mailed a copy of the summons and complaint to the same address pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 415.20(b)

“The return of a [registered] process server … establishes a presumption, affecting the burden of producing evidence, of the facts stated in the return.” (Evid. Code § 647; see also Floveyor Internat., Ltd. v. Superior Court (1997) 59 Cal.App.4th 789, 795 [filing of proof of service that complies with the applicable statutory requirements creates a rebuttable presumption of proper service].) “Because of the statutory presumption, [Defendant is] thus required to produce evidence that he was not served.” (Am. Express Centurion Bank v. Zara (2011) 199 Cal.App.4th 383, 390.)

Here, Defendant declares that he was not properly served with the summons and complaint. He declares that “[o]n or about February 28, 2026, [he] first became aware of the existence of this action when a subtenant at the property informed [Defendant] that he had been given some type of legal papers at the property located at 418 N. Serrano Avenue, Los Angeles, California. This is a property where [Defendant has] subleased individual units and rooms. Prior to that date, [Defendant] had not received any direct notice of this action, nor had [he] been personally served with any legal documents related to this matter.” (Teruna Decl., ¶ 3.) From late February 2026 to present, Defendant “was not served personally or served at all with the Summons and Complaint in this matter even though [he has] resided at all times at a tri-plex in Log Angeles, CA from February 2024 to the present.” (Id. ¶ 4.) Defendant further declares that he does not “utilize the sub-leased property herein] as either my residence or place of business and never have.” (Id. ¶ 5.)

The only evidence that Defendant provides is a self-serving and uncorroborated declaration that he was not personally served and that he actually resides at an unidentified apartment complex different from where service was effectuated. (See Teruna Decl.) “The trial court [is] not required to accept this self-serving evidence contradicting the process server's declaration[.]” (Am. Express Centurion Bank v. Zara, supra, 199 Cal.App.4th at p. 390; Rodriguez v. Cho (2015) 236 Cal.App.4th 742, 751 [court “ ‘was not required to accept this self-serving evidence ....’ ”]; Lee v. Yan (2025) 115 Cal.App.5th 975, 978–979 [presumption of proper service not rebutted by “incredible” declaration disputing service].) The process server provides that service was attempted at 418 N Serrano Avenue multiple times, until service was effectuated on a co-occupant, who, according to the process server, provided that Defendant was not home, insinuating that Defendant indeed resided at that address.

This case is unlike Ahmed v. Collect Access, LLC (2025) 114 Cal.App.5th 1092. There, the appellate court found that the trial court erred in finding that the defendant’s declaration, asserting that he never lived or received mail at the address where service was effectuated and never conducted business or worked there, did not rebut the presumption of proper service. (Id. at pp. 1096-1097.) Similarly, the process server gave the summons and complaint to a co-occupant, but the defendant also attested that he never resided at the address where service was effectuated. (Id. at p. 1099.) However, there, the defendant, unlike Defendant here, declared where he actually was residing, declaring that he was “homeless” and “primarily living in [his] car[.]” (Id. at p. 1096.) The defendant also declared that he did not have actual notice of lawsuit until many years later. (Ibid.) In contrast, Defendant here does have actual notice of the lawsuit.  (See Gibble v. Car–Lene Research, Inc. (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 295, 31 [“[i]t is well settled that strict compliance with statutes governing service of process is not required. Rather, in deciding whether service was valid, the statutory provisions regarding service of process should be liberally construed to effectuate service and uphold the jurisdiction of the court if actual notice has been received by the defendant.”; see also Dill v. Berquist Construction Co. (1994) 24 Cal.App.4th 1426, 1436 [“[S]trict compliance is not required.”].)

As such, the Court does not find that Defendant has sufficiently rebutted the statutory presumption of facially proper service. (See People v. Chavez (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 1471, 1483 [“If some fact be presumed, the opponent of that fact bears the burden of producing or going forward with evidence sufficient to overcome or rebut the presumed fact”].)

Defendant’s motion is denied.

IV.             CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, Defendant’s motion to quash service of summons and complaint

is DENIED. Defendant is to file an answer or other responsive pleading within thirty (30) days of this order.
