DEPARTMENT P LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 24SMCV06020    Hearing Date: September 23, 2026    Dept: P

Tentative Ruling

Jorge Lopez v. Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC, Case No. 24SMCV06020

Motions:

(1)   Plaintiff’s Motion to Withdraw or Amend Deemed Admissions

(2)   Defendant’s Motion for Summary Judgment

Hearing Date: September 23, 2026

Background

This is a lemon law case. Plaintiff Jorge Lopez sues Defendant Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC and Mercedes Benz of Beverly Hills, alleging that Mercedes-Benz failed to repair or replace Plaintiff’s 2021 Mercedes Benz S-Class vehicle, in violation of the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act. (Complaint, ¶ 8.)

On December 11, 2024, Plaintiff filed the operative complaint.

On March 18, 2026 and March 20, 2026, Mercedes-Benz filed five motions to compel plaintiff’s response to Mercedes-Benz’s request for production of documents, interrogatories, deposition, and a motion to deem RFAs admitted. Lopez served responses on May 8, 2026, stating “Verification to follow.” (Wong Decl. ¶ 2, Ex. B.) On May 20, 2026, the Court granted all five motions to compel and deemed the RFAs admitted.

On June 5, 2026, Mercedes-Benz moved for summary judgment based on the RFAs deemed admitted. Lopez then obtained the verifications and on June 8, 2026, sent the verifications to Mercedes-Benz. (Id. at ¶¶ 5-6.)

On September 1, 2026, Lopez moved to withdraw the RFAs deemed admitted. On September 10, 2026, Mercedes-Benz filed opposition.

Motion to Withdraw Admission

“A party may withdraw or amend an admission made in response to a request for admission only on leave of court granted after notice to all parties.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.300, subd. (a).) The Court may only permit withdrawal or amendment if it determines that (1) the admission was “the result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect” and (2) the party who obtained the admission “will not be substantially prejudiced in maintaining that party’s action or defense on the merits.” (Id., § 2033.300, subd. (b).) Additionally, where a court grants such a motion, it may impose conditions on further discovery. (Id., § 2033.300, subd. (c).)  

“The trial court’s discretion in ruling on a motion to withdraw or amend an admission is not unlimited, but must be exercised in conformity with the spirit of the law and in a manner that serves the interests of justice.” (New Albertsons, Inc. v. Superior Court (2008) 168 Cal.App.4th 1403, 1420 (Albertsons).) Any doubts in applying section 2033.300 must be resolved in the moving party’s favor. (Ibid.) “[T]he court’s discretion to deny a motion under the statute is limited to circumstances where it is clear that the mistake, inadvertence, or¿neglect was inexcusable, or where it is clear that the withdrawal or amendment would substantially prejudice the party who obtained the admission in maintaining that party’s action or defense on the merits.” (Id. at pp. 1420–1421.) 

Discussion

Lopez argues that the only reason the Court granted the Motion to Deem RFAs admitted was because the RFAs were not verified. Lopez argues that verifications were later sent and that the delay in bringing this motion was because of an internal communication failure that meant that Lopez’s counsel was never notified that the RFAs were received and sent to opposing counsel.

In opposition, Mercedes-Benz argues that Mr. Wong’s declaration, cited in the Motion to Withdraw, was not filed concurrently with the motion and because Plaintiff’s evidence does not establish excusable neglect or diligence because law office neglect is not generally considered excusable. Mercedes-Benz argues that if any relief is provided, the Court should re-open discovery and require Plaintiff’s responses by provided by a date certain, continue trial, and require Plaintiff to pay Mercedes Benz’s reasonable attorneys’ fees and costs incurred in preparing and filing the mooted motion for summary judgment.

First, the Lopez’s supporting declaration is attached to his ex parte application and motion to withdraw deemed admissions, filed concurrently with this motion. Though there appears to be a technical error in the file name, Mercedes-Benz received the declaration with the evidence referenced in the motion and was not prejudiced by the error. The Court will consider the Wong declaration submitted in support of the ex parte application as in support of this motion as well, since it is clearly the referenced document.

On February 11, 2025, Mercedes-Benz served Lopez with RFAs No. 1-31. (Wong Decl. ¶ 2, Ex. A.) Lopez served responses on May 8, 2026, stating “Verification to follow.” (Id. ¶ 3, Ex. B.) The Court granted Mercedes-Benz’s Motion to deem RFAs Admitted on May 20, 2026, because the responses were unverified. (Id. ¶ 5.) Lopez then obtained the verifications and sent them to Mercedes-Benz on or around June 8, 2026. (Id. at ¶¶ 5-6.) Mercedes-Benz does not dispute that it received the verifications on or around June 8, 2026. Though verifications were delivered to Mercedes-Benz in June, Lopez’s attorney did not discover that they had been received or served until he was finalizing Plaintiff’s opposition to Mercedes-Benz’s Motion for Summary Judgment. (Id. at ¶ 9.) He then filed this motion. (Ibid.)

Lopez moves to withdraw those RFAs deemed admitted pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.300. He argues that his failure to include certain RFAs in his responses was due to his mistake and excusable neglect in failing to realize that someone else at his firm had received and transmitted the RFA verifications. Whether such a failure is excusable is debatable, as argued by Defendant. However, Albertsons instructs that any doubts in the application of section 2033.300 must be resolved in the moving party’s favor.” (Albertsons, supra 168 Cal.App.4th at 1420.) And it is not clear to the Court that Plaintiff’s mistake was inexcusable, as required to deny relief under Albertsons. Nor is Mercedes-Benz at risk of being prejudiced if Plaintiff’s motion is granted. While Mercedes-Benz protests the expense involved in defending this action, Mercedes-Benz itself has filed multiple motions within just the past few months. Thus, the Court finds that Lopez is entitled to relief under section 2033.300 and that Mercedes-Benz will not suffer prejudice from Lopez’s admissions being withdrawn. 

Conclusion

Lopez’s Motion to Withdraw Admissions is GRANTED.

Defendant’s Motion for Summary Judgment rests entirely on Plaintiff’s deemed admissions. Because the Court grants Plaintiff relief pursuant to section 2033.300, Defendant’s motion is DENIED. 

The Court agrees with Mercedes-Benz that a trial continuance is warranted. Mercedes-Benz, however, cites no authority authorizing an award of fees or sanctions on this motion and did not request sanctions on its initial motions to compel. The Court therefore declines to award fees.

Plaintiff is ordered to produce code-compliant responses to Mercedes-Benz’s RFAs within ten (10) days. The Court will discuss the length of any trial continuance at the hearing.


Case Number: 25SMCV06181    Hearing Date: September 23, 2026    Dept: P

Tentative Ruling

N Family Services v. Palisades Capital Fund Management, LLC, et al., Case No. 25SMCV06181

Ronald A. Waters and Danielle C. Waters’ Demurrer with Motion to Strike

Hearing Date: September 23, 2026

Background

Plaintiff N Family Services, LLC sues Defendants Palisades Capital Fund Management, LLC; Palisades Funding, Inc.; The Palisades Capital Partners; Patrick D. McKenna; Leslie H. Gornik; Ronald A. and Danielle A.Waters in their individual capacity and in their representative capacity as Trustee of Texas Property Trust, and Does 1-10 for breach of contract, unlawful withholding of security deposit, and fraud. Plaintiff alleges that Defendants developed property located at 15975 Alcima Ave., Pacific Palisades, CA and held several loans on the property. (Complaint, ¶ 1.) Plaintiff alleges that Defendants fraudulently induced it to loan Defendants $2.75 million to cover junior deeds of trust on the property and pay a $600,000 security deposit, constituting four months rent. (Ibid.) Plaintiff alleges that Defendants spent the security deposit money paying off creditors and failed to disclose loans in default, resulting in a foreclosure by which Plaintiff lost its equity in the property and security deposit. (Id. at ¶ 3.)

On November 26, 2025, Plaintiff filed the initial complaint.

On February 2, 2026, Plaintiff entered default against Palisades Capital Fund Management, Palisades Funding, Inc., and The Palisades Capital Partners.

On March 30, 2026, Ronald A. Waters and Danielle C. Waters (“the Waters”) filed a demurrer to the complaint.

On May 19, 2026, Plaintiff filed the operative First Amended Complaint (“FAC”).

On June 22, 2026, the Waters filed this demurrer and motion to strike. On September 10, 2026, Plaintiff filed opposition. On September 15, 2026, the Waters replied.

Arguments

The Waters demur to Plaintiff’s fourth cause of action for fraud on the grounds that it fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action against the Waters and on the grounds that it is uncertain. The Waters contend that the FAC fails to state a claim against the Waters for fraud because it does not allege with specificity that the Waters made any misrepresentations to Plaintiff. (Demurrer, p. 9.) The Waters also argue that the FAC fails to plead fraudulent concealment because it does not allege that the Waters concealed or failed to disclose any material facts. (Id. at p. 10.) The Waters also argue that the FAC fails to plead reliance and is uncertain because it does not identify any acts by the Waters defendants with specificity. (Id. at pp. 11-15.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that the FAC states a claim for civil conspiracy to defraud Plaintiff by joining a scheme, masterminded by McKenna and Gornik, who falsely represented that the other Defendants approved Plaintiff’s junior encumbrance, while the Waters knowingly facilitated and benefitted from the scheme. (Opposition, p. 5, 8-9.) Plaintiff also argues that the FAC is not uncertain. (Id. at p. 14.) In the alternative, Plaintiff requests leave to amend. (Id. at p. 15.)

In reply, the Waters argue that the FAC does not allege actual knowledge that the fraud was planned and intent to aid in the commission of the alleged fraud. (Reply, p. 4.) The Waters argue that the FAC’s allegation that the Waters knew about the alleged fraud is insufficient because the FAC does not allege facts showing how the Waters would have known about the alleged fraud. (Id. at p. 5.) The Waters argue that Plaintiff’s caselaw is distinguishable because the cases concern tacit agreement, which is not what the FAC alleges. (Id. at p. 7.) The Waters argue that the underlying fraud claim is insufficient and that the repayment allegations do not adequately connect the Waters defendants to the alleged false promises. (Id. at p. 9.)

In a demurrer proceeding, the defects in the complaint must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) “A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Ibid.) The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Id.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.) However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245).

Before filing a demurrer, parties must meet and confer in person or by phone. (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41.)

Discussion

Meet and Confer

The Waters’ counsel met and conferred with Plaintiff’s counsel on June 18, 2026, before filing this demurrer. The meet and confer requirement is satisfied.

Conspiracy to Defraud

A complaint states a claim for civil conspiracy where it alleges facts showing (1) defendants’ agreement to the objective and course of action to injure; (2) wrongful act pursuant to such agreement; and (3) resulting damage. (Klistoff v. Superior Court (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 469, 479 [“The elements of an action for civil conspiracy are (1) formation and operation of the conspiracy, and (2) damage to plaintiff resulting from an act or acts done in furtherance of the common design.”]; Wyatt v. Union Mortgage Co. (1979) 24 Cal.3d 773, 784-786 [conspiratorial agreements may be circumstantially inferred from circumstances including the nature of the acts, the relations of the parties-- e.g., family business, or corporate officers or directors), and the interests of the alleged conspirators].)

The Waters argue that the FAC alleges insufficient facts to support its conclusory allegation that the Waters participated in a conspiracy to defraud Plaintiff. “While a complaint must contain more than a bare allegation the defendants conspired, a complaint is sufficient if it apprises the defendant of the ‘character and type of facts and circumstances upon which she was relying to establish the conspiracy.’” (AREI II Cases (2013) 216 Cal.App.4th 1004, 1022, citing Schessler v. Keck (1954) 125 Cal.App.2d 827, 833.) In the AREI II Cases, which the Waters cite in reply, the Court found that a complaint’s allegations that Morgan Keegan & Co, Inc., which assisted AREI in structuring joint ventures that concealed AREI’s owner’s criminal history, was involved with AREI’s development of a master deal for several months, gained detailed insider knowledge about AREI’s operations, and acted as a go-between between AREI and prospective lenders were sufficient to state a claim for conspiracy. (Id. at 1022-1023.)

Here, Plaintiff’s allegations describe a complicated pattern of loans on the subject property. Plaintiff alleges that the four Palisades Entity defendants (hereinafter “Palisades Entities”) were operated and managed by Defendants McKenna and Gornik. (FAC, ¶ 10-11.) Plaintiff alleges that the Palisades Entities purchased and developed the subject property. (Id. at ¶ 4.) Plaintiff alleges that in March 2022, the Waters loaned one of the Palisades Entities $600,000. (Id. at ¶ 22.) Plaintiff alleges that in April 2022, McKenna borrowed $13,750,000 from Partners Capital Solutions REIT I, LLC (“PCS”) on the condition that the loan would be a superior to all other encumbrances and that McKenna would not assume new encumbrances without PCS’s approval. (Id. at ¶ 20.) Plaintiff alleges that as development progressed, McKenna and Gornik developed a scheme to fund development by encumbering the property with junior deeds of trust and not disclosing these deeds to PCS. (Id. at ¶ 19.) Plaintiff alleges that the Waters structured these junior liens with McKenna and Gornik. (Id. at ¶¶ 19, 21.) Plaintiff alleges that McKenna and Gornik made a series of misrepresentations to induce Plaintiff to loan the Palisades Entities $2.4 million. (Id. at ¶¶ 25-27.) Plaintiff alleges that the Waters participated in the scheme by accepting funds from Plaintiff’s loan to satisfy the Waters’ $600,000 debt while knowing that PCS was unaware of the junior encumbrances. (Id. at ¶ 72.) Plaintiff also alleges that the Palisades Entites made no payments on the Waters’ loan and had a relationship with McKenna that involved loaning money to other entities. (Ibid.)

In support of Plaintiff’s contention that the FAC states a claim for conspiracy, it cites Wyatt v. Union Mortgage Company (“Wyatt”) (1979) 24 Cal.3d 773, a mortgage fraud case. In Wyatt, the Supreme Court of California found that evidence that a corporate officer instructed other company officials that “late charges were a great source of income,” and that “it had been a policy of the company that if the first payment was late, all the rest of the payments would be automatically late,” sufficient to state a claim for conspiracy, combined with evidence that the mortgage lender was a tight-knit operation under the officer’s personal control. (Wyatt, supra 24 Cal.3d at 785.)

Here, after careful review the Court finds that the allegations in the FAC fall short of both Wyatt and the AREI II Cases. In the context of the complex scheme alleged, the FAC does not describe the relationship between the Waters and McKenna and Gornik in sufficient detail to support a claim that the Waters conspired with McKenna and Gornik to defraud Plaintiff. Here, Plaintiff alleges only that Waters loaned McKenna and Gornik a comparatively small amount, knew that they were accepting junior loans without telling PCS, and later were paid out of Plaintiff’s money. These allegations are not sufficient to show an agreement between the Waters and McKenna and Gornik to defraud Plaintiff. As such, the Waters’ Demurrer to Plaintiff’s fourth claim of fraud is SUSTAINED.

Plaintiff requests leave to amend. (Opposition, p. 15.) The FAC’s allegations portray a complicated web of loans connected to the subject property and the involvement of several entities and individuals, including the Waters and their trust. As this is the first demurrer in this action, the Court is not prepared to find that there is no reasonable possibility that an amendment will not state a claim for fraud against the Waters individually or as trustees of the Texas Property Trust. Therefore, the Court grants leave to amend.

Motion to Strike

The Waters move to strike Plaintiff’s claim for punitive damages and attorneys’ fees. Plaintiff’s claims for punitive damages and attorneys’ fees is based on its fraud claim. Since the Court sustains Plaintiff’s demurrer with leave to amend, the motion to strike is moot.

Conclusion

The Waters’ demurrer is SUSTAINED with leave to amend.

The Waters’ Motion to Strike is DENIED as moot.
