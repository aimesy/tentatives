MOTION FOR
SUMMARY JUDGMENT, or in the alternative, summary adjudication



 



LEGAL
STANDARD – SUMMARY JUDGMENT/SUMMARY ADJUDICATION



 



The
function of a motion for summary judgment or adjudication is to allow a
determination as to whether an opposing party cannot show evidentiary support
for a pleading or claim and to enable an order of summary dismissal without the
need for trial. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co.¿(2001) 25 Cal.4th
826, 843.) Code Civ. Proc.¿§437c(c) “requires the trial judge to grant summary
judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably
deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or
evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to¿any material fact and that
the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.”¿ (Adler v.
Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.)¿ “The function of
the pleadings in a motion for summary judgment is to delimit the scope of the
issues; the function of the affidavits or declarations is to disclose whether
there is any triable issue of fact within the issues delimited by the
pleadings.”¿ (Juge¿v. County of Sacramento¿(1993) 12 Cal.App.4th 59, 67
[citing¿FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima¿(1991) 231 Cal. App. 3d 367,
381-382].)¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿



 



As to
each claim as framed by the complaint, the party moving for summary judgment
must satisfy the initial burden of proof by presenting facts to negate an
essential element, or to establish a defense. (Code Civ.¿Proc.¿§437c(p)(2);¿Scalf¿v.
D. B. Log Homes, Inc.¿(2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1520.) Courts “liberally
construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and
resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.” (Dore v.
Arnold Worldwide, Inc.¿(2006) 39 Cal.4th 384, 389.)¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿



 



Once
the defendant has met that burden, the burden shifts to the opposing party to
show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to that cause
of action or a defense thereto. To establish a triable issue of material fact,
the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence. (Sangster
v.¿Paetkau¿(1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)



 



DISCUSSION



 



A.   
A triable issue of material fact
exists as to the Negligence Causes of Action – First Cause of Action (Negligent
Hiring, Supervision, and Retention of An Unfit Employee), Fourth Cause of Action
(Negligent Supervision of a Minor), and Fifth Cause of Action (Negligence).



 



SCVA asserts that (1) there is
generally no duty to protect others from third-party conduct; (2) the
special relationship doctrine is an exception to this rule; (3) the special
relationship doctrine requires a foreseeability analysis; and (4) the
foreseeability analysis is measured by what the defendant actually knew.  SCVA argues that it did not breach any duty or
ratify any improper conduct as Lowe was not SCVA’s agent or employee and,
assuming that he was, SCVA was never in the custody, care, and control of
students, including Plaintiff (SCVA’s UMF No. 13); all volleyball events
attended by Plaintiff had adequate staffing and/or security (id., No.
14); SCVA is unaware of any improper conduct by Lowe at any SCVA event (id.,
No. 15); SCVA conducted a background check on Lowe which he passed (id.,
No. 5); Lowe had required coaching certifications prior to becoming a member (ibid.);
Lowe agreed to a code of ethics (id., No. 6); SCVA did not receive any
complaints or concerns about Lowe until it received notice of a Los Angeles
Police Department (“LAPD”) investigation (id., Nos. 8-11); and Lowe has
not attended a SCVA event or been an SCVA member since. (Id., No. 11.)



 



“The elements of breach of duty
and causation are ordinarily questions of fact for the jury’s determination.” (Hernandez
v. Jensen (2021) 61 Cal.App.5th 1056, 1064.) “[I]f the circumstances permit
a reasonable doubt whether the defendant’s conduct violates the standard of due
care, the doubt must be resolved by the jury as an issue of fact rather than of
law by the court.” (Musgrove v. Ambrose Properties (1978) 87 Cal.App.3d
44, 53.) And the court may only decide causation as a matter of law where there
is no reasonable dispute. (Constance B. v. State of California (1986)
178 Cal.App.3d 200, 207.)



 



“[A] special relationship between
the defendant and the dangerous third party is one that ‘entails an ability to
control [the third party's] conduct.” (Safechuck v. MJJ Productions, Inc.
(2023) 94 Cal.App.5th 675, 691–692.) “‘[I]t is the right to control the means
and manner in which the result is achieved that is significant in determining
whether a principal-agency relationship exists.’ [Citation.]” (People v. JTH
Tax, Inc. (2013) 212 Cal.App.4th 1219, 1242.) “It is not essential that the
right of control be exercised or that there be actual supervision of the work
of the agent; the existence of the right establishes the relationship.” (Ibid.)
“‘The key in each [special relationship] is that the defendant's relationship
with ... the tortfeasor ... places the defendant in the best position to
protect against the risk of harm.’ [Citations.] Thus, the defendant's ability
to control the person who caused the harm must be such that ‘if exercised, [it]
would meaningfully reduce the risk of the harm that actually occurred.’
[Citations.]” (Barenborg v. Sigma Alpha Epsilon Fraternity (2019) 33
Cal.App.5th 70, 78 [holding that national fraternities have no duty to control
local chapters or their members.]) “Examples of special relationships with the
persons posing the risks include ‘a parent with dependent children,’ ‘a
custodian with those in its custody,’ and ‘an employer with employees when the
employment facilitates [an] employee's causing harm to third parties.’” (Id.,
at 77–78 [quoting Rest.3d Torts, Liability for Physical and Emotional Harm, §
41].)¿



 



The adequacy of supervision is
generally a question of fact for determination by the jury. (Rodrigues v.
San Jose Unified School Dist. (1958) 157 Cal.App.2d 842, 848.) And
“foreseeability in evaluating negligence and causation requires a ‘more
focused, fact-specific’ inquiry that takes into account a particular
plaintiff’s injuries and the particular defendant's conduct [Citations.].” (Laabs
v. Southern California Edison Co. (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 1260, 1273; see
also Ballard v. Uribe (1986) 41 Cal.3d 564, 572, fn. 6 [“[T]he jury may
consider the likelihood or foreseeability of injury in determining whether, in
fact, the particular defendant’s conduct was negligent in the first place.
Second, foreseeability may be relevant to the jury’s determination of whether
the defendant’s negligence was a proximate or legal cause of the plaintiff’s
injury.”].)



 



An independent intervening act is
a superseding cause relieving the actor of liability for [their] negligence
only if the intervening act is highly unusual or extraordinary and hence not
reasonably foreseeable. [Citation.] Reasonable foreseeability in this context
is a question for the trier of fact.” (Cline v. Watkins (1977) 66
Cal.App.3d 174, 178.) The existence of a superseding cause is an affirmative
defense that SCVA must establish. (See Green v. Healthcare Services, Inc. (2021)
68 Cal.App.5th 407, 415.)



 



SCVA does not deny that it has a
special relationship with Plaintiff, but argues that the facts, taken as a
whole, show that SCVA did not have the right to control the means and manner in
which Lowe conducted his responsibilities. All facts presented by SVCA are
supported by the Declaration of Shannon Davenport (“Davenport”), the Director
of Special Operations for SCVA. As this affirmative evidence weighs against the
existence of an employment or agency relationship between SCVA and Lowe, the
Court finds SCVA has met its moving burden as to the non-existence of a special
relationship between SCVA and Plaintiff. Thus, the burden shifts to Plaintiff
to raise a triable issue of material facts.



 



Plaintiff contends that SCVA has
conceded a special relationship and, as such, the factors stated in Rowland
v. Christian (1968) 69 Cal. 2d 108, must be addressed. (J. Opp., at p.10.) Plaintiff
argues that an application of the Rowland factors supports the assertion
that SCVA owed a duty to Plaintiff: (1) the first Rowland factor,
foreseeability, is supported by Brown v. USA Taekwondo (2019) 40
Cal.App.5th 107 (“Brown”), as it was readily foreseeable that youth
athletes might be sexually molested by their coaches regardless of whether
there existed knowledge of any prior sexual misconduct by Lowe or any other
SCVA affiliate; (2) the second Rowland factor, degree of certainty, is
supported by Juarez v. Boy Scouts of Am., Inc. (2000) 81 Cal. App. 4th
377 (“Juarez”) which held that “[t]he significant emotional trauma
caused by childhood sexual abuse, with its related societal costs, is well
documented[;]” and (3) the third factor, closeness of connection between a
defendant’s conduct and the injury suffered, supports a duty as SCVA could have
reduced the risk of Plaintiff being abused by limiting inappropriate contact
between coaches and athletes.



 



Plaintiff disputes that the
evidence is sufficient to carry SCVA’s burden for the following reasons: SCVA
undertook approval of all coaches and chaperones of SCVA member teams,
including Lowe (J.’s UMF, Nos. 17-18); SCVA admits that youth team
players did not receive training regarding the prevention of sexual assault and
harassment and SCVA did not have a sexual harassment prevention policy (id.,
No. 19); SCVA admits it did not provide employee or staff training at
tournaments in recognizing suspected child abuse (id., No. 20); and SCVA
admits it did not monitor its teams’ lodging arrangements (id., No. 21).
Plaintiff also asserts that SCVA failed to provide adequate training to
coaches, staff, minor athletes, and their families. (Id., Nos. 28-29.) Specifically,
as to coaches, Plaintiff asserts that SCVA failed to provide training on the
dimensions of sexual harassment and boundary crossing and how to identify and
respond to such harassment. (Id., No. 28.)  SCVA failed to define appropriate coaching
behaviors towards minor athletes, to identify boundary crossing and sexual
misconduct red flags, to clarify such conduct is prohibited, and to teach
reporting protocols. (Id., No. 29.)



 



Plaintiff has offered an expert
opinion on whether SCVA breached a duty owed to Plaintiff. The question of duty
is a legal question, and an expert may not opine on whether a duty exists. (Asplund
v. Selected Investments in Financial Equities, Inc. (2000) 86 Cal.App.4th
26, 50.) The Court does not consider the opinion of Plaintiff’s expert Charol
Shakeshaft (“Shakeshaft”) for the purpose of duty. As taken from Plaintiff’s
material fact Numbers 28 and 29, the Court interprets Shakeshaft’s declaration
as discussing measures that SCVA could have taken to meet its alleged duty of
care to Plaintiff.



 



As noted above, when determining
whether a duty exists, courts must look to “pertinent factors,” commonly known
as the Rowland factors, including “foreseeability of harm to the
plaintiff, the degree of certainty that the plaintiff suffered injury, the
closeness of the connection between the defendant's conduct and the injury
suffered, the moral blame attached to the defendant's conduct, the policy of
preventing future harm, the extent of the burden to the defendant and
consequences to the community of imposing a duty to exercise care with
resulting liability for breach, and the availability, cost, and prevalence of
insurance for the risk involved.” (Rowland, supra, 69 Cal. 2d
108, 112-13 [Partially superseded by statute on a different issue as stated in Calvillo-Silva
v. Home Grocery (1998) 19 Cal.4th 714, 722; disapproved on a different
issue in Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 853.
See also Smith v. Freund (2011) 192 Cal. App. 4th 466,473-74
[maintaining that the Rowland factors are still the factors for
determination of duty].)



 



In regard to foreseeability,
“‘while it is the province of the jury, as trier of fact, to determine whether
an unreasonable risk of harm was foreseeable under the particular facts of a
given case, the…court must still decide as a matter of law whether there was a
duty in the first place, even if that determination includes a consideration of
foreseeability. [Citation.]’ [Citation.]” (M. W. v. Panama Buena Vista Union
School Dist. (2003) 110 Cal. App. 4th 508, 516 (“M.W.”) [citing
Romero v. Superior Court (2001) 89 Cal.App.4th 1068, 1078].) “The existence of
a duty and foreseeability, when analyzed to determine the scope of a duty, is a
question of law. . .” (Wiener v. Southcoast Childcare Centers, Inc.
(2004) 32 Cal. 4th 1138, 1146 [cited in M.W., supra, Cal.App.4th
508, 516].) “[T]he analysis of foreseeability for purposes of assessing the
existence or scope of a duty is different, and more general, than it is for
assessing whether any such duty was breached or whether a breach caused a
plaintiff's injuries. ‘[I]n analyzing duty, the court's task ‘“‘is not to
decide whether a particular plaintiff's injury was reasonably foreseeable in
light of a particular defendant's conduct, but rather to evaluate more
generally whether the category of negligent conduct at issue is sufficiently
likely to result in the kind of harm experienced that liability may
appropriately be imposed on the negligent party.’”’”’ (Staats v. Vintner's
Golf Club, LLC (2018) 25 Cal. App. 5th 826, 837 [citing Laabs, supra,
175 Cal.App.4th 1260, 1272–1273].)



 



SCVA concedes that there is a
special relationship between itself and Plaintiff. Plaintiff has submitted
evidence that Lowe’s membership as a coach was controlled by SCVA. (See Pl.’s
UMF No. 12; Pl.’s Volume of Exs., Ex. 2, Deposition of Davenport, Volume II 154:
5-18; 158:21-159:4; 156: 10-17].) Thus, as SCVA has conceded a special
relationship exists and Plaintiff has provided evidence raising an issue as SCVA’s
control over Lowe, the Court determines that a triable issue of fact exists as
to whether SCVA had a duty to Plaintiffs.  Once such a duty is determined to exist, the
Court must consult the Rowland factors to determine whether relevant
policy considerations counsel limiting that duty. (See Doe v. Lawndale
Elementary School Dist. (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 113, 127.)



 



Applied here, the question
regarding foreseeability is whether it is reasonably foreseeable that
organizations or entities that provide services primarily or exclusively for
children have employees who may sexually abuse a child if the organization
fails to take reasonable measures to prevent the abuse. (Doe v. United
States Youth Soccer Assn., Inc. (2017) 8 Cal.App.5th 1118 (“United
States Youth Soccer Assn.”) In United States Youth Soccer Assn.,
the Court provides a Rowland analysis that is nearly identical to this
case. The Court in that case held that the soccer organization directed to
minors had a duty to require and conduct criminal background checks of
defendants' employees and volunteers who had contact with children in their
programs and failed to do so. (Id., at 1137-38.) Here, SCVA conducted a
background check, but, according to the Davenport Declaration submitted by
SCVA, the background check did not show information other than certifications.
(Davenport Decl., ¶ 8, Ex. 2.) The other Rowland factors support this
Court’s conclusion: Plaintiff suffered injury, the connection between the
injury and SCVA’s background check was close, and preventing harm to children
is a paramount goal of our society. (United States Youth Soccer Assn., supra,
8 Cal.App.5th at 1138.) Thus, Plaintiff has met her burden to show that a
triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to that cause of action
or a defense thereto as to the negligence-based causes of action within the Complaint.



 



LAUSD’s objections to SCVA’s
evidence (i.e., statements within Davenport’s Declaration) are
overruled.



 



For the reasons explained above, having found that disputed
issues of material fact exist, the Motion for Summary Judgment is DENIED. For
the same reasons, the Motion for Summary Adjudication as to the
Negligence-Based Actions (First Cause of Action (Negligent Hiring, Supervision,
and Retention of An Unfit Employee), Third Cause of Action (Negligent Failure
to Warn, Train, or Educate), Fourth Cause of Action (Negligent Supervision of a
Minor), and Fifth Cause of Action (Negligence) is DENIED.



B.    SCVA
does not meet its initial burden regarding the Second Cause of Action (Breach of Mandatory Duty: Failure to Report Suspected Child
Abuse).



 



SCVA argues that it had no duty to
report under the California Child Abuse and Neglect Reporting Act (“CANRA”) as
it did not receive any complaint concerning or relating to Lowe until it
received notice of a Los Angeles Police Department (“LAPD”) investigation (SCVA’s
UMF, at Nos. 8-11) and, upon receiving this notice, reported Lowe to the U.S.
Center for Safe Sport per mandatory reporting requirements on October 30, 2017
(id., at No. 10). SCVA contends that it did not fail to report.



 



CANRA (codified under Pen. Code §
11164 et seq.) “was enacted to protect children from abuse and neglect through
a comprehensive reporting scheme aimed at identifying and protecting children
who are being abused or neglected.” (Holman v. County of Butte (2025)
111 Cal.App.5th 177, 182 (“Holman”).) CANRA requires a mandated reporter
“to make a report to a law enforcement agency or a county welfare department
‘whenever the mandated reporter, in his or her professional capacity or within
the scope of his or her employment, has knowledge of or observes a child whom
the mandated reporter knows or reasonably suspects has been the victim of child
abuse or neglect.’” (B.H. v. County of San Bernardino (2015) 62 Cal.4th
168, 186 (“B.H.”) [subsequent rehearing unpublished]; see Pen.
Code, § 11165.7.) Failure to make the required report is a misdemeanor. (Pen.
Code, § 11166, subd. (c).) In addition, an injured minor may bring a civil
action where “‘a breach of the mandated reporter's duty to report child abuse’”
causes the minor's injuries. (B.H., supra, 62 Cal.4th at p. 188,
fn. 6; accord, All Angels Preschool/Daycare v. County of Merced (2011)
197 Cal.App.4th 394, 405; see Alejo v. City of Alhambra (1999) 75
Cal.App.4th 1180, 1188 [89 Cal. Rptr. 2d 768] [“allegations [a] defendant
failed to make the report required by the statute support[]” a cause of action
“under the doctrine of negligence per se”, disapproved on another ground in B.H.,
supra, at p. 188, fn. 6].) CANRA employs an objective standard for
evaluating the reasonableness of a mandated reporter's suspicion. Penal Code § 11166(a)(1)
states “‘reasonable suspicion’ means that it is objectively reasonable for a
person to entertain a suspicion, based upon facts that could cause a reasonable
person in a like position, drawing, when appropriate, on the person's training
and experience, to suspect child abuse or neglect.” (See also B.H., supra,
62 Cal.4th at 193 [“Mandated reporters have mandatory reporting duties which
are governed by an objective standard.”].)



 



Here, the alleged wrongful act is SCVA’s
failure to report suspect child abuse to a law enforcement agency or child
protective services as required. (Complaint ¶ 30.) SCVA’s evidence shows that,
upon receiving information from the LAPD of an investigation of Lowe for sexual
relationships with minor females, SCVA reported the incident to the U.S. Center
for SafeSport. (SCVA’s UMF Nos. 9-10; Davenport Decl. ¶¶ 12-13, Ex. 5.)



 



SCVA does not offer evidence,
however, that the U.S. Center for SafeSport is a law enforcement agency, a
county welfare department, or a proper agency under CANRA. (See Pen. Code § 1165.9.)
The attached report to the U.S. Center for SafeSport states: “If you are a
Covered Adult (an individual over the age of 18 who is subject to SafeSport
policies), you are also required to report all instances of possible child
abuse and/or neglect to appropriate law enforcement.” (Davenport Decl., at ¶ 13, Ex. 5.)



 



Without this evidence, SCVA does
not meet its initial burden to negate an essential element, or to establish a
defense as to the CANRA claim. Because SCVA has not met its initial burden on
this claim, the Court may and does deny summary adjudication of this cause of
action notwithstanding Plaintiff’s failure to discuss the CANRA claim in
Opposition.



 



The Motion for Summary
Adjudication as to the Second Cause of Action (Breach of Mandatory Duty:
Failure to Report Suspected Child Abuse) is DENIED.



 



C.   
The Sixth Cause of Action (IIED)
is limited by the pleadings.



 



As noted above, SCVA asserts that
it had no notice of facts suggesting that Lowe would sexually assault a player.
Therefore, SCVA argues that no reasonable trier of fact could find that SCVA
acted with intent to inflict injury on Plaintiff or that SCVA’s personnel
engaged in conduct that was beyond the bounds of what is usually tolerated in a
civilized community.



 



“A cause of action for intentional
infliction of emotional distress exists when there is ‘(1) extreme and
outrageous conduct by the defendant with the intention of causing, or reckless
disregard of the probability of causing, emotional distress; (2) the
plaintiff’s suffering severe or extreme emotional distress; and (3) actual and
proximate causation of the emotional distress by the defendant’s outrageous
conduct.’ A defendant’s conduct is ‘outrageous’ when it is so ‘extreme as to
exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated in a civilized community.’ And the
defendant’s conduct must be ‘intended to inflict injury or engaged in with the
realization that injury will result.’” (Hughes v. Pair (2009) 46 Cal.4th
1035, 1050-1051.) “[T]he trial court initially determines whether a defendant’s
conduct may reasonably be regarded as so extreme and outrageous as to permit
recovery. Where reasonable men can differ, the jury determines whether the conduct
has been extreme and outrageous to result in liability. Otherwise stated, the
court determines whether severe emotional distress can be found; the jury
determines whether on the evidence it has, in fact, existed.” (Plotnik v.
Meihaus (2012) 208 Cal.App.4th 1590, 1614.) 



 



Plaintiff alleges the following
conduct in connection with the IIED claim:



 



Defendants Jerome A. Lowe; and DOES 1 through 50, inclusive,
and each of them, each exhibited conduct that was done with reckless disregard
for the rights of Plaintiff. Defendant Lowe knew that Plaintiff was a minor,
that she was unable to defend herself, and that Plaintiff relied on Lowe to
protect her from such harmful acts. Defendant Lowe knew that Plaintiff would be
susceptible to his suggestion due to her age and his superior position of power
over Plaintiff.



 



The conduct of Defendant Jerome A. Lowe was intentional and
done for the purpose of causing Plaintiff to suffer from severe mental anguish
and severe and emotional distress.



 



(Complaint ¶¶ 62-63.)



 



“The
function of the pleadings in a motion for summary judgment is to delimit the
scope of the issues; the function of the affidavits or declarations is to
disclose whether there is any triable issue of fact within the issues delimited
by the pleadings.”¿ (Juge¿v. County of Sacramento¿(1993) 12 Cal.App.4th
59, 67 [citing¿FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima¿(1991) 231 Cal. App.
3d 367, 381-382].) As pled, there is no conduct by SCVA to form the basis of
the IIED claim.



 



Because
Plaintiff has not alleged conduct by SCVA in the IIED cause of action, SCVA’s position
that it did not engage in any conduct that could constitute IIED is sufficient
to meet its initial burden. The burden shifts to Plaintiff.



 



Plaintiff
argues that SCVA acted with a reckless disregard when it failed to protect
Plaintiff



from
sexual abuse. But Plaintiff has not made this allegation in the Complaint’s
sixth cause of action. The claim cannot be considered as the issues for the
Motion for Summary Judgment, or in the alternative, Summary Adjudication, are
delimited by the pleadings.



 



For the
reasons explained above, the Court GRANTS the Motion for Summary Adjudication
as to the Sixth Cause of Action.



 



D.   
SCVA fails to meet its initial
burden as to the Seventh Cause of Action (Sexual Harassment under Civ. Code §
51.9 et seq.)



 



SCVA argues that there is no
evidence that it, or any of its agents/employees, engaged in sexual harassment.
SCVA further argues that it had no notice of facts suggesting Lowe would
sexually assault a player prior to any of the alleged instances of abuse
claimed herein.



 



Plaintiff alleges the following
conduct as to the Sexual Harassment claim:



 



Defendant Jerome A. Lowe made sexual advances and/or engaged
in other conduct of a sexual nature or of a hostile nature based on gender that
was unwelcome and pervasive or severe.



 



Defendants DOES 1 through 50, inclusive, and each of them,
knew or should have known of the misconduct and sexually predatory behavior of
Jerome Lowe directed at young children at the school.



 



Despite having knowledge of the sexual harassment of Jerome
Lowe, Defendants DOES 1 through 50, inclusive, and each of them, acted with
deliberate indifference and approved Jerome Lowe’s conduct by failing to take
any action to control, curb, and/or prevent that conduct.



 



(Complaint ¶¶ 70-72.)



 



Unlike the claim for IIED, Plaintiff
alleges in the seventh cause of action that SCVA, initially sued as a Doe
Defendant,: (1) knew or should have known of the misconduct and sexually
predatory behavior of Jerome Lowe directed at young children at the school; and
(2) acted with deliberate indifference and approved Jerome Lowe’s conduct by
failing to take any action to control, curb, and/or prevent that conduct.



 



"[T]he Legislature intended
to conform Civil Code section 51.9 to the California and federal laws
pertaining to sexual harassment in the workplace." (Hughes v. Pair
(2009) 46 Cal.4th 1035, 1048.) As with laws pertaining to sexual harassment in
the workplace, a corporation can be held liable for sexual harassment under
section 51.9. (C.R. v. Tenet Healthcare Corp. (2009) 169 Cal.App.4th
1094, 1110.) "[B]y declaring a policy against sexual harassment in the
workplace, the Legislature has indicated such conduct is foreseeable." (Capitol
City Foods, Inc. v. Superior Court (1992) 5 Cal.App.4th 1042, 1048.)



 



A disputed issue of material act
is present as to the foreseeability of Lowe’s act of sexual misconduct during
his time as a member and coach of SCVA. And, as previously discussed, SCVA had
a duty of care to Plaintiff to take all reasonable steps to prevent such an act
from occurring.



 



Accordingly,
SCVA does not meet its initial burden to negate an element or establish a defense
thereto for the sexual harassment claim. The Motion for Summary Adjudication as
to the Seventh Cause of Action is DENIED.

Southern California Volleyball Association’s Motion for
Summary Judgment is DENIED.

TENTATIVE RULING

HEARING DATE:                 September 10, 2026

 

CASE NUMBER:                   24STCV12819

 

CASE NAME:                        Corella v. Collins

 

MOVING PARTY:                 Defendant Harold Jeff Collins

           

OPPOSING PARTY:             Plaintiff John Corella

PROCEEDING:                      MOTION FOR JUDGMENT ON THE PLEADINGS

RULING SUMMARY:          Defendant’s motion is denied. Plaintiff to give notice.

                                                                                                                                                           

 

Background

 

This action arises from allegations of breach of fiduciary duty in connection with a joint venture to create and develop the television series, “Dance Moms.” On May 21, 2024, plaintiff John Corella (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against defendant Harold Jeff Collins (erroneously sued as Jeffrey Collins) (“Defendant”) and Does 1 to 10, alleging causes of action for (1) breach of fiduciary duty; (2) fraud; (3) conversion; and (4) money had and received.

On October 3, 2024, Plaintiff filed the First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) alleging a single cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty.  On January 21, 2025, the Court sustained the demurrer to the FAC with leave to amend.

On February 3, 2025, Plaintiff filed a Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”) again alleging a cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty.  On April 3, 2025, the court granted Defendant’s motion to strike portions of the SAC with leave to amend.

On April 18, 2025, Plaintiff filed a Third Amended Complaint (“TAC”) alleging the cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty.

On April 27, 2026, Defendant filed the operative motion for judgment on the pleadings. Plaintiff has opposed.

Request for Judicial notice

Defendant asks the Court to take judicial notice of Plaintiff’s opposition to Defendant’s demurrer to the FAC filed in this action on January 7, 2025.  Defendant has not attached the document as an exhibit to the request. The request is granted as to the existence of the opposition and the date of its filing, and not as to the truth of the matters alleged in it. (Evid. Code, § 452, subd. (d).)

motion for judgment on the pleadings[1]

 

I.         Legal Standard

 

“A motion for judgment on the pleadings performs the same function as a general demurrer, and hence attacks only defects disclosed on the face of the pleadings or by matters that can be judicially noticed.”  (Burnett v. Chimney Sweep (2004) 123 Cal.App.4th 1057, 1064.)  “In deciding or reviewing a judgment on the pleadings, all properly pleaded material facts are deemed to be true, as well as all facts that may be implied or inferred from those expressly alleged.”  (Fire Ins. Exchange v. Superior Court (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 446, 452.)  When considering demurrers and judgment on the pleadings, courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (Wilson v. Transit Authority of City of Sacramento (1962) 199 Cal.App.2d 716, 720-21.) A motion for judgment on the pleadings does not lie as to a portion of a cause of action.  (Id.)  “In the case of either a demurrer or a motion for judgment on the pleadings, leave to amend should be granted if there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action.”  (Gami v. Mullikin Medical Ctr. (1993) 18 Cal.App.4th 870, 876.)  A non-statutory motion for judgment on the pleadings may be made any time before or during trial. (Stoops v. Abbassi (2002) 100 Cal.App.4th 644, 650.)¿¿ 

 

II.        Discussion

 

Defendant moves for a judgment on the pleadings based on the statute of frauds. (Civ. Code, § 1624.)  Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s claim is barred because the alleged joint venture agreement continues “in perpetuity” and thus could not have been performed within one year. (Mot., at p.3; TAC ¶¶ 28, 35, 133, 136.)  In addition, there is no written agreement subscribed by Defendant.  (Mot., at p. 3.) Defendant relies on Clarke v. Yu (2026) 119 Cal.App.5th 199, 206-208, where the Court of Appeal held that “a claim for breach of fiduciary duty arising out of an oral or implied joint venture agreement that by its terms cannot be performed within a year is barred by the statute of frauds and must be dismissed.”

Relying on White Lighting Co. v. Wolfson (1968) 68 Cal.2d 336, 343-345, and Foley v. Interactive Data Corp. (1988) 47 Cal.3d 654, 672-675, Plaintiff argues in opposition that Civil Code section 1624, subdivision (a)(1) does not apply here because the alleged acts of creating, developing, and exploiting the television series were capable of being performed within one year. (Opp., at pp. 6-8.) Plaintiff argues that the holding in Clarke, the case cited by Defendant, is narrow in scope, applying only “where the agreed objective itself necessarily requires more than one year to accomplish,” and that, because Clarke was resolved on summary judgment, it is inapposite.  (Opp., at p.7.)  Lastly, Plaintiff contends that his full performance independently defeats Defendant’s statute of frauds defense. (Opp., at pp. 11-12.)

In Clarke, the Court of Appeal held that “oral and implied joint ventures are subject to the statute of frauds if the agreement, by its terms, cannot be performed within a year from its making.” (Clarke, supra, 119 Cal.App.5th at p.207.) Affirming the trial court’s order granting summary judgment to defendants, the appellate court concluded that the joint venture agreement at issue in that case was barred by the statute of frauds because the Court “found no genuine disputed issue of material fact exists as to whether this alleged joint venture agreement could, by its terms, be performed within one year because it would take longer than a year to develop the C-H activation technology underlying CHange.” (Ibid.) The submitted evidence showed that the technology could not be developed within one year. (Id., at p. 208.)

The question, here, turns on whether the joint venture’s terms contemplated performance within one year. The answer to this inquiry is a question of fact that cannot be discerned form the face of the TAC.  Plaintiff acknowledges his “consistent position that the Attachment Agreement did not create or fully define the Joint Venture.” (Opp., at p. 10.) Thus, the inquiry turns to the oral agreement that preceded the Attachment Agreement.

Plaintiff’s allegations as to the oral joint venture agreement are minimal. As alleged in the TAC, the joint venture was created on August 10, 2010 “for the development of entertainment product about competitive dance kids and their families. The joint venture created and developed the series and Franchise entitled ‘Dance Moms.’” (TAC ¶ 12, 136.) Plaintiff argues in opposition to the MJOP that “none of the acts constituting the alleged purpose of the Joint Venture necessarily required performance extending beyond one year . . . .”  (Opp., at p. 7.)  From the face of the TAC, it is unclear whether the acts to accomplish the purpose of the Joint Venture could be performed within one year. Plaintiff alleges that Defendant “was the only one of the three joint venturers who had any relevant experience in television entertainment production specifically in reality television.” (TAC ¶ 10.) Plaintiff acknowledges “that the oral Joint Venture contains no express duration exceeding one year,” but the TAC also fails to allege any facts supporting the contention that the agreement could be performed within one year. (Opp., at p. 10.) Plaintiff fails to allege the agreement’s terms or to offer insight into the contemplated duration of contractual performance.

As the moving party, Defendant has the burden to establish the bar on the face of the pleadings, and any uncertainty weighs in favor of Plaintiff.  Defendant’s references to Plaintiff’s opposition to a previous demurrer are not persuasive because Plaintiff states that the Attachment Agreement, not the Joint Venture, defines the rights “in perpetuity.” (Opp. to Demurrer, at p. 4:20-21.)  Defendant fails to sustain his burden to establish that the statute of frauds bars Plaintiff’s claims as a matter of law.

Plaintiff argues in the alternative that even if the Court were to conclude that the statute of frauds applies, his full performance independently overcomes the defense. (Opp., at pp. 11-12.)

The Court finds that Plaintiff sufficiently alleges in the TAC that he fully performed his obligations pursuant to the agreement. In Zakk v. Diesel (2019) 33 Cal.App.5th 431, 454, the Court of Appeal found that Plaintiff’s allegations that he fully performed all of his obligations under the contract successfully took the contract out of the statute of frauds at the demurrer stage.

Plaintiff, here, alleges that he is a professional dancer who “had the access to the kids and families who would be the stars of the show” and “also acted as the de facto talent scout for the families and dancers, assessing their talent, personalities, and potential for the project. He was the Dance Guy of what would become Dance Moms.” (TAC ¶ 9.) The series “began production in or about April of 2011 and began airing soon thereafter on the ‘Lifetime network.’” (TAC ¶ 40.)  Under the joint venture, Defendant was “to pay the individual members” through his control of Collins Ave. (TAC ¶ 26.) Between 2013-2019, while the series originally aired, payments were made by Defendant through his entities. (TAC ¶ 64.) In January 2020, Collins Ave shut down and sold “Dance Moms” to Asylum Entertainment Group. (TAC ¶ 81.)  Per the TAC, Defendant was solely responsible for accounting and payment. (TAC ¶¶ 68-69 [“Defendant told Plaintiff he had no right to such records or accountings as it was the agreement between the parties that Defendant Collins would be the sole person to access those records and documents. Plaintiff made no such agreement with his partners in the Joint Venture and did not waive his right to accountings of transactions or financial matters relating to the Joint Venture.”].) Plaintiff’s allegations that he fully performed his obligations pursuant to the agreement precludes a finding that the statute of frauds bars his claims at the pleading stage. 

Conclusion

 

Defendant’s motion for judgment on the pleadings is denied.

Case Number: 25STCV08532    Hearing Date: September 10, 2026    Dept: 413

TENTATIVE RULING

HEARING DATE:                 September 10, 2026

 

CASE NUMBER:                   25STCV08532

CASE NAME:                        Solorio, et al. v. General Motors, LLC

 

MOVING PARTY:                 Defendant General Motors, LLC

OPPOSING PARTY:             Plaintiffs Arturo Zavala Solorio, Silvia Becerra Zavala, and Sylvia Zavala

PROCEEDING:                      COMPLIANCE PURSUANT TO C.C.P. § 871.26

RULING SUMMARY:          Defendant’s motion for compliance pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26 is GRANTED in part.  The depositions of Plaintiffs that have not yet been taken and completed shall be scheduled for a date available to Defendant’s counsel within 30 days of this Ruling.  The court imposes sanctions of $1,500.00 on Plaintiffs’ counsel. Defendant to give notice.  Plaintiffs’ counsel shall pay the sanctions to Defendant’s counsel by September 21, 2026.  

                                                                                               

 

Background

 

This is a Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act case. Plaintiffs Arturo Zavala Solorio, Silvia Becerra Zavala, and Sylvia Zavala (collectively, “Plaintiffs”) allege that their 2024 Chevrolet Silverado 1500 suffers from defects and nonconformities to warranty, including but not limited to transmission, engine, and electrical defects.

 

On March 24, 2025, Plaintiffs filed a complaint against defendant General Motors, LLC (“Defendant”) and Does 1 through 10, alleging causes of action for (1) violation of subdivision (d) of Civil Code section 1793.2; (2) violation of subdivision (b) of Civil Code section 1793.2; (3) violation of subdivision (a)(3) of Civil Code section 1793.2; (4) breach of the implied warranty of merchantability; and (5) fraudulent inducement – concealment.

 

On June 26, 2026, Defendant filed the motion for compliance pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26 and a request for sanctions. Plaintiffs oppose.

 

compliance pursuant to code of civil procedure section 871.26

 

I.         Legal Standard

 

Effective January 1, 2025, in any action seeking restitution or replacement of a motor vehicle under section 871.20, the parties must follow these rules: within 120 days after an answer or other responsive pleading is filed, each party shall have the right to conduct initial depositions of the plaintiff. (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26, subdivision (c)(1).)¿The Court must impose sanctions for failure to comply with section 871.26, absent good cause. (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26, subd. (j).)

 

The California Legislature enacted section 871.20, et seq., which added requirements for certain lemon law actions. (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.20, subd. (a) [“Notwithstanding any other law, this chapter applies to an action, brought against a manufacturer who has elected under Section 871.29 to proceed under this chapter, seeking restitution or replacement of a motor vehicle pursuant to subdivision (b) or (d) of Section 1793.2, Section 1793.22, or Section 1794 of the Civil Code, or for civil penalties pursuant to subdivision (c) of Section 1794 of the Civil Code, where the request for restitution or replacement is based on noncompliance with the applicable express warranty”]; subd. (b) [“This chapter does not apply to service contract claims under Section 1794 of the Civil Code or any action seeking remedies that are not restitution or replacement of a motor vehicle”].)  

 

II.        Discussion

 

A.    Compel Deposition

Defendant moves for an order compelling Plaintiffs to appear for initial deposition, as required under Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26, subdivision (c)(1).  Section 871.26, subdivision (c)(1), entitles Defendant to conduct an initial deposition of Plaintiffs within 120 days after filing any responsive pleading. Defendant filed a demurrer on July 17, 2025.  Therefore, Defendant was entitled to request and conduct Plaintiffs’ initial deposition at any point prior to November 14, 2025.

Defendant sent a meet and confer letter on July 2, 2025, to Plaintiffs’ attorney, outlining that this action is subject to Assembly Bill 1755’s provisions and indicating that Defendant was issuing a notice for Plaintiffs to appear for deposition. (Stark Decl., ¶ 4, Ex. A.)  On July 17, 2025, Defendant emailed a Notice of Deposition (“Notice”) for Plaintiffs to appear for deposition on August 13, 2025. (Id., ¶ 5, Ex. B.) On August 6, Plaintiffs’ counsel objected to the Notice on the grounds that the deposition was unilaterally noticed, but indicated a willingness to meet and confer to reschedule for a mutually convenient date. (Id., ¶ 6, Ex. C.) Defendant sent follow-up emails requesting availability in November 2025 and February 2026 but did not receive a response. (Id., ¶¶ 7-8, Exs. D-E.)

Plaintiffs argue that the motion is moot because since the motion’s filing, the parties met and conferred and Defendant served an Amended Notice of Deposition setting Plaintiff Arturo Zavala Solorio’s deposition for August 12, 2026. (Opp., at p. 1; Smith Decl., ¶¶ 6-7.) The deposition was conducted. (Id.)

While Mr. Solorio’s deposition has been taken, and Plaintiffs’ counsel states that the remaining two Plaintiffs are ready and willing to complete their depositions, their availability has not been determined, and the parties have not set a deposition as of the opposition’s filing. (Smith Decl., ¶ 7.) Plaintiffs also offer no explanation for their failure to proactively offer dates following the initial objection or to reply to Defendant’s November 2025 and February 2026 emails requesting dates.

Defendant made diligent attempts to complete Plaintiffs’ initial deposition during the 120-day period by serving the Notice and attempting to engage in meet and confer efforts. Plaintiffs failed to make diligent efforts to comply with section 871.26 requirements and produce Plaintiffs for deposition.

As Silvia Becerra Zavala’s and Sylvia Zavala’s depositions have still yet to occur, despite Defendant’s efforts to exercise their discovery rights, the Court grants the motion to compel Silvia Becerra Zavala’s and Sylvia Zavala’s initial depositions. 

B.    Sanctions

Defendant requests sanctions of $1,500.00 against Plaintiffs’ counsel, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26, subdivision (j)(2). Section 871.26, subdivision (j)(2), provides that,  unless the party failing to comply shows good cause, the court shall impose $1,500.00 in sanctions upon plaintiff’s counsel for failure to comply with deposition requirements.

Plaintiffs argue that sanctions should not be imposed because they have shown good cause:  According to Plaintiffs,“[a] party who objects to a unilaterally chosen date, offers to reschedule, and then agrees on a date has not engaged in the kind of unjustified noncompliance the statute is designed to deter.” (Opp., at p.4.)

The Court finds that no evidence or explanation is provided as to why Plaintiffs’ counsel failed to provide any dates until after this motion was filed, more than a year after the initial time period, despite Defendant's attempts to meet and confer. Even in the email exchange solidifying Mr. Solorio’s deposition, Plaintiffs’ counsel still did not offer dates and times for Sylvia Becerra Zavala. (Smith Decl., Ex. A.)

Plaintiffs’ counsel has not shown good cause for failure to comply. Accordingly, the Court imposes sanctions of $1,500.00 on Plaintiffs’ counsel.

The Court denies Plaintiffs’ request for sanctions under Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.010, subdivision (h), because the court granted Defendant’s motion in part.

Conclusion

 

Defendant’s motion for compliance pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26 is GRANTED in part.  The depositions of Plaintiffs that have not yet been taken and completed shall be scheduled for a date available to Defendant’s counsel within 30 days of this Ruling.  The court imposes sanctions of $1,500.00 on Plaintiffs’ counsel. Defendant to give notice.  Plaintiffs’ counsel shall pay the sanctions to Defendant’s counsel by September 21, 2026.
