BACKGROUND

On January 5, 2026, the Court granted Plaintiff leave to file a First Amended Complaint. Plaintiff filed the First Amended Complaint (FAC) on January 6, 2026. LEGAL STANDARD

 

“[A] demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the allegations in a complaint.” (Lewis v. Safeway, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 385, 388.) A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. (See Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) For purposes of ruling on a demurrer, all facts pleaded in a complaint are assumed to be true, but the Court does not assume the truth of conclusions of law. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 967.)

 

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

 

Defendant’s Request for Judicial Notice of Exhibits A through I is granted. The Court takes notice of the filing and contents of the government claim and court records, Plaintiff’s prior deposition testimony and interrogatory responses for the limited purpose of evaluating the asserted inconsistency with the FAC, and the geographic information reflected in the maps. The Court does not take judicial notice of the truth of disputed factual assertions contained in those materials. (Gong v. City of Rosemead (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 363, 368, fn. 1; Del E. Webb Corp. v. Structural Materials Co. (1981) 123 Cal.App.3d 593, 604-605; Evid. Code, § 452, subds. (c), (d), (h).)

 

DISCUSSION

 

Government Claims Act

 

Defendant demurs to the first cause of action under Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (e), arguing that it fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action because the FAC alleges a different accident location than the location identified in Plaintiff’s government claim. Defendant contends that the discrepancy means Plaintiff did not substantially comply with Government Code sections 910 and 945.4. Specifically, Defendant argues that “a pothole at one intersection is a materially different dangerous condition than a pothole at another intersection, requiring different maintenance records, different notice analysis, and different causation proof.” (Reply, p. 2.) Defendant further argues that the incorrect location deprived it of an opportunity to investigate the claim. (Demurrer, p. 15.)

 

Government Code section 910, subdivision (c), requires a claim to state “[t]he date, place and other circumstances of the occurrence or transaction which gave rise to the claim asserted.” The purpose of the claim statutes is “to provide the public entity sufficient information to enable it to adequately investigate claims and to settle them, if appropriate, without the expense of litigation.” (Stockett v. Association of California Water Agencies Joint Powers Insurance Authority (2004) 34 Cal.4th 441, 446.) A claim substantially complies when it contains sufficient information to permit that investigation. (Johnson v. City of Oakland (1961) 188 Cal.App.2d 181, 183.) “When it falls short of that test, it is insufficient.” (Ibid.) Because the purpose is notice sufficient to investigate and evaluate the claim, not elimination of meritorious actions, the claims statute should not be applied to “snare the unwary where its purpose has been satisfied.” (Stockett, supra, 34 Cal.4th at p. 446.)

 

“California courts have taken a liberal view of claims statutes where a reasonable attempt has been made to comply with the law in good faith.” (Johnson v. City of Oakland, supra, 188 Cal.App.2d at p. 183.) That approach is reflected in a line of cases holding that an erroneous accident location did not defeat substantial compliance with the claim-presentation requirements. In Johnson v. City of Los Angeles (1955) 134 Cal.App.2d 600, 601-604, the claim identified the southeast corner of an intersection instead of the southwest corner. In Parodi v. City and County of San Francisco (1958) 160 Cal.App.2d 577, 581-584, the Court applied that approach more broadly where the claim identified an intersection approximately 11 blocks from the location described at trial and nevertheless found substantial compliance under the circumstances. In Johnson v. City of Oakland, supra, 188 Cal.App.2d at pages 182-184, the claim identified 1819 35th Avenue instead of 1819 34th Avenue. In Mace v. City of Pasadena (1962) 199 Cal.App.2d 522, 523-526, the claim identified the wrong intersecting street approximately one block away. In Rowan v. City and County of San Francisco (1966) 244 Cal.App.2d 308, 312-313, the claim identified 3350 Scott Street instead of 3358-3360 Scott Street.

 

Hall v. City of Los Angeles (1941) 19 Cal.2d 198 held that the claim failed to sufficiently designate the place of the accident. (Id. at pp. 202-203.) Johnson v. City of Los Angeles later characterized Hall as a case in which the designation was “so general and indefinite that there was no semblance of compliance with the statute.” (Johnson v. City of Los Angeles, supra, 134 Cal.App.2d at p. 603.) By contrast, Johnson held that an erroneous designation of the southeast rather than southwest corner of an intersection did not defeat substantial compliance. (Id. at pp. 603-604.)

 

In Hernandez v. City of Stockton (2023) 90 Cal.App.5th 1222, 1227, the government claim specifically identified an “uplifted sidewalk” as the dangerous condition causing the plaintiff’s fall. The plaintiff later disclosed in deposition that he instead fell into a hole created by an empty tree well. (Id. at p. 1228.) The Court held that the empty tree well did not further describe or clarify the alleged uplifted sidewalk, but was an “entirely different description.” (Id. at p. 1234.)

 

Here, the FAC alleges that Plaintiff timely submitted government claims with the public entity defendant. (FAC ¶ 21.) The FAC alleges that on September 12, 2022, at approximately 3:50 a.m., Plaintiff was riding his electric bicycle eastbound on Cole Avenue, made a slight left turn onto Cahuenga Boulevard, and thereafter approached De Longpre Avenue and Cahuenga Boulevard, where his bicycle made contact with a pothole near 6408 De Longpre Avenue, causing him to fall and sustain injuries. (FAC ¶ 6.) The government claim states that Plaintiff was traveling eastbound on Fountain Avenue toward Cahuenga Boulevard and that, as he reached the intersection to make a left turn onto Cahuenga Boulevard, his bicycle “got caught on a pothole,” causing him to lose control and sustain injuries. (RJN, Ex. A at 1.) The claim identifies the location as Cahuenga Boulevard and Fountain Avenue. (Ibid.) In response to the claim form’s question asking what act or omission caused the injury, Plaintiff stated, “Multiple potholes.” (Ibid.) As compared with the government claim, the FAC therefore changes the approach street from Fountain Avenue to Cole Avenue and the alleged accident location from Fountain Avenue and Cahuenga Boulevard to near De Longpre Avenue and Cahuenga Boulevard. Exhibit H reflects a distance of approximately 700 feet between 6408 De Longpre Avenue and Cahuenga Boulevard and Fountain Avenue. (RJN, Ex. H.)

 

The Court finds substantial compliance. The government claim was not so general or indefinite as to provide “no semblance of compliance” with the place requirement. (Johnson v. City of Los Angeles, supra, 134 Cal.App.2d at p. 603.) It identified Cahuenga Boulevard and Fountain Avenue and described the occurrence as Plaintiff traveling eastbound on Fountain Avenue, turning left onto Cahuenga Boulevard, and striking a pothole at approximately 3:50 a.m. on September 12, 2022. (RJN, Ex. A at 1.) Although the FAC places the accident near 6408 De Longpre Avenue, approximately 700 feet from Cahuenga Boulevard and Fountain Avenue, Johnson, Parodi, Mace, and Rowan establish that an erroneous location does not necessarily defeat substantial compliance where the claim otherwise sufficiently identifies the occurrence for investigation. Hernandez is distinguishable because the alleged dangerous condition there changed. Here, both the government claim and the FAC identify a pothole as the condition that caused the accident. The discrepancy concerns its location.

 

Accordingly, the claim substantially complied with Government Code section 910. The demurrer is overruled on this ground.

 

Sham Pleading

 

Defendant separately argues that the FAC is a sham pleading because Plaintiff identified Fountain Avenue and Cahuenga Boulevard as the accident location in his government claim, original Complaint, discovery responses, and deposition testimony, but changed the location after Defendant moved for summary judgment. Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s repeated prior statements and the timing of the amendment demonstrate that the change was made to avoid summary judgment rather than to correct a mistake. (Demurrer, pp. 15-17; Reply, pp. 9-10.)

 

Under the sham pleading doctrine, a plaintiff may not amend a complaint to omit harmful allegations, without explanation, to avoid a demurrer or motion for summary judgment. (Deveny v. Entropin, Inc. (2006) 139 Cal.App.4th 408, 425-426.) The doctrine likewise permits a Court to disregard facts added in an amended complaint that are inconsistent with prior pleadings unless the inconsistency is satisfactorily explained. (Ibid.) The doctrine is not intended to prevent correction of erroneous allegations, but to prevent abuse of process. (Id. at p. 426.) In Deveny, the Court declined to apply the doctrine where the plaintiffs offered a plausible explanation for the amendment and demonstrated that the original pleading resulted from mistake. (Id. at pp. 426-427.)

 

Here, the original Complaint alleged that the accident occurred at Fountain Avenue and Cahuenga Boulevard. (RJN, Ex. B ¶ 6.) Defendant thereafter moved for summary judgment, arguing that GPS data showed Plaintiff was never at that location. (RJN, Ex. I, pp. 2-3.) The FAC now alleges that the accident occurred near De Longpre Avenue and Cahuenga Boulevard. (FAC ¶ 6.) Plaintiff previously explained that his identification of Fountain Avenue and Cahuenga Boulevard was based on his recollection while injured and disoriented in the dark and that his recollection was later refreshed after reviewing Lime GPS data produced by Defendant. (Jan. 5, 2026 Minute Order, p. 6.) The Court considered Defendant’s sham pleading objection and found that explanation “plausible enough to allow the trier of fact to resolve the conflict, rather than barring the amendment at the pleading stage.” (Ibid.)

 

Defendant argues that the explanation is not satisfactory because Plaintiff repeatedly identified Fountain Avenue and Cahuenga Boulevard and did not change the location until after Defendant moved for summary judgment. (Reply, pp. 9-10.) Those circumstances bear on the credibility of Plaintiff’s explanation, but they do not establish on demurrer that the explanation is a sham. Resolving whether Plaintiff’s explanation is true would require the Court to weigh disputed facts, which it may not do on demurrer. (Del E. Webb Corp. v. Structural Materials Co., supra, 123 Cal.App.3d at pp. 604-605.)

 

The Court therefore finds that the inconsistency has been satisfactorily explained for purposes of the sham pleading doctrine and that the FAC is not a sham pleading. The demurrer is overruled on this ground.

 

CONCLUSION

 

Defendant City of Los Angeles’s Demurrer to the First Amended Complaint is overruled. Defendant shall answer or otherwise plead to the First Amended Complaint within 10 days. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1320(j)(1).

DISCUSSION

Motions for Protective Order

 

When specially prepared interrogatories or requests for admission exceed 35 and the responding party challenges the number by protective order, the propounding party bears the burden of justifying the additional requests. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.040, subd. (b), 2033.040, subd. (b).) There is no corresponding numerical limit on Requests for Production. The Court shall restrict the frequency or extent of discovery if it determines that the discovery is unreasonably cumulative or duplicative or that the selected method is unduly burdensome or expensive. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2019.030, subd. (a).) For good cause shown, the Court may reduce the number of interrogatories or admission requests, excuse production of particular items or categories, and extend the time to respond. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.090, subd. (b), 2031.060, subd. (b), 2033.080, subd. (b).)

 

Plaintiff served Defendant Shauly with Discovery Set Two consisting of 382 Special Interrogatories, 269 Requests for Production, and 280 Requests for Admission. (Do Decl., ¶ 4.) Plaintiff served Defendant Alon West Hills with Discovery Set Two consisting of 382 Special Interrogatories, 269 Requests for Production, and 127 Requests for Admission. (Spivey Decl., ¶¶ 3-6.)

 

The quantity and complexity of the issues warrant discovery beyond the statutory limit of 35. Plaintiff has not, however, justified the number of Special Interrogatories and Requests for Admission actually served. The record also reflects substantial overlap among the Requests for Production and between the present and prior discovery. The Court previously afforded the parties an additional opportunity to narrow the requests. Plaintiff continued to maintain all of the discovery directed to Shauly. (Joint Statement, pp. 3-4, 7.) Plaintiff likewise maintained all discovery directed to Alon West Hills. (Status Report, p. 2.)

 

The Motions are therefore granted in part. As to Defendant Shauly, Plaintiff may pursue 150 Special Interrogatories, 100 Requests for Admission, and 100 Requests for Production from Discovery Set Two. As to Defendant Alon West Hills, Plaintiff may separately pursue 150 Special Interrogatories, 100 Requests for Admission, and 100 Requests for Production from Discovery Set Two.

 

Within 30 days, Plaintiff shall identify by number a separate selection for each Defendant consisting of the 150 Special Interrogatories, 100 Requests for Admission, and 100 Requests for Production she elects to pursue from that Defendant’s Discovery Set Two. Plaintiff may not substitute new requests or revise their wording.

 

Shauly and Alon West Hills shall each serve code-compliant responses within 60 days after service of Plaintiff’s respective selection and shall concurrently produce all responsive documents required by their responses to the selected Requests for Production. The 60-day period is warranted by the volume of discovery that remains after reduction, the document collection and review required by the selected production demands, and the August 16, 2027 trial date. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.090, subd. (b)(3), 2031.060, subd. (b)(2), 2033.080, subd. (b)(3).)

 

Shauly and Alon West Hills filed their Motions before responses were due. The Court thereafter ordered that they were not required to respond to the March 13, 2026 discovery pending resolution of the Motions. (6/29/26 Minute Order.) Their objections therefore are not waived and may be asserted where otherwise proper.

 

In light of the mixed result and the parties’ substantial justification for their respective positions concerning the permissible scope of discovery, the Court finds monetary sanctions unwarranted. The requests for sanctions relating to the protective-order Motions are denied.

 

Plaintiff’s Discovery Motions Against Defendants Iris Shapiro, Alon Investments, LLC, and Walter Floridia

 

1.      Electronic Service

 

Defendants contend Plaintiff’s March 13, 2026 discovery was not effectively served because Plaintiff electronically transmitted it to Natalya Samsonova, who had left Lewis Brisbois. The January 12, 2026 Answer identified Michael J. Early and Timothy R. Windham as counsel and listed their Lewis Brisbois email addresses. (Answer, p. 1.) The Court’s March 4, 2026 electronic service certificate, however, continued to identify both Early and Samsonova as electronic service recipients for Lewis Brisbois. (3/4/26 Clerk’s Certificate of Service.)

 

Early declares that Samsonova had left the firm and that an automatic reply to her email address stated, “This Email is NOT being monitored and has not been delivered to Natalya Samsonova, who is no longer with the firm.” (Early Decl. filed 6/29/26, ¶ 8.) Plaintiff’s service records reflect transmission of the March 13 discovery to Samsonova’s Lewis Brisbois address. (5/8/26 Mots., Proofs of Service and Exs.)

 

A party required to accept electronic service must provide an electronic service address and promptly notify all parties and the Court of any change to that address. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.251(c)(4), (g)(1).) The record does not show that Lewis Brisbois filed and served notice removing or changing Samsonova’s electronic service address before March 13, 2026. The Court itself continued to use that address for electronic service while also serving Early.

 

On this record, the Court finds Plaintiff’s March 13, 2026 electronic service effective. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1010.6; Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.251.) The same conclusion applies to Plaintiff’s May 11, 2026 service of Form Interrogatories.

 

Defendants did not timely respond. Their objections were therefore waived. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.290, subd. (a), 2031.300, subd. (a), 2033.280, subd. (a).) Defendants later sought to join Shauly’s and Alon West Hills’ protective-order Motions, but the Court denied that request without prejudice on June 29, 2026. (6/29/26 Minute Order.) Although Defendants thereafter participated in further meet-and-confer efforts, Shapiro, Alon Investments, and Floridia did not separately notice and file motions seeking relief from the statutory waiver or protective relief. No order granting such relief has been entered.

 

 

2.      Motions to Deem Requests for Admission Admitted

 

Defendants’ September 3, 2026 Opposition asserts that they served verified discovery responses and identifies the dates of service, but Defendants did not submit the responses themselves with the Opposition. (Early Decl. filed 9/3/26, ¶ 7.) Plaintiff submitted copies of Defendants’ responses with her September 8, 2026 Reply. Because Defendants rely on those responses in arguing that the pending discovery Motions are moot, the Court considers the responses submitted with Plaintiff’s Reply in determining whether they substantially comply with the governing discovery statutes.

 

If a party fails to timely respond to Requests for Admission, the Court shall deem the matters admitted unless, before the hearing, the responding party serves a proposed response substantially complying with Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.220. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (c).)

 

Substantial compliance is determined from the proposed response as a whole. (St. Mary v. Superior Court (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 762, 779-782.) Waived objections do not necessarily prevent substantial compliance when the response otherwise contains substantive answers. (Katayama v. Continental Investment Group (2024) 105 Cal.App.5th 898, 908-910.) A proposed response containing waived objections but no substantive answers to the matters at issue may fail that standard. (Id. at p. 909, fn. 14.)

 

Here, Alon Investments served a response captioned as Requests for Admission, Set One, and identifying Set No. One, although its introductory paragraph and internal heading refer to “Set Three.” (Alon Investments Resp. to RFAs, pp. 1, 3.) The response provides substantive answers to Requests Nos. 1 through 35 and then asserts a single numerical objection to purported Requests Nos. 36 through 310. (Id. at p. 9.) Plaintiff’s Requests for Admission, Set One, however, end at Request No. 308. Because Alon Investments’ numerical objection was waived, the response provides no substantive answer to Requests Nos. 36 through 308. Considered as a whole, the proposed response does not substantially comply with section 2033.220.

 

Floridia served a response captioned as Requests for Admission, Set One, and identifying Set No. One, although its introductory paragraph and internal heading refer to “Set Three.” (Floridia Resp. to RFAs, pp. 1, 3.) The response provides substantive answers to Requests Nos. 1 through 35 and then asserts a single numerical objection to Requests Nos. 36 through 243. (Id. at p. 9.) Because that objection was waived, the response provides no substantive answer to Requests Nos. 36 through 243. Considered as a whole, Floridia’s proposed response does not substantially comply with section 2033.220.

 

Shapiro’s response is captioned as Requests for Admission, Set Three, and renumbers the requests beginning with Request No. 173. The substance of the requests, however, corresponds to the Requests for Admission, Set Two, at issue in Plaintiff’s Motion. Shapiro provides substantive answers to renumbered Requests Nos. 173 through 208, corresponding to Set Two, Nos. 1 through 36. She then asserts a single numerical objection to renumbered Requests Nos. 209 through 342, corresponding to Set Two, Nos. 37 through 170. (Shapiro Resp. to RFAs, pp. 3-9.) Shapiro provides no answer to Set Two, Nos. 171 and 172. Because the numerical objection was waived, the response provides no substantive answer to Plaintiff’s Set Two, Nos. 37 through 172. Considered as a whole, Shapiro’s proposed response does not substantially comply with section 2033.220.

 

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s Motions to Deem Requests for Admission Admitted as to Alon Investments, Floridia, and Shapiro are granted. The matters specified in the Requests for Admission at issue in each Motion are deemed admitted. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (c).) This ruling is without prejudice to any properly noticed motion under Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.300 to withdraw or amend the admissions.

 

3.      Motions to Compel Interrogatory and Production Responses

 

As noted above, the actual responses were submitted by Plaintiff with her Reply. Those responses show that Shapiro, Alon Investments, and Floridia continued to assert objections notwithstanding the statutory waiver.

                         

A party that fails to timely respond to interrogatories waives all objections, including objections based on privilege and work product, unless relief from waiver is obtained. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (a).) The same rule applies to inspection demands. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300, subd. (a).)

 

The subsequent service of untimely responses does not deprive the Court of authority to decide a pending motion to compel initial responses. (Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pacific Healthcare Consultants (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, 407-409.)

 

Shapiro, Alon Investments, and Floridia subsequently served responses while continuing to assert objections. (Early Decl. filed 9/3/26, ¶ 7.) Those objections were waived. Unlike Shauly and Alon West Hills, these Defendants did not obtain protective relief limiting the discovery directed to them. (6/29/26 Minute Order.) The Court therefore does not impose through these Motions the numerical limitations ordered on the separately noticed protective-order Motions.

The untimely service of responses, however, did not restore objections that had already been waived. (Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pacific Healthcare Consultants (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, 407-409.) No order relieving Shapiro from waiver has been entered.

4.      Sanctions

 

Plaintiff’s requests for issue and evidence sanctions are denied. Code of Civil Procedure sections 2030.290, subdivision (c) and 2031.300, subdivision (c) authorize nonmonetary sanctions upon a subsequent failure to obey an order compelling responses. No such prior order preceded the discovery defaults addressed by these Motions. The Court may not use the general sanctions provisions to override limitations prescribed by the applicable method-specific provisions. (City of Los Angeles v. PricewaterhouseCoopers, LLP (2024) 17 Cal.5th 46, 75.)

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.280, subdivision (c) makes monetary sanctions mandatory against the party or attorney whose failure to serve timely responses necessitated the motion. Plaintiff's requested amounts, however, are calculated entirely from the value of her own legal work as a self-represented litigant. (See, e.g., 5/8/26 Alon Investments Mot., pp. 25-26.) A self-represented litigant may not recover attorney fees for her own legal work. (Kravitz v. Superior Court (2001) 91 Cal.App.4th 1015, 1020.) Plaintiff has not identified any separate recoverable expense incurred as a result of the discovery violations. Plaintiff’s monetary-sanction request on the Combined Motion to Compel Special Interrogatories and Requests for Production is likewise based on the value of her own legal work. Plaintiff identifies no separate recoverable expense incurred in connection with that Motion. Accordingly, there is no recoverable monetary amount to award on the showing made. Accordingly, there is no recoverable monetary amount to award on the showing made.
