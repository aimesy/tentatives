MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT

 

Background

 

            Plaintiff is the owner of real property known as 13060 Discovery Creek, Playa Vista, CA 90094 (the “Property”).  Plaintiff alleges Defendant Icon Community Association (the “HOA”) is the homeowner’s association responsible for making repairs to common areas in the community where the Property is located.  Plaintiff alleges he has reported multiple violations of the Covenants Conditions and Restrictions (“CC&Rs”) that govern the community, including improper use of the fire lanes.  Plaintiff alleges that under Article XII, Section 12.1.6 of the CC&Rs, any owner may enforce the CC&Rs and has the right of action against the HOA for failing to comply with the CC&Rs.

 

            On May 23, 2025, Plaintiff filed a complaint against the HOA alleging (1) breach of the CC&Rs; and (2) private nuisance. 

 

            On April 6, 2026, Defendant HOA filed the instant Motion for Summary Judgment.  On June 10, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition to the Motion.  On June 18, 2026, Defendant filed a reply.

 

Motion for Summary Judgment Standard

 

            The purpose of a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication “is to provide courts with a mechanism to cut through the parties’ pleadings in order to determine whether, despite their allegations, trial is in fact necessary to resolve their dispute.”  (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.)  “Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (c), requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.”  (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.)

 

            “On a motion for summary judgment, the initial burden is always on the moving party to make a prima facie showing that there are no triable issues of material fact.”  (Scalf v. D.B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1519.)  A defendant or cross-defendant moving for summary judgment or summary adjudication “has met his or her burden of showing that a cause of action has no merit if the party has shown that one or more elements of the cause of action . . . cannot be established, or that there is a complete defense to the cause of action.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).)  “Once the defendant or cross-defendant has met that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff or cross-complainant to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.”  (Code of Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).)  “If the plaintiff cannot do so, summary judgment should be granted.”  (Avivi v. Centro Medico Urgente Medical Center (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 463, 467.) 

 

            “When deciding whether to grant summary judgment, the court must consider all of the evidence set forth in the papers (except evidence to which the court has sustained an objection), as well as all reasonable inferences that may be drawn from that evidence, in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment.”  (Id. at p. 467; Code of Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c).)

 

Analysis

 

            Defendant’s Separate Statement complies with California Rules of Court Rule 3.1350

 

            Plaintiff argues that Defendant’s Separate Statement fails to segregate material facts by cause of action as required under Rule 3.1350 subdivision (b), which applies to motions for summary adjudication.  However, Defendant is not moving for summary adjudication.  Defendant is moving for summary judgment.  Defendant is not required to comply with Rule 3.1350 subdivision (b). Plaintiff’s objection to Defendant’s Separate Statement is overruled.

 

            Defendant’s argument that Plaintiff’s Responsive Separate Statement is defective is also overruled.  Defendant argues Plaintiff improperly uses the Responsive Separate Statement as an opportunity to make additional legal arguments rather than disputing the material fact and setting forth supporting evidence.  Plaintiff’s Responsive Separate Statement complies with Rule 3.1350.  The insertion of argument does not render it procedurally improper.  Plaintiff’s states whether a fact is disputed or undisputed and why. Defendant’s objection is overruled. 

           

First Cause of Action for Breach of CC&Rs

 

            “A cause of action for damages for breach of contract is comprised of the following elements: (1) the contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance, (3) defendant’s breach, and (4) the resulting damages to plaintiff.”  (Daniels v. Select Portfolio Servicing, Inc. (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 1150, 1173.)

 

“Where a duly constituted community association board, upon reasonable investigation, in good faith and with regard for the best interests of the community association and its members, exercises discretion within the scope of its authority under relevant statutes, covenants and restrictions to select among means for discharging an obligation to maintain and repair a development's common areas, courts should defer to the board's authority and presumed expertise. Thus, we adopt today for California courts a rule of judicial deference to community association board decisionmaking that applies, regardless of an association's corporate status, when owners in common interest developments seek to litigate ordinary maintenance decisions entrusted to the discretion of their associations' boards of directors.”  (Lamden v. La Jolla Shores Clubdominium Homeowners Assn. (1999) 21 Cal.4th 249, 253 (board’s decision to use secondary, rather than primary, treatment in addressing the development’s termite problem was subject to deferential review, reversing Court of Appeals’ reversal of judgment in HOA’s favor).)

 

“Based on Lamden, Haley, Harvey, Watts, and Dolan-King, the judicial deference rule applies to an association board's discretionary decisions concerning the operation of the common interest development, e.g., the board's maintenance and repair decisions (Lamden), its selection of the appropriate means to remedy a violation of the CC&Rs (Haley), its designation of storage space in a common area (Harvey), its adoption of rules relating to short-term rentals (Watts), or its approval or rejection of a homeowner's improvement plan (Dolan-King). As we observed in Watts, ‘Common interest developments are best operated by the board of directors, not the courts.’”  (Eith v. Ketelhut (2018) 31 Cal.App.5th 1, 16.) 

 

            Plaintiff alleges Defendant HOA breached Article IV, Section 4.2.6 of the CC&Rs when it failed to take any action in response to his complaints regarding improper use of fire lanes in the community.  (Complaint, ¶25.)  Plaintiff alleges numerous prohibited activities were taking place in the fire lanes that violated the CC&Rs.  (Id.)  Plaintiff seeks an injunction ordering the HOA to enforce the guidelines and ensure the prohibited conduct occurring the fire lanes ceases.  (Id. at ¶26.)

 

            Defendant HOA moves for summary judgment on grounds that it exercised its discretion in responding to Plaintiff’s complaints of alleged violations of the CC&Rs.  Defendant argues it has discretion to enforce the CC&Rs.  Defendant argues Plaintiff admittedly has used the fire lane as an area to load and unload in violation of the Los Angeles Fire Code.  Defendant argues it exercised its discretion not to enforce the fire code provision against him.  Defendant argues it therefore maintains a standardized procedure applicable to all members of the community and Plaintiff cannot claim arbitrary, discriminatory or unequal enforcement of governing rules.   

 

            Defendant fails to establish entitlement to the presumption of judicial deference articulated in Lamden.  The judicial deference rule articulated in Lamden is an affirmative defense.  (Affan v. Portofino Cove Homeowners Assn. (2010) 189 Cal.App.4th 930, 940.)  “Thus, the defendant has the burden of establishing the requisite elements for applying the rule.”  (Id.)

 

Lamden stated that a rule of judicial deference to an HOA’s discretionary decisionmaking applies, “[w]here a duly constituted community association board, upon reasonable investigation, in good faith and with regard for the best interests of the community association and its members, exercises that discretion….”  (Lamden, supra, 21 Cal.4th at 253.)  “It is important to note the narrow scope of the Lamden rule. It is a rule of deference to the reasoned decisionmaking of homeowners association boards concerning ordinary maintenance. It does not create a blanket immunity for all the decisions and actions of a homeowners association.”  (Affan, supra, 189 Cal.App.4th at 940.) 

 

            Defendant’s separate statement includes fourteen material facts.  Eleven of those material facts pertain to Plaintiff’s use of the fire lanes, the HOA’s response to his use of the fire lane, Plaintiff’s admission that he did not observe any fraudulent activity by the Board, Plaintiff’s lack of damages or injury and Plaintiff’s admission that he has violated the CC&Rs.  (Defendants’ SSUMF Nos. 4-14.)  Defendant’s remaining three material facts are (1) Plaintiff is owner of the Property; (2) the HOA is governed by the CC&Rs; and (3) the HOA’s discretion to enforce the CC&Rs provisions at its discretion.  (Defendants’ SSUMF Nos. 1-3.) 

 

            None of these facts establish Defendant’s reasoned decisionmaking in response to each of Plaintiff’s specific complaints regarding improper use of the fire lane.  (Affan, supra, 189 Cal.App.4th at 943 (“key prerequisites” of Lamden rule not met where HOA failed to present any evidence of a “reasonable investigation” prior to decision or evidence that it acted “in good faith and with regard for the best interest of the community association and it is members because no one testified about the board’s decisionmaking process”).)  The mere fact that Defendant has not enforced the CC&Rs against Plaintiff does not establish as an issue of law that Defendant exercised its discretion reasonably in response to Plaintiff’s complaints.  In fact, Defendant does not make any showing regarding its response to Plaintiff’s complaints of improper use of the fire lane.  The Court cannot find the judicial deference rule under Lamden applies as an issue of law where Defendant makes no showing as its response to Plaintiff’s complaints or how it decided on that response. 

 

            In addition, Defendant’s cites to pages 6, 31 and 32 of the CC&Rs in support of its SSUMF No. 3, “The Association has discretion to enforce certain provisions of the Association’s governing documents.”  (Defendant’s SSUMF No. 3; Shahinfar Dec., ¶7, Ex. D, pp. 6, 31-32.)  A review of the highlighted portions of those pages do not vest the HOA with “discretion to enforce certain provisions of the CC&Rs.”  The highlighted clauses creates general and specific duties and powers of the HOA.  There is no reference to whether enforcement is discretionary or mandatory. 

 

            More fundamentally, Defendant fails to provide evidence of how enforcement of fire lane usage is treated under the CC&Rs.  Defendant only submits excerpts of the CC&Rs and fails to identify what clause or provision thereof governs enforcement of fire lane use.  As acknowledged by both parties, improper use of a fire lane would qualify as a violation of the Los Angeles City Fire Code.  The portions of the CC&Rs submitted by Defendant do not address how legal violations such as improper use of fire lanes are treated or the HOA’s obligation under the CC&Rs to ensure that the community complies with applicable laws and regulations. 

 

            On a motion for summary judgment, the moving party’s evidence is construed strictly while the opposing party’s evidence is construed liberally.  (Government Employees Ins. Co. v. Sup. Ct. (2000) 79 Cal.App.4th 95, 100.)  “All doubts as to the propriety of granting the motion (whether there is any issue of material fact [Code of Civil Procedure] § 437c) are to be resolved in favor of the party opposing the motion (i.e., a denial of summary judgment).” (Hamburg v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 497, 502.) Applying this standard, Defendant fails to establish each element of its affirmative defense based on the Lamden rule of judicial deference.  Triable issues of fact remain as to the first cause of action for breach of the CC&Rs.

 

            Defendant only moved for summary judgment.  Defendant did not move for summary adjudication.  The motion for summary judgment shall be granted if all the papers submitted show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to a judgment as a matter of law.  Because triable issues of material fact remain as to the first cause of action for breach of CC&Rs, the motion for summary judgment is DENIED.

 

            Second Cause of Action for Private Nuisance

 

            For the reasons stated above, the Court need not address the second cause of action.  Triable issues of material fact remain as to the first cause of action, which requires denial of the motion for summary judgment.

           

Conclusion

            Defendant’s Motion for Summary Judgment is DENIED.

                        Plaintiffs,

            v.

 

LEON GURVIN, an individual, and MICHAEL C. BOCK, an individual,

 

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.: 25SMCV06496

  Hearing Date: June 30, 2026

  [TENTATIVE] order RE:

  defendant’S DEMURRER TO

  COMPLAINT

 

 

 

BACKGROUND

This is a breach of contract case.  Plaintiff Martin Kempe (“Plaintiff”) claims an interest in the proceeds of the Harold S. Gurvin Trust, which defendant Leon Gurvin (“Leon”) resolved in 2025. Defendant Michael C. Bock (“Defendant”) represented defendant Leon in the Harold S. Gurvin trust dispute and offered Plaintiff a settlement regarding the interest in proceeds from the trust. Plaintiff altered the settlement agreement and Leon elected not to move forward with settlement.   

This action ensued. On December 18, 2025, Plaintiff filed the original Complaint alleging two causes of action: (1) breach of contract and (2) fraud. On February 27, 2026, the Court sustained Defendant’s demurrer to the Complaint and granted Plaintiff leave to amend. On April 7, 2026, Plaintiff filed the First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) alleging (1) breach of contract, (2) fraud, and (3) declaratory relief.  

This hearing is on Defendant’s demurrer to the FAC, filed May 11, 2026.  Defendant argues that (1) Plaintiff’s entire complaint fails due to uncertainty; (2) the first cause of action for breach of contract is uncertain and inadequately pled; (3) the second cause of action for fraud is uncertain and inadequately pled; and (4) the third cause of action for declaratory relief is uncertain.

Plaintiffs filed an opposition on June 17, 2026. Defendant has not filed a reply.

MEET AND CONFER

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41 requires that before the filing of a demurrer the moving party “shall meet and confer in person or by telephone” with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer.  (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41(a).)  The parties are to meet and confer at least five days before the date the responsive pleading is due. (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41(a)(2).)  Thereafter, the moving party shall file and serve a declaration detailing their meet and confer efforts. (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41(a)(3).)  Defendant states he met and conferred with Plaintiff’s counsel on May 5, 2026, via telephone. (Bock Decl., ¶¶ 4-5.) Defendant declares that Plaintiff’s counsel indicated intent to file a second amended complaint, but they did not follow through, and a resolution was not ultimately reached. (Bock Decl., ¶ 5.) Accordingly, the meet and confer requirement has been met.

LEGAL STANDARD

“[A] demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the allegations in a complaint.” (Lewis v. Safeway, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 385, 388.)  A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable.  (See Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994 [in ruling on a demurrer, a court may not consider declarations, matters not subject to judicial notice, or documents not accepted for the truth of their contents].)  For purposes of ruling on a demurrer, all facts pleaded in a complaint are assumed to be true, but the reviewing court does not assume the truth of conclusions of law. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hosp. Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 967.)        

Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (See Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349 [court shall not “sustain a demurrer without leave to amend if there is any reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment”]; Kong v. City of Hawaiian Gardens Redevelopment Agency (2002) 108 Cal.App.4th 1028, 1037 [“A demurrer should not be sustained without leave to amend if the complaint, liberally construed, can state a cause of action under any theory or if there is a reasonable possibility the defect can be cured by amendment.”].) The burden is on the complainant to show the Court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

DISCUSSION

Uncertainty

“A demurrer for uncertainty is not intended to reach the failure to incorporate sufficient facts in the pleading but is directed at the uncertainty existing in the allegations actually made”. (Butler v. Sequeira (1950) 100 Cal.App.2d 143, 145-146.) Moreover, demurrers for uncertainty are disfavored and will only be sustained where the pleading is so bad that the defendant cannot reasonably respond, i.e., he or she cannot reasonably determine what issues must be admitted or denied, or what counts or claims are directed against him or her. (Khoury v. Maly’s of Calif. Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.)

Defendant argues that the entire FAC is uncertain because it attempts to reference a former Complaint that has been superseded by the FAC and not incorporated as an exhibit. (Mot., p. 7.) Further, Defendant argues that the FAC is unclear and specific allegations are difficult to parse out. (Ibid.) Due to the level of alleged uncertainty, Defendant argues that the pleading is incomplete. (Ibid.)

Here, the FAC is uncertain and does not lay out causes of action alleged against Defendant and Leon. The FAC largely focuses on frustrations and alleged fraud arising from failure to serve Leon with the original Complaint. (FAC, ¶¶ 1-4.) The FAC alleges causes of action for breach of contract, fraud, and declaratory relief but is not specific about which party the causes of action are being asserted against. (See FAC.) Plaintiff sues against both Leon and Defendant but begins the FAC with alleging misrepresentation only against Defendant. (FAC, p. 1.) Additionally, Plaintiff does not identify which facts go to which causes of action. (Ibid.) Defendant cannot reasonably determine from the body of the FAC what issues must be admitted or denied, what claims are directed against him, or sparse out any specific causes of action. Moreover, Plaintiff attempts to incorporate the original Complaint and its allegations into the FAC but does not incorporate the original Complaint as an exhibit. (FAC, ¶ 6.) "Amended pleading" means a pleading that completely restates and supersedes the pleading it amends for all purposes. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 5.74.) Since the FAC completely supersedes the original complaint, the FAC cannot simply refer to the original complaint and allegations therein. Thus, the FAC is incomplete in its allegations and uncertain.

Accordingly, Defendants’ demurrer to the entire complaint on the grounds of uncertainty is SUSTAINED with leave to amend.

Breach of Contract

In addition to general uncertainty, Defendants argue that Plaintiff fails to plead facts sufficient to state the cause of action of breach of contract against Defendant. The Court agrees.

To state a cause of action for breach of contract, Plaintiff must be able to establish “(1) the existence of the contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance, (3) defendant’s breach, and (4) the resulting damages to the plaintiff.” (Oasis West Realty, LLC v. Goldman (2011) 51 Cal.4th 811, 821.)

If a breach of contract claim “is based on alleged breach of a written contract, the terms must be set out verbatim in the body of the complaint or a copy of the written agreement must be attached and incorporated by reference”. (Harris v. Rudin, Richman & Appel (1999) 74 Cal.App.4th 299, 307.) In some circumstances, a plaintiff may also “plead the legal effect of the contract rather than its precise language.” (Construction Protective Services, Inc. v. TIG Specialty Ins. Co. (2002) 29 Cal.4th 189, 198-199.)

Here, the FAC completely omits any facts which allege a cause of action for breach of contract, and fails to attach any written agreement. Thus, in addition to general uncertainty discussed above, the Court finds Plaintiff fails to sufficiently state the cause of action of breach of contract.

Accordingly, Defendant’s demurrer to the breach of contract claim is SUSTAINED with leave to amend.

Fraud 

In addition to general uncertainty, Defendants argue that Plaintiff fails to plead facts sufficient to state the cause of action of fraud against Defendant. The Court agrees.

“The elements of fraud are (a) a misrepresentation (false representation, concealment, or nondisclosure); (b) scienter or knowledge of its falsity; (c) intent to induce reliance; (d) justifiable reliance; and (e) resulting damage.” (Hinesley v. Oakshade Town Ctr. (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 289, 294.) The facts constituting the alleged fraud must be alleged factually and specifically as to every element of fraud, as the policy of “liberal construction” of the pleadings will not ordinarily be invoked. (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 645.) To properly allege fraud against a corporation, the plaintiffs must plead the names of the persons allegedly making the false representations, their authority to speak, to whom they spoke, what they said or wrote, and when it was said or written. (Tarmann v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. (1991) 2 Cal.App.4th 153, 157.)

Here, the FAC largely focuses on alleged misrepresentation. The FAC alleges that Defendant misrepresented that Leon was authorized to settle the claim for interest in the trust account. (FAC, ¶ 1.) The FAC further alleges that Plaintiff detrimentally relied on the promise to settle and did not serve Leon or elect to sue. (Ibid.) The FAC does not allege specific damages resulting from the fraud or Defendant’s knowledge of falsity, thus the FAC does not allege required elements of fraud. Thus, in addition to general uncertainty discussed above, the Court finds Plaintiff fails to sufficiently state the cause of action of fraud.

Accordingly, Defendant’s demurrer to fraud claim is SUSTAINED with leave to amend.

Declaratory Relief

In addition to general uncertainty, Defendants argue that Plaintiff fails to plead facts sufficient to state the cause of action of declaratory relief against Defendant. The Court agrees.

            “To qualify for declaratory relief, a party would have to demonstrate its action presented two essential elements: (1) a proper subject of declaratory relief, and (2) an actual controversy involving justiciable questions relating to the party’s rights or obligations.” (Jolley v. Chase Home Finance, LLC (2013) 213 Cal.App.4th 872, 909, quotation marks and brackets omitted.)

A cause of action for declaratory relief should not be used as a second cause of action for the determination of identical issues raised in another cause of action. (General of America Insurance Co. v. Lilly (1968) 258 Cal.App.2d 465, 470.) “The availability of another form of relief that is adequate will usually justify refusal to grant declaratory relief” (California Insurance Guarantee Association v. Superior Court (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 1617, 1624), and a duplicative cause of action is subject to demurrer (Palm Springs Villas II Homeowners Association, Inc. v. Parth (2016) 248 Cal.App.4th 268, 290). Further, “there is no basis for declaratory relief where only past wrongs are involved.” (Osseous Technologies of America, Inc. v. DiscoveryOrtho Partners LLC (2010) 191 Cal.App.4th 357, 366, quotation marks omitted.)        

Plaintiff requests that the court declare that Plaintiff has a corresponding lien against Defendant’s “IOLTA Account’s Attorney Client Trust Funds” in the amount of $75,000 “subject to the herein Breach of Contract and/or Fraud” causes of action. (FAC, ¶ 7.)

            Here, Plaintiff does not demonstrate in the FAC that the relief sought is proper for declaratory relief. Moreover, it is unclear from the allegation in the FAC that Plaintiff’s requested declaratory relief is not completely derived of the alleged breach of contract and fraud. It seems that Plaintiff is seeking that the Court declare Plaintiff is entitled to damages resulting from the alleged misrepresentation. Thus, in addition to general uncertainty discussed above, the Court finds Plaintiff fails to sufficiently state the cause of action of declaratory relief.

Accordingly, the Court SUSTAINS the demurrer to the declaratory relief claim with 20 days’ leave to amend.

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Based on the foregoing, the Court SUSTAINS the demurrer with leave to amend.  

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

BACKGROUND

This is a defamation, false light, infliction of emotional distress, and negligence case, arising from the publication, republication, and monetization of a video pertaining to Plaintiffs (Video). Plaintiffs Erik Rommel Blackwell, Jr. (Blackwell) and Wonder Kwesi Abor (Abor) (collectively Plaintiffs) allege that on or about December 24, 2021, Defendant Flo Malcolm posted a defamatory and fabricated video falsely accusing Plaintiffs of attempting to drug women with date-rape drugs. (Compl., ¶ 1.) Plaintiffs contend the video was then republished and promoted by Defendant WorldStarHipHop, LLC (WorldStar) to millions of viewers on multiple platforms. (Compl., ¶ 2.)

Plaintiffs allege the allegations in the video were false, that the woman portrayed as the alleged victim later admitted in a private conversation that her accusations against Plaintiffs were the result of coercion and threats by Flo Malcolm, that the alleged victim put out a public retraction statement, and that despite Plaintiffs efforts to establish that the alleged incident never took place, WorldStar again republished the clip of Plaintiffs’ alleged assault on May 7, 2025. (Compl., ¶¶ 3-4.) Plaintiffs allege that these actions resulted in reputational harm, loss of employment opportunities, online harassment, psychological trauma, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), anxiety, depression, and financial loss.

On December 17, 2025, Plaintiffs filed their complaint, asserting causes of action against all Defendants for (1) defamation (libel and slander), (2) false light invasion of privacy, (3) intentional infliction of emotional distress, (4) civil conspiracy, (5) aiding and abetting, (6) negligent infliction of emotional distress, and (7) injunctive relief. Against only WorldStar, Plaintiffs assert causes of action for (1) negligence and (2) negligent supervision or negligent publication. Against only Flo Malcolm, Plaintiffs assert causes of action for (1) civil extortion (attempted / completed) and (2) fraudulent misrepresentation.

On February 10, 2026, Plaintiffs filed an amendment to the complaint, naming WSHH Holdings, LLC as DOE 1.

On February 23, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a request for dismissal as to Flo Malcom only, which was granted by the Court the same day.

On February 27, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a request for dismissal as to WorldStarHipHop only, also granted by the Court the same day.

On April 1, 2026, Plaintiffs filed an ex parte application for an order vacating the dismissal of Flo Malcom on the grounds that the request for dismissal was due to inadvertent clerical error. The Court denied the request on the grounds that no exigency was shown.

On April 14, 2026, Plaintiffs again moved to vacate Flo Malcom’s dismissal on the same grounds via a regularly noticed motion. The Court denied the motion for Plaintiffs’ failure to provide sufficient proof of service of notice.

On May 20, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a request for dismissal as to WorldStarHipHop, LLC only. Dismissal was entered the same day.

As such, the only remaining Defendants in this case are WSHH Holdings, LLC (WSHH) and DOES 2-50.

On May 26, 2026, WSHH, doing business as WorldStarHipHop (WorldStar) filed a special motion to strike the complaint.

On June 23, 2026, Plaintiffs filed their opposition.

No reply has been filed.

This hearing is on WSHH’s motion to specially strike each and every cause of action in the Complaint. Defendant argues that in republishing the Video, it was engaged in protected activity under California’s anti-SLAPP statute, that Plaintiffs cannot establish a probability of prevailing on their claims, that Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act (CDA) bars claims based on WorldStar’s republication of third-party content, that many of the claims are barred by the statute of limitations, and that Plaintiffs fail to allege facts showing actual malice. (Motion, p. 1:18-26.)

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

LEGAL STANDARD

 A strategic lawsuit against public participation (SLAPP) “is a civil lawsuit that is aimed at preventing citizens from exercising their political rights or punishing those who have done so.” (Simpson Strong-Tie Co., Inc. v. Gore (2010) 49 Cal.4th 12, 21.) California Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16 authorizes “the filing of a special motion to strike to expedite the early dismissal of these unmeritorious claims.” “To encourage ‘continued participation in matters of public significance’ and to ensure ‘that this participation should not be chilled through abuse of the judicial process,’ the Legislature expressly provided that the anti-SLAPP statute ‘shall be construed broadly.’” (Ibid.)

“A cause of action against a person arising from any act of that person in furtherance of the person’s right of petition or free speech under the United States Constitution or the California Constitution in connection with a public issue shall be subject to a special motion to strike, unless the court determines that the plaintiff has established that there is a probability that the plaintiff will prevail on the claim.”¿ (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (b)(1).) Acts protected under the statute include “(1) any written or oral statement or writing made before a legislative, executive, or judicial proceeding, or any other official proceeding authorized by law, (2) any written or oral statement or writing made in connection with an issue under consideration or review by a legislative, executive, or judicial body, or any other official proceeding authorized by law, (3) any written or oral statement or writing made in a place open to the public or a public forum in connection with an issue of public interest, or (4) any other conduct in furtherance of the exercise of the constitutional right of petition or the constitutional right of free speech in connection with a public issue or an issue of public interest.” (Id., subd. (e).)

The trial court applies a burden shifting analysis when considering a special anti-SLAPP motion to strike brought under section 425.16.¿¿(See Soukup¿v. Law Offices of Herbert¿Hafif¿(2006) 39 Cal.4th 260, 278, citing Navellier v. Sletten (2002) 29 Cal.4th 82, 88.)¿¿First, the moving party must make a threshold showing that the challenged cause of action arises from “any act of that person in furtherance of the person’s right of petition or free speech under the [federal or state constitution] with a public issue.”¿ (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (b); see¿Soukup, supra, 39 Cal.4th at p. 278.)¿¿Second,¿if the moving party meets its threshold burden, the burden then shifts to the opposing party to¿demonstrate¿a probability of prevailing on the merits of the claims¿at issue.¿(Equilon¿Enterprises v. Consumer Cause, Inc.¿(2002) 29 Cal.4th 53, 67.) 

For mixed causes of action “involving allegations of both protected and unprotected activity, the plaintiff is required to establish a probability of prevailing on any claim for relief based on allegations of protected activity. Unless the plaintiff can do so, the claim and its corresponding allegations must be stricken.” (Baral v. Schnitt (2016) 1 Cal.5th 376, 395.)

DISCUSSION

Plaintiffs’ Untimely and Procedurally Defective Opposition

            Plaintiffs” Opposition was filed late on June 23, 2026 – five court days before the hearing on this motion.  There also are multiple procedural deficiencies in the Opposition, as outlined in the late Reply filed by defendant.  The late reply, however, is understandable given that Defendant’s reply would have been due on the same date Plaintiffs’ filed their Opposition.  Plaintiff’s late and defective Opposition is, by itself, enough to justify granting Defendant’s motion.  Nevertheless, the Court will proceed to consider that Opposition in the discussion below.

California’s Anti-SLAPP Statute

            WSHH argues that it was engaged in a protected activity pursuant to California’s anti-SLAPP statute. As the moving party, WSHH bears the burden of establishing that the conduct in question amounts to protected activity.

a.     WSHH’s Burden – Establishing Protected Activity Under the Anti-SLAPP Statute

The moving party has the initial burden in bringing an anti-SLAPP motion “to demonstrate that the act or acts of which the plaintiff complains were taken ‘in furtherance of the [moving party]'s right of petition or free speech under the United States or California Constitution in connection with a public issue,’ as defined in the statute.” (Jarrow Formulas, Inc. v. LaMarche (2003) 31 Cal.4th 728, 733.) “There is no further requirement that the [moving party] initially demonstrate his or her exercise of constitutional rights of speech or petition was valid as a matter of law.” (Soukup, supra, 39 Cal.4th at p. 286 (citing Navellier, supra, 29 Cal.4th at pp. 94–95.).) Moving parties also face “no additional requirement of proving the plaintiff’s subjective intent” in filing the lawsuit or “demonstrat[ing] that the action actually has had a chilling effect on the exercise of such rights.” (Navellier, supra, 29 Cal.4th at 88.)

i.                 Conduct on Which Plaintiffs’ Causes of Action Arise

In Bonni v. St. Joseph Health Sys. (2021) 11 Cal.5th 995, the Supreme Court established that “courts should analyze each claim for relief—each act or set of acts supplying a basis for relief, of which there may be several in a single pleaded cause of action—to determine whether the acts are protected and, if so, whether the claim they give rise to has the requisite degree of merit to survive the motion.” (Bonni, supra, 11 Cal.5th at 1010.) In satisfying his burden, a moving party “must identify the acts alleged in the complaint that it asserts are protected and what claims for relief are predicated on them. In turn, a court should examine whether those acts are protected and supply the basis for any claims.” (Ibid.) “A defendant need only make a prima facie showing at this stage.” (Ojjeh v. Brown (2019) 43 Cal.App.5th 1027, 1035–1036.)

WSHH argues all causes of action asserted against it arise from the publication and republication of the Video to its online platform. (Motion, p. 3:1-4.) WSHH specifically argues that said publication and republication fall squarely within the ambit of protected activity as defined by California Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16, subdivisions (e)(3)-(4), which defines protected activity, in relevant part, as “any written or oral statement or writing made in a place open to the public or a public forum in connection with an issue of public interest” and “any other conduct in furtherance of the exercise of the constitutional right of petition or the constitutional right of free speech in connection with a public issue or an issue of public interest.”

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue the case “arises from the publication, republication, promotion, endorsement, and monetization of false accusations.” (Opp., p.2:5-6.) Plaintiffs further contend WSHH acted as more than a passive host for third-party content, but rather, that WSHH actively participated in the development, creation, amplification, and dissemination of the defamatory narrative “by publishing its own headlines, captions, and editorial commentary.” (Opp., p. 2:9-12.)

The Court notes that generally, the Complaint alleges that Defendant Flo Malcolm is the individual who recorded the Video, that WorldStar published, edited, captioned, monetized, and circulated the Video to a large audience, and DOEs 1-50, including WSHH as DOE 1, “aided, abetted, promoted, monetized, or conspired to defame Plaintiffs.” (Compl., ¶¶ 12-14.) Each cause of action in Plaintiffs’ complaint directly references the publication and republication of the Video, captions, headlines, or commentary as the basis for the claim. Thus, the Court finds that all causes of action against WSHH directly arise from and relate to WSHH’s publication and republication of the Video, headlines, captions, and/or editorial commentary.

ii.               WSHH’s Publications Were Made in a Public Forum

WSHH first avers its publications were statements made in a public forum as they were posted on the internet. In California, for purposes of the anti-SLAPP statute, it is well established that websites accessible to the public are considered public forums. (See Huntingdon Life Sciences, Inc. v. Stop Huntingdon Animal Cruelty USA, Inc. (2005) 129 Cal.App.4th 1228, 1247 (disapproved on other grounds) [public forums not limited to physical settings and includes other forms of public communication; statements made on a website accessible to anyone who chose to visit the site could hardly be more public]; Wilbanks v. Wolk (2004) 121 Cal.App.4th 883, 895 [anti-SLAPP case found statements were posted to a public forum where posted on a public website]; ComputerXpress, Inc. v. Jackson (2001) 93 Cal.App.4th 993. 1007 [websites satisfied criteria of public forums where they constituted a ‘place that is open to the public where information is freely exchanged.’ (citing Damon v. Ocean Hills Journalism Club (2000) 85 Cal.App.4th 468, 475)].) Plaintiffs do not address this issue in their opposition.

Thus, because WSHH’s conduct in question is publication, republication, and creation of false statements made on its website and various social media platforms on the internet, the Court finds that the statements were made in a public forum.

iii.             WSHH’s Publications Concerned a Matter of Public Interest

WSHH next argues the content at issue concerns a matter of public interest. Courts will find that a statement concerns a matter of public interest where it falls into one of three categories: (1) the statement concerns a person or entity in the public eye, (2) the statement concerns conduct that could directly affect a large number of people beyond the direct participants, or (3) the statement concerns a topic of widespread, public interest. (Rivero v. Am. Fed'n of State, Cty. & Mun. Emps., AFL-CIO (2003) 105 Cal.App.4th 913, 924.)

WSHH argues that its statements here concerned conduct endangering the public safety, which could directly affect a large number of people beyond the participating parties, is within the public interest (Motion, p. 4:23-25, 5:2-3.) WSHH points to the well-documented public safety concern of drug-facilitated assault in public social spaces in support of its characterization of the underlying conduct. (Id. at 5:3-5.) WSHH also points to the fact that the published content was viewed over 321,400 times, republished by multiple third parties, and high level of public engagement to demonstrate widespread public interest. (Id. at 4:24-28.)

The Court also notes the apparent widespread discourse surrounding the content at issue and, while unfortunate, the “mob harassment” allegedly suffered by Plaintiffs as a result of the publication. (Hurt Decl., ¶ 8, Compl., ¶ 30.) Further, Plaintiffs have identified themselves as public figures within their communities, as Blackwell claims to be a professional entertainer and magician whose reputation is essential to his livelihood, and Abor claims to be a respected public figure within his professional community. (Compl., ¶¶ 10-11.) In opposition, Plaintiffs do not argue that the published information was not a matter of public interest. Plaintiffs appear to concede this fact in their opposition. (Opp., p. 5:9-10.)

Nygard is instructive, as the court established that “cases and the legislative history that discusses them suggest that ‘an issue of public interest’ within the meaning of section 425.16, subdivision (e)(3) is any issue in which the public is interested. In other words, the issue need not be ‘significant’ to be protected by the anti-SLAPP statute—it is enough that it is one in which the public takes an interest.” (Nygard, Inc. v. Uusi-Kerttula (2008) 159 Cal. App. 4th 1027, 1042.)

Here, it is clear that the publications at issue garnered widespread attention, interest, and engagement from the public and that Plaintiffs have identified themselves as public figures. Thus, the publications fall within the three categories of statements held to concern a matter of public interest.

Accordingly, this Court finds that the statements and conduct of WSHH, specifically the publications and republication of the Video, headlines, commentary, and captions, pertain to a matter of public interest. Thus, WSHH has satisfied its burden of showing the conduct giving rise to Plaintiffs’ complaint and all causes of action therein constitutes protected activity pursuant to the anti-SLAPP statute. The burden thus shifts to Plaintiffs to make a showing of probability of success on the merits.

 

b.     Plaintiffs’ Burden – Establishing Probability of Success on the Merits

Once the court has found the moving party has made the requisite showing, “it then determines whether the plaintiff has demonstrated a probability of prevailing on the claim.” (Jarrow, supra, 31 Cal.4th at p. 733.) In considering whether the opposing party has met its burden, “[t]he court does not weigh evidence or resolve conflicting factual claims. Its inquiry is limited to whether the plaintiff has stated a legally sufficient claim and made a prima facie factual showing sufficient to sustain a favorable judgment. It accepts the plaintiff's evidence as true, and evaluates the defendant's showing only to determine if it defeats the plaintiff's claim as a matter of law.” (Sweetwater Union High Sch. Dist. v. Gilbane Bldg. Co. (2019) 6 Cal.5th 931, 940.)

Notably, it is well established that “[a]s to the second step inquiry, a plaintiff seeking to demonstrate the merit of the claim ‘may not rely solely on its complaint, even if verified; instead, its proof must be made upon competent admissible evidence.’” (Id. at 940.) “[E]vidence may be considered at the anti-SLAPP motion stage if it is reasonably possible the evidence set out in supporting affidavits, declarations or their equivalent will be admissible at trial.” (Id at 940, 947.)

 

i.                 First Cause of Action – Defamation (Libel and Slander)

“A claim for defamation requires proof of a false and unprivileged publication that exposes the plaintiff ‘to hatred, contempt, ridicule, or obloquy, or which causes him to be shunned or avoided, or which has a tendency to injure him in his occupation.” (Civ. Code, § 45.) A statement is defamatory when it tends ‘directly to injure [a person] in respect to his office, profession, trade or business, either by imputing to him general disqualification in those respects which the office or other occupation peculiarly requires, or by imputing something with reference to his office, trade, profession, or business that has a natural tendency to lessen its profits[.]’ (Civ. Code, § 46, subd. 3.) Statements that contain such a charge directly, and without the need for explanatory matter, are libelous per se. (Civ. Code, § 45a.)” (McGarry v. University of San Diego (2007) 154 Cal.App.4th 97, 112.)

“The elements of a defamation claim are (1) a publication that is (2) false, (3) defamatory, (4) unprivileged, and (5) has a natural tendency to injure or causes special damage.” (Wong v. Jing (2010) 189 Cal.App.4th 1354, 1369.) Because Plaintiffs are, admittedly and as discussed above, public figures for the purposes of the anti-SLAPP statute, WSHH may not be held liable for libelous statements against them unless they are both (1) actually false, and (2) made with malice. (Christian Research Institute v. Alnor (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 71, 84.) The plaintiff “must demonstrate ‘actual malice’ by clear and convincing evidence.” (Ibid.) “Actual malice ‘is not measured by whether a reasonably prudent man would have published, or would have investigated before publishing. There must be sufficient evidence to permit the conclusion that the defendant in fact entertained serious doubts as to the truth of his publication.’ [Citation.] Lack of due care is not the measure of liability, nor is gross or even extreme negligence.’ [Citation.] Thus, ‘mere failure to investigate the truthfulness of a statement, even when a reasonably prudent person would have done so, is insufficient’ to demonstrate actual malice. [Citation.]” (Id., at 90.)

            The Court finds that Plaintiffs have failed to establish a probability of success on the merits based on the evidence presented. While it is true courts must consider the pleadings and must generally accept an opposing party’s evidence as true in deciding whether there is a showing of probability of success, unlike a demurrer, the Court does not generally accept all allegations of fact pleaded in a complaint as true in ruling on an anti-SLAPP motion. (See Sweetwater, supra, 6 Cal.5th at 940.)

Here, in opposition, Plaintiffs only rely on the contents of their Complaint, and furnish only the Declaration of Dimitri D. Hurt (Hurt Declaration) as further evidence for the Court to consider and accept as true. No exhibits or further evidence is attached to the Hurt Declaration. In their opposition, Plaintiffs state they have provided sufficient evidence to prove a probability of success, but only reference specific allegations made in the complaint. (Opp., p. 7:12-8:24.) As established, Plaintiffs cannot rely on the allegations of their complaint in opposing the present motion, and must provide or point to evidence potentially admissible at trial to support their cause of action in opposing an anti-SLAPP motion. (Ibid.; see Abir Cohen Treyzon Salo, LLP v. Lahiji (2019) 40 Cal.App.5th 882, 887 [plaintiff failed to carry burden of showing claim had minimal merit where factual assertions were unsupported by any evidence in the record].)

Plaintiffs’ complaint alleges a number of facts which, if substantiated, may support this cause of action. However, the complaint itself only points to one tangible piece of evidence which may be admissible at trial, being the written documentation confirming the alleged victim’s admission that she had never been drugged. (Compl., ¶ 28.) No other evidence is sufficiently referenced, identified, or provided in the complaint.

Turning to the Hurt Declaration provided in opposition, the relevant evidence presented is as follows: (1) Hurt observed content portraying Plaintiffs as individuals who allegedly attempted to drug women with date-rape drugs and engaged in criminal and predatory conduct (Hurt Decl., ¶ 5); (2) Hurt observed statements made, published, or disseminated through WSHH’s platform including “These Two Young Men Were Reportedly Trying To Drug A Group Of Women"; "Real Hero In Action? Brah Checked A Creep For Dropping A Date Rape Drug In Girls' Drinks"; and additional captions, headlines, and promotional statements characterizing Plaintiffs as individuals engaged in criminal conduct (Id. at ¶¶ 6-7); (3) the publications were disseminated to substantial audiences by WSHH and generated significant public engagement (Id. at ¶ 8); (4) Hurt became aware of information disputing the truthfulness of the accusations contained in the publications, including information indicating that the alleged victim disputed the accusations and denied Plaintiffs engagement in the alleged criminal conduct (Id., ¶ 11-12); and (5) Hurt reviewed information indicating the challenged publications remained available and continued to circulate after information disputing the accusations became available (Id. at ¶ 13.) The Hurt Declaration also states, generally, that he believes additional evidence concerning the publication is within WSHH’s possession, custody, or control, but does not further identify the evidence. (Id., ¶ 18.)

            The Hurt Declaration, taken as fact and unrefuted by any reply, is insufficient to establish a probability of success on the merits. To start with, the Hurt Declaration does not establish malice. Neither the Hurt Declaration nor Plaintiffs’ complaint provides or identifies any evidence that establishes that WSHH knew of the falsity of the publication or acted with reckless disregard as to the truth of the information. The Hurt Declaration vaguely establishes that Hurt reviewed information indicating the alleged victim disputed accusations and denied Plaintiffs’ criminal conduct, but does not identify or provide the evidence reviewed, nor does it claim the information reviewed was presented to WSHH before the publication or republications, when it was presented, or acknowledge receipt. It also does not establish that the information reviewed as to falsity was public information sufficient to establish reckless disregard on behalf of WSHH. The vague references in the Hurt Declaration to information available to him and the allegations within Plaintiffs’ complaint, unsubstantiated by any additional evidence, are not sufficient to establish a minimal probability of success on the merits.

            WSHH also argues Plaintiffs’ defamation claims are barred by the applicable statute of limitations. (Motion, p. 9:6-13.) An action for libel or slander must be brought within one year. (Code Civ. Proc., § 340, subd. (c).) “In California the accrual of causes of action growing out of the publication of defamatory or other tortious statements is governed by the single-publication rule. Under the rule, one cause of action will arise, and the statute of limitations will commence running, upon the first general publication or broadcast of a tortious statement, notwithstanding how many copies of the publication are distributed or how many people hear or see the broadcast. Any subsequent republication or rebroadcast gives rise to a new single cause of action.” (Traditional Cat Assn., Inc. v. Gilbreath (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 392, 395.) The single publication rule applies to statements posted on websites. (Ibid.)

            Defendants argue that because the action arises from a publication, republication, and monetization of a statement published on or about December 24, 2021, the defamation cause of action is barred by the statute of limitations because the complaint was not filed until December 17, 2025, more than four years later. (Motion, p. 9:6-13.) The Court agrees in part. While Plaintiffs argue their defamation claim is not barred due to the ongoing nature of monetization, dissemination, and republication of the content, the single-publication rule, in giving rise to a separate and distinct cause of action upon republication of allegedly defamatory statements, makes clear that defamation is not a continuing tort which extends the statute of limitations upon subsequent offenses. Further, it appears from the circumstances alleged by Plaintiffs that they were made aware of the publication rather promptly.

However, Plaintiffs allege WSHH republished the Video on or around May 7, 2025. (Compl., ¶ 22.) Pursuant to the single-publication rule, subsequent republication gives rise to a new single cause of action for the republication. Thus, the Court finds that Plaintiffs cause of action for defamation is barred by the statute of limitations to the extent that the alleged defamation took place on or before December 16, 2024.

Notwithstanding the maintenance of claims falling within the statute of limitations, because no proof was made upon competent admissible evidence, the Court finds that Plaintiffs have failed to meet their burden as to the first cause of action.

 

ii.               Second Cause of Action – False Light Invasion of Privacy

“A ‘false light’ cause of action is in substance equivalent to a libel claim, and should meet the same requirements of the libel claim, including proof of malice” if required. (Medical Marijuana, Inc. v. ProjectCBD.com (2016) 6 Cal.App.5th 602, 615-616.) Furthermore, California Code of Civil Procedure section 340, subdivision (c) also governs false light claims and imposes a one-year statute of limitations for said claims. Plaintiffs put forth the same evidence for their false light claim as they do for their defamation claim.

Accordingly, for the reasons stated above, certain of Plaintiffs claims are barred by the one-year statute of limitations, and Plaintiffs have otherwise failed to establish minimal probability of success on the merits as to the second cause of action.

 

iii.             Third and Ninth Causes of Action – Intentional and Negligent Infliction of Emotional Distress

“A cause of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress requires: (1) extreme and outrageous conduct by appellants with the intention of causing, or reckless disregard of the probability of causing, emotional distress; (2) severe or extreme emotional distress; and (3) actual and proximate causation of the emotional distress by the appellants' outrageous conduct. [Citation.] Conduct is considered outrageous when it is ‘so extreme as to exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated in a civilized community.’ [Citation.].” (Belen v. Ryan Seacrest Productions, LLC (2021) 65 Cal.App.5th 1145, 1164.)

            Plaintiffs again rely solely on the allegations set forth in their complaint as to the third cause of action. No evidence has been presented, in the Hurt Declaration or otherwise, establishing intent or reckless disregard on behalf of WSHH. Furthermore, while Plaintiffs’ claim specific emotional distress and suffering, no competent evidence has been submitted to the Court by way of declarations of either Plaintiff, medical records or history, diagnoses of medical conditions as alleged in the complaint, or any other affidavit or documentary showing.

            As to Plaintiffs’ claim for negligent infliction of emotional distress, the Court notes that in California, “there is no independent tort of negligent infliction of emotional distress. [Citation.] ‘The tort is negligence, a cause of action in which a duty to the plaintiff is an essential element.’ (Ibid.) ‘A claim of negligent infliction of emotional distress is not an independent tort but the tort of negligence to which the traditional elements of duty, breach of duty, causation, and damages apply.’ [Citation.]” (Belen, supra, 65 Cal.App.4th at 1165.)

            Here, Plaintiffs failed to state a cause of action for negligence against WSHH in their complaint, as the complaint does not allege that WSHH owed them a duty or that said duty was breached. Furthermore, as with the above discussed causes of action, no evidence has been presented by Plaintiffs upon which a probability of success on the merits can be found.

            WSHH also argues Plaintiffs’ intentional infliction of emotional distress and negligence-based claims are barred by the statute of limitations. (Opp., p.9:16-10:4.) Generally, any action for injury to an individual caused by the wrongful act or neglect of another must be brought within two years. (Code Civ. Proc., § 335.1.) Courts have held that a cause of action pertaining to emotional distress accrues “when the physical manifestations of the emotional trauma first appear.” (Aldaco v. Tropic Ice Cream Co. (1980) 110 Cal.App.3d 523, 526.) With respect to both emotional distress causes of action, Plaintiffs’ claim they have suffered “[p]sychological impairment requiring 3-5 years of treatment” as a result of WSHH’s alleged conduct. (Compl., ¶ 56(f).) Plaintiffs also alleged they have both incurred “[t]herapy costs of $50,000-$75,000.” (Compl., ¶ 32(c).)

            Here, the Court cannot definitively rule on whether these claims are time-barred. The complaint does not specify whether the 3-5 years of therapy is only for past psychological treatment, or if this figure includes ongoing and future treatment. Further, while the therapy costs incurred by Plaintiffs at the time of filing lean towards a reasonable inference that Plaintiffs have previously undergone psychological treatment for the emotional harm caused by WSHH’s conduct, this inference lacks the certainty required to bar these claims on statute of limitations grounds.

            Regardless, because Plaintiffs have failed to produce any competent evidence to support a minimal probability of success on the merits, Plaintiffs have failed to meet their burden as to the third and ninth causes of action.

 

iv.             Sixth Cause of Action – Civil Conspiracy

Civil conspiracy is “not an independent cause of action,” but a “theory of co-equal legal liability under which certain defendants may be held liable for ‘an independent civil wrong.’” (Navarrete v. Meyer (2015) 237 Cal.App.4th 1276, 1291.) “Under a conspiracy theory of recovery, liability depends on the actual commission of a tort.” (Ibid., emphasis added.)’ The elements of an action for civil conspiracy are the formation and operation of the conspiracy and damage resulting to plaintiff from an act or acts done in furtherance of the common design.’” (Applied Equipment Corp. v. Litton Saudi Arabia Ltd. (1994) 7 Cal.4th 503, 511, citation omitted.)

Plaintiffs assert there is reasonable probability they will prevail on their claims for civil conspiracy because they have alleged facts which support a reasonable inference that WSHH and dismissed Defendant Flo Malcolm acted in concert to publish, amplify, and profit from the accusations against Plaintiffs. However, Plaintiffs again failed to present any evidence of the commission of a tort. No evidence has been presented by way of declaration or otherwise as to the formation and operation of the conspiracy, nor the damage resulting to Plaintiffs.

Accordingly, Plaintiffs fail to establish probability of success as to the sixth cause of action.

v.               Seventh Cause of Action – Aiding and Abetting

“California has adopted the common law rule for subjecting a defendant to liability for aiding and abetting a tort. ‘‘Liability may … be imposed on one who aids and abets the commission of an intentional tort if the person (a) knows the other's conduct constitutes a breach of duty and gives substantial assistance or encouragement to the other to so act or (b) gives substantial assistance to the other in accomplishing a tortious result and the person's own conduct, separately considered, constitutes a breach of duty to the third person.’ [Citation.]” (Casey v. U.S. Bank Nat. Ass'n (2005) 127 Cal. App. 4th 1138, 1144.)

Here, Plaintiffs fail to present evidence that an intentional tort was committed by Flo Malcolm. Additionally, the complaint itself does not properly allege the cause of action, as it fails to state that a duty was owed to them by Flo Malcolm, that the duty was breached by Flo Malcolm, or that WSHH knew Flo Malcolm’s conduct constituted a breach of duty. Plaintiffs also present no evidence that WSHH provided substantial assistance or encouragement to Flo Malcom to act as alleged. Further, Plaintiffs fail to allege or establish with any evidence that WSHH owed a duty to Plaintiffs and that said duty was breached. Plaintiffs argue that direct evidence of an agreement is rarely available before discovery and that conspiracy allegations may be established through circumstantial evidence and reasonable inferences arising from coordinated conduct. (Opp., p. 15:13-16.) However, Plaintiffs’ reliance on the allegations in their complaint, as discussed, is misplaced, and no declaration or affidavit setting forth the circumstantial evidence upon which Plaintiffs rely was proffered.

            Accordingly, Plaintiffs have failed to establish a probability of success as to the seventh cause of action.

vi.             Eleventh Cause of Action – Injunctive Relief

“Injunctive relief is a remedy and not, in itself, a cause of action, and a cause of action must exist before injunctive relief may be granted.” (Shell Oil Co. v. Richter (1942) 52 Cal.App.2d 164, 168.) As discussed, Plaintiffs have failed to meet their burden as to all causes of action asserted against WSHH. Accordingly, Plaintiffs have failed to establish a probability of success in securing injunctive relief.

ix.             WSHH’s Immunity under Section 230 of the CMA

The Court acknowledges both parties’ detailed and reasoned arguments as to WSHH’s immunity pursuant to section 230 of the CMA. The Court notes that the court’s ruling in Eizenga v. MediaLab.AI Inc., et al. is not binding on this Court, nor did it inform the Court’s ruling on the above causes of action. As stated above, in analyzing the present motion, the Court is obligated to accept Plaintiffs’ evidence as true and only evaluate WSHH’s showing to determine whether it defeats Plaintiffs’ claims as a matter of law. (Sweetwater, supra, 6 Cal.5th at 940.) While federal immunity of WSHH may bear weight on Plaintiffs’ probability of success, because the Court found that Plaintiffs failed to support their causes of action with the requisite competent evidence, the Court presently declines to issue a ruling as to whether federal immunity under section 230 of the CMA applies to WSHH.

Accordingly, the Court’s ruling on this motion is not determinative of WSHH’s section 230 immunity.

WSHH requests attorney’s fees and costs incurred and expected in association with the present motion in the amount of $5,620.00.

“[A] prevailing [moving party] on a special motion to strike shall be entitled to recover that [party]’s attorney’s fees and costs.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 425.16, subd. c(1).) “A party who partially prevails on an anti-SLAPP motion must generally be considered a prevailing party unless the results of the motion were so insignificant that the party did not achieve any practical benefit from bringing the motion. The determination whether a party prevailed on an anti-SLAPP motion lies within the broad discretion of the trial court.” (Mann v. Quality Old Time Serv., Inc. (2006) 139 Cal.App.4th 328, 330)

Because WSHH’s motion is granted as to all causes of action asserted against it, WSHH is entitled to recover its attorney’s fees and costs.

WSHH’s counsel Maurice D. Pessah (Pessah) has been licensed to practice law in California since 2021, and is currently licensed to practice law in six states. (Pessah Decl., ¶ 3.) Pessah bills at an hourly rate of $500 per hour. (Id., ¶ 7.) Pessah contends he spent 6.5 hours drafting the present motion at $500 per hour, for a total of $3,250, and expects to incur an additional $1,250, reflecting an anticipated 2.5 hours in reviewing the opposition and drafting a reply, $500 for one hour spent preparing for the hearing on this motion, and $500 for one hour spent attending the hearing on this motion. (Id., ¶ 8.) Pessah contends WSHH will incur a total of $125 in filing fees for the filing of this motion and to provide courtesy copies to the Court. (Id., ¶ 9.)

The Court finds this to be a reasonable rate given Pessah’s experience in civil litigation and the depth and length of the present motion and opposing papers. Thus, WSHH’s request for attorney’s fees is GRANTED in the requested amount of $5,620, the amount requested less $750 for 1.5 hours of time drafting a reply at $500 per hour.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Based on the foregoing, the Court GRANTS Defendant WSHH Holdings, LLC’s special motion to strike the causes of action in the complaint as to Defendant WSHH Holdings, LLC. Defendant’s request for attorney’s fees is GRANTED in the amount of $5,620.

Background

 

            Plaintiff filed this action based on a nonconforming vehicle subject to express warranties.  Plaintiff alleges the defects were covered by the warranties.  Plaintiff provided Defendants with sufficient opportunity to remedy the defects.  Plaintiff alleges Defendants were unable to do so. Plaintiff alleges Defendant Jaguar Land Rover of North America has refused Plaintiff’s demand for revocation and refused to provide Plaintiff with the remedies to which he is entitled upon revocation. 

 

            On April 16, 2026, Plaintiff filed a complaint against Defendants Jaguar Land Rover North America, LLC and Land Rover Pasadena alleging (1) breach of express warranty under the Song Beverly Consumer Warranty Act; (2) breach of implied warranty under the Song Beverly Consumer Warranty Act; (3) violation of Business and Professions Code §17200; and (4) negligent repair.

 

            Plaintiff’s complaint contains several significant inconsistencies.  The plaintiff is identified inconsistently as Gary Gezalyan (Complaint, Caption and Preamble, p. 1) and Arman Shakaryan (Complaint, ¶1).  The complaint alleges inconsistently that the subject vehicle was leased (Complaint ¶2) and purchased (Complaint, ¶12.)

 

Demurrer Standard

 

            A demurrer is a pleading used to test the legal sufficiency of other pleadings.  It raises issues of law, not fact, regarding the form or content of the opposing party’s pleading.  It is not the function of the demurrer to challenge the truthfulness of the complaint; and for purpose of the ruling on the demurrer, all facts pleaded in the complaint are assumed to be true, however improbable they may be.

 

            A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack; or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable.  (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311.)  No other extrinsic evidence can be considered (i.e., no “speaking demurrers”).  Specifically, a demurrer may be brought per Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10 subdivision (e) if insufficient facts are stated to support the cause of action asserted.  Furthermore, a demurrer for uncertainty will be sustained only where the complaint is so bad that the defendant cannot reasonably respond.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (f).)

 

            However, in construing the allegations, the court is to give effect to specific factual allegations that may modify or limit inconsistent general or conclusory allegations.  (Financial Corporation of America v. Wilburn (1987) 189 Cal.App.3rd 764, 769.)  And, if the facts pled in the complaint are inconsistent with facts which are incorporated by reference from exhibits attached to the complaint, the facts in the incorporated exhibits control.  Further, irrespective of the name or label given to a cause of action by the plaintiff, a general demurrer must be overruled if the facts as plead in the body of the complaint state some valid claim for relief.  Special demurrers are not allowed in limited jurisdiction courts.  (Code of Civ. Proc., § 92, subd. (c).)

 

            Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment.  (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.)  The burden is on the complainant to show the Court that a pleading can be amended successfully.  (Id.)

 

            Finally, Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, requires that “[b]efore filing a demurrer pursuant to this chapter, the demurring party shall meet and confer in person or by telephone with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a).)  The parties are to meet and confer at least five days before the date the responsive pleading is due.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(2).)  Thereafter, the demurring party shall file and serve a declaration detailing their meet and confer efforts.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(3).) 

 

Motion to Strike Standard

 

            A party may move to strike matters from an opposing party’s pleading if it is irrelevant, false or improper.  (See Code Civ. Proc. §§ 435, 436, subd. (a).)  Motion to strike may also target all or any part of pleadings not filed or drawn in conformity with the applicable laws, rules or orders.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (b).)  A Motion to Strike is the proper procedure for testing the adequacy of a punitive damages allegation because punitive damages are not a cause of action.  (Grieves v. Superior Ct. (1984) 157 Cal.App.3d 159, 164.) 

 

            Prior to filing a motion to strike, the moving party is required to satisfy their meet and confer obligations pursuant to Code of Civ. Proc., § 435.5, and demonstrate that they so satisfied their meet and confer obligation by submitting a declaration pursuant to Code of Civ. Proc., § 435.5, subd. (a)(2) & (3).

 

Analysis

            Defendants demur to the third cause of action for violation of the unfair competition law and the fourth cause of action for negligence.  Defendants erroneously switched the numbering of each cause of action, referring to the negligence claim as the third cause of action and the unfair competition claim as the fourth cause of action.

 

            Defendants moves to strike Plaintiff’s (1) prayer for relief in connection with the unfair competition claim seeking an order of accounting; (2) prayer for relief in connection with the unfair competition claim seeking an order requiring Defendants to abstain from unfair business acts; (3) prayer for relief for punitive or exemplary damages; and (4) two times general and special damages per the Song Beverly Act.

 

            Third Cause of Action for Violation of Business and Professions Code §17200

           

            Defendants argue Plaintiff fails to plead a UCL claim, because (1) he fails to allege the purported wrongful conduct with particularity; (2) he fails to allege injury in fact or reliance; and (3) he has no available remedy under the UCL, which limits remedies to injunctive relief and restitution.

 

            In cases involving a direct competitor, an unfair business practice is narrowly defined. “When a plaintiff who claims to have suffered injury from a direct competitor's ‘unfair’ act or practice invokes section 17200, the word ‘unfair’ in that section means conduct that threatens an incipient violation of an antitrust law, or violates the policy or spirit of one of those laws because its effects are comparable to or the same as a violation of the law, or otherwise significantly threatens or harms competition.” Cel-Tech Communications, Inc. v. Los Angeles Cellular Telephone Co. (1999) 20 Cal.4th 163, 187.

 

            In consumer cases, it is unclear if the Cel-Tech definition also exclusively applies or if the broad pre-Cel-Tech definition applies: “[S]ection 17200 of the Business and Professions Code defines ‘unfair competition’ to include, as relevant, ‘unlawful, unfair or fraudulent business practice(s)’...It would be impossible to draft in advance detailed plans and specifications of all acts and conduct to be prohibited, since unfair or fraudulent business practices may run the gamut of human ingenuity and chicanery.” (Motors, Inc. v. Times Mirror Co. (1980) 102 Cal.App.3d 735, 740.)  Thus, whether a business is unfair is a fact intense inquiry that requires the Court to act as a court in equity. “It is really quite impossible” to make such a determination on demurrer.  (Id.)

 

“‘Unlawful business activity' proscribed under section 17200 includes anything that can properly be called a business practice and that at the same time is forbidden by law.”  (Farmers Ins. Exchange v. Supr. Ct. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 377, 383.)  An action under B&PC 17200 based on an unlawful business practice “borrows violations of other laws and treats these violations, when committed pursuant to business activity, as unlawful practices independently actionable under section 17200 et seq. and subject to the distinct remedies provided thereunder.”  (Id.)

 

“In order to state a cause of action under the fraud prong of the UCL a plaintiff need not show that he or others were actually deceived or confused by the conduct or business practice in question. “The ‘fraud’ prong of [the UCL] is unlike common law fraud or deception. A violation can be shown even if no one was actually deceived, relied upon the fraudulent practice, or sustained any damage. Instead, it is only necessary to show that members of the public are likely to be deceived.”  (Schnall v. Hertz Corp. (2000) 78 Cal.App.4th 1144, 1167.) 

 

By virtue of a 1992 amendment, the UCL covers single acts of misconduct.  (Klein v. Earth Elements, Inc. (1997) 59 Cal.App.4th 965, 969, fn. 3.)  A business “practice” can violate the UCL even if it affects only a single “victim.”  (Allied Grape Growers v. Bronco Wine Co. (1988) 203 Cal.App.3d 432, 452-453 (“There is nothing in the word ‘practice’ that necessarily limits section 17200 cases to those actions involving multiple victims”).) 

 

            Under B&PC 17203, only plaintiffs who have suffered injury capable of restitutionary disgorgement or have basis for injunctive relief may sue under Business and Professions Code section 17200. A plaintiff cannot obtain damages of any other kind.  (Korea Supply Co. v. Lockheed Martin Corp. (2003) 29 Cal.4th 1132, 1148.) 

 

            Plaintiff alleges conduct that is unlawful, unfair and fraudulent.  Plaintiff is alleging a UCL violation based on a consumer contract, not as a competitor.  Plaintiff alleges violations of the Song Beverly Act in the first and second causes of action, the sufficiency of which Defendants do not challenge.  Plaintiff also alleges Defendant Jaguar Land Rover North America intentionally and knowingly placed a defective vehicle into the stream of commerce without warning or remedying the defect, Defendants intentionally failed to diagnose the vehicle issues and Defendant Land Rover Pasadena knew that Defendant Jaguar Land Rover North America would not reimburse them for multiple repair attempts on the same issue.  (Complaint, ¶¶51-54.) 

 

            Plaintiff seeks “damages” for “injury in fact” based on loss of car value, money spent traveling to and from the dealership and hiring counsel to pursue warranty remedies.  (Complaint, ¶56.)  Such damages are consequential, special damages, which are not recoverable under 17200.  Plaintiff also includes other impermissible requests for relief in connection with the third cause of action, including an accounting.  Although Plaintiff alleges restitution in the amount equal to the price paid to Defendants in his prayer for relief in connection with the third cause of action, Plaintiff is only entitled to restitution or injunction.  The inclusion of the improper remedies is grounds for a motion to strike, however, not demurrer.

 

            Defendants’ demurrer to the third cause of action for violation of Business and Professions Code section 17200 is overruled.    

 

            Fourth Cause of Action for Negligence

           

            Defendants demur to the fourth cause of action for negligence on grounds that (1) Plaintiff fails to allege what the dealership allegedly did or failed to do; (2) the economic loss rule bars Plaintiff’s negligence claim; and (3) Plaintiff fails to allege facts demonstrating that Defendants caused Plaintiff’s damages. 

 

            The only named defendant in the fourth cause of action is defendant “Repair Facilities,” which Plaintiff defines as Land Rover Pasadena.  (Complaint, ¶5.)  Plaintiff alleges Defendant Land Rover Pasadena failed to “use ordinary care and skill by failing to properly store, diagnose and repair the Subject Vehicle in accordance with industry standards.”  (Id. at ¶61.) 

 

Defendant Jaguar Land Rover North America, LLC is not named in the fourth cause of action.  Plaintiff’s failure to allege any negligence against it is therefore not grounds for demurrer.

 

Defendants also fail to establish that Plaintiff’s negligence claim fails due to the economic loss rule.  “The economic loss rule is not a defense to a cause of action. Rather, the existence of damages other than purely economic loss is an element of a plaintiff's common law cause of action.”  (Greystone Homes, Inc. v. Midtec, Inc. (2008) 168 Cal.App.4th 1194, 1215.) 

 

The economic loss rule only applies to preclude tort recovery where the harm is purely financial, unaccompanied by any physical or property damage.  “The economic loss rule provides that, in general, there is no recovery in tort for negligently inflicted ‘purely economic losses,’ meaning financial harm unaccompanied by physical or property damage.”  (Dhital v. Nissan North America, Inc. (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 828, 837 (economic loss rule bars recovery for negligently inflicted economic losses “unaccompanied by…property damage”); Erlich v. Menezes (1999) 21 Cal.4th 543, 551 (recognizing long line of cases in which tort damages were allowed for a breach of contract where the conduct resulted in injury to person or property); Robinson Helicopter Co., Inc. (2004) 34 Cal.4th 979, 989-990 (economic loss rule does not bar tort damages where conduct is also a breach of contract where intentional tort is involved, where conduct violates duty independent of contractual duties, or in contract cases where breach of duty directly causes physical injury).)

 

Defendants fail to identify any allegation establishing that Plaintiff only suffered purely economic loss.  Plaintiff alleges that he suffered “damages,” which may or may not include damages other than purely economic loss. 

 

In addition, “[a] contract to perform services gives rise to a duty of care which requires that such services be performed in a competent and reasonable manner. A negligent failure to do so may be both a breach of contract and a tort.”  (North American Chemical Co. v. Superior Court (1997) 59 Cal.App.4th 764774; see also Michaelis v. Benavides (1998) 61 Cal.App.4th 681, 688; 3 Witkin, Cal. Proc. (6th ed. 2023) Actions §155 (“If a contract creates an interest protected by the law of torts, breach of the contract may constitute a tort, and the plaintiff’s election to sue in tort is generally recognized.”))

 

The Supreme Court in Erlich discussed North American by noting that “conduct amounting to a breach of contract becomes tortious only when it also violates a duty independent of the contract arising from principles of tort law.  An omission to perform a contract obligation is never a tort, unless that omission is also an omission of a legal duty.  (Erlich, supra, 21 Cal.4th at 551.)  The mere negligent breach of a contract is insufficient to state a claim for damages sounding in tort.  (Id.)   The remedy for a breach of contract is generally limited to contract law, and recovery in tort is not permitted unless: (1) the breach is accompanied by a traditional common law tort, such as fraud or conversion; (2) the means used to breach the contract are tortious, involving deceit or undue coercion or; (3) one party intentionally breaches the contract intending or knowing that such a breach will cause severe, unmitigable harm in the form of mental anguish, personal hardship, or substantial consequential damages.”  (Id. at pp. 553–554.)

 

Most recently, the Supreme Court in Sheen v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2022) 12 Cal.5th 905 acknowledged that consumer contracts for professional services can give rise to tort actions “even though they arise from, and are not independent of, a contract.”  (Sheen, supra, 12 Cal.5th 905, 929).  “It is true that we have, in certain contexts, allowed tort actions to proceed even though they arise from, and are not independent of, a contract, despite the economic loss rule. Specifically, we have allowed for tort recovery in some cases involving insurance policies and contracts for professional services.”  (Id. at 929.)

 

            Plaintiff has alleged a consumer contract for professional repair services.  Under Sheen and North America, Plaintiff is allowed to proceed on a negligence claim, despite the general rule that a plaintiff cannot recover in tort for a purely economic loss resulting from a breach of contract.

 

            Defendants’ demurrer to the fourth cause of action is overruled.

 

            Motion to Strike

 

            Defendants’ motion to strike the prayer for relief requesting punitive damages in connection with the fourth cause of action for negligence is granted.  Plaintiff does not allege any conduct that would rise to the level of malice, fraud or oppression.

 

            Defendants’ motion to strike the request for “an order requiring Defendant to account for all monies that they have received as a result of the acts and practices found to constitute unfair competition pursuant to Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code 17200 et seq.” is granted.  Relief under 17200 is limited to restitution and injunctive relief, not an accounting or nonrestitutionary disgorgement.  (Korea Supply Co. v. Lockheed Martin Corp. (2003) 29 Cal.4th 1132, 1148.) 

 

            Defendants’ motion to strike the request for “an order requiring Defendants to abstain

from any further acts and practices found to constitute unfair competition pursuant to Cal. Bus. &

Prof. Code 17200 et seq.” is granted.  Again, restitution or injunctive relief is the only relief allowed under 17200.  Requesting an order requiring Defendants’ abstention from any further acts of unfair competition would have the same effect as an injunction but it is not a clear and unambiguous request for injunctive relief. 

 

            Defendants’ motion to strike the requests for Song Beverly civil penalties is granted.  Defendants argue Plaintiff was required to plead compliance with Civil Code section 871.24 in order to seek civil penalties.  Defendants fails to cite any authority, including language from the statute itself, requiring Plaintiff to allege compliance with Civil Code section 871.24 in order to allege a claim for civil penalties.  However, the statute imposes pre-litigation notice requirements on the consumer and Plaintiff does not argue that he is not required to plead compliance with section 871.24 in order to allege a claim for civil penalties under Song Beverly.  Plaintiff also argues he complied with the statute.  Given these facts, Defendants motion to strike the request for civil penalties is granted with leave to amend.

 

            Plaintiff is also ordered to amend the complaint to resolve the inconsistencies regarding the Plaintiff’s identity, as well as the nature of the transaction (sale versus lease).

 

Conclusion

            Defendants’ Demurrer to the Third and Fourth Causes of action for violation of Business and Professions Code section 17200 and negligent repair is overruled. 

 

            Defendants’ Motion to Strike is (1) granted without leave as to the requests for punitive damages, “an order requiring Defendant to account for all monies that they have received as a result of the acts and practices found to constitute unfair competition pursuant to Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code 17200 et seq.,” and “an order requiring Defendants to abstain from any further acts and practices found to constitute unfair competition pursuant to Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code 17200 et seq. and (2) granted with 20 days leave as to the request for Song Beverly civil penalties. 

 

            Plaintiff is also ordered to amend the complaint to resolve ambiguities regarding the identify of the plaintiff and the nature of the underlying transaction, i.e. sale versus lease.
