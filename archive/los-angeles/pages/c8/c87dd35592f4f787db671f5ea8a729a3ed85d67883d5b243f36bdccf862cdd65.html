ANALYSIS:

 

I.               
Background

 

On January 21, 2026, Plaintiff Jamie
Merchain (“Plaintiff”) filed a Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”) against
Defendant Los Angeles Dodgers, LLC (“Defendant”) and Does 1-50, alleging causes
of action for Premises Liability and Negligence. The underlying action involves
Plaintiff tripping and falling over an “uplifted portion of raised asphalt,
believed to have been caused by an uplifted tree root… positioned at/near the
Centerfield entrance gate located in the Right Pavilion between the parking lot
and Dodger Stadium on April 20, 2022… The fall caused Plaintiff to suffer
severe bodily injuries.” (SAC, p. 2.)

 

On March 4, 2026, the Court held an
Informal Discovery Conference.

 

On June 1, 2026, Plaintiff filed the
instant Motion to Quash Deposition Subpoena for Production of Business Records
Served on Third-Party BD&J Law Firm (“BD&J Motion”); Motion to Quash
Deposition Subpoena for Production of Business Records Served on Third-Party
Jacob Seropian, Esq. (“Seropian Motion”); Motion to Quash Deposition Subpoena
of Business Records Served on Third-Party Martin Kanarek, Esq. (“Kanarek
Motion”); and Motion to Quash Deposition Subpoena of Business Records Served on
Third-Party the Barnes Law Firm (“Barnes Motion”) (the “Motions”).

 

On July 23, 2026, Defendants filed an
Omnibus Opposition to the Kanarek Motion, Seropian Motion, and BD&J Motion.

 

On July 24, 2026, Defendants filed an
Opposition to the Barnes Motion.

 

On July 29, 2026, Plaintiff filed Replies
in support of the BD&J Motion, Barnes Motion, Kanarek Motion, and Seropian
Motion. Plaintiff also filed a Supplemental Declaration in support of the
Kanarek Motion, BD&J Motion, and Seropian Motion.

 

II.             
Procedural
Issues

 

The Motions

 

As an initial matter, there is no
separate statement accompanying any of the Motions. (See Cal Rules of Court,
rule 3.1345, subd. (a)(5).) Thus, the Motions are procedurally deficient.

 

Further, the Motions fail to include
the electronic service addresses to which the Motions were served. However,
given the Oppositions filed addressing all four Motions, the Court exercises
its discretion to address the Motions on the merits.

 

Finally, the Court notes that the
Court’s reservation system states five motions are on calendar for the instant
hearing date; however, the Court’s docket reflects only four motions are
substantively set for hearing on the instant hearing date. Plaintiff is
cautioned to avoid duplicate entries in making motion reservations in the
Court’s system.

 

The Oppositions

 

The Court notes Defendants’ “Omnibus
Opposition” is improper given the separate and distinct nature of the Motions. The
Court also notes the Omnibus Opposition is just a memorandum of points and
authorities and fails to include any evidentiary support.

 

Additionally, the Barnes Opposition is
untimely filed and served under Code of Civil Procedure section 1005. However,
given the Reply filed, the Court exercises its discretion to address the Barnes
Opposition on the merits.

 

The Replies

 

Finally, the Court notes that the
Reply to the Seropian Motion includes an argument improperly raised for the
first time in the Reply brief – namely, the Seropian Subpoena was never served.
(See Seropian Reply, p. 2.)

 

III.           
Legal
Standard

 

Code of Civil Procedure section
1987.1, subdivision (a) provides, in pertinent part, as follows:

 

If a subpoena requires the attendance
of a witness or the production of books, documents, electronically stored
information, or other things before a court, or at the trial of an issue
therein, or at the taking of a deposition, the court, upon motion reasonably
made by any person described in subdivision (b), or upon the court’s own motion
after giving counsel notice and an opportunity to be heard, may make an order
quashing the subpoena entirely, modifying it, or directing compliance with it
upon those terms or conditions as the court shall declare, including protective
orders. In addition, the court may make any other order as may be appropriate
to protect the person from unreasonable or oppressive demands, including
unreasonable violations of the right of privacy of the person.

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.1, subd.
(a).)

 

IV.           
Discussion

 

A.    The Motions

 

Barnes Motion

 

Turning first to the Barnes Motion,
the Court notes that Defendants, the moving parties for the subpoena in
question, have stated a willingness to withdraw the subpoena as to Barnes. (See
Barnes Opp.)

 

Based on the subpoenaing party’s
willingness to withdraw the subpoena, the Court GRANTS the Barnes Motion.

 

BD&J Motion, Kanarek Motion, and
Seropian Motion

 

Next, the Court addresses the BD&J,
Kanarek, and Seropian Motions. The Court addresses these motions together given
that the subpoenas rendered to each non-party contain nearly identical requests;
the Motions are nearly identical; the nature of each non-party is similar in
kind in that they are all attorneys/law firms representing the same client in
separate litigation – namely, Maria De Los Angeles Perez (see BD&J,
Kanarek, and Seropian Motions, pp. 1-2, Koppelman Decls.); and Defendants filed
an Omnibus Opposition to all three Motions.

 

Plaintiff moves to quash the subpoenas
directed at third party BD&J, a law firm; third party Kanarek, an attorney
from BD&J; and Seropian, an attorney, on the grounds the subpoenas are
overbroad, seek irrelevant material, and seek communications and documents protected
by the attorney-client privilege and work product privilege as well as the
common interest doctrine. (BD&J, Kanarek, and Seropian Motions, pp. 4-8.)
Further, Plaintiff contends Defendants have not shown good cause of direct
relevance for the records sought in the BD&J, Kanarek, and Seropian
subpoenas. (BD&J, Kanarek, and Seropian Motions, pp. 8-9.) Should the Court
be disinclined to grant the Motions, Plaintiff requests the Court issue a
protective order modifying and limiting the subpoenas. (BD&J, Kanarek, and
Seropian Motions, pp. 8-9.) Finally, Plaintiff requests sanctions of $2,500-$4,000
be awarded against Defendants for each motion. (BD&J, Kanarek, and Seropian
Motions, pp. 9-10.)

 

In the Omnibus Opposition, Defendants state
Plaintiff has not met his burden to demonstrate the common interest doctrine
applies in this case at all let alone to all the requested documents in the
subpoenas. (Omnibus Opp., pp. 3-8.) Defendants further state the subpoenas are
not fishing expeditions, and sanctions should not be awarded as the subpoenas
were issued in good faith and seek discoverable information directly relevant
to the action. (Omnibus Opp., pp. 8-9.)

 

Here, the Court finds a majority of
the BD&J, Kanarek, and Seropian subpoena requests are overly broad in scope.
While the Court acknowledges there is some relevance to some of the subpoenas’
requests as they seek information from witnesses to the underlying incident in
this matter – namely, it is undisputed by the parties that Kanarek and Seropian
were witnesses to Plaintiff’s injury in the instant action (see Motions,
Koppelman Decls.) – the subpoenas effectively ask for all communications and
documents in each non-party’s possession relating to the instant litigation or the
non-parties’ case with their client Perez. Given the sweeping nature of these
requests, the Court finds the majority of the BD&J, Kanarek, and Seropian subpoenas
are unduly burdensome and thus unenforceable. (See e.g., Calcor Space
Facility, Inc. v. Sup. Ct. (Thiem Indus., Inc.) (1997) 53 Cal.App.4th 216,
222-223.)

 

Further, the Court finds that to the
extent the subpoenas request communications or documents between BD&J,
Kanarek, or Seropian and their client, Perez, such requests violate the
attorney-client and work product privileges. (See Evid. Code, § 952.)

 

Insofar as the subpoenas request communications
between BD&J, Kanarek, and Seropian and Plaintiff’s counsel in this matter
regarding Plaintiff, Defendants, or this action, the Court proceeds to address
the work product privilege and the applicability of the common interest
doctrine in an abundance of caution.

 

The ‘joint defense privilege’ and the ‘common
interest privilege’ have not been recognized by statute in California. [Citations.]
For this reason, we will refer to the joint defense or common interest doctrine,
rather than the joint defense or common interest privilege, to avoid suggesting
that communications between parties with common interests are protected from
disclosure by virtue of a privilege separate from the attorney-client
privilege, the work product doctrine, or any other statutorily recognized
evidentiary privilege.

…

[T]he party seeking to invoke the
doctrine must first establish that the communicated information would
otherwise be protected from disclosure by a claim of privilege. For example,
the content of the communication may comprise information shared in confidence
by a client with his or her attorney, a legal opinion formed and advice given
by the lawyer in the course of the attorney-client relationship, or a writing
reflecting an attorney's impressions, conclusions, or theories. (See Evid.
Code, § 952; Code Civ. Proc., § 2018, subd. (c).) The next step in the
analysis is to determine whether disclosing the information to a party outside
the attorney-client relationship waived any applicable privileges.

…

‘There is no statutory provision
governing waiver of work product protection. [Citation.]’ [Citation.] However,
California courts have recognized that the waiver doctrine is applicable to the
work product rule as well as the attorney-client privilege. [Citation.] The
work product protection may be waived ‘by the attorney's disclosure or consent
to disclosure to a person, other than the client, who has no interest in
maintaining the confidentiality … of a significant part of the work product.’
[Citation.] Thus, work product protection ‘is not waived except by a disclosure
wholly inconsistent with the purpose of the privilege, which is to safeguard
the attorney's work product and trial preparation.’

            …

Thus, ‘[f]or the common interest
doctrine to attach, most courts seem to insist that the two parties have in
common an interest in securing legal advice related to the same matter—and
that the communications be made to advance their shared interest in securing
legal advice on that common matter.’

 

(OXY Resources California LLC v.
Superior Court (2004) 115 Cal.App.4th 874, 889-891, as modified.)

 

Applying these principles to the
instant circumstances, the Court finds the communications or documents sought
by Defendants’ subpoenas between the subject non-parties and Plaintiff’s
counsel meet the common interest doctrine requirements.

 

First, the subpoenas’ requested
communications or documentations are so broadly phrased that they encapsulate
protected attorney work product of both the non-parties and Plaintiff – e.g., writings
reflecting an attorney’s impressions, conclusions, opinions, legal research, or
theories. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2018.030.)

 

Indeed, the Court finds the requests
here are broader than other requests where appellate courts have found a
party’s refusal to produce all requested documents to a nonparty law firm
unjustified due to overbreadth. (See Getz v. Superior Court (2021) 72
Cal.App.5th 637, 657-658.) For example, in Getz, the Third District
Court of Appeal found that the common interest doctrine only applied to some of
the requested emails from defendant to a nonparty law firm, not all, and thus defendant’s
refusal to “produce the text of the 42,852 indexed emails” on grounds of
overbreadth was unjustified. (Id., at p. 657.) By contrast, the subpoena
requests here fail to even specify the kind or manner of communications
or documents sought from the subject non-parties and instead make sweeping
requests that are impossible to meaningfully and narrowly distinguish or
differentiate. As such, the Court finds the first requirement of the common
interest doctrine is satisfied.

 

Next, both Plaintiff’s counsel and BD&J,
Kanarek, and Seropian in this matter have in common an interest in securing
legal advice related to the same matter of their clients who suffered harm from
an identical uplift on Defendants’ property in close temporal proximity, and
these communications to each other were made to advance their shared interest
in securing legal advice and strategy on the common matter of Defendants’
liability for the uplift. (See id.; BD&J, Kanarek, and Seropian Motion,
pp. 6-7, Koppelman Decls.)

 

Further, the non-parties and
Plaintiff’s counsel did not waive the work product protection as each had an
interest in maintaining the confidentiality of a significant part of the work
product given that there was “pooled discovery, shared photographs and video,
deposition strategy on common witnesses, and analysis of Defendant’s policies”
between the non-parties and Plaintiff’s counsel that were exchanged to further “efficient
and informed prosecution of overlapping claims.” (BD&J, Kanarek, Seropian Motions,
pp. 6-7, Koppelman Decls.; OXY Resources California LLC, supra,
115 Cal.App.4th at pp. 889-991.)

 

Accordingly, the Court finds Plaintiff
has satisfactorily shown the common interest doctrine applies to the
communications and documents between the subject non-parties and Plaintiff’s
counsel sought by Defendants’ subpoenas.

 

To the extent that Defendants state BD&J,
Kanarek, and Seropian are required to produce at least some requested documents
in response to the subpoenas or request an Evidence Code section 915 in-camera
hearing with the Court regarding the alleged protected/privileged materials, the
Court finds an overly broad and unduly burdensome subpoena does not warrant
compliance. Further, the Court may not order disclosure of a communication
claimed to be privileged to allow a ruling on the claim of privilege. (See Costco
Wholesale Corp. v. Superior Court (2009) 47 Cal.4th 725, 739.)

 

Finally, while a majority of the
subpoena requests are overbroad and/or impermissibly infringe on privileges,
the Court finds that certain of the subpoena requests warrant a privilege log. Specifically,
the Court finds that Request Nos. 1, 8, 13, 14, 17, and 20 in the BD&J,
Kanarek, and Seropian subpoenas warrant a privilege log to ascertain the
validity of the asserted privilege claims.

 

For all these reasons, the
Motions are GRANTED IN PART AND CONTINUED IN PART.

 

The Motions are GRANTED as
to all items except for the 1st, 8th, 13th, 14th, 17th, and 20th requests in
all the subject subpoenas.

 

The Motions are CONTINUED
as to these six items. The moving party on each respective motion to quash is
to produce a privilege log as to Request Nos. 1, 8, 13, 14, 17, and 20 in the
BD&J, Kanarek, and Seropian subpoenas. Thereafter, the parties are to
submit supplemental briefing prior to the next scheduled hearing to address whether
any dispute regarding privilege still exists as to these items.

ANALYSIS:

 

I.                Background  

 

On March 13, 2026, Plaintiff Ethan He (“Plaintiff”) filed the instant action against Defendants Citibank, N.A. (“Citibank”) and Miguel Valenzuela (“Valenzuela”) (“Defendants”) and Does 1-80, alleging eight causes of action arising from Plaintiff’s employment with Defendants, which began in April 2020. (Compl., pp. 1-3.)

 

On April 10, 2026, Plaintiff filed a First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) against Defendants and Does 1-80, alleging eight causes of action arising from Plaintiff’s employment with Defendants.

On May 15, 2026, Defendants filed an Answer.

 

            On June 18, 2026, Defendants filed the instant Motion to Compel Arbitration and to Stay Action (the “Motion”).

 

On July 23, 2026, Plaintiff filed an Opposition and Evidentiary Objections to the Sadloski declaration filed in support of the Motion.

 

On July 29, 2026, Defendants filed a Reply and a Supplemental Declaration.

 

II.              Evidentiary Objections

 

In conjunction with the Opposition, Plaintiff submits various objections to the declaration of Mark Sadloski filed in conjunction with the Motion. (Opp., Evid. Objections, pp. 1-53.)

 

The Court OVERRULES all objections to the Sadloski declaration as the Court finds the statements challenged contain sufficient personal knowledge and foundation and are relevant.

 

III.            Legal Standard & Discussion

 

Defendants bring the instant Motion seeking to compel Plaintiff to submit to arbitration based on the arbitration clause (“Arbitration Agreement”) included in Plaintiff’s employment contract with Defendants. (Motion, pp. 10-16.)

 

A.    Governing Law

 

Here, the Arbitration Agreement expressly states it is governed by the Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”). (Motion, Sadloski Decl., Exh., B, ¶ 21.)

 

The Federal Arbitration Act, set forth at 9 USC, section 2, provides: 

 

A written provision in any maritime transaction or a contract evidencing a transaction involving commerce to settle by arbitration a controversy thereafter arising out of such contract or transaction, or the refusal to perform the whole or any part thereof, or an agreement in writing to submit to arbitration an existing controversy arising out of such a contract, transaction, or refusal, shall be valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, save upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract. 

 

      (9 U.S.C., § 2.)  

 

      Accordingly, under both the FAA and California Law, arbitration agreements are valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, except on such grounds that exist at law or equity for voiding a contract. (Winter v. Window Fashions Professions, Inc. (2008) 166 Cal.App.4th 943, 947.)

 

 Thus, the Court applies the FAA’s procedural rules, and California law when appropriate and when not preempted by the FAA. (See e.g., Western Bagel Co. v. Superior Court (Calderon) (2021) 66 Cal.App.5th 649, 664-666.)

 

B.    Arbitrability

 

In the Reply, Defendants assert for the first time that there is an express delegation clause in the parties’ Agreement that delegates enforceability determinations to the arbitrator. (Reply, p. 5.)

 

While this argument is improperly raised for the first time in the Reply, the Court addresses this contention in an abundance of caution.

 

“In general, when a motion to compel arbitration is filed and accompanied by prima facie evidence of a written agreement to arbitrate the controversy, the trial court must determine whether a valid agreement exists and, if any defense is asserted, whether the agreement is enforceable. [Citation.] The trial court sits as a trier of fact, weighing the evidence submitted to reach a final determination. [Citation.]” (Wright v. WellQuest Elk Grove, LLC (2026) 119 Cal.App.5th 267, 274.)

 

However, parties can agree to arbitrate certain questions of arbitrability such as “whether the parties have agreed to arbitrate or whether their agreement covers a particular controversy.” (Id., at pp. 274-275, citations and quotations omitted.) A delegation clause must be clear and unmistakable and must not be revocable under state contract defenses. (Id., at p. 274.)

 

Here, the Arbitration Agreement states, in pertinent part:

 

Except as otherwise provided by this Policy, the arbitrator shall interpret and apply these procedures as they relate to the arbitrator's powers and duties; all other procedures shall be interpreted and applied by the AAA or FINRA, as applicable. Except as otherwise expressly agreed upon, and except as otherwise provided by this Policy, any dispute as to the arbitrability of a particular claim made pursuant to this Policy shall be resolved in arbitration.

 

(Motion, Sadloski Decl., Exh., B, ¶ 27.)

 

The Court finds the Arbitration Agreement is “silent as to the issues of interpretation, enforceability, and unconscionability of the agreement.” (See Wright, supra, 119 Cal.App.5th at pp. 276-277.) While a link to the AAA rules is included in the Arbitration Agreement, the Court does not find this reference alone is enough under the circumstances to constitute a clear delegation clause. As such, the Court “cannot find that language reflects the parties’ clear and unmistakable intent to delegate all issues of arbitrability to the arbitrator.” (See id., at p. 277.) Accordingly, the Court proceeds to address the existence of the Arbitration Agreement and defenses to its enforcement.

 

C.    Existence of Arbitration Agreement

 

In deciding a motion to compel arbitration, trial courts must first decide whether an enforceable arbitration agreement exists between the parties, and then determine the second gateway issue of whether the claims are covered within the scope of the agreement. (See Chiron Corp. v. Ortho Diagnostic Systems, Inc. (2000) 207 F.3d 1126, 1130 [“The court’s role under the [FAA] is therefore limited to determining (1) whether a valid agreement to arbitrate exists and, if it does, (2) whether the agreement encompasses the dispute at issue”].)

 

“The burden of persuasion is always on the moving party to prove the existence of an arbitration agreement with the opposing party by a preponderance of the evidence.” (Gamboa v. Northeast Community Clinic (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 158, 165.)

 

Defendants present an authenticated copy of the Agreement, which incorporates by reference the Arbitration Agreement, between the parties purportedly bearing Plaintiff’s signature and thus meets its initial burden. (Motion, Sadloski Decl., Exhs. A-B; see Bannister v. Marinidence Opco, LLC (2021) 64 Cal.App.5th 541, 543-544 [The moving party “can meet its initial burden by attaching to the [motion or] petition a copy of the arbitration agreement purporting to bear the [opposing party's] signature”].)

 

The burden then shifts to Plaintiff as Plaintiff challenges the existence of the Arbitration Agreement by contesting the validity of Plaintiff’s signature. (Gamboa, supra, 72 Cal.App.5th at p. 165 [“If the moving party meets its initial prima facie burden and the opposing party disputes the agreement, then in the second step, the opposing party bears the burden of producing evidence to challenge the authenticity of the agreement].) Plaintiff submits a declaration by Plaintiff made under oath and disputes the Arbitration Agreement’s existence on the grounds that Plaintiff does not recall signing the agreement and/or never signed the agreement. (Opp., pp. 1-3, 7-10, He Decl., ¶¶ 7-16.)

 

The Court notes that there is a split of authority in California as to the legal effect of a plaintiff’s lack of recall of signing an agreement (see Gamboa, supra, 72 Cal.App.5th at p. 167; but see Iyere v. Wise Auto Group (2023) 87 Cal.App.5th 747, 757-758; Ramirez v. Golden Queen Mining Co. LLC (2024) 102 Cal.App.5th 821); however, the Second District Court of Appeal has taken the approach that an employee’s lack of recollection in signing the agreement is enough to meet the employee’s burden to challenge the signature’s authenticity, regardless of whether the signature is electronic or handwritten. (See Gamboa, supra, 72 Cal.App.5th at p. 167.) As such, the Court finds Plaintiff’s lack of recollection in electronically signing the agreement, made under penalty of perjury, is enough to meet Plaintiff’s burden to challenge the signature’s authenticity. (See id., at pp. 165, 167.) Further, Plaintiff also testifies that Plaintiff never signed and/or does not remember signing the Arbitration Agreement, which also suffices to challenge the authenticity of the Arbitration Agreement. (See id., at p. 165.)

 

The burden shifts to Defendants to rebut the challenge by establishing by a preponderance of evidence that the agreement was valid. The Court finds Defendants have met their burden. Defendants have submitted admissible evidence of two declarations from Mark Sadloski, a Group Manager at Citibank that previously worked in Citibank’s HR Shared Services, who has personal knowledge of Citibank’s hiring and onboarding process and Citibank’s Workday online electronic software system; notably, Workday is Citibank’s human resources information system, “which houses information regarding Citibank applicants and employees, including but not limited to signed policies and agreements.” (See Motion, Sadloski Decl., ¶¶ 2-3; Reply, Supp. Sadloski Decl., ¶¶ 1-3.) Further, Citibank “uses Workday to electronically send applicants documents to view and sign during the application process, and to employees at the commencement of their employment and during the course of their employment.” (Motion, Sadloski Decl., ¶ 4.) Sadloski describes that Citibank uses Workday as part of its onboarding process, and Workday requires employees, including Plaintiff, to create a unique login and password known only to the employee and is associated only with the employee’s unique email address. (See Motion, Sadloski Decl., ¶¶ 5-8, 10; Reply, Supp. Sadloski Decl., ¶¶ 6-7.) Sadloski attests that “Citibank cannot edit the policies and agreements once they have been signed by the prospective employee.” (Motion, Sadloski Decl., ¶ 8.) Once onboarding documents are signed, the employee signature is assigned an identifying code – for Plaintiff’s signature of the 2020 Agreement, Arbitration Policy, Citibank’s Principles of Employment by DocuSign, and Citibank’s 2017 U.S. Employee Handbook, the code assigned to Plaintiff is 10732A9AA6A148B. (Motion, Sadloski Decl., ¶¶ 11-15, Exhs. A-D; Reply, Supp. Sadloski Decl., ¶¶ 8-13, Exhs. A-E.) For Plaintiff’s signature of the 2022 revised Citibank U.S. Employee Handbook, “which acknowledged both his consent to abide by the handbook and the Employment Arbitration Policy attached as an appendix to the handbook,” the code assigned to Plaintiff is 915B39B268B74A0. (Motion, Sadloski Decl., ¶¶ 17-18, Exh. E; Reply, Supp. Sadloski Decl., ¶¶ 15-16, Exh. F.)

 

Plaintiff contends Defendants’ evidence is insufficient as Plaintiff’s signature differs from that on the onboarding documents; Plaintiff never met or worked with Mark Sadloski; Sadloski lacks personal knowledge of Plaintiff’s actions; and Citibank’s contentions are inconsistent with its own documentation of Plaintiff’s employment records.  (Opp., pp. 5-10.)

 

The Court finds that Defendants have sufficiently demonstrated that Plaintiff’s electronic signature was the act of Plaintiff as Defendants adequately made “ ‘a showing of the efficacy of any security procedure applied to determine the person to which the electronic record or electronic signature was attributable.’ ” (Ruiz, supra, 232 Cal.App.4th at p. 843, citing Code. Civ. Proc., § 1633.9, subd. (a).) As such, the Court finds that Sadloski’s declarations are distinguishable from those found to be deficient, such as the declarations present in Ruiz.

 

In Ruiz, the appellate court affirmed the trial court’s denial of a motion to compel arbitration. (See Ruiz, supra, 232 Cal.App.4th 836.) In so holding, the appellate court determined that the defendant failed to meet its evidentiary burden to authenticate plaintiff’s signature as defendant’s supporting declarations merely included unsupported assertions that plaintiff was the person who electronically signed the arbitration agreement. (See Ruiz, supra, 232 Cal.App.4th at pp. 843-844.) By contrast, here, Sadloski’s declarations provide a detailed explanation of how Plaintiff’s electronic signature came to be placed on the Arbitration Agreement, including that Plaintiff’s electronic signature could only have been placed on the Arbitration Agreement by using Plaintiff’s unique login and password; that all Citibank employees were required to use their unique login and password when they logged into the Workday and signed onboarding forms and agreements; and the electronic signature on the 2020 Arbitration Agreement was therefore apparently made by Plaintiff. (See id., at p. 844; Motion, Sadloski Decl.; Reply, Supp. Sadloski Decl.)

 

Thus, the Court finds Defendants’ evidence adequately rebuts Plaintiff’s challenges and demonstrates the authenticity of Plaintiff’s signature on the Arbitration Agreement.

 

Finally, Plaintiff does not dispute that Plaintiff’s claims in the FAC are within the scope of the Arbitration Agreement.

 

Accordingly, the Court finds Defendants met their burden to prove the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by a preponderance of the evidence.

 

D.    Enforceability – Unconscionability

 

“ ‘The prevailing view is that [procedural and substantive unconscionability] must¿both¿be present in order for a court to exercise its discretion to refuse to enforce a contract or clause under the doctrine of unconscionability.’ [Citation] But they need not be present in the same degree. “Essentially a sliding scale is invoked which disregards the regularity of the procedural process of the contract formation, that creates the terms, in proportion to the greater harshness or unreasonableness of the substantive terms themselves.” . . . In other words, the more substantively oppressive the contract term, the less evidence of procedural unconscionability is required to come to the conclusion that the term is unenforceable, and vice versa.” (Armendariz v. Foundation Health Psychcare Services, Inc.¿(2000) 24 Cal.4th 83, 114 (“Armendariz”).) 

 

Procedural Unconscionability

 

Plaintiff contends there is a high level of procedural unconscionability in this matter given that the Arbitration Agreement is an adhesion contract; Plaintiff was under pressure by Defendants to complete the paperwork; Defendants did not inform Plaintiff of the hyperlink to the AAA rules; Plaintiff was not presented with any documents other than his four-page offer letter; and the Offer Letter’s “attachments” involve shocking and surprising terms, such as the Additional Terms Addendum. (Opp., pp. 10-13.)

 

In Reply, Defendants contend that Plaintiff failed to show the Agreement is adhesive; Plaintiff acknowledged the Agreement and Addendum as attachments to the Offer Letter, which was signed by Plaintiff on March 24, 2020; and Plaintiff received sufficient information regarding AAA rules and, moreover, Plaintiff does not challenge the AAA Rules or the Agreement’s reliance on those rules. (Reply, pp. 5-7.)

 

“ ‘Procedural unconscionability’ concerns the manner in which the contract was negotiated and the circumstances of the parties at that time. It focuses on factors of oppression and surprise. The oppression component arises from an inequality of bargaining power of the parties to the contract and an absence of real negotiation or a meaningful choice on the part of the weaker party. The component of surprise arises when the challenged terms are ‘hidden in a prolix printed form drafted by the party seeking to enforce them.’ ” (Nyulassy v. Lockheed Martin Corp. (2004) 120 Cal.App.4th 1267, 1281.) 

 

As to procedural unconscionability, the Court finds there is inequality in bargaining power between Plaintiff and Citibank given the nature of their roles that makes it likely that the Plaintiff felt at least some pressure to sign the Arbitration Agreement. (See Sanchez v. Western Pizza Enterprises, Inc. (2009) 172 Cal.App.4th 154, 174.)

 

Further, the instant Arbitration Agreement appears to be an adhesion contract; however, “ ‘the fact that the arbitration agreement is an adhesion contract does not render it automatically unenforceable.’ ” (Carlson v. Home Team Pest Defense Inc. (2015) 239 Cal.App.4th 619, 631.) An adhesion contract alone “ ‘establish[es] only a modest degree of procedural unconscionability.’ ” (Nguyen, supra, 4 Cal.App.5th at p. 248.)

 

Besides alleging inequality of bargaining power and an adhesion contract, the latter of which pertains to the oppressiveness of the Agreement (see Nyulassy, supra, 120 Cal.App.4th at p. 1281), the Court finds Plaintiff’s other challenges to the Arbitration Agreement do not demonstrate any level, let alone a high level, of oppression or surprise.

 

The Court finds that Plaintiff’s failure to read the Arbitration Agreement does not demonstrate a high level of procedurally unconscionability. (See Ramirez, supra, Cal.App.3d at p. 754; see also Iyere v. Wise Auto Group (2023) 87 Cal.App.5th 747, 759 [“It is hornbook law that failing to read an agreement before signing it does not prevent formation of a contract”].) Moreover, Plaintiff was required to act diligently before signing the Arbitration Agreement, and Defendants were not required to explain the Arbitration Agreement to Plaintiff. (See Rowland, supra, (1992) 4 Cal.App.4th at p. 286.)

 

As to Plaintiff’s challenge to the Arbitration Agreement on the grounds that the AAA rules were not highlighted or included in the Arbitration Agreement, the Court finds “Plaintiff does not claim anything was hidden in the [AAA] rules. Rather, [Plaintiff] contests only matters related to the agreement itself. Therefore… the failure to attach the applicable [AAA] rules did not increase the procedural unconscionability of the… arbitration provision.” (Nguyen, supra, 4 Cal.App.5th at p. 249.)

 

To the extent Plaintiff asserts he was externally pressured to sign the Arbitration Agreement, the Court finds Plaintiff’s evidence refutes this assertion. Indeed, Plaintiff’s declaration filed in support of the Opposition states Plaintiff received the onboarding documents in a link, and Plaintiff “was alone when [Plaintiff] opened the link.” (Opp., He Decl., ¶ 5.) The Court finds these circumstances do not demonstrate pressure or time constraints for Plaintiff to sign the Arbitration Agreement. Insofar as Plaintiff states Plaintiff opened the link using his personal cell phone, which had a small screen, the Court is not persuaded that Plaintiff’s apparent choice of using his phone to open the onboarding documents, including the Arbitration Agreement, demonstrates a high level of procedural unconscionability on behalf of Defendants. (Opp., He Decl., ¶ 5.) Indeed, Plaintiff provides no evidence stating that Plaintiff was unable to access a larger screen to review the paperwork at the time the Arbitration Agreement was signed. Further, Plaintiff does not provide any evidence or argument that the Arbitration Agreement or Agreement was hard to read on the phone screen, the paragraphs were dense, or the referenced attachments were not provided, which distinguishes the instant circumstances from those found in the case law relied upon by Plaintiff in the Opposition – namely, Hasty v. American Automobile Assn. etc. (2023) 98 Cal.App.5th 1041, 1056-1058.

 

Notably, the Court also highlights that Plaintiff does not dispute that the Arbitration Agreement itself was clear and not difficult to read.

 

Finally, the Court finds Plaintiff’s unsupported contention that the Additional Terms Addendum included with the Agreement demonstrates a high level of procedural unconscionability unpersuasive and insufficiently supported. (See Opp., pp. 12-13.)

 

Thus, the Court finds that the circumstances surrounding Plaintiff’s signing of this Agreement and Arbitration Agreement contain a low, or at most moderate, level of procedural unconscionability.

 

The Court now proceeds to address whether the Arbitration Agreement is substantively unconscionable.

 

Substantive Unconscionability

 

In Opposition, Plaintiff contends the Arbitration Agreement is substantively unconscionable on the following grounds: (1) the Arbitration Agreement contains an impermissible representative and/or PAGA claim waiver; (2) the Arbitration Agreement requires waiver of administrative remedies; (3) the Agreement requires overly broad confidentiality of all employment-related arbitrations; (4) the Arbitration Agreement reserves a one-sided carve-out for Defendant to sue Plaintiff in court; (4) the Arbitration Agreement requires Plaintiff to agree in advance to broad and unlimited remedies to Citibank; (5) the Arbitration Agreement requires waiver of Citibank’s need to show irreparable harm to obtain forms of judicial relief; (6) the Arbitration Agreement requires Plaintiff to pay arbitration-related expenses; (7) the Arbitration Agreement contains an illegal cost-shifting clause; and (8) the Arbitration Agreement does not recognize tolling of statutes of limitation that would otherwise be effective in court. (Opp., pp. 13-16.)

 

In Reply, Defendants contend that Plaintiff has not asserted any representative or PAGA claims; the confidentiality provision is not unconscionable and permitted by case law; “the Agreement is mutual, requires both parties to arbitrate any dispute, and the terms and conditions are mutually binding. Furthermore, the Agreement is not only mutual on its face, it also is mutual in application; meaning, it does not exempt claims from arbitration that Citibank is more likely to bring”; “Plaintiff’s arguments about costs unique arbitration are misguided, given that the Agreement requires Citibank agreed to pay all arbitrator and arbitration fees”; and applicable law dismisses Plaintiff’s contentions regarding the injunctive relief provision in the Arbitration Agreement. (Reply, pp. 7-9.)

 

Substantive unconscionability focuses on the actual terms of the agreement and evaluates whether¿the terms¿create “overly harsh” or “one-sided” results as to shock the conscience.¿ (Armendariz, supra, 24 Cal.4th at p. 114.)

 

As to substantive unconscionability, the Court finds the Agreement only holds a low or at most moderate level of substantive unconscionability.  

 

First, the Court finds Plaintiff’s contentions regarding the expenses and fees provision of the Arbitration Award are unpersuasive and mischaracterized as this provision of the Arbitration Agreement provides that Citibank will pay for all fees and costs associated with arbitration with certain exceptions.

 

Next, the Court finds Plaintiff’s challenge to the confidentiality provision in the Arbitration Agreement is insufficiently supported as to the specific circumstances in this action. Plaintiff’s reliance on Murrey v. Superior Court (2023) 87 Cal.App.5th 1223, 1253-1254 appears inapt and is unpersuasive as there are many distinguishing characteristics between Murrey and the instant matter, including the terms of the respective confidentiality provisions and the causes of action at issue.

 

Further, the Court finds Plaintiff mischaracterizes the terms of the Agreement as it pertains to numerous of the remaining arguments, including the parties’ ability to seek injunctive relief from a court of competent jurisdiction – contrary to Plaintiff’s representations in the Opposition, the Arbitration Agreement states this ability is mutually permitted for both Plaintiff and Citibank. (See Motion, Sadloski Decl., Exh. B, p. 3.) Thus, the Court does not find these arguments demonstrate a high level of substantive unconscionability.

 

As to Plaintiff’s contentions that the Arbitration Agreement contains an unconscionable waiver of administrative remedies, the Court agrees. (See Hasty, supra, 98 Cal.App.5th at pp. 1060-1061.)

 

As to Plaintiff’s allegations regarding an impermissible representative waiver in the Arbitration Agreement, the parties do not dispute that there is not a representative or PAGA claim alleged in the FAC. (See FAC.; Opp.; Reply) The Court finds that in the instant case, an impermissible representative or PAGA waiver would be one of two surviving grounds for Plaintiff’s assertion that the Arbitration Agreement is substantively unconscionable which could be severed from the Arbitration Agreement given that the Arbitration Agreement is not permeated with unconscionability. (See Subcontracting Concepts (CT), LLC v. De Melo (2019) 34 Cal.App.5th 201, 215; Viking River Cruises, Inc. v. Moriana (2022) 596 U.S. 639.)

 

Under the circumstances, the Court finds that, at most, the Arbitration Agreement contains a low or moderate level of substantive unconscionability.

 

“As with any contract, the unconscionability inquiry requires a court to examine the totality of the agreement's substantive terms as well as the circumstances of its formation to determine whether the overall bargain was unreasonably one-sided.” (Sonic-Calabasas A, Inc. v. Moreno (2013) 57 Cal. App. 4th 1109, 1146.) 

 

Based on the foregoing, the Motion is GRANTED.
