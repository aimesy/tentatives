MOTION TO BE RELIEVED AS COUNSEL

 

Background

On November 14, 2022, Plaintiffs Xiang Hao Cui (“Cui”), for himself and derivatively on behalf Nominal Defendant Stanford Plaza Association, Inc., 800 East Pico Blvd., LLC (“800 East Pico”), 808 East Pico Blvd., LLC (“808 East Pico”), and Stanford Plaza Holdings, LLC filed this action against Defendants Dae Yong Lee aka David Lee, Hellen Lee, Young Sung Cho, William Hong, Stanford Pico Plaza, LLC, and Secured Properties Management Group, Inc. (“SPMG”) (collectively, “Defendants”) and Nominal Defendant Stanford Plaza Association, Inc. (the “Association”).

The complaint alleges six causes of action for (1) removal of directors, (2) breach of fiduciary duty, (3) aiding and abetting breach of fiduciary duty, (4) accounting, (5) declaratory relief, and (6) injunctive relief.

On February 27, 2025, the Court granted Defendants’ motion for summary judgment as to Cui only, which summarily dismissed the complaint’s first cause of action for removal of directors because Plaintiff Cui, who was the sole plaintiff on that claim, lacked standing. (Minute Order 2/27/25.)

Brian M. Grossman and Robert Paredes of Tesser Grossman LLP (jointly, “Counsel”) moves to be relieved as counsel for Plaintiffs Cui, 800 East Pico, 808 East Pico, and Stanford Plaza Holdings LLC (collectively, “Plaintiffs”). Defendants filed both an opposition and an amended opposition. Counsel replied and filed a request to strike or disregard Defendants’ amended opposition.

Request for Judicial Notice

The Court grants Plaintiffs’ request for judicial notice of Exhibit A.

Discussion

A.    Procedural Issues

As a preliminary matter, Defendants filed an amended opposition, in addition to their original opposition. Counsel seeks to strike or request that the Court disregard this, based on California Rules of Court, rule 3.1112. Defendants assert that “Rule 3.1112 enumerates the papers that may be filed in support of or in opposition to a motion; neither an ‘amended opposition’ nor a ‘sur-reply’ appears among them. The reply is the final brief the rules contemplate, and it belongs to the moving party. There is no provision permitting the opposing party to file a second opposition, much less one filed after the movant’s reply, without first obtaining leave of court.” (Obj. to Add. Opp., 2:16-20.) Further, Counsel asserts that “[a] filing is judged by its substance, not its caption. The Amended Papers change one thing of substance: they remove Defendants’ reliance on the vacated Sergenian judgment – the precise point the Firm raised for the first time in its Reply – and replace it with a different document and a different number. That is the defining feature of a sur-reply: a brief whose purpose is to answer the reply. Relabeling that response an ‘Amended Opposition’ does not change what it is.” (Obj. to Add. Opp., 3:3-7.) Defendants’ amended opposition was filed after Counsel filed their reply papers. Because of the timing, the amended opposition serves as a sur-reply rather than merely correcting an inadvertent error. Accordingly, the Court declines to consider Defendants’ amended opposition.

B.    Discussion

“The attorney in an action or special proceeding may be changed at any time before or after judgment or final determination … [u]pon the order of the court, upon the application of either client or attorney, after notice from one to the other.” ((Code Civ. Proc., § 284, subd. (2).) California Rules of Court, rule 3.1362 prescribes the procedural requirements an attorney must follow when moving to be relieved as counsel.

Rule 1.16(b) of the State Bar Rules of Professional Conduct allows an attorney to permissively withdrawal from representing a client, inter alia, if “the client by other conduct renders it unreasonably difficult for the lawyer to carry out the representation effectively; … the inability to work with co-counsel indicates that the best interests of the client likely will be served by withdrawal; [or] the lawyer believes in good faith, in a proceeding pending before a tribunal, that the tribunal will find the existence of other good cause for withdrawal.” When “the duty not to reveal confidences prevent[s] counsel from further disclosure and the court accept[s] the good faith of counsel’s representations, the court should find the conflict sufficiently established and permit withdrawal.” ((Aceves v. Superior Court (1996) 51 Cal.App.4th 584, 592.)

Counsel contends that there has been a breakdown of the attorney-client relationship, but the nature of which is confidential. Counsel attests that, if the Court wishes, they consent to “an in camera hearing outside the presence of all other parties” and will provide the Court with “specific facts demonstrating good cause.” (Decl. in Support, ¶ 2.)

Defendants oppose the instant motions, contending that Cui is intentionally trying to delay the trial. Defendants state that, although Counsel’s “motion does not state the reason for the breakdown in their attorney-client relationship, it appears that Plaintiffs’ failure to pay [Counsel’s] fees is the root of the problem. But Plaintiffs’ apparent decision not to pay their counsel is not for lack of money.” (Opp., 4:17-20.) Defendants point to Jose Villacreses’ declaration, the owner of VIP Unlimited that provides security services to Stanford Plaza, who attests that, after asking Cui when the lawsuit would be resolved, “Mr. Cui laughed and said that he could delay the trial for another year if he wanted.” (Villacreses Decl., ¶ 4.)

Defendants further assert that “Plaintiffs would rather withhold payments to their own counsel, while simultaneously claiming to pay Association vendors and utilities. In doing so, Plaintiffs are attempting to force the withdrawal of their counsel, thereby thumbing their nose at our judicial process and attempting to force a continuance of the July 15, 2026 trial. Finally, this is not the first time that Plaintiffs have forced the withdrawal of their counsel and attempted to delay the proceedings in this action. On May 3, 2024, just a month prior to Defendant Stanford Plaza Association, Inc.’s then-pending motion for summary judgment, Plaintiffs’ prior counsel, David Sergenian, moved to be relieved as counsel, which the Court granted.” (Opp., 5:15-23.)

Finally, Defendants assert that “[i]f this Court grants the pending motions for withdrawal, then Plaintiffs, who are each corporate entities who cannot proceed pro per, could effectively force a continuance of the July 15 trial simply by delaying their engagement of new counsel until after July 15, 2026. Even assuming Plaintiffs engaged new counsel immediately, which would be their third set of counsel in this case, Plaintiffs’ new counsel will no doubt seek a continuance of the July 15 trial, as forewarned in Tesser Grossman’s June 3, 2026 ex parte application.” (Opp., 6:5-10.)

Counsel responds, asserting that “[t]he supporting declarations establish that the attorney-client relationship has broken down and that the specific facts are confidential and privileged. That showing is sufficient.” (Reply, 2:22-23.) Moreover, Counsel asserts that they do “not ask the Court to ‘rubber stamp’ the motions on a bare assertion; to the contrary, the Firm has expressly offered to disclose the good-faith basis for withdrawal to the Court in-camera, outside the presence of Defendants, so that the Court can satisfy itself that good cause exists without forcing the Firm to breach its duties to its clients.” (Reply, 2:24-27.)

Counsel also asserts that “Defendants’ delay theory rests almost entirely on the Declaration of Jose Villacreses, who recounts a remark Mr. Cui allegedly made to him on May 2, 2026. The declaration cannot bear that weight.” (Reply, 3:4-6.) Counsel contends that not only is the declaration hearsay, but it also proves nothing, since Mr. Villacreses “has no personal knowledge of why the attorney-client relationship broke down or of any connection between Mr. Cui’s remark and these motions.” (Reply, 3:17-19.)

Counsel also refutes Defendants’ contention that Cui’s previous attorney ceased representation due to nonpayment, because Defendants rely on an order that Counsel contends “[t]he supposed proof that Mr. Cui refuses to pay his lawyers is a default judgment that a court has found was improperly obtained and has now erased.” (Reply, 4:17-18.) Moreover, Counsel contends that “Defendants’ central premise – that granting withdrawal will delay trial – is speculation, and the record cuts the other way. Granting a motion to be relieved does not vacate the trial date. The July 15, 2026 trial remains on calendar unless and until the Court orders otherwise.” (Reply, 4:24-26.)

Lastly, Counsel asserts that “Defendants argue that because the LLC plaintiffs cannot appear in propria persona, withdrawal will force a continuance. California law squarely rejects that reasoning. A court may not deny an otherwise proper motion to withdraw simply because the client is an entity that cannot represent itself.” (Reply, 5:12-15.) As Counsel notes, the law provides the contrary: “For the uncooperative corporate client who has not been willing to bring in new counsel, granting of the withdrawal motion will put extreme pressure on it to obtain new counsel of record for should it fail to do so it risks forfeiture of its rights through nonrepresentation.” (Thomas G. Ferruzzo, Inc. v. Superior Court (1980) 104 Cal.App.3d 501, 504.)

Although Defendants’ concerns are reasonable, they are based on speculation. The Court will not deny Counsel’s good-faith motion and force Counsel to continue representation of a client when the attorney-client relationship has broken down. Counsel complied with all the relevant procedural requirements. (See Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362.) Counsel filed MC-051, MC-052, and MC-053 forms, provided sufficient reason for withdrawal, and filed proof of mail and email service.

Accordingly, the Court grants Counsel’s motions to be relieved as counsel.

 

Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, the motions to be relieved as counsel are GRANTED.

Counsel is ordered to give notice of this Order.

REVIEW OF DEFAULT JUDGMENT PACKAGE

           

Plaintiff Schwan Cosmetics Germany GmbH & Co. KG (“Schwan”) appears to request entry of default judgment against Defendant Freck Yourself, Inc. (“Defendant”) in the total amount of $132,710.73 as demanded in the complaint.

The Court notes various defects with the submitted package.

First, Schwan filed multiple CIV-100 forms with different addresses for Defendant. The address on the most recently filed CIV-100 form does not match where service was effectuated. Schwan does not explain the reason for these discrepancies.

Second, Schwan does not check the box declaring Defendant’s nonmilitary status. ((Cal. Rules of Court, rule 31800(5).)

Third, Schwan’s CIV-100 form has multiple issues. It is not signed “under penalty of perjury” for sections 7 and 8. Under section 7, the total amount requested says $0.00, which does not reflect the request for $132,710.73. Section 5 of the CIV-100 form is also incomplete.

Fourth, Schwan does not provide any admissible evidence in support of the requested judgment. ((Id. R., rule 31800(8).) Although Schwan’s counsel submits and authenticates a declaration for the default judgment package, this is inadequate. A declarant must have percipient knowledge to be competent to testify; otherwise, it is inadmissible hearsay. (Evid. Code, § 1200, subds. (a)-(b) [“‘Hearsay evidence’ is evidence of a statement that was made other than by a witness while testifying at the hearing and that is offered to prove the truth of the matter stated.”].) As is the case here, attorney declarants are not usually percipient. Schwan must submit admissible evidence of someone with personal knowledge to support the requested judgment amount.

The Court notes that Schwan attached various exhibits to the complaint, but these are not authenticated. Without proper authentication, the attached exhibits are not admissible evidence, and the Court cannot consider them. (See Code of Civil Procedure, § 2015.5 [“Whenever, under any law of this state or under any rule, regulation, order or requirement made pursuant to the law of this state, any matter is required or permitted to be supported, evidenced, established, or proved by the sworn statement, declaration, verification, certificate, oath, or affidavit, in writing of the person making the same…, such matter may with like force and effect be supported, evidenced, established or proved by the unsworn statement, declaration, verification, or certificate, in writing of such person which recites that it is certified or declared by him or her to be true under penalty of perjury, is subscribed by him or her, and (1), if executed within this state, states the date and place of execution, or (2), if executed at any place, within or without this state, states the date of execution and that it is so certified or declared under the laws of the State of California.”].) “[D]efault judgment on…claims can be entered only upon proof to the court of the damage sustained.” ((Taliaferro v. Hoogs (1963) 219 Cal.App.2d 559, 560.)

Based on the foregoing, Schwan’s request for default judgment is denied without prejudice. Schwan is ordered to file a new default judgment package. The hearing on the default judgment package is set on _____________ at 10:00 a.m. in Dept. 508. Courtesy copies of the default judgment package must be delivered to Dept. 508 concurrently with filing.

DEFENDANTS 2018 GLENDON AVE, LLC KAYVAN ABSELET, AND DENNIS P. BLOCK & ASSOCIATES, APC’S MOTION TO STRIKE THE FIRST AMENDED COMPLAINT

           

            Background

On September 9, 2025, Plaintiff Raul Ortiz, in pro per, filed a complaint against Defendants 2018 Glendon Ave, LLC., Kayvan Abselet, and Dennis P. Block & Associates, APC (erroneously sued as Dennis Block & Associates) (collectively, “Defendants”) alleging seven causes of action for (1) discrimination - Cal. Gov. Code § 12940 et seq. & California Constitution, Article I, §§ 7 & 31; (2) defamation; (3) intentional infliction of emotional distress; (4) negligent infliction of emotional distress; (5) financial loss/distress; (6) violation of civil rights - 42 U.S.C. §§1981, 1982 & California Civil Code §§51, 52; and (7) wrongful eviction.

On September 22, 2025, Plaintiffs Raul Ortiz and Rita Ortiz (jointly, “Plaintiffs”) filed a first amended complaint (“FAC”) alleging nine causes of action. The FAC added two causes of action for (8) fraud and (9) breach of contract.

On October 6, 2025, Defendants filed a special motion to strike Plaintiff Raul Ortiz’s complaint under the Strategic Lawsuit Against Public Participation statute (“anti-SLAPP”). On February 10, 2026, the Court granted Defendants’ special motion to strike in its entirety. In the Order, the Court noted that “Defendants’ anti-SLAPP addresses the complaint, not the FAC. It is unclear whether Plaintiff served Defendants because the Court does not have proof of service for the FAC. Plaintiff did not file an opposition.” (Order 2/10/26, 2:16-18.) On February 19, 2026, Plaintiffs filed proof of service for the FAC.

Defendants now move to strike the FAC in its entirety or, in the alternative, paragraphs one, eleven, and twelve, as well as the second through seventh causes of action. Plaintiffs did not oppose.

Request for Judicial Notice

The Court grants Defendants’ requests for judicial notice of Exhibits A, B, and C.

Legal Standard

A court may “[s]trikeout any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading” or “[s]trike out all or any part of any pleading “not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court.¿” (¿¿Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subds. (a)-(b)¿¿.) “¿The grounds for a motion to strike shall appear on the face of the challenged pleading or from any matter of which the court is required to take judicial notice.¿” (¿¿Code Civ. Proc., § 437¿¿.)

“Where the defect raised by a motion to strike or by demurrer is reasonably capable of cure, leave to amend is routinely and liberally granted to give the plaintiff a chance to cure the defect in question. [Citations.] A pleading may be stricken only upon terms the court deems proper [citation], that is, terms that are just. [Citations.] It is generally an abuse of discretion to deny leave to amend, because the drastic step of denial of the opportunity to correct the curable defect effectively terminates the pleader’s action.” ((Velez v. Smith (2006) 142 Cal.App.4th 1154, 1174-1175 [internal quotations omitted].)     

Discussion

Defendants contend that the FAC should be stricken because “Defendants filed an anti-slapp motion to strike which sought to strike the identical matters that are the subject of the present notice of motion, except that the present motion refers to the First Amended Complaint, rather than to the original Complaint.” (Mot., 5:1-4.)

“‘[A] plaintiff whose complaint is stricken by a successful anti-SLAPP motion cannot try again with an amended complaint.’ [Citation.] ‘Once the trial court has determined the speech at issue is constitutionally protected, it may not grant leave to amend to omit facts to take the claim out of the protection of section 425.16.’ [Citation.] Allowing an amendment ‘once the court finds the prima facie showing has been met would completely undermine the statute by providing the pleader a ready escape from section 425.16’s quick dismissal remedy. Instead of having to show a probability of success on the merits, the SLAPP plaintiff would be able to go back to the drawing board with a second opportunity to disguise the vexatious nature of the suit through more artful pleading.’” ((Finato v. Keith A. Fink & Associates (2021) 68 Cal.App.5th 136, 149.)

Defendants assert that “[t]he anti-slapp motion was granted by the court, and Plaintiff then proceeded to file a First Amende[d] Complaint realleging the same matters that were the subject of the court's order granting the anti-slapp motion. Based on the foregoing authorities, this motion to strike should be granted with respect to the First Amended Complaint.” (Mot., 5:6-9.) The Court notes that the FAC purports to add two new causes of action; however, the body of the FAC does not include clear headings or allegations for the added fraud and breach of contract causes of action. Accordingly, the FAC is almost identical to the complaint for which the Court granted the anti-SLAPP.

Although Plaintiffs filed the FAC before Defendants filed their anti-SLAPP motion, Plaintiffs’ proof of service demonstrates that service was effectuated on December 2, 2025. Notably, this is two months after Defendants filed the anti-SLAPP. Under Code of Civil Procedure section 472, subdivision (a), a party normally may amend a complaint as of right before a “demurrer or motion to strike is heard if the amended pleading is filed and served no later than the date for filing an opposition to the demurrer or motion to strike.” However, this section explicitly excludes “a special motion brought pursuant to Section 425.16.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 472, subd. (b).) Without proper service on Defendants prior to their filing of the anti-SLAPP, the FAC cannot supersede the original complaint. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 471.5, subd. (a) [“If the complaint is amended, a copy of the amendments shall be filed, … and a copy of the amendments or amended complaint must be served upon the defendants affected thereby. The defendant shall answer the amendments, or the complaint as amended, within 30 days after service thereof…” (emphasis added)].)

Accordingly, the FAC is improper and must be stricken.

Conclusion

            Based on the foregoing, Defendants’ motion to strike is GRANTED. Defendants are ordered to file a proposed form of judgment within ten days of this Order.

Defendants are ordered to provide notice of this Order.

PLAINTIFF APOLLO MANAGEMENT, LP’S MOTION TO SEAL CONFIDENTIAL PORTIONS OF COMPLAINT AND EXHIBITS

           

Background

On October 10, 2025, Plaintiff Apollo Management Holdings, LP (“Apollo”) filed this action against Defendants Cornerstone International Industries, Inc., Wendy Hickman, and Laird Robertson (collectively, “Defendants”), alleging four causes of action for (1) breach of contract, (2) fraudulent inducement, (3) promissory fraud, and (4) negligent misrepresentation.

Apollo now “move[s] for an order to seal any ‘Confidential Information’ as defined in the underlying Office Lease between Plaintiff and Plaintiff’s Landlord and incorporated in the parties’ January 10, 2025 Agreement of Sublease (the ‘Sublease Agreement’) and certain financial information contained in the Complaint and referenced in the wire instructions on the parties’ Escrow Agreement dated February 18, 2025 (the ‘Escrow Agreement’).” (Notice of Mot., 2:5-9.) No opposition was filed.

Legal Standard

Generally, “[u]nless confidentiality is required by law, court records are presumed to be open.” (¿Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.550(c)¿.) If the presumption of access applies, “[t]he court may order that a record be filed under seal only if it expressly finds facts that establish: (1) There exists an overriding interest that overcomes the right of public access to the record; (2) The overriding interest supports sealing the record; (3) A substantial probability exists that the overriding interest will be prejudiced if the record is not sealed; (4) The proposed sealing is narrowly tailored; and (5) No less restrictive means exist to achieve the overriding interest.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.550(d)¿.)

Discussion

Apollo seeks an Order to seal the Sublease Agreement and all references to Confidential Information and sensitive financial information. Specifically, Apollo “seeks to seal: (1) any reference to ‘Confidential Information’ in the Complaint alleging Breach of Contract, Fraudulent Inducement, Promissory Fraud, and Negligent Misrepresentation (‘Complaint’) as defined in underlying Office Lease between Plaintiff and Plaintiff’s Landlord and incorporated in the parties’ January 10, 2025 Agreement of Sublease (the ‘Sublease Agreement’); (2) bank account and routing number information referenced in the wire instructions attached to the parties’ Escrow Agreement dated February 18, 2025 (the ‘Escrow Agreement’); (3) the Base Rent and Escrow Deposit as defined in the Escrow Agreement; (4) sensitive financial information found in Paragraph 27 of the Complaint; and (5) rental amounts and payment figures referenced in Paragraphs 17, 19, 23, 29, 42, 43, 74, 84(a), 90(a), and the Prayer for Relief of the Complaint.” (Mot., 4:5-14.)

Apollo seeks to seal confidential information within the Sublease Agreement because “the terms of the Sublease Agreement prohibit any party or their representatives from publicly disclosing ‘the contents of this Lease and any related documents.’” (Mot., 4:19-21.) Apollo contends that “[a]ccordingly, Exhibit A and the Confidential Information contained in Paragraphs 11 and 12 of Exhibit B should be sealed because the information is subject to a nondisclosure agreement.” (Mot., 4:25-27.) Apollo “also requests the Court to seal certain confidential financial information.” (Mot., 5:1.) This includes “the base-rental figure, escrow-deposit figure, and bank account and routing number information referenced in the wire instructions attached to the parties’ Escrow Agreement[,] … sensitive financial information found in Paragraph 27 of the Complaint,” and “rental amounts and payment figures referenced in the Complaint.” (Mot., 5:4-8.)

Apollo contends that its “request satisfies all five requirements articulated in NBC Subsidiary v. Superior Court (1999) 20 Cal. 4th 1178, 1218 and codified in Rule of Court 2.550(d).” (Mot., 5:13-14; see NBC Subsidiary v. Superior Court (1999) 20 Cal.4th 1178, 1217-1218 [“a trial court must hold a hearing and expressly find that (i) there exists an overriding interest supporting closure and/or sealing; (ii) there is a substantial probability that the interest will be prejudiced absent closure and/or sealing; (iii) the proposed closure and/or sealing is narrowly tailored to serve the overriding interest; and (iv) there is no less restrictive means of achieving the overriding interest.”].)

First, Apollo contends that “Paragraph 29.28 of the Office Lease provides a confidentiality provision” (Mot., 6:17-18), and a “contractual obligation not to disclose can constitute an overriding interest within the meaning of rule 243.1(d).” ((Universal City Studios, Inc. v. Superior Court (2003) 110 Cal.App.4th 1273, 1283 (Universal).) Apollo contends that “[t]here is no legal or factual basis for the public interest to override the parties’ interest, as enumerated in the Sublease Agreement.” (Mot., 6:18-20.) Additionally, Apollo asserts that the request to seal financial information is not only generally appropriate, but is also required under California Rules of Court, rule 1.201(a), which states that “[t]o protect personal privacy and other legitimate interests, parties and their attorneys must not include, or must redact where inclusion is necessary, the following identifiers from all pleadings and other papers filed in the court’s public file, … [f]inancial account numbers.” Apollo asserts the financial information attached to the Escrow Agreement includes “account and routing number information,” paragraph 27 of the complaint “contain[s] data that would reveal cost structures, profit margins, and forecasts,” and “the rental amounts and payments contain commercial terms that competitors could exploit.” (Mot., 7:6-11.) The Court finds that Apollo presents an overriding interest.

Second, Apollo asserts that “the parties’ overriding interest in preventing disclosure of Confidential Information and certain financial information supports sealing portions of this case. Plaintiff seeks only to seal the Confidential Information and discrete financial information.” (Mot., 7:16-18.) The Court agrees that an overriding interest to protect their privacy supports sealing the financial information. Apollo’s only assertion that there is an overriding interest to seal the Sublease Agreement is that “Confidential Information is necessarily included in Exhibit A” and that the agreement contains a confidentiality provision. (Mot., 6:20.) The Court finds that this is sufficient to serve as Apollo’s overriding interest.

Third, Apollo asserts prejudice will ensue if the documents are not sealed because “[r]evealing the Confidential Information would create a conflict between the Court’s filing procedures and the parties’ contractual covenant. Revealing account and routing information in Exhibit C would expose Plaintiff to financial crime and identity theft. Public disclosure of rental terms and financial information in Exhibits B and C would substantially impair Plaintiff’s bargaining position and provide unfair leverage to competitors.” (Mot., 7:24-8:1.) Apollo demonstrates that they will suffer prejudice if the Court declines to seal the sensitive and private financial information. However, Apollo does not present convincing evidence that Exhibit A, the Sublease Agreement, should be sealed. As the court in Universal, supra, 110 Cal.App.4th at p. 1283 noted, even if a party demonstrates that there is “a binding contractual agreement not to disclose,” the party must still show that there is “a substantial probability that it will be prejudiced absent closure or sealing.” In that case, the defendant had a binding contractual agreement not to disclose, but the Court of Appeal found that the defendant did not “show[] a substantial probability any such interest in the present case will be prejudiced.” (Ibid.) Apollo uses this case to support its contention that a confidentiality provision warrants sealing, but the case merely allows a party to use a confidentiality provision to demonstrate an overriding interest; it does not automatically satisfy the other elements. Accordingly, Apollo does not provide sufficient support for its argument that it will be prejudiced by not sealing the Sublease Agreement.

Fourth, Apollo avers that the “proposed sealing is narrowly tailored. Exhibit A should be sealed in full because complete sealing is the only way to comply with the confidentiality provision. Only discrete figures and identifiers are redacted from Exhibit B. And Exhibit C is redacted solely as to the base rental amount, the escrow deposit amount, and account and routing number information. In addition, Plaintiff has filed public, redacted versions of these documents.” (Mot., 8:4-8.) As Apollo notes, Exhibits B and C are both narrowly redacted to hide information that would prejudice the parties. The Court finds this reasonable. However, Exhibit A is redacted in full due to the confidentiality provision in the Sublease Agreement. Although the Court recognizes the fundamental freedom to contract, this freedom is not absolute and cannot override the general right of access by the public to court records without justification. (See Cal. Rules of Court, rules 2.503(a), 2.550(c).) Apollo does not explain to the Court why this is the narrowest form of redaction and why the contents of the agreement should be sealed, beyond the confidentiality provision requiring sealing of Exhibit A in full. The Court finds this insufficient. Apollo does not provide proper justification for redacting the agreement in full.

Fifth, Apollo contends there is no less restrictive means to keep the information private. Regarding the Sublease Agreement, Apollo contends that “[i]f Exhibit A is publicly filed, the Confidential Information becomes public and out of the parties’ control, contrary to the confidentiality provision incorporated in the parties’ Sublease Agreement. Full sealing is the is the only effective option.” (Mot., 8:12-15.) Apollo also asserts that “line-by-line redactions are the least restrictive means to keep private financial information in Paragraph 27 of the Complaint and rental amounts and payment figures in Paragraphs 17, 19, 23, 29, 42, 43, 74, 84(a) 90, and the Prayer for Relief. As to Exhibit C, California Rule of Court 1.201 requires redacting account identifiers and is the narrowest way to protect against fraud. And only the Base Rent and Escrow Deposit figures are redacted on the Escrow Agreement itself.” (Mot., 8:15-20.) The Court agrees that the financial information properly redacts the information in the least restrictive means. However, Apollo still does not adequately support redacting Exhibit A in full, beyond the agreement no longer being in the parties’ control. Apollo does not explain the confidential nature of each aspect of the agreement, besides the contract’s “confidentiality clause.”

Based on the foregoing, the Court finds that Apollo demonstrated its need to seal confidential financial information, including rental amounts and payments, bank account and routing numbers, base rent and escrow deposits. However, the Court finds that Apollo does not meet its burden, demonstrating the need to redact the Lease Agreement, Exhibit A, in its entirety. Contracting with another party is an important right, but Apollo must still carry its burden to demonstrate its necessity by satisfying all five enumerated factors in California Rules of Court, rule 2.550(d).

Conclusion 

Based on the foregoing, Apollo’s motion to seal is GRANTED IN PART. The Court orders that confidential information and financial information contained in the complaint and exhibits are sealed. The Court notes that Apollo has already filed redacted versions of the documents. However, the request to seal Exhibit A entirely is DENIED without prejudice because Apollo did not carry its burden to demonstrate the need to seal the document in full.

Pursuant to California Rules of Court, rule 2.551(e), the Court directs the clerk to file this Order, maintain the records ordered sealed in a secure manner, and clearly identify the records as sealed by this Order. The Court further orders that no persons other than the Court and Court staff, as necessary, are authorized to inspect the sealed records.

Apollo is ordered to provide notice of this Order.

Demurrer

A.    Legal Standard

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. ((Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) In testing the sufficiency of the complaint, the court assumes the truth of properly pleaded factual allegations, facts reasonably inferred from those expressly pleaded, and judicially noticed matters. ((Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) “A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the¿evidence or other extrinsic matters. Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed.” ((SKF Farms v. Superior Court (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 902, 905.) Accordingly, “[w]hether the plaintiff will be able to prove the pleaded facts is irrelevant to ruling upon the demurrer.” ((Stevens v. Superior Court (1986) 180 Cal.App.3d 605, 609-610.)

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivisions (e) and (f), a demurrer may be filed if the pleading is uncertain or does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. For purposes of ruling on a demurrer, all facts pleaded in a complaint are assumed to be true, but the reviewing court does not assume the truth of conclusions of law. ((Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 967.)

Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment, otherwise, it is abuse of discretion. ((Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349.)

B.    Allegations of the Complaint

Plaintiff alleges that “SPS Servicing (hereafter ‘SPS’) is, on information and belief, a mortgage loan servicing and payment processing company. SPS is believed to be acting as a sub-servicer or agent for Assure (or for the holder of Plaintiff’s mortgage loan) to collect monthly mortgage payments from Plaintiff. At all relevant times, Plaintiff made his monthly mortgage payments to SPS, which in turn was responsible for applying those payments to Plaintiff’s loan obligations, including principal, interest, taxes, and insurance (PITI).” (Compl., ¶ 4.) While “RTI Properties is the holder or beneficiary of the second mortgage (Second Deed of Trust) on Plaintiff’s property. This was a loan of approximately $300,000 extended to Plaintiff, with a total current balance of $443,390.” (Compl., ¶ 6.)

Plaintiff alleges he has owned a “single-family residence located at 4132 Vanetta Place in Studio City, California” for twenty years. (Compl., ¶ 11.) “In or about late 2023, Plaintiff accidentally and involuntarily missed one of his first mortgage payments. As a result, in November 2023 defendant Assure recorded a Notice of Default against the Property and initiated foreclosure proceedings.” (Compl., ¶ 13.) Plaintiff asserts that shortly after, he “cured the default on the first mortgage. In the same month (November 2023), Plaintiff paid the overdue amounts necessary to reinstate the loan, and the first mortgage was brought current. By curing the default, Plaintiff was entitled under California law to reinstatement of the loan and a stop to the foreclosure process.” (Compl., ¶ 14.) “Following the cure, Plaintiff remained in good standing on the first mortgage from late 2023 through early 2025.” (Compl., ¶ 15.)

Plaintiff also alleges that “[i]n or about October or November 2023, Plaintiff obtained a homeowners insurance policy from Defendant CA FAIR Plan to cover fire damage to the Property.” (Compl., ¶ 16.) Plaintiff believes that when his “first mortgage went into default in November 2023 and the Notice of Default was recorded, the mortgage company (Defendant Assure, through its agents) communicated this fact to Defendant CA FAIR Plan. In other words, Assure or its agents notified the FAIR Plan in late 2023 that Plaintiff’s Property was ‘in foreclosure.’” (Compl., ¶ 19.) Plaintiff alleges that “Defendant CA FAIR Plan, upon learning of the foreclosure status in late 2023, decided that it would non-renew Plaintiff’s fire insurance policy at the end of the policy term (October 2024),” but “did not directly notify Plaintiff in late 2023 that it had decided not to renew the policy. Plaintiff did not receive any written notice addressed to him, at his mailing address, informing him of an upcoming non-renewal or cancellation. California law (Insurance Code § 675 et seq., including § 678) requires insurers to provide written notice of non-renewal to the named insured at least 45 days before a homeowners policy expiration. CA FAIR Plan did not mail or deliver any such notice to Plaintiff as required. If any notice was sent at all, it was sent only to Plaintiff’s insurance broker via email, as described below, and not to Plaintiff himself. Plaintiff remained personally unaware of any intent not to renew the policy.” (Compl., ¶¶ 20-21.)

Plaintiff alleges that “Defendant Assure Mortgage Co., Defendant SPS Servicing and Defendant RTI Properties knew or should have known that Plaintiff’s fire insurance policy was not renewed in October 2024. Typically, when a homeowner’s insurance policy is about to expire or is canceled, the insurer sends notice not only to the insured but also to any mortgagee or lienholder (since the lender has an interest in the property being insured).” (Compl., ¶ 24.) “Despite these signs, Assure, RTI Properties and SPS took no timely action to inform Plaintiff that his property was no longer insured against fire as of November 2024. They did not contact Plaintiff to warn him of the lapsed policy, nor did they arrange for lender-placed hazard insurance…, which is a standard protective measure lenders take when a borrower’s insurance lapses. Instead, Assure, RTI Properties and SPS simply continued to collect the same monthly payment from Plaintiff (which included amounts intended for insurance), without securing any actual insurance coverage for the property. By doing so, the mortgage company and servicer breached the duties they owed both to Plaintiff and to themselves (as the property’s secured creditors) to ensure continuous insurance coverage on the collateral. Their inaction left the Property effectively uninsured as of the end of October 2024.” (Compl., ¶ 25.)

Plaintiff further alleges that “[o]n January 8, 2025, a devastating wildfire swept through parts of Southern California, including the area of Plaintiff’s Property. On that date, Plaintiff’s house was caught in the wildfire and suffered catastrophic fire damage… Plaintiff sustained roughly $1.3 million in property damage.” (Compl., ¶ 26.)

Plaintiff pleads that SPS “[n]egligently and/or fraudulently failed to handle Plaintiff’s escrow funds properly, did not pay for or secure renewal of the insurance, did not inform Plaintiff of the lapse, and misrepresented by conduct that insurance was in place while accepting payments designated for insurance. SPS’s acts are closely tied with Assure’s, as SPS acted as Assure’s agent in servicing the loan.” (Compl., ¶ 32.) Plaintiff also alleges that RTI, as the second mortgagee, “did not maintain continuous insurance coverage, failed to notify Plaintiff or arrange lender-placed insurance, and collected insurance impounds under false pretenses.” (Ibid.)

D.    Third Cause of Action for Breach of Contract - Deed of Trust/Loan Agreement

SPS first demurs to the breach of contract cause of action, asserting that “Plaintiff’s claims are not substantiated by the express language of his First Deed of Trust as it is his contractual obligation to obtain and maintain insurance.” (Dem., 12:26-27.)

“If the action is based on an alleged breach of a written contract, the terms must be set out verbatim in the body of the complaint or a copy of the written instrument must be attached and incorporated by reference.” (Otworth v. Southern Pac. Transportation Co. (1985)166 Cal.App.3d 452, 459.) “The standard elements of a claim for breach of contract are ‘(1) the contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance, (3) defendant’s breach, and (4) damage to plaintiff therefrom. [Citation.]’” (Wall St. Network, Ltd. v. New York Times Co. (2008) 164 Cal.App.4th 1171, 1178.)

Plaintiff’s complaint alleges breach of contract, but Plaintiff does not include explicit language of the contract, nor does Plaintiff attach it as an exhibit. SPS requested that the Court judicially notice the first Deed of Trust, Exhibit 1, which contains the language of the parties’ contract.

SPS first asserts that “Plaintiff suggests SPS breached the Deed of Trust by not notifying Plaintiff of the nonrenewal. (Complaint, par. 55.) He does not, however, identify any portion of the First Deed of Trust which would require such a notification. This is glaring in light of the fact that it was Plaintiff’s own obligation to obtain and maintain insurance. (RJN, Ex. ‘1’, §5.)” (Dem., 13:7-10; see also RJN, Ex. 1 [“Borrower shall keep the improvements now existing or hereafter erected on the Property insured against loss by fire, hazards included within the term ‘extended coverage,’ and any other hazards…”].) SPS asserts that Plaintiff “asks this Court to impose substantive duties or limits beyond those specified within his own agreement. He thus fails to plead any breach of contract claim relating to a purported duty to notify him of a coverage lapse.” (Dem., 13:16-18.)

SPS also asserts that Plaintiff’s assertion that SPS breached its duty because it needed to arrange for lender-placed hazard insurance (see Compl., ¶ 25) is improper, because “Plaintiff has not, and cannot, point to any contractual obligation that required SPS to obtain such insurance. Section 5 of the Deed of Trust provides ‘If Borrower fails to maintain any of the coverages,’ including fire coverage, the ‘Lender may obtain insurance coverage, at Lender’s option and Borrower’s expense.’ (RJN, Ex. “1”, §5 (emphasis added).) Section 5 expressly states the ‘Lender is under no obligation to purchase any particular type or amount of coverage.’ (RJN, Ex. ‘1’, §5 (emphasis added).) It further states ‘such coverage shall cover Lender, but might or might not protect Borrower, Borrower’s equity in the Property, or the contents of the Property, against any risk, hazard or liability.’” (Dem., 13:26-14:5.)

SPS further asserts that “Plaintiff suggests that because his insurance premiums were paid by SPS as an escrow item, that SPS breached some portion of the loan agreement. (Complaint par. 55(a).) Section 3 of the Deed of Trust provides, in part, that the lender may ‘collect and hold fund in an amount (a) sufficient to permit Lender to apply the Funds at the time specified under RESPA, and (b) not to exceed the maximum amount a lender can require under RESPA.’ (RJN, Ex. ‘1’, § 3.) It further provides that the Lender shall estimate the amount of Funds due on the basis of current data and reasonable estimates of expenditures of future Escrow Items or otherwise in accordance with Applicable Law.’ (RJN, Ex. ‘1’, § 3.) Where there is a ‘surplus of Funds held in escrow, as defined under RESPA, Lender shall account to Borrower for the excess funds in accordance with RESPA.’ (RJN, Ex. ‘1’, § 3.) Plaintiff alleges SPS collected funds from Plaintiff and paid these escrow items. It was obligated to account for those funds, and adjust the amounts collected periodically pursuant to RESPA. Plaintiff does not allege that SPS breached any particular contractual provision relating to escrow items.” (Dem., 14:11-23.)

Plaintiff opposes SPS’s contentions, asserting that SPS’s “argument relies on a selective, incomplete reading of the Deed of Trust, completely ignoring the escrow provisions and the federal regulations incorporated therein.” (Opp., 5:16-17.) Plaintiff contends that “[t]he Deed of Trust expressly permits the lender to ‘collect and hold fund[s]’ for ‘Escrow Items,’ which are explicitly defined to include ‘premiums for any and all insurance required by Lender under Section 5’. Critically, Section 3 explicitly ties the administration of these funds to the Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act (RESPA). It states that the lender shall estimate and apply the funds ‘in accordance with RESPA’ and ‘Applicable Law’. Under fundamental principles of California contract law, all applicable laws and regulations in existence when an agreement is made become a part of the contract as fully as if incorporated by reference. Because the Deed of Trust explicitly invokes RESPA, the regulatory mandates of RESPA constitute binding contractual obligations upon the servicer.” (Opp., 5:20-6: 2.) Plaintiff avers that he “alleges that SPS collected his monthly escrow impounds but failed to make the timely payments necessary to prevent the FAIR Plan policy from lapsing. By failing to remit the funds to the insurer, SPS breached the explicit contractual terms of Section 3, as informed by RESPA.” (Opp., 6:11-14.)

Plaintiff also asserts that, under the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, “SPS continuously collected Plaintiff’s monthly PITI payment—including the specific portion designated for the fire insurance premium—while simultaneously allowing the policy to lapse and failing to notify the Plaintiff. By actively collecting the insurance impound, SPS created a reasonable expectation that the insurance was being maintained. Taking Plaintiff’s money for a specific purpose, failing to execute that purpose, and concealing the failure entirely destroys Plaintiff’s right to receive the benefits of the escrow agreement. This constitutes a textbook breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing.” (Opp., 6:24-7:2.)

However, as SPS points out in response, “Plaintiff alleges SPS collected funds from Plaintiff and paid these escrow items. It was obligated to account for those funds, and adjust the amounts collected periodically pursuant to RESPA. Plaintiff does not allege that SPS breached any particular contractual provision relating to escrow items.” (Reply, 5:1-4.) SPS avers that “[t]here is no provision in the First Deed of Trust that places any obligation on the lender to provide insurance. There is also no portion of the Deed of Trust which would require a particular notification of the non-renewal of Plaintiff’s own insurance policy.” (Reply, 5:10-12.) Although Plaintiff directs the Court to law, as SPS asserts, Plaintiff does not “point to any basis under which SPS was obligated to force-place insurance… Force-placed insurance is an option, not a requirement. Plaintiff may not base a contract claim on an option which was not an obligation.” (Reply, 5:17-24.)

Since this is a breach of contract cause of action, Plaintiff must identify a provision of the contract SPS breached. However, Plaintiff does not identify a specific, mandatory provision of the Deed of Trust showing that SPS was required to obtain force-placed insurance or inform Plaintiff of the lapse in coverage. Moreover, Plaintiff cannot rely on the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing to impose substantive, affirmative duties, limits, or obligations that do not exist within the terms of the contract. “The covenant of good faith and fair dealing, implied by law in every contract, exists merely to prevent one contracting party from unfairly frustrating the other party's right to receive the benefits of the agreement actually made. The covenant thus cannot be endowed with an existence independent of its contractual underpinnings. It cannot impose substantive duties or limits on the contracting parties beyond those incorporated in the specific terms of their agreement.” (Guz v. Bechtel Nat. Inc. (2000) 24 Cal. 4th 317, 349-350 [internal quotations and citations omitted].) Accordingly, the demurrer is sustained, with leave to amend, due to Plaintiff’s failure to demonstrate the contractual provisions SPS is alleged to have violated.

E.    Fourth Cause of Action for Negligence

SPS next demurs to the fourth cause of action for negligence.

“The elements of a negligence cause of action are duty, breach, causation and damages.” ((County of Santa Clara v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2006) 137 Cal.App.4th 292, 318.) “To prevail in a negligence action, a plaintiff must show that the defendant owed a legal duty, the defendant breached that duty and the breach proximately caused injury to the plaintiff.” (J.L. v. Children’s Institute, Inc. (2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 388, 396.) “A tort involves a violation of a legal duty, imposed by statute, contract or otherwise, owed by the defendant to the person injured. Without such a duty, any injury is an injury without wrong. [Citation.] A person is ordinarily not liable for the actions of another and is under no duty to protect another from harm, in the absence of a special relationship of custody or control.” ((Roman Catholic Bishop v. Superior Court (1996) 42 Cal.App.4th 1556, 1564.)

            SPS contends that Plaintiff does not plead facts “relating to any role or conduct beyond the role of a lender or loan servicer to implicate any legal duty. Plaintiff is improperly attempting to create a tort claim where the parties’ relationship is based in contract. No legal duty is implicated to support a negligence cause of action.” (Dem., 16:17-20.) SPS points to Sheen v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2022) 12 Cal.5th 905, 948 (Sheen), where the Supreme Court stated that “when a borrower requests a loan modification, a lender owes no tort duty sounding in general negligence principles.” Additionally, SPS contends that “Plaintiff fails to allege any act of breach of any legal duty as to SPS. Plaintiff claims SPS should have notified him about the insurance lapse or force-placed insurance, but he does not plead facts that SPS actually knew that the CA FAIR Plan policy had lapsed. As stated above, he essentially claims SPS ‘must have known’ based on the fact that SPS may have made one or two payments on his behalf after the policy had lapsed. This is based entirely on conjecture and speculation.” (Dem., 16:24-28.) Lastly, SPS contends that “[e]ven if Plaintiff could identify some injury…, no causal connection exists to suggest any injury was caused by SPS. It was Plaintiff’s own responsibility to obtain and maintain fire insurance on the Property (RJN, Ex. ‘1’ § 5), and he pleads no obligation by SPS to notify him of a lapse (let alone that it knew of the lapse, or force-place insurance.” (Dem., 17:10-14.)

            In opposition, Plaintiff asserts that Sheen, supra, 12 Cal.5th is inapposite because its ruling is based in the economic loss rule, and “reasoned that a loan modification is essentially the renegotiation of an existing debt, and a lender’s involvement in a modification is merely an assessment of how best to recoup its money. However, the administration of a borrower's escrow account is fundamentally distinct from the adversarial renegotiation of a loan.” (Opp., 7:27-8:4.) Plaintiff draws important distinctions between the loan modification in Sheen and the escrow management in the case at bar. For loan modification, Plaintiff contends the servicer’s role is as an “[a]dversarial creditor acting to maximize its own debt recovery,” there is “[n]o federal statute compel[ling] a lender to approve a modification,” and “[e]conomic loss rule bars tort duty during contract negotiations.” (Opp., 8:8-19.) Whereas, for escrow management, Plaintiff states there is an “[a]ffirmative, ongoing administration of the borrower's funds for payment to third parties,” the servicer is an “[a]dministrative intermediary acting as a conduit for borrower’s premium payments,” RESPA mandates timely escrow disbursements, and “Good Samaritan rule and Negligence Per Se establish independent tort duties.” (Ibid.) Further, Plaintiff avers that “[w]hen a servicer collects and manages escrow funds, its involvement exceeds the ‘conventional role as a mere lender of money’. It undertakes a specialized, heavily regulated administrative function.” (Opp., 8:22-24.)

Plaintiff asserts that “[e]ven if a common law duty were absent, Plaintiff has sufficiently pleaded a duty of care arising from statute.” (Opp., 8:26-27.) The doctrine of negligence per se states that “[t]he failure of a person to exercise due care is presumed if: (1) He violated a statute, ordinance, or regulation of a public entity; (2) The violation proximately caused death or injury to person or property; (3) The death or injury resulted from an occurrence of the nature which the statute, ordinance, or regulation was designed to prevent; and (4) The person suffering the death or the injury to his person or property was one of the class of persons for whose protection the statute, ordinance, or regulation was adopted.” ((Evid. Code, § 669, subd. (a).)

RESPA, the Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act, and its implementing Regulation X, imposes a strict mandate on a servicer to make timely disbursements from an escrow account. “If the terms of any federally related mortgage loan require the borrower to make payments to the servicer of the loan for deposit into an escrow account for the purpose of assuring payment of taxes, insurance premiums, and other charges with respect to the property, the servicer shall make payments from the escrow account for such taxes, insurance premiums, and other charges in a timely manner as such payments become due. Any balance in any such account that is within the servicer’s control at the time the loan is paid off shall be promptly returned to the borrower within 20 business days or credited to a similar account for a new mortgage loan to the borrower with the same lender.” ((12 U.S.C. § 2605(g).) Moreover, “[i]f the terms of any federally related mortgage loan require the borrower to make payments to an escrow account, the servicer must pay the disbursements in a timely manner, that is, on or before the deadline to avoid a penalty, as long as the borrower’s payment is not more than 30 days overdue.” ((12 C.F.R. § 1024.17(k)(1).)

Plaintiff contends that “[t]he Complaint explicitly alleges that SPS violated these regulations by allowing the policy to lapse without making the premium payment and by failing to provide the mandatory warning notices. The resulting injury—the catastrophic, uninsured destruction of the property— is exactly the harm these RESPA provisions were designed to prevent. Plaintiff, as the homeowner and borrower, is squarely within the class of persons RESPA was enacted to protect. Therefore, SPS owed a statutory duty of care to Plaintiff, which it breached, establishing a cognizable claim for negligence per se.” (Opp., 9:11-17.)

Plaintiff also asserts that SPS is negligent under the six factors identified in Biakanja v. Irving (1958) 49 Cal.2d 647, 650, which “involves the balancing of various factors, among which are the extent to which the transaction was intended to affect the plaintiff, the foreseeability of harm to him, the degree of certainty that the plaintiff suffered injury, the closeness of the connection between the defendant's conduct and the injury suffered, the moral blame attached to the defendant's conduct, and the policy of preventing future harm.”

Plaintiff asserts that all factors are met because, first, the transaction affected Plaintiff because “[t]he escrow impound is specifically intended to ensure that the plaintiff’s property is insured against destruction, directly protecting the plaintiff's equity and habitation.” (Opp., 9:22-24.) Next, Plaintiff contends that “[i]t is entirely foreseeable that if a servicer collects insurance money but fails to pay the insurer, the policy will lapse, and the plaintiff will suffer an uninsured loss if a disaster strikes.” (Opp., 9:25-27.) Plaintiff alleges that “[t]he injury is absolute; Plaintiff lost a $1.3 million asset with zero insurance recovery.” (Opp., 10:1-2.) As for the connection between SPS’s conduct and the injury, Plaintiff asserts that “SPS’s failure to remit the funds or warn the Plaintiff was the direct cause of the property being uninsured on the day of the fire.” (Opp., 10:4-5.) Fifth, Plaintiff contends that “[c]ontinuing to siphon $2,500 a month from a homeowner while silently allowing their catastrophic risk insurance to lapse carries severe moral blame, bordering on deceit.” (Opp., 10:6-8.) Lastly, Plaintiff avers that “[r]ecognizing a duty of care in escrow administration incentivizes servicers to maintain accurate accounting systems and communicate effectively, preventing the exact type of catastrophic financial ruin seen here.” (Opp., 10:9-11.)

Based on the assertions set forth by Plaintiff and the complaint’s allegations, Plaintiff demonstrates that the complaint pleads that SPS had a duty of care because SPS took Plaintiff’s money and held it in his escrow account, while allegedly knowing the bill could not be paid. (See Compl., ¶ 62c [“If SPS did not know the policy lapsed, it was negligent in not knowing what it reasonably should have; if SPS did know and just kept collecting, that’s even worse. Either scenario constitutes negligent handling of Plaintiff’s money and trust. A competent servicer would have either paid a renewal premium or, if not possible, at least stopped charging or refunded the insurance portion. Doing nothing and holding the funds while coverage lapsed is a clear breach of duty.”].) Plaintiff’s complaint sufficiently alleges that SPS breached this duty when it continued to collect monthly insurance premiums, despite knowing that Plaintiff’s insurance had lapsed. Plaintiff properly alleges harm that arose from SPS’s breach, because Plaintiff alleges that “[b]ut for their negligence, one of two likely outcomes would have protected Plaintiff: either the insurance would have been maintained (so his loss would be reimbursed) or at least he would have been warned and could try to protect himself or pressure for coverage. It is reasonably certain that had Plaintiff been notified or had insurance been force-placed, his post-fire situation would be vastly better (either not losing equity or being able to rebuild).” (Compl., ¶ 63.)

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s complaint alleges SPS’s negligence, and the demurrer to the fourth cause of action is overruled.

F.     Sixth Cause of Action for Breach of Fiduciary Duty

“To establish a cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty, a plaintiff must demonstrate the existence of a fiduciary relationship, breach of that duty and damages.” ((Charnay v. Cobert (2006) 145 Cal.App.4th 170, 182.) “‘[B]efore a person can be charged with a fiduciary obligation, he must either knowingly undertake to act on behalf and for the benefit of another, or must enter into a relationship which imposes that undertaking as a matter of law.’” ((City of Hope National Medical Center v. Genentech, Inc. (2008) 43 Cal.4th 375, 386.)

            SPS demurs to the sixth cause of action, asserting that there is no fiduciary relationship between SPS and Plaintiff because “[c]ourts have consistently held that no fiduciary relationship exists between a lending institution and its borrower.” (Dem., 17:25-27; see also Ragland v. U.S. Bank National Assn. (2012) 209 Cal.App.4th 182, 206 [“No fiduciary duty exists between a borrower and lender in an arm’s length transaction.”].) Moreover, in Sutake v. Orange County Federal Credit Union (1986) 186 Cal.App.3d 140, 144, the Court of Appeal stated that “a lender does not have a legal duty to ensure the borrower has liability insurance.” SPS asserts that “Plaintiff bases this cause of action entirely on duties arising out of the contractual relationship with his lender. Plaintiff cannot assert a claim for breach of fiduciary duty as a duty is lacking.” (Dem., 18:2-4.)

            Plaintiff opposes, contending that “[w]hen a loan servicer collects escrow funds from a borrower, it does so as an agent for the specific purpose of paying insurance premiums and property taxes on the borrower’s behalf. This arrangement places the servicer in a position of trust and confidence regarding those specific funds. The servicer controls the disbursement of the borrower’s money to satisfy critical obligations necessary to protect the property.” (Opp., 10:27-11:3.)

However, as SPS points out in response, “Plaintiff argues the fact that SPS administered an escrow account created a fiduciary duty owed to him. (Opposition, p. 10.) Plaintiff glaringly cites no authority in support of this proposition… Absent special circumstances, a loan transaction is at arm’s length and there is no fiduciary relationship between the borrower and lender.” (Reply, 7:15-20.)

Plaintiff does not present the Court with legal support demonstrating that the loan transaction between SPS and Plaintiff created an arrangement beyond a standard, customary lending arrangement that does not give rise to a fiduciary duty. Without evidence or support that SPS’s act of administering the escrow account implicated something more, SPS did not owe Plaintiff a fiduciary duty. Accordingly, the Court sustains the demurrer, without leave to amend.

G.    Seventh Cause of Action for Fraud and Negligent Misrepresentation

SPS demurs to the seventh cause of action based on fraud because SPS contends Plaintiff does not plead any elements for a fraud cause of action.

“[T]he elements of an action for fraud and deceit based on concealment are: (1) the defendant must have concealed or suppressed a material fact, (2) the defendant must have been under a duty to disclose the fact to the plaintiff, (3) the defendant must have intentionally concealed or suppressed the fact with the intent to defraud the plaintiff, (4) the plaintiff must have been unaware of the fact and would not have acted as he did if he had known of the concealed or suppressed fact, and (5) as a result of the concealment or suppression of the fact, the plaintiff must have sustained damage.” (Marketing West, Inc. v. Sanyo Fisher (USA) Corp. (1992) 6 Cal.App.4th 603, 612-613.) Less specificity is required if it appears from the nature of allegations that the defendant must necessarily possess full information, or if the facts lie more in the knowledge of opposing parties.¿(Alfaro v. Community Housing Improvement System & Planning Assn., Inc.¿(2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1356, 1384-1385.)

“The elements of a cause of action for negligent misrepresentation are: 1. The defendant must have made a representation as to a past or existing material fact…2. The representation must have been untrue…3. Regardless of his actual belief the defendant must have made the representation without any reasonable ground for believing it to be true…4. The representation must have been made with the intent to induce plaintiff to rely upon it…5. The plaintiff must have been unaware of the falsity of the representation; he must have acted in reliance upon the truth of the representation and he must have been justified in relying upon the representation…6. And, finally, as a result of his reliance upon the truth of the representation, the plaintiff must have sustained damage.” ((Friedman v. Merck & Co. (2003) 107 Cal.App.4th 454, 476 [internal quotations omitted].)

            In the complaint, Plaintiff pleads that “SPS … made false representations to Plaintiff, and/or concealed material facts from Plaintiff, in the course of handling his loan payments and insurance escrow, with knowledge of the falsity or with reckless disregard for the truth, and with the intent to induce Plaintiff’s reliance,” because it “continu[ed] to collect the monthly payment of $2,500 from Plaintiff (which included an amount for insurance) even after they knew or should have known that the fire insurance policy was not renewed.” (Compl., ¶ 81a.) Further, Plaintiff pleads that “[e]ach monthly mortgage statement or payment acceptance without comment on insurance was effectively a communication to Plaintiff that everything was normal — that his loan was current and his insurance was being paid. This was a false representation by conduct.” (Ibid.) Plaintiff also alleges that SPS “deliberately concealed from Plaintiff the critical fact that his fire insurance policy had lapsed or would lapse without renewal. They had a duty to disclose this fact because (1) they had exclusive or superior knowledge of it (since they typically receive notices or have servicer records indicating insurance status), (2) it was a material fact – indeed, crucial to the security of the property and loan – and (3) Plaintiff justifiably relied on the assumption that if something was wrong, the servicer would inform him (especially given the impound relationship). By failing to disclose the lapse, these Defendants engaged in fraudulent concealment.” (Compl., ¶ 81b.) Plaintiff alleges justifiable reliance “on Defendants’ misrepresentations and omissions to his detriment,” because he relied on the implicit representation that he had insurance, and did not take steps to remediate this. (Compl., ¶ 84.) Plaintiff alleges that his reliance is a substantial factor in his damages because “when the fire occurred, he was uninsured and suffered the full magnitude of the loss. Had the true facts been known, either the loss would have been covered or the scenario altogether might have been different.” (Compl., ¶ 85.) Plaintiff also alleges that SPS acted “intentionally or at least in conscious disregard when concealing the insurance lapse.” (Compl., ¶ 86.)

            Plaintiff adequately pleads the requisite elements to demonstrate fraud. Additionally, as Plaintiff points out in his opposition, “SPS attacks the Seventh Cause of Action by arguing that Plaintiff fails to allege a specific, affirmative misrepresentation, lacks facts showing SPS’s knowledge of falsity (scienter), and fails to demonstrate reliance or resulting damages. SPS’s arguments rely on an overly rigid application of pleading standards, ignoring both the doctrine of fraudulent concealment and the ‘superior knowledge’ exception to the strict pleading requirements for fraud in California.” (Opp., 11:13-18.) The Court finds that the superior knowledge exception is present here because SPS is a corporate entity that understands the intricacies of its mortgage system and payments. Plaintiff further asserts that “Plaintiff, who was unlawfully deprived of his non-renewal notice by the FAIR Plan, cannot possibly know at the pleading stage the exact date, time, or identity of the SPS employee who reviewed the file and noted the absence of a renewal policy. SPS possesses superior, if not exclusive, knowledge of its internal accounting systems and its communications with the FAIR Plan.” (Opp., 12:2-6.) Utilizing this exception, Plaintiff’s complaint is pled with adequate specificity. Because Plaintiff pleads the elements demonstrating that SPS engaged in fraud, the demurrer is overruled.

H.    Ninth Cause of Action for Failure to Provide Required Notices (Statutory Violations)

SPS contends that Plaintiff fails to allege SPS violated any particular statute.

In the complaint, Plaintiff first alleges “California Insurance Code §§ 675, 676, 677.4, 678, and related provisions govern the cancellation and non-renewal of insurance policies. Specifically, Insurance Code § 678 (applicable to property insurance policies covering residential properties) requires that an insurer provide written notice to the named insured at least 45 days before the expiration of the policy if the insurer intends not to renew the policy.” (Compl., ¶ 97.) Plaintiff appears to direct this section at Defendant CA FAIR Plain, but “SPS submits that it is not an ‘insurer’ for purposes of these sections, and each of those sections places obligations only on ‘insurers’ as to cancellation and non-renewal.” (Dem., 20:17-18.) The Court agrees that the statutory violation is inapposite because SPS is not an insurer.

Plaintiff also pleads violations under Civil Code section 2954, but Plaintiff does not point to a portion of the statute that SPS violated. SPS asserts that “[w]hile the section provides the requirement that a lender provide an annual accounting, nothing in the section created an obligation to notify Plaintiff of an insurance lapse, let alone within the two months between the October 24, 2024 lapse and the January 8, 2025 fire. Furthermore, Civil Code Section 2954.6 requires lenders to notify borrowers about cancellation rights for private mortgage insurance or mortgage guaranty insurance, but this statute specifically addresses mortgage insurance protecting the lender against borrower default, not hazard insurance protecting the property itself.” (Dem., 21:4-10.) Plaintiff does not appear to address this statute in the opposition, and the Court finds it inapplicable.

Next, Plaintiff pleads SPS “did not send Plaintiff any notice that his hazard insurance had lapsed or was about to lapse. This is a direct violation of the spirit if not the letter of 12 C.F.R. § 1024.37.” (Compl., ¶ 100.) SPS asserts that “[g]laringly, Plaintiff does not point to any portion of § 1024.37 that SPS is alleged to have violated. While the section provides various requirements as to notices and the procedure for force-placing insurance, nothing in that section requires a lender to obtain force-placed insurance.” (Dem., 20:24-27.) Plaintiff does not allege any aspect of 12 C.F.R. § 1024.37 that would apply here, as this regards forced-place insurance, insurance SPS did not obtain and was not required to obtain, per the Deed of Trust.

Plaintiff’s complaint also pleads that statutory violations constitute unfair and unlawful business practices on behalf of SPS. “The Unfair Business Practices Act defines ‘unfair competition’ as any ‘unlawful, unfair or fraudulent business practice and unfair, deceptive, untrue or misleading advertising….’ (§ 17200.) The Legislature intended this ‘sweeping language’ to include ‘anything that can properly be called a business practice and that at the same time is forbidden by law.’” ((Bank of the West v. Superior Court (1992) 2 Cal.4th 1254, 1266.) “An ‘unlawful’ practice requires violation of another statute, and a business practice may be ‘unfair’ even if not otherwise proscribed by statute as long as the practice is not expressly authorized by law.” ((People ex rel. Dept. of Motor Vehicles v. Cars 4 Causes (2006) 139 Cal.App.4th 1006, 1016.)

“Unlawful business activity proscribed under section 17200 includes anything that can properly be called a business practice and that at the same time is forbidden by law.” (Farmers Ins. Exch. v. Superior Ct. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 377, 383 [internal quotations omitted].) “[A]n action based on Business and Professions Code section 17200 to redress an unlawful business practice ‘borrows’ violations of other laws and treats these violations, when committed pursuant to business activity, as unlawful practices independently actionable under section 17200 et seq. and subject to the distinct remedies provided thereunder.” (Ibid.) “A plaintiff alleging unfair business practices under these statutes must state with reasonable particularity the facts supporting the statutory elements of the violation.” (Khoury v. Maly’s of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal. App. 4th 612, 619.)

Plaintiff’s complaint alleges that “Defendant SPS, Assure and RTI Properties, in violating servicing laws and regulations, likewise engaged in unlawful and unfair practices. Plaintiff has standing under §17204 because he lost money and property (the insurance premiums paid, his property value lost, etc.) as a result of these practices. While the primary relief under §17200 is injunctive and restitutionary, Plaintiff specifically seeks in this cause of action any such equitable relief: for instance, an injunction requiring Defendants to adhere to notice requirements henceforth, and restitution of any insurance premium monies collected for coverage that was wrongfully allowed to lapse. This is in addition to the damages sought under other causes, but is pleaded in the alternative and to bolster that relief.” (Compl., ¶ 102.)

As a preliminary matter, Plaintiff does not make any fraudulent business practices assertions. Next, Plaintiff must anchor his unlawful allegation to a statutory basis. In Plaintiff’s opposition, he identifies that “RESPA and Regulation X (12 C.F.R. § 1024.17(k)) strictly require servicers to make timely payments from an escrow account to prevent a lapse in hazard insurance. The Complaint alleges SPS failed to do so.” (Opp., 14:5-7.) However, the complaint only makes mention of 12 C.F.R. § 1024.37, notably, not section 1024.17(k), which is the applicable section. Accordingly, the unlawful prong cannot be met because it fails to identify a statute SPS could have allegedly violated and lacks particularity.

Moreover, SPS contends “[t]he Complaint further fails to state a claim under the unfairness prong, as Plaintiff cannot allege that the servicing of this particular loan offends a public policy tethered to any underlying constitutional, statutory or regulatory provision.” (Dem., 21:28-22:3.) SPS asserts that “Plaintiff has not identified how the alleged behavior is likely to deceive the public and or that other consumers have been similarly deceived.” (Dem., 22:3-4.) As SPS asserts, Plaintiff’s complaint lacks these allegations.

Lastly, SPS asserts that Plaintiff lacks standing to bring a UCL cause of action. To establish standing to bring a cause of action under the Unfair Competition Law (UCL), a private party must “(1) establish a loss or deprivation of money or property sufficient to qualify as injury in fact, i.e., economic injury, and (2) show that that economic injury was the result of, i.e., caused by, the unfair business practice or false advertising that is the gravamen of the claim.” ((Campbell v. FPI Management, Inc. (2024) 98 Cal.App.5th 1151, 1167-1168.) SPS asserts that “Plaintiff was obligated to maintain insurance on his Property. He alleges no contractual obligation or statutory duty wherein SPS was obligation to force-place insurance, or notify him of a lapse.” (Dem., 22:18-20.)

Plaintiff’s opposition asserts that “Plaintiff alleges that SPS collected escrow funds intended for insurance but failed to remit them, essentially taking Plaintiff’s money without providing the corresponding service. The loss of these specific premium dollars constitutes an injury in fact directly caused by the unfair practice. Moreover, the broader property loss ($1.3 million) is directly tied to the unlawful failure to notify Plaintiff of the lapse under RESPA.” (Opp., 14:17-21.)

Plaintiff demonstrates that he has standing to bring a UCL cause of action. However, as noted above, Plaintiff does not anchor the unlawful UCL allegation to an applicable statute, nor does Plaintiff plead that SPS engaged in unfair business practices.

Accordingly, the demurrer to the ninth cause of action for failure to provide required notices (statutory violations) is sustained, with leave to amend.

NOTICE OF HEARING ON CLAIM OF EXEMPTION

 

Background

On October 27, 2015, Plaintiff Investment Retrievers, Inc (“Plaintiff”) filed an Application for Entry of Judgment on Sister-State Judgment in this action against Defendant Marianne M. Szymanski (“Defendant”).

On May 10, 2017, an Amended Judgment Based on Sister-State Judgment was entered in this action. On July 20, 2022, a Writ of Execution (Money Judgment) was issued as to the Judgment entered on May 10, 2017. The Writ of Execution states that the total amount due is $53,821.68.

            On March 30, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Notice of Hearing on Claim of Exemption, as well as a “Notice of Opposition to Claim of Exemption (Wage Garnishment).”

            At the hearing on April 29, 2026, “the Court continue[d] the matter so Plaintiff can file the claim of exemption and Defendant can file a response.” (Order 4/29/26, 2:25-26.)

            On July 6, 2026, Defendant filed a “Claim of Exemption (Wage Garnishment).” From the document, it appears that Defendant faxed the claim on March 24, 2026.

 

Discussion

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 703.510, subdivision (a), “[e]xcept as otherwise provided by statute, property that has been levied upon may be claimed to be exempt as provided in this article.” Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 703.520, subdivision (a), “[t]he claimant may make a claim of exemption by filing with the levying officer, either in person or by mail, a claim of exemption together with a copy of the claim.” Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 703.520, subdivision (b), “[t]he claim of exemption shall be executed under oath and shall include all of the following:

 

(1) The name of the claimant and the mailing address where service of a notice of opposition to the claim may be made upon the claimant.

 

(2) The name and last known address of the judgment debtor if the claimant is not the judgment debtor.

 

(3) A description of the property claimed to be exempt. If an exemption is claimed pursuant to Section 704.010 or 704.060, the claimant shall describe all other property of the same type, including exempt proceeds of the property of the same type, owned by the judgment debtor alone or in combination with others on the date of levy and identify the property, whether or not levied upon, to which the exemption is to be applied. If an exemption is claimed pursuant to subdivision (b) of Section 704.100, the claimant shall state the nature and amount of all other property of the same type owned by the judgment debtor or the spouse of the judgment debtor alone or in combination with others on the date of levy.

 

(4) A financial statement if required by Section 703.530.

 

(5) A citation of the provision of this chapter or other statute upon which the claim is based.

 

(6) A statement of the facts necessary to support the claim.”

 

As set forth above, on July 6, 2026, Defendant filed a “Claim of Exemption (Wage Garnishment).” However, Defendant does not appear to provide a “citation of the provision of this chapter or other statute upon which the claim is based.” ((Id., § 703.520, subd. (b)(5).) Defendant also does not appear to provide “[a] statement of the facts necessary to support the claim.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 703.520, subd. (b)(6).)

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 703.570, subdivision (b), “[n]ot less than 10 days prior to the hearing, the judgment creditor shall serve a notice of the hearing and a copy of the notice of opposition to the claim of exemption on the claimant and on the judgment debtor, if other than the claimant. Service shall be made personally or by mail.” On March 30, 2026, Plaintiff filed a proof of service indicating that a notice of hearing and notice of opposition were served on Defendant by mail on March 27, 2026. On June 17, 2026, Plaintiff also filed proof of service for the “Notice of Ruling” from the hearing on April 29, 2026. The Notice of Ruling demonstrates that the hearing was continued to July 10, 2026. Accordingly, Plaintiff met the statutory service requirement.

Plaintiff’s “Notice of Opposition to Claim of Exemption” asserts that the earnings claimed as exempt are not exempt. (Notice of Opp., Item 4(a).) The Notice of Opposition also provides an attachment. In it, Plaintiff asserts that “California decisional case law allows a judgment debtor to seek an exemption or relief from garnishment due to the amount of necessary family expenses they can claim. ‘Necessary’ expenses normally include housing costs, food, insurance, automobile costs, etc. However, the court must consider the circumstances surrounding each individual case—what is ‘necessary’ in some circumstances may be a luxury in others.” (Notice of Opp., Item 5c, Attach. 6.) Plaintiff cites J. J. MacIntyre Co. v. Duren (1981) 118 Cal.App.3d Supp. 16 in support.

In J. J. MacIntyre Co., the court stated that “[t]he determination of what is necessary for the support of the judgment debtor or his family has not been subject to a precise definition and differs with each debtor. Many things, such as recreation, music lessons, and insurance, may be within the meaning of a necessary for ... support. In actions on debts incurred for common necessaries of life, the judgment debtor is subject to a garnishment or levy of execution of 25 percent of his disposable earnings or disposable earnings less 30 times the federal minimum hourly wage, whichever is less even if necessary for the support of himself and his family. The term common necessaries of life, while strictly construed by cases interpreting the former code provisions, means essentials commonly required by all persons for the sustenance of life, whatever their employment status and includes medical care.” (J. J. MacIntyre Co. v. Duren, supra, 118 Cal.App.3d Supp. at pp. 18-19 [internal quotations and citations omitted].) Plaintiff asserts that “[i]n this case the judgment debtor has not produced any records to verify her alleged ‘Necessary Expenses’ and should be required to meet her burden of proof. In fact, the debtor shows net monthly income of $3,910.21 and monthly expenses of $3,150.00. This leaves net income balance of $760.21. Therefore, the judgment debtor is able to make payments on this legal obligation.” (Notice of Opp., Item 5c, Attach. 6.)

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 703.580, subdivision (b), “[a]t a hearing under this section, the exemption claimant has the burden of proof.” Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 703.580, subdivision (c),¿“[t]he claim of exemption is deemed controverted by the notice of opposition to the claim of exemption and both shall be received in evidence. If no other evidence is offered, the court, if satisfied that sufficient facts are shown by the claim of exemption (including the financial statement if one is required) and the notice of opposition, may make its determination thereon.” Defendant must make a proper showing at the hearing that she is eligible for a claim of exemption.

Conclusion
