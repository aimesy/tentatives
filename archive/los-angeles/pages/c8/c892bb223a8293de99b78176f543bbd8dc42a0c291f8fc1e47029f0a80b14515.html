Case Number: 22NWCV01597    Hearing Date: August 13, 2026    Dept: D

MARIA AVINA JACOBO, ET AL. V. AMERICAN HONDA MOTOR CO., INC.

CASE NO.: 22NWCV01597

HEARING:  08/13/2026 @ 9:30 a.m.

 

#12

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Plaintiffs Maria Avina Jacobo and Alfredo Lopez Gomez’s motion for attorney’s fees is GRANTED.

 

Defendant American Honda Motor Co. Inc.’s motion to strike, or in the alternative, tax the memorandum of costs filed by Plaintiffs Maria Avina Jacobo and Alfredo Lopez Gomez is DENIED.

 

Defendant American Honda Motor Co. Inc. is ordered to pay the awarded amount within 90 days of this Order.

 

Moving parties to give notice.

 

Plaintiffs Maria Avina Jacobo and Alfredo Lopez Gomez (collectively, Plaintiffs) move for an order awarding attorneys’ fees, costs, and expenses in the amount of $70,030.62.

 

Defendant American Honda Motor Co. Inc. (Defendant) moves to strike the memorandum of costs filed by Plaintiffs, or in the alternative, tax costs.

 

Background

 

This is a lemon law action.  On December 14, 2022, Plaintiffs filed the operative complaint against Defendant and Does 1 through 10, inclusive, asserting one causes of action for Violation of Song-Beverly Act—Breach of Express Warranty.

 

Motion for Attorney’s Fees

 

If a buyer-plaintiff prevails in an action under Civil Code section 1794, “the buyer shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorneys’ fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.”  (Civ. Code, § 1794 (d).)¿

 

Here, it is undisputed that Plaintiffs are the prevailing parties in this action and are entitled to reasonable attorneys’ fees.  The only issue pending before this court is the amount of the award.

 

          Reasonableness of Hourly Rate

 

Plaintiff’ counsel requests the following hourly rates:

 

·        Colby Meagle: $450 (2026)

·        Deepak Devabose: $450 (2024)

·        Debra Reed: $495 (2023)

·        Elvira Kamosko: $350 (2024)

·        Harita Nandivada: $375 (2024); $400 (2025)

·        Jacob Cutler: $550 (2025)

·        Keishunn Johnson: $375 (2025)

·        Marisa Melero: $395 (2023)

·        Russell Higgins: $600 (2025)

·        Summer Smith: $575 (2024)

·        Sundeep Samra: $400 (2024); $450 (2025)

·        Timothy Lupinek: $415 (2023); $450 (2024); $500 (2025)

·        Thach Tran: $425 (2024)

·        Stephanie Rosado: $295 (2024)

·        Diana Folia (Paralegal): $250 (2026)

 

(Motion p. 10, Kirnos Decl., ¶ 2, Ex. A.)

 

The matter of reasonableness of a party’s attorney fees is within the sound discretion of the trial court.  (Bruckman v. Parliament Escrow Co. (1989) 190 Cal.App.3d 1051, 1062.)  ¿Normally, a “reasonable” hourly rate is the prevailing rate charged by attorneys of similar skill and experience in the relevant community.  (PLCM Group, Inc. v. Drexler¿(2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095.)  That amount may then be adjusted through the consideration of various factors, including “(1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, and (4) the contingent nature of the fee award.”  (Ketchum v. Moses¿(2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132.) 

 

In opposition, Defendant argues that Plaintiffs have failed to establish that the requested hourly rates are reasonable for a routine, non-complex Song-Beverly action.  (Opp. pp. 11-13.)  Defendant contends that Plaintiffs rely on inflated and unverified rates drawn largely from contingency fee counsel, without evidence that clients actually pay those rates for comparable non-contingent lemon law work in Los Angeles County.  (Ibid.)  Defendant additionally relies on Mikhaeilpoor v. BMW of North America, LLC (2020) 48 Cal.App.5th 240 (Mikhaeilpoor ) and Hernandez v. FCA US LLC (C.D. Cal. Jan. 4, 2019) 2019 WL 2932637, arguing that lower rates have been found reasonable in comparable consumer cases.  (Opp., pp. 12-13.) 

 

In reply, Plaintiffs argue that Defendant has not rebutted the reasonableness of the requested hourly rates because Plaintiffs submitted evidence of prevailing rates in Los Angeles lemon law cases, including declarations and prior fee awards approving comparable rates.  (Reply pp. 7-9.) 

 

The Court finds that Plaintiffs have shown that their requested hourly rates reflect the prevailing market rates for attorneys of comparable skill and experience in the relevant community.  Plaintiffs’ attorneys take cases on a contingency and, unlike defense counsel who work for large companies, there is no guarantee that Plaintiffs’ attorneys will get paid when they agree to take a case.  It is not unreasonable that the hourly rates for plaintiffs’ lemon law attorneys are higher than¿the hourly rates for defense counsel in certain circumstances. 

 

Accordingly, the Court concludes that Plaintiffs’ requested rates are reasonable.

 

          Reasonableness of Hours Billed

 

The trial court has an obligation to award only those attorneys’ fees that are reasonable.¿  (See¿PLCM Group v. Drexler¿(2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1096; see also¿Ketchum v. Moses¿(2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132 (Ketchum).)  Although a verified fee bill is “prima facie evidence the costs, expenses and services listed were necessarily incurred,” (Hadley v. Krepel (1985) 167 Cal.App.3d 677, 682), counsel still has the burden to demonstrate the reasonableness of charges.  (Mikhaeilpoor v. BMW of North America, LLC (2020) 48 Cal.App.5th 240, 247.)

 

Plaintiffs seek an award of attorney’s fees based on 91 hours of billed time, resulting in a lodestar of $38,666.00, and request a 1.5 lodestar multiplier for a total fee award of $57,999.00.  (Kirnos Decl., ¶¶ 2-3, Ex. A.)

 

The Court has reviewed and considered Plaintiffs' counsel's attached billing records, together with Defendant's objections to the reasonableness of certain billing entries. 

 

·        Counsel billed 6.5 hours on April 30, 2024, to attend Plaintiff Maria Lopez’s deposition.  Defendant submits that the deposition commenced at 11:07 a.m. and concluded at 3:17 p.m., for a total of approximately 4.2 hours.  Plaintiffs do not explain the additional 2.3 hours included in the billing entry.  Accordingly, the Court finds that 4.2 hours was reasonably necessary and will strike 2.3 hours.

·        Counsel billed 3.0 hours on May 8, 2024, for travel to and from the vehicle inspection.  Plaintiffs do not provide evidence regarding the distance traveled or otherwise explain why three hours of travel was reasonably necessary.  The Court finds that 1.5 hours was reasonably necessary for travel and will strike 1.5 hours.

·        Plaintiffs’ paralegal billed a total of 8.8 hours between December 30, 2024 and January 2, 2025, in connection with preparation of trial exhibits and the exhibit list.  Although Plaintiffs persuasively argue that preparation of trial exhibits requires substantive review and analysis, the entries also include clerical tasks.  The Court therefore finds a reduction warranted and will strike 2.0 hours.

·        Counsel billed 0.8 hours on March 20, 2025, to prepare notes following Plaintiffs’ expert’s deposition after billing 4.4 hours to attend the deposition.  Plaintiffs do not specifically explain why 0.8 additional hours was necessary.  The Court finds that 0.5 hours was reasonably necessary and will strike 0.3 hours.

·        Counsel billed 0.1 hour on April 29, 2025, for communication concerning an unpaid expert invoice.  The Court finds this entry administrative in nature and will strike 0.1 hour.

 

(Kirnos Decl., ¶¶ 2-3, Ex. A; Opp., pp. 15-18.)

 

Overbilling, Block Billing, and Excessive Time

 

The Court has considered Defendant’s arguments regarding alleged block billing, excessive internal communications, overstaffing, excessive time devoted to discovery and deposition preparation, use of form or template documents, trial preparation, and administrative or clerical work.  Defendant contends that Plaintiffs’ use of multiple timekeepers resulted in duplicative work and inefficiencies and that counsel billed excessive time for routine Song-Beverly tasks.  (Opp., pp. 9-10, 14-18.)

 

Plaintiffs respond that counsel’s use of attorneys who specialize in particular stages of litigation increased efficiency, that the challenged internal communications involved modest amounts of time and legitimate litigation strategy, and that the use of templates reduced rather than increased the time billed.  (Reply, pp. 9-11.)  Plaintiffs further explain that the challenged discovery, deposition, and trial-preparation entries required case-specific review and analysis.  (Id. at p. 11.)  

 

The Court finds that, except for the specific reductions identified above, the challenged entries are sufficiently related to the litigation, reasonably documented, and not excessive under the circumstances.  Accordingly, the Court declines to make any further deductions from the requested fees.

 

                   Section 998 Offer

 

Defendant argues that the February 2023 offer provided Plaintiffs the option of a $46,000 repurchase, a statutory repurchase in an amount to be proven, or $20,000 with Plaintiffs retaining the vehicle, together with either $10,000 in attorney’s fees and costs or fees and costs determined by motion.  (Opp., pp. 6-8.)  Plaintiffs rejected the offer on April 10, 2023 and continued litigating the action for approximately two years before ultimately accepting Defendant’s later section 998 offer providing for a $46,000 repurchase and reasonable attorney’s fees and costs to be determined by motion.  (Ibid.)  Relying on Reck v. FCA US LLC (2021) 64 Cal.App.5th 682 (Reck), Defendant acknowledges that the Court may not categorically cut off all fees incurred after the February 2023 offer, but argues that Reck permits the Court to consider the reasonableness of the rejected offer in relation to the result ultimately obtained and to evaluate whether Plaintiffs’ manner of prosecuting the action thereafter resulted in overstaffing, excessive time, or otherwise unreasonable fees.  (Id. at pp. 7-8.)

 

In reply, Plaintiffs argue that Defendant’s February 2, 2023 section 998 offer was invalid because it contained multiple alternative options, including an unspecified statutory-repurchase amount that could not be determined when the offer was made.  (Reply, pp. 1-7.)  Relying principally on Duff v. Jaguar Land Rover North America, LLC (2022) 74 Cal.App.5th 491, Plaintiffs contend that the offer created an impermissible “moving target” and was insufficiently certain to permit Plaintiffs or the Court to determine whether a subsequent recovery was more favorable.  (Id. at pp. 1-6.)  

 

In Reck, the Court of Appeal held that a trial court may not categorically deny or reduce post-offer attorney’s fees based on a plaintiff’s refusal to accept a section 998 offer where the plaintiff ultimately obtains a more favorable recovery. (Reck, supra, 64 Cal.App.5th at 693, 699-700.)  However, Reck also recognized that a trial court may consider the reasonableness of a rejected section 998 offer in relation to the result ultimately obtained and may evaluate whether the manner in which the plaintiff prosecuted the action thereafter resulted in overstaffing, excessive time, or otherwise unreasonable fees.  (Id. at 699-700.)

 

Here, the Court need not determine the validity or effect of the February 2023 offer for purposes of the present motion.  Defendant does not contend that section 998 operates to categorically bar Plaintiffs from recovering fees incurred after the offer.  Rather, relying on Reck, Defendant asks the Court to consider the offer and Plaintiffs’ subsequent litigation conduct as factors in determining whether the fees sought were reasonably incurred.

 

Thus, Reck does not preclude the Court from considering the earlier offer as one factor in assessing whether the fees incurred thereafter were reasonably necessary, but it does preclude the Court from treating Plaintiffs’ rejection of the offer as an automatic cutoff of all subsequent fees.  The relevant inquiry remains whether the fees incurred after the offer were reasonable considering the way the litigation proceeded.  The Court has reviewed Plaintiffs’ billing records and, as set forth above, has reduced those entries it finds excessive, duplicative, administrative, or otherwise unreasonable.  

 

          Lodestar Multiplier

 

The Court declines to apply a lodestar multiplier.  While Plaintiffs' counsel undertook the representation on a contingent basis, the contingency risk in this matter does not warrant an enhancement.  The risk of nonpayment was mitigated by the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act's mandatory fee-shifting provision, which provides prevailing plaintiffs with a statutory right to recover reasonable attorney's fees and costs.  Moreover, this was a relatively routine lemon law action that did not present novel or particularly complex legal issues.  The matter resolved through settlement before trial, and Plaintiffs have not demonstrated that counsel faced an exceptional risk of nonrecovery or that the lodestar amount fails to adequately compensate counsel for the work performed.

 

Accordingly, the Court strikes a total of 6.2 hours from Plaintiffs’ fee request, resulting in a reduction of $2,082.50.  The Court therefore awards Plaintiffs’ attorney’s fees in the amount of $36,583.50.

 

Motion to Strike or Tax Plaintiffs’ Memorandum of Costs

 

A prevailing party is entitled to recover costs, including attorneys’ fees, as a matter of right, except as otherwise expressly provided by statute.  (See Code Civ. Proc., §§ 1032, subd. (a)(4), 1032, subd. (b), 1033.5.) 

 

It is undisputed that Plaintiffs are the prevailing parties.

 

“A ‘verified memorandum of costs is prima facie evidence of [the] propriety’ of the items listed on it, and the burden is on the party challenging these costs to demonstrate that they were not reasonable or necessary.”  (Adams v. Ford Motor Co. (2011) 199 Cal.App.4th 1475, 1486-1487 [italics and brackets omitted].)  Costs otherwise allowable as a matter of right may be disallowed if the court determines they were not reasonably necessary, and the court has power to reduce the amount of any cost item to an amount that is reasonable.  (See Perko’s Enterprises, Inc. v. RRNS Enterprises (1992) 4 Cal.App.4th 238, 245 [finding that¿“the intent and effect of section 1033.5, subdivision¿(c)(2) is to authorize a trial court to disallow recovery of costs, including filing fees, when it determines the costs were incurred unnecessarily”].)¿

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5 sets forth the items of costs which may or not be recoverable in a civil action. 

 

In a lemon law action, as here, Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d) provides that a prevailing buyer “shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses . . . determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.”¿

 

Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d) “permits¿the prevailing buyer to recover both ‘costs’ and ‘expenses.’  Examining the language of the statute [citation], it is clear the Legislature intended the word ‘expenses’ to cover items not included in the detailed statutory definition of ‘costs.’”  (Jensen v. BMW of North America, Inc.¿(1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 112, 137, as¿modified¿on denial of reh’g (June 22, 1995).)  The Legislature included awards of “costs and expenses” in the statute “‘to cover such out-of-pocket expenses as filing fees, expert witness fees,¿marshall’s¿fees, etc.’”  (Id.¿at 138, quoting¿Assem. Com. on Labor, Employment & Consumer Affairs, Analysis of¿Assem. Bill No. 3374 (May 24, 1978) at 2.)

 

          Section 998 Offer

 

As a preliminary matter, in its reply, Defendant argues for the first time that Plaintiffs are barred from recovering costs incurred after February 2, 2023 because Plaintiffs rejected Defendant’s February 2, 2023 section 998 offer and ultimately failed to obtain a more favorable recovery.  (Reply, pp. 2-7.)  Defendant did not raise this argument in its moving papers.  Accordingly, the Court declines to consider Defendant’s new section 998 cost-shifting argument raised for the first time in reply.

 

In any event, as discussed above in connection with Plaintiffs’ motion for attorney’s fees, the Court has considered Defendant’s February 2023 section 998 offer in evaluating the reasonableness of the fees and expenses incurred in this action.  The Court declined to treat Plaintiffs’ rejection of that offer as an automatic cutoff of all subsequent fees and instead evaluated whether the amounts incurred thereafter were reasonably necessary considering the manner in which the litigation proceeded.  For the same reasons, even if the Court were to consider Defendant’s newly raised section 998 argument here, the Court would decline to categorically disallow all post-offer costs solely because Plaintiffs did not accept the February 2023 offer.

 

          Defendant’s Objections

 

Defendant objects to the following costs:

 

·        $341.10: Service of Process

·        $5,795.13: Expert Witness Fees for George Magana

·        $548.03: Attorney Services and Messenger Court Filings and Service

·        $65.00: Court Appearance Professionals

·        $75.00: Travel Expenses

·        $10.00: Minute Orders

 

The Court declines to tax Plaintiffs’ claimed service-of-process costs, attorney service and messenger fees, court appearance professional fee, travel expenses, and minute-order charges.  Plaintiffs have provided supporting information demonstrating that these expenses were incurred in connection with the prosecution of this action, and Defendant has not shown that they were unreasonable or unnecessary.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (c)(2), (4); Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).)

 

As to the expert witness fees, Plaintiffs submitted itemized invoices showing that George Magana prepared for and attended Defendant’s inspection of the subject vehicle, transported the vehicle to and from the inspection, and prepared for his deposition noticed by Defendant.  (Opp., pp. 6-7.)  Plaintiffs further explain that Magana’s participation was necessary to evaluate the vehicle’s alleged defects and to respond to Defendant’s expert evidence.  Accordingly, the Court finds that Plaintiffs have established that the expert witness fees were reasonably incurred in connection with the prosecution of this action.

 

Under Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d), a prevailing buyer may recover litigation expenses reasonably incurred in connection with the action, even where such expenses would not otherwise be recoverable under Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5.  (Jensen v. BMW of North America, Inc. (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 112, 137-138; see also Warren v. Kia Motors America, Inc. (2018) 30 Cal.App.5th 24, 42-43.)  Because Defendant has not demonstrated that any of the challenged costs were unreasonable or unnecessary, the Court declines to tax the requested costs.

 

Therefore, Defendant's Motion to Tax Costs is DENIED. 

 

Conclusion

 

Based on the foregoing, Plaintiffs Maria Avina Jacobo and Alfredo Lopez Gomez’s motion for attorney’s fees is GRANTED.

 

Defendant American Honda Motor Co. Inc.’s motion to strike, or in the alternative, tax the memorandum of costs filed by Plaintiffs Maria Avina Jacobo and Alfredo Lopez Gomez is DENIED.

 

The Court awards Plaintiffs’ attorney’s fees in the reduced amount of $36,583.50 and costs in the amount of $12,031.62, for a total award of $48,615.12.

 

Defendant American Honda Motor Co. Inc. is ordered to pay the awarded amount within 90 days of this Order.

NIGEL WRIGHT V. KAISER FOUNDATION HOSPITALS, ET AL.

CASE NO.:  25NWCV00098

HEARING:  08/13/2026 @ 9:30 a.m.

 

#2

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Defendant Kaiser Foundation Hospitals’ unopposed motion to compel arbitration is GRANTED.

 

The action is STAYED pending completion of arbitration.

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

Defendant Kaiser Foundation Hospitals (Defendant) moves for an order to compel Plaintiff Nigel Wright (Plaintiff) to arbitrate her claims against Defendant.  Defendant also requests staying this action pending the outcome of the arbitration.

 

Background

 

On January 9, 2025, Plaintiff filed the operative Complaint against Defendants Kaiser Foundation Hospitals, Kaiser Permanente, and Does 1 through 25, inclusive, asserting two causes of action: (1) General Negligence and (2) Premises Liability.

 

Plaintiff alleges that on January 10, 2024, he fell after encountering a dangerous condition at Defendant’s premises at 9333 Imperial Highway in Downey, sustaining injuries and damages.

 

On March 13, 2026, Defendant filed the instant motion.

 

No opposition or reply has been filed.

 

Legal Standard

 

Parties may be compelled to arbitrate a dispute upon the court finding that: (1) there was a valid agreement to arbitrate between the parties; and (2) said agreement covers the controversy or controversies in the parties’ dispute.¿  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2; see also¿Omar v. Ralphs Grocery Co.¿(2004)¿118 Cal.App.4th 955, 961.)  A party petitioning to compel arbitration has the burden of establishing the existence of a valid agreement to arbitrate and the party opposing the petition has the burden of proving, by a preponderance of the evidence, any fact necessary to its defense.  (Banner Entertainment, Inc. v. Superior Court¿(1998) 62 Cal.App.4th 348, 356-57.)

 

“If a court of competent jurisdiction. . .has ordered arbitration of a controversy which is an issue involved in an action or proceeding pending before a court of this State, the court in which such action or proceeding is pending shall, upon motion of a party to such action or proceeding, stay the action or proceeding until an arbitration is had in accordance with the order to arbitrate or until such earlier time as the court specifies.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.4.)¿¿¿

 

Analysis

 

Defendant moves to compel Plaintiff to arbitrate his claims.  According to Defendant, Plaintiff’s claims are subject to binding arbitration pursuant to the provisions of the 2024 Group Agreement and Evidence of Coverage (EOC) between Asurion, Plaintiff’s employer, and Kaiser Foundation Health Plan, Inc. (Health Plan).  Plaintiff was enrolled in the Kaiser Health Plan through Asurion’s employee benefit plan for the 2023 and 2024 plan years and remained enrolled through January 1, 2025.  (Petition, ¶ 6; Adkins Decl., ¶¶ 5-6, Ex. B; Asurion Decl., ¶ 4, Ex. D.)  Defendant contends that Plaintiff personally enrolled through Asurion’s online benefits enrollment system and, during the November 2022 and November 2023 enrollment periods, electronically accepted the Kaiser Foundation Health Plan Arbitration Agreement, which states:

 

Kaiser Foundation Health Plan Arbitration Agreement — California

I understand that (except for Small Claims Court cases, claims subject to a Medicare appeals procedure or the ERISA claims procedure regulation, and any other claims that cannot be subject to binding arbitration under governing law) any dispute between myself, my heirs, relatives, or other associated parties on the one hand and Kaiser Foundation Health Plan, Inc. (KFHP), any contracted health care providers, administrators, or other associated parties on the other hand, for alleged violation of any duty arising out of or related to membership in KFHP, including any claim for medical or hospital malpractice (a claim that medical services were unnecessary or unauthorized or were improperly, negligently, or incompetently rendered), for premises liability, or relating to the coverage for, or delivery of, services or items, irrespective of legal theory, must be decided by binding arbitration under California law and not by lawsuit or resort to court process, except as applicable law provides for judicial review of arbitration proceedings. I agree to give up our right to a jury trial and accept the use of binding arbitration. I understand that the full arbitration provision is contained in the Evidence of Coverage.

 

By enrolling in a Kaiser Permanente plan, I understand that this action will serve as my electronic signature of agreement to the conditions provided in Kaiser Foundation Health Plan Arbitration Agreement (above) and that by law this electronic signature will have the same effect as a signature on a paper form.

 

Note: If you do not wish to accept the arbitration agreement above you must make a new Health Plan selection.

 

(Petition Ex. E.) 

 

The Agreement/EOC contains a provision that provides for binding arbitration of Plaintiff’s claims against Defendant.  The Binding Arbitration states, in part:

 

Scope of arbitration Any dispute shall be submitted to binding arbitration if all of the following requirements are met:

 

·       The claim arises from or is related to an alleged violation of any duty incident to or arising out of or relating to this EOC or a Member Party’s relationship to Kaiser Foundation Health Plan, Inc. (Health Plan), including any claim for medical or hospital malpractice (a claim that medical services were unnecessary or unauthorized or were improperly, negligently, or incompetently rendered), for premises liability, or relating to the coverage for, or delivery of, services or items, irrespective of the legal theories upon which the claim is asserted.

·       The claim is asserted by one or more Member Parties against one or more Kaiser Permanente Parties or by one or more Kaiser Permanente Parties against one or more Member Parties

·       Governing law does not prevent the use of binding arbitration to resolve the claim

[…]

“Kaiser Permanente Parties” include:

·       Kaiser Foundation Health Plan, Inc.

·       Kaiser Foundation Hospitals

·       KP Cal, LLC

[…]

·       Any employee or agent of any of the foregoing

(Petition Ex. A.)

 

“The petitioner bears the burden of proving the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by a preponderance of the evidence, and a party opposing the petition bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence any fact necessary to its defense. In these summary proceedings, the trial court sits as the trier of fact, weighing all the affidavits, declarations, and other documentary evidence, as well as oral testimony received at the court’s discretion, to reach a final determination.”  (Giuliano v. Inland Empire Personnel, Inc. (2007) 149 Cal.App.4th 1276, 1284.)  Because the existence of the agreement is a statutory prerequisite to granting the motion, the moving party bears the burden of proving its existence by a preponderance of the evidence.  If the opposing party raises a defense to enforcement, such as fraud in the execution voiding the agreement, that party bears the burden of producing evidence of, and proving by a preponderance of the evidence, any fact necessary to the defense.  The facts are to be proven by affidavit or declaration and documentary evidence, with oral testimony taken only in the court’s discretion.  The trial court’s role is to resolve these factual issues, not merely to determine whether evidence opposing the petition has sufficient substantiality.  (Rosenthal v. Great Western Financial Sec. Corp. (1996) 14 Cal.4th 394, 413-414.)

 

Here, Defendant has established the existence of an agreement to arbitrate by a preponderance of the evidence.  Defendant presents evidence that Plaintiff personally enrolled in the Kaiser Health Plan through Asurion's online benefits enrollment system and electronically accepted the Kaiser Foundation Health Plan Arbitration Agreement as part of that enrollment.  (Petition, ¶¶ 6, 11-13; Adkins Decl., ¶¶ 5-6; Asurion Decl., ¶ 4.)  The Agreement/EOC expressly requires arbitration of claims arising out of a member's relationship with Health Plan, including claims for premises liability, and expressly identifies Defendant as a “Kaiser Permanente Party” subject to the arbitration provision.  (Petition, Ex. A.)

 

Plaintiff's claims fall within the scope of the arbitration agreement.  Plaintiff alleges that he sustained injuries after encountering a dangerous condition at a Kaiser facility and asserts causes of action for negligence and premises liability against Defendant.  Because the arbitration provision expressly encompasses premises liability claims asserted by a Health Plan member against Kaiser Foundation Hospitals, the present controversy is subject to arbitration.

 

Plaintiff has not filed an opposition to the motion and therefore presents no evidence disputing the existence or enforceability of the arbitration agreement.

 

Accordingly, Defendant’s motion is GRANTED.

 

Motion to Stay Proceedings

 

California Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.4 states that the court shall stay the action or proceeding if the court has ordered arbitration.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.4.)

 

Accordingly, the Court will STAY proceedings pending arbitration.

 

Conclusion

 

Based on the foregoing, Kaiser Foundation Hospitals’ unopposed motion to compel arbitration is GRANTED.

 

The action is STAYED pending completion of arbitration.
