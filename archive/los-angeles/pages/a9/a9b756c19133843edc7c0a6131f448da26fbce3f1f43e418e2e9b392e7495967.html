Case Number: 21STCV02534    Hearing Date: July 27, 2026    Dept: B

Tentative Ruling

Kelly v. Westfield Topanga Owner, Case no. 21STCV02534

Hearing date July 27, 2026

Plaintiff Kelly’s Motion to Compel Compliance/RFP re: CSI Business Records

Plaintiff Kelly sues defendants Westfield Topanga Owner LLC, associated Westfield entities, CSI Electrical Contractors, Inc. and others for injuries sustained when plaintiff, who was working on electrical wiring at the Westfield Topanga property, fell from a ladder. Plaintiff served an amended deposition notice and inspection demand on CSI on 4/22/26 and moves to compel compliance. CSI opposes.

A deposition subpoena for production of business records is governed by Code Civ. Proc. §1985.3. When the subpoenaed party fails to comply, the court "may make an order directing compliance with the subpoena" pursuant to Code Civ. Proc. §1987.1(a). Further, the court must impose monetary sanctions for failing to respond unless substantial justification is shown. See Code Civ. Proc. §§2025.480(j), 2020.410(d).

Plaintiff asserts CSI refuses to produce documents relating to “how CSI controlled safety on this project, its Project Safety Audits, its Activity Hazard Analysis forms, its coordination and look-ahead schedules, its post-incident review of the… accident at issue, and its communications about the ladder and the project’s ladder policy.” Mtn. 2:27-3:2. Plaintiff argues CSI claimed “no responsive investigative reports exist,” and “no responsive documents regarding the ladder” are in its possession. Id. at 3:3-5. Plaintiff alleges these claims are false, and CSI’s relevance and scope objections are brought in bad faith in an attempt to prevent plaintiff from obtaining discovery as to CSI’s alleged control of the subject ladder and worksite safety procedures.

CSI argues plaintiff’s motion procedurally fails to comply with Code Civ. Proc. §1005(b). Per section 1005(b), supporting papers must be filed and served sixteen court days before hearing. CSI argues plaintiff failed to attach the deposition subpoena and attached demand for production and CSI’s objections, only attaching meet-and-confer communications. Decl. Henriks, exh. 1.

CSI attached the discovery to the opposition, and plaintiff to the reply. Both parties are aware of the specific discovery and objections at issue. The parties have complete information to bring and defend this motion. The procedural argument is unavailing.

CSI argues the subject ladder was not in its possession or control, so documents relating to CSI’s own employees’ usage of ladders is irrelevant. Discovery in California is broad. At issue is not merely the subject ladder, but CSI’s general control and enforcement of safety policies at the jobsite. Plaintiff is entitled to discovery regarding CSI employees’ use of ladders at the jobsite.

CSI stands on its relevance arguments and fails to show substantial justification. CSI argues the documents are irrelevant, not in its possession and control or have previously been produced. CSI argues “all Weekly Subcontractor/ Foreman Coordination Meeting Minutes, including all Look-ahead schedules and Production Lists for the project through May 2019” have been produced. See Opp. 11:8-10. CSI argues there are “no other Post Incident Audit and walkthroughs/ reviews conducted by Paul Sanders other than the January 28, 2019, report which CSI produced.” Id. at 11:11-12. CSI argues it “produced to Plaintiff all AHA reports in its possession, custody and control.” Id. at 11:18. CSI argues it produced “a three-page report related to the January 24, 2019, Stand-Down Safety Meeting… This is all CSI had in its possession, custody and control.” Id. at 11:20-21. CSI argues it does not have any documents regarding the location, examination or storage of the subject ladder.

CSI’s arguments are made in its opposition, not under penalty of perjury. Counsel Ross’ declaration, under penalty of perjury, confirms the production referenced was actually made. Decl. Ross, paras. 10-11, 14-15. Ross declares “Plaintiff is not entitled to any further production of documents as CSI has produced or will have produced at the time of the hearing on this Motion to Compel all relevant documents in its possession, custody and control and no additional documents can or should be ordered to be produced.” Id. at para. 15.

This statement is concerning, as counsel does not state CSI has no documents relating to safety or control, as alleged in the body of the motion. Further, an assertion that CSI “will have” produced all relevant discovery by the time of hearing does not provide clarity as to what CSI has produced and what it intends to produce prior to the hearing. To the extent CSI states it will serve documents, which should have been produced in response to the inspection demand, CSI cannot show substantial justification in withholding such documents.

As CSI withheld discovery relating to its own employees’ usage of ladders and acted without substantial justification, the court must impose sanctions. See Code Civ. Proc. §§2025.480(j), 2020.410(d). Plaintiff requests a rate of $650/hour. See Decl. Henriks, para. 11. This is unreasonable for a simple discovery dispute.

The court finds $450/hour for work on this motion is reasonable. Plaintiff requests 1.5 hours for review of correspondence and discovery production. Id. at para. 12. Sanctions are not awardable for basic litigation conduct which would naturally occur, regardless of this motion being filed. Plaintiff requests 1.5 hours for separate statement drafting. Id. Plaintiff requests 4.5 hours for legal research and motion drafting and 1 hour for revisions. Id. Seven hours on a basic discovery motion is not reasonable; the court awards 4 hours for research, drafting and revision.

GRANTED; CSI to produce all non-privileged documents relating to employees’ usage of ladders at the worksite within 30 days. The court to confer with counsel regarding CSI’s alleged lack of further responsive documents and further production since filing of this motion. Sanctions of 5.5 hours at $450/hour against CSI only, totaling $2,475, payable within 30 days.


Case Number: 23STCV26846    Hearing Date: July 27, 2026    Dept: B

Tentative Ruling

Avalos v. Allied Universal Security Services, Case no. 23STCV26846

Hearing date July 27, 2026

Defendant Trejo’s Motion for Summary Adjudication as to Punitive Damages

Plaintiff Avalos sues defendants Allied Universal Security Services, LLP, White Memorial Medical Center and Trejo, alleging he was forcibly removed from White Memorial by Trejo, causing injuries. The court granted leave for plaintiff to file an amended complaint alleging exemplary/punitive damages against Trejo. 3/10/26 Min. Order. Trejo moves for summary adjudication on punitive damages. Plaintiff opposes.

The rules applicable to summary judgments apply to motions for summary adjudication. Blue Shield of California Life & Health Ins. Co. v. Superior Court (2011) 192 Cal.App.4th 727. Moving party bears the burden of persuasion that there is no triable issue of material fact and it is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850. Defendant may: (1) affirmatively negate one or more element of the cause of action; or (2) establish “plaintiff does not possess, and cannot reasonably obtain, needed evidence...” Id.; Code Civ. Proc. §437c(o)(2). Once moving party meets the initial burden, it shifts to respondent to show a triable issue of material fact. Aguilar, supra.

Plaintiff is only entitled to punitive damages on a showing of oppression, fraud or malice. Civ. Code §3294(a). Facts giving rise to a claim for punitive damages must be pleaded specifically; vague or conclusory allegations are insufficient. G.D. Searle & Company v. Superior Court (1975) 49 Cal.App.3d 22, 29. Recklessness or negligence, even gross negligence, are insufficient to support an award of punitive damage; carelessness or ignorance are not enough. Dawes v. Superior Court (1980) 111 Cal.App.3d 82, 88.

Trejo moves for summary adjudication, arguing plaintiff cannot establish malice, oppression or fraud as required by Civ. Code §3294. Trejo argues the evidence demonstrates he was “responding defensively to Plaintiff’s aggression, not acting with willful disregard of Plaintiff’s rights or safety.” Mtn., 4:6-7.

The subject incident occurred on 6/25/23 outside defendant White Memorial Medical Center in Los Angeles. SSMF 1. Plaintiff presented with injuries sustained when he was assaulted by two non-party individuals. SSMF 2. Trejo was employed as a security professional at White Memorial by defendant Allied. SSMF 3. While waiting for treatment, plaintiff became agitated. SSMF 4. Plaintiff got into an argument with a nurse and used profanity toward staff. SSMF 5-6. The nurse requested plaintiff be escorted off the property. SSMF 8.

Nonparty security professionals attempted to remove plaintiff. SSMF 9. Plaintiff remained uncooperative; Trejo was called in to assist. SSMF 10-11. After removing plaintiff from the property, plaintiff continued making threats towards Trejo and began moving back towards the hospital. SSMF 15-17. Trejo told plaintiff to leave the area, after which plaintiff struck Trejo and continued making threats. SSMF 18-23. Plaintiff was restrained by other Allied employees and moved further away from the hospital. SSMF 24, 26.

Trejo saw plaintiff place a hand in a pocket. SSMF 27. Trejo asserts “Due to the ongoing threats towards his own safety and [p]laintiff’s aggression in starting the altercation… Trejo feared that [p]laintiff may have a weapon.” SSMF 28. Trejo “took action to try to neutralize any potential threat to his life.” SSMF 29. Plaintiff alleges Trejo called him a “ni**er.” SSMF 33. Trejo admits to using the slur during the altercation. SSMF 34. Trejo states he is “an African American and use of that word is culturally normal and used in the same manner as ‘dude,’ ‘bro,’ or ‘man.’” SSMF 35. Trejo asserts it was not intended as a racial slur or in a derogatory manner. Id. Trejo asserts plaintiff referred to Trejo as a “ni**er.” SSMF 36. This is sufficient to shift the burden to plaintiff.

Plaintiff argues Trejo’s evidence as to plaintiff’s alleged threats and insults arises from Trejo’s self-serving declaration. Per Trejo’s deposition testimony, Trejo followed plaintiff into the street where Trejo allegedly insulted plaintiff. Plaintiff argues how the physical altercation began is disputed.

Allied’s use-of-force policies prevent use of physical force unless an employees feels their life is threatened. SSAMF 37-43. Allied’s procedures require employees to call the Los Angeles Police Department if a party is non-cooperative. SSAMF 43. The LAPD was not called during the subject incident by Trejo or another Allied employee. SSAMF 44.

Trejo was the only Allied employee to re-approach plaintiff after plaintiff was removed from White Memorial. SSAMF 45. A non-party Allied employee instructed Trejo to “walk away,” but Trejo refused to comply. SSAMF 46; Depo. Peralez, 51:7-21. Trejo admitted he followed plaintiff off the property. SSAMF 47; Depo. Trejo, 236:1-15. Trejo admitted to punching plaintiff one time. SSAMF 49; Depo. Trejo, 272:3-7. Trejo admitted he never saw plaintiff with a weapon. SSAMF 50; Depo. Trejo, 237:2-18.

Trejo admitted to calling plaintiff “ni**er” “a bunch of times” and that he was “cussing at him, calling him a ni**a” while following plaintiff into the street. SSAMF 51; Depo. Trejo, 190:8-21, 237:19-25; 246:15-25. Plaintiff testified Trejo kicked plaintiff, causing him to temporarily lose consciousness in the street. SSAMF 52; Depo. Avalos, 107:18-22.

It is clear a physical altercation between plaintiff and Trejo occurred. Insults, including racial slurs, were exchanged. The evidence that Trejo pursued plaintiff into the street and off the property that Trejo was hired to protect, as well as evidence that other Allied employees warned Trejo to walk away, and Trejo refused to do so, create a triable issue of fact as to who initiated the confrontation and how it unfolded. This creates a triable issue of fact as to whether Trejo acted with malice in pursuing, insulting and striking plaintiff.

“Malice” means conduct intended by defendant to cause injury to plaintiff, or despicable conduct by defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of rights or safety of others. Civ. Code §3294(c)(1). Malice does not require an actual intent to harm. Conscious disregard for the safety of another is sufficient where defendant is aware of the probable dangerous consequences of his conduct and willfully and deliberately fails to avoid those consequences. See Taylor v. Superior Court (1979) 24 Cal.3d 890, 895-896; See also Hoch v. Allied-Signal, Inc. (1994) 24 Cal.App.4th 48, 60-61. Pursuing plaintiff off hospital property, using epithets and striking plaintiff could be found by a reasonable trier of fact to be proof of malice. Summary adjudication is not appropriate. DENIED.


Case Number: 26VECV00775    Hearing Date: July 27, 2026    Dept: B

Tentative Ruling

Rize Credit Union v. Tsatinian, Case no. 26VECV00775

Hearing date July 27, 2026

Defendant Tsatinian’s Motion to Set Aside Default Judgment

Plaintiff Rize Credit Union, formerly known as SCE, sued defendant Tsatinian for collection and repossession on an automobile lease. Defendant was served via substitute service on 2/15/26 via service on his co-occupant Barbasian. See 2/18/26 Proof of Serv. Defendant, in pro se, moves to set aside the default, asserting he never received notice of this lawsuit. Plaintiff opposes.

Plaintiff argues the motion is procedurally improper, as defendant failed to serve the motion on plaintiff. Defendant filed no proof of service as to this motion. Plaintiff did file substantive opposition, accompanied by supporting affidavits. The court will consider the motion.

Plaintiff requests judicial notice of: (1) summons and complaint filed 2/6/26; (2) proof of substitute service filed 2/18/26; (3) 6/30/26 minute order; (4) Padilla declaration filed 7/13/26; (5) Espinoza declaration filed 7/13/26; (6) Curiel declaration filed 7/13/26; and (7) Abeshyan declaration filed 7/13/26. GRANTED pursuant to Evid. Code §452(d).

Defendant moves to set aside the 4/8/26 default and 4/9/26 default judgment as void, asserting he never received notice of this lawsuit. See Decl. Tsatinian, paras. 1-3. Defendant asserts he did not learn of this lawsuit until he independently checked court records. Id. at para. 4. Defendant does not dispute that substitute service was made at the address where he resides. Defendant offers no details explaining how or when he became aware of this lawsuit or explaining why he chose to check court records. Defendant fails to offer evidence, such as a declaration of Barbasian, defendant’s co-occupant and the party on whom substitute service was purportedly made, regarding whether service was actually accomplished and whether documents were provided to defendant.

Plaintiff argues the motion is unsupported by facts or details which would permit setting aside the default and default judgment. Plaintiff argues defendant’s address, as confirmed by the Post Master, is the address where defendant resides and is the proper address for service. See Decl. Padilla, exh. 1. This is also the address listed on defendant’s motion. See Mtn., p. 1. Plaintiff argues co-occupant Barbasian was informed of the contents of the summons and complaint when substitute service was made. See Decl. Padialla, exhs. 3-4.

A proof of service by a registered process server creates a rebuttable presumption that service was effective. The undisputed fact that the address used for service is indeed defendant’s residence supports this presumption. However, California favors resolving disputes on their merits, and defendant filed this motion and expressed an intent to defend this litigation.

The motion as presented lacks facts sufficient to permit the court to set aside the default. The court will continue the hearing to allow defendant to file supporting evidence, such as an affidavit stating when and where defendant learned of this lawsuit, or an affidavit from Barbasian explaining how defendant did not receive notice of this lawsuit.
