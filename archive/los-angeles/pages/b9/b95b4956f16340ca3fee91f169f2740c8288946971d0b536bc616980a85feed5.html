DEPARTMENT 407 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 24STCV14592    Hearing Date: July 2, 2026    Dept: 407

Tentative Ruling

 

Judge Brock T. Hammond, Department 407

 

 

HEARING DATE:     July 2, 2026                                                    TRIAL DATE:  Not set

                                                          

CASE:                         Hong Tao Su, et al. v. First General Bank, et al.

 

CASE NO.:                 24STCV14592

 

 

DEMURRER WITH MOTION TO STRIKE

 

MOVING PARTY:              Defendant First General Bank

 

RESPONDING PARTY:     Plaintiffs Hong Tao Su and Xiao Ling Tao

 

 

Plaintiffs Hong Tao Su (Su) and Xiao Ling Tao (Tao) (collectively, Plaintiffs) have been tenants of Unit B at 314 E. Mission Road, San Gabriel, California 91766 since August 1, 2021 (the Property). In February 2024, Franklin Shen aka Feiuye Shen (Shen) and SJS Associates, Inc. (SJS) (collectively, the Trustee) conducted a non-judicial foreclosure of the Property at the request of First General Bank (the Bank).  Plaintiffs allege that the Bank and Trustee thwarted their right to bid on the Property.

 

I.          INTRODUCTION

 

On June 11, 2024, Plaintiffs filed a Complaint against defendants Bank and Shen dba Lender’s Foreclosure Services for (1) Failure to Give Notice of Non-Judicial Foreclosure; (2) Violation of Plaintiffs’ Rights as Eligible Tenant Buyers; (3) Unlawful Chilling Restraint of Bids; (4) Negligence; (5) Cancellation of Instrument (Trustee’s Deed); (6) Violation of Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200, et seq; and (7) Declaratory Relief.

 

On January 17, 2025, the Bank filed a motion for judgment on the pleadings (MJOP) on the Complaint.

 

On March 3, 2025, the court granted the motion as to the first, second, fifth, sixth, and seventh causes of action, and denied as to the third cause of action.  With the exception of the first cause of action, Plaintiff was granted leave to amend.

 

On March 17, 2025, Plaintiffs filed the First Amended Complaint. 

 

On June 5, 2025, the court granted Plaintiffs’ motion for leave to file the Second Amended Complaint (SAC). The SAC alleged causes of action against the Bank, Shen, and SJS Associates, Inc. (SJS) (collectively, Defendants) for (1) Violation of Plaintiffs’ Rights as Eligible Bidders; (2) Unlawful Chilling Restraint of Bids; (3) Tort In Essence; (4) Negligence; (5) Fraud; (6) Cancellation of Instrument (Trustee’s Deed); (7) Equitable Cause of Action to Set Aside Foreclosure Sale; (8) Violation of Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200, et seq; and (9) Declaratory Relief. 

 

On December 4, 2025, the court sustained the demurrers to the 1st, 4th, 5th, 6th, 7th, 8th, and 9th causes of action and granted leave to amend the 5th, 6th, and 7th causes of action only.  The court overruled the demurrers to the 2nd and 3rd causes of action.

 

On December 15, 2025, Plaintiffs filed the Third Amended Complaint (TAC) against Defendants alleging causes of action for (1) Unlawful Chilling Restraint of Bids; (2) Tort In Essence; (3) Negligence; (4) Fraud; (5) Cancellation of Instrument (Trustee’s Deed); and (6) Equitable Cause of Action to Set Aside Foreclosure Sale.

 

On January 16, 2026, the Bank filed this Demurrer and Motion to Strike portions of the TAC.

 

On March 24, 2026, Shen filed a joinder to the Bank’s demurrer.

 

On May 6, 2025, Plaintiffs filed oppositions to the Bank’s demurrer and motion to strike, as well as Shen’s joinder.

 

On May 12, 2026, the Bank filed replies.  On the same day, Shen filed a separate reply.

 

The court begins by addressing the Bank’s demurrer.

 

II.        DISCUSSION RE DEMURRER

 

            A.  Legal Standard

 

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action.¿ (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)¿ When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context, accepting the alleged facts as true.¿ (Nolte v. Cedars-Sinai Medical Center (2015) 236 Cal.App.4th 1401, 1406.)¿ “Because a demurrer challenges defects on the face of the complaint, it can only refer to matters outside the pleading that are subject to judicial notice.”¿ (Arce ex rel. Arce v. Kaiser Found. Health Plan, Inc. (2010) 181 Cal.App.4th 471, 556.)¿¿¿ 

 

B.  Judicial Notice

 

The Bank requests judicial notice of five documents filed in this case and legislative history.  The court previously granted this request in connection to the Bank’s demurrer to the SAC.

 

Shen requests judicial notice of two Fictitious Business Name Statements.  The unopposed request is GRANTED.  (Evid. Code, § 452(c), (g), (h).)

 

C.  Analysis

 

The Bank demurs to the third, fourth, fifth, and sixth causes of action.  The court addresses each demurrer in turn.

1.  Negligence (3rd Cause of Action)

Previously, the court sustained the Bank’s demurrer to the negligence cause of action in the SAC because Plaintiffs did not clearly state the basis of their negligence claim against the Bank.  (See Minute Order, 12/04/25.)  Plaintiffs now advance three theories in support of the negligence claim: (1) the Bank negligently chose a substitute trustee who has a history of flouting the rules that govern his business (TAC, ¶¶ (a), (b), and (h)); (2) the Bank negligently failed to identify the true tenants in Unit B (for the purpose of giving them notice), despite its knowledge that Plaintiffs lived there and had statutory rights to buy the Property (TAC, ¶¶ 97 (c) – (e)); and (3) the Bank (at least negligently) entered into a conspiratorial agreement with Shen that the Bank owned the Property before the time for bidding had closed, and actively aided and abetted Shen’s trespass in Plaintiffs’ home and invasion of Plaintiffs’ privacy (as purported “agent” for the Bank as “owner”), for the purpose of creating a false record that Unit B was supposedly “vacant” (TAC, ¶¶ 97(f), (g), and (i)). 

The Bank argues the negligence claim fails because (i) Plaintiffs improperly rely on purported violations of Civil Code section 2924m and (ii) the TAC does not allege an actionable duty owed by the Bank to Plaintiffs.[1]  The court finds the first argument is meritorious.  First, the court sustained the Bank’s demurrer to the first cause of action in the SAC which was based on Civil Code section 2924m.  Plaintiffs’ “new” allegations ultimately relate only to whether they were given notice of the auction under Civil Code section 2924m.  As the court discussed in its ruling, Section 2924m does not provide for a private cause of action.  (Minute Order, 12/04/25.)  Having so concluded, the court does not address the Bank’s third grounds for demurrer of the negligence claim.

The demurrer to the third cause of action is SUSTAINED.  Leave to amend is DENIED.

2.      Fraud (4th Cause of Action)

Previously, the court sustained the Bank’s demurrer to the fraud cause of action in the SAC because the SAC focused on the Trustee’s conduct, did not identify any misrepresentations by the Bank made directly to Plaintiffs, and did not allege specific facts to establish that the Bank engaged in any conduct amounting to fraud. 

After reviewing the fraud cause of action in the TAC, the court finds the TACs still does not state a fraud claim against the Bank.

To establish a claim for intentional misrepresentation, the plaintiff must allege seven essential elements: (1) the defendant represented to the plaintiff that an important fact was true; (2) that representation was false; (3) the defendant knew that the representation was false when the defendant made it, or the defendant made the representation recklessly and without regard for its truth; (4) the defendant intended that the plaintiff rely on the representation; (5) the plaintiff reasonably relied on the¿representation;¿(6) the plaintiff was harmed; and (7) the plaintiff's reliance on the defendant's representation was a substantial factor in causing that harm to the plaintiff.¿ (Manderville¿v. PCG & S Group, Inc.¿(2007) 146 Cal.App.4th 1486, 1498; CACI No. 1900.)¿¿¿¿¿ 

Here, Plaintiffs do not add any allegations of misrepresentations that the Bank made to the Plaintiffs. This alone is fatal to Plaintiffs’ fraud claim. 

In opposition, Plaintiffs cite South Bay Building Enterprises, Inc. v. Riviera Lend-Lease, Inc. (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 1111 (South Bay) for the proposition that fraudulent actions taken by the beneficiary of a deed of trust can give rise to liability in tort.  That case is inapposite.  The Court of Appeal in South Bay held that the trustor or junior lienor as defined under Civil Code section 2924h(g) have the right to recover damages from the trustee and the beneficiary of the foreclosing lien if there have been material irregularities in the conduct of the foreclosure.  As alleged, Plaintiffs are neither trustors nor junior lienors.  The fraud claim against the Bank is deficient.

The demurrer to the fourth cause of action is SUSTAINED. Leave to amend is DENIED.

3.      Cancellation of Instrument (Trustee’s Deed) (5th Cause of Action)

“A written instrument, in respect to which there is a reasonable apprehension that if left outstanding may cause serious injury to a person against whom it is void or voidable, may, upon his application, be so adjudged, and ordered to be delivered up or canceled.” (§ 3412.) “[A]bsent an interest in the property, a party has no standing to ask the court to quiet title in the property or to obtain damages for the cloud on title. An action to cancel a trustee's deed or other instrument purportedly transferring title is no different. ‘One without any title or interest in the property cannot maintain such an action.’”  (Chao Fu, Inc. v. Chen (2012) 206 Cal.App.4th 48, 59 citing Osborne v. Abels (1939) 30 Cal.App.2d 729, 731.)

Previously, the court sustained the Bank’s demurrer to the cancellation of instrument cause of action against the Bank because it was predicated on a fraud claim which was not sufficiently pled.  The court also rejected Plaintiffs’ argument that Civil Code section 2934a could provide an additional basis for cancellation of the trustee’s deed because Section 2934a applies to sales conducted by a substituted trustee, which is not a fact alleged in this case.  Section 2934a is inapposite for the further reason that it applies only when a trustee has been substituted in after a Notice of Trustee’s Sale has been recorded.  The court granted leave to amend based on Plaintiffs’ representation that a trustee preceded Shen and SJS, thus triggering Section 2934a.  The court has reviewed the fifth cause of action in the TAC.  Plaintiffs do not add any such allegation.  The fifth cause of action fails.

The demurrer to the fifth cause of action is SUSTAINED.  Leave to amend is DENIED.

4.      Equitable Cause of Action to Set Aside Foreclosure Sale (6th Cause of Action)

This cause of action is based on Defendants’ fraudulent conduct.  (SAC, ¶ 108.)  Because the court has found the fraud cause of action is insufficiently pleaded against the Bank, the court also finds the sixth cause of action is insufficiently pled.  Accordingly, the demurrer to the sixth cause of action is SUSTAINED.  Leave to amend is DENIED.

D.  Conclusion

The Bank’s demurrer is Sustained.  Leave to amend is Denied.

III.       DISCUSSION RE SHEN’S JOINDER TO BANK’S DEMURRER

 

            Plaintiffs oppose Shen’s joinder on the ground it is not authorized by the Code of Civil Procedure.  In support, Plaintiffs cite Haah v. Kim (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 45.  Haah does not aid Plaintiffs.  In Haah, the Court of Appeal merely quoted without approval or disapproval the trial court’s sustaining of a joinder to a demurrer.  (Haah, at pp. 51-52.)  Absent authority that a party cannot “join” a demurrer, the court must consider whether the Bank’s arguments apply equally to Shen.  On this point, the court rules in favor of Plaintiffs.  Perhaps the Bank’s arguments apply to Shen.  But the Bank and Shen are not similarly situated.  Shen was the Trustee and conducted the sale, not the Bank.  Plaintiffs allege conduct by Shen separate and distinct from the Bank.  The court will not wade through the allegations in the TAC to piece together Shen’s basis for demurrer.  Accordingly, Shen’s joinder is OVERRULED.

 

IV.       DISCUSSION RE MOTION TO STRIKE & JOINDER TO MOTION TO STRIKE

 

            The Bank seeks an order striking several allegations from the TAC on the ground Plaintiffs attempt to circumvent the court’s orders granting judgment on the pleadings and sustaining the Bank’s demurrer to Plaintiffs’ previous causes of action for failure to give notice of trustee’s non-judicial foreclosure sale and violation of Civil Code section 2924m.  (See Minute Orders, 3/3/25 and 12/04/25.)  The court agrees.  Plaintiffs cannot simply assert the same defective allegations in support of other causes of action when the court has already ruled that a violation of Civil Code section 2924f is not grounds to invalidate a foreclosure sale nor does a violation of Civil Code section 2924m provide for a private cause of action.  

           

            Accordingly, the motion to strike and the joinder are GRANTED.  Leave to amend is DENIED.

           

V.        DISPOSITIONS

 

            1.  The Bank’s Demurrer to the TAC is Sustained.  Leave to Amend is Denied.

            2.  Shen’s Joinder to the Bank’s Demurrer is Overruled.

            3.  The Bank’s Motion to Strike is Granted.

            4.  Shen’s Joinder to the Bank’s Motion to Strike is Granted.

 

            Plaintiffs are ordered to serve and file the Fourth Amended Complaint for the sole purpose of removing the defective causes of action and allegations.  The amended pleading is to be served and file within 15 days.

 

Defendant to give notice, unless waived.

Case Number: 25STCV23624    Hearing Date: July 2, 2026    Dept: 407

Tentative Ruling

 

Judge Brock T. Hammond, Department 407

 

 

HEARING DATE:     July 2, 2026                                        TRIAL DATE:  January 10, 2018

                                                          

CASE:                         Luis Gudiel, on behalf of himself and current and former aggrieved employees v. Landscape Development LLC, et al.

 

ASE NO.:                    25STCV23624

 

 

DEFENDANTS’ MOTION TO COMPEL

PLAINTIFF LUIS GUDIEL TO ARBITRATION

 

MOVING PARTY:               Defendants Landscape Development, Inc. and Landscape Development, LLC

 

RESPONDING PARTY:     No opposition

 

 

I.          BACKGROUND

On August 11, 2025, Plaintiff, Luis Gudiel, commenced this PAGA action against Defendants, Landscape Development, Inc. and Landscape Development, LLC, on behalf of himself and all current and former aggrieved employees. 

 

On October 1, 2025, Defendants filed an Answer to the Complaint. 

 

            On April 15, 2026, Defendants filed this motion to compel arbitration.

                     

            The motion is unopposed.

 

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

 

Under California¿law,¿public policy favors arbitration as an efficient and less expensive means of resolving private disputes.¿ (Moncharsh¿v.¿Heily¿&¿Blase¿(1992) 3 Cal.4th 1, 8-9;¿AT&T Mobility LLC v. Concepcion¿(2011) 563 U.S. 333, 339 (Concepcion).) ¿Similarly, the Federal Arbitration Act (FAA) reflects a liberal federal policy favoring arbitration and the fundamental principle that arbitration is a matter of contract.¿ (Concepcion, 563 U.S. at p. 339.)¿ In line with these principles, courts must place arbitration agreements on an equal footing with other contracts and enforce them according to their terms.¿ (Ibid.)¿ “[U]nder both the FAA and California law, ‘arbitration agreements are valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, save upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract.’”¿ (Higgins v. Sup. Ct. (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 1238, 1247.)¿ Accordingly, whether an agreement is governed by the California Arbitration Act (CAA) or the¿FAA, courts resolve doubts regarding the scope of arbitrable issues in favor of arbitration.¿ (Moncharsh, 3 Cal.4th at p. 9; Comedy Club, Inc. v. Improv WestAssocs.¿(9th Cir. 2009) 553 F.3d 1277, 1284.) 

 

While the arbitration agreement may be governed by the FAA, the agreement may be enforced via the summary procedures provided by California arbitration law.¿(Rosenthal v. Great Western Financial Securities Corp. (1996) 14 Cal.4th 394, 409-410.) It is a “general and unassailable proposition . . . that States may establish the rules of procedure governing litigation in their own courts,” even though the controversy is governed by substantive federal law. (Felder v. Casey¿(1988) 487 U.S. 131, 138.) By the same token, however, a state procedural rule must give way “if it impedes the uniform application of the federal statute essential to effectuate its purpose, even though the procedure would apply to similar actions arising under state law.” (McCarroll v. L.A. County etc. Carpenters¿(1957) 49 Cal.2d 45, 61, 62.) 

 

A party to an arbitration agreement may seek a court order compelling the parties to arbitrate a dispute covered by their agreement.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2.)  California statutes create a “summary proceeding” for resolving petitions or motions to compel arbitration. (Engalla v. Permanente Medical Group, Inc. (1997) 15 Cal.4th 951, 972.)  

 

When a party has filed a petition to compel arbitration, the trial court must determine in a summary proceeding whether an “agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists.” (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 1281.2, 1290.2; Rosenthal, 14 Cal.4th at pp. 412–413.)  In that proceeding, because the existence of the agreement is a statutory prerequisite to granting the petition, “[t]he petitioner bears the burden of proving the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by the preponderance of the evidence, and a party opposing the petition bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence any fact necessary to its defense. [Citation.] In these summary proceedings, the trial court sits as a trier of fact, weighing all the affidavits, declarations, and other documentary evidence, as well as oral testimony received at the court's discretion, to reach a final determination.” (Engalla, 15 Cal.4th at p. 972; Rosenthal, 14 Cal.4th at p. 413.) 

 

The court should grant the motion unless it finds either (1) no written agreement to arbitrate exists; (2) the right to compel arbitration has been waived; (3) grounds exist for rescission¿of the agreement; or (4) litigation is pending that may render the arbitration unnecessary or create conflicting rulings on common issues.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2; Condee v. Longwood Management Corp. (2001) 88 Cal.App.4th 215, 218-219.) 

 

The trial court typically decides the threshold issues of enforceability of the arbitration agreement and the scope of issues to be arbitrated. (Aanderud v. Sup.Ct. (Vivint Solar Developer, LLC) (2017) 13 Cal.App.5th 880, 891.) 

 

III.      DISCUSSION 

 

The court must decide two issues: (1) Is there an arbitration agreement? (2) Does the agreement cover Plaintiff’s claims?¿ For the reasons stated herein, the court denies the motion.

A.  Existence of an Arbitration Agreement

Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.2¿requires the trial court to order arbitration of a controversy “[o]n petition of a party to an arbitration agreement alleging the existence of a written agreement to arbitrate a controversy and that a party to the agreement refuses to arbitrate that controversy ... if it determines that an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists.”  On a motion to compel arbitration, the threshold question is whether there is an agreement to arbitrate the dispute.¿ (Trinity v. Life Ins. Co. of North America¿(2022) 78 Cal.App.5th 1111, 1120.)  The party seeking to compel arbitration bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence an agreement to arbitrate a dispute exists. (Pinnacle Museum Tower Assn. v. Pinnacle Market Development (US), LLC¿(2012) 55 Cal.4th 223, 236 (Pinnacle Museum);¿Rosenthal, supra, 14 Cal.4th at p. 413.)  To meet this burden, the moving party must first produce “prima facie evidence of a written agreement to arbitrate the controversy.”  (Rosenthal, supra, 14 Cal.App.4th at p. 413; accord,¿Gamboa v. Northeast Community Clinic¿(2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 158, 165.)  “‘If the moving party meets its initial prima facie burden and the opposing party disputes the agreement, then ... the opposing party bears the burden of producing evidence to challenge the authenticity of the agreement.” (Gamboa, 72 Cal.App.5th at p. 165.)  “If the opposing party produces such evidence, then ‘the moving party must establish with admissible evidence a valid arbitration agreement between the parties.’ ” (Trinity, supra, 78 Cal.App.5th at p. 1120.) “Despite the shifting burden of production, ‘[t]he burden of proving the agreement by a preponderance of the evidence remains with the moving party.’ ” (Ibid.)¿ 

Here, Defendants makes a prima facie case for the existence of a valid arbitration agreement.¿ Defendants submit a document titled “Mutual Agreement to Arbitrate All Claims” (hereafter, the “MAA”).  The MAA states in pertinent part:

“The Company and I agree and acknowledge that we will utilize binding arbitration as the sole and exclusive means to resolve all disputes that may arise out of or be related in any way to my application for employment and/or my employment, including but not limited to the termination of my employment and my compensation…

(Ochoa Decl., Ex. A (“MAA”).)  The copy of the MAA indicates that, on April 26, 2024, Plaintiff signed the MAA by wet signature.  The MAA provides that it is governed by the FAA.  Defendants meet their initial burden.¿ Plaintiff, having not filed an opposition, does not meet his burden to show otherwise. Accordingly, the court finds there is a valid arbitration agreement. ¿ The court proceeds to consider whether the arbitration agreement covers Plaintiff’s claims.

 

B.  Scope of the MAA

 

After reviewing the MAA, the court finds this action cannot be compelled to arbitration for two reasons.  First, the MAA includes an impermissible waiver of PAGA class actions.  It is well-settled that employment agreements compelling the waiver of representative claims under PAGA is contrary to public policy and unenforceable.  (Iskanian v. CLS Transportation Los Angeles, LLC (2014) 59 Cal.4th 348, 384, overruled on other grounds by Viking River Cruises, Inc. v. Moriana (2022) 596 U.S. 639.)  Accordingly, Plaintiff’s representative PAGA claims cannot be compelled to arbitration.

 

Second, the MAA contains a severability clause which reveals the agreement does not cover any individual or representative PAGA claims.  Specifically, the MAA states,

 

If my agreement to waive my right to bring an action under the California Private Attorneys General Act of 2004 is declared void or unenforceable and I bring an action that includes claims subject to this agreement and claims under the California Private Attorneys General Act of 2004, both the Company and I agree to stay any claims under the California Private Attorneys General Act of 2004 until the claims subject to this agreement are fully arbitrated.

 

(MAA, ¶ 13, emphasis added.)  The highlighted language makes clear that the parties did not agree to arbitrate any PAGA claim.  Because Plaintiff brings only PAGA claims, the court finds the MAA does not direct arbitration of Plaintiff’s claims.

 

IV.       CONCLUSION 

 

The motion to compel arbitration is DENIED. 

 

Plaintiff is ordered to give notice, unless waived.

Case Number: 25STCV37281    Hearing Date: July 2, 2026    Dept: 407

Tentative Ruling

 

Judge Brock T. Hammond, Department 407

 

 

HEARING DATE:     July 2, 2026                                                    TRIAL DATE:   Not set

                                                          

CASE:                         Luis E. Valle, On Behalf of Himself and Tax Payers of the State of California v. Richard Camacho, et al.

 

CASE NO.:                 25STCV37281

 

 

MOTION FOR PRELIMINARY INJUNCTION

 

MOVING PARTY:               Plaintiff Luis E. Valle

 

RESPONDING PARTY:     Defendant Los Angeles Community College District

 

 

I.          INTRODUCTION

 

            On December 23, 2025, Plaintiff Luis E. Valle dba as Valle Grande Construction, on behalf of himself and tax payers of the State of California, commenced this action against defendants Richard Camcho, Vanir Construction Management, Inc., Hector Diaz, VIS Inspection Services, Inc., Janay Wilson, Cordoba Corporation, Marco Tarantino, Julio Benitez, Jacobs Engineering Group Inc., Los Angeles Community College District (“LACCD”).  Plaintiff brings causes of action for breach of contract, breach of implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, statutory retaliation, civil extortion, civil conspiracy, declaratory and injunctive relief, and retaliatory breach of contract.   

 

            On May 20, 2026, Plaintiff filed a motion for preliminary injunction.  Plaintiff seeks an order directing LACCD to deposit or release the undisputed contractual sum of $350,000.

 

            On June 4, 2026, LACCD filed an opposition.

 

            On the same day, Plaintiff filed a reply.

 

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

In determining whether to issue a preliminary injunction, the trial court considers two factors: (1) the reasonable probability that the plaintiff will prevail on the merits at trial; and (2) a balancing of the “irreparable harm” that the plaintiff is likely to sustain if the injunction is denied compared to the harm that the defendant is likely to suffer if the court grants a preliminary injunction.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 526(a); 14859 Moorpark Homeowner’s Assn. v. VRT Corp. (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 1396, 1402; Pillsbury, Madison & Sutro v. Schectman (1997) 55 Cal.App.4th 1279, 1283.)¿¿¿¿ 

The court’s determination is guided by a “mix” of the potential-merit and interim-harm factors; the greater the plaintiff’s showing on one, the less must be shown on the other to support an injunction.  (Butt v. State of California (1992) 4 Cal.4th 668, 678 (Butt).)  The court must consider both factors.  The two factors are a sliding scale – the stronger the showing of probability of prevailing, the lesser showing is required for irreparable harm.  (Butt, 4 Cal.4th at p. 678; The Right Side Coalition v. Los Angeles Unified School District (2008) 160 Cal.App.4th 336 (reversing denial of preliminary injunction based solely on balancing of hardships without considering probability of prevailing).)¿ The plaintiff must make some showing of each factor.¿ (Jessen v. Keystone Savings & Loan Assn. (1983) 142 Cal.App.3d 454, 459.)  A court may not issue a preliminary injunction if the plaintiff cannot possibly prevail on the merits even if a strong showing of irreparable harm has been made.  (Butt, 4 Cal.4th at pp. 677-78.)  

The court’s ruling on a preliminary injunction is not an adjudication of the merits, is not a trial, and does not require a statement of decision. (Cohen v. Board of Supervisors (1985) 40 Cal.3d 277, 286; People v. Landlords Professional Services, Inc. (1986) 178 Cal.App.3d 68, 70-71.)¿ The court is not required to state its reasons for granting or denying a preliminary injunction; a cursory statement is sufficient.  (City of Los Altos v. Barnes (1992) 3 Cal.App.4th 1193, 1198.) 

III.       DISCUSSION

 

            After consideration of the briefing and the case file, the court will deny the motion.  Plaintiff has an adequate remedy at law: money damages.  Further, Plaintiff does not demonstrate a likelihood of success on the merits.  Plaintiff submits only a Notice of Default sent by LACCD to Plaintiff which demonstrates only that there is dispute over Plaintiff’s claims to unpaid contractual sums.  (See Exhibit attached to the Valle Decl.) 

 

IV.       CONCLUSION

 

            Based on the foregoing, the motion for preliminary injunction is DENIED.

 

            Defendant is ordered to give notice, unless waived.

Case Number: 26STCP00554    Hearing Date: July 2, 2026    Dept: 407

Tentative Ruling

 

Judge Brock T. Hammond, Department 407

 

 

HEARING DATE:     July 2, 2026                                                    TRIAL DATE:  N/A

                                                            

CASE:                         Mobilitas Insurance Company of Arizona v. Ethmadalage Amitha Herbert Perera

 

CASE NO.:                 26STCP00554

 

 

MOTION TO COMPEL RESPONDENT ETHMADALAGE AMITHA HERBERT

PERERA’S RESPONSES TO PETITIONER’S FORM INTERROGATORIES, SPECIAL INTERROGATORIES, REQUESTS FOR PRODUCTION OF DOCUMENTS, REQUEST FOR ADMISSIONS AND REQUEST FOR SANCTIONS

 

MOVING PARTY:               Petitioner Mobilitas Insurance Company of Arizona

 

RESPONDING PARTY:     No opposition

 

 

I.          BACKGROUND

 

On February 6, 2026, Petitioner, Mobilitas Insurance Company of Arizona, filed a petition to open unlimited civil court file to establish jurisdiction over an uninsured motorist arbitration with Respondent, Ethmadalage Amitha Herbert Perera.  

 

On March 2, 2026, Petitioner filed these motions to compel Respondent’s responses to Petitioner’s first set of Form Interrogatories, Special Interrogatories, and Requests for Production of Documents, and a motion to deem admitted the truth of the facts and genuineness of documents in Petitioner’s first set of Requests for Admissions.  Petitioner requests sanctions against Respondent and its counsel of record.

 

The motions are unopposed.

 

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

 

If a party to whom interrogatories and inspection demands were directed fails to serve a timely response, the propounding party may move for an order to compel responses without objections.¿(Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.290(b), 2031.300(b).)¿If a party to whom requests for admission are directed fails to serve a timely response, the propounding party may move for an order that the truth of the matters specified in the requests be deemed admitted.¿(Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280(b).)¿ Failure to timely serve responses waives objections to the requests. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.290(a), 2031.300(a), 2033.280(a).)¿A party moving to compel discovery responses under these statutory provisions is not required to meet and confer prior to filing the motion.¿(See Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.290(b), 2031.300(b); see also Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pacific Healthcare Consultants (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, 411 [citing Leach v. Superior Court (1980) 111 Cal.App.3d 902, 906 for the proposition that “meet and confer” requirement “did not apply when propounding party sought order compelling responses to interrogatories and sanctions for responding party's failure to respond ‘within the statutorily permitted time’”].)¿¿¿¿¿¿ 

¿ 

Monetary Sanctions¿ 

¿ 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.030 is a general statute authorizing the Court to impose discovery sanctions for “misuse of the discovery process,” which includes (without limitation) a variety of conduct such as: making, without substantial justification, an unmeritorious objection to discovery; making an evasive response to discovery; and unsuccessfully and without substantial justification making or opposing a motion to compel or limit discovery.¿ (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.010.)¿¿¿¿¿¿¿ 

¿¿¿¿¿ 

If sanctions are sought, Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.040 requires that the notice specify the identity of the person against whom sanctions are sought and the type of sanction requested, that the motion be supported in the points and authorities, and the facts be set forth in a declaration supporting the amount of any monetary sanction.¿¿¿¿¿¿ 

¿¿¿¿¿ 

If the court finds that a party has unsuccessfully made or opposed a motion to compel responses, the court “shall impose a monetary sanction . . . unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.”¿(Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.290(c), 2031.300(c).) With respect to untimely responses to admissions requests, sanctions are mandatory.¿ (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280(a).)¿¿¿ 

 

III.       DISCUSSION

 

Petitioner moves the court for an order compelling Respondent to provide responses to Defendant’s first set of Form Interrogatories, Special Interrogatories, and Requests for Production of Documents, and to deem admitted the Requests for Admission, Set One, against Respondent.

 

It is undisputed that Petitioner properly served Respondent with the at-issue discovery requests on November 21, 2025.¿ (See Jackson Decls., ¶ 1, Exs. A.)  Despite providing extensions to respond to discovery, Respondent has not served responses.¿ (Jackson Decls., ¶¶ 3-8.)  Accordingly, all objections to the discovery requests are waived.  Petitioner is entitled to an order compelling responses to the interrogatories and inspection demands and deeming the admissions¿requests as admitted against Respondent.
