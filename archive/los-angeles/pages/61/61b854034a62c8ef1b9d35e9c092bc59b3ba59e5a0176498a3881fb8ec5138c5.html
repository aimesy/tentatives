DEPARTMENT F43 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 25CHCV00039    Hearing Date: July 21, 2026    Dept: F43

Dept. F43

Hearing Date: 07-21-26

Case # 25CHCV00039, Fabian, et al. v. Joers, et al.

Trial Date: 08-07-28

 

MOTION TO COMPEL RESPONSES TO FORM INTERROGATORIES

 

MOVING PARTY:  Plaintiff Omar Morales

RESPONDING PARTY:  Attorney Shelby L. Clark

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Order compelling defendant John Carl Joers to serve verified responses to plaintiff Morales’s first set of form interrogatories and to impose monetary sanctions of $1,197.50 against defendant Joers and his counsel of record.

 

RULING:  Motion is granted.  The court awards plaintiff Morales $935 in monetary sanctions.

 

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

 

The personal injury arises from a motor vehicle collision in which defendant John Carl Joers rearended plaintiffs Estela Yadhira Fabian and Omar Morales.  Plaintiffs allege defendant Joers was driving a vehicle owned by defendant McLogan Supply Co., Inc. and acting within the scope of his employment with McLogan Supply.  On January 6, 2025, Plaintiffs filed a complaint against both defendants, asserting negligence, vicarious liability, and negligent entrustment.  McLogan Supply Co. answered on March 24, 2025, and Joers answered on November 6, 2025.

 

On April 7, 2026, plaintiff Fabian electronically served defendant Joers with her first set of form interrogatories.  (Declaration of Taylor Scott Kruse ¶ 1, Exh. 1.)  Responses were due May 11, 2026.  On May 12, 2026, plaintiff Fabian extended the deadline for defendant Joers to serve objection-free responses to May 15, 2026.  (Id. ¶ 3, Exh. 2.)  No responses were ever served.

 

On April 7, 2026, plaintiff Omar Morales electronically served his first set of form interrogatories on defendant Joers.  (Declaration of Taylor Scott Kruse ¶ 1, Exh. 1.)  Coers failed to serve responses by the May 11, 2026, deadline or the May 15, 2026, deadline extension.  (Kruse Decl., Exh. 2 [emailing attorney Shelby L. Clark, Candice Cromer, and RLR Cases at Hinshaw & Culbertson].)

 

On June 10, 2026, plaintiff Morales filed a motion to compel defendant John Carl Joers’s objection-free, verified responses to her first set of form interrogatories and to impose $1,197.50 in monetary sanctions against defendant Joers and his counsel of record, Shelby Clark and Hinshaw & Culbertson, LLP.

 

On July 17, 2026, attorney Shelby Clark filed an opposing declaration.  (Declaration of Shelby L. Clark.)  Mr. Clark claims counsel was informed that a transfer of counsel would occur, but that due to inadvertence the transfer never went through, and he was unaware his firm was responsible for the discovery requests until July 16, 2026.

 

 

MEET AND CONFER

 

No meet and confer communication is required before filing a motion to compel initial discovery responses when the opposing party has failed to serve any responses.  (Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pacific Healthcare Consultants (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, 408-409.)

 

 

ANALYSIS

 

A.  Compelling Responses to Form Interrogatories

 

A propounding party may move to compel responses to form interrogatories where the responding party fails to provide any responses.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (b).)  The propounding party must show the interrogatories were properly served, that the time to respond expired, and no response has been served.  (Leach v. Superior Court (1980) 111 Cal.App.3d 902, 905-906.)  Unless excused by a protective order, the responding party must serve responses (an answer, objection, or election to allow inspecting or copy records) within 30 days after the interrogatories are served or according to an agreed upon deadline extension.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.210, subd. (a), 2030.270.)  Failing to respond within these time limits waives objections.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (a).)

 

The court has reviewed the moving papers and finds that plaintiff Morales properly served his form interrogatories and granted defendant Joers a deadline extension.  However, defendant Joers has failed to serve any responses.

 

Defense counsel’s opposing declaration fails to provide a substantial justification for failing to provide responses or an update on the transferring on counsel.

 

Mr. Clark claims that on February 19, 2026, both he, and attorney Robert L. Reisinger, received an email from defendants’ insurance carrier, transferring the case to new counsel.  (Declaration of Shelby L. Clark ¶ 2, Exh. A.)  Mr. Clark’s firm promptly initiated the transfer, a process with which Mr. Clark was engaged in at his firm.  (Id.)  According to Mr. Clark, plaintiffs’ counsel knew of the transfers as of March 16, 2026.  (Id. ¶ 3, Exh. B.)  Around that same time, plaintiffs’ medical examinations were tentatively set for March 24, 2026.  (Id. ¶ 4.)

 

When plaintiff Morales’s discovery was served, Mr. Clark believed the transfer to new counsel had been completed, and the legal assistant who assigned to Mr. Clark returned to her prior assignment within the firm.  (Clark Decl. ¶ 5.)  Because of this, Mr. Clark did not realize the Substitution of Attorney form had not been completed or signed.  (Id.)  Mr. Clark first learned of the incomplete form on May 13, 2026, and followed up with staff to ensure its completion.  (Id. ¶ 6.)  The court notes that no such form has been filed.

 

An objection to a notice of deposition was served over Mr. Clark’s signature on June 19, 2026, but the document had been reviewed by another attorney, not Mr. Clark, and thus, Mr. Clark did not realize the transfer was not complete.  (Clark Decl. ¶ 7.)  Mr. Clark then went on vacated from June 26, 2026 through July 6, 2026, and was unaware of any communications concerning the case transfer.  (Id. ¶ 8.)  It was only on July 16, 2026, that Mr. Clark realized the transfer had not been completed and discovery remained his firm’s responsibility.  (Id. ¶ 9.)  Mr. Clark claims he believed his firm was only carbon copied on the discovery responses and he never received a phone call from plaintiffs’ counsel concerning the lack of response and the instant motions.

 

Although the evidence demonstrates plaintiffs’ counsel knew of the intent to transfer to different defense counsel, Mr. Clark does not dispute receiving the discovery requests and extension email from plaintiffs’ counsel.  Nor does the Clark Declaration indicate whether the name of the new case manager was provided to counsel.  Both the motion and the extension email were sent before Mr. Clark went on vacation.  Furthermore, a review of the May 12, 2026, “extension” email presented by plaintiff Morales clearly indicates the email was sent to Mr. Clark, with plaintiffs’ counsel carbon copying other from his firm.  The court notes Mr. Clark first learned about the incomplete Substitution form on May 13, 2026.  However, if Mr. Clark learned the form remained incomplete the day after plaintiffs’ counsel sent the substitution email, it is unclear why Mr. Clark did not check to ensure whether other communications concerning this matter had slipped through the cracks.  Finally, Mr. Clark fails to state how he learned about the outstanding discovery in this matter on July 16, 2026, or state when discovery responses will be sent to plaintiff Morales.

 

Because the court finds defense counsel fails to provide a substantial justification for failing to serve responses to discovery before plaintiff Morales filed his motion, the court grants plaintiff Omar Morales’s motion to compel defendant John Carl Joers’s responses to his first set of form interrogatories.

 

Defendant Coers is ordered to serve objection-free, verified responses within 30 days of the issuing of this order.

 

 

            B.  Sanctions

 

Plaintiff Omar Morales asks the court to impose monetary sanctions of $1,197.50 against defendant Joers and his counsel of record, Shelby Clark with Hinshaw & Culbertson, LLP, jointly and severally.  (Kruse Decl. ¶ 5.)

 

The court must impose sanctions against any party who “unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel responses to interrogatories unless, it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (c).)

 

Plaintiff Fabian is entitled to sanctions for the seasons stated above in Section A.

 

Plaintiff’s counsel charges $650 per hour.  (Kruse Decl. ¶ 7(a).)  The request includes:  0.75 hours preparing the motion and supporting papers—$487.50; 0.50 hours reviewing and replying to an opposition—$325; 0.50 hour preparing for and attending the motion hearing remotely—$325; and a $60 filing fee.

 

The court finds the requested hourly rate is excessive, considering counsel provides no information concerning his experience in order to support the rate.  The court lowers attorney Kruse’s hourly rate to $500 per hour.  The court finds the time requested is reasonable.

 

Therefore, the court awards plaintiff Estela Yadhira Fabian monetary sanctions in the reduced amount of $935.

 

 

CONCLUSION and ORDER

 

The court grants plaintiff Omar Morales’s motion to compel defendant John Carl Joers’s responses to his first set of form interrogatories.

 

The court orders defendant Joers to serve code-compliant, objection-free, and verified responses within thirty (30) days of the issuing of this order.

 

The court awards plaintiff Estela Yadhira Fabian $935 in monetary sanctions.  Defendant Joers and his attorney of record are ordered to pay plaintiff’s counsel within thirty (30) days of the issuing of order.

 

Plaintiff Omar Morales to give notice.


Case Number: 25CHCV00356    Hearing Date: July 21, 2026    Dept: F43

 

Dept. F43

Hearing Date: 07-21-26

Case # 25CHCV00356, Jefferson v. Cole Hill, et al.

Trial Date: 01-16-29

 

DEMURRER AND MOTION TO STRIKE

 

MOVING PARTY:  Defendant Susan Cole Hill

RESPONDING PARTY:  Plaintiff Francois Jefferson

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Order sustaining defendant Cole Hill’s demurrer to the Second Amended Complaint’s fourth, seventh, and eighth causes of action.

 

Order striking punitive damages from the Second Amended Complaints first, second, third, fourth, sixth, seventh, and eighth causes of action and the prayer from relief.

 

RULING:  Demurrer is sustained with leave to amend.  Motion to strike is moot.

 

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

 

On January 29, 2025, plaintiff Francois Jefferson (Plaintiff) filed this personal injury action against defendants Susan Cole Hill (Hill) and Tyson Kilmer, asserting eight causes of action:  (1) assault;  (2) battery;  (3) intentional infliction of emotional distress;  (4) false imprisonment;  (5) negligence;  (6) premises liability;  (7) violation of the Unruh Civil Rights Act, Civ. Code, § 51;  and (8) violation of the Bane Act, Civ. Code, § 52.1.  Defendant Hill previously filed a motion to strike punitive damages from the Complaint.  The court granted the motion in part for the Seventh and Eighth Causes of Action and denied in part for the First, Second, Third, Fourth, Fifth, and Sixth Causes of Action.  (12/01/25 Minute Order.)

 

On December 30, 2025, Plaintiff filed and served a First Amended Complaint (FAC), asserting the same causes of action.  On March 4, 2025, defendant Hill demurred to the FAC’s Fourth, Sixth, Seventh, and Eighth Causes of Action and moved to strike punitive damages from the FAC’s first, second, third, fourth, fifth, sixth, seventh, and eighth causes of action.  This court is sustained the demurrer in part as to the fourth, seventh, and eighth causes of action but overruled in part as to the sixth cause of action.  The court granted the motion to strike as to the fourth, seventh, and eighth causes of action and denied the motion as to the first, second, third, fifth, and sixth causes of action.  The court also granted leave to amend within thirty days of the order.  (4/24/26 Minute Order.)

 

Plaintiff filed a Second Amended Complaint (SAC) on May 7, 2026, asserting the same causes of action against defendants Hill and Kilmer but asserting new “aiding and abetting” allegations. 

 

On June 8, 2026, defendant Hill filed the instant demurrer to the Second Amended Complaint’s Fourth, Seventh, and Eighth Causes of Action for failing to sufficiently allege causes of action and for being uncertain, ambiguous, and unintelligible.  Defendant Hill also moves to strike punitive damages from the Third, Fourth, Seventh, and Eighth Causes of Action (Paragraphs 40,[1] 48, 86, 99) and the Prayer for Relief (page 21 at line 25, page 22 at line 7).  Plaintiff filed oppositions on July 8, 2026, and defendant Hill replied on July 14, 2026.

 

 

MEET AND CONFER

 

Demurrers and motions to strike must be accompanied by a “meet and confer” declaration stating that the parties met and conferred “in person, by telephone, or by video conference” and stating that (1) the parties failed to reach an agreement resolving the issues raised in the demurrer and motion to strike;  or (2) that the party who filed the pleading subject to the demurrer or motion to strike failed to respond to the meet and confer request or failed to meet and confer in good faith.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 430.41, subd. (a)(3), 435.5, subd. (a)(3).)  In Department F43, meet and confer means in person or via phone, not by letter or email.  (Department F43 Courtroom Information, p. 2.)

 

The “meet and confer” requirement is met because counsel spoke by telephone on June 8, 2026.  (Declaration of Noorhan Buttres ¶ 5.)

 

 

REQUESTS FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

 

Plaintiff asks the court to take judicial notice of the following pursuant to Evidence Code sections 452 and 453:

 

·         Exhibit 1:  This court’s Minute Order entered on December 1, 2025.

·         Exhibit 2:  This court’s Minute Order entered on April 8, 2026.

 

The court grants Plaintiff’s request pursuant to Evidence Code section 452, subdivision (d).

 

 

THE SECOND AMENDED COMPLAINT’S ALLEGATIONS

 

Defendant Hill owned, operated, and controlled the property located at 18782 Fairfield Road in Porter Ranch, CA 91326 (the Property).  (SAC ¶ 2.)  Hill employed defendant Kilmer, who also resided at the Property as a houseguest.  (Id. ¶¶ 3-4, 14.) 

 

On February 3, 2023, Plaintiff, a Black man, was at the Property at the invitation of defendant Hill.  (SAC ¶¶ 15, 72.)  That same day, defendant Kilmer was also on the Property.  (Id. ¶ 16.)  At some point, Kilmer became intoxicated and violent, and committed an unprovoked attack on Plaintiff, striking Plaintiff with his fists with the intent to cause bodily injury to Plaintiff.  (Id. ¶ 15.)  Kilmer also deprived Plaintiff of his freedom of movement and confined Plaintiff by using physical barriers and force, threats of force, menace, fraud, deceit, or unreasonable duress.  (Id. ¶ 42.)  Plaintiff feared further harm if he attempted to leave the Property and remained on the Property.  (Id. ¶ 42.)  Plaintiff also alleges Kilmer, Hill, and other Doe defendants interfered with his constitutional and statutory rights:  (1) protection from bodily restrain and harm pursuant to Civil Code section 43; (2) to be free and independent, and to enjoy and defend life and liberty and to pursue and obtain safety, happiness, and privacy as secured by Article I, Section 1 of the California Constitution; and (3) to be free from hate crimes as set forth in Penal Code sections 422.55, 422.6, and 11410.  (Id. ¶ 92.)

 

Before the unprovoked attack, defendant Hill knew Kilmer had a predisposition and propensity to engage in violent, racially motivated attacks against Plaintiff and other Black invitees.  (SAC ¶¶ 77-78.)  Hill also warned Kilmer several times to “stay in his lane,” demonstrating Hill knew about Kilmer’s discriminatory predisposition to deny and abridge Plaintiff’s civil rights.  (Id. ¶ 78.)  Furthermore, Hill had actual knowledge of Kilmer’s violent and discriminatory conduct because she personally witnessed at least one prior unprovoked attack and racial outburst by Kilmer toward Plaintiff, and other witnesses specifically told Hill about the other incidents.  (Id. ¶ 76.)

 

Despite Hill’s prior knowledge concerning Kilmer’s conduct, Hill aided, abetted, incentivized, and ratified Kilmer’s discriminatory predisposition, violence toward Plaintiff, and Kilmer’s intentional confinement, restraining of Plaintiff, and denying Plaintiff equal accommodations and privileges.   (SAC ¶¶ 43, 47, 74, 80, 88, 98.)  Hill provided substantial assistance and encouragement to Kilmer by condoning Kilmer’s conduct and failing to intervene, continuing to employ, house, and assign Kilmer to interact with invitees to the Property, continuing to drink alcohol with Kilmer, and encouraging him to remain on the Property in contact with Plaintiff.  (Id. ¶¶ 43, 47, 77, 85, 98.)

 

As a result, Plaintiff suffered damages to his body and nervous system and incurred medical and incidental expenses.  (SAC ¶¶ 5, 8, 15, 24.)

 

 

ANALYSIS

 

A.  Demurrer

 

A party may respond to a pleading against it by demurrer based on one or more of eight enumerated grounds, including that “the pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action” and is uncertain, meaning “ambiguous and unintelligible.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subds. (e), (f).)  The grounds for demurring must be apparent from either the face of the complaint or a matter of which the court may take judicial notice.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.30, subd. (a); see also Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

 

The purpose of a demurrer is to challenge the sufficiency of a pleading “by raising questions of law.”  (Postley v. Harvey (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 280, 286.)  “In the construction of a pleading, for the purpose of determining its effect, its allegations must be liberally construed, with a view to substantial justice between the parties.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 452.)  The court “treat[s] the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law[.]”  (Berkley v. Dowds (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 518, 525.)  In applying these standards, the court liberally construes the complaint to determine whether a cause of action has been stated.  (Picton v. Anderson Union High School Dist. (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 726, 733.)

 

 

            1.  Fourth Cause of Action:  False Imprisonment

 

To establish the tort of “false imprisonment,” the complaint must allege “the nonconsensual, intentional confinement of a person, without lawful privilege, for an appreciable length of time, however short.”  (Fermino v. Fedco, Inc. (1994) 7 Cal.4th 701, 715, citation omitted.)  “Restraint may be effectuated by means of physical force, threat of force or of arrest, confinement by physical barriers, or by means of any other form of unreasonable duress.”  (Ibid.)  “The only mental state required to be shown to prove false imprisonment is the intent to confine, or to create a similar intrusion.”  (Id., at p. 716 [first citing Moffat v. Buffums’ Inc. (1937) 21 Cal.App.2d 371, 374; then citing Vandiveer v. Charters (1930) 110 Cal.App. 347, 351; then citing Schanafelt v. Seaboard Finance Co. (1951) 108 Cal.App.2d 420, 423; and then citing Rest.2d Torts, § 40A].)

 

Plaintiff newly alleges an aiding and abetting theory of liability against Hill.  (SAC ¶¶ 42-43.)

 

To establish an “aiding and abetting” claim for false imprisonment, the plaintiff must allege both the underlying elements of civil false imprisonment and the elements of aiding and abetting liability.  Based on a common law rule from the Restatement (Second) of Torts section 876, there are two alternative pathways for aiding and abetting liability.  “Liability may also be imposed on one who aids and abets the commission of an intentional tort if the person (a) knows the other’s conduct constitutes a breach of duty and gives substantial assistance or encouragement to the other to so act or (b) gives substantial assistance to the other in accomplishing a tortious result and the person’s own conduct, separately considered, constitutes a breach of duty to the third person.”  (Saunders v. Superior Court (1994) 27 Cal.App.4th 832, 846, [Rest.2d Torts, § 876, subds. (b), (c)]; see also American Master Lease, LLC v. Idanta Partners, Ltd. (2014) 225 Cal.App.4th 1451, 1477-1478.)

 

Plaintiff fails to allege facts concerning the first theory (knowledge of Kilmer’s breach of duty and substantial assistance) because Plaintiff does not state a duty which Kilmer owed and that Hill gave substantial assistance or encouragement in breaching as to the Fourth Cause of Action.  The Second Amended Complaint fails to mention any duty of care until the Fifth and Sixth causes of action.  At most, Plaintiff alleges Hill provided Kilmer with alcohol on the day of the incident and failed to intervene in the incident despite knowing Kilmer’s history of violence when drunk.  Plaintiff alleges no other facts demonstrating ratification concerning false imprisonment.  (SAC ¶¶ 43, 37.)

 

Plaintiff’s allegations also fail as to the second theory (substantial assistance and defendant Hill’s independent breach of duty).  Plaintiff alleges no independent duty owed by Hill to Plaintiff which Hill breached by giving Kilmer alcohol and failing to intervene in the incident as to the Fourth Cause of Action.  Plaintiff’s general factual allegations and false imprisonment allegations as to defendant Hill are primarily conclusory without more.

 

Plaintiff alleges Hill “aided, abetted, incentivized, and ratified Defendant Kilmer and Does 11 through 20’s intentional confinement and restrain of Plaintiff by use of physical barriers and/or force, threats of force, menace, fraud, deceit, or unreasonable duress, as described above.”  (SAC ¶ 42.)  Hill “gave substantial assistance and/or encouragement to, Kilmer, and Does 11 through 20 to commit all tortious, violent, and discriminatory acts described herein, and, thus, the conduct of Hill, and Does 1 through 10, was a substantial factor in causing harm to Plaintiff.”  (Id. ¶ 43.)  Plaintiff further alleges Hill ratified Kilmer’s tortious conduct by training and incentivizing her employees to use unreasonably violent force in the course and scope of providing house services but fails to list the incentives.  (Id. ¶ 46.)  Furthermore, Plaintiff alleges Hill knew that false imprisonment was being or going to be committed by Kilmer and still provided substantial assistance to Kilmer in carrying out the false imprisonment.  (Id. ¶ 47.)

 

The court struggles to connect Plaintiff’s “fear of further harm” with his claims of confinement.  (SAC ¶ 42.)  Plaintiff alleges Kilmer beat him due to his status as Black man and because Kilmer was drunk, but at the same time Plaintiff feared further harm for attempting to leave and avoid further attacks  (Id.)  Nothing Plaintiff’s allegations establishes Kilmer acted in a manner where if Plaintiff attempted to leave, Kilmer would further harm him.  Thus, it is unclear how Kilmer’s violent acts confined Plaintiff to the Property.  Although restraint may be effectuated through physical force, physical barriers, or other forms of unreasonable duress (Fermino v. Fedco, Inc. (1994) 7 Cal.4th 701-715-716), it is unclear how Kilmer effectuated such confinement to a particular space.  (See Rest.2d Torts, § 40A, illustration (2) [“A, the owner of a store, for the purpose of preventing B, a customer, from leaving the store, but without any privilege to do so, seizes and retains B's purse, which contains a large sum of money. B reasonably believes that he can leave the store only at the risk of losing the purse and the money, and therefore remains in the store. A has confined B.”].)

 

Therefore, the court sustains defendant Hill’s demurrer to the Second Amended Complaint’s Fourth Cause of Action.

 

The court grants Plaintiff one more opportunity to amend the Fourth Cause of Action.

 

 

2.  Seventh Cause of Action:  Violation of Civil Code § 51 et seq. (Unruh Civil Rights Act)

 

The Unruh Civil Rights Act (UCRA) requires all business establishments to provide “full and equal accommodations, advantages, facilities, privileges, or services” to all persons within the jurisdiction of California regardless of certain protected characteristics, including disabilities.  (Civ. Code, § 51, subd. (b).)

 

To establish “intentional discrimination” under UCRA, the plaintiff must allege facts demonstrating that (1) the defendant is a business establishment; (2) the plaintiff was denied full and equal access to accommodations, advantages, facilities, privileges, or services; (3) the denial was based on a protected characteristic listed in the statute;  and (4) the discrimination was intentional.  (Martinez v. Cot’n Wash, Inc. (2022) 81 Cal.App.5th 1026, 1036.)  The conduct must be “willful, affirmative misconduct with the specific intent to accomplish discrimination in a business establishment on the basis of a protected trait.”  (Id., at p. 1036, citations omitted;  Civ. Code, § 51, subd. (b) [protected characteristics include sex, race, color, ancestry, and national origin].)

 

Plaintiff alleges he is a member of a protected class, a Black man, and that defendants Kilmer and Hill knew he is and was a member of such a protected class.  (SAC ¶¶ 72-73;  Civ. Code, § 51, subd. (b) [discrimination based on “race” and “color”].)  The alleged incident occurred while Plaintiff was at defendant Hill’s Property, with each defendant denying him full and equal accommodations by intentionally discriminating against him based on his “race and color.”  (SAC ¶¶ 79-81.)

 

Defendant Hill challenges whether Plaintiff has adequately alleged a “business establishment” because Plaintiff alleges the conduct occurred at a private residence.

 

The UCRA applies where an entity’s “activities reasonably could be found to constitute a business establishment.”  (Brennon B. v. Superior Court (2022) 13 Cal.5th 662, 678 [quoting Warfield v. Peninsula Golf & Country Club (1995) 10 Cal.4th 594, 615];  Civ. Code, §§ 51, 52.)  The entity must “effectively operate as a business or a commercial enterprise or ‘engage[] in behavior involving sufficient ‘businesslike attributes’” such as the sale of goods and services to the public.  (See id. at p. 681.  See generally, e.g., Warfield v. Peninsula Golf & Country Club (1989) 214 Cal.App.3d 646, 655 [“operates a restaurant and bar and recreational facilities for its members and their guests and also leases its facilities to others”];  Payne v. Anaheim Memorial Med. Center, Inc. (2005) 130 Cal.App.4th 729, 732-733 [“operates the hospital as a business enterprise which offers its facilities to qualified physicians, who are not its employees, in exchange for fees and other considerations”];  Liapes v. Facebook, Inc. (2023) 95 Cal.App.5th 910, 916, 925-924 [social media site provides is a social networking service with over two billion monthly users, companies pay Facebook to place ads on users’ News Feeds, and over 98 percent of its revenue comes from advertisers].)

 

Here, Plaintiff alleges Hill employed Kilmer to provide “house services” at the Property.  (SAC ¶¶ 4, 25, 84.)  Plaintiff does not state what these services entailed and does not deny the Property is a private residence.  Instead, Plaintiff states the precise, structure, scope, and commercial character of Hill’s business will reveal itself during discovery.  (Opposition at p. 6:7-11.)  At most, Plaintiff’s allegation is ambiguous and does not clarify whether Kilmer was employed to provide services as a housekeeper to Hill or as a housekeeper to Hill and all invitees and guests.  Considering Plaintiff refers to himself as an invitee (SAC ¶ 15) but does not distinguish between a business invitee versus a public invitee, the court seeks further clarification concerning the specific services Hill offered and for what purpose she hired Kilmer.  (See O’Keefe v. South End Rowing Club (1966) 64 Cal.2d 729, 739-740 [discussing the difference between invitees according to the Restatement Second of Torts section 332]; see also Hoffmann v. Young (2022) 13 Cal.5th 1257.)  Thus, the SAC fails to clearly allege a “business establishment” for the purposes of a UCRA violation.

 

Without further information concerning Hill’s “business establishment,” the court cannot determine how Hill denied Plaintiff full and equal accommodations as Plaintiff has not stated the specific accommodations which were denied.

 

Plaintiff does allege discriminatory conduct by Kilmer as well as Hill’s ratification of said conduct.  According to Plaintiff, Kilmer discriminated against him by committing unprovoked, violent, and threatening acts toward Plaintiff while he was a lawful guest at the business, by physically accosting Plaintiff and directing racially derogatory language and slurs at Plaintiff because of Plaintiff’s race.  (SAC ¶ 75.)  These attacks occurred before February 3, 2023.  (Id.)

 

Hill had actual knowledge of Kilmer’s violent and racially discriminatory conduct toward Plaintiff prior to February 3, 2023, because she personally witnessed at least one of Kilmer’s prior attacks and racial outbursts towards Plaintiff and other witnesses told her about the incident.  (SAC ¶ 76.)  Hill also knew about Kilmer’s discriminatory predisposition and propensity to deny and abridge Plaintiff’s civil rights, because Hill told Kilmer several times to “stay in his lane.”  (Id. ¶ 78.)  It is unclear how this phrase demonstrates Hill’s actual knowledge of Kilmer’s supposed discriminatory predisposition.

 

Despite her actual knowledge of the prior incident and Kilmer’s discriminatory predisposition and propensity to engage in violent, racially motivated attacks against Plaintiff and other Black invitees (SAC ¶¶ 76, 78), Hill ratified Kilmer’s conduct by continuing to employ, house, and assign Kilmer to interact with invitees at the Property and encouraging Kilmer to remain on the Property in contact with Plaintiff.  (Id. ¶¶ 77-78, 84-85.)  But the court is unclear concerning whether Hill continued to employ, house, and encourage Kilmer after the February 3, 2023, incident.  This would serve as a clear indication of ratification.

 

The court fails to find a connection between Hill providing Kilmer with alcoholic beverages prior to the incident and offering him alcohol on the day of the incident.  (See SAC ¶¶ 15, 16-17.)  Although Plaintiff alleges Kilmer was intoxicated and aggressive during the February 3, 2023, incident, Plaintiff fails to demonstrate that Kilmer was intoxicated during his prior attacks on Plaintiff and that Hill knew Kilmer would become aggressive when intoxicated.

 

Moreover, Hill’s other conduct, continuing to employ, house, and assign Kilmer to interact with invitees at the Property and encouraging Kilmer to remain on the Property in contact with Plaintiff, despite witnessing and knowing about the prior racially motivated attacks, demonstrates Hill’s prior notice and aiding and abetting of Kilmer’s discriminatory conduct by continuing to employ him and provide him with various resources, without punishment.  This demonstrates ratification of Kilmer’s prior conduct but does not demonstrates ratification of the February 3, 2023, incident, the basis of Plaintiff’s causes of action.

 

Therefore, the court sustains defendant Hill’s demurrer to the Second Amended Complaint’s Seventh Cause of Action with leave to amend.

 

 

 

            3.  Eighth Cause of Action:  Violation of Civil Code § 52.1 (Tom Bane Civil Rights Act)

 

To adequately plead a claim for a violation of the Tom Bane Civil Rights Act, plaintiff must allege (1) intentional interference or attempted interference with a state or federal constitutional or legal right, and (2) the interference or attempted interference was by threats, intimidation, or coercion.”  (Allen v. City of Sacramento (2015) 234 Cal.App.4th 41, 67, citations omitted.)  Plaintiff must allege that defendant interfered with rights secured by the U.S. Constitution or laws of the United States or the California Constitution and laws of the state.  (Civ. Code, § 52.1, subd. (b).)  Plaintiff must allege actual “threats, intimidation, and coercion.”  (Julian v. Mission Community Hospital (2017) 11 Cal.App.5th 360, 395 [speech is insufficient].)

 

Plaintiff adequately alleges threats, intimidation, and violence as to defendant Kilmer based on his unprovoked attack on Plaintiff.  (SAC, ¶¶ 15-16.)  Plaintiff does not allege any threats, intimidation, or violence by Hill but does allege she “aided, incited, and otherwise engaged with Kilmer’s discriminatory predisposition and propensity to threaten, intimidate, and commit acts of violence toward Plaintiff.”  (Id. ¶ 88.)

 

Plaintiff newly alleges Defendants interfered with three constitutional rights:  (1) right of protection from bodily restrain and harm pursuant to Civil Code section 43; (2) right to be free and independent, and to enjoy and defend life and liberty and to pursue and obtain safety, happiness, and privacy as secured by Article I, Section 1 of the California Constitution; and (3) right to be free from hate crimes as set forth in Penal Code sections 422.55, 422.6, and 11410.  (SAC ¶ 92.)  According to Plaintiff, Kilmer’s and Hill’s conduct “intentionally interfered with, and/or attempted to interfere with [Plaintiff’s] civil rights by threats, intimidation, or coercion” and intended to deprive Plaintiff of his enjoyment of the interests protected by the statutory and constitutional rights.  (SAC ¶¶ 91, 95.)

 

Civil Code section 52.1 requires a showing of “specific intent” to demonstrate interference with a protected right or at minimum acted in reckless disregard of such right.  (Cornell v. City and County of San Francisco (2017) 17 Cal.App.5th 766.)  Cornell established a two-prong for finding specific intent.  The court must first determine as a matter of law whether the right at issue is clearly delineated and plainly applicable under the circumstances of the case.  (Murchison v. County of Tehama (2021) 69 Cal.App.5th 867, 896, citations omitted.)  If the right is vague or novel, the specific intent standard cannot be satisfied.  If the first condition is satisfied, the jury must make a factual determination of whether the defendant committed the act in question with the particular purpose of depriving the victim of the enjoyment of the interests protected by that right.  (Ibid.)   Subjective awareness of wrongdoing is not required, but reckless disregard of a clearly established right is sufficient.

 

Civil Code section 43 provides that every person, “subject to the qualifications and restrictions provided by law, the right of protection from bodily restraint or harm, from personal insult, from defamation, and from injury to his personal relations.”  Court’s have recognized this is a right private actors can violate.  (Jones v. Kmart Corp. (1998) 17 Cal.4th 329, 335-338.)  Kilmer violated this right when he physically attacked Plaintiff and used racially derogatory language and slurs towards Plaintiff.  (SAC ¶ 75.)  Article I, Section 1 of the California Constitution declares “[a]ll people are by nature free and independent and have inalienable rights, including enjoying and defending life and liberty and pursuing and obtaining safety, happiness, and privacy.”  This is also a valid basis for Kilmer’s violent and threatening conduct against Plaintiff.  (Jones, supra, 17 Cal.4th at p. 336-338.)

 

Penal Code section 422.55 defines a “hate crime” as a criminal act committed in whole or in part because of one or more actual or perceived characteristics of the victim, including race or ethnicity.  (Pen. Code, § 422.55, subd. (a)(4).)  Penal Code section 422.6 prohibits any person, whether or not acting under color of law, by force or threat of force, willfully injuring, intimidating, interfering with, oppressing, or threatening any other person in the free exercise or enjoyment of a right secured by the Constitution or laws of this state or the United States, in whole or in part because of one or more actual or perceived characteristics of the victim listed in § 422.55.  (Pen. Code, § 422.6, subd. (a).) Section 422.6 is the criminal enforcement arm of the same Bane Act legislation that includes Civil Code section 52.1.  Penal Code section 11410 is primarily a legislative findings and definitions section that declares the right of every person “to be secure and protected from fear, intimidation, and physical harm caused by the activities of violent groups and individuals.”  (Pen. Code, § 11410, subd. (a).)  Section 11410 does not itself create a specific prohibition or penalty and does not independently constitute a clearly delineated right for Bane Act purposes.  Section 11410’s definitions support the broader hate crime statutory scheme and can be read in conjunction with Sections 422.55 and 422.6 to inform the scope of the right to be free from hate crimes.  As with the other two stated rights, Plaintiff has sufficiently alleged conduct by Kilmer demonstrating a specific intent to interfere with these rights as to Plaintiff.

 

Plaintiff alleges defendant Hill aided and abetted by providing substantial assistance and encouragement despite notice of his discriminatory and violent propensity, by failing to intervene, continuing to employ, house, and assign Kilmer to interact with invitees to the Property, continuing to drink alcohol with Kilmer, and encouraging him to remain on the Property in contact with Plaintiff.  (SAC ¶¶ 43, 47, 77, 85, 90, 98.)  Although this does not demonstrate a subjective intent to interfere with Plaintiff’s rights, defendant Hill’s conduct after witnessing the prior discriminatory attacks, her subsequent conduct (failing to intervene, continuing to employ, house, and assign Kilmer to interact with invitees, and encouraging Kilmer to remain on the Property in contact with Plaintiff) indicates some disregard for Plaintiff’s rights and the possibility that Hill might attack him again.  As Plaintiff alleges, Kilmer attacked him on February 3, 2023.  However, Plaintiff fails to demonstrate how Hill encouraged  Kilmer to remain on the Property in contact with Plaintiff, whether through conduct or specific direction.

 

Thus, Plaintiff has not fully established defendant Hill’s specific intent to interfere with his rights by aiding and abetting defendant Kilmer’s conduct.

 

Finally, Plaintiff again alleges he had a right to be on the Property and be free from violent threats and intimidation.  (SAC ¶ 89.)  As with the court’s previous ruling, Plaintiff’s allegations suggest he may be a public invitee, business invitee, or visitor to “business property.”  And invitee’s right to be on business property is established under common law rather than statutory or constitutional law.  (Clawson v. Stockton Golf and Country Club (1963) 220 Cal.App.2d 886, 889, 890.)  Plaintiff fails to reference a valid provision which codifies and confers such a right.

 

Therefore, the court sustains defendant Hill’s demurrer to the Second Amended Complaint’s Eighth Cause of Action with leave to amend.

 

 

            B.  Motion to Strike

 

“Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading may serve and file a notice of motion to strike the whole or any part thereof.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 435.)  A court may, upon motion or at any time, strike from the complaint “any irrelevant, false, or improper matter” or “all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court.”  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 436, subds. (a)-(b), 187; Greshko v. County of Los Angeles (1987) 194 Cal.App.3d 822, 830 [noting the court’s inherent power to limit the focus of the proceedings to the issues framed by the remaining viable pleadings].)  An “irrelevant matter” is an allegation that “is not essential to the statement of a claim or defense,” “is neither pertinent to nor supported by an otherwise sufficient claim or defense,” or “a demand for judgment requesting relief not supported by the allegations of the complaint or cross-complaint.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 431.10, subds. (b)-(c).)  The court must read the factual allegations in the complaint as a whole and assume their truth.  (Clauson v. Superior Court (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1253, 1255.) 

 

Defendant Hill moves to strike punitive damages from the Second Amended Complaint:

 

  • Page 12, Paragraph 48;
  • Page 19, Paragraph 86;
  • Page 21, Paragraph 99;
  • Prayer for Relief, page 21, item number 3 under the heading “ON FIRST, SECOND, THIRD, FOURTH, FIFTH, AND SIXTH CAUSES OF ACTION” (“For punitive damages”);  and
  • Prayer for Relief, page 22, item number 6 under the heading “ON SEVENTH AND EIGHTH CAUSES OF ACTION” (“For punitive damages”).

 

 

Because the court sustained the demurrer to the Fourth, Seventh, and Eighth Causes of Action, defendant Hill’s motion to strike is moot.

 

 

CONCLUSION and ORDER

 

Demurrer to the Second Amended Complaint’s Fourth, Seventh, and Eighth Causes of Action is sustained with leave to amend.  Motion to strike is moot.

 

Plaintiff may file and serve an amended complaint within 20 days of the issuing of this order.

 

Plaintiff to give notice.

Case Number: 25CHCV02505    Hearing Date: July 21, 2026    Dept: F43

Dept. F43

Hearing Date: 07-21-26

Case # 25CHCV02505, Quintero v. Geragosian

Trial Date: 10-30-28

 

 

MOTION TO COMPEL RESPONSES TO FORM INTERROGATORIES AND SPECIAL INTERROGATORIES

 

MOVING PARTY:  Defendant Saro Geragosian

RESPONDING PARTY:  Plaintiff Lilia Quintero

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Order compelling plaintiff Lilia Quintero’s objection-free responses to defendant’s first sets of form interrogatories and special interrogatories and imposing monetary sanctions of $435 against plaintiff Quintero and her counsel of record, Igor Fradkin and Downtown LA Law Group, jointly and severally.

 

RULING:  Motion is granted.  The court awards Defendant $435 in monetary sanctions.

 

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

 

The personal injury action arises out of a vehicle collision that occurred on State Route 118 on August 31, 2023.  Plaintiffs Lilia Quintero and Daniela Haro allege that defendant Saro Geragosian (Defendant) collided with their vehicle while changing lanes and immediately fled the scene of the collision.  As a result, Plaintiffs suffered injuries and damages.  On July 16, 2025, Plaintiffs filed a complaint against Defendant, pleading four causes of action:  (1) motor vehicle;  (2) negligence;  (3) misdemeanor hit and run in violation of Vehicle Code section 20001, subdivision (a);  and (4) intentional infliction of emotional distress.

 

On October 8, 2025, Defendant served plaintiff Lilia Quintero with his first sets of form and special interrogatories.  (Declaration of Nageen C. Jalali ¶ 2, Exh. A.)  The response deadline was November 10, 2025.  Defendant granted plaintiff Quintero six deadline extensions from November 7, 2025 through January 19, 2026, and denied a seventh extension request.  (Id. ¶¶ 3-4, Exh. B.)  On January 30, 2026, defense counsel emailed plaintiff’s counsel, requesting a status update.  (Id. ¶ 4, Exh. B at p. 1.)  Plaintiff Quintero unilaterally selected a responsive deadline of February 2, 2026, to serve responses.  (Id. ¶ 4.)  No responses were served by that date.

 

On May 27, 2026, Defendant filed the instant motion to compel plaintiff Lilia Quintero’s objection-free, verified responses to his first sets of form interrogatories and special interrogatories and to impose $435 in monetary sanctions against plaintiff Quintero and her counsel of record, jointly and severally.

 

On July 17, 2026, plaintiff Quintero filed a late opposition, asserting code-compliant responses were served on July 16, 2026, and sanctions should be denied because the delay was due to change in handling attorneys.

 

 

ANALYSIS

 

A.  Compelling Responses to Interrogatories

 

A propounding party may move to compel responses to form or special interrogatories where the responding party fails to provide any responses.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (b).)  The propounding party must show the interrogatories were properly served, that the time to respond expired, and no response has been served.  (Leach v. Superior Court (1980) 111 Cal.App.3d 902, 905-906.)  Unless excused by a protective order, the responding party must serve responses (an answer, objection, or election to allow inspecting or copy records) within 30 days after the interrogatories are served or according to an agreed upon deadline extension.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.210, subd. (a), 2030.270.)  Failing to respond within these time limits waives objections.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (a).)

 

When the propounding party serves more than 35 special interrogatories, the responding need only respond to the first 35 special interrogatories unless the propounding party attaches a declaration of additional discovery to the special interrogatories.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.030, subds. (b)-(c), 2030.50, 2030.070, subd. (a).)  However, if the responding party fails to timely object to additional special interrogatories, the responding party waives the objection.  (See Leach, supra, 111 Cal.App.3d at pp. 905-906.)

 

After reviewing the moving papers, the court finds Defendant properly served plaintiff Quintero’s counsel, Igor Fradkin, with the special interrogatories and form interrogatories.  But plaintiff Quintero failed to serve responses by the original deadline, the subsequent deadline extensions, or February 2, 2026.

 

Plaintiff Quintero’s late opposition insists responses to the interrogatories were served on July 16, 2026, but failed to attach the responses to the opposing declaration.  (Declaration of Gevork Gazaryan ¶ 5, Exhs. A, B.)  Plaintiff’s current counsel, Gevork Gazaryan, claims counsel was working diligently on responses and that the prior handling attorney, Mr. Fradkin, requested extensions due to difficulty connecting with plaintiff Quintero.  (Id. ¶ 4.)  Thus, the missed deadline was due to inadvertence.  However, Mr. Gazaryan fails to state when handling counsel changed or why the other individuals from his first who were copied on the email chains did not address the delay issue sooner.

 

As such, the court finds plaintiffs’ counsel fails to provide a substantial justification for the delayed responses or proof that the responses were actually completed and served.

 

Therefore, the court grants defendant Saro Geragosian’s motion to compel plaintiff Lilia Quintero’s responses to his first sets of form interrogatories and special interrogatories.

 

Plaintiff Quintero is ordered to serve objection-free, verified responses and responsive documents within 30 days of this order.

 

 

            B.  Sanctions

 

Defendant asks the court to impose $435 in monetary sanctions against plaintiff Quintero and her counsel of record.  (Jalali Decl. ¶ 6.)

 

The court must impose sanctions against any party who “unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel responses to interrogatories unless, it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (c).)  The court may also award sanctions even if “the requested discovery is provided to the moving party after the motion was filed.”  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1030(a).)

 

For the reasons stated above in Section A, Defendant is entitled to sanctions.

 

Defendant’s counsel, Nageen C. Jalali, charges $250 per hour.  (Jalali Decl. ¶ 6.)  The request includes:  (1) 1.5 hours preparing the motion—$375;  and (2) a $60 filing fee.

 

The court finds the requested hourly rate and time billed is reasonable.

 

Therefore, the court grants Defendant’s request for $435 in monetary sanctions.

 

 

CONCLUSION and ORDER

 

The court grants defendant Saro Geragosian’s motion to compel plaintiff Lilia Quintero’s responses to defendant’s first sets of form interrogatories and special interrogatories.

 

The court orders plaintiff Quintero to serve code-compliant, objection-free, and verified responses within thirty (30) days of the issuing of this order.

 

The court awards defendant Saro Geragosian $435 in monetary sanctions.  Plaintiff Quintero and her attorney of record are ordered to pay defense counsel within thirty (30) days of the issuing of order.

 

Defendant Saro Geragosian to give notice.
