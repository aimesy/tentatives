DEMURRER WITH MOTION TO STRIKE

 

MOVING PARTY: Defendant, General Motors LLC

RESPONDING PARTY: Plaintiff, Oscar Martin

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Demurrer to First Amended Complaint

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

This is a lemon law action. Plaintiff Oscar Martin sued Defendant General Motors LLC alleging Defendant failed to promptly replace his 2020 GMC Sierra 1500 or make restitution after it was unable to conform the car to its express warranty after a reasonable number of repair attempts. Plaintiff asserts claims under the Song-Beverly Act and the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act. Plaintiff additionally alleges that Defendant fraudulently induced the sale of the car by concealing the existence of a transmission defect.

 

RULING

Demurrer: Overruled.

Defendant demurs to Plaintiff’s First Amended Complaint, arguing that he fails to sufficiently state his claims. It also contends that Plaintiff’s fraudulent inducement claim is time-barred. Plaintiff opposes the demurrer, denying his fraud claim is untimely and maintaining the sufficiency of the allegations in support of his claims. Although Plaintiff’s opposition was 2-court days late, the Court finds no prejudice to Defendant and considers the opposition in ruling on the demurrer. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1005, subd. (b).) Defendant’s demurrer is overruled.

 

First through Fifth Causes of Action: Song-Beverly Act and Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act

Defendant argues that Plaintiff fails to state his Song-Beverly Act claims because Plaintiff alleges that he is a Georgia resident and does not state where he bought his car. Accordingly, Plaintiff’s failure to plead his Song-Beverly Act claims also means that his Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act claim fails as well.

 

The demurrer to the warranty claims is overruled. While it is true that the Song-Beverly Act only applies to vehicles sold in California (Cummins, Inc. v. Superior Court (2005) 36 Cal.4th 478, 493), the complaint does not indicate that the car was sold outside of the state. The mere allegation that Plaintiff is a Georgia resident is insufficient to infer an out-of-state purchase. Because Plaintiff’s Song-Beverly claims do not fail for this reason, the Magnuson-Moss claim stands. (Daugherty v. American Honda Motor Co., Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 824, 833 [“[F]ailure to state a warranty claim under state law necessarily constituted a failure to state a claim under Magnuson-Moss”].) The demurrer to these claims is therefore overruled.

 

Sixth Cause of Action: Fraudulent Inducement

Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s fraudulent inducement by concealment claim is barred by the statute of limitations and is not pled with requisite particularity.

 

Plaintiff’s claim is not barred by the statute of limitations. “A demurrer based on a statute of limitations will not lie where the action may be, but is not necessarily, barred. [Citation.] In order for the bar ... to be raised by demurrer, the defect must clearly and affirmatively appear on the face of the complaint; it is not enough that the complaint shows that the action may be barred.” (Com. for Green Foothills v. Santa Clara County Bd. of Supervisors (2010) 48 Cal.4th 32, 42.) The statute of limitations for fraud is three years. (Code Civ. Proc., § 338, subd. (d).) The cause of action does not accrue “until the discovery, by the aggrieved party, of the facts constituting the fraud or mistake.” (Ibid.) Here, the face of the complaint does not affirmatively show that the fraud claim is barred because no facts indicate when Plaintiff discovered, or reasonably should have discovered, Defendant’s alleged fraud. The mere fact that “[d]efects and nonconformities to warranty manifested themselves within the applicable express warranty period” does not mean that Plaintiff discovered or was put on notice of the alleged fraudulent omission at that time. [FAC ¶ 11.] The fraud claim is thus not barred by the statute of limitations.

 

Defendant next argues that Plaintiff’s fraud claim is not pled with particularity. It contends that Plaintiff does not allege the specific facts Defendant failed to disclose, Defendant’s knowledge of those facts, and the advertisements or other materials Defendant could have disclosed them. Defendant further argues that Plaintiff does not plead Defendant’s intent to defraud and a transactional relationship giving rise to a duty to disclose.

 

“The required elements for fraudulent concealment are (1) concealment or suppression of a material fact; (2) by a defendant with a duty to disclose the fact; (3) the defendant intended to defraud the plaintiff by intentionally concealing or suppressing the fact; (4) the plaintiff was unaware of the fact and would have acted differently if the concealed or suppressed fact was known; and (5) plaintiff sustained damage as a result of the concealment or suppression of the material fact.” (Rattagan v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1, 40.) As with all other fraud claims, fraudulent concealment must be pled with specificity “even though the focus of inquiry shifts to the unique elements of the claim.” (Id. at p. 43.) Mere conclusory allegations are insufficient. (Id. at p. 44.)

 

A duty to disclose may arise if “the material facts are known or accessible only to defendant, and defendant knows those facts are not known or reasonably discoverable by plaintiff (i.e., exclusive knowledge).” (Rattagan, supra, 17 Cal.5th at p. 40.) This circumstance presupposes “a preexisting relationship between the parties, such as ‘between seller and buyer, employer and prospective employee, doctor and patient, or parties entering into any kind of contractual agreement. All of these relationships are created by transactions between parties from which a duty to disclose facts material to the transaction arises under certain circumstances.’” (Id. at pp. 40-41, quoting LiMandri v. Judkins (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 326, 337.) “If the duty allegedly arose by virtue of the parties’ relationship and defendant’s exclusive knowledge or access to certain facts . . . the complaint must also include specific allegations establishing all the required elements, including (1) the content of the omitted facts, (2) defendant’s awareness of the materiality of those facts, (3) the inaccessibility of the facts to plaintiff, (4) the general point at which the omitted facts should or could have been revealed, and (5) justifiable and actual reliance, either through action or forbearance, based on the defendant’s omission.” (Id. at pp. 43-44.)

 

Plaintiff sufficiently alleges the content of the omitted facts, Defendant’s knowledge of those facts, and Defendant’s intent to defraud him. Plaintiff alleges Defendant failed to disclose the existence of a defect in the 8-speed transmission “that can result in various problems, including, but not limited to hard or harsh shifts, jerking, lurching, hesitation on acceleration, surging and/or inability to control the vehicle’s speed, acceleration, or deceleration.” [FAC ¶ 69.] Defendant presents no authority requiring Plaintiff to allege what manner the disclosure should have been made; it suffices that disclosure should have been made at the time of sale. Plaintiff also alleges that Defendant had knowledge of the defect “through sources not available to consumers such as Plaintiff, including but not limited to pre-production and post-production testing data; early consumer complaints about the Transmission Defect made directly to Defendant GM and its network of dealers; aggregate warranty data compiled from Defendant GM’s network of dealers; testing conducted by Defendant GM in response to these complaints; as well as warranty repair and part replacements data received by Defendant GM from Defendant GM’s network of dealers, amongst other sources of internal information.” [Id. ¶ 70.] Finally, Plaintiff pleads Defendant’s intent to defraud because Defendant omitted the existence of the defect to induce the sale of the car. [Id. ¶¶ 71-75.] Defendant’s particularity arguments in this regard are therefore unavailing.

 

Plaintiff further alleges a transactional relationship sufficient to trigger a duty to disclose. He alleges that on or about September 9, 2020, he entered into a “warranty contract” with Defendant regarding his car. [FAC ¶ 6.] Such a contractual relationship may support a duty to disclose on behalf of Defendant. (Rattagan, supra, 17 Cal.5th at pp. 40-41.) Defendant’s provision of a warranty to Plaintiff is not a transaction between Defendant and “the public at large” as Defendant provided the warranty directly to Plaintiff. (Id. at p. 41.) Plaintiff has thus alleged a transactional relationship between him and Defendant. Defendant’s demurrer to the fraudulent inducement claim is therefore overruled.     

 

Motion to Strike: Denied.

Defendant moves to strike Plaintiff’s prayer for punitive damages in sub-paragraph “h” of his prayer for relief. It argues that Plaintiff does not plead a claim supporting the request for punitive damages. Plaintiff does not oppose the motion. The motion to strike is denied because Plaintiff pleads a fraud claim that forms the basis of his punitive damages request.

 

To support punitive damages, the plaintiff’s complaint “must allege ultimate facts of the defendant’s oppression, fraud, or malice.” (Cyrus v. Haveson (1976) 65 Cal.App.3d 306, 316-17; Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (a).) “Fraud” is “an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known to the defendant with the intention on the part of the defendant of thereby depriving a person of property or legal rights or otherwise causing injury.” (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (c)(3).)

 

As explained in the Court’s ruling on Defendant’s demurrer, Defendant’s challenge to Plaintiff’s fraudulent inducement by concealment claim fails. The fraud cause of action is sufficient to support Plaintiff’s punitive damages claim. (Stevens v. Superior Court (1986) 180 Cal.App.3d 605, 610.) Thus, Plaintiff has sufficiently alleged facts to support his punitive damages claim against Defendant and the motion to strike is denied.

 

Defendant to answer the operative complaint within 10 days.

JUDGMENT ON THE PLEADINGS

 

MOVING PARTY: Defendant, LAD-MB, LLC

RESPONDING PARTY: Plaintiff, Celene Fregoso

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings as to Third Cause of Action

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

This is a lemon law action. Plaintiff Celene Fregoso sued Defendant Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC (MBUSA) alleging MBUSA failed to promptly replace her 2023 Mercedes-Benz C-Class or make restitution after it was unable to conform the car to its express warranty after a reasonable number of repair attempts. Plaintiff also asserts a claim for breach of implied warranty against MBUSA. She additionally brings a negligent repair cause of action against Defendant LAD-MB, LLC.

 

RULING: Granted with leave to amend.

Defendant LAD-MB moves for judgment on the pleadings as to Plaintiff’s third cause of action in her complaint for negligent repair. It argues that Plaintiff insufficiently pleads the element of damages and the claim is barred by the economic loss rule. Plaintiff opposes the motion, contending she adequately pleads her damages and the “component exception” to the economic loss rule applies. Because Plaintiff fails to plead damages, Defendant’s motion is granted with leave to amend.

 

“A motion for judgment on the pleadings serves the function of a demurrer, challenging only defects on the face of the complaint.” (Richardson-Tunnell v. School Ins. Program for Employees (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 1056, 1061.) A defendant may move for judgment on the pleadings if the plaintiff’s complaint fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action against the defendant. (Code Civ. Proc., § 438, subd. (c)(1)(B)(ii).) The motion may be made as to the entire complaint or to any of the causes of action stated therein. (Id. § 438, subd. (c)(2)(A).) The grounds for the motion must “appear on the face of the challenged pleading or from any matter of which the court is required to take judicial notice.” (Id. § 438, subd. (d).) The plaintiff’s factual allegations are accepted as true and are given a liberal construction. (Gerawan Farming, Inc. v. Lyons (2000) 24 Cal.4th 468, 515-16.)

 

Plaintiff fails to plead the requisite element of damages. Rather, she merely alleges that Defendant’s alleged breach “was a proximate cause of Plaintiff’s damages.” [Compl. ¶ 52.] Plaintiff’s prayer for general and special damages is insufficient to plead this element. Not only is her allegation insufficient to plead damages, but it also prevents a determination as to the applicability of the economic loss rule. Without more, the Court is unable to analyze whether Plaintiff is seeking tort recovery for purely economic losses. (See Rattagan v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1, 38.) Because this is Plaintiff’s original complaint and there exists a reasonable possibility that she can cure the pleading defect, she is granted leave to amend.

 

Plaintiff is granted 30 days’ leave to amend. Plaintiff may only add facts in support of the successfully challenged negligent repair claim. (Harris v. Wachovia Mortgage, FSB (2010) 185 Cal.App.4th 1018, 1023.) Any new causes of action outside the scope of the order may be addressed via a motion to strike.

 

Defendant to give notice.

COMPEL ARBITRATION

 

MOVING PARTY: Defendant, Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC

RESPONDING PARTY: Plaintiffs, Elham Sefaradi and Serena Yaghoubi

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Motion to Compel Arbitration and Stay Action

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

This is a lemon law action. Plaintiffs Elham Sefaradi and Serena Yaghoubi sued Defendant Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC, alleging Defendant failed to promptly replace their 2023 Mercedes-Benz GLA-Class or make restitution after it was unable to conform the car to its express warranty after a reasonable number of repair attempts. Plaintiffs also assert a claim for breach of implied warranty against Defendant. They additionally bring a negligent repair cause of action against Defendant LAD-MB, LLC.

 

RULING: Granted.

Request for Judicial Notice: Granted.

The Court takes judicial notice of the existence of the state and federal court appellate decisions and the truth of the results reached therein. (Lockley v. Law Office of Cantrell, Green, Pekich, Cruz & McCort (2001) 91 Cal.App.4th 875, 885-86.)

 

Evidentiary Objections: Overruled.

 

Defendant Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC moves to compel arbitration under the Motor Vehicle Lease Agreement (Agreement). It argues that it can compel arbitration as an intended third-party beneficiary of the Agreement. Plaintiffs oppose the motion, challenging Defendant’s standing to enforce the Agreement. Because Defendant can enforce the Agreement as a third-party beneficiary, its motion is granted.

 

A written agreement to submit to arbitration an existing controversy or a controversy thereafter arising is valid, enforceable and irrevocable, save upon such grounds as exist for the revocation of any contract.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.) “On petition of a party to an arbitration agreement alleging the existence of a written agreement to arbitrate a controversy and that a party thereto refuses to arbitrate such controversy, the court shall order the petitioner and the respondent to arbitrate the controversy if it determines that an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists, unless it determines that: (a) The right to compel arbitration has been waived by the petitioner; or (b) Grounds exist for the revocation of the agreement.” (Id. § 1281.2.)

 

Defendant moves to compel arbitration under the Federal Arbitration Act (FAA). The Agreement explicitly states that it is governed by the FAA. [White Decl. ¶ 2, Ex. 1, p. 4.] This is sufficient to invoke application of the FAA to the arbitration agreement. (Victrola 89, LLC v. Jaman Properties 8 LLC (2020) 46 Cal.App.5th 337, 355.) While the FAA governs the rules for conducting arbitration, barring citation to a case precluding California law, motions to compel arbitration are still governed by California law. (Adolph v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (2023) 14 Cal.5th 1104, 1119; Viking River Cruises, Inc. v. Moriana (2022) 596 U.S. 639, 653; Volt Information Sciences, Inc. v. Board of Trustees of Leland Stanford Junior University (1989) 489 U.S. 468, 477-79; Victrola 89, supra, 46 Cal.App.5th at p. 346.)

 

The law creates a general presumption in favor of arbitration. In a motion to compel arbitration, the moving party must prove by a preponderance of evidence the existence of the arbitration agreement and that the dispute is covered by the agreement. The burden then shifts to the resisting party to prove by a preponderance of evidence a ground for denial (e.g., fraud, unconscionability, etc.). (Rosenthal v. Great Western Fin’l Securities Corp. (1996) 14 Cal.4th 394, 413-14; Hotels Nevada v. L.A. Pacific Ctr., Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 754, 758.) Any challenges to the formation of the arbitration agreement should be considered before any order sending the parties to arbitration. The trier of fact weighs all evidence, including affidavits, declarations, documents, and, if applicable, oral testimony to determine whether the action goes to arbitration. (Hotels Nevada, supra, 144 Cal.App.4th at p. 758.)

 

The moving party bears the initial burden of producing “prima facie evidence of a written agreement to arbitrate the controversy.” (Gamboa v. Northeast Community Clinic (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 158, 165.) This burden may be met “by attaching to the [motion or] petition a copy of the arbitration agreement purporting to bear the [opposing party’s] signature” or “setting forth the agreement’s provisions in the motion.” (Id. at p. 165.) If the opposing party disputes the agreement, then the burden shifts to the opposing party to produce “evidence to challenge the authenticity of the agreement.” (Ibid.) The opposing party can do this by testifying under oath or declaring under penalty of perjury “that the party never saw or does not remember seeing the agreement, or that the party never signed or does not remember signing the agreement.” (Ibid.) If the opposing party meets its burden, the burden then shifts to the moving party to “establish with admissible evidence a valid arbitration agreement between the parties.” (Ibid.) “The burden of proving the agreement by a preponderance of the evidence remains with the moving party.” (Id. at p. 166.)

 

Defendant has proven the existence of an arbitration agreement between the parties. It attaches to the motion a copy of the Agreement containing Plaintiffs’ signatures. [White Decl. ¶ 2, Ex. 1, p. 6.] Defendant can enforce the Agreement as a nonsignatory because it is an intended third-party beneficiary of the arbitration agreement. A nonsignatory may enforce an arbitration agreement against a signatory if it is a third-party beneficiary of the agreement. (Ronay Family Limited Partnership v. Tweed (2013) 216 Cal.App.4th 830, 839.) A third-party beneficiary must show that: (1) “the third party would in fact benefit from the contract”; (2) “a motivating purpose of the contracting parties was to provide a benefit to the third party”; and (3) permitting the third party to enforce the contract “is consistent with the objectives of the contract and the reasonable expectations of the contracting parties.” (Goonewardene v. ADP, LLC (2019) 6 Cal.5th 817, 830.) Here, Defendant would benefit from the Agreement because it permits Defendant to compel arbitration in the event of a dispute. [White Decl. ¶ 2, Ex. 1, p. 4.] A motivating purpose of the contracting parties was to provide a benefit to Defendant because the Agreement explicitly names Defendant as a third-party beneficiary and provides that it can enforce the Agreement as such. [Ibid.] Permitting Defendant to compel arbitration is consistent with the objectives of the Agreement and the reasonable expectation of the parties because the parties expressly agreed that Defendant could do so. [Ibid.] (Cf. Ford Motor Warranty Cases (2023) 89 Cal.App.5th 1324, 1338-40 [manufacturer was not a third-party beneficiary where sales contract did not name manufacturer and limited right of enforcement to purchaser and dealer].) 

 

Plaintiffs do not challenge the authenticity of the Agreement. Any dispute as to whether the Agreement covers Plaintiffs’ claims is properly before the arbitrator due to the existence of a delegation clause in the Agreement. The Agreement provides that “[a]ny claim or dispute, whether in contract, tort or otherwise (including any dispute over the interpretation, scope, or validity of this lease, arbitration section or the arbitrability of any issue) . . . shall . . . be resolved by a neutral, binding arbitration and not by a court action.” [White Decl. ¶ 2, Ex. 1, p. 4.] This demonstrates a clear and unmistakable intent to have such issues decided by the arbitrator. (Pinela v. Neiman Marcus Group, Inc. (2015) 238 Cal.App.4th 227, 239-40.) Plaintiffs present no challenge to the delegation clause. Defendant’s motion is therefore granted.

 

Plaintiffs and Defendant to arbitrate the express and implied warranty claims.

 

“If a court of competent jurisdiction, whether in this State or not, has ordered arbitration of a controversy which is an issue involved in an action or proceeding pending before a court of this State, the court in which such action or proceeding is pending shall, upon motion of a party to such action or proceeding, stay the action or proceeding until an arbitration is had in accordance with the order to arbitrate or until such earlier time as the court specifies.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.4.) The action is stayed.

 

Defendant to give notice.

PRO HAC VICE

 

MOVING PARTY: Plaintiff, Emmanuel D. Pacquiao

RESPONDING PARTY: Unopposed/Defendants, Gabriel Rueda et al.

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Motion for Pro Hac Vice Admission

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

Plaintiff Emmanuel D. Pacquiao filed the instant malicious prosecution action against Defendants Gabriel Rueda a/k/a Gabriel Salvador, Withers Bergman LLP, Pierce Bainbridge Beck Price & Hecht, LLP, Khan Law Office, PC, and Amman Khan. He alleges that Defendants knowingly prosecuted baseless claims against him arising out of an alleged “finder’s fee” for his 2015 fight with boxing legend Floyd Mayweather. Defendants also accused Plaintiff of orchestrating a “terror campaign” in retaliation for Rueda’s demand for money, consisting of stalking, threats, and other malicious actions. Plaintiff prevailed on all claims. Plaintiff alleges that the prior lawsuit was initiated to inflict reputational damage and coerce payment.    

 

RULING: Granted.

Plaintiff moves for pro hac vice admission of attorney Brian S. Cohen.

 

Pro hac vice admission in California is governed by California Rules of Court, rule 9.40. To be admitted pro hac vice, one must be “an attorney in good standing of and eligible to practice before the bar of any United States court or the highest court in any state, territory, or insular possession of the United States.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(a).) However, in no case shall an attorney appear pro hac vice if the attorney is a resident of California, regularly employed in California, or “regularly engaged in substantial business, professional, or other activities in the State of California.” (Ibid.)

 

An attorney seeking pro hac vice admission must file a verified application in court establishing:

 

(1) [t]he applicant’s residence and office address; (2)[t]he courts to which the applicant has been admitted to practice and the dates of admission; (3)[t]hat the applicant is a licensee in good standing in those courts; (4)[t]hat the applicant is not currently suspended or disbarred in any court; (5)[t]he title of each court and cause in which the applicant has filed an application to appear as counsel pro hac vice in this state in the preceding two years, the date of each application, and whether or not it was granted; and (6)[t]he name, address, and telephone number of the active licensee of the State Bar who is attorney of record.

 

(Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(d).)

 

The application complies with California Rules of Court, rule 9.40. Cohen lives and practices in New York. He has applied for pro hac vice admission in California state court once within the last two years. While the proof of service indicates that the State Bar was served by mail and Withers Bergman via email, service must have been made on Withers Bergman by mail. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(c)(1).) The application for pro hac vice admission is granted, with counsel to file the requisite proof of service.

 

Plaintiff to give notice.
