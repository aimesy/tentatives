[TENTATIVE] ORDER DENYING MOTION FOR JUDGMENT ON THE PLEADINGS

 

Dept. 506

8:30 a.m.

September 17, 2026

 

On January 30, 2026, Plaintiff Keyshawn Johnson filed a first amended complaint (“FAC”) against Defendant Christopher Ellison.  The FAC alleges (1) breach of oral contract; (2) quantum meruit; (3) breach of the duty of good faith and fair dealing; (4) violation of California’s Unfair Competition Law (“UCL”); (5) negligence; and (6) declaratory judgment.

On August 20, 2026, Defendant (proceeding in pro per) filed a motion for judgment on the pleadings.

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

Defendant’s request for judicial notice is denied.  Defendant uses his 12 exhibits for the truth of their contents, which the Court cannot take judicial notice of and cannot rely on for judgment on the pleadings.

DISCUSSION

A motion for judgment on the pleadings is the functional equivalent to a general demurrer.  (Lance Camper Mfg. Corp. v. Republic Indemnity Co. of Am. (1996) 44 Cal.App.4th 194, 198.)  Like demurrers, motions for judgment on the pleadings challenge the legal sufficiency of the allegations, not their veracity.  (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)  The Court “must accept as true all material facts properly pleaded, but does not consider conclusions of law or fact, opinions, speculation, or allegations contrary to law or facts that are judicially noticed.”  (Stevenson Real Estate Services, Inc. v. CB Richard Ellis Real Estate Services, Inc. (2006) 138 Cal.App.4th 1215, 1219-1220.)

A.        The Court Cannot Adjudicate Defendant’s Defense of Illegality at This Stage.

Defendant argues that Plaintiff violated the Miller-Ayala Athlete’s Agent Act.  (Motion at p. 7.)  Defendant argues that, “due to the fact that all of Plaintiff’s claims are prediacted [sic] on the same set of facts, which are unlawful, all of Plaintiff’s claim should be dismissed.”  (Ibid.)

As established in Plaintiff’s Opposition, this is outside the scope of the FAC’s allegations, and Defendant’s illegality defense presents factual and legal questions that are contrary to the pleadings and cannot be resolved at this stage.  (Opposition at pp. 7-8.)

The motion is denied on this ground.

B.        Breach of Oral Contract is Sufficiently Pleaded.

Defendant argues that the oral contract is vague and uncertain because “Plaintiff has failed to plead legally recoverable damages in the FAC.”  (Motion at pp. 8-9.)  Defendant relies on the outside, un-pleaded facts that “Mr. Doubs has not entered into a new contract with any NFL team entitling Plaintiff to the amount alleged and projected by Plaintiff in the FAC,” “the fees from future NFL seasons (2025-2029) have not been earned nor paid,” and “it is entirely possible that the aforementioned players never earn another dollar in the NFL and/or Defendant does not charge agent fees at all and/or the amounts alleged by Plaintiff in the FAC.”  (Id. at p. 9.)

Plaintiff alleges that the parties “entered into an oral agreement pursuant to which Plaintiff would identify, contact, and recruit NFL players for representation by Defendant, and Defendant would pay Plaintiff one-third (33.33%) of the commissions Defendant received from representing each such player.”  (FAC ¶ 38; see FAC ¶¶ 13-16.)  Plaintiff alleges the details and terms of four specific player contracts for which Defendant has failed and refused to pay Plaintiff his agreed-upon share.  (FAC ¶¶ 25-32.)  These oral contracts are sufficiently alleged.

Defendant also argues that the alleged oral contract violates that statute of frauds because it cannot be performed within one year.  (Motion at p. 8.)  As Plaintiff observes, “[t]he alleged agreement contains no term requiring performance for more than one year. . . . Nothing in that arrangement foreclosed Plaintiff from recruiting a player, Defendant securing the representation, receiving a commission, and paying Plaintiff within one year.  The FAC expressly alleges that the arrangement was capable of performance within one year and that particular recruitment and payment obligations could be, and were, performed within that year.”  (Opposition at p. 9; see FAC ¶ 44.)

The motion is denied for the first cause of action.

Defendant also argues that the claim for breach of the covenant of good faith and fair dealing must fail in the absence of a contract.  (Motion at p. 11.)  This is Defendant’s only argument, and the Court finds that a contract is sufficiently alleged.

The motion is denied for the third cause of action.

C.        Quantum Meruit Is Sufficiently Pleaded.

“To recover on a claim for the reasonable value of services under a quantum meruit theory, a plaintiff must establish both that he or she was acting pursuant to either an express or implied request for services from the defendant and that the services rendered were intended to and did benefit the defendant.”  (Ochs v. PacifiCare of California (2004) 115 Cal.App.4th 782, 794.)

Defendant argues that “Plaintiff fails to allege a specific benefit that he bestowed upon Defendant as it applies to the four professional athletes named in the FAC.  Moreover, if there was a specific benefit bestowed upon Plaintiff, Plaintiff fails to establish what that benefit consisted of and the value of said benefit.”  (Motion at p. 10.)  Plaintiff alleges that “[a]t Defendant’s request and with Defendant’s knowledge and encouragement,” he provided recruitment and referral services, including identifying, contacting, cultivating, and recruiting NFL players to retain Defendant as their certified agent.  (FAC ¶ 47; Opposition at p. 10.)  Plaintiff also alleges that his efforts and personal endorsement resulted in Defendant obtaining representation agreements with four specific players.  (FAC ¶ 48; Opposition at p. 10.)  As a result, Defendant allegedly received commissions arising from the NFL contracts of players recruited through Plaintiff’s efforts, totaling approximately $2,887,005, while Plaintiff received no compensation for those particular services.  (FAC ¶¶ 49-51; Opposition at p. 10.)

Defendant also argues that because quantum meruit is a quasi-contractual equitable remedy that lies only where the plaintiff has no alternative right on an enforceable contract, this claim cannot be brought along with Plaintiff’s claim for breach of oral contract.  (Motion at p. 10.)  Plaintiff may plead in the alternative; a claim for quantum meruit may survive when a claim for breach of written contract fails.

The motion is denied for the second cause of action.

D.        Violation of the UCL Is Sufficiently Pleaded.

California’s UCL includes any unlawful, unfair, or fraudulent business act or practice and unfair, deceptive, untrue, or misleading advertising.  (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 17200.)  The UCL embraces “anything that can properly be called a business practice and that at the same time is forbidden by law.”  (Cel-Tech Communications, Inc. v. Los Angeles Cellular Telephone Co. (1999) 20 Cal.4th 163, 180.)  “By proscribing any unlawful business practice, section 17200 borrows violations of other laws and treats them as unlawful practices that the unfair competition law makes independently actionable.”  (Ibid.; see Klein v. Earth Elements, Inc. (1997) 59 Cal.App.4th 965, 969 [“Virtually any law can serve as the predicate for a section 17200 action.”].)

Defendant argues that Plaintiff does not allege unlawful conduct as a basis for the UCL claim.  (Motion at pp. 11-12.)  Not so.  Plaintiff has sufficiently alleged a fraudulent scheme by Defendant, and a private plaintiff has standing when he has lost money or property due to the challenged conduct.  (Kwikset Corp. v. Superior Court (2011) 51 Cal.4th 310, 322-323; see FAC ¶¶ 76, 80.)

The motion is denied for the fourth cause of action is denied.

E.        Negligence Is Sufficiently Pleaded.

Defendant argues that “The gravamen of Plaintiff’s’= [sic] insufficient pleading is that it fails to describe the ‘ultimate facts’ of defendant’s conduct that give rise to alleged liability.  Without more detail, defendant cannot investigate or prepare any defense to Plaintiff’s unsubstantiated claims.  Thus, the ‘conclusion of law--ultimate fact’ dichotomy is important in this case, because Defendant cannot defend himself if Plaintiff fails to describe facts about Defendant’s conduct and/or inaction that purportedly caused injury to Plaintiff.”  (Motion at p. 12.)

The FAC clearly and specifically alleges Defendant’s negligent conduct.  Defendant failed to maintain accurate records and accounting of commission payments, made false representations, conducted business below the standard of care, and disregarded professional obligations.  (FAC ¶ 92.)  These allegations are bolstered by earlier, more detailed allegations that are incorporated by reference.  (FAC ¶ 87; see, e.g., FAC ¶ 76, 81, 88-90.)

The motion is denied for the fifth cause of action.

Defendant’s motion does not address the sixth cause of action.

CONCLUSION

The motion for judgment on the pleadings is DENIED.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING MOTION TO COMPEL ARBITRATION

 

Dept. 506

8:30 a.m.

September 17, 2026

 

On April 8, 2026, Plaintiff Brent Webber (proceeding in pro per) filed this action against Defendant Fitness International, LLC.  Plaintiff alleges that a third party “struck Plaintiff from the side and chest” at Defendant’s gym.  (Complaint ¶ 22.)

On June 22, 2026, Defendant filed a motion to compel arbitration.

DISCUSSION

When seeking to compel arbitration of a plaintiff’s claims, the defendant must allege the existence of an agreement to arbitrate.  (Condee v. Longwood Management Corp. (2001) 88 Cal.App.4th 215, 219.)  The burden then shifts to the plaintiff to prove the falsity of the agreement.  (Ibid.)  After the Court determines that an agreement to arbitrate exists, it then considers objections to its enforceability.  (Ibid.)

The Court must grant a petition to compel arbitration unless the defendant has waived the right to compel arbitration or if there are grounds to revoke the arbitration agreement.  (Ibid.; Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2.)  Under California law and the Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”), an arbitration agreement may be invalid based upon grounds applicable to any contract, including unconscionability, fraud, duress, and public policy.  (Sanchez v. Western Pizza Enterprises, Inc. (2009) 172 Cal.App.4th 154, 165-166.)

A.        Plaintiff’s Preliminary Arguments Lack Merit.

Plaintiff argues that Defendant fails to meet its burden because “[o]n Page 4, Section II of its Memorandum of Points and Authorities, Defendant explicitly states that Plaintiff entered into an agreement requiring him to resolve his claims against ‘Forest Lawn’ through binding arbitration. Defendants’ brief, including its references to ‘Forest Lawn’, is verified under penalty of perjury.”  (Opposition at p. 4.)  This is merely a drafting error in the memorandum of points and authorities, and Defendant filed a Notice of Errata three days after filing the motion.  There is no such discrepancy in the Arbitration Agreement itself.

Plaintiff argues that Defendant failed to comply with Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41 and the Local Rules because it did not meaningfully meet and confer.  (Opposition at pp. 5-6.)  Plaintiff cites the requirement of meeting and conferring before filing a demurrer, not a motion to compel arbitration.  Defendant filed a Declaration of Demurring Party in Support of Automatic Extension on May 22, 2026.  Defendant ultimately decided to file a motion to compel arbitration rather than a demurrer.  There is no further meet-and-confer requirement before filing a motion to compel arbitration.  (See Code Civ. Proc., §§ 1281.2, 1281.7.)

Plaintiff argues that Defendant engaged in “an Initial Bad-Faith Calendar Reservation Designed to Manufacture an Inequitable Delay” and “initially weaponized this statutory rule [staying proceedings upon filing the motion] by utilizing the online scheduling portal to secure an abusive, distant placeholder hearing date.”  (Opposition at p. 6.)  There is no indication of bad faith. Defendant’s original hearing date of April 13, 2027 is not unusual, given the Court’s growing calendar and limited hearing reservations.  And as Plaintiff acknowledges, he was successful in advancing the hearing date via his ex parte application.

Plaintiff argues that Defendant waived its right to compel arbitration.  (Opposition at pp. 7-8.)  According to Plaintiff, “Defendant’s conduct here is the textbook definition of such waiver.  Moving counsel deliberately leveraged professional extensions of time under the explicit, affirmative pretense of preparing responsive preliminary pleadings—specifically threatening a demurrer and a motion to strike under CCP § 430.41.”  (Id. at p. 5.)  Plaintiff contends that “Defendant weaponized the court’s scheduling portal and executed a bad-faith pivot to a factually bankrupt motion to compel arbitration,” and “[t]his calculated bait-and-switch operates as an absolute waiver and forfeiture under California law.”  (Ibid.)  Not so.  Defendant filed this motion only two months after being served with the summons and complaint.  Defendant took no other actions besides filing its Declaration of Demurring Party in Support of Automatic Extension and then filing this motion.

B.        Defendant Has Shown the Existence of An Arbitration Agreement.

On June 11, 2025, Plaintiff entered into and signed a Membership Agreement with Defendant.  (Motion at p. 4; Pham Decl., Ex. 1.)  The Membership Agreement included an Agreement to Arbitrate.  (Motion at pp. 4-5; Pham Decl., Ex. 1 at p. 2 [“Arbitration Agreement”].)  The parties agreed to arbitrate “ANY DISPUTE (OTHER THAN ONE FILED IN A COURT THAT IS LIMITED TO ADJUDICATING SMALL CLAIMS).”

Defendant has satisfied its burden of showing the existence of an agreement to arbitrate that covers Plaintiff’s claims.

C.        Unconscionability Is Delegated to the Arbitrator.

Plaintiff argues that the Arbitration Agreement is unconscionable due to its adhesive nature and lack of bilaterality.  (Opposition at pp. 8-9; see id. at p. 12.)  Plaintiff also argues that “pre-injury consumer adhesion contracts cannot be deployed to insulate a corporate entity from statutory civil rights liabilities or hate crime allegations,” and “[c]ompelling private arbitration over a verified civil rights and hate crime record defeats the explicit legislative purpose of California’s consumer protection architecture.”  (Id. at pp. 9-10; see id. at pp. 11-12.)  Plaintiff contends that post-incident conduct by Defendant shows that “the foundational corporate relationship is built on bad faith gaslighting and administrative coercion, [so] contract formation has completely collapsed from inception, rendering the embedded arbitration clause an unenforceable nullity.”  (Id. at p. 10.)

As Defendant observes, Plaintiff’s unconscionability argument fails and, in any event, the Arbitration Agreement delegates that issue to the arbitrator.  (Reply at p. 6.)  Under California law, for a delegation clause to be effective, the language of the clause must be clear and unmistakable, and the delegation must not be revocable under state contract defenses such as fraud, duress, or unconscionability.  (Aanderud v. Superior Court (2017) 13 Cal.App.5th 880, 892.)  An unconscionability challenge must be specifically directed at the delegation provision, not the entire arbitration agreement.  (Id. at p. 895.)  Plaintiff does not challenge the delegation clause itself, which provides: “THE ARBITRATOR SHALL INTERPRET AND DETERMINE THE VALIDITY OF THE ARBITRATION PROVISION, INCLUDING UNCONSCIONABILITY.”

Defendant has satisfied its burden of showing the existence of an agreement to arbitrate, and that agreement contains a delegation clause.

CONCLUSION

Defendant’s motion to compel arbitration is GRANTED.  The entire action is STAYED pending the conclusion of the arbitration.

A Status Conference re: Arbitration is scheduled for September 17, 2027 at 8:30 a.m. in Department 506 at Stanley Mosk Courthouse.  Five court days before, the parties are to file a joint report stating the name of their retained arbitrator and the status of arbitration.
