[TENTATIVE] RULING ON PETITION FOR WRIT OF MANDATE

 

Petitioner Jasmine Martin (“Petitioner”) petitions for a writ of administrative mandate against Respondent Department of Motor Vehicles (“Respondent”) directing it to set aside its decision to suspend her driver license.

The petition for writ of mandate is denied.

I.                Factual Background

On May 27, 2025, Petitioner was involved in an accident with Jaramie Noel Olson (“Olson”). (AR 11, 48-49.)

Olson submitted to Respondent a Report of Traffic Accident Occurring in California, DMV Form SR 1 (“Accident Report”). (AR 11, 48-49.) According to the Accident Report, Olson sustained damages of over $1,000 in damages to his vehicle and Petitioner and her three-year-old sustained injuries. (AR 11, 48-49.) The Accident Report further indicated Petitioner had no insurance coverage. (AR 11, 48-49.)

Respondent suspended Petitioner’s license for failure to have insurance at the time of an accident resulting in over $1,000 of damage. (AR 7.)

On July 21, 2025, Respondent granted Petitioner’s request for a hearing and stayed the suspension. (AR 7, 42.) That day and the next, Respondent provided Petitioner with “discovery in compliance with § 11507.6 of the Government Code,” including Petitioner’s driving record printout, a notice of hearing, and the Accident Report. (AR 50, 56.) Respondent advised Petitioner that it would call Olson to testify. (AR 56.) The notice of hearing indicated the hearing would take place remotely on August 12, 2025. (AR 51-52.) The notice advised Petitioner she may represent herself or be represented by counsel, and that she would be allowed to present evidence, respond to evidence, examine witnesses, and present rebuttal evidence. (AR 51-52.)

On July 22, 2025, Respondent also issued Olson a subpoena and document production requests for “any and all records pertaining to the diagnosis of injury, or medical bills for injury and/or repair estimates or bills for damage allegedly sustained as a result of an accident which occurred on May 27, 2025.” (AR 44-46.)

On August 12, 2025, Hearing Officer Cecilia Tijero (“Hearing Officer”) heard Petitioner’s case. (AR 16-38.) Petitioner represented herself. (AR 16-38.) Olson was present and offered testimony. (AR 16, 20-32.)

During the hearing, the Hearing Officer identified Respondent’s exhibits 1 and 2: (1) Petitioner’s driving record print-out (AR 6-9), and (2) the Accident Report (AR 48-49). (AR 20-22.) Petitioner indicated she had no objections, and the Hearing Officer entered the exhibits into evidence. (AR 20-22.) Olson presented a May 28, 2025 estimate from Longo Toyota, a facility approved by his insurance, showing $2,306.17 in damage to his vehicle (“Estimate”). (AR 13, 14, 22-26.) Petitioner expressed no objection, and the Hearing Officer entered the Estimate into evidence. (AR 25.)

At the hearing, Petitioner acknowledged she was uninsured at the time of the accident. (AR 34.)

On August 13, 2025, Hearing Officer issued her hearing report and notice of findings and decision (collectively, “Decision”). (AR 2-5.) The Hearing Officer reimposed a one-year suspension, effective September 16, 2025, on the basis that Petitioner “failed to provide any type of financial responsibility or insurance for the vehicle involved in the accident.” (AR 2, 5.)

On or about September 12, 2025, Petitioner requested a departmental review. (AR 40.)

II.             Procedural History

On September 22, 2025, Petitioner filed this petition. A proof of service filed November 27, 2025 shows Petitioner served the petition on Respondent by personal service on October 15, 2025. On March 5, 2026, Respondent filed its answer to the petition.

On November 21, 2025, the Court denied Petitioner’s ex parte application for a stay and expedited hearing. On December 4, 2025, the Court granted petitioner’s ex parte application for a temporary stay of her license suspension.

On December 4, 2025, the Court granted Petitioner’s ex parte application to stay the suspension of her driver license.

On April 3, 2026, Petitioner filed her opening brief. On May 26, 2026, Respondent filed its opposition. On June 30, 2026, Petitioner filed her reply.

III.           Standard of Review

Code of Civil Procedure (“CCP”) section 1094.5 is the administrative mandamus provision that structures the procedure for judicial review of adjudicatory decisions rendered by administrative agencies. (Topanga Ass’n for a Scenic Community v. County of Los Angeles (1974) 11 Cal.3d 506, 514-15) [“Topanga”].) Section 1094.5 does not specify which cases are subject to independent review, leaving that issue to the courts. (Fukuda v. City of Angels (1999) 20 Cal.4th 805, 811 [“Fukuda”].) In cases reviewing decisions that affect a vested, fundamental right, the trial court exercises independent judgment on the evidence. (Bixby v. Pierno (1971) 4 Cal.3d 130, 143 [“Bixby”]; see CCP § 1094.5(c).)

While driving is a privilege, not a right, suspension or revocation of a driver license is a fundamental right for purposes of judicial review and requires application of the independent judgment test. (Berlinghieri v. Department of Motor Vehicles (1983) 33 Cal.3d 392, 398.) Under the independent judgment test, “the trial court not only examines the administrative record for errors of law but also exercises its independent judgment upon the evidence disclosed in a limited trial de novo.” (Id. at 143.) The court must draw its own reasonable inferences from the evidence and make its own credibility determinations. (Morrison v. Housing Authority of the City of Los Angeles Board of Commissioners (2003) 107 Cal.App.4th 860, 868.) In short, the court substitutes its judgment for the agency’s regarding the basic facts of what happened, when and why, and the credibility of witnesses. (Guymon v. Board of Accountancy (1976) 55 Cal.App.3d 1010, 1013-16.)

“In exercising its independent judgment, a trial court must afford a strong presumption of correctness concerning the administrative findings, and the party challenging the administrative decision bears the burden of convincing the court that the administrative findings are contrary to the weight of the evidence.” (Fukuda, supra, 20 Cal.4th at 817.) Unless it can be demonstrated by petitioner that the agency’s actions are not grounded upon any reasonable basis in law or any substantial basis in fact, the courts should not interfere with the agency’s discretion or substitute their wisdom for that of the agency. (Bixby, supra, 4 Cal.3d at 150-151; Bank of America v. State Water Resources Control Board (1974) 42 Cal. App.3d 198, 208.)

Where the issue is whether a fair administrative hearing was conducted, a petitioner is entitled to an independent judicial determination of the issue. (Sinaiko v. Superior Court (2004) 122 Cal.App.4th 1133, 1141; Pomona Valley Hospital Medical Center v. Superior Court (1997) 55 Cal.App.4th 93, 101.) Therefore, the court must independently review the fairness of the administrative proceedings as a legal issue. (Rosenblit v. Superior Court, (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 1434, 1438.)

The agency’s decision must be based on a preponderance of the evidence presented at the hearing. (Board of Medical Quality Assurance v. Superior Court, (1977) 73 Cal.App.3d 860, 862.) The hearing officer is only required to issue findings that give enough explanation so that parties may determine whether, and upon what basis, to review the decision. (Topanga, supra, 11 Cal.3d at 514-15.) Implicit in CCP section 1094.5 is a requirement that the agency set forth findings to bridge the analytic gap between the raw evidence and ultimate decision or order. (Id. at 515.)

An agency is presumed to have regularly performed its official duties (Evid. Code § 664), and the petitioner therefore has the burden of proof on mandamus. (Steele v. Los Angeles County Civil Service Commission (1958) 166 Cal.App.2d 129, 137.) “[T]he burden of proof falls upon the party attacking the administrative decision to demonstrate wherein the proceedings were unfair, in excess of jurisdiction or showed prejudicial abuse of discretion.” (Alford v. Pierno, (1972) 27 Cal.App.3d 682, 691.)

IV.           Analysis

Petitioner seeks administrative mandamus to set aside Respondent’s decision to suspend her license, raising two core issues: [1] (1) Respondent did not proceed in the manner required by law; and (2) Respondent’s finding that Olson sustained over $1,000 in damage to his vehicle is not supported by the evidence.

Vehicle Code [2] section 16020(a) states that drivers and owners of a motor vehicle “shall at all times be able to establish financial responsibility pursuant to Section 16021, and shall at all times carry in the vehicle evidence of the form of financial responsibility in effect for the vehicle.” (§ 16020(a).)

Section 16000(a) provides, in relevant part, that the “driver of a motor vehicle who is in any manner involved in an accident originating from the operation of the motor vehicle on a street or highway…that has resulted in damage to the property of any one person in excess of one thousand dollars ($1,000), or in bodily injury,…shall report the accident, within 10 days after the accident . . . .” (§ 16000(a).) Whenever a driver involved in an accident described in section 16000 fails to provide evidence of financial responsibility at the time of the accident as required by section 16020, the DMV shall suspend the privilege of the driver to drive a motor vehicle. (§ 16070(a).)

1.               Respondent Proceeded in the Manner Required by Law

Petitioner argues Respondent did not proceed in the manner required by law because it did not disclose the Estimate to her in advance of the hearing, and did not give her an opportunity to review the Estimate and object to its admission at the hearing. Therefore, Petitioner contends, Respondent failed to comply with Government Code section 11507.6.

Under Government Code section 11507.6, Petitioner may make a written request to Respondent for certain categories of evidence prior to the hearing and within 30 days after service by the agency of the initial pleading or within 15 days after service of any additional pleading. (Gov. Code § 11507.6.) Petitioner would then be entitled to “inspect and make a copy” of certain categories of evidence “in the possession or custody or under the control of” Respondent, including “a statement pertaining to the subject matter of the proceeding made by any party to another party or person,” “all writings . . . and things on which the party then proposes to offer in evidence,” and “any other writing or thing which is relevant and which would be admissible in evidence.” (Gov. Code § 11507.6.)

In opposition, Respondent argues that, at the time of compliance with Government Code section 11507.6, it was not in possession of the Estimate. Respondent points out that it provided Petitioner with the documents it had in its custody and control, which, at the time of the disclosure, did not include the Estimate later provided by Olsen. (Opp., pp. 9-10; AR 022-025.) Petitioner does not address this argument in her reply. (See generally Reply.)

On July 21 and 22, 2025, Respondent made disclosures pursuant to Government Code section 11507.6. (AR 50-56.) On July 22, 2025, Respondent subpoenaed Olson and requested documents. (AR 44-45.) It is not clear from the record exactly when Respondent obtained possession, custody, or control of the Estimate (though it may have been August 6 [see AR 24]). Importantly, Petitioner does not argue, let alone show, that Respondent had the Estimate before making its Government Code section 11507.6 disclosures. Without evidence that Respondent possessed the Estimate at the time those disclosures were required, Petitioner cannot establish a violation of Government Code section 11507.6.

Accordingly, Petitioner has not met her burden to show Respondent did not proceed in the manner required by law. [3]

2.               The Weight of the Evidence Supports the Hearing Officer’s Findings

Petitioner argues that the Hearing Officer’s findings, specifically the finding that Olson suffered over $1,000 in property damage, were not supported by the weight of the evidence. Petitioner approaches the argument in several different ways, but each amounts to the same legal position: the Estimate is not reliable evidence as to the amount of the damage to Olson’s vehicle. (See Pet. Op. Br. at 5-10.)

Petitioner argues that the Decision “treats the [Estimate] as establishing the amount of damage incurred.” (Pet. Op. Br. at 6.) Petitioner contends that the estimate is not the true cost because, as testimony establishes, Olson did not get his vehicle repaired by the establishment that issued the Estimate. The true cost, Petitioner argues, must be what Olson actually incurred.

In opposition, Respondent argues that the finding that damages exceeded $1,000 is supported by the evidence. Specifically, Respondent notes the Decision references the Accident Report, testimony from Petitioner, testimony from Olson, and the Estimate. Respondent also argues the Estimate alone is sufficient to establish the damages.

Even under independent judgment, the Court affords a significant degree of deference to the agency on review. Unless it can be demonstrated by a petitioner that the agency’s actions are not grounded upon any reasonable basis in law or any substantial basis in fact, the courts should not interfere with the agency’s discretion or substitute their wisdom for that of the agency. (Bixby, supra, 4 Cal.3d at 150-151; Bank of America v. State Water Resources Control Board (1974) 42 Cal. App.3d 198, 208.)

Petitioner devotes most of her briefing attacking the Estimate and its reliability. But the record contains multiple, independent sources of evidence supporting the conclusion that the damage to Olson’s vehicle exceeded $1,000, and Petitioner identifies no contrary evidence. The Hearing Officer relied on the Accident Report, testimony from Petitioner, testimony from Olson, and the Estimate. (AR 4-5.) The Accident Report indicated damage in excess of $1,000. (AR 11.) Olson likewise testified that the damage exceeded $1,000. (AR 29 [“HEARING OFFICER TIJERO: Okay. And it was over $1,000, correct? [¶] JEREMY OLSON: Yes.”].) The Estimate placed the damage at approximately $2,306.17. (AR 13-14.) Petitioner challenges only the Estimate, but even if the Court were to disregard it entirely, which it does not, Olson’s testimony and the Accident Report stand unrebutted and are unaffected by the concerns Petitioner raises. Petitioner points to no evidence suggesting the damage was less than $1,000, nor does she meaningfully grapple with the fact that the evidence other than the Estimate could itself provide a sufficient factual basis for the Hearing Officer’s conclusion.

Accordingly, Petitioner has not met her burden to show the Decision was not supported by the weight of evidence.

 

V.              Conclusion

The petition for administrative mandamus is DENIED.

Pursuant to Local Rule 3.231(n), respondent Department of Motor Vehicles shall prepare, serve, and ultimately file a proposed judgment. The Court sets a Non-Appearance Case Review for August 6, 2026.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: MOTION TO COMPEL ATTENDANCE AT DEPOSITION

 

Petitioner Daniel Lifschitz (“Petitioner”) moves to compel respondents Beverly Hills Unified School District (“District”), Judith Manouchehri (“Manouchehri”), Sigalie Sabag (“Sabag”), and Russel Stuart (“Stuart”) (collectively, “Respondents”) to attend deposition and produce documents.

The motion is granted.

I.                Background

1.               Factual Allegations

The petition alleges as follows.

The Petition alleges the District’s Board of Education (“Board”) appointed Sabag as Vice President in violation of the District’s bylaws. (Pet., ¶5.) Thereafter, the Board determined to hold a new election for the same position and term, on the basis that there had been a Brown Act violation and the Board needed to cure the violation. (Pet., ¶ 7.) Respondent set on its agenda for the January 27, 2026 meeting an item to amend the bylaw provision Petitioner argues Respondent previously violated, and then thereafter to hold the election. (Pet., ¶¶ 10-11.)

On January 26, 2026, Petitioner filed the petition. On April 9, 2026, the Court largely denied Respondent’s demurrer to the petition.

II.             Requests for Judicial Notice

Petitioner requests judicial notice of the following exhibits: (1) the District’s video recording of the public Board meeting held December 16, 2025 (2) the District’s video of the public Board meeting held January 13, 2026; (3) the District’s video of the public Board meeting held January 27, 2026. Petitioner supplies internet links to videos available on the District’s YouTube channel.

The videos are not official acts, legislative enactments, nor any other judicially noticeable category. The requests are denied.

Respondents request judicial notice of the following exhibits (1) the court reporter’s transcript of the April 9, 2026 hearing in this matter (Resp. RJN 1); (2) the agenda for the Board’s January 27, 2026 meeting (Resp. RJN 2); (3) the approved minutes for the Board’s January 27, 2026 meeting (Resp. RJN 3); (4) the agenda for the Board’s January 13, 2026 meeting (Resp. RJN 4); (5) the approved minutes for the Board’s January 13, 2026 meeting (Resp. RJN 5).

The requests are granted. (Evid. Code § 452(c), (d).)

III.           Discussion

Under the Code of Civil Procedure (“CCP”), a motion to compel attendance (1) shall set forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the production for inspection of any document or tangible thing described in the deposition notice, and (2) shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under CCP section 2016.040 stating facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt at an informal resolution of each issue presented by it or, when the deponent fails to attend the deposition and produce the documents or things described in the deposition notice, by a declaration stating that the petitioner has contacted the deponent to inquire about the nonappearance. (CCP §2025.450(b)(1)-(2).)

In Beverly Hills National Bank & Trust Co. v. Superior Court, (1961) 195 Cal.App.2d 861 (“Beverly Hills”), the court held that an order denying the taking of a deposition is the ultimate in protective orders, and that situations will seldom arise where an order that the deposition shall not be taken will be appropriate. The party objecting to the taking of the deposition has the burden of showing good cause for such an order. (Id. at 867.)

1.               Service of the Moving Papers Was Timely

Respondents contend service and filing of the motion is untimely under CCP section 1005(b).

Under CCP section 1005(b), moving papers must be served and filed sixteen court days before a motion is to be heard. (CCP § 1005(b).) This section also provides for additional required time under certain service methods.

Respondents’ counsel declares that Petitioner electronically served the motion on June 29, 2026. (Snarr Decl., ¶ 9.) Respondents provide no documentary evidence to support this assertion. Petitioner declares that Respondents were served with the motion on June 26, 2026. (Lifschitz Reply Decl., ¶ 3.) Petitioner provides a copy of the email serving the motion which shows it was sent June 26, 2026. (Lifschitz Reply Decl., Ex. V.) A proof of service filed July 2, 2026 also states Respondents were served June 26, 2026.

Considering the proof of service, declaration by Petitioner, and supplied copy of the email transmitting the motion, the Court finds the motion was served electronically on June 26, 2026. CCP section 1005(b) does not require additional time for electronic service. (CCP § 1005(b).) The instant hearing is set for July 21, 2026. Sixteen court days before July 21, 2026 is June 26, 2026.

Based on the foregoing, Petitioner timely served the moving papers on Respondents.

2.               Depositions Are Appropriate Here

Respondents contend that depositions are unnecessary in this case because Petitioner challenges official actions taken by the Board during publicly noticed meetings. Therefore, Respondents argue, the Court’s review is focused on the legality of the official action, not the Board member’s mental processes.

Respondents overlook the defense they raise to Petitioner’s mandamus claim: namely, Respondents contend the Board actions Petitioner challenges are justified as a cure for a Brown Act violation. The Brown Act (Gov. Code § 54950, et seq.) prohibits legislative bodies of local agencies from meeting outside of noticed, open and public meetings. (Gov. Code § 54953(a).) By relying on one or more Brown Act violations to justify the Board’s actions, Respondents are affirmatively asserting that unlawful unofficial or closed Board meetings occurred. As Petitioner argues, he is entitled to investigate the validity of Respondent’s alleged Brown Act violation, including by deposing members of the Board who allegedly participated in such violation.

Respondents have not met their burden[1] to show good cause for a protective order on this basis.

3.               Privilege Issues

Respondents argue the discovery sought is protected by attorney-client privilege, the work product doctrine, and deliberative process privilege.

“The party claiming the privilege has the burden of establishing the preliminary facts necessary to support its exercise.” (Costco Wholesale Corp. v. Superior Court (2009) 47 Cal.4th 725.)

a.                Respondents Have Not Waived Attorney Client Privilege or the Work Product Doctrine

As an initial matter, Petitioner acknowledges that he seeks material ordinarily covered by attorney client privilege and the work product doctrine. (Mot. at 18-20.) Petitioner then argues Respondents waived privilege expressly relying upon counsel’s investigation and legal advice to justify amending the District’s bylaws. Consequently, Petitioner contends, Respondents have waived attorney client privilege through “the deliberate injection of the advice” it the case. (Mot. at 19, quoting Transamerica Title Ins. Co. v. Superior Court (1987) 188 Cal.App.3d 1047, 1053 (“Transamerica”.))

Respondents contend that they have not waived privilege merely by acting on counsel’s advice. Rather, they assert, reliance must be an element of a claim or defense to create an implied waiver.

While Respondents offer no legal authority to support this position, they are correct. In Aetna Casualty & Surety Co. v. Superior Court, (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 467 (“Aetna”), plaintiff argued that defendant waived attorney-client privilege by relying on advice of counsel as a defense to an action for bad faith denial of insurance coverage. (Aetna at 474.) The Court of Appeal disagreed with plaintiff. That court determined that defendant “[was] not saying that their conduct was reasonable because their counsel opined so, but rather that their conduct was reasonable because the facts indicated that no valid claim existed.” (Id. at 475.) In other words, defendant was “prepared to defend itself on the basis of that asserted correctness rather than the mere fact of the advice.” (Ibid.) Therefore, that court determined that defendant had not waived privilege. (Ibid.)

Respondents contend they have not invoked counsel’s advice as either a claim or defense but have essentially just acknowledged that they received legal advice.

In reply, Petitioner argues that Respondents have already relied on counsel’s advice at the January 26, 2026 Board meeting. Petitioner further argues that Respondent cannot be trusted not to rely on advice from counsel later after the Court has already denied Petitioner here.

As to the former, Petitioner is incorrect. Respondents “relied” on the advice of counsel only in the way any client ordinarily does: Respondents’ counsel gave advice, and Respondents determined to follow that advice.

As to the latter, the Court notes that Respondents’ privilege remains intact at this stage because Respondents have not invoked the existence or substance of counsel’s advice as a basis for establishing the legality of the Board’s actions. However, Respondents are advised that if, at any later stage of this proceeding, they attempt to rely (either directly or indirectly) on counsel’s investigation, legal conclusions, or advice to establish an element of a claim or defense or to justify the Board’s actions, such reliance may result in an implied waiver of the attorney-client privilege and work-product protection under Aetna, supra, 153 Cal.App.3d 467, Transamerica, supra, 188 Cal.App.3d 1047, and related authority. Any later attempt to affirmatively use counsel’s advice as a substantive justification for the Board’s conduct would permit Petitioner to renew his request for discovery into the communications forming the basis of that justification, or to seek other remedies (e.g., ask that the Court disregard any defense premised on privileged advice.)

If Respondents will affirmatively represent to the Court now that they will not later rely on advice of counsel by, as Aetna describes, offering the existence and content of the advice to meet an element of a claim or defense, then Petitioner has no grounds to pierce attorney client privilege and the work product doctrine and the matter can proceed to trial normally.

b.               Respondents Have Not Shown Deliberative Process Privilege

Respondents argue that, under Westly v. Superior Court (2004) 125 Cal.App.4th 907 (“Westly”), the party seeking material potentially protected by deliberative process privilege must demonstrate why disclosure is sufficiently necessary to outweigh the interest underlying deliberative process privilege. (Opp. at 12, citing Westly at 918-920.) Respondents argue that “Petitioner performs no meaningful balancing at all. Instead, he simply asserts that the requested testimony is relevant.” (Opp. at 12.)

Respondents misstate the burden. First, Respondents cite to nonexistent pages from Westly. Second, Westly does discuss deliberative process privilege. (See generally Westly.) Finally, case law addressing deliberative process privilege states the reverse. To successfully invoke deliberative process privilege, Respondents must show the sought material would expose the District’s decision-making process “in such a way as to discourage candid discussion within the agency and thereby undermine the agency’s ability to perform its functions.” (State of California v. Superior Court (2024) 101 Cal.App.5th 214, 235, 240; Times Mirror Co. v. Superior Court (1991) 52 Cal.3d 1325, 1342; Dudman Communications Corp. v. Department of Air Force (D.C. Cir. 1987) 815 F.2d 1565, 1568.)

Just as Respondents accuse Petitioner, Respondents make no attempt to meet this burden. Respondents have not met their burden to show deliberative process privilege applies.

In sum, Respondents have not shown deliberative process privilege applies, but Petitioner has not shown Respondents waived attorney client privilege or the work product doctrine.

4.               Narrowing Requests

Respondent seeks to narrow the deposition and document requests.

a.                Person Most Qualified Topic 13

Respondents argue that Topic 13 to Petitioner’s subpoena to the District’s Person Most Qualified is not reasonably particular. Respondent contends this request “effectively incorporates every interrogatory, every request for production, and every factual issue in the litigation.” (Opp. at 14.)

Topic 13 covers “[i]nformation responsive to the discovery requests propounded upon BHUSD in this action.” (Lifschitz Decl., Ex. I.) Respondents do not provide or describe Petitioner’s prior discovery. Without some showing of what the discovery requests propounded upon the District include, the Court cannot conclude that Topic 13 is not reasonably particular. Respondents, therefore, have not met their burden.

b.               Deposition Document Requests

Respondents argue that the deposition document requests are overbroad and cumulative. Specifically, Respondents argue the document requests duplicate requests for production already served in this action.

As noted above, Respondents have not provided the prior requests for production. Nor have Respondents given the Court any indication as to which document requests were duplicative of prior requests. Accordingly, Respondents have not shown that Petitioner seeks duplicative discovery.

c.                Deposition Content

Respondents request that the Court limit testimony to specifically identified, nonprivileged factual matters relevant to the Petition. The Court denies Respondents’ request. Respondents may assert privilege if privileged matters arise at the deposition.

5.               Sanctions

If a motion to compel attendance is granted, the court shall also impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 of the CCP (commencing with section 2023.010) against the deponent or the party with whom the deponent is affiliated, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust. (CCP §2025.450(g)(1).)

Here, Petitioner did not notice or argue sanctions, so the Court does not award them.

 

IV.           Conclusion

The motion is GRANTED. Respondents shall produce the District’s Person Most Qualified (“PMQ”) for deposition, and Respondents Manouchehri, Sabag, and Stuart shall each appear for deposition. The District and the individual Respondents shall make themselves available and shall appear for deposition, and shall produce all documents described in the corresponding deposition notices, within ten (10) calendar days of the date of this Order.

The parties shall meet and confer promptly to select mutually agreeable dates within the ten-day compliance period. If Respondents do not meaningfully engage in meeting and conference to do so, Petitioner may unilaterally set a date with at least five days’ notice of the deposition (notice extended per Code if by other than personal service). Respondents are ordered to appear for the deposition at a date, time, and location to be noticed by Petitioner.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: DEMURRER

 

Respondent City of Santa Monica (“Respondent”) and Real Party Stanford Del Mar (“RPI”), named as a respondent, (collectively, “Movants”) demur to Stanford Neighbors Against City Overreach’s (“Petitioner”) verified first amended petition for writ of mandate and complaint for declaratory relief (“FAP”).

As detailed below, the demurrer is sustained.

I.                Factual Allegations

The FAP, filed on April 29, 2026, alleges as follows.

RPI owns property located at 1101 Stanford St., Santa Monica, CA 90403 (“Property”). (FAP, ¶ 6.)

On October 1, 2023, RPI filed an application to separate the Property into two parcels with four residential units on each. (FAP, ¶ 22.) On February 13, 2024, Respondent City Planner Ross Fehrman (“Fehrman”) preliminarily approved the application, including a tentative map. (FAP, ¶ 23.)

Petitioner challenged RPI’s division project in SNACO v. City of Santa Monica, et al., LASC no. 24STCP02065, and, on May 9, 2025, RPI abandoned its division project. (FAP, ¶ 25.)

On June 10, 2025, the City passed Emergency Interim Zoning Ordinance (“EIZO”) 2816 to permit accessory dwelling units (“ADUs”) in projects under the Starter Home Revitalization Act (“SHRA”) (Government Code[1] sections 66499.41 and 65852.28, as amended effective July 1, 2025) effective for sixty days. (FAP, ¶ 26.) On July 29, 2025, Respondent passed EIZO 2821 extending the provisions of EIZO 2816 through December 31, 2026. (FAP, ¶ 30.) On October 24, 2025, Petitioner filed SNACO v. City of Santa Monica, LASC no. 25STCP04108, challenging EIZO 2821. (FAP, ¶ 31.)

On October 29, 2025, RPI filed an SHRA application to subdivide the Property into eight residential units (“Project”). (FAP, ¶ 32.) RPI did not utilize EIZO 2821’s ADU allowance. (Ibid.)

On December 22, 2025, Respondent City Planner Ross Fehrman (“Fehrman”) approved the vesting tentative map in RPI’s application. (FAP, ¶ 33.) Fehrman found the Project was not a multiple-unit project as defined by Santa Monica Municipal Code (“SMMC”) section 9.64.020, therefore concluding no Affordable Housing Fee was required under SMMC section 9.65.070. (FAP, ¶ 34.)

Petitioner alleges that the vesting tentative map does not conform with SMMC section 9.28.120, subdivision (9)(a), constraining driveway size and number, and the front setback was improperly approved. (FAP, ¶ 35.) Fehrman did not consider whether the vesting tentative map conflicts with local ordinances. (FAP, ¶ 36.)

Petitioner further alleges that the Property is located in a delineated earthquake fault zone, and therefore is not eligible for SHRA subdivision until demonstration of compliance with applicable seismic protection building code standards. (FAP, ¶ 37.) Fehrman did not consult any seismic report for the Property, and no such report was included with the application. (FAP, ¶ 38.)

Fehrman did not consider whether the Project would have a specific, adverse impact on public health and safety with no feasible method to satisfactorily mitigate or avoid. (FAP, ¶ 39.) Evidence suggests the Project might negatively impact parking, sewage, and garbage collection in the neighborhood and limit fire and emergency access to a parking lot. (FAP, ¶ 39.)

The FAP brings its first cause of action for traditional mandamus on the basis that SHRA only allows ministerial approval of tentative maps, not vesting tentative maps, because the Property is not eligible for subdivision under SHRA for lack of compliance with seismic protection building code standards, and because the vesting tentative map does not comply with various sections of the SMMC. (FAP, ¶¶ 45-64.) Petitioner seeks a writ of mandate directing Respondent to set aside its approval of RPI’s SHRA application, and directing Respondent to deny the application or, alternatively, referring the application to Respondent’s Planning Commission with direction to the Planning Commission to apply the Affordable Housing Fee. (FAP, Prayer, ¶¶ 1-4.)

The FAP brings its second cause of action for declaratory relief seeking a declaration that SHRA does not provide local agencies with the power to ministerially approve vesting tentative maps. (FAP, ¶¶ 65-74, Prayer, ¶ 5.)

III.           Legal Standard

A demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the pleading alone and will be sustained where the pleading is defective on its face.

A demurrer may be asserted on any one or more of the following grounds: (a) The court has no jurisdiction of the subject of the cause of action alleged in the pleading; (b) The person who filed the pleading does not have legal capacity to sue; (c) There is another action pending between the same parties on the same cause of action; (d) There is a defect or misjoinder of parties; (e) The pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action; (f) The pleading is uncertain (“uncertain” includes ambiguous and unintelligible); (g) In an action founded upon a contract, it cannot be ascertained from the pleading whether the contract is written, is oral, or is implied by conduct; (h) No certificate was filed as required by Code of Civil Procedure (“CCP”) section 411.35 or (i) by CCP section 411.36. (CCP §430.10.) Accordingly, a demurrer tests the sufficiency of a pleading, and the grounds for a demurrer must appear on the face of the pleading or from judicially noticeable matters. (CCP §430.30(a); Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) The face of the pleading includes attachments and incorporations by reference (Frantz v. Blackwell (1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 91, 94); it does not include inadmissible hearsay. (Day v. Sharp, (1975) 50 Cal.App.3d 904, 914.)

The sole issue on demurrer for failure to state a cause of action is whether the facts pleaded, if true, would entitle the plaintiff to relief. (Garcetti v. Superior Court (1996) 49 Cal.App.4th 1533, 1547 [“Garcetti”]; Limandri v. Judkins (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 326, 339.) The question of plaintiff’s ability to prove the allegations of the complaint or the possible difficulty in making such proof does not concern the reviewing court. (Quelimane Co. v. Stewart Title Guaranty Co., (1998) 19 Cal.4th 26, 47.) The ultimate facts alleged in the complaint must be deemed true, as well as all facts that may be implied or inferred from those expressly alleged. (Marshall v. Gibson, Dunn & Crutcher, (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 1397, 1403 [“Marshall”].) Nevertheless, this rule does not apply to allegations expressing mere conclusions of law, or allegations contradicted by the exhibits to the complaint or by matters of which judicial notice may be taken. (Vance v. Villa Park Mobilehome Estates, (1995) 36 Cal.App.4th 698, 709 [“Vance”].)

V.              Analysis

Movants demur to the first and second causes of action of the FAP on the grounds that they fail to allege facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action.

1.               Mandamus

Generally, mandamus will lie when (1) there is no plain, speedy, and adequate alternative remedy, (2) the respondent has a duty to perform, and (3) the petitioner has a clear and beneficial right to performance.” (Pomona Police Officers’ Assn. v. City of Pomona (1997) 58 Cal.App.4th 578, 583-84 [internal citations omitted].)

a.                The SHRA Authorizes Ministerial Approval of Vesting Tentative Maps

Movants argue the mandamus claim fails because, as a matter of law, a vesting tentative map is a tentative map and can therefore be used in place of, and be processed in the same manner as, a tentative map. Petitioner argues that, where the SHRA expedites the process for a tentative map but does not mention a vesting tentative map, it applies only to a non-vesting tentative map. The disagreement is purely a matter of law.

The relevant portions of the statutory regime provide as follows:

Section 66424.5 defines the terms “tentative map” and “vesting tentative map”. (§ 66424.5.) “‘Tentative map’ refers to a map made for the purpose of showing the design and improvement of a proposed subdivision and the existing conditions in and around it and need not be based upon an accurate or detailed final survey of the property.” (§ 66424.5(a).) “‘Vesting tentative map’ refers to a map which meets the requirements of subdivision (a) and Section 66452.” (§ 66424.5(b).) Section 66452, in turn, sets out the procedures governing tentative maps. Subdivision (a) describes how a tentative map is filed. (§ 66452(a).) Subdivision (b) provides that a vesting tentative map “shall be filed and processed in the same manner as a tentative map, except as otherwise provided by this division or by a local ordinance adopted pursuant to this division.” (§ 66452(b).) Subdivision (c) adds that a vesting tentative map must conspicuously display the words “Vesting Tentative Map” on its face. (§ 66452(c).)

SMMC section 9.54.030 sets out the procedures for processing both tentative maps and vesting tentative maps. (SMMC §¿9.54.030.) SMMC section 9.54.140 establishes special procedures for projects proposing ten or fewer units that satisfy the requirements of Government Code section 66499.41. (SMMC §¿9.54.140(B)(1)(b).) Under these special procedures, eligible projects are exempt from Planning Commission approval when subdividing pursuant to section 66499.41. (SMMC §¿9.54.140(C)(2)(a).)

Government Code section 66499.41 requires local agencies to “ministerially consider, without discretionary review or a hearing, a parcel map or a tentative and final map” for qualifying housing developments.

Movants contend that this ministerial directive necessarily encompasses vesting tentative maps because the Subdivision Map Act defines a vesting tentative map as a form of tentative map and mandates that it be filed and processed in the same manner as a tentative map. (Gov. Code §§¿66424.5(b), 66452(b).) Petitioner responds that the Legislature’s use of the term “tentative map,” without expressly mentioning “vesting tentative maps,” invokes the exception in section 66452(b) permitting different treatment where “otherwise provided” by statute or by local ordinance.

The Court agrees with Movants. Section 66424.5(b) provides that a vesting tentative map “meets the requirements” of the definition of a tentative map; the Legislature thus established that a vesting tentative map is a subset of tentative maps. Section 66452(b) reinforces this by requiring that vesting tentative maps “shall be filed and processed in the same manner as a tentative map,” unless another provision or ordinance affirmatively prescribes a different rule. In other words, a vesting tentative map is a tentative map in the same way that a blue car is a car. Read together, these provisions indicate that the term “tentative map” includes “vesting tentative map” unless a statute or ordinance expressly distinguishes between them.

No such contrary provision applies here. Accordingly, when the SHRA directs local agencies to “ministerially consider, without discretionary review or a hearing, parcel map or a tentative and final map,” that directive applies equally to vesting tentative maps. (§ 66499.41(a).)

Because this is a pure question of law, and because Movants have shown that Respondent had statutory authority to ministerially approve the vesting tentative map, the FAP does not state a cause of action for mandamus on this theory. Leave to amend is not warranted.[2]

b.               The FAP Does Not State a Claim Under the AHPP

Petitioner argues that, because it alleges the Project consists of eight townhouses which are by definition multiple-unit projects (SMMC § 9.64.020), and because the Court must accept Petitioner’s allegations as true for the purpose of demurrer, the Court must conclude the Project is a multiple-unit project under the Affordable Housing Production Program (“AHPP”). Movant counters that Petitioner’s allegation that the Project consists of eight townhouses is a legal conclusion (“Reply at 9 [“On demurrer, the Court is not required to accept petitioner’s label for the project as a substitute for facts.”]), or that even if it is a fact, judicially noticeable evidence proves the Project units are not townhouses.

The SMMC does not define “townhouse,” and neither party offers a definition. The Court takes judicial notice of the Merriam-Webster Dictionary’s definition of “town house” in its primary sense: “a usually single-family house of two or sometimes three stories that is usually connected to a similar house by a common sidewall.”

The Court first addresses whether, under the demurrer standard, it must accept Petitioner’s allegation that the Project units are townhouses. Because “townhouse” is not a defined term in the SMMC and is not a legal term of art, the characterization is a factual allegation rather than a legal conclusion. As such, the Court must accept it as true at this stage. (Marshall, supra, 37 Cal.App.4th at 1403.) Although the Court need not accept allegations contradicted by judicially-noticeable materials (Vance, supra, 36 Cal.App.4th at 709), Movants’ judicially noticed documents do not establish that the Project units are not townhouses. Accordingly, for purposes of demurrer the Court accepts the FAP’s allegation that the units are townhouses.

The Court next addresses the interpretation of “multiple-unit project” under the Code. SMMC section 9.64.020 defines “multiple-unit project” as “a multiple-unit residential development, including, but not limited to, apartments, condominiums, townhouses or the multiple-unit residential component of a mixed-use project, for which City permits and approvals are sought.” (SMMC §¿9.64.020.) Relatedly, SMMC section 9.51.020(A)(3) defines a “multiple-unit dwelling” as “[t]wo or more dwelling units within a single building or within 2 or more buildings on a site or parcel.” (SMMC §¿9.51.020(A)(3).)

Movants interpret “multiple-unit project” to mean a project involving the construction of a multiple-unit dwelling, whereas Petitioner argues that any project involving more than one dwelling unit qualifies. [3] The text of SMMC section 9.64.020 supports Movants’ interpretation. The non-exhaustive list in section 9.64.020 (apartments, condominiums, townhouses, and the multiple-unit residential component of a mixed-use project) enumerates development types that, under the SMMC’s use classifications, fall within or correspond to “multiple-unit dwellings.” (SMMC §¿9.51.020(A)(3).) The inclusion of “the multiple-unit residential component of a mixed-use project” further indicates that the ordinance contemplates projects with distinct components, only some of which may qualify as “multiple-unit projects.” This structure is incompatible with Petitioner’s interpretation, which would deem any mixed-use project containing more than one dwelling unit a “multiple-unit project” in its entirety, regardless of component type.

Additionally, although the list in section 9.64.020 is not exhaustive, it conspicuously excludes projects involving multiple single-unit dwellings on separate parcels, which is precisely the type of development the City approved here. Nothing in the ordinance suggests that constructing multiple single-unit dwellings transforms a project into a “multiple-unit project” when those dwellings do not collectively form a “multiple-unit dwelling.” Although the Court accepts, for purposes of demurrer, Petitioner’s allegation that the units are townhouses in the ordinary architectural sense, that characterization does not determine whether the Project qualifies as a “multiple-unit project” under the SMMC. The legal classification turns on the Code’s definitional structure, which requires a multiple-unit dwelling rather than multiple single-unit dwellings of any particular architectural type.

Finally, long-standing authority holds that “an agency’s view of the meaning and scope of its own ordinance is entitled to great weight unless clearly erroneous or unauthorized.” (Friends of Davis v. City of Davis (2000) 83 Cal.App.4th 1004, 1015; Anderson v. San Francico Rent Stabilization & Arbitration Bd. (1987) 192 Cal.Ap.3d 1336, 1343; Anderson First Coalition v. City of Anderson (2005); 130 Cal.App.4th 1173, 1193; City of Monterey v. Carrnshimba (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 1068, 1091; Harrington v. City of Davis (2017) 16 Cal.App.5th 420, 434.) The City interpreted the AHPP not to apply because each dwelling is located on its own single parcel and is classified as a single-unit dwelling. Petitioner does not identify a basis to find this interpretation clearly erroneous.

Accordingly, the Court accepts Movants’ interpretation that a “multiple-unit project” refers to a project to construct a multiple-unit dwelling. The FAP therefore does not state a claim based on the AHPP.

c.                The Vesting Tentative Map Is Not Inconsistent with Objective Standards

While the FAP alleges the Project conflicts with several portions of the SMMC, Movants argue that the judicially noticed approved application shows the Project is consistent with the SMMC. The FAP identifies that the driveway for Lot 8 is less than ten feet wide in violation of SMMC section 9.28.120, subdivision (B)(9)(a).[4] (FAP, ¶¶ 35, 56-57, 64.) The FAP also identifies that the driveways have widths totaling over 28% of the frontage of their lots in violation of SMMC section 7.04.180.[5] (FAP, ¶¶ 35, 54, 64.)

Movant offers the approved Project application which shows the Lot 8 driveway will be ten feet wide. (RJN Ex. B at p. 9.) In opposition, Petitioner argues that, plan notwithstanding, the driveway will be less than ten feet, noting an easement it alleges will prevent RPI from constructing the driveway in accordance with the plan. (Opp. at 6-7; FAP, ¶ 57.)

Petitioner’s allegation to the contrary is insufficient to overcome the judicially noticed approved plan, which demonstrates that the Lot 8 driveway will be ten feet wide.

Movant argues that the SMMC section 7.04.180 restriction provides an exception for lots zoned for single-family use allowing such lots to have a single driveway not exceeding fourteen feet in width regardless of the frontage percentage. Movant offers the approved Project plan to show the lots are zoned for single-family use, and each lot has a single driveway not wider than fourteen feet. (RJN Ex. B at 9.) In opposition, Petitioner argues that the SMMC “clearly does not contemplate eight driveways for eight adjacent (and narrow) parcels, even if they are all zoned for single family residential use.” (Opp. at 7.)

The Court disagrees. The SMMC appears to specifically contemplate single-family parcels so narrow that they cannot have a sufficiently large driveway without that driveway exceeding 28% of the frontage. (SMMC § 7.04.180.) In those cases, the SMMC allows a driveway not exceeding fourteen feet wide notwithstanding frontage length. (SMMC § 7.04.180.)

Petitioner also argues the FAP alleges the insufficient curb lengths will cause safety issues. (Opp. at 7; FAP, ¶ 55.) Petitioner urges the court to overrule the demurrer “to allow the parties to obtain more information regarding whether and how eight driveways serving eight narrow parcels might affect neighborhood safety standards and/or obstruct emergency access.” (Opp. at 7.) However, this is not the standard on demurrer. The pleadings on their face must state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. (Garcetti, supra, 49 Cal.App.4th at 1547.)

All alleged inconsistencies with the SMMC are contradicted by judicially noticeable facts. Accordingly, the FAP fails to state a claim for mandamus on this basis. As judicially noticed evidence shows compliance with all identified SMMC sections, leave to amend is not appropriate.

2.               Declaratory Relief

Movants allege the second cause of action for declaratory relief is duplicative and derivative of the first cause of action for traditional mandamus. Petitioner does not address this argument (see generally Opp.). The Court agrees.

Accordingly, the second cause of action survives only as to the applicability of the Affordable Housing Fee.

VI.           Conclusion

The demurrer is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.
