DISCUSSION

Applicable Law

The purpose of a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication “is to provide courts with a mechanism to cut through the parties’ pleadings in order to determine whether, despite their allegations, trial is in fact necessary to resolve their dispute.” (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) “Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (c), requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.) 

“In ruling on the motion, the court must consider all of the evidence and all of the inferences reasonably drawn therefrom [citation] and must view such evidence [citations] and such inferences [citations] in the light most favorable to the opposing party.” (Aguilar, supra, at pp. 844-845 [quotation marks omitted].) 

“On a motion for summary judgment, the initial burden is always on the moving party to make a prima facie showing that there are no triable issues of material fact.” (Scalf v. D. B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1519.) “A defendant or cross-defendant has met his or her burden of showing that a cause of action has no merit if the party has shown that one or more elements of the cause of action, even if not separately pleaded, cannot be established, or that there is a complete defense to the cause of action.  Once the defendant or cross-defendant has met that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff or cross-complainant to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.  The plaintiff or cross-complainant shall not rely upon the allegations or denials of its pleadings to show that a triable issue of material fact exists but, instead, shall set forth the specific facts showing that a triable issue of material fact exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 437c, subd. (p)(2).)

 To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence. (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.) “If the plaintiff cannot do so, summary judgment should be granted.” (Avivi v. Centro Medico Urgente Medical Center (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 463, 467.) “Summary adjudication motions are procedurally identical to summary judgment motions.” (Serri v. Santa Clara University (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 830, 859.)

Analysis

A.    Deficiencies in Plaintiff’s Amended Separate Statement

The Court has reviewed the amended separate statement of Plaintiff, which was filed in

response to the Court’s August 18, 2026 order.

            An opposition separate statement in a motion for summary adjudication must be set forth in a two-column format. (Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1350(h).) Moreover, an opposition separate statement “must unequivocally state whether that fact is disputed or undisputed.” (Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1350(f)(2).) “Citation to the evidence in support of the position that a fact is controverted must include reference to the exhibit, title, page, and line numbers.” (Id.)

            The Court finds that the amended separate statement of Plaintiff is procedurally improper. Plaintiff has not cured the defects identified in the Court’s August 18, 2026 minute order.

            First, as with the prior separate statement of Plaintiff, the Court finds that Plaintiff has still not set forth some of the additional material facts in the two-column format. (See Plaintiff’s Amended Separate Statement Filed August 31, 2026 (“Amended Separate Statement”) at pp. 4-5, 12.) As stated in the Court’s August 18, 2026 order, the lack of a two-column format prevents Defendant from responding in reply. (August 18, 2026 Minute Order at p. 2.)

            Moreover, the Amended Separate Statement still sets forth abbreviated facts under issues 2-6. (Amended Separate Statement at pp. 4-11.) Plaintiff was advised that such abbreviations of fact were improper and put an undue burden on the Court. (August 18, 2026 Minute Order at p. 2.)

            Additionally, the Court notes that Plaintiff has failed to unequivocally state whether certain facts are disputed or undisputed. For instance, in response to Defendant’s UMF Nos. 13-14, Plaintiff indicates that such facts are disputed in part. (See Amended Separate Statement at p. 2.) There are numerous instances where Plaintiff has failed to unequivocally indicate whether a fact is disputed or undisputed. (Id. at pp. 2, 4-6.) Due to Plaintiff’s failure to comply with the California Rules of Court, the Court therefore deems such facts as undisputed.

            Furthermore, there are instances where Plaintiff has failed to cite to the paragraph of Plaintiff’s declaration offered in support of a disputed fact. (Amended Separate Statement at p. 2.) A general citation to the declaration of Plaintiff without citation to the paragraph, page, or line number is much too conclusory and is not compliant with Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1350(f)(2).

            Also, Plaintiff cites to evidence that is not attached as an exhibit to Plaintiff’s declaration in support of the opposition. For instance, Plaintiff cites to NHTSA reports and provides a website link; however, such reports are not attached to the declaration of Plaintiff in support of the opposition. (Amended Separate Statement at p. 11.) Critically, no exhibits are attached to the declaration of Plaintiff in support of the opposition.

            In sum, the Amended Separate Statement is deficient. As stated above, to the extent that Plaintiff has failed to unequivocally indicate whether a fact is disputed or undisputed, the Court will deem such facts as undisputed. The Court has given Plaintiff the opportunity to file a compliant separate statement. Plaintiff is informed that the purpose of a procedurally compliant opposition separate statement is to provide proper notice to the moving party. (San Diego Watercrafts, Inc. v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2002) 102 Cal.App.4th 308, 316.)

            Due to the lack of a procedurally compliant opposition separate statement, such fact constitutes “a sufficient ground . . . for granting the motion.” (Rush v. White Corp. (2017) 13 Cal.App.5th 1086, 1097.) The Court has given Plaintiff an opportunity and notice to correct the deficiencies in the initial opposition separate statement. (Id. at p. 1100 [finding that granting of summary judgment was proper where plaintiffs had an opportunity to cure defects in separate statement but failed to cure such defects].) However, Plaintiff has failed to cure the deficiencies.

            The deficiencies of the Amended Separate Statement constitute an independent ground for granting the instant motion. (See Rush v. White Corp., supra, 13 Cal.App.5th 1086.) The Court, however, will still assess the merits of the motion.

C.    ISSUE NO. 1, 2, and 3: Plaintiff’s First, Second, and Third Causes of Action are Time-Barred

Defendant contends that the first, second, and third causes of action in the operative FAC are time-barred. Plaintiff argues that the claims are timely.

Because the Song-Beverly Act is silent on any limitations period for express warranty claims brought thereunder, they are governed by the statute of limitations for warranties in Commercial Code section 2725. (Krieger v. Nick Alexander Imports, Inc. (1991) 234 Cal.App.3d 205, 213-215, fn.5; accord, Mexia v. Rinker Boat Co., Inc. (2009) 174 Cal.App.4th 1297, 1305-1306 [“California courts have held that the statute of limitations for an action for breach of warranty under the Song-Beverly Act is governed by the same statute that governs the statute of limitations for warranties arising under the California Uniform Commercial Code: section 2725”].) Accordingly, “the same rule of accrual must apply.” (Krieger, 234 Cal.App.3d at 219.) Under section 2725(1), an action for breach “must be commenced within four years after the cause of action has accrued.” (Cal. Com. Code § 2725(1).) “A cause of action accrues when the breach occurs, regardless of the aggrieved party’s lack of knowledge of the breach.” (Cal. Com. Code § 2725(2).) “A breach of warranty occurs when tender of delivery is made, except where a warranty explicitly extends to future performance of the goods and discovery of the breach must await the time of such performance the cause of action accrues when the breach is or should have been discovered.” (Ibid.)

Defendant’s Burden

Defendant presents the following undisputed material facts: on September 29, 2018, Plaintiff purchased a 2018 Honda Civic (the “Subject Vehicle”) from Robertson Palmdale Honda. (See Amended Separate Statement at UMF 1.) Plaintiff presented the Subject Vehicle to Robertson Palmdale Honda on November 15, 2018, for a loss of power concern. (Id. at UMF 13.) Plaintiff presented the Subject Vehicle to a Honda dealership for persisting concerns on three occasions: January 10, 2019, April 30, 2019, and October 15, 2019. (Id. at UMF 14.)

Defendant presents evidence that Plaintiff knew that his vehicle was “broken” at around 2,000 miles, which was prior to his first repair visit on November 15, 2018. (Defendant’s Compendium of Evidence (COE), Ex. A at pp. 71:22-73:11.) Plaintiff did not conduct any internet research about the problems with the Subject Vehicle. (Id., Ex. A at p. 141:9-11.) Plaintiff alleges that Defendant breached the express warranty under Civ. Code §§ 1793.2 and 1794 no later than April 30, 2019, which is when Defendant’s authorized repair facility completed its third unsuccessful repair attempt at approximately 10,263 miles. (Defendant’s COE, Ex. B at pp. 75, 78.)

The Court finds that Defendant has met its burden in showing that the first, second, and third causes of action are barred by the applicable statute of limitations. Defendant has presented evidence that Plaintiff knew the Subject Vehicle was “broken” prior to the first repair visit on November 15, 2018. Moreover, in response to Defendant’s special interrogatories, Plaintiff indicated that the express warranty was breached no later than April 30, 2019. (Defendant’s COE, Ex. B at pp. 75, 78.) The instant action was not filed until February 3, 2025.

Thus, Defendant has met its burden in showing that the first, second, and third causes of action are untimely. The burden now shifts to Plaintiff to show a triable issue of material fact.

Plaintiff’s Burden

In opposition, Plaintiff declares, inter alia, that he purchased the Subject Vehicle on or about September 29, 2018. (Declaration of Noe Estrada Filed July 30, 2026 (“Estrada Decl.”) ¶ 2.) Prior to purchasing the Subject Vehicle, Plaintiff relied on the reputation of Honda as a manufacturer of reliable automobiles. (Id. ¶ 3.) “No one at the dealership and no materials [Plaintiff] reviewed told [Plaintiff] that the Vehicle or its engine had any known defects.” (Id.) Plaintiff would not have purchased the Subject Vehicle had Plaintiff known about the engine defects. (Id. ¶ 3.) Plaintiff began experiencing problems shortly after purchasing the Subject Vehicle. (Id. ¶ 4.)

On November 15, 2018, Plaintiff brought the Subject Vehicle to the dealership for a loss of power concern. (Estrada Decl. ¶ 5.) Plaintiff presented the Subject Vehicle to the dealership on January 10, 2019, April 30, 2019, and October 15, 2019 for concerns. (Id. ¶¶ 6-8.) Each time Plaintiff took the Subject Vehicle to the dealership, Plaintiff “trusted that the dealership and Honda would eventually fix the problem.” (Id. ¶ 9.) “The technicians never told [Plaintiff] that the problem could not be fixed.” (Id.) No one ever advised Plaintiff of “Technical Service Bulletins, warranty extensions, campaigns, or recalls . . . .” (Id. ¶ 10.) Plaintiff still owns the Subject Vehicle and still experiences engine issues. (Id. ¶ 15.) Plaintiff only learned of a recall concerning the issue he has been experiencing with the Subject Vehicle on November 5, 2024, which is when Plaintiff brought the Subject Vehicle to AutoNation Honda in Valencia for a fuel pump motor safety recall. (Id. ¶ 13.)

The Court finds that Plaintiff has failed to create a triable issue of material fact as to when he discovered or should have discovered the transmission defect with the Subject Vehicle. (FAC ¶¶ 11-17.) A cause of action accrues “when [a plaintiff] at least suspects a factual basis, as opposed to a legal theory, for its elements, even if he lacks knowledge thereof . . . .” (Norgart v. Upjohn (1999) 21 Cal.4th 383, 397.) Here, Plaintiff does not present any evidence contradicting that he testified at deposition that the Subject Vehicle was “broken” prior to the first repair visit.

Moreover, in responses to discovery, Plaintiff alleges the date of breach as no later than April 30, 2019. Plaintiff should have had reason to suspect that a defect existed with the Subject Vehicle no later than October 15, 2019. (Norgart, supra, 21 Cal.4th 383, 398.) Plaintiff cannot now attempt to contradict his sworn discovery responses to avoid summary adjudication. (See D’Amico v. Board of Medical Examiners (1974) 11 Cal. 3d 1, 21-22.) Plaintiff has not produced substantial responsive evidence to create a triable issue of material fact on the statute of limitations issue. Plaintiff has also not presented admissible evidence that Plaintiff exercised reasonable diligence. (Nguyen v. Western Digital Corp. (2014) 229 Cal.App.4th 1522, 1553.)

Therefore, summary adjudication is GRANTED as to Issues No. 1, 2, and 3.

D.    ISSUE NO. 4: The Breach of Implied Warranty Cause of Action is Untimely

Defendant contends that the fourth cause of action for breach of the implied warranty of merchantability is untimely.

“When there has been a breach of the implied warranty of merchantability, a buyer may bring an action for the recovery of damages and other legal and equitable relief.” (Mexia v. Rinker Boat Co., Inc., supra, 174 Cal.App.4th 1297, 1303 [internal quotations omitted].) “The implied warranty of merchantability may be breached by a latent defect undiscoverable at the time of sale.” (Id. at p. 1304.) “In the case of a latent defect, a product is rendered unmerchantable, and the warranty of merchantability is breached, by the existence of the unseen defect, not by its subsequent discovery.” (Id. at p. 1305.) “The statute of limitations for breaches of the implied warranty of merchantability is four years.” (Montoya v. Ford Motor Company (2020) 46 Cal.App.5th 493, 495.) “A breach of warranty occurs when tender of delivery is made, except that where a warranty explicitly extends to future performance of the goods and discovery of the breach must await the time of such performance the cause of action accrues when the breach is or should have been discovered.” (Mexia v. Rinker Boat Co., Inc., supra, 174 Cal.App.4th 1297, 1306.)¿¿

The Court references its recitation of the parties’ evidence as well as its analysis from above and incorporates such recitation herein. The Court finds that the fourth cause of action for breach of the implied warranty of merchantability is untimely. As stated above, the breach of warranty should have been discovered in any event no later than October 15, 2019. Here, the complaint was not filed until February 3, 2025. Thus, the fourth cause of action is time-barred.

Summary adjudication as to Issue No. 4 is GRANTED.  

E.     ISSUE NO. 5: The Fraudulent Concealment Claim is Time-Barred

Defendant contends that the fifth cause of action for fraudulent concealment is time-barred.

A cause of action for fraudulent concealment is subject to a three-year statute of limitations. (Rivas v. Safety-Kleen Corp. (2002) 98 Cal.App.4th 218, 229.)

The Court references its recitation of the parties’ respective evidence from above and incorporates such recitation herein. The Court finds that the fifth cause of action for fraudulent concealment is time-barred. Plaintiff has not presented sufficient evidence to warrant a finding that the purported fraudulent concealment of Defendant should toll the statute of limitations. To invoke fraudulent concealment to toll the statute of limitations, Plaintiff must show: (1) the substantive elements of fraud; and (2) an excuse for the late discovery of the facts. (Investors Equity Life Holding Co. v. Schmidt (2011) 195 Cal.App.4th 1519, 1533.) Plaintiff has not presented admissible evidence of the substantive elements of fraud. (See Lazar v. Superior Court 1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 638 [explaining the elements of fraud].) Moreover, Plaintiff has not presented a sufficient explanation for the late discovery of the facts. Critically, as stated above, Plaintiff knew of issues with the Subject Vehicle as early as November 2018. In any event, Plaintiff should have had some indicia of wrongdoing by October 15, 2019. Plaintiff cannot now attempt to contradict his deposition testimony and discovery responses to defeat summary adjudication.

Due to the Court’s finding that the fifth cause of action is time-barred, the Court need not address the other arguments raised as to the fifth cause of action.

Summary adjudication as to Issue No. 5 is GRANTED.

F.     ISSUE NO. 6: Plaintiff’s Prayer for Punitive Damages Fails

Defendant contends that the prayer for punitive damages fails because Plaintiff has no causes of action to support a finding of punitive damages.

The Court finds that since summary adjudication has been granted as to each cause of action in the FAC (as well as the deficient Amended Separate Statement serving as an independent basis for granting of the motion), the prayer for punitive damages is improper.

Assuming arguendo that Plaintiff had a viable cause of action to support punitive damages, the Court would have found that there was no basis for punitive damages pursuant to Civil Code Section 3294 as Plaintiff has presented insufficient evidence to make a showing of oppression, fraud, or malice as to Defendant.

Summary adjudication as to Issue No. 6 is GRANTED.

CONCLUSION

Defendant’s Motion for Summary Adjudication is GRANTED in its entirety.

BACKGROUND

On February 27, 2025, Plaintiff Wilmington-Lomita Boulevard, LLC (“Plaintiff”) filed a Complaint alleging causes of action for inverse condemnation and trespassing against Defendants Port of Long Beach aka Long Beach Harbor Department, Long Beach Board of Harbor Commissioners, California Department of Transportation aka CalTrans (“Defendant” or “CalTrans”) and R.J. Noble Company.

On September 4, 2026, the Court denied Defendant’s motion to disqualify Mark B. Simpkins from serving as counsel for Plaintiff.

On September 17, 2026, the Court granted Plaintiff’s ex parte application for a temporary restraining order and set the instant hearing on the motion for preliminary injunction.

On September 21, 2026, Defendant filed opposition to the preliminary injunction.

As of September 23, 2026, Plaintiff has not filed a reply.

DISCUSSION

Applicable Law

The purpose of a preliminary injunction is to preserve the status quo pending a decision on the merits. (Major v. Miraverde Homeowners Ass’n. (1992) 7 Cal. App. 4th 618, 623.)  In deciding whether or not to grant a preliminary injunction, the court looks to two factors, including “(1) the likelihood that the plaintiff will prevail on the merits, and (2) the relative balance of harms that is likely to result from the granting or denial of interim injunctive relief.”  (White v. Davis (2003) 30 Cal.4th 528, 553-54.) The factors are interrelated, with a greater showing on one permitting a lesser showing on the other. (Dodge, Warren & Peters Ins. Services, Inc. v. Riley (2003) 105 Cal.App.4th 1414, 1420.) However, the party seeking an injunction must demonstrate at least a reasonable probability of success on the merits. (IT Corp. v. County of Imperial (1983) 35 Cal.3d 63, 73-74.) The party seeking the injunction bears the burden of demonstrating both a likelihood of success on the merits and the occurrence of irreparable harm. (Savage v. Trammell Crow Co. (1990) 223 Cal.App.3d 1562, 1571.) Irreparable harm may exist if the plaintiff can show an inadequate remedy at law.  (CCP § 526(a).)  

“The granting or denying of a preliminary injunction does not constitute an adjudication of the ultimate rights in controversy.”  (Cohen v. Board of Supervisors (1985) 40 Cal.3d 277, 286; see also Yee v. American National Ins. Co. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 453, 457-458 [“a preliminary injunction is not a determination on the merits….”].) Furthermore, a preliminary injunction ordinarily¿cannot take effect unless¿and until the party applying for it provides an¿undertaking¿to the effect that the applicant will pay to the party enjoined such damages as the party may sustain by reason of the injunction, if the court finally decides that the applicant was not entitled to the injunction [see CCP §¿529(a)].¿ (City of South San Francisco v. Cypress Lawn Cemetery Assn . (1992) 11 Cal.App.4th 916, 920.)

Analysis

  1. Terms of the Preliminary Restraining Order 

The Complaint seeks damages as a result of Defendant’s RJ Noble’s use of a 5,000 square foot portion (“the South Parcel”) of a vacant parcel leased by Plaintiff from the City of Long Beach for use as a construction staging area. The claims remaining for trial are inverse condemnation against CalTrans and trespass against R.J. Noble Company, arising from CalTrans's direction to its contractor to occupy the South Parcel.

Plaintiff provides the following facts in support of this preliminary injunction:

CalTrans has announced that within a month it will build a fence along the entire State Route 103 frontage of the two parcels Plaintiff leases from the City of Long Beach, in its words "to stop all access to/from SR 103." It announced this in a letter dated August 19, 2026 that it never served on Plaintiff's counsel. Counsel learned of the letter on September 11, 2026, when he reviewed the exhibits CalTrans proposes to offer at the trial that begins September 28, 2026. Measured from the date of the letter, the work is to begin on or about September 19, 2026-five days before the final status conference and nine days before trial. The openings the fence would close are the only way the North Parcel is reached. They were approved by the City of Long Beach, were required by the City of Long Beach, and exist because the City of Long Beach required and paid for them. The improvement schedule attached to Ground Lease No. 30946 specifies "5-60' wide gate openings-2 North, 2 South, and 1 emergency egress at midway" and a six-foot block wall "running length of property along Route 103," and the City credited their cost against rent. They were built in 2009 and have been used continuously ever since by the crane company that subleases the North Parcel. In the seventeen years since, CalTrans has never served a notice to remove an encroachment, never commenced an abatement action, never denied a permit application, and never identified a collision, a hazard or a complaint. What CalTrans has done instead is concede liability and litigate value. In its September 9, 2026 motion for a court view it told this Court that it "does not contest that a physical taking of the Subject Property occurred" and that "liability is established." In the declaration supporting that motion, its counsel swore-after visiting the property on September 4, 2026 without notice to Plaintiff-that the parcel "is landlocked." Access is therefore not a collateral matter. It is the principal contested fact bearing on the value of what CalTrans admits it took, and it is the fact CalTrans proposes to settle with a fence rather than with evidence. Plaintiff does not ask this Court to decide who owns the access rights. Plaintiff asks only that the frontage be left as it is until the Court has tried the case. The relief costs CalTrans nothing it has said is urgent: its own letter gave a month's lead time, which is an admission that nothing about the present condition requires immediate correction. Denial, by contrast, would shut down an operating business, end the sole income of the LLC's only member, and destroy the physical condition that both sides-and CalTrans's own pending motion-ask the trier of fact to evaluate.”

 

(Mot., pp. 6-7.) 

Plaintiff seeks an order restraining Defendant CalTrans as follows:

1.       Restraining CalTrans and its officers, employees, agents, contractors, and all persons acting in concert with or at the direction of any of them, from doing any of the following as to the North Parcel and the South Parcel demised under City of Long Beach Ground Lease No. 30946, as amended:

(a) installing, constructing, erecting or placing any fence, barrier, guardrail, barricade, K-rail, delineator, curb, berm or other obstruction along, across or within the State Route 103 frontage of either parcel;

(b) closing, blocking, narrowing, removing, paving over, filling or otherwise obstructing or altering any existing gate, gate opening, driveway, culvert, ditch crossing or other means by which either parcel is presently reached from State Route 103; and

(c) otherwise altering the physical condition of the frontage of either parcel, including the block wall, the chain link fencing, the truck run-off area, the ground surface and the drainage ditch and its crossings.

2. An order fixing the undertaking required by Code of Civil Procedure section 529, subdivision (a), in a nominal amount, on the ground that the order restrains nothing but the postponement for a matter of weeks of a condition CalTrans has tolerated since 2009 and identifies no cost that the delay would impose.

3. In the alternative, if the Court declines to restrain the work, an order (i) precluding CalTrans from offering at trial any evidence, testimony, opinion or argument concerning the condition of the frontage as altered after August 19, 2026, including any contention that either parcel is landlocked, and (ii) denying CalTrans's pending motion for a court view under Code of Civil Procedure section 651.

(Ex Parte Application, pp. 3-4.)

  1. Likelihood of Success on the Merits  

Inverse condemnation requires the following: (1) Plaintiff owned the real property; (2) the property was taken or damaged; (3) the cause was a public project; and (4) damage to private property substantially caused by an inherent risk presented by the deliberate design, construction, or maintenance of the public improvement. (Cal. State Automobile Assn. v. City of Palo Alto (2006) 138 Cal.App.4th 474, 480, disapproved on other grounds by City of Oroville v. Superior Court (2019) 7 Cal.5th 1091, 1105 [rejecting simple causation, and replacing with above-stated causation element].) 

Plaintiff states that it has a reasonable probability of prevailing on the merits, but does not clearly argue the elements of inverse condemnation in its papers. Instead, Plaintiff largely recites facts giving rise to the injunction request (blocking the proposed new fencing on the North Parcel) rather than connecting the facts in the Complaint, that are germane to the underlying action, to the claim of inverse condemnation of the South Parcel. However, in the interests of justice, the Court gives a generous reading to Plaintiff’s facts that may go to specific elements of inverse condemnation.

As to the first element that Plaintiff owned real property, Plaintiff contends that it owns the subject property after the State relinquished the segment in 2000 and the instrument reserved nothing. As to the second element that the property was taken or damaged, Plaintiff contends that CalTrans has conceded liability as to taking and now only litigates value, based on the following: “In its September 9, 2026 motion for a court view, CalTrans told this Court that it ‘does not contest that a physical taking of the Subject Property occurred’ and that ‘liability is established.’ In the declaration supporting that motion, its counsel swore-after visiting the property on September 4, 2026 without notice to Plaintiff-that the parcel ‘is landlocked.’ ” (Ex Parte Application, p. 6.)

As to the third element that the cause of the taking was a public project and the fourth element that the taking giving rise to the Complaint caused damages, Plaintiff makes no clear argument.

Here, Plaintiff fails to carry its burden to establish a likelihood of success on the merits against CalTrans. Plaintiff does not clearly identify the original taking, makes no connection between the taking and a public project, and does not discuss damages arising from the taking that is the subject of the Complaint. It is Plaintiff’s burden to show a reasonable probability of prevailing on inverse condemnation, and the Court cannot look back at the Complaint to piece together these elements for itself. In seeking to establish a likelihood of success on the merits, Plaintiff largely discusses the new proposed fencing, rather than the originally alleged taking of the South Parcel giving rise to this Action. The purpose of this element of the preliminary injunction analysis is to show a likelihood of success on the merits of the inverse condemnation claim, not on the injunction request itself.

Overall, Plaintiff fails to carry its burden to establish a likelihood of success on the merits.  

  1. Balance of Harms 

For the second factor, the court must consider “the interim harm that the plaintiff would be likely to sustain if the injunction were denied as compared to the harm the defendant would be likely to suffer if the preliminary injunction were issued.”  (Smith v. Adventist Health System/West (2010) 182 Cal.App.4th 729, 749.)  “Irreparable harm” generally means that the defendant’s act constitutes an actual or threatened injury to the personal or property rights of the plaintiff that cannot be compensated by a damages award.  (See Brownfield v. Daniel Freeman Marina Hospital (1989) 208 Cal.App.3d 405, 410.)   

Plaintiff argues that without court intervention, if the fence goes up, “the Court will try the value of the leasehold as of 2024 while looking at a property that CalTrans reconfigured nine days before trial, and will do so on CalTrans's own motion to go look at it.” (Ex Parte Application, p. 15.) Plaintiff contends that “the alteration announced here would be deliberate and would follow a written preservation demand. An evidentiary inference, however, is a poor substitute for the evidence itself, which is why Plaintiff asks that the condition be preserved rather than that its destruction be penalized.” (Ibid.)

Plaintiff further argues that sealing the North Parcel’s frontage, which is an  operating crane yard, “would strand the equipment inside it, halt the subtenant's business, and end the rent that is the sole income of Plaintiff's only member, the widow of the member who founded the company.” (Simpkins Decl., 2, 3 & 7.) Plaintiff contends that interference with the possession and use of a specific parcel of real property is not the kind of injury for which money is an adequate substitute, and section 526, subdivision (a)(2) is written in exactly those terms: "waste, or great or irreparable injury."

Here, Plaintiff has failed to establish that it would suffer irreparable harm should the preliminary injunction not issue. As an initial matter, Plaintiff asks this Court to interfere with CalTrans’s use of the North Parcel, which is not the subject of the Complaint and is thus beyond the scope of this action. Further, Plaintiff  fails to establish an inadequate remedy at law, stating that the proposed fencing would “strand the equipment inside it, halt the subtenant's business, and end the rent that is the sole income of Plaintiff's only member.” Monetary damages alone would address Plaintiff's concerns about loss of rent due to an interruption of the subtenant’s business in the North Parcel. (CCP § 526(a).)

In opposition, CalTrans establishes that the balance of harms weighs in its favor. CalTrans asserts that Plaintiff is not landlocked as it possesses the physical capability to construct a lawful alternative entrance elsewhere, such as Willow Street. Most importantly, CalTrans highlights that the harm to the public is great should the injunction not issue. CalTrans seeks to enact fencing blocking the illegal driveways from the North Parcel, which allow Plaintiff’s heavy, slow moving vehicles and cranes to enter a freeway acceleration lane where vehicles are driving at high speeds while attempting to enter the highway. CalTrans asserts that this creates an extreme, immediate hazard, which CalTrans is statutorily compelled to correct and safeguard under Government Code Section 835 which governs liability for “dangerous condition of public property.” CalTrans puts forth evidence that Transportation Engineer, Mr. Mario Orso, observed, analyzed, and determined that the North Parcel’s driveways connected to SR 103 create a Dangerous Condition of Public Property. (Orso Decl. ¶ 7.). Mr. Orso declares as follows: the start of SR 103 at Willow Street is an onramp, which allows vehicles to accelerate at high speeds onto the highway. (Id., ¶ 8.) This particular highway has a unique volume of traffic because of the heavy machinery and oversized cranes that travel on SR 103. (Ibid.) The illicit driveways pose a unique danger in that “oncoming motorists exercising due care cannot anticipate or safely reach to a multi-ton crane slowrolling into their direct path.” (Id. at ¶ 9)

The Court finds that CalTrans has established that without the proposed fencing, there is a risk of high speed vehicular collisions, severe injury, or even death should the cranes in the North Parcel continue to slowly move into oncoming traffic. The Court agrees that the public safety interest overrides Plaintiff’s interest in continuing to receive rental income from its subtenant in the North Parcel. CalTrans is also correct that while Plaintiff argues that continuous illegal use of the driveways along SR 103 provide Plaintiff a legal right to continue its use, there is no prescriptive easement against the State of California under California Civil Code Section 1007. Plaintiff concedes this as well: “Plaintiff does not contend that a prescriptive right can be acquired against a public entity, and does not rely on one.” (Application, p. 13.)

Accordingly, the balance of harms weighs in favor of denying the request for preliminary injunction. 

CONCLUSION

Plaintiff’s Motion for Preliminary Injunction is DENIED.


Case Number: 25LBCV01263    Hearing Date: September 24, 2026    Dept: S28

BACKGROUND

On April 22, 2025, Plaintiff filed a Song-Beverly Act complaint for alleged defects with a 2024 Honda Accord vehicle. On June 30, 2026, the court granted the unopposed motion for summary judgment in favor of American Honda Motor Co., Inc. (Honda). The court entered judgment on July 9, 2026. On July 27, 2026, the court struck Plaintiffs filed but unserved statement of disqualification.

On August 21, 2026, Plaintiff filed a motion for relief from Summary Judgment. Honda filed opposition on September 11, 2026. The court filing system shows no reply.

 

DISCUSSION

Applicable Law

“Both a motion for new trial under section 1008 and a motion for relief from the judgment under section 473 are appropriate means for seeking trial court relief from an order granting summary judgment or adjudication.” (Mink v. Superior Court (1992) 2 Cal.App.4th 1338, 1342.)

 

Analysis

Plaintiff moves for relief under Code of Civil Procedure section 473 subdivision (b). Plaintiff maintains both a mandatory and discretionary basis of relief. Plaintiff essentially moves for relief on grounds of mistake, inadvertence and/or excusable neglect in failing to oppose the motion for summary judgment. Honda in opposition challenges the sufficiency of any showing for  relief. Honda also challenges the timing of the motion and contends any relief would be futile either way.

Although not specifically identified, a party seeking relief from summary judgment may also proceed under the guise of a motion for new trial on the basis of “[a]ccident or surprise, which ordinary prudence could not have guarded against.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 657(3).) The court in its discretion can consider such a basis for relief. (Sole Energy Co. v. Petrominerals Corp. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 187, 194; Passavanti v. Williams (1990) 225 Cal.App.3d 1602, 1610.) Nevertheless, such a motion was required less than 15 days following the entry of the judgment. (Code Civ. Proc., § 659.) Plaintiff made no such motion, and any attempt would be  untimely either way. Plaintiff also makes no motion under the reconsideration statute, which would also be untimely. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1008.)

Plaintiff first presents the mandatory relief provision under Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b). The position finds support in that the granting of a summary judgment due, at least in part, to the lack of any filed opposition or appearance at the hearing as a result of counsel oversight constitutes a dismissal without an opportunity to present the merits of the claim. (Avila v. Chua (1997) 57 Cal.App.4th 860, 868 disapproved of by The Urban Wildlands Group, Inc. v. City of Los Angeles (2017) 10 Cal.App.5th 993.) The Urban Wildlands Group, Inc. case however specifically limited the mandatory provisions to default judgments and dismissal, and declined to more expansive definition provided to summary judgment orders. Both holdings were issued by the Second Appellate District, with the later case taking precedent. (Id. at p. 1000.) Other courts are also in accord with The Urban Wildlands Group, Inc. holding. (Prieto v. Loyola Marymount University (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 290, 297; Henderson v. Pacific Gas & Electric Co. (2010) 187 Cal.App.4th 215, 228–229; Las Vegas Land & Development Co., LLC v. Wilkie Way, LLC (2013) 219 Cal.App.4th 1086, 1090-1091.)

The court therefore declines to utilize the mandatory relief standard, and considers the motion under the discretionary relief provisions. “While the motion lies within the sound discretion of the trial court, ‘the trial court's discretion is not unlimited and must be exercised in conformity with the spirit of the law and in a manner to subserve and not to impede or defeat the ends of substantial justice.’ (Citation.) The law strongly favors trial and disposition on the merits. Therefore, any doubts in applying section 473 must be resolved in favor of the party seeking relief. When the moving party promptly seeks relief and there is no prejudice to the opposing party, very slight evidence is required to justify relief. We will more carefully scrutinize an order denying relief than one which permits a trial on the merits.” (Mink v. Superior Court , supra, 2 Cal.App.4th at p. 1343; Henderson v. Pacific Gas & Electric Co., supra, 187 Cal.App.4th at pp. 229-230; Las Vegas Land & Development Co., LLC v. Wilkie Way, LLC, supra, 219 Cal.App.4th at pp. 1092-1093.)

“In determining whether the attorney's mistake or inadvertence was excusable, the court inquires whether a reasonably prudent person might have made the same mistake under the same or similar circumstances. (Citation.). Thus, discretionary relief is available only from attorney error that is ‘fairly imputable to the client, i.e., mistakes anyone could have made.’ [Citation.] ‘Conduct falling below the professional standard of care, such as failure to timely object or to properly advance an argument, is not therefore excusable. To hold otherwise would be to eliminate the express statutory requirement of excusability and effectively eviscerate the concept of attorney malpractice.’ …

“The terms mistake, inadvertence, surprise, and excusable neglect warranting relief under section 473(b) are defined as follows: ‘Mistake is not a ground for relief under section 473, subdivision (b), when ‘the court finds that the “mistake” is simply the result of professional incompetence, general ignorance of the law, or unjustifiable negligence in discovering the law....’ (Citation.) Further, ‘[t]he term “surprise,” as used in section 473, refers to some condition or situation in which a party ... is unexpectedly placed to his injury, without any default or negligence of his own, which ordinary prudence could not have guarded against.’ (Citation.) Finally, as for inadvertence or neglect, ‘[t]o warrant relief under section 473 a litigant's neglect must have been such as might have been the act of a reasonably prudent person under the same circumstances. The inadvertence contemplated by the statute does not mean mere inadvertence in the abstract. If it is wholly inexcusable it does not justify relief.” (Citation.) …

“Generally speaking, the trial court's ruling on a discretionary motion for relief is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. (Citation.) Since ‘the law strongly favors trial and disposition on the merits, any doubts in applying section 473 must be resolved in favor of the party seeking relief from default.’(Citation.) For that reason, ‘a trial court order denying relief is scrutinized more carefully than an order permitting trial on the merits.” (Citation.) (Henderson v. Pacific Gas & Electric Co., supra, 187 Cal.App.4th at pp. 229–230.)

The court finds the excuse, e.g. the failure to present any opposition due to internal operational error of counsel, meets the standard for discretionary relief. Counsel presumably intended to oppose the motion on the substantive merits of the motion. [Declaration of Larry Chae.] The motion was filed less than two months from the date of the order granting the motion. The timeline is reasonable.

The motion was granted at least in part due to the lack of opposition. Requiring Plaintiff to pursue a potential legal malpractice action against counsel remains the lesser alternative. As cited above, public policy favors relief and adjudication on the merits, when the oversight of counsel improperly imputes said error on the client, when the client is otherwise not at fault. The court finds nothing in the record indicating any improper conduct by the client in deferring to counsel for the handling of the opposition to the motion for summary judgment.

The court otherwise declines to consider the futility positions challenging the merits of any potential opposition. Any challenges can be addressed in a substantive reply to the opposition.

The court therefore grants the motion.

The court vacates the order granting summary judgment. Honda may reserve a new hearing date utilizing the original motion as the basis for the reservation. Any new opposition and new reply will be due pursuant to statutory guidelines of the new hearing date.

CONCLUSION

Plaintiff’s motion for relief from the order for summary judgment is GRANTED.


Case Number: 25LBCV01832    Hearing Date: September 24, 2026    Dept: S28

BACKGROUND

            On March 8, 2024, Plaintiffs purchased a 2024 Hyundai IONIQ 6, which was manufactured and distributed by Defendant Hyundai Motor America (“Hyundai”).  Plaintiffs filed this action on June 24, 2025 alleging violations of the Song Beverly Act.

            On April 16, 2026, the Court denied Defendant’s Motion to Compel Arbitration.

            On June 15, 2026, Defendant filed the instant Motion for Summary Adjudication (“MSA”).

            On June 16, 2026, Plaintiff filed a First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) alleging violations of the Song Beverly Action.  Plaintiff’s FAC alleges (1) violation of subdivision (d) of Civil Code section 1793.2; (2) violation of subdivision (b) of Civil Code section 1793.2; (3) violation of subdivision (a)(3) of Civil Code section 1793.2; (4) breach of the implied warranty of merchantability (Civil Code sections 1791.2; 1794; 1795.5f); and (5) fraudulent concealment.

            On July 20, 2026, Defendant field a demurrer to the FAC.

            On September 4, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition to the MSA.

            On September 11, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition to the demurrer.

            On September 17, 2026, Defendant filed a reply to Plaintiff’s opposition to the demurrer.

            No reply to the summary judgment has been filed or served as of September 21, 2026.

DISCUSSION

I.  Demurrer to FAC

A.  Applicable Law

Code of Civil Procedure (“CCP”) section 430.10 subdivision (e) is grounds for a demurrer when the complaint fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. CCP § 430.10(f) is grounds for a demurrer when the complaint is uncertain (vague and ambiguous). For purposes of ruling on a demurrer, material facts properly pleaded in the complaint must be taken as true. (Serrano v. Priest (1971) 5 Cal.3d 584, 491.)

A demurrer may challenge only defects that appear on the face of the pleading or from matters which are judicially noticeable. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 31; Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994; California Practice Guide, Civil Procedure Before Trial, ¶7:8 (The Rutter Group 2024).)

The function of a demurrer is to test the legal sufficiency of a complaint, but not the truthfulness of the allegations. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co., supra, 116 Cal.App.4th at 994; Lewis v. Safeway, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 385, 388; SJJC Aviation Services, LLC v. City of San Jose (2017) 12 Cal.App.5th 1043, 1051-1052; California Practice Guide, Civil Procedure Before Trial, ¶7:5 (The Rutter Group 2024).) Demurrers are to be sustained where a pleading fails to plead adequately any essential element of the cause of action. (Cantu v. Resolution Trust Corp. (1992) 4 Cal.App.4th 857, 879-80.)

“'A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 430.30, 430.70). The only issue involved in a demurrer hearing is whether the complaint, as it stands, unconnected with extraneous matters, states a cause of action.'” (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747; Accord McKenney v. Purepac Pharmaceutical Co. (2008) 162 Cal.App.4th 72, 79.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (McKenney, supra, 167 Cal.App.4th at 77; Taylor v. City of Los Angeles Dept. of Water and Power (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 1216, 1228.)

“If the complaint states a cause of action under any theory, regardless of the title under which the factual basis for relief is stated, that aspect of the complaint is good against a demurrer.” (Quelimane Co. v. Stewart Title Guaranty Co. (1998) 19 Cal.4th 26, 38.)

B.  Meet and Confer

Defendant’s meet and confer declaration is inadequate.  Code of Civil Procedure section 430.31 requires in person, telephonic or video conference meet and confer attempts.  Defendant’s declaration merely states that counsel “attempted to meet and confer with Plaintiffs’ Counsel and that parties were unable to resolve the objections raised in the demurrer.”  (Demurrer, Kurtz Dec., ¶2.)  Defendant is admonished to comply with the requirements of section 430.31 in the future or face sanctions.    

C.  Parties Positions

Defendant demurs to the fifth cause of action for fraudulent concealment on several grounds.  Defendant argues (1) the fraudulent concealment claim is not alleged with sufficient specificity; (2) insufficient allegations are made establishing Defendant’s knowledge of the defect and vague references to non-public data regarding Plaintiffs’ battery defect are insufficient; (3) Plaintiffs fail to allege justifiable reliance; (4) Plaintiffs fail to allege facts supporting a duty of disclosure under these circumstances; and (5) Plaintiffs’ fraudulent concealment claim is barred by the economic loss rule. 

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue a prima facie case is alleged pursuant to Dhital v. Nissan N. Am., Inc. (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 828, 843-33.  Plaintiffs argue less specificity is required where concealment is alleged.  Plaintiffs argue direct privity is not required to support a duty of disclosure.  Plaintiffs argue Defendant had a duty to disclose material facts to immediate and subsequent purchasers because it knew its product would be resold.  Plaintiffs argue they have a contractual relationship with Hyundai by virtue of Hyundai’s warranty contract.  Plaintiffs also argue the economic loss rule does not bar a fraudulent concealment claim per Dhital.  In the alternative, Plaintiffs ask the Court for leave to amend.

In reply, Defendant reiterates its arguments in its opening brief.  Defendant maintains there are no facts supporting a duty to disclose.   Defendant argues Plaintiffs are judicially estopped from maintaining that the warranty booklet is a contractual relationship.  Defendant argues that, in opposition its prior Motion to Compel Arbitration, Plaintiffs adopted the position that the warranty booklet was not a contract.  Defendant argues Plaintiffs cannot adopt the opposition position here.  Defendant argues there are no allegations of justifiable or actual reliance.  Defendant argues that Rattagan controls and Plaintiffs cannot allege a fraud claim bootstrapped onto a lemon law claim. 

C.  Fraud is alleged with sufficient specificity

Fraud based on concealment requires that “(1) the defendant must have concealed or suppressed a material fact, (2) the defendant must have been under a duty to disclose the fact to the plaintiff, (3) the defendant must have intentionally concealed or suppressed the fact with the intent to defraud the plaintiff, (4) the plaintiff must have been unaware of the fact and would not have acted as he did if he had known of the concealed or suppressed fact, and (5) as a result of the concealment or suppression of the fact, the plaintiff must have sustained damage.”  (Bigler-Engler v. Breg, Inc. (2017) 7 Cal.App.5th 276, 310-311.)  Less specificity is required to plead fraud by concealment.  (Id.)  “Even under the strict rules of common law pleading, one of the canons was that less particularity is required when the facts lie more in the knowledge of the opposite party.”  (Alfaro v. Community Housing Improvement System & Planning Assn., Inc. (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1256, 1384.)

There are “four circumstances in which nondisclosure or concealment may constitute actionable fraud: (1) when the defendant is in a fiduciary relationship with the plaintiff; (2) when the defendant had exclusive knowledge of material facts not known to the plaintiff; (3) when the defendant actively conceals a material fact from the plaintiff; and (4) when the defendant makes partial representations but also suppresses some material facts.”  (LiMandri v. Judkins (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 326, 336.)  A duty to disclose in the absence of a fiduciary relationship and in “each of the other three circumstances in which nondisclosure may be actionable presupposes the existence of some other relationship between the plaintiff and defendant in which a duty to disclose can arise.”  (Id. at 336-337 (counsel for debtors could not state a c/a for concealment against counsel for creditors, because there was no relationship or transaction between the two attorneys that would give rise to a duty of disclosure).)

Plaintiffs’ claim is for concealment and less specificity is required.  (Tenet Healthsystem Desert, Inc. v. Blue Cross of California (2016) 245 Cal.App.4th 821, 838 (less specificity required in connection with fraud claim where facts are more within the knowledge of the opposing party).)  Plaintiffs allege the date of the vehicle purchase and provision of the warranty agrement.  (FAC, ¶¶6-7.)  Plaintiff also alleges the information concealed by Hyundai when it issued its warranty—“that the subject vehicle and its lithium-ion battery were defective and susceptible to sudden and premature failure.”  (FAC, ¶47.)   Plaintiffs specify the nature of the “Battery Defect.”  (FAC, ¶50.)  Plaintiffs allege Hyundai “was well aware and knew that the lithium-ion battery installed in the subject vehicle was defective but failed to disclose this fact to Plaintiffs at the time of sale and thereafter” and that it acquired that knowledge before Plaintiffs’ purchase from information that was not publicly available.  (FAC, ¶¶49, 51.)  Plaintiffs allege they would not have leased or purchased the vehicle if they had known of the Battery Defect.  (Id. at ¶¶52, 54.)  Plaintiffs allege Defendant knowingly and intentionally concealed the material facts.  (Id. at ¶55.)  Plaintiffs allege they reviewed Defendant’s marketing and advertising materials, viewed Hyundai’s vehicle-specific window sticker and took the vehicle for a test drive and Defendant never advised Plaintiffs of the defective lithium-ion battery .  (Id. at ¶9.) 

Defendant argues its knowledge of the defect is insufficiently alleged.  However, Plaintiff need only allege ultimate facts, not evidentiary facts.  (Semole v. Sansoucie (1972) 28 Cal. App. 3d 714, 721.)  Defendant’s knowledge of the battery defect is itself an ultimate fact.  (5 Witkin, Cal. Proc. (6th ed. 2023), Plead §723 (discussing fraud)(intent, like knowledge, is a fact and general allegation of intent is sufficient).)  Plaintiffs’ bare allegation that Defendant knew of the battery defect at the time of Plaintiffs’ purchase is therefore sufficient. 

Plaintiffs have alleged concealment with sufficient specificity.  Defendant’s demurrer to the fifth cause of action for fraudulent concealment based on failure to allege justifiable reliance is overruled.

D.  Actual or Justifiable Reliance

            Justifiable reliance is a question of fact that can only be decided as an issue of law where reasonable minds could only come to one conclusion.  (Hoffman v. 162 North Wolfe LLC (2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 1178, 1194.)  “Justifiable reliance is ordinarily a question of fact that is not properly determined on demurrer, but whether a party's reliance was justified may be decided as a matter of law if reasonable minds can come to only one conclusion based on the facts.”  (Amiodarone Cases (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 1091, 1111.) 

            Plaintiffs allege sufficient facts to establish justifiable reliance based on Defendant’s affirmative representations made in the warranty booklet, marketing and advertising materials and Hyundai’s vehicle-specific window sticker and Defendant’s failure to disclose any issues with the battery.  (Id. at ¶55.)  The Court cannot find based on the allegations that Plaintiffs’ reliance was unjustifiable as a matter of law. 

            Defendant’s demurrer to the fifth cause of action for fraudulent concealment based on failure to allege justifiable reliance is overruled.

E.  Duty to Disclose

The degree of “relationship” necessary to support a duty to disclose under LiMandri is unclear and evolving.  Thus, in Jones v. ConocoPhillips Co. (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 1187, the Court of Appeals found that an employee who used hazardous chemicals purchased by his or her employer could state a claim for fraudulent concealment against the manufacturer or distributor from whom the employer purchased the chemicals.  (Jones, supra, 198 Cal.App.4th at 1199.)  Plaintiffs alleged a duty of disclosure based on (1) defendant Dow’s awareness of the toxic nature of their products, (2) Dow’s sole knowledge of that fact; and (3) decedent who worked with the product did not have that knowledge available to him.  (Jones, supra, 198 Cal.App.4th at 1199.) 

“Although, typically, a duty to disclose arises when a defendant owes a fiduciary duty to a plaintiff, a duty to disclose may also arise when a defendant possesses or exerts control over material facts not readily available to the plaintiff.”  (Id.)   

The analysis in Jones was based on LiMandri, which stated that, outside of a fiduciary relationship, a duty to disclose can arise in three other circumstances:  when the defendant had exclusive knowledge of material facts not known to the plaintiff; (3) when the defendant actively conceals a material fact from the plaintiff; and (4) when the defendant makes partial representations but also suppresses some material facts.  (LiMandri, supra, 52 Cal.App.4th at 336-337.)  “These three circumstances, however, presuppose the existence of some other relationship between the plaintiff and defendant in which a duty to disclose can arise.”  (Id.) 

The Court of Appeals in Bigler-Engler v. Breg, Inc. (2017) 7 Cal.App.5th 276 raised further questions regarding the nature of the “relationship” required under LiMandri to impose a duty of disclosure.  Bigler-Engler held that such a relationship must be the result of some transaction.  (Bigler-Engler, supra, 7 Cal.App.5th at 312.)  “Such a transaction must necessarily arise from direct dealings between the plaintiff and the defendant; it cannot arise between the defendant and the public at large.”  (Id. (emphasis added).)  The Court reversed a jury verdict on the plaintiff’s intentional concealment claim on grounds that the record did not support a finding of a duty of disclosure.  (Id. at 315.)

Relying on this dicta in Bigler-Engler, the Court of Appeals in Bader v. Johnson & Johnson (2022) 86 Cal.App.5th 1094 (“Bader”) distinguished Bigler-Engler on grounds that (1) the undisputed evidence demonstrated that the plaintiff’s parents kept defendant’s powder product in the house and that plaintiff used the defendant’s powder and (2) there was also evidence that the defendant manufacturer “was involved in retail sales of JBP to consumers and profited therefrom.”  (Bader v. Johnson & Johnson, supra, 86 Cal.App.5th at 1132.)  Unlike Bigler-Engler, in Bader there was therefore evidence of a direct transaction or relationship between the plaintiff and defendant manufacturer and of a defendant’s direct sales to consumers and profit therefrom. 

The lack of clarity caused by Bigler-Engler and Bader was recently discussed in Bjoin v. J-M Manufacturing Co., Inc. (2025) 113 Cal.App.5th 884, 903-904.  In Bjoin, the plaintiff was a laborer who allegedly developed lung cancer from cutting defendant manufacturer’s asbestos cement pipe with a power saw.  (Bjoin, supra, 113 Cal.App.5th at 888.)  Bjoin rejected the plaintiff’s attempt to expand LiMandri’s “relationship” requirement to include his situation and rejected plaintiff’s claim that Bigler-Engler and Bader expanded the types of relationships that would support a duty to disclose for purposes of fraudulent concealment.  (Id. at 902-903.)  Bjoin acknowledged that Bader expanded the basis for a duty to disclose based on Bigler-Engler but simply disagreed with its holding:  “We recognize that the Bader court did treat Bigler as expanding the basis for a duty to disclose, but it did so without any analysis and we do not find the adoption persuasive.”  (Bjoin, supra, 113 Cal.App.5th at 903.)  Bjoin also stated that even if Bader and Bigler-Engler had expanded the basis for a duty to disclose to include manufacturers who “directly advertised to consumers such as” the plaintiff or “derived any monetary benefit directly” from plaintiff, the plaintiff failed to identify evidence of either.  (Id. at 904 (emphasis added).)

As stated in Bigler-Engler, “a duty to disclose facts arises only when the parties are in a relationship that gives rise to the duty, such as…parties entering into any kind of contractual arrangement.”  (Id. at 311 (quoting Shin v. Kong (2000) 80 Cal.App.4th 498, 509.)  The warranty between Plaintiffs and Defendant is something more than a relationship between Defendant and the public at large. 

Defendant’s interpretation of the direct dealings necessary to give rise to a duty of disclosure is too narrow.  As stated in Bigler-Engler, “a duty to disclose facts arises only when the parties are in a relationship that gives rise to the duty, such as…parties entering into any kind of contractual arrangement.”  (Id. at 311 (quoting Shin v. Kong (2000) 80 Cal.App.4th 498, 509).)  Plaintiffs alleges that Defendant provided them with a warranty for the vehicle.  This a direct dealing between Plaintiffs and Defendant.  Plaintiffs’ and Defendant’s warranty relationship removes Plaintiff from the category of being a member of the public at large, as discussed in Bigler-Engler, supra, 7 Cal.App.5th at 312.

Plaintiffs also argue they sought out and purchased the vehicle based on Defendant’s direct advertising to consumers like them, window stickers and other marketing materials.  (Id. at ¶55.)  Under the standard set forth in Bader, such direct advertisement to consumers combined with Plaintiffs’ reliance on that direct advertisement could satisfy the “some other relationship” requirement under LiMandri.  (Bader, supra, 86 Cal.App.5th at 1132.) 

On reply, Defendant argues Plaintiffs are judicially estopped from asserting that the warranty booklet is an agreement between the parties based on Plaintiffs’ position that it was not an agreement in opposition to the motion to compel arbitration.  Judicial estoppel should “quite clearly should be applied in the following situation: when (1) the same party has taken two positions; (2) the positions were taken in judicial or quasi-judicial administrative proceedings; (3) the party was successful in asserting the first position (i.e., the tribunal adopted the position or accepted it as true); (4) the two positions are totally inconsistent; and (5) the first position was not taken as a result of ignorance, fraud, or mistake.”  (Thomas v. Gordon (2000) 85 Cal.App.4th 113, 118.) 

Plaintiffs’ position is not “totally inconsistent” with their position in opposition to the motion to compel arbitration.  In opposition to the motion to compel arbitration, Plaintiffs asserted that the warranty booklet was not an agreement to arbitrate.  Here, the question is not whether there was a mutually consented to arbitration agreement between the parties sufficient to support an order compelling arbitration.  Here, the question is whether there was “some other relationship” between the parties to support imposition of a duty to disclose the “battery defect” to Plaintiffs prior to their purchase of the vehicle.  The warranty relationship is sufficient to satisfy the requirement that the parties be in “some other relationship” to support a duty of disclosure. 

In addition, the Second District Court of Appeals found that a warranty booklet containing an arbitration provision qualified as an enforceable arbitration agreement sufficient to support a motion to compel arbitration in Kostandian v. American Honda Motor Co. (2026) 120 Cal.App.5th 872, 881 (manufacturer satisfied its burden of establishing existence of applicable arbitration agreement based on arbitration provision contained in warranty booklet and evidence confirming plaintiff’s receipt of warranty booklet).  Kostandian was issued on April 28, 2026. This Court denied Defendant’s prior motion to compel arbitration on April 16, 2026 on grounds that the vehicle’s owner’s handbook was not an enforceable contract.    

Plaintiffs sufficiently allege facts supporting Defendant’s duty to disclose the battery defect.  Defendant’s demurrer based on failure to allege a duty of disclosure is overruled. 

F.  Economic Loss Rule

A “demurrer based on an affirmative defense cannot properly be sustained where the action might be barred by the defense, but is not necessarily barred.”  (CrossTalk Productions, Inc. v. Jacobson (1998) 65 Cal.App.4th 631, 635.)  Defendant demurs to the fraudulent concealment action based on the affirmative defense of the economic loss rule. 

Under the economic loss rule, recovery in negligence is barred for “pure economic losses when such claims would disrupt the parties’ private ordering, rendering contracts less reliable as a means of organizing commercial relationships, and stifle the development of contract law.”  (Sheen v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2022) 12 Cal.5th 905, 915.)  The economic loss rule generally bars tort claims for contract breaches, thereby limiting contracting parties to contract damages.  (Aas v. Superior Court (2000) 24 Cal.4th 627, 643 (“A person may not ordinarily recover in tort for the breach of duties that merely restate contractual obligations.”) As explained in Robinson Helicopter Co., Inc. v. Dana Corp. (2004) 34 Cal.4th 979, “conduct amounting to a breach of contract becomes tortious only when it also violates a duty independent of the contract arising from principles of tort law.” (Robinson Helicopter Co., Inc., supra, 34 Cal.4th at 989.)

The economic loss rule only applies to preclude tort recovery where the harm is purely financial, unaccompanied by any physical or property damage.  “The economic loss rule provides that, in general, there is no recovery in tort for negligently inflicted ‘purely economic losses,’ meaning financial harm unaccompanied by physical or property damage.”  (Dhital v. Nissan North America, Inc. (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 828, 837 (economic loss rule bars recovery for negligently inflicted economic losses “unaccompanied by…property damage”); Erlich v. Menezes (1999) 21 Cal.4th 543, 21 Cal.4th 543, 551 (recognizing long line of cases in which tort damages were allowed for a breach of contract where the conduct resulted in injury to person or property); Robinson Helicopter Co., Inc., supra, 34 Cal.4th at 989-990 (economic loss rule does not bar tort damages where conduct is also a breach of contract where intentional tort is involved, where conduct violates duty independent of contractual duties, or in contract cases where breach of duty directly causes physical injury).)

Defendant argues the economic loss rule bars Plaintiff’s fraudulent concealment claim.  Defendant argues that Plaintiff cannot allege a fraudulent inducement – concealment claim, because there was no damage to physical property or personal injury.  Under such circumstances, Defendant argues the economic loss rule limits Plaintiff to a claim for breach of contract and any fraud claim is barred.

In Dhital, the Court of Appeals addressed the precise issue raised by Defendant’s motion for summary adjudication of the fraudulent inducement by concealment cause of action.  Like Plaintiff in this action, the plaintiff in Dhital alleged “fraudulent inducement – concealment.”  (Dhital, supra, 84 Cal.App.5th at 834.)  The fraud allegations in Dhital were essentially identical to Plaintiff’s allegations in the FAC, alleging that Nissan knew of the transmission defect but failed to disclose that information to plaintiff prior to the sale.  (Id.) 

The Court of Appeals reversed the trial court’s order sustaining demurrer to the fraud claim without leave to amend based on the economic loss rule.  (Id. at 833.)  “Here, the fraudulent inducement exception to the economic loss rule applies. Plaintiffs allege that Nissan, by intentionally concealing facts about the defective transmission, fraudulently induced them to purchase a car.”  (Id. at 838.)  The Court of Appeals explained that the economic loss rule does not apply where “the duty that gives rise to tort liability is either completely independent of the contract or arises from conduct which is both intentional and intended to harm.  [¶]  In our view the independence is present in the case of fraudulent inducement (whether it is achieved by intentional concealment or intentional affirmative misrepresentation), because a defendant’s conduct in fraudulently inducing someone to enter a contract is separate from the defendant’s later breach of the contract or warranty provisions agreed to.”  (Id. at 841.)  

Dhital’s reasoning applies to Defendant’s argument that the economic loss rule bars Plaintiff’s fraudulent inducement – concealment claim.  Defendant fails to distinguish Dhitla.  Instead, Defendant argues Plaintiff’s fraudulent concealment claim fails the test set forth in Rattagan v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1 to determine whether a fraudulent concealment claim is barred by the economic loss rule.  Rattagan is inapposite, because it did not address fraudulent inducement by concealment that occurred prior to entering the subject contract.  Rattagan addressed the following question:  “Can a plaintiff assert an independent claim of fraudulent concealment in the performance of a contract?  The answer to this question is also yes.  A plaintiff may assert a tort claim for fraudulent concealment based on conduct occurring in the course of a contractual relationship, if the elements of the cause of action can be established independently of the parties' contractual rights and obligations and the tortious conduct exposes the plaintiff to a risk of harm beyond the reasonable contemplation of the parties when they entered into the agreement.”  (Rattagan, supra, 17 Cal.5th at 38.) 

Plaintiff is not alleging fraud based on conduct occurring in the course of the parties’ contractual relationship.  Plaintiff is alleging fraudulent inducement – concealment, which is based on conduct prior to the parties entering into their transactional relationship.  The Supreme Court noted in Rattagan that the issues before it were distinct from those raised in Dhital, because the tort claims in Rattagan were “based on alleged conduct committed during the contractual relationship but outside the parties’ chosen rights and obligations.”  (Id. at 41, fn 12.)  Rattagan stated it was not addressing the issues raised in Dhital.  (Id.)  Thus, Plaintiff would not be required to satisfy the requirements set forth in Rattagan as to its fraudulent inducement – concealment cause of action to qualify as an exception to the economic loss rule.  

Defendant fails to establish that the fifth cause of action is barred by the economic loss rule.  Defendant’s demurrer based on the economic loss rule is overruled.    

G. Conclusion

Defendant’s demurrer to the fifth cause of action of the FAC is overruled.

II.  Motion for Summary Adjudication of Complaint

            A.  Defendant’s MSA is moot in light of the FAC

            “Because there is but one complaint in a civil action, the filing of an amended complaint moots a motion directed to a prior complaint.”  (State Compensation Ins. Fund v. Supr. Ct. (2010) 184 Cal.App.4th 1124, 1131.)  “Thus, once an amended complaint is filed, it is error to grant summary adjudication on a cause of action contained in a previous complaint… Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (f), thus contemplates a ‘two-step process—first weighing the sufficiency of the causes of action stated, and then if necessary, determining whether there are triable issues underlying those causes.’”  (Id.)

Defendant’s motion for summary adjudication seeks judgment on Plaintiff’s fifth cause of action for fraudulent concealment in Plaintiffs’ complaint, as well as Plaintiff’s request for punitive damages and Plaintiff Nuris Clinkscales’ standing.  Defendant’s motion was filed before Plaintiffs filed their FAC.  Defendant’s motion for summary adjudication is therefore technically moot.  The pleading to which it was directed is no longer operative.  This is particularly true in light of Defendant’s arguments that fifth cause of action alleged in the original complaint is “factually devoid.”  (MSA, 4:25-29-5:18.) 

Defendant’s motion for summary adjudication is denied as moot due to the filing of the FAC.

B.  In addition, Defendant fails to satisfy its burden as moving party on the motion for summary adjudication of the fifth cause of action and the punitive damages claim

            i.  Applicable Law

The purpose of a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication “is to provide courts with a mechanism to cut through the parties’ pleadings in order to determine whether, despite their allegations, trial is in fact necessary to resolve their dispute.” (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) “Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (c), requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.) 

“In ruling on the motion, the court must consider all of the evidence and all of the inferences reasonably drawn therefrom [citation] and must view such evidence [citations] and such inferences [citations] in the light most favorable to the opposing party.” (Aguilar, supra, at pp. 844-845 [quotation marks omitted].) 

“On a motion for summary judgment, the initial burden is always on the moving party to make a prima facie showing that there are no triable issues of material fact.” (Scalf v. D. B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1519.) “A defendant or cross-defendant has met his or her burden of showing that a cause of action has no merit if the party has shown that one or more elements of the cause of action, even if not separately pleaded, cannot be established, or that there is a complete defense to the cause of action.  Once the defendant or cross-defendant has met that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff or cross-complainant to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.  The plaintiff or cross-complainant shall not rely upon the allegations or denials of its pleadings to show that a triable issue of material fact exists but, instead, shall set forth the specific facts showing that a triable issue of material fact exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 437c, subd. (p)(2).)

 Unless and until a defendant meets that burden, the plaintiff has no burden to present controverting evidence. (Consumer Cause, Inc. v. SmileCare (2001) 91 Cal.App.4th 454, 468 [“There is no obligation on the opposing party . . . to establish anything by affidavit unless and until the moving party has by affidavit stated facts establishing every element . . . necessary to sustain a judgment in his favor.”].) If a defendant fails to meet that burden, summary judgment must be denied, even if the plaintiff fails to file an opposition and fails to proffer any evidence.

On the other hand, if the defendant “has met that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff . . . to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).)  The plaintiff may not merely rely on allegations or denials in its pleadings to show that a triable issue of material fact exists, but instead “shall set forth the specific facts showing that a triable issue of material fact exists as to the cause of action.”  (Id.)  To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence.  (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)  “If the plaintiff cannot do so, summary judgment should be granted.” (Avivi v. Centro Medico Urgente Medical Center (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 463, 467.)   

ii.  Defendant fails to satisfy its burden as moving party as to the fifth cause of action based on lack of evidence

Lack of evidence. Defendant moves for summary adjudication of the fifth cause of action on grounds that Plaintiffs have not provided evidence and no evidence exists to support each of the elements of the fraudulent concealment claim.  Defendant also argues there is no basis to impose a duty of disclosure on it, because it was not a party to the sales transaction.

As the moving party, Defendants have the initial burden of either presenting evidence affirmatively rebutting Plaintiff’s allegations or evidence that Plaintiff lacks evidence on an essential element of the claim.  (Union Bank v. Supr. Ct. (1995) 31 Cal.App.4th 574, 590.  For this reason, a defendant does not meet its burden under CCP §437(c)(o)(1) by merely “pointing out” or “arguing” that the plaintiff does not possess or cannot reasonably obtain necessary evidence.  (Id.; Lona v. Citibank, N.A. (2011) 202 Cal.App.4th 89 (defendant bank did not meet its burden on summary judgment of wrongful foreclosure by merely arguing that plaintiff lacked evidence to support her claim and only documentary evidence submitted were the loan documents); Gaggero v. Yura (2003) 108 Cal.App.4th 884, 890 (defendant did not establish plaintiff’s lack of evidence or inability to reasonably obtain evidence of plaintiff’s ability and willingness to perform by pointing to plaintiff’s refusal to answer certain questions during deposition on grounds of a meritless privacy objection; such a refusal, regardless of its merit, was neither an admission nor a factually devoid discovery response).)

A defendant may rely upon a plaintiff’s factually devoid responses to establish that plaintiff does not possess and cannot reasonably obtain necessary evidence.  (Union Bank, supra, 31 Cal.App.4th at 590.)  “Circumstantial evidence supporting a defendant's summary judgment motion can consist of factually devoid discovery responses from which an absence of evidence can be inferred, but the burden should not shift without stringent review of the direct, circumstantial and inferential evidence.”  (Andrews v. Foster Wheeler LLC (2006) 138 Cal.App.4th 96, 101.)

In Union Bank, plaintiff sued defendants for various fraud claims. Defendants moved for summary judgment based on plaintiff’s responses to three RFAs and the accompanying Form Rog 17.1. Plaintiff refused to admit that defendants (i) did not commit fraud against Plaintiff or (ii) that Defendants did not conspire to defraud Plaintiff.  (Id. at 578.)  In connection with Form Rog 17.1 plaintiff could cite to no evidence or facts supporting their refusal to admit either of these facts.  (Id.)  Compounding the damaging effect of these factually devoid responses was plaintiff’s admission that defendant “took no inappropriate action in connection with its role in the transactions.”  (Id.)  The combination of these facts sufficiently met defendant’s moving burden.  (Id. at 592.)  Due to plaintiff’s admission that defendants did not engage in any “inappropriate action,” plaintiff failed to raise a triable issue of fact based on evidence that potentially raised a reasonable inference of fraud.  (Id. at 593.) 

Defendant submits evidence that it was not a party to the sales transaction.  (Defendant’s Table of Contents, Kurtz Dec., ¶5; Goel Dec., ¶7.)  Plaintiffs do not allege that Defendant was a party to the sales transaction.  Defendant’s evidence that it was not a party to the sales transaction is therefore immaterial.  As discussed in connection with Defendant’s demurrer to the FAC, Defendant’s alleged duty of disclosure based on the parties’ warranty agreement, Defendant’s advertisements that Plaintiffs relied upon to purchase the vehicle and Defendant’s superior and exclusive knowledge of the Battery Defect.  In combination, Plaintiffs allege a duty of disclosure based on these facts and their relationship to Defendant is distinct from that of Defendant’s relationship to the public at large.  (Bigler-Engler, supra, 7 Cal.App.5th at 312 (transaction sufficient to support duty of disclosure must involve “direct dealings between the plaintiff and the defendant; it cannot arise between the defendant and the public at large”).)    The declarations of Kurtz and Goel do not negate any of these material allegations.

Defendant also states that Plaintiffs have no evidence that it “intentionally concealed a ‘battery defect’ in their 2024 Hyundai Ioniq 6” and “that it did not public or privately disclose any information about alleged battery defects.”  (Defendant’s SSUMF Nos. 7 and 8.)  In support of these material facts, Defendant only cites generally to the entirety of Kurtz’s declaration and the original complaint.  (Id. at Nos. 7-11.) 

Again, the original complaint has been superseded and its purported insufficiency cannot be relied upon to satisfy Defendant’s evidentiary burden.  Kurtz does not attest to Plaintiffs’ failure to produce evidence in response to discovery, any factually devoid discovery responses or any circumstances that would indicate Plaintiffs’ inability to reasonably obtain evidence of these allegations.  Defendant therefore fails to satisfy its burden as moving party based on Plaintiffs’ inability to establish the essential elements of their claim.

Economic loss rule.  Defendant also moves for adjudication of the fifth cause of action based on the economic loss rule.  As discussed in connection with Defendant’s demurrer to the FAC, the Court finds the economic loss rule does not bar Plaintiffs’ fraudulent concealment claim. 

iii.  Defendant fails to satisfy its burden as moving party on the punitive damages claim

            Defendant moves for summary adjudication of the punitive damages claim on grounds that Plaintiffs have not provided any evidence of ratification by Hyundai of the fraud.  Defendant moves for summary adjudication of the punitive damages claim because it is based on the fraud claim, which fails as a matter of law.

            As with the fraudulent concealment cause of action, Defendant relies entirely on the superseded original complaint and Kurtz’s declaration.  For the same reasons stated in connection with the fraudulent concealment claim, neither of these documents establishes that Defendant did not ratify the alleged fraudulent conduct, nor do they negate Plaintiffs’ allegations of fraudulent concealment.  The fraud claim also survives the motion for summary adjudication and is not grounds to grant adjudication of the punitive damages claim. 

            C.  While Defendant satisfies its burden as moving party on the claims brought by Nuris Clinkscales, she raises a triable issue of material fact

            Defendant moves for summary adjudication of all claims brought by Nuris Clinkscales, because she lacks standing. Defendant argues Nuris Clinkscales did not purchase the vehicle and did not suffer any harm.

            Defendant satisfies its burden on the issue of Nuris Clinkscales’ standing.  Defendant submits a copy of the Purchase Agreement, which is only signed by Keith Clinkscales.  (Defendant’s Table of Contents, Kurtz Dec., Ex. B; Goel Dec., Ex. B.) 

            In response, Plaintiffs submit evidence that Plaintiffs are husband and wife and the vehicle was purchased during marriage with community funds.  (Duane Dec., Ex. E, 7:24; K. Clinkscales Dec., ¶¶3-4.)  Plaintiffs therefore establish Nuris’s present, existing and equal ownership of the vehicle pursuant to Family Code sections 751 and 760.  (Fam. Code, §§ 751 (“respective interests of each spouse in community property during continuance of the marriage relation are present, existing, and equal interests”) and 760.)  Plaintiff Nuris Clinkscales therefore raises a triable issue of fact as to her standing to sue for violations of the Song Beverly Act and breach of warranty.  Defendant’s motion for summary adjudication of the complaint as to Plaintiff Nuris Clinkscales is denied. 

            D.  Evidentiary Objections

            Plaintiffs’ Objections—Sustain as to Objection Nos. 1, 4, 5, 6.

E.  Request for Judicial Notice

Plaintiffs’ Request for Judicial Notice is denied.  Documents issued by Hyundai do not qualify as records judicially noticeable under Evidence Code section 452 subdivisions (c) and (h).

            G.  Conclusion

Defendant’s Motion for Summary Adjudication of the Complaint is denied.  The motion is moot due to the FAC filed on June 16, 2026.  The motion is also denied as to the fifth cause of action for fraudulent concealment and punitive damages based on Defendant’s failure to establish Plaintiff’s lack of evidence on an essential element of either claim.  The motion is denied as to Nuris Clinkscales’ claims because triable issues of fact remain as to her standing to sue. 

CONCLUSION

Defendant’s Demurrer to the Fifth Cause of Action of the FAC is overruled.  Defendant to answer in 20 days.

Defendant’s Motion for Summary Adjudication of the Complaint is denied.

DISCUSSION

Applicable Law

A party may move for an order compelling a further response to interrogatories where: (1) an answer to a particular interrogatory is evasive or incomplete; (2) an exercise of the option to produce documents under CCP § 2030.230 is unwarranted or the required specification of those documents is inadequate; or (3) an objection is without merit or is too general. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subds. (a)(1)-(3).) A motion to compel further responses to interrogatories “shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (b)(1).) The court must impose monetary sanctions in connection with a motion to compel further responses to interrogatories “unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (d).)

On receipt of a response to a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling, the demanding party may move for an order compelling further response to the demand if the demanding party deems that any of the following apply: (1) a statement of compliance with the demand is incomplete; (2) a representation of inability to comply is inadequate, incomplete, or evasive; or (3) an objection to the response is without merit or too general. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (a)(1)-(3).) A motion brought pursuant to Code Civ. Proc. § 2031.310 “shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(2).) The court must impose monetary sanctions in connection with a motion to compel further responses to requests for production of documents “unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (d).) ¿ 

Analysis

A.    The Untimely Further Responses of Plaintiff

Initially, the Court notes that Plaintiff has now served verified further responses to the

discovery at issue. (See Declarations of Paul Peterson Filed August 21, 2026 ¶ 3; see also Declaration of Paul Peterson Filed August 25, 2026 ¶ 3.)

            In the omnibus reply, Defendant argues that “Plaintiff’s belated service of discovery responses after [Defendant] incurred the expense of preparing and filing the motions does not preclude an award of monetary sanctions.” (Reply Filed August 25, 2026 at p. 2:7-8.)

            Given that Plaintiff has now served verified further responses to the discovery at issue, and Defendant does not take issue with the sufficiency of such responses, the requests to compel further responses to form interrogatories, special interrogatories, and request for production, set one are DENIED as MOOT.

B.     Monetary Sanctions

Defendant requests the imposition of monetary sanctions in the sum of $1,500.00 per motion against counsel for Plaintiff. (See Declarations of Alina Mooradian Filed June 23, 2026 (“Mooradian Decls.”) ¶ 7.) Counsel spent 1.5 hours drafting each of the Motions and anticipates spending 1.5 hours drafting the replies and attending the hearing. (Id.) Counsel has an hourly rate of $500.00 per hour. (Id.)

While the Court recognizes that the failure of Plaintiff to provide timely further discovery responses warranted the Motions (See Mooradian Decls. ¶¶ 4-6), the Court denies the request for monetary sanctions. The Court notes that defense counsel only sent a meet and confer letter via e-mail to Plaintiff’s counsel concerning the purportedly deficient discovery responses. (Id. ¶ 5, Ex. C.) Defendant was required to meet and confer “either in person, by telephone, or by videoconference.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2016.040, subd. (a).) Meeting and conferring via letter is insufficient. Defendant has not satisfied the meet and confer requirement as to any of the Motions.

Due to the failure of defense counsel to properly meet and confer, the Court DENIES the request for monetary sanctions as to each of the Motions.

CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, the Motions are DENIED.

The requests to compel further responses to set one of Defendant’s form interrogatories, special interrogatories, and request for production are DENIED as MOOT.

The request for monetary sanctions as to each of the Motions is DENIED.

            In opposition, Plaintiffs maintain the motions are moot.  Plaintiffs served further responses on July 20, 2026 and verifications on August 11, 2026.  (Oppositions to MTCF to RFAs and RFPs, Abdo Dec., ¶3; Oppositions to MTCG Special Interrogatories and Form Interrogatories, Serri Dec., ¶3.)  Plaintiffs attach their supplemental responses to the RFPs to their opposition to the motion to compel further responses to the RFPs, but they do not provide the Court with their supplemental responses to the form and special interrogatories or the RFAs.  (Opposition to MTCF to RFPs, Abdo Dec., ¶5; Opposition to MTCF to RFAs, Abdo Dec.;  Oppositions to MTCF Special Interrogatories and Form Interrogatories, Serri Dec. 

As discussed in Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pacific Healthcare Consultants (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, the Court has several options if untimely responses are served once a motion to compel is filed.  “The trial court might compel responses without objection if it finds no legally valid responses have been provided to one or more interrogatories; it might deny the motion to compel responses as essentially unnecessary, in whole or in part, and just impose sanctions; it might treat the motion as one under section 2030.300 and either determine that further answers are required, or order the propounding party to “meet and confer” (§ 2030.300, subd. (b)) and file a separate statement (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1020(a)(2), (c)); or it might take the motion off calendar, thereby requiring the propounding party to file a motion under section 2030.300 [statute governing motions to compel further responses to interrogatories].”  (Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc., supra, 148 Cal.App.4th at 409.)

In light of the further responses served on July 20, 2026 and verified on August 11, 2026, the substantive request that Plaintiffs be ordered to serve further responses is moot.  The motions to compel further responses are denied as moot. 

C.  Defendant’s meet and confer efforts were insufficient

Plaintiffs also argue the meet and confer attempts were insufficient.  “A meet and confer declaration in support of a motion shall state facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt, either in person, by telephone, or by videoconference, to informally resolve each issue presented by the motion.”  (Code Civ. Proc., §2016.040, subd. (a).)  Defendant sent a single meet and confer letter on May 21, 2026 and did not follow up prior to filing the motions to compel on June 29, 2026 and July 2, 2026.  Defendant’s motions are properly denied on this alternative ground.

D.  Sanctions are denied

Defendant’s requests for sanctions, however, are still at issue.  Plaintiffs did not serve further responses until after the motions were filed.  However, as Plaintiffs’ point out Defendant did not meet and confer as required by section 2016.040.  In fact, the failure to meet and confer as required is grounds to impose sanctions on Defendant.  (Code of Civ. Proc., §2023.020.)  For this reason, Defendant’s request for sanctions is denied because Plaintiffs acted with substantial justification. 

CONCLUSION

            Defendant’s Motions to Compel Further Responses to (1) Requests for Admission (Set One), (2) Special Interrogatories (Set One), (3) Form Interrogatories (Set One) and (4) Requests for Production (Set One) are denied as moot.  Defendant’s requests for sanction are denied.  Plaintiffs acted with substantial justification in light of Defendant’s failure to adequately meet and confer.

Case Number: 25LBCV03081    Hearing Date: September 24, 2026    Dept: S28

BACKGROUND

On November 10, 2025, Plaintiff Mindy Noelle Grasska Friedman (“Plaintiff” or “Friedman”) filed a complaint against Andrea Wieck, Jasmyn Ruthie Rachelle Wieck, Jason Wieck, and Does 1 through 10 (collectively, “Defendants”) for motor vehicle and general negligence.  The action arises out of an alleged automobile collision that took place on November 16, 2023, in Long Beach, California.  Plaintiff alleges physical injuries and seeks damages that include medical expenses, wage loss, loss of earning capacity, pain and suffering, and physical limitations affecting aspects of her daily life.

Defendants filed an Answer on February 13, 2026.

On July 6, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant Motion to Quash and/or Modify Subpoenas and for Protective Order (“Motion”).  Defendants filed an Opposition on September 11, 2026, and Plaintiff filed a Reply on September 16, 2026.

DISCUSSION

Applicable Law

Code of Civil Procedure section 1987.1, subdivision (a), provides,

(a) If a subpoena requires the attendance of a witness or the production of books, documents, electronically stored information, or other things before a court, or at the trial of an issue therein, or at the taking of a deposition, the court, upon motion reasonably made by any person described in subdivision (b), or upon the court's own motion after giving counsel notice and an opportunity to be heard, may make an order quashing the subpoena entirely, modifying it, or directing compliance with it upon those terms or conditions as the court shall declare, including protective orders.  In addition, the court may make any other order as may be appropriate to protect the person from unreasonable or oppressive demands, including unreasonable violations of the right of privacy of the person.

Analysis

Defendants issued three subpoenas—one for medical records, one for billing records, and one for films or radiology records—to each of the following six providers: Women’s Advantage Men’s Optimal Health Physical Therapy, MemorialCare Medical Group, Wave Imaging Palo Verde, Constance Shin, LMFT, Mission Advanced Pain Management & Spine Center, and Beverly Radiology Medical Group, for a total of eighteen subpoenas.  The subpoenas generally cover the period from November 16, 2013 to the present and seek categories of records pertaining to Plaintiff.

First, Plaintiff requests that the three subpoenas directed to Constance Shin, LMFT, be quashed in their entirety because Plaintiff has not placed her mental health at issue in this litigation, rendering these psychotherapeutic records strictly privileged.  Second, Plaintiff requests that the remaining fifteen (15) subpoenas be modified to strike the overbroad language and expressly limit the scope of production to the physical injuries identified in paragraph 6 of the concurrently filed Declaration of Mindy Friedman, which states the physical injuries at issue in this case.  Third, Plaintiff asks the Court to order the parties to submit the Stipulation and Protective Order attached as Exhibit 2 to the Declaration of Michael Schachter, based on the Los Angeles Model Protective Order, and that any records produced in response to the subpoenas at issue in this motion be subject to that protective order.

Plaintiff states that on June 16, 2026, and July 5, 2026, she produced all medical records and documents relating to medical expenses from the date of the incident to the present that relate to the specific physical injuries she sustained as a direct result of the subject incident.  Plaintiff states that the incident caused serious physical injuries that significantly affect her daily life, including severe neck pain with documented cervical spine conditions, ongoing left-shoulder pain that limits basic physical tasks, debilitating dizziness and headaches that have required multiple medical visits, and persistent fatigue related to her neck injury.  Plaintiff does not seek damages for mental health treatment.

Plaintiff’s counsel repeatedly sent Defendants a proposed protective order in late May and early June 2026, but Defendants declined to stipulate or narrow their subpoenas.  Shortly afterward, on June 12, 2026, Defendants served eighteen subpoenas on six medical and mental-health providers using identical boilerplate language seeking all records from 2013 to the present without any subject-matter limitation.

Plaintiff states that her medical records, including documents related to her accident injuries, contain highly private information about unrelated conditions, including sensitive pregnancy-related details and family medical history.  Such unrelated information routinely appears within records addressing her incident-related injuries, which is why she redacted non-incident-related material before producing her records.

In their Opposition, Defendants argue that the subpoenas are necessary because Plaintiff’s own production was incomplete, filtered, and withheld key information.  Plaintiff’s ongoing treatment meant her production could not be comprehensive, later records were added, and her claims summary removed items she deemed unrelated.  Her interrogatory responses also reserved or withheld substantive details about injuries, symptoms, treatment, prior complaints, and economic losses.  Defendants contend provider-direct subpoenas are needed to verify completeness, obtain authenticated records, review the full treatment history, evaluate medical findings, confirm billing information, and obtain full imaging.

Moreover, Defendants argue that Plaintiff’s prelitigation submissions to Defendants’ insurer are irrelevant to the present discovery dispute as these submissions are not a substitute for formal discovery and may be incomplete and unauthenticated.  Nor does an insurer’s fault determination control Defendants’ litigation rights or limit their ability to conduct discovery. 

Defendants also argue that the subpoenas cover a defined ten-year period before the incident and seek records regarding the onset, cause, or aggravation of the injuries at issue, especially given Plaintiff’s degenerative cervical and thoracic findings and other potential contributing factors reflected in her records.  Pre-incident records may be relevant because Plaintiff refused to say whether prior similar complaints existed and declined to produce any such records.  If overbreadth is a concern, the proper remedy is narrowing the subpoenas, not quashing them entirely.

Finally, Defendants maintain that any privacy concerns can be addressed with “reasonable subject-matter limitations” or “targeted redactions” as they do not seek public dissemination of Plaintiff’s medical information.  (Opposition, pp. 4-5).

In her Reply, Plaintiff argues that Defendants concede many of Plaintiff’s arguments.  They agree that the subpoenas should be modified rather than enforced as written, acknowledge that the ten-year timeframe can be reasonably narrowed, and confirm that ordinary pain and suffering does not waive psychotherapist privilege.  They also state that privacy concerns can be addressed through subject-matter limits, redactions, or in camera review.  These concessions align with Plaintiff’s request to modify the fifteen subpoenas.  Moreover, despite claiming they do not seek public disclosure of Plaintiff’s medical information, Defendants filed Plaintiff’s medical records on the public docket without redaction or a sealing request, underscoring the need for the protective order they previously refused.  Plaintiff is not seeking to bar discovery from her providers but rather opposing Defendants’ attempt to obtain her entire medical history.  With a protective order in place, provider-direct production would give Defendants the complete, unredacted records they claim to need.  Defendants also omit that they deposed Plaintiff on August 19, 2026, questioning her extensively about her injuries, treatment, imaging, surgical recommendations, medications, and functional limitations.

A.    Subpoenas Directed to Constance Shin, LMFT

The three subpoenas directed to Constance Shin, LMFT seek Plaintiff’s mental health and psychotherapeutic records.  (Schachter Decl., Exs. 12-14.) 

Under Evidence Code section 1014, confidential communications between a patient and a psychotherapist are absolutely privileged.  However, the privilege is waived “concerning the mental or emotional condition of the patient if such issue has been tendered by” the patient.  (Evid. Code, §1016.)  Here, Plaintiff does not seek mental health treatment damages and expressly states in her declaration that she is not seeking damages for mental health treatment in this case.  The billing subpoena to Shin also seeks diagnostic codes, procedure codes, and dates/frequency of psychotherapy, but Defendants offer no showing of relevance connecting dates or frequency of counseling to Plaintiff’s physical injuries.  Moreover, diagnoses are encompassed in the definition of “confidential communication between patient and psychotherapist.”  (Evid. Code, § 1012.)  Thus, section 1016 does not apply and the privilege is not waived.

Defendants argue that Plaintiff should clarify whether she intends to claim any psychological or emotional impairment.  If she does, limited discovery into that condition would be appropriate; if she does not, her psychotherapy communications should remain protected, with only relevant non-privileged administrative information discoverable.  They also ask that Plaintiff not be permitted to later add mental-health damages without reopening related discovery.

The Court finds the subpoenas improper.  Plaintiff’s mental health is not at issue and all of the psychotherapy communications, including diagnostic information, remain privileged.  The Motion to Quash the three subpoenas directed to Constance Shin, LMFT is GRANTED.  The Court does not grant Defendants’ request, made in the Opposition, for an order prospectively precluding Plaintiff from altering her damages claims as Defendants provide no basis or authority for this request.

B.     Subpoenas to Physical Medical Providers

The fifteen subpoenas at issue seek all medical, billing, and radiology records without subject-matter limitation.  According to Code of Civil Procedure section 2020.410, subdivision (a), “[a] deposition subpoena that commands only the production of business records for copying shall designate the business records to be produced either by specifically describing each individual item or by reasonably particularizing each category of item.”  A blanket demand for documents may properly be quashed when its breadth makes it a fishing expedition.  (People v. Serrata (1976) 62 Cal.App.3d 9, 15 (1976).)  Here, the subpoenas are overbroad.

Moreover, California’s Constitution expressly grants a right to privacy that includes the protection of medical information.  (Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 552; Hill v. National Collegiate Athletic Assn. (1994) 7 Cal. 4th 1, 35 (“Legally recognized privacy interests…[include] interests in precluding the dissemination or misuse of sensitive and confidential information.”)  “A person's medical profile is an area of privacy infinitely more intimate, more personal in quality and nature than many areas already judicially recognized and protected.”  (Board of Medical Quality Assurance v. Gherardini (1979) 93 Cal.App.3d 669, 678, overruled in part on other grounds.)

Plaintiff has shown that her medical records— even those relating to claimed incident-related injuries—contain unrelated private information, such as sensitive pregnancy-related details and family medical history.  Defendants declined Plaintiff’s early request for a protective order governing these records.

Defendants contend that any modification should allow discovery of records relating to all conditions, diagnoses, symptoms, and contributing factors reflected in Plaintiff’s records, while still excluding unrelated medical information.

In her Reply, Plaintiff proposes the following modification to the subpoenas:

Production shall be limited to records concerning the diagnosis, treatment, or evaluation of the following conditions and anatomical regions: the cervical spine, thoracic spine, neck, left shoulder, and left upper extremity, including cervicalgia, cervical spondylosis, central canal and foraminal stenosis, disc protrusion and disc bulge, cervical and thoracic radiculopathy, and associated neurologic symptoms; and dizziness, headaches, and fatigue.

As to those conditions and regions only, production shall include the related examination and progress notes, diagnostic imaging and reports, medications, treatment rendered, prognosis, surgical recommendations, billing and payment records, and any documented physical restrictions arising from those conditions. This limitation does not extend to records concerning obstetric, gynecologic, prenatal, postpartum, reproductive, hormonal, or mental health care, or to any condition not listed above.

(Reply, p. 6.)

            The Court finds that the proper remedy is to modify the subpoenas.  Here, Plaintiff has established a legally protected interest and Defendant has raised an important countervailing interest in obtaining the information for purposes of litigation.  (See Williams, 3 Cal. 5th at 552.)  The Court must balance these competing considerations.  (Ibid.)  The Court grants Plaintiff’s request to impose subject matter limitations on the subpoenas and rejects an unrestricted category for alternative or contributing causes proposed by Defendants as it is not tied to a claimed condition or anatomical region and would reintroduce the same catch-all defect that requires modification.  The Court also declines to authorize discovery into all medical conditions that might affect parenting, childcare, employment, or activities of daily living.

            Accordingly, the fifteen subpoenas shall be modified to limit the subject matter at issue and preclude production of records concerning obstetric, gynecologic, prenatal, postpartum, reproductive, hormonal, or mental health care.  The Court declines to include an untethered category for “alternative or contributing causes” as an independent subject; causation and aggravation discovery is preserved as limited to the defined anatomical regions and conditions above, which is sufficient for relevance and proportionality.

C.    Protective Order

Plaintiff argues that even when properly narrowed, her medical records will inevitably contain highly sensitive, unrelated medical information related to her pregnancy and family medical history.  Although Plaintiff’s counsel has attempted to obtain Defendants’ stipulation to a standard Los Angeles Model Protective Order, Defendants have refused.  Plaintiff requests that the Court order the parties to submit the Stipulation and Protective Order, attached as Exhibit 2 to Michael Schachter’s declaration, and order that any records produced in response to the subpoenas at issue in this Motion be subject to that protective order.

Defendants argue Plaintiff has not shown good cause for a protective order.  They note that a court must balance interests before restricting dissemination and that the party seeking protection bears the burden of demonstrating specific harm.  Plaintiff’s assertion that her records contain “highly private, sensitive information” about unrelated conditions is viewed as conclusory, lacking detail about any actual risk of annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression.  Defendants therefore contend these statements do not justify a blanket protective order.

As noted above, Defendants publicly filed Plaintiff’s medical records—Exhibits E through H—without redaction, sealing, or in-camera review.  These records revealed diagnoses, potential surgical plans, reproductive-health information unrelated to the case, and notes about Plaintiff’s mental state.  Because Defendants placed this sensitive information on the public docket while the motion was pending, the risk of further disclosure will continue without a protective order.

The Court finds that Plaintiff has shown that records addressing the physical injuries at issue may include sensitive information about unrelated medical conditions, pregnancy, family members, procedures, and medications.  Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1987.1, the Court may condition subpoena compliance on a protective order and may take measures necessary to prevent unreasonable invasions of privacy.  

The Court GRANTS Plaintiff’s request for a protective order.

CONCLUSION

Plaintiff Mindy Noelle Grasska Friedman’s Motion to Quash and/or Modify Subpoenas and for Protective Order is GRANTED IN PART as follows:

The medical-records, billing-records, and films or radiology subpoenas directed to Constance Shin, LMFT are QUASHED in their entirety.

The 15 subpoenas directed to Women’s Advantage Men’s Optimal Health Physical Therapy, MemorialCare Medical Group, Wave Imaging Palo Verde, Mission Advanced Pain Management & Spine Center, and Beverly Radiology Medical Group are MODIFIED as follows:

·         Production shall be limited to records concerning the diagnosis, treatment, evaluation, or documented history of the following conditions and anatomical regions: the cervical spine, thoracic spine, neck, left shoulder, and left upper extremity, including cervicalgia, cervical spondylosis, central-canal or foraminal stenosis, disc protrusion or disc bulge, cervical or thoracic radiculopathy, and associated neurologic symptoms; and dizziness, headaches, and fatigue.  As to those conditions and anatomical regions only, production may include examination and progress notes, prior similar complaints, diagnostic imaging and reports, medications, treatment rendered, prognosis, surgical recommendations, billing and payment records, and documented physical restrictions arising from those conditions.  Records concerning a possible alternative or contributing cause may be produced only to the extent the record expressly connects that cause to one or more of the conditions or anatomical regions identified above.

·         Excluded matters. Production shall not include records concerning unrelated obstetric, gynecologic, prenatal, postpartum, reproductive, hormonal, or mental-health care, except that a discrete portion of an otherwise responsive record may be produced if it expressly connects such a matter to a condition or anatomical region identified in the previous paragraph.  Any production under this exception shall be narrowly limited to the expressly stated connection and shall otherwise be redacted.

·         The subpoenas remain limited to records dated from November 16, 2013 through the date of production.

Within 10 days of the Court’s order, the parties must submit a stipulated protective order substantially in the form of Exhibit 2 to the Schachter Declaration, with any mutually agreed revisions, for signature.  All records produced in response to the modified subpoenas shall be designated and treated as Confidential under that order unless otherwise agreed or ordered.  No party shall publicly file medical records produced pursuant to these subpoenas without redacting information excluded by this Order or obtaining an appropriate court order governing the filing.


Case Number: 25LBCV03441    Hearing Date: September 24, 2026    Dept: S28

BACKGROUND

On December 18, 2025, Plaintiff Vien Ho (“Plaintiff”), in propria persona, commenced this action against HSBC Bank USA, National Association (“HSBC”), Affinia Default Services, LLC (“Affinia”), Nationstar Mortgage LLC dba Mr. Cooper (“Nationstar”), Mortgage Electronic Registration Systems, Inc. (“MERS”), ServiceLink Agency Sales and Posting, LLC[1] (“ServiceLink”), and Does 1 through 20 (collectively, “Defendants”), asserting causes of action for: (1) wrongful foreclosure; (2) failure to provide statutory notice; (3) cancellation of trustee’s deed upon sale; (4) quiet title; (5) slander of title; (6) fraud/constructive fraud; (7) violation of Civil Code section 2924; (8) declaratory relief; (9) unjust enrichment/constructive trust; (10) void foreclosure; (11) void credit bid; (12) lack of recorded assignment; (13) rescission of foreclosure sale; (14) wrongful eviction; (15) accounting; (16) negligence per se; and (17) judicial estoppel.

On March 24, 2026, Defendant ServiceLink filed a demurrer to the Complaint.  On April 28, 2026, the Court overruled the demurrer on the basis of res judicata, but sustained the demurrer to the first, second, fourth, fifth, eighth, and thirteenth causes of action with 30 days leave to amend.

On May 27, 2026, Plaintiff filed the operative First Amended Complaint (“FAC”).

On July 24, 2026, ServiceLink filed the instant Demurrer to the FAC (“Demurrer”).  Plaintiff filed an Opposition on September 14, 2026, and Defendant filed a Reply on September 17, 2026.

DISCUSSION

Applicable Law

A demurrer is a pleading that may be used to test the legal sufficiency of the factual allegations in the complaint.  (Code Civ. Proc., §430.10.)  There are two types of demurrers: general demurrers and special demurrers.  (See McKenney v. Purepac Pharmaceutical Co. (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 72, 77.)

General demurrers can be used to attack pleadings for failure to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action or for lack of subject matter jurisdiction.  (Code Civ. Proc., §430.10, subd. (e); McKenney, 167 Cal.App.4th at 77.)  Such demurrers can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable; evidence or extrinsic matters are not considered.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§430.30, 430.70; Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318; Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)  For the purpose of testing the sufficiency of the cause of action, the Court admits “all material facts properly pleaded” and “matters which may be judicially noticed,” but does not consider contentions, deductions, or conclusions of fact or law. (Blank, 39 Cal.3d at 318.)  It gives these facts “a reasonable interpretation, reading it as a whole and its parts in their context.”  (Ibid.)  At the pleading stage, a plaintiff need only allege ultimate facts sufficient to apprise the defendant of the factual basis for the claim against him.  (Semole v. Sansoucie (1972) 28 Cal. App. 3d 714, 721.)  The face of the complaint includes exhibits attached to the complaint.  (Frantz v. Blackwell (1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 91, 94.) "If facts appearing in the exhibits contradict those alleged, the facts in the exhibits take precedence."  (Holland v. Morse Diesel Internat., Inc. (2001) 86 Cal.App.4th 1443, 1447.)

Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment.  (Goodman v. Kennedy¿(1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349.)  The burden is on the Plaintiff to show the Court that a pleading can be amended successfully.  (Lewis v.¿Youtube, LLC¿(2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.)

Request for Judicial Notice

1.      Deed of Trust recorded July 3, 2007, as Document No. 20071586415 in Official Records, Recorder’s Office, Los Angeles County, California.

2.      Notice of Default recorded October 31, 2019, as Document No. 20191174126 in Official Records, Recorder’s Office, Los Angeles County, California.

3.      Corporation Assignment of Deed of Trust recorded November 24, 2010, as Document No. 2010710501 in Official Records, Recorder’s Office, Los Angeles County, California.

4.      Grant Deed recorded October 21, 2021, as Document No. 20211587558 in Official Records, Recorder’s Office, Los Angeles County, California.

5.      Notice of Trustee’s Sale recorded September 28, 2021, as Document No. 20211471386 in Official Records, Recorder’s Office, Los Angeles County, California.

6.      Trustee’s Deed Upon Sale recorded December 7, 2022, as Document No. 20221144711 in Official Records, Recorder’s Office, Los Angeles County, California.

7.      Warranty Deed recorded December 27, 2022, as Document No. 20221199975 in Official Records, Recorder’s Office, Los Angeles County, California.

8.      April 28, 2026 Minute Order issued in this action regarding Moving Party’s Demurrer to Plaintiff’s initial complaint.

The Court GRANTS these requests, as to their existence and recording, as these are documents from the Official Records of the County of Los Angeles, State of California, and from this Court.  Evidence Code section 452, subdivision (h) allows the Court to take judicial notice of “[f]acts and propositions that are not reasonably subject to dispute and are capable of immediate and accurate determination by resort to sources of reasonably indisputable accuracy.” However, the Court does not accept the truth or legal effect of their contents, as that is a disputed factual issue that cannot be resolved on demurrer.

Analysis

A.    Meet and Confer

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41 requires that “[b]efore filing a demurrer pursuant to this chapter, the demurring party shall meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer.”  (Code Civ. Proc., §430.41, subd. (a).)  The parties are to meet and confer at least five days before the date the responsive pleading is due.  (Code Civ. Proc., §430.41, subd. (a)(2).)  Thereafter, the demurring party shall file and serve a declaration stating either (1) the means by which the parties met and conferred and that the parties did not reach an agreement to resolve the issues raised in the demurrer or (2) the party that filed the pleading failed to respond to the meet and confer request or otherwise failed to meet and confer in good faith.  (Code Civ. Proc., §430.41, subd. (a)(3).)  However, “[a] determination by the court that the meet and confer process was insufficient shall not be grounds to overrule or sustain a demurrer.”  (Code Civ. Proc., §430.41, subd. (a)(4).

Here, Defendant’s counsel explains that between June 23 and July 26, 2026, counsel sent two emails and then called Plaintiff to schedule a meet-and-confer regarding the demurrer.  (Shaheen Decl., ¶2, Ex. A.)  Plaintiff did not respond to the emails and did not answer or return the phone call.  Although the parties did not successfully meet and confer prior to the filing of the Demurrer, the Court acknowledges Defendant’s counsel’s efforts to meet and confer and proceeds with adjudication of the merits of the Demurrer.

B.     Demurrer to the First Cause of Action for Wrongful Foreclosure

ServiceLink demurs to the first cause of action on the grounds that Plaintiff fails to state facts on which relief may be granted against moving party.

The elements of wrongful foreclosure under common law are: (1) the trustee of mortgagee caused illegal, fraudulent, or willfully oppressive sale of real property pursuant to power of sale in mortgage or trust deed; (2) the trustor or mortgagor or other party attacking the sale was prejudiced or harmed; and (3) the trustor or mortgagor tendered the amount of the secured indebtedness or was excused from that.  (Miles v. Deutsche Bank National Trust Co. (2015) 236 Cal.App.4th 394, 408.)  The tender requirement applies to “any cause of action for irregularity in the sale procedure.”  (Abdallah v. United Sav. Bank (1996) 43 Cal.App.4th 1101, 1109.)

Here, Defendant argues that Plaintiff did not meet the tender requirement and, absent tender, the claim fails as a matter of law.  (Demurrer, pp. 3-4.)  In its ruling on Defendant’s demurrer to the original complaint, the Court found that Plaintiff makes no mention of tender in the allegations for wrongful foreclosure and distinguished Plaintiff’s reference to Dimock v. Emerald Properties (2000) 81 Cal.App.4th 868 as pertaining to an unlawful detainer action, not wrongful foreclosure.  Defendant argues that Plaintiff has not cured this deficiency and thus, the instant Demurrer to the first cause of action should be sustained without leave to amend.

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that the FAC alleges that ServiceLink participated in conducting, facilitating, and certifying the foreclosure auction and acted as an agent and participant in effectuating the challenged foreclosure sale.  (FAC ¶40.)  The FAC also specifically alleges the following grounds on which tender is excused: (1) no valid substitution authorized Affinia to conduct the foreclosure; (2) the beneficiary identified in the Trustee's Deed materially differs from the beneficiary identified in the recorded assignment; (3) no recorded assignment to the foreclosing entity has been identified; and (4) mandatory statutory notice was not provided to HREBT, the alleged record owner.  (FAC ¶¶41-43.)  Thus, the FAC alleges that the sale was void for lack of lawful foreclosure authority, rather than merely voidable because of an ordinary procedural irregularity.

The Court finds that the FAC sufficiently alleges multiple facts that excuse the tender requirement.  Tender, for purposes of a wrongful foreclosure claim, has been excused when “(1) the underlying debt is void, (2) the foreclosure sale or trustee's deed is void on its face, (3) a counterclaim offsets the amount due, (4) specific circumstances make it inequitable to enforce the debt against the party challenging the sale, or (5) the foreclosure sale has not yet occurred.”  (Chavez v. Indymac Mortgage Services (2013) 219 Cal.App.4th 1052, 1062.)  Specifically, the FAC alleges: no valid substitution of trustee authorizing Affinia to foreclose (FAC ¶41); inconsistent beneficiary trust designations and lack of any recorded assignment to the foreclosing entity (FAC ¶42); mandatory statutory notice was not provided to the record owner HREBT (FAC ¶¶34-35, 41); and foreclosure was void for lack of foreclosure authority, not merely voidable (FAC ¶¶32-33, 39, 43).  These allegations, which must be accepted as true at the demurrer stage, demonstrate recognized exceptions to the tender rule under California law.  Thus, the FAC has cured the deficiency noted in the Court’s prior order by presenting allegations that the tender rule is excused in this case.

            The Court also rejects Defendant’s contention presented in the Reply that it holds no relevance to the authority-based defects alleged in the FAC.  Although Plaintiff does not allege that Defendant was the trustee, mortgagee, or beneficiary, the FAC pleads that Defendant materially participated in effectuating the foreclosure sale, including acting as foreclosure-sale agents, conducting auction activities, and issuing or facilitating the Certificate of Sale that declared the Property “Reverted to the Lender.”  The FAC further alleges that Defendant published and facilitated foreclosure-sale documentation integral to the challenged transfer of title.  The Court does not have to adjudicate the merits of Plaintiff’s claims at this stage.  These allegations sufficiently connect Defendant to the foreclosure process underlying Plaintiff’s claims and contradict Defendant’s assertion that all authority-based defects are directed solely at other parties.

            For these reasons, the Court finds that the FAC contains sufficient allegations for a wrongful foreclosure cause of action.  The Demurrer to the first cause of action is OVERRULED.

C.    Demurrer to the Fifth Cause of Action for Slander of Title

ServiceLink demurs to the fifth cause of action on the grounds that Plaintiff fails to state facts on which relief may be granted against moving party and the cause of action is time-barred by the statute of limitations.

“The elements of a cause of action for slander of title are (1) a publication, which is (2) without privilege or justification, (3) false, and (4) causes pecuniary loss.”  (La Jolla Group II v. Bruce (2012) 211 Cal.App.4th 461, 472.)

Defendant argues that Plaintiff fails to plead the “false publication” and “pecuniary loss” elements against the moving party.  The FAC alleges that Defendant facilitated publication of the “Certificate of Sale at Public Auction dated October 21, 2022,” (Exhibit 9 to FAC) but fails to allege that Exhibit 9 is a false publication.  Furthermore, the FAC excludes Exhibit 9 when listing documents that allegedly caused Plaintiff pecuniary loss.  (FAC ¶77.)  Defendant argues that Plaintiff has not cured any of the deficiencies outlined in the Court’s April 28, 2026, order, which noted that the initial complaint did not allege that Defendant authored/recorded the alleged publication or engaged in any wrongdoing.

Defendant also argues that the claim is time-barred because the statute of limitations for a slander of title to real property cause of action is subject to a three-year statute of limitation pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 338, subdivision (g).  The only publication attributed to Defendant is the “Certificate of Sale at Public Auction dated October 21, 2022” (Exhibit 9).  Given that the cause of action commenced on October 21, 2022, the statute of limitations expired on October 21, 2025.  The initial complaint in this action was filed on December 18, 2025, two months after the statute of limitations expired.

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that paragraph 72 of the FAC alleges that ServiceLink participated in publishing foreclosure-sale documentation, including the Certificate of Sale at Public Auction dated October 21, 2022.  Paragraph 74 specifically alleges that ServiceLink Agency Sales and Posting, LLC prepared, issued, and published that Certificate.  Paragraph 75 further identifies the allegedly false representation: the Certificate represented that the Property had "Reverted to the Lender" notwithstanding the alleged defects in foreclosure authority, beneficiary identity, substitution authority, and statutory notice.  Paragraph 76 alleges that its issuance and publication contributed to clouding title, effectuating the challenged transfer, and causing Plaintiff's loss of possession and property interests.  Although paragraph 77 does not list the Certificate of Sale when describing pecuniary damages, Paragraph 76 expressly alleges that issuance and publication of the foreclosure-sale documentation contributed to clouded title, the challenged transfer, and Plaintiff's loss of possession and property interests.  Paragraph 77 further alleges direct pecuniary loss, loss of the Property, and expenses incurred to clear title.

Plaintiff also opposes ServiceLink’s contention that the slander-of-title claim necessarily accrued on October 21, 2022.  Plaintiff acknowledges that the FAC does not allege with specificity the time and manner by which Plaintiff discovered ServiceLink's issuance and publication of the Certificate.  If the Court determines that additional delayed-discovery allegations are required, Plaintiff can amend to allege that ServiceLink did not mail, deliver, or otherwise provide Plaintiff with the Certificate when it was issued in October 2022; that Plaintiff was unaware at the time of the foreclosure auction that ServiceLink had prepared, issued, or published the Certificate; and that Plaintiff became aware of ServiceLink's involvement only later, in connection with the unlawful-detainer proceedings and foreclosure-related materials associated with HSBC and its agents.  The FAC already alleges that Defendants commenced the unlawful-detainer proceedings in 2023 based upon their claimed acquisition of the Property through foreclosure.  (FAC ¶142.)

The Court finds that the allegations in the FAC are sufficient to plead the falsity and harm elements for a slander of title cause of action.  However, the FAC does not contain sufficient allegations challenging the date of accrual of the cause of action as stated on the Certificate.  Given the arguments presented in the Opposition, the Court provides Plaintiff with one additional opportunity to cure the deficiencies outlined in the fifth cause of action.

Defendant’s Demurrer on the cause of action for slander of title is SUSTAINED with 30 days leave to amend.

D.    Demurrer to the Eighth Cause of Action for Declaratory Relief

ServiceLink demurs to the eighth cause of action on the grounds that Plaintiff fails to state facts on which relief may be granted against moving party.

“Any person interested under a written instrument … who desires a declaration of his or her rights or duties with respect to another, or in respect to, in, over or upon property, … may, in cases of actual controversy relating to the legal rights and duties of the respective parties, bring an original action … for a declaration of his or her rights and duties in the premises….”  (Code Civ. Proc. § 1060.)  To receive declaratory relief, the moving party must plead facts sufficient to find an actual controversy.  (Nede Mgmt. Inc. v. Aspen American Ins. Co. (2021) 68 Cal.App.5th 1121, 1132.)

Defendant argues that the FAC fails to state an actual controversy against Defendant as both the first cause of action for wrongful foreclosure and the fifth cause of action for slander of title contain insufficient allegations.  The FAC contains the same deficiencies identified in the initial complaint and fails as a matter of law.

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that FAC alleges that ServiceLink participated in and certified the foreclosure-sale process and issued the Certificate of Sale at Public Auction stating that the Property had “Reverted to the Lender.”  (FAC ¶¶ 87–88.)  The FAC further alleges an actual controversy concerning ServiceLink’s participation in facilitating and effectuating the foreclosure; ServiceLink’s issuance and publication of the Certificate of Sale; Plaintiff’s contention that the sale suffered from defective foreclosure authority, beneficiary identity, substitution authority, and statutory notice; and ServiceLink’s foreclosure-sale documentation representing that the Property had “Reverted to the Lender.” (FAC ¶¶ 94–97.)  These allegations expressly identify the controversy.  Furthermore, the FAC adequately alleges wrongful foreclosure and slander of title against ServiceLink.

Given that the Court overrules Defendant’s Demurrer to the first cause of action, the Court finds an underlying controversy exists between Defendant and Plaintiff.  A controversy also arises from conflicting legal assertions regarding the Certificate of Sale, the validity of the foreclosure documents, and the parties’ respective rights.  Accordingly, the Demurrer to the eighth cause of action is OVERRULED.

CONCLUSION

Defendant ServiceLink Agency Sales and Posting, LLC’s Demurrer is OVERRULED as to the first and eighth causes of action and SUSTAINED with 30 days leave to amend as to the fifth cause of action.
