DEPARTMENT 517 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 25STCV19495    Hearing Date: June 23, 2026    Dept: 517

Plaintiffs Armida Corral and Aleksander Blagojevic sued

Defendant General Motors, LLC (“Defendant”).  Plaintiffs’ claims arise out of their purchase of a motor vehicle that Defendant manufactured and distributed (“the Subject Vehicle”).  Defendant has demurred to the claims in the Plaintiffs’ First Amended Complaint (“the FAC”) for breach of the implied warranty of merchantability under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act (“the SBA”) and for common law fraudulent inducement-concealment.  The Court is sustaining the demurrer with leave to amend on the ground that both the implied warranty claim and the fraud claim are barred by the applicable statutes of limitations.  

 

Plaintiffs shall file and serve a Second Amended Complaint within 45 days of this order.  The Court is continuing the case management conference that was on calendar today to January 26, 2027 at 8:30 a.m.

 

Implied Warranty Claim  

Defendant argues in the demurrer that Plaintiffs’ claim for breach of the implied warranty of merchantability under the SBA is barred by the applicable statute of limitations.  The Court agrees.

 

“Unlike express warranties, which are basically contractual in nature, the implied warranty of merchantability arises by operation of law.”   (Hauter v. Zogarts 1975) 14 Cal.3d 104, 117. )  Under the SBA, “[t]he duration of an the implied warranty of merchantability. . . shall be coextensive in duration with an express warranty which accompanies the consumer goods, provided the duration of the express warranty is reasonable; but in no event shall such implied warranty have a duration of less than 60 days nor more than one year following the sale of new consumer goods to a retail buyer.  ( Civil Code §

1791.1(c).) 

 

The statute of limitations on a claim for breach of an implied warranty of merchantability is four years.  (Montoya v. Ford Motor Co. (2020) 46 Cal.App.5th 493, 495.)  Section 2725(2) of the Commercial Code states that such claims “accrue[] when the breach occurs, regardless of the aggrieved party’s lack of knowledge of the breach.  A breach of [implied]warranty occurs when tender of delivery is made. . . . .”  (Commercial Code § 2725(2).)  Courts routinely have applied the tender of delivery accrual rule of section 2725(2) of the Commercial Code to implied warranty claims under the SBA.  (Hindsman v. General Motors LLC, No. 17-cv-05337-JSC, 2018 WL 2463113, at *14 (N.D. Cal. June 1, 2018) [“Section 2725 provides that the cause of action accrues when the breach occurs; that is, in the implied warranty context, when the sale is made.”]; see also Ferris v. Ford Motor Company, Case No. 18-cv-03216-JSW, 2019 WL 1100376 at *2 (N.D. Cal. Mar. 8, 2019) [Ferris]; Philips v. Ford Motor Company, No, 14-CV-02989-LHK, 2016 WL 1745948 *13 (N.D. Cal. May 3, 2016); MacDonald v. Ford Motor Co. (N.D. Cal. 2014) 37 F.Supp.3d 1087, 1101.)

 

In the demurrer, Defendant argues that, based on the allegations in the FAC, delivery of the Subject Vehicle occurred on June 9, 2021, when the Plaintiffs entered into a warranty contract with Defendant. purchased the Subject Vehicle.  Plaintiffs initiated this action more than four years later, on June 30, 2025.   The implied warranty claim is thus time-barred. 

 

Plaintiffs do not contest that June 9, 2021 was the delivery date of the Subject Vehicle.  Plaintiffs argue instead that the implied warranty claim did not accrue until they discovered the basis of the claim, and that given this delayed discovery, the statute of limitations on the claim has not expired.  Plaintiffs’ argument is mistaken.  There is no delayed-discovery rule with respect to accrual of a claim for breach of an implied warranty.  (Cardinal Health 301, Inc. v. Tyco Electronics Corp. (2008) 169 Cal. App. 4th 116, 129, 134.)  Mexia v. Rinker Boat Co. (2009) 174 Cal.App.4th 1297 is not to the contrary.  The Court of Appeal in Mexia stated that a defect need not be discovered during the SBA’s one-year implied warranty period to be actionable.  (Id. at pp. 1310-1311.)  But it also said “[i]n the case of a latent defect, a product is rendered unmerchantable, and the warranty of merchantability is breached, by the existence of the unseen defect, not by its subsequent discovery.” (Id. at 1304-1305.)  In short, Mexia does not establish a delayed discovery rule for SBA claims for breach of the implied warranty of merchantability that differs from the rule of section 2775.2 of the Commercial Code.  (Ferris, supra, 2019 WL 1100376 at *3) [interpreting what Mexia does, and does not, do].) 

 

Claim for Fraudulent Inducement – Concealment

 

On the face of the FAC, Plaintiffs’ claim for fraudulent inducement - concealment is barred by the three-year of statute of limitations for fraud claims set forth in Code of Civil Procedure section 338(d). 

 

To be sure, Section 338(d) states that a fraud claim "is not deemed to have accrued until the discovery, by the aggrieved party, of the facts constituting the fraud . . . ." This provision in Section 338(d) codifies the "discovery rule," which “postpones accrual of a cause of action until the plaintiff discovers, or has reason to discover, the cause of action.”  (Fox v. Ethicon Endo-Surgery, Inc. (2005) 35 Cal.4th 797, 807.)  In essence, the discovery rule "delays accrual until the plaintiff has, or should have, inquiry notice of the cause of action.”  (Ibid.)  This means that plaintiffs “are required to conduct a reasonable investigation after becoming aware of an injury, and are charged with knowledge of the information that would have been revealed by such an investigation.”  (Id. at p. 808.)  The limitations period begins once the plaintiff has notice or information of circumstances to put a reasonable person on inquiry.  (Alexander v. Exxon Mobil (2013) 219 Cal.App.4th 1236, 1251, citation omitted).  The discovery rule imposes a pleading requirement on a party seeking the protection of the rule in the face of a demurrer.  Specifically, “[i]n order to rely on the discovery rule for delayed accrual of a cause of action, ‘a plaintiff whose complaint shows on its face that his claim would be barred without the benefit of the discovery rule must specifically plead facts to show (1) the time and manner of discovery, and (2) the inability to have made earlier discovery despite reasonable diligence.’”  (Fox, supra, 35 Cal.4th at p. 809, citation omitted.)

 

Plaintiffs allege in the FAC they did not discover the basis of their fraud claim until shortly before they filed suit; from there, Plaintiffs argue in the opposition to the demurrer that it was only then that the statute of limitations began to run.  The FAC fails, however, to allege that Plaintiffs were unable to discover the basis of the fraud claim through the exercise of reasonable diligence prior to that date.  This failure is magnified by allegations in the FAC that the Subject Vehicle’s manifested themselves as early as June 2022. 

 

In light of the Court’s determination that Plaintiffs’ fraud claim is time-barred, the Court need not reach the other argument that Defendant made as to that claim in the notice of the demurrer, albeit not in the memorandum in support of the demurrer – namely, that the claim is barred by the economic loss rule.
