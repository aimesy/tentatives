I.            INTRODUCTION

Plaintiff Jose Pena (“Plaintiff”) moves for an award of attorneys’ fees consisting of a “lodestar” amount of $56,443.50, a lodestar enhancement of 1.5 in the amount of $28,221.75, and $17,813.43 in costs and expenses.

Defendant American Honda Motor Co. (“Defendant”) filed an opposition brief on June 12, 2026.

Plaintiff filed a reply brief on June 18, 2026.

On June 26, 2026, Defendant filed a “notice of binding authority” arguing that Plaintiff’s fee motion was untimely pursuant to Hatlevig v. General Motors LLC (2026) 118 Cal.App.5th 644,646-47.

The Court continued the hearing to July 27, 2026, to allow Plaintiff an opportunity to submit a supplemental brief addressing Defendant’s “notice of binding authority.”

Plaintiff filed his supplemental brief on July 7, 2026.

II.          DISCUSSION

A.   Timeliness and Defendant’s Notice of Binding Authority

Under California Rules of Court, rule 3.1702, a motion for fees incurred up to the time of judgment must be served and filed within the time for filing a notice of appeal, i.e. 60 days after service of notice of entry of judgment or 180 days after entry of judgment, whichever is earlier. A voluntary dismissal pursuant to a settlement is a judgment within the rule prescribing the time to appeal. (Hatlevig v. General Motors LLC (2026) 118 Cal.App.5th 644, 649.) The purpose of rule 3.1702 is to provide time limits within which all motions for attorney fees in civil cases must be made, including cases that end in a voluntary dismissal. (Ibid.)

Defendant argues that the motion was filed more than 60 days after dismissal. However, dismissal was entered on December 5, 2025. Sixty days from December 5, 2025, is February 3, 2026. Therefore, Plaintiff’s motion, filed on February 3, 2026, is timely.

B.   Attorneys’ Fees

Plaintiff submits the declaration of Roger Kirnos, wherein he attests to the qualifications of the attorneys and paralegals who performed 123.9 hours of work on this matter. The declaration sets forth the reasonableness of the attorneys’ hourly rates, which range from $295-$625, as well as that of the paralegal’s hourly rate of $145. Plaintiff also argues that a lodestar multiplier of 1.5 is appropriate due to the contingent nature of the case.

In opposition, Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s counsel should not recover more than $28,582 and that the amount billed reflects “bloated hours worked by 17 different timekeepers.) Defendant argues that “[t]o the extent any original work was performed, counsel’s overstaffing must inevitably have resulted in duplicative billing and excessive amounts of time on rote tasks.” (Opp., p. 5.) Defendant also argues that the rates billed by Plaintiff’s counsel are excessive and proposes a blended hourly rate of $350 for all attorneys and staff.

In the Court’s experience, the hourly rates charged by Plaintiff’s counsel are reasonable in comparison to other lemon law attorneys who prosecute cases on a contingency basis. The number of hours billed for the work is also reasonable. Despite pointing out the number of timekeepers staffed on the matter, Defendant does not identify any billing entries that demonstrate overstaffing or inefficiency. Defendant points to the amount of time spent on deposition preparation. However, the fact that Defendant did not conduct lengthy depositions is not evidence that the time spent preparing for them was excessive. Furthermore, Defendant’s complaints of improper block-billing are unfounded because the entries are sufficiently descriptive. Last, Defendant’s claims that Plaintiff’s counsel improperly billed for clerical work is unsupported. Therefore, the Court declines to make any reductions to the lodestar. In fact, the Court adds the fees incurred by Plaintiff’s counsel in responding to Defendant’s notice of binding authority to the lodestar in the reduced amount of $665, consisting of 2 hours at a law clerk’s hourly rate of $175 and 0.7 hours at Nitin Sapra’s hourly rate of $450. In total, the lodestar is $57,108.50.

With respect to Plaintiff’s request for a lodestar multiplier, the Court notes as an initial matter that Plaintiff requests a 1.5 lodestar enhancement in the beginning of its moving papers and three different multipliers (1.2, 1.3, and 1.5) in three consecutive sentences towards the end. (Compare Motion, p. 2 with p. 13). Plaintiff argues that a lodestar is appropriate because this case involved risk on three different levels. Plaintiff argues that counsel risked not being paid at all and claims that Plaintiff’s counsel “risked the amount of hours” as well as their hourly rates. This attempt to exaggerate Plaintiff’s counsel’s risk exposure is unpersuasive because all three “levels” involves the same risk present in contingency matters: not being paid. Additionally, there is nothing in this case that is out of the ordinary, which is evidenced by Plaintiff’s counsel’s admitted ability to make extensive use of templates.

C.   Costs

Defendant did not challenge the costs claimed by Plaintiff. Therefore, the motion is granted with respect to costs.

III.        CONCLUSION

Plaintiff’s motion is GRANTED in part and Plaintiff’s counsel is awarded fees and costs in the amount of $74,921.93, consisting of $57,108.50 in fees and $17,813.43 in costs.

I.      INTRODUCTION

          On September 25, 2025, plaintiff Soraya Velasquez (“Plaintiff”) filed this action against defendant Avalon Bay Communities, Inc. (“Defendant”). The action arises from Plaintiff’s tenancy in a rental home located at 4121 Oak Street #3, Burbank, CA 91505 (“Rental Home”).

On January 13, 2026, the Court sustained Defendant’s demurrer to her initial complaint with 20 day’s leave to amend. Defendant served notice of the Court’s order on January 14, 2026.

On February 3, 2026, Plaintiff timely filed the operative First Amended Complaint. (Code Civ. Proc. § 472b.)

On March 4, 2026, Defendant filed a demurrer to Plaintiff’s Sixth Cause of Action for fraud and moved to strike the FAC’s punitive damages allegations.

          Plaintiff filed opposition briefs on July 13, 2026.

          Defendant filed reply briefs on July 20, 2026.  

II.     LEGAL STANDARDS

A.   Demurrer

A demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the pleadings and will be sustained only where the pleading is defective on its face. (City of Atascadero v. Merrill Lynch, Pierce, Fenner & Smith, Inc. (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 445, 459.) “We treat the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law. We accept the factual allegations of the complaint as true and also consider matters which may be judicially noticed. [Citation.]” (Mitchell v. California Department of Public Health (2016) 1 Cal.App.5th 1000, 1007; Del E. Webb Corp. v. Structural Materials Co. (1981) 123 Cal.App.3d 593, 604 [“the facts alleged in the pleading are deemed to be true, however improbable they may be”].) Allegations are to be liberally construed. (Code Civ. Proc., § 452.) In construing the allegations, the court is to give effect to specific factual allegations that may modify or limit inconsistent general or conclusory allegations. (Financial Corporation of America v. Wilburn (1987) 189 Cal.App.3rd 764, 769.)

B.   Motion to Strike

          Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading may serve and file a notice of motion to strike the whole or any part thereof. (Code Civ. Proc., § 435, subd. (b)(1).) The court may, upon a motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a); Stafford v. Shultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782 [“Matter in a pleading which is not essential to the claim is surplusage; probative facts are surplusage and may be stricken out or disregarded”].) The court may also strike all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with California law, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (b).) An immaterial or irrelevant allegation is one that is not essential to the statement of a claim or defense; is neither pertinent to nor supported by an otherwise sufficient claim or defense; or a demand for judgment requesting relief not supported by the allegations of the complaint. (Code Civ. Proc., § 431.10, subd. (b).) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437.)

III.    DISCUSSION

A.   Demurrer

Defendant demurs to Plaintiff’s Sixth Cause of Action for fraud on the grounds that it is not pleaded with specificity. (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 645; Committee on Children's Television, Inc. v. General Foods Corp. (1983) 35 Cal.3d 197, 216.)

Plaintiff alleges that on or about October 2023, a leasing office representative represented to her that the Rental Home had not sustained any previous water damage, did not have any mold contamination, and had been maintained in good condition. Plaintiff also was told that any problems would be promptly and timely repaired by qualified professionals. (FAC, ¶ 98.)  

In sustaining Defendant’s demurrer to Plaintiff’s previous complaint, the Court noted that Plaintiff did not plead the names of any individuals and did not state with sufficient specificity when those statements were made. Plaintiff now alleges that an unidentified leasing office representative spoke with her but Defendant still contends that Plaintiff’s allegations remain deficient because the representative’s name is not alleged. Defendant argues that Plaintiff is required to allege not only the representative’s name, but her job title, duties, authority, or even facts establishing that the unidentified person was employed by Defendant. However, less specificity is required when the facts lie more in the defendant’s possession. (Committee On Children’s Television, supra, 35 Cal.3d at p. 217.) Therefore, this additional information about the leasing office representative is not required.

Nevertheless, Plaintiff merely alleges that the statements were made on or about October 2023, even though she personally participated in this alleged conversation and should be able to plead at least some approximate date or other facts describing when the discussion took place.

Accordingly, the demurrer to the Sixth Cause of Action is SUSTAINED with leave to amend.  

B.   Motion to Strike

Defendant moves to strike Plaintiff’s prayer for punitive damages and related allegations of willful, malicious, oppressive, or fraudulent conduct.

A corporate employer shall not be liable for punitive damages based on the actions of an employee, unless an officer, director, or managing agent of the corporation was personally guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice, or had advance knowledge of the unfitness of the employee and employed him for a conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others or authorized or ratified the wrongful conduct. (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (b).)

Since the allegations supporting a request for punitive damages must be alleged with specificity; the motion to strike is GRANTED because Plaintiff fails to identify an officer, director, or managing agent of Defendant who engaged in, ratified, or authorized the alleged wrongful conduct. (Smith v. Superior Court (1992) 10 Cal.App.4th 1033, 1041-1042.)         

IV.    CONCLUSION

The demurrer to the Sixth Cause of Action is SUSTAINED with 20 days’ leave to amend.

The motion to strike is GRANTED with leave to amend.

I.            INTRODUCTION

On December 15, 2023, plaintiff Luis Marquez (“Plaintiff”) filed this complaint against defendant Gordon B. Chang (“Defendant”) for damages and injunctive relief for violations of the Americans with Disabilities Act and Unruh Civil Rights Act. Plaintiff alleges that he visited the Exclusive Rosemead Cannabis Dispensary facility located at or about 1630 Potrero Grande Dr., Rosemead, CA 91770 in January 2025 and encountered barriers to access.

On April 10, 2026, Plaintiff filed this motion for a protective order and request for sanctions. Defendant noticed Plaintiff’s deposition for April 17, 2026, a day on which Plaintiff’s counsel is unavailable. Defendant chose to notice the deposition for this date despite knowing that Plaintiff’s counsel was not available since the parties had conferred on rescheduling a mandatory evaluation conference (“MEC”) and discussed several possible dates, one of which was April 17, 2026.

Plaintiff also argues that a protective order should issue because Defendant’s deposition notice requests the production of privileged and irrelevant information.

The Court issued a tentative ruling on Plaintiff’s motion for a protective order ahead of the hearing on May 6, 2026. At the hearing, the tentative was not adopted. Instead, the Court continued the hearing and scheduled an Order to Show Cause re: Sanctions for the misuse of case citations in Defendant’s opposition brief; the Court ordered supplemental briefs to be filed by July 13, 2026.

On July 13, 2026, Plaintiff filed a supplemental brief and a declaration from one of his attorneys, Dennis Price.

On July 14, 2026, one day after the Court’s deadline, defense counsel Larry Rothman filed a declaration in response to the OSC.

On July 21, 2026, another one of Plaintiff’s attorneys, Amanda Seabock, filed a declaration in response to Mr. Rothman’s declaration.

II.          MOTION FOR PROTECTIVE ORDER

Before, during, or after a deposition, a party may seek a protective order. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.420, subd. (a).) The scope of relief permitted is broad, and includes both scheduling, as well as document production. (Id., subd. (b).)

The Court first notes that the motion for a protective order appears to be moot with respect to any scheduling issues. (Reply Decl., ¶ 13.) Therefore, the Court will only address the propriety of Defendant’s document requests contained in his deposition notice.

A.   Documents Requesting Privileged and Irrelevant Information

Plaintiff argues that a protective order is needed because Defendant demands the production of a privilege log even though these document requests seek, on their face, explicitly privileged materials and are improper. (Motion, p. 2.) Plaintiff also argues that Defendant’s requests for privileged materials is harassing and designed to generate substantial work without resulting in any admissible evidence. (Motion, p. 3.)

Plaintiff additionally argues that information regarding his litigation history and personal life is not discoverable since whether he is a “tester engaged in serial litigation” is irrelevant. (Motion, p. 5.) Plaintiff has agreed to provide a list of recent litigation but contends that Defendant’s document requests are overbroad and unduly burdensome. (Reply, pp. 8-9.) Plaintiff further argues that “intent to return” is not relevant to any issues in this case because it is a consideration of Article III standing in federal courts for purposes of issuing injunctive relief, not state court. (Motion, p. 4; Reply, pp. 5-6.)

The Court agrees that the deposition notice includes multiple document requests that explicitly seek privileged information and work product, as well as irrelevant information about Plaintiff’s litigation history and personal life, specifically, RFP Nos. 1, and 7 through 9:

·       RFP No. 1 requests “[a]ll lawsuits filed by Plaintiff Luis Marquez.” Defendant then lists three bullet points listing: (1) all complaints, petitions, verifications, and amendments filed by Plaintiff in any court within the past five years, (2) all settlement agreements, releases, or dismissals related to those lawsuits, and (3) all communications with counsel in those lawsuits relating to disability access, intent to return, or claimed barriers.

·       RFP No. 2 demands: Plaintiff’s calendars, schedules, planners, or digital entries. Below are two bullet points identifying (1) all calendars (paper or electronic) showing Plaintiff’s activities, appointments, or travel for January 2025 and the six months before and after, and (2) any entries referencing the subject property at 1630 Potrero Grande Dr.

·       RFP No. 6 seeks “[a]ll documents relating to Plaintiff’s intent to return”, including “[a]ny notes, emails, texts, or communications showing Plaintiff intended to return to the property” and “[a]ny documents showing Plaintiff’s regular travel to or presence in Rosemead.”

·       RFP No. 7 seeks “[a]ll documents relating to Plaintiff’s disability-rights advocacy”, specifically “[a]ny materials Plaintiff uses in connection with his stated role as a “disability rights advocate” and “[a]ny communications with advocacy groups, attorneys, or consultants.”

·       RFP No. 8 demands “[a]ll documents Plaintiff intends to use at trial”, specifically “[a]ny exhibits, demonstratives, photographs, or summaries.”

·       RFP No. 9 requests “[a]ll documents Plaintiff has received from any third party relating to this case”, including communications with Seabock Price, investigators, and any CASp inspector.

In response, Defendant does not submit a response to Plaintiff’s separate statement, making it difficult for the Court to ascertain which arguments are made in response to any particular document request. Rather, Defendant claims in his opposition brief that Plaintiff’s claims of privilege are legally incorrect, while appearing to agree that “[a]ll communications” with Seabock Price, attorneys, and consultants are “categorically privileged” and that “[n]o log is required.” (Opp., p. 5.) Defendant also argues that Plaintiff’s litigation history and credibility are discoverable but cites three cases which do not address discoverability. (Opp., p. 6.) Rather, the three cases cited affirm that tester plaintiffs have standing to sue. Moreover, Defendant does not cite to any case law supporting his contention that “intent to return” is relevant to an Unruh Act claim.

Defendant essentially concedes that his requests seek facially privileged information and the Court is not persuaded that the extensive discovery sought regarding Plaintiff’s litigation history and daily activities is reasonably calculated to lead to admissible evidence. Accordingly, the motion is GRANTED with respect to RFP Nos. 1, 2, 6-9. No privilege log needs to be produced in response to RFP No. 9 and Plaintiff does not need to respond to RFP Nos. 1, 2, and 6-8.

B.   Information Implicating Plaintiff’s Right to Privacy

Last, Plaintiff argues in his separate statement that Defendant’s request for medical information is overbroad. RFP No. 4, labeled as “All documents relating to Plaintiff’s alleged disability or injury”, includes: (1) medical records, diagnoses, treatment notes, or evaluations relating to Plaintiff’s mobility impairment, (2) any documents supporting Plaintiff’s claim that he uses a wheelchair, and (3) any documents showing limitations, restrictions, or functional capacity. Plaintiff states that he will produce records demonstrating his disability, but objects to the suggestion that all his medical history is necessary. The Court agrees that RFP No. 4, as drafted, is overbroad. The protective order is GRANTED and Plaintiff need not produce documents in response to RFP No. 4. Defendant can re-serve a deposition notice with substantially narrowed requests for information regarding Plaintiff’s disability.

III.        ORDER TO SHOW CAUSE RE: SANCTIONS FOR MISUSE OF CASE CITATIONS

The Court now addresses the parties’ supplemental briefing on Mr. Rothman’s treatment of Plaintiff’s counsel, Ms. Seabock, and Mr. Rothman’s misuse of AI in preparing his brief and determines whether to report Mr. Rothman to the State Bar or sanction him pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure (“CCP”) section 128.7. By way of background, Plaintiff’s motion included details of Mr. Rothman’s harassing and discriminatory conduct including addressing Ms. Seabock by her first name, Amanda, while addressing her male colleague, Dennis Price, as Mr. Price. Mr. Rothman also referred to Ms. Seabock as Mr. Price’s “attorney wife” in an email. Plaintiff’s reply also pointed out that Defendant relied on cases which did not reflect the legal propositions for which they were cited and in one instance, included a nonexistent case name and citation.

Whenever a judge has personal knowledge, or concludes in a judicial decision, that a lawyer has committed misconduct or has violated any provision of the Rules of Professional Conduct, the judge shall take appropriate corrective action, which may include reporting the violation to the appropriate authority. (Code Jud. Ethics, canon 3D(2).) Sanctions may also be imposed under CCP section 128.7 if a judge, on the judge's own motion, enters an order describing the sanctionable conduct that appears to violate CCP section 128.7(b) and directs an attorney to show cause why it was not violated, unless within 21 days of service of the OSC, the challenged paper, claim, defense, contention, allegation, or denial is withdrawn or corrected appropriately. (Code Civ. Proc., § 128.7, subd. (c)2).)

First, California Rules of Professional Conduct (“RPC”) rule 8.4.1 prohibits an attorney from unlawfully harassing or discriminating against any person on the basis of any protected characteristic, which includes marital status, sex, and gender. (Cal. Rule of Prof. Conduct R. 8.4.1). In response to the OSC, Mr. Rothman declares that he has “never abused, insulted, demeaned, or mistreated attorney Amanda Seabock.” (Response to OSC, Rothman Decl., ¶ 4.) He claims he only spoke with her to request an extension at the beginning of the case, which she denied. (Id.) He also claims that his use of the phrase “attorney wife” was an “inadvertent typographical error, not a statement of bias.” (Id.)

Mr. Rothman’s assertions are not credible. First, the record shows his interactions with Ms. Seabock were not limited to requesting an extension to the deadline for filing a responsive pleading, nor was the extension he requested denied. Ms. Seabock agreed to an extension pursuant California Rules of Court rule 3.110, which allows the parties to “stipulate without leave of court to one 15-day extension beyond the 30-day time period prescribed for the response after service of the initial complaint.” (Seabock Decl., ¶¶ 3-4.) Afterwards, Mr. Rothman and Ms. Seabock exchanged written communications and spoke on the telephone on numerous occasions regarding potential settlement and the sufficiency of Defendant’s responsive pleading. (Seabock Decl., ¶¶ 5-11.)

Mr. Rothman does not deny referring to Ms. Seabock as “Amanda” while choosing to accord more respect to her male colleague by addressing him as “Mr. Price.” He omits any discussion of that aspect of misconduct and offers no apologies for this discriminatory and patronizing behavior. He also does not deny referring to her as Mr. Price’s “attorney wife.” Instead, he claims that he merely made an “inadvertent typographical error.” (Response to OSC, Rothman Decl., ¶ 4.)

This is a preposterous suggestion. One cannot contemplate what Mr. Rothman could have possibly intended to write while “inadvertently” impugning Ms. Seabock’s professional career and reputation, nor can one imagine making the same misogynistic “typographical error” twice in the same email. (Supp. Price Decl., Ex. Ex. 1.) Worst of all, Mr. Rothman does not appear to have recognized that his behavior is sexist, unprofessional, and insulting. Instead, he chooses to dismiss his statements as inadvertent mistakes without acknowledging their inherently biased nature and meaningfully apologizing for them.

Mr. Rothman’s lack of contrition extends to his misuse of AI, warranting sanctions under section 128.7. As mentioned above, Defendant’s opposition brief cited three cases that did not address the issues for which they were cited. These cases, identified by Plaintiff in his reply brief, are Angelucci v. Century Supper Club (2007) 41 Cal.4th 160, 178, Munson v. Del Taco., Inc. (2009) 46 Cal.4th 661, 676, and White v. Square, Inc. (2019) 7 Cal.5th 1019. None of them support, either directly or indirectly, Defendant’s assertion that a plaintiff’s litigation history is relevant or discoverable.

In response to the Court’s OSC, Mr. Rothman argues that an inadvertent citation error is not grounds for sanctions and points out that a notice of erratum was filed, which replaced the mistaken citation with the correct statutory authority. The notice of erratum referenced by Mr. Rothman does not address Angelucci, Munson, or White, but the nonexistent case Do It Urself Moving & Storage, Inc. v. Brown (2008) 168 Cal.App.4th 161, 174. Moreover, Mr. Rothman fails to explain how this nonexistent authority “inadvertently” made its way into the brief he prepared.

Mr. Rothman also states that he “do[es] not use generative AI for legal work” and “rel[ies] exclusively on Lexis and primary sources.” This assertion, like his claims of typographical error when engaging in misogynistic behavior, is implausible. The opposition brief appears to be the product of copy-and-pasted AI prompts because it was partly written in the second person and formatted with bullet points. His supplemental brief in response to the OSC also contains what appears to be an excerpt of a query posed to AI written in the second person:

Monarch holds:

 

Courts must give parties notice and opportunity to be heard before imposing sanctions.

 

You received no prior notice that the Court viewed the citation error as sanctionable until the OSC was issued.

 

Thus, sanctions violate due process.

 

(Response to OSC, p. 6.)  

          Furthermore, Mr. Rothman’s reliance on Del Biaggio v. Bansen (2026) 2026 WL 1998607) is unavailing to him and in fact, supports the imposition of sanctions. The Del Biaggio court addressed an attorney who admitted the unverified use of AI, offered “some explanation” for how the misrepresentations of case law occurred, and eventually sent a letter identifying and withdrawing the misstatements in accordance with his duty of candor. (Id. at *9.) Even though the Court accepted the attorney’s misrepresentation that there was no intent to mislead, the court found it appropriate to order the attorney pay a sanction of $1,500. (Id., *8-9.) Sanctions have also been imposed in other circumstances where attorneys admitted the use of AI and accepted responsibility for inadvertently misrepresenting authority. (Quinteros v. Harbor Distributing, LLC (2026) 121 Cal.App.5th 60, 78; Noland v. Land of the Free, L.P. (2025) 114 Cal.App.5th 426, 445-446.) Here, Mr. Rothman’s refusal to acknowledge his mischaracterization of the law and minimization of the burden placed on the Court and opposing counsel to address and correct those misrepresentations underscores the necessity of sanctions. (Slichter v. Kennedy (2025) 116 Cal.App.5th 24, 32-33.)

IV.        CONCLUSION

Plaintiff’s motion is GRANTED and the Court narrows Defendant’s requests for production as follows:
