DEPARTMENT I LAW AND MOTION RULINGS

If the parties wish to submit on the tentative ruling and avoid a court appearance on the matter, the moving party must contact the opposing party and all other parties who have appeared in the action and confirm that each will submit on the tentative ruling. Please call the court no later than 4:30 p.m. on the court day before the hearing, leave a message with the court clerk at (310) 260-3629 advising her that all parties will submit on the tentative ruling and waive hearing, and finally, serve notice of the Court's ruling on all parties entitled to receive service. If any party declines to submit on the tentative ruling, then no telephone call is necessary, and all parties should appear at the hearing.

Case Number: 23SMCV05984    Hearing Date: July 15, 2026    Dept: I

This is an ex parte application to continue the trial.  The trial was already continued once and the court warned the parties that it would not do so again.  Defendant claims that it only learned at plaintiff’s most recent deposition that she had seen additional doctors and that documentation had not been provided.  To be clear, it does appear that plaintiff had recently gone to a physical therapist and seen an optometrist but records had not been provided.  They should have been provided in response to the supplemental interrogatory and RFP.  After almost 45 minutes of deposition, things devolved.  Things had been going ok—plaintiff had said that recent documents would be provided and the questions and answers were going forward.  But when plaintiff’s counsel stated that it had been almost 45 minutes, things went badly.  Apparently, plaintiff claims that counsel had agreed to 45 minutes because that was what was left on the 7 hours.  Plaintiff’s counsel stated that he would not be super-strict about it, but that it was about 45 minutes, and not a whole lot more.  Defense counsel then said plaintiff’s counsel was lying—something counsel said repeatedly on the record.  That caused plaintiff’s counsel to say the same thing.  Defense counsel then started asking more hostile questions, such as whether plaintiff ought to be allowed to drive or whether anyone reported her to the DMV.  That caused plaintiff’s counsel to instruct the witness not to answer.  Defense counsel then justified the questions by calling plaintiff a liar on the record. 

 

In the ex parte, defense contends that medical information was “deliberately withheld.”  That is not a fair characterization.  It might have been withheld, but more likely by accident because it was recent.  Defendant also claims that plaintiff has refused to produce those records.  At the deposition, there was no such refusal, but apparently they have still not been forthcoming.  And defendant states that plaintiff was not allowed to testify about her handwritten journal, although the court may have missed the evidence on that.

 

Defense counsel is not behaving well.  The court is not sure what the agreement was between counsel prior to the deposition, but stating on the record and in front of the client that a lawyer is lying is bad conduct and ought never have happened.  Not just once, but many times.  Obviously frustrated, defense counsel’s questions did start getting hostile.  Defense counsel also, again on the record, called plaintiff a liar.  That is also very inappropriate.  And at some point, plaintiff’s counsel was right to shut it down.  Defense counsel also did not mention that plaintiff’s counsel had apparently only agreed to about 45 minutes anyway—or at least that such was plaintiff’s counsel’s position.

 

At this point, based on an ex parte without much chance for plaintiff to really respond, the court will CONTINUE the application to the FSC.  However, there was an opposition.  In it, plaintiff states that the medical records were in fact produced shortly before the deposition, it is just that defendant apparently had not seen them.  Plaintiff’s counsel also states that the parties had finished the deposition a while ago except for the potential need for additional time relating to plaintiff’s handwritten journal (which did not really seem to be the basis of the deposition at issue now).  And that defense counsel mischaracterizes the agreement the parties had and did not note the objection plaintiff’s counsel submitted before the deposition.  The parties are encouraged, in the meantime, to work this out between themselves.  Depositions are to go forward as if it were in court—same level of professionalism and courtesy.  If defense counsel believes that the court would have tolerated this kind of behavior at trial, defense counsel is mistaken, at least in this courtroom.  The court will not reward that sort of thing by way of ex parte relief.  Nor does the court believe that the parties have truly engaged in a good faith effort to resolve this.  Plaintiff, for its part, has apparently not been willing to talk to defense counsel since the deposition.  That is also not proper.  The court notes that it is not denying the application.  If the application is ultimately granted, the court is not sure when the case can be restored to the calendar.  Right now, the end of the line is April 2028.


Case Number: 24SMCV04716    Hearing Date: July 15, 2026    Dept: I

This is a fee motion in a Song-Beverly case.  Plaintiff seeks about $98,000 in lodestar fees and a 50% enhancement.  Defendant opposes.  Defendant agrees plaintiff is entitled to fees, but believes the fees are too high.

 

Plaintiff sets forth some significant rates.  $650/hour for the partner through 6/30/25, and $750 after that.  A senior attorney is billed at $550 until 6/30 and $670 after that.  A more senior attorney is billed at $700.  And a second senior attorney is also billed at $700.  Plaintiff suggests that under the Laffey Matrix, the fees ought to be even higher.  Plaintiff’s counsel also states that these fees have been awarded by other courts.  The court will consider the Laffey Matrix, but as to fees awarded by other courts, given that plaintiff views it as relevant, the court will ask plaintiff’s counsel to set forth the hourly rates that have been awarded in every contested Song-Beverly case litigated in Los Angeles over the last two years for the attorneys at issue.  The court agrees with plaintiff’s counsel that Song-Beverly cases are a specialty, and that it is appropriate to recognize that in setting the hourly rate.  Defendant suggests the 2023 Real Rate Report.  Given that, defendant suggests that the rates ought to be between $295 and $495/hour.  However, this court has no idea how the rates in the Real Rate Report were derived or why the 2023 report is used rather than the 2025 report.

 

Equally to the point is the number of hours.  About 100 attorney hours are logged on the case.  And, as defendant notes, many of the hours are billed at high rates for tasks that ought to be handled by a paralegal or very junior lawyer.  Further, the defense suggests that there are generic entries, like reviewing the work of others or documents, and that some tasks—like drafting discovery—is really ministerial because it comes from the form file.  The defense also questions whether 13 hours for the fee motion is proper.  (Most plaintiff’s firms charge 4 hours.)

 

The real problem is this.  The court has noticed a marked uptick in Song-Beverly fee motions.  The plaintiff’s bar has suggested that this is due to a concerted effort by the defense bar to make it more difficult and expensive for plaintiff to recover legitimate fees.  And the court has seen examples of motions like that—where the plaintiff’s proposed fee is pretty reasonable and the attacks seem to be not terribly well reasoned.  Where the court sees that, the court has awarded an enhancement for that reason alone, which the court did very recently.  On the other hand, the court has heard from the defense bar that they are sick and tired of paying inflated bad faith fees and accuse the plaintiff’s bar of padding the hourly rate and the number of hours.  The court has seen that as well.  Where that is found, not only does the court reduce the fees accordingly, the court also applies a negative enhancement to account for the lack of trust that the court has in plaintiff’s time records.

 

The court has tried to encourage the parties to actually meet and confer about this.  But what tends to occur is that the parties engage in a flea market tactic; they each throw numbers at each other with no real discussion as to why entries were made or the work actually done or why it was done.  For guidance, the court notes that defendants often attack work done before litigation.  This court views such work as appropriate, though.  Plaintiff’s counsel is entitled to screen clients, and indeed they ought to do that in order to take cases with merit and refuse those without.  That is perfectly reasonable and necessary.  On the other hand, excessive “client conferences” even when nothing is happening in the case, or lots of time being billed on pleadings coming from the form file, or high billing rates for routine tasks (like reviewing a Notice of Remote Appearance) is something to which the court could understand a defendant looking askance.  Having a senior attorney review a document that attorney will sign is appropriate, but ought to take very little time if another competent attorney drafted it.  In any case, you get the picture.

 

The court sees no evidence that anyone had that kind of discussion here.  Given that, the court will CONTINUE this motion to so allow.  The court does note that this bill is at the higher end of bills, although the court does appreciate that this case went into written discovery and was not resolved right at the start, as often happens.  The court also notes that there was motion practice here, but sanctions were awarded in plaintiff’s favor.  The court assumes, therefore, that plaintiff is willing to reduce the fee award for time related to that motion.

Case Number: 26SMCV00052    Hearing Date: July 15, 2026    Dept: I

This is a demurrer to a cross complaint.  In particular, the cross complainant is the person who hired the cross defendant, who contracted to be the general contractor.  According to the cross complaint, Handcrafted (the cross defendant) stated that its people had skill and experience when in fact, they did not.  Handcrafted alleges that this is no more than the tortification of contract; turning what is really a contract dispute into a fraud case.  The court has some sympathy for the point.  The promise is pretty generic.  But it is enough.  It is not just “we will do a good job,” which is too vague to form a statement of fact upon which reasonable reliance can be had.  It is a specific representation as to the skill levels and experience that could be brought to bear.  That can be proven or not.  If in fact the people on the job had appropriate relevant experience, the statement is probably true; if they were just licensed last week, then it is a false statement, and knowingly false when made to boot.  The bottom line is that a demurrer is the wrong place to test this.  Handcrafted also pleads the economic loss rule.  That rule does not apply to bar fraud causes of action, although the rule may have a lot more bite in the construction context.  But again, a demurrer is the wrong place to test it.

 

The demurrer is OVERRULED.  But this is without prejudice to a motion for summary judgment or adjudication.  Handcrafted has 30 days to answer the complaint.  The court will hold the CMC.

Case Number: 26SMCV00607    Hearing Date: July 15, 2026    Dept: I

This is a demurrer brought by the management company in a case brought by the former tenant and also by the owner, TIC.  Both defendants demur to various causes of action. 

 

According to plaintiff, there were problems that became apparent in July 2024.  Repairs were done, plaintiff agrees, but they were not adequate.  The problems continued and the unit was unhealthful.  Plaintiff continued to complain, but adequate repairs were allegedly never done.  Plaintiff claims that the unit’s condition wound up being so bad that plaintiff was forced to vacate—a constructive eviction. 

 

TIC argues that the breach of contract cause of action should be dismissed as duplicative.  The court agrees that there is some duplication, but the habitability and quiet enjoyment causes of action have other elements.  The causes of action are distinct enough to survive demurrer.  The demurrer on that basis is OVERRULED.  As to habitability, defendant contends that plaintiff failed to allege the requisite elements.  The court disagrees.  Plaintiff alleges that there was toxic mold in the unit and that it persisted even after remediation, and that defendants were aware of the situation because plaintiff informed them of it.  That is enough to withstand a pleading challenge and the demurrer on that ground is OVERRULED.  TIC makes a similar argument regarding quiet enjoyment.  Here, the allegation is that the presence of the toxic mold meant that plaintiff could not enjoy the use of the unit.  Again, that is enough for a pleading motion, and the demurrer on that ground is OVERRULED as well.  As to nuisance, TIC argues (again) that it is duplicative.  Perhaps, but it is not clear, and even if it is duplicative, it does no mischief.  The court will therefore OVERRULE the demurrer on that basis as well.  Finally, as to the constructive eviction cause of action, TIC contends that the elements have not been alleged as required in Bakersfield Laundry Ass’n v. Rubin (1955) 131 Cal.App.2d Supp. 862.  More specifically, TIC argues that plaintiff did not surrender possession within a reasonable time.  In Bakersfield, the appellate division of the Superior Court concluded that waiting six months (with regard to a 1 year lease) was not reasonable.  The court has not seen a whole lot of authority on this point, and Bakersfield is not really often cited for this proposition.  However, the court believes that what is or is not reasonable will depend in large part on the facts and circumstances of the case, which cannot be adjudicated readily on demurrer.  The demurrer is OVERRULED on this ground as well.  TIC also moves to strike the request for punitive damages as to the negligence cause of action, interest, fees, and costs.  The court agrees as to punitive damages for negligence.  Negligence does not allow such an award.  As to the negligence cause of action, and that cause of action only, the motion to strike is GRANTED.  It is DENIED in all other respects.

 

Glen West joins in TIC’s arguments, and the rulings are therefore the same.  But Glen West also contends that it owed plaintiff no duty as a matter of law because it did not own the property.  The court disagrees.  Whether a property manager owes a duty to a tenant will depend on the circumstances.  For example, in some instances a property manager might have no discretion to approve or do much of anything.  But in other cases, the property manager might well have a great deal of discretion.  In the former, it might well be that the lack of any authority will negate a duty of care, but in the latter, a duty of care might well exist.  The court cannot tell from the pleadings.  The demurrer on this basis is OVERRULED.  Glen West also demurs to the nuisance cause of action.  Even though there are no specific allegations about Glen West’s conduct with regard to the nuisance, reading the complaint liberally and broadly (as the court must do on demurrer), it could be that Glen West had sufficient authority to be liable for the tort.  For example, if Glen West deliberately ignored the mold even though it knew that there was toxic mold in the unit and that the remediation would not or did not resolve the problem, that could be enough even though Glen West was not the owner.  Again, the demurrer is OVERRULED.  As to constructive eviction, Glen West seems to be arguing that because plaintiff dismissed the contract cause of action against Glen West (because it was not a signatory to the contract), there can be no constructive eviction either.  The court is not convinced, and Glen West cites not authority for that proposition.  Constructive eviction does not lie solely in contract.  It is not clear to the court that a property manager with sufficient discretion cannot be liable for constructive eviction.  Accordingly, the demurrer is OVERRULED as to that ground as well.  The motion to strike is GRANTED IN PART AND DENIED IN PART for the same reasons that TIC’s motion was partially granted. 

 

In short, as to both demurrers and motions to strike, the demurrers are OVERRULED; the motions to strike are GRANTED IN PART AND DENIED IN PART.  Defendants have 30 days to answer.
