DEPARTMENT 511 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 22STCV35239    Hearing Date: July 21, 2026    Dept: 511

Tentative Ruling

 

Judge Upinder S. Kalra, Department 511

 

HEARING DATE:   July 21, 2026                                     

 

CASE NAME:           Reginald Hoffman v. County of Los Angeles, et al.

 

CASE NO.:                22STCV35239

 

MOTION FOR PRODUCTION OF PEACE OFFICER PERSONNEL RECORDS

 

MOVING PARTY:  Plaintiff Reginald Hoffman

 

RESPONDING PARTY(S): Defendant County of Los Angeles

 

REQUESTED RELIEF:

 

1.      An order compelling production of highly relevant peace officer personnel records, including records from two interrelated internal investigations into Plaintiff (an ICB criminal investigation initiated against Plaintiff that the District Attorney rejected in 2025, and an ongoing IAB administrative investigation), and Personnel of Equity complaints and IAB records concerning Defendant Captain Pilar Chavez for similar harassment and retaliation against two other LASD employees at the East Los Angeles station.

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

Granted in part, denied in part.

 

 

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

 

On November 3, 2022, Plaintiff Reginal Hoffman (Plaintiff) filed a complaint against Defendants County of Los Angeles (County) and Richard Mejia (Mejia) (sometimes collectively Defendants) with five causes of action for: (1) racial discrimination in violation of FEHA; (2) failure to take all reasonable steps to prevent discrimination in violation of FEHA; (3) harassment and hostile work environment in violation of FEHA; (4) retaliation in violation of FEHA; and (5) unlawful retaliation in violation of Lab. Code § 1102.5.

 

Plaintiff, an African-American LASD supervisor, alleges he was subjected to persistent racial harassment, discrimination, and hostile treatment by Mejia and members of the Banditos deputy gang, including being denied earned promotions and demeaned with racist remarks. He further alleges that after reporting racist conduct, deputy-gang retaliation, and unsafe practices—such as Banditos withholding backup for certain deputies—he faced escalating retaliation, adverse assignments, manipulation of promotion processes, and efforts to discredit him. Plaintiff further asserts the County knowingly allowed this discriminatory and retaliatory environment to persist for years, ratifying the conduct and failing to intervene even as Plaintiff suffered significant professional and personal harm.

 

On January 20, 2023, County filed a demurrer with motion to strike which the court OVERRULED the demurrer as to the third and fourth causes of action, DENIED the motion to strike as to portion Nos. 1, 4-9, 11, and GRANTED the motion to strike as to 2, 3, 10, 12-15.

 

On October 4, 2023, Plaintiff filed a stipulation and order for leave to amend which the court GRANTED.

 

On October 16, 2023, Plaintiff filed the operative first amended complaint (FAC). The FAC contains the same five causes of action as the original complaint. It added new Defendant Pilar Chavez (Chavez). It also adds new factual allegations concerning Chavez, expanded retaliation allegations, additional timeframe and events, expanded allegations regarding station culture and racist gangs, and updated damages allegations.

 

On December 6, 2023, County and Mejia filed an answer to the FAC.

 

On June 12, 2025, Chavez filed an answer to the FAC.

 

On May 14, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion for discovery of peace officer personnel records (Pitchess Motion). On July 8, 2026, County timely filed an opposition. On July 13, 2026, Plaintiff timely filed a reply.

 

LEGAL STANDARD:

 

There is a special two-step procedure for obtaining disclosure of peace or custodial officer personnel records.¿ (Warrick v. Superior Court (2005) 35 Cal.4th 1011, 1019.)¿ First, the party seeking disclosure must file a motion that includes all of the following:¿¿ 

  1. Identification of the proceeding in which discovery or disclosure is sought, the party seeking discovery or disclosure, the peace or custodial officer whose records are sought, the governmental agency that has custody and control of the records, and the time and place at which the motion for discovery or disclosure shall be heard.¿¿¿ 
  1. A description of the type of records or information sought.¿¿¿ 
  1. Affidavits showing good cause for the discovery or disclosure sought, setting forth the materiality thereof to the subject matter involved in the pending litigation and stating upon reasonable belief that the governmental agency identified has the records or information from the records.¿¿¿ 

(Evid. Code, § 1043, subd. (b).)¿¿¿ 

 

The “good cause” declaration must be sufficiently specific “to preclude the possibility of [the movant] simply casting about for any helpful information.”¿ (People v. Mooc (2001) 26 Cal.4th 1216, 1226.)¿ The moving party need show only a “plausible factual foundation” for discovery -- i.e., a scenario of officer misconduct that might occur or could have occurred. (Warrick, supra, 35 Cal.4th at p. 1026; see also Blumberg v. Superior Court (2011) 197 Cal.App.4th 1245, 1248 [“‘[T]he good cause requirement embodies a “relatively low threshold” for discovery’ [citation], under which a defendant need demonstrate only ‘a logical link between the defense proposed and the pending charge’ and describe with some specificity ‘how the discovery being sought would support such a defense or how it would impeach the officer’s version of the events’ [citation].”]; Becerrada v. Superior Court (2005) 131 Cal.App.4th 409, 413 [“A showing of good cause is measured by ‘relatively relaxed standards’ that serve to ‘insure the production’ for trial court review of ‘all potentially relevant documents’”].)¿ A declaration by counsel, on information and belief, may be sufficient.¿ (People v. Oppel (1990) 222 Cal.App.3d 1146, 1153, fn. 6.)¿¿¿ 

 

Second, if the court finds good cause, then an in camera examination must be held.¿ (Slayton v. Superior Court (2006) 146 Cal.App.4th 55, 61.)¿ After examining the records in camera, the trial court shall order disclosure of peace officer personnel records that are “‘relevant to the subject matter involved in the pending litigation.’”¿ (People v. Mooc, supra, 26 Cal.4th at p. 1226.)¿ The court must exclude from disclosure “[f]acts sought to be disclosed that are so remote as to make disclosure of little or no practical benefit.”¿ (Evid. Code, § 1045, subd. (b)(2).)¿ “In determining relevance where the issue in litigation concerns the policies or pattern of conduct of the employing agency, the court shall consider whether the information sought may be obtained from other records maintained by the employing agency in the regular course of agency business which would not necessitate the disclosure of individual personnel records.”¿ (Evid. Code, § 1045, subd. (c).)¿ If disclosure is ordered, the court must also order that the disclosed information may not be used “for any purpose other than a court proceeding pursuant to applicable law.”¿ (Evid. Code, § 1045, subd. (e).)¿¿ 

 

ANALYSIS:

 

Plaintiff moves for production of the following records:

 

1.      The full file of the ICIB (Internal Criminal Investigations Bureau) criminal investigation initiated against Plaintiff Sergeant Reginald Hoffman (and members of his team, Deputy Jacob Dominguez, Deputy Alberto Maldonado, Deputy Joel Calderon, Deputy Israel Martinez, Deputy Joanna Palombi, Deputy Johnny Kang) on allegations of timecard fraud, which was concluded before LASD referred it to the Los Angeles County District Attorney who rejected the case in 2025, or, at minimum, all documents and records relating to the initiation, basis, investigation, evidence, findings, disposition, and outcome of that investigation, including but not limited to: (a) all documents initiating the investigation; (b) the complaint or referral underlying the investigation, including any complaint by or attributable to Defendant Pilar Chavez; (c) all investigator logs and case chronologies; (d) all witness and subject interview transcripts, recordings, and notes; (e) all documents obtained or generated by the investigators, including timecards, schedules, and any analyses of Plaintiff’s hours; (f) the investigation summary and final and supplementary reports; (g) the District Attorney’s rejection memorandum or charge evaluation worksheet; and (h) any disposition documents and any discipline imposed, proposed, or recommended.

2.      The full file of the IAB (Internal Affairs Bureau) administrative investigation initiated by LASD against Plaintiff Sergeant Reginald Hoffman (and members of his team, Deputy Jacob Dominguez, Deputy Alberto Maldonado, Deputy Joel Calderon, Deputy Israel Martinez, Deputy Joanna Palombi, Deputy Johnny Kang)) on the same or related timecard allegations, or, at minimum, all completed portions of the investigation and all existing documents relating to the initiation, basis, investigation, evidence, findings, proposed disposition, and any proposed or recommended discipline, including but not limited to: (a) all documents initiating the investigation, including any documents used to open the IAB investigation against Plaintiff; (b) all investigator logs and case chronologies maintained by any sergeant, lieutenant, or captain investigator or supervising investigator assigned to the matter; (c) all witness and subject interview transcripts, recordings, and notes (Plaintiff understands all interviews are complete); (d) all documents obtained or generated by the investigators; (e) the investigation summary or final report; and (f) any disposition documents, findings, and any discipline imposed, proposed, or recommended.

3.      The full file of the Personnel of Equity (“POE”) complaint filed against Defendant Captain Pilar Chavez by Lieutenant Shawn O’Donnell (POE intake date January 19, 2023; incident date January 18, 2023), and all related IAB records arising from or relating to that POE complaint, or, at minimum, all documents and records relating to the initiation, basis, investigation, witness interviews, evidence, findings, disposition, and any discipline imposed, proposed, or recommended, including all documents initiating the investigation, investigator logs, interview transcripts, the investigation summary, disposition documents, and any discipline imposed, proposed, or recommended.

4.      The full file of the Personnel of Equity (“POE”) complaint filed against Defendant Captain Pilar Chavez by Matt Taylor (POE filed February 15, 2023; incident date January 18, 2023), and all related IAB records arising from or relating to that POE complaint, or, at minimum, all documents and records relating to the initiation, basis, investigation, witness interviews, evidence, findings, disposition, and any discipline imposed, proposed, or recommended including all documents initiating the investigation, investigator logs, interview transcripts, the investigation summary, disposition documents, and any discipline imposed, proposed, or recommended.

Plaintiff contends good cause exists since all of the documents requested are relevant to Plaintiff’s allegations and causes of action including for whistleblower retaliation and retaliation in violation of FEHA, racial discrimination, harassment, and failure to prevent discrimination. He adds that he satisfied the statutory requirements supporting this motion and that he must have access to this information to ensure a fair trial.

 

County argues: (1) Plaintiff has failed to establish good cause for the discovery

sought under Evidence Code Section 1043 and 1045; (2) the Motion is not tailored to any specific officer misconduct and instead seeks entire investigative files as an impermissible fishing expedition; (3) the supporting declaration, made on information and belief, fails to set forth the plausible factual foundation of materiality required by Warrick v. Superior Court, particularly as to the two third-party POE complaints; (4) the Motion improperly seeks the confidential personnel and investigative records of six non-party deputies against whom no misconduct is alleged; (5) the IAB administrative investigation remains ongoing and no findings or discipline have been issues, rendering production premature; (6) portions of the records sought are not peace officer personnel records within the meaning of Penal Code section 832.8 and/or are in the custody of a separate agency; and (7) any disclosure, must, in all events, be limited to in camera review under Evidence Code section 1045, subject to the mandatory exclusions of section 1045(b) and an appropriate protective order.

 

Plaintiff replies he seeks records at the center of his FEHA and whistleblower-retaliation claims, Plaintiff has made his showing of a relatively relaxed good-cause standard with a plausible factual foundation and logical link to the claims. He adds that any document-specific concerns can be addressed during in camera review.

 

1. Plaintiff Sergeant Reginald Hoffman – ICIB/IAB Investigation

GRANTED as to in camera review. Plaintiff has demonstrated good cause under Evidence Code sections 1043 and 1045. The requested ICIB and related IAB investigations concern Plaintiff himself and arise from the very investigations alleged to have been initiated in retaliation for his protected activity and race. Plaintiff has established a plausible factual foundation that the records are material to his FEHA and Labor Code section 1102.5 claims. (Warrick v. Superior Court (2005) 35 Cal.4th 1011.) Because Plaintiff is the subject of the investigations and a percipient witness to the underlying events, in camera review shall include those portions of the investigative files relevant to the claims and defenses in this action. To the extent disclosure is ultimately ordered, the court will determine the proper scope following its in camera review.

 

2. Plaintiff's Team (Deputies Jacob Dominguez, Alberto Maldonado, Joel Calderon, Israel Martinez, Joanna Palombi, and Johnny Kang)

DENIED. Plaintiff has not established good cause for in camera review of these deputies' personnel records. Although the deputies were also subjects of the same investigations, Plaintiff has not identified any specific misconduct by these deputies, any similar complaints contained within their personnel files, or articulated how their confidential personnel records are material to the claims at issue. The request is therefore overbroad and does not satisfy the materiality showing required by Evidence Code section 1043 and Warrick. The mere fact that these deputies participated in the same investigation does not justify in camera review of their personnel files.

 

3. Captain Pilar Chavez – Prior POE Complaints by Lieutenant Shawn O'Donnell and Matt Taylor

GRANTED as to in camera review. Plaintiff has made the relatively low threshold showing of good cause required for in camera review. Plaintiff specifically identifies two contemporaneous complaints against Captain Chavez alleging similar harassment and retaliation, identifies the complainants, relevant dates, and articulates that the complaints are offered as evidence of motive, intent, pattern, and absence of mistake in support of Plaintiff's FEHA retaliation and discrimination claims. This constitutes a plausible factual foundation demonstrating materiality under Warrick. Moreover, Evidence Code sections 1043 and 1045 do not limit in camera review to officers who directly participated in the events involving the plaintiff where the requested records are otherwise shown to be material. (Riske v. Superior Court (2016) 6 Cal.App.5th 647.) Because Captain Chavez was not merely a percipient witness to the underlying investigations but the subject of separate similar complaints, any subsequent disclosure, if warranted after in camera review, will ordinarily be limited to the identifying information of prior complainants and witnesses absent a further showing that such disclosure would be insufficient. (Haggerty v. Superior Court (2004) 117 Cal.App.4th 1079.)

 

 

CONCLUSION:

 

            For the foregoing reasons, the Court decides the pending motion as follows:

 

Granted in part, denied in part.

 

Custodian of records to schedule in camera review with the court.

 

Moving party is to give notice.

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Case Number: 25STCV03540    Hearing Date: July 21, 2026    Dept: 511

Tentative Ruling

 

Judge Upinder S. Kalra, Department 511

 

HEARING DATE:   July 21, 2026                                     

 

CASE NAME:           Edmy X. Torres v. General Motors LLC

 

CASE NO.:                25STCV03540

 

MOTION FOR ATTORNEYS’ FEES

 

MOVING PARTY:  Plaintiff Edmy X. Torres

 

RESPONDING PARTY(S): None

 

REQUESTED RELIEF:

 

1.      An order awarding $21,031.60 in attorneys’ fees.

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

1.      Motion for attorneys’ fees is granted, as modified, for a total award of $18,031.60.

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

 

On February 7, 2025, Plaintiff Edmy X. Torres (Plaintiff) filed a Song-Beverly complaint against Defendant General Motors LLC (Defendant) with causes of action for: (1) violation of Civ. Code § 1793.2(d); (2) violation of Civ. Code § 1793.2(b); (3) violation of Civ. Code § 1793.2(a)(3); (4) breach of express written warranty; and (5) breach of implied warranty of merchantability.

 

On March 17, 2025, Defendant filed an answer.

 

On February 25, 2026, Plaintiff filed a notice of settlement.

 

On March 17, 206, Plaintiff filed a memorandum of costs.

 

On April 20, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion for attorneys’ fees. Oppositions were due on or before July 8, 2026. As of July 16, 2026, the court has not received an opposition.

 

LEGAL STANDARD:

 

A prevailing buyer in an action under Song-Beverly “shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees based on actual time expended, determined by the Court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.”¿ (Civ. Code, § 1794(d).) By¿permitting¿buyers who prevail under Song-Beverly to recover their attorneys’ fees, “our Legislature has provided injured consumers strong encouragement to seek legal redress in a situation in which a lawsuit might not otherwise have been economically feasible.” (Murillo v. Fleetwood Enterprises, Inc. (1998) 17 Cal.4th 985, 994.)¿¿¿¿ 

¿¿ 

The prevailing party has the burden of showing that the requested attorney fees are reasonable. (Robertson v. Fleetwood Travel Trailers of California Inc.¿(2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 785, 817.) The party seeking attorney fees “is not necessarily entitled to the compensation of the value of attorney services according to [his or her] own notion or to the full extent claimed¿. . . .”¿ (Levy v. Toyota Motor Sales, USA, Inc.¿(1992) 4 Cal.App.4th 807, 816.) If the “time expended or the monetary charge being made for the time expended are not reasonable under all circumstances, then the court must take this into account and award fees in a lesser amount.” (Nightingale v. Hyundai Motor America¿(1994) 31 Cal.App.4th 99, 104.)¿¿¿¿ 

¿¿ 

A calculation of attorneys’ fees for a Song-Beverly action begins with the “lodestar” approach, under which the Court fixes the lodestar at “the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate.” (Margolin v. Regional Planning Com.¿(1982) 134 Cal.App.3d 999, 1004-1005.) “California courts have consistently held that a computation of time spent on a case and the reasonable value of that time is fundamental to a determination of an appropriate attorneys’ fee award.” (Ibid.)¿¿¿ 

¿¿ 

“It is appropriate for a trial court to reduce a fee award based on its reasonable determination that a routine, non-complex case was overstaffed to a degree that significant inefficiencies and inflated fees resulted.”¿ (Morris v. Hyundai Motor America¿(2019) 41 Cal.App.5th 24, 39.) It is also¿appropriate to¿reduce a fee award based on “inefficient or duplicative efforts” in the billing record. (Id. at p. 38.) However, the analysis must be “reasonably specific” and cannot rely on general notions about the fairness of the fee award. (Kerkeles¿v. City of San Jose¿(2015) 243 Cal.App.4th 88, 102.) Moreover, in conducting the analysis, courts are not¿permitted¿to tie any reductions in the fee¿award¿to some proportion of the buyer’s damages recovery. (Warren v. Kia Motors America, Inc.¿(2018) 30 Cal.App.5th 24, 39.)¿¿¿¿ 

¿¿ 

The lodestar figure may also be adjusted, based on consideration of factors specific to the case,¿in order to¿fix the fee at the¿fair market value¿for the legal services provided. (Serrano v. Priest¿(1977) 20 Cal.3d 25, 49;¿PLCM Group, Inc. v. Drexler¿(2000) 22 Cal.App.4th 1084, 1095.)¿The factors considered in¿determining¿the modification of the lodestar include the nature and difficulty of the litigation, the amount of money involved, the skill¿required¿and employed to handle the case, the attention given, the success or failure,¿and other circumstances in the case. (EnPalm, LLC v.¿Teitler¿Family Trust¿(2008) 162 Cal. App. 4th 770, 774 (emphasis in original).)¿A negative modifier was¿appropriate when¿duplicative work had been performed. (Thayer v. Wells Fargo Bank,¿N.A.¿(2001) 92 Cal.App.4th 819.)¿¿ 

 

ANALYSIS:

 

  1. Prevailing Party¿¿¿ 

 

On November 6, 2025, the parties settled the matter in principle. (Declaration of Joseph Liu (Liu Decl.) ¶ 22.) The parties finalized and executed a long form settlement agreement on January 12, 2026. (Id.at ¶ 23.) The settlement agreement provides that Plaintiff is the prevailing party for purposes of this fee motion. (Ibid.) Therefore, Plaintiff is the prevailing party.

 

  1. Lodestar Fees¿¿¿ 

 

The lodestar method looks at the time spent on a matter multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate. (Serrano, supra¿20 Cal.3d at 49).¿The two-step process begins with the lodestar method, which is the time spent on the matter multiple by the hourly rate. After the lodestar method, the second step is¿determining¿whether a multiplier should be applied. The factors that¿Courts¿look at to determine if a multiplier is reasonable are: 1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, (4) the contingent nature of the fee award.” (Ketchum v. Moses¿(2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132).¿¿ 

 

  1. Reasonableness of Hourly Rate 

 

Plaintiffs contend their hourly rates of $540 (partner), $515-525 (senior associate), $215 (paralegal) per hour are reasonable due to their years of experience in this area of law and as indicated in their incredible result. 

 

“In¿determining¿hourly rates, the court must look to the “prevailing market rates in the relevant community.” (Bell v. Clackamas County¿(9th Cir.2003) 341 F.3d 858, 868.) The rates of comparable attorneys in the forum district are usually used. (See¿Gates v. Deukmejian¿(9th Cir.1992) 987 F.2d 1392, 1405.) In making its calculation, the court should also consider the experience, skill, and reputation of the attorney requesting fees.” (Heritage Pacific Financial, LLC v. Monroy¿(2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 972, 1009.)¿¿ 

¿ 

The court finds that these hourly rates are reasonable based on the hourly rate of similarly situated attorneys in the Los Angeles area. Notably, Plaintiffs provide evidence of the attorneys’ and paralegals’ experience and previous rates. (Liu Decl. ¶¶ 26-31.) Based on the Laffey Matrix, attorneys with similar years of experience as Plaintiff’s counsel as identified above have comparable, if not a higher hourly rate.

 

Thus, the hourly rates are appropriate.

 

  1. Reasonableness of Hours Spent 

 

 Plaintiff seeks to recover for 39.5 hours of work. (Liu Decl. ¶ 40, Exhibit 9.)

To¿determine¿if the requested amount is reasonable, California courts¿utilize¿the lodestar method. The two-step process begins with the lodestar method, which is the time spent on the matter multiple by the hourly rate. After the lodestar method, the second step is¿determining¿whether a multiplier should be applied. The factors that¿Courts¿look at to determine if a multiplier is reasonable are: 1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, (4) the contingent nature of the fee award.” (Ketchum v. Moses¿(2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132.)¿¿¿ 

 

Although a verified fee bill is “prima facie evidence the costs, expenses and services listed were necessarily incurred,” (Hadley v. Krepel¿(1985) 167 Cal.App.3d 677, 682), ultimately,¿Counsel¿still has the burden to¿demonstrate¿the reasonableness of charges.¿(Mikhaeilpoor,¿supra,¿48 Cal.App.5th¿at p.¿247.)

 

Here, there is no opposition to the fees sought. However, that means the proposed time to review and respond to an opposition ($3,000) is not warranted. Plaintiffs have provided a fee bill. Here, the Court has reviewed the detailed fee bill with an eye for excessive, duplicative or unreasonable charges as contended by Defendant. After a careful review, the Court finds that the hours were reasonable.

 

 

Accordingly, the court GRANTS Plaintiff’s motion for attorney’s fees, as modified, for an award of $18,031.60.

 

CONCLUSION:

 

            For the foregoing reasons, the Court decides the pending motion as follows:

 

1.      Motion for attorneys’ fees is granted, as modified, for a total award of $18,031.60.

Moving party is to give notice.

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Case Number: 25STCV13419    Hearing Date: July 21, 2026    Dept: 511

Tentative Ruling

 

Judge Upinder S. Kalra, Department 511

 

HEARING DATE:   July 21, 2026                                     

 

CASE NAME:           Gossamer Fourteen, LLC v. Sang Chyun aka Daniel Chyun

 

CASE NO.:                25STCV13419

 

MOTION FOR ATTORNEYS’ FEES

 

MOVING PARTY:  Plaintiff Gossamer Fourteen, LLC

 

RESPONDING PARTY(S): None

 

REQUESTED RELIEF:

 

1.      An order awarding $22,012.50 in attorneys’ fees.

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

1.      Motion for attorneys’ fees is granted for a total award of $$22,012.50.

 

 

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

 

On May 7, 2025, Plaintiff Gossamer Fourteen, LLC (Plaintiff) filed a complaint against Defendant Sang Chyun aka Daniel Chyun (Defendant) with two causes of action for: (1) breach of contract; and (2) promissory fraud.

 

According to the complaint, the parties entered into a written lease agreement for commercial space on July 1, 2019. Plaintiff alleges that Defendant promised to do work to maintain and repair the other units on the property in exchange for a rent reduction. Plaintiff further alleges that Defendant breached the agreements in various ways and his promise was falsely made to induce Plaintiff to lease the premises to Defendant.

 

On June 4, 2025, Defendant filed an answer.

 

On October 17, 2025, a non-jury trial commenced and concluded. The court awarded judgment for Plaintiff on the first cause of action for breach of contract for $37,310.00 and judgment for Defendant on the second cause of action.

 

On December 10, 2025, the court entered judgment for $38,976.17 in damages and prejudgment interest with attorneys’ fees and costs per code.

 

On December 16, 2025, Plaintiff filed a memorandum of costs seeking $772.31 in costs.

 

On January 30, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion for attorneys’ fees. On April 24, 2026, Plaintiff filed a supplemental declaration regarding electronic service in support of their motion. On April 27, 2026, Plaintiff filed a second supplemental declaration.

 

On April 30, 2026, the motion was called for hearing. After oral argument, the court continued the hearing to July 21, 2026 to afford Defendant an opportunity to file an opposition.[1]

 

As of July 16, 2026, the court has not received any opposition.

 

LEGAL STANDARD:

 

With respect to attorney fees and costs, unless they are specifically provided for by statute (e.g., Cal.¿Code Civ.¿Proc. (CCP)¿§§ 1032, et seq.), the measure and mode of compensation of attorneys and counselors at law is left to the agreement, express or implied, of the parties.¿(CCP § 1021.)¿  

 

The court, upon notice and motion by a party, shall¿determine¿the prevailing party and shall fix, as an element of the costs of¿suit, the reasonable attorney fees.¿ (Civ. Code § 1717(a), (b).)¿ Any notice of motion to claim attorney fees as an element of costs under shall be served and filed before or at the same time the memorandum of costs is served and filed; if only attorney fees are claimed as costs, the notice of motion shall be served and filed within the time specified in CRC 3.1700 for filing a memorandum of costs. (CRC 3.1702;¿Gunlock Corp. v. Walk on Water, Inc.¿(1993) 15 Cal.App.4th 1301, 1303, fn. 1.)¿¿¿ 

¿ 

“It is well¿established¿that the determination of what constitutes reasonable attorney fees is committed to the discretion of the trial court, whose decision cannot be reversed in the absence of an abuse of discretion. [Citation.]”¿ (Melnyk v. Robledo¿(1976) 64 Cal.App.3d 618, 623 624.) The fee setting inquiry in California ordinarily “begins with the ‘lodestar’ [method], i.e., the number of hours¿reasonably expended¿multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate.”¿ (Graciano v. Robinson Ford Sales, Inc.¿(2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 140, 154.)¿ “[A] computation of time spent on a case and the reasonable value of that time is fundamental to a determination of an appropriate attorneys’ fee award.”¿ (Margolin v. Reg’l Planning Comm’n¿(1982) 134 Cal.App.3d 999, 1004.)¿ The lodestar figure may then be adjusted, based on consideration of factors specific to the case,¿in order to¿fix the fee at the¿fair market value¿for the legal services provided.¿ (See¿Serrano v. Priest¿(1977) 20 Cal.3d 25, 49 [discussing factors relevant to proper attorneys’ fees award].)¿ Such an approach anchors the trial court’s analysis to an objective determination of the value of the attorney’s services, ensuring that the amount awarded is not arbitrary.¿ (Id. at 48, fn. 23.)¿ The factors considered in determining the modification of the lodestar include “(1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, (4) the contingent nature of the fee award.”¿ (Mountjoy v. Bank of Am.¿(2016) 245 Cal.App.4th 266, 271.)¿¿¿¿ 

 

In challenging attorney fees as excessive because too many hours of work are claimed, it is the burden of the challenging party to point to the specific items challenged, with a sufficient argument and citations to the evidence.¿ (Premier Medical Management Systems, Inc. v. California Ins. Guaranty Assoc. (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 550, 564.)¿ General arguments that¿fees¿claimed are excessive, duplicative, or unrelated do not suffice.¿ (Ibid.)¿ 

 

ANALYSIS:

 

Plaintiff is the prevailing party pursuant to the judgment after bench trial and can recover attorneys’ fees pursuant to their lease agreement.

 

  1. Rate¿ 

 

Plaintiff provides that following hourly rates: $635 for Mark Share; $475 for Stacy Bandhold; and $395 for Craig Newton.

 

“In¿determining¿hourly rates, the court must look to the “prevailing market rates in the relevant community.” (Bell v. Clackamas County¿(9th Cir.2003) 341 F.3d 858, 868.) The rates of comparable attorneys in the forum district are usually used. (See¿Gates v. Deukmejian¿(9th Cir.1992) 987 F.2d 1392, 1405.) In making its calculation, the court should also consider the experience, skill, and reputation of the attorney requesting¿fees.”¿(Heritage Pacific Financial, LLC v. Monroy¿(2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 972, 1009.)

 

The hourly rates here appear appropriate.¿However, billing at a high rate comes with the expectation that the attorney also works in an efficient manner that reflects the premium paid for his or her services. The court considers this fact in addressing the reasonableness of the hours¿expended, below.¿¿

 

 

  1. Hours¿ 

 

Plaintiff seeks to recover 41.50 hours of work on this matter. Plaintiffs have provided a fee bill in support.

 

“The basis for the trial court's calculation must be the actual hours counsel has devoted to the case, less those that result from inefficient or duplicative use of time.”¿ (Horsford v. Board¿Of¿Trustees¿Of¿California State University¿(2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 359, 395.)¿ “The law is clear, however, that an award of attorney fees may be based on counsel's declarations, without¿production of detailed time records.”¿ (Raining Data Corp. v. Barrenechea¿(2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 1363, 1375.)¿

 

Here, there is no opposition to the fees sought. Nonetheless, the Court has reviewed the detailed fee bill with an eye for excessive, duplicative or unreasonable charges as contended by Defendant. After a careful review, the Court finds that the hours were reasonable.

 

 

CONCLUSION:

 

            For the foregoing reasons, the Court decides the pending motion as follows:

 

1.      Motion for attorneys’ fees is granted for a total award of $$22,012.50.

 

Moving party is to give notice.

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

 

On September 15, 2025, Plaintiffs Mark Kim, Euphemia Yue Fay Lee, New Four Corners Mgmt, LLC, and JPG & MK Corp Management (Plaintiffs) filed a complaint against Defendants Paul Baik, PCB Bank, Hwa Chong Kang, Sean Seunguk Youn, Kyu Hun Kwak, Central Escrow LA, Inc., and Julia Kim (Defendants) with eleven causes of action for: (1) unlawful business practices in violation of Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200; (2) fraud; (3) breach of implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing; (4) intentional misrepresentation; (5) negligent misrepresentation; (6) conspiracy to defraud; (7) constructive fraud; (8) usury violation; (9) money had and received; (10) small business truth-in-lending law violation; and (11) breach of fiduciary duty. 

 

According to the complaint, Plaintiffs claim they were targeted in a predatory loan sharkstyle lending scheme orchestrated by multiple defendants, including PCB Bank, loan officer Paul Baik, private lenders, and Central Escrow LA with escrow agent Julia Kim. Plaintiffs allege that Defendants induced them into taking a series of high interest, fee heavy loans secured by their real properties, beginning with an approximately $800,000 loan in 2022. They assert that Defendants misrepresented loan terms, concealed excessive fees, and structured loans so that balloon payments could not realistically be satisfied, forcing Plaintiffs into subsequent loans (L2 around $3 million, and L3 for $1 million) and “extensions” requiring tens of thousands of dollars in additional undisclosed fees. Plaintiffs contend that Defendants acted in concert, failed to disclose material terms, charged usurious interest, and used escrow to facilitate the scheme. They assert damages including loss of loan proceeds, excessive fees, risk of property loss, and financial harm from being trapped in an “unending cycle” of debt. 

 

On October 23, 2025, Defendants Kyu Hun Kwak (Kwak), Sean Sunguk Youn (Youn) and Paul Baik (Baik) each filed general denials. 

 

On December 31, 2025, Plaintiffs filed a first amended complaint (FAC). The FAC expands on previous allegations regarding the loan scheme and excludes Julia Kim from the first cause of action. 

 

On January 29, 2026, Defendants Central Escrow LA, Inc. and Julia Kim (Moving Defendants) filed a demurrer to the FAC which the court SUSTAINED with leave to amend. 

 

On February 13, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a request for dismissal as to Defendant PCB Bank only.

On June 8, 2026, Defendant Kang filed the instant demurrer to the SAC.

 

On June 23, 2026, Baik filed a notice of deceased defendant Kwak.

 

On July 7, 2026, Central Escrow and Kim filed an answer to the SAC.

LEGAL STANDARD:

 

Meet and Confer 

 

Prior to filing a demurrer, the demurring party is required to satisfy their meet and confer obligations pursuant to Code of Civ. Proc. §430.41, and demonstrate that they so satisfied their meet and confer obligation by submitting a declaration pursuant to Code of Civ. Proc. §430.41(a)(2) & (3). ¿The meet and confer requirement also applies to motions to strike. (CCP § 435.5.)¿

 

Here, the meet and confer requirements are not met. Still, insufficient meet and confer is not a reason, alone, to sustain or overrule a demurrer or to deny a motion to strike.

 

Demurrer – Sufficiency of Facts 

 

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action.¿(Hahn v. Mirda¿(2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context.¿In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice.¿(Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)¿“A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. …. The only issue involved in a demurrer hearing is whether the complaint, as it stands, unconnected with extraneous matters, states a cause of action.”¿(Hahn¿147 Cal.App.4th at 747.)¿¿ 

¿ 

When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context, accepting the alleged facts as true. (Nolte v. Cedars-Sinai Medical Center (2015) 236 Cal.App.4th 1401, 1406.) Courts also consider exhibits attached to the complaint and incorporated by reference. (See Frantz v. Blackwell (1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 91, 94 (Frantz).)¿ 

 

Demurrer – Uncertainty

 

CCP § 430.10(f) provides that a party may demurrer to a pleading because: “The pleading is uncertain. As used in this subdivision, “uncertain” includes ambiguous and unintelligible.”

 

However, “demurrers for uncertainty are disfavored, and are granted only if the pleading is so incomprehensible that a defendant cannot reasonably respond.” (Morris v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A. (2022) 78 Cal.App.5th 279, 292.) “A demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Chen v. Berenjian (2019) 33 Cal.App.5th 811, 822.) “A demurrer for uncertainty should be overruled when the facts as to which the complaint is uncertain are presumptively within the defendant’s knowledge.” (Ibid.)

 

Motion to Strike 

 

The court may, upon a motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (CCP § 436(a).) The court may also strike all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Id., § 436(b).) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (Id.¿§¿437.)¿“When the defect which justifies striking a complaint is capable of cure, the court should allow leave to amend.” (Vaccaro v. Kaiman¿(1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 761, 768.)¿¿¿

 

ANALYSIS:

 

Request for Judicial Notice

 

The court DENIES Baik & Youn’s request for judicial notice as superfluous to this ruling.

 

Demurrer

 

First Cause of Action – Fraud

 

Baik & Youn contend this claim fails because: (1) the SAC does not allege any specific or actionable statements by them; (2) the SAC confusingly jumbles all Defendants together in the fraud claim and makes no distinction as to who did or said what; and (3) any representations that are alleged are non-actionable opinions or puffery instead of fact. They also contend some of this claim is time-barred.

 

Kang contends this claim fails because the allegations are general and conclusory without articulating who said what. Additionally, Kang contends there are no allegations she ever spoke with Plaintiffs, met with Plaintiffs, sent Plaintiffs anything in writing, made any oral representations, or directed any communication to Plaintiffs of any kind before any loan was made.

 

Plaintiff concedes that some of the claims are time-barred and request leave to amend. They also argue, among substantial block quotes from the SAC, that the claims are sufficiently plead and characterization of puffery is an attempt to justify highway robbery.

 

Baik & Youn reply the concession that the claim is time-barred destroys this claim.

 

Kang replies that block quoting from the SAC is not an opposition and the claim otherwise relies on prediction of future events.

 

“The elements of fraud, which give rise to the tort action for deceit, are (a) misrepresentation (false representation, concealment, or nondisclosure); (b) knowledge of falsity (or 'scienter'); (c) intent to defraud, i.e., to induce reliance; (d) justifiable reliance; and (e) resulting damage.” (Lazar v. Superior Court¿(1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 638.)¿¿¿ 

 

“In California, fraud must be pled specifically; general and conclusory allegations do not suffice…this particularity requirements necessitates pleading¿facts¿which show how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were tenders.” (Lazar v. Superior Court¿(1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 645;¿Cansino v. Bank of America¿(2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 1462, 1469;¿Perlas v. GMAC Mort., LLC¿(2010) 187 Cal.App.4th 429, 434 [requiring plaintiffs who claim fraud against a corporation to allege the names of the persons who made the misrepresentations, their authority to speak for the corporation, to whom they spoke, what they said or wrote, and when it was said or written.]) Fraud allegations need not be liberally construed, general pleading of the legal conclusion of fraud is insufficient, and every element of the cause of action for fraud must be alleged fully,¿factually¿and specifically. (Wilhelm v. Pray, Price, Williams & Russell¿(1986) 186 Cal. App. 3d 1324, 1331.)¿¿ 

 

Based on Plaintiffs’ concession that the fraud claims are time-barred, the court SUSTAINS the demurrers to the first cause of action.

 

Second Cause of Action – Breach of Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing

 

Baik & Youn contend this claim fails because: (1) Baik is not a party to any agreement so the claim fails as to him; (2) as to Youn, there is no tort recovery in ordinary commercial lending relationships; (3) all the allegations pertain to statements made before the loan agreements were executed; and (4) the claim is confusing since it groups three loans together making it impossible to determine who did what with respect to which contract.

 

Kang contends this claim fails because it lacks a well-plead contract and otherwise did not plead interference allegations coherently.

 

Plaintiffs argue that a principal is liable for the acts of his agent and that a mortgage loan broker acts in a fiduciary capacity.

 

Baik & Youn reiterate their arguments on reply.

 

Kang replies that Plaintiffs conceded her arguments by asking to amend to add the loan documents.

 

Every contract¿contains¿an implied¿covenant¿of¿good faith¿and fair dealing that neither party will do anything to interfere with the other party’s right to receive the benefits of the agreement. (Howard v. American¿Nat’l¿Fire Ins. Co.¿(2010) 187 Cal.App.4th 498, 528.)¿¿¿ 

¿¿¿ 

The elements of a claim for breach of implied¿covenant¿of¿good faith¿and fair dealing are:¿(1) the existence of a contractual relationship; (2) an implied duty; (3) breach; and (4) causation of damages. (See, e.g.,¿Smith v. City and County of San Francisco¿(1990) 225 Cal.App.3d 38, 49;¿Careau¿& Co. v. Security Pacific Business Credit, Inc.¿(1990) 222 Cal.App.3d 1371,¿1395¿[“allegations¿which assert such a claim must show that the conduct of the defendant…demonstrates a failure or refusal to discharge contractual responsibilities, prompted … by a conscious and deliberate act, which unfairly frustrates the agreed common purposes and disappoints the reasonable expectations of the other party thereby depriving that party of the benefits of the agreement. Just what conduct will meet¿this criteria¿must be¿determined¿on a¿case by case¿basis….”].)¿“If the allegations do not go beyond the statement of a mere contract breach and, relying on the same alleged acts, simply seek the same damages or other relief already claimed in a companion contract cause of action, they may be disregarded as superfluous as no additional claim is actually stated.” (Careau, supra, 222 Cal.App.3d at p. 1395.)¿Additionally, a plaintiff must allege a “special relationship” between the parties that¿warrants¿imposing tort damages for, what¿is essentially, breach¿of contract. (Id.¿at p.¿1398-1400.)¿

 

In the absence of a contract, this claim fails.

 

As such, the court SUSTAINS the demurrers to the second cause of action.

 

 

Third Cause of Action – Intentional Misrepresentation

 

These are the same arguments as for the first cause of action.

 

For the same reason as the first cause of action, the court SUSTAINS the demurrers to the third cause of action.

 

Fourth Cause of Action – Negligent Misrepresentation

 

These are the same arguments as for the first cause of action.

 

For the same reason as the first cause of action, the court SUSTAINS the demurrers to the third cause of action.

 

Fifth Cause of Action – Conspiracy to Defraud

 

Baik & Youn contend this claim fails because the underlying fraud claims fail and otherwise lack facts showing a relationship with any other defendant or how they conspired or agreed to conspire.

 

Kang contends this claim fails because the underlying fraud claims fail and is otherwise insufficiently plead.

 

Plaintiffs did not address this in their opposition.

 

A claim for conspiracy is not a standalone cause of action. (Applied Equipment Corp. v. Litton Saudi Arabia Ltd. (1994) 7 Cal.4th 503, 511.) “It must be activated by the commission of an actual tort.” (Ibid.)

 

Here, this claim depends on the insufficient fraud claims.

 

Accordingly, the court SUSTAINS the demurrers to the fifth cause of action.

 

Sixth Cause of Action – Constructive Fraud

 

Baik & Youn contend this claim fails because there are no allegations they had a fiduciary or confidential relationship with Plaintiffs.

 

Kang contends this claim fails because an arm’s-length lender-borrower transaction, without more, does not create a fiduciary relationship.

 

Plaintiffs did not address this in their opposition.

 

As a fraud claim, this claim is also subject to a three year statute of limitations. (CCP § 338(d).)

 

Thus, for the same reasons articulated in the first cause of action, the court SUSTAINS the demurrers to the sixth cause of action.

 

Seventh Cause of Action – Usury Violation

 

Baik & Youn contend this claim fails because Baik is not described anywhere as a lender of money, and, if Baik & Youn were brokers, then they are exempt from usury liability by working within the scope of their agency for a bank. They also contend this claim is time-barred.

 

Kang contends this claim fails because she is not tied to any specific loan or any specific rate of interest and the SAC pins the wrongful conduct on a dismissed defendant (PCB Bank).

 

Plaintiffs concede that this claim is time-barred and request leave to amend. Plaintiffs also argue that the loans were funded by Defendants, not PCB.

 

Baik & Youn reiterate their arguments on reply.

 

Kang replies that Plaintiffs concede her point and requested leave to amend.

 

In light of the concessions by Plaintiffs, the court SUSTAINS the demurrers to the seventh cause of action.

 

Eighth Cause of Action – Money Had and Received

 

Baik & Youn contend this claim fails because the claims are uncertain, it is unclear what money either of them received from Plaintiffs, Plaintiff’s did receive funds from the loans as alleged in the SAC, and there is no certain sum of money alleged.

 

Kang contends this claim fails because Plaintiffs did not allege a certain sum Kang received, when she received it, or any factual basis that the money was intended to benefit Plaintiffs.

 

Plaintiffs admit they inadvertently included this claim while previously agreeing to withdraw it.

 

Accordingly, the court SUSTAINS the demurrers to the eighth cause of action without leave to amend.

 

Ninth Cause of Action – Small Business Truth-In-Lending Law (Cal. Fin. Code § 22800, et seq.)

 

Baik & Youn contend this claim fails because the loans are secured by real property – an exemption under the statute – and there is no private right of action under the statute.

 

Kang contends this claim fails because she is not a provider under the statute.

 

Plaintiffs will withdraw this claim.

 

Accordingly, the court SUSTAINS the demurrers to the ninth cause of action without leave to amend.[1]

 

Tenth Cause of Action – Breach of Fiduciary Duty

 

Baik & Youn contend this claim fails because there is no alleged fiduciary duty as a commercial lender, or the allegations are contradictory to their roles.

 

Kang contends this claim fails because there is no alleged fiduciary duty.

 

Plaintiffs arguments here are the same as for the second cause of action.

 

Baik & Youn reply that as loan officers, they owe no fiduciary duty to a borrower in an arms-length loan transaction.

 

Kang’s reply raises the same points as Baik & Youn.

 

The¿elements of a claim for breach of fiduciary duty are (1) the existence of a fiduciary relationship, (2) its breach, and (3) damage proximately caused by that breach.” (O'Neal v. Stanislaus County Employees' Retirement Assn.¿(2017) 8 Cal.App.5th 1184, 1215.)¿¿“A fiduciary relationship is any relation existing between parties to a transaction wherein one of the parties is duty bound to act with the utmost good faith for the benefit of the other party.” (Wolf v. Superior¿Court¿(2003) 107 Cal.App.4th 25, 29 (Wolf).)¿A¿person must “knowingly undertake to act on behalf and for the benefit of another” or “must enter into a relationship which imposes that undertaking as a matter of law.” (City of Hope National Medical Center v. Genentech¿(2008) 43 Cal.4th 375, 386 (City of Hope)¿[finding no¿indication¿that the defendant¿entered into¿the contract “with the view of acting primarily for the benefit” of the plaintiff.])¿“Traditional examples of fiduciary relationships in the commercial context include trustee/beneficiary, directors and majority shareholders of a corporation, business partners, joint adventurers, and agent/principal.” (Wolf, supra,¿at p. 30.)¿¿ 

 

At issue is whether there is a fiduciary relationship alleged between Plaintiffs Baik, Youn, and Kang.

 

First, Baik is alleged to be a loan officer of PCB (a bank). (SAC ¶ 30.) There are no allegations that he exceeded this role as PCB’s loan officer. (See Rufini v. CitiMortgage, Inc. (2014) 227 Cal.App.4th 229, 312 [“a financial institution owes no duty of care to a borrower when the institution’s involvement in the loan transaction does not exceed the scope of its conventional role as a mere lender of money.”]) Therefore, the SAC insufficiently alleges a fiduciary duty owed by Baik.

 

Next, Youn and Kang are alleged to be lenders. (SAC ¶ 51.) The loans were both residential and commercial. (SAC ¶ 49.) As to Youn and Kang, a lender does not owe a fiduciary duty to a borrower. (Nymark v. Heart Fed. Savings & Loan Assn. (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 1089, 1096.) This claim therefore fails as to them.

 

Accordingly, the court SUSTAINS the demurrers to the tenth cause of action.

 

Eleventh Cause of Action – Violation of Civ. Code § 2943, et seq.

 

Baik & Youn contend this claim fails because it is uncertain by failing to identify which Defendants are part of this claim, a prospective lender is not an entitled person under the statute, and the allegations exceed the scope of leave to amend.

 

Kang contends this claim fails because she is not a beneficiary of any specific deed of trust on any specific Plaintiff property, does not allege a proper written demand she received, does not allege willful failure to deliver, and the damages allegation is conclusory.

 

Plaintiffs argue that they sufficiently alleged this claim.

 

Baik & Youn reply that this claim exceeded the scope of leave to amend.

 

Kang replies that the statutory elements of this claim are still missing.

 

Here, this claim is defective because it failed to identify the parties it is directed against. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.112(4).)

 

Accordingly, the court SUSTAINS the demurrers to the eleventh cause of action.

 

Motion to Strike

 

Motion to strike is DENIED, without prejudice, as moot.

 

Leave to Amend

 

Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment.¿ (See Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349 [court shall not “sustain a demurrer without leave to amend if there is any reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment”]; Kong v. City of Hawaiian Gardens Redevelopment Agency (2002) 108 Cal.App.4th 1028, 1037 [“A demurrer should not be sustained without leave to amend if the complaint, liberally construed, can state a cause of action under any theory or if there is a reasonable possibility the defect can be cured by amendment.”]; Vaccaro v. Kaiman (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 761, 768 [“When the defect which justifies striking a complaint is capable of cure, the court should allow leave to amend.”].)¿ The burden is on the complainant to show the Court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)¿ 

 

Here, Plaintiffs again requested leave to amend. The court will entertain oral argument to allow Plaintiffs to offer proof as to how they could amend to address these defects.

 

CONCLUSION:

 

            For the foregoing reasons, the Court decides the pending motion as follows:

 

1.      Demurrers to the SAC are SUSTAINED in full;

2.      Motion to strike is DENIED, without prejudice, as moot.

Defendant Kang is to give notice.
