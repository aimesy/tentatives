CCP §583.410 permits the Court to dismiss an action if the case is not brought to trial within two years after filing.  Defendant provides evidence that the case has been called for trial on five occasions and each time Plaintiffs’ attorney has appeared and indicated “not ready” for trial.  On the fifth such occasion, the Court specially set this hearing on Defendant’s motion to dismiss the action. 

 

This action was filed on 7/25/24 and this matter is set for hearing on 7/28/26, more than two years thereafter.  Any opposition to the motion was due on or before 7/15/26.  Plaintiffs have not filed timely opposition papers, and the Court will not consider late-filed opposition papers. 

 

In light of Plaintiffs’ repeated failure to be ready for trial, the passage of more than two years since the filing of the complaint, and the lack of opposition, the motion to dismiss is granted.  Defendant is ordered to prepare a judgment of dismissal for processing.

 

Defendant is ordered to give notice.

Case Number: 24LBCV01808    Hearing Date: July 28, 2026    Dept: S27

Background

            Song Beverly Warranty Act action.  On August 23, 2025, Plaintiffs Allison Hedgpeth and Glenn Bronson Hedgpeth filed a complaint against American Honda Motor Co., Inc. alleging (1) violation of Civil Code section 1793.2(d); (2) violation of Civil Code section 1793.2(b); (3) violation of Civil Code section 1793.2(a)(3); (4) breach of the implied warranty of merchantability (Civ. Code §§1791.1, 1794 and 1795.5). 

            Trial in this case commenced on December 5, 2025.  The jury returned a verdict finding no liability on December 8, 2025.  Judgment was entered in Defendant’s favor on January 6, 2026.  The judgment included an award of costs.

            On January 29, 2026, Defendant filed a memorandum of costs.  On February 13, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a Motion to Strike and/or Tax Defendant’s Fees and Costs. 

 

Legal Standard

            To obtain costs, the prevailing party must file and serve a memorandum of costs, which “must be verified by a statement of the party, attorney, or agent that to the best of his or her knowledge the items of cost are correct and were necessarily incurred in the case.” (Cal. Rules of Ct. Rule 3.1700(a).)

            If the items on the face of the cost memorandum appear to be proper charges, the verified cost memorandum is prima facie evidence of their propriety, and it is the challenging party who bears the burden to show the costs were not reasonable or necessary.  (Ladas v. California State Auto. Assn. (1993) 19 Cal.App.4th 761, 774.)  The only identifiable requirement for a memo of costs is that it set forth the costs claimed and be verified.  (Cal. Rules of Ct. Rule 3.1700(a)(1).) 

            The mere filing of a motion to tax costs may be a proper objection to an item if the necessity of that item appears doubtful, does not appear to be proper on its face (Nelson, supra, 72 Cal.App.4th at 131), or presents a legal question (Fennessy v. Deleuw-Cather Corporation (1990) 218 Cal.App.3d 1192, 1195–1996 (motion to tax questioning entitlement to deposition costs incurred by all six defendants represented by the same counsel was sufficient to place the cost at issue without additional declarations or affidavits).) 

            However, if section 1033.5 expressly allows the particular item and it appears proper on its face, the burden is on the objecting party to show the costs to be unnecessary or unreasonable. (Nelson, supra, 72 Cal.App.4th at 131.)  “The court's first determination, therefore, is whether the statute expressly allows the particular item, and whether it appears proper on its face.  If so, the burden is on the objecting party to show them to be unnecessary or unreasonable.”  (Id.)

            If costs are properly placed at issue, the burden of proof shifts to the party seeking costs to justify them by providing evidence and supporting documentation that the costs were reasonable and necessarily incurred. (Ladas v. California State Auto. Assn., supra, 19 Cal.App.4th at 774; Jones, supra, 63 Cal.App.4th at 1267.) 

 

Analysis

            Plaintiffs move to strike the following costs: (1) Item 4 Deposition Costs in the amount of $2,008.10; (2) Item 8 Witness Fees in the amount of $3,870; (3) Item 9 Court-ordered transcripts in the amount of $2,967.60; and (4) Item 15 “Other” costs in the amount of $3,158.17 or $3,259.37.

            Plaintiffs argue Defendant fails to provide invoices or receipts in support of the disputed cost items.  Plaintiffs argues Defendant fails to satisfy its burden of demonstrating that the costs are reasonable and necessary under Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5. 

            Defendant’s verified memorandum of costs is prima facie evidence of the reasonableness and necessity of the costs listed on the memorandum of costs.  Defendant was not required to attach copies of bills, invoices, statements, or other documentation to the memorandum (Jones v. Dumrichob (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 1258, 1267), but it was required to provide enough detail in the memorandum to determine whether the cost sought is statutorily awardable (Nelson v. Anderson (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 111, 132.) 

            Defendant provides sufficient information in Item 4 to determine whether the deposition costs are recoverable.  Deposition costs are expressly allowed for in section 1033.5 subdivision (a)(3).  Item 4 lists the deposition costs for Plaintiffs’ depositions.   The deposition costs are proper on their face and Plaintiffs fail to raise any issue with the deposition costs other than lack of receipts and invoices.

            Defendant’s Item 8 fails to provide sufficient information to determine whether the costs are statutorily awardable.  The information provided is also ambigiuous.  Defendant identifies the experts as Bob Pascarella and Bob Pascarella Technical Assistants.  Defendant lists the expert fees in Item 8(b) of the Memorandum of Costs (Worksheet), which expressly states “per Code of Civil Procedure section 998.”  (Memorandum of Costs filed on January 29, 2026, Memorandum of Costs, Item 8(b).)  Defendant provides the identity of the experts, the amount of the expert fees and the statutory basis for the fees.  This is sufficient to establish that they are statutorily awardable. 

            Defendant chose to submit invoices in support of Item 8.  The only invoice submitted for Bob Pascarella is for $976.  Plaintiffs also claim an invoice in the amount of $941.40 for expert James Jongkind.  The requested amount of $3,870 is unsupported and doubtful on the face of the memorandum.  Defendant did not oppose.  Plaintiffs’ motion to tax is granted as to Item 8 in the amount of $1,952.60 is granted.

            Defendant provides sufficient information in support of Item 9 for court-ordered transcripts in the amount of $2,967.60 to determine whether the costs are statutorily awardable.  The transcript costs are for the depositions of its experts Pascarella and Jongkind.  (Memorandum of Costs filed on January 29, 2026, Memorandum of Costs, Item 9.)   These costs are improper on their face, as they are not court-ordered transcripts but expert deposition transcripts.  Defendant did not file an opposition.  Plaintiff’s motion to tax is granted as to the entirety of Item 9.

            Defendant provides insufficient information for the Court to determine whether the entirety of Item 15 for “other” costs are recoverable.  Defendant includes hotel fees and rental car fees but provides no invoices.  On their face, the reasonable necessity of hotel and rental car fees is doubtful.  Defendant did not file an opposition explaining these costs are reasonably necessary to the litigation. 

            In addition, parking is listed as one of the “other” costs.  No other information is provided to establish the nature of the parking costs.  “Routine expenses for local travel by attorneys or other firm employees are not reasonably necessary to the conduct of litigation.”  (Ladas, supra, 19 Cal.App.4th at 775–776 (local travel expenses, including parking fees, cab fares and “mileage/parking” for attorneys and paralegals were not necessary to the litigation as opposed to being merely convenient.) 

            Defendant also included the cost of in-house black and white printing and lunch.  It is unclear what this “in house” printing was and whether they were essentially photocopies that were not exhibits.  Photocopying charges that are not for exhibits are expressly not recoverable under Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5(b)(3). 

            Defendant also includes the costs of lunches during trial, which were not reasonably necessary to the litigation.  “Nor can meal expenses be justified as ‘necessary to conduct the litigation’ since attorneys have to eat, whether they are conducting litigation or not.”  (Ladas, supra, 19 Cal.App.4th at 774 (attorney lunches during depositions were not recoverable expenses as they were not expressly recoverable nor were they reasonably necessary to litigation.) 

            Plaintiffs also call into question the reasonable necessity of pulling two Carfax Reports on the same vehicle.  Defendant also lists two different totals for Item 15 on the memorandum and worksheet ($3,158.17) and on the attachment to the Worksheet ($3,259.37).  Defendant did not file an opposition explaining either of these issues. 

            Based on the listed expenses, Defendant’s “other” costs are not recoverable because they were not reasonably necessary to the litigation.  Defendant did not file an opposition.  Plaintiffs’ motion to tax Item 15 in its entirety is granted. 

            Plaintiffs’ motion to strike and/or tax Defendant’s costs is denied as to Item 4, granted as to Item 8 in the amount of $1,952.60 and granted as to the entirety of Items 9 and 15.

BACKGROUND

On March 25, 2024, Plaintiff Jose Villa alleges tripping on the side of a step at the Port of Los Angeles, thereby causing him to fall into a glass door and/or window. On December 20, 2024, Plaintiff filed a complaint for Premises Liability and General Negligence against Defendants, Port of Los Angeles, City of Los Angeles, County of Los Angeles, SSA Marine, a Carrix Enterprise, SSA Marine, Inc., SSA Pacific of America, Carrix, Inc., Stevedoring Services of America, Inc., Berth 55 Warehouse, and AltaSea at the Port of Los Angeles for premises liability.

On March 27, 2025, SSA Pacific, Inc. and City of Los Angeles filed a cross-complaint for apportionment of fault, indemnification, declaratory relief, and breach of contract, against Plaintiff Jose Villa, U.S. Expeditors. Villa Transportation, Inc., Inner Valley Transport, Inc., and IVT Logistics Worldwide, Inc. On September 3, 2025, Cross-Complainants dismissed U.S. Expeditors.

On January 29 and February 10, 2026, City of Los Angeles dismissed the cross-complaint. On March 3, 2026, the court sustained the demurrer of Jose Villa to the first, second and third causes of action in the cross-complaint without leave to amend, and continued the hearing on the breach of contract cause of action. On March 25, 2026, the court entered an order dismissing the entire cross-complaint as to Plaintiff Villa. On April 2, 2026, the court sustained the demurrer of IVT Logistics Worldwide, Inc. to the remaining cross-complaint of SSA Pacific of America without leave to amend.  On April 17, 2026, the court entered a judgment of dismissal on the SSA Pacific, Inc. demurrer. On July 16, 2026, SSA Pacific Inc. filed a notice of appeal on the demurrer to the cross-complaint.

 

DISCUSSION

Applicable Law

The pleadings frame the issues for motions, “since it is those allegations to which the motion must respond. (Citation.)” (Scolinos v. Kolts (1995) 37 Cal. App. 4th 635, 640-641; FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 367, 382-383; Jordan-Lyon Prods., LTD. v. Cineplex Odeon Corp. (1994) 29 Cal.App.4th 1459, 1472.) The purpose of a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication “is to provide courts with a mechanism to cut through the parties’ pleadings in order to determine whether, despite their allegations, trial is in fact necessary to resolve their dispute.” (Aguilar v. Atl. Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.)  “Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (c), requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.”  (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.) 

“On a motion for summary judgment, the initial burden is always on the moving party to make a prima facie showing that there are no triable issues of material fact.” (Scalf v. D.B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1519.) A defendant moving for summary judgment “has met his or her burden of showing that a cause of action has no merit if the party has shown that one or more elements of the cause of action . . . cannot be established.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) “Once the defendant . . . has met that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff . . . to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.” (Ibid.)

“A party may move for summary adjudication as to one or more causes of action within an action... or one or more issues of duty if the party contends that the cause of action has no merit, that there is no affirmative defense to the cause of action that there is no merit to an affirmative defense as to any cause of action, that there is no merit to a claim for damages, as specified in Section 3294 of the Civil Code, or that one or more defendants either owed or did not owe a duty to the plaintiff or plaintiffs. A motion for summary adjudication shall be granted only if it completely disposes of a cause of action, an affirmative defense, a claim for damages, or an issue of duty.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (f)(1).)

“When deciding whether to grant summary judgment, the court must consider all of the evidence set forth in the papers (except evidence to which the court has sustained an objection), as well as all reasonable inference that may be drawn form that evidence, in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment.” (Avivi v. Centro Medico Urgente Medical Center (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 463, 467; see also Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c).) “An issue of fact can only be created by a conflict in the evidence.  It is not created by speculation, conjecture, imagination or guesswork.”  (Lyons v. Security Pacific National Bank (1995) 40 Cal.App.4th 1001, 1014 (citation omitted).)

 

Analysis

Defendant SSA Pacific Inc. (SSA) moves for summary judgment on the complaint for premises liability and negligence. SSA moves on grounds of denial of any dangerous condition, no evidence SSA created said dangerous condition, lack of actual notice of any dangerous condition, and lack of proximate cause evidence regarding the condition and Plaintiff’s injuries.

Plaintiff in opposition contends triable issues of material fact exist as to the existence of a non-code compliant unsafe condition, whereby SSA remains liable as the permit holder and therefore in control of the premises.

The court electronic filing system shows no reply on file on or after the July 17, 2026, filing deadline. (Code Civ. Proc., §437c, subd. (b)(4).)

 

1st Cause of Action: Premises Liability

2nd Cause of Action: Premises Liability

SSA only moves for summary judgment. The standard of review for both causes of action presents a singular test. “The elements of a negligence claim and a premises liability claim are the same: a legal duty of care, breach of that duty, and proximate cause resulting in injury.” (Kesner v. Superior Court (2016) 1 Cal.5th 1132, 1158.) “In order to prevail in an action based upon a defendant's alleged negligence, a plaintiff must demonstrate that the defendant owed the plaintiff a legal duty, that the defendant breached the duty, and that the breach was a proximate or legal cause of his or her injuries.” (Morris v. De La Torre (2005) 36 Cal.4th 260, 264.)

 

Trivial Defect and Open and Obvious

SSA first relies on a characterization of the condition as either a trivial defect or open and obvious condition. “A condition is not a dangerous condition within the meaning of this chapter if the trial or appellate court, viewing the evidence most favorably to the plaintiff, determines as a matter of law that the risk created by the condition was of such a minor, trivial or insignificant nature in view of the surrounding circumstances that no reasonable person would conclude that the condition created a substantial risk of injury when such property or adjacent property was used with due care in a manner in which it was reasonably foreseeable that it would be used.” (Gov. Code, § 830.2.) “[T]o constitute a dangerous condition, the height differential, and the area surrounding it, must have posed ‘a substantial (as distinguished from a minor, trivial or insignificant) risk of injury’ when ‘used with due care in a manner in which it is reasonably foreseeable that it will be used.’” (Huckey v. City of Temecula (2019) 37 Cal.App.5th 1092, 1110.)

“Generally, if a danger is so obvious that a person could reasonably be expected to see it, the condition itself serves as a warning, and the landowner is under no further duty to remedy or warn of the condition. (Citation.) However, this is not true in all cases. ‘[I]t is foreseeable that even an obvious danger may cause injury, if the practical necessity of encountering the danger, when weighed against the apparent risk involved, is such that under the circumstances, a person might choose to encounter the danger. The foreseeability of injury, in turn, when considered along with various other policy considerations such as the extent of the burden to the defendant and consequences to the community of imposing a duty to remedy such danger [citation] may lead to the legal conclusion that the defendant’ owed a duty of due care to the person injured.” (Krongos v. Pacific Gas & Electric Co. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 387, 393.) “[B]ecause the possessor or operator of a given premises is not an insurer of the safety of invitees onto his premises, he is entitled to assume that any such invitee will perceive that which should be obvious to him in the ordinary use of his [or her] senses.” (Danieley v. Goldmine Ski Associates, Inc. (1990) 218 Cal.App.3d 111, 121.)

SSA appears to rely on this position without any apparent objective support other than Plaintiff previously visiting the area within the course and scope of employment as a truck driver in various times of day impacting lighting conditions. [Declaration of Heather Mills, Compendium of Evidence, Ex. F: Deposition of Jose Villa, 32:18-33:11, 34:10-35:2, 47:24-48:3, 52:15-53:4, Ex H: Deposition Exhibit.] The position lacks sufficient articulation establishing either an open and obvious or trivial defect defense simply based on prior visits. “A court should decide whether a defect may be dangerous only after considering all of the circumstances surrounding the accident that might make the defect more dangerous than its size alone would suggest.” (Caloroso v. Hathaway (2004) 122 Cal.App.4th 922, 927.)

Plaintiff specifically challenges any factual finding of a trivial defect due to the non-compliant code conditions, and height differential. Plaintiff also denies any open and obvious condition, but even if the specific section constituted an open and obvious hazard, Plaintiff asserts no notice of such danger.

SSA fails to shift the burden establishing either a trivial defect or open and obvious position. Even if SSA shifted the burden, Plaintiff submits expert testimony establishing triable issues of material fact as to both a trivial defect and open and obvious condition. [Declaration of Babak Kheiri, Ex. C: Declaration of Brad Avrit, PE.] The expert concludes that the slope change constitutes an “unexpected and significant hazard for pedestrians to traverse.” Such types of area often go “unnoticed” in that a pedestrian may not necessarily recognize a danger simply based on the height differential and change, due to the lack of a “salient” visual cue. [Id., ¶¶ 7-10.] The declaration is unchallenged and qualitatively establishes a basis for requirement of consideration by the trier of fact for any determination of the applicability of the two defenses. (See Blumberg v. M. & T. Inc. (1949) 34 Cal.2d 226, 229-230 [Spaces in floor mats created a foreseeable and actual dangerous condition to invitees]; Martinez v. Chippewa Enterprises, Inc. (2004) 121 Cal.App.4th 1179, 1184 [Wet condition on premises constituted dangerous condition].)

 

Actual or Constructive Notice

            SSA denies any notice of a defective or dangerous condition. SSA represents regular inspections and denies any prior complaints. Plaintiff denies relief from liability based on prior efforts to demarcate the area. Plaintiff also notes the change to the area following the incident.

            The later modifications are not considered as evidence of prior notice of a dangerous condition. “The plaintiff need not show actual knowledge where evidence suggests that the dangerous condition was present for a sufficient period of time to charge the owner with constructive knowledge of its existence. Knowledge may be shown by circumstantial evidence ‘which is nothing more than one or more inferences which may be said to arise reasonably from a series of proven facts.’” (Ortega v. Kmart Corp. (2001) 26 Cal.4th 1200, 1206–1207.)

“When an unsafe condition which causes injury to an invitee has been created by the owner of the property himself or by an employee within the scope of his employment, the invitee need not prove the owner's notice or knowledge of the dangerous condition; the knowledge is imputed to the owner.” (Sanders v. MacFarlane's Candies (1953) 119 Cal.App.2d 497, 501.) Denial of notice without establishing the lack of a non-trivial, dangerous condition will not meet the standard for SSA to shift the burden regarding a lack of actual or constructive notice. Additionally, triable issues of material fact exist regarding the reasonableness of the lapse of time regarding inspection and rectification of the dangerous condition before the incident. (Howard v. Omni Hotels Management Corp. (2012) 203 Cal.App.4th 403, 431 accord Ortega v. Kmart Corp., supra, 26 Cal.4th at pp. 1206-1207.)

 

Causation

            Finally SSA denies any causation based on the denial of any dangerous condition. Again, SSA fails to shift the burden based on the lack of any factually and legally supported position establishing the lack of a dangerous condition. (Saelzler v. Advanced Group 400 (2001) 25 Cal.4th 763, 768.) Further triable issues of material fact exist based on Plaintiff’s establishment of the dangerous condition through the unchallenged expert declaration. The circumstances establish a basis of causation not resolved as a matter of law in the instant motion. (Id. at p. 781.)

 

CONCLUSION

The motion for summary judgment is DENIED due to the failure to shift the burden on any and all defenses and triable issues of material fact on each challenge.

Final Status Conference and Trial remain set for October 16 and 19, 2026, respectively.

F: Deposition of Jose Villa, 32:18-33:11, 34:10-35:2, 47:24-48:3, 52:15-53:4, Ex H: Deposition Ex.] The position lacks sufficient articulation establishing either an open and obvious or trivial defect defense based on prior visits. “A court should decide whether a defect may be dangerous only after considering all of the circumstances surrounding the accident that might make the defect more dangerous than its size alone would suggest.” (Caloroso v. Hathaway (2004) 122 Cal.App.4th 922, 927.)

Plaintiff specifically challenges any factual finding of a trivial defect due to the non-compliant code conditions, and height differential. Plaintiff also denies any open and obvious condition, but even if the specific section constituted an open and obvious hazard, Plaintiff asserts no notice of such danger.

City fails to shift the burden establishing either a trivial defect or open and obvious position. Even if City shifted the burden, Plaintiff submits expert testimony establishing triable issues of material fact as to both a trivial defect and open and obvious condition. [Declaration of Babak Kheiri, Ex. C: Declaration of Brad Avrit, PE.] The expert concludes that the slope change constitutes an “unexpected and significant hazard for pedestrians to traverse.” Such types of area often go “unnoticed” in that a pedestrian may not necessarily recognize a danger simply based on the height differential and change, due to the lack of a “salient” visual cue. [Id., ¶¶ 7-10.] The declaration is unchallenged and qualitatively establishes a basis for requirement of consideration by the trier of fact for any determination of the applicability of the two defenses. (See Blumberg v. M. & T. Inc. (1949) 34 Cal.2d 226, 229-230 [Spaces in floor mats created a foreseeable and actual dangerous condition to invitees]; Martinez v. Chippewa Enterprises, Inc. (2004) 121 Cal.App.4th 1179, 1184 [Wet condition on premises constituted dangerous condition].)

 

Actual or Constructive Notice

            City relies on the existence of the Permit to co-defendant SSA Pacific, as delegating all operational control and duty to inspect the premises to SSA Pacific. City denies any notice of prior complaints as well.

            Plaintiff challenges any finding of a non-delegable duty, and maintains an ongoing duty to inspect. A lack of notice of prior incidents will not relieve City from liability for a dangerous condition.

            The delegation of duty position again provides no legal support, and instead only relies on the deposition of Seung-Hyun Kevin Kim, a presumed employee of the City, but no specifically identified position. [Mills Decl., Ex. A: Deposition of Seung-Hyun Kevin Kim.] Kim acknowledges the operative permit [25:23-26:11] and maintains the terms of the permit require acceptance in an “as is” condition with the occupant obligated to maintain the premises [58:20-59:23]. Kim interprets section 2.4 of the permit as a basis for the City to deny any liability for injuries occurring due to the condition of the premises. [Id, 89:9-25.]

            The opinion of Kim constitutes nothing more than a statement without address of any legal standard or qualification to make this determination. City therefore fails to shift the burden establishing this defense.

            Even considering the defense, no valid basis of a non-delegable duty finds support.  “Where the law imposes a definite, affirmative duty upon one by reason of his relationship with others, whether as an owner or proprietor of land or chattels or in some other capacity, such persons can not escape liability for a failure to perform the duty thus imposed by entrusting it to an independent contractor. ... It is immaterial whether the duty thus regarded as ‘nondelegable’ be imposed by statute, charter or by common law.” (Van Arsdale v. Hollinger (1968) 68 Cal.2d 245, 251 overruled on other grounds by Privette v. Superior Court (1993) 5 Cal.4th 689.)

            Plaintiff cites to Government Code section 818.6 for purposes of establishing a duty to inspect regardless of any permit terms. “A public entity is not liable for injury caused by its failure to make an inspection, or by reason of making an inadequate or negligent inspection, of any property, other than its property (as defined in subdivision (c) of Section 830), for the purpose of determining whether the property complies with or violates any enactment or contains or constitutes a hazard to health or safety.” (Gov. Code, § 818.6.) The indirectly worded statute imposes a duty of care on the government entity in control of its property. The existence of the permit in no way relieves City from liability on grounds of non-delegable duty. “The essential justification for this rule is one of ensuring accountability of licensees so as to safeguard the public welfare. ... If a licensee were not liable for the actions of his independent contractor, ‘effective regulation would be impossible. He could contract away the daily operations of his business to independent contractors and become immune to disciplinary action by the licensing authority.’ The principle that a licensee will be held liable for the acts of its agents is one that has been applied whether the agent is an independent contractor or an employee.” (California Assn. of Health Facilities v. Department of Health Services (1997) 16 Cal.4th 284, 296.)

On lack of notice, again, City only cites to the deposition of the terminal manager denying any reported claims for 16 years. [Mills Decl., Ex. C: Deposition of Kevin Nielsen, 93:7-15.] The legally unsupported position fails to shift the burden establishing a valid basis for reliance on lack of notice.

“When an unsafe condition which causes injury to an invitee has been created by the owner of the property himself or by an employee within the scope of his employment, the invitee need not prove the owner's notice or knowledge of the dangerous condition; the knowledge is imputed to the owner.” (Sanders v. MacFarlane's Candies (1953) 119 Cal.App.2d 497, 501.) Given the finding of a non-trivial and not open an obvious hazard, triable issue of material fact  exist on grounds that “that the hazardous condition existed long enough for the owner to have discovered it, if an owner exercising reasonable care would have learned of it” (Howard v. Omni Hotels Management Corp. (2012) 203 Cal.App.4th 403, 431 accord Ortega v. Kmart Corp. (2001) 26 Cal.4th 1200, 1206-1207.)

 

Causation

            Finally City denies any causation based on the denial of any dangerous condition. Again, City fails to shift the burden based on the lack of any factually and legally supported position establishing the lack of a dangerous condition. (Saelzler v. Advanced Group 400 (2001) 25 Cal.4th 763, 768.) Further triable issues of material fact exist based on Plaintiff’s establishment of the dangerous condition through the unchallenged expert declaration. The circumstances establish a basis of causation not resolved as a matter of law in the instant motion. (Id. at p. 781.)

 

CONCLUSION

The motion for summary judgment is DENIED due to the failure to shift the burden on any and all defenses and triable issues of material fact on each challenge.

DISCUSSION

Applicable Law

The pleadings frame the issues for motions, “since it is those allegations to which the motion must respond. (Citation.)” (Scolinos v. Kolts (1995) 37 Cal. App. 4th 635, 640-641; FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 367, 382-383; Jordan-Lyon Prods., LTD. v. Cineplex Odeon Corp. (1994) 29 Cal.App.4th 1459, 1472.) The purpose of a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication “is to provide courts with a mechanism to cut through the parties’ pleadings in order to determine whether, despite their allegations, trial is in fact necessary to resolve their dispute.” (Aguilar v. Atl. Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.)  “Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (c), requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.”  (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.) 

“On a motion for summary judgment, the initial burden is always on the moving party to make a prima facie showing that there are no triable issues of material fact.” (Scalf v. D.B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1519.) A defendant moving for summary judgment “has met his or her burden of showing that a cause of action has no merit if the party has shown that one or more elements of the cause of action . . . cannot be established.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) “Once the defendant . . . has met that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff . . . to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.” (Ibid.)

“A party may move for summary adjudication as to one or more causes of action within an action... or one or more issues of duty if the party contends that the cause of action has no merit, that there is no affirmative defense to the cause of action that there is no merit to an affirmative defense as to any cause of action, that there is no merit to a claim for damages, as specified in Section 3294 of the Civil Code, or that one or more defendants either owed or did not owe a duty to the plaintiff or plaintiffs. A motion for summary adjudication shall be granted only if it completely disposes of a cause of action, an affirmative defense, a claim for damages, or an issue of duty.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (f)(1).)

“When deciding whether to grant summary judgment, the court must consider all of the evidence set forth in the papers (except evidence to which the court has sustained an objection), as well as all reasonable inference that may be drawn form that evidence, in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment.” (Avivi v. Centro Medico Urgente Medical Center (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 463, 467; see also Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c).) “An issue of fact can only be created by a conflict in the evidence.  It is not created by speculation, conjecture, imagination or guesswork.”  (Lyons v. Security Pacific National Bank (1995) 40 Cal.App.4th 1001, 1014 (citation omitted).)

 

Request for Judicial Notice

Exhibits A-C: The court grants judicial notice as to the filing and existence of the pleadings, but cannot take judicial notice as to the content of any pleading for the truth of any matter asserted. (Sosinsky v. Grant (1992) 6 Cal.App.4th 1548, 1568-1569.)

Exhibit F-G: Denied. The court cannot take judicial notice of applicant submitted information. (Cal. Evid. Code § 452, subd. (c); Friends of Shingle Springs Interchange, Inc. v. County of El Dorado (2011) 200 Cal.App.4th 1470, 1484; Elmore v. Oak Valley Hospital Dist. (1988) 204 Cal.App.3d 716, 721.)

Exhibit I: The court takes judicial notice of its own prior minute order.

 

Procedural

Any later attempted request for summary adjudication is denied. “The motion must be denied if the movant fails to establish an entitlement to summary adjudication of the matters thus specified; the court cannot summarily adjudicate other issues or claims, even if a basis to do so appears from the papers. (Citation.)” Schmidlin v. City of Palo Alto (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 728, 744 accord Gonzales v. Superior Court (1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 1542, 1545.)

Case Number: 26LBCV00328    Hearing Date: July 28, 2026    Dept: S27

  1. Background Facts

Plaintiff, Jamie Anderson filed this action against Defendants, Century Villages at Cabrillo, Inc., Century Villages Property Management, LLC, City of Long Beach, and County of Los Angeles for damages arising out of a trip and fall on a hole in the lawn of Defendants’ property.  Plaintiff’s complaint includes causes of action for general negligence against the Century defendants and premises liability against all defendants.  Plaintiff has dismissed the County. 

 

  1. Demurrer

a.     Legal Standard on Demurrer

A demurrer is a pleading used to test the legal sufficiency of other pleadings. It raises issues of law, not fact, regarding the form or content of the opposing party’s pleading.  It is not the function of the demurrer to challenge the truthfulness of the complaint; and for purpose of the ruling on the demurrer, all facts pleaded in the complaint are assumed to be true, however improbable they may be.

 

A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack; or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. Blank v. Kirwan 39 Cal.3d 311 (1985). No other extrinsic evidence can be considered (i.e., no “speaking demurrers”). A demurrer is brought under CCP § 430.10 [grounds], § 430.30 [as to any matter on its face or from which judicial notice may be taken], and § 430.50(a) [can be taken to the entire complaint or any cause of action within].  Specifically, a demurrer may be brought per CCP § 430.10(e) if insufficient facts are stated to support the cause of action asserted.  Per CCP §430.10(a) a demurrer may be brought where the court has no jurisdiction of the subject of the cause of action alleged in the pleading.  Furthermore, demurrer for uncertainty will be sustained only where the complaint is so bad that the defendant cannot reasonably respond.  CCP § 430.10(f). 

 

However, in construing the allegations, the court is to give effect to specific factual allegations that may modify or limit inconsistent general or conclusory allegations. Financial Corporation of America v. Wilburn, 189 Cal.App.3rd 764, 769 (1987). And, if the facts pled in the complaint are inconsistent with facts which are incorporated by reference from exhibits attached to the complaint, the facts in the incorporated exhibits control. Further, irrespective of the name or label given to a cause of action by the plaintiff, a general demurrer must be overruled if the facts as pled in the body of the complaint state some valid claim for relief. Special demurrers are not allowed in limited jurisdiction courts. (CCP § 92(c).)

 

Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. Goodman v. Kennedy, 18 Cal.3d 335, 348 (1976). The burden is on the complainant to show the Court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Id.)

 

Finally, CCP section 430.41 requires that “[b]efore filing a demurrer pursuant to this chapter, the demurring party shall meet and confer in person or by telephone with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer.” (CCP §430.41(a).) The parties are to meet and confer at least five days before the date the responsive pleading is due. (CCP §430.41(a)(2).) Thereafter, the demurring party shall file and serve a declaration detailing their meet and confer efforts. (CCP §430.41(a)(3).)

 

b.     Meet and Confer

Defense Counsel declares he sent a meet and confer letter and invited Plaintiff to meet and confer, but Plaintiff did not respond.  The Code requires meet and confer “in person or over the telephone.”  The Court will rule on the demurrer despite this requirement, but Defense Counsel must make active efforts to do so in the future in this and other actions.    

 

c.     Gov Code §945.5

Defendant demurs to complaint, correctly noting that the sole claim for premises liability against it is fatally defective per Cal Gov Code §945.5, which requires a plaintiff to submit a government tort claim with respect to the acts upon which his complaint is based prior to filing suit against the public entity.  Failure to allege compliance with the statute is grounds for demurrer.  Wills v. City of Carlsbad (2020) 48 Cal.App.5th 1104, 1119.  The demurrer is sustained due to Plaintiff’s failure to allege compliance with the Government Tort Claims Act. 

 

d.     Statutory Basis for Second Cause of Action

Defendant also argues the sole cause of action pled against it, the second cause of action, fails because Plaintiff did not state a statutory basis for the claims.  Gov Code §815(a) requires a statutory basis for all claims pled against a governmental entity.  Pursuant to Miklosy v. Regents of University of California (2008) 44 Cal.4th 876, 899, §815 abolished all common law claims against government entities.  Because the sole cause of action pled against the City is the common law claim of premises liability, the demurrer is sustained.

 

e.     Leave to Amend

It is not up to the Court to figure out how the complaint can be amended to state a cause of action.  Rather, the burden is on the plaintiff to show in what manner he or she can amend the complaint, and how that amendment will change the legal effect of the pleading.  Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349; Hendy v. Losse (1991) 54 Cal.3d 723, 742.

 

A plaintiff may not amend as a matter of right, and in order to be in a position to complain upon appeal of an abuse of discretion in sustaining a demurrer without leave to amend, he must ask permission to amend, unless it is made to appear that he had no opportunity to ask such permission.  Williams v. Hinckley (1930) 109 Cal.App. 574, 576.

 

In light of the lack of opposition, Plaintiff has necessarily failed to meet this burden, and leave to amend is denied.
