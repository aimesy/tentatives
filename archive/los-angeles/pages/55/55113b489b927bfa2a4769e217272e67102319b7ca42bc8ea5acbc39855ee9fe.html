ANALYSIS:

 

I.                Background

 

On October 31, 2024, Plaintiffs Allstate Northbrook Indemnity Company and Allstate Insurance Company (“Plaintiffs”) filed the instant action against Defendants Scott Spriggs and Kinkle Rodiger & Spriggs P.C. (“KRS PC”) (“Defendants”) and Does 1-50, alleging causes of action for Legal Malpractice and Breach of Fiduciary Duty. (Compl., p.1.) The underlying action pertains to Defendants’ inadequate legal representation of Plaintiffs’ insureds in a separate matter. (Compl., pp. 1-16.)  

 

On February 5, 2025, Defendants filed an Answer.

 

On March 10, 2026, an Informal Discovery Conference (“IDC”) was held. (3/10/26 Order.)

 

On March 11, 2026, Defendants filed the instant Motions to Compel Further Responses to Requests for Production (“RPDs”) (Set 1 and Set 2) and Special Interrogatories (“SROGs”) (collectively, the “Motions”) and requests for sanctions.

 

On May 27, 2026, an IDC was held. (5/27/26 Order.)

 

On July 2, 2026, Plaintiffs filed an Opposition to the Motions.

 

On July 9, 2026, Defendants filed a Reply to the Motions.

 

II.              Procedural Issues

 

As an initial matter, the Motions are procedurally defective as they have separate notices and separate statements but a consolidated memorandum, supporting declarations, and proof of service. This amalgamation of motion papers improperly combines three motions into one and/or constitutes piecemeal filing of three motions’ components.

 

III.            Legal Standard

 

Responses to interrogatories must be “as complete and straightforward as the information reasonably available to the responding party permits.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.220, subd. (a).) If an interrogatory cannot be answered completely, then it must be answered to the extent possible. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.220, subd. (b).) “If the responding party does not have personal knowledge sufficient to respond fully to an interrogatory, that party shall so state, but shall make a reasonable and good faith effort to obtain the information by inquiry to other natural persons or organizations, except where the information is equally available to the propounding party.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.220, subd. (c).)

 

A party must respond to requests for production of documents within 30 days after service. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.260, subd. (a).) If a party to whom requests for production of documents are directed does not provide timely responses, the requesting party may move for an order compelling responses to the discovery. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300, subd. (c).) The party also waives the right to make any objections, including one based on privilege or work-product protection. (Code Civ. Proc., §2031.300, subd. (a).)

 

Motions to Compel Further Responses

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.300, subdivision (a), provides that “[o]n receipt of a response to interrogatories, the propounding party may move for an order compelling a further response if the propounding party deems that any of the following apply: (1) [a]n answer to a particular interrogatory is evasive or incomplete [;] (2) [a]n exercise of the option to produce documents under Section 2030.230 is unwarranted or the required specification of those documents is inadequate [; or] (3) [a]n objection to an interrogatory is without merit or too general.”

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.310, subdivision (a), provides that “[o]n receipt of a response to a demand for inspection … the demanding party may move for an order compelling further response to the demand if the demanding party deems that any of the following apply:  (1) [a] statement of compliance with the demand is incomplete[;] (2) [a] representation of inability to comply is inadequate, incomplete, or evasive[; or] (3) [a]n objection in the response is without merit or too general.” 

 

In California, discovery statutes “must be construed liberally in favor of disclosure unless the request is clearly improper.” (Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 541.) “Under the discovery statutes, information is discoverable if it is unprivileged and is either relevant to the subject matter of the action or reasonably calculated to reveal admissible evidence.” (John B. v. Superior Court (2006) 38 Cal.4th 1177, 1186.) “Discovery may relate to the claim or defense of the party seeking discovery or of any other party to the action.” (Ibid.) “When discovery requests are grossly overbroad on their face, and hence do not appear reasonably related to a legitimate discovery need, a reasonable inference can be drawn of an intent to harass and improperly burden.” (Obregon v. Superior Court (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 424, 431.)

IV.            Discussion

 

A.    Timeliness of Motion

 

Notice of the motion must be given within 45 days of service of the verified response, otherwise, the propounding party waives any right to compel a further response. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300, subd. (c); 2031.310, subd. (c).)

 

            Here, the Motions were filed within 45 days of service of the responses.

 

B.    Meet and Confer

 

The Motions shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300, subd. (b)(1); 2031.310, subd. (b)(2).)

 

Here, the Motions are not accompanied by meet and confer declarations.

 

However, the Court notes it is undisputed that the parties participated in IDCs regarding the instant discovery requests in March 2026 and May 2026. (3/10/26 Order; 5/27/26 Order.)

 

C.    Requests to Compel Further Responses to Special Interrogatories and Requests for Production, Set One and Set Two

 

The parties do not dispute that Plaintiffs provided supplemental responses following the Motions being filed and prior to the instant hearing date. (See Opp.; Reply.)

 

However, KRS PC alleges that the supplemental responses to the RPDs are still deficient for the following reasons: Plaintiffs did not utilize certain search terms in locating documents to produce in response to RPDs, such as Request Nos. 1 and 2 (Reply, pp. 2-3); Plaintiffs’ productions lack essential metadata and thus certain documents were not produced properly (Reply, pp. 4-7); and Plaintiffs improperly provided only objections to Request No. 8 from RPDs Set One and Request Nos. 1, 4-10, 13-17, and 22 of RPDs Set No. 2 (Reply, p. 9).

 

Procedural Issues

 

As an initial matter, the Court notes that while KRS PC states in the Reply that Plaintiffs’ supplemental responses to all discovery requests were reviewed prior to the instant hearing date, KRS PC failed to file an updated Separate Statement specifying which of Plaintiffs’ responses remain deficient in conjunction with the Reply.

 

The Court proceeds to address the Motions to the best of its ability while operating from only a global perspective regarding the supplemental responses.

 

Requests for Production

 

As to the RPDs Motions, Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.030 requires that each RPD must designate “the documents, tangible things, land or other property, or electronically stored information to be inspected, copied, tested, or sampled either by specifically describing each individual item or by reasonably particularizing each category of item.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.030, emphasis added; see Calcor Space Facility, Inc. v. Sup. Ct. (Thiem Indus., Inc.) (1997) 53 Cal.App.4th 216, 222.)  

 

The Court finds the RPDs here are overbroad and fail to reasonably particularize each category of item requested. (Ibid.) For example, Request No. 8 of RPDs Set One seeks: “YOUR underwriting file RELATING TO Marilyn Chausse and/or Shayla Nash.” (Opp., Sep. Statement, p. 22.) Request No. 4 of RPDs Set Two seeks: “Any of YOUR organizational charts that include individuals or organizations responsible for maintaining electronic processing systems, networks, servers, and data security measures.” (Opp., Sep. Statement, p. 12.) Further, Request No. 1 of RPDs Set Two seeks: “Any DOCUMENT relating to any LITIGATION HOLD that relates to the UNDERLYING MATTER.” (Opp. Sep. Statement, p. 2.) Request No. 8 of RPDs Set Two seeks: “Any DOCUMENT that relates to YOUR policies, procedures or guidelines with respect to file naming conventions and standards.” (Opp., Sep. Statement, p. 20.) While the Court acknowledges there may be some relevance to aspects of the moving parties’ requests, narrowly tailored requests seeking specific information would be more appropriate than the instant sweeping requests.

 

In sum, the Court finds the subject RPDs are overbroad and not reasonably particularized to the disputed issues and time periods pertinent to the case. Furthermore, responses on relevant materials in supplemental responses consistently state that “After a diligent search and reasonable inquiry, all responsive, non-privileged documents within Responding Party’s possession, custody and control have been produced.” Thus, the responses are largely code-compliant.

 

            Regarding KRS PC’s allegations regarding deficient metadata in the supplemental responses, Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.030, subdivision (a)(2) states that “[a] party demanding inspection, copying, testing, or sampling of electronically stored information may specify the form or forms in which each type of electronically stored information is to be produced.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2021.030, subd. (a)(2).) However, the record does not show that any specific metadata instructions were included in the RPDs requests, Set One or Set Two. (See Motions, Sep. Statements; Opp., Sep. Statements; Reply.) Accordingly, KRS PC’s allegations regarding metadata do not warrant granting of the RPDs Motions.

 

For the foregoing reasons, the RPDs Motions are DENIED.

 

Special Interrogatories

 

As the SROGs Motion, the Reply alleges that the only remaining deficiency with the SROGs supplemental responses provided prior to the instant hearing is that two individuals are not identified by name in SROGs seeking names of Plaintiffs’ ESI custodians. (See Reply, pp. 9-10.)

 

However, upon the Court’s review of SROGs propounded upon Plaintiffs, the Court finds the requests to be overbroad and vague, including SROGs Nos. 2, 8, and 9 referenced in the Reply. For instance, SROG No. 8 states: “IDENTIFY any PERSON who is or was employed or hired by YOU who has generated any DOCUMENT with respect to the UNDERLYING MATTER.” (Opp., Exh. 9.) SROG No. 9 states: “IDENTIFY any PERSON who is or was employed or hired by YOU who has generated any.” (Opp., Exh. 9.) The Court finds the requests are overbroad, and narrowly tailored interrogatories seeking specific information would be more appropriate than the instant sweeping requests. Thus, the Court finds these interrogatories are overbroad and not narrowly tailored to the disputed issues of the case.

 

For the foregoing reasons, the SROGs Motion is DENIED.

 

For all the foregoing reasons, the Motions are DENIED.

 

D.    Sanctions

 

“The court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel a further response to interrogatories, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (d).)

 

“Except as provided in subdivision (d), the court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel compliance with a demand, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.320, subdivision (b).)

 

Here, the Motions were unsuccessful and thus the Court imposes monetary sanctions against moving party. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300, 2031.320.)

 

Plaintiff requests $8,625.00 in sanctions against KRS PC, reflecting 21 hours incurred at an hourly rate of $375.00 to bring the Opposition and accompanying documents, plus two hours incurred to review the reply and prepare for and attend the Motions’ hearing. (Notice of Motion, p. 1, Motion, Macan Decl., p. 5.)

 

Given the consolidated Opposition, Reply, and hearing date, the Court GRANTS Plaintiff’s request for sanctions in the reduced amount of $4,875.00, reflecting 13 hours incurred at $375.00 per hour.

 

V.              Conclusion & Order

 

For the foregoing reasons, Defendant Kinkle, Rodiger & Spriggs, P.C.’s Motions to Compel Further Responses to Requests for Production, Set 1 and Set 2, and Special Interrogatories are DENIED.

 

Plaintiffs’ request for sanctions is GRANTED against Defendant Kinkle, Rodiger & Spriggs, P.C. in the reduced amount of $4,875.00, payable to Plaintiffs’ counsel within 30 days of this Court’s order.

 

Moving party is to give notice of ruling.

ANALYSIS:

 

I.                Background

 

On December 17, 2024, Plaintiff Claire Manson (“Plaintiff”) filed a Complaint for Violation of Statutory Obligations against Defendants FCA US, LLC (“FCA US”) and Glendale Chrysler Jeep Dodge Ram (“Glendale Chrysler”) (“Defendants”) and Does 1-10.

 

On January 22, 2025, Defendants filed Answers.

 

On May 19, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant Motion to Compel Defendant’s Compliance with Defendant’s Own Response to Plaintiff’s Request for Production of Documents (“RPDs”), Set One (the “Motion”) and request for sanctions.

 

On July 2, 2026, FCA US filed an Opposition.

 

No Reply has been filed.

 

II.              Legal Standard

 

A party must respond to requests for production of documents within 30 days after service. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.260, subd. (a).) If a party to whom requests for production of documents are directed does not provide timely responses, the requesting party may move for an order compelling responses to the discovery. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300, subd. (c).) The party also waives the right to make any objections, including one based on privilege or work-product protection. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300, subd. (a).) There is no time limit for a motion to compel responses to production of documents other than the cut-off on hearing discovery motions 15 days before trial. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2024.020, subd. (a), 2031.300.) No meet and confer efforts are required before filing a motion to compel responses to the discovery. (Code Civ. Proc., §  2031.300; Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pacific Healthcare Consultants (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, 411.)

 

III.            Discussion

 

A.    RPDs

 

Plaintiff contends that FCA US failed to timely respond to Plaintiff’s RPDs on or by the statutory deadline.

 

In Opposition, FCA US states the Motion is moot and should be denied on the following grounds – although “the written responses were timely served, the accompanying document production was not transmitted due to an administrative oversight… After confirming the oversight, FAC US completed and served its responsive production on July 2, 2026.” (Opp., p. 2, Fonseca Decl., ¶¶ 7-10.) FCA US submits a declaration from defense counsel in support of the Opposition’s contentions. (Opp., Fonseca Decl.)

 

Here, Plaintiff’s counsel served the first set of RPDs on FCA US on April 10, 2025, and FCA US served unverified responses to the RPDs on May 30, 2025, without any accompanying document production. (Motion, Rucker Decl., ¶¶ 4-5, Exhs. 1-2.)

 

FCA US represents in the Opposition and the accompanying declaration of defense counsel that “all responsive, verified, non-privileged documents” have been produced to Plaintiff on July 2, 2026. Thus, the Court is satisfied that FCA US has submitted the production and verified responses to the RPDs. (Opposition, Fonseca Decl.) No reply brief has been filed.  

 

            Based on the foregoing, the Motion is DENIED as moot.

 

B.    Sanctions

 

“The court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel a further response to interrogatories, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (d).)\

 

            Considering that FCA US has provided verified responses to Plaintiff’s RPDs, and due to an administrative oversight cited in the defense counsel’s declaration, the Court finds the imposition of sanctions would be unjust. Thus, sanctions request is DENIED.

 

IV.            Conclusion & Order

 

For the foregoing reasons, Plaintiff Motion to Compel Defendant’s Compliance with Defendant’s Own Response to Plaintiff’s Request for Production of Documents, Set One, is DENIED as moot. Plaintiff’s request for monetary sanctions is DENIED.

 

Moving party is ordered to give notice.

ANALYSIS:

 

I.                Background

 

On November 18, 2025, Plaintiffs Bok Hee Cha (“Cha”) and Eminent Hospice Care, Inc. (“EHC”) (“Plaintiffs”) filed a complaint against Defendants Jeong Mee Moon (“Defendant”) and Does 1-20, alleging causes of action for Breach of Written Contract, Fraud and Deceit, Unjust Enrichment, and Money Had and Received.

 

On April 3, 2026, the Court sustained Defendant’s Demurrer to the Complaint with leave to amend only as to the first cause of action against EHC and second cause of action, and otherwise overruled the Demurrer. (4/3/26 Order.)

 

On May 4, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) against Defendant and Does 1-20, alleging causes of action for Breach of Written Contract, Breach of Contract Third Party Beneficiary, Fraud and Deceit, Unjust Enrichment, and Money Had and Received.

 

On June 5, 2026, Defendant filed the instant Demurrer to Amended Complaint (the “Demurrer”) and Motion to Strike Second Cause of Action of Amended Complaint (the “Motion”).

 

On June 23, 2026, Plaintiffs filed an Opposition.


            On June 25, 2026, Defendant filed a Reply.

 

On June 29, 2026, the Court continued the hearing on the Demurrer and Motion.

 

II.              Allegations in the FAC

 

Since April 2019, EHC’s shares were equally owned by Cha and Defendant. (FAC, ¶¶ 11-12.) In or about May 2024, Cha and Defendant entered into a written agreement wherein Defendant agreed to sell her fifty thousand shares of EHC, representing 50 percent ownership, to Cha, and Cha agree to purchase these shares for $500,000 per the terms of the written agreement. (FAC, ¶ 14.) The agreement contains a liability allocation provision that, in sum, states any liability incurred by EHC prior to the closing date of the aforementioned transaction is to be “assumed 50% each by both Defendant Moon and [Plaintiff] Cha.” (FAC, ¶ 15.)

 

In May 2024, the transaction successfully closed and Cha timely wire-transferred $500,000 to Defendant in consideration of the purchase of Defendant’s shares in EHC. (FAC, ¶ 16.)

 

In July 2025, EHC received a letter from the Center of Medicare & Medicaid Servies (“CMS”) demanding reimbursement for a $306,732.14 overpayment to EHC that occurred during the “Cap Year ending 09/30/23.” (FAC, ¶ 17, Exh. B.) Plaintiffs believe that the overpayment to CMS “was the result of CMS’ audit on the incorrect and/or inaccurate billing requests made to CMS by EHC under the direction of Defendant Moon during the CAP Year ending 09/30/2023 who was responsible for the billing and accounting activities of EHC during the period.” (FAC, ¶ 18.)

 

Cha demanded Defendant pay 50 percent of the CMS reimbursement amount per the agreement, but Defendant failed to respond or pay 50 percent of the CMS reimbursement amount. (FAC, ¶¶ 19-21, Exhs. C-D.) As a result, Cha, on behalf of EHC, proposed and began payments on a six-month installment payment plan to CMS. (FAC, ¶¶ 21-23, Exhs. E-F.)

 

As of the date of the FAC, Defendant “has not made any contribution to the foregoing reimbursement payments made to CMS.” (FAC, ¶ 24.)

 

III.            Legal Standard

 

“As a general matter, in a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) “A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Ibid.) The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)

A general demurrer may be brought under Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (e) if insufficient facts are stated to support the cause of action asserted or under section 430.10, subdivision (a), where the court has no jurisdiction of the subject of the cause of action alleged in the pleading. All other grounds listed in Section 430.10, including uncertainty under subdivision (f), are special demurrers.

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Id.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.) However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245).

IV.            Discussion

 

A.    Meet and Confer

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41 requires that “[b]efore filing a demurrer pursuant to this chapter, the demurring party shall meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a).) The parties are to meet and confer at least five days before the date the responsive pleading is due. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(2).) Thereafter, the demurring party shall file and serve a declaration detailing their meet and confer efforts. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(3).) 

Here, Defendant filed a declaration attesting that the parties telephonically met and conferred regarding the issues raised in the Demurrer prior to filing as required by Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, subdivision (a). (Demurrer, Colorado Decl.) The parties were unable to reach a resolution. (Demurrer, Colorado Decl.)

 

Thus, the Court finds the meet and confer requirement is satisfied.

 

B.    Demurrer

 

Defendant demurs to the first cause of action solely as to EHC and to the third cause of action for failure to state sufficient facts to constitute a cause of action. (Demurrer, p. 3.) As to the first cause of action for breach of contract, Defendant states that EHC is not a party to the written agreement between Cha and Defendant, and thus Plaintiffs cannot allege a cause of action for breach of contract as to EHC. (Demurrer, pp. 6-7.) As to the third cause of action for fraud and deceit, Defendant contends the FAC merely alleges a breach of contract claim, which does not constitute fraud. (Demurrer, pp. 8-9.)

 

In Opposition, Plaintiffs concede that the first cause of action is only applicable to Cha and not EHC, and are amenable to amending the FAC to make this distinction clearer should the Court require. (Opp., p. 5.) Plaintiffs state they have also “already extended this offer to Defendant at the previous meet-and-confer telephone conference.” (Opp., p. 5.) As to the third cause of action, Plaintiffs state the FAC pleads sufficient facts to state a fraud and deceit cause of action as paragraph 45 of the FAC alleges oral representations made by Defendant that are distinct and separate from the facts making up the first and second causes of action. (Opp., pp. 6-7.)

 

In Reply, Defendant addresses Plaintiffs’ concession regarding the first cause of action and stipulate that the “First Cause of Action may be pursued, but the ruling on this demurrer should make clear that it may be pursued only on behalf of Bok Hee Cha. Eminent Hospice Care is not the proper plaintiff to the First Cause of Action and is not the injured party.” (Reply, pp. 1-2.) Defendant reiterates the arguments made in the Demurrer as to the third cause of action. (Reply, pp. 3-4.)

 

                        Cause of Action No. 1 – Breach of Contract

 

“To establish a cause of action for breach of contract, the plaintiff must plead and prove (1) the existence of the contract, (2) the plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance, (3) the defendant’s breach, and (4) resulting damages to the plaintiff.  [Citation.]”  (Maxwell v. Dolezal (2014) 231 Cal.App.4th 93, 97-98.) “If the action is based on an alleged breach of a written contract, the terms must be set out verbatim in the body of the complaint or a copy of the written agreement must be attached and incorporated by reference.” (Harris v. Rudin, Richman & Appel (1999) 74 Cal.App.4th 229, 307.) “A written contract may be pleaded either by its terms – set out verbatim in the complaint or a copy of the contract attached to the complaint and incorporated therein by reference – or by its legal effect. [Citations.]” (McKell v. Washington Mutual, Inc. (2006) 142 Cal.App.4th 1457, 1489.)

 

Here, Plaintiffs concede that the first cause of action is only applicable to Cha and not EHC, and are amenable to amending the FAC to make this distinction clearer should the Court require. (Opp., p. 5.) Plaintiffs state they have also “already extended this offer to Defendant at the previous meet-and-confer telephone conference.” (Opp., p. 5.) In Reply, Defendant stipulates that the “First Cause of Action may be pursued, but the ruling on this demurrer should make clear that it may be pursued only on behalf of Bok Hee Cha. Eminent Hospice Care is not the proper plaintiff to the First Cause of Action and is not the injured party.” (Reply, pp. 1-2.)

 

Given the Court’s prior ruling and the parties’ concessions and stipulations in the briefings to the Demurrer, the Demurrer as to the first cause of action is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND pursuant to the terms of the parties’ stipulation.

 

Cause of Action No. 3 – Fraud

 

The elements of fraud are: “ ‘(a) misrepresentation (false representation, concealment, or nondisclosure); (b) knowledge of falsity (or ‘scienter’); (c) intent to defraud, i.e., to induce reliance; (d) justifiable reliance; and (e) resulting damage.’ ” (Charnay v. Cobert (2006) 145 Cal.App.4th 170, 184, citing Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 638.)  A cause of action for fraud must be “ ‘pled specifically; general and conclusory allegations do not suffice. … “ ‘This particularity requirement necessitates pleading facts which “ ‘show how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were tendered.’ ” ’ ” (Small v. Fritz Companies, Inc. (2003) 30 Cal.4th 167, 184, citing Lazar, supra, 12 Cal.4th at p. 645.)

 

“ ‘A promise made without any intention of performing it’ constitutes actual fraud.” (Church of Merciful Savior v. Volunteers of America, Inc. (1960) 184 Cal.App.2d 851, 858.) “Civil Code section 1566 provides that consent to a contract is voidable when obtained by, among other things, fraud. Civil Code section 1571 distinguishes between actual fraud and constructive fraud…. Civil Code section 1572 is limited to acts committed by one party to a contract with intent to deceive another party to the contract or to induce someone to enter into a contract.” (Masters v. San Bernardino County Employees Retirement Assn. (1995) 32 Cal.App.4th 30, 41.)

 

Here, the FAC alleges:

 

On or about May 1, 2024, at the office of her counsel S. Calvin Myung’s office, Defendant Moon met Plaintiff Cha and orally represented to Plaintiff Cha that upon successful closing of the Agreement between the parties, Defendant Moon will remain liable for Fifty Percent (50%) of any and all unknown and outstanding liabilities of EHC which were incurred on or prior to the Closing Date of the Agreement with the exception of those liabilities outlined in Exhibit “A” of the Agreement. At the time Defendant Moon made the foregoing promise to Plaintiff Cha, Defendant Moon had no intention of performing it. The foregoing promise was made by Defendant Moon with the intent to induce Plaintiff Cha to enter into the Agreement and purchase Defendant Moon’s Fifty Thousand (50,000) shares in EHC and pay Defendant Moon a sum of Five Hundred Thousand Dollars ($500,000.00) plus Eighty Thousand Dollars ($80,000.00) in additional consideration. Plaintiff Cha, at the time the foregoing promise was made and at the time Plaintiff Cha performed her promise under the Agreement as herein alleged, was ignorant of Defendant Moon’s secret intentions not to perform as promised; and Plaintiff Cha could not, in the exercise of reasonable diligence, have discovered Defendant Moon’s secret intentions.

 

In justifiable reliance on the foregoing promise of Defendant Moon, Plaintiff Cha Wilshire Blvd., Suite 2150 Los Angeles, CA 90010-2003 Kim Park Choi & Yi paid Five Hundred Thousand Dollars ($500,000.00) plus Eighty Thousand Dollars ($80,000.00) in additional consideration to Defendant Moon for the purchase Fifty Thousand (50,000) shares of EHC from Defendant Moon. If Plaintiff Cha had known the actual intentions of Defendant Moon not to perform the foregoing promise, Plaintiff Cha would not have taken such action.

 

As a proximate result of the fraudulent conduct of Defendant Moon as herein alleged, Plaintiffs were induced to incur the liability of Three Hundred Twenty-One Thousand Fifty-Seven Dollars and Seventy-Six Cents ($321,057.76) claimed by CMS on the CMS’ Reimbursement Demand in addition to the payment of Five Hundred Eighty Thousand Dollars ($580,000.00) in consideration under the Agreement to Defendant Moon.

 

Aforementioned conduct of Defendant Moon was an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, and/or concealment of a material fact known to Defendant Moon with the intention on the part of Defendant Moon of thereby depriving Plaintiffs of property or legal rights or otherwise causing injury, and was despicable conduct that subjected Plaintiffs to a cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of Plaintiffs’ rights, so as to justify an award of exemplary and punitive damages

 

(FAC, ¶¶ 45-51, Exh. A.)

 

Accordingly, the Court finds that the FAC’s allegations suffice to plead fraud – specifically, the FAC adequately alleges Defendant made a promise without any intention of performing as it pertains to the agreement, and Plaintiff’s consent to contract with Defendant occurred due to this fraud. (See Masters, supra, 32 Cal.App.4th at p. 41.)

 

Thus, the Demurrer as to the third cause of action is OVERRULED.

 

Accordingly, the Demurrer is SUSTAINED IN PART and OVERRULED IN PART.

 

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman, supra,18 Cal.3d at p. 348.) “If there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman, supra, 70 Cal.2d at p. 245). Given the arguments raised in the Opposition, the Demurrer to the FAC is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND as to the first cause of action.

C.    Motion to Strike

 

Defendant moves to strike the second cause of action from the FAC on the grounds that Plaintiffs did not seek leave of court to add this new cause of action to the FAC. (Motion, pp. 10-11.)

 

In Opposition, Plaintiffs contend that the Court’s ruling to sustain Defendant’s prior demurrer with 30 days leave to amend did not limit the Plaintiffs’ ability to add a cause of action to address the Court’s concerns with Plaintiffs’ contract assertions as to EHC. (Opp., pp. 7-8.) To the contrary, Plaintiffs state that the addition of the second cause of action was properly in compliance with the Court’s prior ruling. (Opp., p. 8.)

 

In Reply, Defendant states that the “new Second Cause of Action for breach of contract based on a theory of third-party beneficiary is a completely different cause of action from the First Cause of Action” alleged in the original Complaint. (Reply, p. 2.) Defendant states that this addition to the complaint exceeds the scope of amendment authorized by the Court in its prior ruling. (Reply, p. 3.)

 

Here, the Court finds the new cause of action does not exceed the bounds of amendment previously authorized by the Court in its 4/2/26 ruling. (4/3/26 Order.) Indeed, the Court finds that the new separate cause of action addressing Plaintiffs’ third-party beneficiary allegations for EHC directly addresses the Court’s concerns with the Complaint raised on page 3 of its 4/3/26 order. (See 4/3/26 Order, p. 3.)

 

            As such, the Motion is DENIED. Given that Defendant moved solely to strike the second cause of action from the FAC and did not demur to the second of cause of action, the Court need not further address the content of the FAC’s second cause of action at this time.

 

IV.            Conclusion & Order

 

For the foregoing reasons, Defendant Jeong Mee Moon’s Demurrer to Amended Complaint is SUSTAINED WITH 30 DAYS’ LEAVE TO AMEND IN PART and OVERRULED IN PART.

 

The Demurrer is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND as to the first cause of action. The Demurrer is OVERRULED as to the third cause of action.

 

Defendant Jeong Mee Moon’s Motion to Strike Second Cause of Action of Amended Complaint is DENIED.

 

Moving party is ordered to give notice.
