23VECV04263 TAMJIDI v. ASHLER OAKS, LLC, et al.

September 24, 2026, at 8:30 a.m.

Van Nuys Courthouse East, Dept. I

 Motion to be Relieved as Counsel

 

I.        Background

 

This is a landlord-tenant action arising out of the rental agreement between Plaintiff Hamid Reza Tamjidi (“Plaintiff”) and Defendants Ashler Oaks, LLC (“Ashler”), Tanglewood Apartments, LLC (“Tanglewood”), Cheyenne Vista, LLC (“CVL”), Redgate Arlington, LLC (“Redgate”), and the Lighthouse Group, LLC  (“Lighthouse”) (collectively, “Defendants”), for renting the real property located at 6220 Owensouth Ave., #102, Woodland Hills. CA 91367 (the “Subject Property”). On September 26, 2023, Plaintiff filed the operative Complaint against Defendants and DOES 1 through 50, alleging causes of action for: (1) Negligence – Cal. Civ. Code §§ 1714(a), 3333; (2) Breach of Warranty of Habitability; (3) Breach of Covenant of Quiet Enjoyment; (4) Premises Liability; (5) Nuisance; and (6) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress.

 

On July 12, 2024, Defendants filed an Answer to the Complaint and Cross-Complaint against Plaintiff and ROES 1 through 10 (collectively, “Cross-Defendants”), alleging causes of action for: (1) Breach of Contract; and (2) Common Counts.

 

On October 7, 2024, Plaintiff filed an Answer to the Cross-Complaint.

 

On July 1, 2026,  Non-Party Shahram Tamjidi (“Tamjidi”) filed a Motion to Determine Successor-in-Interest and Authorizing Substitution of Party Plaintiff. The matter was fully briefed and came for hearing on August 28, 2026; the motion was granted.

 

On August 31, 2026, Macauley Expenisi, Esq. of Oceanbridge Law Firm, APC (“Counsel”) filed the instant Motion to be Relieved as Counsel for Tamjidi. Any opposition would have been due on September 11, 2026. To date, no opposition has been filed.

 

II.     Discussion

 

The court may order that an attorney be changed or substituted at any time before or after judgment or final determination upon request by either client or attorney and after notice from one to the other. (Code Civ. Proc., § 284, subd. (2).) “The determination whether to grant or deny a motion to withdraw as counsel lies within the sound discretion of the trial court.” (Manfredi & Levine v. Superior Court (1998) 66 Cal.App.4th 1128, 1133.)    

An application to be relieved as counsel must be made on Judicial Counsel Form MC-051 (Notice of Motion and Motion) (Cal. Rules of Ct., Rule 3.1362, subd. (a)), MC-052 (Declaration) (Cal. Rules of Ct., Rule 3.1362, subd. (c)), and MC-053 (Proposed Order) (Cal. Rules of Ct., Rule 3.1362, subd. (e)). The proposed order must specify all hearing dates scheduled in the action or proceeding, including the date of trial, if known. (Cal. Rules of Ct., Rule 3.1362, subd. (e).)    

 

Further, the requisite forms must be served on the client and all other parties who have appeared in the case. (Cal. Rules of Ct., Rule 3.1362, subd. (d).) The court may delay the effective date of the order relieving counsel until proof of service of a copy of the signed order on the client has been filed with the court. (Cal. Rules of Ct., Rule 3.1362(e).) A motion to withdraw will not be granted where withdrawal would prejudice the client. (Ramirez v. Sturdevant (1994) 21 Cal.App.4th 904, 915.) 

           

            Here,¿Counsel has filed forms MC-051 and MC-052 and has lodged with the court a copy of the proposed order on form MC-053 as¿required.¿(Cal.¿Rules of Court, rule 3.1362.)¿ Counsel¿states¿the instant motion is filed for the following reason: “An irreconcilable breakdown in the attorney-client relationship has occurred, such that effective representation is no longer possible. Following the substitution of Shahram Tamjidi as successor-in-interest and Party Plaintiff in this action, a conflict of interest has arisen. The substituted Plaintiff has expressly requested that this office withdraw from representation in this matter. On August 29, 2026, by an email plaintiff terminated our firm. Withdrawal is necessary and appropriate under these circumstances. I am not aware of any prejudice to any party that would result from this withdrawal, and the case status, including any scheduled dates, is set forth in the accompanying motion papers. Continuing as attorney in the case will only result in further conflict of interest between a party and his attorney’s office.” (See MC-051, ¶3; MC-052, ¶2.) The court finds this is a valid reason to withdraw as counsel. (See Rules Prof. Conduct, rule 1.16,¿subd.¿(b)(4).) 

 

Counsel further declares¿the moving papers and¿accompanying¿forms have been served on Plaintiff via first-class mail on August 31, 2026 at Tamjidi’s last known address confirmed via his June 22, 2026 affidavit of successor-in-interest. (MC-052, ¶3b; POS, 9/1/26.)¿The proof of service accompanying the moving papers also¿demonstrates¿that all the other parties who have appeared in this case were served with required forms.¿(Cal. Rules of Ct., Rule 3.1362, subd.¿(d); POS re Electronic Service, 9/1/26.)¿

 

Moreover, the proposed order includes all of the¿remaining hearings scheduled in this action in accordance with California Rules of Court, rule 3.1362, subdivision (e). Although the jury trial is set for January 11, 2027, which is approximately four months from now, Counsel attests that Tamjidi sought to terminate the attorney-client relationship. Thus, there would be no prejudice in allowing Counsel to withdraw.¿

 

 

III.  Conclusion

 

Accordingly, Counsel Macauley Expenisi, Esq. of Oceanbridge Law Firm, APC’s Motion to be Relieved as Counsel for Shahram Tamjidi is GRANTED.

 

Counsel Macauley Expenisi, Esq. to give notice.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: MOTION TO QUASH SUBPOENA OR, IN THE ALTERNATIVE, FOR PROTECTIVE ORDER

 

Dept. I

HEARING DATE: September 24, 2026

TIME:  8:30 A.M.

 

COMPLAINT FILED: November 8, 2024

TRIAL DATE: March 15, 2027

 

I.       BACKGROUND  

This is a personal injury action arising out of the motor vehicle collision that occurred between Plaintiff Martha Jeannette Reyna (“Plaintiff”) and Defendants Jesus Trujillo, Maria Trujillo, and Sujey Trujillo (jointly, “Trujillo Defendants”). On November 8, 2024, Plaintiff filed a Complaint against Trujillo Defendants, Defendant PeopleReady, Inc. (“PRI”); Defendant TrueBlue, Inc. (“TBI”); and DOES 1 through 100 (collectively, “Defendants”), alleging causes of action for: (1) Motor Vehicle Negligence and (2) General Negligence.

 

On December 20, 2024, PRI and TBI filed their joint Answer to the Complaint. On January 7, 2025, Defendant Sujey Trujillo filed an Answer to the Complaint. On February 19, 2025, Defendants Jesus Trujillo and Maria Trujillo filed their joint Answer to the Complaint.

On June 2, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant Motion to Quash Subpoena or, in alternative, for Protective Order. On August 21, 2026, Defendant Sujey Trujillo filed an Opposition. On August 27, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Reply.

II.          LEGAL STANDARD

Pursuant to the Code of Civil Procedure Section 2025.010, any party may obtain discovery (subject to the scope and restrictions provided by law) “by taking in California the oral deposition of any person, including any party to the action. The person deposed may be a natural person, an organization such as a public or private corporation, a partnership, an association, or a governmental agency.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.010.) A deposition subpoena is the only means by which discovery may be obtained from a nonparty. (Unzipped Apparel, LLC v. Bader¿(2007) 156 Cal.App.4th 123, 127.)  

“If a subpoena requires the attendance of a witness or the production of books, documents, electronically stored information, or other things before a court, or at the trial of an issue therein, or at the taking of a deposition, the court, upon motion reasonably made by any person described in subdivision (b), . . . may make an order quashing the subpoena entirely, modifying it, or directing compliance with it upon those terms or conditions as the court shall declare, including protective orders. In addition, the court may make any other order as may be appropriate to protect the person from unreasonable or oppressive demands, including unreasonable violations of the right of privacy of the person.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.1, subd. (a).) “The following persons may make a motion pursuant to subdivision (a): (1) A party . . .” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.1, subd. (b)(1).)

III.       DISCUSSION

Meet and Confer

 To the extent that Plaintiff seeks a protective order in the alternative, a motion for a protective order “shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.060, subd. (a).) Specifically, “[a] meet and confer declaration in support of a motion shall state facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt, either in person, by telephone, or by videoconference, to informally resolve each issue presented by the motion.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2016.040, subd. (a).) “[A]ttempting informal resolution means more than the mere attempt by the discovery proponent ‘to persuade the objector of the error of his ways” and “a reasonable and good faith attempt at informal resolution entails something more than bickering with [opposing]counsel.... Rather, the law requires that counsel attempt to talk the matter over, compare their views, consult, and deliberate.” (Clement v. Alegre (2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 1277, 1294.)

            Plaintiff submitted the declaration of her counsels of record, Anthony Martirosyan and Raffi Bagdasaryan, attesting the following meet and confer efforts: “I was unavailable to participate in the September 10 telephone conference. I asked Raffi Bagdasaryan, an attorney with my firm, to conduct the conference on Plaintiff’s behalf. Anthony Morales, the declarant who signed the declaration supporting the moving papers, is no longer with The Vartazarian Law Firm.” (Martirosyan M&C Decl., ¶4.) “On September 10, 2026, at approximately 2:00 p.m., I participated in a telephone conference with Kelly W. Migoya, counsel for Defendants. Ms. Migoya and I were the participants on the call.” (Baghdasaryan M&C Decl., ¶3.) The telephonic meet and confer lasted fifteen (15) minutes and they parties were unable to informally resolve this matter. (Id., ¶¶4-7.) Accordingly, the Court finds that Plaintiff has fulfilled her meet and confer obligation.

 

            Merits of the Motion

Plaintiff moves for an order quashing the deposition subpoena of non-party Natasha Watson (hereinafter, “Ms. Watson”) on the grounds that the subpoena is overbroad, oppressive, unduly burdensome, and seeks confidential information. Specifically, Plaintiff argues that the subpoena seeks to obtain employment/performance testimony and medical impeachment from a non-party County employee. Alternatively, Plaintiff seeks a protective order limiting the examination of Ms. Watson to nonprivileged facts within her personal knowledge and prohibiting inquiry into confidential County/DPSS information and medical causation opinion.

In opposition, Defendant Sujey Trujillo (hereinafter, “Trujillo”) argues that the scope of the deposition subpoena is proper because Plaintiff affirmatively alleges loss of income and traumatic brain injury, thus places her employment history; earning capacity; job performance; and cognitive functioning at issue in this case. (Opp. at 1:25-2:3.) “[A]ny party may obtain discovery regarding any matters, not privileged, that is relevant to the subject matter involved in the pending action or to the determination of any motion made in that action, if the matter either is itself admissible in evidence or appears reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2017.010.) Here, Plaintiff does not contest that she alleges loss of income and traumatic brain injury. However, Plaintiff asserts that the examination seeks confidential County employee information from a character witness, thus encroaches upon her right to privacy. (Mot. at 4:2-8, 4:14-21; Morales Decl., ¶¶2-3, 6-7, 9-11, Exs. 1-2, 5-6.)

The right of privacy in the California Constitution (art. I, § 1), “protects the individual’s reasonable expectation of privacy against a serious invasion.” (Puerto v. Superior Court (2008) 158 Cal.App.4th 1242, 1250 [italics in original]; See Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 552 [“In Hill, we established a framework for evaluating potential invasions of privacy. The party asserting a privacy right must establish a legally protected privacy interest, an objectively reasonable expectation of privacy in the given circumstances, and a threatened intrusion that is serious.  The party seeking information may raise in response whatever legitimate and important countervailing interests disclosure serves, while the party seeking protection may identify feasible alternatives that serve the same interests or protective measures that would diminish the loss of privacy. A court must then balance these competing considerations”.].)

Trujillo admits that the deposition subpoena seeks testimony concerning Plaintiff’s employment, job performance, attendance, work limitations, and earning capacity. (Opp. at 6:4-6.) To the extent this scope of questioning could involve testimony about Plaintiff’s internal employment records and information, which are privileged, Ms. Watson does not have personal knowledge of during her time as Plaintiff’s supervisor, and/or requires Ms. Watson to give an expert opinion, the deposition subpoena is overbroad. Notably, Plaintiff’s employer has advised Ms. Watson that Plaintiff’s employment records are confidential. (Morales Decl., ¶3, Ex. 2.) As such, a protective order limiting the scope of the deposition subpoena examination is warranted. Trujillo concedes that a protective order is the appropriate remedy where some privacy interests exists. (Opp. at 6:7-11.)

 

            Request for Discovery Sanctions

            “Except as specified in subdivision (c), in making an order pursuant to motion made under subdivision (c) of Section 1987 or under Section 1987.1, the court may in its discretion award the amount of the reasonable expenses incurred in making or opposing the motion, including reasonable attorney's fees, if the court finds the motion was made or opposed in bad faith or without substantial justification or that one or more of the requirements of the subpoena was oppressive.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.2, subd. (a).)

            Here, Plaintiff’s counsel of record, Anthony Morales seeks $4,500.00 in monetary discovery sanctions for approximately 10.0 hours of time spent on meet and confer efforts, proposed stipulations, correspondence, and preparing this present motion at an hourly rate of $450.00. (Morales Decl., ¶12.) Furthermore, Trujillo fails to show substantial justification for seeking overbroad employment information via deposition subpoena and/or refusal to modify or limit the scope of the subpoena via stipulation. Thus, the Court will award monetary discovery sanctions as requested.

IV.    CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, Plaintiff Martha Jeannette Reyna’s Motion to Quash Subpoena is DENIED.

Alternatively, Plaintiff Martha Jeannette Reyna’s Motion for Protective Order is GRANTED. The deposition of Natasha Watson is limited to examination of nonprivileged matters within her personal knowledge including personally observed changes in Plaintiff’s functioning on the job.

Plaintiff Martha Jeannette Reyna’s Request for Sanctions is also GRANTED. Defendant Sujey Trujillo is ordered to pay $4,500.00 in monetary discovery sanctions within 30 days of this order.

Plaintiff Martha Jeannette Reyna to give notice.

I.       BACKGROUND  

This is a lemon law action arising out of Plaintiffs Maria Tinoco-Juarez (“Juarez”) and Angel Sanchez-Vazquez’s (“Vazquez”) purchase of 2021 Ford F-150, VIN: 1FTFW1E81MFC89048 (the “Subject Vehicle”) manufactured and/or distributed by Defendant Ford Motor Company (“FMC”) and allegedly repaired by Defendant Knight Sunrise Hollywood LLC d/b/a Sunrise Ford of North Hollywood (“Sunrise Ford”).

On December 16, 2024, Plaintiffs filed a Complaint against FMC, Sunrise Ford, and DOES 1 through 10 (collectively, “Defendants”), asserting causes of action for: (1) Violation of Song-Beverly Act – Breach of Express Warranty; (2) Violation of Song-Beverly Act – Breach of Implied Warranty; (3) Violation of Song-Beverly Act – Section 1793.2; (4) Fraudulent Inducement – Concealment; and (5) Negligent Repair.

On February 20, 2025, FMC filed a Demurrer to the Complaint. The matter was fully briefed and came for hearing on March 25, 2025; the demurrer was sustained with 20 days leave to amend as to the fourth cause of action for Fraudulent Inducement – Concealment.

On April 14, 2025, Plaintiffs filed the operative First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) against Defendants.

On May 15, 2025, Sunrise Ford filed its Answer to the FAC.

On June 13, 2025, FMC filed a Demurrer to the FAC. The matter was fully briefed and came for hearing on August 27, 2025 after being taken under submission; the demurrer was sustained without leave to amend as to the fourth cause of action.

On April 9, 2026, FMC filed its Answer to the FAC.

On June 11, 2026, FMC filed the instant Motion for Summary Adjudication. On August 25, 2026, Plaintiffs filed an Opposition. On September 3, 2026, FMC filed a Reply.

II.          LEGAL STANDARD

            “A motion for summary adjudication shall be granted only if it completely disposes of a cause of action, an affirmative defense, a claim for damages, or an issue of duty.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (f)(1).) “A defendant making the motion for summary adjudication has the initial burden of showing that the cause of action lacks merit because one or more elements of the cause of action cannot be established or there is a complete defense to that cause of action. [Citations.] If the defendant fails to make this initial showing, it is unnecessary to examine the plaintiff's opposing evidence and the motion must be denied. However, if the moving papers establish a prima facie showing that justifies a judgment in the defendant's favor, the burden then shifts to the plaintiff to make a prima facie showing of the existence of a triable material factual issue.” (Rehmani v. Superior Court (2012) 204 Cal.App.4th 945, 950.) “A prima facie showing is one that is sufficient to support the position of the party in question.” (Id.)

             “[T]here is no obligation on the opposing party . . . to establish anything by affidavit unless and until the moving party has by affidavit stated facts establishing every element . . . necessary to sustain a judgment in his favor.”  (Consumer Cause, Inc. v. SmileCare (2001) 91 Cal.App.4th 454, 468 (quoting Huynh v. Ingersoll-Rand (1993) 16 Cal.App.4th 825, 830-31 (internal quotation marks omitted)) (alteration in original).)  

III.       DISCUSSION

            Timeliness

            Code of Civil Procedure Section 437c, subdivision (a)(2) provides that “[n]otice of the motion and supporting papers shall be served on all other parties to the action at least 81 days before the time appointed for hearing.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (a)(2); see Cuff v. Grossmont Union High School Dist. (2013) 221 Cal.App.4th 582, 596.) If the notice is served by mail to an address within the State of California, the 81-day period is increased by 5 days. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (a)(2).)

            “The motion shall be heard no later than 30 days before the date of trial, unless the court for good cause orders otherwise. The filing of the motion shall not extend the time within which a party must otherwise file a responsive pleading.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (a)(3).)

            In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that the present motion for summary adjudication (hereinafter, “MSA”) should be denied on the grounds that it was not timely filed. (Opp. at) It is true that the initial trial date was set for October 5, 2026. (Min. Order, 10/10/25.) As such, thirty (30) calendar days before trial would have been September 8, 2026, making the date for service and filing of the MSA due by June 22, 2026 – at least 81 calendar days of the noticed hearing date, i.e., September 8, 2026. Although the MSA was file don June 11, 2026, it was noticed for September 14, 2026, which more than the thirty (30) calendar day requirement. Nevertheless, this issue is moot since the Court on its own motion continued the trial date to November 30, 2026. (Min. Order, 6/24/26.)

            Therefore, the Court will rule on the merits of the MSA.

 

            Evidentiary Objections

            Plaintiffs object to portions of the declaration of Sheerin S. Tehrani in support of FMC’s summary adjudication motion on the grounds of hearsay, lack of foundation, and lack of personal knowledge. (See Plf. Evidentiary Objections, 8/25/26.) Objections based on lack of foundation, hearsay, and lack of personal knowledge should be overruled unless there is a reason to believe that a witness, including an expert witness, would not be able to lay a foundation and authenticate these facts at trial. (See Sweetwater Union High School Dist. v. Gilbane Building Co. (2019) 6 Cal.5th 931, 947-948.)

            Accordingly, Plaintiffs’ evidentiary objections are OVERRULED.

 

            Summary Adjudication

            FMC moves for summary adjudication on the second cause of action for Breach of Implied Warranty and fifth cause of action for Negligent Repair on the grounds that: (1) there is no evidence of any defect manifesting within the first year of ownership that rendered the vehicle unmerchantable or unsuitable for its intended purpose; and (2) the claim for n egligent repair is barred by the economic loss rule.

                        Scope of Pleadings

            The function of the pleadings in a motion for summary judgment is to delimit the scope of the issues; the function of the affidavits or declarations is to disclose whether there is any triable issue of fact within the issues delimited by the pleadings.  (FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima (1991) 231 Cal. App. 3d 367, 381-382.)  Since the pleadings delimit the issues, summary judgment cannot be granted or denied on grounds not raised by the pleadings. (Bostrom v. County of San Bernardino (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 1654, 1663.)

            The FAC alleges, inter alia, that on October 29, 2021, Plaintiffs purchased the Subject Vehicle for $81,596.36 from Galpin Ford, a FMC-authorized dealership. (Compl., ¶¶9, 59.) The sale of the Subject Vehicle was accompanied by an implied warranty that the Subject Vehicle was merchantable pursuant to Civil Code Section 1792. (Id., ¶118.) The Subject Vehicle was delivered to Plaintiffs with latent defects, thus not fit for the ordinary purpose for which goods are used. (Id., ¶¶119-122.) Specifically, the Subject Vehicle was equipped with a 10R80 transmission. (Id., ¶¶60.) Plaintiffs presented the Subject Vehicle to a FMC-authorized dealerships, Galpin Ford and/or Sunrise Ford for repair after experiencing drivability concerns stemming from the 10R80 transmission on or around December 4, 2023; June 18, 2024; and September 5, 2024. (Id., ¶¶65-67, 34.) The service technicians at the dealerships after each repair visit represented that the Subject Vehicle had been repaired and was safe to drive. (Ibid.) Sunrise Ford owed a duty to Plaintiffs to use ordinary care and skill in storage, preparation, and repair of the Subject Vehicle in accordance with industry standards. (Id., ¶35.) Sunrise breached its duty by failing to properly store, prepare, and repair the Subject Vehicle in accordance with industry standards. (Id., ¶36.) Sunrise Ford’s breach was the proximate cause of Plaintiff’s damages. (Id., ¶37.)

 

                        Breach of the Implied Warranty (2nd COA)

            Pursuant to Civil Code Section 1792, “[u]nless disclaimed in the manner prescribed by this chapter, every sale of consumer goods that are sold at retail in this state shall be accompanied by the manufacturer's and the retail seller’s implied warranty that the goods are merchantable. The retail seller shall have a right of indemnity against the manufacturer in the amount of any liability under this section.” (Civ. Code, § 1792.) “[U]nder the implied warranty of merchantability and the Song-Beverly Act: products must ‘(1) pass without objection in the trade under the contract description; (2) [be] fit for the ordinary purposes for which those goods are used; (3) [be] adequately contained, packaged, and labeled; and (4) conform to the promises or affirmation of fact made on the container or label.’ ” (Gagetta v. Walmart, Inc. (N.D. Cal. 2022) 646 F.Supp.3d 1164, 1178.)

            “Such fitness is shown if the product ‘is “in safe condition and substantially free of defects”....’ [Citation.]” (Brand v. Hyundai Motor America (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 1538, 1546.) “Thus, a new car need not ‘be perfect in every detail’; rather, its implied merchantability ‘requires only that a vehicle be reasonably suited for ordinary use.’ ” (Id.) Essentially, “a merchantable vehicle under the statute requires more than the mere capability of ‘just getting from point “A” to point “B.” ’ ” (Id.)

1.      Defendant’s Initial Burden

            Here, FMC present evidence showing, inter alia, that Plaintiffs presented the Subject Vehicle to Galpin Ford for routine warranty maintenance, wherein the dealership performed an oil and filter change, tire rotation, tire pressure check, and multipoint inspection on July 15, 2022; December 10, 2022; May 13, 2023; and August 30, 2024. (Def. Sep. Stmt. of Fact (“DSSF”) nos. 2, 4-5, 9.) On August 3, 2022, Plaintiffs presented the Subject Vehicle to Galpin Ford for recall and field service work, wherein the dealership performed module reprogramming, software updates and recall repairs. (DSSF no. 3.) Basically, the repair records do not reflect that Plaintiffs expressed concerns about harsh shifting, transmission malfunction, rough idle, shaking, loos of power, or drivability during the first year of purchase. (DSSF no. 11.)

            Specifically, Plaintiffs presented the Subject Vehicle to Galpin Ford for concerns that the sync display would shut off while driving, the towing system reported a fault, and the vehicle exhibited a rough idle on December 4, 2023. (DSSF no. 6.) The dealership performed module reprogramming, software updates, and diagnostic and repairs procedures including parts replacement to address these concerns. (Id.) Plaintiffs presented the Subject Vehicle to Galpin Ford for concerns of irregular idle and lack of power on May 14, 2024 but Plaintiffs removed the Subject Vehicle before repairs were completed. (DSSF no. 7.) Plaintiffs presented the Subject Vehicle to Galpin Ford for concerns that the Subject Vehicle shook while driving and exhibited a rough idle on June 18, 2024, which the dealership verified and performed diagnostic testing, software updates, and reprogramming. (DSSF no. 8.) Finally, Plaintiffs presented the Subject Vehicle to Sunrise Ford for concerns that the Subject Vehicle shook while driving/during operation and displayed system fault messages on September 5, 2024. (DSSF no. 10.)

            In light of the evidence presented by FMC, a reasonable jury could find that the repair records for the Subject Vehicle does reflect consumer complaints about latent defects during the first year of purchase, which rendered the Subject Vehicle unmerchantable. Therefore, FMC has to meet its initial burden that no triable issue of material fact exists as to whether the Subject Vehicle was unmerchantable under Civil Code Section 1792.

 

2.      Plaintiffs’ Burden

            Plaintiffs submits evidence showing, inter alia, that Vazquez voiced concerns about the Subject Vehicle that were not listed on the repair order on July 15, 2022. (Plf. Addtl. Stmt of Fact (“PASF”) no. 12.) Furthermore, Vazquez testified that he was informed that the dealership could only address two to three issues at a time. (Id.) Vazquez also experienced shaking while driving the Subject vehicle about four to six months after Plaintiffs’ purchased the Subject Vehicle. (PASF no. 1.) Plaintiffs told the dealership about the shaking issue multiple times after it began and were told the dealership does not know why the Subject Vehicle shakes. (PASF no. 2.) In fact, Juarez testified that on December 10, 2022, she personally brought the Subject Vehicle to Galpin Ford and reported the shaking condition. (PASF nos. 2-4.) Despite not feeling safe in the Subject Vehicle, Plaintiffs continued to use it because they had no alternative means of transportation. (PASF nos. 6-10.)

            Reviewing the evidence submitted by Plaintiffs, a reasonable jury could find that Plaintiffs complained of a latent defects plaguing the Subject Vehicle within the first year of purchase that were not written down by the dealership.

            Thus, the Court finds that Plaintiffs have presented substantial evidence showing a triable issue of material facts exists as to whether the Subject Vehicle was unmerchantable under Civil Code Section 1792.

 

                        Negligent Repair (5th COA)

            “To state a cause of action for negligence, a plaintiff must allege (1) the defendant owed the plaintiff a duty of care, (2) the defendant breached that duty, and (3) the breach proximately caused the plaintiff's damages or injuries.” (Lueras v. BAC Home Loans Servicing, LP (2013) 221 Cal.App.4th 49, 62.) Generally, “[e]ven in actions for negligence, a manufacturer’s liability is limited to damages for physical injuries and there is no recovery for economic loss alone.” (Seely v. White Motor Co. (1965) 63 Cal.2d 9, 18; see also North American Chemical Co. v. Superior Court (1997) 59 Cal.App.4th 764, 780 [“in actions arising from the sale or purchase of a defective product, plaintiffs seeking economic losses must be able to demonstrate that either physical damage to property (other than the defective product itself) or personal injury accompanied such losses; if they cannot, then they would be precluded from any tort recovery in strict liability or negligence. However, such authorities would seem to have little or no application when the commercial relationship of the parties does not involve the sale of goods or products, nor the rules developed under the law merchant and the Uniform Commercial Code, but rather relates only to the performance of services”].)

            Essentially, under the economic loss rule, “[w]here a purchaser’s expectations in a sale are frustrated because the product he bought is not working properly, his remedy is said to be in contract alone, for he has suffered only ‘economic’ losses.”  (Robinson Helicopter Co., Inc. v. Dana Corp. (2004) 34 Cal.4th 979, 988 (Robinson), quotation marks omitted.) 

1.      Defendant’s Initial Burden

            Here, FMC present evidence showing, inter alia, that Plaintiffs presented the Subject Vehicle to Sunrise Ford for repairs on September 5, 2024. (DSSF nos. 14, 18.) Sunrise Ford performed the repair services pursuant to contract. (DSSF nos. 15, 19.) Plaintiffs only allege economic losses relating to the Subject Vehicle. (DSSF nos. 13, 17.) Plaintiffs do not claim that they did not sustain any personal injury arising from the Subject Vehicle. (DSSF no. 21.)

            In light of the evidence presented by FMC, a reasonable jury could find that Plaintiffs’ suffered no physical damage or personal injury that arose as a result of Sunrise Ford’s repair attempt on the Subject Vehicle. Therefore, FMC has to meet its initial burden that no triable issue of material fact exists as to whether the Negligent Repair claim is barred by the economic loss rule. The burden now shifts to Plaintiffs to raise a triable issue of material fact.  

2.      Plaintiffs’ Burden

            Plaintiffs cite to Paragraphs 67 and 33-37 of the FAC for support of their Negligent Repair claim. (Plf. Sep. Stmt of Facts (“PSSF”) nos. 13, 15, 17-19, 21.) However, the cited allegations do not show that Plaintiffs suffered any physical injury or property damage outside of the alleged latent defects. (Ibid.) Moreover, it is undisputed that Sunrise Ford’s repair services were performed pursuant to contract. (DSSF nos. 15, 19.) Plaintiffs assertion that they were exposed to risk of personal injury due to the unresolved claimed defects does not absolve their burden to provide evidence showing that they suffered property damage and/or physical injury as a result of the alleged repairs made by Sunrise Ford to sustain a claim for Negligent Repair. There is no legal authority cited by Plaintiffs that allows for such exposure to risk of personal injury to shield a Negligent Repair claim from being barred by the economic loss rule. Notably, Plaintiffs’ reliance on Robinson Helicopter Co., Inc. v. Dana Corp. (2004) 34 Cal.4th 979 is misplaced because the court found that the economic loss rule did not bar fraud and intentional misrepresentations claims since affirmative misrepresentations constitute a tort independent of a breach of contract. (Robinson Helicopter Co., Inc. v. Dana Corp. (2004) 34 Cal.4th 979, 991.) There is no such claim alleged in this present lawsuit, thus falls outside the scope of the pleadings.

            Reviewing the lack of evidence submitted by Plaintiffs, a reasonable jury could not find that Plaintiffs suffered property damage and/or physical injury as a result of the alleged repairs made by Sunrise Ford.

            Thus, the Court finds that Plaintiffs failed to present substantial evidence showing a triable issue of material facts exists as to whether the Negligent Repair claim is barred by the economic loss rule.

IV.       CONCLUSION

            Based on the foregoing, Defendant Ford Motor Company’s Motion for Summary Adjudication is DENIED IN PART as to the second cause of action and GRANTED IN PART as to the fifth cause of action.

I.       BACKGROUND  

This is a personal injury action arising out of Plaintiff Delissa Chase (“Plaintiff”) being bitten by a dog under the care of Defendants Kelli Rust d/b/a Elite Pet Care (“Rust”), Katie Rose Cullen, erroneously sued as Katie Rose (“Cullen”), and DOES 1 to 20 (collectively, “Defendants”). On December 17, 2024, Plaintiff filed a Complaint against Defendants, alleging causes of action for: (1) General Negligence and (2) Premises Liability.

On March 28, 2025, Cullen filed a Demurrer with Motion to Strike the Complaint. The matter was fully briefed and came for hearing on April 25, 2025; the demurrer was sustained with 20 days leave to amend as to the second cause of action and the motion was granted with 20 days leave to amend.

On May 8, 2025, Plaintiff filed the operative First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) against Defendants.

On June 20, 2025, Cullen filed a Motion to Strike the Complaint. The matter was fully briefed and came for hearing on July 24, 2025; the motion was granted striking the request for punitive damages without leave to amend.

Any reply would have been due on September 14, 2026 per Code of Civil Procedure Section 437c, subdivision (b)(4). To date, no reply has been filed.

II.          LEGAL STANDARD

            A party seeking summary judgment has the burden of producing evidentiary facts sufficient to entitle him/her to judgment as a matter of law. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c); Vesely v. Sager (1971) 5 Cal.3d 153.) The moving party must make an affirmative showing that he/she is entitled to judgment irrespective of whether or not the opposing party files an opposition. (Villa v. McFerren (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 733.) When a Defendant or Cross-Defendant seeks summary judgment , he/she must show either (1) that one or more elements of the cause of action cannot be established; or (2) that there is a complete defense to that cause of action. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).)

             “[T]here is no obligation on the opposing party . . . to establish anything by affidavit unless and until the moving party has by affidavit stated facts establishing every element . . . necessary to sustain a judgment in his favor.”  (Consumer Cause, Inc. v. SmileCare (2001) 91 Cal.App.4th 454, 468 (quoting Huynh v. Ingersoll-Rand (1993) 16 Cal.App.4th 825, 830-31 (internal quotation marks omitted)) (alteration in original).) The trial court cannot properly grant summary judgment if the moving party fails to meet their burden.  (Id.)  A summary judgment granted under those circumstances would have to be reversed even if the opposing party failed to introduce evidence establishing a triable issue of fact.  (Id.)

III.       DISCUSSION

            Timeliness

            Code of Civil Procedure Section 437c, subdivision (a)(2) provides that “[n]otice of the motion and supporting papers shall be served on all other parties to the action at least 81 days before the time appointed for hearing.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (a)(2); see Cuff v. Grossmont Union High School Dist. (2013) 221 Cal.App.4th 582, 596.) If the notice is served by mail to an address within the State of California, the 81-day period is increased by 5 days. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (a)(2).)

            “The motion shall be heard no later than 30 days before the date of trial, unless the court for good cause orders otherwise. The filing of the motion shall not extend the time within which a party must otherwise file a responsive pleading.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (a)(3).)

            Here, the present motion for summary judgment or in the alternative, summary adjudication (“MSJ”) was noticed for September 24, 2026, thus Rust had until July 6, 2026 to file the MSJ because it would at least eighty-one (81) calendar days before September 24, 2026. The MSJ was filed on June 30, 2026. Furthermore, a MSJ must be heard at least thirty (30) calendar days before trial, which in this case is set for October 26, 2026. As such, the MSJ had to be noticed for hearing no later than September 28, 2026. Accordingly, the MSJ was timely filed.

 

            Evidentiary Objections

            Plaintiff object to portions of the declaration of Kelli Rust and select separate statement of material facts in support of Rust’s MSJ on the grounds of hearsay, relevance, argument, speculation, lack of foundation, and lack of personal knowledge. (See Plf. Evidentiary Objections, 9/4/26.) Objections based on lack of foundation, hearsay, and lack of personal knowledge should be overruled unless there is a reason to believe that a witness, including an expert witness, would not be able to lay a foundation and authenticate these facts at trial. (See Sweetwater Union High School Dist. v. Gilbane Building Co. (2019) 6 Cal.5th 931, 947-948.) As such, objection nos. 1, 3, 5 and 6 are OVERRULED.  Objection Nos. 2, and 4 are OVERRULED on the grounds that Rust may testify to her lay opinion and/or state of mind. (See Evid. Code §§ 702, 1250.) Lastly, objection no. 7 is OVERRULED because there is other evidence cited in Defendant’s Separate Statement at No. 25 in addition to the alleged paragraph 31 of Rust’s declaration.  

 

            Summary Judgment

            Rust moves for summary judgment or in the alternative, summary adjudication on the first cause of action for General Negligence and second cause of action for Premises Liability on the grounds that: (1) Rust owed no duty to Plaintiff and cannot be held liable for failure to warn Plaintiff because Rust had no actual or constructive knowledge that the dog presented a danger nor that the bite was foreseeable [issue nos. 1-2]; (2) Rust is not strictly liable under Civil Code Section 3342 because she does not own the dog [issue no. 3]; (3) Rust is not liable under Civil Code Section 846 because she is not the owner of the Premises and Plaintiff was not there for recreational purposes [issue no. 4]; and  (4) Plaintiff has no admissible evidence that Rust acted with malice, oppression, or fraud [issue no. 5]. (Not. Mot. at 2:1-3:3; Mot. at pp. 11-18.)

 

                        Scope of Pleadings

            The function of the pleadings in a motion for summary judgment is to delimit the scope of the issues; the function of the affidavits or declarations is to disclose whether there is any triable issue of fact within the issues delimited by the pleadings.  (FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima (1991) 231 Cal. App. 3d 367, 381-382.)  Since the pleadings delimit the issues, summary judgment cannot be granted or denied on grounds not raised by the pleadings. (Bostrom v. County of San Bernardino (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 1654, 1663.)

            The FAC alleges, inter alia, that Rust operates a dog daycare under the business name, Elite Pet Care (“EPC”) on the real property located at 6944 Geyser Ave, Reseda, CA 91335 (the “Premises”). (FAC, ¶GN-1; ¶¶Prem.L-1-L-3.) Plaintiff lived at the Premises. (Id.) On July 23, 2024, Rust was providing a dog daycare service to Cullen for her Dalmatian dog (the “Dog”). (Id.) Rust or her agents allowed the Dog to access the common area. (Id.) Plaintiff arrived home to disarm the alarm system and turned around to the see the Dog right next to her. (Id.) Plaintiff went to pet the Dog on the head and the Dog bit her hand (the “Incident”). (Id.) Prior to the Incident, Cullen told Rust either in writing via text message and/or verbally not to introduce the Dog to other people because of the Dog’s prior bite to another person’s hand. (Id.) Plaintiff was not informed that the Dog had bitten someone else’s hand before. (Id.) Rust also failed to implement safety protocols despite actual knowledge of the serious risk of harm. (Id.) Cullen knew or should have known EPC operated within a shared residential property where Plaintiff resides and would potentially come into contact with residents including Plaintiff and failed to directly warn Plaintiff or any other residents of the Dog’s dangerous propensities. (Id. ) Defendants are strictly liable. (Id.)

 

                        Negligence and Premises Liability

            “To state a cause of action for negligence, a plaintiff must allege (1) the defendant owed the plaintiff a duty of care, (2) the defendant breached that duty, and (3) the breach proximately caused the plaintiff's damages or injuries.” (Lueras v. BAC Home Loans Servicing, LP (2013) 221 Cal.App.4th 49, 62.) “[W]hether an individual owes a duty of care to another is a question of law.” (Portillo v. Aiassa (1994) 27 Cal.App.4th 1128, 1133.)

            “The elements of a cause of action for premises liability are the same as those for negligence.” (Jones v. Awad (2019) 39 Cal.App.5th 1200, 1207.) “Accordingly, the plaintiff must prove, ‘a legal duty to use due care, a breach of such legal duty, and the breach as the proximate or legal cause of the resulting injury.’ ” (Id.)

            “Under California law, a landlord who does not have actual knowledge of a tenant’s dog’s vicious nature cannot be held liable when the dog attacks a third person. In other words, where a third person is bitten or attacked by a tenant’s dog, the landlord’s duty of reasonable care to the injured third person depends on whether the dog’s vicious behavior was reasonably foreseeable.” (Donchin v. Guerrero (1995) 34 Cal.App.4th 1832, 1838.) “Without knowledge of a dog’s propensities a landlord will not be able to foresee the animal poses a danger and thus will not have a duty to take measures to prevent the attack.” (Id.) Whether the landlord had actual knowledge “can be satisfied by circumstantial evidence the landlord must have known about the dog’s dangerousness as well as direct evidence he actually knew.” (Id.) (See also Drake v. Dean (1993) 15 Cal.App.4th 915, 925 [“In determining the keeper’s liability for negligence for injuries inflicted by a domestic animal, the criterion usually adopted is one of reasonable anticipation of the occurrence, i.e., foreseeability.”].)

1.      Defendant’s Initial Burden

            Here, Rust presents evidence showing, inter alia, that she has owned and operated a licensed dog daycare and boarding facility for over fourteen (14) years under the business name EPC. (Def. Sep. Statement of Facts (“DSSF”) no. 1.) This is undisputed. (Plf. Sep. Statement of Facts (“PSSF”) no. 1; Plf. Addtl. Material Facts (“PAMF”) no. 18.) Cullen owns a neutered male Dalmatian named Token, i.e., the Dog, and is a client of EPC. (DSSF no. 2.) Cullen completed an Intake Form and signed a Boarding Agreement with Rust on or about April 10, 2024, wherein Cullen wrote that the Dog had never attacked anyone and that the Dog was “shy” and preferred people he knew. (DSSF nos. 3, 35.) Prior to the Incident, Plaintiff interacted with the Dog on four or five occasions. (DSSF no. 24.) These interactions were normal including petting the Dog whenever he would walk up to Plaintiff. (DSSF no. 25.) Cullen stated in an text message exchange with Rust after the Incident that she only saw the Dog bite a guy who was rough housing with the Dog, who does not like to be manhandled. (DSSF no. 36.)

            In light of the evidence presented by Rust, a reasonable jury could find that Rust had no prior knowledge of any vicious or dangerous propensities of the Dog to give rise to a duty of care and to warn Plaintiff. Therefore, Rust has met her initial burden that no triable issue of material fact exists as to whether Rust is negligent under general negligence and premises liability theories. The burden now shifts to Plaintiff to raise a triable issue of material fact.

2.      Plaintiffs’ Burden

            Plaintiff submits evidence showing, inter alia, that Rust was responsible for the care, custody and control of the dogs at EPC including the Dog involved in the Incident. (PAMF nos. 2-4.) Rust was also responsible for implementing and enforcing safety protocols at EPC. (PAMF no. 5.) Prior to the Incident, Cullen verbally warned EPC not to introduce the Dog to other people without a proper introduction and stated it would be best if she was present when the Dog was introduced to new people. (PAMF nos. 6, 21-22.) Cullen testified that to her understanding, Plaintiff had never met the Dog before the Incident. (PAMF no. 24.) Furthermore, one occasion prior to the Incident, Rust had informed Cullen via text message that the Dog had been acting up including posturing, stiffness in the body, and not wagging the tail, thus had to be separated from another dog at EPC. (PAMF no. 30.) Moreover, Rust did not warn Plaintiff about the Dog. (PAMF nos. 7, 11, 28.) Additionally, the Dog’s records include reference to severe fear/mild aggression and territorial/protective aggression. (PAMF no. 20.) Rust did not review the Dog’s medical records outside of vaccinations and if the dog is spade or neutered before boarding and had no written policy for investigating bite history or keeping dogs with a bite history out of shared residential areas. (PAMF nos. 31-32.)

            Reviewing the evidence submitted by Plaintiff, a reasonable jury could find that prior to the Incident Rust had at least constructive knowledge that the Dog had dangerous propensities that give rise to a duty of care and to warn Plaintiff. Thus, the Court finds that Plaintiff has presented substantial evidence showing a triable issue of material fact exists as to whether Rust is negligent under general negligence and premises liability theories.

 

                        Strict Liability

            Civil Code Section 3342, subdivision (a) provides in pertinent part: “The owner of any dog is liable for the damages suffered by any person who is bitten by the dog while in a public place or lawfully in a private place, including the property of the owner of the dog, regardless of the former viciousness of the dog or the owner’s knowledge of such viciousness. A person is lawfully upon the private property of such owner within the meaning of this section when he is on such property in the performance of any duty imposed upon him by the laws of this state or by the laws or postal regulations of the United States, or when he is on such property upon the invitation, express or implied, of the owner.” (Civ. Code, § 3342, subd. (a); see also Laverone v. Mangianti (1871) 41 Cal. 138, 140 [“[T]he owner, knowing the vicious propensities and ferocious nature of the animal, keeps it at his own risk, and he should bear the responsibility for any injury inflicted by it upon a person who is free from fault.”].)

            However, “ ‘[a] statute imposing liability on the ‘owner’ of a dog without regard to knowledge of his vicious nature does not apply to one merely harboring or keeping a dog not owned by him, and, to render him liable, previous knowledge of the dog’s vicious nature must appear.’ ” (Buffington v. Nicholson (1947) 78 Cal.App.2d 37, 41; see also Northon v. Schultz (1955) 130 Cal.App.2d 488, 489; Smith v. Royer (1919) 181 Cal. 165, 168.) “The defenses of assumption of the risk and contributory negligence may still be asserted….” (Johnson v. McMahan (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 173, 176; see also Burden v. Globerson (1967) 252 Cal.App.2d 468, 470.)

1.      Defendant’s Initial Burden

            Here, the following facts are undisputed: (1) Rust owned and operated EPC; (2) Rust was responsible for the care, custody, and control of all dogs at EPC including the Dog involved in the Incident; (3) Rust was responsible for implementing and enforcing safety protocols at EPC; (4) Cullen was a client of EPC and the owner of the Dog involved in the Incident; and (5) Plaintiff moved onto the premises knowing that EPC was operated therein. (DSSF nos. 1-3, 12; PAMF nos. 1-5, 14, 18.)

            However, as discussed above, Plaintiff has submitted evidence showing, inter alia, that prior to the Incident, Cullen verbally warned EPC not to introduce the Dog to other people without a proper introduction and stated it would be best if she was present when the Dog was introduced to new people. (PAMF nos. 6, 21-22.) Also, Cullen testified that to her understanding, Plaintiff had never met the Dog before the Incident. (PAMF no. 24.) Moreover, prior to the Incident, Rust texted Cullen that the Dog had been acting up including posturing, stiffness in the body, and not wagging the tail, thus had to be separated from another dog at EPC. (PAMF no. 30.)  Nonetheless, Rust did not warn Plaintiff about the Dog nor did Rust review the Dog’s medical records outside of vaccinations and if the dog is spade or neutered before boarding despite the records indicating that the Dog had severe fear/mild aggression and territorial/protective aggression. (PAMF nos. 7, 11, 20, 28, 31-32.)

            With respect to assumption of the risk, Rust submitted evidence showing that Plaintiff had dogs all her life. (DSSF nos. 11, 22.) Furthermore, Plaintiff entered the gated area where the Dog was contained to disarm the residential alarm. (DSSF no. 32.) Plaintiff pet and kissed the head of the Dog before reaching out to pet him once more. (Id.) Rust presents no evidence that Plaintiff’s prior interactions with her own dogs and/or dogs in general suggests she knew of the Dog’s dangerous propensities.

            In light of the evidence presented by Rust, a reasonable jury could find that Rust was a keeper of the Dog involved in the Incident and knew of the Dog’s dangerous propensities prior to the Incident. Therefore, Rust has failed to meet her initial burden that no triable issue of material fact exists as to whether Rust is strictly liable under Civil Code Section 3342.

 

                        Liability under Civil Code Section 846

            Civil Code Section 846, subdivision (a) states in pertinent part: “An owner of any estate or any other interest in real property, whether possessory or nonpossessory, owes no duty of care to keep the premises safe for entry or use by others for any recreational purpose or to give any warning of hazardous conditions, uses of, structures, or activities on those premises to persons entering for a recreational purpose, except as provided in this section.” (Civ. Code, § 846, subd. (a).)

            “A ‘recreational purpose,’ as used in this section, includes activities such as fishing, hunting, camping, water sports, hiking, spelunking, sport parachuting, riding, including animal riding, snowmobiling, and all other types of vehicular riding, rock collecting, sightseeing, picnicking, nature study, nature contacting, recreational gardening, gleaning, hang gliding, private noncommercial aviation activities, winter sports, and viewing or enjoying historical, archaeological, scenic, natural, or scientific sites.”  (Civ. Code, § 846, subd. (b).)

            “This section does not limit the liability which otherwise exists for any of the following: [¶](1) Willful or malicious failure to guard or warn against a dangerous condition, use, structure or activity. [¶](2) Injury suffered in any case where permission to enter for the above purpose was granted for a consideration other than the consideration, if any, paid to said landowner by the state, or where consideration has been received from others for the same purpose. [¶](3) Any persons who are expressly invited rather than merely permitted to come upon the premises by the landowner.” (Civ. Code, § 846, subd. (d).)

1.      Defendant’s Initial Burden

            Here, Rust presents evidence showing, inter alia, that she offered for Plaintiff to stay at her residence where she operates EPC, in her garage space that was converted into a guest room. (DSSF nos. 8-9.) Although the converted garage space was separated from the main house, Plaintiff was allowed to come into the main house to use the bathroom or kitchen. (Ibid.) Plaintiff accepted Rust’s offer. (DSSF no. 12.) Likewise, Rust expected Plaintiff would help with EPC in exchange for staying at the Premises rent-free. (DSSF no. 13.)

            In light of the evidence presented by Rust, a reasonable jury could find that Plaintiff was not on the Premises at the time of the Incident for recreational purposes but rather upon invite to live on the Premises by Rust. Therefore, Rust has failed to meet her initial burden that no triable issue of material fact exists as to whether the Rust is liable under Civil Code Section 846.

 

                        Punitive Damages

            The California Civil Code Section 3294, subdivision (a) authorizes punitive damages upon a showing of malice, fraud, or oppression. Malice is defined as either “conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff”, or “despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.” (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (c)(1).) “Despicable conduct is conduct which is so vile, base, contemptible, miserable, wretched or loathsome that it would be looked down upon and despised by ordinary decent people.” (Mock v. Michigan Millers Mutual Ins. Co. (1992) 4 Cal. App. 4th 306, 331.) Fraud under California Civil Code Section 3294, subdivision (c)(3) “means an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known to the defendant with the intention on the part of the defendant of thereby depriving a person of property or legal rights or otherwise causing injury.” (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (c)(3).) The California Civil Code Section 3294, subdivision (2) defines oppression as “despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person’s rights.” (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (2).)

            Punitive damages thus require more than the mere commission of a tort. (Taylor v. Superior Court (1979) 24 Cal.3d 890, 894-95.) Specific facts must be pled in support of punitive damages. (Hillard v. A.H. Robins Co. (1983) 148 Cal.App.3d 374, 391-392.) A “conclusory characterization of [a] defendant’s conduct as intentional, wilful and fraudulent [is] [a] patently insufficient statement of oppression, fraud, or malice”. (Brousseau v. Jarrett (1977) 73 Cal.App.3d 864, 865.) Facts must be pled to show that a defendant “act[ed] with the intent to vex, injure or annoy, or with a conscious disregard of the plaintiff’s rights.” (Silberg v. California Life Ins. Co. (1974) 11 Cal.3d 452, 462.) “When nondeliberate injury is charged, allegations that the defendant’s conduct was wrongful, willful, wanton, reckless or unlawful do not support a claim for exemplary damages; such allegations do not charge malice.” (G.D. Searle & Co. v. Superior Court (1975) 49 Cal.App.3d 22, 29.) Conduct that is merely negligent will not support a claim for punitive damages. (Tomaselli v. Transamerica Ins. Co. (1994) 25 Cal.App.4th 1269, 1288.) 

            Here, Plaintiff does not oppose summary adjudication with respect to Rust only, who is the moving party. (Opp. at 17:1-12.) Accordingly, summary adjudication on the issue of punitive damages is GRANTED as to Rust only.

IV.       CONCLUSION

            Based on the foregoing, Defendant Kelli Rust d/b/a Elite Pet Care’s Motion for Summary Judgment is DENIED.

            Alternatively, Defendant Kelli Rust d/b/a Elite Pet Care’s Motion for Summary Adjudication is GRANTED as to issue no. 5 regarding punitive damages as to Defendant Kelli Rust d/b/a Elite Pet Care only and DENIED as to issue nos. 1 through 4.

            Defendant Kelli Rust d/b/a Elite Pet Care to give notice.

25VECV00592 PENA v. LOS ANGELES UNIFIED SCHOOL DISTRICT, et al.

September 24, 2026, at 8:30 a.m.

Van Nuys Courthouse East, Dept. I

Motion to Dismiss Action for Failure to Prosecute

 

 

I.        Background

 

This is an intentional tort action arising out of a physical assault against Plaintiff Sergio Pena (“Plaintiff”), which occurred at West Valley Occupational Center while Plaintiff was under the custody and/or control of Defendants Los Angeles Unified School District (“LAUSD”); Los Angeles County Office of Education (“LACOE”); City of Los Angeles (the “City”); County of Los Angeles (the “County”); West Valley Occupational Center (“WVOC”); John Doe (“Doe”); and DOES 1 through 50 (collectively, “Defendants”). On February 3, 2025, Plaintiff filed the operative Complaint against Defendants, alleging causes of action for: (1) Assault; (2) Battery; (3) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress; (4) Negligent Hiring, Supervision, & Retention of an Unfit Employee; and (5) Negligence.

 

On June 2, 2025, LAUSD filed an Answer to the Complaint.

 

On June 3, 2025, Plaintiff voluntarily dismissed LACOE without prejudice. On June 24, 2025, Plaintiff voluntarily dismissed the County without prejudice. On July 29, 2025, Plaintiff voluntarily dismissed the City without prejudice.

 

On September 4, 2025, default was entered against WVOC. On November 10, 2025, Plaintiff voluntarily dismissed        WVOC without prejudice.

 

On March 2, 2026, Plaintiff filed an Amendment to the Complaint identifying Doe as Dina Diment (hereinafter, “Diment”). On April 10, 2026, Diment filed an Answer to the Complaint.

 

On August 7, 2026, LAUSD and Diment filed the instant Motion to Dismiss Action for Failure to Prosecute. Any opposition would have been due on August 24, 2026 per California Rules of Court, rule 3.1342, subdivision (b). To date, no opposition has been filed.

 

II.     Discussion

 

“Discretionary dismissal is addressed by sections 583.410–583.430.” (Scarzella v. DeMers (1993) 17 Cal.App.4th 1762, 1768.)

 

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure Section 583.410, subdivision (a), “The court may in its discretion dismiss an action for delay in prosecution pursuant to this article on its own motion or on motion of the defendant if to do so appears to the court appropriate under the circumstances of the case.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 583.410, subd. (a).) “Section 583.410 allows the court to dismiss a case for delay in prosecution pursuant to rules adopted by the Judicial Council.” (Kidd v. Kopald (1994) 31 Cal.App.4th 132, 147; see also Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1340, subd. (a) [“The court on its own motion or on motion of the defendant may dismiss an action under Code of Civil Procedure sections 583.410-583.430 for delay in prosecution if the action has not been brought to trial or conditionally settled within two years after the action was commenced against the defendant.”].)

 

Code of Civil Procedure Section 583.420, subdivision (a) states in pertinent part as follows: “The court may not dismiss an action pursuant to this article for delay in prosecution except after one of the following conditions has occurred:

 

(1)   Service is not made within two years after the action is commenced against the defendant.

 

(2) The action is not brought to trial within the following times:

 

(A) Three years after the action is commenced against the defendant unless otherwise prescribed by rule under subparagraph (B).

 

(B) Two years after the action is commenced against the defendant if the Judicial Council by rule adopted pursuant to Section 583.410 so prescribes for the court because of the condition of the court calendar or for other reasons affecting the conduct of litigation or the administration of justice.

 

(3) A new trial is granted and the action is not again brought to trial within the following times:

(A) If a trial is commenced but no judgment is entered because of a mistrial or because a jury is unable to reach a decision, within two years after the order of the court declaring the mistrial or the disagreement of the jury is entered.

 

(B) If after judgment a new trial is granted and no appeal is taken, within two years after the order granting the new trial is entered.

(C) If on appeal an order granting a new trial is affirmed or a judgment is reversed and the action remanded for a new trial, within two years after the remittitur is filed by the clerk of the trial court.”

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 583.420, subd. (a).)

 

“While the discretionary dismissal statute was enacted for the benefit of defendants named in lawsuits, it was also enacted for the public’s benefit, i.e., to expedite the administration of justice by declogging court calendars and putting an end to elderly cases.” (Reid v. Balter (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 1186, 1196.) Furthermore, “[p]ast California decisions have affirmed dismissals as an exercise of inherent power in two situations. First, because delay in prosecution interferes with the orderly process of litigation and may make a fair trial unlikely, California courts have inherent power to dismiss civil cases for unreasonable, inexcusable delay in prosecution. [Citation]. Second, because courts should hear only actual disputes, and should prevent harassment of defendants, California courts possess the inherent authority to dismiss cases that are fraudulent or ‘vexatious.’ [Citation].” (Stephen Slesinger, Inc. v. Walt Disney Co. (2007) 155 Cal.App.4th 736, 758 [66 Cal.Rptr.3d 268, 286] disapproved of on other grounds by City of Los Angeles v. PricewaterhouseCoopers, LLP (2024) 17 Cal.5th 46, 63.) Additionally, “it is now well established that the policy [of preferring to dispose litigation on the merits] only comes into play when a plaintiff makes a showing of some excusable delay.” (Van Keulen v. Cathay Pacific Airways, Ltd. (2008) 162 Cal.App.4th 122, 131.)

 

LAUSD and Diment move for an order dismissing the operative Complaint in this instant case on the grounds that Plaintiff has displayed an unwillingness to meaningfully engage in any part of the litigation process to prosecute his claims. Specifically, LAUSD and Diment contend that Plaintiff has not responded to their counsel’s communication attempts, propounded discovery, and failed to oppose or appear at the discovery hearings. (Mot. at 4:11-15.)

 

In accordance with the discretionary dismissal statutes, the Court cannot dismiss an action for unreasonable delay unless: (1) service of the summons and complaint has not been made within two years [Code of Civil Procedure Section 583.420, subdivision (a)(1)]; (2) Plaintiff has failed to bring the case to trial within three years after the action was commenced [Code of Civil Procedure Section 583.420, subdivision (a)(2)(A)]; (3) Plaintiff has failed to bring the case to trial within two years after the action was commenced because of the condition of the court calendar or for other reasons affecting the conduct of litigation or the administration of justice [Code of Civil Procedure Section 583.420, subdivision (a)(2)(B)]; or (4) Plaintiff has failed to bring the case to trial after a new trial was granted [Code of Civil Procedure Section 583.420, subdivision (a)(3)].

 

Although LAUSD and Diment do not specify which subdivision they seek relief under, LAUSD and Diment generally appeared in this matter by filing their respective Answers to the Complaint, so there was no issue with service of the summons and complaint per subdivision (a)(1). (LAUSD Answer, 6/2/25; Diment Answer, 4/10/26.) Similarly, the initial trial is set for March 29, 2027, so has not taken place as of yet, therefore, subdivision (a)(3) would not apply in this present lawsuit either. However, this case was filed a year and seven months ago, thus subdivision (a)(2)(B) is applicable. (Compl., 2/3/25.) During the one year and seven months that this action was commenced against LAUSD and Diment, Plaintiff has failed to actively participate in litigating this case. LAUSD and Diment has submitted evidence showing that Plaintiff was properly served with discovery requests; Plaintiff failed to provided responses and participate in motion practice involving the discovery requests resulting the motions being granting in their favor; and Plaintiff failed to respond to schedule his deposition. (Fajardo Decl., ¶¶5-15, Exs. D-M.) Notably, Plaintiff has not opposed this instant motion despite being electronically served with the moving papers and supporting documents.

 

III.  Conclusion

 

Accordingly, Defendants Los Angeles Unified School District and Dina Diment’s Motion to Dismiss Action for Failure to Prosecute is GRANTED.
