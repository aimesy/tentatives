Order Regarding Discovery Motions

           

INTRODUCTION

 

             Petitioner Alex G. Romain (“Petitioner”) seeks an order deeming admitted the truth of matters specified in Petitioner’s First Set of Requests for Admission (“RFA”) propounded on Real Party in Interest B.B.  The Court GRANTS this request. 

 

            Respondent Harvard-Westlake School (“Harvard-Westlake”) moves for an order staying and quashing Petitioner’s Notice of Deposition of Person Most Qualified for Respondent Harvard-Westlake’s Request for Production of Documents, or in the alternative, a Motion for the Protective Order.  The Court DENIES the Motion to Quash. The Court GRANTS the Motion for a Protective Order with respect to Topic 11(a)-(c) but otherwise DENIES the motion.

PETITIONER’S REQUESTS FOR ADMISSION MOTION

 

A.     Requests for Admission Legal Standard

 

A party served with requests for admission must respond within 30 days. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.250, subd. (a).) If a party to whom requests for admission are directed fails to serve a timely response, that party has waived any objection to the requests, including ones based on privilege or on the protection for work product. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (a).) The requesting party may move for an order that the genuineness of any documents and the truth of any matters specified in the requests be deemed admitted, as well as for a monetary sanction. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (b).) It is mandatory that the court impose a monetary sanction “under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010)” on the party or attorney whose failure to serve a timely response to requests for admission necessitated a motion to deeming RFAs admitted. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (c).)

 

B.     Analysis

 

B.B. was served on May 13, 2026 and was required to respond no later than Jun 16, 2026. (Romain Decl. ¶¶ 3–4.) B.B. served no response, has served nothing since, and has not sought an extension. (Romain Decl. ¶¶ 5–6.) Because of B.B.’s lack of response, the statute requires the Court to grant the motion to deem the RFAs admitted.

 

Petitioner asks that the Court waive a monetary sanction. (Romain Decl., ¶ 7.) The statute says, “it is mandatory that the court impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) on the party. . . whose failure to serve a timely response to requests for admission necessitated this motion.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (c).) Despite the mandatory language in the statute, the Court does not impose sanctions for two separate and independent reason. First, a reasonable interpretation of that section is that it is mandatory when requested—not that the moving party is forbidden to waive the sanctions. Moreover, these sanctions are to be awarded under Civil Procedure section 2023.010 et seq. Section 2023.030 provides that the sanctions shall be for “reasonable expenses, including attorney’s fees.”  Here, Petitioner has no attorney’s fees as he is representing himself. Moreover, he has not submitted evidence of his expenses. Accordingly, no sanctions are appropriate here.

 

HARVARD-WESTLAKE’S MOTION TO QUASH DEPOSITION OF PMQ

 

A.     Legal Standard

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.230 requires that, where a deposition notice names an organization as the deponent, the notice “shall describe with reasonable particularity the matters on which examination is requested.” The organization must then designate and produce those officers, directors, managing agents, employees, or agents most qualified to testify on its behalf regarding those matters, to the extent of information known or reasonably available to the organization. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.230.)

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.410 permits a party served with a defective deposition notice to serve a written objection at least three calendar days before the deposition. The objecting party may then move for an order staying the deposition and quashing the deposition notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.410, subds. (a), (c).) The taking of the deposition is stayed pending determination of that motion. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.410, subd. (c).)

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.420 separately authorizes the Court, for good cause, to make any order that justice requires to protect any party, deponent, or other affected organization from unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, oppression, or undue burden and expense. Such orders may include that the deposition not be taken at all, that it be taken only on specified terms and conditions, that testimony be taken by written examination, or that another discovery method be used instead of oral deposition. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.420, subd. (b)(1), (5)-(7).)

 

B.     Motion to Quash Analysis

 

Harvard-Westlake objects to the First Amended Notice on the grounds that it seeks testimony into investigations, privilege-log issues, litigation anticipation, retained investigators/consultants, counsel-directed matters, and overbroad multi-year communications. (Motion, 3:18-25.)

 

As an initial matter, it is unclear why Harvard-Westlake seeks to quash the deposition notice. The motion identifies no notice defect under C.C.P. § 2025.410. The motion does not contend any PMQ topic lacks the “reasonable particularity” described in C.C.P. § 2025.230. The motion does not object to providing an appropriate witness on Topics 1, 2, 3, 8, and 9. (Motion, 5:19-20.) Thus, Harvard-Westlake’s motion to quash is DENIED. 

 

C.     Motion for a Protective Order

 

Harvard-Westlake also asks for a protective order under C.C.P. § 2025.420. When requesting a protective order, the burden of showing good cause rests on the moving party. (Nativi v. Deutsche Bank National Trust Co. (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 261, 318.) An objection based upon burden must be sustained by evidence showing the quantum of work required, while an objection of oppression must be sustained based on a showing of either unreasonable burden or that the ultimate effect of the burden is incommensurate with the result sought. (West Pico Furniture Co. of Los Angeles v. Superior Court In and For Los Angeles County (1961) 56 Cal.2d 407, 417.)

 

As a general matter, Harvard-Westlake has failed to meet their burden. The declaration accompanying Harvard-Westlake’s request contains no estimate of preparation hours, no count of necessary witnesses, no volume of records to review, and no identification of any specific record or communication that answering a topic would expose. Harvard-Westlake’s papers amount to generalized concerns about potential requests violating the attorney-client privilege or work product doctrine, but that is already addressed in the Notice of Deposition: “no matter of examination seeks the content of any attorney-client communication, the content of any writing protected by the attorney work-product doctrine, or any opinion, mental impression, conclusion, or legal theory of Harvard-Westlake’s counsel.” (Yousefzadeh Decl., ¶ 2, Exh. A.)

 

Harvard-Westlake offers no argument why they cannot simply object to particular questions during the deposition if they go beyond the just do what the Discovery Act allows them to do: object to a question during a deposition on the ground that it is privileged or protected work product. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.460, subd. (a).)

 

Harvard-Westlake’s motion also purports to challenge particular topics. In the interests of justice, the Court discusses the individual topics Harvard-Westlake has laid out in their motion.

 

1.      Topic 1

 

Topic 1 requests:

 

The searches HARVARD-WESTLAKE conducted for records responsive to the PUPIL-RECORDS REQUESTS, including: (a) the persons whose files, devices, or accounts were searched; (b) the systems, locations, and sources searched (including email accounts, mobile devices, messaging applications, and paper files); (c) the date ranges searched; and (d) the search terms used, if any.

 

(Yousefzadeh Decl., ¶ 2, Exh. A.)

 

            Harvard-Westlake contends that it should not be required to disclose search terms selected by counsel, attorney review methodology, privileged custodial decisions, or attorney mental impressions. The Court tends to agree that an attorney’s search terms should be off-limits and to the extent that Petitioner disagrees, the Court will discuss that issue with the parties at the hearing. In any event, no protective order is necessary as Harvard-Westlake can simply object at the deposition. Moreover, the deposition notice expressly does not ask for privileged information.

 

2.      Topics 4 and 5

 

Topic 4 requests:

 

For each INVESTIGATION: (a) the persons who conducted or participated in the INVESTIGATION; (b) the records the INVESTIGATION generated, including the categories of records created (for example, notes, witness statements or interview notes, reports or findings, and COMMUNICATIONS); and (c) the present custodian of those records.

 

(Yousefzadeh Decl., ¶ 2, Exh. A.)

 

Topic 5 requests:

 

For each INVESTIGATION, whether HARVARD-WESTLAKE has produced to Petitioner: (a) the findings, report, or summary of the INVESTIGATION; (b) notes or other records of interviews of A.R.; and (c) notes or other records of interviews of other witnesses—in each case as HARVARD-WESTLAKE did for the INVESTIGATION described in Definition No. 7(g) (see HW_00052–HW_00054)—and, for each such record that has not been produced, whether the record exists.

 

(Yousefzadeh Decl., ¶ 2, Exh. A.)

 

            Harvard-Westlake, relying on  BRV, Inc. v. Superior Court (2006) 143 Cal.App.4th 742, 747, argues that Topic 4 must be quashed in its entirety because it improperly treats investigative materials as pupil records. This argument puts the cart before the horse: that is a substantive issue to be decided at the trial of this matter. The Court declines to decide it in the context of a discovery motion or recognize this substantive argument as a reason not to provide discovery. Petitioner is entitled to take discovery on the existence of such records, and the question of whether the records must be produced under the Education Code will be decided at trial.

 

3.      Topic 6

 

Topic 6 requests:

 

The investigation conducted by investigator Lexi Zuidema following the February 2024 on-campus arrest of Lucca V., to the extent that investigation concerned A.R. or allegations concerning A.R., including: (a) whether the investigation concerned A.R. or allegations concerning A.R.; (b) the records the investigation generated concerning A.R., including notes or other records of interviews and any findings, report, or summary; and (c) whether HARVARD-WESTLAKE has produced each such record to Petitioner, as it did for the INVESTIGATION described in Definition No. 7(g) (see documents produced by Harvard-Westlake as A.R.’s pupil records, Bates-stamped HW_00052–HW_00054).

 

(Yousefzadeh Decl., ¶ 2, Exh. A.)

 

Harvard-Westlake also argues that this topic may seek testimony regarding investigations, interview notes, retained investigator work, and records generated by investigative activity, which may include counsel-directed work product and attorney-client communications. (Motion, 9:7-9.) However, the Court sees no reason to enter a protective order. The existing deposition notice states that: “no matter of examination seeks the content of any attorney-client communication, the content of any writing protected by the attorney work-product doctrine, or any opinion, mental impression, conclusion, or legal theory of Harvard-Westlake’s counsel.” (Yousefzadeh Decl., ¶ 2, Exh. A.) No privileged information is required. Harvard-Westlake can object as necessary. .

 

Finally, Harvard-Westlake argues that Investigation #6 (the inquiry into the allegations that Lucca V. sexually assaulted another athlete in the Harvard-Westlake pool on or about December 14, 2023, in connection with which inquiry A.R. was interviewed) is not actually about A.R. The deposition notice states, “Each matter for examination below concerns only A.R.—that is, records, information, and events relating to A.R.” The deposition notice is sufficient to address Harvard-Westlake’s concerns about sharing unrelated investigatory material.

 

4.      Topic 7

 

Topic 7 requests:

 

Each COMMUNICATION between HARVARD-WESTLAKE, on the one hand, and Petitioner, Melissa Romain, or A.R., on the other hand, concerning A.R.’s safety and welfare on the Harvard-Westlake campus or on the boys’ water polo team; whether HARVARD WESTLAKE personnel took notes of, or otherwise memorialized, each such COMMUNICATION; the categories of records created; and whether each such record has been produced to Petitioner.

 

(Yousefzadeh Decl., ¶ 2, Exh. A.)

 

            Harvard-Westlake contends that preparing a PMQ witness to testify about oral and written communications over six years is likely to be unduly burdensome and expensive. However, on a motion for a protective order, the burden of showing good cause rests on the moving party. (Nativi v. Deutsche Bank National Trust Co. (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 261, 318.) From Harvard-Westlake’s papers and declaration, the Court has no idea whether “each communication” refers to 10 communications, 75 communications, 500 communications or any other number. Harvard-Westlake’s papers also give no way for the Court to determine how many witnesses would be involved in responding to this request. Thus, it is impossible for the Court to evaluate whether this topic is likely to be unduly burdensome and expensive to Harvard-Westlake.

 

            Therefore, the Court declines to issue a protective order on this topic.

 

 

5.      Topic 10

 

Topic 10 requests:

 

The date on which HARVARD-WESTLAKE first anticipated litigation by or on behalf of A.R.

 

(Yousefzadeh Decl., ¶ 2, Exh. A.)

 

            Havard-Westlake offers to supply “a nonprivileged date” through a supplemental log, a written response, or a declaration. (Motion, § H.). Harvard-Westlake’s offer seems to indicate this information can be supplied without disclosing privileged content. And California law treats such timing facts as discoverable: the Supreme Court compelled an answer to when a party first consulted counsel—a fact about the relationship, not a communication within it. (Coy v. Superior Court of Contra Costa County (1962) 58 Cal.2d 210, 219-220.)

 

Therefore, Topic 10 is a proper subject of a deposition.

 

           

6.      Topic 11

 

Topic 11 requests:

 

For each numbered entry on the PRIVILEGE LOG: (a) the number of distinct documents within the Bates range identified in each entry; (b) the date of each such document; (c) the author and each recipient of each such document; and (d) for each entry referencing a retained investigator or third-party consultant, the name and professional capacity of that investigator or consultant, the date the engagement began, and the identity of the person who initiated or authorized the engagement.

            The Court agrees that subsections (a)-(c) are too unwieldy for a PMQ deposition. A deposition on these subjects has the potential to invade the attorney-client privilege. To the extent that the privilege log is unsatisfactory, Petitioner can demand a better privilege log and bring the issue to the Court’s attention if a problem remains.

            The one portion of Topic 11 that appears to be a legitimate subject for a deposition is subsection (d), which asks for foundational facts about the persons identified in the privilege log. A deposition on that subject can go forward.

            CONCLUSION

 

The Court GRANTS the request to deem the RFAs admitted.

 

The Court DENIES the motion to quash. The Court grants the motion for a protective order as to Topic 11(a)-(c) but otherwise denies the motion.

 

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Case Number: 26STCP02054    Hearing Date: August 5, 2026    Dept: 833

Jane Doe                                                                    Case No. 26STCP02054

                                                                        Hearing: August 5, 2026

v.                                                                                 Location: Stanley Mosk Courthouse

                                                                        Department: 833

Harvey Mudd College and Cindy Martinez           Judge: Joseph Lipner                                                                      

 

Order Granting In Part Motion to Stay Proceedings

 

 

            Petitioner Jane Doe (“Doe”) moves for an order to stay the operation of the administrative action by Respondents Harvey Mudd College (“Harvey Mudd”) and Cindy Martinez (collectively, “Respondents”), pending judicial review of her petition.

 

            The Court GRANTS the motion for stay in part. The administrative decision of Harvey Mudd is to be stayed pending the Court’s ruling on the underlying petition for writ of mandate. During that time, Petitioner’s grade will remain “incomplete” and Petitioner is no longer on Level 2 Probation. In all other respects, the Court DENIES the motion for stay.

 

            The Court also advances the trial setting conference, currently set for September 11, 2026, to today’s date. The Court will discuss trial dates with the parties.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

 

When parties file a writ under Code of Civil Procedure Section 1094.5, the court can stay the operation of an administrative order or decision pending the judgment of the court. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1094.5, subd. (g).) The Court need not make any determinations when granting a stay except to note that a stay is not against the public interest. (Canyon Crest Conservancy v. County of Los Angeles (2020) 46 Cal.App.5th 398, 407.)

DISCUSSION

 

A.    Summary of Facts

 

Doe has completed her junior year at Harvey Mudd College. (Doe Decl., ¶¶ 4-5.)

 

Doe has been accused of cheating from two professors in the Physics Department at Harvey Mudd. (Doe Decl., ¶¶ 7-8.) The first accusation, concerning Professor Tamayo and the Theoretical Mechanics class, is the subject of the pending writ petition.

 

In Theoretical Mechanics, Doe aced her midterms, which were take-home, open book, and open-note exams. (Doe Decl., ¶¶ 9–10.) After the mid-term, Doe missed many classes at the end of the course and performed poorly on the final, which was also in-person, closed-book and closed-note. (Doe Decl. ¶¶ 12, 14–15.) Doe explains this discrepancy between her performance at the mid-term and her poor performance on the final as being caused by a traumatic life event: Doe made the choice to euthanize her lifelong pet who was dying from cancer. (Doe Decl. ¶¶ 11–13.)

 

Apparently in light of the discrepancy between the mid-term and the final, Professor Tamayo accused Doe of cheating on the midterms. He made the accusation on December 19, 2025. (Doe Decl. ¶¶ 16–17.)

 

After a hearing, the Honor Board concluded that there was not sufficient evidence that Doe used the internet or AI on the midterms. (Doe Decl. ¶ 28.) But the Honor Board still concluded that there was “clear and convincing” evidence that Doe used “some sort of nonallowed resource,” despite what Doe contends is evidence to the contrary. (Doe Decl., ¶ 28, Exh. 5, Exh. 8.) The Honor Board never identified what non-allowed resource Doe allegedly used. (Doe Decl. ¶ 30.) The Honor Board sanctioned Doe with a lower grade on the course and placed her on Probation Level 2, which means Doe may be suspended or expelled for any other Honor Code violation. (Doe Decl. ¶ 34.) When Doe appealed, the Appeals Board affirmed. (Doe Decl. ¶ 37.)

 

The second accusation (which is not a subject of the currently-pending petition) was from Professor Lynn in Doe’s Optics Lab course, PHYS 134. Professor Lynn grilled Doe in an hour-long oral examination of material from PHYS 134. (Doe Decl. ¶ 45.) Professor Lynn then emailed Doe afterwards claiming that Doe’s lab report “does not reflect your own work” and believed “this rises to a level of concern” after she “consulted” with her “co-instructors”—one of those co-instructors included Professor Gerbode, who graded Doe’s first midterm in Theoretical Mechanics. (Doe Decl. ¶ 47.) Professor Lynn filed a charge with the Honor Board claiming that Doe used “inappropriate resources” on her lab report. (Doe Decl. ¶ 52.) Professor Lynn made this accusation in April 2026, and the hearing on this accusation will occur in fall 2026. (Doe Decl. ¶ 54.)

 

Doe now moves to (1) stay the college’s disciplinary decision in Theoretical Mechanics and enter what she refers to as her original grade in that course; (2) stay any other disciplinary actions against Doe until her writ petition is resolved and enter grades in all completed courses; and (3) stay any administrative action that would prevent Doe from graduating and obtaining a degree from Harvey Mudd if judicial review of her writ petition is not completed before Doe completes all other required courses for her degree program.

 

B.     The Court Grants a Stay of the Administrative Decision

 

The Court concludes that there is no harm to the public in granting a stay. The public has a strong interest in Doe completing her college education because “[e]ducation is vital and, indeed, basic to civilized society. It is an interest of almost incalculable value, especially to those students who have already enrolled in the institution and begun the pursuit of their college training.” (Boermeester v. Carry (2023) 15 Cal. 5th 72, 89 (citation modified).) In academic discipline, “the controlling facts and the nature of the conduct under challenge are often disputed. The risk of error is not at all trivial, and it should be guarded against if that may be done without prohibitive cost or interference with the educational process.” (Goss v. Lopez I (1975) 419 U.S. 565, 580.)

 

“The goal of a stay is to preserve the status quo while a court determines the merits of the appeal.” (Daly v. San Bernardino County Bd. of Supervisors (2021) 11 Cal.5th 1030, 1051.) To maintain the status quo, a Court should try to set things to the “last actual peaceable, uncontested status which preceded the pending controversy.” (Id., at p. 1045.)

 

The hearing board’s decision should be stayed. The Court will review the administrative decision at trial. Doe (and the general public) have an interest in making sure that this decision was made properly and in accordance with the law. There is no reason that the administrative decision must take effect now.

 

This means that Harvey Mudd must return to the status quo before the issuance of the administrative decision that the Court is now reviewing. The parties dispute how this should be accomplished. Doe asserts that she should be temporarily given “her original grade.” Respondents argues that she should be given an incomplete—something that is already reflected on her record.

 

 The Court agrees with Respondents.  While this action is pending,  Doe’s grade in Theoretical Mechanics will reflect an “incomplete.” Harvey Mudd must also remove any evidence of the administrative decision from Doe’s transcript or record—for all intents and purposes, with the issuance of this order, that decision is no longer “live”, at least until the Court has ruled on the underlying petition. Nor is Doe on probation while the administrative decision is stayed.

 

Doe argues that the Court should require Harvey Mudd to issue an actual letter grade, which she refers to as her original grade. A key problem with this request is that there never has been such a grade. From what the Court can discern from the record, no grade was ever issued in Theoretical Mechanics, and staying the case allows the Court to preserve the status quo, not create a new reality where Doe has a particular grade. While Doe states that the Honor Board issued a sanction of a “one and one-third reduction of my overall course grade” (Doe Decl., ¶ 34), she does not assert that any grade was given.  

 

Doe argues that Harvey Mudd could add up her grades for various assignments and assign a letter grade based on that calculations. She cites Doe v. University of California (2018) 28 Cal.App.5th 26, 34 to show that a court has previously ordered relief of requiring a particular letter grade. The case she cites, however, arose in a very different procedural context. There, the superior court judge had ruled on the merits in petitioner’s favor and against the university. While the appeal was pending, the Court of Appeal issued an order “giving full effect to the superior court’s judgment during the appeal” and thus ordered the granting of a letter grade in accordance with the superior court’s decision. (Id. pp. 33-34.)  This is far different than the current context, where the only ruling thus far has been against Doe, and this Court is merely staying that ruling and requiring adherence to the status quo before the ruling.

 

Moreover, at this stage, the Court leaves to Harvey Mudd the traditional role of determining Doe’s grade, and prior to the decision in the administrative hearing, that was an incomplete. (See generally Paulsen v. Golden Gate University (1979) 25 Cal.3d 803, 808 [courts will not intervene in the academic affairs of schools unless the school acted arbitrarily or in bad faith]; Lachtman v. Regents of University of California (2007) 158 Cal.App.4th 187, 203 [explaining that university faculties must have the widest range of discretion in making judgments as to the academic performance of students].)

 

One of the reasons Doe requests that the Court order Harvey Mudd to impose the grade she would have gotten had there not been a cheating allegation is because of the potential for irreparable harm. Doe explains that (1) she will suffer irreparable harm from submitting less competitive law school applications if she is forced to apply with an “incomplete” rather than a passing grade in Theoretical Mechanics, (2) she will suffer irreparable harm from being denied the opportunity to graduate alongside her friends and classmates if her writ petition is still pending in May 2027, assuming she otherwise has fulfilled all requirements for graduation, and (3) she will suffer irreparable harm from being forced to endure another unlawful disciplinary proceeding related to Lab Optics that may result in her expulsion from the college because she was placed on Probation Level 2 during the Theoretical Mechanics proceeding. (Reply, 6:4-12.)

 

There are a few problems with this irreparable harm analysis. First, there is no requirement that Doe submit law school applications this year. Doe can submit law school applications in a future year and can seek employment immediately after college is complete. Second, as the Court noted above, the administrative decision is now stayed, and Doe is no longer on Probation Level 2. Third, the Court is sensitive to the concerns about timeline and will work with both parties to schedule trial as soon as possible.

 

C.     The Court Does Not Stay Other Disciplinary Actions

 

Doe also asks the Court to stay other pending disciplinary actions until her writ petition is resolved. The Court declines to do this. The other disciplinary action is not ripe for the Court to review, as a final administrative decision has not been issued, and Doe has not exhausted her administrative remedies in that decision. The issuance of the stay, however, means that Doe is not on Probation Level 2 until the Court has ruled on the underlying petition. Harvey Mudd must take this into account in determining how the other disciplinary action ends.

 

Finally, Doe asks the Court to stay any administrative action that would prevent Doe from graduating and obtaining a degree at Harvey Mudd if judicial review of her writ petition is not completed before Doe completes all other required courses for her degree program. This is not an action that the Court has the ability to impose on these facts. It is also unrealistic – Harvey Mudd needs to have discretion to respond to events on their campus and it would make little sense to argue that one student is immune from a university’s discretion to manage the student discipline process.

 

 

CONCLUSION 

 

            The Court GRANTS the motion for stay IN PART. The administrative decision of Harvey Mudd is to be stayed – meaning Doe’s grade will be listed as “incomplete” and Doe is no longer on Level 2 Probation pending the Court’s decision on the underlying petition for writ of mandate.  In all other respects, the Court DENIES the motion for stay.
