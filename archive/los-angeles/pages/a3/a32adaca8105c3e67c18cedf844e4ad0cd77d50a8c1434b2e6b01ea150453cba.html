Case Number: 22SMCV01974    Hearing Date: August 7, 2026    Dept: P

Tentative Ruling

Yaghoub Kermanshahi, et al. v. Orbic Air, et. al., Case No. 22SMCV01974

Motions:

(1)   Plaintiffs’ Motion to Quash Orbic Air, LLC’s Subpoena for production of Sahel Kermanshahi’s employment, personnel, and medical records from CVS Pharmacy, Inc.

(2)   Plaintiffs’ Motion to Quash Orbic Air, LLC’s Subpoena for production of Sahel Kermanshahi’s employment, personnel, and medical records from State Pharmacy

Hearing Date: August 7, 2026

Background

Plaintiffs Simin Korei, individually and as successor to the estate of Yaghoub Kermanshahi, Sahel Kermanshahi, and Saman Kermanshahi sue Defednatns Orbic Air, LLC (“Orbic Air”); Hosking Aviation, Inc., Butterfly Floral and Event Design, Inc., and The Semler Complaies d/b/a Saddlerock Ranch (“Saddlerock Ranch”) for wrongful death, survival and loss of consortium. Plaintiffs allege that Yaghoub Kermanshahi was a guest at a wedding on May 30, 2021, at Saddlerock Ranch, when a helicopter flew over the wedding to drop flower petals, causing a temporary structure to collapse and injure Yaghoub Kermanshahi. (Second Amended Complaint [“SAC”], ¶ 13.) Plaintiffs allege that Yaghoub Kermanshahi suffered severe injuries and died on May 15, 2023. (SAC, ¶ 14.)

On October 25, 2022, Plaintiffs filed the complaint.

On March 23, 2023, Saddlerock Ranch filed a cross-complaint.

On December 7, 2023, Plaintiffs filed the first amended complaint.

On February 8, 2024, Cross-Defendants Sophia Hakakian and Eliot Khakshoy filed a cross-complaint.

On March 7, 2024, Plaintiffs filed the SAC.

On April 2, 2024, Hosking Aviation, Inc. filed a cross-complaint against Orbic Air.

On April 23, 2024, Orbic Air filed an Amended Cross-Complaint.

On June 18, 2024, the Court granted Saddlerock Ranch’s motion for leave to file an amended cross-complaint. On July 10, 2024, Saddlerock Ranch filed an amended cross-complaint.

On August 20, 2024, Osanna Anna Kotelyan filed a cross complaint

On September 23, 2024, the Court overruled Sophia Hakakian and Eliot Khakshoy’s demurrer to Saddlerock Ranch’s amended cross-complaint.

On October 13, 2024, Saddlerock Ranch filed a Second Amended Cross-Complaint.

On January 14, 2025, Eliot Khakshoy and Sophia Hakakian filed an amended cross-complaint.

On June 1, 2026, the Court deemed this case related to LASC case no. 26SMCV00450.

On June 5, 2026, Plaintiffs filed these motions to quash. On July 27, 2026, Orbic Air filed opposition.

Jury trial in this case is scheduled for January 11, 2027.

Arguments

Plaintiffs move to quash Orbic Air’s subpoena of Sahel Kermanshahi’s employment and medical records from her former employer CVS Pharmacy and current employer State Pharmacy. Plaintiffs argue that a plaintiff’s finances and emotional damages in a survival action are not relevant and that juries are specifically instructed not to consider those factors when deciding damages on a survivorship action. (Motion, p. 5.) Plaintiffs also argue that the subpoenas seek privileged and confidential doctor-patient communications and tax documents. (Id. at pp. 6-8.)

In opposition, Orbic Air argues that Plaintiffs’ motion lacks the required separate statement, plaintiff has waived financial or medical privacy privilege by testifying about her financial hardship at her deposition, and that good cause exists to allow the subpoena because Orbic Air has an important interest in obtaining Sahel’s employment records because she claims loss of income.

Governing Law- Motion to Quash Subpoena

“If a subpoena requires the attendance of a witness or the production of books, documents, electronically stored information, or other things before a court, or at the trial of an issue therein, or at the taking of a deposition, the court, upon motion reasonably made by any person described in subdivision (b), or upon the court's own motion after giving counsel notice and an opportunity to be heard, may make an order quashing the subpoena entirely, modifying it, or directing compliance with it upon those terms or conditions as the court shall declare, including protective orders. In addition, the court may make any other order as may be appropriate to protect the person from unreasonable or oppressive demands, including unreasonable violations of the right of privacy of the person.” (Code Civ. Proc. (CCP), § 1987.1, subd. (a).) 

Discussion

Plaintiffs two motions are identical, as are Orbic Air’s oppositions. The same reasoning applies to both motions.

a.      Procedural Issues

California Rules of Court Rule 3.1345 states that “any motion involving the content of a discovery request or the responses to such a request must be accompanied by a separate statement.” This rule applies to motions “to compel or quash the production of documents or tangible things at a deposition.” (CRC rule 2.1345(a)(5).) However, a separate statement is not required where no responses have been produced. (CRC, Rule 3.1345(b)(1).

Plaintiffs’ motions are motions to quash the production of documents at a deposition, so a separate statement is required if CVS Pharmacy or State Pharmacy had produced responsive discovery because Plaintiffs objected to the subpoena. (See Motions, Exhibits 1.) Orbic Air’s opposition confirms that it “paused” the subpoenas pending the Court’s ruling on these motions. (Hembree Decl., ¶ 9.)

Therefore, the Court will rule on the merits of Plaintiffs’ motions.

b.      Survivor Damages in a Wrongful Death Action

Plaintiffs are correct that any evidence of Sahel’s lost wages or financial damages are inadmissible. Survivors in a wrongful death action can recover the pecuniary value of the decedent’s “support, society, comfort, care and protection.”  (Krouse v. Graham (1977) 19 Cal.3d 59, 69.) However, that does not necessarily make the information privileged or non-discoverable. Under the Discovery Act, any matter that is relevant to the subject matter and not privileged is discoverable if it is itself admissible or “appears reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence.” (CCP § 2017.010.)

Defendants argue that Sahel’s discovery responses show that she was financially harmed by the accident and her father’s subsequent death because she took time off work to care for him and later to care for her mother, who was undergoing cancer treatment. Defendants also argue that Sahel claims emotional distress damages in the complaint.

Sahel’s emotional distress damages as a result of her father’s death are not relevant and not admissible on a wrongful death claim without a negligent infliction of emotional distress claim. Drawing the line between compensable “support, society, comfort, care and protection” injuries and non-compensable emotional distress is difficult. “[J]uries may be awarding damages for grief and sorrow instead of limiting recovery to the properly compensable elements of support, society, comfort, care and protection: ‘But while loss of society and comfort, and protection may be an element of the injury sustained by the statutory beneficiaries, it is only the pecuniary, and not the sentimental, value of such loss which may be taken into consideration in the assessment of damages. Nothing can be recovered as a solatium for wounded feelings. . . .’ [citation]” (Krouse v. Graham (1977) 19 Cal.3d 59, 69, citing Ure v. Maggio Bros. Co., Inc. (1938) 24 Cal.App.2d 490.)

Here, Sahel’s deposition testimony and discovery responses indicate that she was emotionally damaged by her father’s death, as is to be expected, but do not show that Orbic Air’s subpoenas are reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence because the records they request are only relevant to inadmissible damages. Orbic Air has not shown good cause for its subpoenas.

Conclusion

Plaintiffs’ Motions to Quash are GRANTED.


Case Number: 25SMCV06165    Hearing Date: August 7, 2026    Dept: P

Tentative Ruling

Taylor Megdal v. Doe 1, et al., Case No. 25SMCV06165

Genneva Maschler’s Motion to Set Aside Default Judgment

Hearing Date: August 7, 2026

Background

Plaintiff Taylor Megdal (“Megdal”), an attorney appearing in pro per, sues Defendant Genneva Maschler (“Maschler”) for fraud, breach of contract, violation of the UCL, and negligence, claiming that Maschler fraudulently failed to disclose a tenant when she sold Plaintiff a six-unit, tenant-occupied property located at 1241 21st Street, Santa Monica, CA.

On November 25, 2025, Megdal filed the complaint.

On January 21, 2026, Megdal filed proof of service by substituted service attesting that Plaintiff was served on December 21, 2025.

On February 9, 2026, Megdal entered default against Maschler.

On April 6, 2026, the Court granted default judgment against Maschler for $886,925.28.

On May 14, 2026, Maschler filed this motion to set aside default and a demurrer. On June 24, 2026, Megdal filed opposition. On July 1, 2026, Maschler replied.

On July 8, 2026, the Court held hearing on this motion and continued the hearing to August 7, 2026 to allow Defendant to file a supplemental declaration and Plaintiff to respond.

On July 20, 2026, Defendant filed a supplemental brief. On July 28, 2026, Plaintiff filed opposition. On July 30, 2026, Defendant replied.

Arguments

Maschler brings this motion to set aside default on the grounds that Megdal improperly served her. Maschler argues that despite the fact that Maschler’s sub-tenant received service and texted Maschler images of the complaint in December 2025. (Motion to Set Aside, p. 3.) Maschler contends that service was improper because Maschler was not permanently residing at the Santa Barbara address where she was served. (Ibid.) Maschler also argues that default should be set aside due to excusable neglect because Megdal’s service was improper and because Maschler received notice of the complaint during the height of Mammoth Lakes ski season, while she was preparing for a three-month trip to Japan from which she only recently returned. (Id. at pp. 6-7.) Maschler argues that despite her vacation, she was able to retain counsel in mid-March, 2026. (Ibid.) Maschler argues that the court lacks jurisdiction under Code Civ. Proc. § 473(d) because of Megdal’s improper service. (Id. at p. 9.)

In opposition, Megdal argues that Maschler’s motion should be denied because she had actual notice of the complaint, he properly served her by substituted service, and because Maschler does not identify any mistake or neglect that would justify setting aside default. (Opposition, pp. 3-5).  Megdal also argues that the record is not clear that service was improper, so such a finding would be inappropriate without an evidentiary hearing. (Id. at pp. 6-7.)

In reply, Maschler contends that Megdal does not prove that Maschler was properly served because he does not submit evidence that the summons and complaint were mailed following substitute service. (Reply, p. 4.) Next, Maschler argued that Megdal failed to serve Maschler at her “usual mailing address,” because he served her at her old Santa Barbara address and not at her current Mammoth Lakes address. (Id. at p. 5.) Then,  Maschler argues that service on her sub-tenant was improper because the sub-tenant was not a member of Maschler’s household. (Id. at pp. 6-7.)

In her supplemental brief, Maschler argues that attorney mistake prevented her from responding timely to the complaint. Maschler argues that her attorney advised her that she need not appear at the April 6, 2026, Case Management Conference and that he would appear for her. He then did not appear at the hearing because he believed that Maschler was improperly served. (Alberts Decl., ¶ 6.)

In opposition, Megdal argues that Maschler’s supplemental brief does not show that Maschler’s attorney made a mistake while representing her and therefore does not trigger the mandatory relief provision. (Opposition, p. 3.) Megdal argues that Maschler’s counsel is trying to take the blame for the adverse ruling after the fact. (Id. at p. 4.) Megdal cites Todd v. Thrifty Corp. (1995) 34 Cal.App.4th 986 and Cisneros v. Vueve (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 906. (Id. at pp. 7-8, 10.)

In reply, Maschler argues that the fiduciary relationship between a client and an attorney extends to pre-retention communications, and that her relationship with her attorney began when she sought out his legal advice. (Reply, p. 3.) Maschler argues that Cisneros is distinguishable because Maschler was in contact with her attorney, and that neglectful attorney advice was a “cause in fact” of the default. (Id. at p. 4.) Maschler argues that Todd does not apply to this case because Maschler’s attorney did not disclaim blame for Maschler’s failure to timely respond to the moving papers. (Id. at p. 6.) In the alternative, Maschler requests discretionary relief. (Ibid.)

Motion to Set Aside Default

CCP section 473(b) provides, in relevant part, that “the court shall, whenever an application for relief is made no more than six months after entry of judgment, is in proper form, and is accompanied by an attorney’s sworn affidavit attesting to his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect, vacate any (1) resulting default entered by the clerk against his or her client, and which will result in entry of a default judgment, or (2) resulting default judgment or dismissal entered against his or her client, unless the court finds that the default or dismissal was not in fact caused by the attorney’s mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect.”  (Id.)

Code of Civ. Proc. § 473.5(a) also provides a mechanism for the court to set aside default judgment if service did not result in actual notice. “The notice of motion shall be served and filed within a reasonable time, but in no event exceeding the earlier of: (i) two years after entry of a default judgment against him or her; or (ii) 180 days after service on him or her of a written notice that the default or default judgment has been entered.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 473.5(a).)

Default judgment may be set aside under Code Civ. Proc. § 473(d) if the judgment is void on its face. (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (d); Kremerman v. White (2021) 71 Cal.App.5th 358, 360-70.) “ ‘ “A judgment or order is said to be void on its face when the invalidity is apparent upon an inspection of the judgment-roll.” ’ [Citation.] This inquiry, however, ‘does not hinge on evidence: A void judgment’s invalidity appears on the face of the record.’ [Citation.]” (Kremerman v. White, supra, 71 Cal.App.5th at p. 370.)  “‘[C]ompliance with the statutory procedures for service of process is essential to establish personal jurisdiction. [Citation.] Thus, a default judgment entered against a defendant who was not served with a summons in the manner prescribed by statute is void. [Citation.]’” (Ellard v. Conway¿(2001) 94 Cal.App.4th 540, 544.) 

“The policy of the law favors a hearing on the merits.” (Purdum v. Holmes (2010) 187 Cal.App.4th 916, 922.) Therefore, when a party in default moves promptly to request relief, very slight evidence is required to justify a trial court's order setting aside a default. (Id.)¿

Analysis

a.      Code Civ. Proc. § 473(b)

Maschler submits the Alberts declaration showing that her attorney mistakenly told her she did not need to worry about appearing at the April case management conference, which led to the default judgment. (Alberts Supplemental Decl., ¶ 6.)

In Cisneros v. Vueve (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 906, the Court of Appeal affirmed a trial court’s finding that an attorney who was not retained until a month after plaintiff entered default judgment could not have made a mistake that caused default. (Cisneros v. Vueve, supra, 37 Cal.App.4th at 912.)

In Todd v. Thrifty Corp. (1995) 34 Cal.App.4th 986 (“Todd”), the trial Court dismissed Todd’s personal injury case after Todd failed to comply with the Court’s order compelling her to respond to Thrifty’s discovery requests. (Todd v. Thrifty Corp., supra, 34 Cal.App.4th at 988.) When Todd moved to vacate the dismissal, arguing that his client’s illness prevented her from responding to discovery. (Id. at 989.) The Court suggested that Todd’s counsel file an “attorney declaration of fault” claiming that his own mistaken failure to appear at the dismissal hearing caused the dismissal. (Id. at p. 990.) Thrifty appealed the Court’s order denying the motion. (Ibid.) The Court of Appeal found that Todd’s attorney’s failure to appear at the hearing on the motion to dismiss did not cause the dismissal, so relief from default was inappropriate. (Id. at 991.) The Court of Appeal also found that Todd’s attorney’s supplemental declaration was covering up for his client because it conflicted with prior statements. (Id. at 992.)

This case is distinct. First, had Maschler or her counsel made a general or special appearance at the April hearing, the Court would not and could not have entered default judgment against her. Second, Maschler’s supplemental declaration is not inconsistent with her prior testimony. Maschler and her attorney consistently took the position that Megdal’s service was improper and that they did not participate in this case sooner because they believed they were not properly served. Maschler’s supplemental declaration adds additional facts—that Maschler’s attorney improperly advised her that she did not need to appear at the April hearing—but does not contradict the original position. Even if Maschler had not yet officially retained Mr. Alberts as her attorney when he told her she need not appear at the April hearing, Mr. Alberts provided her with legal advice and was communicating with her about this case. Since Mr. Alberts’ advice caused the error, the fact that Maschler did not sign the official paperwork until later does not bar relief from default judgment under Cisneros or Todd.  

Maschler has met her burden of showing that default judgment was granted due to attorney mistake.

Conclusion

Maschler’s Motion to Set Aside Default is GRANTED.
