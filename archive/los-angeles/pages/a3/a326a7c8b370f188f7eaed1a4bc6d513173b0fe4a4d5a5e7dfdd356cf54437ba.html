Case Number: 25STCV17256    Hearing Date: July 21, 2026    Dept: 510

No. 12 - Vahik Arzoomanian v. Aqua Masters, Inc., et al.

Defendants’ Two Motions to Compel Further Responses to Requests for Production

Defendants Aqua Masters, Inc. and American Contractors Indemnity Company move to compel Plaintiff Vahik Arzoomanian (“Arzoomanian”) and Cross-Defendant Hagop Yacooubian (“Yacooubian”) to provide further responses to requests for production.

As an initial matter, the Court notes that Defendants make two different requests in their motions. First, they request the Court compel the parties to comply with their verified responses for request nos. 1 and 2. Second, they request the Court compel the parties to provide further responses to request nos. 5-7. As this motion is titled “Motion to Compel Further Responses,” and Defendants move under CCP §2031.310, the Court will only address Defendants’ second request for relief. A motion to compel further responses under section 2031.310 is not the proper means to compel production of documents. Instead, Plaintiff must compel compliance “in accordance with [defendant’s] statement of compliance” under section 2031.320, subdivision (a).

Timeliness

          These motions are timely. Under Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.310(c), “Unless notice of this motion is given within 45 days of the service of the verified response, or any supplemental verified response, or on or before any specific later date to which the demanding party and the responding party have agreed in writing, the demanding party waives any right to compel a further response to the demand.” Arzoomanian and Yacooubian served their verified discovery responses on March 30, 2026. (Matthews Decls., Ex. C.) Defendants’ motions to compel further responses needed to be served 45 calendar days after receipt of the verified responses, on May 14, 2026. However, two extra court days are added due to electronic service (CCP § 1010.6(a)(3)(B)). These motions were thus timely served via email on May 18, 2026.

Further Responses

Defendants are entitled to further responses to request nos. 5-7. A party demanding inspection of documents may move to compel further responses if “[a] statement of compliance with the demand is incomplete,” “[a] representation of inability to comply is inadequate, incomplete, or evasive,” or “[a]n objection in the response is without merit or too general.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (a).)

          After this motion was filed, Arzoomanian and Yacooubian served further responses. (Gilanians Decls., Ex. A.) However, the further responses are not code-compliant. To request nos. 5-7, Arzoomanian and Yacooubian simply wrote: “See [Exhibits], attached hereto.” Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.220 provides, “A statement that the party to whom a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling has been directed will comply with the particular demand shall state that the production, inspection, copying, testing, or sampling, and related activity demanded, will be allowed either in whole or in part, and that all documents or things in the demanded category that are in the possession, custody, or control of that party and to which no objection is being made will be included in the production.”

These responses do not specify Arzoomanian and Yacooubian would produce “all” responsive documents in their possession, custody, or control. A statement of compliance in whole must use the word “all” because it states under oath not only that the responding party will produce some documents, but also that the responding party is not withholding any responsive documents. To the extent they intended to make only partial statements of compliance or are withholding documents, the responses are evasive and do not identify any documents withheld subject to objections, as is required. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.240, subd. (b)(1).)

Sanctions

          Defendants move for $5,775 in sanctions against Arzoomanian and Yacooubian and their counsel of record. “[T]he court shall impose a monetary sanction … against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel further response to a demand, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (CCP § 2031.310(h).)

          Arzoomanian and Yacooubian did not act with substantial justification. They did not respond to meet and confer efforts and provided evasive responses to discovery requests after the motions were filed.

However, Defendants did not reasonably incur $5,775 in expenses for 11 hours of work at a rate of $520 per hour. (Matthews Decl., ¶ 10.) The Court exercises its discretion not to award fees for the 0.5 hours spent meeting and conferring. The Court will reduce the total hours requested for time spent drafting the motions and reply briefs by 50%. These motions were straightforward, and the reply brief was only 2.5 pages long. 9 hours reduced by 50% is 4.5 hours. The Court will award 1.0 hour for attending the hearing. The Court will award fees at a rate of $520 per hour. Thus, Defendants reasonably incurred a total of $2,850 in attorney fees (5.5 hours at $520 per hour).

Disposition

Defendants Aqua Masters, Inc. and American Contractors Indemnity Company’s motions to compel Plaintiff Vahik Arzoomanian and Cross-Defendant Hagop Yacooubian to provide further responses to requests for production are granted. Arzoomanian and Yacooubian are ordered to serve verified code-compliant responses without objections to request nos. 5-7 within 21 days.

Plaintiff Vahik Arzoomanian and Cross-Defendant Hagop Yacooubian are ordered, jointly and severally, to pay Defendants Aqua Masters, Inc. and American Contractors Indemnity Company $2,850 in sanctions within 21 days.


Case Number: 26STCP01919    Hearing Date: July 21, 2026    Dept: 510

No. 14 - Irish Rover Entertainment, LLC, et al. v. Boren, Osher & Luftman, LLP, et al.

          Petition to Vacate Arbitrate Award

In this legal malpractice action, Petitioners Irish Rover Entertainment, LLC (“IRE”) and Jeffrey Kennedy move to vacate the arbitration award entered against them. Respondents Robins Kaplan LLP, Patrick Arenz and Michael A. Geibelson (collectively, “RK”) and Borens, Osher & Luftman, LLP, Stephen Z. Boren and Jeremy J. Osher (collectively, “BOL”) oppose the instant petition and each cross-petition to confirm the arbitration award. IRE filed a reply brief.

As an initial matter, the Court acknowledges there was a dispute regarding whether IRE should have the opportunity to file a reply brief. While a reply brief filed in connection to a petition to vacate an arbitration award is not expressly allowed under statute, it is not technically prohibited. (See CCP § 1285.2.) Parties routinely file a reply brief in this context because the filing of the petition is treated as an ordinary law-and-motion procedure. (See CCP § 1290.2 [“A petition under this title shall be heard in a summary way in the manner and upon the notice provided by law for the making and hearing of motions.”].) Therefore, at the Court’s June 9, 2026 hearing on Petitioners’ ex parte application, the Court authorized a reply brief on the condition that it solely address new issues raised in the opposition briefs, specifically Respondents’ respective requests to confirm the arbitration award. In their reply, Petitioners repeated the same arguments raised in their petition, but framed them as bases to deny Respondents’ cross-petitions to confirm. As such, the Reply was permitted but was superfluous.

Vacate Arbitration Award

Code of Civil Procedure section 1286 provides, “If a petition or response under this chapter is duly served and filed, the court shall confirm the award as made, whether rendered in this state or another state, unless in accordance with this chapter it corrects the award and confirms it as corrected, vacates the award or dismisses the proceedings.” 

Courts have limited authority to vacate an arbitration award. “A court’s power to correct or vacate an erroneous arbitration award is closely circumscribed.” (Heimlich v. Shivji (2019) 7 Cal.5th 350, 367.) “An arbitrator’s legal or factual error in determining which party prevailed may not be reversed.” (Ibid.) “It is within the power of the arbitrator to make a mistake either legally or factually. When parties opt for the forum of arbitration they agree to be bound by the decision of that forum knowing that arbitrators, like judges, are fallible.” (Id. at p. 370, internal quotes and alterations omitted.) 

IRE argues the award should be vacated on four separate grounds. The Court will address each in turn.

1.     Failure to Disclose Ground for Disqualification – CCP § 1286.2(a)(6)

First, Petitioners argue the arbitrator failed to timely disclose a ground for her disqualification, and disqualify herself as required under statute. (CCP § 1286.2(a)(6).)

Specifically, Petitioners contend the arbitrator did not disclose that her real estate entity, Horton Hill, LLC, reported more than $100,000 in gross income during 2018–2020 and identified individuals connected to the subject matter of the underlying copyright litigation as reportable sources of income on publicly filed Form 700 records. (Pet. To Vacate, at pp. 4, 10.) They argue this triggered the arbitrator’s continuing statutory duty to disclose under section 1281.9 and that, after IRE demanded disqualification under sections 1281.9 and 1281.91 and JAMS Rule 15, she declined to step aside. (Id., at p. 12.) Petitioners emphasize that they need not prove actual bias or any prejudice beyond denial of the statutory right to a fully informed choice of neutral. (Ibid.) Specifically, Petitioners explain that the subject persons were connected to a talent agency that, in turn, represented individuals involved with the production of the underlying copyright matter, the resolution of which formed the basis for the instant legal malpractice claims. (Kennedy Decl., ¶¶ 18-20.) Petitioners concede the subject individuals were not parties to the arbitration, framing the question as whether a reasonable person aware of the undisclosed financial relationship might doubt the arbitrator’s impartiality. (Id., at p. 13.)

An arbitrator must disclose “all matters that could cause a person aware of the facts to reasonably entertain a doubt that the proposed neutral arbitrator would be able to be impartial.” (CCP § 1281.9(a).) The test is objective, and is not based on a party’s subjective view of the situation. (Haworth v. Superior Court (2010) 50 Cal.App.4th 372, 385-386 [“The applicable rule provides an objective test by focusing on a hypothetical reasonable person’s perception of bias. … The question here is how an objective, reasonable person would view [the neutral’s] ability to be impartial.”].)

IRE raised this disqualification issue twice in arbitration, and both time the JAMS National Arbitration Panel – not the arbitrator – denied IRE’s motion to disqualify. (Kennedy Decl., ¶¶ 21-22.) The Panel found, in relevant part:

The arbitrator’s alleged rental of a home to four nonparty individuals who appear to have no involvement in the issues related to this arbitration, constitutes little to no materiality under the present circumstances. The totality of the evidence presented in the parties’ submissions does not give rise to justifiable doubt as to the arbitrator’s impartiality, independence, or competence. Nor does the NAC find failure to disclose by the arbitrator.

(Kennedy Decl., ¶ 22, Ex. Q.)

          Upon review of the record, this Court does not find the arbitrator failed to timely disclose a ground for disqualification or otherwise disqualify herself. Petitioners do not provide evidence showing that the tangential relationship between the arbitrator and individuals who were not parties or witnesses in the arbitration would cause a person to “reasonably entertain a doubt” that the arbitrator was impartial. (See CCP § 1281.9(a).) The arbitration itself concerned legal malpractice allegedly committed by Petitioners’ former lawyers, not any liability associated with the parties to the underlying copyright suit.

          The parties dispute whether the conclusions reached by JAMS’ National Arbitration Panel should be given the same deference afforded to arbitrators, i.e., the decision should not overturned unless a basis exists under CCP § 1286 (see, e.g., RK Resp. at p. 13), or if that decision should be reviewed by the Court de novo. The Court does not have sufficient information regarding whether the National Arbitration Panel’s procedures should be deemed to be equivalent to those of the arbitrator, and thus does not adopt the conclusions of the NAP without further review. The Court concludes that the suggested relationship by the arbitrator with individuals in the entertainment industry was at best attenuated, superficial, and immaterial to the arbitration. As such, the arbitrator did not have reason to disqualify, and her failure to disqualify is not a basis to vacate the arbitration award.

2.     Reasonable Impression of Bias – CCP § 1286.2(a)(3)

Second, Petitioners argue that the Court should vacate the arbitration award because the arbitrator’s nondisclosure of the alleged conflict created a reasonable impression of bias. They contend that this bias amounts to “misconduct” that substantially prejudiced Petitioners’ rights in the arbitration under CCP § 1286.2(a)(3).

          As discussed above, the Court does not find that the arbitrator’s nondisclosure was improper, nor that Petitioners provided evidence showing a reasonable person would doubt the arbitrator’s impartiality. Nor do Petitioners meet their evidentiary burden that they did not receive a fair hearing. Petitioners merely assert, in a conclusory manner and without identifying specific unfairness tied to the nondisclosure, that any nondisclosure denied them of “a fundamentally fair proceeding before a neutral.” (Pet. to Vacate, at p. 12.) This is insufficient. Petitioners fail to meet their burden to show that they suffered substantial prejudice because of the arbitrator’s nondisclosure.

3.     Denial of Fair Opportunity to Present Evidence – CCP § 1286.2(a)(5)

Third, Petitioners argue that their rights were substantially prejudiced by the arbitrator’s refusal to hear evidence material to the case. Courts shall vacate an award if “[t]he rights of the party were substantially prejudiced … by the refusal of the arbitrators to hear evidence material to the controversy.” (CCP § 1286.2(a)(5).) 

Petitioners explain that Kennedy was added to the arbitration to determine his liability for any fee award after the proceeding had already been pending for 15 months. (Pet. to Vacate, at p.15.) Petitioners point to various circumstances to support their argument that the arbitrator failed to hear material evidence: (1) Kennedy was ordered to participate in discovery before his jurisdictional Rule 11(b) objection was decided; (2) Kennedy appeared after the hearing date and discovery limits were already set; and (3) the arbitrator suspended IRE’s depositions of Respondents’ witnesses.

          Nothing in the record demonstrates that any of these discrete events denied IRE or Kennedy a fair opportunity to present evidence that substantially prejudiced their rights. According to the arbitrator’s final award, Kennedy filed his Rule 11(b) motion to dismiss on November 25, 2025, and the arbitrator decided the motion on December 8, 2025. (Kennedy Decl., Ex. A, at p. 5.) IRE fails to show how this 13-day period impacted any right to discover or introduce evidence, much less resulted in substantial prejudice. Petitioners do not indicate that Kennedy sought discovery that was precluded based on the timing of his entry into the case. Further, Kennedy was Petitioners’ sole witness, so he would have testified regardless of whether he was a party or when he became a party.

          The parties all report that they agreed to take three depositions, of Kennedy and Respondents’ witnesses Arenz and Osher. Kennedy did not appear for his deposition, so Petitioners agreed not to proceed with the depositions of Arenz or Osher. (Silberfeld Decl., Ex. M.) Respondents successfully moved to have Kennedy ordered to appear for his deposition, which he did. Respondents assert – and Petitioners do not dispute – that Petitioners never asked to put the Arenz and Osher depositions back on calendar at any time prior to the arbitration hearing. (See BOL Resp., at p. 15; RK Resp., at p. 6; Kennedy Decl., ¶ 15.)

These facts contradict Petitioners’ suggestion that the arbitrator suspended the depositions of Respondents’ witnesses. Critically, Petitioners never called Arenz or Osher to testify at the arbitration, where presumably they could have inquired about all matters that they would have covered at their depositions. Petitioners apparently determined that those witnesses were not necessary for their case in chief, so there was no prejudice associated with Petitioners’ decision not to depose them before the arbitration hearing.

In addition, Section 1286.2(a)(5) provides that “the court shall vacate the award if … “[t]he rights of the party were substantially prejudiced by the refusal of the arbitrators to postpone the hearing upon sufficient cause being shown therefor or by the refusal of the arbitrators to hear evidence material to the controversy.” (CCP § 1286.2(a)(5).)

While IRE argues that the arbitrator improperly failed to continue the evidentiary hearing, it presents no evidence as to whether it presented good cause to continue the hearing in the first place, nor how the arbitrator’s denial of said continuance request was prejudicial. The record shows that Kennedy requested a continuance of the hearing on December 15, 2025. (Garbacz Decl., ¶ 30.) The arbitrator denied the request on December 18. (Ibid.) One month later, the arbitration proceeded and Kennedy was Petitioners’ sole witness. There was no suggestion that Kennedy needed additional time to find information to support his own testimony. Nor was there any evidence, or even a suggestion, that Petitioners had other witnesses who were not available to testify during the two days allocated for the arbitration, that an expert witnesses needed additional time to complete an analysis, or that another reason existed to postpone the arbitration. The Court cannot rule now, on this record, that the arbitrator improperly denied a continuance.  

4.     Arbitrator Exceeded Authority – CCP § 1286.2(a)(4)

Lastly, IRE argues that the arbitrator exceeded her authority by adding Kennedy as an individual party to the proceeding. Under CCP § 1286.2(a)(4), courts must vacate an award when “[t]he arbitrators exceeded their powers and the award cannot be corrected without affecting the merits of the decision upon the controversy submitted.”

There is insufficient evidence in the record showing that Kennedy’s addition was improper. By all accounts, Kennedy was always IRE’s principal and most important witness who had always planned to testify at the arbitration. His involvement in the case was requested by BOR only because, as a party to the engagement agreement with BOL, he was jointly liable for fees incurred arising from a dispute under that agreement. IRE does not show how Kennedy’s addition to the proceedings resulted in any prejudice, much less substantial prejudice. Under the facts presented here, the mere fact that Kennedy was found liable – following an evidentiary hearing where he testified – for BOL’s fees incurred in defending the arbitration does not constitute substantial prejudice.

Petitioners also suggest that the arbitrator failed to present unspecified “evidentiary boundaries,” which in some fashion caused prejudice. (Pet. to Vacate, at p. 12 [IRE had requested evidentiary boundaries “regarding mediation privilege and confidentiality restrictions.”].) Petitioners do not clarify what boundaries were sought, or when. Further, and more importantly, Petitioners present no evidence that they sought to introduce any evidence that may have touched on or exceeded these unspecified “boundaries,” or that they received a ruling that any evidence they sought to introduce could not be admitted because it exceeded such a “boundary.” Petitioners fail to meet their burden of proof.

Petition to Confirm Award

Respondents’ petitions to confirm the arbitration award meets all requirements. Respondents show valid grounds to confirm the arbitration award.

Disposition

Petitioners Irish Rover Entertainment, LLC and Jeffrey Kennedy’s motion to vacate the arbitration award is denied.

Respondents’ petitions to confirm arbitration award are granted. The final award issued by arbitrator Hon. Elizabeth White (Ret.) is hereby confirmed. The Court will sign and enter the proposed judgment submitted on June 22, 2026 by BOL.

Petitioners Irish Rover Entertainment, LLC and Jeffrey Kennedy’s Motion to Seal

Petitioners Irish Rover Entertainment, LLC and Jeffrey Kennedy move to seal their petition to vacate arbitration award, the declaration of Kennedy, and supporting exhibits A through AO.

California Rules of Court, rule 2.550(d) provides:

A court may order that a record be filed under seal only if it expressly finds facts that establish:

(1) There exists an overriding interest that overcomes the right of public access to the record;

(2) The overriding interest supports sealing the record;

(3) A substantial probability exists that the overriding interest will be prejudiced if the record is not sealed;

(4) The proposed sealing is narrowly tailored; and

(5) No less restrictive means exist to achieve the overriding interest.

Petitioners do not meet any of the elements under California Rules of Court, rule 2.550(d). Their motion to seal vaguely argues that the petition and all supporting exhibits “necessarily disclose[] settlement terms and conditions protected under express confidentiality provisions in the August 2023 Settlement Agreement.” (Motion, at p. 1.) However, it is unclear to the Court how the entirety of Petitioner’s filing, which includes a 15-page petition to vacate an arbitration award, a 10-page declaration from Kennedy, and 39 exhibits comprising hundreds of pages reveal terms of the parties’ settlement agreement. Petitioners do not specify where any such terms are revealed. Indeed, the settlement terms were irrelevant to whether the arbitration award should be confirmed or vacated, so there was no reason to include those terms in the papers supporting the motion.

Petitioners also do not show how a substantial probability exists that their interests will be prejudiced if the record is not sealed. The supporting declaration asserts, “Based on my review of the Settlement Agreement and related arbitration materials, disclosure of the confidential information contained in the Petition and supporting materials would create a substantial risk of violating the parties’ contractual confidentiality obligations.” (Yeretsian Decl., ¶ 7.) A court may deny a motion to seal if it finds the supporting “declarations conclusory or otherwise unpersuasive.” (In re Providian Credit Card Cases (2002) 96 Cal.App.4th 292, 301.) Neither Kennedy nor his counsel point to any exhibit or filing that reveals terms of the underlying settlement.

          Moreover, the proposed sealing is far from narrowly tailored. Petitioners move to seal all documents related to their petition—not simply the redacted portions of the documents already in the public record. Counsel for Petitioners declares, “I have considered whether less restrictive alternatives to sealing exist. However, because the confidential settlement information is intertwined with the factual allegations and procedural history underlying the Petition, redaction alone would not adequately protect the confidentiality interests at issue or would render the filings substantially unintelligible.” (Yeretsian Decl., ¶ 11.) If “confidential settlement information” is in fact “intertwined” with materials that were filed, there is no reason why redaction alone, as was already performed by Petitioners, would not suffice.

          Even if Petitioners actually seek only to seal the redacted portions of the documents, their request is still not narrowly tailored. As Respondents point out in their limited opposition, many of the redactions omit information that is publicly available online, such as information contained in public LinkedIn and IMDB profiles. Petitioners make no argument concerning why sealing of this public material is necessary or warranted.

Disposition

On this record, the Court cannot make the five express findings required under California Rules of Court, rule 2.550(d) to support sealing. Thus, Petitioners’ motion to seal is denied. 

Under California Rules of Court, rule 2.551(b)(6), the moving party has two options after the court denies a motion to seal. First, Petitioners may notify the Court within 10 days that it chooses for the unredacted records to be filed unsealed. (Ibid.) Second, if Petitioners do nothing within 10 days, the Court will return the paper copies of the lodged records and delete any digital copies. (Ibid.) The redacted versions of the records will remain in the court’s public file.


Case Number: 26STCV09450    Hearing Date: July 21, 2026    Dept: 510

No. 13 - David Navid Nabizadeh v. Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC

          Defendant Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC’s Demurrer to Complaint

Defendant Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC demurs to all causes of action alleged in the complaint by Plaintiff David Navid Nabizadeh for breach of express warranty and breach of implied warranty.

Breach of Express Warranty

Plaintiff does not allege sufficient facts for breach of express warranty. This cause of action alleges a violation of a provision of the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act (the Act). The Act “distinguishes between new and used products and calibrates manufacturers’ and sellers’ obligations accordingly.” (Rodriguez v. FCA US LLC (2024) 17 Cal.5th 189, 200.) Most provisions of the Act apply to “consumer goods (i.e., new products).” (Ibid.) Other provisions apply to “new motor vehicles,” which the California Supreme Court held does not include “a motor vehicle purchased with an unexpired manufacturer’s new car warranty …  unless the new car warranty was issued with the sale.” (Id. at p. 196.) 

Here, Plaintiff alleges he purchased a 2022 Mercedes-Benz EQS-Class Sedan in 2025, thereby indicating that the car was purchased used. (See Compl., ¶ 8.) He alleges “the Subject Vehicle was purchased as a certified pre-owned (“CPO”) with an accompanying MERCEDES-BENZ USA, LLC’s new and full CPO warranty.” (Ibid.) Plaintiff does not allege that the vehicle was new or that Defendant issued a new vehicle warranty with the sale.

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that a CPO warranty is a “new vehicle warranty” as contemplated by Rodriguez because it was issued at the time of sale. The Court disagrees. This is a new warranty on a used car, not a new vehicle warranty.

The demurrer as to this cause of action is sustained without leave to amend.

Breach of Implied Warranty

Defendant argues that Plaintiff cannot state a claim for breach of implied warranty because Plaintiff’s vehicle is not a “new motor vehicle,” and Plaintiff fails to allege that Defendant acted as a seller or distributor of the vehicle.

While the Song-Beverly Act generally applies to “new” consumer goods (see Civ. Code, § 1791), it extends to used consumer goods when “the sale is accompanied by an express warranty” (see Civ. Code, § 1795.5(c)). Rodriguez elaborated on the Act’s distinctions between new and used products: “Separate from the[] provisions concerning new products, the Act includes a distinct section addressing used products.” (Rodriguez, supra, at p. 200.) “[T]he basic framework of the Song-Beverly Act distinguishes between new and used products and ‘provides similar remedies in the context of the sale of used goods, except that the manufacturer is generally off the hook.’ ” (Id. at p. 201 [italics added]; see also Ruiz Nunez v. FCA US LLC (2021) 61 Cal.App.5th 385, 399 [“only distributors or sellers of used goods—not manufacturers of new goods—have implied warranty obligations in the sale of used goods”].)

While Plaintiff did not sufficiently allege that his vehicle is a “new motor vehicle,” he does allege sufficient facts to show that the vehicle is used and was accompanied by an express warranty. Therefore, Plaintiff’s remedies are not entirely foreclosed under the Act for breach of implied warranty.

However, Plaintiff did not sufficiently allege that Defendant acted as a seller or distributor of the vehicle. The complaint only alleges, in a conclusory manner, that “Defendant, MERCEDES-BENZ USA, LLC, is a ‘manufacturer’ and/or ‘distributor’ under the Act.” (Compl., ¶ 22.) Under Kiluk v. Mercedez-Benz USA, LLC (2019) 43.Cal.App.5th 334, 340, a manufacturer was held liable under Civil Code section 1795.5 because it partnered with a dealership to sell the vehicle at issue and therefore “stepped into the role of a retailer and was subject to the obligations of a retailer under section 1795.5.” “[T]he assumption baked into section 1795.5 is that the manufacturer and the distributor/retailer are distinct entities.” (Ibid.) Here, Plaintiff does not allege that Defendant partnered with MB of Los Angeles, the dealership, to sell the vehicle to the public. (Ibid.) Further, the complaint does not otherwise allege that Defendant “stepped into the role of retailer” through its actions. (Ibid.)

The demurrer as to this cause of action is sustained with leave to amend.

Disposition

          Defendant Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC’s demurrer to Plaintiff David Navid Nabizadeh’s complaint is sustained without leave to amend as to the first cause of action, and sustained with 21 days’ leave to amend as to the second cause of action.
