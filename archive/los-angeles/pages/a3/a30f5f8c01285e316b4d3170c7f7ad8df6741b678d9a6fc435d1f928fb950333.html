If there is neither a telephone call nor an appearance, then the matter may either be taken off calendar or ruled on.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: DEFENDANT ABREANNA MORALES’S MOTION FOR PROTECTIVE ORDER.

 

Dept. A

 

DATE: July 16, 2026

TIME: 8:30 A.M.

 

COMPLAINT FILED: 08/28/2024

TRIAL: 01/20/2027

 

MOVING PARTY:              Defendant Abreanna Morales

RESPONDING PARTY:     Plaintiff Maricela Bautista

1.     Background

This is a motor vehicle collision action. Plaintiff Maricela Bautista (“Plaintiff”) alleges that on August 30, 2022, Plaintiff was a passenger in a vehicle driven by defendant Abreanna Morales. Plaintiff alleges that defendants Raymond Calderaz and Abreanna Morales negligently operated their vehicle so as to cause their vehicles to collide, causing Plaintiff personal injury.

 

2.     Discussion

a. Motion for Protective Order

Defendant Abreanna Morales (“Movant”) moves for a protective order directing that Plaintiff shall propound no further discovery into Movant’s medical condition and relieving Movant of any obligation to respond to Plaintiff’s Requests for Admission, Set Three; Special Interrogatories, Set Three; and Form Interrogatories, Set Three, No. 17.1. Movant contends that she has not placed her physical or medical condition at issue and therefore the requested discovery is irrelevant and invasive of Movant’s privacy. In opposition, Plaintiff contends that Movant previously testified as to her medical condition in a prior lawsuit and therefore that Movant’s physical and medical condition are at issue in this lawsuit.

“The court, for good cause shown, may make any order that justice requires to protect any party or other person from unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression, or undue burden and expense.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2031.060, subd. (a).) Such a motion will include a meet and confer declaration pursuant to Code Civ. Proc. § 2016.040. (See id.) Protective orders may be also granted on motion of the deponent or any party, or any third person who could be affected by the disclosure (e.g., a nonparty whose privacy would be impaired). (See Code Civ. Proc. § 2025.420, subd. (a).)

The decision upon whether to enter a protective order lies within the sound discretion of the court. (See Raymond Handling Concepts Corp. v. Sup. Ct. (1995) 39 Cal.App.4th 584, 588, 591; see also Meritplan Ins. Co. v. Superior Ct. (1981) 124 Cal.App.3d 237, 242.) Moving parties have the burden to show good cause for protective order. (See Emerson Elec. Co. v Sup. Ct. (1997) 16 Cal.4th 1101, 1110.) In order to establish good cause, the moving party must make a factual showing demonstrating a specific harm that would arise from denial of the protective order. (See Nativi v. Deutsche Bank National Trust Co. (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 261, 317-318 (Nativi).)

Code of Civil Procedure § 2019.030 permits the court to restrict the frequency or extent of use of a discovery method if it finds that “The discovery sought is unreasonably cumulative or duplicative, or is obtainable from some other source that is more convenient, less burdensome, or less expensive” or “[t]he selected method of discovery is unduly burdensome or expensive, taking into account the needs of the case, the amount in controversy, and the importance of the issues at stake in the litigation.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2019.030.) Code of Civil Procedure § 2017.020 provides that “the court shall limit the scope of discovery if it determines that the burden, expense, or intrusiveness of that discovery clearly outweighs the likelihood that the information sought will lead to the discovery of admissible evidence.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2017.020.)

Plaintiff contends that the court, in its July 2, 2026 minute order, found that Movant’s medical condition was discoverable. Plaintiff misconstrues the court’s order. The court, at that time, found that what Movant publicly testified to in her previous lawsuit was discoverable, but that direct inquiry into Movant’s physical or medical condition was not discoverable.

Plaintiff also contends that Movant, after the filing of the instant motion, served substantive responses to Requests for Admission, Set Three, Nos. 51 through 61 and Special Interrogatories, Set Three, Nos. 75 through 102. The motion is therefore moot as to those discovery requests. Plaintiff contends that Movant’s service of substantive response to some of the discovery at issue demonstrates that Plaintiff’s objections are not privacy driven; however, Plaintiff provides no authority indicating that the provision of substantive responses to some discovery requests waives objections to all other discovery requests in the same set.

When a party objects to a potential invasion of privacy, the party claiming a privacy interest bears the burden of proof on the issue. “The party asserting a privacy right must establish a legally protected privacy interest, an objectively reasonable expectation of privacy in the given circumstances, and a threatened intrusion that is serious.” (Williams v. Superior Court¿(2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 531.) Where the party claiming a privacy interest demonstrates that the requested discovery constitutes an obvious invasion of interests fundamental to personal autonomy, the request must be supported by a compelling interest to overcome the producing party's privacy right. (Id. at p. 557.) However, where the extent and nature of the invasion is lesser, the court must balance the producing party’s privacy interest, expectation of privacy, and the seriousness of the invasion against the requesting party’s countervailing interests, taking into account possible protective measures or feasible alternatives to obtain the same information. (Id. at p. 552.)

A person has a right of privacy to information contained in their medical records. (Grafilo v. Wolfsohn (2019) 33 Cal.App.5th 1024, 1034.) This privacy interest is fundamental to personal autonomy. (See Hill v. National Collegiate Athletic Assn. (1994) 7 Cal.4th 1, 35-36.) Movant’s physical and medical condition are not at issue in this action because Movant has made no claims in this action for personal injury, irrespective of what Movant claimed in a different action.

Plaintiff contends that discovery into Movant’s medical condition is proper because both Plaintiff and Movant occupied the same vehicle in the same collision, and therefore Movant’s medical condition constitutes evidence of the severity of the collision. However, this information may be obtained through examination of Plaintiff’s medical condition without intruding on Movant’s privacy right.

Therefore, information as to Movant’s physical or medical condition is not discoverable and is the proper subject of a protective order. However, there is no right to privacy as to public statements, including testimony, made in the past. (Cox Broadcasting Corp. v. Cohn (1975) 420 U.S. 469, 492-493.)

Based on the court’s review of Requests for Admission, Set Three; Special Interrogatories, Set Three; and Form Interrogatories, Set Three, No. 17.1, provided as Exhibit A through C to the declaration of attorney Bradley V. Mahlstedt, the court finds that:

Requests for Admissions, Set Three, Nos. 62 through 107 solely concern Movant’s prior testimony, and therefore seek discoverable information.

Special Interrogatories, Set Three, Nos. 35 through 74 request information directly related to Movant’s medical condition. Although Movant may have previously testified as to her medical condition in a different action, this does not place Movant’s medical condition at issue in this action and this information remains protected.

Form Interrogatories, Set Three, No. 17.1 does not concern Movant’s physical or medical condition.  A protective order will not be granted as to this request.

The court will therefore grant in part Movant’s motion for a protective order, consistent with the above findings.

    b. Sanctions

Movant requests monetary sanctions against Plaintiff and her counsel of record, Peerali Law, in the amount of $4,260, based on Attorney Mahlstedt’s hourly rate of $360 across seven hours preparing the instant motion and an anticipated four hours spent reviewing the opposition, preparing a reply, and attending the hearing, as well as a $300 filing fee.

“The court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion for a protective order, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (Code of Civil Procedure § 2017.020, subd. (b).)

Plaintiff contends that she acted with substantial justification because the requested discovery is relevant, narrowly tailored, and otherwise discoverable. However, the court has found that, although some of the requested discovery is proper, another part improperly seeks discovery into Movant’s private medical information.

Plaintiff also contends that Movant’s notice of motion only requests sanctions against Plaintiff and Peerali Law, without identifying individual attorneys of record by name as targets for personal liability. It is not clear how this is an issue, as Movant simply has not requested sanctions against individual attorneys.

As Plaintiff has failed to show substantial justification, the court will award monetary sanctions in connection with this motion. The court finds the requested hourly rate to be reasonable. In light of the lack of reply, the court finds the requested hours to be unreasonable and will reduce them accordingly. The court will therefore award monetary sanctions in the total amount of $3,540.

3.     Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, the court GRANTS IN PART Movant’s motion for a protective order. The court GRANTS Movant’s request for a protective order directing that Plaintiff shall propound no further discovery into Movant’s private medical history, symptoms, diagnoses, imaging, treatment, or medical providers, unless Movant puts her medical condition or history at issue in this action. The court GRANTS Movant’s request for relief of any obligation to respond to Special Interrogatories, Set Three, Nos. 35 through 74. The court DENIES Movant’s request for relief of any obligation to respond to Requests for Admission, Set Three, Nos. 62 through 107 and Form Interrogatories, Set Three, No. 17.1.

The court GRANTS Movant’s request for monetary sanctions jointly and severally against Plaintiff and her attorneys of record, Peerali Law, in the amount of $3,540, to be paid not later than August 16, 2026.

Movant is ordered to give notice.

IT IS SO ORDERED.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: CROSS-DEFENDANT’S DEMURRER TO THE CROSS-COMPLAINT

 

Dept. A

 

DATE: July 16, 2026

TIME: 8:30 A.M.

 

COMPLAINT FILED: 01/28/2026

1st AMENDED COMPLAINT FILED: 02/24/2026

CROSS-COMPLAINT FILED: 05/08/2026

TRIAL: 10/30/2028

 

MOVING PARTY:              Plaintiff/Cross-Defendant Tooth & Nail Industries, Inc.

RESPONDING PARTY:     Defendant/Cross-Complainant Bhullar Properties, LLC

1.   Background

This is an action for breach of lease. Plaintiff Tooth & Nail Industries, Inc. (“Plaintiff”) alleges that on October 6, 2023, it entered into a commercial lease with defendant Bhullar Properties, LLC ("Defendant”) to rent the property located at 631 E. University Drive, Carson, CA, 90746 (the “Property”). Plaintiff alleges that Defendant began construction activities at the Property in July 2025, causing damage to the roof of the Property and allowing rainfall to enter the Property. Plaintiff alleges that it notified Defendant of the damage, unsafe conditions, and rodent infestation, but that Defendant failed to take corrective action, resulting in part of the ceiling collapsing on November 16, 2025 and rendering the Property untenantable. Plaintiff alleges that as a result, Plaintiff was constructively evicted and forced to vacate the Property.

Plaintiff additionally alleges that after the filing of the instant action and after Plaintiff had already vacated the Property, Defendant issued a three-day notice to pay rent or quit. Plaintiff alleges that, shortly after posting the notice, Defendant engaged in self-help eviction by changing the exterior locks of the Property.

In its cross-complaint, Defendant alleges that in late 2024, Plaintiff failed to pay rent due under the lease agreement. Defendant also alleges that Plaintiff fraudulently misrepresented the purposes for which Plaintiff leased the Property.

2.   Discussion

Plaintiff demurs to all four causes of action in Defendant’s cross-complaint. Plaintiff contends that the Cross-Complaint fails to state sufficient facts to allege a cause of action. In opposition, Defendant contends that the Cross-Complaint is adequately pled.

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint or cross-complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747 (Hahn).)¿When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (Taylor v. City of Los Angeles Dept. of Water and Power (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 1216, 1228.)¿In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice.¿(Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)¿“A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters.¿Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed.”¿(SKF Farms v. Superior Court (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 902, 905.) “The only issue involved in a demurrer hearing is whether the complaint, as it stands, unconnected with extraneous matters, states a cause of action.”¿(Hahn, supra, 147 Cal.App.4th at p. 747.)¿

The ultimate facts alleged in the complaint or cross-complaint must be deemed true, as well as all facts that may be implied or inferred from those expressly alleged.¿(Marshall v. Gibson, Dunn & Crutcher (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 1397, 1403; see also Shields v. County of San Diego (1984) 155 Cal.App.3d 103, 133 [stating, “[o]n demurrer, pleadings are read liberally and allegations contained therein are assumed to be true.”]) “The court does not, however, assume the truth of contentions, deductions or conclusions of law. [Citation.]” (Durell v. Sharp Healthcare (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 1350, 1358.)

    a. The first cause of action for breach of contract is adequately pled

Plaintiff contends that the first cause of action for breach of contract fails because the Cross-Complaint fails to adequately allege how Defendant fully performed under the agreement, how Plaintiff breached the agreement, or how damages were incurred.  To prevail on a cause of action for breach of contract, the cross-complainant must prove (1) the contract, (2) the cross-complainant’s performance of the contract or excuse for nonperformance, (3) the cross-defendant's breach, and (4) the resulting damage to the cross-complainant. (Richman v. Hartley (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 1182, 1186.)

The Cross-Complaint alleges that Defendant fully performed under the contract. (Cross-Compl. ¶ 25.) The Cross-Complaint also alleges that, pursuant to the contract, Defendant permitted Plaintiff to take possession of the Property. (Cross-Compl. ¶ 15.) This is sufficient to plead Defendant’s performance of the contract.

The Cross-Complaint alleges that Plaintiff breached the contract by failing to pay rent in the total amount of $76,776.29. (Cross-Compl. ¶ 26.) This is sufficient to plead breach and resulting damages.  The first cause of action in the Cross-Complaint is adequately pled. The court will therefore overrule Plaintiff’s demurrer to this cause of action.

b. The second cause of action for breach of covenant of good faith and fair dealing is adequately pled

Plaintiff contends that the second cause of action for breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing fails because it fails to allege any conduct separate and apart from the breach of contract cause of action.

“ ‘Every contract imposes upon each party a duty of good faith and fair dealing in its performance and its enforcement.’ …The covenant of good faith is read into contracts in order to protect the express covenants or promises of the contract.” (Foley v. Interactive Data Corp. (1988) 47 Cal.3d 654, 684, 690.) “If the allegations [of a cause of action for breach of the implied covenant of good faith] do not go beyond the statement of a mere contract breach and, relying on the same alleged acts, simply seek the same damages or other relief already claimed in a companion contract cause of action, they may be disregarded as superfluous as no additional claim is actually stated.” (Careau & Co. v. Security Pacific Business Credit, Inc. (1990) 222 Cal.App.3d 1371, 1395.)

Here, the Cross-Complaint alleges that Plaintiff misrepresented the purposes for which Plaintiff sought to use the Property in order to induce Defendant to enter into the contract and lease the Property to Plaintiff. This allegation is distinct from the facts alleged in Defendant’s cause of action for breach of contract and separately alleges conduct frustrating the purposes of the agreement. Defendant has therefore adequately alleged a cause of action for breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing.

The court will therefore overrule Plaintiff’s demurrer to this cause of action.

c. The third cause of action for fraudulent misrepresentation fails

Plaintiff contends that the third cause of action for fraudulent misrepresentation fails to allege sufficient facts to satisfy the heightened pleading standard for fraud.

“To establish a claim for fraudulent misrepresentation, the plaintiff must prove: ‘(1) the defendant represented to the plaintiff that an important fact was true; (2) that representation was false; (3) the defendant knew that the representation was false when the defendant made it, or the defendant made the representation recklessly and without regard for its truth; (4) the defendant intended that the plaintiff rely on the representation; (5) the plaintiff reasonably relied on the representation; (6) the plaintiff was harmed; and (7) the plaintiff's reliance on the defendant's representation was a substantial factor in causing that harm to the plaintiff.” (Perlas v. GMAC Mortgage, LLC (2010) 187 Cal.App.4th 429, 434.) The heightened pleading standard for fraud requires that the plaintiff or cross-complainant plead facts showing how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were made. (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 645.)

The Cross-Complaint alleges that Plaintiff and Cross-Defendant Lenise Yarber made false representations to Defendant stating that they wished to lease the Property for the purpose of providing hair- and beauty-related services to the public, but that Plaintiff provided dental services in addition to the aforementioned hair- and beauty-related services. The Cross-Complaint alleges that this representation was made in order to induce Defendant to enter into the lease, and that Defendant reasonably relied on this representation. However, the Cross-Complaint does not allege facts showing that any harm arose as a result of the alleged misrepresentation; although the Cross-Complaint alleges that “[a]s a result of Cross-Complainant's reliance on Cross-Defendants' false representations, Cross-Complainant was and continues to be harmed in substantial amounts of money,” (Cross-Compl. ¶ 46), this is a conclusory allegation that is insufficient to allege harm at the demurrer stage. Furthermore, the Cross-Complaint does not allege facts showing how, where, or by what means the representations were made.

The court will therefore sustain Plaintiff’s demurrer to this cause of action in the Cross-Complaint, with 20 days leave to amend.

d. The fourth cause of action for liability under written guaranty is adequately pled

Plaintiff contends that the fourth cause of action for liability under written guaranty fails because it is reliant upon the first cause of action for breach of contract, which Plaintiff contends fails. However, the court has overruled Plaintiff’s demurrer to the first cause of action. The court will therefore overrule Plaintiff’s demurrer to the fourth cause of action in the Cross-Complaint.

3.   Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, the court OVERRULES IN PART and SUSTAINS IN PART Plaintiff’s demurrer to the Cross-Complaint. The court OVERRULES Plaintiff’s demurrer to the first, second, and fourth causes of action in the Cross-Complaint. The court SUSTAINS Plaintiff’s demurrer to the third cause of action in the Cross-Complaint, with 20 days leave to amend.

Plaintiff is ordered to give notice.

IT IS SO ORDERED.
