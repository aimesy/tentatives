DEPARTMENT

CASE NUMBER

24SMCV02809

MOTION

Compel Compliance with Deposition Subpoena

MOVING PARTIES

Plaintiffs 27715 PCH LLC and The Grind Factory Holdings LLC

OPPOSING PARTIES

none

 

This case arises from a dispute concerning the sale of luxury residential property.  On June 11, 2024, Plaintiff 27715 PCH LLC filed suit against Defendants Closing Agents Escrow (“Closing Agents”); First American Title Company (“First American”) and Judith Sender (“Sender”). 

 

The operative Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”) alleges three causes of action by Plaintiffs 27715 PCH LLC and The Grind Factory Holdings LLC (“Plaintiffs”) against Closing Agents and Sender only (“Defendants”) for (1) Conspiracy to Commit Fraud; (2) Aiding and Abetting; and (3) Negligence.

 

Plaintiffs now move for an order compelling third-party Ando Hacopian to attend and testify at deposition and for monetary sanctions.  The motion is unopposed.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

 

“In California, discovery may be obtained from a nonparty through an oral deposition, a written deposition, or a deposition for the production of business records and things. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2020.010, subd. (a).)” (Board of Registered Nursing v. Superior Court of Orange County (2021) 59 Cal.App.5th 1011, 1030 (hereafter Board of Registered Nursing).) “To pursue the deposition of a nonparty, a party must generally serve a deposition subpoena.” (Ibid.).)

 

“‘If a deponent fails to answer any question or to produce any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing under the deponent’s control that is specified in the deposition notice or a deposition subpoena, the party seeking discovery may move the court for an order compelling that answer or production.’ (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.480, subd. (a).)” (Board of Registered Nursing, supra, 59 Cal.App.5th at p. 1031.) “‘[D]iscovery conducted by

way of a business records subpoena is a “deposition.”’ [Citation.]” (Id. at pp. 1031-1032.)  “If a subpoena requires the attendance of a witness or the production of books, documents, electronically stored information, or other things before a court, or at the trial of an issue therein, or at the taking of a deposition, the court, upon motion reasonably made by [a party or a witness] . . . may make an order . . . directing compliance with it upon those terms or conditions as the court shall declare, including protective orders.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.1.)

 

ANALYSIS

 

“A written notice and all moving papers supporting a motion to compel an answer to a deposition question or to compel production of a document or tangible thing from a nonparty deponent must be personally served on the nonparty deponent unless the nonparty deponent agrees to accept service by mail at an address specified on the deposition record.”  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1346.) 

 

            Here, the proof of service indicates Plaintiffs served the moving papers on Hacopian electronically and by U.S. mail, not personal service.  Further, there is no declaration or other evidence demonstrating that Hacopian agreed to accept electronic service or service by mail.  As such, the Court finds the motion to be procedurally defective. 

 

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

 

For the reasons stated, the Court denies the motion as procedurally defective. 

 

 Plaintiffs shall provide notice of the Court’s ruling and file the notice with a proof of service forthwith.

Case Number: 25SMCV00156    Hearing Date: July 2, 2026    Dept: 207

July 2, 2026

CASE NUMBER

25SMCV00156

MOTIONS

Motions to Compel Further Requests for Production of Documents, Form Interrogatories, and Special Interrogatories

MOVING PARTY

Defendant Kapish Haldia

OPPOSING PARTY

Plaintiff Tradewinds Mechanical Parent, LLC

 

MOTION

 

This case arises from allegations that Plaintiff’s former Mergers & Acquisitions Advisor mismanaged and embezzled from Plaintiff’s investment and operating capital. 

 

            The operative Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”) filed by Plaintiff Tradewinds Mechanical Parent, LLC (“Plaintiff” or “Tradewinds”) against Defendant and Cross-Complainant Kapish Haldia (“Defendant”) alleges four causes of action for (1) breach of fiduciary duty; (2) constructive fraud; (3) violation of California Penal Code, § 496; and (4) accounting.

 

            Defendant has cross-complained against Cross-Defendants Matthew Shaw; Morgan Strain; Steven Brayet; Climate Specialists Holdings, Inc.; and Calico Group LLC (“Cross-Defendants”) alleging five causes of action for (1) conversion; (2) breach of fiduciary duty; (3) fraudulent concealment; (4) constructive trust/unjust enrichment; and (5) declaratory relief.  Defendant alleges that Cross-Defendants fraudulently and maliciously ousted Defendant from Tradewinds Mechanical Parent, LLC, the parties’ joint business venture.

           

            Defendant now moves to compel Plaintiff to provide further responses to Requests for Production of Documents, sets one and two (“RPD”); Form Interrogatories, set one (“FROG”); and Special Interrogatories, set one (“SROG”).  Plaintiff opposes the motions and Defendant replies. 

 

PROCEDURAL REQUIREMENTS

 

            Timeliness of Motions

 

            A notice of motion to compel further responses must be given within 45 days of the service of the responses, or any supplemental responses, or on or before any specific later date to which the parties have agreed in writing. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.310, subd. (c).) Failure to file such a motion within this time period constitutes a waiver of any right to compel further responses to interrogatories or requests for production of documents. (Ibid.)

 

            Here, Defendant electronically served Plaintiff with the FROG, SROG, and RPD set 1 on September 17, 2025.  (Gerbe Decl. iso FROG at ¶ 5 and Ex. A; Gerbe Decl. iso RPD and SROG at ¶ 4 and Ex. A.)  Plaintiff responded on November 3, 2025.  Defendant served the RPD set 2 on October 14, 2025 and Plaintiff responded on November 14, 2025.  (Gerbe Decl. iso RPD at ¶¶ 5, 8-9 and Ex. B, C, D.)  Following an informal discovery conference (“IDC”), Plaintiff supplemented its responses on March 24, 2026, and again on April 21, 2026.  (Gerbe Decl. iso FROG at ¶¶ 14, 22 and Ex. D, F; Gerbe Decl. iso RPD at ¶¶ 17, 24 and Ex. G, I; Gerbe Decl. iso SROG at ¶¶ 15-16, 22-23 and Ex. D, F.)

 

            On May 20, 2026, Defendant filed the instant motions.  Therefore, the motions are timely.

 

            Separate Statement

 

            California Rules of Court, rule 3.1345 requires that any motion involving the content of discovery contain a separate statement with the text of each request, the response, and a statement of factual and legal reasons for why an order compelling further responses is warranted.

 

            Defendant has provided a separate statement with respect to the FROG, SROG, and RPD in compliance with the Rules of Court.

 

            Meet & Confer

 

            The Gerbe Declaration indicates the parties met and conferred at length. Therefore, Defendant has satisfied the meet and confer and IDC obligations.

 

LEGAL STANDARD - DISCOVERY – GENERAL PRINCIPLES

 

            “The purpose of the discovery rules is to enhance the truth-seeking function of the litigation process and eliminate trial strategies that focus on gamesmanship and surprise.  In other words, the discovery process is designed to make a trial less a game of blindman's bluff and more a fair contest with the basic issues and facts disclosed to the fullest practicable extent.”  (Juarez v. Boy Scouts of Am., Inc. (2000) 81 Cal.App.4th 377, 389 [cleaned up].)  

 

            Where a party objects, or responds inadequately, to discovery requests, a motion lies to compel further responses, and that party has the burden to justify the objections or inadequate responses. (Fairmont Ins. Co. v. Superior Court (2000) 22 Cal.4th 245, 255; Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (a) [motion to compel further responses lies “[o]n receipt of a response to a demand for inspection”].)  “A trial court's determination of a motion to compel discovery is reviewed for abuse of discretion.  However, when the facts asserted in support of and in opposition to the motion are in conflict, the trial court's factual findings will be upheld if they are supported by substantial evidence.” (Costco Wholesale Corp. v. Superior Court (2009) 47 Cal.4th 725, 733 [cleaned up].)

 

ANALYSIS   

 

1.     FROGs

 

Defendant seeks further responses to FROG Nos. 8.7, 9.2, and 17.1, which seek (1) the amount of Plaintiff’s claimed loss and how it was calculated; (2) the documents supporting that claimed loss; and (3) the factual and documentary bases for Plaintiff’s denials of Defendant’s Requests for Admission (“RFA”).

 

a.     FROG No. 8.7

 

Defendant contends Plaintiff’s responses are inadequate because the Complaint alleges embezzled funds of at least $500,000 and substantial damages, which is reiterated in its response to FROG No. 8.7, yet the itemized transactions Plaintiff has provided total only $215,131. 

 

In opposition, Defendant argues that for FROG No. 8.7, it has identified all categories of damages currently known, but that its investigation remains ongoing, and it reserves the right to claim additional damages, as supported by a final expert analysis.

 

In reply, Plaintiff argues that Defendant has still not bridged the gap between the claimed $500,000 and the $215,131 it has itemized.

 

Ultimately, Defendant cannot provide documents or information it does not have.  Defendant has indicated it is only able to itemize $215,131 of embezzlement damages at present.  Should Defendant later produce an expert report itemizing greater embezzlement damages based on documents and/or information it currently has but failed produce, Plaintiff may pursue appropriate remedies at that time.

 

b.     FROG Nos. 9.2 and 17.1

 

As for FROG Nos. 9.2 and 17.1, which ask Plaintiff to identify each document that supports the claimed damages and Plaintiff’s RFA denials, Defendant argues Plaintiff’s responses are deficient because Plaintiff simply directs Defendant generally to its entire 2,000-page document production as the documents purportedly supporting its damages and the factual/documentary bases for its denials of Defendant’s RFA.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that Defendant is not entitled to a document-by-document evidentiary roadmap identifying the specific records Plaintiff intends to rely upon at trial.

 

While the Court agrees that Plaintiff is not required to yet identify which specific documents Plaintiff intends to rely on at trial, Plaintiff is required to identify, by bates number, each specific document that is responsive, which Plaintiff has failed to do.

 

Therefore, the Court grants Defendant’s motion to compel further responses to FROG Nos. 9.2 and 17.1.

 

2.     RPD

 

Defendant argues Plaintiff’s RPD responses are deficient for the following reasons:

 

·       Responses to RPD Nos. 1-10, 12-14, 16-17, and 19-22 identify sweeping and overlapping bates ranges.

 

·       All responses indicate “discovery is ongoing” and Defendant “will produce” responsive documents.

 

·       Responses to RPD Nos. 13-14 do not confirm that all responsive financial records have been produced.

 

·       Responses to RPD Nos. 21-22 do not provide code-compliant statements of inability to comply, because it indicates both that no such responsive documents are in its possession, custody, or control and that responsive documents may still exist in archived email accounts, without identifying the relevant custodians.

 

·       Response to RFP No. 23 is not code-compliant (indicating compliance, inability to comply, and/or an objection).

 

Taking Defendant’s second argument first, the Discovery Act requires that a party responding to a demand for production of documents must provide “a statement that the party will comply with the particular demand [….]”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.210, subd. (a)(1).)   Thus, to the extent Plaintiff takes issue with Defendant’s language that it will produce documents, such arguments are unfounded.

 

Notwithstanding, regarding Defendant’s first argument, “Any documents or category of documents produced in response to a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling shall be identified with the specific request number to which the documents respond.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.280, subd. (a).) 

 

Thus, Defendant’s generic reference to the entire document production in response to each RPD is insufficient under the Code.

 

With regard to RPD Nos. 13-14 and 21-22, the Court agrees that Plaintiff’s responses are not code-compliant, and Plaintiff does not specifically address these RPD in opposition.

 

Regarding RPD No. 23, it is clear to the Court that Plaintiff is standing on its objections.  Because Defendant’s only objection is that the response is not code-compliant, the Court denies Defendant’s request to compel a further response to RPD No. 23.

 

As such, the Court grants Plaintiff’s motion to compel further responses to RPD No. 1-10, 12-14, 16-17, and 19-22 as discussed above, but not RPD No. 23. 

 

3.     SROG

 

Plaintiff seeks to compel further responses to SROG Nos. 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, 18, 20, 22, and 24 as follows:

 

·       SROG Nos. 6, 10, 14, 18, and 22 repeat the same narrative across separate and distinct legal theories.

 

·       Tradewinds characterizes various expenditures as “unauthorized” without identifying the authorization framework.

 

·       The response to SROG No. 22 is internally inconsistent and does not reconcile the amounts sought.

 

·       SROG Nos. 8, 12, 16, 20, and 24 improperly reference large Bates ranges.

 

a.     SROG Nos. 6, 10, 14, 18, and 22

 

First, Defendant argues Plaintiff’s responses to SROG Nos. 6, 10, 14, 18, and 22 improperly list categorical totals instead of identifying specific charges. 

 

Second, Plaintiff repeats by incorporation the same factual narrative of its breach of fiduciary duty cause of action (SROG No. 6) to its causes of action for constructive fraud (SROG No. 10); Penal Code section 496 (SROG No. 14); accounting (SROG No. 18); and embezzlement (SROG No. 22).

 

Third, Defendant objects again to Plaintiff’s reference to an ongoing investigation.

 

Taking Defendant’s third argument first, as discussed above, Plaintiff is only obligated to respond based on information it has available to it right now. 

 

Further, upon review of Plaintiff’s narrative, the Court finds that it is a comprehensive overview of the totality of the improper conduct Defendant is alleged to have done, which underlies and supports all of Plaintiff’s claims.  As such, the Court does not find the repetition of a comprehensive factual narrative to be deficient.

 

However, the Court agrees with Defendant’s first point, that Plaintiff’s vague subtotals, which are neither itemized nor specifically identify the documents upon which those subtotals are based are deficient.

 

As such, the Court grants Defendant’s motion to compel further responses to SROG Nos. 6, 10, 14, 18, 22 to identify the specific facts underlying each of the subtotals.

 

b.     “Unauthorized”

 

Defendant next objects to Plaintiff’s responses, to the extent they refer generally to “unauthorized” charges without explaining the authorization framework. 

 

In addition to itemizing specific charges Plaintiff contends were unauthorized, Plaintiff’s responses indicate as follows:

 

Each of the above-listed charges were unauthorized. All M&A efforts were managed remotely. Outside of traveling with Steven Brayet or Matt Shaw or attending internal meetings, Defendant was not authorized to travel by himself. Defendant was not authorized, or even expected, to entertain or wine and dine potential deal partners. The extent of Defendant’s authorized spending was limited to reasonable expenses related to travel to Plaintiff’s place of business (i.e., reasonable flights, meals, hotels). Other expenses, like for example, gym memberships, clothing, club memberships, and other merchandise, were not authorized.

 

            The Court finds this sufficient to satisfy Plaintiff’s discovery obligations.

 

c.      SROG No. 22

 

The Court does not find the response to SROG No. 22 to be internally inconsistent merely because it itemizes only $215,131 in embezzlement damages instead of $500,000.  Rather, as discussed above, Plaintiff has indicated at this point in time, it is only able to identify $215,131 in embezzlement damages.

 

d.     SROG Nos. 8, 12, 16, 20, and 24

 

Finally, Defendant objects to Plaintiff’s responses to SROG Nos. 8, 12, 16, 20, and 24 on the grounds that they reference large ranges of Bates numbers instead of the specific documents that are responsive to each of these requests.

 

As discussed above, the Court agrees with Defendant that this is improper.

 

4.     Sanctions

 

            A trial court may sanction a party for engaging in the misuse of discovery, which includes: failure to respond or submit to an authorized method of discovery; making an evasive response to discovery; making, without substantial justification, an unmeritorious objection to discovery; and making or opposing, unsuccessfully and without substantial justification, a motion to compel or to limit discovery. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.010.)

 

In addition, Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.300, subdivision (d) provides: “The court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel a further response to interrogatories, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.”

 

            Defendant seeks monetary sanctions in the amount of $3,550 in connection with the FROG and SROG, representing approximately 8 hours of associate time at an hourly rate of $380 and about 0.75 hours of partner time at a rate of $600 per hour; and $4,310 in connection with the RPD; and $3,550 in connection with the SROG, representing approximately 10 hours of associate time at an hourly rate of $380 and about 0.75 hours of partner time at a rate of $600 per hour, plus the $60 filing fee for each motion.

 

            The Court agrees that Plaintiff’s counsel misused the discovery process by making evasive responses to discovery and unsuccessfully opposing the motions on the grounds that Plaintiff is not obligated to identify the specific documents in the production that respond to each request, without substantial justification. 

 

However, the Court finds the amounts sought excessive, and awards a total of $2460.00, representing 6 hours of attorney time to prepare the motions at $380 per hour, plus the $180 in filing fees.      

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

 

            Therefore, the Court rules as follows:

 

FROG

 

·       Defendant’s motion to compel further responses to FROG No. 8.7 is denied.

 

·       Defendant’s motion to compel further responses to FROG Nos. 9.2 and 17.1 is granted.  Plaintiff shall provide further responses to identify the specific documents that are responsive to each request.

 

RPD

 

·       Defendant’s motion to compel further responses to RPD Nos. 1-10, 12-14, 16-17, and 19-22 is granted.  Plaintiff shall provide further responses identifying by bates numbers, which specific documents in the production correspond to each of the RPD.  Further, with respect to RPD Nos. 13-14 and 21-22, Plaintiff’s existing responses are not code-compliant.  Plaintiff shall provide further responses, indicating whether Plaintiff is complying in full, objecting, or unable to comply because the documents are not in its possession, custody, or control.

 

·       Defendant’s motion to compel a further response to RPD No. 23 is denied, as Plaintiff’s existing response is code-compliant.

 

SROG

 

·       Defendant’s motion to compel further responses to SROG Nos 6, 10, 14, 18, 22 is granted.  Plaintiff shall provide further responses, identifying the specific facts underlying each of the subtotals.

 

·       Defendant’s motion to compel further responses to SROG Nos. 8, 12, 16, 20, and 24 is granted.  Plaintiff shall provide further responses, identifying the specific documents that are responsive to each of these requests.

 

Plaintiff shall provide further, code-compliant responses consistent with this ruling within twenty (20) days of notice of the Court’s orders.

 

Further, Plaintiff’s counsel of record, Kevin J. Cole of KJC Law Group, A.P.C., shall pay monetary sanctions to Defendant, by and through Defendant’s counsel, in the amount of $2460.00 within twenty (20) days of notice of the Court’s orders.

 

            Defendant shall provide notice of the Court’s orders and file the notice with a proof of service forthwith.

Case Number: 25SMCV03400    Hearing Date: July 2, 2026    Dept: 207

TENTATIVE RULING

 

DEPARTMENT          207

HEARING DATE       July 2, 2026

CASE NUMBER        25SMCV03400

MOTION                    Motion to Vacate/Set Aside Entry of Default

MOVING PARTY      Defendant Joel Silver, individually and as trustee of the Joel Silver Trust

OPPOSING PARTY   Plaintiff Ziad Ghandour

 

 

MOTION

 

This case arises from allegations of an unrepaid loan.  On July 2, 2025, Plaintiff Ziad Ghandour (“Plaintiff”) filed suit against Defendant Joe Silver, individually and as trustee of the Joel Silver Trust (“Defendant”) and Does 1 to 25, alleging three causes of action for (1) breach of promissory note; (2) money lent; and (3) money had and received.

 

On March 3, 2026, the Court issued an order granting Plaintiff’s request to serve Defendant by publication, email, and mail. 

 

The summons was published on March 16, 23, 30, and April 6.  Default was entered on May 19, 2026.

 

Defendant now moves to set aside/vacate the entry of default.  Plaintiff opposes the motion and Defendant replies.

 

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

 

            Plaintiff requests judicial notice of the following:

 

1. Order Granting Motion for order authorizing Submitted Service of Summons and Complaint, filed on March 3, 2026 in the above-captioned lawsuit, a true and correct copy of which is attached hereto and incorporated herein, by this reference, as Exhibit "A."

 

2. Proof of Service by First-Class Mail, filed on May 13, 2026 in the above-captioned lawsuit, a true and correct copy of which is attached hereto and incorporated herein, by this reference, as Exhibit "B."

 

3. Proof of Electronic Service, filed on April 23, 2026 in the above-captioned lawsuit, a true and correct copy of which is attached hereto and incorporated herein, by this reference, as Exhibit "C."

 

4. Request for Entry of Default, a true and correct copy of which is attached hereto and incorporated herein, by this reference, as Exhibit "D."

 

Judicial notice may be taken of records of any court in this state.  (Evid. Code, § 452, subd. (d)(1).)  Because the above documents are all part of the Court’s records for this case, the Court may take judicial notice of them.  (Ibid.)   However, “while courts are free to take judicial notice of the existence of each document in a court file, including the truth of results reached, they may not take judicial notice of the truth of hearsay statements in decisions and court files.  Courts may not take judicial notice of allegations in affidavits, declarations and probation reports in court records because such matters are reasonably subject to dispute and therefore require formal proof.”  (Lockley v. Law Office of Cantrell, Green, Pekich, Cruz & McCort (2001) 91 Cal.App.4th 875, 882 [cleaned up].) 

 

Accordingly, the Court takes judicial notice of the existence, filing, and legal consequences of the above documents, but not the truth of the allegations or other hearsay statements contained therein.

 

ANALYSIS

 

                          I.          DISCRETIONARY AND MANDATORY RELIEF

 

“Proceeding to judgment in the absence of a party is an extraordinary and disfavored practice in Anglo–American jurisprudence:  The policy of the law is to have every litigated case tried upon its merits, and it looks with disfavor upon a party, who, regardless of the merits of the case, attempts to take advantage of the mistake, surprise, inadvertence, or neglect of his adversary.”  (Au-Yang v. Barton (1999) 21 Cal.4th 958, 963 [cleaned up].) 

 

Code of Civil procedure section 473 “includes a discretionary provision, which applies permissively, and a mandatory provision, which applies as of right.” (Minick v. City of Petaluma (2016) 3 Cal.App.5th 15, 25 (hereafter Minick).)  “Section 473 is a remedial statute to be “applied liberally” in favor of relief if the opposing party will not suffer prejudice.  Because the law strongly favors trial and disposition on the merits, any doubts in applying section 473 must be resolved in favor of the party seeking relief from default.  Unless inexcusable neglect is clear, the policy favoring trial on the merits prevails.”  (Minick, supra, 3 Cal.App.5th at p. 24 [cleaned up].) 

 

The party or the legal representative must seek such relief “within a reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months, after the judgment, dismissal, order, or proceeding was taken.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b); see Rappleyea v. Campbell (1994) 8 Cal.4th 975, 980 [“because more than six months had elapsed from the entry of default, and hence relief under section 473 was unavailable”]; People v. The North River Ins. Co. (2011) 200 Ca.App.4th 712, 721 [motion for relief under section 473 must be brought “within a reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months”]).  “The six-month limit is mandatory; a court has no authority to grant relief under section 473, subdivision (b), unless an application is made within the six-month period.”  (Arambula v. Union Carbide Corp. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 333, 340, citations omitted.) 

 

A.    DISCRETIONARY RELIEF

 

Per Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b), a court may “relieve a party or his or her legal representative from a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against him or her through his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect.”

 

B.    MANDATORY RELIEF

 

Notwithstanding any other requirements of this section, the court shall, whenever an application for relief is made no more than six months after entry of judgment, is in proper form, and is accompanied by an attorney's sworn affidavit attesting to his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect, vacate any (1) resulting default entered by the clerk against his or her client, and which will result in entry of a default judgment, or (2) resulting default judgment or dismissal entered against his or her client, unless the court finds that the default or dismissal was not in fact caused by the attorney's mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect.

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b).)  “In considering whether the trial court properly denied relief under section 473(b), the first question is the sufficiency of defendants' showing of attorney fault, if believed, to trigger the mandatory relief provisions of that statute.”  (Standard Microsystems Corp. v. Winbond Electronics Corp. (2009) 179 Cal.App.4th 868, 896, disapproved on other grounds by Even Zohar Construction & Remodeling, Inc. v. Bellaire Townhouses, LLC (2015) 61 Cal.4th 830 (hereafter Standard).)  “Under section 473(b), a party is entitled to relief from a default and resulting judgment whenever, on timely application for relief, his attorney ‘attest[s] to his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect’ in connection with the default or the judgment.”  (Ibid.) 

 

Defendant moves to set aside the default on the grounds that he was out of the country when the complaint was served by publication and the default entered, during which time he received conflicting information about whether the case was being dismissed or served by publication and prosecuted.

 

In support, Defendant advances his own declaration, which provides:

 

4.  While this Lawsuit has been pending, I have been out of the country and out of the state. In addition, I have been in communications with Plaintiff and was informed by Plaintiff that the Lawsuit was dismissed and/or was in the process of being dismissed and no response was required. As a result, I did not inquire into the matter further or retain an attorney. While l was communicating with Plaintiff and was out of the country, I received some documents via email, such as the proof of service of publication and the complaint and summons. These documents caused me to be confused because I believed the Lawsuit had been resolved based on representations made to me by Plaintiff: Although some of the documents were confusing, I did not believe any further action was needed on my part again, based on representations made to me by Plaintiff.  In addition, because I was out of the country and out of the state, I was unable to receive notice by publication.  Further, because of my communications with Plaintiff and the fact that I was emailed with the documents and service was made by publication, which resulted in multiple conflicting deadlines, it was unclear whether a response was required and if so, by when.  Further, I was never personally served so I did not know the deadline to respond to the Complaint had even begun.  I have been involved in litigation before and believed that the time for me to respond could not commence until I was personally served so I did not know the time line for me to respond to the Complaint had even begun.

 

5. On or about May 19, 2026, I received the Request for Entry of Default by email.  Upon review of the document, I learned that I had been misled into believing no response was required and Plaintiff was attempting to obtain a judgment against me.  I immediately forwarded the Request for Entry of Default to my attorney to take all appropriate action.  I understand that on or about May 28, 2026, my attorneys requested that Plaintiff’s counsel stipulate to relief from entry of default and allow me to answer the Complaint.  Upon refusing to stipulate to the requested for relief, I filed the Motion.

 

(Silver Decl. ¶¶ 4-5.)

 

            In opposition, Plaintiff argues that Defendant deliberately evaded personal service seven times in July 2025 and opened Plaintiff’s emails warning of the impending default.  Plaintiff also advances a December 11, 2025 email from Defendant acknowledging the lawsuit.  (Ghandour Decl. ¶ 8 and Ex. B.)

 

            Further, within minutes of being served by email, Defendant responded, “Ziad ended this lawsuit.  Please talk to your client.”  Defendant was corrected, “[w]e have spoken with our client, and he is moving forward with the lawsuit.”  Defendant again responded, “No, he is not.”  Defendant was again corrected, “[t]his email shall constitute notice that Mr. Ghandour does not intend to dismiss the lawsuit.”  Defendant responded, “My guess is you no longer represent Mr. Ghandour.  According to your client the lawsuit is dismissed.  I will not engage with you again.  Please stop communicating with me.”  (Cortez Decl. ¶ 11 and Ex. M.)

 

            Defendant then left a voicemail for Plaintiff, indicating, “Ziad, please call me.  This lawsuit doesn’t stop.  Please call me.  It’s Joel.”  (Ghandour Decl. ¶ 9 and Ex. O.)

 

            The Court finds that the evidence advanced by both parties demonstrates confusion on the part of Defendant justifying relief. 

 

            Plaintiff also points out in opposition that the motion is procedurally improper, because Defendant failed to attached a proposed responsive pleading.  However, Defendant has cured this procedural deficiency by attaching a proposed answer to the reply. 

 

            Plaintiff also argues in opposition that if the Court grants relief and sets aside the default, it should impose sanctions pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (c), which provides:

 

(c)(1) Whenever the court grants relief from a default, default judgment, or dismissal based on any of the provisions of this section, the court may do any of the following:

(A) Impose a penalty of no greater than one thousand dollars ($1,000) upon an offending attorney or party.

(B) Direct that an offending attorney pay an amount no greater than one thousand dollars ($1,000) to the State Bar Client Security Fund.

(C) Grant other relief as is appropriate.

 

While Plaintiff contends that Defendant was intentionally avoiding service, Defendant explains that he was out of the country, which is consistent with Plaintiff’s evidence that Defendant’s son told the process server Defendant was not home.  Simply not being home is not grounds to impose a penalty.

 

Plaintiff also asserts that Defendant’s then-counsel, Hill, Farrer & Burrill LLP, whom Defendant retained to negotiate the loan issue, refused to accept service in November 24, 2025.  (Cortez Decl. ¶¶ 5-6 and Ex. J.)  But Defendant has not retained Hill Farrer to represent him in this action.  Not agreeing to retain a specific law firm to represent Defendant in this matter is also not grounds to impose a penalty.

 

Therefore, the Court does not find, on the record before it, that imposition of a penalty is appropriate.

 

CONCLUSION

 

            For the foregoing reasons, the Court grants Defendant’s Motion to Vacate/Set Aside the Default entered against him on May 19, 2026.  Further, finding the evidence does not support a penalty, the Court denies Plaintiff’s request to impose a monetary penalty against Defendant in conjunction with setting aside the entry of default.

1.     MOTION TO STRIKE

 

Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading, may serve and file a motion to strike the whole pleading or any part thereof.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 435, subd. (b)(1); Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1322, subd. (b).)  On a motion to strike, the court may: (1) strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or (2) strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of California, a court rule, or an order of the court.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a)-(b); Stafford v. Shultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782.) 

 

a.     Punitive Damages

 

In ruling on a motion to strike punitive damages, “judges read allegations of a pleading subject to a motion to strike as a whole, all parts in their context, and assume their truth.”  (Clauson v. Superior Court (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1253, 1255.)  To state a prima facie claim for punitive damages, a plaintiff must allege the elements set forth in the punitive damages statute, Civil Code section 3294.  (College Hosp., Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 721.)  Per Civil Code section 3294, a plaintiff must allege that the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice.  (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (a).)   As set forth in the Civil Code,

 

(1) “Malice” means conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.  (2) “Oppression” means despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person's rights.  (3) “Fraud” means an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known to the defendant with the intention on the part of the defendant of thereby depriving a person of property or legal rights or otherwise causing injury.

 

(Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (c)(1)-(3), emphasis added.) 

 

Further, a plaintiff must assert facts with specificity to support a conclusion that a defendant acted with oppression, fraud or malice.  To wit, there is a heightened pleading requirement regarding a claim for punitive damages.  (See Smith v. Superior Court (1992) 10 Cal.App.4th 1033, 1041-1042.)  “When nondeliberate injury is charged, allegations that the defendant’s conduct was wrongful, willful, wanton, reckless or unlawful do not support a claim for exemplary damages; such allegations do not charge malice.  When a defendant must produce evidence in defense of an exemplary damage claim, fairness demands that he receive adequate notice of the kind of conduct charged against him.” (G. D. Searle & Co. v. Superior Court (1975) 49 Cal.App.3d 22, 29 [cleaned up].)  In Anschutz Entertainment Group, Inc. v. Snepp, the Court of Appeal noted that the plaintiffs’ assertions related to their claim for punitive damages were “insufficient to meet the specific pleading requirement.”  (Anschutz Entertainment Group, Inc. v. Snepp (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 598, 643 [plaintiffs alleged “the conduct of Defendants was intentional, and done willfully, maliciously, with ill will towards Plaintiffs, and with conscious disregard for Plaintiff's rights. Plaintiff's injuries were exacerbated by the malicious conduct of Defendants. Defendants' conduct justifies an award of exemplary and punitive damages”]; see also Grieves v. Superior Court (1984) 157 Cal.App.3d 159, 166 [“The mere allegation an intentional tort was committed is not sufficient to warrant an award of punitive damages.  Not only must there be circumstances of oppression, fraud, or malice, but facts must be alleged in the pleading to support such a claim”].)

b.     Attorneys’ Fees

 

            Plaintiffs seek attorneys’ fees pursuant to the private attorney general doctrine of Civil Code section 1021.5 and Civil Code section 1942.4, subd. (b)(2), which provides that if a landlord collects rent on premises in uninhabitable conditions, under certain circumstances, the prevailing tenant may recoup their reasonable attorneys’ fees and costs, and pursuant to Civil Code section 1717 and the underlying lease agreements.

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 1021.5 provides:

 

Upon motion, a court may award attorneys’ fees to a successful party against one or more opposing parties in any action which has resulted in the enforcement of an important right affecting the public interest if: (a) a significant benefit, whether pecuniary or nonpecuniary, has been conferred on the general public or a large class of persons, (b) the necessity and financial burden of private enforcement, or of enforcement by one public entity against another public entity, are such as to make the award appropriate, and (c) such fees should not in the interest of justice be paid out of the recovery, if any. 

 

            Defendants argue that this case, which involves a single unit in a single property will not confer a significant benefit on the general public or a large class of persons, notwithstanding the Complaint’s allegation that any judgment or settlement will impact, “the homeowners and tenants who live in the area around the Subject Property[.]”  (Complaint ¶ 83.)  In opposition, Plaintiffs merely direct the Court to paragraph 83.

 

            The Court agrees with Defendants that Plaintiffs have not alleged a large class of persons will benefit from an action primarily, if not entirely concerning the habitability of a single unit.

 

            Notwithstanding, Civil Code section 1942.4 provides:

 

(a) A landlord of a dwelling may not demand rent, collect rent, issue a notice of a rent increase, […] if all of the following conditions exist prior to the landlord’s demand or notice:

 

(1) The dwelling substantially lacks any of the affirmative standard characteristics listed in Section 1941.1 or violates Section 17920.10 of the Health and Safety Code, or is deemed and declared substandard as set forth in Section 17920.3 of the Health and Safety Code because conditions listed in that section exist to an extent that endangers the life, limb, health, property, safety, or welfare of the public or the occupants of the dwelling.

 

(2) A public officer or employee who is responsible for the enforcement of any housing law, after inspecting the premises, has notified the landlord or the landlord’s agent in writing of his or her obligations to abate the nuisance or repair the substandard conditions.

 

(3) The conditions have existed and have not been abated 35 days beyond the date of service of the notice specified in paragraph (2) and the delay is without good cause. For purposes of this subdivision, service shall be complete at the time of deposit in the United States mail.

 

(4) The conditions were not caused by an act or omission of the tenant or lessee in violation of Section 1929 or 1941.2.

 

(b) (1) A landlord who violates this section is liable to the tenant or lessee for the actual damages sustained by the tenant or lessee and special damages of not less than one hundred dollars ($100) and not more than five thousand dollars ($5,000).

 

(2) The prevailing party shall be entitled to recovery of reasonable attorney’s fees and costs of the suit in an amount fixed by the court.

 

(Civil Code, § 1942.4.)  Plaintiffs allege:

 

96. In or about July 2024, through June 2025, Plaintiffs have complained to Defendants about the poor condition of the premises, as stated above.

 

97. At all relevant times, Plaintiffs timely paid the rent, and Defendants collected without fail, despite the Premises’ substantial defects.

 

98. Plaintiffs are informed and believe that the County of Los Angeles and/or City of Los Angeles has inspected and Defendants’ defective premises, and sometime after visiting the premises, public officer housing law inspectors provided written notices to the Defendants of their obligation to repair the prohibited conditions. These notices, on the dates listed on the notices/code enforcement orders concerning The Property, were issued as alleged herein. The defective conditions were not caused by any act or omission of Plaintiffs.

 

99. Plaintiffs are informed and believe that Defendants failed to abate or repair the conditions identified in the Code enforcement orders within 35 days of receiving those notices, during which time the substandard conditions continued to exist without abatement.

 

100. Even after Defendants were notified by the County and/or City inspector that repairs were needed to the Subject Property, Defendants failed to make timely repairs. Defendants continued to demand and collect rent from the Plaintiffs while the prohibited conditions identified by housing authorities were unabated.

 

(Complaint ¶¶ 96-100.)

 

            When alleging noncompliance with a statute, allegations made on information and belief are only proper where the allegations involve matters peculiarly within the knowledge of the defendants and they are supported by a statement of facts upon which the belief is founded.  (See Doe v. City of Los Angeles (2007) 42 Cal.4th 531, 542.)  Here, Plaintiffs have not alleged the factual basis underlying their belief that the City and/or County of Los Angeles visited the premises and issued written notices to the Defendants to repair, when those notices were issued, or that Defendants failed to repair within 35 days and then continued to collect rent.

 

            As for attorneys’ fees pursuant to the lease agreement, Civil Code section 1717 provides:

 

In any action on a contract, where the contract specifically provides that attorney’s fees and costs, which are incurred to enforce that contract, shall be awarded either to one of the parties or to the prevailing party, then the party who is determined to be the party prevailing on the contract, whether he or she is the party specified in the contract or not, shall be entitled to reasonable attorney’s fees in addition to other costs.

 

            Here, Plaintiffs allege:

 

143. As a direct and proximate result thereof, Plaintiffs have incurred reasonable attorneys' fees and costs, pursuant to the rental agreement, to Civil Code §§ 1717 and 1942.4.

 

(Complaint ¶ 143.)

 

            Defendants argue that the Complaint fails to allege the statutory attorney fee provision in haec verba.  The Court agrees.  Although Plaintiffs seek attorneys’ fees “pursuant to the rental agreement,” Plaintiffs do not actually allege that the rental agreement contains an attorney fee provision.

 

            Therefore, the Court grants Defendants’ motion to strike the requests for attorneys’ fees.

 

2.     LEAVE TO AMEND

 

A plaintiff has the burden of showing in what manner the complaint could be amended and how the amendment would change the legal effect of the complaint, i.e., state a cause of action. (See The Inland Oversight Committee v. City of San Bernardino (2018) 27 Cal.App.5th 771, 779; PGA West Residential Assn., Inc. v. Hulven Int'l, Inc. (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 156, 189.) A plaintiff must not only state the legal basis for the amendment, but also the factual allegations sufficient to state a cause of action or claim. (See PGA West Residential Assn., Inc. v. Hulven Int'l, Inc., supra, 14 Cal.App.5th at p. 189.) Moreover, a plaintiff does not meet his or her burden by merely stating in the opposition to a demurrer or motion to strike that “if the Court finds the operative complaint deficient, plaintiff respectfully requests leave to amend.” (See Major Clients Agency v Diemer (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1116, 1133; Graham v. Bank of America (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 594, 618 [asserting an abstract right to amend does not satisfy the burden].)

 

Here, Plaintiffs request leave to amend additional facts to entitle them to recover punitive damages.  Although Plaintiffs do not specify the facts they could add, the Court finds that all of the deficiencies identified above can be cured by pleading the existing general facts with more specificity. 

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

 

For the reasons stated, the Court grants Defendants’ motion to strike in its entirety with leave to amend. 

 

Plaintiffs shall file and serve an amended complaint in conformance with this ruling on or before July 23, 2026. 

 

Defendants shall provide notice of the Court’s ruling and file the notice with a proof of service forthwith.
