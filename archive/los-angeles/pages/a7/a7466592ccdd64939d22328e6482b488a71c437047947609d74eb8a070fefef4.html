Case Number: 24PSCV04402    Hearing Date: June 25, 2026    Dept: G

Defendant General Motors LLC’s Demurrer to Plaintiff Bernice Castaneda’s Third Amended Complaint

Respondent: NO OPPOSITION

Defendant General Motors LLC’s Motion to Strike Portions of Plaintiff Bernice Castaneda’s Third Amended Complaint

Respondent: NO OPPOSITION

TENTATIVE RULING

Defendant General Motors LLC’s Demurrer to Plaintiff Bernice Castaneda’s Third Amended Complaint is SUSTAINED WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND.

Defendant General Motors LLC’s Motion to Strike Portions of Plaintiff Bernice Castaneda’s Third Amended Complaint is GRANTED.

Defendant General Motors LLC is ordered to file an ANSWER in ten (10) days.

BACKGROUND

This is a Song-Beverly action arising from the purchase of an allegedly defective motor vehicle.  On January 18, 2023, plaintiff Bernice Castaneda (Castaneda) purchased an SUV manufactured by defendant General Motors LLC (GM).

On December 23, 2024, Castaneda filed the Complaint.  On April 7, 2026, Castaneda filed the operative Third Amended Complaint, alleging causes of action for (1) violation of Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (d); (2) violation of Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (b); (3) violation of Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (a)(3); (4) breach of the implied warranty of merchantability; and (5) fraudulent inducement by concealment.

On May 4, 2026, GM filed this demurrer with motion to strike, which are unopposed.

This case is set for a case management conference (further) and a hearing on the demurrer and motion to strike on June 25, 2026.

DEMURRER

GM demurs to the fifth cause of action for fraudulent inducement by concealment.  For the following reasons, the court SUSTAINS the demurrer WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND.

Legal Standard

A party may demur to a complaint on the grounds that it “does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (e).)  A demurrer tests whether the complaint states a cause of action.  (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)  When considering demurrers, courts accept all well pleaded facts as true.  (Fox v. JAMDAT Mobile, Inc. (2010) 185 Cal.App.4th 1068, 1078.)  The court should also draw reasonable inferences in favor of the pleading subject to demurrer.  (Kruss v. Booth (2010) 185 Cal.App.4th 699, 727.)  The court should liberally construe the complaint to bring substantial justice between the parties.  (Addiego v. Hill (1965) 238 Cal.App.2d 842, 845.)  To sustain a demurrer, defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice.  (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994; SKF Farms v. Super. Ct. (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 902, 905, stating, “A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters.  Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed.”; Hahn, supra, at 747, stating, “The only issue involved in a demurrer hearing is whether the complaint, as it stands, unconnected with extraneous matters, states a cause of action.”)

Discussion

The court notes that GM’s demurrer to the Third Amended Complaint raises the same objections as GM’s demurrer to the First Amended Complaint and GM’s demurrer to the Second Amended Complaint.  (Compare Dem. to 1AC with Dem. to 3AC; compare Dem to 2AC with Dem. to 3AC.)  In the interest of judicial efficiency, the court only briefly discusses the issues addressed in its previous ruling.

The court previously sustained GM’s demurrer with leave to amend on the ground that Castaneda failed to allege the elements of fraud with the requisite specificity.  (7/24/2025 Order Re: Court’s Final Ruling, p. 3; see also 3/18/2026 Order Re: Court’s Final Ruling, p. 2, referencing the court’s 7/24/2025 ruling.)  Additionally, the court cautioned Castaneda that “[a] complaint or cross-complaint shall not be amended more than three times, absent an offer to the trial court as to such additional facts to be pleaded that there is a reasonable possibility the defect can be cured to state a cause of action.”  (3/18/2026 Order Re: Court’s Final Ruling, p. 2, quoting Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (e)(1).) 

Despite this caution, Castaneda failed to amend her pleading to add allegations for the elements of fraud that would cure the previously cited defects with the fifth cause of action.  (See Jenkins v. JP Morgan Chase Bank, Nat. Assn. (2013) 216 Cal.App.4th 497, 535, suggesting that the court deny leave to amend based on futility where the facts are not in dispute and no liability exists under substantive law.)

Therefore, the demurrer is SUSTAINED WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND on the grounds that Castaneda failed to allege the elements of fraud with the requisite specificity.  Since the court sustains the demurrer without leave to amend on other grounds, the court HOLDS IN ABEYANCE the demurrer based on the duty to disclose.

MOTION TO STRIKE

GM moves to strike the request for punitive damages.  For the following reasons, the court GRANTS the motion.

Legal Standard

The court may, upon a motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a).)  The court may also strike all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (b).)  The grounds for a motion to strike are that the pleading has irrelevant, false, or improper matter, or has not been drawn or filed in conformity with laws.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subds. (a)–(b).)  The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 437, subds. (a)–(b).)

Discussion

GM argues that Castaneda may not recover punitive damages.  The court agrees.

Claims for damages that may not be imposed may properly be stricken.  (Stafford v. Shultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782; Grieves v. Super. Ct. (1984) 157 Cal.App.3d 159.)  A plaintiff may recover punitive damages if they establish by clear and convincing evidence that a defendant is guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice.  (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (a).)  “In addition to the requirement that the operative complaint set forth the elements as stated in [S]ection 3294, it must include specific factual allegations showing that defendant’s conduct was oppressive, fraudulent, or malicious to support a claim for punitive damages. . . .  Punitive damages may not be pleaded generally.”  (Today’s IV, Inc. v. Los Angeles County Metropolitan Transportation Authority (2022) 83 Cal.App.5th 1137, 1193.)

All of Castaneda’s four remaining causes of action are based on the Song-Beverly Act, which limits recovery of damages, and under limited circumstances, a civil penalty not to exceed two times a plaintiff’s actual damages.  (Civ. Code, §§ 1793.2, subd. (d); 1794; see 3AC, ¶¶ 37–54.)  Furthermore, there are no specific factual allegations that GM’s conduct was oppressive, fraudulent, or malicious; allegations of fraud are pleaded only generally.  (See Today’s IV, Inc., supra, 83 Cal.App.5th at 1193, requiring specific factual allegations to support punitive damages; see, e.g., 3AC, ¶¶ 56, 63, 65, 67, 81, pleading concealment in general terms.)

Therefore, the court GRANTS the motion to strike punitive damages from the Third Amended Complaint.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the demurrer is SUSTAINED WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND, and the motion to strike is GRANTED.

Defendant General Motors LLC is ordered to file an ANSWER in ten (10) days.

Case Number: 25PSCV02398    Hearing Date: June 25, 2026    Dept: G

Plaintiff Cynthia Romero’s Motion for Sanctions for Failure to Comply with Initial Disclosure and Document Exchange

Respondent: Defendant Hyundai Motor America

TENTATIVE RULING

Plaintiff Cynthia Romero’s Motion for Sanctions for Failure to Comply with Initial Disclosure and Document Exchange is GRANTED.

Monetary sanctions are AWARDED in the amount of $2,500.00, payable within fifteen (15) days of the issuance of this order.

BACKGROUND

This is a Song-Beverly action arising out of the purchase of an allegedly defective motor vehicle.  On October 21, 2023, plaintiff Cynthia Romero (Romero) bought a car manufactured by defendant Hyundai Motor America (HMA).

On June 30, 2025, Romero filed the Complaint, alleging a sole cause of action for breach of express warranty in violation of the Song-Beverly Act.

On April 13, 2026, Romero filed this motion for sanctions.  On June 9, 2026, HMA filed the opposition, and on June 17, 2026, Romero filed the reply.

This case is set for an informal discovery conference and a hearing on the motion on June 25, 2026.

ANALYSIS

Romero moves for sanctions based on HMA’s untimely compliance with the initial disclosure and document exchange requirements of the Song-Beverly Act. 

For the following reasons, the court GRANTS the motion.

Legal Standard

In 2024, California reformed the Song-Beverly Act to reduce court congestion and facilitate early resolution by mandating initial disclosures and requiring the parties to participate in mediation shortly after the pleadings are filed.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26, subd. (b).)  The Song-Beverly Act requires parties to exchange initial disclosures and documents within 60 days of the filing of an answer or other responsive pleading, and to participate in mediation within 150 days.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26, subds. (b), (d)–(e).)  Until that mediation is concluded, all other discovery is expressly stayed, aside from the two-hour depositions and the required disclosures.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26, subd. (e).)  Manufacturers may affirmatively opt into the statutory scheme.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 871.30, subd. (a).)

Under the scheme, a vehicle buyer must provide to the vehicle manufacturer the sales or lease agreement, current registration, finance information, repair orders, pre-suit communications with the vehicle manufacturer, current mileage, primary drivers, vehicle location and possession, collision or accident history, and aftermarket modifications.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26, subds. (f)–(g).)  A vehicle manufacturer must provide to the vehicle buyer all vehicle-specific manuals, warranties, sales or lease documents, technical and service bulletins, warranty and service histories, internal communications, and policies or evaluations related to restitution or replacement requests.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26, subd. (h).)  The documents and information must be produced without awaiting a discovery request and without the need for a protective order.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26, subd. (b).)

The scheme also gives the parties the right to conduct an initial two-hour deposition of the other.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26, subd. (c).)  While the statute requires the initial document exchange to occur within 60 days of the responsive pleading and without a discovery request, the statute permits the initial deposition within 120 days.  (Ibid.)  Since the statute does not specify that depositions are required without a discovery request, it requires that a party only participate in the initial deposition when properly noticed.

If a vehicle buyer fails to comply and cannot show good cause, then the court must impose a $1,500 sanction against their attorney for failure to timely produce the documents, payable within 15 business days.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26, subd. (j)(1).)  If a vehicle manufacturer fails to comply and cannot show good cause, then the court must impose a $2,500 sanction against their attorney for failure to timely produce the documents, payable within 15 business days.  (Ibid.)  If a vehicle buyer’s noncompliance is repeated, then the court must dismiss the action without prejudice and award costs to the vehicle manufacturer.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26, subd. (j)(3).)  If a vehicle manufacturer’s noncompliance is repeated, then the court must impose an evidentiary sanction precluding them from introducing evidence regarding whether the motor vehicle had a nonconformity that substantially impaired the use, value, or safety of the motor vehicle, or whether the motor vehicle was repaired to match the written warranty after a reasonable number of opportunities to do so.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26, subd. (j)(4).)

Discussion

Romero argues that HMA failed to timely comply with its obligations under the Song-Beverly Act.  The court agrees.

The Song-Beverly Act requires parties to exchange initial disclosures and documents within sixty (60) days of the filing of an answer.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26, subd. (b).)  Failure to comply results in a $1,500 monetary sanction against a vehicle buyer’s attorney and a $2,500 monetary sanction against a vehicle manufacturer’s attorney.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26, subd. (j)(1).)

HMA filed the Answer on February 4, 2026, so its initial disclosure and document exchange was due on April 6, 2026.  (See Ans., p. 1.)  According to HMA, it complied with the initial disclosure and document exchange on May 22, 2026.  (See Knighton Decl., ¶ 8.)  HMA contends that it had good cause to delay initial disclosures and document exchange because “court-side production had not commenced at the time the Answer was filed.”  (Opp., p. 2, citing Knighton Decl., ¶ 7.) 

However, the documents that a vehicle manufacture must produce, such as manuals, warranties, bulletins, histories, policies, and evaluations, are largely not case-specific.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26, subd. (h).)  A vehicle manufacturer that opts into the initial disclosure scheme of Section 871 should already have these documents and be prepared to provide these documents almost immediately.  Regarding documents that are case-specific, the statute gives the parties sixty (60) days after a responsive pleading is filed to provide those documents.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26, subd. (b).)  The court finds a period of sixty (60) days is sufficient time to provide the vehicle buyer with their sales and/or lease documents and any other documents that are case-specific. 

HMA does not explain why it could not locate and produce these documents within sixty (60) days.  (See generally Knighton Decl., ¶¶ 6–8.)  Furthermore, even if Romero never asked HMA about initial disclosures before the deadline, the statute requires compliance regardless of any party’s request for discovery.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26, subd. (b).)  Since there was no good cause for the failure to comply with the initial disclosure requirement by the deadline, sanctions are mandatory.

Therefore, Plaintiff’s motion for sanctions is GRANTED.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the motion for sanctions for failure to comply with initial disclosure and document exchange is GRANTED.

Counsel for defendant Hyundai Motor America is ORDERED to PAY MONETARY SANCTIONS under Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26, subdivision (j)(1) in the total amount of $2,500.00, payable within fifteen (15) business days of the issuance of this order.

Case Number: 25PSCV04388    Hearing Date: June 25, 2026    Dept: G

Plaintiff American Express National Bank’s Applications for Default Judgment

Respondent: NO OPPOSITION

TENTATIVE RULING

Plaintiff American Express National Bank’s Application for Default Judgment is GRANTED.

BACKGROUND

This is an action for breach of contract.  On December 8, 2025, plaintiff American Express National Bank (American Express) filed the Complaint, alleging a sole cause of action for breach of contract.

On May 1, 2026, American Express filed the first request for entry of default and application for default judgment.  The court entered default against defendant Christian J Simpson (Simpson) and rejected default against defendant Retro Recipes LLC (Retro Recipes).  On May 29, 2026, American Express filed the second request for entry of default and application for default judgment.  The court entered default against Retro Recipes and rejected default against Simpson (because it previously entered default against him).

The applications are set for hearing on June 25, 2026.

LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure section 585 permits entry of a default judgment after a party has filed to timely respond or appear.  A party seeking judgment on the default by the court must file a Request for Court Judgment, and: (1) a brief summary of the case; (2) declarations or other admissible evidence in support of the judgment requested; (3) interest computations as necessary; (4) a memorandum of costs and disbursements; (5) a declaration of nonmilitary status; (6) a proposed form of judgment; (7) a dismissal of all parties against whom judgment is not sought or an application for separate judgment under Section 579, supported by a showing of grounds for each judgment; (8) exhibits as necessary; and (9) a request for attorneys’ fees if allowed by statute or by the agreement of the parties.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1800.)

ANALYSIS

As a preliminary matter, the court notes that there are two applications for default judgment, one filed May 1, 2026 and another filed on May 29, 2026.  (See 5/1/2026 CIV-100, p. 1, requesting entry of default and applying for court judgment; see also 5/29/2026 CIV-100, p. 1, requesting entry of default and applying for court judgment.)  Therefore, to avoid confusion, the court DENIES the May 1, 2026 application for default judgment WITHOUT PREJUDICE and considers only the May 29, 2206 application.

American Express seeks default judgment against Simpson and Retro Recipes (collectively, Defendants) in the total amount of $79,868.78, including $79,113.78 demanded in the Complaint and $755.00 in costs.  (See 5/29/2026 CIV-100, ¶ 2.)  American Express submitted sufficient evidence to prove its damages.  (See McCarter Decl., ¶¶ 1–14; see also McCarter Decl., Exhs. A–B.)  Therefore, the court GRANTS the May 29, 2026 application for default judgment.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the May 1, 2026 application for default judgment is DENIED WITHOUT PREJUDICE, and the May 29, 2026 application for default judgment is GRANTED.
