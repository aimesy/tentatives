ANALYSIS

 

                          I.          PROCEDURAL ISSUES

 

“T]he perfecting of an appeal stays proceedings in the trial court upon the judgment or order appealed from or upon the matters embraced therein or affected thereby, including enforcement of the judgment or order[.]”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 916, subd. (a).)

 

“The purpose of the automatic stay provision of section 916, subdivision (a) is to protect the appellate court's jurisdiction by preserving the status quo until the appeal is decided. The [automatic stay] prevents the trial court from rendering an appeal futile by altering the appealed judgment or order by conducting other proceedings that may affect it.”  (Varian Medical Systems, Inc. v. Delfino (2005) 35 Cal.4th 180, 189 [cleaned up].)  Specifically, “the trial court's power to enforce, vacate or modify an appealed judgment or order is suspended while the appeal is pending.”  (Id. at pp. 189–190.)

 

Here, Plaintiff appealed the subject Judgment on August 20, 2026. 

 

                        II.          DISCRETIONARY AND MANDATORY RELIEF

 

“Proceeding to judgment in the absence of a party is an extraordinary and disfavored practice in Anglo–American jurisprudence:  The policy of the law is to have every litigated case tried upon its merits, and it looks with disfavor upon a party, who, regardless of the merits of the case, attempts to take advantage of the mistake, surprise, inadvertence, or neglect of his adversary.”  (Au-Yang v. Barton (1999) 21 Cal.4th 958, 963 [cleaned up].) 

 

Code of Civil procedure section 473 “includes a discretionary provision, which applies permissively, and a mandatory provision, which applies as of right.” (Minick v. City of Petaluma (2016) 3 Cal.App.5th 15, 25 (hereafter Minick).)  “Section 473 is a remedial statute to be “applied liberally” in favor of relief if the opposing party will not suffer prejudice.  Because the law strongly favors trial and disposition on the merits, any doubts in applying section 473 must be resolved in favor of the party seeking relief from default.  Unless inexcusable neglect is clear, the policy favoring trial on the merits prevails.”  (Minick, supra, 3 Cal.App.5th at p. 24 [cleaned up].) 

 

The party or the legal representative must seek such relief “within a reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months, after the judgment, dismissal, order, or proceeding was taken.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b); see Rappleyea v. Campbell (1994) 8 Cal.4th 975, 980 [“because more than six months had elapsed from the entry of default, and hence relief under section 473 was unavailable”]; People v. The North River Ins. Co. (2011) 200 Ca.App.4th 712, 721 [motion for relief under section 473 must be brought “within a reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months”]).  “The six-month limit is mandatory; a court has no authority to grant relief under section 473, subdivision (b), unless an application is made within the six-month period.”  (Arambula v. Union Carbide Corp. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 333, 340, citations omitted.) 

 

A.    DISCRETIONARY RELIEF

 

Per Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b), a court may “relieve a party or his or her legal representative from a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against him or her through his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect.”

 

B.    MANDATORY RELIEF

 

Notwithstanding any other requirements of this section, the court shall, whenever an application for relief is made no more than six months after entry of judgment, is in proper form, and is accompanied by an attorney's sworn affidavit attesting to his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect, vacate any (1) resulting default entered by the clerk against his or her client, and which will result in entry of a default judgment, or (2) resulting default judgment or dismissal entered against his or her client, unless the court finds that the default or dismissal was not in fact caused by the attorney's mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect.

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b).)  “In considering whether the trial court properly denied relief under section 473(b), the first question is the sufficiency of defendants' showing of attorney fault, if believed, to trigger the mandatory relief provisions of that statute.”  (Standard Microsystems Corp. v. Winbond Electronics Corp. (2009) 179 Cal.App.4th 868, 896, disapproved on other grounds by Even Zohar Construction & Remodeling, Inc. v. Bellaire Townhouses, LLC (2015) 61 Cal.4th 830 (hereafter Standard).)  “Under section 473(b), a party is entitled to relief from a default and resulting judgment whenever, on timely application for relief, his attorney ‘attest[s] to his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect’ in connection with the default or the judgment.”  (Ibid.) 

 

Because Plaintiff is self-represented, there is no attorney mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect warranting mandatory relief. 

 

Rather, Plaintiff seeks discretionary relief on the grounds that “mistake, inadvertence, surprise, and excusable neglect materially impaired Plaintiff’s ability at the April 21, 2026 demurrer hearing to marshal and articulate additional allegations responsive to the Court’s curability inquiry[.]”  (Motion at p. 2.)

 

In support, Plaintiff advances his own declaration, which provides as follows:

 

3. When I prepared for the April 21, 2026 hearing, I understood my principal task to be defending the legal sufficiency of the allegations already contained in my First Amended Complaint (‘FAC’). The FAC asserted 18 causes of action and consisted of more than 140 pages of allegations, exclusive of approximately 50 pages of exhibits.

 

4. Immediately preceding the April 21 hearing, I was simultaneously managing numerous overlapping obligations without counsel and while proceeding on a fee waiver. Among other matters, I was addressing alternative-service proceedings beginning around April 1, an application concerning expanded briefing on or around April 3, the April 8 demurrer opposition deadline, continuing service diligence, and additional filings on April 18 concerning completion of service diligence and clarification of Defendants' characterization of my knowledge and suspicions.

 

5. I was also experiencing substantial financial hardship and attempting to preserve basic income while litigating this action against numerous represented Defendants. The volume and timing of the proceedings required me to learn unfamiliar procedural requirements while simultaneously researching, drafting, serving, and filing substantial materials.

 

6. During this same period, I experienced unusual and disturbing occurrences at my residence that substantially affected my concentration and sleep. I do not represent in this Declaration that those occurrences were caused by, connected with, or attributable to any Defendant, and I do not offer them for that purpose. I state them solely because they were circumstances actually affecting my mental concentration, sleep, and ability to prepare during the period immediately preceding the April 21 hearing.

 

7. By April 20 and 21, the combination of overlapping litigation obligations, financial hardship, personal circumstances, impaired concentration, and minimal sleep had left me substantially overwhelmed.

 

8. Although an April 20 Tentative Ruling addressed statute-of-limitations issues, under those circumstances I did not adequately review, absorb, and reorganize my preparation around the dispositive framework reflected in that tentative before appearing the following morning. My failure to do so was not intentional and was not a tactical decision to disregard the Court's analysis. I was attempting to manage numerous immediate obligations while operating with substantially impaired concentration and insufficient rest.

 

9. Consequently, I arrived at the April 21 hearing prepared primarily to defend the sufficiency of what I had already pleaded. I had not prepared a separate inventory of additional allegations that could be added to cure deficiencies if the Court determined the existing pleading insufficient.

 

10. During the hearing, I realized that the Court's inquiry was materially different from the presentation for which I had prepared. I became nervous and had difficulty retrieving, organizing, and articulating from memory additional factual allegations accumulated across years of events and a lengthy pleading.

 

11. When asked what additional allegations I could provide, I stated in substance that I had not prepared or re-memorized those portions of the FAC and requested an opportunity to review it. I did not intend that statement as a representation that no additional facts existed, that amendment was impossible, or that I was abandoning an opportunity to amend. To the contrary, I was attempting to communicate that I needed an opportunity to identify and organize the responsive material rather than improvise it inaccurately.

 

12. My inability to marshal that material spontaneously at the hearing was therefore a failure of preparation and presentation under the circumstances described above, not an intentional concession concerning the existence of additional allegations.

 

(Ouaaz Decl. ¶¶ 3-12.)

 

            Plaintiff’s proffered mistake, inadvertence, surprise, and/or excusable neglect was failing to adequately prepare for and respond to the Court’s questioning at the April 21 hearing due to (1) a lengthy operative complaint (which Plaintiff drafted) and (2) competing demands for Plaintiff to serve the dozens of named defendants, exacerbated by Plaintiff’s financial hardship and unspecified incidents at his residence affecting his sleep and concentration.

 

            Plaintiff also lists facts he could add to the complaint, which demonstrate additional reasons why Plaintiff contemporaneously rationalized away the behavior giving rise to his complaint instead of diligently investigating and pursuing his claims:

 

Based upon what I observed and what was communicated to me, I understood those developments as ordinary creative and production decisions rather than information revealing unlawful conduct directed toward me.  […]

 

16. I likewise did not understand the relative inaccessibility of certain visiting producers to mean that they were avoiding me because of concealed wrongdoing. Production was fast-moving and hierarchical, and my contemporaneous experience with cast members, crew members, and others included substantial positive professional interactions.

 

17. I had requested removal of the Keffiyeh used with my character, and that request was denied. At the time, I understood the denial to reinforce that the symbolism was wanted or approved as part of the creative conception of a politically conscious Muslim rapper.  […]

 

18. Nor did I understand the fact that my series-regular option was not exercised as evidence of wrongdoing. At the time, I understood that producers and networks routinely retain discretion concerning whether characters are expanded, performers return, or contractual options are exercised. […]

 

20. Those circumstances reinforced my contemporaneous understanding that an option or initial publicity did not guarantee promotion, continuation, or series-regular status. I therefore did not interpret my own non-pickup as naturally revealing discrimination, retaliation, blacklisting, or another concealed unlawful motive.

 

21. After my production engagement concluded, Defendants Rodriguez and Hamri did not meaningfully engage with my subsequent outreach. At that time, I attributed this to ordinary entertainment-industry considerations. I understood that my character had been given an opportunity that did not develop into the level of audience exposure or momentum I had hoped for, and I believed the production had simply moved forward.

(Ouaaz Decl. ¶¶ 15-22.)

 

            Plaintiff then explains why these facts were not originally included in the FAC:

 

I therefore made a deliberate, good-faith effort to focus an already lengthy complaint principally upon conduct I attributed to the named Defendants.

 

25. I did not include every surrounding communication, production circumstance, interaction with cast or crew, comparison involving another performer, or other contextual fact known to me. I understood many such matters as secondary or tertiary context rather than material allegations of wrongdoing attributable to Defendants. […]

 

28. (Ouaaz Decl. ¶¶ 24-25, 28.)

 

            Plaintiff further explains that he did not fully appreciate the importance of articulating new facts until after the April 21 hearing, the April 30 Order, and the June 10 proceeding:

 

29. Only after the April 21 hearing and the April 30 Order did I more fully appreciate the significance the Court placed upon those circumstances and upon my inability at the hearing to identify additional allegations spontaneously.

 

30. Accordingly, the preexisting contextual facts identified in my Motion and proposed amendments are not facts I recently invented or intentionally withheld. They are matters whose significance to the Court's dispositive framework I did not adequately understand or marshal at the April 21 hearing.

32. After the April 30 Order, I continued reviewing the FAC, the underlying communications, and the Court's reasoning to determine what additional allegations and materials could address the deficiencies identified by the Court. […]

 

THE JUNE 10 PROCEEDING CLARIFIED THE IMPORTANCE OF THE EARLIER PRESENTATION FAILURE

 

(Ouaaz Decl. ¶¶ 29-32 and p. 30:703-704.)

 

            The Court does not find Plaintiff has adequately demonstrated mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect warranting vacation of the judgment or the April 30 ruling upon which that judgment is based. 

 

            “[W]hen a litigant is self-represented, a judge has the discretion to take reasonable steps, appropriate under the circumstances and consistent with the law and the canons, to enable the litigant to be heard.” (See also ABA Model Code of Jud. Conduct, canon 2, rule 2.2, com. 4 [“[i]t is not a violation of this Rule [regarding impartiality and fairness] for a judge to make reasonable accommodations to ensure pro se litigants the opportunity to have their matters fairly heard”].) The canons and commentary thus provide a path to ensure a self-represented litigant can be fairly heard on the merits while the court maintains its impartiality and does not assume (or appear to assume) the role of advocate or partisan. (See Cal. Code Jud. Ethics, canon 3 [“a judge shall perform the duties of judicial office impartially, competently, and diligently”].)”  (Holloway v. Quetel (2015) 242 Cal.App.4th 1425, 1434.)

 

            Here, the Court did exactly that by repeatedly asking Plaintiff during the April 21 hearing if he could articulate any additional facts that could be added to the complaint to cure the deficiencies identified by the Court.  Rather than answer the question being asked of him or request a continuance to prepare such information, in light of Plaintiff’s apparent sleep issues and other personal problems, Plaintiff simply repeated that the FAC already adequately alleged sufficient facts to state each cause of action. 

 

            Other than giving Plaintiff numerous additional opportunities at the hearing to demonstrate why he should be given an opportunity to re-plead his claims, despite his initial failures to do so in written opposition to the motion and in his initial oral argument presentation,  “[a] party proceeding in propria persona ‘is to be treated like any other party and is entitled to the same, but no greater[,] consideration than other litigants and attorneys.’” (Rappleyea v. Campbell (1994) 8 Cal.4th 975, 985.)  Indeed, “‘the in propria persona litigant is held to the same restrictive rules of procedure as an attorney.’” [Citation.]” (First American Title Co. v. Mirzaian (2003) 108 Cal.App.4th 956, 958, fn. 1; see also Nwosu v. Uba (2004) 122 Cal.App.4th 1229, 1246-1247.)

 

            As applicable to  an attorney, Plaintiff is required to manage the complexities of the lengthy complaint he drafted and the competing demands of service and motion practice pertaining to his case,.  Further, to the extent Plaintiff was physically or mentally unable to advocate for himself at the April 21 hearing, he was obligated to timely alert the Court of that fact and request a hearing continuance, just like any attorney would do so.

 

            Plaintiff’s failure to adequately prepare to address all issues raised in the tentative ruling, or to timely alert the Court of his inability to argue at the April 21 hearing do not constitute mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect warranting discretionary relief from the resulting ruling and judgment.

 

            Further, the additional facts Plaintiff now proffers demonstrate only additional reasons why Plaintiff failed to diligently pursue his claims within the applicable statutes of limitations.  They do not form any basis to toll the statute of limitations.  As such, Plaintiff has not demonstrated the mistaken, inadvertent, or neglectful failure to articulate facts that would have made any difference in the Court’s April 30 ruling.

 

            This point is underscored by the fact that Plaintiff himself admits he did not fully appreciate the legal significance of what the Court was asking of him until after the June 30 proceedings.  But Plaintiff’s ignorance of the law is also not mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect warranting relief from the judgment.  “Mistake is not a ground for relief under section 473, subdivision (b), when ‘the court finds that the ‘mistake’ is simply the result of professional incompetence, general ignorance of the law, or unjustifiable negligence in discovering the law. . . .’” (Hearn v. Howard (2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 1193, 1206, citing 8 Witkin, Cal. Procedure (5th ed. 2008) Attack on Judgment in Trial Court, § 155, p. 749.)

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

 

            Plaintiff’s motion to vacate the judgment is denied because the Court lacks jurisdiction to grant it while Plaintiff’s appeal is pending and because Plaintiff has failed to demonstrate any mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect warranting discretionary relief. 

 

            Plaintiff shall provide notice of the Court’s ruling and file the notice with a proof of service forthwith.

ANALYSIS

 

1.     MOTION TO STRIKE

 

Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading, may serve and file a motion to strike the whole pleading or any part thereof.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 435, subd. (b)(1); Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1322, subd. (b).)  On a motion to strike, the court may: (1) strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or (2) strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of California, a court rule, or an order of the court.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a)-(b); Stafford v. Shultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782.) 

 

a.     Punitive Damages

 

In ruling on a motion to strike punitive damages, “judges read allegations of a pleading subject to a motion to strike as a whole, all parts in their context, and assume their truth.”  (Clauson v. Superior Court (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1253, 1255.)  To state a prima facie claim for punitive damages, a plaintiff must allege the elements set forth in the punitive damages statute, Civil Code section 3294.  (College Hosp., Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 721.)  Per Civil Code section 3294, a plaintiff must allege that the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice.  (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (a).)   As set forth in the Civil Code,

 

(1) “Malice” means conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.  (2) “Oppression” means despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person's rights.  (3) “Fraud” means an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known to the defendant with the intention on the part of the defendant of thereby depriving a person of property or legal rights or otherwise causing injury.

 

(Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (c)(1)-(3), emphasis added.) 

 

Further, a plaintiff must assert facts with specificity to support a conclusion that a defendant acted with oppression, fraud or malice.  To wit, there is a heightened pleading requirement regarding a claim for punitive damages.  (See Smith v. Superior Court (1992) 10 Cal.App.4th 1033, 1041-1042.)  “When nondeliberate injury is charged, allegations that the defendant’s conduct was wrongful, willful, wanton, reckless or unlawful do not support a claim for exemplary damages; such allegations do not charge malice.  When a defendant must produce evidence in defense of an exemplary damage claim, fairness demands that he receive adequate notice of the kind of conduct charged against him.” (G. D. Searle & Co. v. Superior Court (1975) 49 Cal.App.3d 22, 29 [cleaned up].)  In Anschutz Entertainment Group, Inc. v. Snepp, the Court of Appeal noted that the plaintiffs’ assertions related to their claim for punitive damages were “insufficient to meet the specific pleading requirement.”  (Anschutz Entertainment Group, Inc. v. Snepp (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 598, 643 [plaintiffs alleged “the conduct of Defendants was intentional, and done willfully, maliciously, with ill will towards Plaintiffs, and with conscious disregard for Plaintiff's rights. Plaintiff's injuries were exacerbated by the malicious conduct of Defendants. Defendants' conduct justifies an award of exemplary and punitive damages”]; see also Grieves v. Superior Court (1984) 157 Cal.App.3d 159, 166 [“The mere allegation an intentional tort was committed is not sufficient to warrant an award of punitive damages.  Not only must there be circumstances of oppression, fraud, or malice, but facts must be alleged in the pleading to support such a claim”].) 

 

Here, Plaintiffs allege:

Defendants have further engaged in fraudulent manipulation of utility equipment and utility charges for the subject Unit, with the result of forcing Plaintiff to pay utility charges for some/all other tenants where Plaintiffs have no such obligation under the lease or the law. In so doing, Plaintiffs have incurred significant expense caused by the Defendants’ illegal misconduct.

As a result of Defendants’ failure to repair and maintain the Subject Property, in a reasonable safe condition, in that they failed to properly maintain the property, Plaintiffs endured slum-type living conditions resulting in financial loss, personal injury and emotional distress.

            Plaintiffs premise their request for punitive damages on (1) allegations made “on information and belief” that prior tenants and/or government officials previously informed Defendants of the unhabitable conditions; (2) unspecific allegations that Plaintiffs complained about the uninhabitable conditions on numerous occasions, yet Defendants refused to remediate the issue; and (3) allegations that Feig misrepresented to Plaintiffs that the premises were habitable and/or concealed the uninhabitable conditions of the property in or about July 2024 when Defendants leased the property to Plaintiffs.

 

            As for the allegations made on information and belief that prior tenants and/or government officials previously informed Defendants about the uninhabitable conditions, allegations made “on information and belief” are generally insufficient to satisfy heightened pleading requirements “unless the facts upon which the belief is founded are stated in the pleading.”  (Dowling v. Spring Valley. Water Co. (1917) 174 Cal.218, 221.)  Here, Plaintiffs have not stated the specific facts underlying their “information and belief” that Defendants were previously made aware of the uninhabitable conditions by prior tenants or by government officials.

 

            Regarding the second category of allegations—that Plaintiffs repeatedly complained about the uninhabitable conditions, yet Defendants refused to remediate the problem—Plaintiffs have not alleged the specific who, what, when, or how of these communications to allege prior knowledge based on Plaintiffs’ own complaints with requisite specificity to withstand demurrer.  Further, these generalized, conclusory allegations are inconsistent with Plaintiffs’ other allegations that Plaintiffs themselves did not become aware of the causal connection between their health issues, which began around August 2024, and the allegedly uninhabitable conditions of the property until June 5, 2025, which was around the same time they vacated the premises. (FAC ¶¶ 31, 36.)  If Plaintiffs themselves were unaware of how dangerous the condition of the property was, then it would have been impossible for them to convey that information to Defendants.

 

            Finally, Plaintiffs’ fraud allegations fail for the same reasons.  Although Plaintiffs’ allege the who (Feig), what (that the premises were free from mold and toxic contamination), and when (July 2024 and February 2025) of the alleged misrepresentations, Plaintiffs do not adequately allege with requisite specificity the basis for Feig’s purported knowledge that the representations were false when made.  Further, Plaintiffs do not connect the alleged misrepresentations made by Feig to Larocca, other than the conclusory allegations in paragraphs 27-28 that Defendants are agents, servants, employees, partners, alter egos, and/or joint venturers of each other with a unity of interest and ownership. 

 

            Therefore, the Court finds that Plaintiffs have not alleged oppression, fraud, or malice, with requisite specificity to support the claim for punitive damages against Defendants.

 

b.     Attorneys’ Fes

 

            Plaintiffs seek attorneys’ fees pursuant to the private attorney general doctrine of Civil Code section 1021.5 and pursuant to Civil Code section 1942.4, subdivision  (b)(2), which provides that if a landlord collects rent on premises in uninhabitable conditions, under certain circumstances, the prevailing tenant may recoup their reasonable attorneys’ fees and costs, and pursuant to Civil Code section 1717 and the underlying lease agreements.

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 1021.5 provides:

 

Upon motion, a court may award attorneys’ fees to a successful party against one or more opposing parties in any action which has resulted in the enforcement of an important right affecting the public interest if: (a) a significant benefit, whether pecuniary or nonpecuniary, has been conferred on the general public or a large class of persons, (b) the necessity and financial burden of private enforcement, or of enforcement by one public entity against another public entity, are such as to make the award appropriate, and (c) such fees should not in the interest of justice be paid out of the recovery, if any. 

 

            Defendants argue that this case, which involves a single unit in a single property will not confer a significant benefit on the general public or a large class of persons, notwithstanding the FAC’s allegation that any judgment or settlement will impact, “the homeowners and tenants who live in the area around the Subject Property[.]”  (FAC ¶ 84.)  In opposition, Plaintiffs merely direct the Court to paragraph 84.

 

            The Court agrees with Defendants that Plaintiffs have not alleged a large class of persons will benefit from an action primarily, if not entirely concerning the habitability of a single unit.

 

            Civil Code section 1942.4 provides:

 

(a) A landlord of a dwelling may not demand rent, collect rent, issue a notice of a rent increase, […] if all of the following conditions exist prior to the landlord’s demand or notice:

 

(1) The dwelling substantially lacks any of the affirmative standard characteristics listed in Section 1941.1 or violates Section 17920.10 of the Health and Safety Code, or is deemed and declared substandard as set forth in Section 17920.3 of the Health and Safety Code because conditions listed in that section exist to an extent that endangers the life, limb, health, property, safety, or welfare of the public or the occupants of the dwelling.

 

(2) A public officer or employee who is responsible for the enforcement of any housing law, after inspecting the premises, has notified the landlord or the landlord’s agent in writing of his or her obligations to abate the nuisance or repair the substandard conditions.

 

(3) The conditions have existed and have not been abated 35 days beyond the date of service of the notice specified in paragraph (2) and the delay is without good cause. For purposes of this subdivision, service shall be complete at the time of deposit in the United States mail.

 

(4) The conditions were not caused by an act or omission of the tenant or lessee in violation of Section 1929 or 1941.2.

 

(b) (1) A landlord who violates this section is liable to the tenant or lessee for the actual damages sustained by the tenant or lessee and special damages of not less than one hundred dollars ($100) and not more than five thousand dollars ($5,000).

 

(2) The prevailing party shall be entitled to recovery of reasonable attorney’s fees and costs of the suit in an amount fixed by the court.

 

(Civil Code, § 1942.4.)  Here, Plaintiffs allege:

 

97. In or about July 2024, through June 2025, Plaintiffs have complained to Defendants about the poor condition of the premises, as stated above.

 

98. At all relevant times, Plaintiffs timely paid the rent, and Defendants collected without fail, despite the Premises’ substantial defects.

 

99. Plaintiffs are informed and believe that the County of Los Angeles and/or City of Los Angeles has inspected and Defendants’ defective premises, and some time after visiting the premises, public officer housing law inspectors provided written notices to the Defendants of their obligation to repair the prohibited conditions. These notices, on the dates listed on the notices/code enforcement orders concerning The Property, were issued as alleged herein. The defective conditions were not caused by any act or omission of Plaintiffs.

 

100. Plaintiffs are informed and believe that Defendants failed to abate or repair the conditions identified in the Code enforcement orders within 35 days of receiving those notices, during which time the substandard conditions continued to exist without abatement.

 

101. Even after Defendants were notified by the County and/or City inspector that repairs were needed to the Subject Property, Defendants failed to make timely repairs. Defendants continued to demand and collect rent from the Plaintiffs while the prohibited conditions identified by housing authorities were unabated.

 

 

(Complaint ¶¶ 97-100.)

 

            When alleging noncompliance with a statute, allegations made on information and belief are only proper where the allegations involve matters peculiarly within the knowledge of the defendants and they are supported by a statement of facts upon which the belief is founded.  (See Doe v. City of Los Angeles (2007) 42 Cal.4th 531, 542.) 

 

            As discussed above, Plaintiffs have not alleged the factual basis underlying their belief that the City and/or County of Los Angeles visited the premises and issued written notices to the Defendants to repair, when those notices were issued, or that Defendants failed to repair within 35 days and then continued to collect rent.

 

            Civil Code section 1717 provides:

 

In any action on a contract, where the contract specifically provides that attorney’s fees and costs, which are incurred to enforce that contract, shall be awarded either to one of the parties or to the prevailing party, then the party who is determined to be the party prevailing on the contract, whether he or she is the party specified in the contract or not, shall be entitled to reasonable attorney’s fees in addition to other costs.

 

            Here, Plaintiffs allege:

 

142. The residential lease agreement between the parties contains a provision entitling the prevailing party to attorney’s fees and costs.

 

(FAC ¶ 142.)

 

            Defendants argue that the FAC fails to identify the contract provision authorizing recovery of attorneys’ fees. Notwithstanding, the FAC now adequately alleges that the residential lease agreement contains such a provision, and Plaintiffs seek attorneys’ fees pursuant to that provision.  Whether the lease agreement actually contains such a provision, or whether the provision covers the claims in this action, in whole or in part, are factual issues to be resolved at later stages of the litigation. 

 

            Therefore, the Court grants in part Defendants’ motion to strike the requests for attorneys’ fees pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure 1021.5 and Civil Code section 1942.4, but denies Defendants’ motion to strike the request for attorneys’ fees pursuant to the lease agreement and Civil Code section 1717.

 

2.     LEAVE TO AMEND

 

A plaintiff has the burden of showing in what manner the complaint could be amended and how the amendment would change the legal effect of the complaint, i.e., state a cause of action. (See The Inland Oversight Committee v. City of San Bernardino (2018) 27 Cal.App.5th 771, 779; PGA West Residential Assn., Inc. v. Hulven Int'l, Inc. (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 156, 189.) A plaintiff must not only state the legal basis for the amendment, but also the factual allegations sufficient to state a cause of action or claim. (See PGA West Residential Assn., Inc. v. Hulven Int'l, Inc., supra, 14 Cal.App.5th at p. 189.) Moreover, a plaintiff does not meet his or her burden by merely stating in the opposition to a demurrer or motion to strike that “if the Court finds the operative complaint deficient, plaintiff respectfully requests leave to amend.” (See Major Clients Agency v Diemer (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1116, 1133; Graham v. Bank of America (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 594, 618 [asserting an abstract right to amend does not satisfy the burden].)

 

Here, Plaintiffs have failed to meet this burden as they do not specify what additional facts could be added to cure the deficiencies identified above. 

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

 

For the reasons stated, the Court grants in part Defendants’ motion and strikes Plaintiffs’ allegations and requests for punitive damages without leave to amend and without prejudice.

 

Further, the Court strikes Plaintiffs’ allegations and requests for attorneys’ fees to the extent they are premised upon Code of Civil Procedure 1021.5 and/or Civil Code section 1942.4 without leave to amend and without prejudice, but denies Defendants’ request to strike Plaintiff’s requests for attorneys’ fees to the extent they are premised upon the lease agreement and/or Civil Code section 1717.
