BACKGROUND

 

This case arises from allegations that cement used in Plaintiff’s knee replacement surgery became dislodged, causing her pain and disability. 

 

On November 4, 2024, Plaintiffs Judith Seymour (“Judith”) and Eugene Seymour (“Eugene”) (together, “Plaintiffs”) brought suit against Defendants Kevin M. Ehrhart, M.D.; Cedars-Sinai Kerlan-Jobe Institute; Cedars-Sinai Surgery Center of the Pacific; and Cedars-Sinai Health System (“Defendants”), alleging four causes of action for medical malpractice (against each named Defendant); (5) elder abuse; and (6) loss of consortium.  The first five causes of action are alleged by Judith and the sixth cause of action for loss of consortium is alleged by her husband, Eugene. 

 

On June 23, 2025, Plaintiff dismissed the fifth cause of action for elder abuse via oral motion. 

 

Defendants now move for summary judgment on the grounds that Plaintiffs cannot establish breach or causation.  Plaintiffs oppose the motion and Defendants reply.

 

LEGAL STANDARD – MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT

 

“[T]he party moving for summary judgment bears the burden of persuasion that there is no triable issue of material fact and that he is entitled to judgment as a matter of law[.] There is a triable issue of material fact if, and only if, the evidence would allow a reasonable trier of fact to find the underlying fact in favor of the party opposing the motion in accordance with the applicable standard of proof.”  (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850.)  “[T]he party moving for summary judgment bears an initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing of the nonexistence of any triable issue of material fact; if he carries his burden of production, he causes a shift, and the opposing party is then subjected to a burden of production of his own to make a prima facie showing of the existence of a triable issue of material fact.”  (Ibid.)  

 

“On a summary judgment motion, the court must therefore consider what inferences favoring the opposing party a factfinder could reasonably draw from the evidence. While viewing the evidence in this manner, the court must bear in mind that its primary function is to identify issues rather than to determine issues.  Only when the inferences are indisputable may the court decide the issues as a matter of law. If the evidence is in conflict, the factual issues must be resolved by trial.”  (Binder v. Aetna Life Ins. Co. (1999) 75 Cal.App.4th 832, 839 [cleaned up].)  Further, “the trial court may not weigh the evidence in the manner of a factfinder to determine whose version is more likely true.  Nor may the trial court grant summary judgment based on the court's evaluation of credibility.”  (Id. at p. 840 [cleaned up]; see also Weiss v. People ex rel. Department of Transportation (2020) 9 Cal.5th 840, 864 [“Courts deciding motions for summary judgment or summary adjudication may not weigh the evidence but must instead view it in the light most favorable to the opposing party and draw all reasonable inferences in favor of that party”].)

DISCUSSION

 

1.     STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS

 

“In an action for injury […] against a health care provider based upon such person’s alleged professional negligence, the time for the commencement of action shall be three years after the date of injury or one year after the plaintiff discovers, or through the use of reasonable diligence should have discovered, the injury, whichever occurs first.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 340.5.)

 

Defendants argue Plaintiff’s claims are time barred because Judith admitted in deposition that she could have suspected wrongdoing right after her April 22, 2022 knee surgery, making the deadline to file April 22, 2023, yet Plaintiffs did not file the instant lawsuit until March 9, 2026.

 

As a threshold matter, the Court does not read the excerpts from Judith’s deposition transcript the same way.  Rather, Judith testified that while she was frustrated that she was being instructed to slow down her physical activities during the initial post-operative visits following the surgery, yet her swelling and pain persisted, she nonetheless “was relying on the expertise of my surgeon.”  (See Plaintiffs’ Ex. at p. 36:1-19.)  Plaintiff further testified she did not begin to become concerned until six months after the surgery, when she saw her own healing lagging behind friends who had undergone similar surgeries.  (Id. at p. 42:25-43:17.)

 

Further, as Defendants point out in opposition, Dr. Ehrhart and Physician’s Assistant Katherine Nelson Pavlik repeatedly reassured Plaintiffs during the post-operative appointments that her symptoms (and the retained cement) were either normal or Judith’s own fault for overexerting herself.

 

The statute of limitations for professional negligence may be tolled beyond three years where there is intentional concealment or the presence of a foreign body, which has no therapeutic or diagnostic purpose or effect.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 340.5.)  Here, Dr. Ehrhart’s and Ms. Pavlik’s repeated post-operative assurances that there was no anomaly with regard to the surgery could be construed as intentional concealment, and the retained cement inside Judith’s joint itself could be considered a foreign body, warranting tolling.

 

Therefore, the Court cannot find that the professional negligence claim is barred as a matter of law.  There are triable issues of material fact underlying whether the statute of limitations is applicable.

 

2.     PROFESSIONAL NEGLIGENCE

 

“Civil Code section 1714, subdivision (a) establishes the general duty of each person to exercise, in his or her activities, reasonable care for the safety of others.  When applied to medical professionals, this duty of care imposes a duty to use such skill, prudence and diligence as other members of his profession commonly possess and exercise.”  (Flores v. Liu (2021) 60 Cal.App.5th 278, 290 [cleaned up].)  To prevail on a claim for negligence against a medical professional, a plaintiff must demonstrate that: (1) a medical professional had a duty to use the skill, prudence and diligence that members of the profession commonly possess and exercise; (2) breach of that duty; (3) an injury that resulted from the breach of that duty; and (4) actual loss or damage resulting from the breach of that duty.  (Banerian v. O’Malley (1974) 42 Cal.App.3d 604, 612.) 

 

“[T]he legal standard of care required by doctors is the standard of practice required by their own profession.  The courts require only that physicians and surgeons exercise in diagnosis and treatment that reasonable degree of skill, knowledge, and care ordinarily possessed and exercised by members of the medical profession under similar circumstances.  Thus, liability is not found, and the label of malpractice is not placed upon a physician's actions, unless some deviation by the physician from the standard of care that his peers consider appropriate in the situation under review is proven.” (Burgess v. Superior Court (1992) 2 Cal.4th 1064, 1081 [cleaned up].)

 

Expert testimony is generally the only admissible and relevant evidence on whether a medical professional has breached the standard of care.  (Landeros v. Flood (1976) 17 Cal.3d 399, 410 [“ ‘The standard of care against which the acts of a physician are to be measured is a matter peculiarly within the knowledge of experts; it presents the basic issue in a malpractice action and can only be proved by their testimony (citations), unless the conduct required by the particular circumstances is within the common knowledge of the layman’ ”].)  As the Court of Appeal has held, in reversing summary judgments for medical professionals:  “The standard of care against which the acts of a physician are to be measured is a matter peculiarly within the knowledge of experts; it presents the basic issue in a malpractice action and can only be proved by their testimony.  California courts have incorporated the expert evidence requirement into their standard for summary judgment in medical malpractice cases. When a defendant moves for summary judgment and supports his motion with expert declarations that his conduct fell within the community standard of care, defendant is entitled to summary judgment unless the plaintiff comes forward with conflicting expert evidence.”  (Hanson v. Grode (1999) 76 Cal.App.4th 601, 606-607 [cleaned up].)  Further, a plaintiff “is entitled to all favorable inferences that may reasonably be derived from” an expert’s declaration which must be liberally construed.  (See Fernandez v. Alexander (2019) 31 Cal.App.5th 770, 782.)  

 

a.     Moving Defendants’ Evidence        

 

In support of the motion, Moving Defendants advance the expert declaration of Donald B. Longjohn, a board-certified orthopaedic surgeon who has been licensed to practice medicine in since 1996.  Dr. Longjohn is also an Assistant Professor in the Department of Orthopaedic Surgery at the Keck School of Medicine, the Chief of the Surgical Arthritis Service and Rancho Los Amigos National Rehabilitation Center, and a Physician Specialist at Los Angeles General Medical Center.  (Longjohn Decl. ¶ 2.)  Dr. Longjohn is familiar with the standard of care for knee arthroplasty procedures, like that alleged by Plaintiff.  (Longjohn Decl. ¶ 3.) 

 

In forming his opinions, Dr. Longjohn reviewed the complaint as well as Judith’s medical records from Surgery Center of the Pacific, Cedars Sinai, UCLA Health Systems, Roaring Fork PT, Rite Aid, Providence St. John, Sports Fit & PT, Providence Medical Institute, Steadman Clinic, and Aspen Valley Hospital, as well as Plaintiffs’ responses to Defendants’ written discovery requests and the deposition transcripts for the depositions of both Plaintiffs.  (Longjohn Decl. ¶ 4.)

 

In his review of the materials, Dr. Longjohn’s understanding of the basic facts are:  after exhausting a series of injection treatments that provided only mild relief, Judith, who was then 76 years old, underwent a right knee medial unicompartmental arthroplasty with robotic assistance, performed by Dr. Ehrhart on April 22, 2022.  (Longjohn Decl. ¶ 5 (a)-(i).)  Following the procedure, Judith’s pain level improved somewhat, but some pain persisted, especially as she continued working out every day, ultimately leaving Judith to feel disappointed with the procedure.  (Longjohn Decl. ¶ 5(j)-(bb).) 

 

A right knee arthroscopy with synovectomy and loose body removal performed by Dr. Lee on February 9, 2024 revealed retained cement along the tibial tray and two fragments in the posterior joint measuring 10 x 4 x 8 mm and 12 x 6 x 2 mm.  (Longjohn Decl. ¶ 5(qq).)

 

Based on his review of the materials and understanding of the facts, Dr. Longjohn declared:

           

6. Based upon my review of the materials in this case, and further based upon my education, training and experience, it is my opinion that the care provided to JUDITH SEYMOUR by Defendants were all within the standard of care.

 

7. As a preliminary matter, CEDARS-SINAI KERLAN-JOBE INSTITUTE and CEDARSSINAI HEALTH SYSTEM their nurses, residents, and non-physician staff did not breach the standard of care. Plaintiff’s allegations are related to an operation and post-operative care, including an alleged delay in diagnosis. These allegations are related to actions of a physician and not of CEDARS-SINAI KERLAN-JOBE INSTITUTE and CEDARS-SINAI HEALTH SYSTEM their nurses, residents, and non-physician staff.

 

8. There was no breach in the standard of care by Defendant CEDARS-SINAI SURGERY CENTER OF THE PACIFIC. The only day that Plaintiff was seen at CEDARS-SINAI SURGERY CENTER OF THE PACIFIC was the date of the operation. There are no allegations that any of the nurses, residents or non-physician staff at CEDARS-SINAI SURGERY CENTER OF THE PACIFIC breach the standard of care. Rather, the allegations are related to the operation performed by Dr. Erhart. Dr. Erhart is the surgeon and the captain of the ship. There was no breach of the standard of care related to nurses, residents or non-physician staff at CEDARS-SINAI SURGERY CENTER OF THE PACIFIC because they did not perform the operation. Rather, Dr. Erhart performed the operation. As discussed below, there was no breach in the standard of care by Dr. Erhart related to the operation.

 

9. There was no breach in the standard of care related to the decision of Dr. Ehrhart to perform the right knee medial unicompartmental arthroplasty with robotic assistance. The imaging on April 23, 2021, showed that the right knee medial joint space looked good, then on December 1, 2021, the joint space looked narrower, and finally then on March 1, 2022, the medial joint space had decreased further on right. These imaging studies coupled with the patient’s report of discomfort make the operation indicated. Thus, there was no breach in the standard of care related to the decision of Dr. Ehrhart to perform the right knee medial unicompartmental arthroplasty with robotic assistance.

 

10. There was no breach in the standard of care related to the performance of the right knee medial unicompartmental arthroplasty with robotic assistance by Dr. Ehrhart. Incidents of retained cement can be around 20% and can be retained absent negligence. The majority of these cases are asymptomatic with only a small number requiring surgery to remove cement fragments. With a partial knee, it is challenging to remove extruded cement because of the smaller incision which limits visibility and access to posterior extruded bone cement. There are tools to attempt to essentially blindly remove the cement, but it is challenging. In addition, there is a limited amount of time to do this since most of the extruded cement needs to removed before it hardens, leaving only a few minutes to perform the task. Thus, with a partial knee there is limited visibility and limited physical access to the back of the knee creating in essence a blind spot behind the tibial implant, and as such, it is not uncommon or a breach in the standard of care for some cement to remain. Further, according to Dr. Lee, he had to break up much of the cement, which might mean that the cement was adjacent to the bone implant interface rather than free floating. Adherent extruded cement flush along the edge of the implant at the interface may be difficult to visual and remove. Also, if this cement is fairly flush with the medial edge of the implant and bone, and is not loose or unstable, it may not cause any problems. Loose pieces of cement typically become a problem if they migrate into the joint causing impingement. Often these fragments remain in the posterior capsular recess and remain asymptomatic. Therefore, it is not a breach in the standard of care if there was cement retained.

 

11. As to the follow-up care, it was reasonable and within the standard of care for Dr. Erhart and/or Katherine Nelson Pavlik, PA to believe that overuse was causing pain and swelling. Katherine Nelson Pavlik, PA is a physician’s assistant and worked under the supervision of Dr. Erhart. The patient’s pain would come and go. The records clearly reflect that she was very active and when she rested, she would improve. Finally, Katherine Nelson Pavlik, PA ordered imaging studies to determine if there was another source of the complaints. The results of the x -ray do not look consistent with how the symptoms presented. The little piece of cement on the x-ray was posterior but the pain she was reporting was more medial. On the x-ray, the little piece of cement was not big, but she was doing a lot. She was biking and running almost daily. In addition, she was walking the dog and working out. When she decreased activities, she was better. Thus, based on the clinical presentation, the patient’s reports, and the imaging studies, it was reasonable and within the standard of care for Dr. Erhart and/or Katherine Nelson Pavlik, PA to believe that the patient’s symptoms were related to overuse. Therefore, there was no breach in the standard of care. In addition, there was a plan for an MRI if the symptoms did not improve. By the 3/1/2023 office visit the symptoms and appearance of the knee had improved so an MRI was not obtained. Dr. Ehrhardt did not discharge the patient from his care but asked her to followup with him in 3 months. The patient did not return to Dr. Erhart but instead chose to be evaluated at the Steadman Clinic.

 

12. It is my opinion that, to a reasonable degree of medical probability, there was no act or omission on the part of the nurses, non-physician staff or residents at CEDARS-SINAI SURGERY CENTER OF THE PACIFIC, CEDARS-SINAI HEALTH SYSTEM, and CEDARS-SINAI KERLANJOBE INSTITUTE that caused or contributed to JUDITH SEYMOUR’s alleged injuries. In sum, based upon my education, training and experience, as well as my review of the materials outlined above, there was nothing that the nurses, non-physician staff or residents at CEDARS-SINAI SURGERY CENTER OF THE PACIFIC, CEDARS-SINAI HEALTH SYSTEM, and CEDARS-SINAI KERLAN-JOBE INSTITUTE did, or failed to do, that caused JUDITH SEYMOUR’s alleged injuries. The allegations in this matter are related to surgical and attending physician care and not that of the nurses, non-physician staff or residents.

 

13. It is my opinion that, to a reasonable degree of medical probability, there was no act or omission on the part of Dr. Erhart and/or Katherine Nelson Pavlik, PA that caused or contributed to JUDITH SEYMOUR’s alleged injuries. To a reasonable degree of medical probability, fragments of retained acrylic bone cement are usually do not cause symptoms. In sum, based upon my education, training and experience, as well as my review of the materials outlined above, there was nothing that Dr. Erhart and/or Katherine Nelson Pavlik, PA did, or failed to do, that caused JUDITH SEYMOUR’s alleged injuries.

 

(Longjohn Decl. ¶¶ 6-13.)

 

Therefore, Defendants have met their initial burdens of production and persuasion to demonstrate that (1) none of the named Defendants breached the applicable standard of care and (2) none of the named Defendants caused or contributed to Judith’s alleged injuries. 

 

b.     Plaintiff’s Evidence 

 

Having met their initial burdens, the burden shifts to Plaintiffs to create a triable issue of material fact.  Plaintiffs advance the expert Declaration of Jason Brustein, D.O., a board-certified orthopaedic surgeon, who completed his residency in Pittsburgh 2019, fellowship in New Jersey in 2020, and currently practices as an orthopaedic surgeon in McDonough, Georgia.  (Ex. A to Brustein Decl.)

 

Dr. Brustein reviewed Plaintiff’s medical records from Kerlan-Jobe and the surgery center, Steadman Clinic, Compass Peak Imaging, Dr. Jared T. Lee, as well as Plaintiffs’ depositions and Dr. Longjohn’s declaration.

 

Dr. Brustein points out that Dr. Ehrhart’s surgical report from the April 22, 2022 surgery indicates, “[a]ll excess acrylic was removed” and that cement was visible on the x-rays taken on October 7, 2022, yet Dr. Ehrhart’s progress notes do not mention the cement, that Dr. Ehrhart improperly treated Judith with steroid injections, and that after a follow-up examination on March 1, 2023, Dr. Ehrhart inaccurately diagnosed Plaintiff with “tertiary synovitis.”  (Brustein Decl. ¶¶ 8, 12-15, 20-21.)   Dr. Brustein further opines:

 

28. There is no acceptable reason for leaving behind visible or retrievable cement fragments within the joint space. This is particularly true here where there were many pieces, some of which measured more than three quarters of an inch.

 

29. While minimal microscopic residue may occur, retained macroscopic cement fragments are not an acceptable outcome and fall below the standard of care.

 

30. In this case, retained cement fragments were left within the joint following surgery.

 

31. These fragments were not microscopic or trivial-they were sizable and visible on imaging.

 

32. The presence of such retained cement establishes that:

 

• The cement was not adequately visualized and removed intraoperatively; and/or

 

• The joint was not properly irrigated to remove residual cement debris

 

33. Because the operative field is fully visible during cementing and cleanup, these fragments should have been identified and removed before closure. That is required by the standard of care.

 

34. The failure to remove these cement fragments constitutes a clear breach of the standard of care.

 

35. In addition to the intraoperative breach, Defendants failed to properly diagnose the cause of Plaintiffs postoperative symptoms.

 

36. Postoperative x-rays demonstrated retained cement within the knee joint.

 

37. At the same time, imaging showed:

 

• Well-positioned prosthetic components

 

• No fractures

 

• No dislocations

 

• No degenerative abnormalities

 

38. In this clinical context, the most conspicuous and medically probable cause of Plaintiff's ongoing pain and swelling was the retained cement. Plaintiff testified that Dr. Ehrhart acknowledged to her when be drained the knee that the blood fluid removal indicated that something was irritating the knee but that he did not see anything on the x-rays. Clearly the cement was on the x-rays and thus Dr. Ehrhart failed to properly diagnose the cause of Plaintiffs irritated knee despite having all the information he needed to do so.

 

39. Despite this, Dr. Ehrhart repeatedly told Plaintiff that her symptoms were due to activity level or overuse.

 

40. This conclusion fell below the standard of care because it ignored objective imaging findings and the known mechanical effects of intra-articular cement.

 

41. This misdiagnosis delayed appropriate treatment and prolonged Plaintiffs suffering.

 

42. In addition, the Cortisone injection is strongly discouraged in patient such as Plaintiff and not likely to result in benefit to the patient.

 

43. Retained intra-articular cement is well known to cause:

 

• Mechanical irritation

 

• Synovitis and inflammation

 

• Persistent swelling

 

• Pain with motion

 

• Joint dysfunction

 

44. Plaintiffs symptoms-particularly posterior medial and posterior lateral knee pain and swelling-are entirely consistent with mechanical irritation from retained cement.

 

45. No amount of rest, activity modification, or conservative care could resolve symptoms caused by a retained foreign body within the joint.

 

46. Plaintiffs continued pain persisted until the cement was surgically removed.

 

47. The subsequent identification and removal of cement by Dr. Anderson and Dr. Lee confirm that the retained cement was the true source of Plaintiffs symptoms.

 

48. It is my opinion, to a reasonable degree of medical probability, that:

 

• The retained cement was a substantial factor in causing Plaintiffs pain and dysfunction; and

 

• The delay in diagnosis prolonged her pain and suffering unnecessarily

 

49. As a result of Defendants' breaches of the standard of care, Plaintiff suffered:

 

• Prolonged pain and swelling

 

• Loss of function

 

• Delay in appropriate treatment

 

• Need for additional diagnostic workup

 

• Requirement of revision surgery

 

50. These injuries were avoidable had the standard of care been met both during surgery and in postoperative evaluation.

 

(Brustein Decl. ¶¶ 28-50.)

 

            Thus, Plaintiffs have met their burden of production to create triable issues of material fact as to whether Defendants Kevin M. Ehrhart, M.D. and Cedars Sinai Surgery Center of the Pacific breached the applicable standard of care by leaving retained cement inside the surgery site and whether the acts/omissions of those Defendants caused or contributed to Judith’s injuries.

 

            As for Defendants Cedars-Sinai Kerlan-Jobe Institute and Cedars-Sinai Health System, Plaintiffs explained at the hearing that their theory of liability against these Defendants is vicarious, on the ground that Plaintiff’s post-surgical medical records, including the one where Physician’s Assistant Katherine Nelson Pavlik told Plaintiffs that having retained cement in the joint is normal, all identify Cedars-Sinai Kerlan-Jobe (Plaintiffs’ Exhibit 5), and Defendants’ response to Form Interrogatory No. 3.6 demonstrates that Cedars-Sinai Kerlan-Jobe is a dba for Cedars-Sinai Health System (see Plaintiffs’ Exhibit 4.)

 

            Thus, the Court denies summary adjudication as to all Defendants.

 

3.     LOSS OF CONSORTIUM

 

Because Eugene’s sixth cause of action for loss of consortium is premised entirely upon Judith’s knee surgery injury, it rises or falls with the others.  As such, the Court similarly denies summary adjudication of Eugene’s sixth cause of action for loss of consortium as to all Defendants. 

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

 

Finding Plaintiffs have met their burdens of production to create triable issues of material fact as to whether Dr. Ehrhart of Cedars-Sinai Surgery Center of the Pacific and/or Physician’s Assistant Katherine Nelson Pavlik of Cedars-Sinai Kerlan-Jobe dba Cedars-Sinai Health System breached the applicable standard(s) of care or caused or contributed to Judith’s injuries (and Eugene’s resulting loss of consortium) by leaving retained cement inside the joint during surgery and representing to Plaintiffs after the surgery that the retained cement was normal, the Court denies Defendants’ motion for summary judgment.

 

Counsel for Defendants shall provide notice of the Court’s Order/ruling, and file the notice with a proof of service forthwith.

Monetary Sanctions

BACKGROUND

 

This case arises from allegations that Defendant failed to repair or replace a vehicle it sold to Plaintiff in violation of the Song-Beverly Act. 

 

On October 21, 2025, Plaintiff Mehrdad Dayani (“Plaintiff”) filed suit against Defendant Kia America, Inc. (“Defendant”) alleging three causes of action for violations of the Song-Beverly Act.

 

Plaintiff now moves for monetary sanctions in the amount of $2,500 against Defendant’s counsel of record pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26, subdivision (j)(2) for failure to produce a witness to testify to the categories as set forth in Section 871.26, subdivision (i), within 120 days of filing its Answer, in violation of Section 871.26, subdivision (c).

 

Defendant opposes the motion and Plaintiff replies.

 

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

 

            Plaintiff requests judicial notice of the fact that Defendant elected to opt-in to the procedures for manufacturers pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 871.20 through 871.30, as evidenced by the official list published online by the Department of Consumer Affairs.

 

            Judicial notice may be taken of official acts of the legislative, executive, and judicial departments of the United States and of any state of the United States.  (Evid. Code, § 452, subd. (c). 

 

            Thus, the Court may take judicial notice of the fact that Defendant appears on the Department of Consumer Affairs’ official list of manufacturers who have opted in to the procedures outlined in Code of Civil Procedure sections 871.20 through 871.30 as an official act, but not the absolute truth of the contents of that list.

 

ANALYSIS   

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26, subdivision (c) provides:  “Within 120 days after the filing of the answer or other responsive pleading, all parties have the right to conduct initial depositions, each not to exceed two hours, of […] the person who is most qualified to testify on the defendant’s behalf.  This deposition shall be limited to the topics listed in subdivision (i).” 

 

Subdivision (j) provides, “Unless the party failing to comply with this section shows good cause, notwithstanding any other law and in addition to any other sanctions imposed pursuant to this chapter, a court shall impose […] a one-thousand-five-hundred dollar ($1,500) sanction against the plaintiff’s attorney or two-thousand-five-hundred-dollar ($2,500) sanction against the defense attorney respectively, paid within 15 business days for failure to comply with the provisions relating to depositions as prescribed in subdivision (c).” 

 

Here, Defendant’s Answer was filed on December 22, 2025.  One hundred and twenty (120) days thereafter was April 21, 2026. 

 

On December 23, 2025, Plaintiff noticed Defendant’s deposition for March 17, 2026.  (Greco Decl. ¶¶ 2-3 and Ex. 1.)  Additionally, Plaintiff attempted to meet and confer regarding the deposition date.  (Greco Decl. ¶ 5 and Ex. 2.)

 

On March 12, 2026, Defendant electronically served objections to the deposition notice that included objections to the noticed date.  (Greco Decl. ¶ 5 and Ex. 3.)

 

On April 24, 2026—three days after the statutory deadline—Defendant agreed to produce its witness for deposition on May 18, 2026.  (Greco Decl. at ¶ 6 and Ex. 4.)  Accordingly, Plaintiff served a new notice for that date.  (Greco Decl. at ¶ 7 and Ex. 5.)

 

On May 18, 2026, Plaintiff’s counsel deposed the witness Defendant produced, David Sakadjian, who is not an employee of Defendant’s, but rather a contract employee through a company called Advantage Technical who merely reviewed a series of documents Defendant had provided him.  (Greco Decl. ¶ 8.)  As a result, according to Plaintiff, Sakadjian was unable to answer basic questions within the categories enumerated in Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26, subdivision (i), including:

 

·       whether any warranty claims had been submitted and denied,

 

·       whether any repairs had been made during the vehicle’s prior ownership,

 

·       whether any repairs were conducted as part of the process to certify the pre-owned vehicle for re-sale,

 

·       the results of the certification inspection,

 

·       whether additional information regarding repairs was communicated to Defendant after October 30, 2025,

 

·       how specific software applied to the vehicle would address Plaintiff’s concerns,

 

·       a description of the “open campaign” referenced in Defendant’s document production,

 

·       whether that campaign was performed,

 

·       whether Defendant had access or information regarding diagnostic trouble codes for the vehicle from its proprietary tool.

 

(Greco Decl. ¶ 8.)

 

In opposition, Defendant argues that Plaintiff has failed to demonstrate that Defendant lacked good cause, and because Defendant produced a witness, Plaintiff’s quarrel is with the sufficiency of the witness, not Defendant’s outright failure to comply with the statute.

 

Defendant also argues that its witness did in fact testify as to the various categories above that Plaintiff said he was unable to testify about. 

 

Specifically, Defendant points out Sakadjian testified about the warranty coverage and claims as follows:

 

Q: On this document here, is there any information related the CPO warranty?

 

A: Yes.

 

Q: All right.  Where do I find that?

 

A: I will see there is an area marked CPO, and there’s a Y, then a contract number associated with it.

 

[…]

 

Q: […] can you tell me what the document is we’re looking at on page 1?

 

A: This document is a list of the warranty claims associated with the vehicle.

 

Q: Do you know if there have been any additional warranty claims since April 8, 2025? […]

 

A: Based on the documentation I have reviewed, they speak for themselves.  If I’m correct, it would be these only.

 

[…]

            However, Sakadjian was unable to testify about repairs during the car’s prior ownership or during the certified pre-owned vehicle inspection process, although he testified about the certified pre-owned vehicle inspection process generally.  (See Ex. 6 at pp. 34:3-35:3.)  Further, although Sakadjian was unable to testify from personal knowledge whether Plaintiff’s vehicle received an over-the-air update, he indicated that there would be specific documentation in the vehicle’s repair history about that.  (Ex. 6 at pp. 26:25-27:5.)

 

Defendant also argues Sakadjian’s inability to testify about events occurring after October 30, 2025 was a reflection of the scope of records he reviewed, not his incompetence as a witness.

 

Ultimately, the record does not demonstrate that Defendant failed to comply with the statutory requirements in bad faith.  The evidence demonstrates Plaintiff’s counsel did not follow up about re-scheduling the PMK deposition after it received Defendant’s timely objections until the afternoon of April 23—two days after the statutory deadline—to which Defendant’s counsel responded the very next morning, offering the May 18 deposition date.  (Ex. 4 to Greco Decl.)  Thus, the initial failure to timely conduct a PMK deposition appears to be Plaintiff’s fault, not Defendant’s.

 

Further, as demonstrated above, Defendant’s PMK did substantially testify at the May 18 deposition about the majority of the categories Plaintiff claims he did not.   For those questions he was unable to definitively answer, he nonetheless pointed Plaintiff to where the answers to those questions would appear in the documents. 

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

 

            Therefore, finding the evidence does not demonstrate that Defendant failed to comply with the requirements of Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26, subdivision (c), the Court denies Plaintiff’s motion for sanctions.

MOTION

Motion to Serve Cross-Complaint by Publication

MOVING PARTY

Defendant and Cross-Complainant Peninsula Escrow, Inc.

OPPOSING PARTY

none

 

MOTION

 

            This lawsuit arises from a dispute over real property. 

 

            On November 7, 2025, Plaintiff Dorothea Schiro (“Plaintiff”) filed suit against Defendants Commonwealth Land Title Insurance Company (“Commonwealth”); Peninsula Escrow, Inc. (“Peninsula”); Giancarlo Marcaccini (“Marcaccini”); and Nourmand and Associates (“Nourmand”) (together, “Defendants”) alleging six causes of action for (1) quiet title/declaratory relief; (2) constructive trust and accounting; (3) conversion; (4) negligence/negligent misrepresentation; and (6) fraud and aiding & abetting.

 

            On January 30, 2026, Peninsula cross-complained in interpleader against Cross-Defendants Ocean Towers Housing Corp. (“Ocean Towers”); John Spahi, individually and as trustee of the John Spahi Family Trust Dated August 1, 2012 (“Spahi”); Gelato Trust (2015) (“Gelato”); and Dorothea Schiro (“Schiro”) (together, “Cross-Defendants”).

 

            Peninsula now moves to serve Ocean Towers and Spahi by publication.  The motion is unopposed.  Notwithstanding, the Court notes that a noticed hearing on an Application for Publication is unnecessary.  (See Rios v. Singh (2021) 65 Cal.App.5th 871, 883 [“an application for an order of publication of the summons may be made at an ex parte hearing”].) 

 

LEGAL STANDARD

 

“In the absence of a voluntary submission to the authority of the court, compliance with the statutes governing service of process is essential to establish that court's personal jurisdiction over a defendant.”  (Dill v. Berquist Construction Co. (1994) 24 Cal.App.4th 1426, 1439–1440.)  “In order to obtain in personam jurisdiction through any form of constructive service there must be strict compliance with the requisite statutory procedures.  (Zirbes v. Stratton (1986) 187 Cal.App.3d 1407, 1417, quoting Stamps v. Superior Court (1971) 14 Cal.App.3d 108, 110.) 

 

For service on persons within California, generally, service of summons and complaint must be done by personal service.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 415.10.)  However, “[i]f a copy of the summons and complaint cannot with reasonable diligence be personally delivered to the person to be served,” a plaintiff may serve an individual defendant “by leaving a copy of the summons and complaint at the person's dwelling house, usual place of abode, usual place of business, or usual mailing address . . . , in the presence of a competent member of the household or a person apparently in charge of his or her office, place of business, or usual mailing address . . . , at least 18 years of age, who shall be informed of the contents thereof, and by thereafter mailing a copy of the summons and of the complaint by first-class mail, postage prepaid to the person to be served at the place where a copy of the summons and complaint were left.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 415.20, subd. (b).) 

 

A state may also exercise jurisdiction over an individual who consents to such jurisdiction.  (Nobel Floral, Inc. v. Pasero (2003) 106 Cal.App.4th 654, 658.)  “Express consent to a court's jurisdiction will occur by generally appearing in an action or by a valid forum-selection clause designating a particular forum for dispute resolution regardless of residence.”  (Ibid. [cleaned up].)  “Consent to a court's jurisdiction may also be implied by conduct.”  (Ibid.)  For example, a nonresident plaintiff who commences an action thereby submits to that court’s personal jurisdiction on any cross-complaint filed against him by the defendant.”  (Ibid.)  “Similarly, a nonresident plaintiff who has filed a suit in California against particular parties has consented to jurisdiction in California when these same parties later sue him in a related action.”  (Sea Foods Co., Ltd. v. O.M. Foods Co., Ltd. (2007) 150 Cal.App.4th 769, 786, as modified (May 23, 2007).)  “By choosing a particular forum, [the] plaintiff is considered to have voluntarily submitted to the court's jurisdiction ‘for all purposes for which justice to the defendant requires his presence.”  (Ibid.)

 

A summons may also be served within the state by mailing the summons and complaint with a notice and acknowledgment of receipt and a return envelope, postage prepaid, addressed to the sender.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 415.30, subd. (a).)  Such service is deemed complete on the date the written acknowledgement of receipt of summons is executed, if such acknowledgment is thereafter returned to the sender.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 415.30, subd. (c).)  If the person to whom the summons, complaint, and notice are mailed fails to complete and return the acknowledgement within 20 days, that person shall be liable for reasonable expenses thereafter incurred in serving or attempting to serve them by another method.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 415.30, subd. (d).)

 

Failing all that, “[a] summons may be served by publication if upon affidavit it appears to the satisfaction of the court in which the action is pending that the party to be served cannot with reasonable diligence be served in another manner specified in this article[.]”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 415.50, subd. (a).)  But “[i]f a defendant's address is ascertainable, a method of service superior to publication must be employed, because constitutional principles of due process of law, as well as the authorizing statute, require that service by publication be utilized only as a last resort.”  (Watts v. Crawford (1995) 10 Cal.4th 743, 749, fn. 5.)

 

Finally, “if a plaintiff, despite exercising reasonable diligence, has been unable to effect service of the summons by any of the methods authorized under this chapter, the court in which the action is pending may, upon motion, direct that summons be served in a manner that is reasonably calculated to give actual notice to the party to be served, including by electronic mail or other electronic technology, and that proof of such service be made as prescribed by the court.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 413.30, subd. (a)(1).)  “A plaintiff seeking to establish reasonable diligence under this section shall set forth facts detailing all attempts to serve the defendant by each of the methods prescribed by statute, including facts demonstrating why each method was unsuccessful at every address or location where the defendant is likely to be found.

 

ANALYSIS

 

Peninsula seeks to serve Cross-Defendants Ocean Towers and Spahi by publication. 

 

Concerning Ocean Towers, the Court finds the motion to be procedurally defective.  A party may not serve the summons and complaint on a corporation or limited liability company by publication.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 416.10; Corp. Code, § 17701.16; see also Weil & Brown, Cal. Practice Guide:  Civil Procedure Before Trial (The Rutter Group 2026) ¶¶ 4:135 et seq., pp. 21 et seq.)     

 

Concerning Spahi, Peninsula advances Declarations of Diligence, which provide as follows:  After 3 attempts, the process server obtained access to Spahi’s residential apartment where a female occupant who refused to open the door told the process server that Spahi was not home.  (Ex. B to Latman Decl.)

 

            But the Court does not find Peninsula has exhausted all reasonably diligent efforts to serve Cross-Defendant Spahi.  As a threshold matter, there is no evidence that Peninsula attempted to serve Spahi by mail at Spahi’s last known address with a notice and acknowledgment of receipt.  Further, the response from the female occupant of Spahi’s residence suggests that that address is current, yet there is no evidence that Peninsula attempted to serve him by mail at that address.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

 

For the reasons stated, the Court denies Peninsula’s motion to serve Cross-Defendants Ocean Towers Housing Corp. and John Spahi by publication. 

 

Peninsula shall provide notice of the Court’s ruling and file the notice with a proof of service forthwith.

26SMCV01179

MOTION

Motion to Strike Portions of First Amended Complaint

MOVING PARTIES

Defendants Brandon Michael Berges and Michael Berges

OPPOSING PARTY

Plaintiff Troy Cleckley

 

MOTION

 

This case arises from a motor vehicle collision.

 

Plaintiff Troy Cleckley (“Plaintiff”) filed the instant action on March 3, 2026 against Defendants Brandon Michael Berges (“Brandon”) and Michael Berges (“Michael”) (together, “Defendants”).  The operative First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) alleges two causes of action for (1) motor vehicle negligence and (2) general negligence.  

 

Defendants now move to strike the request for exemplary/punitive damages from the FAC.  Plaintiff opposes the motion and Defendants reply.[1]

 

ANALYSIS

 

1.     MOTION TO STRIKE

 

Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading, may serve and file a motion to strike the whole pleading or any part thereof.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 435, subd. (b)(1); Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1322, subd. (b).)  On a motion to strike, the court may: (1) strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or (2) strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of California, a court rule, or an order of the court.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a)-(b); Stafford v. Shultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782.) 

 

In ruling on a motion to strike punitive damages, “judges read allegations of a pleading subject to a motion to strike as a whole, all parts in their context, and assume their truth.”  (Clauson v. Superior Court (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1253, 1255.)  To state a prima facie claim for punitive damages, a plaintiff must allege the elements set forth in the punitive damages statute, Civil Code section 3294.  (College Hosp., Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 721.)  Per Civil Code section 3294, a plaintiff must allege that the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice.  (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (a).)   As set forth in the Civil Code,

 

(1) “Malice” means conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.  (2) “Oppression” means despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person's rights.  (3) “Fraud” means an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known to the defendant with the intention on the part of the defendant of thereby depriving a person of property or legal rights or otherwise causing injury.

 

(Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (c)(1)-(3), emphasis added.) 

 

Further, a plaintiff must assert facts with specificity to support a conclusion that a defendant acted with oppression, fraud or malice.  To wit, there is a heightened pleading requirement regarding a claim for punitive damages.  (See Smith v. Superior Court (1992) 10 Cal.App.4th 1033, 1041-1042.)  “When nondeliberate injury is charged, allegations that the defendant’s conduct was wrongful, willful, wanton, reckless or unlawful do not support a claim for exemplary damages; such allegations do not charge malice.  When a defendant must produce evidence in defense of an exemplary damage claim, fairness demands that he receive adequate notice of the kind of conduct charged against him.” (G. D. Searle & Co. v. Superior Court (1975) 49 Cal.App.3d 22, 29 [cleaned up].)  In Anschutz Entertainment Group, Inc. v. Snepp, the Court of Appeal noted that the plaintiffs’ assertions related to their claim for punitive damages were “insufficient to meet the specific pleading requirement.”  (Anschutz Entertainment Group, Inc. v. Snepp (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 598, 643 [plaintiffs alleged “the conduct of Defendants was intentional, and done willfully, maliciously, with ill will towards Plaintiffs, and with conscious disregard for Plaintiff's rights. Plaintiff's injuries were exacerbated by the malicious conduct of Defendants. Defendants' conduct justifies an award of exemplary and punitive damages”]; see also Grieves v. Superior Court (1984) 157 Cal.App.3d 159, 166 [“The mere allegation an intentional tort was committed is not sufficient to warrant an award of punitive damages.  Not only must there be circumstances of oppression, fraud, or malice, but facts must be alleged in the pleading to support such a claim”].) 

 

In Taylor v. Superior Court, the California Supreme Court held:  “We consider whether punitive damages are recoverable in a personal injury action brought against an intoxicated driver.  As will appear, we have concluded that the act of operating a motor vehicle while intoxicated may constitute an act of “malice” under section 3294 if performed under circumstances which disclose a conscious disregard of the probable dangerous consequences.”  (Taylor v. Superior Court (1979) 24 Cal.3d 890, 892 [cleaned up].)  The California high court further held that “[o]ne who voluntarily commences, and thereafter continues, to consume alcoholic beverages to the point of intoxication, knowing from the outset that he must thereafter operate a motor vehicle demonstrates, in the words of Dean Prosser, “such a conscious and deliberate disregard of the interests of others that his conduct may be called wilful or wanton.”  (Id. at p. 899.)  But the California high court also stated, “Although the circumstances in a particular case may disclose similar wilful or wanton behavior in other forms, ordinarily, routine negligent or even reckless disobedience of traffic laws would not justify an award of punitive damages.”  (Id. at pp. 899-900.) 

 

Here, Plaintiff alleges:

 

On May 12, 2024, at approximately 2:15 a.m., Plaintiff TROY CLECKLEY, an individual (“Plaintiff”) lawfully operated his motor vehicle westbound on the US-101, in Calabasas, California when he was the subject of a violent, high-velocity, multi-vehicle freeway collision. While Plaintiff lawfully traveled straight, Defendants BRANDON MICHAEL BERGES, an individual; MICHAEL BERGES, an individual; and DOES 1 TO 20, inclusive, (“Defendants”) made an unsafe turning movement, under the influence, and directly struck Plaintiff’s vehicle. The impact was so severe that Plaintiff’s vehicle was pushed into another innocent vehicle, driven by Rodd Danesh.

 

[…]

 

Plaintiff alleges that the conduct of Defendants were carried out with malice, oppression, and conscious disregard for the rights and safety of others, thereby entitling Plaintiff to an award of exemplary and punitive damages pursuant to California Civil Code section 3294.

 

Plaintiff is informed and believes, and thereon alleges, that prior to the subject incident, Defendants knowingly and voluntarily consumed alcoholic beverages to the point of intoxication. At the time Defendants consumed said alcohol, Defendants knew, or should have known, that they would thereafter operate a motor vehicle.

 

Plaintiff is further informed and believes, and thereon alleges, that Defendants thereafter operated a motor vehicle while under the influence of alcohol, despite knowing that their faculties were impaired, and that driving in such condition created a high probability of injury to others.

 

Defendants’ decision to consume alcohol to the point of intoxication, combined with the conscious decision to thereafter drive a motor vehicle, constitutes despicable conduct carried on with a willful and conscious disregard for the safety of others.

 

As a direct and proximate result of Defendants’ malicious and despicable conduct, Plaintiff suffered injuries and damages. Accordingly, Plaintiff seeks an award of punitive and exemplary damages in an amount sufficient to punish Defendants and deter similar conduct in the future. 

           

While voluntarily drinking past the point of intoxication, knowing from the outset that one has to operate a motor vehicle can constitute malice sufficient to state a claim for punitive damages, allegations made “on information and belief” are generally insufficient to satisfy heightened pleading requirements “unless the facts upon which the belief is founded are stated in the pleading.”  (Dowling v. Spring Valley. Water Co. (1917) 174 Cal.218, 221.)

 

Thus, excluding the allegations made “on information and belief,” the Complaint merely alleges that Defendants operated their vehicle in an unsafe manner, causing it to collide with Plaintiff’s at a high rate of speed, pushing it into another vehicle.  But this does not suffice to satisfy the heightened pleading requirement to allege malice.

 

As a threshold matter, it is unclear how two separate defendants both simultaneously drove the same vehicle into Plaintiff.  At a minimum, the Complaint lacks specific facts about who the intoxicated driver is alleged to be. 

 

Moreover, Plaintiff does not specify the specific factual information underlying his belief that the driver of the vehicle that collided with his was intoxicated at the time.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff cites to Dawes v. Superior Court (1980) 111 Cal.App.3d 82, where the appellate court held that intoxicated driving plus other circumstances showing a probable risk of injury, such as zig-zagging in and out of traffic at 65 miles per hour in a crowded beach recreation area, are sufficient to state a claim for punitive damages.

 

But Dawes does not cure the defects in Plaintiff’s allegations as noted above.  Further, although Plaintiff alleges Defendants’ vehicle struck Plaintiff’s at a high rate of speed, there are no allegations that Defendants were zig-zagging in and out of traffic, like in Dawes, or that it was a crowded recreation area.  As alleged, the accident could simply have involved a relatively ordinary (but unfortunate) lane change on the US-101 freeway.

 

2.     LEAVE TO AMEND

 

A plaintiff has the burden of showing in what manner the complaint could be amended and how the amendment would change the legal effect of the complaint, i.e., state a cause of action. (See The Inland Oversight Committee v. City of San Bernardino (2018) 27 Cal.App.5th 771, 779; PGA West Residential Assn., Inc. v. Hulven Int'l, Inc. (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 156, 189.) A plaintiff must not only state the legal basis for the amendment, but also the factual allegations sufficient to state a cause of action or claim. (See PGA West Residential Assn., Inc. v. Hulven Int'l, Inc., supra, 14 Cal.App.5th at p. 189.) Moreover, a plaintiff does not meet his or her burden by merely stating in the opposition to a demurrer or motion to strike that “if the Court finds the operative complaint deficient, plaintiff respectfully requests leave to amend.” (See Major Clients Agency v Diemer (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1116, 1133; Graham v. Bank of America (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 594, 618 [asserting an abstract right to amend does not satisfy the burden].)

 

Here, Plaintiff, in the memorandum of points and authorities in support of the opposition to the motion, has indicated that additional facts may be asserted to support a claim for punitive damages against Defendants.  As such, the Court will grant Plaintiff leave to amend.   

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

 

For the reasons stated, the Court grants Defendants’ motion to strike exemplary/punitive damages from the FAC with leave to amend.

 

Further, the Court orders Plaintiff to file and serve a second amended complaint, in conformity with the ruling, on or before July 28, 2026. 

 

Further, on the Court’s own motion, the Court continues the Case Management Conference from July 9, 2026 to October 9, 2026 at 8:30 A.M. in Department 207.  All parties shall comply with California Rules of Court, rules 3.722, et seq., regarding Initial and Further Case Management Conferences.  In particular, all parties shall adhere to the duty to meet and confer (Rule 3.724) and to the requirement to prepare and file Case Management Statements (Rule 3.725). 

 

Defendants shall provide notice of the Court’s ruling and file the notice with a proof of service forthwith.
