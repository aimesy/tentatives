ERIN
NYHAN, et al. v. FORD MOTOR COMPANY, et al. [25STCV19452]

 

DEFENDANT
FORD MOTOR COMPANY’S DEMURRER TO THE SECOND AMENDED COMPLAINT

 

MEET AND
CONFER: DEFECTIVE Ford’s
counsel declares that he attempted to meet and confer on at least three dates,
but Plaintiffs’ counsel did not respond. (Liu Decl., ¶ 3.)

 

BACKGROUND:
Lemon law action

 

TIMELINE:

 

6/1/2019: Plaintiffs Erin M. Nyhan and Damon W.
Nyhan (“Plaintiffs”) lease a 2019 Ford Explorer (the “Vehicle”), which was
manufactured and/or distributed by Defendant Ford Motor Company (“Ford”), from
non-party Future Ford. (SAC, Exh. A.) In connection with the lease, Plaintiffs enter
into a warranty contract with Ford regarding the Vehicle. Thereafter,

3/2/2022: Plaintiffs present the Vehicle to

failing to properly open. At pick up on March 22, 2022, the repair facility
represented that the Vehicle had been repaired.

 

3/29/2022: Plaintiffs present the Vehicle to Ford’s
repair facility with complaints regarding the driver door keypad, damaged

repair facility represented that the Vehicle had been repaired.

 

2/13/2024: Plaintiffs present the Vehicle to Ford’s
repair facility with complaints regarding multiple warning lights and
acceleration issues. At pick up the following day, the repair facility
represented that the Vehicle had been repaired.

 

6/30/2025: Plaintiffs file the Complaint. Plaintiffs
filed a First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) on 8/20/2025.[2]¿ The
operative Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”), filed 8/10/2026, alleges causes of
action for:

¿ 

1.  
Violation
of Civil Code § 1793.2, subd. (d)¿ 

2.  
Violation
of Civil Code § 1793.2, subd. (b)¿ 

3.  
Violation
of Civil Code § 1793.2, subd. (a)(3)¿ 

4.  
Fraudulent
Inducement-Concealment¿ 

 

8/19/2026: Ford files this Demurrer to the SAC,
which is followed by Plaintiffs’ Opposition (9/14/2026) and Ford’s Reply (9/17/2026).

 

TENTATIVE
RULING: DEFENDANT FORD MOTOR COMPANY’S DEMURRER TO THE SECOND AMENDED COMPLAINT
is OVERRULED in part and SUSTAINED with LEAVE TO AMEND in part.

 

DEMURRER

 

Ford
demurs to each cause of action asserted in the SAC on the grounds that
Plaintiffs fail to allege sufficient facts to state any cause of action against
Ford.

 

A.  
1st
through 3rd Causes of Action: Song-Beverly Act Claims — OVERRULED

 

Ford
argues Plaintiffs’ claims for violations of the Song-Beverly Act (“SBA”) are
time barred by the six-year statute of repose under Code of Civil Procedure section
871.21, subd. (b).[3] Under this
statute of repose, “an action covered by Section 871.20 shall not be brought
later than six years after the date of original delivery of the motor vehicle.”
(Code Civ. Proc., § 871.21, subd. (b).) It is ruling on Ford’s motion for
judgment on the pleadings as to the FAC, this Court previously determined that
each of Plaintiffs’ SBA claims are enumerated under section 871.20, and are
therefore governed by section 871.21, subd. (b). (Minute Order, 7/29/2026.) The
Court incorporates by reference its previous analysis by reference herein. (Ibid.)
Accordingly, Plaintiffs’ three SBA claims must have been brought within six
years “after the date of original delivery of the motor vehicle,” or otherwise
demonstrate that a tolling exception to the repose period applies. (Code Civ.
Proc., § 871.21, subds. (b)-(c).)

 

Here,
Plaintiffs allege they executed their warranty contract with Ford for their
Vehicle on or about June 1, 2019. (FAC, ¶ 7.) Although Plaintiffs still fail to
specifically identify when the Vehicle was delivered to them, it can be
inferred that the Vehicle was delivered on or around the same date as the
warranty contract. (Ibid.) Thus, absent tolling, the six-year deadline
to file the instant SBA claims would have expired on June 1, 2025.
Nevertheless, Plaintiffs did not file the Complaint in the instant lawsuit
until June 30, 2025, 29 days after the expiration of the six-year repose
period. (See Compl., generally.)

 

Notwithstanding,
section 871.21, subd. (c) provides limited tolling exceptions to the section
871.21, subd. (b) statute of repose, including that the six-year repose period
“shall be tolled” “[f]or the time the motor vehicle is out of service by reason
of repair for any nonconformity.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.21, subd. (c)(2).) In
the SAC, Plaintiffs have now added factual allegations clarifying that the
Vehicle was under repair with Ford’s authorized repair facilities on at least
five occasions between 2021 and 2024, totaling a combined period of more than
two months during which the Vehicle was out of service. (FAC, ¶¶ 20-24.) Assuming
the truth of these allegations, the Court finds that, for pleading purposes,
Plaintiffs have now alleged sufficient facts to show that the exception under
section 871.21, subd. (c)(2) applies to extend the repose period past the
filing date of June 30, 2025. Contrary to Ford’s argument, the Court disagrees
that Plaintiffs must expressly invoke the exception under section 871.21, subd.
(c)(2) or calculate the total number of days the Vehicle was out of service to
successfully invoke this exception here. The SAC contains sufficient facts to
reach these inferences and to give notice of the application of this tolling
exception, as Ford itself highlights by responding to the exception in its
motion. (Mot., at p. 12.)

 

Accordingly,
based on the application of the tolling exception under section 871.21, subd.
(c)(2), the Court finds that Plaintiffs’ SBA claims are not necessarily
time-barred under section 871.21, subd. (b). Therefore, Ford’s demurrer to the
first through third causes of action in the SAC is OVERRULED.

 

B.   
4th
Cause of Action: Fraudulent Inducement-Concealment — SUSTAINED with LEAVE TO AMEND

 

Ford
argues that Plaintiffs fails to state a claim for fraudulent
inducement-concealment[4]
because: (1) it is barred by the three-year statute of limitations, (2) it
fails to sufficiently plead the essential elements of fraud with the requisite
level of particularity, and (3) it is barred by the economic loss rule and
independent tort principle.

 

i.            
Statute
of Limitations

 

The
newly added factual allegations in the SAC suggest that Plaintiffs’ fraud claim
is not necessarily time barred. The three-year limitations period for fraud
does not begin to run until the aggrieved party suspects or should suspect that
her injury is caused by wrongdoing. (Code Civ. Proc. § 338, subd. (d); Brandon
G. v. Gray (2003) 111 Cal.App.4th 29, 35.) To invoke the delayed discovery
rule as a basis for tolling, “the complaint must allege (1) when the fraud was
discovered; (2) the circumstances under which it was discovered; and (3) that
the plaintiff was not at fault for failing to discover it or had no actual or
presumptive knowledge of facts sufficient to put him on inquiry.” (Community
Cause v. Boatwright (1981) 124 Cal.App.3d 888, 899; see Fox v. Ethicon
Endo-Surgery, Inc. (2005) 35 Cal.4th 797, 803.)

 

Here,
Plaintiffs now allege that they presented the Vehicle for repairs through at
least February 13, 2024, and on each occasion, Ford’s repair facility
represented that the Vehicle had been repaired and was working as designed.
(FAC, ¶¶ 20-24.) Plaintiffs allege that they did not discover Ford’s
concealment and wrongful conduct until after this time, despite diligence in
performing regular maintenance and seeking repairs. (Id. ¶¶ 25-27.) In
can reasonably be inferred that Ford’s repair facilities’ representations
regarding the Vehicle created a delay in Plaintiffs’ ability to discover Ford’s
purported fraud. Assuming the truth of Plaintiffs’ allegations that they did
not discover and did not have reason to discover Ford’s alleged fraud until on
or after February 13, 2024, the Court finds that Plaintiffs’ fraud claim did
not accrue for limitations purposes until February 13, 2024. Plaintiffs filed
their Complaint in this action on June 30, 2025, well before the expiration of
the three-year limitations period under Code of Civil Procedure section 338,
subd. (d).

 

ii.           
Heightened
Pleading Standard

 

Notwithstanding
the above, the Court finds that Plaintiffs still fail to allege the elements of
fraudulent inducement-concealment with the requisite level of specificity for
pleading fraud claims. Fraud, including concealment, must be pled specifically,
and “general and conclusory allegations do not suffice.” (Lazar v. Superior
Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 645.) “California courts apply the same
specificity standard to evaluate the factual underpinnings of a
fraudulent concealment claim at the pleading stage, even though the focus of
inquiry shifts to the unique elements of the claim.” (Rattagan v. Uber
Technologies, Inc. (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1, 43.) Additionally, “[t]he
requirement of specificity in a fraud action against a corporation requires the
plaintiff to allege the names of the persons who made the allegedly fraudulent
representations, their authority to speak, to whom they spoke, what they said
or wrote, and when it was said or written.” (Tarmann v. State Farm Mut.
Auto. Ins. Co. (1991) 2 Cal.App.4th 153, 157.)¿¿ 

 

Here,
Plaintiffs allege that Ford committed fraud by concealing the Vehicle’s Defects
and their associated safety risks from Plaintiffs at the time of the sale,
repair, and thereafter. (FAC, ¶¶ 76-79, 81, 83.) Not only did Ford never
disclose the Defects to Plaintiffs before or during their ownership of the
Vehicle, but Ford also failed to instruct its dealerships to disclose the
Defects to potential purchasers or lessees and failed to notify consumers,
dealerships, or auto-technicians of the Defects, thereby preventing efficient
diagnosis and repair. (Id. ¶¶ 92, 94.) Meanwhile, Ford continues to
represent that its vehicles were of “high quality” and trained its dealers to
“specifically tout the supposedly superior attributes of the 3.5L engine,
without ever mentioning” the Defects. (Id. ¶ 96.)

 

The
issue with these allegations is that Plaintiffs still fail to clarify the
source of the specific false representations and omissions made by or on behalf
of Ford during either the sale and/or service transactions with Plaintiffs in
particular or with respect to Plaintiffs’ specific Vehicle. While Plaintiffs
now identify where they leased the Vehicle (i.e. Future Ford of Concord),
Plaintiffs have still not alleged sufficient factual details describing the
nature or extent of the specific sales interactions between Ford and
Plaintiffs, such as whether and when Plaintiffs spoke to Ford’s sales
representatives, the names or positions of any such representatives (whether at
Future Ford or elsewhere), or personally reviewed other sources of information
about their specific Vehicle. (SAC, ¶ 9.) Plaintiffs vaguely allege that Ford
authorized Future Ford to “convey information from Ford to prospective
purchasers (including Plaintiffs) using methods that Ford directs such as
marketing brochures and floor displays” but they do not specifically describe the
content of the information conveyed to Plaintiffs by Future Ford in particular.
(Id. ¶ 10.) At most, Plaintiffs allege that they viewed “Ford’s
vehicle-specific window sticker” and submit a photograph of the
vehicle-specific window sticker which describes the 3.5L engine, but Plaintiffs
do not explain what should have been disclosed on the window-sticker or what
representations Ford’s representatives made about the scope of the information
presented in the window-sticker. (Id. ¶¶ 11, 15.) Likewise, while
Plaintiffs highlight Ford’s website and Ford’s “television, print, and other
media advertisements,” and vaguely allege that Plaintiffs “reviewed Defendant
Ford’s marketing and advertising materials,” Plaintiffs fail to specifically
connect these materials to Plaintiffs by alleging, for example, that Plaintiffs
personally reviewed the information on Ford’s website or identifying
which specific types of advertising materials Plaintiffs personally consulted,
when they reviewed such materials, and whether the advertisements discussed the
specific Vehicle model sought by Plaintiffs. (Id. ¶¶ 12-15.) Finally,
although Plaintiffs have now identified the dates on which they presented their
Vehicle for repairs, they have not identified the specific names of the
authorized repair facilities, the names of specific repair technicians acting
on behalf of Ford, or the scope of the repair facilities’ authority to make
representations on behalf of Ford.

 

Overall,
while the SAC reflects significant improvement in the level of factual
specificity compared to the FAC, more factual details describing the specific
interactions between Ford and Plaintiffs (as opposed to the public at large)
are necessary to overcome the heightened pleading requirement for fraud. The
SAC still fails to allege the names and/or positions of Ford’s sales and repair
representatives, what Plaintiffs were specifically told by the sales
representatives (if any) about the Vehicle and when, whether and when they
personally reviewed Ford’s website, the types of marketing materials Plaintiffs
personally consulted and when, etc. Because Plaintiffs appear to be alleging
both that Ford’s representatives fraudulently concealed information about the
Vehicle and also made affirmative misrepresentations about the Defects, more
facts must be pled to distinguish the source, content, and scope of the
fraudulent misrepresentations, as compared to the fraudulent omissions so that
Ford can reasonably identify the specific nature of the fraud at issue.

 

Therefore,
Plaintiffs’ fourth cause of action for fraud is insufficiently pled.

 

iii.         
Economic
Loss Rule

 

In its
prior ruling on Ford’s motion for judgment on the pleadings, the Court
concluded that Plaintiffs’ fraud claim is not necessarily barred by the
economic loss rule. The Court adopts its prior analysis with respect to the
economic loss rule. (See Minute Order, 7/29/2026.)

 

Therefore,
Ford’s demurrer to Plaintiffs’ fourth cause of action for fraud is SUSTAINED
with LEAVE TO AMEND.

# 20   TENTATIVE RULING                9:00 a.m., Monday, Sept. 28, 2026

 

KEITH FEDER, M.D., INC. v. CEDARS SINAI HEALTH VENTURES [26STCV05470]

 

DEFENDANT CEDARS-SINAI MEDICAL CENTER’S DEMURRER TO THE FIRST AMENDED COMPLAINT

 

MEET AND CONFER: DEFECTIVE Counsel met and conferred on August 28 in compliance with Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41. (Bauch Supp. Decl., ¶ 2.)

 

BACKGROUND: FAC alleges claims for negligent misrepresentation and promissory estoppel in connection with medical billing dispute

 

TIMELINE:

 

4/2/2021 to 10/30/2023: Plaintiff Keith Feder, M.D., Inc. (“Plaintiff” or the “Medical Provider”) provides injections, consultations, and surgeries to Patient J.B. (the “Patient”) to treat the Patient’s medical condition. The Patient was an insured, member, policyholder, or certificate-holder of insurance through a policy issued and underwritten by Defendant Cedars-Sinai Medical Center (“Defendant” or “Cedars”).

 

4/1/2022 and 2/8/2023: The Medical Provider’s employee, J.E., calls and speaks with Defendant’s representatives, Chris and Verna S., respectively. In connection with the calls, Defendant Anthem Blue Cross Life and Health Insurance Co. (“Anthem”) (acting as an agent on behalf of Cedars) represents to the Medical Provider that Cedars would pay the UCR (usual, customary, and reasonable) rate for the Medical Provider’s services to the Patient and Defendant’s payment would not be based on the Medicare Fee Schedule.

 

Unknown Date: After the Patient’s procedure, the Medical Provider submits to Cedars (through Anthem) all billing information required by Cedars and Anthem, including operative reports, chart notes, and other medical records for the Patient. The Medical Provider’s billing submissions totaled $87,650.00, but Anthem (acting on behalf of Cedars) only paid a total of $8,906.22— well below the UCR amount promised by Anthem/Cedars.

 

2/19/2026: Plaintiff files the Complaint. The operative First Amended Complaint (“FAC”), filed 7/29/2026, alleges causes of action for:

1.   Negligent Misrepresentation

2.   Promissory Estoppel

 

8/27/2026: Cedars files this Demurrer, which is followed by Plaintiff’s Opposition (9/10/2026) and Cedars’s Reply (9/18/2026).

 

TENTATIVE RULING: DEFENDANT CEDARS-SINAI MEDICAL CENTER’S DEMURRER TO THE FIRST AMENDED COMPLAINT is OVERRULED.

 

DEMURRER

 

Cedars demurs to both causes of action in the FAC pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subds. (e) and (f). Plaintiff asserts two causes of action against Cedars: (1) negligent misrepresentation and (2) promissory estoppel. (See FAC, generally.) Plaintiff’s justifiable reliance on Defendant’s statements is an essential element of both claims. (Tindell v. Murphy (2018) 22 Cal. App. 5th 1239, 1252 [“The elements of a negligent misrepresentation are (1) the misrepresentation of a past or existing material fact, (2) without reasonable ground for believing it to be true, (3) with intent to induce another's reliance on the fact misrepresented, (4) justifiable reliance on the misrepresentation, and (5) resulting damage.”]; US Ecology, Inc. v. State of California (2005) 129 Cal.App.4th 887, 901 [“The elements of a promissory estoppel claim are ‘(1) a promise clear and unambiguous in its terms; (2) reliance by the party to whom the promise is made; (3) [the] reliance must be both reasonable and foreseeable; and (4) the party asserting the estoppel must be injured by his reliance.’”].)

 

Here, Cedars argues that the entire FAC is defective because Plaintiff could not have reasonably relied on Defendant’s telephone representations in April 2022 and February 2023, given that Plaintiff had already begun treating the Patient in April 2021 before Defendant’s representations were made. Specifically, Plaintiff alleges that it “relied on and provided services solely based on Defendant’s statements, promises and representations” and “provided services based solely on those promises and representations.” (FAC, ¶ 40.) Defendant’s alleged misrepresentations/promises occurred in phone calls on April 1, 2022 and February 8, 2023. (Id. ¶¶ 24, 26.) However, Plaintiff simultaneously alleges that the Patient began receiving treatment from the Medical Provider on April 2, 2021, almost one year before the first alleged communication between Plaintiff and Anthem on behalf of Cedars. (Id. ¶ 23.) Cedars argues that the inconsistency between Plaintiff’s allegations of reliance and the alleged timeline of the Patient’s treatment from the Medical Provider precludes Cedars from determining “what statements Plaintiff alleges it relied upon, and/or what actions it claims to have taken based on them.” (Mot., at p. 7.)

 

Plaintiff responds that Cedars ignores the entire period of treatment alleged in the FAC, which includes treatments both before and after Cedars’s alleged misrepresentations/promises to the Medical Provider. Indeed, Plaintiff alleges that the Patient continued receiving services from the Medical Provider until October 30, 2023, over 18 months after the first phone call and almost nine months after the second phone call. (FAC, ¶¶ 23-24, 26.) At minimum, Cedars fails to meaningfully challenge the reliance element of both causes of action with respect to the Medical Provider’s treatment services that were provided on or after April 1, 2022. (Id. ¶ 24.) As Plaintiff correctly points out, a general demurrer ordinarily “does not lie as to a portion of a cause of action and if any part of a cause of action is properly pleaded, the demurrer will be overruled.’” (Elder v. Pac. Bell Tel. Co. (2012) 205 Cal.App.4th 841, 856, fn. 14 (Elder).)

 

Furthermore, “demurrers for uncertainty are disfavored, and are granted only if the pleading is so incomprehensible that a defendant cannot reasonably respond.” (A.J. Fistes Corp. v. GDL Best Contractors, Inc. (2019) 38 Cal.App.5th 677, 695, internal citations omitted.) Here, there is no genuine uncertainty regarding the timeline of the Medical Provider’s services. It can reasonably be inferred that Plaintiff is alleging that it continued performing services for the Patient after April 1, 2022 in reliance on Cedars’s billing and reimbursement-related representations through Anthem. (FAC, ¶¶ 28, 31, 33, 48-49, 55-58.) Whether Plaintiff was justified in relying on Defendant’s representations when performing the Patient’s treatments after April 1, 2022 and whether Plaintiff’s damages request is limited to only those specific services are factual questions that are not suitable for resolution on the pleadings. (Id. ¶¶ 41-43; see Alliance Mortgage Co. v. Rothwell (1995) 10 Cal.4th 1226, 1238 [“Except in the rare case where the undisputed facts leave no room for a reasonable difference of opinion, the question of whether a plaintiff's reliance is reasonable is a question of fact.”].) Based on the facts and dates pleaded on the face of the FAC, the Court finds that Plaintiff has alleged sufficient information to enable Cedars to determine the “nature, source, and extent” of Plaintiff’s claims and reasonably respond thereto. (Elder, supra, 205 Cal.App.4th at p. 858.) Accepting the allegations in the FAC as true, Plaintiff has alleged sufficient facts to plead the reliance element of both claims asserted against Cedars.

 

Therefore, Cedars’s demurrer to the FAC is OVERRULED.

ERNESTINA VERONICA BLANCO ROLON, et al. v. MOISES FRAGOSO, et al. [26STCV11770]

TENTATIVE RULING: DEFENDANTS MOISES FRAGOSO, INDIVIDUALLY AND AS TRUSTEE OF THE BYPASS TRUST, CREATED UNDER THE AGUILA TRUST, DATED DECEMBER 24, 2007 AND SHALOME PROPERTY MANAGEMENT, LLC’S MOTION TO STRIKE is GRANTED with LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

MOTION TO STRIKE

 

Defendants moves to strike Plaintiffs’ request for punitive/exemplary damages from the Complaint pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 436. Plaintiffs seek punitive damages in connection with the first through fourth causes of action in the Complaint. (Compl., ¶¶ 34, 43, 48, 56; Prayer, ¶¶ 72, 75, 78, 81.)

 

Punitive damages are recoverable in tort actions where “the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice.” (Civ. Code § 3294, subd. (a).) California follows a heightened pleading standard for punitive damages— vague and conclusory allegations are insufficient. (G. D. Searle & Co. v. Superior Ct. (Ct. App. 1975) 49 Cal. App. 3d 22, 29.)¿In the case of an entity defendant, “the punitive damage statute requires proof of malice among corporate leaders,” (i.e. the officers, directors, or managing agents). (Cruz v. HomeBase (2000) 83 Cal. App. 4th 160, 167, citing Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (b); Grieves v. Superior Ct. (1984) 157 Cal. App. 3d 159, 167 [“a corporate employer may be liable only if the knowledge, authorization, ratification or act was on the part of an officer, director or managing agent of the corporation.”].) 

 

The issue with Plaintiffs’ punitive damages request here is that it impermissibly relies on group pleading without tailoring the allegations of malice, oppression and/or fraud to each individual defendant, rendering the punitive damages request largely vague and conclusory. For example, Plaintiffs allege that Defendants (collectively) “had actual and/or constructive notice” of the defects on the Property, but “repeatedly and intentionally ignored” the tenant’s complaints and failed to repair and abate the defects to save money. (Compl., ¶¶ 16, 18.) Plaintiffs also vaguely allege that Defendants conduct was “directed or ratified by Defendants and their respective employees,” without naming any particular individuals or employees acting on behalf of the Property’s owners and/or managers. (Id. ¶ 19.) Plaintiffs allege that Defendants (collectively) acted maliciously and “with the intent to knowingly take advantage of, oppress, and injure Plaintiffs” in conscious disregard for the rights [and] safety of Plaintiffs.” (Id. ¶¶ 34, 43, 48, 56.)

 

While, in theory, these types of allegations could suffice to show the requisite malice and/or oppression for punitive damages if tailored to the specific defendants, Plaintiffs have not identified the specific conduct of each Defendant to permit such a conclusion with respect to the Moving Defendants specifically. There are no specific allegations identifying the acts or omissions of either Fragoso or Shalome, in particular, to justify the finding that Moving Defendants themselves acted maliciously or oppressively (as opposed to the other various defendants). Plaintiffs vaguely group all four property managers/owners together without specifying the roles of each distinct individual or entity. With respect to Shalome in particular, an entity defendant, Plaintiffs do not identify any particular corporate leader acting on behalf of Shalome who engaged in malicious, oppressive, or fraudulent conduct on behalf of the entity as required under Civil Code section 3294, subd. (b). In sum, more factual details tailored to the conduct of the specific Moving Defendants are required for Plaintiffs to overcome the heightened pleading standard for punitive damages.

 

Therefore, Moving Defendants’ motion to strike the request for punitive damages is GRANTED with LEAVE TO AMEND.
