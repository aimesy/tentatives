LEAVE TO AMEND

 

MOVING PARTY: Plaintiff, Christopher L. Boggs

RESPONDING PARTY: Defendants, Cetera Financial Group, Inc. and First Allied Securities, Inc.

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Motion for Leave to File First Amended Complaint

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

Plaintiff Christopher L. Boggs sued Defendants Cetera Financial Group, Inc. and First Allied Securities, Inc. seeking a judicial declaration that enforcement of four promissory notes he signed was barred by the statute of limitations. Defendants then filed a cross-complaint to collect the balance outstanding on the notes. Defendants also assert a claim for breach of a settlement agreement connected to the notes. The Court later granted Defendants’ motion for judgment on the pleadings as to Plaintiff’s complaint, holding that Plaintiff was not entitled to declaratory or injunctive relief because the statute of limitations for Defendants’ claims was tolled.

 

RULING: Denied.

Plaintiff moves for leave to file a First Amended Complaint asserting 6 causes of action.[1] He argues that leave should be granted because the proposed claims are based on the same facts put in issue by the affirmative defenses raised in his answer to Defendants’ cross-complaint. Defendants oppose the motion, contending that the amended claims are futile, Plaintiff’s thirty-month delay in filing the motion prejudices them, and no operative complaint exists. Because there is no operative complaint that Plaintiff can amend, his motion is denied.

 

A motion for leave to amend must comply with the requirements set forth in California Rules of Court, rule 3.1324, which states as follows:

 

(a)        Contents of motion

A motion to amend a pleading before trial must:

(1)               Include a copy of the proposed amendment or amended pleading, which must be serially numbered to differentiate it from previous pleadings or amendments;

(2)               State what allegations in the previous pleading are proposed to be deleted, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the deleted allegations are located; and

(3)               State what allegations are proposed to be added to the previous pleading, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the additional allegations are located.

 

(b)        Supporting declaration

A separate declaration must accompany the motion and must specify:

(1)        The effect of the amendment;

(2)        Why the amendment is necessary and proper;

(3)        When the facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered; and

(4)               The reasons why the request for amendment was not made

earlier.

 

Dilatory delays and prejudice to the opposing parties is a valid ground for denial. (Hirsa v. Superior Court (1981) 118 Cal.App.3d 486, 490.) Prejudice exists where the amendment would require delaying the trial, resulting in loss of critical evidence, or added costs of preparation such as an increased burden of discovery. (Magpali v. Farmers Group, Inc. (1996) 48 Cal.App.4th 471, 486-88.)

 

Leave to amend is generally liberally granted. (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (a); Mesler v. Bragg Management Co. (1985) 39 Cal.3d 290, 296.) The Court will not generally consider the validity of the proposed amended pleading in ruling on a motion for leave, instead deferring such determinations for a demurrer or motion to strike, unless the proposed amendment fails to state a valid claim as a matter of law. (Kittredge Sports Co. v. Superior Court (1989) 213 Cal. App.3d 1045, 1048; California Casualty Gen. Ins. Co. v. Superior Court (1985) 173 Cal.App.3d 274, 280-81 disapproved of on other grounds by Kransco v. American Empire Surplus Lines Ins. Co. (2000) 23 Cal.4th 390.)

 

Plaintiff’s motion must be denied because there is no operative complaint for Plaintiff to amend after the Court granted Defendants’ motion for judgment on the pleadings on September 21, 2023. That ruling did not grant Plaintiff leave to amend his complaint. Plaintiff cannot use his reply to transform his motion to one for leave to file a compulsory cross-complaint under Code of Civil Procedure section 426.50. (Maleti v. Wickers (2022) 82 Cal.App.5th 181, 227-28.) The motion for leave is therefore denied.

 

The instant ruling in no way bars Plaintiff from seeking leave to file a compulsory cross-complaint.

 

Plaintiff to give notice.

  

 

 


[1] In his reply, Plaintiff withdrew the seventh and eighth proposed causes of action for declaratory judgment and injunctive relief. [Reply at p. 2.]

MOTION TO DISMISS

Motion

SUMMARY
OF ACTION



Plaintiff
Christopher L. Boggs sued Defendants Cetera Financial Group, Inc. and First
Allied Securities, Inc. seeking a judicial declaration that enforcement of four
promissory notes he signed was barred by the statute of limitations. Defendants
then filed a cross-complaint to collect the balance outstanding on the notes.
Defendants also assert a claim for breach of a settlement agreement connected
to the notes. The Court later granted Defendants’ motion for judgment on the
pleadings as to Plaintiff’s complaint, holding that Plaintiff was not entitled
to declaratory or injunctive relief because the statute of limitations for
Defendants’ claims was tolled.



 



RULING: Denied.



Request
for Judicial Notice: Granted.



The
Court takes judicial notice of the existence of the Financial Industry
Regulatory Authority’s Letter of Acceptance, Waiver and Consent. (Evid. Code, §
452, subd. (h).)



 



Plaintiff
moves to dismiss Defendants’ cross-claims, arguing that the claims are subject
to arbitration and thus not properly before the Court. Defendants oppose the
motion, contending that there is no statutory basis for the motion, Plaintiff
waived any right to arbitrate, the Cetera notes require a judicial forum, and that
dismissing the note claims would only fragment the case as the breach of
settlement agreement claim would remain. Because the settlement agreement claim
is not subject to arbitration, Plaintiff’s motion is denied.



 



“[W]here
the only issue litigated is covered by the arbitration clause, and where
plaintiff has not first pursued or attempted to pursue his arbitration remedy,
it should be held that (1) plaintiff has impliedly waived his right to
arbitrate, such that defendant could elect to submit the matter to the
jurisdiction of the court; (2) defendant may also elect to demur or move for
summary judgment on the ground that the plaintiff has failed to exhaust
arbitration remedies; and (3) defendant may also elect to move for a stay of
proceedings pending arbitration if defendant also moves to compel arbitration.”
(Charles J. Rounds Co. v. Joint Council of Teamsters No. 42 (1971) 4
Cal.3d 888, 899.) But “[w]here plaintiff has attempted to exhaust its
arbitration remedy or raises issues not susceptible to arbitration or not
covered by the arbitration agreement, defendant may not merely assert failure
to arbitrate an issue as an affirmative defense; a stay rather than dismissal
of the suit is then proper. Defendant must therefore demand arbitration if it
seeks such a stay.” (Id. at p. 899.)



 



Even
treating Plaintiff’s motion as one for judgment on the pleadings and assuming
that the Cetera notes are subject to arbitration, the motion to dismiss must be
denied because the breach of settlement agreement claim is not subject to arbitration.[1]
Thus, Plaintiff may only seek a stay of the action through a motion to compel
arbitration. The motion is therefore denied.

to give notice.












[1] The
Court acknowledges that the settlement agreement provides that “Boggs agrees
that FINRA shall have jurisdiction relating to any action arising out of a
breach of this Agreement.” [Cross-Complaint ¶ 2, Ex. 3.] That provision,
however, does not make arbitration of the settlement dispute mandatory. In any
event, Plaintiff does not move to dismiss the settlement agreement claim.

ATTORNEY’S FEES

 

MOVING PARTY: Plaintiff, Rex Wise

RESPONDING PARTY: Defendants, Sunlight Financial LLC and Cross River Bank

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Motion for Attorney’s Fees

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

On August 24, 2023, Plaintiff Rex Wise filed a First Amended Complaint against Defendants Solgen Construction LLC (Solgen), Sunlight Financial LLC (Sunlight), and Cross River Bank, alleging forgery of his signature on a 30-year loan for financing of solar panels installed on his property. Plaintiff asserted causes of action for: (1) Fraudulent Concealment; (2) Negligence; (3) Violations of the Elder Abuse and Dependent Adult Civil Protection Act; (4) Violations of the Rosenthal Fair Debt Collection Practices Act; (5) Violations of the Home Solicitation Sales Act; (6) Violations of Business and Professions Code §7150, et seq.; (7) Violations of the Truth in Lending Act; (8) Violations of the Credit Services Act; and (9) Violations of the Unfair Competition Law.

 

RULING: Granted.

Plaintiff moves for an award of $230,111.55 consisting of $156,103.62 in attorney’s fees, $2,531.12 in costs, and a 1.5 lodestar multiplier on pre-fee motion work. Defendants Sunlight and Cross River Bank oppose the motion, challenging the reasonableness of Plaintiff’s fee request. Plaintiff’s motion is granted, and he is awarded $134,168.62 in fees and costs.

 

The accepted Code of Civil Procedure section 998 offer designated Plaintiff as the prevailing party and specifically delegated determination of fees to the Court, thereby entitling Plaintiff to bring the subject motion. (Engle v. Copenbarger & Copenbarger, LLP (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 165, 168-69.) [McNeile Decl. ¶ 4, Ex. A, p. 2.] A motion for attorney’s fees must be served and filed within the time for the filing of a notice of appeal in a civil case. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1702(b)(1).) The time for the filing of a notice of appeal in an unlimited action is 60 days if a “Notice of Entry” of Judgment was served by the court clerk or a party to the action, or within 180 days of entry of judgment. (Id. rule 8.104.) On January 6, 2026, the Court dismissed the action without prejudice under Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6 and retained jurisdiction to enforce the terms of the parties’ settlement agreement. Plaintiff filed his motion 15 days later on January 21, 2026. The motion is therefore timely. (Hatlevig v. General Motors LLC (2026) 118 Cal.App.5th 644, 649.)

 

Defendants present no specific challenge to the claimed costs, instead requesting that the Court verify their reasonableness. The Court finds no improperly listed items and the items appear reasonable. [McNeile Decl. ¶ 16, Ex. D, pp. 33-35.] The Court thus awards all claimed costs.

Plaintiff has “the burden of showing that the fees incurred [are] “‘allowable,’ [are] ‘reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation,’ and [are] ‘reasonable in amount.’” (Levy v. Toyota Motor Sales, U.S.A., Inc. (1992) 4 Cal.App.4th 807, 816.) The lodestar method for determination of fees applies. (Robertson v. Fleetwood Travel Trailers of California, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 785, 819.) “‘[T]he lodestar is the basic fee for comparable legal services in the community; it may be adjusted by the court based on factors including, as relevant herein, (1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, (4) the contingent nature of the fee award. [Citation.] The purpose of such adjustment is to fix a fee at the fair market value for the particular action. In effect, the court determines, retrospectively, whether the litigation involved a contingent risk or required extraordinary legal skill justifying augmentation of the unadorned lodestar in order to approximate the fair market rate for such services.’” (Graciano v. Robinson Ford Sales, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 140, 154.)

 

The reasonableness of attorney fees lies within the discretion of the trial court. (PLCM Group v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1096.) The Court makes it determination based on the consideration of a number of factors, including, “the nature of the litigation, its difficulty, the amount involved, the skill required in its handling, the skill employed, the attention given, the success or failure, and other circumstances in the case.” (Ibid.) The Court should apply an objective standard of reasonableness. (Id. at p. 1098.) “A fee request that appears unreasonably inflated is a special circumstance permitting the trial court to reduce the award or deny one altogether.” (Serrano v. Unruh (1982) 32 Cal.3d 621, 635.) The Court adheres to the “market rate” approach for contingency cases, and finds the “Laffey” charts general and only suggestive. (Pasternack v. McCullough (2021) 65 Cal.App.5th 1050, 1057; Center for Biological Diversity v. County of San Bernardino (2010) 188 Cal.App.4th 603, 619-20.)

 

Counsel lists a total of 260.9 hours, consisting of 124.5 hours by attorney Adam McNeile @ $750/hour for a total of $93,375, 87.5 hours by attorney Katie Sass @ $500/hour for a total of $43,750, and 48.9 hours by paralegal Sean Barry @ $275/hour for a total of $13,447. The subject action was taken on a contingency basis, with statutory recovery of attorney’s fees an assumed condition for any recovery of fees by counsel. The Court finds the billable rates reasonable and represent different ranges of experience well within the rates for a consumer advocate litigator in Los Angeles County. (Mikhaeilpoor v. BMW of North America, LLC (2020) 48 Cal.App.5th 240, 247, 256; Center for Biological Diversity, supra, 188 Cal.App.4th at pp. 619-20; see Lindy Bros. Builders, Inc. of Phila. v. American Radiator & Standard Sanitary Corp. (3d Cir. 1973) 487 F.2d 161, 167.) The Court likewise finds Barry’s paralegal rate reasonable given his over 30 years of experience as a litigation paralegal. [McNeile Decl. ¶¶ 115-16.] While Defendants argue the listed rates are unreasonable, they provide no controverting evidence of prevailing market rates for litigators with similar experience handling comparable matters or for paralegals with similar extensive experience.

 

The Court next considers the listed entries. “In referring to ‘reasonable’ compensation, we indicated that trial courts must carefully review attorney documentation of hours expended; ‘padding’ in the form of inefficient or duplicative efforts is not subject to compensation.” (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132; Levy, supra, 4 Cal.App.4th at p. 817.) The Court considers the listings rather than engage in a categorical determination of entitlement. (Morris v. Hyundai Motor America (2019) 41 Cal.App.5th 24, 40; Mountjoy v. Bank of America, N.A. (2016) 245 Cal.App.4th 266, 280-81; Christian Research Institute v. Alnor (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 1315, 1329.)

 

Defendants contend the expended hours are excessive and that certain entries are duplicative and consist of unrecoverable administrative work. While Defendants do not point to specific objectionable entries, the Court agrees that some entries reflect excessive hours on discovery and motion practice, bill for duplicate and/or administrative work, and consist of impermissible block billing. The following hours/entries are thus excluded: 15.1 hours from McNeile [McNeile Decl. ¶ 16, Ex. D: 2/28/2025; 4/16/2025; 2/7/2025; 11/21/2024; 4/24/2024]; 13.9 hours from Sass [Id.: 5/13/2024; 5/14/2024; 2/3/2025; 8/2/2024; 8/5/2024; 8/6/2024]; and 2.4 hours from Barry [Id.: 7/21/2023; 11/1/2023; 9/23/2024; 2/5/2025; 3/5/2025; 4/10/2024; 2/9/2024; 9/30/2024; 10/22/2024.] This accordingly results in total hours of 109.4 for McNeile, 73.6 for Sass, and 46.5 for Barry. The Court finds the remaining hours and entries reasonable.

 

Plaintiff requests a lodestar multiplier of 1.5 on pre-fee motion work. When considering a lodestar enhancement request, the Court must consider a series of factors. “[T]he lodestar is the basic fee for comparable legal services in the community; it may be adjusted by the court based on factors including, as relevant herein, (1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, (4) the contingent nature of the fee award.” (Ketchum, supra, 24 Cal.4th at p. 1132.) “Contingency and delay factors” apply in consideration of the application of a lodestar enhancement in order to reflect the “semiguaranteed” recovery of fees upon a successfully prevailing party, but any consideration must still acknowledge the “contingency” nature of representation in general. (Horsford v. Board of Trustees of California State University (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 359, 400, fn. 11.)

 

“In Serrano IV, applying the same principles to the statutory fee award under Code of Civil Procedure section 1021.5, we reiterated that fee awards should be fully compensatory. We approved the calculation of attorney fees beginning with a lodestar figure based on the reasonable hours spent, multiplied by the hourly prevailing rate for private attorneys in the community conducting noncontingent litigation of the same type. (Citation.) [¶] We held in Serrano IV that, absent circumstances rendering the award unjust, an attorney fee award should ordinarily include compensation for all the hours reasonably spent, including those relating solely to the fee. (Citation.) We explained that the purpose behind statutory fee authorizations—i.e., encouraging attorneys to act as private attorneys general and to vindicate important rights affecting the public interest—‘will often be frustrated, sometimes nullified, if awards are diluted or dissipated by lengthy, uncompensated proceedings to fix or defend a rightful fee claim.’ (Citation.) [¶] We acknowledged the discretion of the trial court in setting attorney fees, but emphasized that because the determination of the lodestar figures is so fundamental to arriving at an objectively reasonable amount, ‘the exercise of that discretion must be based on the lodestar adjustment method.’ (Ibid.) We also reiterated that the lodestar figure may be increased by application of a fee enhancement, or reduced as appropriate, after the trial court has considered other factors concerning the lawsuit, including the contingent nature of the fee award.” (Ketchum, supra, 24 Cal.4th at pp. 1133-34.)

 

The Court declines to award a multiplier on the subject action. “The lodestar amount is presumptively the reasonable fee amount, and thus a multiplier may be used to adjust the lodestar amount upward or downward only in “‘rare’ and ‘exceptional’ cases, supported by both ‘specific evidence’ on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts” that the lodestar amount is unreasonably low or unreasonably high.” (Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mut. Life Co. (9th Cir. 2000) 214 F.3d 1041, 1045.) The Court finds the lodestar determination sufficiently addresses the fee. Even considering the uncertainty caused by Sunlight’s bankruptcy filing and Solgen going out of business, as well as the results obtained, the Court does not find the instant action to be a rare or exceptional case requiring an enhancement. (Blum v. Stenson (1984) 465 U.S. 886, 901; Hensley v. Eckerhart (1983) 461 U.S. 424, 434; see Thayer v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2001) 92 Cal.App.4th 819, 844.)    

 

Plaintiff’s motion is thus granted, and he is awarded $134,168.62, consisting of $131,637.50 in fees and $2,531.12 in costs.

 

Because the amount of fees exceeds $5,000, the order is immediately appealable. (Code Civ. Proc., § 904.1, subd. (a)(12); Doe v. Luster (2006) 145 Cal.App.4th 139, 146.)

RELEASE OF DIGITAL ASSETS

 

MOVING PARTY: Petitioner, Maria I. Candelas

RESPONDING PARTY: Unopposed/Respondent, Apple, Inc.

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Petition for Order Authorizing Release of Digital Assets

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

Petitioner Maria I. Candelas, in pro per, seeks an order directing Respondent Apple, Inc. to provide copies of digital content from accounts associated with her deceased daughter’s Apple ID.  

 

RULING: Denied without prejudice.

The petition is denied without prejudice because it was not properly served on Apple. The proof of service indicates that the petition was served via mail to Apple’s legal department in California. However, the legal department is not a “person” that can be served on behalf of Apple. (Code Civ. Proc., § 416.10.) Furthermore, the proof of service does not indicate that 2 copies of the notice and acknowledgement form and “a return envelope, postage prepaid, addressed to the sender” were mailed to Apple or that Apple signed and returned a copy of the acknowledgement form. (Id. § 415.30.) The petition is therefore denied without prejudice and Plaintiff may refile the petition with proper service on Apple.

 

Petitioner to give notice.
