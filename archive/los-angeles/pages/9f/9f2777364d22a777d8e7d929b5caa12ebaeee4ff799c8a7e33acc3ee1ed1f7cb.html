      I.          Introduction and Background

 

Jeffrey Gilmore (Decedent) was a highway maintenance worker for Caltrans from June 2006 to August 2020 (Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”) ¶ 4.) For the last decade of his time at Caltrans, Decedent maintained highway berms, using various gasoline-powered tools. It is alleged that Decedent was exposed to gasoline fumes when filling up this equipment, and that he breathed in gasoline exhaust fumes as he worked on the highways.   (SAC, ¶¶ 3, 11.) Decedent contracted  Acute Myeloid Leukemia (“AML”). He died from this disease on September 1, 2023. (SAC, ¶¶ 12-13.)

 

 

On September 12, 2024, Decedent’s widow,  Carolyn Agaza Gilmore (Gilmore) and The Estate of Jeffrey Allen Gilmore (Estate) (together Plaintiffs) brought a wrongful death Complaint against Defendants Exxon, Chevron, State of California Department of Transportation (DOT), Phillips 66 Company (Phillips), and Atlantic Richfield Company (ARCO) (collectively Defendants). The crux of the lawsuit is that Defendant oil companies refine and distribute gasoline with unsafe levels of benzene, and that exposure to benzene, through gasoline vapors and automobile exhaust, caused Decedent’s cancer. (SAC, ¶¶ 14-16.)

 

 

 

 

 

All the defendant oil companies have moved for summary judgment. Before the Court now are the motions for summary judgment or, in the alternative, for summary adjudication (“Motions”) of defendants Exxon and Chevron (“Moving Defendants”).

 

For the reasons stated below, the Court must grant the Motions.

    V.          Legal Standard

Standard for Motion for Summary Judgment – Summary judgment is proper “if all the papers submitted show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c).) The moving party bears the initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing that there are no triable issues of material fact. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850.) A defendant moving for summary judgment must show either (1) that one or more elements of the cause of action cannot be established or (2) that there is a complete defense to that cause of action. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p).) A defendant may discharge this burden by furnishing either (1) affirmative evidence of the required facts or (2) discovery responses conceding that the plaintiff lacks evidence to establish an essential element of the plaintiff's case. If a defendant chooses the latter option, he or she must present evidence “and not simply point out that plaintiff does not possess and cannot reasonably obtain needed evidence.” (Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at 865-66.)

Under the latter approach, a defendant's initial evidentiary showing may ‘consist of the deposition testimony of the plaintiff's witnesses, the plaintiff's factually devoid discovery responses, or admissions by the plaintiff in deposition or in response to requests for admission that he or she has not discovered anything that supports an essential element of the cause of action.’ [Citation.] In other words, a defendant may show the plaintiff does not possess evidence to support an element of the cause of action by means of presenting the plaintiff's factually devoid discovery responses from which an absence of evidence may be reasonably inferred. [Citation.]

(Leyva v. Garcia (2018) 20 Cal.App.5th 1095, 1103 (internal citations omitted). Until the moving defendant has discharged its burden of proof, the opposing plaintiff has no burden to come forward with any evidence. Once the moving defendant has discharged its burden as to a particular cause of action, however, the plaintiff may defeat the motion by producing evidence showing that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to that cause of action. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 437c(p)(2).) On a motion for summary judgment, the moving party's supporting documents are strictly construed and those of his opponent liberally construed, and doubts as to the propriety of summary judgment should be resolved against granting the motion.  (D’Amico v. Board of Medical Examiners (1974) 11 Cal.3d 1, 21.)

The court must grant a motion for summary judgment if, after all admissible evidence is considered, there is no triable issue as to any material fact. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 437c(c).) The court has no discretion to deny a summary judgment motion where the moving party has met their burden and the opposing party has failed to establish any triable issue of material fact exists, and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. (Krasley v. Superior Court (1980) 101 Cal.App.3d 425, 431.)

 

A party may also move for summary adjudication as to one or more causes of action within an action, one or more claims for damages, or one or more issues of duty if the party contends the cause of action has no merit, that there is no merit to a claim for damages, or that one or more defendants did not owe a duty to the plaintiff. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (f)(1).) A motion for summary adjudication may be made by itself or as an alternative to a motion for summary judgment, shall proceed in all procedural respects as a motion for summary judgment, and shall only be granted if it completely disposes of a cause of action, a claim for damages, or an issue of duty. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (f)(1)-(2).)

 VI.          Discussion

 

Moving Defendants move for summary judgment pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 437c on the grounds that 1) Plaintiffs’ claims are preempted by the Clean Air Act and 2) Plaintiffs cannot show that Defendants caused Decedent’s injuries or death. (Motion, p. 6:7-9.) In the alternative, Moving Defendants move pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (f) for an order summarily adjudicating the issues that Plaintiff lacks admissible evidence to prove her (1) strict liability design defect claim, (2) negligent design defect claim, (3) strict liability manufacturing defect claim, (4) negligent manufacturing defect claim, (5) strict liability failure to warn claim, and (6) negligent failure to warn claim. (Motion, p. 6:10-17.)

 

a.     Preemption Under the Clean Air Act

 

Moving Defendants first move for summary judgment on the grounds that the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) regulates gasoline and vehicle exhaust pursuant to the Clean Air Act (CAA), which expressly and impliedly preempts Plaintiffs’ lawsuit. (Motion, p. 7:7-9.)

 

“The supremacy clause of the United States Constitution establishes a constitutional choice-of-law rule, makes federal law paramount, and vests Congress with the power to preempt state law. [Citation.]” (Viva! Internat. Voice for Animals v. Adidas Promotional Retail Operations, Inc. (2007) 41 Cal.4th 929, 935.) The four types of federal preemption are express, conflict, obstacle, and field. (Ibid.)

 

Moving Defendants argue all of Plaintiffs claims are preempted by the CAA. First, they argue the claims are expressly preempted on the grounds that the preemption statutes bar Plaintiffs’ causes of action because they are based on emissions from new motor vehicles and inclusion of benzene in the gasoline sold by Defendants. (Motion, p. 15:10-18.) Next, they argue the claims are also precluded through field preemption because Congress has preempted the field with regard to emissions, motor vehicle fuels, and fuel additives, and the CAA provides a full set of standards regarding gasoline characteristics and composition fulfilling Congressional intent to ensure uniformity of gasoline throughout the nation, relying in part on Washington v. General Motors Corp. (1972) 406 U.S. 109. (Motion, p. 16:9-20.) Finally, Moving Defendants argue obstacle preemption applies, as Plaintiffs’ claims are inconsistent with federal objectives and would have the Court and a jury find that Defendants breached their duty under California law to remove certain components, namely benzene, from gasoline before selling it to consumers, which would pose an obstacle to the EPA’s goal of ensuring gasoline uniformity. (Motion, p. 17:14-18.) Moving Defendants specifically argue that a “no benzene” duty would serve as an obstacle to the EPA’s objective not to lower benzene content of gasoline, as the EPA determined an average benzene level below 0.62% would be unreasonable and achievable only by 50% of refineries. (Motion, p. 17:19-18:3; Ex. 8, p. 90, 71 Fed. Reg. 15804, 15866 (2006).)

 

The relevant undisputed facts are as follows: (1) Plaintiff alleges Moving Defendants’ gasoline is defective because it contains benzene and when combusted, creates exhaust containing benzene (PSUMF, ¶ 1); (2) Under the CAA, the Environmental Protection Agency (“EPA”) regulates the benzene content of gasoline (PSUMF, ¶ 3); (3) the EPA permits gasoline and vehicle exhaust to contain benzene[1] (PSUMF, ¶ 4); and (4) Moving Defendants deliver gasoline, gasoline products, and diesel fuel to retail gasoline service stations throughout California and specifically in the Antelope Valley in Los Angeles County (Motion, p. 9:13-16; SAC, ¶ 5.)

 

In support, Moving Defendants provide Plaintiff’s responses to Special Interrogatories propounded by each Moving Defendant, in which Plaintiff identifies the presence of benzene in the fuel despite the alleged existence of safer alternatives as the crux of her strict liability and negligent design, manufacturing, and warning claims. (Ex. 2-5.) Moving Defendants also provide the Proposed Rules from the EPA regarding control and reduction of benzene and other hazardous air pollutants form mobile sources and proposed controls on gasoline, vehicles, and portable gasoline containers, in which the EPA proposed the current 0.62% benzene content annual average and states: “As the most stringent case, we evaluated a national average benzene content standard of 0.52 vol%. Our analysis indicates that a standard at this level would require all refiners to invest in the most effective technologies used today…. Because of the almost universal need for refineries to use the most expensive reformate-based benzene control technologies, we believe a standard of 0.52 vol% would be very challenging economically for many refineries, and we believe that such a standard would not be achievable taking costs into consideration, as we are required to do under section 202(l),” that 0.62 vol% was achievable under the assessments, and “implementing an average benzene standard of 0.62 vol% would achieve several other important program goals.” (Ex. 8, p. 90.)

 

Moving Defendants also provide a publication, “ToxFAQs,” from the Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry, a part of the US Department of Health and Human Services, which states that “[t]ypically, gasoline contains more than 150 chemicals, including small amounts of benzene, toluene, xylene, and sometimes lead. How the gasoline is made determines which chemicals are present in the gasoline mixture and how much of each is present. The actual composition varies with the source of the crude petroleum, the manufacturer, and the time of year.” (Ex. 10, p. 123.) Also attached in support is an EPA TEACH chemical summary about benzene, which states it is “an organic compound found most often in air as a result of emissions from burning coal and oil, gasoline vapors at gasoline service stations, motor vehicle exhaust, cigarette smoke, wood-burning fires, some adhesives, and other sources,” and that benzene is a “natural part of crude oil [and] gasoline (1% to 2% benzene).” (Ex. 12, p. 132, 141.) Moving Defendants provide a Gasoline Overview from the EPA, which shows that the EPA has implemented a regulatory program, Air Toxics Standards for Gasoline, to limit the content of toxic chemicals in gasoline, including benzene, and that the program is implemented at the federal level. (Ex. 13, p. 147-48.)

 

Finally, Moving Defendants provide three non-binding out of state authorities cited in support of its motion: (1) Exxon Corp. v. New York (2d Cir. 1977) 548 F.2d 1088, (2) Jackson v. GMC (S.D.N.Y. 2011) 770 F. Supp. 2d 570, and (3) American Auto. Mfrs. Ass'n v. Commissioner (D.Mass. 1997).

 

Our Supreme Court in Viva! Internat. Voice for Animals v. Adidas Promotional Retail Operations, Inc., (2007) 41 Cal. 4th 929, 936 described the different types of federal preemption:

 

First, express preemption arises when Congress “define[s] explicitly the extent to  which its enactments pre-empt state law. [Citation.] Pre-emption fundamentally is a question of congressional intent, [citation], and when Congress has made its intent known through explicit statutory language, the courts' task is an easy one.” (English v. General Electric Co., supra, 496 U.S. at pp. 78–79, 110 S.Ct. 2270; accord, Jevne v. Superior Court, supra, 35 Cal.4th at p. 949, 28 Cal.Rptr.3d 685, 111 P.3d 954.) Second, conflict preemption will be found when simultaneous compliance with both state and federal directives is impossible. (Hillsborough County v. Automated Medical Labs. (1985) 471 U.S. 707, 713, 105 S.Ct. 2371, 85 L.Ed.2d 714; Olszewski v. Scripps Health (2003) 30 Cal.4th 798, 815, 135 Cal.Rptr.2d 1, 69 P.3d 927.) Third, obstacle preemption arises when “ ‘under the circumstances of [a] particular case, [the challenged state law] stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the full purposes and objectives of Congress.’ ” (Crosby v. National Foreign Trade Council, supra, 530 U.S. at p. 373, 120 S.Ct. 2288, quoting Hines v. Davidowitz (1941) 312 U.S. 52, 67, 61 S.Ct. 399, 85 L.Ed. 581; accord, Bronco Wine Co. v. Jolly, supra, 33 Cal.4th at p. 955, 17 Cal.Rptr.3d 180, 95 P.3d 422.) Finally, field preemption, i.e., “Congress' intent to pre-empt all state law in a particular area,” applies “where the scheme of federal regulation is sufficiently comprehensive to make reasonable the inference that Congress ‘left no room’ for supplementary state regulation.” (Hillsborough County, at p. 713, 105 S.Ct. 2371, quoting Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp. (1947) 331 U.S. 218, 230, 67 S.Ct. 1146, 91 L.Ed. 1447.)

 

 

(See also Williamson v. Mazda Motor of Am., Inc. (2011) 562 U.S. 323, 328-30; Geier v. Am. Honda Motor Co. (2000) 529 U.S. 861, 868; Cipollone v. Liggett Group, Inc. (1992) 505 U.S. 504.)

 

With respect to all forms of preemption, the pertinent statutory authority is the CAA. The CAA authorizes the EPA to establish standards for gasoline, fuel component, and emissions standards for motor vehicles, and the EPA has in turn developed emissions and gasoline regulations, enforcement provisions, penalties, and preemptive effects. (42 U.S.C. § 7521.) Under this authority, the EPA has established fuel-specific specifications for sulfur content (40 C.F.R. §§ 1090.200, 1090.205(a)), vapor pressure (40 C.F.R. § 1090.215), and benzene content (40 C.F.R. §§ 1090.200, 1090.210(a)-(b); 42 U.S.C. § 7521(l)), among a host of other natural gasoline components and additives. Currently, the EPA standard requires gasoline to “meet a benzene average standard of 0.62 volume percent” and “a maximum benzene average standard of 1.30 volume percent.” (40 C.F.R. § 1090.210.) The EPA granted California an exception, and California imposes a modified standard that the average benzene content of gasoline cannot exceed 0.7%, with a 0.8% maximum. (Cal. Code Regs. tit. 13, § 2262.)

 

Express Preemption. The CAA contains two applicable express preemption provisions. First, the CAA states that “[n]o State or any political subdivision thereof shall adopt or attempt to enforce any standard relating to the control of emissions from new motor vehicles or new motor vehicle engines subject to this part.” (42 U.S.C. § 7543(a).) Second, “no State (or political subdivision thereof) may prescribe or attempt to enforce, for purposes of motor vehicle emission control, any control or prohibition respecting any characteristic or component of a fuel or fuel additive in a motor vehicle or motor vehicle engine” if “the Administrator has prescribed under paragraph (1) a control or prohibition applicable to such characteristic or component of a fuel or fuel additive, unless State prohibition or control is identical to the prohibition or control prescribed by the Administrator.” (42 U.S.C. § 7545(c)(4)(A)(ii).)

 

Nothing in either preemption clause purports to address private tort claims. As shown at Section 7604(e), Congress knew how to distinguish private tort claims from State regulation or enforcement. The omission of private actions from the express preemption provisions is therefore significant.

 

Section 7604(e) matters in another important respect. The Section itself concerns citizen’s suits to enforce the CAA, and contains the following savings clause: “Nothing in this section shall restrict any right which any person (or class of persons) may have under any statute or common law to seek enforcement of any emission standard or limitation or to seek any other relief.” This savings clause anticipates lawsuits not just to seek enforcement of the CAA but for “any other relief.” The “any other relief” language runs counter to construing the CAA to otherwise expressly preempt suits for other relief.  

 

Furthermore, the express preemption clauses of Sections 7543(a) and 7545(c)(4)(A)(ii) do not encompass one of Plaintiff’s two theories of causation – that Decedent contracted cancer from inhaling vapors from gasoline in containers. That alleged harm implicates no “standard relating to the control of emissions from new motor vehicles or new motor vehicle engines” nor an attempt to enforce, “for purposes of motor vehicle emission control,” a control or prohibition on the characteristic of gasoline. (Emphases added.) 

 

For these reasons, the Court finds that the CAA does not expressly preempt the private tort claims asserted here.

 

At the same time, the Court disagrees with Plaintiff that the savings clause of Section 7604(e) means that no preemption, express or implied, can stand. Plaintiff construes the ending phrase “any other relief” to mean that the CAA as a whole cannot bar any action for relief under even an implied preemption theory. While the last part of the sentence (“to seek any other relief”) is important, so is the first part, - “Nothing in this section. . . .” The “section” in question concerns the mechanism for bringing citizen suits for violations of emission standards under the CAA. (42 U.S.C. § 7604(a).) Thus, the provisions for bringing citizens suits do not expressly preempt private tort actions for “any relief.” But the limitation of the savings clause to “this section” leaves open whether any other section of the CAA impliedly preempts Plaintiff’s claims. The Court therefore considers ways other than express preemption that could bar the claims here. 

 

Implied, Obstacle, and Field Preemption. Moving Defendants argue that, regardless of express preemption, preemption is otherwise implied. As shown above, the savings clause of Section 7604 does not preclude such analysis.

 

Plaintiffs do not argue that Moving Defendants violated the CAA or the EPA’s federal standards, but seek relief in state tort law.

 

However, Plaintiffs’ causes of action are nonetheless impliedly preempted by the CAA. Plaintiffs’ wrongful death cause of action is based on the assertion that Defendants “negligently and carelessly designed, refined, manufactured and/or sold the unleaded gasoline described…that it was dangerous and unsafe for intended uses” and that as a result of Defendants’ manufacture and sale of gasoline, “[D]ecedent was seriously injured through his exposure to gasoline exhaust and gasoline fumes during the period June 30, 2006 until August 14, 2020 while using the gasoline and gasoline products in the manner in which it was intended.” (SAC, ¶¶ 22-23.) Plaintiffs’ strict products liability cause of action is based on their assertion that “the gasoline and gasoline products and their component parts were defective as to design, manufacture and warnings causing the gasoline and gasoline products and its component parts to be in a defective condition that made them dangerous and unsafe for their intended uses,” because “[a]ll of these products contain benezene which is known to cause Acute Myeloid Leukemia, the condition which lead to the demise of [Decedent].” (SAC, ¶ 28.) Plaintiff’s discovery responses further establish that her claims are based on Moving Defendants selling gasoline containing benzene. (See Exs. 2-5.)

 

Based on the allegations in the SAC and Plaintiff’s discovery responses, Plaintiffs’ action clearly is related to, has a connection with, and has reference to the regulation of the composition, standards, and federal standards for fuel composition and fuel emissions. Based on the unrefuted evidence provided, these assertions (1) are contradicted by the evidence presented by Moving Defendants, which establishes that benzene is a natural component of crude oil and not an additive, and that the EPA found that lowering the current federal regulatory standard of benzene from 0.62 vol% to even 0.52 vol% is not feasible, (2) seek to hold Moving Defendants liable notwithstanding their compliance with EPA standards, and (3) therefore attempt, in effect, to have a jury in this case enforce a standard of permissible levels of benzene in gasoline under California state law that is not identical to the current federal regulatory standards of the EPA.

 

“[O]bstacle preemption arises when ‘under the circumstances of [a] particular case, [the challenged state law] stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the full purposes and objectives of Congress.’ [Citation.]” (Viva, supra, 41 Cal.4th at 935.) Moving Defendants have provided evidence that the EPA expressly considered, assessed, and rejected the feasibility of lowering the federally prescribed national standard to 0.52 vol% of benzene in fuel. The EPA also stated that a 0.62 vol% of benzene is feasible, reasonable, will reduce pollution and risks to the public, and achieves several other important federal program goals. There is also a clear Congressional purpose and objective to delegate exclusive authority to the EPA for establishing appropriate nationwide standards for fuel and motor vehicle emissions and to preempt all contrary state laws, regulations, and standards. As Moving Defendants argue, should Plaintiffs’ action proceed, the case would have the Court and a jury find that Defendants breached a duty under California state law to remove all benzene from their gasoline before selling it to consumers, which would pose an obstacle to the EPA’s express purpose and objective in ensuring gasoline uniformity and avoiding the placement of undue burdens on gasoline refiners, as further nationwide reduction from the current federal standard has already been found to be unachievable by the EPA. (Motion, p. 17:14-18.)

 

Finally, field preemption, i.e., ‘Congress’ intent to pre-empt all state law in a particular area,’ applies ‘where the scheme of federal regulation is sufficiently comprehensive to make reasonable the inference that Congress ‘left no room’ for supplementary state regulation.’ [Citation.]” (Viva, supra, 41 Cal.4th at 935.) “Field preemption occurs when federal law occupies a ‘field’ of regulation so comprehensively that it has left no room for supplementary state legislation. Where Congress occupies an entire field, even complementary state regulation is impermissible.” (County of Butte v. Department of Water Resources (2022) 13 Cal.5th 612, 643.) In Washington, the Supreme Court noted that “Congress has largely pre-empted the field with regard to ‘emissions from new motor vehicles,’ and motor vehicle fuels and fuel additives.” (Washington, supra, 406 U.S. 109, 114.) Congress did so "to ensure uniformity throughout the nation, and to avoid the undue burden on motor vehicle manufacturers which would result from different state standards." (Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass'n v. New York State Dep't. of Envtl Conservation (N.D.N.Y. 1993) 810 F. Supp. 1331, 1337.)

 

As discussed, the CAA provides sweeping, exclusive regulatory authority to the EPA on the issue of fuel composition and emissions, sweeping express preemption provisions prohibiting state and local regulation without the EPA’s express permission, and the EPA has established a broad, detailed federal regulatory framework setting forth a full and complete set of standards governing vehicle fuel and emissions. To hold that Plaintiffs’ claims, based entirely on the alleged insufficiency of the fuel composition and emission of gasoline manufactured and sold by Moving Defendants, may proceed would be to maintain an action which seeks to impose obligations on Moving Defendants that have already been expressly established by the EPA in a field already comprehensively occupied by federal regulation. Because Congress intended to completely occupy this field, and did so, Plaintiffs’ action is preempted by the CAA.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues the EPA only set a maximum limit for benzene content, but does not compel manufacturers to use exactly the amount of 0.62%, and that compliance with federal regulations does not provide immunity from civil suit. (Opp., p. 9:1-5.)

 

A similar argument was rejected in Geier v. Am. Honda Motor Co. (2000) 529 U.S. 861. In Geier, the plaintiffs brought suit against a car manufacturer for common law negligent and defective design because the vehicle lacked an airbag, asserting that while federal standard required auto manufacturers to equip some, but not all, of their vehicles with restraints, the defendant manufacturer, who was in compliance with the federal standard, nonetheless should have equipped their automobile with air bags as a safer option than meeting only the minimum requirements of the federal standard. (Id. at 864-65.) The Supreme Court found that the plaintiffs’ tort action was dependent upon its claim that the defendant manufacturers had a duty to install an airbag, that a rule of state tort law imposing such a duty, by its terms, would require manufacturers of all similar cars to install airbags to avoid state-specific liability, rather than other systems authorized under the standards sought by the federal regulation, and therefore would have presented an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the federal objectives sought by the regulation of granting manufacturers a choice as to what safety systems to employ, and the tort claims were thus preempted. (Id. at 881.)

 

Similarly here, Plaintiffs’ wrongful death and strict products liability causes of action rest upon their assertion that Moving Defendants owed a duty to Decedent not to include any benzene in their gasoline and instead utilize safer alternatives. Imposition of a California state law duty on manufacturers and distributors of gasoline to remove all benzene from gasoline prior to sale and distribution, or otherwise a duty to reduce benzene concentration or replace benzene with an alternative additive, conflicts with the CAA and presents an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the federal objectives sought by Congress through the CAA and EPA in establishing national uniformity, and any action attempting to do so is therefore preempted.

 

Plaintiff also relies on the broad savings clause in 42 U.S.C. § 7604(e), which again reads: “Nothing in this section shall restrict any right which any person (or class of persons) may have under any statute or common law to seek enforcement of any emission standard or limitation or to seek any other relief.” (Opp., p. 17:23-18:5.) As analyzed above, that savings clause is limited to a statutory section concerning the initiation of citizen’s suits. As a result of that limitation, the clause has no bearing on whether the CAA as a whole preempts common law tort actions seeking to impose liability on manufacturers pursuant to standards that conflict with or frustrate federal standards. Here, Plaintiffs’ state law causes of action, including wrongful death resulting from fuel emissions and product liability based on the design and manufacture of fuel, turn entirely on the composition and makeup of Moving Defendants’ gasoline products, and the two cannot be separated.

 

Returning to Geier, the Supreme Court confronted the issue of whether a savings clause in a federal regulatory scheme removes a common law action from ordinary preemption principles and held that it does not. (Geier, supra, 529 U.S. at 867.) Specifically, the Court held that where express preemption does not apply because of a savings clause permitting common law actions, the savings clause alone does not foreclose through negative implication any possibility of implied preemption, and does not serve to limit the operation of ordinary preemption principles where state laws and common law rules actually conflict with federal standards. (Id. at 869.) That is not quite the case here, as the savings clause is tailored to a narrow section of the CAA. But even so, Geier is instructive, as the Court “declined to give broad effect to saving clauses where doing so would upset the careful regulatory scheme established by federal law” and found the action was preempted because the common law causes of action attempted to impose a duty under state law that presented an obstacle to the federal objective of giving auto manufacturers the choice of which safety provisions they wished to employ in complying with the federal standard. (Id. at 868, 870, 881.)

 

Here, as was the case in Geier, Plaintiffs are attempting to impose on Moving Defendants, and effectively all entities manufacturing and distributing gasoline in California, a duty that is not imposed by the federal standard, was rejected by the EPA, and presents an obstacle to federal objectives in a field completely occupied by Congress. Though Plaintiffs claims are not directly related to enforcement of the CAA, they are directly related to an attempt at creating and imposing a standard in conflict with the federal standard. The operative language of 42 U.S.C. § 7543(a) is that no state, or state law claim, may “adopt or attempt to enforce any standard relating to the control of emissions from new motor vehicles or new motor vehicle engines subject to this part.” Also fatal to Plaintiff’s argument is the language of 42 U.S.C. § 7545(c)(4)(A)(ii), which states that any attempt to “prescribe or attempt to enforce, for purposes of motor vehicle emission control, any control or prohibition respecting any characteristic or component of a fuel or fuel additive in a motor vehicle or motor vehicle engine” where “the Administrator has prescribed…a control or prohibition applicable to such characteristic or component of a fuel or fuel additive” and the control attempted to be prescribed or enforced is not “identical to the prohibition or control prescribed by the Administrator” is preempted. Plaintiffs’ action, resting on their one central contention that Moving Defendants breached a duty in manufacturing and selling gasoline containing benzene, effectively attempts to enforce a ban of benzene, a component of a fuel in a motor vehicle or motor vehicle engine, under California law, where the EPA has already prescribed an express control of 0.62 vol%.

 

Finally, as argued by Defendants, the cases relied on by Plaintiffs are inapposite. Nelson v. Superior Court (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 689 does not discuss preemption and addresses groundwater pollution, not air pollution or air emissions under the CAA. California v. Navy (9th Cir. 1980) 624 F.2d 885 addresses aircraft emissions and aircraft engines, not motor vehicle fuel or fuel emissions. Plaintiff also cites to Buccery v. General Motors Corp. (1976) 60 Cal.App.3d 533, where the Court permitted an action to proceed on common law theories of liability despite the defendant’s compliance with federal standards, relying in part on Arbet v. Gussarson (1975) 66 Wis.2d 551, in which the Wisconsin Supreme Court rejected the argument that Congress preempted the field of automobile safety regulation by authorizing the establishment of federal standards, reasoning that “Congress felt that federal regulations should be supplementary to the common law of products liability.” Regardless of the applicability of that reasoning to the facts of Buccery, which concerned unsafe design and a product defect claim for lack of a head restraint in a vehicle, the reasoning applied by Arbet regarding Congressional intent is inapplicable to the case at hand based on the express language of the CAA and its clear intent to remove gasoline components and fuel emissions from state reach.

 

In short, Plaintiffs’ state law claims attempt to hold Moving Defendants liable under California state law pursuant to a standard in direct conflict with federal standards, and therefore, Plaintiffs’ claims are preempted by the CAA.

 

b.     Causation

 

In any event, the Court also finds that Defendants are entitled to summary judgment on the independent ground that Plaintiff has failed to establish causation through competent, admissible evidence.

 

To establish the element of actual or legal causation, a plaintiff must show “the defendant’s act or omission was a substantial factor in bringing about the plaintiff’s harm.” (Leslie G. v. Perry & Associates (1996) 43 Cal.App.4th 472, 481.) The “plaintiff must show some substantial link or nexus between the omission and injury.” (Saelzler v. Advanced Group 400 (2001) 25 Cal.4th 763, 778.) The plaintiff must supply evidence that affords a reasonable basis for the conclusion that “it is more likely than not that the conduct of the defendant was a cause in fact of the result,” a mere possibility of causation is not sufficient and the court shall direct a verdict for the moving defendant when the matter remains one of pure speculation or conjecture. (Ortega v. Kmart Corp. (2001) 26 Cal.4th 1200, 1205-06.)

 

Moving Defendants move on the grounds that Plaintiff lacks admissible evidence that Defendants’ gasoline caused Decedent’s AML or death. (Motion, p. 7:10-11.) Plaintiff alleges Decedent’s workplace exposures to gasoline were a cause of his AML and death. (PSUMF, ¶ 5.) It is undisputed that Moving Defendants supply gasoline in California. (PSUMF, ¶ 6.) The causation argument has two parts. First, Moving Defendants argue that Plaintiffs lack evidence that Decedent was exposed to Moving Defendants’ gasoline. Second, they argue that Plaintiffs lack evidence that any such exposure caused Decedent to contract AML. Failure to muster evidence for either part of the causation chain breaks the chain, and Plaintiffs’ claims fail. “If there has been no exposure, there is no causation.” (McGonnell v. Kaiser Gypsum Co. (2002) 98 Cal.App.4th 1098, 1103.) “The mere ‘possibility’ of exposure does not create a triable issue of fact,” and “[i]t is not enough to produce just some evidence,” and it “must be of sufficient quality to allow the trier of fact to find the underlying fact in favor of the party opposing the motion for summary judgment.” (Andrews v. Foster Wheeler LLC (2006) 138 Cal.App.4th 96, 108.)

 

 

Moving Defendants establish that the exposures that allegedly caused Decedent to develop AML occurred from June 2006 to August 2020 while working for DOT, where he was responsible for maintaining the grassy berms along California highways, primarily in the Antelope Valley using “gasoline-powered lawn mowers, gasoline-powered weed-eaters, gasoline-powered chain saws and gasoline-powered hedge trimmers.” (Ex. 1, p. 9, SAC ¶ 11; Ex. 4, p. 37, Interrogatory No. 3, p. 38, Interrogatory No. 6; Ex. 5, p. 51, Interrogatory No. 3, p. 52, Interrogatory No. 6.) The exposure allegedly happened through two ways: 1) inhalation of gasoline vapors from containers at Decedent’s job site, and 2) inhalation of automobile emissions.

 

Moving Defendants present the transcript of the Deposition of Stephen Nicholas Stromberg, who testified on behalf of DOT that it cannot identify the manufacturer of the gasoline in the containers, that the gasoline at the maintenance facility was delivered by Pinnacle Fuel (Pinnacle), and that Pinnacle does not say where the gasoline delivered originated. (Ex. 6, p. 13:23-14:6, 15:19-16:12.) Liz McKinnley testified on behalf of Pinnacle that it purchases gasoline from various sources, that the gasoline it purchased and supplied was unbranded, obtained from either an unbranded pipeline tank fed from multiple sources or purchased from local distributors who do not disclose what producers contribute gasoline or gasoline products into the pipelines Pinnacle draws from, and that there are no records establishing which facilities Pinnacle purchased gasoline products from during the relevant time period. (Ex. 7, p. 11:12-14, 13:10-17, 13:24-15:11.)

 

Moving Defendants also establish that while Decedent was exposed to exhaust from the gasoline powered vehicles using the highways he maintained, Plaintiff does not know or possess evidence of the manufacturers of the gasoline used in the vehicles, but can only speculate or assume that some of the cars used Moving Defendants’ gasoline. (PSUMF, ¶ 10; Ex. 4, p. 36, Interrogatory No. 1; Ex. 5, p. 51, Interrogatory No. 3.) In Plaintiff’s response to Moving Defendants’ interrogatories, which read: “State every fact in support of YOUR contention that [Decedent] was exposed to [Moving Defendants’] gasoline, Plaintiff states: “[Defendants] deliver[] gasoline, gasoline products and diesel fuel to retail gasoline service stations throughout California and specifically in the Antelope Valley in Los Angeles County…. As these fuels are sold at or near freeway entrances near the location where [Decedent] was working, it is reasonable to assume that some of the exhaust fumes inhaled by [Decedent] emanated from these products.” (Ex. 2, p. 20, No. 1; Ex. 3, p. 28, No. 1; Ex. 4, p. 36, No. 1; Ex. 5, p. 50, No. 1.) In response to Moving Defendants’ interrogatories requesting facts to support her contention that Moving Defendants delivered the fuel sold near the location where Decedent worked, Plaintiff states she presumes that because Moving Defendants’ business locations near Decedent’s work locations provide retail gasoline, and said locations advertise use of these fuels, the fuels are delivered to the retail business locations by Moving Defendants. (See Ex. 4, 5.)

 

With respect to whether any such exposure caused Decedent’s cancer, Moving Defendants further provide the Declaration of Dominik Alexander, PhD., M.S.P.H., a retained epidemiologist. Alexander asserts that recognized risk factors for AML include advancing age, male gender, white race, family history of hematopoietic malignancies, tobacco use, increased body weight, and therapeutic radiation, and based on review of the totality of analytical epidemiologic studies, the epidemiologic evidence does not indicate an association between work in highway maintenance or exposure to diesel fuel and risk of AML. (Alexander Decl., ¶¶ 9-10, 12.) Alexander notes that as of November 2025, the International Agency for Research on Cancer has not identified work in highway maintenance or exposure to diesel fuel or gasoline exhaust as having even limited evidence of carcinogenicity for AML, and in IARC monograph 105 that evaluated the carcinogenicity of diesel exhaust, the working group concluded that there was inadequate evidence in humans for the carcinogenicity of gasoline engine exhaust. (Alexander Decl., ¶ 10.) Alexander asserts the exposure conditions necessary to increase the risk of AML from benzene exposure are well established in the literature and are defined as occurring when a cumulative exposure of at least 40-200 ppm-years is obtained within 15 years of diagnosis, citing numerous studies in which no increased risks of AML were reported with less than 40 ppm-years of cumulative exposure to benzene, and states that potential exposure to benzene from exhaust exposure near roadways has been shown to be orders of magnitude lower than the level of benzene required to cause AML (e.g., < 1 ppm). (Alexander Decl., ¶ 11.)

 

Ultimately, Alexander concludes to a reasonable degree of scientific and epidemiologic certainty that the epidemiological evidence does not support an increased risk of AML among highway maintenance workers, that generalizability of data from benzene-exposed study populations to highway maintenance workers is not valid due to the tasks, duties, and exposures associated with highway work, that if highway work entailed exposures to benzene that caused AML, it would have been identified in the voluminous epidemiologic literature, that Decedent had some key risk factors for development of AML, including being a white male with an increased BMI and extensive history of tobacco use, and that a significant portion of AML cases occur in the general population in the absence of identifiable etiologic factors. (Alexander Decl., ¶¶ 12-16.)

 

The evidence presented thus satisfies Moving Defendants’ burden of showing (1) Plaintiff cannot establish that Decedent was exposed to Moving Defendants’ gasoline in his hand tools or from vehicles on the highway, and (2) Plaintiff cannot establish that gasoline emissions or vehicle exhaust were the cause of Decedent’s AML.

 

As a preliminary matter, most of the evidence presented by Plaintiff in opposition is inadmissible hearsay. Plaintiff’s counsel has submitted a declaration attaching a variety of articles and writings on technical and scientific matters,  apparently downloaded from the Internet and offered for the truth of the matter asserted. (Evid. Code, § 1200.) Counsel provides no basis for how he is capable of authenticating the Exhibits. No hearsay exception has been established for any of the Exhibits for which Moving Defendants’ objections were sustained. Plaintiff’s discovery responses also may not be used by Plaintiff in her own favor on summary judgment. (Code of Civil Procedure, § 2030.410; Great American Ins. Cos. v. Gordon Trucking, Inc. (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 445, 450 [a “party may not use its own interrogatory responses in its own favor”].)

 

As to the Declaration of Dr. Clinton Merrill, there are several problems.

 

First, the Court sustains Defendant’s objection to the document in its entirety, as Dr. Merrill was purportedly not disclosed as an expert until August 11, 2026, days before Plaintiff’s opposition was filed and beyond the May 28, 2026 disclosure deadline, and Moving Defendants did timely exchange their expert witness information. (Bonneville Decl., ¶¶ 2-9, Ex. 14, 16, 19.) Code of Civil Procedure section 2034.300 provides that “on objection of any party who has made a complete and timely compliance with Section 2034.260, the trial court shall exclude from evidence the expert opinion of any witness that is offered by any party who has unreasonably failed to do any of the following: (a) List that witness as an expert under Section 2034.260.” Thus, “[a] party opposing a motion for summary judgment ‘may not use declarations of experts not previously disclosed if the time for disclosure under [Code of Civil Procedure section] 2034.210 et seq. has expired.’” (Wong v. Stillwater Ins. Co. (2023) 92 Cal. App. 5th 1297, 1324.) Therefore, the Declaration of Dr. Clinton Merrill must be excluded in its entirety for the purposes of this motion.

 

Even if the Court were to excuse the late submission of the Merrill Declaration (which the Court may not), there are fundamental problems with the Declaration itself. Merrill is a retired treating oncologist. He does not appear to have any training as an epidemiologist. (Merrill Decl., Ex. A.) None of his lectures or publications appear to concern epidemiology or environmental causes of cancer. (Id.) He states that he has experience “in the field of diagnosing, treating, and identification of the sources of blood borne cancers including acute myeloid leukemia,” ( Merrill Decl., ¶ 1) but does not explain what is meant by “the sources of blood borne cancers” and whether that includes the type of analysis he conducted here. His analysis here consisted of reviewing Decedent’s medical records (a task Dr. Merrill is undoubtedly qualified for), and also a review of the scientific literature to determine “the cause of the decedent’s [AML], and whether decedent was at an increased risk for development of this disease from exposure to the defendant’s products. . . .” (Id., ¶ 4.) Whether Dr. Merrill has the sufficient training or expertise to perform a competent search, review, and analysis of the literature on varying exposures to hydrocarbons and causation of types of cancer is not established by either his declaration or CV. As such, the Court would be inclined to exclude his opinion even if timely disclosed. (See Sargon Enterprises, Inc. v. University of Southern California (2012) 55 Cal.4th 747, 769-772 (trial court acts as a gatekeeper to exclude expert opinion testimony that lacks a reliable foundation or is speculative); Evid. Code, § 720 (a witness is qualified to testify as an expert only if he or she has special knowledge, skill, experience, training, or education sufficient to qualify as an expert on the subject to which the testimony relates).)

 

There is also a problem with the degree of confidence in which Dr. Merrill expresses his opinion. While Dr. Merrill concludes his declaration with a statement his opinions are “made to a reasonable degree of scientific hematological oncology certainty” (Merrill Decl., at 13), but nowhere does Merrill assert the opinion that, to a reasonable degree of medical certainty, Decedent’s exposure to Moving Defendants’ products caused Decedent’s cancer.  Dr. Merrill opines that Decedent’s medical records are “consistent with benzene-induced AML” (page 13, line 13) or that computer models show that AML “may” arise at benzene exposure levels lower than those identified by Moving Defendants’ expert (id., at page 4, 7-10) but these opinions fall short of asserting that, to a reasonable degree of medical probability, exposure to Moving Defendants’ product caused Decedent’s AML. Plaintiff must come forward with such expert opinion at summary judgment to demonstrate that there is a triable issue of fact as to causation. (Onglyza Product Cases (2023) 90 Cal.App.5th 776, 792-793 (affirming summary judgment for drug manufacturers where a biostatistician’s opinion that the data showed only a statistical association between the medication and an increased risk of harm was insufficient to create a triable issue as to general causation).)

As to Decedent’s exposure to Moving Defendants’ specific gasoline products, Plaintiff has not presented competent admissible evidence to establish the exposure-in-fact necessary to create a triable issue on causation. Plaintiff provides the California Energy Commission’s Data Report as of July 8, 2026 regarding California oil refinery locations and capacities, which states that Defendant Chevron produces 38.4% of the California crude oil capacity. The Court notes that this source is hearsay, not subject to judicial notice, and includes a note, stating that the data provided “represents total crude oil capacity not gasoline, distillate production, diesel fuel production or production of other products.” Given the capacity of Moving Defendants’ and their respective production and sale outputs of gasoline, Plaintiff argues that it stands to reason that a proportion of the product to which Decedent was exposed came from the Moving Defendants. (Opp., p. 13:12-15.)

 

However, speculation, assumption, and possibility of causation are not enough to raise a triable issue of material fact on summary judgment. Plaintiff has not provided any evidence that Moving Defendants maintained retail business locations near Decedent’s working sites, or that Moving Defendants supplied the gasoline to the retail business locations near Decedent’s work sites, or that the vehicles driving on the highway were carrying and burning gasoline manufactured by or purchased from Moving Defendants, or that Moving Defendants supplied gasoline to Pinnacle, or that Pinnacle then supplied that gasoline to DOT, or that the specific gasoline supplied by Moving Defendants was then used to power Decedent’s hand tools. McKinnley’s deposition testimony that major oil companies generally have their branded Chevron gasoline for retail sales and unbranded Chevron gasoline that goes to resellers like Pinnacle is also insufficient to raise a triable issue of material fact as to whether Plaintiff possesses any admissible evidence to establish more than a possibility of Decedent’s contact with Moving Defendants’ gasoline or vapor, particularly because McKinnley also testified that Pinnacle is not currently purchasing gasoline from Exxon, that she does not know or remember what gasoline was provided to Pinnacle at the relevant times, and that all gasoline bought and sold by Pinnacle is unbranded and the source unknown. (Plaintiff’s Ex. 12, p. 14:4-5, 14:10-16:6.) Plaintiffs’ arguments as to causation are purely speculative and inferential. Reasonable as they may be, inferences based solely on Plaintiffs’ allegations in the SAC without admissible evidence are insufficient to raise a triable issue of material fact in light of Moving Defendants’ unrefuted evidence establishing that Plaintiff has no admissible evidence establishing Decedent’s contact with their specific gasoline or products.

 

As to causation of cancer, “‘the unknown and mysterious etiology of cancer’ is beyond the experience of laymen and can only be explained through expert testimony” that “establishes a reasonably probable causal connection between an act and a present injury.” (Jones v. Ortho Pharm. Corp. (1985) 163 Cal. App. 3d 396, 403.) As the Merrill Declaration has been excluded in its entirety, as with the articles and studies Plaintiff’s counsel sourced from the Internet, Plaintiff has submitted no evidence to establish Decedent’s AML was caused by benzene exposure, let alone benzene from gasoline supplied by Moving Defendants.

 

Accordingly, Moving Defendants’ Motions for Summary Judgment are GRANTED.

 

Given the Court’s rulings on preemption and causation as determinative of the Motions for Summary Judgment, the Court declines to reach the Motions for Summary Adjudication. (See Samara v. Matar (2018) 5 Cal.5th 322, 335 (“Trial courts can decline to reach issues that are unnecessary for judgment”).)

 III.          Legal Standard

Standard for Motions to Compel Further Discovery Responses - Interrogatories – In responding to a set of interrogatories, a party must provide one of the following: (1) an answer “containing the information sought to be discovered”; (2) “an exercise of the party’s option to produce writings”; or (3) an objection to the particular interrogatory.¿(Code Civ. Proc.,  § 2030.210, subd. (a).) Each answer to an interrogatory “shall be as complete and straightforward as the information reasonably available to the responding party permits.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.220, subd. (a).) If any objections are made, “the specific ground for the objection shall be set forth clearly in the response.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.240, subd. (b).) 

Upon receipt of responses to interrogatories, the propounding party may move for an order compelling further responses if they deem the responses to be “evasive or incomplete” or if “[a]n objection to an interrogatory is without merit or too general.” (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300(a)(1), (a)(3).) A motion to compel further responses to interrogatories must be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration showing “a reasonable and good faith attempt at an informal resolution of each issue presented by the motion.” (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2016.040, 2030.300, subd. (b)(1).) The motion must also be accompanied by a separate statement containing the text of the interrogatories at issue, the text of each response at issue, and a statement of the reasons why a further response is required, though the court may permit a party to submit “a concise outline of the discovery request and each response in dispute” in lieu of a separate statement. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (b)(2); Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1345.)  

A motion to compel further response lies where the party to whom the interrogatories were directed gave responses deemed improper by the propounding party; e.g., objections, or evasive or incomplete answers. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300.) Unlike a motion to compel further responses to a request for production, there is no “good cause” requirement applicable to a motion to compel further responses to interrogatories. “The ruling usually is based on consideration of the following factors:  the relationship of the information sought to the issues framed in the pleadings; the likelihood that disclosure will be of practical benefit to the party seeking discovery; the burden or expense likely to be encountered by the responding party in furnishing the information sought.” (Weil, et al., Cal. Prac. Guide, Civil Procedure Before Trial, Ch. 8F-7, ¶ 8:1181; see also Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc v. Superior Court (1968) 263 Cal.App.2d 12, 18–19 [“[I]n deciding a motion under [former] section 2030 the trial court must, of necessity, consider not only the stated objections to the interrogatories, but also the interrogatories themselves, as well as the pleadings, and the contentions of the interrogating party as to the purpose and validity of the interrogatories. If the interrogatories stray too far and seek information which cannot reasonably serve the acknowledged purposes of pretrial discovery, the motion should be denied.”].) If a timely motion to compel has been filed, the burden is on the responding party to justify any objection or failure to fully answer. (Coy v. Superior Court of Contra Costa County (1962) 58 Cal.2d 210, 220–221.) 

If a party fails to serve a timely response to interrogatories, the party waives the right to exercise the option to produce writings under Section 2030.230 and waives any objection to the demand, including objections based on privilege or work product. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (a).) The Court may relieve a party of this waiver only if the party has subsequently served a substantially code-compliant response and the party’s failure to serve a timely response was the result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (a)(1)-(2).)

Standard for Motions to Compel Further Discovery Responses – Requests for Production of Documents – Motions to compel further responses to requests for production must set forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought by the request. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(1).)¿To establish good cause, a discovery proponent must identify a disputed fact that is of consequence in the action and explain how the discovery sought will tend in reason to prove or disprove that fact or lead to other evidence that will tend to prove or disprove the fact.¿(Digital Music News LLC v. Superior Court (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 216, 224 (disapproved on other grounds by Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531).)

A demanding party may move for an order compelling further response to RPDs if (1) a statement of compliance with the demand is incomplete, (2) a representation of inability to comply is inadequate, incomplete, or evasive, or (3) an objection in the response is without merit or too general. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (a).) “A representation of inability to comply with the particular demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling shall affirm that a diligent search and a reasonable inquiry has been made in an effort to comply with that demand. This statement shall also specify whether the inability to comply is because the particular item or category has never existed, has been destroyed, has been lost, misplaced, or stolen, or has never been, or is no longer, in the possession, custody, or control of the responding party. The statement shall set forth the name and address of any natural person or organization known or believed by that party to have possession, custody, or control of that item or category of item.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.230.)

If a party fails to serve a timely response to a request for production, the party waives any objection to the demand, including objections based on privilege or work product. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300, subd. (a).) The Court may relieve a party of this waiver only if the party has subsequently served a substantially code-compliant response and the party’s failure to serve a timely response was the result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300, subd. (a)(1)-(2).)

 IV.          Discussion

 

Application – Plaintiff moves to compel Ford’s further responses to Special Interrogatories, Nos. 10, 26, 47, and 49-58, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 2030.300, subdivision (e) and 2023.030, subdivision (g) on the grounds that Ford has failed to provide code-compliant answers responsive to the request and has asserted meritless objections. a.     Special Interrogatories

 

At issue are Special Interrogatory Nos. 10, 26, 47, and 49-58.

 

No. 10 asks Ford to state all facts supporting its contention that the vehicle does not qualify for repurchase under the Song-Beverly Act. Ford responded that the facts in support of its contention are or may be found in a number of documents produced with Ford’s initial disclosure, listed the relevant documents, and objected on the grounds that the interrogatory was overly broad, unduly burdensome, seeks irrelevant information because it is not limited to a reasonable or relevant timeframe, to the components or systems at issue, or to the allegations in Plaintiff’s Complaint, seeks expert opinions and/or premature disclosure of information developed by Ford’s experts and/or consultants, and seeks information protected from disclosure by the attorney-client privilege and/or the work product doctrine.

 

Plaintiff argues Ford’s reliance on reference to documents provided in response is insufficient, as there is no way for Plaintiff to know what facts within the documents, if any, are responsive to the request.

 

The documents referenced by Ford relate to their general records regarding the vehicle, communications regarding the vehicle, repair and service information, reimbursements to Ford dealers, service contracts, and other generally applicable documents like TSBs and an Owner’s Manual and Warranty Guide. The Court finds Ford’s response generic and non-compliant. Ford argues that it provided documents containing the responsive facts, and they are therefore equally available to Plaintiff. (Opp., p. 7:2-3.) While the general facts themselves may be contained within those documents, what is not available to Plaintiff are what facts Ford is relying on when contending that  Plaintiff’s vehicle does not qualify for repurchase. Ford needs to state why it has determined the vehicle does not qualify.  

 

Interrogatory No. 26 asks Ford to describe all training provided from 2022 to present regarding how to evaluate a pre-litigation repurchase request under the Song-Beverly Act. Ford responded by referring Plaintiff to its Warranty Policy and Procedure Manuals for 2022 to present subject to a protective order, objected on the same grounds as stated above, on relevance grounds as Plaintiff did not request a repurchase or replacement of the subject vehicle before filing this lawsuit, and asserts that the facts and circumstances surrounding vehicle repurchases or replacements vary widely depending on any number of factors, so the training materials made available may vary or differ depending on the circumstances of a repurchase request.

 

Plaintiff again argues Ford’s reliance on reference to documents provided in response is insufficient, as there is no way for Plaintiff to know what facts within the documents, if any, are responsive to the request. Here, the Court disagrees.

 

In response to this request, Ford offered to produce certain documents which outline its training policies and procedures from 2022 through 2025, when Plaintiff filed suit. Ford’s counsel Adam Trott asserts that upon the stipulation and protective order being filed on June 18, 2026, Ford served a Workshop Manual for the vehicle and Ford’s warranty policy and procedure applicable from 2022 through 2025. (Trott Decl., ¶ 10, Ex. A.) (Trott Decl., Ex. B.) Here, unlike Interrogatory No. 10, the information regarding training provided for evaluation of a pre-litigation repurchase request is available to Plaintiff through these documents. There is no basis provided justifying an order requiring Ford to separately describe this information where it has been made available to Plaintiff pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.230.

 

Interrogatory No. 47 asks Ford to describe its efforts to preserve relevant and discoverable information in this case, including preservation letters, the custodians to whom such preservation letters were sent, and the efforts taken to prevent against deletion or destruction of information. Ford objected on the same grounds as stated above and on the ground that the request is irrelevant as Plaintiff had not identified any documents that Ford has failed to produce and that Ford’s records management program is confidential and proprietary. Interrogatory Nos. 49-54 ask Ford to identify each person responsible for the operations, maintenance, expansion, archiving and upkeep of the network where its customer contact call logs (No. 49), documents regarding any pre-litigation repurchase analysis it performed for the vehicle (No. 50), policies and procedures for evaluating a repurchase or replacement under the Song-Beverly Act (No. 51), technical assistance cases between Ford and its authorized repair facilities (No. 52), documents evidencing any consumer complaints for defects in materials and workmanship made to Ford (No. 53), and warranty claim records (No. 54) are electronically stored from 2022 to present. Interrogatory Nos. 55-58 ask Ford to state its document/data retention or destruction policies used by those who evaluate California consumer requests for repurchase or replacement (No. 55), and that govern customer call log records (No. 56), technical assistance cases between Ford and its authorized repair facilities (No. 57), and warranty claim records (No. 58) from 2022 to the present.

 

Ford objected on the same grounds asserted above, that the requests are overly broad, unduly burdensome, seek irrelevant information, seek expert opinions and/or premature disclosure of information developed by Ford’s experts and/or consultants, seek information protected from disclosure by the attorney-client privilege and/or the work product doctrine, and seek confidential and proprietary information.

 

Plaintiff cites to federal authority holding that a defendant is required to disclose certain information about electronically stored information sources it has preserved in support of compelling further responses to these requests, and asserts that Ford’s record retention and policies directly relate to whether Ford has withheld or destroyed responsive evidence and relate to willfulness. In opposition, Ford cites to a number of federal decisions holding that discovery about discovery is, by definition, not information reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence. (Opp., p. 12:15-13:3.)

 

While the Court finds that some of Ford’s objections are without merit, including objections on the basis of expert opinion, premature disclosure of information developed by Ford’s experts, and attorney-client privilege, the Court agrees that these requests are largely irrelevant, overbroad in scope, and the Court likewise fails to see how Ford’s data retention and destruction policies dating back to 2022 are associated with Ford’s repair analysis of Plaintiff’s vehicle or Plaintiff’s ability to prove his case. Plaintiff argues that this information is necessary to confirm Ford’s compliance with their discovery requests, but the Court is not aware of any law permitting discovery to the extent sought here to obtain the names of every person who has been responsible for archival and maintenance of records for three years. Furthermore, as discussed below, it does not appear that Ford failed to produce responsive documents on the grounds that the documents do not exist or have been overwritten or deleted in the normal course of implementation its records retention policies. In short, the information sought is irrelevant to Plaintiff’s claims.

 

Furthermore, Plaintiff’s argument on reply, if nothing else, establishes that these requests are not necessarily specific to the case at hand. Plaintiff argues that his counsel routinely litigates Song-Beverly actions against Ford’s counsel and has observed varying and inconsistent productions by Ford across numerous other cases, raising questions as to whether Ford’s general search, retention, and preservation practices are being consistently applied in every case. (Reply, p. 4:14-18.) Plaintiff does not point to any specific deficiencies in the production in this case to overcome the validity of Ford’s relevance objections, and discovery produced by Ford in unrelated, separate cases has no bearing on the relevancy of the documents sought here.

 

As a final note, Plaintiff relies, in part, on Kwan v. Mercedes-Benz of North America, Inc. (1994) 23 Cal.App.4th 174, 186 to support its argument as to the relevance of retention policies. The passage cited by Plaintiff from Kwan related to a “written policy on implementation of the Act's provisions,” where the plaintiff argued that the defendant’s “lack [of a Song-Beverly implementation policy] is circumstantial evidence of a corporate state of mind that intended to violate the Act.” (Kwan, supra, 23 Cal.App.4th at 186.) The Kwan Court made no mention of relevancy of retention policies.

 

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s request is GRANTED as to Special Interrogatory No. 10. The remainder of Plaintiff’s requests are DENIED.

   II.          Preliminary Procedural Issues

 

Cross-Complaint’s Noncompliance with Pleading Requirements – The FACC is not in compliance with the pleading requirements of the California Rules of Court, which require for all documents filed with the Court specific line spacing, consecutive line numbering and detailed placement and alignment, consecutive page numbering, a footer with the requisite specifications and containing the title of the paper, and compliance with various other formatting rules. (See Cal. Rules of Court rules 2.108 – 2.111.)

 

Meet and Confer Requirement – Before filing a demurrer or motion to strike, the demurring or moving party is required to meet and confer at least five days before the date the responsive pleading is due, in person, by telephone, or by video conference, with the party who filed the pleading for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer or motion to strike. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 430.41, subd. (a), 435.5, subd. (a).).) The demurring or moving party shall file and serve with the demurrer or motion to strike a declaration stating the means by which the parties met and conferred and that the parties did not reach an agreement to resolve the issues raised, or that the party who filed the pleading demurred or objected to failed to respond or otherwise failed to meet and confer in good faith. (Ibid.) 

 

Cross-Defendants each filed a declaration of demurring or moving party regarding meet and confer efforts, and state that Cross-Complainant failed to respond to their requests to meet and confer or otherwise failed to meet and confer in good faith. The Court finds this requirement satisfied.

 III.          Legal Standard

Standard for Demurrer – A party against whom a complaint has been filed may object by demurrer to the pleading on the ground that the pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action, the pleading is uncertain, or that it cannot be ascertained from the pleading whether the contract is written, oral, or implied by conduct in an action founded upon a contract. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (e) – (g).) A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations reasonably, liberally, and in context. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

As a general matter, in a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) “A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Ibid.) Courts must “treat the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Blank, supra, 39 Cal.3d at 318.) The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action. (Hahn, supra, 147 Cal.App.4th at 747.)

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Id.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 126.) The threshold for meeting this burden is low, and “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245).

 

Standard for Motion to Strike – A motion to strike lies where either (1) there is “irrelevant, false or improper matter inserted in any pleading,” or (2) where a pleading or part thereof is “not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule or order of court.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) The Court has the authority to strike out any part of a pleading not filed in conformity with an order of the court. (See Ibid; Ferraro v. Camarlinghi (2008) 161 Cal.App.4th 509, 528.) 

When the pleadings are defective, “the defect may be raised by demurrer or motion to strike, or by motion for judgment on the pleadings.” (Coyne v. Krempels (1950) 36 Cal.2d 257, 262.) The grounds for a motion to strike shall appear on the face of the challenged pleading or form any matter of which the court is required to take judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437, subd. (a).) A matter to be judicially noticed shall be specified in the notice of motion. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437, subd. (b).) The court then may strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading and strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) When the defect which justifies striking a complaint is capable of cure, the court should allow leave to amend. (Perlman v. Municipal Court (1979) 99 Cal.App.3d 568, 575.)

 IV.          Discussion

 

Application – Cross-Defendants demur to the FACC pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.010, subdivisions (e) – (g) on the grounds that the FAC fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action because there is no causal connection between Cross-Defendants’ alleged conduct and damages, is unintelligible, ambiguous, and uncertain as it cannot be ascertained whether an alleged contract is written, oral, or implied in fact, the FACC does not sufficiently plead the elements necessary for each cause of action, and Cross-Complainant references a probate case to which Cross-Defendants are not parties without reference to a real estate sales agreement or agreement with Cross-Defendants. (Demurrers, p. 3:5-4:24.)

 

a.     Cross-Defendants’ Demurrers to the FACC

 

                                                  i.     Uncertain, Ambiguous, and Unintelligible

 

As a preliminary matter, Defendants demur to the FACC pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (f) on the basis that the FACC and allegations within are uncertain, ambiguous, and unintelligible.

 

“A defendant may demur to a complaint… on the ground that the ‘pleading is uncertain.’ (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (f).) The term ‘uncertain’ includes ‘ambiguous” and ‘unintelligible.’ (Ibid.).” (Van Horn v. Dep't of Toxic Substances Control (2014) 231 Cal. App. 4th 1287, 1295.) A “[d]emurrer for uncertainty is directed at the allegations actually made. [Citation.] Demurrer for uncertainty does not lie as to immaterial matter. [Citation.] Nor will a demurrer for uncertainty lie where the facts are presumptively within the knowledge of the demurring parties. [Citation.]” (Dumm v. Pac. Valves (1956) 146 Cal. App. 2d 792, 799.) “A special demurrer on the ground that an answer is (a) ambiguous, (b) unintelligible, or (c) uncertain is insufficient unless the demurrer points out specifically wherein the pleading is ambiguous, uncertain or unintelligible. [Citation.].” (Coons v. Thompson (1946) 75 Cal. App. 2d 687, 690 (citation omitted).)

 

Cross-Defendants specifically argue the FACC is uncertain because it does not allege required facts, contains conclusory allegations, and fails to comply with the California Rules of Court’s pleading standards as it has no page or line numbers. Here, the Court does not find that the FACC is so uncertain, ambiguous, or unintelligible that Cross-Defendants are not on notice of what is being alleged. Here, there are clear legal causes of action and related facts asserted throughout the pleading. Furthermore, Cross-Defendants’ arguments are, in effect, that the FACC fails to plead facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action, which is an issue raised appropriately pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.010, subdivision (e), not subdivision (f).

 

Cross-Defendants’ demurrers are OVERRULED to the extent the demurrers are brought pursuant to Section 430.10(f) for ambiguity. The Court will consider Cross-Defendants’ arguments as to failure to state facts sufficient to support a cause of action pursuant to subdivision (e) and, where applicable, (g).

 

                                                ii.     Action Founded Upon Contract

 

Cross-Defendants argue that it cannot be ascertained from the FACC whether the contract referenced throughout the FACC is written, oral, or implied by conduct, and therefore the FACC is subject to demurrer. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.010, subd. (g).) However, the FACC makes clear that the Agreement at issue is a written contract, as it alleges that Hernandez obtained Cross-Complainant’s signature on the Agreement and related addenda, that the Agreement was C.A.R. Form RLA, that an addendum was also signed by Menjivar as co-agent, cites to specific paragraphs within the Agreement, and establishes that the subsequent purchase agreement presented was written. (FACC, ¶¶ 9, 11-12, 13-14.)

 

Cross-Defendants’ demurrers are OVERRULED to the extent they are brought pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.010, subdivision (g).

 

                                              iii.     First Cause of Action – Breach of Fiduciary Duty

 

“The elements of a cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty are the existence of a fiduciary relationship, breach of fiduciary duty, and damages.” (Oasis West Realty, LLC v. Goldman (2011) 51 Cal.4th 811, 820.) Facts giving rise to a confidential, fiduciary or trustee relationship must be pled, and a “bare allegation that defendants assumed a fiduciary relationship” is a conclusion. (Zumbrun v. Univ. of So. Cal. (1972) 25 Cal.App.3d 1, 13.) “A fiduciary relationship is created where a person reposes trust and confidence in another and the person in whom such confidence is reposed obtains control over the other person’s affairs.” (Lynch v. Cruttenden & Co. (1993) 18 Cal.App.4th 802, 809.) But “[t]he mere placing of a trust in another person does not create a fiduciary relationship...[and] an agreement to communicate one’s knowledge, exercising his special knowledge and skill in the area of learning concerned, does not create a trust but only a contractual obligation.” (Zumbrun, supra, 25 Cal.App.3d at 13.)

 

Cross-Defendants argue the FACC does not plead damages connected to the allegations and does not allege what duty is owed to Cross-Complainant. (Demurrers, p. 5:19-22.)

 

The FACC alleges facts which, taken as true, establish the following: (1) Cross-Defendants entered into the Agreement with Cross-Complainant, establishing that Hernandez was listing agent of record and Menjivar was co-agent (FACC, ¶ 9); (2) by undertaking to act as Cross-Complainant’s listing agents, Cross-Defendants assumed a fiduciary relationship with Cross-Complainant and owed her the affirmative duties of utmost care, integrity, honesty, and loyalty under Civil Code section 2079.16 and Business and Professions Code section 10176 (FACC, ¶ 25); (3) Cross-Defendants engaged in numerous breaches of these duties supported by specific factual allegations, including, but not limited to, obtaining Cross-Complainant’s signature on a defective Agreement before she had any legal authority to bind the estate in knowing violation of Probate Code section 840, failing to submit the Agreement to his broker within 72 hours of execution thereby eliminating all oversight, utilizing the improper form Agreement as required under Probate Code section 10160, failed to disclose the relationship between Menjivar and Buyer and denying the relationship when confronted, blocking the return of Buyer’s deposit against Cross-Complainant’s direction, filing an incorrectly prepared bond waiver petition and falsely representing that refiling was impossible, keeping Cross-Complainant legally incapacitated, and filing litigation to enforce contracts they know are void, non-compliant, and unsupported (FACC, ¶ 26); and (4) that as a direct and proximate result of these breaches, Cross-Complainant has suffered and continues to suffer damages, including the costs and burden of defending this action, loss of the ability to administer and protect her late mother's estate for nearly a year, emotional distress arising from the coordinated exploitation of her grief and financial vulnerability, and other damages in amounts to be proven at trial (FACC, ¶¶ 23, 27.)

 

Thus, the FACC states specific factual allegations that Cross-Defendants owed Cross-Complainant specific fiduciary duties under specific statutes as listing agents, that they breached those duties, and Cross-Defendant suffered damages as a result. (See Branch v. Homefed Bank (1992) 6 Cal.App.4th 793, 800 [recovery of emotional distress damages is permitted where the defendant’s conduct constitutes a breach of fiduciary duties].) Moreover, “‘[t]he law imposes on a real estate agent ‘the same obligation of undivided service and loyalty that it imposes on a trustee in favor of his beneficiary.’ [Citations.] This relationship not only imposes upon him the duty of acting in the highest good faith towards his principal but precludes the agent from obtaining any advantage over the principal in any transaction had by virtue of his agency. [Citation.] ‘Such an agent is charged with the duty of fullest disclosure of all material facts concerning the transaction that might affect the principal’s decision.’ [Citations.]” (Roberts v. Lomanto (2003) 112 Cal. App. 4th 1553, 1562-1563.)

 

Finally, “‘A written contract may be pleaded either by its terms—set out verbatim in the complaint or a copy of the contract attached to the complaint and incorporated therein by reference—or by its legal effect. [Citation.] In order to plead a contract by its legal effect, plaintiff must ‘allege the substance of its relevant terms. This is more difficult, for it requires a careful analysis of the instrument, comprehensiveness in statement, and avoidance of legal conclusions.’” (Heritage Pacific Financial, LLC v. Monroy (2013) 215 Cal. App. 4th 972, 993.) Here, the legal effect of the Agreement between Cross-Complainant is sufficiently pleaded, as Cross-Complainant sets forth the exact standardized form used, certain terms under the Agreement, and the legal effect in that Cross-Defendants assumed responsibility as acting agents for the Property.

 

The FACC pleads facts sufficient to state a cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty. Cross-Defendants’ demurrers are OVERRULED.

 

Cross-Defendants did not demur to individual causes of action asserted within the Cross-Complaint and demurred only to the Cross-Complaint as a whole on the grounds that all causes of action fail to state facts sufficient to constitute causes of action. Accordingly, that the demurrer is not sustained as to one cause of action necessarily means that Cross-Defendants’ demurrer will be overruled in full. Cross-Defendant may, consistent with the Code of Civil Procedure, bring a motion for judgment on the pleadings as to such individual claims.

 

b.     Cross-Defendants’ Motions to Strike Portions of the FACC

 

Cross-Defendants also move to strike the requests for punitive damages in paragraphs 28, 34, and the Prayer for Relief in the FACC. (Mtns, p. 3:1-3.)

 

Punitive damages may be recovered upon a proper showing of malice, fraud, or oppression. (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (a).) “Malice” is defined as conduct intended to cause injury to a person or despicable conduct carried on with a willful and conscious disregard for the rights or safety of others. (Turman v. Turning Point of Cent. Cal., Inc. (2010) 191 Cal.App.4th 53, 63.) “Oppression” means despicable conduct subjecting a person to cruel and unjust hardship, in conscious disregard of the person’s rights. (Ibid.) “Fraud” is an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known by defendant, with intent to deprive a person of property, rights or otherwise cause injury. (Ibid.) Conclusory allegations, devoid of any factual assertions, are insufficient to support a conclusion that parties acted with oppression, fraud or malice. (Smith v. Superior Court (1992) 10 Cal.App.4th 1033, 1042.)

 

To succeed on a motion to strike punitive damages allegations, it must be said as a matter of law that the alleged behavior was not so vile, base, or contemptible that it would not be looked down upon and despised by ordinary decent people. (Angie M. v. Superior Court (1995) 37 Cal. App. 4th 1217, 1228-1229.) “Under the statute, malice does not require actual intent to harm.  Conscious disregard for the safety of another may be sufficient where the defendant is aware of the probable dangerous consequences of his or her conduct and he or she willfully fails to avoid such consequences.  Malice may be proved either expressly through direct evidence or by implication through indirect evidence from which the jury draws inferences.”  (Pfeifer v. John Crane, Inc. (2013) 220 Cal. App. 4th 1270, 1299 (internal quotations omitted).) 

 

“In order to state a prima facie claim for punitive damages, a complaint must set forth the elements as stated in the general punitive damage statute, Civil Code section 3294.” (Turman v. Turning Point of Central California, Inc. (2010) 191 Cal.App.4th 53, 63.) “In addition to the requirement that the operative complaint set forth the elements as stated in section 3294, it must include specific factual allegations showing that defendant's conduct was oppressive, fraudulent, or malicious to support a claim for punitive damages. [Citation.] Punitive damages may not be pleaded generally.” (Today's IV v. Los Angeles County Metropolitan Transportation Authority (2022) 83 Cal. App. 5th 1137, 1193.) “Not only must there be circumstances of oppression, fraud or malice, but facts must be alleged in the pleading to support such a claim.” (Grieves v. Superior Court (1984) 157 Cal. App. 3d 159, 166.)

 

As to Cross-Complainant’s request for punitive damages for Cross-Defendants’ alleged breach of fiduciary duty, the FACC sufficiently alleges fraud. Indeed, breach of fiduciary duty in a confidential relationship alone is “a species of fraud,” that “fraud itself implies malice,” and the maintenance of breach of fiduciary duty claim based on fraud and intentional misrepresentations, wrongful conduct, and concealment in violation of these duties, where Cross-Complainant was approached by Cross-Defendants shortly after the passing of her mother so they may control Probate proceedings and engage in a conflicted sale of the Property to a listed co-agent and deprive Cross-Complainant of the Estate Property is sufficient to maintain a punitive damages request. (Nicholson v. Rose (1980) 106 Cal.App.3d 457, 462.)

 

For the same reasons, and because the FACC sufficiently alleges intentional fraudulent conduct on behalf of Cross-Defendants intended to deprive Cross-Complainant of her rights to the Property in the context of a fiduciary relationship, the request for punitive damages for fraud is sufficiently pled to survive Cross-Defendants’ motions to strike.

 

Accordingly, Cross-Defendants’ Motions to Strike are DENIED.
