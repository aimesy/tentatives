Shaheriav Kham v. Los Angeles County Metropolitan Transportation Authority, et al.

 

Wednesday, July 1, 2026

 

 

 

CASE NUMBER: 23STCV02798

 

UNOPPOSED

Defendants ABM Industry Groups, LLC, Morlin Asset Management, LP, and Los Angeles County Metropolitan Transportation Authority’s Motion for Terminating Sanctions or to Dismiss Complaint for Want of Prosecution ___________________________________________________________________

 

Facts: This is an action for premises liability. The Complaint alleges as follows. Plaintiff Shaheriav Kham (“Plaintiff”) slipped and fell on a puddle at Union Station in Los Angeles. (Complaint at p. 4.) 

Procedural History: Plaintiff filed the Complaint on February 8, 2023, alleging one cause of action for premises liability.

The court on October 24, 2025, granted Defendant ABM Industry Groups, LLC’s (“ABM”) motion to continue trial.

On October 30, 2024, the court granted nonparty County of Los Angeles’ motion to withdraw answer.

On December 6, 2024, the court denied Defendant ABM’s motion to compel responses to form interrogatories from Plaintiff as moot.

On April 28, 2025, the court denied Defendant ABM’s motion to compel further as moot, following an informal discovery conference.

Defendant Los Angeles County Metropolitan Transit Authority (“LACMTA”) had default entered against it on July 28, 2025.

On July 31, 2025, Jesse Terrell’s motion to be relieved as counsel for Plaintiff was granted.

On October 15, 2025, the court granted Defendant LACMTA’s motion for relief from default.

LACMTA filed an answer on February 2, 2026.

On February 10, 2026, this matter was transferred from Department 27 to Department 61.

 

Defendants filed the present motion for terminating sanctions or to dismiss case for want of prosecution on

 

Analysis

I.                   MOTION FOR TERMINATING SANCTIONS

The court may impose terminating sanctions, including an order striking pleadings, an order dismissing an action, or an order rendering judgment by default against a party, for conduct that is a misuse of the discovery process. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030.) This conduct includes “[f]ailing to respond or to submit to an authorized method of discovery,” and “[d]isobeying a court order to provide discovery.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.010.)

Ultimate discovery sanctions are justified where there is a willful discovery order violation, a history of abuse, and evidence showing that less severe sanctions would not produce compliance with discovery rules.  (Van Sickle v. Gilbert (2011) 196 Cal.App.4th 1495, 1516.)  Dismissal is a drastic measure, and terminating sanctions should only be ordered when there has been previous noncompliance with a rule or order and it appears a less severe sanction would not be effective.  (Link v. Cater (1998) 60 Cal.App.4th 1315, 1326.)  “[A] penalty as severe as dismissal or default is not authorized where noncompliance with discovery is caused by an inability to comply rather than willfulness or bad faith.”  (Brown v. Sup. Ct. (1986) 180 Cal.App.3d 701, 707.)

Defendants ABM Industry Groups, LLC (“ABM”), Morlin Asset Management, LP (“Morlin”), and Los Angeles County Metropolitan Transportation Authority (“LACMTA”, or collectively “Defendants”) move for terminating sanctions against Plaintiff Shaheryar Khan (named in the complaint as “Shaheriav Kham”, referred to herein as “Plaintiff”)for violation of discovery obligations. Defendants alternately seek discretionary dismissal under Code of Civil Procedure § 583.410, based on Plaintiff’s delay in prosecution.

Defendants seek relief on the following facts. Plaintiff filed the complaint on February 8, 2023, and was then represented by counsel. Plaintiff did not initially serve responses to Defendant ABM’s discovery until ABM filed a motion to compel on November 4, 2024, after which Plaintiff served responses consisting only of objections, which had by then been waived. (Lescaille Decl. ¶¶ 8–9.) ABM filed a motion to compel further, which was heard on April 28, 2025, which was resolved when Plaintiff’s counsel stated that the attorney-client relationship had broken down, a motion to be relieved as counsel was imminent, and volunteered to orally withdraw the objections. (Lescaille Decl. ¶¶ 10–12.) The court granted the motion to be relieved as counsel brought by Plaintiff’s former attorney on July 31, 2025, after which Plaintiff appeared at hearings to represent that he was seeking new counsel. (Lescaille Decl. ¶¶ 13–14.)

Plaintiff’s deposition was initially noticed on February 4, 2025, and scheduled for April 18, 2025, though the deposition (and the then-calendared trial date of June 23, 2025) had to be rescheduled due to the motion to be relieved as counsel. (Lescaille Decl. ¶¶ 16–17.) A second deposition notice was served on February 4, 2026, with the deposition to take place on March 10, 2026, but Plaintiff did not object or appear, and a certificate of non-appearance was taken on that date. (Lescaille Decl. ¶¶ 4–5, Exh. C.) 

Plaintiff has also failed to appear for his independent medical examination, noticed to take place on November 3, 2026. (Lescaille Decl. ¶ 22.)

Plaintiff has served no discovery in this action. (Lescaille Decl. ¶  25.) And despite appearing for hearings following the withdrawal of his counsel on July 31, 2025, October 15, 2025, December 8, 2025, and January 30, 2026, Plaintiff has not appeared for his deposition, has not served discovery, and has filed no opposition to the present motion.

Terminating sanctions are of doubtful availability on the above facts. Although there appears to be little question concerning Plaintiff’s abdication of his discovery obligations, he has heretofore not been subject to any order compelling him to provide responses or appear for deposition. Dismissing the case for failure to comply with discovery obligations would be a hard sanction where no prior order had directed compliance upon the penalty of lesser sanctions.

However, Defendants have shown entitlement to dismissal pursuant to the court’s discretionary power to dismiss a case for lack of prosecution under Code of Civil Procedure § 583.410. Under that statute, “The court may in its discretion dismiss an action for delay in prosecution pursuant to this article on its own motion or on motion of the defendant if to do so appears to the court appropriate under the circumstances of the case.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 583.410,, subd. (a).) Dismissal may be granted upon the following applicable conditions:

The action is not brought to trial within the following times:

(A) Three years after the action is commenced against the defendant unless otherwise prescribed by rule under subparagraph (B).

(B) Two years after the action is commenced against the defendant if the Judicial Council by rule adopted pursuant to Section 583.410 so prescribes for the court because of the condition of the court calendar or for other reasons affecting the conduct of litigation or the administration of justice.

(Code Civ. Proc. § 583.420, subd. (a)(2).) The California Rules of Court (“CRC”)permit discretionary dismissal under this latter subdivision “if the action has not been brought to trial or conditionally settled within two years after the action was commenced against the defendant.” (CRC Rule 3.1340, subd. 9a).) The rules prescribe consideration of the following non-exclusive factors:

In ruling on the motion, the court must consider all matters relevant to a proper determination of the motion, including:

(1) The court's file in the case and the declarations and supporting data submitted by the parties and, where applicable, the availability of the moving party and other essential parties for service of process;

(2) The diligence in seeking to effect service of process;

(3) The extent to which the parties engaged in any settlement negotiations or discussions;

(4) The diligence of the parties in pursuing discovery or other pretrial proceedings, including any extraordinary relief sought by either party;

(5) The nature and complexity of the case;

(6) The law applicable to the case, including the pendency of other litigation under a common set of facts or determinative of the legal or factual issues in the case;

(7) The nature of any extensions of time or other delay attributable to either party;

(8) The condition of the court's calendar and the availability of an earlier trial date if the matter was ready for trial;

(9) Whether the interests of justice are best served by dismissal or trial of the case; and

(10) Any other fact or circumstance relevant to a fair determination of the issue.

The court must be guided by the policies set forth in Code of Civil Procedure section 583.130.[1]

(CRC Rule 3.1342, subd. (e).)

The facts set forth in the moving papers favor dismissal here. This is a simple slip-and-fall case, with a Complaint alleging one cause of action for premises liability. It has nonetheless been pending since February 8, 2023, more than three years before Defendants filed the present motion on May 13, 2026. Originally set for trial on June 23, 2025, the matter has been repeatedly continued based on the withdrawal of Plaintiff’s counsel and Plaintiff’s subsequent inability to find counsel to represent himself. Said withdrawal was based on a breakdown of communication with Plaintiff’s former counsel, which now appears to have replicated itself with respect to Defendants, who have been unable to secure Plaintiff’s appearance for deposition despite repeated notices and attempts to meet and confer. And despite the continuances of trial, Plaintiff has filed no substitution of counsel form to indicate that counsel has been retained. Plaintiff has served no discovery in this matter, did not appear for the last status conference on April 21, 2026, and has failed to file any opposition to the present motion. This last fact “may be construed by the court as an admission that the motion is meritorious, and the court may grant the motion without a hearing on the merits.” (CRC Rule 3.1342, subd. (b).)[2]

Accordingly, the motion for sanctions is DENIED, and the motion to dismiss is GRANTED. No monetary sanctions are ordered, as such sanctions are sought in connection with the application for discovery sanctions and Plaintiff’s failure to appear for deposition, when no motion to compel such deposition has been made. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 2025.450, subd. (g) [permitting sanctions].)

Heaton v. State of California, et al.

 

Wednesday, July 1, 2026

 

 

 

CASE NUMBER: 24STCV32684

 

OPPOSED

___________________________________________________________________

 

Defendants Department of Consumer Affairs, Bureau of Automotive Repair, Devin Taylor, and George Urena’s Demurrer and Motion to Strike Portions of Plaintiff Jamie Heaton's First Amended Complaint.

___________________________________________________________________

 

Facts: This is an action for wrongful termination. The Complaint alleges as follows. Jamie Heaton (Plaintiff) is a 51-year-old male who was employed by the State of California (Defendants) for 13 years as a Program Representative with the Department of Consumer Affairs Bureau of Automotive Repair. (See Compl. 4:2–5.) Plaintiff suffered from a disability that required him to take diabetes medication, which had a side effect that caused excessive diarrhea and thus required Plaintiff to be accommodated with immediate access to a bathroom. (See id. at 4:13–16.) In April 2018, Plaintiff requested an accommodation for immediate restroom access, which was granted through reassignment to the gas audits unit. (See id. at 4:17–21.)

 

However, in July 2021, Plaintiff's supervisor attempted to reassign him to roadside duties despite Plaintiff's medical restrictions, and Plaintiff alleges he was only temporarily accommodated with less suitable duties. (See id. at 5:8–20.) In September 2022, Plaintiff agreed and accepted a transfer to Paramount, Cal. based on an alleged promise by Defendants of eventual relocation to Culver City, Cal., which was not fulfilled. (See id. at 5:21–28; 6:1–11.) Following a March 2023 office flood, Plaintiff alleges he lacked safe restroom access, developed a respiratory infection, and was ultimately placed on administrative leave before being constructively discharged on November 30, 2023. (See id. at 6:13–28; 7:1–22.)

 

Procedural History: Plaintiff filed the Complaint on December 11, 2024, alleging eight causes of action:

 

1.      Discrimination in Violation of the FEHA

2.      Hostile Work Environment Harassment in Violation of the FEHA

3.      Retaliation in Violation of the FEHA

4.      Failure to Provide Reasonable Accommodation in Violation of the FEHA

5.      Failure to Engage in the Interactive Process in Violation of FEHA

6.      Failure to Prevent Discrimination, Harassment, or Retaliation in Violation of FEHA

7.      Whistleblower Retaliation (Labor Code § 1102.5)

8.      Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress

 

On July 8, 2025, this court sustained Defendants’ demurrer to the seventh and eighth causes of action, and motion to strike prayer for punitive damages, with leave to amend.

 

Plaintiff filed a First Amended Complaint on July 28, 2025, alleging the same causes of action.

 

Defendants filed the present demurrer and motion to strike on August 27, 2025.

 

Plaintiff filed oppositions on June 17, 2026.

 

Defendants filed replies on June 24, 2026.

 

Analysis:

I.                    DEMURRER

A demurrer should be sustained only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed. (Code Civ. Pro., §§ 430.30, et seq.) In particular, as is relevant here, a court should sustain a demurrer if a complaint does not allege facts that are legally sufficient to constitute a cause of action. (See id. § 430.10, subd. (e).) As the Supreme Court held in Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311: “We treat the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law. . . . Further, we give the complaint a reasonable interpretation, reading it as a whole and its parts in their context.” (Id. at p. 318; see also Hahn. v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747 ["A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed."])

 

“In determining whether the complaint is sufficient as against the demurrer … if on consideration of all the facts stated it appears the plaintiff is entitled to any relief at the hands of the court against the defendants the complaint will be held good although the facts may not be clearly stated.”  (Gressley v. Williams (1961) 193 Cal.App.2d 636, 639.)

 

“A demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Khoury v. Maly’s of Cal., Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.) Such demurrers “are disfavored, and are granted only if the pleading is so incomprehensible that a defendant cannot reasonably respond.” (Mahan v. Charles W. Chan Insurance Agency, Inc. (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 841, 848.)

 

A demurrer should not be sustained without leave to amend if the complaint, liberally construed, can state a cause of action under any theory or if there is a reasonable possibility the defect can be cured by amendment. (Schifando v. City of Los Angeles, supra, 31 Cal.4th at 1081.) The demurrer also may be sustained without leave to amend where the nature of the defects and previous unsuccessful attempts to plead render it probable plaintiff cannot state a cause of action. (Krawitz v. Rusch (1989) 209 Cal.App.3d 957, 967.)

 

Defendants Department of Consumer Affairs, Bureau of Automotive Repair, (sued as State of California) and Devin Taylor and George Urena (collectively “Defendants”), demurrer to the eighth cause of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress (“IIED”) in the First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) filed by Plaintiff Jamie Heaton (“Plaintiff”).

 

To state a prima facie case for IIED, a plaintiff must allege: (1) extreme and outrageous conduct by the defendant; (2) intent to cause, or reckless disregard of the probability of causing, emotional distress; (3) severe or extreme emotional distress; and (4) actual and proximate causation. (Miller v. Fortune Commercial Corp. (2017) 15 Cal.App.5th 214, 228–29.) “Outrageous conduct” is that which exceeds all bounds of decency in a civilized society. (Ibid.) The defendant's conduct must be directed to the plaintiff, but malicious or evil purpose is not essential to liability.” (So v. Shin (2013) 212 Cal.App.4th 652, 671.)

 

Defendants argue that the allegations supporting the IIED claim consist entirely of personnel management activity, which cannot on its own form the basis for an IIED claim. (Demurrer at pp. 7–10.) This court previously sustained Defendants’ demurrer to the same claim on the same basis, reasoning that Plaintiff had pleaded only the failure to accommodate and the imposition of administrative leave, which constitute personnel management actions insufficient to create liability for outrageous conduct. (7/8/2026 Order.)

The allegations supporting the IIED claim in the FAC are essentially the same as those supporting the claim in the prior iteration of the pleadings. The allegations of outrageous conduct consist of personal management activity –i.e. the failure to provide a requested accommodation, and the placing of Plaintiff on administrative leave. Such conduct has been deemed insufficient as a matter of law to support an IIED claim. “Managing personnel is not outrageous conduct beyond the bounds of human decency, but rather conduct essential to the welfare and prosperity of society.” (Janken v. GM Hughes Electronics (1996) 46 Cal.App.4th 55, 80.) Even if improperly motivated, personnel decisions do not rise to the level required for IIED. (Ibid.) Plaintiff’s Complaint alleges employment-related decisions such as transfers, assignments, and administrative leave, it fails to state a valid IIED claim.

The only addition made to these claims with the filing of Plaintiff’s FAC is made in paragraph 14c, in which Plaintiff alleges that in January 2020, before his remote assignment during the COVID pandemic, his return to work in an accommodated position, his September 2022 reassignment to the Paramount location, and his November 2023 administrative leave (FAC ¶¶ 14d, 14e, 14i, 15), that supervisor Jose Mauberis retaliated against Plaintiff by “shunning him” and “micromanaging him.” (FAC ¶ 14c; Opposition at pp. 4–5.) This de minimis addition is akin to the personnel management activity already alleged. (See Light v. Department of Parks & Recreation (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 75, 102 [holding insufficient to constitute outrageous conduct evidence that supervisor “refused to listen to Light's complaints about retaliation, organized the anti-Hurley counseling session after the gun scope incident, encouraged Seals's efforts to silence Light, awarded Seals a commendation after the February 23 incident, and participated in the Department's retaliation against Light (including denying promised training and shifting Light's work location),” because such conduct constituted “common—though ultimately misguided—supervisory actions”].) The new allegation is insufficient to support a claim for outrageous conduct on its own, and does not support the creation of an IIED claim considered in connection with the other personnel management activity alleged. 

The demurrer is therefore SUSTAINED as to the eighth cause of action. Although Plaintiff seeks leave to amend (Opposition at pp. 7–8), the present FAC is itself the product of amendment, leave for which was granted specifically to address the precise defect discussed above. As Plaintiff has failed to rectify the defect, and presents no basis to believe further amendment would cure it, no leave to amend is granted.

 

II.                 MOTION TO STRIKE 

Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading, may serve and file a notice of motion to strike the whole or any part thereof. (Code Civ. Proc., § 435(b)(1)). The notice of motion to strike a portion of a pleading shall quote in full the portions sought to be stricken except where the motion is to strike an entire paragraph, cause of action, count or defense. (California Rules of Court Rule 3.1322.)

The grounds for a motion to strike shall appear on the face of the challenged pleading or form any matter of which the court is required to take judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437(a)). The court then may strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading and strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) When the defect which justifies striking a complaint is capable of cure, the court should allow leave to amend. (Perlman v. Municipal Court (1979) 99 Cal.App.3d 568, 575.)

Under Civil Code section 3294(a), punitive damages are only permitted in non-contract actions where the defendant is guilty of “oppression, fraud, or malice.” Subdivision (c) defines (1) “malice” as conduct intended to cause injury or despicable conduct carried out with a willful and conscious disregard of others’ rights or safety; (2) “oppression” as despicable conduct that subjects someone to unjust hardship in conscious disregard of their rights; and (3) “fraud” as intentional misrepresentation or concealment made to deprive someone of property or legal rights. Mere commission of a tort is insufficient—punitive damages require more. (Taylor v. Superior Court (1979) 24 Cal.3d 890, 894.) Negligence, gross negligence, or recklessness does not satisfy the standard. (Dawes v. Superior Court (Mardian) (1980) 111 Cal.App.3d 82, 88–89.) However, punitive damages may be recovered in negligence cases if the defendant acted with conscious disregard for others' safety. (Pfeifer v. John Crane, Inc. (2013) 220 Cal.App.4th 1270, 1299.) Conduct based on conscious disregard must be both despicable and willful. (College Hosp. Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 713.)

Plaintiff here seeks punitive damages against individual Defendants Devin Taylor and George Urena.[1] The FAC alleges that Plaintiff repeatedly complained to Taylor that he required accommodations, but Taylor did not provide them. (FAC ¶¶ 14h, 14k, 14q.) Similar allegations are made against Urena, along with the allegation that Urena placed Plaintiff on administrative leave. (FAC ¶¶ 14q, 15.) The FAC adds the following new addition to paragraph 14k: “Despite Goodwin, Taylor, and Urena being aware of Plaintiff’s need and request for accommodations, they continuously failed to relocate Plaintiff which caused emotional distress in reckless disregard for Plaintiff’s interests.” (FAC ¶ 14k.)

Plaintiff’s amended pleading is sufficient to state a prayer for punitive damages against the individual defendants. Plaintiff alleges that he was entitled to a medical accommodation, that the individual defendants were actually aware of his need for the same, and did not provide the accommodations in reckless disregard for Plaintiff’s rights. This suffices at the pleading stage to allege malice and oppression, and thus entitlement to punitive damages. (See Perkins v. Superior Court (1981) 117 Cal.App.3d 1, 6 [allegation of “retaliation” as motive supported allegation of malice].) Unlike the prior Complaint, which contained only omnibus allegations of malice, oppression, and fraud, as to all Defendants (Complaint ¶ 18), Plaintiff here pleads specific allegations in relation to the individual defendants.

Accordingly, the motion to strike is DENIED.

Hilda Martinez v. Kia America, Inc., et al.

 

Wednesday, July 1, 2026

 

 

 

CASE NUMBER: 26STCV04713

 

OPPOSED

___________________________________________________________________

 

Defendant Kia America, Inc.’s Motion for Protective Order ___________________________________________________________________

 

Facts: This is a lemon law action. The Complaint alleges as follows. Plaintiff Hilda Martinez (“Plaintiff”) purchased a 2025 Kia Sorento in February 2025, with an express written warranty. (Complaint ¶¶ 5–7.) The vehicle contained or developed defects that the manufacturer has been unable to remedy within the warranty period. (Complaint ¶¶ 8–12.)  

Procedural History: Plaintiff filed the Complaint on February 11, 2026, alleging two causes of action:

1.    Breach of Implied Warranty of Merchantability

2.    Breach of Express Warranty

Defendant Kia America Inc. filed an answer on April 3, 2026.

Defendant filed the present motion for protective order on June 1, 2026.

Plaintiff filed an opposition on June 16, 2026.

Defendant filed a reply on June 22, 2026.

Analysis

I.                   MOTION FOR PROTECTIVE ORDER

“When an inspection, copying, testing, or sampling of documents, tangible things, places, or electronically stored information has been demanded, the party to whom the demand has been directed, and any other party or affected person, may promptly move for a protective order.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2031.060, subd. (a).) The party moving for the order must make a showing of good cause to spare them from “unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression, or undue burden and expense.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2031.060, subd. (b).) A party responding to interrogatories may seek a protective order upon similar terms. (Code Civ. Proc. §2030.090.)

Defendant Kia America, Inc. (“Defendant”) seeks entry of a protective order governing discovery in this action, in which a party disclosing information would be permitted to designate it as “confidential” and thereby limit its use to litigation in the present action, and limit access to parties, their representatives, and associated persons participating in the litigation. (Proposed Order.) Per the proposed order, a party may challenge any “confidential” designation and thereby require the designating party to seek an order from the court upholding its confidential designation. (Ibid.)

Defendant argues that the protective order is necessary because the initial disclosures required by Code of civil Procedure § 871.26 implicate confidential information that would cause Defendant competitive harm if disclosed to the public. (Motion at pp. 5–8.) The motion is supported by the declaration of Defendant employee Art Mendoza, who specifically addresses the contemplated release of  “[Defendant] KA’s internal warranty and pricing data, KA’s Consumer Assistance Center Case Report, KA’s Techline Assistance Case Center Report and Service Policies and Procedures.” (Mendoza Decl. ¶ 4.) Mendoza states that Defendant treats this information “as confidential proprietary information, providing limited access to this information to only certain individuals in a secure manner.” (Mendoza Decl. ¶ 5.) He states that “the handling of consumer contact and warranty repairs is a central part of KA’s business model,” and that automotive manufacturers compete with one another on the basis of customer satisfaction. (Mendoza Decl. ¶ 6.)

Mendoza states that “warranty repair and pricing data shows the amount reimbursed to a KA authorized service dealer for a specific repair,” and such amounts are competitively sensitive. (Mendoza Decl. ¶ 7.)  Techline Assistance Center Case Reports likewise “contain diagnosis, repair, or other matters involving a particular vehicle or model of vehicles,” and thus contain “specific technical processes” that “are considered proprietary and confidential to KA.” (Mendoza Decl. ¶ 8.) Mendoza likewise asserts that records of communications between Defendant and its customers are also considered confidential. (Mendoza Decl. ¶ 9.)

Plaintiff Hilda Martinez (“Plaintiff”) opposes the motion, arguing that Defendant cannot seek a protective order in relation to initial disclosures required by Code of Civil Procedure § 871.26, and that any objections to disclosure under that statute have been waived by Defendant’s opting into that statutory framework. (Opposition at pp. 4–7.) Plaintiff further argues that the declaration supporting the motion is conclusory and Defendant has not shown good cause for the order sought. (Opposition at pp. 7–9.) 

The state has two substantial interests in regulating pretrial discovery. The first is to facilitate the search for truth and promote justice. The second is to protect the legitimate privacy interests of the litigants and third parties. [Citation.] ‘The interest in truth and justice is promoted by allowing liberal discovery of information in the possession of the opposing party. [Citation.] The interest in privacy is promoted by restricting the procurement or dissemination of information from the opposing party upon a showing of “good cause.” [Citations.]’ [Citation.] The trial court is in the best position to weigh fairly the competing needs and interests of parties affected by discovery. [Citation.]” (Stadish v. Superior Court, supra, 71 Cal.App.4th at p. 1145, 84 Cal.Rptr.2d 350.) A trial court must balance the various interests in deciding “whether dissemination of the documents should be restricted.” (Id. at p. 1146, 84 Cal.Rptr.2d 350.) Further, even where a motion for a protective order is denied in whole or in part, the trial court may still impose “terms and conditions that are just.”

(Nativi v. Deutsche Bank National Trust Co. (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 261, 317.)

Plaintiff has not shown that Defendant may not seek a protective order as to the documents at issue under Code of Civil Procedure § 871.26. That statute requires, in certain lemon law actions, initial disclosures of certain categories of documents within 60 days of the filing of a responsive pleading “without awaiting a discovery request.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 871.26, subd. (b).) The statute requires disclosures to the other party, but Defendant here does not seek to withhold disclosure, but to provide disclosure conditioned upon entry of a protective order permitting confidential designations. Nothing in the statute purports to limit the producing party’s right to seek a protective order upon the grounds set forth in Code of Civil Procedure § 2031.060. While Defendant by opting in to the statutory scheme under Code of Civil Procedure § 871.29 might be said to have waived objections to disclosure, again, nothing in the statute indicates that the disclosing party may not seek to limit the use of disclosed materials that are confidential in nature pursuant to a timely motion for protective order.

The motion is also supported by good cause. The Mendoza declaration does not provide mere conclusory attestations of harm (See Nativi, supra,  223 Cal.App.4th at p. 315), but specific identifications of the proprietary information at issue, including warranty repair and pricing data and technical fixe, and the competitive harm to be suffered by disclosure.

Plaintiff finally objects that the proposed protective order would inhibit their ability to conduct non-party discovery. (Opposition at p. 10.) However, the protective order permits disclosure of confidential materials to “any deposition or non-trial hearing witness,” provided they are informed of the protective order and its terms. (Proposed Order, ¶ 7f.) Plaintiff has thus not demonstrated any prejudice resulting from the protective order.

The motion is GRANTED.
