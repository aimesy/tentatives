PLAINTIFF BRIGHT UGOCHUKWU EKE’S MOTION FOR LEAVE TO FILE FIRST AMENDED COMPLAINT TO ALLEGE PUNITIVE DAMAGES

 

Background

Plaintiff Bright Ugochukwu Eke (“Plaintiff”) filed this action on July 30, 2024 against Defendants Prime Vip Limo, LLC, Ally Limo, Inc., Precious Memories Limo, Inc., and Devon Pernell Logan (“Logan”) (collectively, “Defendants”). The complaint alleges four causes of action for (1) negligence, (2) negligence per se, (3) negligent entrustment, and (4) negligent hiring, supervision & retention.

Plaintiff now moves to file a first amended complaint (“FAC”). Defendants oppose. Plaintiff replied.

Legal Standard

Pursuant to ¿Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (a)(1)¿, “[t]he court may, in furtherance of justice, and on any terms as may be proper, allow a party to amend any pleading.” Amendment may be allowed at any time before or after commencement of trial. ((Code Civ. Proc., § 576.) “[T]he court’s discretion will usually be exercised liberally to permit amendment of the pleadings. The policy favoring amendment is so strong that it is a rare case in which denial of leave to amend can be justified.” ((Howard v. County of San Diego (2010) 184 Cal.App.4th 1422, 1428 (internal citations omitted).) “If the motion to amend is timely made and the granting of the motion will not prejudice the opposing party, it is error to refuse permission to amend and where the refusal also results in a party being deprived of the right to assert a meritorious cause of action or a meritorious defense, it is not only error but an abuse of discretion.” ((Morgan v. Superior Court of Los Angeles County (1959) 172 Cal.App.2d 527, 530.) Prejudice includes “delay in trial, loss of critical evidence, or added costs of preparation.” ((Solit v. Tokai Bank (1999) 68 Cal.App.4th 1435, 1448.) However, “even if a good amendment is proposed in proper form, unwarranted delay in presenting it may—of itself—be a valid reason for denial.” ((Roemer v. Retail Credit Co. (1975) 44 Cal.App.3d 926, 939-940.) In most cases, the factors for timeliness are: (1) “lack of diligence in offering the amendment after knowledge of the facts” and (2) “effect of the delay on the adverse party.” ((Id. at p. 940.)

Ordinarily, the court will not consider the validity of the proposed amended pleading in ruling on a motion for leave since grounds for a demurrer or motion to strike are premature; however, the Court does have discretion to deny leave to amend where “a proposed amendment [fails] to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action” and “the insufficiency could not be cured by further appropriate amendment.” (California Casualty General Ins. Co. v. Superior Court (1985) 173 Cal.App.3d 274, 280-281 (California Casualty), overruled on other grounds by Kransco v. American Empire Surplus Lines Ins. Co. (2000) 23 Cal.4th 390, 403-406.) 

A motion to amend a pleading before trial must include a copy of the proposed amendment or amended pleading, which must be serially numbered to differentiate it from previous pleadings or amendments. ((Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1324(a)(1).) The motion must also state what allegations are proposed to be deleted or added, by page, paragraph, and line number. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1324(a)(2)-(3).) Finally, “[a] separate declaration must accompany the motion and must specify: (1) The effect of the amendment; (2) Why the amendment is necessary and proper; (3) When the facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered; and (4) The reasons why the request for amendment was not made earlier.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1324(b), emphasis added.)

Discussion

A.    Procedural Requirements

The Court finds that Plaintiff complied with the procedural requirements of California Rules of Court, rule 3.1324. Plaintiff seeks to add punitive damages to his complaint, and Plaintiff’s counsel explains that “[t]he proposed amendment is necessary and proper because the facts supporting punitive damages were not known to Plaintiff when the original Complaint was filed.” (Neubauer Decl., ¶ 12.) Plaintiff’s counsel also explains that “[t]he effect of the proposed amendment is to add factual allegations and a prayer for punitive damages against Defendant Logan and the corporate defendants. The proposed First Amended Complaint does not add new parties. It does not change the underlying incident, the parties involved, the vehicle involved, or the core negligence and corporate-negligence theories already pleaded.” (Neubauer Decl., ¶ 13.)

Further, Plaintiff’s counsel describes the facts and timeline for when Plaintiff learned about the facts and allegations giving rise to this amendment. Specifically, “Plaintiff’s proposed punitive damages allegations are based on facts learned through discovery, including Logan’s felony history, his false verified discovery response denying any felony conviction, his later deposition admission, his supplemental discovery responses identifying the felony convictions, and the corporate defendants’ hiring, screening, training, supervision, retention, and entrustment of Logan to operate a commercial passenger vehicle” (Neubauer Decl., ¶ 14), and Plaintiff’s counsel explains the timeline of discovery responses and taking Logan’s deposition. (See Neubauer Decl., ¶¶ 4-11.)

In regard to the corporate defendants, Plaintiff’s counsel explains that “Plaintiff has also sought discovery from the corporate defendants concerning hiring, screening, training, supervision, retention, policies and procedures, safe driving policies, collision investigation, and entrustment. Those topics are directly relevant to Plaintiff’s existing negligent entrustment and negligent hiring, supervision, and retention claims. They are also directly relevant to whether corporate officers, directors, or managing agents authorized, ratified, or consciously disregarded Logan’s unfitness within the meaning of Civil Code section 3294(b).” (Neubauer Decl., ¶ 15.) Counsel further states that “Defendants have refused to produce Persons Most Qualified on the corporate negligence topics, relying on Diaz v. Carcamo and a purported admission of vicarious liability or course and scope.” (Neubauer Decl., ¶ 16.) “On June 4, 2026, Plaintiff sent a detailed meet-and-confer letter to defense counsel regarding the PMQ deposition and punitive damages issues. In that letter, Plaintiff proposed a compromise: Defendants would produce PMQ testimony on the noticed topics, including hiring, training, supervision, and retention, and if the testimony did not support punitive damages, Plaintiff would withdraw the punitive damages issue and not seek leave to amend.” (Neubauer Decl., ¶ 18.) Plaintiff’s counsel explains that Defendants declined, and due to this, it “places Plaintiff in an unfair catch-22: Defendants refuse to provide discovery regarding corporate knowledge, hiring, screening, training, supervision, retention, entrustment, authorization, ratification, and managing-agent conduct on the ground that punitive damages have not yet been pleaded with sufficient specificity, while also opposing Plaintiff’s ability to amend the pleading based on facts already discovered.” (Neubauer Decl., ¶¶ 20-21.)

Plaintiff’s counsel attests that “Plaintiff did not seek this amendment earlier because the facts supporting punitive damages were not known when the original Complaint was filed. The relevant facts were learned through discovery, including Logan’s deposition testimony and supplemental discovery responses.” (Neubauer Decl., ¶ 22.) Further, “Plaintiff has acted diligently. Plaintiff took Logan’s deposition, obtained supplemental discovery responses, pursued PMQ discovery, participated in the Court’s informal discovery process, and sent a substantive meet-and-confer letter proposing a procedure that could have eliminated the need for this motion if PMQ testimony did not support punitive damages.” (Neubauer Decl., ¶ 23.)

Plaintiff’s counsel also attaches the proposed FAC as Exhibit E to her declaration. The proposed FAC includes the proposed additions in bold, and the proposed deletions crossed out, thus complying with California Rules of Court, rule 3.1324(a)(2)-(3). Accordingly, Plaintiff meets the procedural requirements necessary for leave to be granted.

B.     Substantive Arguments

Defendants’ opposition dedicates the entirety of the motion to arguing the facts of the case. Defendants assert that Plaintiff’s causes of action do not support punitive damages; the complaint does not meet the pleading standard for punitive damages; and Plaintiff is attempting to circumvent the holding in Diaz v. Carcamo (2011) 51 Cal.4th 1148.

In response, Plaintiff asserts that “Defendants’ opposition fails to identify any actual prejudice that would justify denying Plaintiff leave to file a First Amended Complaint. Instead, Defendants rely primarily on timing and generalized claims that the proposed amendment will require them to respond to additional allegations. That is not legal prejudice under California's liberal amendment policy.” (Reply, 2:2-5.) Plaintiff properly contends Defendants do not assert prejudice. However, the Court recognizes that leave to amend may also properly be denied when “the insufficiency of the proposed amendment is established by controlling precedent and … [can]not be cured by further appropriate amendment.” (California Casualty, supra, 173 Cal.App.3d at pp. 280-281; see, e.g., Foxborough v. Van Atta (1994) 26 Cal.App.4th 217, 231 [not an abuse of discretion to deny leave to amend when “proposed amendment would have been futile because it was barred by the statute of limitations” with no indication of relating back to the original complaint].)

Regarding the sufficiency of the allegations, Plaintiff asserts that he has a “good-faith factual basis supporting the proposed allegations. The evidence developed during discovery establishes that Logan concealed material information concerning his criminal history by falsely denying under oath that he had ever sustained a felony conviction. Logan later admitted that denial was false and subsequently identified convictions for robbery and forcible oral copulation involving a minor by force or fear. Plaintiff does not contend that the existence of the convictions alone establishes punitive liability. Rather, the convictions, the concealment of those convictions, the false verified discovery responses, and the circumstances surrounding Logan’s operation of a commercial passenger vehicle collectively provide a good-faith basis to investigate and allege conduct constituting oppression, fraud, malice, or conscious disregard for the rights and safety of others within the meaning of Civil Code section 3294.” (Mot., 8:14-25.)

Plaintiff also contends that “Civil Code section 3294(b) authorizes punitive damages against a corporate employer where an officer, director, or managing agent authorized or ratified wrongful conduct, employed or retained an unfit employee with conscious disregard for the rights or safety of others, or was otherwise personally guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice. The evidence developed thus far establishes that Logan possessed a criminal history bearing directly upon his fitness to operate a commercial passenger vehicle transporting members of the public. The circumstances further suggest that a reasonable hiring and screening process may have revealed information relevant to Logan’s fitness. The extent of the corporate defendants’ knowledge, investigation, hiring practices, retention decisions, supervision, training, and ratification remains uniquely within Defendants’ possession.” (Mot., 9:5-15.)

Defendants make assertions regarding Plaintiff’s inability to meet the pleading standard for punitive damages. However, the Court finds that these assertions are properly the subject of demurrer.

As to Defendants’ assertions that Plaintiff cannot have a cause of action for punitive damages due to futility, Defendants first assert that “[t]he felony at issue has nothing to do with Defendant Logan’s driving capabilities, impairment of [sic] the date of the accident, or inability to competently operate a motor vehicle.” (Opp., 7:10-12 [emphasis omitted].) The Court is not persuaded, that from the pleadings, Logan’s felony cannot be related to the assertion of punitive damages and negligence. Additionally, Plaintiff asserts in response that “[t]he punitive damages allegations arise from the same incident and from Defendants’ own conduct, including issues relating to hiring, screening, training, supervision, retention, and entrustment. These issues are not collateral or unrelated. They go directly to the circumstances under which Defendant Logan was permitted to operate the vehicle involved in the collision.” (Reply, 5:21-24.)

Defendants also contend that “Plaintiff seeks to take advantage of the ‘carve out’ from the doctrine set forth in Diaz by claiming punitive damages. If Plaintiff were to successfully allege punitive damages, Plaintiff could maintain the discoverability and relevance of employer misconduct. However, the ‘carve out’ is unavailable in this action by virtue of lack of any facts even indicating, let alone establishing, ‘malice, fraud, or oppresssion’ [sic] by either Defendant Logan or the Corporate Defendants.” (Opp., 8:19-23.) Although Defendants contend this carve out is unavailable due to Plaintiff’s lack of facts, Defendants do not demonstrate that the alleged insufficiencies cannot be cured by further amendment. In light of the policy of liberally granting leave to amend, it is preferable to allow the amendment and permit a challenge to the amended pleadings on demurrer, motion for judgment on the pleadings, or otherwise. ((See Kittredge Sports Co. v. Superior Court (1989) 213 Cal.App.3d 1045, 1048 [“even if the proposed legal theory is a novel one, ‘the preferable practice would be to permit the amendment and allow the parties to test its legal sufficiency by demurrer, motion for judgment on the pleadings or other appropriate proceedings.’”].) Accordingly, the Court finds that granting leave to amend is proper.

Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, Plaintiff’s motion for leave to file an amended complaint is GRANTED.

Plaintiff is ordered to give notice of this Order.

DEFENDANT GENERAL MOTORS, LLC.’S DEMURRER TO THE FIFTH CAUSE OF ACTION OF PLAINTIFF’S FIRST AMENDED COMPLAINT

           

            Background

On June 30, 2025, Plaintiff Jesus Gutierrez Becerra (“Plaintiff”) filed this lemon law action against Defendant General Motors, LLC (“GM”). On December 29, 2025, Plaintiff filed a first amended complaint (“FAC”) alleging five causes of action for (1) violation of subdivision (d) of Civil Code section 1793.2; (2) violation of subdivision (b) of Civil Code section 1793.2; (3) violation of subdivision (a)(3) of Civil Code section 1793.2; (4) breach of the implied warranty of merchantability; and (5) fraudulent inducement - concealment.

GM now demurs to the fifth cause of action of the FAC for fraudulent inducement - concealment. Plaintiff opposes. GM replied.

Legal Standard

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda¿(2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) In testing the sufficiency of the complaint, the court assumes the truth of properly pleaded factual allegations, facts reasonably inferred from those expressly pleaded, and judicially noticed matters. ((Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) “A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the¿evidence or other extrinsic matters. Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed.” (SKF Farms v. Superior Court¿(1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 902, 905.) Accordingly, “[w]hether the plaintiff will be able to prove the pleaded facts is irrelevant to ruling upon the demurrer.” (Stevens v. Superior Court (1986) 180 Cal.App.3d 605, 609-10.)

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10 subdivisions (e) and (f), a demurrer may be filed if the pleading is uncertain or does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. For purposes of ruling on a demurrer, all facts pleaded in a complaint are assumed to be true, but the reviewing court does not assume the truth of conclusions of law. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hosp. Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 967.)

Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment, otherwise, it is abuse of discretion. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349.)

Discussion

GM demurs to Plaintiff’s fifth cause of action for fraudulent inducement - concealment because the complaint fails to allege facts sufficient to state a cause of action. Specifically, GM asserts that the claim is barred by the economic loss rule.

A.    Allegations of the FAC

Plaintiff alleges that “[o]n or about January 3, 2022, Plaintiff entered into a warranty contract with Defendant GM regarding a 2022 GMC Sierra 1500, . . . which was manufactured and/or distributed by Defendant GM.” (FAC, ¶ 6.) Plaintiff alleges that “[t]he warranty contract contained various warranties, including but not limited to the bumper-bumper warranty, powertrain warranty, emission warranty, etc.” (FAC, ¶ 7.)

Plaintiff alleges that “[d]efects nonconformities to warranty manifested themselves within the applicable express warranty period, including but not limited to transmission defects, engine defects, electrical defects; among other defects and non-conformities.” (FAC, ¶ 18.) Plaintiff pleads that the defects rendered the vehicle “worthless and/or de minimis.” (FAC, ¶ 20.) Thus, “GM had an affirmative duty to promptly offer to repurchase or replace the Subject Vehicle at the time if failed to conform the Subject Vehicle to the terms of the express warranty after a reasonable number of repair attempts,” but “failed to either promptly replace the Subject Vehicle or to promptly make restitution.” (FAC, ¶¶ 21-22.)

B.     Economic Loss Rule

As a preliminary matter, GM appears to demur to the FAC on the grounds that Plaintiff’s fifth cause of action for fraudulent inducement - concealment is barred by the economic loss rule. GM asserts that “assuming arguendo the existence of the warranty alone is sufficient to establish the requisite transactional relationship to trigger a duty to disclose, the fraud claim is barred by the economic loss rule under Rattagan as the claim cannot ‘be established independently of the parties’ contractual rights and obligations’ nor ‘exposes the plaintiff to a risk of harm beyond the reasonable contemplation of the parties when they entered into the contract.’” (Dem., 6:25-7:1.) However, GM does not include any further discussion of the economic loss rule in its papers.

The Court notes that the Rattagan v. Uber Technologies (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1, 20 (Rattagan) test does not apply here because Plaintiff alleges fraud in the inducement, not in performance of the contract. The Supreme Court in Rattagan stated that “Rattagan’s tort claims are, of course, based on alleged conduct committed during the contractual relationship but purportedly outside the parties’ chosen rights and obligations.” (Rattagan, supra, 17 Cal.5th at p. 41, fn. 12 [emphasis in original].) Dhital v. Nissan North America (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 828 (Dhital) is the more apposite precedent here because the case addressed the application of the economic loss rule to claims against vehicle manufacturers for fraudulent inducement by concealment. The Dhital Court expressly held that the economic loss rule does not bar such claims. (Id. at p. 843.) The Court of Appeal reasoned that “the plaintiffs’ fraudulent inducement claim alleges presale conduct by Nissan (concealment) that is distinct from Nissan’s alleged subsequent conduct in breaching its warranty obligations.” (Id. at p. 841.)

However, given GM’s failure to present a substantive discussion on this, the Court declines to discuss the merits of the economic loss rule any further. Accordingly, Plaintiff’s fraudulent inducement - concealment cause of action is not barred by the economic loss rule.

C.     Failure to State a Cause of Action

            GM demurs to the FAC on the grounds that Plaintiff’s fifth cause of action is insufficiently alleged because Plaintiff failed to plead fraud with the requisite specificity and does not establish a duty.

GM contends that Plaintiff fails to establish the existence of a duty to disclose, stating that “[e]ven if Plaintiff has pled their actual and justifiable reliance and factual content of GM’s omission, they fail to allege that GM had a legal duty to disclose those facts.” (Dem., 9:26-27.) GM asserts that because “Plaintiff does not allege any kind of fiduciary relationship with GM,” Plaintiff “must allege a direct transactional relationship with GM.” (Dem., 10:11-12.)

“There are four circumstances in which nondisclosure or concealment may constitute actionable fraud: (1) when the defendant is in a fiduciary relationship with the plaintiff; (2) when the defendant had exclusive knowledge of material facts not known to the plaintiff; (3) when the defendant actively conceals a material fact from the plaintiff; and (4) when the defendant makes partial representations but also suppresses some material facts.” (Bigler-Engler v. Breg, Inc. (2017) 7 Cal.App.5th 276, 311 (Bigler-Engler).) When a fiduciary relationship does not exist, “only the latter three circumstances may apply;” however, those circumstances “‘presuppose[] the existence of [a] relationship between the plaintiff and defendant in which a duty to disclose can arise.” (Ibid.) In the absence of a fiduciary duty, “[a] duty to disclose facts arises only when the parties are in a relationship that gives rise to the duty, such as seller and buyer, employer and prospective employee, doctor and patient, or parties entering into any kind of contractual arrangement.” (Ibid.) “Although, typically, a duty to disclose arises when a defendant owes a fiduciary duty to a plaintiff [citation], a duty to disclose may also arise when a defendant possesses or exerts control over material facts not readily available to the plaintiff.” (Jones v. ConocoPhillips Co. (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 1198, 1199.)

In the FAC, Plaintiff pleads that “GM acquired its knowledge of the Transmission Defect and its potential consequences prior to Plaintiff acquiring the subject vehicle, though sources not available to consumers such as Plaintiff, including but not limited to pre-production testing data, early consumer complaints about the Transmission Defect made directly to Defendant GM and its network of dealers, aggregate warranty data compiled from GM’s network of dealers, testing conducted by Defendant GM in response to these complaints, as well as warranty repair and part replacements data received by Defendant GM from Defendant GM’s network of dealers, amongst other sources of internal information.” (FAC, ¶ 57a.) Plaintiff also pleads that “GM was in a superior position from various internal sources to know (or should have known) the true state of facts about the material defects contained in vehicles equipped with the 8-speed transmission.” (FAC, ¶ 57b.) Plaintiff states that he “could not reasonably have been expected to learn of or discover the Vehicle’s Transmission Defect and its potential consequences until well after Plaintiff purchased the Vehicle.” (FAC, ¶ 57c.) Plaintiff pleads that GM had exclusive knowledge of non-public and internal facts, how and why GM had this knowledge, and that this occurred prior to the sale of Plaintiff’s vehicle. The Court finds that these statements sufficiently demonstrate that Plaintiff pleaded that GM “possesse[d] or exert[ed] control over material facts not readily available to” him, which demonstrates the existence of a duty. (Jones v. ConocoPhillips Co., supra, 198 Cal.App.4th at p. 1199.) However, a cause of action for fraud has a heightened pleading standard, and as GM contends, Plaintiff’s FAC “fails to plead specific facts to satisfy fraud’s heightened pleading standard.” (Dem., 6:10-11.)

“[T]he elements of an action for fraud and deceit based on concealment are: (1) the defendant must have concealed or suppressed a material fact, (2) the defendant must have been under a duty to disclose the fact to the plaintiff, (3) the defendant must have intentionally concealed or suppressed the fact with the intent to defraud the plaintiff, (4) the plaintiff must have been unaware of the fact and would not have acted as he did if he had known of the concealed or suppressed fact, and (5) as a result of the concealment or suppression of the fact, the plaintiff must have sustained damage.” (Marketing West, Inc. v. Sanyo Fisher (USA) Corp. (1992) 6 Cal.App.4th 603, 612-613.) Less specificity is required if it appears from the nature of allegations that the defendant must necessarily possess full information, or if the facts lie more in the knowledge of opposing parties.¿(Alfaro v. Community Housing Improvement System & Planning Assn., Inc.¿(2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1356, 1384-1385 (Alfaro).)

As to specificity, GM first asserts that “Plaintiff’s fraudulent concealment claim fails to allege with particularity the defect GM allegedly concealed. The Complaint merely describes a list of ways in which the 2022 GMC Sierra 1500’s transmission may be defective.” (Dem., 9:7-9.)  GM also asserts that “Plaintiff fails to identify by name who made the alleged omissions when they purchased the Subject Vehicle.” (Dem., 9:23-24.) Moreover, GM asserts that each element under the fraud cause of action lacks specificity.

            In opposition, Plaintiff contends that they pled with the requisite specificity because when fraud occurs through non-disclosure, it is not practical to allege facts for something that did not happen. (Alfaro, supra, 171 Cal.App.4th at p. 1384 [“How does one show ‘how’ and ‘by what means’ something didn’t happen, or ‘when’ it never happened, or ‘where’ it never happened?”].) Instead, such details, in the context of a fraudulent concealment claim, “are properly the subject of discovery, not demurrer.” (Id., at pp. 1384-1385.) Specifically, Plaintiff contends that he “alleged (1) misrepresentation/concealment, (2) knowledge of falsity, (3) intent to induce reliance, (4) justifiable reliance, and (5) damages.” (Opp., 3:25-27.) However, in the complaint, Plaintiff only includes general, boilerplate assertions.

Thus, GM’s argument is persuasive because, although Plaintiff pleads general facts that fit the required elements for a fraud claim, they are not pled with the specificity that a fraud claim requires. Alfaro prescribes a lower standard for specificity, but Plaintiff must include some level of specificity, such as pleading what specific misrepresentations or omissions he relied on that induced his purchase. Accordingly, the demurrer is granted as to the fifth cause of action, with leave to amend.

Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, GM’s demurrer to the fifth cause of action is SUSTAINED with leave to amend.

The Court orders Plaintiff to file and serve an amended complaint, if any, within 20 days of this Order. If no amended complaint is filed within 20 days of this Order, GM is ordered to file and serve its answer within 30 days of this Order.

GM is ordered to give notice of this Order.

However, given GM’s failure to present a substantive discussion on this, the Court declines to discuss the merits of the economic loss rule any further. Accordingly, Plaintiff’s fraudulent inducement - concealment cause of action is not barred by the economic loss rule.

C.    Failure to State a Cause of Action

            GM demurs to the FAC on the grounds that Plaintiff’s fifth cause of action is insufficiently alleged because Plaintiff failed to plead fraud with the requisite specificity and does not establish a duty.

GM contends that Plaintiff fails to establish the existence of a duty to disclose, stating that “[e]ven if Plaintiff has pled their actual and justifiable reliance and factual content of GM’s omission, they fail to allege that GM had a legal duty to disclose those facts.” (Dem., 9:26-27.) GM asserts that because “Plaintiff does not allege any kind of fiduciary relationship with GM,” Plaintiff “must allege a direct transactional relationship with GM.” (Dem., 10:11-12.)

“There are four circumstances in which nondisclosure or concealment may constitute actionable fraud: (1) when the defendant is in a fiduciary relationship with the plaintiff; (2) when the defendant had exclusive knowledge of material facts not known to the plaintiff; (3) when the defendant actively conceals a material fact from the plaintiff; and (4) when the defendant makes partial representations but also suppresses some material facts.” (Bigler-Engler v. Breg, Inc. (2017) 7 Cal.App.5th 276, 311 (Bigler-Engler).) When a fiduciary relationship does not exist, “only the latter three circumstances may apply;” however, those circumstances “‘presuppose[] the existence of [a] relationship between the plaintiff and defendant in which a duty to disclose can arise.” (Ibid.) In the absence of a fiduciary duty, “[a] duty to disclose facts arises only when the parties are in a relationship that gives rise to the duty, such as seller and buyer, employer and prospective employee, doctor and patient, or parties entering into any kind of contractual arrangement.” (Ibid.) “Although, typically, a duty to disclose arises when a defendant owes a fiduciary duty to a plaintiff [citation], a duty to disclose may also arise when a defendant possesses or exerts control over material facts not readily available to the plaintiff.” (Jones v. ConocoPhillips Co. (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 1198, 1199.)

In the FAC, Plaintiff pleads that “GM acquired its knowledge of the Transmission Defect and its potential consequences prior to Plaintiff acquiring the subject vehicle, though sources not available to consumers such as Plaintiff, including but not limited to pre-production testing data, early consumer complaints about the Transmission Defect made directly to Defendant GM and its network of dealers, aggregate warranty data compiled from GM’s network of dealers, testing conducted by Defendant GM in response to these complaints, as well as warranty repair and part replacements data received by Defendant GM from Defendant GM’s network of dealers, amongst other sources of internal information.” (FAC, ¶ 57a.) Plaintiff also pleads that “GM was in a superior position from various internal sources to know (or should have known) the true state of facts about the material defects contained in vehicles equipped with the 8-speed transmission.” (FAC, ¶ 57b.) Plaintiff states that he “could not reasonably have been expected to learn of or discover the Vehicle’s Transmission Defect and its potential consequences until well after Plaintiff purchased the Vehicle.” (FAC, ¶ 57c.) Plaintiff pleads that GM had exclusive knowledge of non-public and internal facts, how and why GM had this knowledge, and that this occurred prior to the sale of Plaintiff’s vehicle. The Court finds that these statements sufficiently demonstrate that Plaintiff pleaded that GM “possesse[d] or exert[ed] control over material facts not readily available to” him, which demonstrates the existence of a duty. (Jones v. ConocoPhillips Co., supra, 198 Cal.App.4th at p. 1199.) However, a cause of action for fraud has a heightened pleading standard, and as GM contends, Plaintiff’s FAC “fails to plead specific facts to satisfy fraud’s heightened pleading standard.” (Dem., 6:10-11.)

“[T]he elements of an action for fraud and deceit based on concealment are: (1) the defendant must have concealed or suppressed a material fact, (2) the defendant must have been under a duty to disclose the fact to the plaintiff, (3) the defendant must have intentionally concealed or suppressed the fact with the intent to defraud the plaintiff, (4) the plaintiff must have been unaware of the fact and would not have acted as he did if he had known of the concealed or suppressed fact, and (5) as a result of the concealment or suppression of the fact, the plaintiff must have sustained damage.” (Marketing West, Inc. v. Sanyo Fisher (USA) Corp. (1992) 6 Cal.App.4th 603, 612-613.) Less specificity is required if it appears from the nature of allegations that the defendant must necessarily possess full information, or if the facts lie more in the knowledge of opposing parties.¿(Alfaro v. Community Housing Improvement System & Planning Assn., Inc.¿(2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1356, 1384-1385 (Alfaro).)

As to specificity, GM first asserts that “Plaintiff’s fraudulent concealment claim fails to allege with particularity the defect GM allegedly concealed. The Complaint merely describes a list of ways in which the 2022 GMC Sierra 1500’s transmission may be defective.” (Dem., 9:7-9.)  GM also asserts that “Plaintiff fails to identify by name who made the alleged omissions when they purchased the Subject Vehicle.” (Dem., 9:23-24.) Moreover, GM asserts that each element under the fraud cause of action lacks specificity.

            In opposition, Plaintiff contends that they pled with the requisite specificity because when fraud occurs through non-disclosure, it is not practical to allege facts for something that did not happen. (Alfaro, supra, 171 Cal.App.4th at p. 1384 [“How does one show ‘how’ and ‘by what means’ something didn’t happen, or ‘when’ it never happened, or ‘where’ it never happened?”].) Instead, such details, in the context of a fraudulent concealment claim, “are properly the subject of discovery, not demurrer.” (Id., at pp. 1384-1385.) Specifically, Plaintiff contends that he “alleged (1) misrepresentation/concealment, (2) knowledge of falsity, (3) intent to induce reliance, (4) justifiable reliance, and (5) damages.” (Opp., 3:25-27.) However, in the complaint, Plaintiff only includes general, boilerplate assertions.

Thus, GM’s argument is persuasive because, although Plaintiff pleads general facts that fit the required elements for a fraud claim, they are not pled with the specificity that a fraud claim requires. Alfaro prescribes a lower standard for specificity, but Plaintiff must include some level of specificity, such as pleading what specific misrepresentations or omissions he relied on that induced his purchase. Accordingly, the demurrer is granted as to the fifth cause of action, with leave to amend.

Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, GM’s demurrer to the fifth cause of action is SUSTAINED with leave to amend.

The Court orders Plaintiff to file and serve an amended complaint, if any, within 20 days of this Order. If no amended complaint is filed within 20 days of this Order, GM is ordered to file and serve its answer within 30 days of this Order.

GM is ordered to give notice of this Order.

PETITIONER MOBILITAS INSURANCE COMPANY OF ARIZONA’S MOTION TO STAY ARBITRATION PROCEEDINGS

 

Background

On April 18, 2024, Petitioner Mobilitas Insurance Company of Arizona (“Petitioner”) filed a “Petition to Open Superior Court File for Purpose of Establishing Superior Court Jurisdiction Over Uninsured Motorist Arbitration Matter” against Respondents Amir Zaytoun, Carey Wood, and Arman Grigoryan (“Respondent”).

Petitioner now moves to stay arbitration proceedings pending resolution of Respondent’s occupational accident insurance claim. Respondent opposes, and Respondents Amir Zaytoun and Carey Wood filed a notice of joinder in the opposition. Petitioner replied.

Legal Standard

“On petition of a party to an arbitration agreement alleging the existence of a written agreement to arbitrate a controversy and that a party to the agreement refuses to arbitrate that controversy, the court shall order the petitioner and the respondent to arbitrate the controversy if it determines that an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists, unless it determines that: … (c) A party to the arbitration agreement is also a party to a pending court action or special proceeding with a third party, arising out of the same transaction or series of related transactions and there is a possibility of conflicting rulings on a common issue of law or fact.” (Code of Civ. Proc., § 1281.2, subd. (c).) “If the court determines that a party to the arbitration is also a party to litigation in a pending court action or special proceeding with a third party as set forth under subdivision (c), the court … may stay arbitration pending the outcome of the court action or special proceeding.” (Code of Civ. Proc., § 1281.2, subd. (d)(4).)

“Once a dispute is submitted to arbitration, the [California Arbitration Act] contemplates only limited, if any, judicial involvement.” (Briggs v. Resolution Remedies¿(2008) 168 Cal.App.4th 1395, 1400 (Briggs).) “[A]n arbitration has a life of its own outside the judicial system. [Citation.] The trial court may not step into a case submitted to arbitration and tell the arbitrator what to do and when to do it....”¿(Id. at p. 1401 [internal quotations omitted].)

Discussion

In support of the motion, Petitioner explains that “[t]his is an uninsured motorist arbitration case. At the time of the automobile accident, Respondent was driving for Lyft. Lyft has occupational accident insurance with United Fire Insurance Company/Blue Star Claims under Business & Professions Code Section 7455(a). Occupational accident insurance is the equivalent of worker’s compensation insurance for purposes of underinsured motorist arbitration. (Bus. & Prof. Code § 7455(e).) Thus, Mobilitas is entitled to reduce underinsured motorist benefits by the amount paid in occupational accident insurance benefits. Respondent has explicitly refused to file a claim with Blue Star under the belief that he is not obligated to do so. This is wrong.” (Notice of Mot., 2:3-10.) Petitioner asserts that “Claimant is required to resolve an occupational accident insurance claim before arbitration can proceed.” (Mot., 4:21-22.)

Petitioner also asserts that “[a]ny benefits received from occupational accident insurance are considered amounts payable under worker’s compensation law or disability benefits under Insurance Code Section 11580, et seq. (Bus & Prof. Code § 7455(e).).” (Mot., 4:11-13.) Insurance Code section 11580.2, subdivision (f) provides in part as follows:

 

“The policy or an endorsement added thereto shall provide that the determination as to whether the insured shall be legally entitled to recover damages, and if so entitled, the amount thereof, shall be made by agreement between the insured and the insurer or, in the event of disagreement, by arbitration… If the insured has or may have rights to benefits, other than nonoccupational disability benefits, under any workers’ compensation law, the arbitrator shall not proceed with the arbitration until the insured’s physical condition is stationary and ratable. In those cases in which the insured claims a permanent disability, the claims shall, unless good cause be shown, be adjudicated by award or settled by compromise and release before the arbitration may proceed. Any demand or petition for arbitration shall contain a declaration, under penalty of perjury, stating whether (i) the insured has a workers’ compensation claim; (ii) the claim has proceeded to findings and award or settlement on all issues reasonably contemplated to be determined in that claim; and (iii) if not, what reasons amounting to good cause are grounds for the arbitration to proceed immediately…”

In the instant motion, Petitioner states that Respondent has “no intention to comply with the explicit language of the Mobilitas Policy by his refusal to file a claim with Blue Star,” and Blue Star confirmed they do “not have a claim filed by Respondent.” (Mot., 4:23-5:1.) Petitioner asserts that “[t]he Court should stay the arbitration until the Respondent has filed an occupational accident insurance claim and said claim is resolved… Allowing the arbitration to go forward without the Respondent resolving an occupational accident insurance claim would violate Mobilitas’ insurance policy and potentially provide the Respondent with double recovery.” (Mot., 5:5-10.) Further, Petitioner contends that “[s]taying arbitration is the only way to prevent double recovery, because the Petitioner cannot assert a lien for worker’s compensation benefits, and will not know the amount paid for the occupational accident insurance claim until the claim resolves.” (Mot., 5:22-24.)

Respondent responds by asserting that he “has no rights under any workers’ compensation law” because he is an app-based driver, making him “outside the workers’ compensation system entirely.” (Opp., 4:16-19.) Respondent states that “[u]nder Proposition 22, an app-based driver who meets the statutory conditions ‘is an independent contractor and not an employee.’” (Opp., 4:16-18, citing Bus. & Prof. Code, § 7451.) Additionally, Respondent cites Castellanos v. State of California (2024) 16 Cal.5th 588, 552, where the court upheld Proposition 22, defining app-based drivers as independent contractors. Respondent contends “Proposition 22 required network companies to purchase private occupational accident insurance for their drivers (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 7455, subd. (a)) — because those drivers have no workers’ compensation coverage. An occupational accident policy is a contract of private insurance issued by a commercial carrier; it is not a ‘workers’ compensation law,’ and rights under it do not arise ‘under any workers’ compensation law’ within the meaning of section 11580.2, subdivision (f).” (Opp., 4:22-27.)

Respondent also asserts that “Petitioner’s reliance on Business and Professions Code section 7455, subdivision (e), is misplaced.” Business and Professions Code section 7455, subdivision (e) states that “[a]ny benefits provided to an app-based driver under subdivision (a) or (b) of this section shall be considered amounts payable under a worker's compensation law or disability benefit for the purpose of determining amounts payable under any insurance provided under Article 2 (commencing with Section 11580) … the Insurance Code.” Respondent contends that “[b]y its plain terms, subdivision (e) is a narrow deeming provision directed at the calculation of amounts payable — i.e., the offset an insurer may take under Insurance Code section 11580.2, subdivision (h)(1), for amounts ‘paid and the present value of all amounts payable’ under a workers’ compensation law. It says nothing about the procedural stay in subdivision (f), and it operates only on benefits actually ‘provided.’ No benefits have been provided to Respondent here. A statute directed at how to count an offset cannot be rewritten into a mandate that an insured create the offset by filing a claim he never made. The structure of subdivision (f) confirms it has no application here. The stay it contemplates is tethered to workers’ compensation adjudication: arbitration is deferred until the insured’s condition is ‘stationary and ratable,’ and permanent disability claims must be ‘adjudicated by award or settled by compromise and release’ — terms of art belonging to proceedings before the Workers’ Compensation Appeals Board.” (Opp., 5:6-22.)

Insurance Code section 11580.2 subdivision (f) requires a stay of arbitration if there are possible rights under workers’ compensation law. However, here, Respondent does not have workers’ compensation rights because he is an independent contractor. Respondent is covered by private occupational accident insurance, to which workers’ compensation does not apply. Thus, Insurance Code section 11580.2 subdivision (f) is inapplicable.

Without the language under Insurance Code section 11580.2 subdivision (f) authorizing a stay, the Court does not have jurisdiction to grant the relief Petitioner seeks. “When it has been determined that arbitration should be pursued and all judicial proceedings have been suspended until completion of the arbitration, it would be wholly incompatible with established policies of the law to permit the court thereafter to intervene in, and necessarily to interfere with, the arbitration ordered.” (Briggs, supra, 168 Cal.App.4th at p. 1400.) Here, Respondents Amir Zaytoun and Carey Wood sought uninsured motorist arbitration on May 20, 2025, while Respondent did so on January 23, 2026. The “parties agreed to consolidate all claims into a single arbitration, engaged in written discovery, and jointly selected an arbitrator. The consolidated arbitration is scheduled for July 14, 2026.” (Opp., 3:26-4:1.) The Court only has jurisdiction to interfere with arbitration pursuant to statutory authorization, and Insurance Code section 11580.2 does not confer the Court with such jurisdiction to stay a pending arbitration while there is allegedly a private insurance claim. (Briggs, supra, 168 Cal.App.4th at pp. 1400-1401.) Accordingly, the Court denies Petitioner’s motion.

Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, Petitioner’s petition is DENIED.  

Petitioner is ordered to provide notice of this Order.
