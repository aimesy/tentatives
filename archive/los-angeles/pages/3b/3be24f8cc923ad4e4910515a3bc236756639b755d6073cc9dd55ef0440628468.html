MOTIONS

Motion to Continue Trial

MOVING PARTY

Defendant Reconstruction Experts Inc.

OPPOSING PARTIES

none

 

MOTION

 

This case arises from a dispute between homeowner and homeowner’s association. 

 

On February 5, 2024, Plaintiff Sebastian Giefer as Co-Trustee of the Giefer Family Trust (“Plaintiff”) brought suit against Defendants Carolwood Homeowners Association Inc. (“Carolwood”); Coro Community Management and Consulting, LLC (“Coro”); and Reconstruction Experts Inc. (“Reconstruction”) (together, “Defendants”).  Plaintiff’s operative Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”) alleges three causes of action for (1) breach of contract; (2) negligence; and (3) breach of fiduciary duty, arising from allegations that Defendants neglected to properly repair and manage a leaking roof that caused damage to Plaintiff’s unit.  

 

A jury trial is currently scheduled in this matter for August 31, 2026. 

 

Reconstruction now moves to continue the trial and all related discovery and motion cut-off dates.  The motion is unopposed.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

 

            California Rules of Court, rule 3.1332(c), provides:

 

Although continuances of trials are disfavored, each request for a continuance must be considered on its own merits.  The court may grant a continuance only on an affirmative showing of good cause requiring the continuance.  Circumstances that may indicate good cause include:

 

(1)  The unavailability of an essential lay or expert witness because of death, illness, or other excusable circumstances;

 

(2)  The unavailability of a party because of death, illness, or other excusable circumstances;

 

(3)  The unavailability of trial counsel because of death, illness, or other excusable circumstances;

 

(4)  The substitution of trial counsel, but only where there is an affirmative showing that the substitution is required in the interests of justice;

 

(5)  The addition of a new party if:

 

(A)  The new party has not had a reasonable opportunity to conduct discovery and prepare for trial; or

 

(B)  The other parties have not had a reasonable opportunity to conduct discovery and prepare for trial in regard to the new party's involvement in the case;

 

(6)  A party's excused inability to obtain essential testimony, documents, or other material evidence despite diligent efforts; or

 

(7)  A significant, unanticipated change in the status of the case as a result of which the case is not ready for trial.

 

Factors the Court considers in ruling on a motion for continuance include:

 

(1)  The proximity of the trial date;

 

(2)  Whether there was any previous continuance, extension of time, or delay of trial due to any party;

 

(3)  The length of the continuance requested;

 

(4)  The availability of alternative means to address the problem that gave rise to the motion or application for a continuance;

 

(5)  The prejudice that parties or witnesses will suffer as a result of the continuance;

 

(6)  If the case is entitled to a preferential trial setting, the reasons for that status and whether the need for a continuance outweighs the need to avoid delay;

 

(7)  The court's calendar and the impact of granting a continuance on other pending trials;

 

(8)  Whether trial counsel is engaged in another trial;

 

(9)  Whether all parties have stipulated to a continuance;

 

(10)  Whether the interests of justice are best served by a continuance, by the trial of the matter, or by imposing conditions on the continuance; and

 

(11)  Any other fact or circumstance relevant to the fair determination of the motion or application.

 

(Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1332(d).)

 

ANALYSIS

 

            Reconstruction requests the trial continuance on the grounds that the hearing on its motion for summary judgment is currently scheduled for August 4, 2026, less than a month before the August 31, 2026 trial date. 

 

The parties also scheduled a mediation for May 27, 2026, which was unable to go forward due to a scheduling conflict with the mediator.  (Rui Decl. ¶¶ 1.)  Further, Reconstruction argues good cause exists to continue the trial date in light of the parties’ ongoing settlement efforts, though Reconstruction does not provide any evidentiary support for this contention.

 

Ultimately, the Court does not find Reconstruction has presented good cause why the trial should be continued.  The Court can simply hear the pending motion for summary judgment on August 4 as scheduled, notwithstanding that it is slightly past the statutory deadline for such motions.

 

With regard to mediation, Reconstruction has not presented any evidence that the parties are attempting to re-schedule the missed mediation, or what is the new mediation date.

 

Further, there is no evidence demonstrating that a continuance of the discovery cut-off dates is necessary or warranted.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

 

      Finding no good cause, the Court denies Reconstructions’ motion to continue the trial date.  To the extent the first available hearing date for the currently-scheduled motion for summary judgment is slightly within the 30-day window before trial, the Court grants Reconstruction an exception and agrees to hear the motion as currently scheduled. 

 

Reconstruction shall provide notice of the Court’s Orders and file the notice with a proof of service forthwith.

MOTION

Motion for Leave to Amend Complaint

MOVING PARTY

Plaintiff Galina Kopelev

OPPOSING PARTY

Defendant The Churchill Condominium Association

 

MOTION

 

This case arises from a dispute between homeowner and the condominium association. 

 

On June 10, 2024, Plaintiff Galina Kopelev (“Plaintiff”) filed suit against Defendant The Churchill Condominium Association (“Defendant” or “Churchill”) alleging ten causes of action for (1) breach of the governing documents; (2) breach of the covenant of good faith and fair dealing; (3) breach of fiduciary duties; (4) breach of the covenant of quiet enjoyment; (5) negligence; (6) public nuisance; (7) private nuisance; (8) trespass; (9) intentional infliction of emotional distress; and (10) negligent infliction of emotional distress.

 

Plaintiff now moves for leave to amend the Complaint to add three defendants (Defendant’s contractor and the two co-owners of Unit 6E) and corresponding allegations, adding an eleventh cause of action for trespass to chattels, and adding allegations that Defendant breached its fiduciary duties, causing additional damages to Plaintiff’s unit.

 

Defendant opposes the motion and Plaintiff replies.

 

The Court continued the hearing on the grounds that Plaintiff failed to provide ample notice of the motion, in violation of Defendant’s due process rights, and invited supplemental briefing, in light of the initial procedural defect.  Defendant did not file any supplemental opposition.  Plaintiff filed a notice that Defendant had not filed any supplemental opposition, to which Defendant responded by filing an attorney declaration.  Plaintiff filed evidentiary objections to the attorney declaration in a supplemental reply.

 

EVIDENTIARY OBJECTIONS

 

            The Court overrules Plaintiff’s evidentiary objections to Defendant’s attorney declaration filed in a supplemental reply.  The objections are not in proper form, and the Court disagrees with Plaintiff that the declaration mischaracterizes the Court’s prior minute order.

           

LEGAL STANDARD

 

Amendments to Pleadings: General Provisions

 

            Under Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (a)(1), “The court may, in furtherance of justice, and on any terms as may be proper, allow a party to amend any pleading or proceeding by adding or striking out the name of any party, or by correcting a mistake in the name of a party, or a mistake in any other respect; and may, upon like terms, enlarge the time for answer or demurrer. The court may likewise, in its discretion, after notice to the adverse party, allow, upon any terms as may be just, an amendment to any pleading or proceeding in other particulars; and may upon like terms allow an answer to be made after the time limited by this code.”

 

            To wit, without notice to the other party the Court has wide discretion to allow either party (i) to add or strike the name of a party or (ii) to correct a mistake in the name of a party or a mistake in any other respect “in furtherance of justice” and “on any terms as may be proper.” (See Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (a)(1); see also Marriage of Liss (1992) 10 Cal.App.4th 1426, 1429.) Alternatively, after notice to the other party, the Court has wide discretion to allow either party to amend pleadings “upon any terms as may be just.” (See Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (a)(1). Similarly, Code of Civil Procedure section 576 states “Any judge, at any time before or after commencement of trial, in the furtherance of justice, and upon such terms as may be proper, may allow the amendment of any pleading or pretrial conference order.”

 

            Judicial policy favors resolution of all disputed matters between the parties and, therefore, leave to amend is liberally granted. (Berman vs. Bromberg (1986) 56 Cal.App.4th 936, 945; Hirsa v. Superior Court (1981) 118 Cal.App.3d 486, 488-489 [this has been an established policy in California since 1901] (citing Frost v. Whitter (1901) 132 Cal. 421, 424; Thomas v. Bruza (1957) 151 Cal.App.2d 150, 155).) The Court of Appeal in Morgan v. Superior Court held “If the motion to amend is timely made and the granting of the motion will not prejudice the opposing party, it is error to refuse permission to amend and where the refusal also results in a party being deprived of the right to assert a meritorious cause of action or a meritorious defense, it is not only error but an abuse of discretion.” (Morgan v. Superior Court (1959) 172 Cal.App.2d 527, 530, citations omitted.) Moreover, “it is an abuse of discretion for the court to deny leave to amend where the opposing party was not misled or prejudiced by the amendment.” (Kittredge Sports Co. v. Superior Court (1989) 213 Cal.App.3d 1045, 1048 [opposing party did not establish harm by the delay in moving to amend the complaint].)

 

            “The court may grant leave to amend the pleadings at any stage of the action.” (Weil & Brown, Cal. Practice Guide: Civil Procedure Before Trial (The Rutter Group 2026) ¶ 6:636 (hereafter Weil & Brown).) Denial of a motion to amend is rarely justified if the motion is timely made and granting the motion will not prejudice the opposing party. (Id. at ¶ 6:639, citations omitted.) However, if the party seeking the amendment has been dilatory, and the delay has prejudiced the opposing party, the judge has discretion to deny leave to amend. (Id. at ¶ 6:655, citations omitted. Absent prejudice, any claimed delay alone is not grounds for denial. “If the delay in seeking the amendment has not misled or prejudiced the other side, the liberal policy of allowing amendments prevails. Indeed, it is an abuse of discretion to deny leave in such a case even if sought as late as the time of trial. (Id. at ¶ 6:653 (citing Higgins v. Del Faro (1981) 123 Cal.App.3d 558, 564-565).) “Prejudice exists where the amendment would result in a delay of trial, along with loss of critical evidence, added costs of preparation, increased burden of discovery, etc. . . . But the fact that the amendment involves a change in legal theory which would make admissible evidence damaging to the opposing party is not the kind of prejudice the court will consider.” (Weil & Brown, supra, at ¶ 6:656, citations omitted.)

 

            “Even if some prejudice is shown, the judge may still permit the amendment but impose conditions, as the Court is authorized to grant leave ‘on such terms as may be proper.’” (Weil & Brown, supra, at ¶ 6:663, citation omitted.) For example, the court may cause the party seeking the amendment to pay the costs and fees incurred in preparing for trial. (Id. at ¶ 6:664 (citing Fuller v. Vista Del Arroyo Hotel, 42 Cal.App.2d 400, 404).)

 

California Rules of Court, rule 3.1324: Procedural Requirements

 

            Pursuant to California Rules of Court, rule 3.1324(a), a motion to amend a pleading before trial must:

 

“(1) Include a copy of the proposed amendment or amended pleadings, which must be serially numbered to differentiate it from previous pleadings or amendments;

(2) state what allegations in the previous pleading are proposed to be deleted, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the deleted allegations are located; and

(3) State what allegations are proposed to be added to the previous pleading, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the additional allegations are located.”

 

            In addition, under Rule 3.1324(b), a motion to amend a pleading before trial must be accompanied by a separate declaration that specifies the following:

 

“(1) the effect of the amendment;

(2) why the amendment is necessary and proper;

(3) when the facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered; and

(4) the reasons why the request for amendment was not made earlier.”

 

DISCUSSION

 

I.                Timeliness

 

            As a threshold matter, Code of Civil Procedure section 1005, subdivision (b) requires that moving and supporting papers be filed and served no later than sixteen (16) court days prior to the hearing.  That timeframe is extended by two (2) court days when service is made electronically.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 1010.6, subd. (a)(3)(B).)

 

In opposition, Defendant indicates Plaintiff served the motion by email on May 22, 2026.  But eighteen court days before the June 15 hearing, when accounting for Memorial Day – a Court holiday, is May 19, 2026.

 

Plaintiff does not contest this in reply.

 

The Court continued the hearing on the motion to permit the parties to file supplemental papers addressing the merits of the motion.  Therefore, the Court finds that the procedural issues have been cured.

 

II.             Merits

 

Plaintiff seeks leave to amend the complaint to add three additional defendants, add one additional cause of action for trespass to chattels, and to amend a few of the causes of action to allege additional facts regarding the newly-named defendants concerning water and sewer leak damages to Plaintiff’s property, after informal attempts to resolve the dispute with those defendants failed and the resulting property damage have become more extensive.

 

Plaintiff has attached a redlined copy of the proposed amended complaint which shows the proposed additions. 

 

In support of the motion, Plaintiff advances her own declaration which provides as follows:

 

1. I am the Owner Resident of the Condo Unit in the Churchill Condo Association, 13 story condominium building located at 10450 Wilshire Boulevard in Los Angeles, which I purchased in 2015.

 

2. 1am a widow and occupy the Unit SE on the fifth floor by myself as my primary residence.

 

3. During the years of my Residency, being a very experienced business woman, well-educated and knowledgeable in the Davis Sterling Act, I have observed that the Churchill Association along with its Agent Bell Property Management (during the last three years) has and continues to issue improper decisions in violation of law, demonstrating failure of the Fiduciary Duty, a lack of Transparency (opting instead to operate in secret and without full disclosure to its members), concealment of the Financial data, Mismanagement, lack of Maintenance, resulting in the Depreciation of the Condo Values, Frustration and Concerns of the Homeowners, which gave rise to the Causes of Action stated in my Complaint.

 

4. Evaluating the Churchill’s CC&R, I have discovered that the Association was governed by the outdated, archaic covenants, conditions, and restrictions (“CC&Rs”), which based on the information and believe were first recorded in 1976, the Deficiencies of which have been causing the numerous legal actions against Churchill, including this one.

 

5. Around the time I have filed my Complaint, I suffered from two independent Episodes of the Water and Sewage leakages to my expensively and beautifully designed Bathroom from the Unit 6E Upstairs severally damaging it, specifically:

 

a. Leakage to my Bathroom and Closet, extensively damaging my walls, floors, ceiling, etc. resulting from the unlawfully and unpermitted by the Los Angeles Department of Building and Safety (LADBS) installed in the Closet the Laundry machines without proper drainage.

 

b. Sewage pipe leakage from the Unit 6E Toilet to my Unit, due to the broken (practically non-existent) part of the Sewage pipe, which has caused a terrible disgusting smell, and was discovered when hired by me plumber made openings in the wall.

 

6. Since the damages were discovered, I was in the written communications with the Manager and the Owners of the Unit 6E on the regular basis, the evidence of which is available and will be presented at Trial.

 

7. I made endless, documented Good-Faith attempts to mitigate damages and conserve Judicial Resources.

 

8. Being a blameless Victim in this Incidents, caused by the Owners of the Unit 6E and/or Churchill Condo Association, I did not specifically address in My Complaint the damages to my bathroom, hoping for the obvious to me Administrative and Out-of-Court resolution.

 

9. Unfortunately despite large volume of the written communications, the Defendants (Condo owners of the Unit 6E on one hand and the Churchill Manager on another), refused to take Responsibility, blaming each other.

 

10. I have been living in the Unit (with the damaged unusable Bathroom), with wet walls, terrible smell, which affect my health. At this point I see no other option but to bring the Bell Management and the Condo owners of the Unit 6E as additional Defendants.

 

11. The Defendants have created a Deadlock for me by disputing their respective responsibilities among themselves, and refusing to take responsibility for the Damages to my property.

 

12. I don’t see other venue for myself but Court Intervention where these competing liabilities can be sorted out. Denying me the Leave to amend my Complaint, would effectively and unfairly punish me for the Defendants' internal gridlock.

 

(Kopelev Decl. ¶¶ 1-12.)

 

            Thus, Plaintiff’s declaration demonstrates the effect of the amendment (to add new defendants), that the proposed amendments are necessary and proper to litigate all of Defendants’ competing liabilities in one action, and that Plaintiff did not request leave to amend sooner because she was first attempting to informally resolve the dispute without court intervention.  Finally, Plaintiff discovered the new Defendants’ potential liability during informal discussions, during which the Defendants blamed each other.

 

            In opposition, Defendant Churchill argues that Plaintiff unreasonably delayed because she knew of the water and sewage intrusions since 2024, yet waited two years, until the eve of trial, to move for leave to amend.  Further, Churchill argues that Plaintiff cannot bring these claims under the relation-back doctrine because Plaintiff was not genuinely ignorant of their identities, and as such the proposed new amendments are time-barred.

 

            Taking Churchill’s second argument first, the Court does not generally consider the merits of the amendment in determining whether to grant leave to amend.  If Churchill is correct, those claims can subsequently be addressed via a motion challenging the pleading or a dispositive motion.[1]

 

            Regarding unreasonable delay, Plaintiff, who is proceeding in pro per, indicates the delay was due to Plaintiff’s attempt to informally resolve the dispute.  Further, delay alone is insufficient grounds to deny the motion.  Churchill does not point to any evidence that will be lost or any other such prejudice that will result from granting Plaintiff leave to amend the complaint.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

 

            Finding the motion procedurally and substantively proper, the Court grants Plaintiff’s motion for leave to amend the complaint. 

 

            Further, the Court orders Plaintiff to facilitate the issuance of a new Summons in light of the addition of new parties, and thereafter, orders Plaintiff to file and serve the proposed First Amended Complaint on or before August 10, 2026. 

 

            Further, the Court orders Plaintiff upon service of the amended Summons and Complaint to file the attendant proofs of service. 

 

            Further, on the Court’s own motion, the Court vacates the Final Status Conference and Jury Trial, and sets a Case Management Conference on November 30, 2026 at 8:30 A.M. in Department 207.  All parties shall comply with California Rules of Court, rules 3.722, et seq., regarding Initial and Further Case Management Conferences.  In particular, all parties shall adhere to the duty to meet and confer (Rule 3.724) and to the requirement to prepare and file Case Management Statements (Rule 3.725). 

 

Plaintiff shall provide notice of the Court’s orders and the Case Management Conference on all parties forthwith, and file the notice with a proof of service.

BACKGROUND

 

This case arises from a dispute between employee and employer. 

 

On July 28, 2025, Plaintiff Khaled Ouaaz a/k/a Kade Wise (“Plaintiff”) brought suit against Defendants 20th Century Television, Inc., fka 20th Century Fox Television, Inc. (“20th Century”); Lee Daniels Entertainment, Ltd. (“Lee Daniels Ent.”); Danny Strong Productions (“Danny Strong Prod.”); Imagine Entertainment, Inc. (“Imagine”); Dana Walden (“Walden”); Gary Newman (“Newman”); Lee Daniels (“Daniels”); Danny Strong (“Strong”); Sanaa Hamri (“Hamri”); Paolo Nieddu (“Nieddu”); Brian Grazer (“Grazer”); Carlito Rodriguez (“Rodriguez”); Jonnie Davis (“Davis”); Carolyn Cassidy (“Cassidy”); Howard Kurtzman (“Kurtzman”); Brett Mahoney (“Mahoney”); Diane Ademu-John (“Ademu-John”); Ilene Chaiken (“Chaiken”); Craig Berger (“Berger”); Matt Pyken (“Pyken”); Dennis Hammer (“Hammer”); Joshua Allen (“Allen”); Eric Haywood (“Haywood”); Jasheika James; Janeika James; Dianne Houston (“Houston”); and Jamie Rosengard (“Rosengard”). 

 

The operative First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) was filed on September 15, 2025, alleging eighteen causes of action as follows:

 

1.     Breach of Contract

2.     Intentional Misrepresentation

3.     Fraudulent Concealment

4.     Actual Fraud

5.     Defamation of Character

6.     Breach of Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing

7.     Intentional Interference with Contractual Relations

8.     Intentional Interference with Prospective Economic Relations

9.     Demotion Discrimination

10.  Promotion Discrimination

11.  Political Discrimination

12.  Intersectional Discrimination

13.  Disparate Impact Discrimination

14.  Retaliation

15.  Right of Publicity

16.  Civil Conspiracy

17.  Intention Infliction of Emotional Distress

18.  Negligence

 

On April 30, 2026, the Court sustained the demurrer of Defendants 20th Century, Lee Daniels Ent., Imagine, Walden, Newman, Daniels, Hamri, Grazer, Cassidy, Ademu-John, Chaiken, Brewer, Allen, Haywood, and Rosengard without leave to amend on the grounds that, despite being offered numerous opportunities, Plaintiff was ultimately unable to articulate any facts that could be added to the FAC to correct the deficiencies identified by the Court.

 

On June 10, 2026, the Court denied Plaintiff’s motion for leave to amend the complaint to re-allege the causes of action to which the Court previously sustained the demurrer without leave to amend.

 

Plaintiff now moves to correct the record of the Court’s June 10, 2026 Minute Order.  The motion is unopposed.

 

ANALYSIS

 

            Every court has the power to amend and control its process and orders so as to make them conform to law and justice.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 128, subd. (a)(8).)  In this regard, the Court may, on its own motion or upon motion of the injured party, correct clerical mistakes in its orders or judgment as entered, so as to conform to the order or judgment directed.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (d).

 

            Plaintiff contests the Court’s characterization of Plaintiff’s position that Plaintiff “dismissed concerns” or “found reasons not to investigate” his potential claims, whereas Plaintiff’s position is that he lacked suspicion altogether until years after the fact. 

 

The Court is well aware of Plaintiff’s position that it did not dawn on him that he had potential claims until years later.  But ultimately, his claims are based on facts Plaintiff was aware of at the time of the events in question.  The Court’s “characterization” that Plaintiff “dismissed concerns” that he was aware of in real time during the events described in the operative complaint, yet “found reasons not to investigate” constitute the Court’s findings and interpretation of the facts.  The Court is not obligated to adopt Plaintiff’s framing of the issues in making its own findings of fact and conclusions of law.  Although Plaintiff may disagree with the Court’s findings that he was on inquiry notice of his claims, yet failed to diligently investigate and pursue them, does not turn the Court’s findings into clerical errors in need of correction.

 

            Nor was it a clerical error to determine that Plaintiff could not cure the fundamental deficiency by pleading more specific facts or “expand[ing] upon” the chronology of events.  The basic chronology already pled demonstrates that Plaintiff was on inquiry notice as a matter of law at or around the time of the events, yet failed to timely investigate and pursue his potential claims.  More factual details would not change that fact. 

 

            Nor did the Court mischaracterize the record by stating that Plaintiff failed to articulate what specific facts he could add to cure the identified deficiency.  Vague references to “claim-specific chronology” and “claim-specific factual allegations” are not facts themselves.  Further, at the hearing, Plaintiff repeatedly emphasized that the Complaint, as alleged, was already sufficient, and focused on how he could re-frame the issues to be more convincing to the Court, not the correcting, clarifying, or adding new facts that would change the factual narrative into one that was not time-barred, as the Court has requested of him.

           

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

 

            Finding no errors warranting correction, clerical or otherwise, the Court denies Plaintiff’s motion to correct the record. 

 

            Plaintiff shall provide notice of the Court’s ruling and file the notice with a proof of service forthwith.

MOTION

Motion to Serve Defendant by Publication

MOVING PARTY

Plaintiff Ruslan Magidov

OPPOSING PARTY

MOTION

 

This case arises from a dispute between real estate business partners. 

 

On August 6, 2025, Plaintiff Ruslan Magidov (“Plaintiff”) filed suit against Defendant Hasib Siddique (“Defendant”) alleging twelve causes of action for (1) breach of contract; (2) breach of the covenant of good faith and fair dealing; (3) breach of fiduciary duty; (4) equitable indemnity; (5) intentional misrepresentation; (6) conversion; (7) unjust enrichment; (8) replevin; (9) civil theft (Penal Code 496(c)); (10) intentional infliction of emotional distress; (11) extortion/coercion; and (12) declaratory relief.

 

Service was purportedly effectuated on Defendant via substitute service on a co-occupant of Defendant’s residence.  But on December 18, 2025, the Court granted Defendant’s motion to quash the service of the summons on the grounds that Defendant’s declaration and exhibits adequately rebutted the presumption of proper service and Plaintiff did not meet his burden of proof to demonstrate otherwise.

 

Plaintiff now moves for an order to serve Defendant by publication.[1]  The motion is unopposed.

           

LEGAL STANDARD

 

“In the absence of a voluntary submission to the authority of the court, compliance with the statutes governing service of process is essential to establish that court's personal jurisdiction over a defendant.”  (Dill v. Berquist Construction Co. (1994) 24 Cal.App.4th 1426, 1439–1440.)  “In order to obtain in personam jurisdiction through any form of constructive service there must be strict compliance with the requisite statutory procedures.  (Zirbes v. Stratton (1986) 187 Cal.App.3d 1407, 1417, quoting Stamps v. Superior Court (1971) 14 Cal.App.3d 108, 110.) 

 

For service on persons within California, generally, service of summons and complaint must be done by personal service.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 415.10.)  However, “[i]f a copy of the summons and complaint cannot with reasonable diligence be personally delivered to the person to be served,” a plaintiff may serve an individual defendant “by leaving a copy of the summons and complaint at the person's dwelling house, usual place of abode, usual place of business, or usual mailing address . . . , in the presence of a competent member of the household or a person apparently in charge of his or her office, place of business, or usual mailing address . . . , at least 18 years of age, who shall be informed of the contents thereof, and by thereafter mailing a copy of the summons and of the complaint by first-class mail, postage prepaid to the person to be served at the place where a copy of the summons and complaint were left.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 415.20, subd. (b).) 

 

A summons may also be served within the state by mailing the summons and complaint with a notice and acknowledgment of receipt and a return envelope, postage prepaid, addressed to the sender.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 415.30, subd. (a).)  Such service is deemed complete on the date the written acknowledgement of receipt of summons is executed, if such acknowledgment is thereafter returned to the sender.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 415.30, subd. (c).)  If the person to whom the summons, complaint, and notice are mailed fails to complete and return the acknowledgement within 20 days, that person shall be liable for reasonable expenses thereafter incurred in serving or attempting to serve them by another method.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 415.30, subd. (d).)

 

Further, “[a] summons may be served by publication if upon affidavit it appears to the satisfaction of the court in which the action is pending that the party to be served cannot with reasonable diligence be served in another manner specified in this article[.]”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 415.50, subd. (a).)  But “[i]f a defendant's address is ascertainable, a method of service superior to publication must be employed, because constitutional principles of due process of law, as well as the authorizing statute, require that service by publication be utilized only as a last resort.”  (Watts v. Crawford (1995) 10 Cal.4th 743, 749, fn. 5.)

 

Finally, “if a plaintiff, despite exercising reasonable diligence, has been unable to effect service of the summons by any of the methods authorized under this chapter, the court in which the action is pending may, upon motion, direct that summons be served in a manner that is reasonably calculated to give actual notice to the party to be served, including by electronic mail or other electronic technology, and that proof of such service be made as prescribed by the court.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 413.30, subd. (a)(1).)  “A plaintiff seeking to establish reasonable diligence under this section shall set forth facts detailing all attempts to serve the defendant by each of the methods prescribed by statute, including facts demonstrating why each method was unsuccessful at every address or location where the defendant is likely to be found.

ANALYSIS

 

1.     DEMURRER

 

“It is black letter law that a demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the allegations in a complaint.”  (Lewis v. Safeway, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 385, 388.)  In testing the sufficiency of a cause of action, a court accepts “[a]s true all material facts properly pled and matters which may be judicially noticed but disregard contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.  [A court also gives] the complaint a reasonable interpretation, reading it as a whole and its parts in their context.”  (290 Division (EAT), LLC v. City & County of San Francisco (2022) 86 Cal.App.5th 439, 450 [cleaned up]; Hacker v. Homeward Residential, Inc. (2018) 26 Cal.App.5th 270, 280 [“in considering the merits of a demurrer, however, “the facts alleged in the pleading are deemed to be true, however improbable they may be”].)

 

Further, in ruling on a demurrer, a court must “liberally construe” the allegations of the complaint “with a view to substantial justice between the parties.”  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 452.)  “This rule of liberal construction means that the reviewing court draws inferences favorable to the plaintiff, not the defendant.”  (Perez v. Golden Empire Transit Dist. (2012) 209 Cal.App.4th 1228, 1238.)  

 

In summary, “[d]etermining whether the complaint is sufficient as against the demurrer on the ground that it does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action, the rule is that if on consideration of all the facts stated it appears the plaintiff is entitled to any relief at the hands of the court against the defendants the complaint will be held good although the facts may not be clearly stated, or may be intermingled with a statement of other facts irrelevant to the cause of action shown, or although the plaintiff may demand relief to which he is not entitled under the facts alleged.”  (Gressley v. Williams (1961) 193 Cal.App.2d 636, 639.)

 

A.    UNCERTAINTY

 

“[D]emurrers for uncertainty are disfavored.”  (Lickiss v. Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (2012) 208 Cal.App.4th 1125, 1135.)  “A demurrer for uncertainty will be sustained only where the pleading is so bad that the responding party cannot reasonably respond - i.e., [a party] cannot reasonably determine what issues must be admitted or denied, or what counts or claims are directed against [the party].”  (Weil & Brown, Cal. Practice Guide:  Civil Procedure Before Trial (The Rutter Group 2026) ¶ 7:85, p. 45, emphasis in original.) 

 

As such, where a demurrer is made upon the ground of uncertainty, the demurrer must distinctly specify exactly how or why the pleading is uncertain, and where such uncertainty appears by reference to page and line numbers.  (See Fenton v. Groveland Comm. Services Dist. (1982) 135 Cal.App.3d 797, 809 [“Generally, the failure to specify the uncertain aspects of a complaint will defeat a demurrer based on the grounds of uncertainty”].)   

 

Moving Defendants demur in part on the grounds that the claims are uncertain because they refer to all “Defendants” in a general and conclusory fashion, without specifying what conduct is attributable to which defendants.  To wit, Plaintiff alleges:

 

123.  [D]efendants knowingly made affirmative untrue statements to prospective employers with the intent to prevent him from obtaining employment . . . .

 

130. Defendants failed to use reasonable care to determine the truth or falsity of the statements.

            However, the Complaint also clarifies that the individual defendants (except for Carey) are members of BHUSD’s Board of Directors, except for Hasty, who is BHUSD’s assistant superintendent.  (See Complaint ¶¶ 13-18.)  As such, they would each be responsible for employment decisions made by BHUSD, as well as public statements BHUSD makes about Plaintiff. 

 

            Therefore, while the Complaint groups Moving Defendants together, the Court does not find that makes the Complaint “is so bad that the responding party cannot reasonably respond,” as the individual Moving Defendants are all either administrators or members of BHUSD’s board, making them potentially culpable individually and collectively for the alleged wrongful conduct. 

 

Therefore, the Court declines to sustain the demurrer on the grounds of uncertainty. 

 

B.    FAILURE TO STATE A CAUSE OF ACTION

 

                                                         i.          Individual Defendants’ Immunity

 

Moving Defendants first argue that the individual moving defendants are immune from liability because public employees are not personally liable for their discretionary acts, whether or not such discretion is abused. 

 

Indeed, “Except as otherwise provided by statute, a public employee is not liable for an injury resulting from his act or omission where the act or omission was the result of the exercise of the discretion vested in him, whether or not such discretion be abused.”  (Gov. Code, § 820.2.)

 

This discretionary immunity applies to school board members.  (See Caldwell v. Montoya (1995) 10 Cal.4th 972, 976 (hereafter Caldwell).)  It has also been applied to superintendents.  (See, e.g., Wormuth v. Lammersville Union Sch. Distr. (E.D. Cal. 2018) 305 F.Supp.3d 1108, 1130.)

 

Notwithstanding, a public employee is generally liable for injurious acts or omissions to the same extent as a private person.  (Gov. Code, § 820, subd. (a).)  As such, the immunity for exercising discretionary authority “applies only to deliberate and considered policy decisions in which a conscious balancing of risks and advantages” occurred; it does not immunize lower-level or negligent ministerial acts within the scope of their authority that merely implement an already-formulated policy.  (See Caldwell, supra, 10 Cal.4th at pp. 979, 981.)

 

Indeed, high level board decisions about hiring or firing are covered under discretionary immunity, at least with respect to statutes, like FEHA, which do not specifically provide for individual liability.  (Caldwell, supra, 10 Cal.4th at p. 982.)  However, the moving individual defendant are not immune from claims under California’s whistle-blower statute, Government Code section 19683, which provides for such individual liability.  (See Shoemaker v. Myers (1992) 2 Cal.App.4th 1407, 1423-1424; Caldwell, supra, 10 Cal.4th at p. 986 & fn. 7.)

 

Here, Plaintiff’s fourth cause of action for retaliation is brought under Labor Code section 1102.5 and Plaintiff’s seventh cause of action is brought under Labor Code section 1050.

 

Plaintiff argues that because Shoemaker and this action both involve whistle-blower retaliation claims, the immunity should similarly not bar Plaintiff’s whistle-blower claims here.  But unlike the whistle-blower statute in Shoemaker, Government Code section 19683, which specifically calls for “a just and proper penalty” against both the employer and the specific manager, supervisor, or employee who unjustly retaliated against a whistleblower, there is no such analogous provision in the Labor Code.  To the contrary, only “an employer is liable for a civil penalty” for each violation and “in all prosecutions under this chapter, the employer is responsible for the acts of his managers, officers, agents, and employees.”  (Lab. Code §§ 1102.5, subd. (f); 1104.)

 

Because Government Code section 820.2 exempts employees from discretionary liability “except as otherwise provided by law,” the Labor Code’s failure to hold the offending managers, agents, and/or employees individually liable is fatal to Plaintiff’s retaliation claims against them.

 

However, an agent or officer who violates a provision of Labor Code section 1050 is civilly liable to the aggrieved party.  (Lab. Code, § 1054.)

 

Thus, the Court finds that the individual moving defendants are immune from liability under the fourth cause of action only.

 

The individual Moving Defendants also argue that any defamatory statements they made about Plaintiff are privileged, as they were made in the proper discharge of an official duty, pursuant to Civil Code section 47, subdivision (a), and/or in the course of board meetings which are “official” and “legislative” proceedings under Civil Code section 47, subdivision (b).  Whether the statements, if any, were all made in the proper discharge of an official duty or within the course of an official, legislative proceeding requires the resolution of facts at later stages of the litigation.

 

Therefore, the Court sustains the individual Moving Defendants’ demurrer to the fourth cause of action only on the grounds of discretionary immunity.

 

                                                       ii.          Second Cause of Action - Negligence

 

The elements for a negligence cause of action are duty, breach, causation, and damages.  (Castellon v. U.S. Bancorp (2013) 220 Cal.App.4th 994, 998.)  Moving Defendants demur to the second cause of action on the grounds that Plaintiff has failed to allege the District owes any duty.  Indeed, Plaintiff alleges:

 

135. At all relevant times, Romeo Carey, BHUSD’s board, employees, and agents were acting in the scope of their agency and employment when they harmed Mr. Mead.

 

136. Carey, in harassing and defaming Mr. Mead, and BHUSD in permitting and encouraging Carey’s harassment and defamation of Mr. Mead, were negligent.

 

137. Mr. Mead was harmed by said negligence.

 

138. Said negligence was a substantial factor in causing Mr. Mead’s harm.

 

            The Court agrees with Moving Defendants that the allegations allege breach, causation, and damages, but no duty.

 

            Therefore, the Court sustains the demurrer to the second cause of action.

 

                                                     iii.          Fifth Cause of Action – Wrongful Discharge and Demotion

 

Moving Defendants demur to the fifth cause of action on the ground that the Supreme Court has held that a claim for wrongful discharge cannot be brought against a public entity.  (See Miklosy v. Regents of Univ. of Cal. (2008) 44 Cal.4th 876, 900.)  Plaintiff does not contest this argument in opposition.

 

Therefore, the Court sustains the demurrer to the fifth cause of action.

 

                                                     iv.          Sixth Cause of Action – Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress

 

To prevail on a cause of action for Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress (“IIED”), a plaintiff must plead and ultimately prove: “(1) extreme and outrageous conduct by the defendant with the intention of causing, or reckless disregard of the probability of causing, emotional distress; (2) the plaintiff’s suffering severe or extreme emotional distress; and (3) actual and proximate causation of the emotional distress by the defendant’s outrageous conduct.” (Hughes v. Pair (2009) 46 Cal.4th 1035, 1050-1051.) A defendant’s conduct is outrageous when “it is so extreme as to exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated in a civilized community.” (Ibid. [cleaned up].)  Further, the defendant’s conduct must be “intended to inflict injury or engaged in with the realization that injury will result.” (Id. at p. 1051 [cleaned up].)  “Severe emotional distress means emotional distress of such substantial quality or enduring quality that no reasonable person in civilized society should be expected to endure it.”  (Ibid.)

 

Moving Defendants demur to the sixth cause of action on the ground that simple acts of managing personnel alleged against them do not constitute extreme or outrageous conduct beyond the bounds that are usually tolerated in a civil society.

 

Plaintiff concedes this point in opposition and agrees to withdraw the sixth cause of action as to all Defendants except non-moving Defendant Carey.

 

Therefore, the Court sustains the demurrer to the sixth cause of action.

 

                                                       v.          Seventh Cause of Action – Labor Code, § 1050

 

Moving Defendants demur to Plaintiff’s seventh cause of action on the grounds that Labor Code section 1050 applies only to conduct that occurs after an employee is discharged, yet Plaintiff remains employed with the district.

 

However, the Complaint alleges Plaintiff was discharged.  (See Complaint ¶ 162 [“Defendant BHUSD discharged and demoted Mr. Mead.”]

 

Further, as discussed above, because Labor Code section 1054 expressly provides that agents and employees who violate Section 1050 are civilly liable, individual immunity does not bar Plaintiff’s claims against the individual Moving Defendants.

 

Therefore, the Court overrules the demurrer to the seventh cause of action.

 

                                                     vi.          Eighth Cause of Action – Due Process

 

Moving Defendants demur to the eighth cause of action on the grounds that unlike a dismissal, a mere demotion does not implicate the employee’s due process rights.  They explain that, while a public employee who is subject to dismissal only for cause may not be dismissed without procedural safeguards in furtherance of that employee’s due process rights (Jones v. Omnitrans (2004) 125 Cal.App.4th 273, 279), an employee is not entitled to those same due process protections when merely being removed from a promotional level position (Council of Dir. And Supp. Of Los Angeles City Sch. v. Los Angeles Uni. Sch. Dist. (1973) 35 Cal.App.3d 147, 154 [hereafter “Council.”)  In Council, the appellate court explained that, because recipients lacked tenure in their promotional positions, their expectation of continued employment in their promotional level positions was not an interest warranting constitutional protection.

 

Thus, Moving Defendants argue that, because Plaintiff alleges only that he was discharged from his promotional position (see Complaint ¶ 121 [“the District notified Mr. Mead that his contract would not be renewed” but it guaranteed his right to return as a teacher with a $66,000 reduction in pay]), his due process rights were not violated.

 

Indeed, the eighth cause of action is premised entirely upon this demotion.  “Defendants, acting under color of law, removed Plaintiff from his administrative position and nonrenewed his contract, which constituted a deprivation of Plaintiff’s property and/or liberty interests in continued employment and reputation.”  (Complaint ¶ 185, emphasis added.)

 

Plaintiff does not contest this argument in opposition.

 

Therefore, the Court sustains the demurrer to the eighth cause of action.

 

2.     MOTION TO STRIKE

 

Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading, may serve and file a motion to strike the whole pleading or any part thereof.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 435, subd. (b)(1); Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1322, subd. (b).)  On a motion to strike, the court may: (1) strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or (2) strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of California, a court rule, or an order of the court.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a)-(b); Stafford v. Shultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782.) 

 

BHUSD moves to strike punitive damages, liquidated damages, and statutory penalties on the grounds that they are barred under Government Code section 818 and that the allegations lack the requisite specificity.

 

The individual Moving Defendants move to strike punitive damages only on the grounds that they have immunity and the allegations lack the requisite specificity.

 

a.     Punitive Damages - Specificity

 

All Moving Defendants move to strike the punitive damages on the ground that the allegations lack the requisite specificity.

 

In ruling on a motion to strike punitive damages, “judges read allegations of a pleading subject to a motion to strike as a whole, all parts in their context, and assume their truth.”  (Clauson v. Superior Court (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1253, 1255.)  To state a prima facie claim for punitive damages, a plaintiff must allege the elements set forth in the punitive damages statute, Civil Code section 3294.  (College Hosp., Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 721.)  Per Civil Code section 3294, a plaintiff must allege that the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice.  (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (a).)   As set forth in the Civil Code,

 

(1) “Malice” means conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.  (2) “Oppression” means despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person's rights.  (3) “Fraud” means an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known to the defendant with the intention on the part of the defendant of thereby depriving a person of property or legal rights or otherwise causing injury.

 

(Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (c)(1)-(3), emphasis added.) 

 

Further, a plaintiff must assert facts with specificity to support a conclusion that a defendant acted with oppression, fraud or malice.  To wit, there is a heightened pleading requirement regarding a claim for punitive damages.  (See Smith v. Superior Court (1992) 10 Cal.App.4th 1033, 1041-1042.)  “When nondeliberate injury is charged, allegations that the defendant’s conduct was wrongful, willful, wanton, reckless or unlawful do not support a claim for exemplary damages; such allegations do not charge malice.  When a defendant must produce evidence in defense of an exemplary damage claim, fairness demands that he receive adequate notice of the kind of conduct charged against him.” (G. D. Searle & Co. v. Superior Court (1975) 49 Cal.App.3d 22, 29 [cleaned up].)  In Anschutz Entertainment Group, Inc. v. Snepp, the Court of Appeal noted that the plaintiffs’ assertions related to their claim for punitive damages were “insufficient to meet the specific pleading requirement.”  (Anschutz Entertainment Group, Inc. v. Snepp (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 598, 643 [plaintiffs alleged “the conduct of Defendants was intentional, and done willfully, maliciously, with ill will towards Plaintiffs, and with conscious disregard for Plaintiff's rights. Plaintiff's injuries were exacerbated by the malicious conduct of Defendants. Defendants' conduct justifies an award of exemplary and punitive damages”]; see also Grieves v. Superior Court (1984) 157 Cal.App.3d 159, 166 [“The mere allegation an intentional tort was committed is not sufficient to warrant an award of punitive damages.  Not only must there be circumstances of oppression, fraud, or malice, but facts must be alleged in the pleading to support such a claim”].) 

 

Moreover, “the imposition of punitive damages upon a corporation is based upon its own fault.  It is not imposed vicariously by virtue of the fault of others.”  (City Products Corp. v. Globe Indemnity Co. (1979) 88 Cal.App.3d 31, 36.)  “Corporations are legal entities which do not have minds capable of recklessness, wickedness, or intent to injure or deceive.  An award of punitive damages against a corporation therefore must rest on the malice of the corporation’s employees.  But the law does not impute every employee’s malice to the corporation.  Instead, the punitive damages statute requires proof of malice among corporate leaders:  the officers, directors, or managing agents.”  (Cruz v. Home Base (2000) 83 Cal.App.4th 160, 167 [cleaned up].) 

           

            Here, as to the Moving Defendants, the Complaint lacks the requisite specificity as to the who, what, when, where, to whom, and how the allegedly defamatory statements and adverse employment decisions were made.  Instead, grouping all Defendants together, Plaintiff alleges in conclusory fashion:

 

133. Defendants knew the video and statements were false, had serious doubts about the truth of the video and/or statements, and acted with malice, oppression, fraud, and wilful disregard of Mr. Mead’s rights. Plaintiff is entitled to punitive damages within the meaning of California Civil Code section 3294 in a sum sufficient to punish and deter Defendants’ future conduct.

 

139. Defendants acted with malice, oppression, fraud, and willful disregard of Mr. Mead’s rights. Plaintiff is entitled to punitive damages within the meaning of California Civil Code section 3294 in a sum sufficient to punish and deter Defendants’ future conduct.

 

158. Defendants Jason Hasty, Rachelle Marcus, Judith Manouchehri, Amanda Stern, Sigalie Sabag, Russell Stuart, and Does 1-10 acted with malice, oppression, fraud, and willful disregard of Mr. Mead’s rights. Plaintiff is entitled to punitive damages within the meaning of California Civil Code section 3294 in a sum sufficient to punish and deter Defendants’ future conduct.

 

(Complaint ¶¶ 133, 139, 158.)

 

            The Court finds that the allegations are insufficient to satisfy the heightened pleading requirement to allege claim for punitive damages against any of the Moving Defendants.

 

b.     Liquidated Damages

 

Moving Defendants first argue Plaintiff fails to articulate a statutory basis for liquidated damages.  Plaintiff argues in opposition that Plaintiff seeks liquidated damages pursuant to Labor Code section 1102.5, subdivision (f). 

 

As discussed above, the Court sustains Defendants Hasty, Marcus, Manouchehri, Stern, Sabag, and Stuart demurrer to the fourth cause of action on the grounds that discretionary immunity bars Plaintiff’s claims against them under Labor Code section 1102.5.  As a result, Plaintiff is similarly not entitled to recoup liquidated damages as against those Defendants.

 

BHUSD additionally argues that Plaintiff may not pursue liquidated damages against it, because such “punitive” or “penalty” damages intended to punish are not available against public entities.  Plaintiff argues in opposition that liquidated damages can be compensatory, and are not necessarily punitive.  In reply, Moving Defendants argue that because Plaintiff also seeks compensatory damages, the request for liquidated damages is necessarily punitive.

 

The Court disagrees that Plaintiff’s competing requests for compensatory and/or liquidated damages is necessarily punitive.  (See Iloff v. LaPaille (2025) 18 Cal.5th 551, 565 [noting that while liquidated damages can serve as a means of punishing or deterring wage and hour violations, they are also a means of compensating employees for actual damages that are too obscure or difficult to prove or estimate].)

 

As such, the Court denies BHUSD’s request to strike the request for liquidated damages.

 

c.      Statutory Penalties

 

BHUSD argues that statutory penalties are unavailable as against public entities as a matter of law.  “[A]s a general rule, statutory penalty provisions do not apply to public entities.  (DuBois v. Workers' Comp. Appeals Bd. (1993) 5 Cal.4th 382, 398.)  Indeed, Government Code section 818 provides, “Notwithstanding any other provision of law, a public entity is not liable for […] damages imposed primarily for the sake of example and by way of punishing the defendant.”

 

Plaintiff does not address this argument in opposition.  Therefore, the Court strikes Plaintiff’s request for statutory penalties against BHUSD.

 

3.     LEAVE TO AMEND

 

A plaintiff has the burden of showing in what manner the complaint could be amended and how the amendment would change the legal effect of the complaint, i.e., state a cause of action. (See The Inland Oversight Committee v. City of San Bernardino (2018) 27 Cal.App.5th 771, 779; PGA West Residential Assn., Inc. v. Hulven Int'l, Inc. (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 156, 189.) A plaintiff must not only state the legal basis for the amendment, but also the factual allegations sufficient to state a cause of action or claim. (See PGA West Residential Assn., Inc. v. Hulven Int'l, Inc., supra, 14 Cal.App.5th at p. 189.) Moreover, a plaintiff does not meet his or her burden by merely stating in the opposition to a demurrer or motion to strike that “if the Court finds the operative complaint deficient, plaintiff respectfully requests leave to amend.” (See Major Clients Agency v Diemer (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1116, 1133; Graham v. Bank of America (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 594, 618 [asserting an abstract right to amend does not satisfy the burden].)

 

Here, Plaintiff has identified additional allegations he can allege with respect to Moving Defendants’ statutory and common law duties.  Therefore, the Court grants leave to amend the second cause of action for negligence.

 

Plaintiff also contends he can allege more specific allegations supporting his request for punitive damages as against Defendants Hasty, Marcus, Manouchehri, Stern, Sabag, and Stuart.  In turn, those Individual Defendants argue that leave should not be granted because they are also immune from liability.  But as discussed above, the Court finds Defendants Hasty, Marcus, Manouchehri, Stern, Sabag, and Stuart are immune from liability only as to the Fourth Cause of Action for whistle-blower retaliation under Labor Code section 1102.5.  To the extent Plaintiff also seeks punitive damages against those Individual Moving Defendants pursuant to the seventh cause of action for violations of Labor Code section 1050, such damages are not necessarily barred by discretionary immunity.  Therefore, the Court grants leave to amend the punitive damages allegations as to Defendants Hasty, Marcus, Manouchehri, Stern, Sabag, and Stuart.

 

However, with respect to punitive damages and statutory penalties, “a public entity is not liable for damages awarded under section 3294 of the Civil Code[.]”  (Government Code section 818.)  As such, allowing Plaintiff leave to amend the claim for punitive damages and statutory penalties would be futile. 

 

Similarly, because Defendants Hasty, Marcus, Manouchehri, Stern, Sabag, and Stuart are immune from whistle-blower retaliation claims under the Labor Code, leave to amend the fourth cause of action or liquidated damages as to the individual Moving Defendants would similarly be futile.

 

Further, Plaintiff did not contest Moving Defendants’ demurrer to the fifth, sixth or eighth causes of action.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

 

For the reasons stated, the Court sustains without leave to amend Defendants Hasty, Marcus, Manouchehri, Stern, Sabag, and Stuart’s demurrer to the fourth cause of action for whistle-blower retaliation under Labor Code section 1102.5 on the grounds that discretionary immunity bars Plaintiff’s claim. 

 

Further, the Court sustains the demurrer to the second cause of action for negligence with leave to amend and sustains the demurrer to the fifth, sixth, and eighth causes of action without leave to amend.

 

Further, the Court overrules the demurrer to the seventh cause of action.

 

Further, the Court grants in part Moving Defendants’ motion to strike and strikes Plaintiff’s request for punitive damages and statutory penalties as to BHUSD without leave to amend, but denies Moving Defendants’ motion to strike Plaintiff’s request for liquidated damages against BHUSD.

 

As to Defendants Hasty, Marcus, Manouchehri, Stern, Sabag, and Stuart, the Court grants the motion to strike and strikes Plaintiff’s request for punitive damages with leave to amend.  Because Plaintiff only seeks liquidated damages as to Defendants Hasty, Marcus, Manouchehri, Stern, Sabag, and Stuart pursuant to the fourth cause of action, and the Court sustains the individual Moving Defendants’ demurrer to the fourth cause of action without leave to amend, the Court similarly denies Plaintiff leave to amend the request for liquidated damages against Defendants Hasty, Marcus, Manouchehri, Stern, Sabag, and Stuart.

 

Further, the Court orders Plaintiff to file and serve an amended complaint in conformity with the rulings on or before July 31, 2026.

 

The Court will enter the proposed Orders lodged on April 1, 2026 in conformity with the rulings, and Moving Defendants shall provide notice of the Court’s Orders and file the notice with a proof of service forthwith.

ANALYSIS

 

                          I.          CODE OF CIVIL PROCEDURE SECTION 473, SUBD. (B) – DISCRETIONARY AND MANDATORY RELIEF

 

“Proceeding to judgment in the absence of a party is an extraordinary and disfavored practice in Anglo–American jurisprudence:  The policy of the law is to have every litigated case tried upon its merits, and it looks with disfavor upon a party, who, regardless of the merits of the case, attempts to take advantage of the mistake, surprise, inadvertence, or neglect of his adversary.”  (Au-Yang v. Barton (1999) 21 Cal.4th 958, 963 [cleaned up].) 

 

Code of Civil procedure section 473 “includes a discretionary provision, which applies permissively, and a mandatory provision, which applies as of right.” (Minick v. City of Petaluma (2016) 3 Cal.App.5th 15, 25 (hereafter Minick).)  “Section 473 is a remedial statute to be “applied liberally” in favor of relief if the opposing party will not suffer prejudice.  Because the law strongly favors trial and disposition on the merits, any doubts in applying section 473 must be resolved in favor of the party seeking relief from default.  Unless inexcusable neglect is clear, the policy favoring trial on the merits prevails.”  (Minick, supra, 3 Cal.App.5th at p. 24 [cleaned up].) 

 

The party or the legal representative must seek such relief “within a reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months, after the judgment, dismissal, order, or proceeding was taken.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b); see Rappleyea v. Campbell (1994) 8 Cal.4th 975, 980 [“because more than six months had elapsed from the entry of default, and hence relief under section 473 was unavailable”]; People v. The North River Ins. Co. (2011) 200 Ca.App.4th 712, 721 [motion for relief under section 473 must be brought “within a reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months”]).  “The six-month limit is mandatory; a court has no authority to grant relief under section 473, subdivision (b), unless an application is made within the six-month period.”  (Arambula v. Union Carbide Corp. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 333, 340, citations omitted.) 

 

A.    DISCRETIONARY RELIEF

 

Per Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b), a court may “relieve a party or his or her legal representative from a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against him or her through his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect.”

 

B.    MANDATORY RELIEF

 

Notwithstanding any other requirements of this section, the court shall, whenever an application for relief is made no more than six months after entry of judgment, is in proper form, and is accompanied by an attorney's sworn affidavit attesting to his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect, vacate any (1) resulting default entered by the clerk against his or her client, and which will result in entry of a default judgment, or (2) resulting default judgment or dismissal entered against his or her client, unless the court finds that the default or dismissal was not in fact caused by the attorney's mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect.

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b).)  “In considering whether the trial court properly denied relief under section 473(b), the first question is the sufficiency of defendants' showing of attorney fault, if believed, to trigger the mandatory relief provisions of that statute.”  (Standard Microsystems Corp. v. Winbond Electronics Corp. (2009) 179 Cal.App.4th 868, 896, disapproved on other grounds by Even Zohar Construction & Remodeling, Inc. v. Bellaire Townhouses, LLC (2015) 61 Cal.4th 830 (hereafter Standard).)  “Under section 473(b), a party is entitled to relief from a default and resulting judgment whenever, on timely application for relief, his attorney ‘attest[s] to his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect’ in connection with the default or the judgment.”  (Ibid.) 

 

Defendant argues that the service by posting was ineffective because Plaintiff failed to demonstrate the requisite diligence in attempting to serve Defendant before resorting to posting because service upon the concierge of Defendant’s controlled-access building was never attempted.

 

The Declaration of Diligence indicates a process server attempted to personally serve Defendant three times, on January 14, 2026 at 2:00 p.m.; on January 15, 2026 at 8:05 a.m.; and on January 17, 2026 at 9:14 a.m.  On at least one of the occasions, security personnel escorted the process server to Defendant’s Penthouse.  At that time, the process server could hear somebody inside the unit, but no one answered when he knocked.

 

On the basis of the Declaration of Diligence, the Court found (and still finds) that Plaintiff exhausted all reasonable efforts to personally serve Defendant before resorting to posting.

 

Therefore, the Court denies Defendant’s motion under Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b) under the theory that service by posting failed to confer personal jurisdiction over Plaintiff. 

 

                        II.          CODE OF CIVIL PROCEDURE SECTION 473.5

 

In the alternative, Defendant argues that the default and default judgment should be set aside under Section 473.5 on the grounds that Defendant did not receive actual notice of the summons.

 

“When service of a summons has not resulted in actual notice to a party in time to defend the action and a default or default judgment has been entered against the party in the action, the party may serve and file a notice of motion to set aside the default or default judgment and for leave to defend the action. The notice of motion shall be served and filed within a reasonable time, but in no event exceeding the earlier of: (i) two years after entry of a default judgment against the party; or (ii) 180 days after service on the party of a written notice that the default or default judgment has been entered.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 473.5.)

 

In support, Defendant advances his own declaration which provides:

 

4. I first learned that this lawsuit existed on June 1, 2026, when a deputy from the Los Angeles County Sheriff's Department came to my door and handed me a notice to vacate. I came to the door and accepted the notice. A true and correct copy of that notice is attached as Exhibit C.

 

5. Until June 1, 2026, I did not know that I had been sued, that a case had been filed against me, or that any judgment had been entered against me.

 

6. I never found a summons and complaint, or any court papers, posted or taped to my door at any time,

 

7. My building is a controlled-access residential building. A visitor cannot reach my unit on his own. A visitor must check in at the concierge or front desk. The concierge then calls or texts me to ask whether I will permit the visitor to come up. Only if I give permission does the concierge escort the visitor, using a key fob, in the elevator to the door of my unit.

 

8. I was not aware that anyone attempted to serve me with legal papers in January 2026. I did not decline to answer the door for any process server, and I did not avoid anyone attempting to serve me.

 

[…]

 

10. No process server ever left any papers with the concierge or front desk for me, and no one at the building ever told me that anyone had tried to serve me with legal papers.

 

11. I never received any copy of the summons and complaint by mail.

 

12. In my building, ordinary mail is delivered to individual mailboxes, but certified mail is signed for by the concierge and held at the front desk until the resident retrieves it.

 

13. I never signed for any certified mail relating to this case. I have never signed for any certified mail packet concerning this matter at any time.

 

14. When the Sheriff’s deputy knocked on June 1, 2026, I answered the door promptly, as I would have for anyone who reached my door.

 

[…]

 

17. I want to defend this case. I did not have the opportunity to do so because I never knew it had been filed until after judgment had already been entered against me.

 

18. During the period when these papers are said to have been posted and mailed, I was having ongoing problems receiving mail and documents at the building. Tracy Robinson, the property manager responsible during that period, left PMP Management at the end of April 2026. Since her departure, I have received my mail and documents at the building without the problems I had experienced before.

 

(Reegan Decl. ¶¶ 4-18.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff challenges Defendant’s purported lack of actual notice by way of a January 28, 2026 email, which Defendant sent the day after the summons was posted to the exterior of Plaintiff’s building, and which raises concerns about management’s conduct, the December Three-Day Notice to Pay Rent or Quit, and demands that evidence be preserved. 

 

But the Court notes that the January 28 email does not address the summons and complaint.  Rather, it addresses only Plaintiff’s grievances with management and the December notice.  As such, the timing of the email alone does not rebut Defendant’s assertion that he was unaware of the instant lawsuit until a Deputy Sheriff knocked on his door on June 1, 2026.

 

Plaintiff also argues that Defendant’s narrative strains credulity and requires that the Court believe that every attempted notice did not reach him.  But Defendant’s declaration explains in detail exactly why he did not receive the mailed notice, any notice that may have been sent to the building’s management, and Plaintiff does not dispute that its process server never personally served Defendant or posted the notice on Plaintiff’s door.  It is not inconceivable that Plaintiff would not have seen a notice posted on the exterior of the building.

 

CONCLUSION
