COSTTREE HOLDINGS, LLC,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

ECIVIS, INC., et al.,

                        Defendants.

 

 

           AND RELATED MATTERS.

 

  Case No.:  22STCV23458

  Hearing Date:  August 12, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

plaintiff costtree holdings, llc’s motion to compel further responses (CRS# 5556)

 

 

I. BACKGROUND

a. The Underlying Complaint

On July 20, 2022, Plaintiff CostTree Holding, LLC (CostTree) filed this action against Defendants eCivis, Inc. (eCivis), GTY Technology Holdings, Inc. (GTY), and James Ha (Ha). On March 15, 2024, Plaintiffs CostTree and Capital Accounting Partners, LLC (CAP) filed the operative Second Amended Complaint (SAC), alleging (1) breach of the covenant of good faith and fair dealing, (2) fraud, (3) negligent misrepresentation, (4) civil conspiracy, (5) breach of contract, and (6) intentional interference with contractual relations.    

            The SAC alleges that CostTree agreed to sell its assets to eCivis through an Asset Purchase Agreement (APA) with eCivis. One of these assets was a certain program developed by CostTree, called Allocate. In exchange for the sale, the APA provided that CostTree would make earn-out payments from Allocate until a certain date and that eCivis would assume certain liabilities of CostTree, including a License Agreement with CAP. eCivis has allegedly failed to adequately promote sales or renewals of Allocate and has not assumed any of CostTree’s liabilities.

eCivis allegedly acted to minimize CostTree’s earn-out payments from Allocate until CostTree’s right to earn-out payments expired under the APA. Meanwhile, Ha, the CEO of eCivis, sought to maximize his own earn-out payments from eCivis’s programs under an agreement with GTY, which acquired eCivis and its programs. Despite increased resources from the GTY acquisition to market eCivis’s programs, eCivis allegedly refused to market or sell Allocate until CostTree’s right to earn-out payments expired. This allegedly allowed eCivis to effectively acquire CostTree’s assets for little to no consideration. Defendants allegedly concocted this scheme to remove a competitor from the market and maximize their own profits.

Defendants also allegedly terminated the Licensing Agreement with CAP without following the procedures for termination outlined in the Licensing Agreement. Defendants allegedly cut off CAP’s access to Allocate, preventing CAP from servicing its clientele for nearly a month. Defendants allegedly did this as retaliation for CostTree filing this action, as Nicolie Wickwire (Wickwire) is the CEO of both CostTree and CAP.

Ha has since been dismissed.  

b. Wickwire’s Related Action

            On October 6, 2023, Wickwire filed the action Nicolie Wickwire v. eCivis, Inc., et al. (23CMCV01613), alleging (1) breach of the covenant of good faith and fair dealing, (2) constructive discharge in violation of public policy, (3) defamation, and (4) declaratory relief. The Wickwire action was consolidated with this action, with this action as the lead case. However, Wickwire subsequently dismissed her complaint with prejudice. 

c. The Cross-Complaint

            On April 29, 2024, eCivis and GTY (dba Euna Solutions) cross-complained against CostTree, CAP, Wickwire, and Stephanie Ratajczak (Ratajczak). eCivis and GTY filed the operative Third Amended Cross-Complaint (TACC) on September 15, 2025, alleging (1) conversion, (2) intentional interference with prospective economic advantage, (3) breach of employment agreement, (4) breach of the APA, (5) breach of the CAP Licensing Agreement, (6) fraud, and (7) civil conspiracy. 

            The TACC alleges that Allocate underperformed as compared to Wickwire’s promises, despite assistance from eCivis and GTY. Upon Wickwire’s job title change, she allegedly refused to sign the new employment agreement and directed her subordinate, Ratajczak, to not sign her agreement either. Ratajczak was allegedly working for CAP at the same time as working for eCivis. Wickwire, Ratajczak, and CAP allegedly sold Allocate services to third party customers who should have contracted with eCivis and GTY. Wickwire, Ratajczak, and CAP allegedly allowed third parties to access and use Allocate without a proper license and without paying fees to eCivis and GTY. These actions allegedly violated the APA, Licensing Agreement, and employment agreements.    

When Wickwire and Ratajczak simultaneously resigned, they allegedly left eCivis and GTY without any knowledgeable employees to manage Allocate. Wickwire allegedly failed to train any other employees on Allocate as was her duty prior to resigning, leaving Allocate with little value and thereby damaging eCivis and GTY. Since their resignations, Wickwire and Ratajczak have allegedly continued to impermissibly access or grant unpermitted access to Allocate beyond the limited access permitted for customers of CAP.

d. The Instant Motion

            On July 9, 2026, CostTree filed the instant motion to compel eCivis’ further responses to Requests for Production, Set Four. eCivis filed an opposition on July 30, 2026. CostTree filed a reply on August 5, 2026.

 

 

II. LEGAL STANDARD

Upon receiving responses to its discovery requests, the propounding party may move for an order compelling further responses if the responses are incomplete or evasive, or objections are without merit or too general. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300(a), 2031.310(a), 2033.290(a).)

III. DISCUSSION

            a. Verification

            CostTree argues that eCivis’ responses are improperly verified on information and belief.

“It is common sense when discovery requests are propounded on a business, particularly a large business, no single person is likely to have personal knowledge of everything the business collectively knows.” (Perry v. Kia Motors America, Inc. (2023) 91 Cal.App.5th 1088, 1097.) “Under such circumstances, ‘information and belief’ is the only realistic way to verify answers on behalf of the entity.” (Ibid.) Thus, it is “entirely proper” for a corporate agent to “sign the verification based on information and belief.” (Ibid.)

Therefore, the verification is proper.

b. Emails (RFP Nos. 121-136)

RFP Nos. 121-136 seek all communications sent to or from the eCivis email accounts of various individuals, with no limit as to time or subject matter. This is overbroad and not tailored to any issue being litigated.

The motion is DENIED as to RFP Nos. 121-136.

c. Ledgers, Income Statements, and Balance Sheets (RFP Nos. 137-163)

RFP Nos. 137-163 seek wholesale financial records for an 8-year period. This is overbroad and not tailored to any issue being litigated.

The motion is DENIED as to RFP Nos. 137-163.

 

 

d. Allocate Revenue (RFP Nos. 164-172)

RFP Nos. 164-172 seek documents reflecting the revenue eCivis received from Allocate between 2018 and 2026. This information is directly relevant to CostTree’s right to earnout payments under the APA. eCivis responded, “Defendant will comply with this request and has already produced responsive documents in its possession, custody, or control.” This is an incomplete statement of compliance. A statement of compliance must be made as follows:

 

A statement that the party to whom a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling has been directed will comply with the particular demand shall state that the production, inspection, copying, testing, or sampling, and related activity demanded, will be allowed either in whole or in part, and that all documents or things in the demanded category that are in the possession, custody, or control of that party and to which no objection is being made will be included in the production.

(Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.220.)

            The motion is GRANTED as to RFP Nos. 164-172.

e. CVs and Conference Records (RFP Nos. 173-176)

RFP Nos. 173-174 seek the CVs of the employees whom eCivis hired to work, or who worked, with Wickwire on Allocate. RFP Nos. 175-176 seek documents identifying the date, purpose, and attendees of each video conference or presentation for outside stakeholders to be conducted by Wickwire or another eCivis employee.

This information is relevant to the allegation that eCivis failed to properly develop and market Allocate, depriving CostTree of earnout payments. However, information on conferences or presentations are limited to those pertaining to Allocate. eCivis argues that the timeframe (2018-2026) is overbroad because CostTree’s right to earnouts expired in 2023. However, the request is already limited in subject matter, i.e., employees who worked with Wickwire on Allocate and conferences/presentations on Allocate. Moreover, CostTree is entitled to discover whether eCivis put more effort into developing and marketing Allocate after CostTree’s right to earnouts expired. 

The motion is GRANTED as to RFP Nos. 173-176, subject to the above limitations.

f. Marketing Materials (RFP No. 177)

RFP No. 177 seeks all writings designed for current or prospective customers of eCivis advertising the Allocate software. Marketing materials are relevant to CostTree’s allegation that eCivis failed to adequately promote Allocate, depriving CostTree of earnout payments. The timeframe (2018-2026) is reasonable, as CostTree is entitled to discover whether eCivis put more effort into developing and marketing Allocate after CostTree’s right to earnouts expired.

The motion is GRANTED as to RFP No. 177.

g. Commissions (RFP Nos. 178-179)

RFP Nos. 178-179 seek documents reflecting the commissions eCivis paid to employees since 2018 for selling the Allocate software or services reliant on Allocate. Commission information is relevant to the marketing and sale of Allocate. The timeframe (2018-2026) is reasonable, as CostTree is entitled to discover whether eCivis put more effort into developing and marketing Allocate after CostTree’s right to earnouts expired.

The motion is GRANTED as to RFP Nos. 178-179.

h. Access Log (RFP No. 180)

RFP No. 180 seeks documents identifying the date and time when eCivis’ customer information was accessed or manipulated in the Allocate software after 2018, as well as identifying information on the user. This wholesale request for access logs for an 8-year period is overbroad and not tailored to the allegation that Wickwire and Ratajczak improperly accessed Allocate.

The motion is DENIED as to RFP No. 180.

 

 

 

IV. CONCLUSION

            Plaintiff CostTree’s motion to compel further responses is GRANTED in part as set forth above. Defendant eCivis shall provide further responses to RFP, Set Four within 20 days of today. Sanctions are denied as the parties acted with substantial justification.

III. DISCUSSION



            a. Verification



            CAP argues that eCivis’ responses
are improperly verified on information and belief.



“It is common sense when discovery
requests are propounded on a business, particularly a large business, no single
person is likely to have personal knowledge of everything the business
collectively knows.” (Perry v. Kia Motors America, Inc. (2023) 91
Cal.App.5th 1088, 1097.) “Under such circumstances, ‘information and belief’ is
the only realistic way to verify answers on behalf of the entity.” (Ibid.)
Thus, it is “entirely proper” for a corporate agent to “sign the verification
based on information and belief.” (Ibid.)



Therefore, the verification is proper.



b. Statement of Compliance/Inability to
Comply



CAP has good cause for the RFPs because
they seek documents supporting the allegations in eCivis’ cross-complaint.
eCivis is entitled to preserve objections while providing a substantive
response. However, its response must be code-compliant. Specifically, a
statement of compliance or inability to comply must be made as follows:



 



A statement that
the party to whom a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling has
been directed will comply with the particular demand shall state that the
production, inspection, copying, testing, or sampling, and related activity
demanded, will be allowed either in whole or in part, and that all documents or
things in the demanded category that are in the possession, custody, or control
of that party and to which no objection is being made will be included in the
production.



(Code
Civ. Proc., § 2031.220.)



 



A representation
of inability to comply with the particular demand for inspection, copying,
testing, or sampling shall affirm that a diligent search and a reasonable
inquiry has been made in an effort to comply with that demand. This statement
shall also specify whether the inability to comply is because the particular
item or category has never existed, has been destroyed, has been lost,
misplaced, or stolen, or has never been, or is no longer, in the possession,
custody, or control of the responding party. The statement shall set forth the
name and address of any natural person or organization known or believed by
that party to have possession, custody, or control of that item or category of
item.



(Code
Civ. Proc., § 2031.230.)



eCivis’ responses do not meet this
standard. The statement of compliance does not state whether the production
will be allowed in whole or in part and does not verify that all documents not
subject to an objection will be produced. The statement of inability to comply
fails to confirm that a reasonable inquiry was made, explain the reason for
noncompliance (lost, misplaced, destroyed, etc.), or identify the individual or
organization believed to be in possession. Therefore, further responses are
warranted.

Fourth, eCivis argues that its financial

available information.



Lastly, eCivis argues that the requests
improperly demand an expert damages calculation. eCivis fails to identify any

To the extent documents do not exist, eCivis must provide a proper statement of

RONALD ACITO,

            v.

 

LANCE SOARES INCORPORATED, et al.,

                        Defendants.

 

 

ALEX VELASQUEZ, et al.,

 

                        Plaintiffs,

 

            v.

 

LANCE SOARES INCORPORATED, et al.,

 

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  23STCV24858 (Lead)

                    23STCV28499 (Consolidated)

  Hearing Date:  August 12, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

plaintiff ronald acito’s motion for leave to file first amended consolidated complaint

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On October 11, 2023, Plaintiff Ronald Acito filed this action (23STCV24858) against Defendants Lance Soares Incorporated (LSI) and Lance Soares, alleging wage violations and retaliation.

On November 21, 2023, Plaintiffs Alex Velasquez and Aaron Blocker filed a substantially similar action against the same Defendants, Alex Velasquez, et al. v. Lance Soares Incorporated, et al. (23STCV28499). The two cases have been consolidated, with 23STCV24858 as the lead case.

On June 23, 2026, Plaintiff Acito filed the instant motion for leave to file a first amended consolidated complaint (FACC). Defendants filed an opposition on July 30, 2026. Plaintiff filed a reply on August 4, 2026.

LEGAL STANDARD

 The court may, in furtherance of justice, and on such terms as may be proper, allow a party to amend any pleading. (Code Civ. Proc, §§ 473(a), 576.) Courts must apply a policy of liberality in permitting amendments at any stage of the proceeding, including during trial, when no prejudice to the opposing party is shown. (Duchrow v. Forrest (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 1359, 1377.) In determining the extent of prejudice to the opposing party, the court must consider various factors, such as whether the amendment would delay trial or increase the discovery burden. (Demetriades v. Yelp, Inc. (2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 294, 306.)

A motion for leave to amend a complaint must be accompanied by a declaration that explains: (1) the effect of the amendment; (2) why the amendment is necessary and proper; (3) when the facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered; and (4) the reasons why the request for amendment was not made earlier. (Cal. Rules of Ct., Rule 3.1324(b).)

DISCUSSION

            The proposed FACC consolidates the claims of all three Plaintiffs, adds detail regarding Acito’s wage complaints, and changes Acito’s adverse employment action to constructive discharge as opposed to firing. (Donahoo Decl. ¶ 4.) The reason for the amendment is to conform to proof. (Ibid.) The facts underlying the amendment were discovered in Plaintiff’s recent June 2026 deposition. (Id., ¶ 6.) The amendment was not made earlier because Plaintiff’s counsel required time to review voluminous discovery. (Id., ¶ 7.)

            These facts justify an amendment. Defendants’ primary opposition is that the proposed FACC introduces a brand-new constructive discharge theory. However, the underlying claims and facts have not changed. Constructive discharge is still an adverse employment action and is tantamount to a firing. “Standing alone, constructive discharge is neither a tort nor a breach of contract, but a doctrine that transforms what is ostensibly a resignation into a firing.” (Turner v. Anheuser-Busch, Inc. (1994) 7 Cal.4th 1238, 1251.) This is a reasonable amendment supported by the record.

            Defendants argue that the proposed amendment is a tactical response to Defendants’ MSA. However, Plaintiff sent the proposed amendment to Defendants before being notified of the MSA. (Donahoo Reply Decl. ¶¶ 3-4.) Any other claimed prejudice is mitigated by the trial continuance to May 2027. This leaves sufficient time for the parties to complete discovery on the new allegations.

CONCLUSION

            Plaintiff’s motion for leave to file First Amended Consolidated Complaint is GRANTED.

           


Case Number: 24STCV32629    Hearing Date: August 12, 2026    Dept: 406

 

ARTHUR SARKISSIAN, et al.,

                        Plaintiffs,

            v.

 

JOSHUA MACIELLO, et al.,

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  24STCV32629

  Hearing Date:  August 12, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

defendant joshua maciello’s demurrer and motion to strike

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On December 10, 2024, Plaintiffs Arthur Sarkissian and Valerie Hambas Sarkissian filed this action against Defendants Joshua Macciello; LP Counsel, PC; and Liza Parnassi. Plaintiffs filed the operative First Amended Complaint on December 13, 2024, asserting (1) fraud, (2) breach of written contract, (3) breach of oral contract, (4) breach of fiduciary duty, and (5) conversion.

            The complaint alleges that Macciello borrowed hundreds of thousands of dollars from Plaintiff under the false pretense that he would repay the loans. Macciello also allegedly induced Plaintiff to become a member of his company to further misappropriate Plaintiff’s money. Macciello allegedly conspired with LP Counsel and Parnassi to conceal financial information from Plaintiff.

            On July 21, 2026, Macciello filed the instant demurrer and motion to strike. Plaintiffs filed oppositions on July 30, 2026. Macciello filed replies on August 5, 2026.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (Taylor v. City of Los Angeles Dept. of Water and Power (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 1216, 1228.) In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or by proper judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.30(a).) A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. (SKF Farms v. Superior Court (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 902, 905.) “California law emphasizes ultimate fact pleading (with some exceptions, notably for fraud and related torts) ‘in ordinary and concise language,’ and the test for adequacy is not absolute but ‘whether the pleading as a whole apprises the adversary of the factual basis of the claim.’” (Lim v. The.TV Corp. Internat. (2002) 99 Cal.App.4th 684, 690.)

Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading, may serve and file a notice of motion to strike the whole or any part of that pleading. (Code Civ. Proc., § 435, subd. (b).) The court may, upon a motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike (1) any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading and (2) all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Id., § 436.) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (Id., § 437.)

MEET AND CONFER

Before filing a demurrer or a motion to strike, the demurring or moving party is required to meet and confer with the party who filed the pleading demurred to or the pleading that is subject to the motion to strike for the purposes of determining whether an agreement can be reached through a filing of an amended pleading that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 430.41, 435.5.) The Court finds that Defendant has satisfied the meet and confer requirement. (See Kaplan Decl.)

DISCUSSION

I. Demurrer

            a. Statute of Limitations

            A claim for fraud is subject to a three-year statute of limitations, accruing upon “the discovery, by the aggrieved party, of the facts constituting the fraud or mistake.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 338(d).) A claim for breach of oral contract is subject to a two-year statute of limitations, accruing upon “the discovery of the loss or damage suffered by the aggrieved party thereunder.” (Id., § 339.)

            In this case, Macciello allegedly approached Plaintiffs in June or July 2017 and asked for money, promising to repay it once he obtained income from his business projects. (FAC ¶ 10.) Macciello allegedly entered into a written loan agreement with Valerie Sarkissian in July 2017, with a due date of September 2017. (Id., ¶ 11.) In August 2017, Macciello allegedly loaned $150,000 from Arthur Sarkissian under an oral agreement, promising to repay it “in less than two months.” (Id., ¶¶ 12, 34.) Macciello allegedly failed to repay the loans within the promised time and admitted that his representations had been false. (Id., ¶ 13.) Macciello allegedly sent Arthur Sarkissian apologetic texts in October and December 2017. (Ibid.) Macciello allegedly continued to acknowledge his debt, as recently as June 2024. (Id., ¶¶ 14-15.)

            Macciello argues that the claims are time-barred because according to the FAC, Plaintiffs would have discovered the alleged fraud and breach in 2017, when Macciello failed to repay the loans and admitted to the fraud.

b. Code of Civil Procedure Section 360

Plaintiffs argue that the claims are timely under Code of Civil Procedure section 360, which provides as follows:

 

No acknowledgment or promise is sufficient evidence of a new or continuing contract, by which to take the case out of the operation of this title, unless the same is contained in some writing, signed by the party to be charged thereby, provided that any payment on account of principal or interest due on a promissory note made by the party to be charged shall be deemed a sufficient acknowledgment or promise of a continuing contract to stop, from time to time as any such payment is made, the running of the time within which an action may be commenced upon the principal sum or upon any installment of principal or interest due on such note, and to start the running of a new period of time, but no such payment of itself shall revive a cause of action once barred.

(Code Civ. Proc., § 360.)

            Plaintiffs argue that Macciello’s continued affirmations of his debt exempt this case from the statute of limitations. Plaintiffs cite to Buescher v. Lastar (1976) 61 Cal.App.3d 73, 75-76 for the proposition that an “unqualified recognition of the legally expired obligation creates by law an implicit waiver of the statute of limitations and a promise by appellant to pay.”

            First, Plaintiffs’ section 360 theory fails at the outset as to Valerie Sarkissian. The purported writings alleged in the FAC are texts to Arthur Sarkissian, not Valerie Sarkissian. (See FAC ¶ 15.) The FAC alleges that Macciello “confirmed his obligation to Arthur Sarkissian.” (Id., ¶ 14.) Thus, Valerie Sarkissian’s fraud claim remains time-barred despite section 360.

            Second, the texts Macciello allegedly sent do not constitute a “writing, signed by the party to be charged.” (See Code Civ. Proc., § 360.) This is distinguishable from Buescher, where the written letters affirming a debt “were signed by the respective senders.” (Buescher, supra, 61 Cal.App.3d at p. 75.) Plaintiff cites to First Nat'l Bank v. Pray (1927) 86 Cal.App. 484, 489 for the proposition that “the code section does not prescribe any form in which an acknowledgment or promise sufficient to lift the bar of the statute of limitations shall be made.” But the code does prescribe that the acknowledgment must be in writing and must be signed. Thus, while the code might allow for variations in form, it always requires a signed writing. Accordingly, the alleged texts are not sufficient to revive the claims under section 360.

           

 

c. Equitable Estoppel

            “A defendant will be estopped to invoke the statute of limitations where there has been ‘some conduct by the defendant, relied on by the plaintiff, which induces the belated filing of the action.’” (Holdgrafer v. Unocal Corp. (2008) 160 Cal.App.4th 907, 925.) “[W]hether an estoppel exists—whether the acts, representations or conduct lulled a party into a sense of security preventing him from instituting proceedings before the running of the statute, and whether the party relied thereon to his prejudice—is a question of fact and not of law.” (Id. at pp. 925-26.)

            Here, Macciello allegedly reaffirmed his debt to Arthur Sarkissian multiple times and promised to repay it. (FAC ¶¶ 14-15.) Whether these affirmations were sufficient to induce Arthur Sarkissian into refraining from legal action is a question of fact. For pleading purposes, it may be reasonably inferred that Macciello’s continued promises to repay the debt convinced Arthur Sarkissian to refrain from filing suit. However, as discussed above, there are no allegations that Macciello reaffirmed his debt to Valerie Sarkissian. Thus, the fraud claim fails as to Valerie Sarkissian regardless of equitable estoppel.

            In sum, the first and third causes of action are not time-barred as to Arthur Sarkissian, but the first cause of action is time-barred as to Valerie Sarkissian.[1]

            d. Specificity

            “The elements of fraud that will give rise to a tort action for deceit are: ‘(a) misrepresentation (false representation, concealment, or nondisclosure); (b) knowledge of falsity (or ‘scienter’); (c) intent to defraud, i.e., to induce reliance; (d) justifiable reliance; and (e) resulting damage.’” (Engalla v. Permanente Medical Group, Inc. (1997) 15 Cal.4th 951, 974, quoting Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 638.) Fraud must be pleaded with specificity rather than with general and conclusory allegations. (Small v. Fritz Companies, Inc. (2003) 30 Cal.4th 167, 184.) The specificity requirement means a plaintiff must allege facts showing how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were made. (Lazar, supra, 12 Cal.4th at p. 645.)

Here, the fraud claim is pled with specificity: “In June and July, 2017, Macciello made the following statements orally to Plaintiffs, in Los Angeles County.” (FAC ¶ 22.) Macciello allegedly represented that he had a net worth of over $1 million, had the ability to repay the loans, and would repay the loans within a few weeks. (Ibid.) At the time Macciello made these alleged statements, he allegedly did not have a net worth of $1 million, did not have the ability to repay the loans, and did not have the intent to repay the loans. (Ibid.) Thus, Macciello made misrepresentations of present, existing facts with knowledge of falsity.  

Macciello argues that the alleged representations pertain to predictions or future conduct, citing Cansino v. Bank of America (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 1462. However, that case is distinguishable because it involved predictions about “the future of the real estate market.” (Id. at p. 1469.) By contrast, Macciello allegedly misrepresented his present financial position and ability to repay.

Moreover, promissory fraud is an actionable claim despite pertaining to future conduct. “A promise to do something necessarily implies the intention to perform; hence, where a promise is made without such intention, there is an implied misrepresentation of fact that may be actionable fraud.” (Engalla v. Permanente Medical Group, Inc. (1997) 15 Cal.4th 951, 973-74; see also Stockton Mortgage, Inc. v. Tope (2014) 233 Cal.App.4th 437, 458 [“a false promise to perform in the future can support an intentional misrepresentation claim”].) Here, Macciello allegedly misrepresented his present state of mind, i.e., his intent to repay the loans.

Lastly, Macciello points to purported inconsistencies in the amounts owed and the dates of the alleged loans. However, these are not material distinctions that defeat the fraud claim. Ambiguities as to the precise amount owed and the precise dates of the loans can be clarified in discovery. The complaint otherwise satisfies the specificity requirement and sufficiently puts Macciello on notice of the basis for the fraud claim.

            e. New Reply Arguments

            In his reply, Macciello raises two additional arguments: that the fraud claim is duplicative of the contract claim; and that the loans were usurious. “[P]oints raised for the first time in a reply brief will ordinarily not be considered, because such consideration would deprive the respondent of an opportunity to counter the argument.” (Tellez v. Rich Voss Trucking, Inc. (2015) 240 Cal.App.4th 1052, 1066.)

            Nonetheless, the arguments lack merit. The fraud claim is not duplicative of the contract claim because it is not merely based on Macciello’s failure to repay the loans. Instead, the fraud claim is based on Arthur Sarkissian being induced into the loans due to Macciello’s misrepresentations about his financial condition. Moreover, usury does not preclude the claims entirely. It does not absolve Macciello of his alleged fraudulent conduct or release him from his obligation to repay the loans. At most, usury would limit the recoverable amount. (See, e.g., Korchemny v. Piterman (2021) 68 Cal.App.5th 1032, 1042.)

II. Motion to Strike

            “In an action for the breach of an obligation not arising from contract, where it is proven by clear and convincing evidence that the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice, the plaintiff, in addition to the actual damages, may recover damages for the sake of example and by way of punishing the defendant.” (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (a).) “‘Malice’ means conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.” (Id., subd. (c)(1).) “‘Oppression’ means despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person’s rights.” (Id., subd. (c)(2).) Fraud is “intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known to the defendant with the intention on the part of the defendant of thereby depriving a person of property or legal rights or otherwise causing injury.” (Id., subd. (c)(3).)

            Here, as discussed above, the FAC contains a valid fraud claim as to Arthur Sarkissian. Fraud is an independent basis for punitive damages. Therefore, the FAC has pled a basis for punitive damages.

CONCLUSION

            Defendant Joshua Macciello’s demurrer is SUSTAINED without leave to amend as to Valerie Sarkissian’s first cause of action and OVERRULED in all other respects. The motion to strike is DENIED.


[1] Valerie Sarkissian is not a plaintiff to the third cause of action.

RUILIAN HUANG,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

DAOMING WANG,  

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  25STCV25421

  Hearing Date:  August 12, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

plaintiff’s motion for leave to file second amended complaint

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On August 29, 2025, Plaintiff Ruilian Huang filed this action against Defendant Daoming Wang, Does 1 to 50, and all persons claiming any interest in the subject real property. Plaintiff filed the operative First Amended Complaint on November 20, 2025. The FAC asserts claims for quiet title, fraud, breach of contract, and breach of fiduciary duty. The FAC stems from the following allegations.

            Plaintiff is a Chinese immigrant who recently arrived in the U.S. with little to no understanding of English. (FAC ¶ 7.) Plaintiff knew Defendant from a prior real estate transaction and knew Defendant was a real estate agent. (Ibid.) In July 2024, Defendant proposed that he and Plaintiff jointly purchase an investment property in Montebello. (Id., ¶ 8.) The parties orally agreed that Plaintiff would contribute $1.3 million, while Defendant would contribute $2 million, with profits split 50/50. (Id., ¶ 9.) Title was to be held in both parties’ names, and Defendant would serve as the real estate agent for the transaction. (Ibid.) 

            Based on Plaintiff’s limited English proficiency, Defendant proposed that Plaintiff execute a power of attorney, and Plaintiff agreed. (FAC ¶ 10.) Without Plaintiff’s knowledge, Defendant used the power of attorney to borrow $2.2 million in Plaintiff’s name and used the funds as his contribution to the purchase, instead of using his own money as promised. (Id., ¶ 11.) This means the entirety of the property was purchased by Plaintiff, entitling Plaintiff to sole title. (Ibid.)  

            On June 24, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion for leave to file a second amended complaint. Defendant filed an opposition on June 29, 2026. Plaintiff filed a reply on August 5, 2026.

LEGAL STANDARD

The court may, in furtherance of justice, and on such terms as may be proper, allow a party to amend any pleading. (Code Civ. Proc, §§ 473(a), 576.) Courts must apply a policy of liberality in permitting amendments at any stage of the proceeding, including during trial, when no prejudice to the opposing party is shown. (Duchrow v. Forrest (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 1359, 1377.) In determining the extent of prejudice to the opposing party, the court must consider various factors, such as whether the amendment would delay trial or increase the discovery burden. (Demetriades v. Yelp, Inc. (2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 294, 306.)

A motion for leave to amend a complaint must be accompanied by a declaration that explains: (1) the effect of the amendment; (2) why the amendment is necessary and proper; (3) when the facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered; and (4) the reasons why the request for amendment was not made earlier. (Cal. Rules of Ct., Rule 3.1324(b).)

DISCUSSION

            The proposed SAC eliminates the quiet title cause of action because Defendant transferred the subject property to Plaintiff, and then the property was sold at foreclosure. (Cheng Decl. ¶¶ 2, 4.) Moreover, the proposed SAC adds Plaintiff’s husband as a plaintiff, adds an aiding and abetting cause of action, and names additional entities and individuals who allegedly helped Defendant defraud Plaintiff. (Id., ¶ 2.) Lastly, the proposed SAC clarifies certain facts to conform to proof. (Id., ¶ 3.) The amendment is necessary to ensure all claims arising from the same transaction are adjudicated in the same proceeding. (Id., ¶ 5.) The amendment was not made earlier because the facts giving rise to the amendment—i.e., the foreclosure which caused Plaintiff to lose the property—occurred in January 2026, and Defendant’s bankruptcy filing stayed the action until May 2026. (Id., ¶¶ 4, 8.)

            These facts justify an amendment. Defendant’s primary opposition is that the amendment is futile because the proposed claims are insufficiently pled. This motion is not the proper place to test the merits of the proposed complaint. Defendant may file a demurrer after the SAC is filed. This is not a reason to preclude the filing entirely. Defendant does not otherwise articulate any prejudice from the amendment. Having to file a demurrer is not prejudice.

CONCLUSION

            Plaintiff’s motion for leave to file second amended complaint is GRANTED. 

           


Case Number: 25STCV26054    Hearing Date: August 12, 2026    Dept: 406

 

ADRIENNE BLAINE EISNER,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

LATITUDE INVESTMENTS, LLC, et al.,

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  25STCV26054

  Hearing Date:  August 12, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

plaintiff’s motion to compel further responses

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On September 5, 2025, Plaintiff Adrienne Blaine Eisner filed this action against Defendants Latitude Investments, LLC (Latitude), Richard Beck (Beck), and Diamond Construction & Remodeling Inc. (Diamond). The complaint asserts (1) negligence, (2) fraud, and (3) negligent misrepresentation.

            According to the complaint, Latitude purchased the property in June 2022 and hired Diamond to remodel it. In March 2023, Plaintiff bought the property from Latitude under a written purchase agreement which Beck signed on behalf of Latitude. Beck allegedly represented to Plaintiff that the remodeling was done competently and according to code. However, after the purchase, Plaintiff allegedly discovered extensive defects with the property.

            On July 1, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion to compel Latitude’s further responses to requests for production. Latitude filed an opposition on July 30, 2026. Plaintiff filed a reply on August 5, 2026.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

Upon receiving responses to its discovery requests, the propounding party may move for an order compelling further responses if the responses are incomplete or evasive, or objections are without merit or too general. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300(a), 2031.310(a), 2033.290(a).) The party seeking production of documents bears the initial burden of showing good cause through a fact-specific showing of relevance. (Kirkland v. Superior Court (2002) 95 Cal.App.4th 92, 98.) Once this showing is made, the burden shifts to the responding party to justify any objections. (Ibid.)

MEET AND CONFER

A motion to compel further must be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration demonstrating an attempt to resolve the matter informally. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300(b)(1), 2031.310(b), 2033.290(b).) The Court finds that Plaintiff has satisfied the meet and confer requirement. (See Sanders Decl.)

DISCUSSION

            The RFPs seek profit and loss statements, documents regarding capitalization, balance sheets and bank statements, and records relating to capital contributions. Latitude responded with objections only.

            Plaintiff argues that the requests are relevant to proving an alter ego relationship between Beck and Latitude. However, the actual requests are not tailored to this purpose. The RFPs are overbroad in seeking “all” documents over a nearly 10-year period. The subject transaction occurred in 2022 and 2023. The RFPs also seek wholesale balance sheets and bank statements with no tailoring to the issues in litigation. Therefore, Plaintiff has not established good cause for the requests.

 

 

CONCLUSION

            Plaintiff’s motion to compel further responses is DENIED. Sanctions are denied as the parties acted with substantial justification.


Case Number: 25STCV28055    Hearing Date: August 12, 2026    Dept: 406

 

AHMED MANSOUR,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

THERMO FISHER SCIENTIFIC INC., et al.,

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  25STCV28055

  Hearing Date:  August 12, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

plaintiff’s motion to quash subpoena

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On September 24, 2025, Plaintiff Ahmed Mansour filed this action for employment discrimination against Defendants Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. and Greg Terterian.

            On March 4, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion to quash a subpoena sent to his current employer, Howmet Aerospace, Inc. Defendant Thermo Fisher filed an opposition on April 16, 2026. Plaintiff filed a reply on April 22, 2026.

LEGAL STANDARD

“If a subpoena requires the attendance of a witness or the production of books, documents, electronically stored information, or other things …, the court, upon motion reasonably made by [a party] . . . may make an order quashing the subpoena entirely, modifying it, or directing compliance with it upon those terms or conditions as the court shall declare, including protective orders.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.1, subd. (a), (b).) Good cause must be shown to compel a nonparty to produce documents. (See Calcor Space Facility, Inc. v. Superior Court (1997) 53 Cal.App.4th 216, 224.)  

DISCUSSION

            The challenged subpoena seeks Plaintiff’s employment records from Howmet, including: (1) pay records; (2) offer letter; (3) benefits information; (4) timekeeping records and work schedules; and (5) records of leave requests.

There is good cause for the information because Plaintiff alleges wrongful termination, which has placed his employment experience, motivation, earning capacity, and subsequent employment conduct at issue. Plaintiff’s personnel records may reveal the reasons Plaintiff identified for leaving Defendant, as well as inconsistencies with his current claims that he was separated for discriminatory or retaliatory reasons. Moreover, Plaintiff’s work performance, pay records, time records, and job duties are directly relevant to his claim for loss of income, in particular mitigation of damages. Thus, the information sought is relevant to the issues in litigation and reasonably calculated to lead to admissible evidence. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2017.010.)

While personnel records can fall within the zone of privacy (Board of Trustees v. Superior Court of Santa Clara County (1981) 119 Cal.App.3d 516, 528-30), the need for disclosure outweighs the interest in privacy in this particular case (Williams v. Sup. Ct. (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 552). Plaintiff’s speculation that the subpoena threatens his current employment at Howmet does not justify quashing relevant discovery. The subpoena does not seek character evidence, but in any case, admissibility is not the test for discovery. (See Volkswagen of America, Inc. v. Superior Court (2006) 139 Cal.App.4th 1481, 1490.)   

The fact that Plaintiff has offered to produce the documents does not preclude Defendant from obtaining the documents directly from Howmet. Howmet may have access to documents that Plaintiff does not. Defendant is entitled to seek complete and thorough information by resorting to multiple sources. “A party is permitted to use multiple methods of obtaining discovery and the fact that information was disclosed under one method is not, standing alone, a proper basis for refusing to provide discovery under another method.” (Irvington-Moore, Inc. v. Superior Court (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 733, 739.)

CONCLUSION

            Plaintiff’s motion to quash subpoena is DENIED. Sanctions are denied as the parties acted with substantial justification.
