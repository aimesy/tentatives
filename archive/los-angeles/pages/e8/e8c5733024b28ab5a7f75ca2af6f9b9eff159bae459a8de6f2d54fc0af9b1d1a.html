LEGAL STANDARD

 

Standard for Approving Minor’s Compromises – Court approval is required for all settlements of a minor’s claim or that of a person lacking the capacity to make decisions.  (Prob. Code, §§ 2504, 3500, 3600 et seq.; Code Civ. Proc., § 372; see Pearson v. Superior Court (2012) 202 Cal.App.4th 1333, 1337.)

 

“[T]he protective role the court generally assumes in cases involving minors, [is] a role to assure that whatever is done is in the minor’s best interests . . .[I]ts primary concern is whether the compromise is sufficient to provide for the minor’s injuries, care and treatment.”  (Goldberg v. Superior Court (1994) 23 Cal.App.4th 1378, 1382.)  

 

A petition for court approval of a compromise or covenant not to sue under Code of Civil Procedure section 372 must comply with California Rules of Court Rules 7.950, 7.951, and 7.952.  The petition must be verified by the petitioner and contain a full disclosure of all information that has “any bearing upon the reasonableness” of the compromise or the covenant.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.950.)  The person compromising the claim on behalf of the minor or person who lacks capacity, and the represented person, must attend the hearing on compromise of the claim unless the court for good cause dispenses with their personal appearance.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.952(a).)

 

An order for deposit of funds of a minor or person lacking decision-making capacity and a petition for the withdrawal of such funds must comply with California Rules of Court Rules 7.953 and 7.954.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1384; see also Super. Ct. L.A. County, Local Rules, rules 4.115-4.118.)

CASE NUMBER:                   25STCV12377

CASE NAME:                        ALLSTATE INSURANCE COMPANY vs JINYU CHENG, et al.

MOVING PARTY:                 Plaintiff Allstate Insurance Company

OPPOSING PARTY:             Defendant Gladys Bursey

PROCEEDING:                      Motion to compel [Form Interrogatories]

RULING SUMMARY:          Plaintiff Allstate Insurance Company’s Motion to Compel Defendant Gladys Bursey’s Responses to Form Interrogatories (Set One) is moot.

 

Plaintiff Allstate Insurance Company’s Request for Sanctions in the amount of $675 is granted. Plaintiff’s request for sanctions in the amount of $675 is granted.  The sum shall be paid to Plaintiff’s counsel in monthly payments of $65 on the first of the month commencing on September 1, 2026 and continuing until paid in full. 

                                                                                                                                               

Background

 

On April 28, 2025, plaintiff Allstate Insurance Company (“Plaintiff”) filed a Complaint against defendants Jinyu Cheng (“Cheng”) and Gladys Bursey (“Bursey”) alleging four causes of action for (1) Negligence, (2) Nuisance, (3) Trespass, and (4) Breach of Contract.

 

Plaintiff alleges that, at all times relevant, it was the insurer for Maria Cervantes Magana (“Insured”), who was the owner of the real property located at 1301 S. Greenwood Avenue, Unit 36, City of Montebello, County of Los Angeles, State of California (the “Property”); Cheng was the owner of 1301 S. Greenwood Avenue, Unit 37, City of Montebello, County of Los Angeles, State of California (“Chen’s Property”), located above the Property; and Bursey was a tenant in Chen’s Property who was responsible for the maintenance, supervision, inspection, care and/or control of said property.  Plaintiff alleges that on or about July 30, 2022, a fire occurred in Chen’s Property as a result of Bursey’s negligent care, control, and maintenance of incense and the fire migrated to the Subject Property, causing damages. 

 

On May 29, 2025, Bursey filed her Answer.  On June 23, 2025, Chen filed his Answer.

 

On May 18, 2026, Plaintiff filed this Motion to Compel Defendant Gladys Bursey’s Responses to Form Interrogatories (Set One) and Request for Monetary Sanctions (“Motion to Compel”) against Bursey.

 

On July 20, 2026, Bursey filed her Opposition.  On July 24, 2026, Plaintiff filed its Reply.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.290 provides that a party may bring a motion to compel responses to interrogatories, where the responding party failed to serve a timely response. Unless otherwise agreed, the responding party is required to serve response within 30 days after service of the discovery demand for production documents. (Code Civ. Proc. §2030.290(b).) If the responding party fails to serve a timely response, the party “waives any objection to the demand, including one based on privilege or on the protection for work product . . .” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.290(a).) The requesting party need not demonstrate “good cause to compel responses, nor that it satisfied the “meet and confer” requirement. (Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pacific Healthcare Consultants (2007) 143 Cal.App.4th 390, 404.)¿ 

¿

Responses to interrogatories are due within 30 days after service of interrogatories. (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.260.) “The party propounding interrogatories and the responding party may agree to extend the time for service of a response to a set of interrogatories, or to particular interrogatories in a set, to a date beyond that provided in Section 2030.260.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.270(a).) While this agreement may be informal, it must be confirmed in writing. (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.270(b).)¿¿

 

DISCUSSION

 

At issue are the form interrogatories Plaintiff propounded to Bursey on December 1, 2025 and February 11, 2026. (Decl. Thomas Palecek (“Palecek”) at ¶¶ 4-5, Exh. A and B.) Plaintiff asserts that, as of filing this motion, Bursey has not provided responses. (Id. ¶ 11.) Plaintiff seeks sanctions under sections 2030.290(c), 2023.010, and 2023.030 et seq. of the Code of Civil Procedure in the amount of $675.00 for time spent on this motion.

 

Bursey asserts that she did not receive the propounded discovery, asked for the documents to be resent, and believed she had served all responses and, only upon obtaining a free legal consultation, learned that separate responses were required and has now served her responses to the propounded form interrogatories. Bursey contends that an order compelling initial responses is no longer necessary and monetary sanctions would be unjust. Among other issues, Bursey asserts that she earns approximately $24.00/hour, and a payment of $675.00 would be a serious hardship and interfere with her ability to pay ordinary living expenses.

 

Plaintiff confirms that responses have now been received. (Reply 2:16.) Plaintiff argues that sanctions are still warranted due to Bursey’s decision not to respond which constitutes an abuse of the discovery process and forced Plaintiff to file this motion.

 

The Motion to Compel is moot as to the responses themselves. The Court turns to the request for sanctions.

 

Bursey cannot use her status as a self-represented litigant to justify failure to respond. (See Nuño v. California State University, Bakersfield (2020) 47 Cal.App.5th 799, 811 [“Thus, as a general principle, a self-represented litigant who is not indigent ‘must expect and receive the same treatment as if represented by an attorney—no different, no better, no worse.’ (Taylor, supra, at p. 1009.) This principle was restated by our Supreme Court: “[W]e make clear that mere self-representation is not a ground for exceptionally lenient treatment. . .”].)

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.290 provides:

 

The court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel a response to interrogatories, unless it finds that one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances made the imposition of sanctions unjust.

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.030 provides:

 

The court may impose a monetary sanction ordering that one engaging in the misuse of the discovery process, or any attorney advising that conduct, or both pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney’s fees, incurred by anyone as a result of that conduct. The court may also impose this sanction on one unsuccessfully asserting that another has engaged in the misuse of the discovery process, or on any attorney who advised that assertion, or on both. If a monetary sanction is authorized by any provision of this title, the court shall impose that sanction unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.

 

While Bursey might be indigent, this does not render her insusceptible to sanctions. (See Code Civ. Proc. §§ § 2030.29, 2023.030 [no exemptions for indigent litigants]; see also Taggares v. Superior Court (1998) 62 Cal.App.4th 94, 105 [holding that it is not "fair nor reasonable" when one party "may be victorious in the discovery dispute and yet unable to recover the expense of obtaining discovery compliance from the other."].) Bursey has offered no documentary evidence to support her statements in her declaration as to her income and expenses. 

 

Plaintiff’s request for sanctions in the amount of $675 is granted.  The sum shall be paid to Plaintiff’s counsel in monthly payments of $65 on the first of the month commencing on September 1, 2026 and continuing until paid in full. 

 

Conclusion

 

Plaintiff Allstate Insurance Company’s Motion to Compel Defendant Gladys Bursey’s Responses to Form Interrogatories (Set One) is moot.

 

Plaintiff Allstate Insurance Company’s Request for Sanctions in the amount of $675 is granted. Plaintiff’s request for sanctions in the amount of $675 is granted.  The sum shall be paid to Plaintiff’s counsel in monthly payments of $65 on the first of the month commencing on September 1, 2026 and continuing until paid in full.

REPLY:                                  04/28/2026

RULING SUMMARY:          Defendant FCA US, LLC’s Demurrer is sustained with leave to amend as to the Sixth Cause of Action (Fraudulent Concealment – Inducement); and overruled as to the First Cause of Action (Violation of Subdivision (d) of Civ. Code § 1793.2), Second Cause of Action (Violation of Subdivision (b) of Civ. Code § 1793.2), Third Cause of Action (Violation of Subdivision (a)(3) of Civ. Code § 1793.2), and Fourth Cause of Action (Breach of the Implied Warranty of Merchantability (Civ. Code §§ 1791.1, 1794, 1795.5).

 

Plaintiff Sheryell Pinkney is to file an amended pleading within ______ days of this Court Ruling.  The Court continues the Case Management Conference to __________, 2026, at 8:30AM, and sets an Order to Show Cause re Amended Complaint for the same date and time.

                                                                                                                                               

Background

 

On June 30, 2025, plaintiff Sheryell Pinkney (“Plaintiff”) filed her Complaint against defendants FCA US LLC (“FCA”) and Aaron Chrysler Dodge Jeep Ram (“Aaron Chrysler”), alleging six causes of action for (1) Violation of Subdivision (d) of Civ. Code § 1793.2 against FCA; (2) Violation of Subdivision (b) of Civ. Code § 1793.2 against FCA; (3) Violation of Subdivision (a)(3) of Civ. Code § 1793.2 against FCA; (4) Breach of the Implied Warranty of Merchantability (Civ. Code §§ 1791.1, 1794, 1795.5) against FCA; (5) Negligent Repair against Aaron Chrysler; (6) Fraudulent Inducement Concealment against Defendant FCA.

 

Plaintiff alleges the following:  On or about November 30, 2017, Plaintiff entered into a warranty contract with FCA regarding a 2018 Chrysler 300 with vehicle identification number 2C3CCAAG5JH190675 (the “Subject Vehicle”), manufactured and/or distributed by FCA; the warranty contract included various warranties; defects and nonconformities to warranty manifested themselves within the applicable express warranty period, including but not limited to an engine defect; FCA had a duty to promptly offer to repurchase or replace the Subject Vehicle at the time it failed to conform the Subject Vehicle to the terms of the express warranty after a reasonable number of repair attempts; and FCA did not do so. Regarding Aaron Chrysler, Plaintiff alleges a theory of negligent repair.

 

On December 22, 2025, Plaintiff filed her First Amended Complaint (“FAC”), alleging the same causes of action against FCA and Aaron Chrysler.

 

On January 23, 2026, FCA filed this Demurrer.  On April 22, 2026, Plaintiff filed her Opposition. On April 28, 2026, Defendant filed its Reply.

 

MEET AND CONFER

 

Before filing a demurrer or motion to strike, the moving party is required to meet and confer with the party who filed the pleading demurred to, in person or telephonically, to determine whether an agreement can be reached through a filing of an amended pleading that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer. (Code Civ. Proc. §§ 430.41, 435.5.)¿¿¿

 

¿The meet and confer requirement¿has¿been met. (Decl. Andrew S. Gahan at ¶ 2.)

 

LEGAL STANDARD

 

A.    Demurrer

 

A demurrer is an objection to a pleading, the grounds for which are apparent from either the face of the complaint or a matter of which the court may take judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.30(a); Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)¿“To survive a demurrer, the complaint need only allege facts sufficient to state a cause of action; each evidentiary fact that might eventually form part of the plaintiff’s proof need not be alleged.”¿(C.A. v. William S. Hart Union High School Dist. (2012) 53 Cal.4th 861, 872.)¿For the purpose of testing the sufficiency of the cause of action, the demurrer admits the truth of all material facts properly pleaded.¿ (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 966-967.)¿A demurrer “does not admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.”¿(Daar v. Yellow Cab Co. (1967) 67 Cal.2d 695, 713.)

 

B.    Leave to Amend

 

“Where the defect raised by a motion to strike or by demurrer is reasonably capable of cure, leave to amend is routinely and liberally granted to give the plaintiff a chance to cure the defect in question.” (CLD Construction, Inc. v. City of San Ramon (2004) 120 Cal.App.4th 1141, 1146.) “A trial court does not abuse its discretion when it sustains a demurrer without¿leave to amend¿if either (a) the facts and the nature of the claims are clear and no liability exists, or (b) it is probable from the nature of the defects and previous unsuccessful attempts to plead that the plaintiff cannot state a claim.” (Cantu v. Resolution Trust Corp.¿(1992)¿4 Cal.App.4th 857, 889.)¿¿ The burden is on the complainant to show the Court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.)¿ 

 

DISCUSSION

 

FCA demurs to the all causes of action within the FAC.

 

A.    Statute of Repose Argument

 

The first issue presented is whether the statutory deadlines under sections 871.20 and 871.21 of the Code of Civil Procedure create a bar to the Song-Beverly Act claims as the Complaint was filed on June 30, 2025, approximately six months after the new statutes were imposed. Defendant directs this argument at Plaintiff’s First through Fourth Causes of Action.

 

Section 871.21 provides, in pertinent part:

 

(a) An action covered by Section 871.20 shall be commenced within one year after the expiration of the applicable express warranty.

 

(b) Notwithstanding subdivision (a), an action covered by Section 871.20 shall not be brought later than six years after the date of original delivery of the motor vehicle.

 

Section 871.20 identifies the causes of action which fall under section 871.21, providing in relevant part:

 

(a) [T]his chapter applies to an action, brought against a manufacturer who has elected under Section 871.29 to proceed under this chapter, seeking restitution or replacement of a motor vehicle pursuant to subdivision (b) or (d) of Section 1793.2, Section 1793.22, or Section 1794 of the Civil Code, or for civil penalties pursuant to subdivision (c) of Section 1794 of the Civil Code, where the request for restitution or replacement is based on noncompliance with the applicable express warranty.

 

(b) This chapter does not apply to service contract claims under Section 1794 of the Civil Code or any action seeking remedies that are not restitution or replacement of a motor vehicle.

 

Plaintiffs’ First, Second, and Third Causes of Action are brought under sections 1793.2(d), 1793.2(b), and 1793.2(a)(3) of the Civil Code.  Subdivisions (b) and (d) of section 1793.2 are expressly covered under the plain language of section 871.20(a).  As such, Plaintiffs’ First Cause of Action (Violation of Subdivision (d) of Civ. Code § 1793.2) and Second Cause of Action (Violation of Subdivision (b) of Civ. Code§ 1793.2) must have been “commenced within one year after the expiration of the applicable express warranty.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 871.21(a).)

 

Section 1793.2(a)(3) states that "[e]very manufacturer of consumer goods sold in this state and for which the manufacturer has made an express warranty shall . . .[m]ake available to authorized service and repair facilities sufficient service literature and replacement parts to effect repairs during the express warranty period." (Civ. Code, §1793.2(a)(3).) 

 

The remedies for violation of this section are in section 1974 of the Civil Code. 

 

Plaintiff alleges:

 

Defendant FCA's failure to comply with its obligations under Civil Code section

1793.2, subdivision (a)(3) was willful, in that Defendant FCA knew of its obligation to provide literature and replacement parts sufficient to allow its repair facilities to effect repairs during the warranty period, yet Defendant FCA failed to take any action to correct its failure to comply with the law. Accordingly, Plaintiff is entitled to a civil penalty of two times Plaintiff's actual damages, pursuant to Civil Code section 1794(c).

 

(FAC at ¶ 80.)

 

Claims for the enforcement of warranties under section 1794 explicitly fall within the covered claims defined by Code of Civil Procedure section 871.20(a).  Thus, Plaintiff’s Third Cause of Action (Violation of Subdivision (a)(3) of Civ. Code § 1793.2) fall within the scope of section 871.21 and must have been “commenced within one year after the expiration of the applicable express warranty.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 871.21(a).)

 

Section 871.21 of the Code of Civil Procedure was operative at the time that Plaintiff filed this action on June 30, 2025. Plaintiffs’ argument that Defendant had not opted into the new procedures at the time this action was filed is unavailing, because the opt-in requirement for manufacturers was not in effect at the time Plaintiff filed this action in June 2025. (Cal. Code Civ. Proc. §§ 871.20, 871.29, 871.30 [effect of opt-in clause effective April 2025].)  Senate Bill 26 pushed back the effective date of the opt-in provision from April 1 2025 to July 2025.  Thus, FCA did not need to opt-in for section 871.21 of the Code of Civil Procedure to be effective when Plaintiffs filed this action.

 

Plaintiffs’ contention that section 871.21 cannot be retroactively applied is also unavailing.  Section 871.30(a) of the Code of Civil Procedure expressly permits a manufacturer to elect to be governed by Chapter 12 for all actions described in section 871.20(a) "with respect to all of its motor vehicles sold in the year 2025 and in all prior years by providing written notice of that election to the Arbitration Certification Program within the Department of Consumer Affairs.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 871.30(a).) As discussed above, the opt-in provision was not in effect at the time of this filing; however, alternatively, after the opt-in provision came into effect, once FCA opted in pursuant section 871.29 of the Code of Civil Procedure, the Subject Vehicle was subject to these provisions. (Code Civ. Proc. § 871.30.) Accordingly, Plaintiff has not demonstrated that the applicable statute of repose does not govern the limitations period for at least the first and second causes of action.

 

The Court next turns to applying the applicable statute of limitations to the allegations at hand.

 

As alleged, Plaintiff entered into the warranty contract with Defendant on or about November 30, 2017 (FAC ¶ 7), and “(d)efects and nonconformities to warranty manifested themselves within the applicable express warranty period, including but not limited to engine defects, transmission defects, electrical defects, among other defects and non-conformities” (FAC ¶ 21).  It appears that the issue alleged is an engine defect. (FAC ¶¶ 11, 24-25.) Plaintiff includes the relevant warranty in the Complaint as Exh. A.  Exh. A provides the following:

 

2.4 Powertrain Limited Warranty

 

A. Who Is Covered?

 

You are covered by the Powertrain Limited Warranty if you are a purchaser for use of the vehicle.

 

B. What’s Covered

 

The Powertrain Limited Warranty covers the cost of all parts and labor needed to repair a powertrain component listed in section 2.4 E below that is defective in workmanship and materials.

 

C. How Long It Lasts

 

The Powertrain Limited Warranty lasts for up to five years or 60,000 miles on the odometer, whichever occurs first, calculated from the start date of the Basic Limited Warranty, as set forth in Section 2.1 E.

 

(Exh. A at p.9.)

 

The Court uses the Powertrain Limited Warranty for calculations as it (1) covers the engine in which the defect is alleged, and (2) is the longer warranty as compared with the Basic Limited Warranty. (Compare Exh. A p.6 and p.9.)

 

The Complaint does not allege the mileage of the Subject Vehicle, and the Court uses the years to compute the statute of limitations.  Five years from November 30, 2017 is November 30, 2022. Thus, the statute of limitations for both the powertrain appears to have expired November 30, 2022.

 

With respect to the three causes of action subject to Section 871.21, Plaintiff contends that equitable tolling should be applied. Section 871.21(c) describes those circumstances under which a court “shall” toll the time limitations, and not those circumstances under which a court may toll the time limitations.  The Court finds that Section 871.21(c) does not preclude tolling for equitable tolling under the appropriate circumstances.

 

As alleged, no dates are given for when the Discovery Rule Tolling begins (FAC at ¶¶ 13-30, 51-16); Plaintiff’s allegations show that Plaintiff was put on notice of potential issues in the Subject Vehicle sometime during the express warranty period (FAC ¶ 21); and no amount of time is provided for the time the Subject Vehicle was out of service by reason of repair for any nonconformity (Code Civ. Proc. § 871.21; see also FAC ¶¶ 57-60). However, class action tolling is integrated into Plaintiff’s Song-Beverly claims as Plaintiff has identified Maugain, et al. v. FCA US LLC, Case No. 1:22-cv-00116 (D.Del.) filed Jan. 28, 2022, which concerns FCA’s 3.6L engines. (FAC at ¶ 50.) Likewise, the Court believes estoppel applies as Plaintiff has specifically pleaded facts that, if proved, would support the theory, in the form of representations from FCA’s repair facility, that the Subject Vehicle had been repaired and was working as described. (Mills v. Forestex Co. (2003) 108 Cal.App.4th 625, 641; see FAC ¶¶ 15-16, 62-63.)

 

Accordingly, the Demurrer to First Cause of Action (Violation of Subdivision (d) of Civ. Code § 1793.2), Second Cause of Action (Violation of Subdivision (b) of Cal. Civ. Code § 1793.2), and Third Cause of Action (Violation of Subdivision (a)(3) of Cal. Civ. Code § 1793.2) is overruled.

 

B.    Implied Warranty Statute of Limitations Argument

 

Defendant does not argue that the Fourth Cause of Action for Breach of Implied Warranty is subject to Section 871.21. Rather, Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s implied warranty claims are subject to a four-year statute of limitations. (Comm. Code, § 2725; Montoya v. Ford Motor Co. (2020) 46 Cal.App.5th 493, 495.) 

 

The argument turns on Mexia v. Rinker Boat Co. (2009) 174 Cal.App.4th 1297 (“Mexia”). In Mexia, the appellate court held that the duration provision of Civil Code section 1791.1(c) did not limit when a defect must be discovered for purposes of the implied warranty, but instead, limited the period during which the implied warranty existed: “[T]he plain language of the statute, particularly in light of the consumer protection policies supporting the Song-Beverly Act, make clear that the statute merely creates a limited, prospective duration for the implied warranty of merchantability; it does not create a deadline for discovering latent defects or for giving notice to the seller.” (Id. at 1301.) 

“The duration provision provides, in essence, that the duration of the implied warranty of merchantability shall be the same as the duration of any reasonable express warranty that accompanies the product, but in no event shorter than 60 days or longer than one year. (Civ. Code, § 1791.1, subd. (c).) There is nothing that suggests a requirement that the purchaser discover and report to the seller a latent defect within that time period.” (Mexia, supra, 174 Cal.App.4th at 1310.) 

“The implied warranty of merchantability may be breached by a latent defect undiscoverable at the time of sale. . . . Indeed, “[u]ndisclosed latent defects … are the very evil that the implied warranty of merchantability was designed to remedy.” . . . In the case of a latent defect, a product is rendered unmerchantable, and the warranty of merchantability is breached, by the existence of the unseen defect, not by its subsequent discovery.” (Id. at 1304-1305.)

“A cause of action accrues when the breach occurs, regardless of the aggrieved party's lack of knowledge of the breach. A breach of warranty occurs when tender of delivery is made, except that where a warranty explicitly extends to future performance of the goods and discovery of the breach must await the time of such performance the cause of action accrues when the breach is or should have been discovered.” (Com. Code, § 2725, subds. (1), (2); Mexia, supra, 174 Cal.App.4th at 1305-1306 [emphasis added].) 

On the face of the FAC, the discovery of the alleged defects occurred sometime during the applicable express warranty period. (FAC ¶ 21.) However, both class action tolling and equitable tolling in the form of estoppel are alleged, as discussed above, and in light of the tolling, the assertion of this cause of action is timely.

 

Accordingly, the Demurrer to the Fourth Cause of Action (Breach of Implied Warranty of Merchantability (Cal. Civ. Code §§ 1791.1, 1794, 1795.5)) is overruled.

 

C.    Sixth Cause of Action (Fraudulent Inducement-Concealment)

 

1.     Statute of Limitations

 

FCA’s statute of limitations argument as to the Sixth Cause of Action is premised on the three-year statute of limitations set out in Code of Civil Procedure section 338. The gravamen of FCA’s argument is that Plaintiff has not pled any facts to establish the tolling doctrine.

 

As discussed above, this is not the case as both class action tolling and equitable tolling in the form of estoppel are alleged.  

 

The statute of limitations does not provide a ground to sustain the Demurrer to the Sixth Cause of Action (Fraudulent Concealment – Inducement).

 

2.     Specificity

 

To state a claim for fraudulent concealment, a plaintiff may plead and prove “a relationship . . . sufficient to give rise to a duty to disclose.” (Bigler-Engler v. Breg, Inc. (2017) 7 Cal.App.5th 276, 314 (“Bigler-Engler”).) Transactional privity is the simplest such relationship. (E.g. Dhital v. Nissan North America, Inc. (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 828, 844.) But it may also consist of “advertising, or . . . direct monetary benefit[.]” (Bader v. Johnson & Johnson (2022) 86 Cal.App.5th 1094, 1132 [citing Bigler-Engler, supra, at 311].) “Under California law, a vendor has a duty to disclose material facts not only to immediate purchasers, but also to subsequent purchasers when the vendor has reason to expect that the item will be resold. [Citations.]” (OCM Principal Opportunities Fund, L.P. v. CIBC World Markets Corp. (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 835, 859, italics in original [defendant investment bank prepared false offering memorandum for notes purchased by plaintiffs].) As a result, “a manufacturer of a product may [generally] be liable for fraud when it conceals material product information from potential users.” (Khan v. Shiley Inc. (1990) 217 Cal.App.3d 848, 858 [heart valve manufacturer concealed defects, sued by injured patient post-surgery].) “ ‘It is not necessary that the maker of the representation ‘have any particular person in mind. It is enough that he intends or has reason to expect to have it repeated to a particular class of persons and that the person relying upon it is one of that class.” . . .’ . . .” (Barnhouse v. City of Pinole (1982) 133 Cal.App.3d 171, 191–192, internal citations omitted [home developer liable to ultimate home purchasers despite no privity].) Plaintiff has pled a relationship sufficient to establish a duty to disclose: that FCA derived direct monetary benefit from the sale of the Subject Vehicle, and that it had reason to expect the Subject Vehicle would ultimately end up in the hands of purchasers, like Plaintiff. (FAC ¶¶ 4, 12, 91-98.) 

 

Addressing FCA’s specificity argument, a concealment cause of action has a more relaxed specificity requirement as compared to a claim based on affirmative misrepresentations; the rule of specifically pleading how, when, where, to whom and by what means, misrepresentations were communicated is intended to apply to affirmative misrepresentations and not to concealment. (Alfaro v. Community Housing Improvement System & Planning Assn, Inc. (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1356, 1384.) The relaxed standard relies on the natural logic that a plaintiff cannot specifically plead a fraud committed by omission, because there is no “how, when”, etc., for a plaintiff identify. (See ibid.) Here, Plaintiffs allege that the engine defect (defective 3.6L engine posing a safety risk) was known by FCA and concealed during specific transactions between Plaintiff and FCA’s agents. (FAC ¶ 34-35, 62, 94.)

 

However, FCA’s argument that the FAC lacks factual allegations as to the Subject Vehicle manifesting the defect itself is persuasive.  Plaintiff alleges that engines installed in vehicles like the Subject Vehicle may suffer from “one or more defects that can result in loss of power, stalling, engine running rough, engine misfires, failure or replacement of the engine.” (FAC ¶¶ 25, 92.) The FAC does not allege that the Subject Vehicle actually experienced any of those symptoms, was diagnosed with the alleged Engine Defect, was presented for repairs because of the defect, or otherwise actually contained or manifested the defect. Alleging that the Engine Defect can occur in vehicles equipped with a 3.6L engine does not, without additional facts, establish that the Subject Vehicle was defective. Further, without these factual allegations, Plaintiff’s FAC is distinguished from those found sufficient in Dhital v. Nissan North America, Inc. (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 828 (“Dhital”).[1]  In Dhital, at least three repair attempts were described. (Id. at 833 [“On three occasions in 2015, plaintiffs took the car to an authorized Nissan repair facility because of transmission problems, including stalling, jerking, and lack of power.”].)

 

Plaintiff has not sufficiently alleged that FCA concealed a material fact concerning the Subject Vehicle or that the alleged omission caused him to purchase a defective vehicle and sustain resulting damage.

 

There is a reasonable possibility that Plaintiff can cure these deficiencies by alleging symptoms experienced by the Subject Vehicle, repair presentations or attempts, or other facts showing that the alleged engine defect existed in or manifested in the Subject Vehicle.

 

The Demurrer to the Sixth Cause of Action (Fraudulent Concealment – Inducement) premised on specificity is sustained with leave to amend.

 

Conclusion

 

Defendant FCA US, LLC’s Demurrer is sustained with leave to amend as to the Sixth Cause of Action (Fraudulent Concealment – Inducement); and overruled as to the First Cause of Action (Violation of Subdivision (d) of Civ. Code § 1793.2), Second Cause of Action (Violation of Subdivision (b) of Civ. Code § 1793.2), Third Cause of Action (Violation of Subdivision (a)(3) of Civ. Code § 1793.2), and Fourth Cause of Action (Breach of the Implied Warranty of Merchantability (Civ. Code §§ 1791.1, 1794, 1795.5).

LEGAL STANDARD

 

A.    Demurrer

 

A demurrer is an objection to a pleading, the grounds for which are apparent from either the face of the complaint or a matter of which the court may take judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.30(a); see also Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)¿“To survive a demurrer, the complaint need only allege facts sufficient to state a cause of action; each evidentiary fact that might eventually form part of the plaintiff’s proof need not be alleged.”¿(C.A. v. William S. Hart Union High School Dist. (2012) 53 Cal.4th 861, 872.)¿For the purpose of testing the sufficiency of the cause of action, the demurrer admits the truth of all material facts properly pleaded.¿ (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 966-967.)¿A demurrer “does not admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.”¿(Daar v. Yellow Cab Co. (1967) 67 Cal.2d 695, 713.)¿¿¿

 

B.    Leave to Amend 

 

“Where the defect raised by a motion to strike or by demurrer is reasonably capable of cure, leave to amend is routinely and liberally granted to give the plaintiff a chance to cure the defect in question.” (CLD Construction, Inc. v. City of San Ramon (2004) 120 Cal.App.4th 1141, 1146.) “A trial court does not abuse its discretion when it sustains a demurrer without¿leave to amend¿if either (a) the facts and the nature of the claims are clear and no liability exists, or (b) it is probable from the nature of the defects and previous unsuccessful attempts to plead that the plaintiff cannot state a claim.” (Cantu v. Resolution Trust Corp.¿(1992)¿4 Cal.App.4th 857, 889.)¿¿ The burden is on the complainant to show the Court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.)¿

DISCUSSION

 

A.    First Cause of Action (Wrongful Foreclosure)

 

Moving Defendants argue that the First Cause of Action is barred by the parties’ settlement agreement. Specifically, Moving Defendants provide that Plaintiffs waived the right to challenge PFN’s acceleration of the loan in their previous settlement agreement, attached as Exhibit C to the FAC. As the settlement agreement is included in the FAC, Moving Defendants cite to Holland v. Morse Diesel Intern., Inc. (2001) 86 Cal.App.4th 1443 (“Holland”) for its holding that when facts in exhibits contradict those alleged, the facts in exhibits take precedence. Moving Defendants note that the previous action in which the settlement was reached was dismissed with prejudice. As an alternative ground, Moving Defendants note Tomer’s declaration, filed with the Court in the UD action, in which Tomer admits that the Residential Listing Agreement was delivered on September 18, 2025, PFN did not receive it within five business days before the September 23, 2025 sale and the Court’s previous statement that the “allegation that PFN violated Civil Code Section 2924f(e) is without merit.” (See RJN, Ex. 13 at p. 12)

 

Plaintiffs argue that the basis for the FAC is an “entirely new wrongful foreclosure based upon conduct occurring months after execution of the Settlement Agreement.” (Opp. 7:14-15.) They reference the allegations in the FAC of conduct by PFN that occurred after the settlement agreement date of April 01, 2025, and argue that section 1668 of the Civil Code prohibits contractual provisions that exempt a party from liability for future violations of law or misconduct. For the same reasons, Plaintiffs contend that the dismissal does not apply to this action.  Plaintiffs further argue that their allegations of compliance with Civil Code section 2924 must be deemed true for the purpose of this Demurrer.

 

In Reply, Moving Defendants reiterate their arguments, in particular, that Plaintiffs’ interpretation of the settlement agreement expressly contradicts the terms of the agreement, pursuant to which the foreclosure sale was postponed until on or after a certain date to give Plaintiffs an opportunity to pay off or refinance the loan.  Moving Defendants argue that, on demurrer, the Court has the ability to consider Plaintiffs’ own party admissions.  Finally, Defendants assert that Plaintiffs’ prior action was dismissed with prejudice, and such a dismissal constitutes a final judgment on the merits.

 

The FAC, while incorporating allegations from 2005, premises the First Cause of Action on the following allegation:

 

The purported default as stated in the Default Letter and the resulting Notice of Default were released on April 1, 2025. Therefore, no valid Notice of Default existed. Because no valid Notice of Default existed, there was no valid Notice of Sale.

 

(FAC ¶ 37.)

 

Exhibit A to the FAC is a copy of a notice of default (“NOD”) signed December 9, 2024 and filed on December 10, 2024. The settlement agreement in Exhibit C to the FAC references the NOD in section 2.5. The settlement terms are as follows:

 

SETTLEMENT TERMS. The Parties agree to the following settlement terms:

4.1 Concurrent with the full execution of this Settlement Agreement, Fridman shall file with the Superior Court in which the Action is pending a dismissal with prejudice of the Complaint in its entirety as against all defendants, including PFN, Suzanne Arancibia, Marc Shenkman and TD Processing.

4.2 Concurrent with the full execution of this Settlement Agreement, PFN and/or TD Processing shall cause the trustee sale that is currently scheduled pursuant to the Notice of Sale to occur on April 10, 2025 to a new date so that the foreclosure sale shall not occur or be conducted prior to May 20, 2025 and Borrower shall have until May 20, 2025 to refinance or payoff the Loan

 

(FAC, Exh. C at p. 2).

 

The Mutual Release section provides:

 

Except for the payment obligations created by the Note, the Deed of Trust and this Settlement Agreement and as otherwise expressly provided herein, on the effective date of this Settlement Agreement, each of the Parties hereto releases, acquits, forever discharges and covenants not to sue each other, and all past and present subsidiary corporations, affiliates, successors, assigns, shareholders, officers, directors, partners, joint venturers, members, managers, agents, principals, attorneys, employees, insurers and heirs from all claims, losses, causes of action, costs, expenses, attorney fees, liabilities (whether statutory, equitable or legal), indemnities, subrogations (contractual or equitable), duties and any and all obligations of every nature, character and description whatsoever at law or in equity, known or unknown, which they ever had, now have, or may have, or arising out of, concerning, pertaining to or connected with any and every matter or thing whatsoever occurred, done, omitted or suffered to be done prior to the effective date hereof, as it relates to the Claims, and/or any claims or defenses which could have been brought in the Action.”

(Id., Exh. C pp. 2-3.)

 

Plaintiffs’ allegations are contradicted by Exhibit C.  If facts appearing in an exhibit contradict the allegations in the pleading, the facts in the exhibit control. (Holland, supra, 86 Cal.App.4th 1443, 1447 [superseded by statute on other grounds].) As shown by Exhibit C, the NOD remained valid, and Moving Defendants obligations under the settlement were to postpone the foreclosure sale until on or after May 20, 2025. The FAC alleges that the sale of the Property occurred on November 18, 2025, almost six months after the agreed upon postponement date in the settlement. (FAC ¶ 31.)

 

The elements of a claim for wrongful foreclosure are: (1) the defendants caused an illegal, fraudulent, or willfully oppressive sale of the property pursuant to a power of sale in a mortgage or deed of trust; (2) the plaintiff suffered prejudice or harm; and (3) the plaintiff tendered the amount of the secured indebtedness or was excused from tendering. (Chavez v. Indymac Mortgage Services (2013) 219 Cal.App.4th 1052, 1062; Lona v. Citibank, N.A. (2011) 202 Cal.App.4th 89, 104.) As alleged, a defect appears on the face of the pleading as Exhibit C shows that there was a valid sale of the Property under the NOD.

 

In their Opposition to the Demurrer, Plaintiff contend that a claim for wrongful foreclosure is stated in the First Amended Complaint, because, as alleged in paragraph 28, they delivered an executed Residential Purchase Agreement to the trustee in the Deed of Trust, on October 2, 2025, and Defendants thereafter failed to comply with Civil Code section 2924f.  This allegation is contradicted by the declaration Tomer submitted in support of his opposition to the motion for summary judgment in the unlawful detainer matter, No. 25STCV36105, of which the Court has taken judicial notice (RJN Exh. 12, § 10, Exh. E.)  In that declaration, Tomer stated under penalty of perjury that attached to his declaration as Exhibit E was a “true and correct copy of the receipt from FedEx as payment for delivery of the Residential listing Agreement to the law trustee, together with FedEx confirmation of Delivery of the listing Agreement that [he] obtained from FedEx.com.”  With this admission, the Court determined that PFN had not violated section 2924f(e)(1) of the Civil Code and that Plaintiffs’ allegation to the contrary was “without merit.”  (RJN, Exh. 13, p.12.) 

 

In connection with PFN’s motion for reconsideration of the Court’s denial of its motion for summary judgment, the Court further determined as a matter of law in Case No. 25STCV36105 the following: 

 

Ultimately, another undisputed fact precludes Defendants from relying on the listing agreement delivered in September 2025 to argue that PFN was required to delay the sale for 45 days: They had previously delivered a purchase and sale agreement to the trustee for that very purpose.  In seeking relief form the automatic stay in Bankruptcy Court, Plaintiff’s submitted a declaration by Marc S. Shenkman asserting that “[t]he day before the July 17, 2025 foreclosure sale, on July 16, 2025 at 9:25 p.m.” Defendants’ attorney Lisa Skaist (“Skaist”), “sought to trigger the statutory automatic 45-day foreclosure sale postponement period under Civil Code Section 2924f(e)(3), by sending the unredacted April 14, 2025 purchase agreement and supporting documents to the foreclosure trustee.” (MSJ RJN Ex. 3(a).)

The statute prohibits a second bite of that apple.  Section 2924f(e) states that “[t]he provisions of this paragraph shall not be used to postpone the scheduled sale date more than once.” Accordingly, Defendants could not use section 2924f(e) a second time to postpone the September 26, 2026 foreclosure sale.

In sum, Defendants have presented no facts or evidence that would preclude the Court from finding that PFN acquired title to the Property via foreclosure sale in compliance with Civil Code section 2924. Even if PFN is not a bona find purchaser of value, this fact does not show that PFN did not properly acquire title at of the Property at the foreclosure and is entitled to immediate possession of the Property.

 

(Ruling filed May 29, 2026, in Case No. 25STCV36105.) 

 

The orders in Case No. 25STCV36105 stem from a ruling on a Motion for Summary Judgment. This matter cannot be relitigated due to the principles of res judicata. (See Henry v. Clifford (1995) 32 Cal.App.4th 315, 321 [“If the issue is alleged in the complaint and resolved by summary judgment, it may not be relitigated in a subsequent action. ‘The reason for this is manifest. A party cannot by negligence or design withhold issues and litigate them in consecutive actions. Hence the rule is that the prior judgment is res judicata on matters which were raised or could have been raised, on matters litigated or litigable.’ ( Sutphin v. Speik (1940) 15 Cal. 2d 195, 202 [99 P.2d 652].)”].)

 

For the reasons explained above, the demurrer to the First Cause of Action is sustained without leave to amend.  An amendment does not appear to be able to rectify the defect as Plaintiffs’ basis for the First Cause of Action is contradicted by Exhibit C to the FAC and precluded by Plaintiffs’ dismissal of its prior wrongful disclosure action with prejudice and prior rulings of the Court in case no. 25STCV36105.

 

Accordingly, the Demurrer to the First Cause of Action is sustained without leave to amend.

 

B.    Second Cause of Action (Breach of Contract)

 

Moving Defendants argue that Plaintiffs’ allegations for breach of contract are contradicted by the settlement agreement attached as Exhibit C to the FAC. Further, Moving Defendants contend that Plaintiffs cannot avoid the plain language of the contract they expressly agreed to and highlight the language of the settlement agreement.

 

Plaintiffs argue that Moving Defendants have premised their argument on the settlement agreement’s release, PFN breached Civil Code section 2924f, and alleged conduct on which the claim is based occurred after April 1, 2025.

 

In Reply, Moving Defendants assert that the settlement agreement did not release Plaintiffs from their obligations to make payments under the loan, and there are no allegations that Plaintiffs made any payments on the loan or were otherwise excused from doing so.

 

As alleged, the Second Cause of Action stems from “[p]roceeding with foreclosure sale after releasing the claims of default used to issue the Notice of Default[.]” (FAC at ¶ 45.) The Court has discussed this above. Plaintiffs’ obligations under the NOD were not released.

 

“To prevail on a cause of action for breach of contract, the plaintiff must prove (1) the contract, (2) the plaintiff’s performance of the contract or excuse for nonperformance, (3) the defendant’s breach, and (4) the resulting damage to the plaintiff.” (Richman v. Hartley (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 1182, 1186.)

 

As alleged, there has been no breach of contract as the settlement agreement did not release Plaintiffs from their obligations and the sale of the Property occurred after the agreed upon postponement date.

 

An amendment does not appear to be able to rectify the defect as Plaintiffs’ basis for the Second Cause of Action is, again, contradicted by Exhibit C to the FAC.

 

The Demurrer to the Second Cause of Action is sustained without leave to amend.

 

C.    Third Cause of Action (Intentional Interference with Contract)

 

Moving Defendants contend that the alleged acts of interference are premised on Plaintiffs’ belief that they were released from the NOD’s obligations. Moving Defendants argue that they were justified in proceeding according to the terms of the parties’ settlement agreement.

 

Plaintiffs argue that the Third Cause of Action is based on PFN’s interference with Plaintiffs’ October 01, 2025 Residential Purchase Agreement and, as such, the settlement agreement has no application.

 

Moving Defendants present that Plaintiffs have failed to address their arguments. Moving Defendants reiterate that their alleged acts of interference are not actionable.

 

As alleged, Plaintiffs entered into a valid contract with Murtaza Dar on October 1, 2025; Moving Defendants were aware of the contract; and Moving Defendants (1) failed and refused to postpone the trustee sale as they were required by California Civil Code 2924f, (2) illegally called into question the validity of the contract as a justification for refusing to postpone the trustee sale, (3) illegally imposed conditions upon Plaintiffs as a condition of postponing the trustee sale, and (4) demanded that the Buyer make its earnest money deposit as a condition of postponing the trustee sale. (FAC at ¶¶ 48-50.)

 

As presented, it appears that Moving Defendants satisfied Civil Code § 2924 before the settlement agreement. (See FAC at ¶¶ 16-17, Exhs. A-B.) However, more importantly, Plaintiffs’ allegations under Civil Code section 2924f disregard the parties’ settlement agreement. There are simply no allegations or arguments presented by Plaintiffs that show the interplay between Civil Code section 2924f and the settlement agreement. Moving Defendants rely on Cal-Medicon v. Los Angeles County Medical Assn. (1971) 20 Cal.App.3d 148, 152, in which the Court states:

 

One may not intentionally interfere with the contractual relations of another without justification. (Rest., Torts, § 766; Imperial Ice Co. v. Rossier, 18 Cal.2d 33 [112 P.2d 631].) Whether an interference is justified "depends on a balancing of the importance, social and private, of the objective advanced by the interference against the importance of the interest interfered with, considering all circumstances, among which the methods and means used and the relation of the parties are important." ( Masoni v. Board of Trade of S.F., 119 Cal.App.2d 738, 742 [260 P.2d 205]; Rest., Torts, §§ 767-774.) CA(3) (3) Although justification is usually a matter of affirmative defense, the complaint may reveal justification on its face. ( Greenberg v. Hollywood Turf Club, 7 Cal.App.3d 968 [86 Cal.Rptr. 885].)

 

As pled, it appears that Moving Defendants’ justification was the settlement agreement. Further, as stated above, there is no allegations related to how the settlement agreement plays into Civ. Code § 2924f and, by the facts alleged in Exh. C, Moving Defendants had waited past the agreed upon date of sale within the settlement.  

 

Moving Defendants’ Request for Judicial Notice provides insight into these matters. Specifically, Exhibit 13 shows that the Court has conducted an analysis on this matter which reads, in relevant part:

 

Third, by entering into the Settlement Agreement, the Fridmans waived the right to challenge PFN’s acceleration of the loan. This includes any allegation that PFN engaged in fraud by wrongfully accelerating the loan. Pursuant to the Settlement Agreement, PFN was entitled to proceed with the foreclosure sale after May 20, 2025. Once PFN purchased the Property at a duly noticed foreclose sale, it became a bona fide purchaser for value because it acquired interest in the Property without notice of a competing claim of title. First, Fridmans had expressly agreed in the Settlement Agreement that the sale of the Property could proceed if they were unable to pay the Loan or refinance it. (PFN RJN Ex. 1.) Second, before the foreclosure sale, the Property was transferred by T. Fridman to I. Fridman on the eve of her bankruptcy case, evidencing that I. Fridman could not pay off the loan or refinance it because it was in bankruptcy. (PFN RJN Ex. 1; 3, 9(a); 9(b).) Property was released by the Bankruptcy Court, and PFN purchased the Property (PFN RJN Ex. 6, 9(a), 9(b), 10.) “Thus, ‘a bona fide purchaser for value who acquires his interest in real property without notice of another's asserted rights in the property takes the property free of such unknown rights. [Citations.]’ ” (Melendrez, supra, 127 Cal.App.4th at p. 1251.)

 

As PFN is a bona find purchaser for value, the Fridmans fail to show that they are entitled to immediate possession of the Property such that the UD action should be stayed while the irregularities in the foreclosure sale process are litigated in a separate action. The Fridmans fail to show that the second point they made at the hearing, related to Civil Code Section 2924(f), has merit.

 

(RJN Exh. 13 at p. 11.)

 

This order stems from a ruling on a Motion for Summary Judgment. This matter cannot be relitigated due to the principles of res judicata. (See Henry v. Clifford (1995) 32 Cal.App.4th 315, 321 [“If the issue is alleged in the complaint and resolved by summary judgment, it may not be relitigated in a subsequent action. ‘The reason for this is manifest. A party cannot by negligence or design withhold issues and litigate them in consecutive actions. Hence the rule is that the prior judgment is res judicata on matters which were raised or could have been raised, on matters litigated or litigable.’ ( Sutphin v. Speik (1940) 15 Cal. 2d 195, 202 [99 P.2d 652].)”].)

 

Finally, as explained above, Plaintiffs’ prior tender of a purported purchase and sale agreement precluded Plaintiffs from seeking a further postponement of the sale by the tender of another purported purchase and sale agreement.

 

Accordingly, the Demurrer to the Third Cause of Action is sustained without leave to amend.

 

D.    Fourth Cause of Action (Negligence Per Se)

 

Moving Defendants argue that Negligence Per Se is not a cause of action and cannot stand alone and the allegation that PFN violated Civil Code Section 2924f(e) is without merit, citing to Exhibit 12 and 13 in the Request for Judicial Notice.

 

Plaintiffs argue that Negligence Per Se is a claim for negligence and, therefore, a negligence claim has been pled in the Fourth Cause of Action.

 

Request for Judicial Notice Exhibit 13 at page 12 shows that the Court held “. . .the allegation that PFN violated Civil Code Section 2924f(e) is without merit.” The Court provided its analysis in the Ruling:

 

In opposition to PFN’s motion for summary judgment, the T. Fridman again purports to attach “a true and correct copy of the receipt from FedEx as payment for delivery of the Residential Lising Agreement to the slaw trustee, together with FedEx confirmation of deliver of the Listing Agreement that I obtained from FedEx.com.” (T. Fridman Decl., § 10, Ex. E.) Exhibit E bears the Tracking ID of “393320295641” and listed the delivery date as “Thursday, 9/18/25 10:21

AM”. (/d.) Assuming that the correct delivery date of Residential Listing Agreement is September 18, 2025, PFN did not receive it within 5 business days before the September 23, 2025 sale. Thus, PFN had no obligation to postpone the foreclosure sale.

 

Consequently, the allegation that PFN violated Civil Code Section 2924f(e) is without merit.

 

(Exh. 13 at p.12.)

 

Exhibit E, which is included in Exhibit 12, is Tomer’s FedEx Confirmation of Delivery.

 

Whether Negligence Per Se is a cause of action is of no consequence as the Court has already issued a ruling on the matter in a motion for summary judgment. (See analysis in Third Cause of Action). Plaintiffs are barred from relitigating the matter.  

 

Accordingly, the Demurrer to the Fourth Cause of Action is sustained without leave to amend.

 

E.    Fifth Cause of Action (Negligence)

 

The Fifth Cause of Action is premised on the allegations concerning the proceeding of the foreclosure. (See FAC at ¶ 62.)

 

Moving Defendants incorporate their previous arguments regarding the settlement agreement.

 

Plaintiffs contend that Moving Defendants take no issue with the allegations in this cause of action. (See Opp. 14:10.) Plaintiffs repeat their statutory requirements argument.

 

In Reply, Moving Defendants argue that Plaintiffs fail to demonstrate any facts that would support a duty by Defendants separate and apart from their mutual contractual obligations under the settlement agreement, the terms of which expressly permitted Moving Defendants to proceed with the foreclosure sale in the event Plaintiffs failed to comply with their payment obligations.

 

“ ‘The elements of a cause of action for negligence are well established. They are “(a) a legal duty to use due care; (b) a breach of such legal duty; [and] (c) the breach as the proximate or legal cause of the resulting injury.” ’ ” (Ladd v. County of San Mateo (1996) 12 Cal.4th 913, 917.)

 

As described above, the settlement agreement did not release Plaintiffs of their obligation under the note and deed of trust, and Moving Defendants fulfilled their obligation to withhold selling the Property May 20, 2025. As pled, there has been no breach of duty.  

 

Conclusion

 

Defendants PFN Lending Group, Inc. and Mark Shenkman’s Demurrer is sustained without leave to amend.
