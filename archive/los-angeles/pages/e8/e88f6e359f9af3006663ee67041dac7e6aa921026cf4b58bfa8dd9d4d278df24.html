Case Number: 21STCV07986    Hearing Date: September 23, 2026    Dept: F43

Dept. F43

Hearing Date: 09-23-26

Case # 21STCV07986, Luna v. Caremeridian, LLC, et al.

Trial Date: 11-02-26

 

APPLICATION TO BE ADMITTED PRO HAC VICE

 

MOVING PARTY:  Attorney Ernest C. Tosh

RESPONDING PARTY:  No opposition was filed.

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Order granting attorney Ernest C. Tosh’s application to appear pro hac vice for plaintiff Abel Luna.

 

RULING:  Application is granted.

 

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

 

On March 1, 2021, plaintiff Abel Luna (Plaintiff) filed this adult abuse and neglect action against defendants CareMeridian, LLC dba Neurorestorative Oso North and Providence Health System - Southern California dba Providence Holy Cross Medical Center.  Providence Health was dismissed. On June 20, 2025, Plaintiff identified National Mentor Healthcare, LLC dba Savita Health as a defendant.

 

On August 27, 2026, plaintiff Abel Luna filed the instant application for attorney Ernest C. Tosh to appear in this action pro hac vice.  The application is unopposed.

 

 

ANALYSIS

 

A.  Pro Hac Vice Applications

 

Any attorney licensed to practice before the bar of any United States court or the highest court in any state, and who has been retained to appear in a particular cause pending in a California court, but who is not a licensee of the California State Bar may apply to appear pro hac vice in California by submitting a written application and mailing notice to all interested parties, as well as notice and a $50.00 application fee to the State Bar Association in San Francisco.¿ (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(a), (c), (e).)¿ An applicant may not reside or work in California, and may not perform regular or substantial business, professional, or other activities here. ¿(Id., rule 9.40(a).)

 

The application must state:

 

(1) The applicant’s residence and office address;

(2) The courts to which the applicant has been admitted to practice and the dates of admission;

(3) That the applicant is a licensee in good standing in those courts;

(4) That the applicant is not currently suspended or disbarred in any court;

(5) The title of each court and cause in which the applicant has filed an application to appear as counsel pro hac vice in this state in the preceding two years, the date of each application, and whether or not it was granted; and

(6) The name, address, and telephone number of the active licensee of the State Bar of California who is attorney of record.

 

(Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(d).)

 

Plaintiff Luna submits the verified application of attorney Ernest C. Tosh to be admitted pro hac vice counsel on his behalf.  (Verified App. of Ernest C. Tosh.)  Mr. Tosh is licensed to practice law in the States of Texas, Arkansas, Oklahoma, North Carolina, and Wyoming and in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of Texas, is in good standing with the state bars, and has never suspended or disbarred by any court.[1]  (Id. ¶ 3.)  Mr. Tosh is a resident of Texas, is not regularly employed in California, and does not regularly engage in substantial business, professional, or other activities in California.  (Id. ¶ 2.)  In the previous two years, Mr. Tosh has applied once to appear as pro hac vice counsel in California, the application was granted, and the case has been resolved.  (Id. ¶ 4.)

 

Attorney Joseph M. Earley, III, plaintiff Luna’s counsel of record, is the California attorney whose contact information has been provided.  (Verified App. ¶ 5;  Declaration of Joseph M. Earley, III.)  Mr. Tosh’s application, the notice of hearing, and payment of the processing fee have been submitted to the State Bar of California.  (Verified App. ¶ 6; Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(c).)

 

Because counsel have fulfilled the requirements of California Rule of Court, rule 9.40(c)-(e), the court grants attorney Ernest C. Tosh’s application.

 

 

CONCLUSION and ORDER

 

Attorney Ernest C. Tosh’s application to appear as counsel pro hac vice is granted.

 

Plaintiff Abel Luna to give notice.

Case Number: 22CHCV01034    Hearing Date: September 23, 2026    Dept: F43

Dept. F43

Hearing Date: 09-23-26

Case # 22CHCV01034, 23CHCV02673, Bruch, et al. v. Prince, et al.

Trial Date: None set.

 

DEMURRER

 

MOVING PARTY:  Defendant Plant Works Group, Inc. (Doe 1)

RESPONDING PARTY:  Plaintiff Isidro Candelario

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Order sustaining defendant Plant Works Group, Inc.’s demurrer to the First, Second, and Third causes of action in plaintiff Cadelario’s Complaint.

 

RULING:  Demurrer is sustained with leave to amend.

 

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

 

This is a consolidated matter involving five separate cases which arise from an explosion and fire that occurred on October 18, 2021 at a building located at 8423 North Canoga Avenue (subject property).  The instant ruling involves Case No. 23CHCV02673, Isidro Candelario v. Randall Senter, et al.  On September 5, 2023, plaintiff Isidro Candelario filed a complaint for injuries and damages suffered at the October 18, 2021, explosion and fire, asserting (1) negligence; (2) strict liability (ultrahazardous activity); (3) premises liability; (4) strict product liability - design defect; (5) strict product liability - manufacturing defect; (6) strict product liability - failure to warn; and (7) negligence product liability.

 

Plaintiff Candelario alleges defendants Canna Systems Corp., Canna Systems Holding, Inc., Conseiller, LLC, RX-Xtracts, LLC, T-Holdings, LLC and Does 6 through 50 caused the explosion and fire by operating an unsafe, and potentially illegal, cannabis extraction, distillation, and conversion operation.  (Compl. ¶ 4.)  The property is owned by defendants the Senter/Prince Trust 2022 and a defendant Doe Trust.  (Id. ¶ 5.)  Defendants Randall Senter, Lynn Prince, and a Does 1 through 5 are administrators of the defendant trusts.  (Id. ¶¶ 5, 20.)  The property owners knew or should have known, about the extraction, distillation, and conversion operation on the subject property and the dangerous practices and innumerable safety violations being committed by defendants Canna Systems Corp., Canna Systems Holding, Inc., Conseiller, LLC, RX-Xtracts, LLC, T-Holdings, LLC and Does 6 through 50.  (Id. ¶ 28.) Yet, the property owners did nothing to discourage the operation or request that the unsafe practices be stopped or done with proper safety measures in place.  Plaintiff was present the day of the explosion performing handyman work on a loft within the premises.  (Id. ¶ 7.)  As a result of the defendants’ conduct, an explosion and fire occurred, and plaintiff Candelario sustained significant, life-changing, permanent injuries, including but not limited to severe second- and third-degree burns requiring grafts, a traumatic brain injury, permanent spine injuries including herniations/bulges in the cervical and lumbar spine requiring surgery, and permanent ear injuries resulting in tinnitus and hearing loss.

 

On May 20, 2026, plaintiff Candelario identified defendant Doe 1 as Plant Works Group, Inc.

 

On August 20, 2026, defendant Plant Works Group, Inc. (Plant Works) filed the instant demurrer to the Candelario Complaint’s First, Second, and Third Causes of Action as uncertain and for failing to state a claim.  Plaintiff Candelario (Plaintiff) opposed on September 8, 2026, and Plant Works replied on September 16, 2026.

 

 

MEET AND CONFER

 

Before filing a demurrer, the parties must meet and confer “in person, by telephone, or by video conference.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a).)  The moving party must file and serve a meet and confer declaration stating either: (1) the means by which the parties met and conferred, that the parties did not reach an agreement resolving the issues raised in the demurrer; or (2) that the party who filed the pleading subject to the demurrer failed to respond to the meet and confer request or failed to meet and confer in good faith.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 430.41, subd. (a)(3).)

 

Counsel met and conferred directly about the issues raised in defendant Plant Works’ demurrer, but the parties did not come to an agreement.  (Declaration of Robert A. Hufnagel ¶ 2.)

 

 

ANALYSIS

 

A.  Demurrer

 

A party may respond to a pleading against it by demurrer based on one or more of eight enumerated grounds, including that “the pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action” and is uncertain, meaning “ambiguous and unintelligible.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subds. (e), (f).)  The purpose of a demurrer is to challenge the sufficiency of a pleading “by raising questions of law.”  (Postley v. Harvey (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 280, 286.)  The grounds for demurring must be apparent from either the face of the complaint or a matter of which the court may take judicial notice.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.30, subd. (a); see also Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)  “In the construction of a pleading, for the purpose of determining its effect, its allegations must be liberally construed, with a view to substantial justice between the parties.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 452.)  The court “treat[s] the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law[.]”  (Berkley v. Dowds (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 518, 525.)  In applying these standards, the court liberally construes the complaint to determine whether a cause of action has been stated.  (Picton v. Anderson Union High School Dist. (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 726, 733.)

 

1.  Plaintiff’s First, Second, and Third causes of action fail to adequately and clearly allege a theory of liability against defendant Plant Works as defendant Doe 1.

 

Defendant Plant Works contends Plaintiff’s allegations are uncertain concerning Plant Works’ role in the underlying causes of action and allegations.  According Plant Works, Plaintiff fails to allege that Plant Works, as Doe 1, or Does 2 through 5 owned, operated, or managed the subject property, that Doe 1 played a role in the property, that Doe 1 owed a duty, or any negligent act demonstrating Doe 1’s involved in the activities that led to the fire or was engaged in any alleged ultra-hazardous activity.

 

To state a claim for negligence, the plaintiff must establish that (1) defendant owed plaintiff a legal duty of care, (2) defendant breached the legal duty, and (3) the breach proximately caused plaintiff’s damages or injuries.  (Romero v. Los Angeles Rams (2023) 91 Cal.App.5th 562, 567;  Miranda v. Bomel Construction Co., Inc. (2010) 187 Cal.App.4th 1326, 1335.)

 

The elements of premises liability mirror those for negligence.  (Nicoletti v. Kest (2023) 97 Cal.App.5th 140, 145.)  To establish premises liability, plaintiff must also allege that:  (1) defendant was the owner, occupier, or lessor of the premises; (2) defendant was negligent in the use, maintenance or management of the premises; and (3) the negligence caused the injury, damage, loss, or harm to plaintiff.  (Brooks v. Eugene Burger Management Corp. (1989) 215 Cal.App.3d 1611, 1619 [“Premises liability is a form of negligence[.]”]; see also Pultz v. Holgerson (1986) 184 Cal.App.3d 1110, 1116-1117 [stating complaint for premises liability based upon negligence is not subject to strict pleading requirements and may be generally pled];  Barnes v. Black (1999) 71 Cal.App.4th 1473, 1478 [“A landowner owes a duty to exercise reasonable care to maintain his or her property in such a manner as to avoid exposing others to an unreasonable risk of injury.”]; Nicoletti, supra, 97 Cal.App.5th at pp. 146-147 [landowners’ duty of care is not negated if it is foreseeable that someone might choose to encounter the condition due to necessity or other circumstances].)  A dangerous condition exists where the landowner was negligent in the use or maintenance of their property.  (See Hassaine v. Club Demonstration Services, Inc. (2022) 77 Cal.App.5th 843, 852 [“By inviting the public to its store, an owner or possessor has the duty ‘to exercise ordinary care and prudence to keep the aisles and passageways of the premises in and through which, by their location and arrangement, a customer in making purchases is induced to go, in a reasonably safe condition so as not unnecessarily to expose the customer to danger or accident.’”].)

 

An activity is ultrahazardous or abnormally dangerous for purposes of strict liability “if it (a) necessarily involves a risk of serious harm to the person, land, or chattels of others which cannot be eliminated by the exercise of the utmost care, and (b) is not a matter of common usage.”  (Smith v. Lockheed Propulsion Co. (1967) 247 Cal.App.2d 774, 785 [quoting Rest.2d Torts, § 520].)  Courts consider the six factors set forth in the Restatement Second of Torts section 520 to determine whether an activity is ultrahazardous:  (a) the existence of a high degree of risk of some harm, (b) the likelihood that resulting harm will be great, (c) the inability to eliminate the risk through reasonable care, (d) the extent to which the activity is not a matter of common usage, (e) the inappropriateness of the activity to the place where it is carried on, and (f) the extent to which its value to the community is outweighed by its dangerous attributes.  (Carmichael v. Cafe Sevilla of Riverside, Inc. (2025) 108 Cal.App.5th 292, 299 [Rest.2d Torts, § 520].)  Whether an activity is abnormally dangerous is determined by the court upon consideration of all the listed factors and the weight each merits based on the facts in evidence, and this determination generally cannot be made on demurrer.  (SKF Farms v. Superior Court (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 902, 906-907.)  Liability does not depend on the defendant’s knowledge of the danger and applies even where the utmost care is exercised to prevent harm.  (Garcia v. Est. of Norton (1986) 183 Cal.App.3d 413, 420.)

 

The finds the Complaint is uncertain as to Plant Work’s role and conduct and fails to allege sufficient facts to state Plaintiff’s three causes of action.

 

According to the Complaint, defendants Senter/Prince Trust 2002 and Doe Trust owned and controlled the subject property.  (Compl. ¶ 20.)  Plant Works is an administrator, executor, or trustee of the Senter/Prince Trust 2002 and the Doe Trust.  Plant Works leased, subleased, and provided access to the subject property to defendants, including Canna Systems Holding, Inc.  (Id. ¶ 21.)  Canna used the subject property for cannabis extraction, distillation, and conversion of an extremely hazardous risky operation which even when done properly and with all safety precautions in place was an extremely hazardous and risky operation.  (Id. ¶¶ 24-25.)  Because of the level of risk, the process is very heavily regulated.  (Id. ¶ 24.)  But Canna did not operate properly, with the necessary safety precautions in place, or follow the regulations, and followed few or none of the regulations and safety standards applicable to the process.  (Id. ¶¶ 24-27.)  Plaintiff claims Plant Works knew or should have known about the unsafe operations taking place at the subject property, including the violations of numerous codes, laws, and regulations and had the ability and power to cease the operations at the subject property, or require the process to be done safely, but refused to exercise that power or authority.  (Id. ¶ 28.)  As a result, the fire occurred at the subject property while Plaintiff was present on October 18, 2021, that caused a chain reaction of other fires and explosions that leveled the facility, killed two people, and badly injured Plaintiff.  (Id. ¶ 29.)

 

Because Plaintiff alleges Plant Works is a trustee of defendant trusts and appears to seek to hold Plant Works personally liable for the trusts’ conduct, Plaintiff must demonstrate the proper basis for personal liability or trustee liability through his allegations.

 

“A trustee is personally liable for obligations arising from ownership or control of trust property only if the trustee is personally at fault.”  (Prob. Code, § 18002.)  “A trustee is personally liable for torts committed in the course of administration of the trust only if the trustee is personally at fault.”  (Prob. Code, § 18002.)  A trustee acting in a representative capacity “cannot be held personally liable under section 18001 for any obligation arising from his ownership or control of trust property, nor can he be held personally liable under section 18002 for any torts committed in the course of his administration of the trust, unless the party seeking to impose such personal liability on the trustee demonstrates that the trustee intentionally or negligently acted or failed to act in a manner that establishes personal fault.”  (Haskett v. Villas at Desert Falls (2001) 90 Cal.App.4th 864, 877-878 [citing Prob. Code, §§ 18001 18002].)  Allegations that the trustee controlled the trust property is insufficient to establish personal liability.  (Id. at pp. 878-879.)

 

Plaintiff alleges direct personal liability against Plant Works but does not allege that Plant Works was working in its representative capacity.  Moreover, the Complaint’s allegations indicate the defendant trusts and Plant Works performed the same negligent conduct in creating a dangerous condition.  At most, Plaintiff’s allegations are conclusory and do not allege specific negligent or intentional conduct by Plant Works or its representatives, officers, or other party acting on Plant Works’ behalf.  Thus, Plaintiff fails to adequately allege personal liability or fault as to Plant Works on the negligence and premises liability claims.  The same issues apply to the strict liability cause of action because Plaintiff alleges the trusts’ conduct and Plant Works’ conduct are the same.  (See Compl. ¶¶ 51-54.)  However, Plaintiff does not allege any specific conduct by Plant Works or its representatives, officers, or other party acting on Plant Works’ behalf which created the ultrahazardous condition.

 

If Plaintiff wishes to hold Plant Works directly liable for conduct within its role as representative or administrator of the defendant trusts, Plaintiff must provide further factual allegations showing as much or amend the Complaint to allege the claims against Plant Works as a separate entity and in its representative capacity.

 

Therefore, the court sustains defendant Plant Works’ demurrer for uncertainty and failure to state a claim to the Complaint’s First, Second, and Third Causes of Action.

 

2.  Plaintiff’s newly discovered information is sufficient to allege liability against defendant Plant Works.

 

Plaintiff asks the court for leave to amend the Complaint to add new facts Plaintiff has learned during discovery concerning Plant Works’ relationship with now deceased David Bruch and defendant Canna Systems Holding, Inc.

 

Plaintiff claims he recently learned that Plant Works had a service contract with defendant Canna Systems Holding, Inc. to provide engineering services for the cannabis extraction operation at the property, including providing surface area pumps and design and process-engineering services.  (Declaration of Christopher S. Kim ¶ 5.)  Plaintiff also asserts Plant Works provided defendant Canna with an organic chemist, David Bruch, to oversee, manage, and control extraction operations at the subject property.  (Ibid.)  Based on this, Plaintiff believes Mr. Bruch may have been responsible for causing the explosion and lost his life.  (Ibid.)

 

Plaintiff presents a copy of the executed service agreement between Plant Works and Canna Systems Holdings, Inc. dated September 22, 2021, and executed by Chris Salinas and David Bruch on October 1, 2021.  (Kim Decl. ¶ 11, Exh. C [marked as Exhibit 1 to the deposition of Sarah Bruch].)  According to the agreement, Plant Works was engaged by Canna Systems in order to assist with “developing certain processes related to cannabis/hemp processing systems utilizing R134 as an extractant and synthesis conversions of CBD feedstock to THC-O acetate, hexahydrocannabinol (HHC) and other compounds (the ‘Services’).”  (Id., Exh. C at p. 1, ¶ C.)  Plant Works’ services included communicating with Canna’s operators about site preparation; Plant Works’ chemical engineer, Mr. David Bruch, would go to the location designated by Canna and process three hundred liters of CSG delta 8 into THC-O acetate; and Plant Works would train the employees Canna designated to process its isolates into delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinal.  (Id., Exh. C at pp. 1-2.)  The agreement does not specify the address of the subject property at issue in this matter.  However, Sarah Bruch testified at her deposition that at the time of the fire, Mr. Bruch was working as an employee for Plant Works.  (Id., Exh. F at p. 19:18-22.)  Ms. Bruch also testified that while Mr. Bruch worked at the subject property, he took direction from an individual identified as Savino, the lead chemical engineer at Plant Works, on how to correct problems with machinery.  (Id., Exh. F at pp. 50:11-18, 169:16-25.)

 

Plaintiff also presents three memos about Mr. Bruch which were authored in 2020 by the human resources department at ExtracTech in Nevada, showing disciplinary action against Mr. Bruch before the alleged incident.  (Kim Decl., Exh. E [February 20, 2020 and March 3, 2020].)  The memos reprimanded Mr. Bruch for smoking inside of a plant in a non-designated smoking area, sleeping on a shift which resulted in a pressure build up and explosion that required an evacuation, and using Extratech’s laboratory equipment for his personal use to make THC - 0 - Acetate (Delta 9 O-Acetate), a schedule 1 drug which is illegal.  (Id., Exh. E.)

 

According to Plaintiff, the new information and findings demonstrate:  “(1) There was an employer-employee relationship between [Plant Works] and David Bruch; (2) There was a valid service agreement between [Plant Works]  and Canna Systems Holding, Inc. that was in effect at the time of incident and directly related to cannabis extraction, distillation, and conversion facility at issue; (3) [Plant Works] supplied the equipment directly linked to the subject extraction operations; (4) Causation: David Bruch directed the correction of [Plant Works]  machinery issues shortly prior to the subject explosion and fire that caused his death; and (5) David Bruch, on behalf of [Plant Works], played a direct and leading role in the subject cannabis extraction process at the property.”  (Kim Decl. ¶ 6, Exh. F.)

 

Based on the newly discovered information and Plaintiff’s proposed allegations, Plaintiff can amend the Complaint to properly allege direct and vicarious tort liability against Plant Works based on David Bruch’s conduct, David Bruch’s employment with Plant Works, and Plant Works’ alleged agreement with Canna.  However, the court is unclear how this new theory of liability comports with Plaintiff’s current theory that Plant Works is liable as a trustee of the defendant trusts in leasing the subject property to Canna for performing an ultrahazardous activity.

 

If Plaintiff can clarify this issue and amend the Complaint to include liability against Plant Works based on its contract with Canna and Mr. Bruch’s own conduct as an employee of Plant Works, the court believes Plaintiff can cure the uncertainty and failure to state a claim defects defendant Plant Works has raised in its Demurrer.

 

Therefore, the court grants Plaintiff leave to amend the Complaint.

 

 

CONCLUSION and ORDER

 

Defendant Plant Works Group, Inc.’s demurrer for uncertainty and failure to state a claim is sustained with leave to amend as to the First, Second, and Third Causes of Action.

 

Plaintiff may file and serve an amended complaint within thirty (30) days of the issuing of this order.

 

Defendant Plant Works Group, Inc. to give notice.


Case Number: 23CHCV01019    Hearing Date: September 23, 2026    Dept: F43

Dept. F43

Hearing Date: 09-23-26

Case # 23CHCV01019, Venegas v. American Honda Motor Co.

Trial Date: N/A

 

MOTION FOR ATTORNEY FEES

 

MOVING PARTY:  Plaintiff Giovanna Oceguera Venegas

RESPONDING PARTY:  Defendant American Honda Motor Company

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Order awarding plaintiff $11,778 in attorney fees and a 1.25 enhancer of $1,944.63.

 

RULING:  Motion is granted in the reduced amount of $9,005 in attorney fees.  The court reserves ruling on Plaintiff’s costs and expenses.

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

 

On April 10, 2023, plaintiff Giovanna Oceguera Venegas (Plaintiff) filed this lemon law action against defendant American Honda Motor Company (Defendant), concerning issues in a 2020 Honda Accord.  Plaintiff asserted three violations of the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act, including breaches of express and implied warranties.  Defendant filed an answer on May 26, 2023.  Since this matter was commenced, the parties litigated whether this matter is subject to arbitration, and Plaintiff filed two motions to enforce a settlement agreement.

 

On May 30, 2024, Plaintiff accepted Defendant’s section 998 Offer to Compromise in the amount of $46,000 plus attorney fees, costs, and expenses pursuant to a noticed motion.  Plaintiff filed the notice of settlement on June 6, 2024.

 

On July 20, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion seeking $13,723.13 in attorney fees and a lodestar enhancement.  Plaintiff also filed a memorandum of costs, seeking $2,110.92.  On September 10, 2026, Defendant filed an opposition, and Plaintiff replied on September 16, 2026.

 

 

ANALYSIS

 

A.  Awarding Attorney Fees

 

A prevailing party is entitled to recover its attorneys’ fees when authorized by contract, statute, or law.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (a)(10); Civ. Code, § 1717, subd. (a).)  “A successful party means a prevailing party, and [a party] may be considered prevailing parties for attorney’s fees purposes if they succeed on any significant issue in litigation which achieves some of the benefit the parties sought in bringing suit.”  (Bowman v. City of Berkeley (2005) 131 Cal.App.4th 173, 178.)  If a buyer prevails in a Song-Beverly action, then the buyer is allowed to recover attorney fees.  (Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).)

 

Because Plaintiff is a “buyer” and this matter settled in Plaintiff’s favor, Plaintiff is the prevailing party pursuant to Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d).

 

1.  Hourly Rates

 

Plaintiff requests hourly rates of $350 to $500 for counsel of record Quill & Arrow, LLP and $210 for paralegal work.  (Declaration of Kevin Y. Jacobson ¶¶ 46-65, Exh. 42.) To support the rates, Plaintiff presents an attorney declaration concerning counsel and paralegal qualifications and experience, several rulings approving similar rates, the Laffey Matrix, and Thomson Reuters’ Law Firm Rates Report for 2026.

 

In opposition, Defendant asserts the supporting declaration is hearsay and relies on improper unpublished, nonbinding opinions of other courts.  (Opp. at pp. 7:19-8:28.)  Defendant also claims Plaintiff’s supporting declaration fails to provide sufficient evidence to support the hourly rates and billed hours for Vivian Lee and Shawn Louis.  Furthermore, the declaration includes templated assertions, cites three rulings that approve attorney Ardi’s rate, and cites other rulings that bill rates for attorneys not named in this motion.

 

In responses, addresses the missing information concerning Vivian Lee (Quill & Arrow’s former case director) and Shawn Louis (former law clerk at Quill & Arrow).  (Reply Declaration of Kevin Y. Jacobson ¶ 11.)  Plaintiff’s reply also includes hours by attorney Derek Chipman at $495 per hour.  (Id. ¶¶ 14, 15(b).)

 

“The reasonable hourly rate is that prevailing in the community for similar work.”  (PLCM Group v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095.)  The moving party bears the initial burden of establishing the prevailing market “through its own affidavits.”  (MBNA Am. Bank, N.A. v. Gorman (2006) 147 Cal.App.4th Supp. 1, 13.)  Once the moving party presents competent evidence of prevailing rates, the burden shifts to the opposing party.  The opposing party may present rulings which concern hourly rates for comparable work from the relevant legal market.  (Bronshteyn v. Dept. of Consumer Affairs (2025) 114 Cal.App.5th 537, 543, 546-547;  Baer v. Tedder (2025) 115 Cal.App.5th 1139, 1160-1162.)  The court must engage in an objective analysis “to determine the prevailing rate in the community for comparable professional legal services[.]”  (Graciano v. Robinson Ford Sales, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 140, 156, citation omitted.)  The court may “consider the attorney’s skill as reflected in the quality of the work, as well as the attorney’s reputation and status.”  (MBNA Am. Bank, N.A., supra, 147 Cal.App.4th Supp. at p. 13.)

 

After reviewing the parties’ declarations, court rulings, and arbitration rulings, the court finds the hourly rates are reasonable with the exception of Vivian Lee and Shawn Louis because counsel includes no information about their experience or particular qualifications.  The Reply Declaration merely states their titles, when they worked at the firm, and states the assigned rates are appropriate because they are below that listed in the Laffey Matrix.

2.  Billing Entries

 

Plaintiff requests fees for 28.50 hours of attorney and paralegal work, including 11.3 hours for the instant motion.

 

The Song-Beverly Consumer Act only entitles Plaintiffs to attorney fees for “actual time expended.”  (Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).)  This means an analysis as to whether the fees and costs sought are reasonable under the circumstances is required.  (Levy v. Toyota Motor Sales, USA, Inc. (1992) 4 Cal.App.4th 807, 816.)  “A prevailing buyer has the burden of ‘showing that the fees incurred were ‘allowable,’ were reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation and were ‘reasonable in amount.’”  (Hanna v. Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC (2019) 36 Cal.App.5th 493, 507, citations omitted.)  A verified fee bill is prima facie evidence that the services listed were necessarily incurred.  (Hadley v. Krepel (1985) 167 Cal.App.3d 677, 682.)  If a fee request is opposed, “[g]eneral arguments that fees claimed are excessive, duplicative, or unrelated do not suffice.” (Premier Med. Mgmt. Sys. v. Cal. Ins. Guarantee Ass’n (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 550, 564.)  Once an objection to the fees has been raised, then plaintiff’s counsel bears the burden of showing that the fees were reasonably necessary, and the court has the discretion to reduce fees awarded.  (En Palm, LLC v Teitler Family Trust (2008) 162 Cal.App.4th 770, 775.)

 

In determining the reasonableness of fees, courts look to the factors from Church of Scientology v. Wollersheim (1996) 42 Cal.App.4th 628, disapproved on other grounds by Equilon Enters. v. Consumer Cause, Inc. (2002) 29 Cal.4th 53, 68 fn.5.  The factors from Wollersheim are (1) the amount of money involved in the litigation; (2) the nature of the litigation and its difficulty and the intricacies and importance of the litigation; (3) the skill required and employed in handling the litigation, the necessity for skilled legal training and ability in trying the case, and counsel’s education and experience in the particular type of work involved; (4) the attention given to the case; (5) the success of the attorneys efforts; and (6) the time consumed by the litigation.  (Id.)

 

Defendant asks the court to reduce billing entries due to counsel’s use of template documents, excessive billing of time, and billing for clerical and administrative tasks such as filing and serving documents.  Plaintiff’s reply addresses several of these issues and provides actual hours incurred to review and reply to the opposition.

 

After reviewing Defendant’s arguments, the parties’ evidence and supporting declarations, and Defendant’s billing entry reduction chart, the court reduces the attorney hours by 3.1 hours and the paralegal and staffing hours by 3.2 hours.  Several entries involve clerical tasks and reviewing, drafting, or editing documents which appear excessive, and Plaintiff failed to provide sufficient support for awarding Vivian Lee and Shawn Louis hours.  The court notes Defendant’s repeated assertions concerning templated documents.  But Defendant has failed to produce evidence (such as template documents filed by Plaintiff’s counsel in other cases) to support its assertions.

3.  Lodestar Enhancement

 

After determining an appropriate lodestar figure, the court may determine whether that figure should be adjusted with a positive or negative multiplier.  (Doppes v. Bentley Motors, Inc. (2009) 174 Cal.App.4th 967, 997.)  Whether a multiplier or negative multiple is appropriate depends on several factors, including (1) the risks presented by the litigation;  (2) the novelty and difficulty of the legal and factual issues involved;  (3) the results obtained on behalf of the plaintiff;  and (4) the skill exhibited by counsel.  (Consumer Privacy Cases (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 545, 556.)  “The ‘results obtained’ factor can properly be used to enhance a lodestar calculation where [1] an exceptional effort produced [2] an exceptional benefit.”  (Graham v. DaimlerChrysler Corp. (2005) 34 Cal.4th 553, 582.)

 

With the revised attorney fee amount, the 1.25x enhancement is $2,251.25.

 

Plaintiff contends the court should apply the 1.25x multiplier because counsel obtained an excellent outcome (a $46,000 settlement plus attorney fees and costs) and took this matter at substantial risk (contingency, paying for all expenses up front, and without any guarantee of success).  (Mot. at pp. 12:1-15:8.)  In opposition, Defendant asks the court to deny the lodestar multiplier and apply a negative multiplier.

 

The court finds Plaintiff’s arguments do not support applying a multiplier because this is a simple Song-Beverly matter that settled in 13 months and the matter presented no complex or novel legal issues.  Moreover, this matter was filed before Civil Code section 871.26, subdivision (e) went into effect.

 

Therefore, the court denies Plaintiff’s request to apply a multiplier and Defendant’s request to apply a negative multiplier.

 

 

            B.  Costs and Expenses

 

For the first time in the Reply, Plaintiff asks the court to include costs of $2,110.92 in the fee award.  (Reply at p. 6:8-13; 7/20/2026 Memorandum of Costs.)

 

“A prevailing party who claims costs must serve and file a memorandum of costs within 15 days after the date of service of the notice of entry of judgment or dismissal by the clerk[.]  The memorandum of costs must be verified by a statement of the party, attorney, or agent that to the best of his or her knowledge the items of cost are correct and were necessarily incurred in the case.”  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1700(a)(1).)  Under the law, the court presumes a verified memorandum of costs is correct.  However, a party may contest the costs that a prevailing party seeks.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1034 subd. (a).)  The challenging party has the burden of demonstrating that those costs are unreasonable or unnecessary.  (Adams v. Ford Motor Co. (2011) 199 Cal.App.4th 1475, 1486.)

 

The court cannot determine if the Memorandum of Costs was timely filed and served.  Although Plaintiff billed hours for preparing and finalizing the notice of the court’s conditional dismissal on January 21, 2026, neither the court docket nor Plaintiff’s moving papers indicate notice of the conditional dismissal was ever served on Defendant.  (1/21/2026 Minute Order.)  The court also notes that Plaintiff’s proposed order does not address the costs.  Furthermore, the court has discretion to disregard issues which are first raised in a reply because the opposing party did not have an opportunity to address the issue.  (See Jay v. Mahaffey (2013) 218 Cal.App.4th 1522, 1537-1538.)  Although the time to challenge the memorandum of costs by a motion tax has expired (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1700) and Defendant has filed no such motion, the court reserves ruling on Plaintiff’s costs to allow oral argument concerning the timeliness of the memorandum of costs.  (See Tidrick v. FCA US, LLC (2025) 112 Cal.App.5th 1147, 1155-1159.)

 

At the hearing on this motion, Plaintiff should be prepared to demonstrate that she served notice of the court’s January 21, 2026, dismissal on Defendant and that the memorandum of costs was timely filed and served, and to explain why the request for costs and expenses is not included in the notice of motion, memorandum of points and authorities, or the proposed order.

Motion for attorney fees is granted in the reduced amount of $9,005.

 

The court reserves ruling on whether Plaintiff is entitled to her costs and expenses.
