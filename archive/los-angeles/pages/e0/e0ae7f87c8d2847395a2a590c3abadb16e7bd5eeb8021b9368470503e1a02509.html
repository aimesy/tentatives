Motion for Approval of PAGA Settlement

The court considered the moving papers filed in connection with this motion.  No opposition papers were filed.

DISCUSSION

Plaintiff Aikeila Jacobs (“Plaintiff”) moves the court for an order approving the Labor Code Private Attorneys General Act of 2004 (Labor Code, §¿2698, et seq.) (“PAGA”) settlement agreement between Plaintiff and Defendant Ventra Health, Inc. (“Defendant").

The parties have reached a gross settlement of $100,000.  (Messrelian Decl., Ex. 1, Settlement Agreement, ¶ 1.11.)

Labor Code section 2699, subdivision (s)(2) provides that “[t]he superior court shall review and approve any settlement of any civil action pursuant to” PAGA.¿ The court’s review of PAGA settlements “ensur[es] that any negotiated resolution is fair to those affected.”¿ (Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 549.)¿ In an effort to aid the court in the determination of the fairness of the settlement, Wershba v. Apple Computer, Inc. (2001) 91 Cal.App.4th 224, 244-245 (disapproved on other grounds), discusses factors that the court should consider when determining the reasonableness of a settlement agreement.¿ “[A] presumption of fairness exists where: (1) the settlement is reached through arm’s-length bargaining; (2) investigation and discovery are sufficient to allow counsel and the court to act intelligently; (3) counsel is experienced in similar litigation; and (4) the percentage of objectors is small.”¿ (Id. at p. 245.)¿ “[T]he test is not the maximum amount plaintiffs might have obtained at trial on the complaint, but rather whether the settlement is reasonable under all of the circumstances.”¿ (Id. at p. 250.)¿¿¿¿¿¿

            The court has reviewed (1) the terms of the Settlement Agreement, including (i) the gross settlement amount of $100,000, attorney’s fees in the amount of 1/3 of the gross settlement amount ($33,333.33), legal costs in the amount of $14,791.69, and administrator costs in the amount of $3,490.00, (ii) the net settlement amount of $48,384.98, of which 75 percent (or $36,288.74) will be paid to the Labor and Workforce Development Agency, and of which the remaining 25 percent (or $12,096.24) will be paid to the PAGA Members, and (iii) the release of claims by the aggrieved employees and (2) the declaration of Plaintiff’s counsel, in which counsel states that (i) the parties exchanged informal discovery prior to mediation, (ii) the parties participated in a full-day, arm’s-length mediation, (iii) Defendant’s maximum exposure for meal period violations would be $108,800, and Plaintiff’s meal period claims contain risks because it would be difficult for Plaintiff’ to show the exact reasons why a meal period appears to be short or late or not taken and Defendant maintained legally compliant meal period policies, such that Plaintiff’s counsel believes that the $100,000 settlement is a substantial, fair, and reasonable recovery, (iv) Defendant’s realistic exposure for rest period violations would be $144,840 and Plaintiff’s rest break violations are difficult to prove because they are not tracked on paper and Defendant maintained legally compliant rest period policies for a maximum exposure of $253,640, and (v) there are substantial issues with proving Plaintiff’s other claims.  (Messrelian Decl., ¶¶ 9, 14, 15, 16, 23; Ex. 1, ¶¶ 1.11, 1.27, 4.2.)

            The court also considered Plaintiffs’ counsel’s declaration to determine the reasonableness of the request for attorney’s fees in the amount of $33,333.33 and finds that the award of attorney’s fees in that amount is reasonable under the percentage approach.  (Messrelian Decl., ¶¶ 25-26.)  (Laffitte v. Robert Half Internat. Inc. (2016) 1 Cal.5th 480, 503-504 [“The choice of a fee calculation method is generally one within the discretion of the trial court, the goal under either the percentage or lodestar approach being the award of a reasonable fee to compensate counsel for their efforts”].)  The court further finds that the requests for legal costs of $14,791.69, with the excess of $15,000 allocated to the Net PAGA Settlement Amount, and administrator estimated expenses of $3,490.00, with the excess allocated to the Net PAGA Settlement Amount, are reasonable.  (Messrelian Decl., ¶¶ 23, 27; Ex. 1 ¶¶ 4.2.1, 4.2.2; Ex. 2 [Apex’s bid for $3,490.00.)

Based on the arguments and evidence set forth in Plaintiff’s motion, the court finds that the settlement agreement is fair, adequate, and reasonable.¿ (Wershba, supra, 91 Cal.App.th at pp. 244-245.)  The court therefore grants Plaintiffs’ motion.

ORDER

            The court grants plaintiff Aikeila Jacobs’s motion for approval of PAGA settlement.

            The court will sign and file the [Proposed] Order on Unopposed Motion for Approval of PAGA Settlement which was lodged with the court on April 1, 2026.

            The court orders plaintiff Aikeila Jacobs to lodge with a court and serve a proposed judgment that is consistent with the terms of the PAGA Settlement Agreement. 

The court sets an Order to Show Cause re: Entry of Judgment for hearing on November 3, 2023 at 8:30 a.m.

            The court orders that the Final Status Conference, scheduled for April 28, 2026 at 8:30 a.m. in Department 513, is vacated.

            The court orders that trial, scheduled for September 9, 2026 at 11:00 a.m. in Department 513, is vacated.  

 

 

            The court orders plaintiff Aikeila Jacobs to give notice of this ruling.

IT IS SO ORDERED.

[tentative] Order RE:

 

PLAINTIFF AND CROSS-DEFENDANT’S DEMURRER TO CROSS-COMPLAINT

 

 

MOVING PARTY:                 Cross-Defendant Hong Liang, M.D. 

 

RESPONDING PARTY:       Cross-Complainant Bin Zhang

Demurrer to Cross-Complaint

The court considered the moving, opposition, and reply papers filed in connection with this demurrer.

DISCUSSION

Cross-defendant Hong Liang, M.D. (“Cross-Defendant”) demurs to each cause of action in the Cross-Complaint on the grounds that each cause of action fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action and each cause of action is uncertain, ambiguous, and unintelligible.

The court overrules Defendant’s demurrer to each cause of action in the Cross-Complaint on the ground of uncertainty because it is not ambiguous or unintelligible and therefore is not uncertain.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (f).)

 

The court sustains Cross-Defendant’s demurrer to the first cause of action for promissory estoppel because it does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action since Cross-Complainant failed to allege that the damages claimed are measured by the extent of the obligation assumed and not performed, that is, Cross-Complainant failed to allege what damages he is seeking to recover based on the promissory estoppel claim.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (e); Toscano v. Greene Music (2004) 124 Cal.App.4th 685, 692 [“The elements of promissory estoppel are (1) a clear promise, (2) reliance, (3) substantial detriment, and (4) damages measured by the extent of the obligation assumed and not performed”] [internal quotations omitted].)

The court sustains Cross-Defendant’s demurrer to the second cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty because it does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action since Cross-Complainant fails to allege there exists a fiduciary duty between the parties.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (e); O'Neal v. Stanislaus Cnty. Employees' Ret. Assn. (2017) 8 Cal. App. 5th 1184, 1215 [“The elements of a claim for breach of fiduciary duty are (1) the existence of a fiduciary relationship, (2) its breach, and (3) damage proximately caused by that breach”].)  The Cross-Complaint does not allege facts that show that a relationship between the parties where Cross-Defendant was duty bound to act with the utmost good faith for the benefit of Cross-Complainant was established because the Cross-Complaint merely alleges that by virtue of Cross-Defendant’s offer, he assumed a fiduciary duty to Cross-Complainant to treat the money given to him as an investment by Cross-Complainant.  (Cross-Compl., ¶ 44.)  (People ex rel. Harris v. Rizzo (2013) 214 Cal.App.4th 921, 950 [“A fiduciary relationship has been defined as any relation existing between parties to a transaction wherein one of the parties is ... duty bound to act with the utmost good faith for the benefit of the other party. Such a relation ordinarily arises where a confidence is reposed by one person in the integrity of another, and in such a relation the party in whom the confidence is reposed, if he voluntarily accepts or assumes to accept the confidence, can take no advantage from his acts relating to the interest of the other party without the latter's knowledge or consent]”] [internal quotations omitted].)  These allegations are not sufficient to create a fiduciary relationship.

The court overrules the demurrer to the third cause of action for breach of oral agreement because Cross-Complainant alleges that (1) (i) Cross-Defendant offered to invest money on behalf of Cross-Complainant and Cross-Defendant would treat all funds provided to him by Cross-Complainant as if they had been invested  by Cross-Complainant for purposes of determining the amounts to be owed by Cross-Defendant to Cross-Complainant, (ii) Cross-Complainant accepted Cross-Defendant’s offer by giving him $700,000, and (iii) subsequently, Cross-Complainant orally agreed to accept fewer shares in Apollo Medical Holdings., Inc., a publicly traded company, that Cross-Complainant believed he was entitled to in exchange for Cross-Defendant’s oral promise to immediately transfer those shares to him (Cross-Compl., ¶¶ 6, 7, 30, 48, 49), (2) Cross-Complainant performed by giving Cross-Defendant the $700,000 (Cross-Compl., ¶ 49), (3) Cross-Defendant breached the agreement by withholding shares that Cross-Defendant agreed to treat as belonging to Cross-Complainant (Cross-Compl, ¶ 51), and (4) Cross-Complainant has been harmed as a result of Cross-Defendant’s breach.  (Cross-Compl., ¶ 51.)  (Wall Street Network, Ltd. v. N. Y. Times Co. (2008) 164 Cal.App.4th 1171, 1178 [“The elements of a breach of contract cause of action are: (1) existence of contract; (2) plaintiffs’ performance or excuse for nonperformance; (3) defendants’ breach (or anticipatory breach); and (4) resulting damage”]; Scolinos v. Kolts (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 635, 640 [“Terms of an oral contract may be pleaded generally as to its effect, because it is rarely possible to allege the exact words”].)

The court overrules Cross-Defendant’s demurrer to the fourth cause of action for unjust enrichment because Cross-Complainant has alleged that he gave Cross-Defendant $700,000 for the express purpose of allowing Cross-Defendant to invest in a medical corporation with which Cross-Defendant was affiliated, Cross-Defendant promised to personally repay those funds to Cross-Complainant in amount equal to its value as an investment to Cross-Defendant, Cross-Defendant has breached that agreement and unjustly retained the benefits thereby damaging Cross-Defendant.  (Cross-Compl., ¶¶ 7, 10, 11, 16, 17, 54-56.)  (Lyles v. Sangadeo-Patel (2014) 225 Cal.App.4th 759, 769 [elements of unjust enrichment].)

The court overrules Cross-Defendant’s demurrer to the fifth cause of action for conversion because Cross-Complainant has alleged that (1) Cross-Defendant entrusted $700,000.00 to Cross-Defendant and Cross-Defendant invested the $700,000.00, (2) Cross-Defendant kept the $700,000 without Cross-Complainant’s consent, and (4) Cross-Complainant has been harmed by Cross-Defendant’s unlawful retention of his money.  (Fremont Indemnity Co. v. Fremont General Corp. (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 97, 119 [the elements of conversion]; Voris v. Lampert (2019) 7 Cal.5th 1141, 1151 [“[A]lthough our law has dispensed with the old requirement that ‘each coin or bill be earmarked,’ it remains the case that ‘money cannot be the subject of an action for conversion unless a specific sum capable of identification is involved’”] [“‘[A] cause of action for conversion of money can be stated only where a defendant interferes with the plaintiff's possessory interest in a specific, identifiable sum’; ‘the simple failure to pay money owed does not constitute conversion’”].)

The court sustains Defendants’ demurrer to the sixth cause of action for constructive trust because it does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action since a request a constructive trust is a remedy and not a cause of action.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (e); PCO, Inc. v. Christensen, Miller, Fink, Jacobs, Glaser, Weil & Shapiro, LLP (2007) 150 Cal.App.4th 384, 398 [“A constructive trust, however, is an equitable remedy, not a substantive claim for relief”] [emphasis in original].)

The court sustains the demurrer to the seventh cause of action for accounting because the facts alleged show that the money owed by Cross-Defendant to Cross-Complainant is a sum that can be made certain by calculation.  (Cross-Compl., ¶¶ 30, 70.)  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (e); St. James Church of Christ Holiness v. Superior Court (1955) 135 Cal.App.2d 352, 359 [holding that an action for accounting is not available where the plaintiff alleges the right to recover a sum certain or a sum that can be made certain by calculation]; Teselle v. McLoughlin (2009) 173 Cal.App.4th 156, 179, citing Brea v. McGlashan (1934) 3 Cal.App.2d 454, 460 [A cause of action for an accounting requires a showing that a relationship exists between the plaintiff and defendant that requires an accounting, and that some balance is due the plaintiff that can only be ascertained by an accounting].)

The court overrules the demurrer to the eighth cause of action for declaratory relief because Cross-Complainant has alleged an actual controversy regarding the breach of oral agreement claim, the unjust enrichment claim, and the conversion claim.  (Nede Mgmt. Inc. v. Aspen American Ins. Co. (2021) 68 Cal.App.5th 1121, 1132 [holding that when an actual controversy is alleged, a demurrer as to declaratory relief should be overruled].)  

The burden is on the cross-complainant “to articulate how it could amend its pleading to render it sufficient.”¿ (Palm Springs Villas II Homeowners Assn., Inc. v. Parth¿(2016) 248 Cal.App.4th 268, 290.)¿ To satisfy that burden, a cross-complainant “must show in what manner he can amend his complaint and how that amendment will change the legal effect of his pleading.”¿ (Goodman v. Kennedy¿(1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349.)¿ The court finds that Cross-Complainant has articulated the facts that he could allege to render his cause of action for promissory estoppel against Cross-Defendant sufficient, and thus, grants leave to amend as to the first cause of action.  The court finds that Cross-Complainant has not articulated the facts that he could allege to render his causes of action for breach of fiduciary duty, constructive trust, and accounting against Cross-Defendant sufficient and therefore does not grant leave to amend as to the second, sixth, and seventh causes of action.

 

ORDER

            The court sustains cross-defendant Hong Liang, M.D’s demurrer to cross-complainant Bin Zhang’s first cause of action with leave to amend.

The court sustains cross-defendant Hong Liang, M.D’s demurrer to cross-complainant Bin Zhang’s second, sixth, and seventh causes of action without leave to amend.

The court overrules cross-defendant Hong Liang, M.D’s demurrer to cross-complainant Bin Zhang’s third, fourth, fifth, and eighth causes of action.

            The court grants cross-complainant Bin Zhang 20 days leave to amend the first cause of action to cure the defects set forth in this order.

 

 

            The court orders cross-defendant Hong Liang, M.D to give notice of this ruling.

IT IS SO ORDERED.

MOVING PARTY:                Defendants Mehrdad Alemozaffar, M.D., Kaiser Foundation Health Plan, Inc., Kaiser Foundation Hospitals, and Southern California Permanente Medical Group

 

RESPONDING PARTY:       Plaintiffs Anthony Blades and Tammie Oliphant

Defendants’ Demurrer to Plaintiffs’ First Amended Complaint

The court considered the moving, opposing, and reply papers.

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiffs Anthony Blades (“Blades”) and Tammie Oliphant (“Oliphant”) (collectively, “Plaintiffs”) filed this action on June 27, 2025, against defendants Dr. Mehrdad Alemozaffar, (Dr. Alemozaffar), Kaiser Permanente International, Kaiser Foundation Health Plan, Inc., and Kaiser Foundation Hospitals (hereinafter, “Kaiser”) (collectively, “Defendants”).  The operative First Amended Complaint (FAC) was filed on February 13, 2026, and includes an additional defendant, Southern California Permanente Medical Group (SCPMG).  The FAC alleges causes of action for (1) professional negligence and (2) loss of consortium.

In the FAC, Plaintiffs allege the following. Dr. Alemozaffar is a urology practitioner in the State of California.  (FAC, ¶ 4.)  In early spring 2023, Defendants diagnosed Blades with prostate cancer.  (FAC, ¶ 14.)  Blades and Defendants decided Blades should undergo a prostatectomy, a procedure to remove Blades’ prostate.  (FAC, ¶ 14.)  On or around April 11, 2023, Alemozaffar performed the prostatectomy.  (FAC, ¶ 15.)  During the procedure, Defendants breached the wall of Blades’ rectum, leading to a vesico-rectal fistula formation.  (FAC, ¶ 16.)  After surgery, Defendants placed “a surgical drainage system in Blades to remove and measure the excess fluids in the affected areas.”  (FAC, ¶ 16.)  On April 14, 2024, Defendants recklessly removed the surgical drainage system, without recognizing that the fluid was consistent with urine.  (FAC, ¶ 17.)

After the removal, Blades experienced severe abdominal pain.  (FAC, ¶ 18.)  Oliphant took Blades to the emergency room around eight hours after removal of the surgical system.  (FAC, ¶ 18.)  Blades was admitted to Kaiser’s medical center.  (FAC, ¶ 18.)  Blades’ pain and condition worsened, and Blades “developed a urinoma requiring placement of nephrostomy tubes, massive pulmonary embolism requiring placement of an inferior vena cava filter, atrial fibrillation requiring anticoagulation, pelvic hematoma, and vesico-rectal fistula formation requiring a colostomy.”  (FAC, ¶ 19.)  Blades remained hospitalized until June 4, 2023.  (FAC, ¶ 20.)  This caused Blades to suffer emotional distress, believing he may die from the life-threatening medical conditions.  (FAC, ¶ 20.)  Blades was unable to see outside medical care and only saw Kaiser doctors and medical staff.  (FAC, ¶ 21.)

Defendants never informed Blades that he suffered a pulmonary embolism, despite family history.  (FAC, ¶ 22.)  Defendants never mentioned Defendants’ removal of Blades’ prostate as a cause of his condition.  (FAC, ¶ 23.)  Blades believed his injuries were a consequence of cancer, rather than a negligent cause, relying on Defendants’ skill and judgment.  (FAC, ¶ 24.)  Defendants cared for Blades until over a year after his hospitalization.  (FAC, ¶ 24.)  Defendants placed many tubes and bags in Blades’ body.  (FAC, ¶ 25.)

Once released from Defendants’ care, Blades was unable to walk.  (FAC, ¶ 25.)  Blades used a wheelchair and assistance from others.  (FAC, ¶ 25.)  Blades could not control his urination or defecation.  (FAC, ¶ 25.)  He was still weak and feeble. (FAC, ¶ 25.)

Blade’s condition remained the same until September 2023.  (FAC, ¶ 26.)  He remained in bed unless he visited Defendants’ medical staff.  (FAC, ¶ 26.)  Defendants hoped Blades’ rectum wall would heal itself, without further corrective measures.  (FAC, ¶ 26.)  Despite frequent visits to Blade’s home by Alemozaffar and an at-home-nurse, Blades visited the emergency room on at least two more occasions.  (FAC, ¶ 26.)

In September 2023, Defendants referred Blades to the USC Institute of Urology (USC) for further treatment.  (FAC, ¶ 27.)  Defendants remained Blades’ insurance carrier and medical providers.  (FAC, ¶ 27.)  Further corrective surgeries were performed by USC, but Defendants required that all tests performed on Blades be performed by Defendants and all work USC wanted to perform on Blades be approved by Defendants.  (FAC, ¶ 27.)  USC expressed disagreement with Defendants’ decision to wait so long to see if Blades’ rectal wall would heal on its own, but USC did not put Plaintiffs on notice that Defendants caused any actual breach of the rectal wall.  (FAC, ¶ 28.)

In September 2023, April 2024, and November 2024, Blades underwent three further corrective surgeries.  (FAC, ¶ 30.)  During that time, Blades was barely ambulatory, suffered from emotional distress and cognitive impairment, and was unable to control his bodily functions.  (FAC, ¶ 30.)  Blades underwent 60 total, twice-weekly hyperbaric chamber sessions at UCLA.  (FAC, ¶ 31.)  Blades was almost entirely dependent on Oliphant as his primary caretaker.  (FAC, ¶ 31.)  Blades used a walker until weeks after treatments ended, and his feet suffered nerve damage, a lingering symptom of pulmonary embolism.  (FAC, ¶ 31.)  Throughout this time, Plaintiffs still believed the cancerous prostate caused Blades’ medical condition.  (FAC, ¶ 33.)

On or around September 15, 2024, Blades ran into an old colleague.  (FAC, ¶ 36.)  Blades’ colleague broached the idea that Defendants negligently performed Blades’ initial surgery and post-surgical care, prompting Blades to seek legal counsel.  (FAC, ¶ 36.)  It was not until this conversation with Blades’ colleague that Blades became aware of Defendants’ potential medical negligence.  (FAC, ¶ 36.)  Thereafter, while investigating, Plaintiffs learned, on information and belief, that Alemozaffar made comments to third parties indicating he was aware of Defendants’ medical negligence.  (FAC, ¶ 37.)  The culmination of Blades’s injuries, hospitalizations, rehabilitation efforts, and physical and mental impairments led to the delayed discovery in September 2024.  (FAC, ¶ 39.)

Blades will have lifelong physical and mental impairments that will prevent Blades from working.  (FAC, ¶ 38.)  Oliphant, Blades’ spouse, suffered loss of consortium.  (FAC, ¶ 40.)

Defendants now demur to the first and second causes of action in the FAC on the grounds that each fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (e).  Defendants also demur to the entire complaint, alleging that it is barred by the one-year statute of limitations under Code of Civil Procedure section 340.5. Plaintiffs oppose the demurrer.

LEGAL STANDARD

A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable.  (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)  “To survive a demurrer, the complaint need only allege facts sufficient to state a cause of action; each evidentiary fact that might eventually form part of the plaintiff's proof need not be alleged.”  (C.A. v. William S. Hart Union High School Dist. (2012) 53 Cal.4th 861, 872.)  For the purpose of testing the sufficiency of the cause of action, the demurrer admits the truth of all material facts properly pleaded.  (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 966-967.)  A demurrer “does not admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.”  (Daar v. Yellow Cab Co. (1967) 67 Cal.2d 695, 713.)

A pleading is uncertain if it is ambiguous or unintelligible.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10(f).)  A demurrer for uncertainty may lie if the failure to label the parties and claims renders the complaint so confusing defendant cannot tell what he or she is supposed to respond to.  (Williams v. Beechnut Nutrition Corp. (1986) 185 Cal.App.3d 135, 139, fn. 2.)  However, “[a] demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.”  (Khoury v. Maly's of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.)

DISCUSSION

            The court overrules the demurrer based on statute of limitations.  Plaintiffs’ complaint is not clearly and affirmatively barred by the statute of limitations.  (Roman v. County of Los Angeles (2000) 85 Cal.App.4th 316, 324-325 [“If the dates establishing the running of the statute of limitations do not clearly appear in the complaint, there is no ground for general demurrer”]; CrossTalk Productions, Inc. v. Jacobson (1998) 65 Cal.App.4th 631, 635 [“demurrer based on an affirmative defense cannot properly be sustained where the action might be barred by the defense, but is not necessarily barred”].) 

            Delayed discovery sufficiently pled

            “In an action for injury or death against a health care provider based upon such person's alleged professional negligence, the time for the commencement of action shall be three years after the date of injury or one year after the plaintiff discovers, or through the use of reasonable diligence should have discovered, the injury, whichever occurs first.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 340.5.)

            As used in section 340.5, “injury” means a person's physical condition and its negligent cause. (Gutierrez v. Mofid (1985) 39 Cal.3d 892, 896.) The injury is not necessarily the ultimate harm suffered but instead occurs when appreciable harm is first manifested. (Brewer v. Remington (2020) 46 Cal.App.5th 14, 24.)  Whether there has been a belated discovery of the cause of action in malpractice cases essentially is a question of fact, and only where reasonable minds can draw but one conclusion from the evidence does the question become a matter of law.  (Drexler v. Petersen (2016) 4 Cal.App.5th 1181, 1188-1189.)

            Section 340.5’s discovery rule “sets forth two alternate tests for triggering the limitations period: (1) a subjective test requiring actual suspicion by the plaintiff that the injury was caused by wrongdoing; and (2) an objective test requiring a showing that a reasonable person would have suspected the injury was caused by wrongdoing.  The first to occur under these two tests begins the limitations period.” (Kitzig v. Nordquist (2000) 81 Cal.App.4th 1384, 1391.) Under the first test, the plaintiff need only suspect someone has done something wrong, which does not require the plaintiff to know the specific facts (e.g., medical cause, diagnosis) necessary to establish a medical negligence claim. (Norgart v. Upjohn Co. (1999) 21 Cal.4th 383, 397–398; Knowles v. Superior Court (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 1290, 1295.)

“A patient is charged with presumptive knowledge of his negligent injury, and the statute commences to run, once he has notice or information of circumstances to put a reasonable person on inquiry, or has the opportunity to obtain knowledge from sources open to his investigation.  It is irrelevant that the plaintiff is ignorant of his legal remedy or the legal theories underlying his cause of action.”  (Carillo v. County of Santa Clara (2023) 89 Cal.App.5th 227, 234-235.) 

For purposes of the 1-year statute, “the accrual date is delayed until plaintiff is aware of her injury and its negligent cause.”  (Jolly v. Eli Lilly & Co. (1988) 44 Cal.3d 1103, 1109.)  The date plaintiff “should have discovered” the injury is when plaintiff suspects or a reasonable person would have suspected that plaintiff's “injury” was caused by wrongdoing.  (Id. at pp. 1110–1111.)  “So long as a suspicion exists, it is clear that the plaintiff must go find the facts; she cannot wait for the facts to find her.”  (Ibid.)

            “[T]he plaintiff discovers the cause of action when he at least suspects a factual basis, as opposed to a legal theory, for its elements, even if he lacks knowledge thereof--when, simply put, he at least suspects that someone has done something wrong to him, ‘wrong' being used, not in any technical sense, but rather in accordance with its ‘lay understanding.’”  (Norgart, supra, 21 Cal.4th 383, 397-398.)  “A plaintiff need not be aware of the specific ‘facts' necessary to establish the claim; that is a process contemplated by pretrial discovery. Once the plaintiff has a suspicion of wrongdoing, and therefore an incentive to sue, she must decide whether to file suit or sit on her rights. So long as a suspicion exists, it is clear that the plaintiff must go find the facts; she cannot wait for the facts to find her.”  (Dolan v. Borelli (1993) 13 Cal.App.4th 816, 822.)

Blades alleges he suffered injuries as a result of a prostatectomy performed by Defendants on April 11, 2023.  (FAC, ¶15.)  Blades alleges his rectal wall was breached leading to a vesico-rectal fistula formation.  (Id. at ¶16.)  Due to Defendants’ negligence on that date, Plaintiff was hospitalized for two months until June 4, 2023 and bedridden after release until September 2023.  (FAC, ¶¶20-26.)  Blades was referred in September 2023 to the USC Institute of Urology and underwent three corrective surgeries following Defendants’ referral in September 2023, April 2024 and November 2024.  (Id. at ¶30.)  Plaintiffs allege that the physicians at USC disagreed with Defendants’ decision to “wait and see” if Blades’s rectal wall would heal on its own.  (Id. at ¶28.) 

Plaintiffs alleges Blades was extremely ill after the prostatectomy and describes his condition as “barely ambulatory,” suffering from urinary and fecal incontinence and subject to twice-weekly hyperbaric chamber sessions at UCLA.  (Id. at ¶¶30-32.)  Blades felt constantly ill even after the April 2024 corrective surgery.  (Id. at ¶34.) 

Plaintiffs believed the injury and the corrective surgeries were due to Blades’s cancerous prostate, not any negligence by the Defendants.  (Id. at ¶33.)  Plaintiffs alleges Blades did not contemplate that the original injury (breach of the rectal wall) and all subsequent remedial measures were due to Defendants’ negligence until September 15, 2024, when a former colleague mentioned the possibility after seeing his condition.  (Id. at ¶36.)  Plaintiffs allege Defendants never mentioned that removal of his prostate was a potential cause of his afflictions, nor did they discuss how the injury occurred.  (Id. at ¶23.) 

            Based on Plaintiffs’ allegations, reasonable minds could differ as to whether the events after April 11, 2023 should have placed Plaintiffs on inquiry notice.  A plaintiff is on inquiry notice “when the plaintiff suspects or should suspect that her injury was caused by wrongdoing, that someone has done something wrong to her.”  (Jolly, supra, 44 Cal.3d at p. 1110.)  For purposes of the one-year period, discovery of the injury means the plaintiff has discovered “both his or her injury and its negligent cause.”  (Gutierrez, supra, 39 Cal.3d at p. 896.)

The court must accept as true Plaintiffs’ allegations of delayed discovery, including their allegation that they believed the injury was due to Blades’s cancer, not Defendants’ negligence.  (Fox, supra, 35 Cal.4th at p. 811 [“we must assume to be true Fox’s allegations that she ‘did not discover, nor suspect, nor was there any means through which her reasonable diligence would have revealed, or through which she would have suspected…’” defendant’s product caused her injury until deposition of surgeon]; see generally, 5 Witkin, Cal. Proc. (6th ed. 2026), §725 [“Intent, like knowledge, is a fact.”]). 

In order for the action to be clearly and affirmatively time-barred on demurrer, the court would have to find that reasonable minds could only conclude based on the allegations that Plaintiffs should have known or suspected that Defendants’ negligence caused Blades’s injury.  While it would be unreasonable for Plaintiffs to claim that they did not know that the prostatectomy had “gone wrong,” it was not unreasonable as a matter of law for Plaintiffs to believe that it had “gone wrong” because of Blades’s cancer, as opposed to Defendants’ wrongdoing.  The court cannot make such a finding based simply on the fact that corrective surgeries were required and plaintiff Blades suffered serious and debilitation injuries after the prostatectomy.  Defendants must identify factual allegations indicating that reasonable persons could only conclude that the injury (breach of the rectal wall) and the corrective surgeries were due to Defendants’ negligence, e.g. a statement by other doctors that such an injury was the result of negligence or wrongdoing or facts supporting res ipsa loquitur. 

Defendants fail to identify any factual allegations from which the court can find as a matter of law that it was objectively unreasonable for Plaintiffs to believe that plaintiff Blades’s injuries were due to his cancer.  Defendants ask, “[f]undamentally, defendants question what was being ‘corrected’ if there was no negligence?”  (Opposition, 4:21-23.)  Defendants’ question assumes that surgeries can only go wrong and require corrective surgeries if there was negligence.  The court cannot make that assumption, nor are there any facts alleged from which the court can conclude on demurrer that the type of injury suffered by Blades does not occur absent negligence. 

As stated in Carillo, a “patient is charged with presumptive knowledge of his negligent injury…once he has notice or information of circumstances to put a reasonable person on inquiry, or has the opportunity to obtain knowledge from sources open to his investigation.”  (Carillo, supra, 89 Cal.App.5th at 234-235.)  Plaintiffs’ allegations do not establish as a matter of law that a reasonable person would have been placed on inquiry notice, as a result of the prostatectomy, the physical injury experienced during recovery or the corrective surgeries.  There are also no factual allegations establishing that Plaintiffs had the opportunity to obtain knowledge that the rectal wall breach was due to Defendants’ wrongdoing by examining sources open to Plaintiffs’ investigation.  In fact, Plaintiffs allege that Defendants controlled Blades’s care at all times, never indicated the injury was the result of any wrongdoing, provided him with reassurances and concealed their medical negligence.  (FAC, ¶¶21-24, 29.)  Plaintiffs are not claiming ignorance of available legal remedies or the legal theories underlying their causes of action.  Plaintiffs are claiming ignorance of the fact that plaintiff Blades’s injuries were the result of wrongdoing, as opposed to being a consequence of his cancer.

Carillo is also factually distinguishable.  The plaintiff in Carillo alleged he was forced to undergo an amputation of his right foot after a nurse popped a blister on that foot and left the wound open and exposed.  (Carillo, supra, 89 Cal.App.5th at p. 230.)  Plaintiff allegedly objected to the nurse popping the blister.  (Ibid.)  The chain of events alleged in Carillo left no room for doubt that “a reasonable person suffering Plaintiff’s injury would have suspected by the end of December 2017 that medical care provided by the nurse before the infection developed had something to do with the injury and that reasonable person would have been on inquiry notice by the end of December 2017.”  (Id. at p. 232.) 

Blades’s injury occurred during surgery.  There are no allegations that Plaintiffs were contemporaneously aware of the specific injury-causing conduct or that they objected to it as it was occurring.  Unlike Carillo, Plaintiffs do not allege facts establishing a clear chain of causation from a clearly identified wrongful act to which they objected and plaintiff Blades’s injury. 

The court also previously stated that “while Blades’s injuries and impairments may show the inability to have made earlier discovery, Plaintiffs did not ‘specifically plead facts to show…the time and manner of discovery’ of the injuries that are the subject of this action.”  (January 23, 2026 Minute Order.)  “Generally speaking, a cause of action accrues at the time when the cause of action is complete with all of its elements.  The ‘discovery rule’ is an exception to this general rule and postpones accrual of a cause of action until the plaintiff discovers, or has reason to discover, the cause of action.  To rely on the discovery rule for delayed accrual of a cause of action, a plaintiff whose complaint shows on its face that his claim would be barred without the benefit of the discovery rule must specifically plead facts to show (1) the time and manner of discovery….”  (Carillo, supra, 89 Cal.App.5th at p. 234.) 

The court already acknowledged in its prior ruling that Blades’s prior allegations of injuries and impairments were sufficient to allege his inability to discover Defendants’ wrongdoing earlier.  Blades’s amendments further establish that inability.  Plaintiffs also allege Blades only discovered his cause of action after a conversation with an old colleague who suggested Blades’s condition was due to negligence in performing the surgery and administration of post-surgical care.  (FAC, ¶36.)  Blades sufficiently alleges the circumstances of his discovery of his cause of action.

In overruling the demurrer, the court applies several fundamental principles.  The case law consistently states that resolution of a statute of limitations issue is “normally,” “generally” and “typically” a “question of fact.”  (Fox, supra, 35 Cal.4th at p. 810; Rosas v. BASF Corp. (2015) 236 Cal.App.4th at 1394; Alexander, et al. v. Exxon Mobil, et al. (2013) 219 Cal.App.4th 1236, 1252.)  It can only be resolved as a question of law if the undisputed facts would “support only one reasonable conclusion” and the facts “do not leave any room for reasonable differences of opinion.”  (Alexander, supra, 219 Cal.App.4th at p. 1252.)  Resolution of the statute of limitations, including whether a plaintiff knew or should have known of defendant’s wrongdoing, is therefore presumptively a question of fact that should not be determined on demurrer and those occasions where it can be determined as a question of law on demurrer are the exception.  (See e.g., Jolly, supra, 44 Cal.3d at p. 1109 [undisputed evidence on summary judgment showed that plaintiff suspected that her injuries were due to her mother’s ingestion of diethylstilbestrol as early as 1972 and limitations period began to run as of that date]; Norgart, supra, 21 Cal.4th at p. 407 [summary judgment properly granted on statute of limitations grounds where undisputed evidence established that plaintiffs’ daughter committed suicide by ingesting Halcion and they were informed of that fact soon after her death; plaintiffs had reason to discover their cause of action against drug manufacturer at that time].)  Plaintiffs’ complaint does not fall within that exception. 

The court is also obligated to construe the allegations of the complaint liberally.  (Sproul v. Vallee (2025) 116 Cal.App.5th 285, 293.)  The court must “construe all factual allegations in the complaint in [plaintiff’s] favor.”  (Lee v. Hanley (2015) 61 Cal.4th 1225, 1229, 1230.)  Thus, in Lee, the Supreme Court found that demurrer was erroneously sustained where “on at least one reasonable construction of the complaint, at least one of [plaintiff]’s claims is not time-barred.”  (Id.)  The court finds the same to be true here.  Under at least one reasonable construction of Plaintiffs’ allegations, the complaint is not time-barred based on delayed discovery.   

Tolling under Code of Civil Procedure section 340.5

“In no event shall the time for commencement of legal action exceed three years unless tolled for any of the following: … (2) intentional concealment….”  (Code Civ. Proc. § 340.5.)  “Intentional concealment” requires specific allegations of affirmative acts beyond the exercise of poor judgment or mere omissions.  (Nichols v. Alghannam (2026) 118 Cal.App.5th 726, 740.)  Plaintiffs’ allegations of intentional concealment are as follows:  (1) plaintiff Blades was unable to see outside medical providers and relied only on Kaiser Defendants’ hospital staff and doctors; (2) Defendants controlled all information as to plaintiff Blades’s condition; (3) Defendants never informed him that he was suffering from a pulmonary embolism; (4) Defendants never mentioned the removal of his prostate as a potential cause of his afflictions; (5) Defendants never spoke about how the injury occurred, only on how to fix it; (6) the circumstances allowed Defendants to conceal their medical negligence; and (7) Plaintiffs relied on Defendants’ reassurances. 

Taking those allegations as true at the demurrer stage, Plaintiffs fail to allege intentional concealment.  Plaintiffs allege concealment but there are no allegations that the concealment was intentional, nor do Plaintiffs allege affirmative acts that would qualify as intentional concealment, e.g., refraining from discussing the cause of plaintiff Blades’s injury to conceal Defendants’ negligence or failing to make a note in plaintiff Blades’s chart regarding the cause of his injury in order to conceal Defendants’ negligence.  Plaintiffs only state that the conditions alleged at paragraphs 21-24 and 29 allowed for Defendants to “conceal,” not “intentionally conceal,” the cause of his injury.  (Nichols, supra, 118 Cal.App.5th at p. 740 (allegation that treating physician allowed medical provider responsible for management of plaintiff’s pain to examine her to “conceal” his negligence failed to allege intentional concealment under Code of Civil Procedure section 340.5); Donabedian v. Manzer (1986) 187 Cal.App.3d 1021, 1027 (allegation that defendants “concealed” their negligence was conclusory and did not allege that defendants “intentionally concealed” defendants’ negligence).)  There are no allegations that Defendants engaged in any affirmative acts with the intention to conceal Defendants’ negligence or the cause of plaintiff Blades’s injury.

Equitable tolling

“The Court of Appeal correctly described one scenario under which equitable tolling may apply: if a plaintiff pursues one of several available legal remedies, causing it to miss the statute of limitations for other remedies it later wishes to pursue. Yet such facts are far from the only circumstances under which the doctrine may apply. To determine whether equitable tolling may extend a statute of limitations, courts must analyze whether a plaintiff has established the doctrine's three elements: timely notice to the defendant, lack of prejudice to the defendant, and reasonable and good faith conduct by the plaintiff.”  (Saint Francis Memorial Hospital v. State Dept. of Public Health (2020) 9 Cal.5th 710, 725–726; Metabyte, Inc. v. Technicolor S.A. (2023) 94 Cal.App.5th 265, 278 [if requirements of equitable tolling “could be satisfied only by pursuing a legal remedy to lessen damages in a different forum, then a legal remedy to lessen damages would de facto be a requirement of equitable tolling.  It is not.”])  Thus, pursuit of one of several available legal remedies is not a required element of equitable tolling; it is only “one scenario under which equitable tolling may apply.”  (Id.) 

Equitable tolling is therefore not inapplicable as a matter of law merely because Plaintiffs were not pursuing one of multiple legal remedies during the requested period of tolling.  Plaintiffs fail, however, to identify any allegations in support of the notice element.  In opposition, Plaintiffs identify the Notice of Intent to Sue mailed in March 2025.  (Amadi Dec., Ex. G.)  The Notice of Intent to Sue is evidence extrinsic to the FAC and may not be considered on demurrer. 

As for lack of prejudice to Defendants, Plaintiffs argue Defendants suffer no relevant prejudice because application of equitable tolling will not prevent Defendants from defending any claim on the merits.  Plaintiffs argue Defendants have already produced much of Blades’s medical records.  (Amadi Dec., ¶5.)  Plaintiffs argue the latest corrective surgery occurred months before service of the Notice of Intent.  These are all facts outside of the FAC and cannot be considered on demurrer.  These facts also do not support a finding that Defendants will be able to defend their claims on the merits.

Plaintiffs’ FAC sufficiently alleges their reasonable and good faith conduct in pursuing their claims.  “Equitable tolling applies only in carefully considered situations to prevent the unjust technical forfeiture of causes of action.  It does not, as courts have explained, extend to garden variety claims of excusable neglect.”  (Saint Francis, supra, 9 Cal.5th at 729–730.)  Equitable tolling only applies where the plaintiff’s conduct was both objectively reasonable and subjectively in good faith.  (Id.)  As discussed above, Plaintiffs did not timely file a complaint against Defendants because plaintiff Blades’s health was in dire straits after the prostatectomy and Plaintiffs allege sufficient circumstances to plead delayed discovery. 

Plaintiffs fail to allege facts in support of the notice or lack of prejudice elements of the  equitable tolling doctrine.  Plaintiffs therefore cannot rely on equitable tolling to avoid demurrer based on statute of limitations.

Equitable Estoppel

“In the statute of limitations context, equitable estoppel may be appropriate where the defendant's act or omission actually and reasonably induced the plaintiff to refrain from filing a timely suit.  The requisite act or omission must involve a misrepresentation or nondisclosure of a material fact bearing on the necessity of bringing a timely suit.”  (DOE v. Marten (2020) 49 Cal.App.5th 1022, 1028-1029.)  “[E]ven a defendant who is ignorant or mistaken as to the real facts may be equitably estopped if the defendant was in such a position that he or she ought to have known the true facts.  This means that an estoppel may be created where the defendant harbored no intent to mislead and did not engage in actual fraud or bad faith.  Rather, it is enough that the defendant's conduct ‘induced’ the plaintiff to delay commencement of an action.”  (Id.)

Plaintiffs argue the FAC alleges Defendants’ knowledge of their own medical malpractice, citing to paragraphs 16, 20-21.  Plaintiffs allege that Defendants breached Plaintiff’s rectal wall at the time of the prostatectomy.  (FAC, ¶16.)  Plaintiffs do not clearly and explicitly allege that Defendants knew that they breached Plaintiff’s rectal wall and knew that their doing so was the result of negligence.  If Plaintiffs were to allege that Defendants knew their negligence caused the breach in plaintiff Blades’s rectal wall and intentionally concealed that fact from Plaintiffs, grounds to apply equitable estoppel would sufficiently alleged.  As pled, Defendants are not equitably estopped from asserting the statute of limitations as a defense. 

ORDER

For the reasons set forth above, the court overrules Defendants’ demurrer to the first and second cause of action alleged in Plaintiffs’ First Amended Complaint. 

The court orders Defendants to file an answer to the First Amended Complaint within 20 days of the date of this order.

The court orders Plaintiffs to give notice of this ruling.

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Ex Parte Application 7/17/26

On July 16, 2026, plaintiffs filed an Ex Parte Application for Temporary Restraining Order to Prevent Demolition of Tenant Garage and Storage Spaces and for Order to Show Cause re Preliminary Injunction (“Ex Parte Application”), which was set on the court’s July 17, 2026 calendar.   

The court denies plaintiffs’ Ex Parte Application in its entirety.

As to plaintiffs’ ex parte application for a temporary restraining order and order to show cause re preliminary injunction, “[a] superior court must evaluate two interrelated factors when ruling on a request for a preliminary injunction:  (1) the likelihood that the plaintiff will prevail on the merits at trial and (2) the interim harm that the plaintiff would be likely to sustain if the injunction were denied as compared to the harm the defendant would be likely to suffer if the preliminary injunction were issued.”  (Smith v. Adventist Health System/West (2010) 182 Cal.App.4th 729, 749.)  Here, the court finds that plaintiffs have not met their burden to show by competent evidence that (1) plaintiffs are likely to prevail at trial on the merits of their claim that the conduct they seek to enjoin violates the Los Angeles Municipal Code (LAMC) Tenant Anti-Harassment Ordinance (LAMC sections 45.33 and 45.35) under their first cause of action alleged in the complaint, or (2) the interim harm that the moving plaintiffs would be likely to sustain if the restraining order/injunction were denied would be greater than the harm the defendants would be likely to suffer if the restraining order/injunction were issued.  The court therefore denies plaintiffs’ ex parte application for a temporary restraining order. 

The court also finds that plaintiffs have not met their burden of making a showing by competent evidence to support their ex parte application to issue an order to show cause re preliminary injunction.  The court therefore denies that request and declines to issue an order to show cause re preliminary injunction.

DEMURRER

Defendants Mark Diamond and Cheryl Diamond (“Defendants”) demur to the Complaint of plaintiffs Ana Heilala Vete and Lesly Hulse (“Plaintiffs”) on the ground that the Complaint alleges violations of overtime requirements that are inapplicable to the Plaintiffs, who, by facts alleged in the complaint, qualify as personal attendants within the definition of Labor Code section 1451, subdivision (d).

The court overrules the demurrer to the first, second, fourth, fifth, and sixth causes of action because Defendants have not shown that the Complaint alleges that Plaintiffs were personal attendants within the definition of Labor Code section 1451 subdivision (d) because Plaintiffs allege that Plaintiffs were hired “to work as Care givers for their daughter” and “that plaintiffs will provide care giver services to the defendants’ daughter in exchange of compensation from the defendants to the plaintiffs.”  (Compl., ¶¶ 18, 20.)  (Lab. Code, § 1451, subd. (d) [“‘Personal attendant’ means any person employed by a private householder or by any third-party employer recognized in the health care industry to work in a private household, to supervise, feed, or dress a child, or a person who by reason of advanced age, physical disability, or mental deficiency needs supervision. The status of personal attendant shall apply when no significant amount of work other than the foregoing is required. For purposes of this subdivision, ‘no significant amount of work’ means work other than the foregoing did not exceed 20 percent of the total weekly hours worked”].)  The Complaint does not allege facts that show that Plaintiffs’ role as care givers was to supervise, feed, or dress Defendants’ daughter, or that Defendants’ daughter was a child or a person who by reason of advanced age, physical disability, or mental deficiency needs supervision.  Thus, Defendants have failed to show that Plaintiffs, as personal attendants under the Domestic Worker Bill of Rights, have different overtime rules that apply to them.  (Lab. Code, § 1454; 8 Cal. Code Regs. § 11150, subds. 1(B), 2(J).)

 

MOTION TO STRIKE

            Defendants move to strike portions of the Complaint, paragraphs 47, 48, 68, and 69, and Prayer for Relief paragraphs 3 and 7, on the ground that the Complaint improperly seeks the imposition of statutory penalties which are barred by the one-year statute of limitations set forth in Code of Civil Procedure section 340, subdivision (a).

            Defendants argue that this statute of limitations applies to Plaintiffs’ request for civil penalties pursuant to Labor Code section 558, subdivision (a) in relation to the first cause of action for failure to pay wages pursuant to Labor Code sections 201 and 1194 and civil penalties pursuant to Labor Code sections 226 and 1174[1] in relation to the fourth cause of action for failure to provide wage and hour statements pursuant to Labor Code section 226, et seq.  Defendants also argue that the Complaint improperly seeks imposition of statutory penalties under Labor Code section 1198.5 and 432.

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 340, subdivision (a), the statute of limitations for an action upon a statute for a penalty or forfeiture is one year.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 340, subd. (a).) 

The court grants Defendants’ motion to strike paragraphs 47, 68, and 69, and Prayer for Relief paragraph 3 because the statutory penalties requested therein are barred by the one-year statute of limitations since (1) Labor Code section 558, subdivision (a) provides for civil penalties against any employer who violates any provision regulating hours and days of work in any order of the Industrial Welfare Commission which is the basis for penalties for the first cause of action, (2) Labor Code sections 226 and 226.3 are the basis for the penalties sought pursuant to the fourth cause of action and wage statement claims are subject to a one-year limitations period, and (3) Plaintiffs’ employment ended in November 2024 and the action was filed on December 8, 2025.  (Compl., ¶ 18.)  (Lab. Code, §§ 558, subd. (a), 226, 226.3; Falk v. Children's Hospital Los Angeles (2015) 237 Cal.App.4th 1454, 1469 [“Claims subject to a one-year limitations period, i.e., the wage statement claim, are therefore time-barred”].)

The court denies Defendants’ motion to strike paragraph 48 because Defendants have not shown that Plaintiffs’ allegation that they are entitled to civil penalties based upon Defendants’ underpayment of minimum and overtime wages is time barred since the Complaint does not allege that this is based on any statute that provides for penalties that fall under Code of Civil Procedure section 340 subdivision (a).  (Compl., ¶ 48.)

Regarding the request for penalties pursuant to Labor Code section 1198.5 subdivision (k), the Complaint does not allege that Defendants failed to permit Plaintiffs within the time specified by subdivision (b), 30 calendar days from the date Defendants received a written request, or as agreed to in writing by the parties, to inspect or copy personnel records.  (Lab Code, § 1198.5, subds. (b)(1), (k).)  Thus, the Complaint does not allege when the statute of limitations ran because the Complaint does not allege when the violation occurred.  Accordingly, the court denies the motion to strike the statutory penalties pursuant to Labor Code § 1198.5 found in paragraph 7 of the Prayer for Relief on the ground that the request is time barred by the one-year statute of limitations, but the court strikes the request on the ground that Plaintiffs failed to allege a violation of Labor Code section 1198.5 beyond a conclusory statement.  (Compl, ¶ 80.)

Labor Code section 432 provides “If an employee or applicant signs any instrument relating to the obtaining or holding of employment, he shall be given a copy of the instrument upon request.”  (Lab. Code, § 432.)  Because section 432 does not provide for any statutory penalties, the court grants the motion to strike the request for statutory penalties pursuant to Labor Code section 432 found in paragraph 7 of the Prayer for Relief.

            The burden is on the plaintiff “to articulate how it could amend its pleading to render it sufficient.”¿ (Palm Springs Villas II Homeowners Assn., Inc. v. Parth¿(2016) 248 Cal.App.4th 268, 290.)¿ To satisfy that burden, a plaintiff “must show in what manner he can amend his complaint and how that amendment will change the legal effect of his pleading.”¿ (Goodman v. Kennedy¿(1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349.)¿ The court finds that Plaintiffs have not articulated the facts that they could allege to render their request for penalties against Defendants sufficient and therefore grants the motion to strike without leave to amend
