24SMCV00068 (Huttar v. Uber Technologies, Inc.) Related Case: 26SMCP00144

 

RELEVANT BACKGROUND

 

            On January 5, 2024, Shau Huttar (Plaintiff) filed his complaint against Uber Technologies Inc. (Uber) alleging one cause of action for libel per se.

 

            On May 14, 2024, the Court granted Uber’s motion to compel arbitration.

 

            On March 5, 2026, the appointed arbitrator, Hon. Elizabeth R. Feffer (Ret.) of ADR, issued a final arbitration award against Plaintiff and in favor of Uber. (Ex. 3.)

                       

INSTANT MOTION

 

            On April 24, 2026, Uber filed the instant petition to confirm arbitration award. Plaintiff filed an opposition in the related cases, 26SMCP00144. Uber filed a reply.

 

            On March 23, 2026, Plaintiff filed a motion to vacate the award in the related case. Uber filed an opposition, and Plaintiff filed a reply.

 

            On August 20, 2026, the Court continued the hearing on the instant motion to September 17, 2026.

 

DISCUSSION

 

            After an arbitration award has been made, any party may petition the court to confirm, correct, or vacate the award. (Code Civ. Proc. § 1285.) A petition to confirm arbitration is required to include: (a) the substance of or attach a copy of the agreement to arbitrate; (b) the names of the arbitrators; and (c) a copy of the arbitration award and written opinion of the arbitrator. (Id., § 1285.4.) A response to the petition can request that the court dismiss, vacate, correct, or confirm the award. (Id., § 1285.2) If a petition to confirm is duly served and filed; and, unless a petition to correct or vacate the award has been timely filed, “the court shall confirm the award as made, whether rendered in this state or another state.” (Id. at § 1286.)

 

            “A petition to confirm an award shall be served and filed not later than four years after the date of service of a signed copy of the award on the petitioner. A petition to vacate an award or to correct an award shall be served and filed not later than 100 days after the date of the service of a signed copy of the award on the petitioner.” (Id., subd. 1288.) “No petition may be served and filed under this chapter until at least 10 days after service of the signed copy of the award upon the petitioner.” (Id., § 1288.4.)

 

            “A proceeding under this title in the courts of this State is commenced by filing a petition. Any person named as a respondent in a petition may file a response thereto. The allegations of a petition are deemed to be admitted by a respondent duly served therewith unless a response is duly served and filed. The allegations of a response are deemed controverted or avoided.” (Id., § 1290.)

 

            “Once a petition to confirm an award is filed, the superior court must select one of only four courses of action: it may confirm the award, correct and confirm it, vacate it, or dismiss the petition.” (EHM Productions, Inc. v. Starline Tours of Hollywood, Inc. (2018) 21 Cal.App.5th 1058, 1063.) “It is well settled that the scope of judicial review of arbitration awards is extremely narrow.” (California Faculty Assn. v. Superior Court (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 935, 943.) “Neither the trial court, nor the appellate court, may ‘review the merits of the dispute, the sufficiency of the evidence, or the arbitrator’s reasoning, nor may we correct or review an award because of an arbitrator’s legal or factual error, even if it appears on the award’s face.’” (Ibid.) The limited grounds on which the Court may vacate an arbitration award are outlined in Code Civ. Proc. § 1286.2, subdivision (a):

 

“Subject to Section 1286.4, the court shall vacate the award if the court determines any of the following:¿¿

¿¿

(1) The award was procured by corruption, fraud or other undue means.¿¿

 

(2) There was corruption in any of the arbitrators.¿¿

 

(3) The rights of the party were substantially prejudiced by misconduct of a neutral arbitrator.¿¿

 

(4) The arbitrators exceeded their powers and the award cannot be corrected without affecting the merits of the decision upon the controversy submitted.¿¿

 

(5) The rights of the parties were substantially prejudiced by the refusal of the arbitrators to postpone the hearing upon sufficient cause being shown therefor or by the refusal of the arbitrators to hear evidence material to the controversy or by other conduct of the arbitrators contrary to the provisions of this title.¿¿

 

(6) An arbitrator making the award either: (A) failed to disclose within the time required for disclosure a ground for disqualification of which the arbitrator was then aware; or (B) was subject to disqualification upon grounds specified in Section 1281.91 but failed upon receipt of timely demand to disqualify himself or herself as required by that provision.

 

(Code Civ. Proc. § 1286.2 (a)(1)-(6).)

 

            The Court finds that Uber has complied with the relevant procedural prerequisites to confirm the award regarding the filing requirements, service of the petition, notice of the hearing, service of the award, and timeliness of the petition. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1283.6, 1285.4, 1288, 1288.4, 1290; See Petition, Ex. 1-3.)

 

            The Court is not persuaded by Plaintiff’s arguments in opposition and in the motion to vacate the award. Plaintiff argues that the arbitrator exceeded her authority, failed to resolve material issues, or refused to apply the governing law. But none of these arguments support Plaintiff’s position that the arbitration award should be vacated. As Uber correctly points out in reply, there are very limited reasons under the Code of Civil Procedure that permit vacating an arbitration award. (See Code Civ. Pro. § 1286.2.) None of the permitted grounds to vacate are present here. Additionally, “the merits of a controversy that has been submitted to arbitration are not subject to judicial review. This means that we may not review the validity of the arbitrator's reasoning, the sufficiency of the evidence supporting the award, or any errors of fact or law that may be included in the award.” (Harris v. Sandro (2002) 96 Cal.App.4th 1310, 1313.)

 

            Plaintiff argues that the arbitrator applied FEHA instead of Proposition 22 or the Unruh Act. (Opp. at p. 4.). But Plaintiff asserts mere legal error, which is not a valid basis to vacate an arbitration award. Next, Plaintiff’s contention that the arbitrator exceeded her powers by failing to resolve a material issue whether Uber followed its own procedures is not correct, as the arbitrator expressly evaluated Uber’s actions and determined that Uber deactivated Plaintiff’s driver account pursuant to its internal policies at the time after receiving a fourth report of suspected impaired driving (See Award at p. 6). But, even if that were not the case, there still would not be grounds to vacate. (See Code Civ. Pro. § 1286.2.) Similarly, Plaintiff’s contention that the arbitrator failed to consider evidence about whether Plaintiff was disabled and whether Uber was aware of such alleged disability is not correct (see Award at p. 8), and this Court may not re-assess liability or otherwise review the validity of the arbitrator’s reasoning.

 

            Accordingly, Uber’s petition to confirm the arbitration award is GRANTED.

            Plaintiff’s petition to vacate the arbitration award is DENIED.


Case Number: 25SMCV06813    Hearing Date: September 17, 2026    Dept: O

TENTATIVE RULING

September 17, 2026

25SMCV06813 (HMM Construction Inc. v. Arya Group, Inc., et. al.)

                       

RELEVANT BACKGROUND

 

            On December 31, 2025, plaintiff HMM Construction Inc. (Plaintiff or HMM) filed its complaint against defendants Arya Group, Inc. (Arya) and Ardeshir Tavangarian (Tavangarian) (collectively, Defendants), alleging six causes of action: 1. Breach of Contract; 2. Breach Of Contract; 3. Open Book Account; 4. Accounts Stated; 5. Quantum Meruit; and 6. Conversion.

 

            Bellagio Project

 

            Plaintiff alleges that it entered into an agreement with Defendants in which Plaintiff agreed to furnish labor, equipment, and materials for the framing of a main house and a guest house located at 10675 Bellagio Road, Bel Air, CA 90077 (the Bellagio Project). (Compl. ¶ 14.) Defendants allegedly promised to pay Plaintiff a total of $950,000 for the framing scope of work, subject to adjustments for changes in work. (Id.) Work began on the Bellagio Project on or about February 10, 2021. (Id.) Defendants allegedly promised to pay HMM an additional $692,151.34 in Time and Material Costs for the Bellagio Project, bringing the total promised on the Original Contract to $1,642,151.34. (Id. ¶ 15.) Plaintiff alleges that it performed the Bellagio Contract work and demanded payment, but there remains due, owing, and unpaid from Defendants to Plaintiff the sum of $262,920.26, plus interest, and attorney's fees and costs, according to law. (Id. ¶ 18.)

 

            Promissory Note

 

            Plaintiff alleges that it entered into a written agreement via a Promissory Note wherein Arya committed to pay Plaintiff $50,000, with interest at the annual rate of 10%, in full upon the sale of the home/property associated with the Bellagio Project or 12 months after completion of the Framing Scope of work on the Project. (Id. ¶ 16.) The Framing Scope of Work was completed on or about March 8, 2023. (Id.)

 

            Beinvenida Project

 

            Further, Plaintiff alleges that it entered into an agreement (the Beinvenida Project), written or oral, with Defendants in which Plaintiff agreed to furnish labor, equipment, and materials for the framing of a first, second, and third story addition to a single family residence located at 1233 Beinvenida, Bel Air, CA 90077 (the "Beinvenida Project"). (Id. ¶ 21.) Defendants promised to pay Plaintiff a total of $250,000 for the framing scope of work, subject to adjustments for changes in work. Work began on the Beinvenida Project on or about July 6, 2021, and the Scope of work was completed on or about November 22, 2022. (Id.) Plaintiff alleges that it performed the Beinvenida Contract work and demanded payment, but there remains due, owing, and unpaid from Defendants to HMM the sum of $17,500, plus interest, and attorney's fees and costs, according to law. (Id. ¶ 23.)

 

INSTANT MOTION

             

            On April 13, 2026, Defendants filed the instant motion to compel arbitration. Plaintiff field an opposition, and Defendants filed a reply.

 

DISCUSSION

 

 A “written agreement to submit to arbitration an existing controversy or a controversy thereafter arising is valid, enforceable and irrevocable, save upon such grounds as exist for the revocation of any contract.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.)

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.2 authorizes the court to order arbitration of a controversy if it finds the parties have agreed to arbitrate that dispute. Section 1281.2 provides, “On petition of a party to an arbitration agreement alleging the existence of a written agreement to arbitrate a controversy and that a party thereto refuses to arbitrate such controversy, the court shall order the petitioner and the respondent to arbitrate the controversy if it determines that an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists, unless it determines that: [¶] (a) The right to compel arbitration has been waived by the petitioner; or [¶] (b) Grounds exist for the revocation of the agreement. [¶] (c) A party to the arbitration agreement is also a party to a pending court action or special proceeding with a third party, arising out of the same transaction or series of related transactions and there is a possibility of conflicting rulings on a common issue of law or fact…” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2.)

 

A party petitioning to compel arbitration has the burden of establishing the existence of a valid agreement to arbitrate and the party opposing the petition has the burden of proving, by a preponderance of the evidence, any fact necessary to its defense. (Banner Entertainment, Inc. v. Superior Court (1998) 62 Cal.App.4th 348, 356-357.)

 

A.    Existence of an Arbitration Agreement

 

“Under both federal and state law, the threshold question presented by a petition to compel arbitration is whether there is an agreement to arbitrate.” (B.D. v. Blizzard Entertainment, Inc. (2022) 76 Cal.App.5th 931, 942.) “The moving party bears the burden of producing prima facie evidence of a written agreement to arbitrate the controversy.” (Gamboa v. Northeast Community Clinic (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 158, 165-66.) “If the moving party meets its initial prima facie burden and the opposing party disputes the agreement, then in the second step, the opposing party bears the burden of producing evidence to challenge the authenticity of the agreement…If the opposing party meets its burden of producing evidence, then in the third step, the moving party must establish with admissible evidence a valid arbitration agreement between the parties. The burden of proving the agreement by a preponderance of the evidence remains with the moving party.” (Ibid.)

 

“With respect to the moving party’s burden to provide evidence of the existence of an agreement to arbitrate, it is generally sufficient for that party to present a copy of the contract to the court.” (Baker v. Italian Maple Holdings, LLC (2017) 13 Cal.App.5th 1152, 1160.)

 

 “‘Under California law, parties may validly incorporate by reference into their contract the terms of another document.’ [Citation.] The reference to the incorporated document must be clear and unequivocal and the terms of the incorporated document must be known or easily available to the contracting parties.” (Slaught v. Bencomo Roofing Co. (1994) 25 Cal.App.4th 744, 748, citing Baker v. Aubry (1989) 216 Cal.App.3d 1259, 1264; Spellman v. Securities, Annuities & Ins. Services, Inc. (1992) 8 Cal.App.4th 452, 457; Chan v. Drexel Burnham Lambert, Inc. (1986) 178 Cal.App.3d 632.)

 

            The Court finds that Defendants have satisfied their initial burden of establishing the existence of an agreement to arbitrate by providing a copy of the agreement. (Tavangarian Decl., Ex. A § 13, Ex. B § 14.) Defendants move to compel arbitration on the grounds that the parties entered into a subcontract for construction work which provides, in part, at page 6:

 

“SECTION 13. CLAIMS RESOLUTION. Any claims resolution procedure incorporated in the contract shall be deemed incorporated in this Agreement and shall apply to any disputes arising in the Arbitration. By executing this agreement, Subcontractor agrees that it is bound to participate in any binding arbitration proceeding that Subcontractor is requested to participate in, as between Contractor and Owner. This includes Subcontractor’s agreement to so participate as a party to such arbitration if requested or as witness. Specifically, this refers to the fact that, as part of the terms of the construction services agreement between Contractor and its Owner.

 

Should arbitration or other legal action be necessary to enforce or interpret the provisions of this Agreement, the prevailing party in such action shall be entitled to recover its attorney’s fees and costs of suit, including the fees and cost of any expert witness.”

 

(Tavangarian Decl., Ex. A.)

 

            The contract referenced in the Subcontract Agreement and incorporated therein is the Prime Contract between ARYA GROUP and 10675 Bellagio Road LLC, which provides, in part, at page 4:

 

“Section 14. Arbitration. Any controversy or claim arising out of or relating to this Contract or its alleged breach, which cannot be resolved by mutual agreement, shall be settled by arbitration in accordance with Construction Industry Rules of the American Arbitration Association in effect on the date of the Contract, and judgment upon the award rendered by the arbitrator(s) may be entered in any court having jurisdiction.”

 

(Id., Ex. B.)

 

            Plaintiff does not dispute that it signed the Subcontract, that Section 13 of the Subcontract incorporates the claims-resolution procedures of the Prime Contract, or that Section 14 of the Prime Contract compels arbitration of “[a]ny controversy or claim arising out of or relating to this Contract or its alleged breach” before the American Arbitration Association (“AAA”) under its Construction Industry Rules.

 

            The Court is not persuaded by Plaintiff’s argument that the phrase “as between Contractor and Owner” in the second sentence of Section 13 limits the entire clause. (Tavangarian Decl., Ex. A § 13.) Plaintiff’s interpretation ignores the first sentence of Section 13, which independently incorporates “[a]ny claims resolution procedure” from the Prime Contract and applies it to “any disputes.” (Id.)

 

            Nor is the Court persuaded by Plaintiff’s argument regarding Remedial Construction Services, LP v. AECOM, Inc. (2021) 65 Cal.App.5th 658. Plaintiff’s reliance on Remedial is misplaced because, among other differences, there was a competing litigation forum-selection clause in the subcontract that expressly preserved the parties’ right to sue in court. (Id. at pp. 664-665.) The Subcontract here contains no such clause.

 

            The Court agrees with Defendants that Slaught, 25 Cal.App.4th 744 is on point.  In that case, the court of appeal enforced a prime-contract arbitration clause that was incorporated into a subcontract by general “bound by all terms” language, similar to what Section 13 of this Subcontract provides. (Id. at p. 749; Tavangarian Decl., Ex. A § 13.)

 

            The Court is not persuaded by Plaintiff’s argument that it was not provided the Prime Contract, because incorporation requires only that the incorporated terms be “known or easily available,” not physically delivered. (Slaught, 25 Cal.App.4th at p. 748.) Again, Plaintiff does not dispute that it signed the Subcontract or that Section 13 of the Subcontract incorporates the claims-resolution procedures of the Prime Contract. Additionally, the subcontract agreement provides in section 3 that “Subcontractor and his subcontractors are bound by the prime contract documents incorporated therein insofar as they relate in any way, directly or indirectly, to the work covered by this Agreement,” and section 18 provides that “Subcontractor shall carefully study and compare the Contract Documents with each other and with information in the Contract Documents…” (Tavangarian Decl., Ex. A § 3, 18; Slaught, 25 Cal.App.4th at p. 749 [“Subcontractors never argued that they were unaware of the document being incorporated. The Subcontracts state specifically that the Subcontractors had fully examined the contract documents.”].)

 

            The Court is unpersuaded by Plaintiff’s argument based upon the fact that it is not a party to the Prime Contract. (Slaught, 25 Cal.App.4th at p. 749, citing Boys Club of San Fernando Valley, Inc. v. Fidelity & Deposit Co. (1992) 6 Cal.App.4th 1266, 1271-1272 [held that by the language of its contract incorporating the construction contract, the surety intended, and agreed, to be bound by the arbitration provision in the construction contract even though it was not a party to the contract.].)

 

            The Court agrees with Defendants that Tavangarian may compel arbitration under alter-ego estoppel principles. Plaintiff cannot plead that Tavangarian and Arya are one and the same to reach his assets while treating him as a contractual stranger to escape arbitration. (See Compl., ¶ 8-12; Rowe v. Exline (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1276, 1287-1288 [“by alleging they are unified with the signatory entity … cannot also adopt the inconsistent position that the arbitration provision … is unenforceable by or against those individuals.”].)

 

            But the Court agrees with Plaintiff that the promissory note and Bienvenida claims fall outside the scope of the arbitration agreement and should be stayed pending arbitration under Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.2. (See Compl. ¶ 20-24.)

 

            The Court finds that an evidentiary hearing is not required or appropriate. (Rosenthal v. Great Western Fin. Securities Corp. (1996) 14 Cal.4th 394, 413 [trial court may resolve disputed facts on a petition to compel by “a summary procedure”].) The interpretation of Section 13, and whether it, together with Section 3 and the incorporated Prime Contract, compels arbitration, are questions of law to be decided on the undisputed documents. (Remedial, 65 Cal.App.5th at p. 663.) Again, Plaintiff does not deny signing the Subcontract and offers no conflicting extrinsic evidence bearing on the meaning of Section 13, only its president’s statement that he did not read the incorporated document, which is immaterial to interpretation. (Slaught, 25 Cal.App.4th at p. 748.)

 

            Lastly, the Court notes that Plaintiff does not raise any arguments with respect to the enforceability of the agreement.

 

            Accordingly, Defendants’ motion to compel arbitration is GRANTED, IN PART, with respect to the claims related to the Bellagio Project.

 

            The motion is DENIED, IN PART, with respect to the promissory note and Bienvenida claims, which are stayed pending the arbitration.

 

            Plaintiff’s request for an evidentiary hearing is DENIED.
