Background

Plaintiffs Nivo 1, LLC, DA BEEC-007, LLC, and Anne Kihagi (“Kihagi”) (collectively, “Plaintiffs”) filed this action on May 6, 2021 against Defendants Lin Dee Lu Services, Yu Sheng Victor Liu, and Xiang Liu. On April 26, 2022, Plaintiffs filed the operative First Amended Complaint, alleging nine causes of action.  

On July 31, 2022, Plaintiffs filed an Amendment to Complaint substituting the name “Lin Dee Liu Services” for the incorrect name “Lin Dee Lu Services.” On December 13, 2022, Plaintiffs filed an Amendment to Complaint naming Eric Liu in place of “Doe 2,” and an Amendment to Complaint naming Building Future Foundation in place of “Doe 3.”

Defendants Lin Dee Liu dba Lin Dee Liu Services, Yu Sheng Victor Liu, Xiang Liu, Eric Liu, and Building Future Foundation (collectively, “Defendants”) now move for reconsideration of the Court’s July 30, 2026 ruling. Plaintiffs oppose. Defendants replied.

 

 

Legal Standard

Code of Civil Procedure section 1008, subdivision (a) provides:

 

When an application for an order has been made to a judge, or to a court, and refused in whole or in part, or granted, or granted conditionally, or on terms, any party affected by the order may, within 10 days after service upon the party of written notice of entry of the order and based upon new or different facts, circumstances, or law, make application to the same judge or court that made the order, to reconsider the matter and modify, amend, or revoke the prior order. The party making the application shall state by affidavit what application was made before, when and to what judge, what order or decisions were made, and what new or different facts, circumstances, or law are claimed to be shown.

 

The legislature intended to restrict motions for reconsideration to circumstances where a party offers the court some fact or circumstance not previously considered, and a valid reason for not offering it earlier. (Gilberd v. AC Transit (1995) 32 Cal.App.4th 1494, 1500) “The burden under [Code of Civil Procedure] section 1008 is comparable to that of a party seeking a new trial on the ground of newly discovered evidence: the information must be such that the moving party could not, with reasonable diligence, have discovered or produced it at the trial.” (New York Times Co. v. Superior Court (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 206, 212-213.)

“[T]he party seeking reconsideration must provide not only new evidence but also a satisfactory explanation for the failure to produce that evidence at an earlier time.” (Mink v. Superior Court (1992) 2 Cal.App.4th 1338, 1342.) Reconsideration cannot be granted based on claims that the court misinterpreted the law in its initial ruling. (Gilberd v. AC Transit, supra, 32 Cal.App.4th at p. 1500.) “[F]acts of which the party seeking reconsideration was aware at the time of the original ruling are not ‘new or different.’” (In re Marriage of Herr (2009) 174 Cal.App.4th 1463, 1468.)

Discussion

Defendants seek reconsideration of the Court’s July 30, 2026 decision, which granted in part Plaintiffs’ ex parte motion for relief from failing to file an updated exhibit list and an updated witness list. Specifically, Defendants “will and hereby do move the Court pursuant to CCP Secs. 1008 and 473 for reconsideration of and to set aside the Court's rulings issued July 30, 2026, which vacated the Court's prior orders of July 27, 2026, July 23, 2026, July 20, 2026, and July 13, 2026, all of which affirmed and ordered that the operative exhibit and witness lists for trial in this matter were those filed May 23, 2024 (the exhibit list as amended filed July 16, 2026 pursuant to the July 13, 2026 order of the Court).” (Notice of Mot., 1:5-10.)

Defendants present a myriad of facts to the Court which relate to the history of the case and substantial prejudice, contending that they could not present these facts to the Court previously, explaining that “Plaintiffs submitted their (Fourth, jurisdictionally-barred) request for reconsideration via ex parte application and Defendants’ counsel were in deposition in another matter, and working on post-trial briefing in two separate additional matters as well as the opposition to Plaintiffs Third ex parte application which the Court had ordered to be filed by July 30, 2026, the same day Plaintiffs brought the subject Fourth ex parte application, and were unable to prepare any opposition.” (Notice of Mot., 2:22-27.)

In support, Defendants provide a declaration attesting to the inability to present this information earlier. Defendants’ counsel attests that “[i]n the exercise of reasonable diligence, we were unable to present these matters to the Court on July 30th. We had only received ex parte notice one day earlier. On that day, I was in deposition from 10:00 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. in Ostroy v. Yelnin, San Mateo SC Case No. 19-FAM01412. After that concluded I had to finish and file a post-trial brief in Rancho WG v. National Integrity, JAMS Reference No. 1120017458. It was filed at 6:23 p.m., then I had to go home. We have two kids (2 yrs and 4 yrs), the elder is special needs autistic and very challenging/difficult. My wife was taking care of them all day (summer school is over now) and I have to get home to help her and relieve her of child duty. I had no chance to prepare an opposition. The most I could do was jot down notes for a brief outline, and it came as a surprise when the Court focused on the FSC issue which had not been a centerpiece of their motion but apparently ‘caught the eye’ of the Court. Also, on July 29th Mr. Tillotson was engrossed in preparing a post-trial brief in Stone v. Hirsch, Marin County SC Case No.CV0005665 as well as preparing the opposition to Plaintiffs’ third reconsideration application, which the Court ordered was due on July 30. Neither of us had an opportunity to review the lengthy convoluted procedural history of this matter and prepare an opposition.” (Sahae Decl., ¶ 11.)

Defendants’ counsel further states, “[t]hat’s the primary reason why these matters could not, in the exercise of reasonable diligence, have been presented to the Court in opposition to Plaintiffs' application. There is also a secondary reason.” (Sahae Decl., ¶ 13.) “Namely, we reasonably expected Plaintiffs’ application to not even be entertained by the Court. After all, the Court had been firm on maintaining its prior orders, and Plaintiffs had not made any effort to satisfy CCP 1008, which is jurisdictional. Relitigation of decided issues is the same conduct that had her deemed a vexatious litigant, which was affirmed by the Court of Appeal. Le Francois v. Goel (2005) 35 Cal.4th 1094, 1108-1109 holds that absent compliance with CCP 1008, or the Court issuing a formal sua sponte notice with opportunity to file briefing, a party does not have to respond to the suggestion that a prior ruling be reconsidered.” (Sahae Decl., ¶ 13.) “In sum, I respectfully submit that ample grounds exist here for the Court to entertain reconsideration of the July 30, 2026 rulings based on facts and law which could not have been presented earlier despite reasonable diligence.” (Sahae Decl., ¶ 14.)

In light of the foregoing, the Court finds that the motion for reconsideration is proper. Defendants explain that they could not have reasonably prepared an opposition to Plaintiffs’ ex parte motion. Although, as Plaintiffs contend in their opposition, the facts presented are not new per se, these facts are new to the Court; the Court did not consider them in its previous ruling, and Defendants show they could not reasonably have presented them earlier. (See Gilberd v. AC Transit, supra, 32 Cal.App.4th at p. 1500 [“clear legislative intent to restrict motions to reconsider to circumstances where a party offers the court some fact or authority that was not previously considered by it.”].)
            Turning to the merits of Defendants’ motion, Defendants’ declaration explains that “[t]he Court granted Plaintiffs’ application on the grounds that there had been no Final Status Conference (‘FSC’). As it turns out, that was not the case.” There was an FSC on May 24, 2024. (Sahae Decl., ¶ 2; Ex. B.) Exhibit B attached to Defendants’ declaration confirms that an FSC was held. (Ibid.) Exhibit B is the minute order from May 24, 2024 which describes the “Nature of Proceedings” as “Final Status Conference.” The minute order confirms that the Final Status Conference was held. More specifically, the minute order indicates that the parties stipulated to a Court trial to be conducted via Zoom; they agreed that Defendant would provide a reporter; the parties would finalize the Stipulated Facts prior to trial and they would file trial briefs. The only remaining task for the Court was to “rule on the one objection to exhibits.” The parties were to contact the courtroom on June 4th to determine the status of the trial ahead of them. The Court indicated that it intended to start their trial thereafter. Nothing else was left to do to start the trial.

Defendants’ counsel declares that “the record is clear that the May 23, 2024 lists were submitted in performance of a duly-noticed and issued Case Management Order setting an FSC. That Order was issued on May 23, 2023. The Order said the parties were ordered to comply with the FSC requirements in the Courtroom Information and lodge trial readiness materials by May 17, 2024. It said, ‘The parties are ordered to comply with the Courtroom Information requirements.’ (bold and italics in original).” (Sahae Decl., ¶ 3; Ex. C.)

Defendants’ declaration sufficiently demonstrates that an FSC occurred before the previous trial date. Los Angeles Super Court Local Rule 3.25(f)(1) specifically provides as follows:

“At least five days prior to the final status conference, counsel must serve and file lists of pre-marked exhibits to be used at trial (Local Rules 3.151, 3.53, and 3.149), jury instruction requests, trial witness lists, and a proposed short statement of the case to be read to the jury panel explaining the case. Failure to exchange and file these items may result in not being able to call witnesses, present exhibits at trial, or have a jury trial. If trial does not commence within 30 days of the set trial date, a party has the right to request a modification of any final status conference order or any previously submitted required exchange list.”

Defendants contend that “Plaintiffs approved and agreed to the May 23, 2024 lists in anticipation of a June 2024 FSC. Plaintiffs were on clear notice from Defense counsel’s September 5, 2025 email that their expanded trial materials constituted a change to the already approved lists. Yet they exercised zero diligence in acting upon that notice: they didn’t move to amend the lists, they didn't make any objection to the four-afternoon trial schedule when it was calendared at their request in January 2026, they did not attempt to show (and have never offered to show) grounds for 473 relief why their approval and agreement to the May 23, 2024 lists constituted excusable neglect, they never even bothered to file their new lists in compliance with Local Rule 3.25(f) until after the July 2026 FSC proceedings were already underway.” (Mot., 1:22-2:3)

Defendants assert that “[t]he procedure is simple. File the lists. Failure to file may result in the inability to use exhibits or call witnesses. If trial does not commence within 30 days, a party can request modification. That means what it says: ‘request modification.’ Whether the reason trial did not commence is by continuance or vacating the trial date due to Plaintiffs’ noncompliance with CRC Rule 3.650 is immaterial. Plaintiffs did not request modification. Mr. Sahae's September 5, 2025 email expressly put them on notice of the need to do so, but they stayed mum. They took their chances. Instead of requesting modification, Plaintiffs simply included amended lists (to which Mr. Sahae had previously objected) in an exhibit binder and delivered it late to the court. That is after they did not secure witnesses presence, did not participate in a court-ordered settlement program and did not meet and confer on stipulated facts. Late delivery of a binder is not a modification request, and their conduct belies their claim of excusable neglect. Plaintiffs argued that ‘Plaintiffs received no advance notice that the June 2024 Joint Witness List or its “preliminary” trial estimate would be treated as a binding limitation on Plaintiffs' presentation of evidence.’ No such notice in addition to the Local Rule was required.” (Mot., 12:21-13:8.)

Defendants contend that “[i]gnorance of local rules is not excusable neglect” (Mot., 13:9), citing McClain v. Kissler (2019) 39 Cal.App.5th 399, 414-415, where the court stated that “[t]he concept of ‘excusable,’ furthermore, is not synonymous with a get-out-of-jail-free card for parties who later come to regret past inaction or sitting on their rights. The inadvertence contemplated by the statute does not mean mere inadvertence in the abstract. If it is wholly inexcusable it does not justify relief. [Citations.] It is the duty of every party desiring to resist an action or to participate in a judicial proceeding to take timely and adequate steps to retain counsel or to act in his own person to avoid an undesirable judgment… Courts neither act as guardians for incompetent parties nor for those who are grossly careless of their own affairs.... The only occasion for the application of section 473 is where a party is unexpectedly placed in a situation to his injury without fault or negligence of his own and against which ordinary prudence could not have guarded.” (Internal quotations and citations omitted.)

Defendants thus assert that “[h]ere, reading Local Rule 3.25(f)(1) is all Plaintiffs needed to do. They have not been placed in their current situation without ‘fault or negligence of his own and against which ordinary prudence could not have guarded.’ Ordinary prudence requires reading local rules. It is not prudent to assume that a court vacating a trial date means that you are not bound by your attorney’s executed agreements. There is no excusable neglect here, there is only the consequence of failing to read, failing to meet and confer and electing a strategy of doing whatever they want rather than requesting prior permission in the form of a request for modification… The same goes for Plaintiffs’ professed cluelessness about whether an FSC was calendared: that again is their own failure of diligence. It was Plaintiffs’ responsibility to monitor the calendar and bring any omissions to the Court's attention.” (Mot., 13:21-14:5.)

As noted by Defendants in their Reply at page 1, lines 10-14, regarding the lack of another FSC when the trial dates were set, “[a] plaintiff has an obligation to monitor the case in the trial court, to keep track of relevant dates, and to determine whether any filing, scheduling, or calendaring errors have occurred.”  Jordan v Superstar Sandcars (2010) 182 Cal. App. 4th 1416, 1422). Although the Jordan case discussed this obligation in the context of the five-year rule, it applies nevertheless to the situation in this case. It was incumbent upon Plaintiffs to bring the omission of another FSC when the trial dates were set if they thought it was needed. Since the only remaining task for the Court at such an FSC was to rule on one objection, the FSC was not truly needed because the ruling on the one objection could easily be done at the start of the court trial without a separate FSC.

In opposition, Plaintiffs focus on the vacated trial date, and that a new FSC was not set. Plaintiffs also assert that “Defendants must first establish that an operative May 2024 FSC order permanently governed the July/August 2026 trial. That predicate is precisely what the procedural record and Local Rule 3.25 do not establish.” (Opp., 14:25-27, emphasis omitted.) However, as Defendants’ moving papers point out, Local Rule 3.25(f)(1) provides that the original exhibit and witness list is still valid when a trial date is vacated, and this merely changes upon a modification request by a party. However, Defendants present no evidence that they moved to modify the witness list before the trial set for July 13, 2026 began. Although the Court found that an FAC did not occur before the most recent trial, the Court had not been presented with Defendants’ opposition demonstrating that an FSC took place on May 24, 2024.

Additionally, Defendants contend that “[a]s set forth in the procedural history [], Plaintiffs have delayed this case through gamesmanship and inexcusable inattention. From 2021-2026 Plaintiffs’ Trial Estimate was 3-4 days. On May 24, 2024 for the first FSC, Mr. Barkin approved and agreed to the 16-hour witness list and 46-exhibit exhibit list. That agreement was binding. The essence of Plaintiffs' request for relief from Barkin’s agreement is conclusory assertions that Mr. King views the case differently. (e.g., 7/28/26 Kihagi Dec. Pars. 10-11 [‘Following Mr. Barkin's withdrawal, the case continued to develop through extensive trial preparation, including the development of additional evidence, witnesses, exhibits, and legal issues’]; 7/29/26 King Dec. Pars. 16-17 [‘The substitution or involvement of different trial counsel, the bankruptcy proceedings, the extended delay, continued development of the documentary record, and Plaintiffs' anticipated use of adverse and subpoenaed witnesses materially affected the time reasonably necessary to present Plaintiffs’ case’].) What ‘additional evidence’? What ‘additional witnesses’? What ‘additional legal issues’? And Why were those matters unknown to Plaintiffs at the time of Barkin’s agreement? How could they possibly be? This is Plaintiffs’ case. Defendants’ answer has been on file since 2022.” (Mot., 11:4-17.)

Defendants then cite People v. Trujillo (1977) 67 Cal.App.3d 547, 554, to assert that “Mr. King’s substitution is irrelevant” (Mot., 11:18), because where the Court of Appeal in that case stated that “[i]t has been held that the mere fact there has been a change of attorneys does not relieve a party of a stipulation entered into by the previous attorney: An attorney who is substituted for another in a cause has no greater rights than his predecessor, nor is his client’s position in the case in in [sic] any way changed by such substitution. He steps into the place of his predecessor, and stands, with reference to the case and to the other party, precisely as did his predecessor, and can repudiate or be relieved from an agreement that had been made by him only to the same extent and in the same manner as could his predecessor.” (Internal quotations and citations omitted.) The court also expressed that “[t]he grounds upon which the trial court may exercise its discretion are that the stipulation was entered into as the result of fraud, misrepresentation, mistake of fact, or excusable neglect, that the facts have changed, or that there is some other special circumstance rendering it unjust to enforce the stipulation.” (Ibid., internal quotations and citations omitted.) Defendants assert that “Plaintiffs made no such showing. Plaintiffs simply ignored the 2024 exhibit and witness lists that they agreed to proceed upon. Where was any showing that Mr. Barkin committed excusable error in agreeing to the joint lists with Mr. Sahae? Or that he was defrauded into doing so? Where was his declaration that he was off in his trial estimate by at least four-fold and that was due to excusable neglect? Where was his declaration that Plaintiffs simply missed 50-60 documents they now claim are essential to prosecution of their case and that was due to excusable neglect?” (Mot., 11:28-12:6.)

Plaintiffs’ opposition briefly discusses People v. Trujillo, supra, asserting that “Plaintiffs rely upon the Court’s subsequent vacatur of the trial and establishment of a new FSC and trial before Mr. King appeared.” (Opp., 13:27-28.) However, Plaintiffs do not sufficiently demonstrate or discuss how the situation of the case has changed from the actions of their previous counsel, as Defendants’ motion addresses. A new trial date and new counsel do not give rise to the circumstances contemplated by People v. Trujillo. As Defendants assert in reply, “[t]he ‘vacatur’ does not mean the parties and Court start anew from square one[.]” (Reply, 4:9.) Defendants cite Loney v. Superior Court (1984) 160 Cal.App.3d 719, 723: “trial dates are often vacated or continued, and that this is done for myriad reasons. If, every time a case was dropped from the trial calendar for however brief a time, the door was opened at the whim of the parties to a new round of pleadings … trials would be delayed and court calendars further congested in an already overburdened system.” Defendants assert, “[t]hat’s exactly what is happening here. Trial has been delayed, and Defendants and this Court are both suffering the burdens of Plaintiffs’ whims.” (Reply, 4:13-15.)

Defendants’ counsel also attests to prejudice, explaining, inter alia, that “[o]pening the door to Plaintiffs’ new 105-exhibit and 89-hour witness lists in the middle of trial is severely prejudicial to Defendants. I have spent roughly three dozen hours preparing trial materials, trial presentation, examination outlines, and preparing my witnesses to testify. Those preparations necessarily relied on the existing exhibit and witness lists. Moreover, the Court repeatedly confirmed that those lists were operative, so I had no reason to incur the expense and effort of preparing based on additional witnesses and exhibits. Most of that time will be wasted if the trial evidence is completely changed in the manner envisioned by Plaintiffs. Even as to parts that I can still use, it would take substantial time to revise it and cross-refer it to the new evidence. Probably 10-20 hours or more.” (Sahae Decl., ¶ 5.) “There will also be additional expense and effort litigating the new lists. By that I mean, so much of the matter is objectionable. Witnesses and documents abound which were not disclosed in discovery. The Court has indicated we need to file a new motion in limine (MIL) to raise these objections. While I will be able to incorporate some material from our prior MIL #1, that motion was denied for lack of specificity. I will have to go through the painstaking process of moving as to each specific exhibit and – since the Court wants to do this as a pretrial motion and not in trial in the context of the evidence – I will have to lay all necessary foundation for the Court to understand the exhibit and why it is objectionable. This motion will be big, and time-consuming. I conservatively estimate at least 20 hours, likely 30 or more, to prepare moving papers and the reply. Argument as to each exhibit targeted by the motion is likely to consume a full trial afternoon altogether.” (Sahae Decl., ¶ 6.) “That’s just the exhibits. Plaintiffs’ excessive witness list will also necessitate an MIL. Most of these witnesses have no relevant testimony to offer, and Plaintiffs cannot show otherwise or make a valid offer of proof because Plaintiffs haven’t deposed any of the witnesses. They have no idea what the witnesses would say if called to the stand, so they are incapable of making an offer of proof that is honest and specific. Plaintiffs are expressly intending to conduct their depositions at trial, on the witness stand. That’s what Ms. Kihagi told me in the August 3, 2026 meet and confer call. In reference to the lender witnesses, i.e. Yu Sheng Victor Liu, Eric Liu, and Xiang Liu, she said ‘we don't know what they’re going to say, let’s find out.’ I was so shocked by that I wrote it down. The witness MIL is likely to require upwards of 15 hours to prepare moving papers, reply, and to conduct hearing argument. Depending on the outcome of that MIL, I can expect to have to spend upwards of 30 hours preparing several new witness examinations (from three total witnesses to a dozen), and preparing Defendants' witnesses for colossally lengthier examinations, with dozens of new exhibits.” (Sahae Decl., ¶ 7.) “The Court has also requested a new trial brief. Again, though I will be able to incorporate material from the prior trial brief, that prior brief addressed the evidence and testimony in reliance upon the signed and agreed lists. I anticipate another 10-20 hours of work to create the new brief. Once the actual trial resumes, it will be in increased duration, again to the expense and financial burden of Defendants. Plaintiffs’ proposed witness list is 89 hours of testimony; if they even bring in half of that it is 11 more trial half-days.” (Sahae Decl., ¶ 8.) The Court recognizes that Defendants also present significant prejudice regarding time and expense if the Court denies this motion for reconsideration.

In Plaintiffs’ declaration in support of the opposition, Anne Kihagi attests that Defendants’ counsel signed the stipulation on behalf of her former counsel, Mr. Barkin, and that this was not authorized. (Kihagi Decl., ¶¶ 9-11.) However, in Defendants’ counsel’s reply declaration, he attests that he “would never, ever, file a signature of opposing counsel without their authorization;” rather, “Mr. Barkin provided the authorization to me orally, via telephone, on May 23, 2024 at about 12:30 p.m. I can be so specific about the date and time of the call because promptly after the phone call I emailed my staff to tell them Barkin approved the lists. Attached hereto as Exhibit A is a true and correct of my email to staff (with other internal communications redacted).” (Sahe Reply Decl., ¶¶ 2-3, Ex. A.) Accordingly, Defendants’ counsel attests to his first-hand experience that he did not sign without authorization. Moreover, as noted in Defendants’ reply papers an attorney has authority “to make such tactical decisions as whether to call a particular witness, and the court and opposing counsel must be able to rely upon the decisions he makes, even when the client voices opposition in open court.” (Knabe v. Brister (2007) 154 Cal.App.4th 1316, 1324.) Defendants contend that “[t]his is the fundamental point of Defendants’ motion and the prejudice they are being forced to suffer: they did rely on the joint filings. So did the Court in rendering its orders, and Defendants further relied on those.” (Reply 5:21-23.)

Based on the foregoing, Defendants demonstrate that an FSC occurred, and Plaintiffs did not demonstrate good cause for relief from former counsel Barkin’s stipulations, nor did Plaintiffs demonstrate that they sought to modify the existing pretrial order that was based on the joint witness and exhibit lists signed by the parties prior to the May 24, 2024 FSC. Accordingly, the Court grants Defendants’ motion for reconsideration.

 

Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, Defendants’ motion for reconsideration is GRANTED. Background

On September 15, 2022, Plaintiff Utomo Tani aka Jimmy Tani (“Tani” or “Plaintiff”) filed this action against Defendants Kam Choi Lin (“Lin”), Chang Ho Chen (“Chen”), Wei Lin Zheng (“Zheng”), Wai Hung Szeto (“Szeto”), and Yan Xian Li (“Li”) (collectively, the “Original Defendants”). Golden Partner Global Investment, Inc., doing business as NBC Seafood Restaurant (“GPG”) is listed as a nominal defendant.

The Complaint alleges ten causes of action for (1) breach of fiduciary duty, (2) constructive fraud, (3) intentional misrepresentation (fraud), (4) negligent misrepresentation, (5) fraud by concealment, (6) conversion, (7) constructive trust, (8) unjust enrichment, (9) inspection of records, and (10) involuntary dissolution of corporation (Corporations Code section 1800).

On September 16, 2022, the case was assigned to Department 19 at the Stanley Mosk Courthouse.

On November 9, 2022, Li aka Kristal Li filed their Cross-Complaint against Tani and Roes 1-20. On December 29, 2023, Li filed their operative Second Amended Cross-Complaint against Tani, alleging three causes of action for (1) retaliation for engaging in protected activity/discrimination based on sex, (2) hostile work environment, and (3) intentional infliction of emotional distress.

On November 10, 2022, Lin, Chen, Zheng, and Szeto filed their Cross-Complaint against Tani, Goodland Global Farm Inc., and Roes 1-20. On August 11, 2023, Lin, Chen, Zheng, and Szeto filed the operative First Amended Cross-Complaint, alleging 11 causes of action: (1) breach of fiduciary duty, (2) accounting, (3) declaratory relief, (4) conversion, (5) constructive fraud, (6) intentional misrepresentation/fraud, (7) negligent misrepresentation/fraud, (8) fraud by concealment, (9) unjust enrichment, (10) inspection of records, and (11) breach of oral contract.

On May 24, 2023, Plaintiff filed a “Motion for Injunctive Relief and Alternatively for the Appointment of Receiver Over [GPG]” (hereafter, the “Motion to Appoint Receiver”).  

On April 12, 2024, Department 19 granted the Motion to Appoint Receiver, ordering as follows:

1. The status quo of the business of Golden Partner Investment Inc. d/b/a NBC Seafood Restaurant must be maintained;

2. Any named defendant or any other person or entity controlled by a named defendant is prohibited from collecting or taking any cash derivative of restaurant operations of the NBC Seafood Restaurant; and

3. All defendants and any person or entity controlled by a named defendant is prohibited from taking any monetary compensation from income derivative of restaurant operations of NBC Seafood Restaurant.

In addition, the Court orders the appointment of a limited purpose Receiver.

The Court orders that Plaintiff file an undertaking in support of the preliminary injunction within ten (10) days hereof. The Court orders a bond in the amount of $10,000. (Code of Civ. Proc. section 529).

The receiver must file an undertaking. Once a receiver is appointed by the Court, an undertaking in the amount of $10,000 will be ordered to be posted. (Code of Civ. Proc. sections 566 and 567).

The parties are ordered to meet and confer with respect to the selection of the Receiver. The parties are ordered to stipulate to one Receiver, or if an agreement cannot be reached, then each party shall file and serve the names of no more than three (3) proposed Receivers, including providing a Resume, information regarding their background, experience, other relevant qualifications, and the hourly rate by April 24, 2024. The Court will select the Receiver based upon the submissions and information presented.

The Court sets a Non-Appearance Case Review Re: Selection of A Receiver For May 1, 2024. The Court sets a Status Conference Re: Receiver for June 8, 2024, at 8:30 a.m.

Order signed this date.” (Minute Order 4/12/2024, pp. 1-2.)

On April 30, 2024, the Original Defendants appealed Department 19’s Order granting Plaintiff’s Motion to Appoint Receiver.

On May 8, 2024, during a Non-Appearance Case Review Re: Selection of a Receiver, the court in Department 19 noted the Original Defendants had neither proposed a receiver nor perfected their appeal of the order granting the Motion to Appoint Receiver. Therefore, the court granted Plaintiff’s unopposed request to appoint Kevin Singer as the receiver (“Receiver”) at NBC Seafood Restaurant. (Singer Decl., ¶ 3; See 5/8/24 Order.) Subsequently, the Receiver filed his Oath of Receiver, posted his $10,000 bond, and assumed his duties. (Singer Decl., ¶ 3.)

On March 14, 2025, the case was transferred to this Court (Department 508).

On November 7, 2025, the Court dismissed the Complaint “for Plaintiff’s repeated failure to obey Court orders and failure to appear.” (Minute Order 11/7/25, p. 1.)

Legal Standard

Code of Civil Procedure section 568 provides that “[t]he receiver has, under the control of the court, power to bring and defend actions in his own name, as receiver; to take and keep possession of the property, to receive rents, collect debts, to compound for and compromise the same, to make transfers, and generally to do such acts respecting the property as the court may authorize.” Receivers are entitled to compensation for their own services and the services performed by their attorneys.¿(Venza v. Venza (1951) 101 Cal.App.2d 678, 680.)¿Generally, the costs of a receivership are paid from the property in the receivership estate.¿(Andrade, supra, at p. 110.)

However, Courts may impose the receiver’s costs on a party who sought the appointment of the receiver or apportion them among the parties for whose benefit the receivership was created, depending upon the equitable circumstances presented.¿(Stanton v. Pratt (1941) 18 Cal.2d 599, 603.) “Courts generally are vested with large discretion in determining who shall pay the cost and expenses of receiverships. The court may assess the costs of a receivership against the fund or property in receivership or against the applicant for the receivership, or it may apportion them among the parties, depending upon circumstances.” ((Baldwin v. Baldwin (1947) 82 Cal.App.2d 851, 856.) In People v. Riverside University (1973) 35 Cal.App.3d 572, 587, the Court of Appeal noted that “[i]t is settled that fees awarded to receivers are in the sound discretion of the trial court and in the absence of a clear showing of an abuse of discretion, a reviewing court is not justified in setting aside an order fixing fees.”

In addition, pursuant to California Rules of Court, rule 3.1183, subdivision (a), “[i]nterim fees are subject to final review and approval by the court. The court retains jurisdiction to award a greater or lesser amount as the full, fair, and final value of the services received.” California Rules of Court, rule 3.1183, subdivision (b) provides that “[u]nless good cause is shown, objections to a receiver’s interim report and accounting must be made within 10 days of notice of the report and accounting, must be specific, and must be delivered to the receiver and all parties entitled to service of the interim report and accounting.” And, per the Court’s April 11, 2024 Order appointing the Receiver, “[f]ailure of a Party to object within this 10-day period shall constitute a waiver of that party’s objection(s) to the fees for that period.” (Order 4/11/24, ¶ 9.)

Discussion

As an initial matter, the Court denies LCZS’s request to strike the Receiver’s Omnibus Reply.

A.    Receiver’s Request and Preliminary Arguments

The instant motion to Approve Final Accounting seeks, among other things, an order awarding the Receiver fees of $542,851.00 and expenses of $14,660.57 incurred during his final receiver services, the preparation of his final accounting, and this motion. (Mot., 6:8-10.)

The Receiver explains that “[o]n November 7, 2025, the Court dismissed Plaintiff Utomo Tani’s (‘Plaintiff’) complaint with prejudice for repeatedly failing to appear. Because my appointment arose from Plaintiff’s complaint, the Court directed me to return control of the Business to Defendants … by November 14, 2025, and to file the instant Motion by November 26, 2025. I timely transitioned control of the Business back to the Defendants.” (Singer Decl., ¶ 4.)

In the Receiver’s declaration in support, he explains that, to date, he has “been paid $499,718.17 toward my total fees and expenses of $557,511.57 for this appointment. There remains an outstanding balance owed to me in the amount of $57,793.40 ($557,511.57 - $499,718.17) (‘Receiver’s Outstanding Balance’), which amount is the sum of my October 2025, partial November 2025, and final accounting invoices, and which can be paid in full from the funds in my possession.” (Singer Decl., ¶ 7.) The Receiver “currently holds $150,000 in his trust account, which amount I held back upon turning over control of the Business and its accounts to the Defendants in order to cover the Receivership Estate’s remaining liabilities… After payment of the Receiver’s Outstanding Balance and any fees and expenses incurred after November 25, 2025, I recommend that all remaining funds in his trust account be returned to the Business pursuant to Defendants’ instructions.” (Singer Decl., ¶ 8.)

“As a general proposition the costs of a receivership are primarily a charge upon the property in the receiver’s possession and are to be paid out of said property.” (Andrade, supra, 216 Cal. at p. 110.)

However, in the Receiver’s supplemental declaration, he explained that since filing the instant motion—the final report on November 26, 2025—“the Court of Appeal reversed the Receiver’s appointment on procedural grounds, holding that the Restaurant was never served with a copy of the summons and complaint, and that the record did not contain substantial evidence that Utomo Tani met the 33.33% share-ownership threshold required by California law.” (Supp. Mot. 2/13/26, 2:8-11.) The Court of Appeal’s Order found that the appointment of a receiver was an abuse of discretion. (See Rondos v. Superior Court (1957) 151 Cal.App.2d 190, 195 [“‘Where a complaint fails by its allegations to show that the conditions required by law have been complied with, an order for the appointment of a receiver is void for all purposes.’”].)

Specifically, GPG points out that “[o]n December 17, 2025, the Court of Appeal of the State of California, Second Appellate District, Division Four (the ‘Appellate Court’) issued a published decision conclusively determining that: (1) the trial court never acquired jurisdiction over Golden Partner Global Investment, Inc. dba NBC Seafood Restaurant (‘GPG-NBC’) because it was never served; (2) the April 12, 2024 receivership and preliminary injunction order was therefore void ab initio; and (3) independent of jurisdiction, the statutory prerequisites for a receivership under Corporations Code § 1800 and injunctive relief were never satisfied. Exhibit A – 12/17/25 Court of Appeal Decision.” (GP Opp., 1:5-11, emphasis omitted.)

In his initial supplement to motion, the Receiver cites Code of Civil Procedure section 917.5. Under Code of Civil Procedure section 917.5, “[t]he perfecting of an appeal shall not stay enforcement of the judgment or order in the trial court if the judgment or order appealed from appoints a receiver, unless an undertaking in a sum fixed by the trial court is given on condition that if the judgment or order is affirmed or the appeal is withdrawn, or dismissed, the appellant will pay all damages which the respondent may sustain by reason of the stay in the enforcement of the judgment.” Accordingly, the Receiver asserts that “an appeal does not stay enforcement of an order appointing a receiver unless the appellant posts an undertaking. Here, no undertaking was posted, no stay was obtained, and the Receiver’s appointment remained in full force throughout the pendency of the appeal. Section 917.5 provided Appellants with the opportunity to stay the receivership, which they chose not to pursue. As a result, the Receiver acted in good faith in administering the Estate for approximately eighteen months while the unstayed appeal was pending. Because no party sought to stay the proceedings, none should be allowed to object to the Receiver’s compensation, which must be paid.” (Supp. Mot. 2/13/26, 2:19-26.) GPG asserts in opposition that “CCP § 917.5 presumes a valid order. A void order is void immediately. No undertaking is required to stay a nullity. A court cannot enforce what never lawfully existed.” (GPG Opp., 4:19-23.)

The Court does not find the Receiver’s Code of Civil Procedure section 917.5 argument controlling. According to case law, the statute’s predecessors (former Code of Civil Procedure sections 939 and 943) were enacted to afford a remedy for “prodigal, unwise, and unwarranted appointments of receivers,” not void orders. ((In re Ringgold (2006) 142 Cal.App.4th 1001, 1010 [stating that current CCP section 917.2 is “in part derived” from former section 943]); ((Jacobs v. Superior Court of San Joaquin County (1901) 133 Cal. 364, 365–366 [“Formerly … there was no appeal from an order appointing a receiver, but in 1897 … section 939 of the Code of Civil Procedure was amended so as to allow such appeal; and at the same time section 943 was amended so as to provide for the staying of an order appointing a receiver by an undertaking on appeal. These amendments were apparently intended to afford a remedy for prodigal, unwise, and unwarranted appointments of receivers…”].) The Receiver did not cite (and the Court has not found) any authority supporting the Receiver’s implied argument that section 917.5 applies to void orders appointing receivers.

B.    Personal Versus Subject Matter Jurisdiction under Sullivan

The Receiver also asserts that “California law has long recognized that a receiver who serves in good faith is entitled to be paid from the estate, notwithstanding an irregularity in the appointment, a dismissal of the underlying case, or a judgment in favor of the defendant, and even if estate funds are insufficient.” (Supp. Mot. 2/13/26, 3:3-5.) The Receiver asserts, “[i]n Andrade v. Andrade (1932) 216 Cal. 108 [(Andrade)], a receiver was appointed over property that the California Supreme Court later determined to belong to a party who had ‘resisted the taking of her property by the receiver,’ which defeated the basis of the receivership. (Id. at p. 109; 111-12.) Nonetheless, the Andrade court approved the receiver’s final report, and ordered payment of his fees and expenses, explaining that the receiver’s fees are ‘a charge upon the property in the receiver’s possession,’ that may be ‘imposed upon the parties to the action,’ and that ‘[s]uch direct liability may result from an irregularity in the appointment.’ (Id. at p. 110.) Even if the receivership is improperly established, the receiver must ‘look for his compensation to the portion of the fund which was properly brought within his jurisdiction...[.’] (Andrade, supra, 216 Cal. at p. 111.) Here, the procedural flaws that voided the Receiver’s appointment are not attributable to the Receiver, who faithfully performed his duties for eighteen months while no stay was in effect. As such, the Receiver’s compensation is properly charged against the funds in the receivership estate.” (Supp. Mot. 2/13/26, 3:9-20.)

            However, GPG points out that Andrade “is based on [a] Receiver appointed over a party that the Court had actual jurisdiction over…” (GPG Opp., 4:5-8; see Andrade, supra, 216 Cal. at p. 108 [“This is an appeal from an order discharging a receiver and directing plaintiff to pay his fees”]; id. at p. 110 [“In our opinion the order appealed from was, and is, a proper order”].) Here, unlike in Andrade, the Court of Appeal found the trial court order appointing the Receiver was void. (See Court of Appeal Opinion, p. 16 [“[B]ecause Tani failed to establish his standing to assert a dissolution cause of action, the trial court abused its discretion in appointing a receiver. (See Rondos v. Superior Court (1957) 151 Cal.App.2d 190, 195 [‘“Where a complaint fails by its allegations to show that the conditions required by law have been complied with[,] an order for the appointment of a receiver is void for all purposes”’])”].)

            Thus, GPG asserts that Andrade cannot control, and the applicable precedent is Sullivan, supra, 145 Cal. 759, where “the California Supreme Court held that where a receiver is improperly or voidly appointed, the receiver’s compensation may not be charged against the estate or against parties not responsible for the appointment. The Court … held that the party who procured the receivership is the proper party, if any, to compensate the receiver.” (GPG Opp., 6:23-27; see Sullivan, supra, 145 Cal. at p. 768 [“The cases are not infrequent where the court has settled the account of a receiver erroneously appointed, and made the amount a charge against the party who has procured the appointment, but no case has been brought to our attention where a court has made such an order when its own judicial act in appointing the receiver was nugatory and void for want of jurisdiction. The receiver is not obliged to accept such appointment, and in such a case as this, as the court was without power to appoint him, it must equally be without power to make any other order depending for its validity upon the order of appointment. The utmost that is left for a receiver appointed under such circumstances is his right of action for compensation against the party procuring his appointment.”].)

            Opposing Parties assert that equity does not create jurisdiction, and Sullivan forecloses the Court’s power to make any orders relating to an invalid appointment. Opposing Parties also assert that an argument differentiating between personal and subject matter jurisdiction does not change the outcome because the Court of Appeal’s opinion identified two issues that make the court’s order void: first, that GPG was never served, so the Court never had personal jurisdiction over GPG and, second, “Tani never satisfied the statutory ownership prerequisite of Corporations Code section 1800, so he had no standing to seek dissolution, and the appointment on that record was ‘void for all purposes’ under Rondos. (Opinion 16.) That is not a defect in the Court’s power over a particular party. It is a defect in the conditions the Legislature requires before any receiver may be appointed at all.” (Supp. Opp., 4:10-14.)

            In the Receiver’s supplemental brief from July 20, 2026, he first asserts that Sullivan, supra, involved a total lack of subject matter jurisdiction, rather than merely personal jurisdiction, which he contends is a key difference to the instant case. The Receiver asserts that “[i]f Sullivan applies here, the Court would be entirely without jurisdiction to enter any order pertaining to the Receiver, and the Receiver would be forced to file a separate lawsuit if he wished to recover fees owed to his office.” (Supp. Mot. 7/20/26, 3:9-12.) The Receiver discusses Sullivan, asserting that “[c]ritically, Sullivan itself provides that courts routinely settle the account of receivers who are ‘erroneously appointed’ and charge the amount to parties; what a court cannot do is award fees where its ‘own judicial act in appointing the receiver was nugatory and void for want of jurisdiction.’ (145 Cal. at p. 768 [‘The cases are not infrequent where the court has settled the account of a receiver erroneously appointed’].) Indeed, Sullivan distinguishes the two: a case where the court has no subject matter jurisdiction ‘does not present the case of a reversal of an order appointing a receiver for mere error or irregularity in its procurement. It presents the case of an order void for want of legal authority, where the receiver, in point of law, was not a receiver but a trespasser.’ (145 Cal. 759, 768.) The alternative rule for such cases, announced in passing in Sullivan – that the trial court can look to the parties to compensate an erroneously appointed receiver – is exactly the rule established in Andrade and relied upon by the Receiver here. This is a case where the Andrade rule applies.” (Supp. Mot. 7/20/26, 4:22-5:5.)

            The Receiver also asserts that “the failure to serve the corporation as a nominal party does not destroy subject matter jurisdiction or invoke the Sullivan rule. Specifically, a judgment entered against a defendant who was never served is void as to that defendant for want of personal jurisdiction.” (Supp. Mot., 7/20/26, 6:13-15.)

            In the instant case, the Appellate Court explicitly stated that the order was void as to GPG. (See Court of Appeal Opinion p. 2 [“the orders as to the Restaurant are void.”]; p. 14 [“the trial court had no jurisdiction over the Restaurant when it made these orders, and as to the Restaurant, the orders are void.”].) The Receiver attempts to read these statements to demonstrate that the Court merely lacks personal jurisdiction, rather than subject matter jurisdiction, so the appointment is voidable rather than void. However, as Opposing Parties contend, “[t]he distinction makes no difference. The appointment is void on a ground that has nothing to do with personal jurisdiction — the statutory conditions for appointing any receiver were never met — and, regardless, a lack of jurisdiction over the affected party is fundamental jurisdiction, the same as a lack of subject matter jurisdiction. Equity cannot supply the power the remittitur withheld.” (Supp. Opp., 4:3-7.) Specifically, the Court of Appeal’s opinion cited Rondos v. Superior Court, Solano County (1957) 151 Cal.App.2d 190, 195, expressly quoting the following language: “Where a complaint fails by its allegations to show that the conditions required by law have been complied with, an order for the appointment of a receiver is void for all purposes.” This statement, read in conjunction with Sullivan, dissolves the subject matter jurisdiction and personal jurisdiction distinction. The Court of Appeal reversed on two separate grounds. First, the trial court’s appointment of the Receiver was void for failing to serve GPG and thereby acquire jurisdiction over the restaurant. Second, the entire appointment was void because the trial court abused its discretion in appointing the Receiver under Corporations Code section 1800 because Tani never satisfied the statutory ownership prerequisites. The Receiver’s personal jurisdiction argument cannot overcome the voidness of the failure to satisfy the statutory conditions.

            Opposing Parties’ reply papers assert that “[t]he Receiver’s own cases confirm that a failure of the statutory conditions is a void, not merely irregular, defect. The Receiver cites Grant v. Los Angeles etc. R.R. Co. (1897) 116 Cal. 71, 72, for the proposition that the appointment there was void because ‘the action was not one in which a receiver could be competently appointed.’ (Opp. 4.) That is this case. Tani never met section 1800’s one-third ownership threshold for a dissolution receiver. Grant went on to hold the derivative fee order equally void: ‘That order [appointing the receiver] being void, the present order must, of necessity, be held, as to appellant, likewise void.’ (Id. at p. 74.) The dividing line Sullivan draws is not the label ‘subject matter’ versus ‘personal’ jurisdiction; it is whether the court had power to appoint a receiver in the action at all. Where the statutory predicate for any receiver is absent, it did not.” (Reply, 2:24-3:4.) The Court finds similarly that, here, the Receiver was not “competently appointed” (see Grant v. Los Angeles & P. Ry. Co. (1897) 116 Cal. 71, 72), because the Receiver could not be legally appointed, and the trial court lacked the statutory predicate to appoint the Receiver.

            As the Court of Appeal determined, and discussed above, the appointment of the Receiver is void, not merely voidable; the same scenario presented in Sullivan. (See Sullivan, supra, 145 Cal. at p. 768 [“where a court has made such an order when its own judicial act in appointing the receiver was nugatory and void for want of jurisdiction.”].) Although the Receiver contends that determining that Sullivan controls would make this Court without jurisdiction to enter any order pertaining to the Receiver, this contention sweeps too broadly, as the Court may enter orders pursuant to the Appellate Court’s opinion. (See Court of Appeal Opinion p. 17 [“The cause [sic] is remanded to the trial court for further proceedings consistent with the views expressed in this opinion.”].)

C.    The Court’s Ability to Charge Fees Against Tani or Other Remaining Parties

Next, the question arises as to whether the Court may charge Tani, the individual who procured the appointment. Opposing Parties rely on Sullivan, supra, asserting that “[a] voidly appointed receiver’s only recourse is a separate action against the party who procured the appointment — not a compensation order in this proceeding. And the complaint in which the receiver was sought has been dismissed.” (Supp. Opp., 7:26-28.) As the court in Sullivan expressed, “an order void for want of legal authority, and where the receiver, in point of law, was not a receiver, but a trespasser.” (Id. at p. 768.) Thus, “as the court was without power to appoint him, it must equally be without power to make any other order depending for its validity upon the order of appointment. The utmost that is left for a receiver appointed under such circumstances is his right of action for compensation against the party procuring his appointment.” (Ibid., emphasis added.)

The Receiver contends that “Baldwin answers the Court’s question directly. There, after the Supreme Court affirmed judgment for the defendant, the trial court found that the receivership had been procured on allegations ‘determined to be untrue’ and that ‘the appointment of said receiver herein was unnecessary, wrongful, improvident and without reasonable or probable cause.’” (Supp. Mot. 7/20/26, 8:5-8, citing Baldwin, supra, 82 Cal.App.2d at p. 856.) The Baldwin court then held that “the plaintiff, and not the defendant, should be the one charged with the fees and expenses connected with the ‘improvident’ receivership.” (Baldwin, supra, 82 Cal.App.2d at p. 856.) In doing so, the court applied the rule stated in Andrade. Specifically, where the Andrade court expressed: “We receive no impropriety in assessing the receiver’s fees and expenses against the plaintiff, who sought and procured his appointment, rather than against the defendant [] or her property. As a general proposition the costs of a receivership are primarily a charge upon the property in the receiver’s possession and are to be paid out of said property. However, this is not an invariable rule. In many cases a direct liability is imposed upon the parties to the action, or upon some of them, for the remuneration of the receiver.” (Andrade, supra, 216 Cal. at p. 110.) However, the Receiver’s authorities pertain to an irregular appointment, rather than a void appointment.

The Receiver also contends that “Sullivan is not to the contrary,” and asserts that “it did not hold that the appointing court is powerless to adjudicate that liability.” (Supp. Mot., 7/20/26, 8:15-18.) However, as quoted above, Sullivan did find that a receiver’s only recourse is a separate action. As Opposing Parties’ reply papers also point out, under Sullivan, “[t]he answer is settled,” and the Receiver’s “recourse is a separate action—not a fee order entered in the very proceeding the appellate court declared void.” (Opp. Parties’ Supp. Reply, 5:28-6:3.)

As for charging the parties that did not procure the appointment, Opposing Parties contend that “[i]f the Receiver cannot obtain a fee order against the procuring party in this proceeding, he certainly cannot obtain one against the parties who opposed the receivership Under Sullivan, the receiver’s only avenue is an action against the party who procured the appointment, and a void appointment will not support a fee order charged to the estate or to parties who did not procure it. [Citations.] The Defendants — Lin, Chen, Zheng, and Szeto — did not procure the receiver. They resisted it for more than two years, prosecuted the appeal, and obtained the ruling that voided it. Kent Tran likewise never sought the appointment. Charging them the costs of a receivership they defeated would stand Sullivan on its head and would penalize the prevailing parties for prevailing.” (Supp. Opp., 9:10-18.)

The Receiver’s arguments involve cases that do not address a void appointment, whereas here, the Court does not have the power to charge the parties to this case. (See Ephraim v. Pacific Bank (1990) 129 Cal.589, 592 [“‘The rule that the compensation of a receiver is a charge upon the fund in his hands has been held not to apply, without qualification, to the case where the appointment was irregularly made and is vacated.’ If he has taken property into his custody under an irregular, unauthorized appointment, he must look for his compensation to the parties at whose instance he was appointed; and the same rule applies if the property of which he takes possession is determined to belong to persons who are not parties to the action, and is taken from his possession by paramount authority.”]; City of Chula Vista v. Gutierrez (2012) 207 Cal.App.4th 681, 686 [“A court may require one or more parties to pay for receiver fees where the property subject to the receivership is inadequate to compensate the receiver and/or where other equitable circumstances support imposing fees on a party. [Citations.] In considering the appropriate source for the compensation, a relevant factor is whether the party to be charged obtained a benefit from the receiver’s services.”].)

The clear rule in Sullivan is that a void receiver, as a trespasser to the Court, must seek payment through a separate action. (See Sullivan, supra, 145 Cal. at p. 768 [“After the judgment declaring the grant of administration and all subsequent proceedings void, the petitioner will be regarded as a trespasser from the beginning. He cannot be regarded as a receiver whose possession is that of the court.”].)

D.    The Court’s Ability to Order Disgorgement

Next, the Opposing Parties request disgorgement for payments previously paid to the Receiver. Specifically, Opposing Parties assert that “[t]his is not a request for a freestanding ‘disgorgement’ remedy, and the Court need not find any new power to grant it. Under California Rules of Court, rule 3.1183(a), every dollar the Receiver drew during the receivership was an interim advance — expressly ‘subject to final review and approval by the court,’ which ‘retains jurisdiction to award a greater or lesser amount as the full, fair, and final value of the services received.’ The final accounting is now before the Court. Fixing the final allowed amount and truing up the advances against it is the ordinary business of a final accounting, not an extraordinary remedy. The Receiver is the agent of the court, not of any party (rule 3.1179(a)); the money he drew was never his to keep — it became his only to the extent, if any, the court allowed it as final compensation.” (Supp. Opp., 10:15-22.)

Opposing Parties further contend that “[h]ere the true-up is simple. Because the appointment was void, no compensation may be allowed from the estate or from the non-procuring parties (Parts IV through VII). The allowable amount is therefore zero — and the Court’s own tentative already denied approval of the final account, which is itself an allowance of zero. Every interim payment was thus an advance against an allowance that does not exist. Sullivan confirms the point from the other side: each interim fee order rested on the void appointment and is ‘itself void’ (Sullivan, supra, 145 Cal. at p. 769), and a receiver who “was not a receiver but a trespasser’ (id. at p. 768) gained no right to keep money drawn under void orders. The mechanics make the result concrete. The Receiver drew approximately $557,511 in interim advances [confirm exact figure against the accounting]. Had the Court allowed $300,000 as the final value of his services, he would have to return roughly $257,511; had it allowed $100,000, he would return roughly $457,511; and because a void appointment permits an allowance of nothing, he must return all of it.” (Supp. Opp., 10:23-11:6.)

Opposing Parties assert that “[t]he Court is not being asked to invent a disgorgement remedy; it is being asked to adjust interim advances at the final accounting, exactly as rule 3.1183(a) contemplates. Independent authority confirms the power to order the funds returned: Code of Civil Procedure section 908 authorizes restitution of all things lost by reason of a reversed order, and a trial court whose order has been reversed ‘has the inherent authority to afford restitutionary relief.’ (Beach Break Equities, LLC v. Lowell (2016) 6 Cal.App.5th 847, 852-854.) Whether the Court calls the relief an adjustment of advances, a refund, or restitution, the Receiver must return what he drew from GPG-NBC under the void appointment, with interest from the dates of payment.” (Supp. Opp., 11:11-18.)

            The Receiver asserts that Opposing Parties’ approach “mischaracterize[s] Rule of Court 3.1183” and they “cite no case law supporting disgorgement of a receiver’s fees and costs where an appointment was erroneous. Nor does any exist. As set forth above, all of the relevant authority under Sullivan, Andrade and the many cases decided pursuant to that authority, address how to pay a receiver where there has been an improvident or improper appointment – none of them discuss disgorgement of fees under such circumstances. And that is appropriate – the Receiver is a neutral with no personal interest in this matter, and to essentially punish him for the Court of Appeal’s reversal is inequitable. Defendants correctly observe that interim fees are ‘subject to final review and approval by the court,’ which ‘retains jurisdiction to award a greater or lesser amount as the full, fair, and final value of the services rendered.’ (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1183(a).) The fact that this Court has jurisdiction to review whether amounts actually paid are sufficient or not does not turn those amounts into ‘advances.’ Moreover, the account of a receiver is examined ‘in a spirit of equity,’ and the receiver is presumed honest until the contrary appears. (People v. Riverside University (1973) 35 Cal.App.3d 572, 586.) Defendants’ submission that the full, fair, and final value of eighteen (18) months of administration is zero as a matter of law is not a valuation, but simply mirrors the Sullivan rule by another name, and fails for the reasons stated in Question One.” (Supp. Mot. 7/20/26, 11:5-22, emphasis in original.)

            The Receiver also addresses Opposing Parties’ argument regarding restitution under Code of Civil Procedure section 908, asserting that the Court’s power to award restitution is “equitable and discretionary” and, “[h]ere the Receiver gained nothing as a litigant. The purpose of restitution is to restore to a party what its adversary gained through the reversed ruling, however, the Receiver is the neutral officer of the court. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1179(a).) Moreover, the Receiver’s interim payments compensated services actually rendered under orders in full force. Belcher v. Aaron (1937) 8 Cal.2d 180 confirms this principle, as the receiver there was required to account only for rents collected after his appointment had been vacated. Defendants have not cited any law that would transform Section 908 from a discretionary remedy allowing the Court to equalize the parties into a mechanism requiring a forfeiture by the court’s own officer. Moreover, the funds that ultimately paid the Receiver’s fees and costs in this case were profits from the operation of the business that otherwise would have been distributed to the shareholders.” (Supp. Mot. 7/20/26, 11:28-12:11.)

            The Receiver further asserts that “Defendants cite no case, in California or anywhere, in which a court has ordered a receiver to disgorge fees and costs paid for services rendered. The lack of caselaw is telling, because disgorgement is an equitable remedy, and equity asks a question Defendants do not answer: disgorge to whom? The Receiver’s fees arose from administrating the estate and providing services that preserved value. Returning the funds would not restore money to a wronged stranger, but would return it to the shareholders, the very parties over whom the Court has jurisdiction and against whom receivership costs may ultimately be allocated. Yet, Defendants’ theory cannot be right, because if this receivership is ‘void for all purposes,’ their refund demand would fail with it.” (Supp. Mot., 7/20/26.)

            Given that the Receiver’s appointment is void, the Court is limited in its powers to provide recourse. Opposing Parties contend that the Court may order disgorgement, which is an equitable remedy. Although now deemed a trespasser, the Receiver asserts that he is a neutral officer of the Court and equity does not demand disgorgement. However, Opposing Parties have not cited (and this Court has not found) any authority authorizing an order disgorging fees previously paid to the Receiver. Similarly to the Court’s inability to charge Tani or the other parties with the Receiver’s fees, the Court lacks jurisdiction to enforce equitable remedies. Likewise, the Court cannot grant the Receiver’s final accounting. The Court finds it is without authority to order disgorgement.

E.    The Disposition of the $150,000 the Receiver Holds

Lastly, at issue is the $150,000 that the Receiver withdrew as a reserve fee from GPG’s accounts when operations terminated. Opposing Parties assert that the Receiver “acknowledged on the record that no portion of it was earned, and that his fees cannot be charged to the corporate estate in any event… No order has ever allowed the Receiver to keep the $150,000 — the Court’s own tentative denied the final account — so it remains GPG-NBC’s identifiable property, held without authority, and it must be returned now. The suggestion that GPG-NBC cannot receive the funds because it is dissolved is wrong as a matter of law. A dissolved California corporation ‘continues to exist for the purpose of winding up its affairs, prosecuting and defending actions by or against it,’ and collecting and dividing its assets. (Corp. Code, § 2010, subd. (a).) GPG-NBC retains full capacity to receive and hold its own funds during winding up. There is no obstacle — and no further motion required — to returning the $150,000 to it.” (Supp. Opp., 11:22-12:5.) Opposing Parties cite North v. Cecil B. De Mille Productions (1934) 2 Cal.2d 55, 58, where the Supreme Court stated that “[t]he receiver acquires no title, but only the right of possession as the officer of the court. The title remains in those in whom it was vested when the appointment was made. The object of the appointment is to secure the property pending the litigation, so that it may be appropriated in accordance with the rights of the parties as they may be determined by the judgment of the action… When a receiver pendente lite is appointed, the legal title remains in the individual or corporation whose property is placed in the hands of a receiver. However, the receiver has complete dominion over the property, subject to the orders of the court; he holds it for the benefit of others; he has possession. All the title, if any, which remains in the individual or corporation is merely the formal legal title which is held by it in trust for the receiver, which title it could be compelled by the court at any time to convey to the receiver for the purposes of the trust.” (Internal quotations omitted.)

The Receiver agrees that under Corporations Code Section 2010, subdivision (a), a “dissolved corporation continues to exist for purposes of winding up and may sue and be sued in that capacity,” so, since GPG is “specially appearing” before the Court, “claims to the funds can be adjudicated with all claimants present.” (Supp. Mot. 7/20/26, 13:25-26, 14:5.) The Receiver further asserts that “as a neutral officer of the Court, [he] cannot determine competing claims to the fund and must instead hold [the $150,000] pending a court order directing its disposition. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1179(a).) Here, three sets of claims attach to this fund: first, the Receiver’s claim for the allowed costs of administration; the shareholders’ claims, which Tani, Defendants, and Kent Tran have each asserted (RT 29:3-8); and any remaining creditor claims in the winding up. These claims set up the classic posture of interpleader, and the law does not force the stakeholder to choose among claimants at his peril, but brings all claimants before one court to adjudicate entitlement… Moreover, because GPG-NBC has dissolved, any distribution must flow to those persons entitled to the corporation’s remaining assets after satisfaction of claims. The Court can therefore resolve all competing claims in its final ruling and direct distribution accordingly.” (Supp. Mot. 7/20/26, 15:3-16.)

Opposing Parties’ reply contends that “GPG-NBC retains full capacity to receive its own funds during winding up (Corp. Code, § 2010, subd. (a)) and—being dissolved, not suspended—is not disabled from appearing… The Receiver’s suggestion that Defendants previously described these funds as shareholder money does not change their character. Undistributed corporate funds remain corporate property until a distribution is declared; a shareholder’s expectancy of an eventual distribution is not title.” (Supp. Reply, 9:25-10:2.) Further, Opposing Parties assert that “[n]or does interpleader warrant continued retention. That remedy is available to a stakeholder who claims no interest in the fund; a party who asserts his own claim to the money he proposes to deposit is not disinterested, and the Receiver claims the very fund he offers to interplead. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 386, subd. (b).) Any distribution among the corporation’s owners, and any claim against a shareholder’s interest by a judgment creditor or lienholder, is a matter for the winding up; none of it justifies the Receiver’s continued retention of corporate funds.” (Supp. Reply, 10:8-13.)

As stated in North v. Cecil B. De Mille Productions, supra, 2 Cal.2d at p. 58, the property that the Receiver holds, i.e., the $150,000, is rightfully GPG’s property. Although the corporation has dissolved, it is in the process of winding up, and as Corporations Code section 2010, subdivision (a) provides, “[a] corporation which is dissolved nevertheless continues to exist for the purpose of winding up its affairs, prosecuting and defending actions by or against it and enabling it to collect and discharge obligations, dispose of and convey its property and collect and divide its assets, but not for the purpose of continuing business except so far as necessary for the winding up thereof.” Opposing Parties properly contend that, regardless of the different claims parties have made to the $150,000 in the Receiver’s possession, that money is GPG’s, and GPG will assess how it must be apportioned as part of winding up. Accordingly, the Court finds that the Receiver must return the $150,000.

F.     Suing the Receiver for Misconduct

            Finally, GPG asserts the following: “GPG and its affected innocent parties seek to also sue the Receiver for misconduct in addition to acting without jurisdiction. In the avoidance of doubt, while this request is not necessary based on prevailing undisputed law, only a validly appointed Receiver by a Court with jurisdiction may claim quasi-immunity and require parties to seek leave of Court before suing the Receiver. Ostrowski v. Miller (1964) 226 Cal.App.2d 79 [Ostrowski]. To avoid further costs and unnecessary delays, GPG and the Defendants respectfully request this Court to confirm that this Court follows the Appellate Court decision and will not require GPG and all affected parties regarding this lack of jurisdiction Receiver appointment as to this action to request leave from this Court to sue the Receiver. Simply stated, the affected parties request an order from this Court that Ostrowski authority is not required to pursue an action against Mr. Singer, his employees, and his agents. See Ostrowski v. Miller (1964) 226 Cal.App.2d 79 seeking leave of Court to sue a Receiver only applies and assumes a validly appointed Receiver by a Court that had jurisdiction when the Receiver was appointed.) This Court should expressly confirm that no leave of court is required.” (GPG Opp., 7:13-26.)

            “A receiver is a court-appointed official who can be sued only by permission of the court appointing him. [Citation.] The rule is established to protect receivers from unnecessary litigation. The law is accurately set forth in 42 California Jurisprudence 2d, Receivers, section 92, page 387, as follows: ‘The court that appointed a receiver may grant leave to sue him in an independent action, or it may deny leave and require the claimant to intervene in the receivership proceedings to assert his claim. Although in a proper case leave to sue the receiver in another court will not be denied, and under some situations the denial of leave may amount to an abuse of discretion, ordinarily the court has a wide discretion in the matter. It may not properly refuse leave to sue when it cannot afford in intervention the same relief as the applicant is entitled to in an independent action, or when, by virtue of a statute or constitutional provision, a particular kind of action must be brought in a jurisdiction other than that in which the original special proceeding is pending. But permission may be denied where full relief can be granted by intervention in the original proceeding. The more common practice, and the one generally recommended, is to hear and determine all rights of action and demands against a receiver by petition in the cause in which he was appointed.’” (Ostrowski, supra, 226 Cal.App.2d at p. 84.)

            Here, the Court agrees with the Receiver that “the request for a declaration that ‘no leave of court is required to sue the Receiver’ … is not properly before the Court on this motion and seeks an advisory opinion.” (Omnibus Reply, 12:1-2.)

            Therefore, the Opposing Parties’ request for leave not to be required to sue the Receiver is denied without prejudice. The opposing parties may raise that issue in a properly noticed motion.

 

 

Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, the Receiver’s Motion for Final Accounting is DENIED IN PART as follows: The request for approving and settling the Receiver’s final report and accounting is DENIED. The request allowing the Receiver to pay his outstanding fees and expenses is DENIED. The request for an order terminating the receivership is GRANTED. The request for an order retaining jurisdiction over the issue regarding the Opposing Parties’ permission to sue the Receiver is GRANTED. The $150,000 in the Receiver’s trust account is ordered returned to GPG. The Opposing Parties’ request for disgorgement is DENIED. The Opposing Parties’ request for an order exempting them from the requirement of obtaining permission to sue the Receiver is DENIED, without prejudice.

            Background

On May 14, 2025, Plaintiffs Eric Bixler (“Bixler”) and Richard Jaramillo (“Jaramillo”) (jointly, “Plaintiffs”) filed this action against Defendant City of Los Angeles (the “City”) alleging one cause of action for whistleblower retaliation (Lab. Code, § 1102.5).

Plaintiffs now move for an order that the City and the Los Angeles Police Department (“LAPD”) produce records. The City opposes. Plaintiffs replied.

Legal Standard

There is a special two-step procedure for securing disclosure of peace officer personnel records. ((Warrick v. Superior Court (2005) 35 Cal.4th 1011, 1019.) First, the party seeking disclosure must file a motion that identifies the peace officer, the agency in possession of the records, a description of the records, who is seeking the records, as well as the time and place of the hearing. ((Evid. Code, § 1043, subd. (b)(1).) The motion must be accompanied by a declaration showing “good cause” for disclosure of the records: setting forth the materiality of the records, and stating upon reasonable belief that the governmental agency has the requested documents. (Evid. Code, § 1043, subd. (b)(3).) This is a so-called “Pitchess” motion, derived from Pitchess v. Superior Court (1974) 11 Cal.3d 531.

The Evidence Code section 1043, subdivision (b) “good cause” declaration must be sufficiently specific “to preclude the possibility of [the movant] simply casting about for any helpful information.” ((People v. Mooc (2001) 26 Cal.4th 1216, 1226.) However, a declaration based upon information and belief and containing hearsay may be used to evidence good cause in support of a Pitchess motion. ((Haggerty v. Superior Court (2004) 117 Cal.App.4th 1079, 1086.) The moving party need show only a “plausible factual foundation” for discovery—i.e., a scenario of officer misconduct that might occur or could have occurred. (Warrick v. Superior Court, supra, at p. 1025.) All that is required is the presentation of a scenario that might have or could have occurred, i.e., a “relatively low threshold.” ((Uybungco v. Superior Court (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 1043, 1048); (see also Blumberg v. Superior Court (2011) 197 Cal.App.4th 1245, 1248 [the good cause requirement of section 1043(b) “embodies a ‘relatively low threshold’ for discovery’ [citation], under which a defendant need demonstrate only ‘a logical link between the defense proposed and the pending charge’ and describe with some specificity ‘how the discovery being sought would support such a defense’”]); (Becerrada v. Superior Court (2005) 131 Cal.App.4th 409, 413 [“A showing of good cause is measured by ‘relatively relaxed standards’ that serve to ‘insure the production’ for trial court review of ‘all potentially relevant documents.’”].)

Second, if the court finds good cause, then an in camera hearing must be held. ((Slayton v. Superior Court (2006) 146 Cal.App.4th 55, 61.) After personally examining the records in camera, the trial court shall order disclosure of peace officer personnel records that are “relevant to the subject matter involved in the pending litigation.” (Evid. Code, § 1045, subd. (a); (People v. Mooc, supra, 26 Cal.4th at p. 1226.) If disclosure is ordered, the court must also order that the disclosed information may not be used “for any purpose other than a court proceeding pursuant to applicable law.” (Evid. Code, § 1045, subd. (e).)

In terms of procedure, notice of the motion must be served in compliance with Code of Civil Procedure section 1005, subdivision (b), on the parties and on the governmental agency that has the records. (Evid. Code, § 1043, subd. (a).) The agency is required to notify the individual officer whose records are sought. (Evid. Code, § 1043, subd. (c); see also Evid. Code, § 1043, subd. (d) [“No hearing upon a motion for discovery or disclosure shall be held without full compliance with the notice provisions of this section except upon a showing by the moving party of good cause for noncompliance, or upon a waiver of the hearing by the governmental agency identified as having the records.”].)

Discussion

A.    Documents Sought

            Plaintiffs seek production of “the following records pursuant to Evidence Code §§ 1043 and 1045:

1. The complaint face sheet and the complete witness interviews in audio or transcript form, or both, for LAPD Internal Affairs investigation Complaint Form (‘CF’) No. 25- 003389 conducted in response to the instant lawsuit, Case No. 25STCV14105.”

(Notice of Mot., 2:7-11.)

B.     Good Cause

Plaintiffs explain that “[i]n this Motion, Plaintiffs seek discovery of the LAPD’s Internal Affairs investigation conducted in response to this civil Complaint, specifically the complaint face sheet and the complete witness interviews in audio or transcript form that LAPD’s Internal Affairs conducted in response to the instant lawsuit, Case No. 25STCV14105 (LAPD IA CF No. 25-003389).” (Mot., 5:11-14.) Plaintiffs assert that “there is good cause for the discovery sought because it is relevant to prove the elements of Plaintiffs’ retaliation claims.” (Mot., 14:14-16.)

In support of the motion, Plaintiffs’ counsel provides a declaration, stating, inter alia, that “Eric Bixler began his employment with the LAPD in or around 1996 and previously served as the Lieutenant II over the U.S. Marshals Task Force since in or around 2018. He has since retired from the LAPD. Plaintiff Richard Jaramillo has been employed as a sworn peace officer in the LAPD since in or around 2000.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 4.) “The Los Angeles Police Department’s U.S. Marshals Task Force … combines the resources and efforts of the United States Marshals Service with those of the LAPD to locate, arrest, and prosecute the most dangerous and violent criminals in Los Angeles,” and, “[d]ue to Task Force’s investigative and surveillance work, its members work undercover and display no police identification until they tactically deploy for an arrest.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶¶ 5, 10.) “Integral to achieving this outcome is the presence of two uniformed police officers, known as a ‘chase unit,’ on standby to effectuate the suspect’s arrest when it is safest to do so. Beginning in or around 2020, by order of the Chief of Police, LAPD Metropolitan Division (‘Metro’) ‘loaned’ police officers to the Task Force to serve as its ‘chase unit.’ The chase unit officers’ loans to the Task Force have historically been long-term in nature so that the chase unit officers are deeply familiar with the way the rest of the Task Force operates and tactically deploys in order to safely effectuate arrests.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 11.) “For these reasons, LAPD Officer Jonathan Pacheco was loaned from Metro to the Task Force to work the Task Force chase unit continuously from November 2021 until mid-2023, and LAPD Officer Derrick Boykins was loaned from Metro to the Task Force to work the same chase unit almost continuously from June 2022 until mid-2023.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 12.)

Plaintiffs’ counsel explains that “[i]n or around early 2023, Karen Bass was sworn in as Mayor of the City of Los Angeles,” and “Chief of Police Michel Moore directed supervisors in Metro to attempt to recruit Black officers specifically for the Mayor’s Detail, which is the bodyguard detail for the Mayor, and falls under Metropolitan Division. (Compl. ¶26.) Chief Moore’s attempt to recruit Black officers for the Mayor’s Detail was not requested from Mayor Bass, nor known by Mayor Bass. Instead, Moore’s recruitment of Black officers for the Detail was an attempt to pander to the Mayor.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶¶ 12-13.) “From in or around January 2023 to February 2023, Police Officer III Derrick Boykins learned that Black officers in Metro were being solicited to work the Mayor’s Detail. In addition, Officer Boykins himself was asked to work the Mayor’s Detail. Boykins is Black.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 14.) “On or about February 3, 2023, Officer Boykins spoke with his supervisors in the Task Force, Plaintiffs Bixler and Jaramillo, to report that the Department’s singling out of Black officers for recruitment to the Mayor’s Detail constituted race discrimination. Boykins was concerned that he and other Black officers in Metro would be subjected to retaliation if they declined to be assigned to the Detail,” and that same day “Officer Boykins and Plaintiff Jaramillo overheard a telephone conversation that Plaintiff Bixler had with his brother, Captain Brian Bixler (then the Captain III over Metropolitan Division), during which Captain Bixler confirmed that Metro had received a directive from above to single out Black officers for recruitment to the Detail. Captain Bixler further stated that if interviewed, he would tell the truth.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶¶ 15-16.)

Thereafter, “[o]n February 4, 2023, per Department policy, Plaintiff Jaramillo initiated a personnel complaint on Officer Boykins’ behalf stating that Boykins believed the Department’s singling out of Black officers for recruitment to the Mayor’s Detail constituted race discrimination.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 17.) “Like Plaintiff Jaramillo, Plaintiff Bixler reasonably believed that the Department’s singling out of Black officers for recruitment to the Mayor’s Detail violated Government Code section 12940, et seq., and other applicable federal, state, and local statutes and regulations.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 21.) Then, “[o]n or about February 9, 2023, Officer Boykins was interviewed by Internal Affairs regarding his personnel complaint. During his IA interview, Officer Boykins identified Chief of Police Michel Moore and Captain II Mario Mota, among others, as being responsible for what he believed to be unlawful discrimination,” and “Officer Boykins identified Plaintiff Bixler, Plaintiff Jaramillo, and Captain Brian Bixler, among others, as witnesses to his complaint.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶¶ 18-19.) “In or around March 2023, Plaintiff Jaramillo was interviewed by Internal Affairs regarding Officer Boykins’ personnel complaint and disclosed information indicating that [] the Department’s singling out of Black officers for recruitment to the Mayor’s Detail constituted race discrimination.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 22.)

Plaintiffs’ counsel attests that “[i]n or around August 2023, Captain Brian Bixler retired, and Captain Mota succeeded him as the Captain III over Metro. Immediately, Captain Mota took actions to retaliate against Boykins and Pacheco, as well as Plaintiffs. First, in the summer of 2023, Captain Mota ended Boykins’s and Pacheco’s long-term loans to the Task Force, resulting in them being returned to Metro Division.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 23.) “Thereafter, from in or around August of 2023 through in or around March of 2024, Captain Mota insisted on Metro officers working the Task Force chase unit be placed on a strict rotation. This resulted in the chase unit being made up of Metro officers with little to no experience in working with the Task Force, and little to no experience utilizing the specialized tactics the Task Force employs.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 24.) “Plaintiff Bixler and Plaintiff Jaramillo repeatedly reported that Metro was sending officers who were not tactically sound to the Task Force, resulting in officer safety issues. Captain Mota, unmoved, continued to rotate officers without the necessary training and experience to the Task Force chase unit.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 25.) “Since the chase unit officers are primarily responsible for the ultimate arrest of the dangerous fugitives the Task Force is sent after, this unreasonably jeopardized the safety of Plaintiffs, who are required to deploy with the Task Force on nearly all operations, as well as the rest of the Task Force members.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 26.) Then, in “August of 2023, Captain Mota took actions to punish Officer Boykins and Officer Pacheco for a non-disciplinary matter (i.e., not wearing their seatbelts during a five-minute vehicle pursuit of an armed robbery suspect). (Compl. ¶42.) That same month, Plaintiff Jaramillo disclosed an email to Internal Affairs indicating that Mota was misusing his position in the LAPD to take retaliatory actions against Officer Boykins and Officer Pacheco.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 27.) “Department supervisors, including Captain Mota, formed the belief that Plaintiff Bixler would similarly provide information to IA to support the conclusion that Mota was retaliating against Officers Boykins and Pacheco.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 28.)

“On December 27, 2023, Officer Boykins and Officer Pacheco each filed a Government Claim for Damages with the City of Los Angeles alleging that their long-term loans to the Task Force were ended in retaliation for Boykins’ race discrimination personnel complaint… Both Government Claims identified Plaintiff Bixler and Plaintiff Jaramillo as witnesses.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 29.) “On multiple occasions thereafter, Captain Mota and Deputy Chief David Kowalski (who is close friends with Mota) have taken multiple actions to materially and adversely impact the terms and conditions of Plaintiffs’ employment, including but not limited to: openly maligning their supervisory skills in front of other LAPD command staff; denying them and their officers resources necessary to keep them safe; and attempting to remove responsibilities from them and the Task Force.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 30.) “For example, Captain Mota and Deputy Chief Kowalski have informed other high-ranking command staff, including but not limited to Interim Chief of Police Dominic Choi, Assistant Chief Blake Chow, and Deputy Chief Michael Rimkunas, that Plaintiff Bixler and Plaintiff Jaramillo are running the Task Force ‘unsafely,’ or words to that effect; that they have no control over their officers; and that Plaintiff Jaramillo is unqualified to serve as a Detective III. These statements are patently false and have been made with the intent to derail Plaintiffs’ careers.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 31.) “As a result of the Department’s actions, Plaintiffs have been placed in unnecessary danger and have suffered damage to their reputations,” and “[t]he Department has further taken other actions to adversely and materially impact the terms and conditions of Plaintiffs’ employment.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶¶ 33-34.)

Plaintiffs assert that they demonstrate good cause because “Plaintiff’s counsel’s declaration has articulated a plausible factual scenario that Plaintiffs were subjected to retaliation by the Department after they witnessed, observed, and reported race discrimination.” (Mot., 14:18-20.) The Court finds that Plaintiffs’ counsel’s declaration demonstrates that retaliation may have occurred.

Plaintiffs next contend that the documents sought are discoverable because “[t]he requested investigation concerns the very incidents that are the subject of the civil claim.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 38.) Plaintiffs’ counsel explains that “[t]he complaint face sheet and the complete witness interviews in audio or transcript form, or both, for LAPD Internal Affairs investigation Complaint Form (‘CF’) No. 25-003389 conducted in response to the instant lawsuit, Case No. 25STCV14105, to determine whether Captain Mota, Deputy Chief Kowalski, and others retaliated against Plaintiffs after they filed this lawsuit.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 36.) “A ‘complaint face sheet’ in the LAPD is a three-page form that is the first document created whenever a personnel complaint is initiated against an LAPD employee.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 37.)

Moreover, Plaintiffs’ counsel attests that “Plaintiffs will have the burden of proving the elements of their retaliation claims at trial. Specifically, Plaintiffs will have the burden of proving that they engaged in protected activity under Labor Code § 1102.5” and “[t]he complaint face sheet and witness interviews from the investigation that Plaintiffs request through this motion plausibly contain admissible evidence that will help Plaintiffs meet this burden.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶¶ 39-40.) “Plaintiffs will also have the burden of proving at trial that their complaints and/ or testimony in support of Boykins and Pacheco motivated the adverse employment actions against them which led to this lawsuit.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 41.) “Department supervisors, including Chief Michel Moore, Deputy Chief Kowalski, and Captain Mota formed the belief that Plaintiffs Bixler and Jaramillo would support Officer Boykins’ IA complaint related to his reporting of race discrimination in violation of the FEHA and other laws, statutes, and regulations. Plaintiff Bixler had heard relevant information and Plaintiff Jaramillo heard relevant information and reported the conduct.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 42.) “Captain Mota then became Captain III of Metro and took retaliatory actions against Boykins, Pacheco, Jaramillo, and Bixler. Mota placed officers in the chase unit on a strict rotation that caused officer safety issues and unreasonably jeopardized the safety of Plaintiffs who deployed with the Task Force.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 43.) “Following Boykins and Pacheco’s Government Claims, Captain Mota and Deputy Chief David Kowalski openly maligned Plaintiffs’ supervisory skills in front of other LAPD command staff; denied them and their officers resources necessary to keep them safe; and attempted to remove responsibilities from them and the Task Force. Mota and Kowalski informed other high-ranking command staff that Plaintiffs Bixler and Jaramillo are running the Task Force ‘unsafely,’ or words to that effect; that they have no control over their officers; and that Plaintiff Jaramillo is unqualified to serve as a Detective III. These statements are patently false and have been made with the intent to derail Plaintiffs’ careers. Kowalski even refused to deploy the LAPD’s SWAT Team to assist the Task Force with apprehension of armed and dangerous felony suspects or has erected barriers to intentionally withhold SWAT’s assistance. As a result, Plaintiffs have been placed in unnecessary danger and have suffered damage to their reputations. Further, each Plaintiff’s ability to advance, promote, and/or obtain coveted positions in the future has been and/or will continue to be adversely affected.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 45.) The foregoing incidents gave rise to the instant lawsuit, and “[i]n November 2025, counsel for Plaintiffs received correspondence from Sergeant II Gretchen Skaggs requesting an interview with Lieutenant Bixler and Detective Jaramillo as part of an investigation based on the lawsuit that they filed. Sergeant Skaggs stated that the main focus of the investigation would be the retaliation reported in the lawsuit. Sergeant Skaggs provided the CF number of the investigation. The LAPD conducted this investigation to determine whether Mota, Kowalski, and others took any retaliatory actions against Plaintiffs in response to their protected activity.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 47.)

“The relevancy of the complaint face sheet and witness interviews from this investigation is self-evident,” and “the witness statements in the investigation file are important for cross-examination and impeachment purposes.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶¶ 48-49.) Plaintiffs’ counsel attests that “[u]pon information and belief, LAPD interviewed Mota, Kowalski, and other employees as part of these investigations[,] Plaintiffs are thus entitled to the interviews and face sheets to impeach testimony and/or refresh recollections.”  (O’Beirne Decl., ¶¶ 50-51.)

Plaintiffs discuss Riske v. Superior Court (2016) 6 Cal.App.5th 647, stating that “[t]he Riske decision provides a model for Pitchess discovery in civil employment cases.” (Mot., 14:4.) In Riske, the plaintiff “sued the City of Los Angeles (City) alleging the Los Angeles Police Department (Department) had retaliated against him for protected whistleblower activity by failing to assign or promote him to several positions, selecting instead less qualified candidates,” and filed a discovery motion under Evidence Code sections 1043 and 1045. (Id. at p. 651.) The City asserted that the plaintiff “failed to show good cause because he provided no specific evidence in his supporting affidavit” and argued the plaintiff was engaged in a “fishing expedition.” (Id. at p. 663.) Pertinently, the Court of Appeal reasoned that “[g]ood cause in the initial phase of a discovery request for officer personnel records requires a plausible factual showing of materiality. Riske made that showing by articulating his whistleblower activity, a history of being maligned by other officers for that activity and his substantial qualifications for each of the 14 positions for which he applied… Further particularly is not required.” (Ibid.) The Court “requir[ed] the City to produce the reports sought by Riske for an in camera inspection pursuant to Evidence Code section 1045 and to thereafter order production of all discoverable information.” (Id. at p. 652.) Plaintiffs assert that Riske demonstrates that “as long as Plaintiffs here set forth a plausible factual foundation for retaliation relating to the requested documents, this Court should grant Plaintiff’s Motion and proceed to an in camera review.” (Mot., 14:10-11.)

Plaintiffs also cite Haggerty v. Superior Court (2004) 117 Cal.App.4th 1079 in support of their request. In Haggerty, the court found that the trial “court did not abuse its discretion in refusing to limit the discovery to the names, addresses and telephone numbers of the witnesses identified in the Internal Affairs report.” (Id. at p. 1091.) The court stated that “the requested discovery was important, not only for determining the events that occurred during the incident, but also for [plaintiff’s] counsel to prepare effective cross-examination of defense witnesses, including to impeach witnesses whose testimony at trial differs from statements made to the investigating officers and/or to refresh the recollections of these witnesses.” (Id. at p. 1089.) The Court of Appeal expressed that there is “a reasonable basis to conclude the internal investigation file of the incident at issue in the lawsuit could contain information that was material to the lawsuit. (See Robinson v. Superior Court (1978) 76 Cal.App.3d 968, 977, 143 Cal.Rptr. 328 [noting that the relevancy of an investigation of the incident that is the basis for the lawsuit is ‘self-evident’].).” (Id. at p. 1087.)

Plaintiffs contend that “[h]ere, as in Haggerty, the requested investigation concerns the very incidents that are the subject of this civil claim. (O’Beirne Decl. ¶ 38.) As in Haggerty, Plaintiffs will have the burden of proving the elements of their retaliation claims at trial. (O’Beirne Decl. ¶39.) Specifically, Plaintiffs will first have the burden of proving at trial that they engaged in a protected activity under Labor Code § 1102.5. (O’Beirne Decl. ¶ 31.) The complaint face sheet and witness interviews from the first investigation that Plaintiffs request through this motion plausibly contain admissible evidence that will help Plaintiffs meet this burden. (O’Beirne Decl. ¶40.).” (Mot., 16:17-22, emphasis omitted.)

Plaintiffs further contend that they “allege that after engaging in the protected activity above, Mota and Kowalski took retaliatory actions against them, which led to this lawsuit. (O’Beirne Decl. ¶¶ 42-45.) The LAPD then conducted an internal investigation to determine whether Mota, Kowalski, and others took any retaliatory actions against Plaintiffs. (O’Beirne Decl. ¶47.) In other words, this internal investigation examined the incidents that are the basis for this lawsuit. (Id.) According to Haggerty, the relevancy of the complaint face sheet and witness interviews from this second investigation is self-evident.” (Mot., 16:24-17:3, emphasis omitted.)

In opposition, the City contends that “Plaintiffs do not articulate good cause for the production of the Internal Affairs (‘IA’) investigation concerning Plaintiffs’ lawsuit—an investigation conducted 1 – 2 years after the events at issue in this case. As made clear in Plaintiffs’ Complaint, the events of this lawsuit occurred between July 2023 and May 2024. The IA investigation, by contrast, would have been conducted sometime after May 13, 2025, upon receipt of Plaintiffs’ lawsuit. Plaintiffs’ Motion alleges that Captain Mario Mota was the ‘bad actor’ who engaged in the alleged unlawful conduct toward them, and neither their pleading nor their Motion indicate that Mota played any role in the LAPD Internal Affairs investigation Complaint (‘CF’) No. 25-003389, conducted in response to the instant lawsuit, Case No. 25STCV14105. Moreover, of paramount importance, Plaintiffs do not claim that they suffered any adverse employment action as a result of the investigation into his lawsuit.” (Opp., 3:11-20.) The City asserts that “Plaintiffs sole cause of action is for whistleblower retaliation under Labor Code §1102.5. They do not have a cause of action for failure to prevent retaliation or discrimination under Govt. Code section 12940, therefore the adequacy or inadequacy of the investigation IA conducted after it received his lawsuit is simply not at issue here.” (Opp., 4:3-6.) The City contends that “[a]s discussed further below, Plaintiffs do not allege that they suffered any adverse employment action as a result of the IA investigation into their lawsuit. The IA Investigation—its scope, analysis, conclusions, and methodology—is not relevant to Plaintiffs’ single causes of action here, and Plaintiffs do not offer any theory under which these documents would be admissible.” (Opp., 4:9-12.)

The City also “opposes wholesale production of third-party sworn officers’ personnel information and work history, pursuant to their privacy rights.” (Opp., 5:3-4.) The City contends that “the information disclosed during the CF investigation at issue were likely provided under compulsion, with the potential penalty of administrative discipline that could result in discharge, or removal from employment with LAPD. Where third party peace and/or custodial officers provide statements under such potential disciplinary consequences, it is of the upmost importance that their privacy rights are protected. Therefore, to the extent that the Court finds that Plaintiffs have not demonstrated any ‘plausible factual foundation’ for how third-party personnel records (particularly as it relates to their work history and disciplinary history that may be contained within the CFs) are material to the subject matter of the pending litigation, Defendant requests an order of non-disclosure.” (Opp., 5:13-20.) Further, the City “opposes wholesale production of the investigation materials in question absent redaction of third-party birthdates, personal telephone numbers, social security numbers, and the like, pursuant to Art. I § 1 of the California Constitution, which entails the right to privacy. Defendant requests that if the Court is inclined to grant the Pitchess Motion, that the order of the Court explicitly permit the City to redact the foregoing private information.” (Opp., 5:21-25.)

Next, the City contends that Plaintiffs are not entitled to information contained in the requested complaint files relating to the identities of other accused officers. The City cites Penal Code section 832.7, subdivisions (b)(1)(E)(5)-(7), which governs the disclosure of law enforcement personnel records. The City asserts that “information relating to the identity, personal data and information, allegations of misconduct, findings, and adjudication of allegations against other officers accused of misconduct in the requested complaint files should be redacted.” (Opp., 6:18-20.) The City contends that “information contained in witness interviews conducted in the requested complaint files pertaining to the identity, personal data and information, allegations of misconduct, findings, and adjudication of allegations against other officers accused of misconduct in these investigations should also be redacted. Witness interviews for such investigations are kept in audio format only. In order to redact information from such witness interviews, the audio interviews must first be transcribed.” (Opp., 6:26-7:3.)

In response, Plaintiffs contend that they “need not make a good cause showing for the ‘scope, analysis, conclusions, and methodology’ of the internal investigation as they are not seeking to undermine those factors. Plaintiffs only seek the statements made by personnel in response to the complaint face sheet generated by their civil Complaint.” (Reply, 4:18-21.)

Plaintiffs further assert that the City’s “Defendant’s assertion that the IA investigation has ‘nothing to do with what Plaintiffs allege in their lawsuit’ is simply false. (Opp. 4:13-14.) Indeed, the investigation was done in direct response to Plaintiff’s lawsuit. In the lawsuit, Plaintiffs enumerate how Captain Mota and others retaliated against them in response to their support for two other officers (Derrick Boykins and Jonathan Pacheco, who are the plaintiffs in the related lawsuit) who had reported race discrimination and retaliation. The lawsuit describes specific acts of retaliation, as discussed in the Complaint and Motion. Presumably, upon receiving Plaintiffs’ lawsuit, the internal investigators examined whether the specific acts alleged by Plaintiffs occurred by asking Captain Mota and others about them. (O’Beirne Decl. ¶13.) The investigation has everything to do with what Plaintiffs allege in the lawsuit.” (Reply, 4:22-5:2.)

Moreover, Plaintiffs contend that the “Plaintiffs are not trying to show that they suffered any adverse employment actions as a result of the investigation into what they alleged in their lawsuit. (Opp. 3: 19-20.) Rather, they are trying to show that they suffered adverse employment actions that led to the investigation into what their lawsuit alleged. (O’Beirne Decl. ¶8.) The internal investigation examined whether they suffered adverse employment actions, i.e. what led to the investigation. (O’Beirne Decl. ¶9.) This explains why the investigators questioned Mota and others about the substance of the lawsuit. (Id.) The answers Mota and others gave to investigators—which is what is sought here—would assist Plaintiffs in proving the elements of their claims, i.e. retaliation, and the attendant adverse employment actions.” (Reply, 3:26-5.)

As for privacy interests, Plaintiffs contend that they “do not seek, nor do Plaintiffs oppose, the redaction of ‘third-party birthdates, personal telephone numbers, social security numbers, and the like’ identified in Defendant’s Section C.” (Reply, 5:11-13.) Plaintiffs also “do not oppose the redaction of other officers’ information in accordance with Penal Code section 832.7, provided the LAPD releases factual information and statements if relevant to a finding against an officer subject to release of information.” (Reply, 5:15-18.)

The Court finds that Plaintiffs demonstrate good cause for the documents requested in this motion. Plaintiffs demonstrate that the documents sought are material to their case because the investigation arose from the allegations in the Complaint or in response to the lawsuit. Plaintiffs also do not appear to seek confidential information pertaining to the third parties, such as their social security numbers. Regardless, Plaintiffs’ reply represents that they do not oppose redactions for privacy concerns or redactions in accordance with Penal Code section 832.7. Further, the Court notes that a finding of good cause is just the first step; so, to the extent that information should be redacted or deemed confidential, the Court will make these determinations after the in camera hearing.

As set forth above, if the court finds good cause, then an in camera hearing must be held. ((Slayton v. Superior Court, supra, 146 Cal.App.4th at p. 61.) After personally examining the records in camera, the trial court shall order disclosure of peace officer personnel records that are “relevant to the subject matter involved in the pending litigation.” (Evid. Code, § 1045, subd. (a); (People v. Mooc, supra, 26 Cal.4th at p. 1226.) If disclosure is ordered, the court must also order that the disclosed information may not be used “for any purpose other than a court proceeding pursuant to applicable law.” (Evid. Code, § 1045, subd. (e); (see Alford v. Superior Court (2003) 29 Cal.4th 1033, 1039-1040 [overruled on other grounds].)

Background

Plaintiff Eric M Ramirez (“Plaintiff”) filed this lemon law action on June 27, 2025, against Defendant General Motors, LLC (“GM”), alleging five causes of action (1) violation of subdivision (d) of Civil Code section 1793.2; (2) violation of subdivision (b) of Civil Code section 1793.2; (3) violation of subdivision (a)(3) of Civil Code section 1793.2; (4) breach of the implied warranty of merchantability; and (5) fraudulent inducement - concealment.

GM now demurs to the Complaint in its entirety. Plaintiff opposes. GM replied.

Request for Judicial Notice

The Court grants GM’s request for judicial notice of Exhibit A.

Legal Standard

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda¿(2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) In testing the sufficiency of the complaint, the court assumes the truth of properly pleaded factual allegations, facts reasonably inferred from those expressly pleaded, and judicially noticed matters. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) “A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the¿evidence or other extrinsic matters.¿Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed.” (SKF Farms v. Superior Court¿(1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 902, 905.) Accordingly, “[w]hether the plaintiff will be able to prove the pleaded facts is irrelevant to ruling upon the demurrer.” (Stevens v. Superior Court (1986) 180 Cal.App.3d 605, 609-10.)

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10 subdivisions (e) and (f), a demurrer may be filed if the pleading is uncertain or does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. For purposes of ruling on a demurrer, all facts pleaded in a complaint are assumed to be true, but the reviewing court does not assume the truth of conclusions of law. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 967.)

Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment, otherwise, it is abuse of discretion. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349.)

Discussion

A.    Allegations of the Complaint

In the Complaint, Plaintiff alleges that on “June 5, 2019, Plaintiff entered into a warranty contract with Defendant GM regarding a 2019 GMC Sierra 1500, … which was manufactured and or distributed by Defendant GM.” (Compl., ¶ 6.) Plaintiff alleges the vehicle came with “various warranties, including but not limited to the bumper-bumper warranty, powertrain warranty, emission warranty, etc.” (Id., ¶ 7; see id., Ex. A, p. 2 [“Coverage is for the first 3 years or 36,000 miles, whichever comes first.”.)

Plaintiff further alleges that “[d]efects nonconformities to warranty manifested themselves within the applicable express warranty period, including but not limited to transmission defects, engine defects, electrical defects; among other defects and non-conformities,” which rendered the value of the vehicle “worthless and /or de minimis.” (Compl., ¶¶ 11, 13.) Plaintiff alleges that “[u]nder the Song-Beverly Act, Defendant GM had an affirmative duty to promptly offer to repurchase or replace the Subject Vehicle at the time if failed to conform the Subject Vehicle to the terms of the express warranty after a reasonable number of repair attempts,” but “GM has failed to either promptly replace the Subject Vehicle or to promptly make restitution in accordance with the Song-Beverly Act.” (Id., ¶¶ 14-15.)

B.    First and Second Causes of Action for Express Warranty: Statute of Repose and Statute of Limitations

GM contends that Code of Civil Procedure section 871.21, effective on January 1, 2025, bars Plaintiff’s first three causes of action[1] because the Song-Beverly Act now prescribes a six-year statute of repose and a shortened statute of limitations.

Assembly Bill 1755, effective for cases filed after January 1, 2025, provides that the statute of limitations for Song-Beverly Act actions is “one year after the expiration of the applicable express warranty.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.21, subd. (a).) However, “[n]otwithstanding subdivision (a), an action covered by Section 871.20 shall not be brought later than six years after the date of original delivery of the motor vehicle.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.21, subd. (b).) This statute of repose imposes a six-year deadline from the date of delivery to the initiation of the lawsuit, irrespective of when the breach occurred or the length of the warranty. (See Inco Development Corp. v. Superior Court (2005) 131 Cal.App.4th 1014, 1020 [“A statute of repose has nothing to do with the date of injury, but bars all suits after the expiration of a specified time from the manufacture or delivery of a product or a transaction.”].) The statute must be opted into, but if a manufacturer does, it applies to “restitution or replacement of a motor vehicle pursuant to subdivision (b) or (d) of Section 1793.2, Section 1793.22, or Section 1794 of the Civil Code.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.20, subd. (a).)

The new code section also provides for tolling of both the statute of limitations and statute of repose in three instances: “(1) As provided by tolling requirements prescribed in subdivision (c) of Section 1793.22 of the Civil Code, as applicable. [¶] (2) For the time the motor vehicle is out of service by reason of repair for any nonconformity. [¶] (3) For the time period after a pre-suit notice is provided to the manufacturer in accordance with Section 871.24, which time period shall not exceed 60 days.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.21, subd. (c).)

Plaintiff opposes GM’s contention, asserting that the statute cannot apply retroactively. Plaintiff cites Rosefield Packing Co. v. Superior Court (1935) 4 Cal.2d 120, 122-123, where our Supreme Court specified that “[i]n accordance with this principle it has been specifically held that the legislature may shorten or extend the period of the statute of limitations, or similar time statutes relating to procedure, and that the changed period may be made applicable to pending proceedings. [Citations.] There is, of course, one important qualification to the rule; where the change in remedy, as, for example, the shortening of a time limit provision, is made retroactive, there must be a reasonable time permitted for the party affected to avail himself of his remedy before the statute takes effect. If the statute operates immediately to cut off the existing remedy, or within so short a time as to give the party no reasonable opportunity to exercise his remedy, then the retroactive application of it is unconstitutional as to such party.” Plaintiff asserts that “Defendant seeks to have this Court apply Code of Civil Procedure section 871.21 retroactively, changing the amount of time for Plaintiff and suddenly leaving Plaintiff with an unreasonably short period of time in which to file suit.” (Opp., 5:4-6.) However, Plaintiff’s contention that applying the statute in this way “violate[s] fundamental constitutional principles of due process” is without merit. (Opp., 5:11.)

“In California, statutes of limitations, being procedural, are normally retroactively applied to accrued causes of action; but the court must inquire whether, in a given case, that retrospective application may violate due process by in effect eliminating the plaintiff’s right. If the time left to file suit is reasonable, no such constitutional violation occurs, and the statute is applied as enacted. If no time is left, or only an unreasonably short time remains, then the statute cannot be applied at all.” (Aronson v. Superior Ct. (1987) 191 Cal.App.3d 294, 297.)

AB 1755 passed in 2024 and was approved by Governor Gavin Newsom and filed with the Secretary of State on September 29, 2024. (Cal. Leg. Information, Code Civ. Proc, § 871.20.) Moreover, a public statement about the signing was released (https://www.gov.ca.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/AB-1755-SIGNING-Message.pdf). This provided Plaintiff with just over three months to file his lemon law case before the statute went into effect, but he did not. Even after the statute went into effect, Plaintiff had six months to file this action, but Plaintiff still did not file in time. Moreover, Plaintiff purchased his vehicle in 2019 and asserts that “[d]efects and nonconformities to warranty manifested themselves within the [three-year] express warranty period,” Plaintiff had ample opportunity to file his Complaint prior to when section 871.20 took effect. (Compl., ¶ 11; Ex. A, p. 2.)

Next, Plaintiff asserts that GM cannot invoke section 871.21’s statutes of repose or limitations because it is contingent on whether GM opted in under the new procedures in section 871.29, which Plaintiff contends did “not become fully effective until July 1, 2025.” (Opp., 6:8.) The statute states that “[w]ithin 30 days of the effective date of the act adding this section, a manufacturer may elect to be governed by this chapter for all actions described in subdivision (a) of Section 871.20 with respect to all of its motor vehicles sold in the year 2025 and in all prior years.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.30, subd. (a).) The statute requires a manufacturer to opt in under Code of Civil Procedure section 871.29 for section 871.21 to apply. This text became effective April 2, 2025, and GM opted in on April 23, 2025. (RJN Ex. A; see also https://www.dca.ca.gov/ acp/accepted_manufacturers.shtml.) Although the text’s effective date is after Plaintiff filed his complaint on June 27, 2025, section 871.30 of the new statutory scheme specifically addresses this issue.

Code of Civil Procedure section 871.30, subdivision (c) provides that if a manufacturer opts in on or before May 1, 2025, the statute applies to all actions filed on or after January 1, 2025. As GM notes in its reply, “[n]ot only did the amendments not change the statute’s effective date, but the amended provisions also make clear that Section 871.21 and each of the other sections apply to all cases filed after January 1, 2025. Specifically, Section 871.30 provides that if a manufacturer opts-in on or before May 1, 2025, the statute applies to all actions filed on or after January 1, 2025.” (Reply, 2:17-20; see Code Civ. Proc., § 871.30, subd. (c) [“Unless a manufacturer has made the election described in subdivision (a), Sections 871.20 to 871.28, inclusive, shall not apply to an action described in subdivision (a) of Section 871.20, including actions already filed between January 1, 2025 and the effective date of the act adding this section, with respect to all of its vehicles sold new in the year 2025 and in all prior years.”].) Because Plaintiff filed his complaint after January 1, 2025, and GM opted into the statute, his first and second causes of action are barred by the time limits expressed by the statute of repose and limitations.

Plaintiff also contends that section 871.21’s time limits may be tolled by equitable tolling doctrines. However, section 871.21 only allows tolling in three circumstances, as noted above, and “Plaintiff does not specifically allege that any of these three tolling circumstances are satisfied here.” (Reply, 6:25-26.) Furthermore, because section 871.21 is also a statute of repose, Plaintiff’s suit is barred after the 6-year date regardless of whether Plaintiff alleges facts that might otherwise extend his time to file under the delayed discovery rule.

Accordingly, the Court sustains GM’s demurrer as to Plaintiff’s first and second causes of action without leave to amend.

C.    Third Cause of Action for Violation of Civil Code Section 1793.2, Subdivision (a)(3)

GM demurs to Plaintiff’s third cause of action for the same points asserted in the preceding section. However, in Plaintiff’s opposition, Plaintiff correctly points out that “Section 871.21, by its plain terms, does not apply to inadequate literature/parts claims under Civil Code section 1793.2(a)(3).” (Opp., 5:16-18.)

For the statute of limitations and repose to apply, the action must fall under Code of Civil Procedure section 871.20, subdivision (a); however, this statute leaves out Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (a)(3). (See Code Civ. Proc., § 871.20, subd. (a) [“Notwithstanding any other law, this chapter applies to an action, brought against a manufacturer who has elected under Section 871.29 to proceed under this chapter, seeking restitution or replacement of a motor vehicle pursuant to subdivision (b) or (d) of Section 1793.2 Section 1793.22, or Section 1794 of the Civil Code, or for civil penalties pursuant to subdivision (c) of Section 1794 of the Civil Code, where the request for restitution or replacement is based on noncompliance with the applicable express warranty.”].) Plaintiff’s third cause of action arises out of GM’s alleged failure to provide “sufficient service literature and replacement parts to effect repairs during the express warranty period.” (Compl., ¶ 38.) Accordingly, Plaintiff contends that his “inadequate literature/parts claim under section 1793.2(a)(3) does not arise under any of the sections enumerated in section 871.20(a). Accordingly, they are not subject to section 871.21’s statutes of limitations.” (Opp., 6:1-3.)

GM does not address this argument in its reply. However, the Court notes two things. First, Plaintiff does not demonstrate that this cause of action is not time-barred, because the Court cannot ascertain when GM failed to comply with the statute. The assertion that it occurred “during the express warranty period” does not demonstrate that this cause of action is not barred by the statute of limitations or that equitable tolling doctrines apply. Second, the cause of action alleges that “Defendant GM's failure to comply with its obligations under Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (a)(3) was willful, in that Defendant GM knew of its obligation to provide literature and replacement parts sufficient to allow its repair facilities to effect repairs during the warranty period, yet Defendant GM failed to take any action to correct its failure to comply with the law. Accordingly, Plaintiff is entitled to a civil penalty of two times Plaintiff’s actual damages, pursuant to Civil Code section 1794(c).” (Compl., ¶ 39.) However, Code of Civil Procedure section 871.20 does apply to “civil penalties pursuant to subdivision (c) of Section 1794 of the Civil Code, where the request for restitution or replacement is based on noncompliance with the applicable express warranty.” Although the cause of action is for Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (a)(3), the relief Plaintiff seeks falls within the statute of limitations and repose. Accordingly, the Court sustains the demurrer with leave to amend because Plaintiff may be able to resolve these issues on amendment.

D.    Fourth Cause of Action for Breach of Implied Warranty: Statute of Limitations

GM contends that Plaintiff’s implied warranty cause of action is time-barred.

Implied warranty causes of action are subject to a four-year statute of limitations. (¿¿Cal. U. Com. Code, § 2725, subd. (1)¿¿ [“An action for breach of any contract for sale must be commenced within four years after the cause of action has accrued.¿”].) “¿A cause of action accrues when the breach occurs, regardless of the aggrieved party’s lack of knowledge of the breach. A breach of warranty occurs when tender of delivery is made, except that where a warranty explicitly extends to future performance of the goods and discovery of the breach must await the time of such performance the cause of action accrues when the breach is or should have been discovered.¿” (¿¿Cal. U. Com. Code, § 2725, subd. (2)¿¿.) Additionally, “[t]he duration of the implied warranty of merchantability and where present the implied warranty of fitness shall be coextensive in duration with an express warranty which accompanies the consumer goods, provided the duration of the express warranty is reasonable; but in no event shall such implied warranty have a duration of less than 60 days nor more than one year following the sale of new consumer goods to a retail buyer.” (Civ. Code, § 179.1, subd. (c).)

GM asserts that because a breach of implied warranty cause of action accrues from the time the purchase was made, and Plaintiff purchased the vehicle on June 5, 2019, “Plaintiff’s breach of implied warranty cause of action accrued on that date and expired four years later on June 5, 2023. Plaintiff did not file suit; however, until June 27, 2025, two years after the statute expired.” (Dem., 13:21-23.)

Plaintiff asserts that the implied warranty cause of action is not time-barred, and Plaintiff cites to ¿Mexia v. Rinker Boat Co., Inc. (2009) 174 Cal.App.4th 1297, 1304¿, where the Court of Appeal articulated that “¿[t]he implied warranty of merchantability may be breached by a latent¿defect undiscoverable at the time of sale.¿” The Mexia Court noted that “¿[i]n the case of a latent defect, a product is rendered unmerchantable, and the¿warranty of merchantability is breached, by the existence of the unseen defect, not by its subsequent discovery.¿” (¿Id. at p. 1305.) Plaintiff states that he “had no way of knowing about Defendant’s wrongdoing until the defect manifested itself and Defendant was unable to repair it after a reasonable number of repair attempts.” (Opp., 12:11-13.)

In response, GM contends that courts have consistently held that implied warranties do not extend to future performance of the goods; however, to support this contention, GM cites Cardinal Health 301, Inc. v. Tyco Elecs. Corp. (2008) 169 Cal.App.4th 116, an implied warranty case that does not involve the Song-Beverly Act. Thus, Mexia, a Song-Beverly Act case, is the applicable precedent here.

In Plaintiff’s Complaint, he alleges that he “discovered Defendant’s wrongful conduct alleged herein shortly before the filing of the complaint, as the Vehicle continued to exhibit symptoms of defects following GM’s unsuccessful attempts to repair them.” (Compl., ¶ 24.) Plaintiff also alleges that “[a]t the time of sale, the subject vehicle was sold with one or more latent defect(s).” (Compl., ¶ 43.) Accordingly, Plaintiff alleges that he did not discover the defects until recently and that the vehicle’s defects existed during the applicable warranty period. Thus, Plaintiff meets the pleading standard on demurrer, and the Court overrules GM’s demurrer as to the fourth cause of action.

E.    Fifth Cause of Action for Fraudulent Inducement - Concealment: Statute of Limitations

            GM demurs to the Complaint on the grounds that Plaintiff’s fraudulent inducement - concealment cause of action is barred by the statute of limitations.

Pursuant to ¿Code of Civil Procedure section 338, subdivision (d)¿, there is a three-year statute of limitations for “¿[a]n action for relief on the ground of fraud or mistake.¿” “¿The cause of action in that case is not deemed to have accrued until the discovery, by the aggrieved party, of the facts constituting the fraud or mistake.¿” (¿Ibid.¿)

GM asserts that “[t]o assert a fraud claim based upon alleged Fraudulent Inducement-Concealment, Plaintiff had to file a claim no later than June 5, 2022. Plaintiff did not. Plaintiff did not file the Complaint until June 27, 2025 — three years too late.” (Dem., 14:2-4.) GM also asserts that “Plaintiff cannot invoke the delayed discovery rule.” (Dem., 14:5.)

Plaintiff counters that “these facts do not establish that Plaintiff’s fraudulent inducement-concealment claim regarding the Transmission Defect must run from the date of sale. Under Code of Civil Procedure section 338, subdivision (d), the cause of action only accrues once a plaintiff discovers the facts constituting the fraud. The fact that the alleged fraud by omission occurred on the date of sale does not establish that Plaintiff discovered facts constituting fraud on that date.” (Opp., 12:25-13:2.) Plaintiff correctly contends that the statute of limitations for fraud does not begin to run until the discovery of the alleged fraud. However, a plaintiff must allege sufficient facts to support the invocation of the discovery rule. “¿The discovery-related facts should be pleaded in detail to allow the court to determine whether the fraud should have been discovered sooner.¿” (Cansino v. Bank of America (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 1462, 1472; see also CAMSI IV v. Hunter Technology Corp. (1991) 230 Cal.App.3d 1525, 1536-1537 [“¿A plaintiff whose complaint shows on its face that his or her claim would be barred by the applicable orthodox statute of limitations, and who intends to rely on the discovery rule to toll the orthodox limitation period, ‘must specifically plead facts which show (1) the time and manner of discovery and (2) the inability to have made earlier discovery despite reasonable diligence. [Citations.] Mere conclusory assertions that delay in discovery was reasonable are insufficient and will not enable the complaint to withstand general demurrer. [Citation.]’¿”].) 

The Court notes that Plaintiff alleges he “discovered Defendant’s wrongful conduct alleged herein shortly before the filing of the complaint, as the Vehicle continued to exhibit symptoms of defects following GM's unsuccessful attempts to repair them.” (Compl., ¶ 24.) Plaintiff also pleads that he “could not reasonably have been expected to learn of or discover the Vehicle's Transmission Defect and its potential consequences until well after Plaintiff purchased the Vehicle.” (Compl., ¶ 53c.) Although Plaintiff pleads why he was unable to make the discovery earlier, he does not adequately detail the time and manner of discovery. Plaintiff’s allegation that he made the discovery shortly before filing the Complaint is conclusory and does not meet the delayed discovery pleading standard. Plaintiff alleges when the defect generally manifested and how he generally became aware of the defect, but this lacks the proper specificity. Thus, the Court finds that Plaintiff failed to plead sufficient facts to show that he is entitled to the benefit of tolling under the discovery rule. Based on the foregoing, the Court sustains GM’s demurrer to the fifth cause of action, with leave to amend.

In light of the ruling on the statute of limitations issue, the Court need not address the other issues raised by GM in the demurrer.

Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, GM’s demurrer to the first and second causes of action is SUSTAINED without leave to amend. GM’s demurrer to the third and fifth causes of action is SUSTAINED with leave to amend. GM’s demurrer to the fourth cause of action is OVERRULED.
