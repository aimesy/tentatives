Legal Standard

“The purpose of the law of summary
judgment is to provide courts with a mechanism to cut through the parties’
pleadings in order to determine whether, despite their allegations, trial is in
fact necessary to resolve their dispute.” (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co.
(2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision
(c), “requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence
submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and
uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable
issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment
as a matter of law.” (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7
Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.) “The function of the pleadings in a motion for summary
judgment is to delimit the scope of the issues; the function of the affidavits
or declarations is to disclose whether there is any triable issue of fact
within the issues delimited by the pleadings.” (Juge v. County of Sacramento
(1993) 12 Cal.App.4th 59, 67, citing FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima
(1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 367, 381-382.)

As to each cause of action as framed by
the complaint, a defendant moving for summary judgment or summary adjudication
must satisfy the initial burden of proof by presenting facts to show “that one
or more elements of the cause of action ... cannot be established, or that
there is a complete defense to the cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c,
subd. (p)(2); see also Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at pp. 850-851;
Scalf v. D. B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1520.) Once
the defendant has met that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to show
that a “triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of
action or a defense thereto.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2); see also Aguilar,
supra, 25 Cal.4th at pp. 850-851.)

A plaintiff or cross-complainant moving
for summary judgment or summary adjudication must satisfy the initial burden of
proof by presenting facts to prove “each element of the cause of action
entitling the party to judgment and must also show “that there is no defense to
[the] cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(1).) Once the
plaintiff or cross-complainant has met that burden, the burden shift to the
defendant or cross-defendant to show that a “triable issue of one or more
material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.” (Ibid.)

The party opposing a motion for summary
judgment or summary adjudication may not simply “rely upon the allegations or
denials of its pleadings” but must instead “set forth the specific facts
showing that a triable issue of material fact exists.” (Code Civ. Proc., §
437c, subds. (p)(1) & (p)(2). To establish a triable issue of material
fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive
evidence. (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

Courts
“liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary
judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.” (Dore
v. Arnold Worldwide, Inc. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 384, 389.)

On this record, however, it

& Exh. 23.)

Case Number: 25STCV09460    Hearing Date: September 17, 2026    Dept: 514

Houston Specialty Insurance Company v. Tressler, LLP
25STCV09460
Plaintiff’s Motion to Quash Subpoena for Production of Records to Non-Party Wood Smith Henning & Bennett, LLP

Tentative Ruling

The motion to quash the subpoena is granted.

On March 12, 2026, Defendants issued a subpoena for the production of business records to Non-Party Wood Smith Henning & Bennett, LLP (“WSHB”).  In the subpoena, Defendants seek all, or substantially all, of the WSHB’s file in connection with the firm’s representation of Plaintiff’s insured Shu Luu Hoang.

 

Ms. Hoang was previously represented by Defendants.  As Ms. Hoang (and Plaintiff) assert that Defendants committed legal malpractice in their representation of Ms. Hoang, Evidence Code section 958 likely applies to attorney-client communications between Ms. Hoang and Defendants.  But that does not mean that Ms. Hoang has waived her right to assert the attorney-client privilege with regard to communications with her current attorneys, WSHB, whom she retained after the termination of her attorney-client relationship with Defendants.

 

The extremely broad subpoena issued by Defendants on March 12 extends to matters plainly covered by the attorney-client privilege and also likely the work product doctrine.  It is so extensive, and covers so much protected material, that the Court exercises its discretion to quash the subpoena. 

 

On that basis, the subpoena is quashed. 

 

This ruling does not preclude Defendants from issuing a more narrowly tailored subpoena to WSHB seeking information not protected from disclosure. 

 

Moving party is ordered to give notice.

 


Case Number: 25STCV31940    Hearing Date: September 17, 2026    Dept: 514

Gibbs v. United Parcel Service, Inc.
25STCV31940
Demurrer of Defendant Marquez Mitchell

Tentative Ruling

The demurrer is overruled.

Background 

This case arises out of an employment relationship.

On November 3, 2025, Brittney Gibbs (“Plaintiff”) filed the complaint in this action.

On January 20, 2026, Plaintiff filed a First Amended Complaint (the “FAC”) against United Parcel Service, Inc. (“UPS”), Marquez Mitchell, Jim Perez, and Does 1 through 25, asserting causes of action for (1) gender/pregnancy discrimination in violation of the California Fair Employment and Housing Act (the “FEHA”); (2) retaliation in violation of the FEHA; (3) failure to engage in the interactive process in violation of the FEHA; (4) failure to accommodate in violation of the FEHA; (5) failure to prevent discrimination, harassment and retaliation in violation of the FEHA; (6) retaliation in violation of Labor Code section 1102.5; (7) harassment in violation of the FEHA; (8) failure to produce personnel file under Labor Code section 1198.5; and (9) intentional infliction of emotional distress.

On May 12, 2026, the Court sustained UPS’s demurrer to the Eighth and Ninth Causes of Action.

On May 26, 2026, UPS filed an answer.

On June 3, 2026, the Court, at the request of Plaintiff, dismissed Defendant Jim Perez.

On June 4, 2026, Marquez Mitchell (“Defendant” or “Mitchell”) filed this demurrer to Plaintiff’s FAC.  Plaintiff filed an opposition on August 4, and Defendant filed a reply on August 11.

On August 18, 2026, the Court continued the hearing and ordered the parties to meet and confer as required by statute.

On September 9, 2026, Defendant filed the supplemental declaration of counsel.

Trial is set for July 5, 2028.

Legal Standard

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10 provides:

“The party against whom a complaint or cross-complaint has been filed may object, by demurrer or answer as provided in Section 430.30, to the pleading on any one or more of the following grounds:

…

(e) The pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action.  ...”

A general demurrer under Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (e), tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Lewis v. Safeway, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 385, 388; Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747; see also 1 Weil & Brown, California Practice Guide: Civil Procedure Before Trial [2025], ¶ 7:40.)

“We treat the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.  We also consider matters which may be judicially noticed.”  (Centinela Freeman Emergency Medical Associates v. Health Net of California (2016) 1 Cal.5th 994, 1010; Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318; accord Yvanova v. New Century Mortgage Corp. (2016) 62 Cal.4th 919, 924; Marina Pacific Hotel and Suites v. Fireman’s Fund Insurance Company (2022) 81 Cal.App.5th 96, 104.)  All reasonable inferences must be drawn in favor of the pleading.  (Schifando v. City of Los Angeles (2003) 31 Cal.4th 1074, 1081; Marina Pacific Hotel and Suites, supra, 81 Cal.App.5th at p. 104.)  Even “improbable” facts alleged in the pleading must be accepted as true.  (Marina Pacific Hotel and Suites, supra, 81 Cal.App.5th at pp. 104-105.)

Courts must “liberally construe the pleading” and “give the complaint a reasonable interpretation, reading it as a whole and its parts in context.”  (Id. at p. 105.)

A complaint must contain a “statement of facts constituting the cause of action, in ordinary and concise language.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.10, subd. (a)(1); see also C.A. v. William S. Hart High School Dist. (2012) 53 Cal.4th 861, 872 [“the complaint need only allege facts sufficient to state a cause of action”].)  Ordinarily, a complaint “is sufficient if it alleges ultimate rather than evidentiary facts.”  (Doe v. City of Los Angeles (2007) 42 Cal.4th 531, 550.)  Ultimate facts are those upon which “the right to recover depends” and are “essential” to the cause of action.  (Estes v. Eaton Corp. (2020) 51 Cal.App.5th 636, 643 fn. 2; see also 1 Weil & Brown, supra, ¶ 6:124.)

A plaintiff is required to plead only “the essential facts of [its] case” that are sufficient “to acquaint a defendant with the nature, source and extent of [the] cause of action.”  (Doe, supra, 42 Cal.4th at p. 550.)  Mere boilerplate or pleading of legal conclusions is not sufficient.  (Id. at p. 551 fn. 5.)  But a plaintiff need not allege “each evidentiary fact that might eventually form part of plaintiff’s proof” at trial.  (C.A., supra, 53 Cal.4th at p. 872.)

A demurrer can be sustained only when it disposes of an entire cause of action. (Poizner v. Fremont General Corp. (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 97, 119; Kong v. City of Hawaiian Gardens Redev. Agency (2003) 108 Cal.App.4th 1028, 1046.)

Leave to amend should be granted when “there is a reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment.”  (Centinela Freeman, supra, 1 Cal.5th at p. 1010; Blank, supra, 39 Cal.3d at p. 318; Shaeffer v. Califia Farms (2020) 44 Cal.App.5th 1125, 1145.)

Meet and Confer

Before filing a demurrer, “the demurring party shall meet and confer in person or by telephone with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41, subd. (a).) The requirement is met. (Supp. Prine Decl., ¶ 13.)

Discussion             

Defendant Mitchell demurs to Plaintiff’s Seventh Cause of Action, for harassment in violation of the FEHA.

Under Government Code section 12940, subdivision (j)(1), it is an unlawful employment practice for an employer “to harass an employee” based on (among other things) a person’s sex, gender, age, or physical disability.   An employee of the employer “is personally liable for any harassment prohibited by this section that is perpetrated by [that] employee, regardless of whether the employer or covered entity knows or should have known of the conduct and fails to take immediate and appropriate corrective action.”  (Gov’t Code, § 12940, subd. (j)(3).)

The elements of a prima facie cause of action under Government Code section 12940, subdivision (j) in this context are: (1) that the plaintiff and individual defendant were employed by the entity defendant; (2) that the individual defendant participated in, assisted in, or encouraged harassment of the plaintiff; (3) that the harassment occurred because of plaintiff’s sex, gender, age, disability, or other protected status; (4) that the harassing conduct was severe or pervasive; (5) that a reasonable person would have considered the work environment to be hostile, intimidating, offensive, oppressive, or abusive; (6) that the plaintiff considered the work environment to be hostile, intimidating, offensive, oppressive, or abusive; (7) that a supervisor engaged in the conduct or knew or should have known of the conduct and failed to take immediate and appropriate corrective action; and (8) that plaintiff suffered harm as a result of the harassment.  (Reid v. Google, Inc. (2010) 50 Cal.4th 512, 540-541, 545; Martin v. Board of Trustees of California State Univ. (2023) 97 Cal.App.5th 149, 170; Ortiz v. Dameron Hospital Assn. (2019) 37 Cal.App.5th 568, 581; Serri v. Santa Clara Univ. (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 830, 869; Lewis v. City of Benicia (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 1519, 1524; see also CACI Nos. 252AA, 2523.)

In support of his demurrer, Defendant Mitchell makes essentially three arguments: (1) that Plaintiff’s allegations “primarily” concern personnel management (such as job assignments) and the remaining allegations against him do not constitute actionable harassment (Mem., at pp. 5-6); (2) that Mitchell’s comments and actions did not communicate a hostile message regarding pregnancy (Mem., at pp. 6-7); and (3) the conduct of Mitchell is not sufficiently severe or pervasive to be actionable (Mem., at p. 7.)

In the FAC, Plaintiff alleges that when she first provided her supervisor (Zachary Brown) with a doctor’s note confirming her pregnancy and a weight restriction on June 21, 2023, she was sent home without pay and told that her work would be paused until Defendant Mitchell returned on June 27.  (FAC, ¶¶ 12-13.)  Upon Mitchell’s return, Mitchell was asked (with Plaintiff present) whether he approved of Plaintiff’s accommodation request; Mitchell ignored Plaintiff, denied the request, and walked away.  (FAC, ¶ 15.)  This, Plaintiff alleges, “communicated an offensive and demeaning message that [Plaintiff] was unwelcome because of her pregnancy-related restrictions and contributed to a hostile, intimidating, and abusive work environment.”  (Ibid.)

In the month of July and into August 2023, Plaintiff reported to work each day to see whether she had been scheduled a work shift.  “On each occasion,” Plaintiff alleges, “Mitchell told her to go home and did so in a dismissive manner, often appearing to smirk while speaking to her and in front of others.”  (FAC, ¶ 17.)  Mitchell refused to assign Plaintiff work, “referring to her pregnancy and calling her a ‘liability.’”  (FAC, ¶ 23.)  On or around July 10, Mitchell told Plaintiff that she could apply for light duty but used a tone that was “irritated and sarcastic, making it [clear] he would not approve the request.”  (Ibid.)  Mitchell’s “tone and words conveyed hostility toward [Plaintiff] because of her pregnancy-related restrictions.”  (Ibid.)

Following a grievance meeting on August 16, 2023, Mitchell “continued to retaliate against [Plaintiff] due to her condition and for making protected complaints.”  (FAC, ¶ 21.)  On August 23, Mitchell demoted Plaintiff and assigned her to a physically strenuous position; Mitchell “appeared irritated” and when Plaintiff objected, Mitchell “walked away during the conversation.”  (Ibid.)  Shortly after, at a meeting with Mitchell, Plaintiff, and a union representative, Mitchell raised his voice, asked “how am I picking on you?,” and again “appeared frustrated and walked away.”  (Ibid.)  Plaintiff alleges that the “reassignment, demeaning comments and repeated walk-offs, conveyed a hostile, biased message toward [Plaintiff’s] pregnancy.”  (Ibid.)

Plaintiff remained off work until August 28, 2023.  (FAC, ¶ 22.)  On that date, Mitchell initially assigned Plaintiff to work as a package driver; when Plaintiff expressed concern that some of the packages were near the upper limit of her pregnancy-related restriction, Mitchell “responded condescendingly,” stated that “he didn’t see a problem,” and again walked away.  (Ibid.)  Mitchell reassigned her to a UPS store, where there were many packages exceeding her pregnancy-related restriction; when Plaintiff called her supervisor to express her concern, Plaintiff overheard Mitchell saying, “Oh my god” in what Plaintiff alleges was a “frustrated tone.”  (Ibid.)

This is not, as Defendant contends, a set of allegations that primarily relate to work assignments.  Plaintiff alleges that over a period of approximately two months, Defendant Mitchell repeatedly made remarks and engaged in conduct that harassed Plaintiff because of her pregnancy and her pregnancy-related work restrictions.  Moreover, even official employment actions may provide an “evidentiary basis of the harassment cause of action, because the supervisor used those official actions as his means of conveying his offensive message.”  (Roby v. McKesson Corp. (2009) 47 Cal.4th 686, 708.)  “[I]n some cases, the hostile message that constitutes the harassment is conveyed through official employment actions.”  (Ibid; see also Pollock v. Tri-Modal Distribution Services, Inc. (2021) 11 Cal.5th 918, 932.)  “[S]ome official employment actions done in furtherance of a supervisor’s managerial role can also have a secondary effect of communicating a hostile message.”  (Roby, supra, 47 Cal.4th at p. 709.)  In these situations, the plaintiff may plead the cause of action as harassment (or as discrimination, or both).  (Pollock, supra, 11 Cal.5th at p. 932.) 

Whether the conduct complained of is sufficiently pervasive must be determined “from the totality of the circumstances.” (Fisher v. San Pedro Peninsula Hospital (1989) 214 Cal.App.3d 590, 609.) The plaintiff must plead, and ultimately prove, that the defendant’s conduct would have interfered with a reasonable employee's work performance and would have seriously affected the psychological well-being of a reasonable employee and that she was actually offended.  (Id. at pp. 609-610.)

The Court has considered the allegations in the FAC and the arguments presented by both sides. Taking the allegations in the FAC as true, as the Court must on demurrer, and drawing all reasonable inferences in favor of the pleading, the Court determines that Plaintiff has sufficiently alleged each of the elements of her cause of action for unlawful harassment under the FEHA by Defendant Mitchell, including that she was subjected to harassing conduct that was severe or pervasive. Some of the actions of Defendant Mitchell may have been official employment actions, but they are alleged to have had the secondary effect of communicating a hostile message: that Plaintiff did not belong in the workplace because of protected characteristics. The conduct was repeated over an extended period of time. The pleading is sufficient.

The demurrer to the Seventh Cause of Action is overruled.

Conclusion 

The Court OVERRULES the demurrer filed by Defendant Marquez Mitchell.

Defendant Mitchell is to file an answer to the FAC by October 8, 2026.

Moving party is ordered to give notice.


Case Number: 26STCV01760    Hearing Date: September 17, 2026    Dept: 514

Brand v. BMW of North America, LLC
26STCV01760
Defendant’s Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings

Tentative Ruling

The motion is denied.

Background

This case arises out of a lease of a 2025 BMW iX (the “Vehicle”) by Plaintiff Gary Brand (“Plaintiff”)

On January 16, 2026, Plaintiff filed the complaint in this action against BMW of North America, LLC (“Defendant”) and Does 1 through10 for (1) breach of express warranty obligations, (2) breach of implied warranty obligations, (3) failure to repair in a good and workmanlike manner, and (4) failure to repair within thirty days.

On March 12, 2026, Defendant filed an answer.

On June 25, 2026, Defendant filed this motion for judgment on the pleadings.  Plaintiff filed an opposition on August 21, and Defendant filed a reply on August 27.

The motion was set for hearing on September 3.  On September 3, the Court continued the hearing.

Trial is set for May 22, 2028.

Legal Standard

California practice recognizes two types of motions for judgment on the pleadings. First, there is a statutory motion. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 438.) A plaintiff may bring a statutory motion for judgment on the pleadings when “the complaint states facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action … and the answer does not state facts sufficient to constitute a defense to the complaint.” (Id., subd. (c)(1)(A).) A statutory motion for judgment on the pleadings must be filed within certain time limits, and it is subject to a statutory meet-and-confer requirement. (Id., subd. (e); Code Civ. Proc., § 439.) The grounds for a statutory motion for judgment on the pleadings must “appear on the face of the challenged pleading or from any matter of which the court is required to take judicial notice.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 438, subd. (d).)

Second, there is a nonstatutory motion for judgment on the pleadings, which “may be made at any time either prior to the trial or at the trial itself.” (Ion Equipment Corp. v. Nelson (1980) 110 Cal.App.3d 868, 877; see also Smiley v. Citibank (South Dakota) 11 Cal.4th 138, 145 fn. 2; Tarin v. Lind (2020) 47 Cal.App.5th 395, 402; Stoops v. Abbassi (2002) 100 Cal.App.4th 644, 650.)

A motion for judgment on the pleadings is the functional equivalent of a demurrer. (People ex rel. Harris v. Pac Anchor Transp. (2014) 59 Cal.4th 772, 77; City of Rancho Palos Verdes v. State (2025) 114 Cal.App.5th 13, 23.) “The standard for granting a motion for judgment on the pleadings is essentially the same as that applicable to a general demurrer, that is, under the state of the pleadings, together with matters that may be judicially noticed, it appears that a party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Bezirdjian v. O'Reilly (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 316, 321-322; accord Los Angeles Unified School Dist. v. Torres Construction Corp. (2020) 57 Cal.App.5th 480, 494; Templo v. State (2018) 24 Cal.App.5th 730, 735; Southern California Edison Co. v. City of Victorville (2013) 217 Cal.App.4th 218, 227; see also 1 Weil & Brown, California Practice Guide: Civil Procedure Before Trial [2025] ¶ 7:275.)

Request for Judicial Notice and Evidentiary Objections

Defendant asks the Court to take judicial notice of Defendant’s Statement of Information filed with the State of California, Office of the Secretary of State, dated November 11, 2024. The Court takes judicial notice of the filing of the document but not of the truth of the matters asserted within the document.  

Discussion

Defendant moves for judgment on the pleadings on each of the four causes of acion in the complaint.

First, Second and Fourth Causes of Action

-          Failure to Allege the Vehicle was Purchased in California

The first four causes of action in the complaint all arise under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act, Civil Code sections 1790 through 1795.8 (the “Act”). The provisions of the Act generally apply only to the sale of “consumer goods” in the State of California. Defendant moves for judgment on the pleadings on these four causes of action on the ground that Plaintiff fails to allege in the Complaint that the sale occurred in California. (Mem., at p. 6.)

In the Complaint, Plaintiff alleges that Defendant is in the business of (among other things) manufacturing, distributing, and selling automobiles in Los Angeles County and in the State of California; and that “[o]n or about August 6, 2024, Plaintiff entered into a lease with Defendant regarding a 2025 BMW iX, vehicle identification number WB523CF03SCT07779 (hereafter "Vehicle"), which was manufactured by Defendant.” (Compl., ¶¶ 1, 4, 7.) Liberally construing the complaint, and drawing all reasonable inferences in favor of the pleading, the Court determines that Plaintiff has sufficiently alleged, at the pleading stage, that he purchased the Vehicle, a consumer good, in the State of California.

If the facts are to the contrary, Defendant may raise this issue by motion for summary adjudication or at trial.

-          Failure to Allege Terms of Express Warranty as to the First Cause of Action

Next, Defendant contends that the First Cause of Action for breach of express warranty fails because he did not attach the exact terms of the express warranty. (Mem. at p.6, citing  Harris v. Rudin, Richman & Appel (1999) 74 Cal.App.4th 299, 307.) In opposition, Plaintiff contends that Harris relates to pleading a breach of contract claim, and that the pleading standard under Song-Beverly allows to show that the cause of action arises under the language of the statute, which Plaintiff has pled.

Reviewing the Complaint, Plaintiff alleges that there was a warranty offered by Defendant for the Vehicle, that Defendant breached this warranty by “failing to perform the proper repairs, parts replacements, and/or adjustments” to the Vehicle. (Compl., ¶¶ 8, 19.)

The Court finds that the allegations of the complaint are sufficient, at the pleading stage, to state a cause of action under Civil Code section 1793.2, subd. (d).

-          Failure to Allege Sufficient Repair Attempts

Next, Defendant argues Plaintiff fails to meet the requirements set forth in Silvio v. Ford Motor Co. (2003) 109 Cal.App.4th 1205. In Silvio, the court held that the Song-Beverly Act calls for “reasonable number of attempts” and one alleged attempt does not meet the statutory threshold.  (Silvio v. Ford Motor Co. (2003) 109 Cal.App.4th 1205, 1208-09.) Defendant contends Plaintiff alleges he brought the Vehicle in once for repair.

Reviewing the Complaint, Plaintiff alleges that “[t]he vehicle was delivered for repair of defects on or about March 24, 2025, at 2,484 miles, on or about July 9, 2025, at 3,296 miles, on or about August 19, 2025, at 3,682 miles, and on or about December 22, 2025, at 4,791 miles. The defects have not been properly repaired or repaired in a reasonable number of attempts or amount of time.” (Compl., ¶ 11.)  Plaintiff properly alleges multiple repair attempts.

-          Defendant is a Distributor, Negating the Second Cause of Action

Defendant argues it is a distributor and not a manufacturer. (Mem. at pp. 8-10.)  Defendant alleges in the complaint, however, that Defendant is a manufacturer.  (Compl., ¶ 7.)

Defendant seeks to contradict this express allegation through judicial notice of the truth of a factual assertion in Defendant’s Statement of Information filed with the Secretary of State.  This is improper.  “Courts can take judicial notice of the existence, content and authenticity of public records and other specified documents, but do not take judicial notice of the truth of the factual matters asserted in those documents.” (Dominguez v. Bonta (2022) 87 Cal.App.5th 389, 400.)

Plaintiff has sufficiently alleged, at the pleading stage, that Defendant is a manufacturer.

-          Failure to Repair Within 30 days as to Fourth Cause of Action

Defendant argues that Plaintiff fails to allege facts to support the Fourth Cause of Action that Defendant failed to render repairs within 30 days. (Mem. at p. 10.) However, Plaintiff’s Complaint includes in the Third Cause of Action that Defendant “failed to commence repairs within a reasonable time and/or to complete them within 30 days.” (Compl., ¶ 37.) This allegation is realleged as to the Fourth Cause of Action in paragraph 42. Furthermore, Plaintiff alleges in the Fourth Cause of Action that Defendant “failed to repair the subject vehicle to conform to the applicable warranties within thirty days as evidenced by the service history.” (Compl., ¶ 44.) Plaintiff has met the pleading requirement.

Accordingly, the motion for judgment on the pleadings is denied as to the First, Second, and Fourth Causes of Action.

Third Cause of Action

Last, Defendant argues that as a distributor, Defendant does not offer repairs, and thus, the Third Cause of Action raised against it fails. (Mem. at pp. 10-11.)  Again, however, the allegations in the complaint are to the contrary.  Plaintiff alleges that Defendant “engages in the business of providing service or repair to new or used consumer goods.” (Compl., ¶ 38.) This is sufficient to meet the pleading requirement for Civil Code section 1796.5.

Accordingly, the motion for judgment on the pleadings is denied.

Conclusion

The Court DENIES Defendant BMW of North America, LLC’s motion for judgment on the pleadings.  

Moving Party is ORDERED to give notice.

Case Number: 26STCV12618    Hearing Date: September 17, 2026    Dept: 514

The Woodlands Group, Inc. v. T-Mobile USA, Inc.
26STCV12618
Defendant’s Motion to Dismiss or Stay

Tentative Ruling

The motion is granted.

Background

This case arises out of a contract for telecommunication services.

On April 20, 2026, The Woodlands Group, Inc. (“Plaintiff”) filed the complaint in this action against T-Mobile USA, Inc. (“Defendant”), Robert Keeler, and Does 1 through 50, asserting causes of action for (1) breach of contract, (2) breach of implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, (3) tortious interference with prospective economic advantage, (4) defamation (libel and slander), (5) negligent misrepresentation, (6) conversion, (7) unjust enrichment, (8) quantum meruit, and (9) negligence.

On September 10, 2026, Defendant Keeler filed an answer.

On July 27, 2026, Defendant filed this motion to dismiss or stay the action.  Plaintiff filed an opposition on September 2, and Defendant filed a reply on September 10.

No trial date has been set.

Legal Standard

Even when a court has subject matter and personal jurisdiction, the court may stay or dismiss the action on the ground of inconvenient forum.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 418.10, subd. (a)(2); 1 Weil & Brown, California Practice Guide: Civil Procedure Before Trial (2026), ¶ 3:407.)

Code of Civil Procedure section 410.30, subdivision (a), provides:

“When a court finds that in the interest of substantial justice an action should be heard in a forum outside this state, the court shall stay or dismiss the action in whole or in part on any conditions that may be just.”  

Section 410.30 codifies the common law doctrine of forum non conveniens.  Forum non conveniens is not a jurisdictional doctrine; rather, it is “an equitable doctrine invoking the discretionary power of a court to decline to exercise the jurisdiction [to stay or dismiss] it has over a transitory cause of action when it believes that the action may be more appropriately and justly tried elsewhere.”  (Stangvik v. Shiley Inc. (1991) 54 Cal.3d 744, 751; In re Christopher B. (1996) 43 Cal.App.4th 551, 559.)

Discussion

The complaint in this matter is based on a contract between Plaintiff and Defendant referred to as the “Master Agreement,” a copy of which is attached to the complaint.

Paragraph 11.6 of the Master Agreement provides:

“Governing Law. This Agreement will be governed in all respects by, and construed and enforced in accordance with, the laws of the state of Washington without regard to any rules governing conflicts of laws. The parties consent to the exclusive jurisdiction over and venue in the state and federal courts located in King County, Washington.”

Defendant now moves to stay or dismiss this action based on the forum selection provisions of paragraph 11.6 of the Master Agreement.

The law in this area is well established.  Forum selection clauses are presumed to be valid.  (EpicentRx, Inc. v. Super. Ct. (2025) 18 Cal.5th 58, 67.)  A party seeking to resist enforcement of a forum selection clause bears the burden of showing that enforcement would be, under the circumstances of the case, unfair or unreasonable.  (Richtek USA, Inc. v. uPI Semiconductor Corp. (2015) 242 Cal.App.4th 651, 661; Trident Labs, Inc. v. Merrill Lynch Commercial Finance Corp. (2011) 200 Cal.App.4th 147, 153-154; Miller-Leigh LLC v. Henson (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 1143, 1149.)

“Mere inconvenience or additional expense is not the test of unreasonableness ... of a mandatory forum selection clause.” (Olinick v. BMG Entertainment (2006) 138 Cal.App.4th at 1294 [citations omitted].)  In addition, a forum selection clause is not unenforceable merely because it is “contained in a contract of adhesion and was not the subject of bargaining.”  (Cal-State Business Products & Services, Inc. v. Ricoh (1993) 12 Cal.App.4th 1666, 1679.)  But a forum selection clause may be unenforceable if it would be contrary to a strong California public policy or result in the loss of the protection of statutory provisions enacted to protect California citizens.  (EpicentRx, supra, 18 Cal.5th at p. 75; G Companies Mgmt., LLC v. LREP Arizona, LLC (2023) 88 Cal.App.5th 342, 353-355; see also 1 Weil & Brown, supra, ¶ 3:185.)

In opposition to the motion, Plaintiff argues primarily that enforcement of the forum selection clause in the Master Agreement would violate Code of Civil Procedure section 410.42.  That statute provides:

“(a) The following provisions of a contract between the contractor and a subcontractor with principal offices in this state, for the construction of a public or private work of improvement in this state, shall be void and unenforceable:

(1) A provision which purports to require any dispute between the parties to be litigated, arbitrated, or otherwise determined outside this state.

(2) A provision which purports to preclude a party from commencing such a proceeding or obtaining a judgment or other resolution in this state or the courts of this state.

(b) For purposes of this section, “construction” means any work or services performed on, or materials provided for, a work of improvement, as defined in Section 8050 of the Civil Code, and for which a lien may be claimed pursuant to Section 8400 of the Civil Code (whether or not a lien is in fact claimed) or for which such a lien could be claimed but for Section 8160 of the Civil Code.”

The evidence in the record, however, does not establish that the Master Agreement is a contract between a contractor and a subcontractor.  The agreement itself indicates that it is a contract for construction services.  Paragraph 9.2 prohibits Plaintiff from subcontracting any of the work without Defendant’s written consent.  No extrinsic evidence is provided.  And in the complaint Plaintiff alleges it contracted with Defendant for telecommunications construction and maintenance services; nothing in the complaint asserts that Plaintiff was a subcontractor for Defendant. (See Compl., ¶¶ 11-13.)

Section 410.42 does not bar enforcement of the forum selection clause.

Next, Plaintiff argues relatively briefly (1) that enforcement violates public policy, (2) that contract is one of adhesion, and (3) that Plaintiff’s tort claims are grounded in and should not be swept out of California.

Of course, courts will not enforce a forum selection clause if the result “would substantially diminish the rights of California residents in a way that violates our state's public policy.” (Verdugo v. Alliantgroup, L.P. (2015) 237 Cal.App.4th 141, 147.)  But Plaintiff does not provide any evidence or substantial legal argument to support these assertions.  Plaintiff has not shown that the Master Agreement (or any part of it) is unconscionable or otherwise not valid and enforceable.  On this record, Plaintiff has not carried its burden to show that enforcement of the forum selection clause is unfair or unreasonable.

The motion is granted.

Defendant is dismissed, without prejudice, so that Plaintiff may pursue its claims in the forum chosen by the parties.

Conclusion

The Court GRANTS the motion of Defendant T-Mobile USA, Inc. to dismiss or stay.

The Court DISMISSES the complaint WITHOUT PREJUDICE as to Defendant T-Mobile USA, Inc. ONLY.

Moving Party is to provide notice.
