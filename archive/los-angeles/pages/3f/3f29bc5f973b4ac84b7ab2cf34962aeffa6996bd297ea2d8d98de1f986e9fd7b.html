DEPARTMENT M LAW AND MOTION RULINGS



Case Number: 21SMCV01401    Hearing Date: June 25, 2026    Dept: M

CASE NAME:           Bowmont Holdings LLC, et al. v. CAS Construction, et al.

CASE NO.:                21SMCV01401

MOTION:                  Motion to Deem Matters Admitted

HEARING DATE:   6/24/2026

 

Legal Standard

 

Pursuant to CCP section 2033.280(b), a party may move for an order that the genuineness of any documents and the truth of any matters specified in the requests be deemed admitted, as well as for a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010). “Failure to timely respond to RFA does not result in automatic admissions. Rather, the propounder of the RFA must ‘move for an order that the genuineness of any documents and the truth of any matters specified in the requests be deemed admitted, as well as for a monetary sanction’ under § 2023.010 et seq.” (CCP, § 2033.280(b).) The court “shall” grant the motion to deem RFA admitted, “unless it finds that the party to whom the requests for admission have been directed has served, before the hearing on the motion, a proposed response to the requests for admission that is in substantial compliance with Section 2033.220.” (CCP, § 2033.280(c).) 

 

ANALYSIS 

 

Defendants Bowmont Estates Association and Bartlein & Company, Inc. moves to deem admitted all 62 Requests for Admission (“RFAs”) served on Plaintiffs Bowmont Holdings, LLC, Haig Tacorian, and Gilda Tacorian (“Plaintiffs”).

 

Defendants demonstrate that Plaintiffs failed to serve timely responses despite receiving the subject RFAs. (Geibel Decl., Exs. A-C.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that they have served code compliant responses to all RFAs. (Cowles Decl., ¶¶ 5-8, Ex. A.) However, as Defendants observe in reply, Plaintiffs have only provided responses by Plaintiff Bowmont Holdings, and not any responses by the individual plaintiffs Haig and Gilda Tacorian. If the individual plaintiffs do not serve verified responses by the hearing, then the Court will deny the motion as to Plaintiff Bowmont, but grant the motion as to the individual plaintiffs. (CCP § 2033.280(c).) 

 


Case Number: 23SMCV03702    Hearing Date: June 25, 2026    Dept: M

CASE NAME:           Jones Builders, et al., v. Drake, et al. 

CASE NO.:                23SMCV03702

MOTION:                  Demurrer to the Cross-Complaint  

HEARING DATE:   6/25/2026

 

LEGAL STANDARD 

 

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context. In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed. (CCP §§ 430.30, 430.70.) At the pleading stage, a plaintiff need only allege ultimate facts sufficient to apprise the defendant of the factual basis for the claim against him. (Semole v. Sansoucie (1972) 28 Cal. App. 3d 714, 721.) A “demurrer does not, however, admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law alleged in the pleading, or the construction of instruments pleaded, or facts impossible in law.” (S. Shore Land Co. v. Petersen (1964) 226 Cal.App.2d 725, 732, internal citations omitted.) 

 

A special demurrer for uncertainty is disfavored and will only be sustained where the pleading is so bad that defendant cannot reasonably respond—i.e., cannot reasonably determine what issues must be admitted or denied, or what counts or claims are directed against him/her. (CCP § 430.10(f); Khoury v. Maly’s of Calif., Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.) Moreover, even if the pleading is somewhat vague, “ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Ibid.)  

 

Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading may serve and file a notice of motion to strike the whole or any part thereof. (CCP § 435(b)(1); Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1322(b).) The court may, upon a motion or at any time in its discretion and upon terms it deems proper: (1) strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or (2) strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of California, a court rule, or an order of the court. (CCP §§ 436(a)-(b); Stafford v. Shultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782 [“Matter in a pleading which is not essential to the claim is surplusage; probative facts are surplusage and may be stricken out or disregarded”].) 

 

“Liberality in permitting amendment is the rule, if a fair opportunity to correct any defect has not been given.” (Angie M. v. Superior Court (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 1217, 1227.) It is an abuse of discretion for the court to deny leave to amend where there is any reasonable possibility that plaintiff can state a good cause of action. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349.) The burden is on plaintiff to show¿in what manner¿plaintiff can amend the complaint, and¿how¿that amendment will change the legal effect of the pleading.¿(Id.) 

  

ANALYSIS 

 

Cross-Defendants James A. Atkins and Studio Atkins, Inc. (Roes 14 & 15) demur to the Cross-Complaint filed by Cross-Complainant Jones Builders Group Inc. Cross-Defendants also move to strike the Roe Amendments nos. 14 and 15 filed on April 7, 2026, which named them as Roe defendants.

 

The Court notes that Cross-Complainant subsequently dismissed all causes of action except one, two and ten as to the Atkins Cross-Defendants. The Court therefore finds that the demurrer and motion to strike is well taken as to all causes except for the first, second and tenth causes of action. The Court will address those causes below, after considering the motion to strike the doe amendments.

 

Motion to Strike – Roe Amendment

 

Cross-Defendants argue that Cross-Complainant was not actually ignorant of Atkin’s identity or role. Cross-Defendants bring a Barrows motion. (Barrows v. American Motors Corp., (1983) 144 Cal.App.3d 1.)

 

CCP section 474 provides, in pertinent part, as follows: “When the plaintiff is ignorant of the name of a defendant, he must state that fact in the complaint, or the affidavit if the action is commenced by affidavit, and such defendant may be designated in any pleading or proceeding by any name, and when his true name is discovered, the pleading or proceeding must be amended accordingly…” “The purpose of Code of Civil Procedure section 474 is to enable a plaintiff who is ignorant of the identity of the defendant to file his complaint before his claim is barred by the statute of limitations.”¿(Barrows, supra, 144 Cal.App.3d at 7.)¿An amendment that replaces a Doe defendant with a named defendant will relate back to the filing of the original complaint if the original complaint stated a cause of action against the Doe defendant, the allegations against the defendant that is being added arise from the same facts or circumstances giving rise to the original complaint, and the original complaint alleged that plaintiff was ignorant of the true name and capacity of each Doe defendant.¿ (Fireman’s Fund Ins. Co. v. Sparks Const., Inc., (2004) 114 Cal.App.4th 1135, 1143-1144.) Ignorance includes lack of sufficient facts to believe in probable liability. (Fuller v. Tucker (2000) 84 Cal.App.4th 1163, 1170-1171; see Marasco v. Wadsworth (1978) 21 Cal.3d 82, 88 [ignorance encompasses the connection between the person and the injury].)

 

Courts have summarized the points from Barrows as follows:  

 

First, section 474 includes an implicit requirement that a plaintiff may not ‘unreasonably delay’ his or her filing of a Doe amendment after learning a defendant’s identity. Second, a defendant named in an action by a Doe amendment under section 474 may challenge the amendment by way of an evidence-based motion, which argues that the plaintiff ‘unreasonable delayed’ his or her filing of the challenged amendment. Third, ‘unreasonable delay’ …includes a prejudice element, which requires a showing by the defendant that he or she would suffer prejudice from plaintiff's delay in filing the Doe amendment. 

 

(A.N. v. County of Los Angeles (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1058, 1066-1067.) Under any title, a Barrows motion is reviewed in the same manner as a ruling on a motion for leave to file an amended pleading. (Id., at 1067.)

 

Section 474 is only available “when the plaintiff is actually ignorant of the facts establishing a cause of action against the party to be substituted for a Doe Defendant.”¿(McClatchy v. Coblentz, Patch, Duffy & Bass, LLP (2016) 247 Cal.App.4th 371-372.)¿“‘Ignorance of the facts giving rise to a cause of action is the ignorance required by section 474, and the pivotal question is, ‘did plaintiff know facts?’ not ‘did plaintiff know or believe that [he] had a cause of action based on those facts?’”¿ (Id. at 372, quoting General Motors Corp. v. Superior Court (1996) 48 Cal.App.4th 580, 594.) “‘Although it is true that a plaintiff’s ignorance of the defendant’s name must be genuine (in good faith) and not feigned [citations] and that a plaintiff need not be aware of each and every detail concerning a person’s involvement before the plaintiff loses his ignorance [citations], it is equally true that the plaintiff does not relinquish [his] rights under section 474 simply because [he] has a suspicion of wrongdoing arising from one or more facts [he] does know.’”¿ (Id.)¿The fact that the plaintiff had the means to obtain knowledge is irrelevant. (Id.)¿In other words, actual knowledge of defendants’ identity must be shown, and “constructive or legal knowledge will not deprive the plaintiff of the remedy.”¿ (Sobeck & Associates, Inc. v. B&R Investments No. 24 (1989) 215 Cal.App.3d 861, 867; see also McOwen, supra, 153 Cal.App.4th at 943-944 [“reasonable diligence is not germane to determining whether a Doe amendment was timely.”].) 

 

Generally, the CC states that cross-defendants are liable on indemnity theories for damages arising from Drake Parties’ complaint against Cross-Complainant. The Drake Parties have commenced an action against Cross-Complainant alleging Cross-Complainant is responsible in some manner for general and special damages arising out of alleged construction defects and related circumstances at the subject Property. (CC ¶ 4.) Cross-Complainant was a contractor for the construction project performed at the Subject Property. (CC ¶ 7.) The first cause of action alleges that, if Cross-Complainant is found legally responsible in any manner for any damage as alleged by the Drake Parties or any other party, such damage “was primarily and ultimately caused by the acts and omissions of the Cross-Defendants herein, and each of them, in doing or failing to do the acts relevant to the design, development, manufacture, construction, inspection, supervision, testing, maintenance, management, repair, and observation of the Subject Property and its components, whereas the acts of JBG herein, if any, were secondary, passive, and derivative in nature only. Cross-Defendants are liable in contract, tort, and on statutory grounds as a result thereof.” (CC ¶ 8.) The second cause of action alleges entitlement to equitable indemnity, apportionment of liability, and contribution among and from the Cross-Defendants, and each of them, according to their respective fault, for the injuries and damages allegedly sustained by any party complaining or finding against JBG, if any, by way of sums paid by way of defense, settlement or judgment rendered against JBG in this action. (CC ¶ 11.) The declaratory relief cause seeks a determination of indemnification rights and obligations between the parties (CC ¶ 43.)

 

Cross-Defendants establish that Cross-Complainant was not truly ignorant of Cross-Defendants’ role in this case, including the connection between Atkins conduct and the Drake Parties’ damages. At the outset of the case, Cross-Complainant was aware of Cross-Defendants’ existence and their general role in the controversy. In January through March 2021, Atkins worked with Jones officers/agents, including discussing the scope of the project, monitoring Jones’s work, identifying defects, and raising issues with Jones’ personnel, sometimes resulting in corrective work. (Atkins Decl., ¶¶ 3-5.) After Jones was terminated on March 6, 2023, Atkin continued in the same role, overseeing the Project, liaising with replacement contractors, and evaluating remedial work. (Id., ¶ 6.) Jones’s own complaint identifies James Atkins by name, alleging he contacted a Jones officer by phone and email to discuss the project and scope of work, and that Atkins requested multiple budget revisions after meetings with his clients. (Compl., ¶¶ 11-12.) Discovery efforts also show Jones’s actual knowledge of Atkins’s role in directing the post-termination repairs on the property. Drakes identified James Anthony Atkins as a witness with “superior knowledge of the construction project.” (Seabolt Decl. ¶ 5 & Ex. 1 at p. 7.) On May 5, 2025, Jones served verified responses to special interrogatories confirming that it was investigating Atkins and his involvement as a designer of the Project. (Id., Ex. 2, at pp. 9, 11.) Drakes identified Atkins as their intended expert. (Seabolt Decl., ¶ 4.) A site inspection was held on March 14, 2025, led by Atkins, and attended by Jones’s counsel and Jones’s expert, where Atkins identified alleged defects and responded to extensive questions. (Id., ¶ 4; Atkins Decl., ¶ 8.) On August 5, 2025, Atkins was made available to Jones for a dedicated question-and-answer session regarding the Property and alleged defects. (Seabolt Decl., ¶ 7; Atkins Decl., ¶ 9.) Critically, repairs at the Property were discussed, as was Atkins’ role in supervising those repairs. (Atkins Decl., ¶ 9.) Jones’s expert had certain questions and requests that needed further investigation, and Atkins agreed to assist in obtaining answers to the questions posed and to gather documents responsive to Jones’s expert’s requests. (Atkins Decl., ¶ 9; Seabolt Decl., ¶ 7.) Atkins was also available to Jones’s experts and counsel for a second question and answer session at mediation on March 11, 2026. (Id., ¶ 8; Atkins Decl., ¶ 10.) The evidence suggests that Jones knew of the facts of Cross-Defendants’ liability by August 2025, including that Atkins’ role in supervising those repairs, which is allegedly the basis of Cross-Defendants’ liability. (Atkins Decl., ¶ 9; Adams Decl., ¶¶ 8-9.)

 

In turn, Cross-Complainant’s evidence does not establish their ignorance at the time of pleading.  Cross-Complainant does not cite specific evidence concerning its discovery of Cross-Defendants’ role in contributing to the Drakes Parties’ alleged damages. Jones only presents its counsel’s declaration, claiming that at the March 20, 2026, property inspection, Jones learned of “previously undisclosed repair work” and that Mr. Atkins “described and demonstrated a broader role than had previously been disclosed, including involvement in repair coordination, design-related decisions, and authorization of work affecting the condition of the property.” (Id., ¶ 8.) However, since the outset, Cross-Defendant knew that Atkins monitored work at the project, identified defects and ordered corrective work. As noted, since August 2025, Atkins had full discussions with Jones’ agents regarding the repairs at the Property and Mr. Atkins’ role in supervising those repairs. (Atkins Decl., ¶ 9.)

 

Cross-Defendants have also established that Cross-Complainant’s delay in seeking amendment by seven months, or longer, resulted in prejudice to both Cross-Defendants and the Drake parties. For years, the Atkins Defendants participated as non-parties—without counsel—sitting for questioning, assisting inspections, and supporting claim evaluations. (Atkins Decl., ¶¶ 8-11.) Had Cross-Defendants been named earlier, Mr. Atkins would not have participated without representation, would have asserted privileges, objected to questioning, and preserved defenses differently. (Id.) The belated amendment places him in an adversarial posture with the Drakes after years of cooperative participation. Furthermore, Cross-Complainant’s attempt to insert the Drake parties’ known expert into this action on the eve of trial, the day after expert designations went out, suggests gamesmanship. (See A.N., supra, 171 Cal.App.4th at 1068 [“[I]t does not require speculation to recognize that a party who is drawn into litigation on the eve of trial will face difficulties in preparing a defense in such short order.”].) By converting him into a defendant at this late stage, there is a significant possibility that Jones will have prevented the Drake Parties from early engagement of a truly neutral expert. (Id., ¶¶ 7-8.) A deposition of Mr. Atkins was at that time scheduled for April 9, 2026, which could not go forward due to the amendment. (Seabolt Decl., ¶ 11.) Trial was originally set for May 26, 2026, with a discovery cutoff at the end of April. The Atkins Defendants must now prepare an entire defense in less than six months, without having directly participated in the prior discovery. While some of the prejudice might be alleviated by the trial continuance (and possible future trial continuance), it would be difficult to entirely remedy these issues.

 

Accordingly, the motion to strike is GRANTED. As there is no doe amendment, there is no cause of action stated against Cross-Defendants as Roes. Accordingly, the demurrer is moot.


Case Number: 23SMCV05788    Hearing Date: June 25, 2026    Dept: M

CASE NAME:           Daly v. Vintage at 425 Broadway Apartment, et al.

CASE NO.:                23SMCV05788

MOTION:                  Motion to Compel Initial Discovery Responses

HEARING DATE:   6/25/2026

 

Legal Standard

 

            Where there has been no timely response to a CCP section 2031.010 inspection demand, the demanding party must seek an order compelling a response. (CCP § 2031.300.) Failure to timely respond waives all objections, including privilege and work product. Thus, unless the party to whom the demand was directed obtains relief from waiver, he or she cannot raise objections to the documents demanded. There is no deadline for a motion to compel responses. Likewise, for failure to respond, the moving party need not attempt to resolve the matter outside court before filing the motion. Where the motion seeks only a response to the inspection demand, no showing of "good cause" is required. 

 

If a party to whom interrogatories are directed fails to serve a timely response, the propounding party may move for an order compelling responses and for a monetary sanction. (CCP § 2030.290(b).) The statute contains no time limit for a motion to compel where no responses have been served. All that need be shown in the moving papers is that a set of interrogatories was properly served on the opposing party, that the time to respond has expired, and that no response of any kind has been served. (Leach v. Superior Court (1980) 111 Cal. App. 3d 902, 905-906.) 

 

Pursuant to CCP section 2033.280(b), a party may move for an order that the genuineness of any documents and the truth of any matters specified in the requests be deemed admitted, as well as for a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010). “Failure to timely respond to RFA does not result in automatic admissions. Rather, the propounder of the RFA must ‘move for an order that the genuineness of any documents and the truth of any matters specified in the requests be deemed admitted, as well as for a monetary sanction’ under § 2023.010 et seq.” (CCP, § 2033.280(b).) The court “shall” grant the motion to deem RFA admitted, “unless it finds that the party to whom the requests for admission have been directed has served, before the hearing on the motion, a proposed response to the requests for admission that is in substantial compliance with Section 2033.220.” (CCP, § 2033.280(c).) 

 

ANALYSIS 

 

Defendant Orkin Services of California Inc. moves to compel Plaintiff Star Daly to serve initial discovery responses as to a) Request for Production of Documents (Set One); b) Form Interrogatories, Set One; c) Special Interrogatories, Set One; and d) Request for Admission, Set One. Defendant requests sanctions against Plaintiff at $2,370.00 per motion.

 

Defendant demonstrates proper service of the subject discovery. (Anselmo Decl., ¶¶ 2-4, Exs. A.) Plaintiff did not respond to the subject discovery. (Id., ¶¶ 4-5.)

 

Accordingly, the motions are GRANTED. Plaintiff is ordered to provide code compliant responses to the RPDs, FROGs and SROGs, without objection, within 20 days. The RFAs are deemed admitted. Monetary sanctions are granted in the total amount of $2,550.00 against Plaintiff. Sanctions are to be paid to defendant’s counsel of record within 30 days.


Case Number: 23STCV25071    Hearing Date: June 25, 2026    Dept: M

CASE NAME:           Hunter, v. Krauss, et al.

CASE NO.:                23STCV25071

MOTION:                  Motion to Compel Answers at Deposition

HEARING DATE:   6/25/2026

 

Legal Standard

 

Compel Further Answers at Deposition

 

CCP section 2025.480 permits a party to seek a motion to compel deposition answers where a deponent fails to answer any question or produce any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing under the deponent's control. The court shall order the production or answer to be given if it determines that the matter sought is subject to discovery. (CCP § 2025.480(i).) Deponents are to answer deposition questions unless they pertain to privileged matters, or improper matters revealing a motive to harass or annoy, and “witnesses are expected to endure an occasional irrelevant question without disrupting the deposition process.” (Stewart v. Colonial Western Agency, Inc. (2001) 87 Cal.App.4th 1006, 1015.) 

 

The moving party must lodge, at least 5 days before the hearing on the motion, a certified copy of whatever parts of the transcript are relevant to the motion. (CCP § 2025.480 (h).)

  

The motion to compel must be “made no later than 60 days after the completion of the record of the deposition.” (CCP § 2025.480(b).)

 

Monetary sanctions are mandatory against an unsuccessful moving or opposing party unless they acted with substantial justification or imposition of sanctions would be unjust. (CCP § 2025.480(j).)   

 

Reopen Discovery

 

Except as otherwise provided, “any party shall be entitled as a matter of right to complete discovery proceedings on or before the 30th day, and to have motions concerning discovery heard on or before the 15th day, before the date initially set for trial of the action.” (CCP, § 2024.020(a).) “[A] continuance or postponement of the trial date does not operate to reopen discovery proceedings” unless a motion to reopen discovery is filed and granted pursuant to CCP section 2024.050. (CCP, § 2024.020(b); Pelton-Shepherd Industries, Inc. v. Delta Packaging Products, Inc. (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 1568.) CCP section 2024.050 provides that “[o]n motion of any party, the court may grant leave to complete discovery proceedings, or to have a motion concerning discovery heard, closer to the initial trial date, or to reopen discovery after a new trial date has been set.” (CCP, § 2024.050(a).) “The purpose of imposing a time limit on discovery is to expedite and facilitate trial preparation and to prevent delay.¿Without a cutoff date, the parties could tie up each other and the trial court in discovery and discovery disputes right up to the eve of trial or beyond. Furthermore, . . . to be effective the cutoff date must be firm or some litigants will manipulate the proceedings to avoid the cut-off date.”¿(Beverly Hosp. v. Superior Court¿(1993) 19 Cal.App.4th 1289, 1295.) The reopening of discovery is a matter that is committed to the trial court’s sound discretion.¿(CCP, § 2024.050(a), (b).)¿ In exercising that discretion, the trial court considers “any matter relevant to the leave requested,” including:¿

 

(1) The necessity and the reasons for the discovery.¿

 

(2) The diligence or lack of diligence of the party seeking the discovery or the hearing of a discovery motion, and the reasons that the discovery was not completed or that the discovery motion was not heard earlier.¿

 

(3) Any likelihood that permitting the discovery or hearing the discovery motion will prevent the case from going to trial on the date set, or otherwise interfere with the trial calendar, or result in prejudice to any other party.¿

 

(4) The length of time that has elapsed between any date previously set, and the date presently set, for the trial of the action.¿

 

(CCP, § 2024.050(b).)¿

 

The court shall impose a monetary sanction against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to extend or to reopen discovery, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust. (CCP § 2024.050(c).)

 

Analysis

 

Plaintiff James W. Hunter moves for the following orders:

 

1. Compelling nonparty deponent Sierra Fino to reappear for the resumption and completion of her deposition, which was prematurely terminated on February 16, 2026;

2. Compelling Sierra Fino, on the resumption of the deposition, to answer the deposition questions identified in Plaintiff’s Separate Statement filed and served concurrently herewith;

3. Permitting Plaintiff to continue the deposition on the obstructed subject areas, including but not limited to:

a. the preparation, drafting, transmission, execution, and factual basis of Sierra Fino’s declaration filed in support of Defendant Krauss’s motion for summary judgment in this action;

b. the October 15, 2022 settlement meeting, including who was present, what occurred, whether Plaintiff attempted to leave, and the role of the unidentified male visible in the meeting video;

c. the timeline and nature of communications between Plaintiff and Sierra Fino in 2019 and 2020, including inconsistencies between deposition testimony and prior sworn testimony;

4. Ordering that the resumed deposition proceed in conformity with Code of Civil Procedure sections 2025.460 and 2025.470, including that no participant may unilaterally terminate the deposition and that no instruction not to answer may be made except on a valid privilege ground or upon a proper demand to suspend for protective-order relief;

5. Awarding Plaintiff the balance of any unused deposition time, plus such additional time as is reasonably necessary because the February 16, 2026 examination was impeded and delayed by repeated instructions not to answer, broad topic-level prohibitions, argumentative colloquy, interruptions, countdowns imposed by counsel, and improper termination; and

6. Awarding monetary sanctions, jointly and severally, against Sierra Fino, A. George Glasco, and Todd Krauss, or against such of them as the Court deems proper, in the amount of $1,701, representing transcript costs, and other reasonable expenses caused by the conduct that necessitated this motion.

 

Hunter also moves to reopen discovery for the limited purpose of completing the deposition of Sierra Fino.

 

Plaintiff’s objections to the Fino and Glasco declarations submitted in opposition are OVERRULED. Plaintiff’s objections to the Glasco declaration submitted in sur-reply are SUSTAINED.

 

The opposing parties, including Glasco and Fino, raise service issues. They demonstrate that the deponent (and counsel) were not served in compliance with CCP § 2025.480 and California Rules of Court, rule 3.1346. No proof of service exists for the initial hearing date. Plaintiff only submitted proofs of service which show personal service on Ms. Fino on May 31, 2026, and on Mr. Glasco on June 1, 2026. However, Glasco claims to have been in San Diego at the time of service, specifically in federal court in San Diego from 2-3 p.m. on June 1. He claims he left early that morning to avoid traffic. He also claims to have video evidence showing no service occurred at his house at 9 a.m. Fino states that she was at work at the time. Neither Mr. Glasco or Ms. Fino substantively respond to the motion.

 

The Court finds the Fino and Glasco declarations to be more persuasive than Plaintiff’s submitted declarations, including the declaration from his unregistered process server. Thus, the motion remains unserved. Moreover, even if Plaintiff served Glasco or Fino on May 29 or June 1, this service would not be within the statutory 60-day time limit. (CCP § 2025.480; see Weinstein v. Blumberg (2018) 25 Cal.App.5th 316, 320 [motion to compel further responses to deposition was not “made” until required supporting papers were served].) As such, the motion would be untimely.

 

Accordingly, the motion is DENIED. The opposing parties do not notice or substantiate any monetary sanctions against Plaintiff.


Case Number: SS026368    Hearing Date: June 25, 2026    Dept: M

CASE NAME:           Yu Lesebeg v. Feld

CASE NO.:                SS026368

MOTION:                  Motions to Tax/Strike Costs

HEARING DATE:   6/23/2026

 

Legal Standard

 

The judgment creditor is entitled to “reasonable and necessary costs” of enforcing a judgment. (CCP § 685.040.) Attorney's fees incurred in enforcing a judgment are included as costs collectible under this title if the underlying judgment includes an award of attorney's fees to the judgment creditor pursuant to subparagraph (A) of paragraph (10) of subdivision (a) of Section 1033.5. (Ibid.) The judgment creditor may claim certain delineated costs of enforcing a judgment in a memorandum of costs before the judgment is satisfied but not later than two years after the costs have been incurred. (CCP § 685.070(a)-(b).) Within 10 days after the memorandum of costs is served on the judgment debtor, the judgment debtor may apply to the court on noticed motion to have the costs taxed by the court and the court shall make an order allowing or disallowing the costs to the extent justified under the circumstances of the case. (CCP § 685.070(c).) If no motion to tax is made, the costs claimed in the memo are allowed. (CCP § § 685.070(d).)

 

Unless objection is made to the entire cost memorandum, the motion to strike or tax costs must refer to each item objected to by the same number and appear in the same order as the corresponding cost item claimed on the memorandum of costs and state why each item is objectionable. (CRC rule 3.1700(b)(2).) Any grounds not stated are waived. (Boyd v. Oscar Fisher Co. (1989) 210 Cal. App. 3d 368, 383.)

 

A verified memorandum of costs is prima facie evidence that the costs, expenses, and services therein listed were necessarily incurred. (Rappenecker v. Sea-Land Serv., Inc. (1979) 93 Cal.App.3d 256, 266.) “Allowable costs shall be reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation rather than merely convenient or beneficial to its preparation.” (CCP § 1033.5(c)(2).) “If the items appearing in a cost bill appear to be proper charges, the burden is on the party seeking to tax costs to show that they were not reasonable or necessary.” (Ladas v. California State Auto. Assn. (1993) 19 Cal. App. 4th 761, 774.) On the other hand, if the items are properly objected to, they are put in issue and the burden of proof is on the party claiming them as costs. (Id.) 

 

Analysis

 

This hearing regards three separate motions by Judgment Debtor Rolan Feld (“JD”) to strike three separate cost memorandums submitted by Judgment Creditor (“JC”) Yu Leseberg.

 

Evidentiary Issues

 

            JD’s request for judicial notice is GRANTED.

 

JC’s request for judicial notice is GRANTED.

 

Bankruptcy Trustee’s request for judicial notice is GRANTED.

 

March 17, 2025, (Amended) Memorandum

 

The March 2025 cost memorandum claimed $453,586.48 in new costs incurred in the last two years, consisting entirely of attorneys’ fees. The March 2025 memo acknowledged previously allowed costs of $532,120.62 stating that the total of all post-judgment costs was $985,707.10 (the new costs plus previously allowed costs). It also acknowledged total payments of $2,847,555.05, with $1,099,715.78 credit to accrued interest, and $1,752,352.65 credit to judgment principal. The cost memo states $1,710,854.74 remained in principal and $30,684.86 of accrued interest remained due.

 

JD argued that the requested costs are non-descript and excessive. The Court concurs that the request for $453,586.48 in attorneys’ fees are facially excessive. The record shows very little case activity from the prior cost memo filed on March 29, 2023. (The March 2023 memo already recognized the previously allowed costs of $532,120.62.) The only significant work revealed by the court record shows JC’s opposition to the motion to tax the March 2023 memo, a writ of execution filed on July 2023, and a notice of sale of copyright interest filed on March 4, 2025. Thus, it is likely that the entire fee was not reasonably incurred. The motion is therefore properly brought. JC has the burden to justify the sought fees.

 

JC argues that the fees in the March 17, 2025, Memorandum of Costs represent fees that were incurred from January 1, 2020 through March 17, 2025. (Cummiskey Decl., ¶¶ 3, 4.) JC presents the following supporting exhibits: 1) Cummiskey Decl., Ex. A, which reflects billing entries for Wolf Wallenstein between January 1, 2020, and through March 17, 2025, in the amount of $276,647.30; 2) Yu Decl., Ex. A, which reflects time and billing entries for Cinque & Cinque, LLP, a New York firm retained to collect royalties paid in New York on the Copyrights, including billing entries between January 1, 2020 and through March 17, 2025 in the amount of $144,966.80; and 3) Yu Decl., Ex. B, which shows time and billing entries for Drinker Biddle Reath between January 1, 2020 and through March 17, 2025 in the amount of $31,972.38.

 

JC fails to show that it would be entitled to the requested fees. Generally, fees for legal work performed in connection with collection efforts would be reasonably incurred and properly awarded. However, a cost memorandum may only claim costs incurred in the last two years. (CCP § 685.070(a)-(b).) JC fails to distinguish between the allowable fees incurred between March 17, 2023, to March 17, 2025, from fees incurred prior to March 17, 2023. JC instead inappropriately includes fees outside that period. Without making a distinction between the time and untimely fees, JC has failed to demonstrate its allowable fees. Additionally, a cursory review of the submitted records shows that the allowable fees would not approach $453,586.48.

 

There are some additional issues with the details of the supporting exhibits. The invoices from Cinque do not include billing descriptions, time entries, or billing rates. The invoices only state that the fee relates to professional services rendered re: New York Supreme Court Action Yu Leseberg v. Feld (Docket # P000088625). Without the rate and time spent on an enforcement task, the court would have difficulty concluding that the Cinque fees were reasonably incurred. Similarly, the billing entries of Drinker Biddle Reath are bank records which do not include billing entries, billing descriptions, time entries, or rates. Additionally, the bank records only show payment prior to March 17, 2023. Thus, the Drinker Biddle Reath fees are improperly included in the March 2025 cost memorandum. The invoices from the Law Office of John M. McCoy III attached as exhibit C to the Yu declaration are not verified. (Yu Decl., ¶ 4.) She states that exhibit C reflects expert services regarding copyright valuation. These would not be attorneys’ fees. Additionally, the McCoy fees were all incurred prior to March 17, 2023. Thus, such fees would not be properly included in the March 2025 cost memo.

 

In sum, JC has failed to justify the requested costs in the March 2025 cost memo. Accordingly, the motion to tax/strike the March 2025 cost memo is GRANTED.

 

February 17, 2026, Memorandum

 

JC contends that the February 2026 memo was superseded by the March 12, 2026, memo. The court will accordingly strike the February 2026 memo for that reason, and only consider the propriety of any overlapping costs claimed by the March 12, 2026, memo.

 

March 12, 2026, Memorandum

 

The March 2026 memo claims $551,014.69 in new costs incurred within the last two years, consisting entirely of attorneys’ fees. This is in addition to $532,120.62 of purported allowed costs. The memo also acknowledges $2,953,041.42 in payments from JD, with $1,459,929.41 in credit against accrued interest, and $1,493,112.01 in credit against the principal. The memo asserts that the judgment has a remaining principal of $1,792,357.63 and accrued interest of $100,515.58.

 

JD again argues that the requested fee costs are non-descript and excessive. The Court concurs that the request for $551,014.69 in attorneys’ fees for the last two years of enforcement efforts are likely excessive. The motion is therefore properly brought and JC has the burden to justify the sought fees. JC only attempts to present evidence of $65,250.50 in fees incurred during the two years preceding the March 2026 memo. Specifically, JC submits evidence of the following two categories of fees:

 

(a) $35,520.50 in post-November 11, 2025 enforcement costs (representing 50.10 hours of work performed by Saul Ewing LLP and Wolf Wallenstein P.C. through the March 12, 2026, filing of the operative Memorandum of Costs); and

 

(b) $29,730.00 in additional enforcement costs (representing 42.00 hours of work performed by Saul Ewing LLP and Wolf Wallenstein P.C.) incurred between March 13, 2026 and the May 18, 2026, hearing.

 

As to the post-November 11, 2025, enforcement costs, JC submits competent evidence of $35,520.50 in reasonably incurred attorneys’ fees. This includes work on : (1) reconciliation work performed in coordination with the Chapter 7 Trustee’s counsel; (2) preparation and filing of YL’s March 11, 2026 Reply in Support of the Receiver’s Sale Confirmation Motion;  (3) preparation for and appearance at the March 17, 2026 sale confirmation hearing, including participation in the court-supervised overbid process and bidding on behalf of YL and (4) initial review of, and preparation of the preliminary response to, Feld’s March 25, 2026 Motion to Tax Costs. (Cummiskey Decl. ¶ 4.) As to the additional enforcement costs of $29,730.00, JC explains that she incurred these costs between March 13, 2026, and May 18, 2026. (Cummiskey Decl., ¶ 5.) Generally, such fees associated are compensable and appear reasonably incurred based on the time records.

 

JC is otherwise not entitled to any other costs claimed by the subject March 2026 Memo. The claimed sum of $508,053.22 apparently includes fees which were incurred more than two years prior to the filing and service of the subject Memo. Any fees incurred prior to March 12, 2024, are not permitted on the subject Memo. (CCP § 685.070(b).) 

 

Accordingly, the motion to tax is GRANTED in part. The Court finds allowable costs of $65,250.50. The remainder of the costs claimed by the March 2026 memo is taxed.

 

JD also moves to strike the interest calculations. In light of the ruling on the March 2025 memorandum of costs, the interest calculations of the March 2026 memo are incorrect. The statutory scheme does not show that the interest calculations in a memo are binding in the same sense as the claimed costs in a memo. (CCP § 685.010-685.030 [explaining how interest accrues].) CCP § 685.070 does not address attacking any interest calculations. Thus, it is uncertain whether JD may move to tax or correct the interest claimed in the memo of costs. Interest calculations may presumably be performed at any time to determine the total judgment, based on the judgment, allowed costs (which become part of the judgment) and credits (including proof of payment), irrespective of the principal/interest calculations on cost memos.

 

Additionally, the request for credit does not need to be resolved on these motions to tax. The proper vehicle for such a demand would be a motion for partial satisfaction of judgment. (CCP § 724.110.)
