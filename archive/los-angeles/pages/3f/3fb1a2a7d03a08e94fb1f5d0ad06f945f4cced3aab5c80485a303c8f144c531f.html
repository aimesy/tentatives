     CASE NO.: 24VECV02637

 

ORDER GRANTING DEFENDANTS’ MOTION TO TAX COSTS

 

I.              BACKGROUND 

Daniel Drake filed this lemon law action against Galpin Jaguar Lincoln-Mercury, Inc., d/b/a Jaguar Van Nuys, Land Rover Van Nuys, Galpin Aston Martin, Galpin Lotus (“LRVN”) and Jaguar Land Rover North America, LLC (“JLRNA”) (jointly, “Defendants”). Defendants move to tax Plaintiff’s costs.

II.           PROCEDURAL HISTORY

On June 5, 2024, Plaintiff filed a Complaint.

On June 24, 2025, Plaintiff filed a Notice of Settlement.

On July 29, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Memorandum of Costs.

On August 17, 2026, JLRNA filed the instant Motion to Tax Costs.

On September 8, 2026, Plaintiff filed an Opposition.

On September 14, 2026, JLRNA filed a Reply.

III.        LEGAL STANDARD

The Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act authorizes recovery of costs “reasonably incurred by the buyer.” (Civ. Code § 1794(d).) [1]  “If the items appearing in a cost bill appear to be proper charges, the burden is on the party seeking to tax costs to show that they were not reasonable or necessary.” (Ladas v. California State Auto. Assn. (1993) 19 Cal. App. 4th 761, 774.) “On the other hand, if the items are properly objected to, they are put in issue, and the burden of proof is on the party claiming them as costs.” (Id.) 

“[T]he mere filing of a motion to tax costs may be a ‘proper objection’ to an item, the necessity of which appears doubtful, or which does not appear to be proper on its face. [citation.] However, ‘[i]f the items appear to be proper charges, the verified memorandum is prima facie evidence that the costs, expenses and services therein listed were necessarily incurred by the defendant [citations], and the burden of showing that an item is not properly chargeable or is unreasonable is upon the [objecting party].’” (Nelson v. Anderson (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 111, 131.)  

IV.         ANALYSIS

JLRNA moves to tax costs in the entire amount of $2,020.70 on grounds Plaintiff did not timely serve its Memorandum of Costs and Motion for Attorney’s Fees. JLRNA cites Rules of Court, rule 3.1702(b)(1), which governs deadlines for a motion for attorney’s fees, and rule 3.1700(a)(1), which governs deadlines for a memorandum of costs. (See Mot. at 2:17-3:1.) JLRNA argues because Plaintiff voluntarily stipulated to dismiss the case without prejudice on November 10, 2025, his deadline to file and serve its Memorandum of Costs and Motion for Attorney’s Fees seeking costs, was May 11, 2026. (See Mot. at 3:10-20, citing Matthew Hatlegiv v. General Motors LLC (2026) 118 Cal.App.5th 644, 649.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues the November 10, 2025, dismissal did not trigger the 180-day deadline because the Court dismissed the action without prejudice pursuant to § 664.6 and consistent with the parties’ 998 Offer. (See Opp. at 3:9-17.) Plaintiff argues § 664.6 expressly permits a court to dismiss an action without prejudice and retain jurisdiction “until performance in full of the terms of the settlement.” (See Mot. at 3:9-17.) Plaintiff submits a copy of the 998 Offer providing for Plaintiff’s “recoverable court costs, expenses, and reasonably incurred attorney fees pursuant to Civil Code section 1794(d) to be determined by the Court by way of a noticed motion...” (Declaration of Laura E. Goolsby filed July 29, 2026, ¶ 13, Ex. C at ¶ 1.) Plaintiff also points out the 998 Offer provides “Upon receipt of the Settlement Amount and Attorney’s Fees Amount, Plaintiff will execute and file a request for dismissal with prejudice of the Action in its entirety.” (Id., ¶ 13, Ex. C at ¶ 6.) Plaintiff argues Hatlevig is distinguishable because the dismissal in that action occurred in 2023, before the current version of § 664.6 became operative. (See Mot. at 4:9-17.) Plaintiff argues even if the Court concludes Rule 3.1700 precludes recovery of costs through the Memorandum of Costs, Plaintiff can still separately recover costs pursuant to Civil Code § 1794(d). (See id., at 5:1-4.)

In reply, JLRNA argues Plaintiff provides no authority that Rules 3.1702 and 3.1700 do not apply to Code of Civil Procedure § 1794(d). JLRNA argues the Hatlevig Court also dismissed the complaint without prejudice and the parties in that case agreed that fees would be determined upon noticed motion. JLRNA argues Plaintiff does not explain why the dismissal pursuant to § 664.6 is material. (See id., at 1:15-27.)

The Court will grant the Motion. Rules of Court, rule 3.1700, provides “A prevailing party who claims costs must serve and file a memorandum of costs within 15 days after…the date of service of written notice of entry of judgment or dismissal, or within 180 days after entry of judgment, whichever is first.”

Rules of Court, rule 3.1702, governs claims for statutory attorney fees in civil cases. “A notice of motion to claim attorney's fees for services up to and including the rendition of judgment in the trial court-including attorney’s fees on an appeal before the rendition of judgment in the trial court-must be served and filed within the time for filing a notice of appeal under rules 8.104 and 8.108 in an unlimited civil case or under rules 8.822 and 8.823 in a limited civil case.” (Rules of Court, rule 3.1702(b)(1).) The clock starts to run on the time to move for attorney fees from either the service of notice of entry of judgment or dismissal, starting a 60-day clock under the rule governing attorney fees, or, if no such notice is given, the entry of judgment or dismissal, starting a 180-day clock under the rule. (See Hatlevig v. General Motors LLC (2026) 118 Cal.App.5th 644, 649 [discussing rule 3.1702(b)(1)].) “A voluntary dismissal is ‘effectively a “judgment”’ within the meaning of the rule prescribing the time to appeal.” (Id.)

Hatlevig recognized that “[plaintiff] had at most 180 days after the dismissal to serve and file his motion.” (Id., at 650; see Catlin Ins. Co., Inc. v. Danko Meredith Law Firm, Inc. (2022) 73 Cal.App.5th 764, 781 [“Rule 3.1702(b)(1) and (c)(1) specify a bifurcated set of deadlines that establish an ‘an outer time limit after judgment within which statutory fee claims [may] be made.’…These deadlines are triggered by the ‘conclusion of the litigation’”]; Carpenter v. Jack in the Box Corp. (2007) 151 Cal.App.4th 454, 468 [“The history of rule 3.1702 indicates that the “outside” time limit for claiming prejudgment statutory attorney fees was intended to be entry of a final judgment—not entry of a prejudgment appealable order.”]

As an initial matter, because parties in lemon law actions generally agree to determine fees and costs by way of a noticed motion, as occurred here, the Court generally does not strictly apply the requirement that a memorandum of costs must be filed and that it be subject to the 15-day deadline in rule 3.1700 deadline since the parties arguably conceded to not follow the standard memorandum procedure for requesting costs. However, as the parties agreed to determine fees and costs by noticed motion and Plaintiff’s fee motion requests costs, the Court does apply the deadline in rule 3.1702 to determine whether Plaintiff’s requests for costs is untimely. Accordingly, although this briefing is brought with respect to Plaintiff’s claimed costs, the Court’s ruling on timeliness here applies the same to the pending Motion for Attorney’s Fees, which is the agreed upon method for determining costs in this lemon law action.

The Court finds Hatlevig is controlling. In Hatlevig, the parties reached settlement, agreed that plaintiff was the prevailing party for purposes of attorney fees, and agreed that defendant would pay plaintiff the amount of fees determined by the trial court upon noticed motion. (See Hatlevig, 118 Cal.App.5th at 646.) Because the plaintiff served the motion beyond the 180-day deadline specified by Rules of Court, rules 3.1702 and 8.104(a)(1), the Court of Appeal affirmed the motion for attorney fees was untimely. (See id., at 650.)

Plaintiff’s reliance on Code of Civil Procedure § 664.6 as a basis to depart from the requirements of rule 3.1702(b)(1), is not persuasive. Plaintiff cites no authority establishing  § 664.6 creates an exception to the rule governing attorney fee motions. Hatlevig did not address § 664.6 in any respect, and Plaintiff offers no support for the assumption the settlement in Hatlevig omitted a § 664.6 provision, or that its presence or absence would had any bearing on that court’s analysis. Accordingly, that this Court retained jurisdiction to enforce the parties’ settlement, including an attorneys’ fee dispute, does not disturb rule 3.1702’s deadline. Though the Court retained jurisdiction under § 664.6 to hear and decide an attorneys’ fees motion as provided for by the terms of a settlement agreement, a party must still timely file its motion.

Here, the date of dismissal for purposes of starting the clock on the time Plaintiff had to seek costs was November 10, 2025, when the Court dismissed the action without prejudice pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure § 664.6. (See November 10, 2025, Minute Order [notice waived].) Plaintiff filed and served its Memorandum of Costs and Motion for Attorney’s Fees on July 29, 2026. Because Plaintiff had at most 180 days to file his motion seeking fees and costs and the Memorandum of Costs was filed 261 days after the date of dismissal, Plaintiff’s request for costs is untimely.

Based on the foregoing, the Court GRANTS JLRNA’s Motion to Tax Costs.

V.            CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, the Court the Court GRANTS JLRNA’s Motion to Tax Costs.

IT IS SO ORDERED.

ORDER DENYING DEFENDANT GREGORY A MOGAB’S MOTION TO SET ASIDE DEFAULT AND DEFAULT JUDGMENT

 

I.              BACKGROUND 

Plaintiff JPMorgan Chase Bank N.A. (“Plaintiff”) filed this breach of contract action against Defendants Gregory A Mogab (“Mogab”) and Gregory A Mogab, A Professional Accountancy Corporation (“Mogab Corporation”) (jointly, “Defendants”). Plaintiff alleged Mogab was the guarantor for the loan Plaintiff made to Mogab Corporation, the borrower.

The Court entered default judgment against Defendants on August 19, 2025.

Mogab moves to set aside default and default judgment entered against him.

II.           PROCEDURAL HISTORY

On April 24, 2025, Plaintiff filed a Complaint for (1) Breach of Contract and (2) Breach of Guaranty.

On June 10, 2025, the Court entered default against Defendants.

On August 19, 2025, the Court entered default judgment against Defendants.

On August 18, 2026, Mogab filed the instant Motion.

On September 4, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Notice of Stay of Proceedings.

III.        LEGAL STANDARD

A.            Discretionary or Mandatory Relief – Code of Civil Procedure § 473(b)

“The court may, upon any terms as may be just, relieve a party or his or her legal representative from a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against him or her through his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect. Application for this relief shall be accompanied by a copy of the answer or other pleading proposed to be filed therein, otherwise the application shall not be granted, and shall be made within a reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months, after the judgment, dismissal, order, or proceeding was taken…” (Code Civ. Proc. § 473(b).) [1] 

Code of Civil Procedure § 473(b) “provides for both discretionary and mandatory relief. [Citation.]” (Pagnini v. Union Bank, N.A. (2018) 28 Cal.App.5th 298, 302.) For discretionary relief, “[t]he court may, upon any terms as may be just, relieve a party or his or her legal representative from a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against him or her through his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect. Application for this relief shall be accompanied by a copy of the answer or other pleading proposed to be filed therein, otherwise the application shall not be granted, and shall be made within a reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months, after the judgment, dismissal, order, or proceeding was taken.”¿(Code Civ. Proc. § 473(b).)  

A moving party need only establish “mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect” by a preponderance of the evidence. (Luz v. Lopes (1960) 55 Cal.2d 54, 62.) Excusable neglect exists where counsel or the party acted as a reasonably prudent person would under the circumstances. (See Hearn v. Howard (2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 1193, 1206; See also Zamora v. Clayborn Contracting Group, Inc. (2002) 28 Cal.4th 249, 258.)

An application for discretionary or mandatory relief must be made no more than six months after entry of the judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding from which relief is sought. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 473(b); see also English v. IKON Bus. Solutions (2001) 94 Cal.App.4th 130, 143.)

B.            Void Judgment or Order – Code of Civil Procedure § 473(d)

Code of Civil Procedure § 473(d) provides, “[t]he court may… on motion of either party after notice to the other party, set aside any void judgment or order.” (Code Civ. Proc. §473(d); see also Zisk v. City of Roseville (1976) 56 Cal.App.3d 41, 47; accord Bastajian v. Brown (1941) 19 Cal.2d 209, 216; Baird v. Smith (1932) 216 Cal. 408, 411.) “[T]he trial court ‘has no statutory power under section 473, subdivision (d) to set aside a judgment [or order] that is not void....” (Pittman v. Beck Park Apartments Ltd. (2018) 20 Cal.App.5th 1009, 1020 [internal citations omitted].)

A judgment may be void for lack of improper service of summons. A motion for relief from a judgment valid on its face, but void for improper service, must be brought with a “reasonable” time after the default judgment. (See California Capital Ins. Co. v. Hoehn (2024) 17 Cal.5th 207, 212, 225-226 [disapproving prior authority that applied 2-year time limit to § 473(d) motions to vacate].) In contrast, there is no time limit on a motion for relief under § 473 where it is clear from the face of the record that the judgment should not have been entered. (See Plotitsa v. Superior Ct. (Kadri) (1983) 140 Cal.App.3d 755, 761.)

C.            Lack of Actual Notice - Code of Civil Procedure § 473.5

Code of Civil Procedure § 473.5  permits the Court to vacate a default when service of a summons has not resulted in actual notice to a party in time to defend the action and a default or default judgment has been entered against him. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 473.5(a).) “The notice of motion shall be served and filed within a reasonable time, but in no event exceeding the earlier of: (i) two years after entry of a default judgment against him or her; or (ii) 180 days after service… of a written notice that the default or default judgment has been entered.” (Id.)

A defendant is entitled to relief under § 473.5 if defendant has not received actual notice of the proceedings. Imputed or constructive notice is not “actual” notice. (See Rosenthal v. Garner (1983) 142 Cal.App.3d 891, 895.)

The phrase actual notice means genuine knowledge and does not include constructive or imputed notice to the client. (See Tunis v. Barrow (1986) 184 Cal.App.3d 1069, 1077.) “A defendant seeking vacation of a default judgment entered against him must further show that his lack of actual notice in time to defend the action was not caused by his inexcusable neglect or avoidance of service.” (Id. at 1077-1078, citing Code Civ. Proc. § 473.5(c).) Additionally, “the party shall serve and file with the notice a copy of the answer, motion, or other pleading proposed to be filed in the action.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 473.5(b).)

IV.         ANALYSIS

Mogab moves to set aside default and default judgment entered against him pursuant to § 473(b), § 473(d), and § 473.5 on grounds that he was not personally served. (See Mot. at 4:6-10.) Mogab attests he departed the country to permanently reside in the Philippines on October 2, 2024, and hasn’t lived in this country since. (See Declaration of Gregory Mogab filed August 18, 2025, ¶ 2.) Mogab attests he was not in the country on the service date, and he did not live nor was present at the service address on the service date. (See id., ¶¶ 3-4.) Mogab attests “If I overlooked any notice relating to this case, it was due purely to inadvertence due to the difficulty of keeping track of business and financial matters from overseas.” (Id., ¶ 6.) Mogab attests he first learned of this action while attempting to settle another case and learned an abstract of judgment had been recorded related to this case. (See id., ¶ 7.)

As an initial matter, the Court notes this application is brought on behalf of Mogab and not Mogab Corporation.[2]

Next, the Court finds Mogab has not shown he is entitled to relief. Here, the proof of service shows Mogab was personally served on April 29, 2025, at 19136 Harliss St. (See Proof of Service filed May 2, 2025.) A proof of service containing a declaration from a registered process server invokes a rebuttable presumption of valid service. (See American Express Centurion Bank v. Zara (2011) 199 Cal.App.4th 383, 390; see also Evid. Code § 647.) The party seeking to defeat service of process must present sufficient evidence to show that the service did not take place as stated. (See Palm Property Investments, LLC v. Yadegar (2011) 194 Cal.App.4th 1419, 1428.) Merely denying service took place without more is insufficient to overcome the presumption. (See id., at 1428; Bonzer v. City of Huntington Park (1993) 20 Cal.App.4th 1474, 1481, 25 Cal.Rptr.2d 278 [upon credible evidence that document was never received, presumption of receipt from mailing “ceased to exist”].) Moreover, the court is “not required to accept…self-serving evidence contradicting the process server’s declaration.” (Rodriguez v. Nam Min Cho (2015) 236 Cal.App.4th 742, 751.)

Mogab’s declaration merely denies he was personally served on April 29, 2025. This evidence, without more, is insufficient to overcome the presumption established by the registered process server’s declaration he personally served Mogab on April 29, 2025. Mogab’s insufficient evidence neither shows the proof of service is void nor that he did not have actual notice of this action; thus Mogab has not shown he is entitled to relief under § 473(d) or § 473.5.

Mogab also does not show he is entitled to relief under § 473(b). Mogab’s general reference to overlooking any notice is insufficient to establish grounds for discretionary relief. Mogab identifies no specific act of neglect for which he had a reasonable excuse. Thus, the Court finds Mogab has not shown he is entitled to relief under § 473(b).

Based on the foregoing, the Court DENIES Mogab’s Motion to Set Aside Default and Default Judgment.

V.            CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, the Court DENIES Mogab’s Motion to Set Aside Default and Default Judgment.

         IT IS SO ORDERED.

III.        LEGAL STANDARD

The function of a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party cannot show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. (See Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) In analyzing such motions, courts must apply a three-step analysis: “(1) identify the issues framed by the pleadings; (2) determine whether the moving party has negated the opponent’s claims; and (3) determine whether the opposition has demonstrated the existence of a triable, material factual issue.” (Hinesley v. Oakshade Town Center (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 289, 294.) Thus, summary judgment is granted when, after the Court’s consideration of the evidence set forth in the papers and all reasonable inferences accordingly, no triable issues of fact exist, and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 437c(c);[1] Villa v. McFarren (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 733, 741.)¿ 

“On a motion for summary judgment, the initial burden is always on the moving party to make a prima facie showing that there are no triable issues of material fact.” (Scalf v. D.B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1519.) As to each claim as framed by the complaint, a defendant moving for summary judgment must satisfy the initial burden of proof by presenting facts to negate or establish an essential element. (See Scalf v. D.B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1520.) Courts “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.” (Dore v. Arnold Worldwide, Inc.¿(2006) 39 Cal.4th 384, 389.) A motion for summary judgment must be denied where the moving party’s evidence does not prove all material facts, even in the absence of any opposition. (See Leyva v. Superior Court (1985) 164 Cal.App.3d 462, 475; Salesguevara v. Wyeth Labs., Inc. (1990) 222 Cal.App.3d 379, 384.)

Once the moving party has met its burden, the burden shifts to the opposing party to show via specific facts that a triable issue of material fact exists to a cause of action or a defense thereto. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 437c(o)(2).)

V.            MARMALADE’S MOTION IS DENIED

1.              Evidentiary Objections

The Court rules as follows on Marmalade’s objections to the Declaration of Philip L. Rosescu, P.E., M.S.:

Sustained: Nos. 2, 3, 5, and 7-11.

Overruled: Nos. 1, 4, and 6.

2.             Moving Defendants’ Undisputed Material Facts

Plaintiffs allege they sustained injuries at Marmalade Café, operated by Marmalade, located at 4783 Commons Way, Suite E, Calabasas, California 91302 on May 9, 2024. (See UMF No. 1, 30.) Plaintiffs’ negligence and premises liability claims allege before they sat down at their table, Carol tripped on the subject condition —a dirt hole—causing Nelson to fall when he tried to prevent her fall. (See id., Nos. 2-8.)

Carol was in the walkway, past the alleged condition, before she turned around. (See id., Nos. 11-12.) Nelson had not yet reached the alleged condition when Carol had already walked past it. (See id., No. 13.) When Nelson stopped, the alleged condition was approximately one foot in front of him and to his left. (See id., No. 14.) When Carol turned to look for Nelson immediately before the fall, she was not looking down. (See id., No. 15.) Nelson did not step into or fall into the alleged condition. (See id., No. 27.) Nelson attempted to catch Carol as she was falling but could not catch her because of the momentum of her fall, which pushed Nelson backward and caused him to fall. (See id., Nos. 28-29.)  Plaintiffs had no knowledge that anyone else had fallen into the alleged condition before her accident. (See id., Nos. 22-23.)

Before the May 9, 2024, incident, Marmalade had received no prior complaints or had records of injury concerning the alleged patio condition. (See id., Nos. 24-25.) Before the May 9, 2024, incident, no employee, manager, customer, vendor, or third party reported to Marmalade the alleged patio condition posed a tripping hazard. (See id., No. 26.)

The restaurant includes an outdoor flagstone patio dining area. (See id., No. 31.) The condition Plaintiffs describe as a “dirt hole in the patio dining area” is a tree well/circular depression in the patio remaining from the removal of a patio tree. (See id., No. 32.)

Marmalade regularly inspects its outdoor patio area as part of its normal operations, and before the May 9, 2024, incident, those inspections did not identify the area described by Plaintiffs as unsafe, hazardous, or in need of repair or warning. (See id., No. 38.) Before May 9, 2024, Marmalade had no record of any request, work order, or directive to repair, fill, cover, barricade, or warn about the patio condition identified by Plaintiffs. (See id., No. 39.) The May 9, 2024, incident was the first complaint or report of any kind that Marmalade received concerning the area of the patio identified by Plaintiffs. (See id., No. 40.) Exhibit A to the Arnold Declaration is a photograph of the subject tree well taken on May 9, 2024, shortly after the incident, and it accurately depicts the tree well. (See id., No. 41.)

3.             Plaintiff’s Additional Material Facts

Plaintiffs were led to their table by a host, with Carol walking in front of Nelson. (See Plf.’s Add.’l Material Facts (“AMF”), No. 45.) When they arrived at the table, Carol then turned around to her left to face Nelson behind her. As she turned and stepped towards Nelson, her right foot fell into a circular dirt hole in the patio area she had not seen. (See id., Nos. 46-47, 55.) Nelson had not reached the hole yet and was about one foot from it. (See id., No. 48.) Nelson fell when Carol fell forward onto him. (See id., Nos. 49-51.)

No verbal warning by a Marmalade employee was given to Plaintiffs regarding the hole and no visual warning or signage was present to warn of the hole or instruct diners to walk a particular path or direction within the patio area. (See id., No. 56.) The dirt in the hole was not backfilled flush with the surrounding landscape, creating an approximate 1- to 2-inch height differential between the dirt surface and the surrounding grade. (See id., No. 57.) Only a tree stump and small unused electrical box remained in the dirt hole. (See id., No. 58.) The dirt and tree stump within the hole were both a similar brownish color to the flagstone of the patio and was shaded. (See id., Nos. 59-60.)

About six months before the incident, Director of Operations, Lindsay Arnold, the restaurant manager, and the landlord engaged in discussions of options to address the hole, including simply covering it with the same type of flagstone that existed on the patio area. This work was never performed prior to the date of the incident. (See id., No. 62.) After Plaintiffs fell and were injured, Marmalade, LLC filled the hole with bright, contrasting white rocks and a large potted plant. (See id., No. 63.) Marmalade, LLC did not need approval from the landlord to fix the area as it did. (See id., No. 64.)

4.             Negligence and Premises Liability Causes of Action

The elements of a cause of action for negligence and for premises liability are the same: a duty of care, a breach of such duty, an injury, and the breach as the proximate cause of such injury. (See Jones v. Awad (2019) 39 Cal.App.5th 1200, 1207.) “The owner of premises is under a duty to exercise ordinary care in the management of such premises in order to avoid exposing persons to an unreasonable risk of harm. A failure to fulfill this duty is negligence.” (Brooks v. Eugene Burger Management Corp. (1989) 215 Cal.App.3d 1611, 1619.) “To comply with this duty, a person who controls property must inspect [the premises] or take other proper means to ascertain their condition and, if a dangerous condition exists that would have been discovered by the exercise of reasonable care, has a duty to give adequate warning of or remedy it.” (Staats v. Vintner's Golf Club, LLC (2018) 25 Cal.App.5th 826, 833.)

 “[B]reach of duty is usually a fact issue for the jury. If the circumstances permit a reasonable doubt whether the defendant’s conduct violates the standard of due care, the doubt must be resolved by the jury as an issue of fact rather than of law by the court. Like breach of duty, causation also is ordinarily a question of fact which cannot be resolved by summary judgment. The issue of causation may be decided as a question of law only if, under undisputed facts, there is no room for a reasonable difference of opinion.” (Nichols v. Keller (1993) 15 Cal. App. 4th 1672, 1687.)

         Marmalade argues there was no duty of care because the tree well was open and obvious and it was not necessary for Plaintiffs to encounter it. (See Mot. at 11:8-17; Reply 4:8-9, 5:18-25.) Marmalade submits evidence in the form of a photograph of the tree well. (See UMF, No. 41.) The image depicts Carol’s legs with her two feet inside the tree well. The tree well is a circular depression in the ground containing a tree stump, dirt, a few weeds, and a metal box fixture. Marmalade cites Gonzalez v. Mathis (2021) 12 Cal.5th 29, 40, 50, in support of its position the tree well was an open and obvious condition. (See Mot. at 11:22-24.) Second, Marmalade also argues there was no breach. (See Mot. at 12: 18.) Marmalade submits the undisputed facts that Carol had already walked past the tree well in approaching their table and that from Nelson’s position, the tree well was to his left. (See UMF Nos. 12-14.) On these facts, Marmalade argues it had no liability to ensure that a patron who had already walked past a visible condition would not step into it if she turned around while not looking at the ground. (See Mot., at 12:18-24; Reply at 4:13-16.) Marmalade also submits the undisputed fact its inspections had not identified the tree well as unsafe. (See Mot., at 13:1-3; UMF No. 38.)

         Third, Marmalade argues Plaintiffs’ claims fail for lack of notice. (See Mot. at 13:4-7, citing Ortega v. Kmart Corp. (2001) 26 Cal.4th 1200, 1206–1207.) Marmalade submits evidence showing they had received no complaints about the tree well, recorded a prior accident, or received notice of its potential danger from any employee, manager, customer, or otherwise. (See UMF Nos. 22-26.) Fourth, Marmalade argues causation cannot be established because but for Carol voluntarily turning around and failing to look down, she would not have tripped on the tree well. (See Mot. at 14:15-26; UMF No. 11-12, 15.)

Fifth, Marmalade argues Nelson’s claim fails because he did not contact the tree well and his claim is derivative of Carol’s. (See Mot. at 15:4-6; Reply at 9:26-10:5 UMF Nos. 27-29.)

“Whether a duty should be imposed on a defendant [in a premises liability action] depends on a variety of policy considerations, known as the Rowland factors.” (Jacobs v. Coldwell Banker Residential Brokerage Co. (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 446.) The “most important” of these considerations or factors is “the foreseeability of injury to another.” (Osborn v. Mission Ready Mix (1990) 224 Cal.App.3d 104, 122) “Foreseeability of harm is typically absent when a dangerous condition is open and obvious.” (Id., at 447.) “‘Generally, if a danger is so obvious that a person could reasonably be expected to see it, the condition itself serves as a warning, and the landowner is under no further duty to remedy or warn of the condition.’” (Montes v. Young Men's Christian Assn. of Glendale, Cal. (2022) 81 Cal.App.5th 1134, 1140.) “There may be a duty of care owed even where a dangerous condition is open and obvious when ‘it is foreseeable that the danger may cause injury despite the fact that it is obvious (e.g., when necessity requires persons to encounter it).’” (Id.) 

In Chance v. Lawry's, Inc. (1962) 58 Cal.2d 368, 372-373, the Supreme Court rejected Lawry's argument the planter box located in the foyer at the entrance to its restaurant “was so obvious that [defendant] could reasonably anticipate that patrons would see and apprehend the danger [of losing their balance and falling into the planter box as did plaintiff].” (Id., 58 Cal.2d at 374.) The Supreme Court stated: “Whether the danger created by the open planter box was sufficiently obvious to relieve Lawry's of its duty to warn [the plaintiff] of its existence was peculiarly a question of fact to be determined by the jury.” (Id.) In Chance, the Supreme Court also rejected defendant’s argument plaintiff was contributorily negligent because if she had looked, they would have seen the planter box. (See id., 58 Cal.2d at 374-375.) The Supreme Court reasoned: “It cannot be said as a matter of law that Mrs. Chance was contributively negligent for not seeing the planter box or in not looking back before she stepped backward... Whether she made a reasonable use of her faculties and acted as a reasonable person, under the circumstances, was a factual question for the jury's determination.” (Id., 58 Cal.2d at 375.)

The Court will deny Marmalade’s Motion. The Court finds Marmalade does not meet its burden of showing the tree well Carol tripped on was open and obvious or not necessary to encounter as a matter of law. Marmalade’s evidence on the open and obvious issue is one photograph. (See UMF, No. 41.) The Court finds this single photograph insufficient to meet Marmalade’s burden. While this photograph offers a close-up of the tree well on the ground there are no other photographs depicting other vantage points including Plaintiffs’ eye-level as they approached their table or a more distant perspective showing where the tree well lies in relation to surrounding tables. [2] This single photograph is also insufficient to show the tree well wasn’t necessary to encounter, as the photograph’s close-up image of the tree well does not establish it was not located on any path leading to any table patrons walked to and from upon arrival, departure, to use the restroom, or for other reasons. This photograph only depicts the base of one table. Finally, the Court is unclear on whether this was the table Plaintiffs were walking to so as to have a perspective on their path to their table as there is no undisputed material fact on this point.

Assuming arguendo Marmalade met its burden on these issues, Plaintiffs raise triable issues on the issue of open/obvious and necessity. Plaintiffs’ evidence includes more photographs with a few additional perspectives from which a trier of fact could conclude that the tree well is not open and obvious to a patron approaching their dining table because it blends in with the stone covered floor in the patio and is in the center of various tables. (See Disputed Fact (“DF”), No. 10; Opp at 12:8-14.) As to the issue of necessity, the Court also finds a trier of fact could find based on Plaintiffs’ photographs showing the tree well is located in the middle of several tables and evidence there is no one single path to reach the table, that the tree well could have been necessarily encountered any time a patron approached or left the table. (See DF, Nos. 9-10; AMF No. 45; Opp. at 12:15-24.) Thus, the Court finds Marmalade has not established its open and obvious defense to negate duty as a matter of law.

The Court also finds Marmalade does not negate lack of notice. “Because the owner is not the insurer of the visitor's personal safety [citation], the owner's actual or constructive knowledge of the dangerous condition is a key to establishing its liability. Although the owner's lack of knowledge is not a defense, ‘[t]o impose liability for injuries suffered by an invitee due to [a] defective condition of the premises, the owner or occupier ‘must have either actual or constructive knowledge of the dangerous condition or have been able by the exercise of ordinary care to discover the condition, which if known to him, he should realize as involving an unreasonable risk to invitees on his premises....’” (Ortega, 26 Cal.4th at 1206 [internal citation omitted, emphasis added].) Marmalade’s undisputed facts establish it was on notice of the tree well, including through its own inspections; it just did not have notice of any prior accidents or was informed that the tree well posed a trip and fall hazard. (See UMF, No. 38-40.) However, it is not dispositive that Marmalade was not informed that the tree well posed a risk. The Court finds Marmalade there are triable issues of fact as to whether it should have reasonably appreciated the risk the tree well posed to patrons as it knew of the existence of the tree well, its proximity to tables, that the well itself and its contents were clearly not level with the stone floor, and that it was not visually demarcated by anything so as to alert passerby. (See Opp. at 15:5-6; cf. Jones v. Awad (2019) 39 Cal.App.5th 1200 [plaintiff didn’t establish a discernible feature of the garage steps that would notify defendants of an unreasonable risk of harm or any prior incidents that would alert defendants to a dangerous condition’s existence].)

Assuming arguendo Marmalade did meet its burden on this issue, Plaintiffs raise triable issues showing the tree well posed a risk. (See AMF Nos. 55, 57.) In addition to the photographs depicting the tree well and its contents, Plaintiffs submitted the affidavit of Philip L. Rosescu, a Licensed Professional Engineer in the discipline of Civil Engineering and has extensive experience evaluating trip-and-fall incidents involving walkway surface discontinuities and changes in level. (See id.) Rosescu attests the tree well possessed “an approximate 1- to 2-inch height differential between the dirt surface and the surrounding grade” which was “large enough to cause a misstep”. (See id.) This evidence shows that the tree well was depressed by a certain number of inches that sufficiently posed a trip risk.

As to Marmalade’s argument Carol caused her own injury by failing to look down to observe the ground when she turned around, Chance is on point. In Chance, the Supreme Court also rejected a similar argument that “[the plaintiff] was contributively negligent for not seeing the planter box or in not looking back before she stepped backward.” (See Chance, 58 Cal.2d 368, at 374-75.) The Supreme Court reasoned: “It cannot be said as a matter of law that [plaintiff] was contributively negligent for not seeing the planter box or in not looking back before she stepped backward...Whether she made a reasonable use of her faculties and acted as a reasonable person, under the circumstances, was a factual question for the jury's determination.” (Id., at 375.) Accordingly, the question of the extent to which Carol was contributively negligent in her fall, is at least a jury question, as the Court cannot find that it can only draw the single inference from the undisputed evidence that Carol was solely responsible for her fall.

For the reasons above, the Court also finds Marmalade does not meet its burden in negating Nelson’s claims based on the argument they necessarily fail because Carol’s fail.

         Based on the foregoing, the Court DENIES Marmalade’s Motion for Summary Judgment or, in the alternative, Summary Adjudication.

VI.         CONCLUSION

The Court DENIES Marmalade’s Motion for Summary Judgment or, in the alternative, Summary Adjudication.
