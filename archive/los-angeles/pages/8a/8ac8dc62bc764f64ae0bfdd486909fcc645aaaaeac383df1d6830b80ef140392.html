PLAINTIFF BRITECAP FINANCIAL, LLC’S MOTION FOR JUDGMENT ON THE PLEADINGS

           

            Background

Plaintiff BriteCap Financial, LLC (“BriteCap”) initiated this action against Defendant Javier Mora, an individual and sole proprietor of DJ Ventura Attorney Services (“Mora”), on February 11, 2025, alleging three causes of action for (1) common counts, (2) common counts, and (3) breach of contract

Mora filed an answer on May 7, 2025.

BriteCap now moves for judgment on the pleadings. Mora did not respond.

Legal Standard

“A [plaintiff] may move for judgment on the pleadings” when “the complaint states facts sufficient to constitute a cause or causes of action against the defendant and the answer does not state facts sufficient to constitute a defense to the complaint.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 438, subds. (b)(1) and (c)(1)(A).) “A motion for judgment on the pleadings performs the same function as a general demurrer, and hence attacks only defects disclosed on the face of the pleadings or by matters that can be judicially noticed. [Citations.] Presentation of extrinsic evidence is therefore not proper on a motion for judgment on the pleadings.” (Cloud v. Northrop Grumman Corp. (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 995, 999, citations omitted.)

The standard for ruling on a motion for judgment on the pleadings is essentially the same as that applicable to a general demurrer: that under the state of the pleadings, together with matters that may be judicially noticed, it appears that a party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. (Schabarum v. California Legislature (1998) 60 Cal.App.4th 1205, 1216.) The court “must accept as true all material facts properly pleaded, but does not consider conclusions of law or fact, opinions, speculation, or allegations contrary to law or facts that are judicially noticed.” (Stevenson Real Estate Services, Inc. v. CB Richard Ellis Real Estate Services, Inc. (2006) 138 Cal.App.4th 1215, 1219-1220.)

Discussion

BriteCap moves for judgment on the pleadings on Mora’s answer to the complaint because “the entire complaint states facts sufficient to constitute causes of action against the Defendant, and the Defendant’s Answer admits to all of the facts and allegations contained therein.” (Notice of Mot., 1:24-26.) BriteCap requests a total judgment of $36,033.36, comprising $35,508.36 sought in the complaint, $435.00 for a filing fee, and $90.00 for service of process.

A.    Allegations of the Complaint

For the first cause of action, common counts, BriteCap’s complaint alleges that Mora became indebted to BriteCap within the last four years, “because an account was stated in writing by and between plaintiff and defendant in which it was agreed that defendant was indebted to plaintiff.” (Compl., p. 3, CC-1, a(2).) As for the second common counts cause of action, BriteCap alleges Mora became indebted to BriteCap within the last four years “for money lent by plaintiff to defendant at defendant’s request.” (Compl., p. 4, CC-1, b(4).) The third cause of action for breach of contract alleges that BriteCap and Mora entered into a written agreement on April 22, 2024, and on July 12, 2024, Mora breached the agreement by “[f]ailing to remit any further payments to the Plaintiff under the loan.” (Compl., p. 5, BC-1, a, BC-2.) The complaint alleges that BriteCap “performed all obligations to defendant except those obligations plaintiff was prevented or excused from performing.” (Compl., p. 5, BC-3.) BriteCap alleges it suffered damages in the amount of $35,508.36. (Compl., p. 5, BC-4.) BriteCap attached the parties’ loan agreement to the complaint. (Compl., Ex. 1.)

B.    Answer to the Complaint

            Mora’s answer to the complaint alleges the following: he is “self-employed by DJ VENTURA ATTORNEY SERVICES[.] I have first-hand knowledge of the following facts and if called upon to do so could and would testify competently to them.” (Answer, ¶ 1.) “I Admit All claims on complaint[.] On February 11, 2025, I tried to make an arrangement regarding lower payments due to lack of work we’been [sic] losing some clients at the time and before this complaint was filed , My company was directed financially damage by L.A. Fires due to this event some clients have to resined [sic].” (Answer, ¶ 2.) “For a period of time I was paying this loan for about 43% and then financially problems strocke [sic] the company. I proceeded to ask the financial teller Lamar how can we resolve this without going to court, if is possible to make some arreagments [sic] of lower monthly payments of $300.00 as to the whereabouts of the document and she informed me that they had no record of the document ever being submitted to them. The clerk then suggested that I should submit another copy of the document.” (Answer, ¶ 3.) In Mora’s prayer, he requests that “Britecap Financial, LLC deny relief requested for me to parforme [sic] on lower monthly payments in order to continue paying it off.” (Answer, 2:18-19.)

C.    Discussion

In support of the instant motion, BriteCap cites Hardy v. Admiral Oil Co. (1961) 56 Cal.2d 836. There, the trial court entered judgment for the plaintiff on the pleadings, without leave to amend. (Id. at p. 842.) The Supreme Court found that the trial court did not abuse its discretion because the defendant “had previously been given three successive opportunities to amend and thus had ample notice of the defective state of its pleadings, yet had repeatedly failed to remedy such defects.” (Ibid.) The court stated that the defendant’s failure to file a “pleading alleging that the contract as pleaded does not express the true agreement and seeking to reform it” was a “fatal admission[].” (Id. at p. 841.)

BriteCap asserts that “Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure Section 438 and case law cited above, Plaintiff is entitled to a judgment on the pleadings. Plaintiff filed its complaint on March 19, 2025 and Defendant filed its answer on May 7, 2025. In its complaint, Plaintiff sufficiently stated facts to sustain its cause of action for Account Stated, Money Lent, Open Book Account, Breach of Written Contract, and Breach of Written Guarantee. Defendant’s Answer admitted all these allegations. Therefore, judgment should be entered in favor of Plaintiff for the relief prayed for in their complaint.” (Mot., 4:16-22.)

Mora’s answer to the complaint explicitly states that “I Admit All claims on complaint.” (Answer, ¶ 2.) Mora does not allege any defenses. Additionally, BriteCap’s complaint adequately alleges its causes of action against Mora and attaches the loan agreement. Based on this, BriteCap is entitled to judgment on the pleadings. Mora did not file an opposition, so no request to amend was made.

Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, BriteCap’s motion for judgment on the pleadings is GRANTED, without leave to amend. Mora is ordered to file an amended answer, if any, within 20 days of the date of this Order. The Court will sign the proposed judgment.

BriteCap is ordered to give notice of this Order.

DEFENDANT HARRIS SHUKRAN’S MOTION TO SET ASIDE/VACATE JUDGMENT AFTER TRIAL

           

            Background

On August 28, 2025, Plaintiff 6230 Yucca Urban Housing, LLC (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Defendants Harris Shukran (“Shukran”) and Kansept c/o Shala Shukran (jointly, “Defendants”) for unlawful detainer.

On October 8, 2025, Shukran filed an answer to the complaint.

On November 19, 2025, Plaintiff filed a request to set the case for trial. Thereafter, on December 8, 2025, the Court held a non-jury trial. Plaintiff appeared, but Defendants did not.

On February 13, 2026, the Court signed a judgment in favor of Plaintiff and against Defendants, awarding Plaintiff damages in the amount of $79,622.26.

Shukran “moves for an order (1) vacating the judgment entered after the December 8, 2025 nonjury trial and reflected in the February 18, 2026 minute order; (2) recalling and quashing the writ of possession issued February 20, 2026; (3) staying enforcement of the judgment pending determination of this motion; and (4) restoring this matter to the trial calendar so Defendant may present his answer and defenses.” (Notice of Mot., 2:1-7.) Plaintiff did not file an opposition.

Legal Standard

Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b) provides in pertinent part as follows:

“The court may, upon any terms as may be just, relieve a party or his or her legal representative from a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against him or her through his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect. Application for this relief shall be accompanied by a copy of the answer or other pleading proposed to be filed therein, otherwise the application shall not be granted, and shall be made within a reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months, after the judgment, dismissal, order, or proceeding was taken. However, in the case of a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding determining the ownership or right to possession of real or personal property, without extending the six-month period, when a notice in writing is personally served within the State of California both upon the party against whom the judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding has been taken, and upon the party’s attorney of record, if any, notifying that party and the party’s attorney of record, if any, that the order, judgment, dismissal, or other proceeding was taken against the party and that any rights the party has to apply for relief under the provisions of Section 473 of the Code of Civil Procedure shall expire 90 days after service of the notice, then the application shall be made within 90 days after service of the notice upon the defaulting party or the party's attorney of record, if any, whichever service shall be later. No affidavit or declaration of merits shall be required of the moving party.”

 “[T]he moving party has the burden of showing that the neglect leading to default was excusable.” (JJackson v. Bank of Am. (1983) 141 Cal.App.3d 55, 58.) “[B]ecause the law strongly favors trial and disposition on the merits, any doubts in applying section 473 must be resolved in favor of the party seeking relief from default.” (Elston v. City of Turlock (1985) 38 Cal.3d 227, 233 [superseded by statute on other grounds as stated in Tackett v. City of Huntington Beach (1994) 22 Cal.App.4th 60, 64].) Where the party in default moves promptly to seek relief, and no prejudice to the opposing party will result from setting aside the default, “very slight evidence will be required to justify a court in setting aside the default.” (Ibid.) “While section 473 authorizes a court to relieve a party from default suffered through inadvertence, surprise, excusable neglect or mistake, ‘these words are not meaningless, and the party requesting such relief must affirmatively show that the situation is one which clearly falls within such category.’” (Kendall v. Barker, 197 Cal. App. 3d 619, 623 (1988) (emphasis in original).)

For discretionary relief, “[t]he six-month time limit for granting statutory relief is jurisdictional and the court may not consider a motion for relief made after that period has elapsed. [Citation.] The six-month period runs from entry of default, not entry of judgment.” (Manson, Iver & York v. Black (2009) 176 Cal.App.4th 36, 42.) When accompanied by an attorney declaration, the six-month clock for mandatory relief from an attorney mistake runs from the entry of default judgment. (Sugasawara v. Newland (1994) 27 Cal.App.4th 294, 296-297.)

Discussion

Shukran moves under Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b), to set aside the judgment against him due to mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect. Shukran asserts that “[t]he statute now contains a shorter 90-day period when a specified written notice concerning a judgment determining possession is personally served in California; the file should be checked for strict compliance with that provision. The public docket reflects electronic service of the February 18 notice, not personal service of the special statutory notice.” (Mot., 5:25-28.) As Shukran points out, he was not personally served with the judgment. Accordingly, the 90-day deadline for relief is inapplicable here. Rather, the six-month deadline to seek relief applies. Here, Shukran filed the instant motion on July 23, 2026, five months after entry of judgment on February 13, 2026. Thus, the motion is timely.

Shukran submits a declaration in support of the motion. In Shukran’s declaration, he attests that “[b]efore this action, Plaintiff filed an unlawful-detainer action concerning the Premises on or about June 26, 2025. That first action was voluntarily dismissed on or about August 6, 2025. [See Case No. 25STCV18551].” (Shukran Decl., ¶ 3.) Shukran explains that management locked him out of the premises on July 29, 2025, but his “personal property remained in the Premises” and he “continued to regard the Premises as my residence and intended to retain possession. I did not return all keys, execute a surrender agreement, or authorize Plaintiff to remove or dispose of my belongings.” (Shukran Decl., ¶¶ 5-6.) Shukran explains that “Plaintiff filed this second unlawful-detainer action on August 28, 2025, after management had already taken possession and excluded me from the Premises.” (Shukran Decl., ¶ 13.) Shukran further declares that he “learned of this action and filed an Answer on October 8, 2025. My Answer showed that I intended to defend the case and contest Plaintiff’s right to recover possession and the amounts claimed.” (Shukran Decl., ¶ 14.)

Shukran further attests the following: “[a]ccording to the court docket, Plaintiff filed a Request to Set Case for Trial on November 19, 2025, and the court issued an order setting trial and a certificate of mailing on November 20, 2025. The nonjury trial occurred on December 8, 2025.” (Shukran Decl., ¶ 15.) “I DID NOT RECEIVE the trial-setting notice before December 8, 2025. The address(es) at which I was receiving mail during that period were supposed to be the address I was illegally locked out of. I was homeless for a long period of time. Mail sent to the Premises was not reliably available to me because I had been excluded from the unit.” (Shukran Decl., ¶ 16.) “Had I known of the trial in time and understood that my appearance was required on December 8, 2025, I would have appeared and presented my defenses, documents, and testimony.” (Shukran Decl., ¶ 18.)

Shukran explains that he “dispute[s] the judgment. Before Plaintiff filed this action, management had already taken possession and denied me meaningful access. I therefore dispute that Plaintiff was entitled to obtain possession from me through this later action or to charge holdover damages for a period when Plaintiff had already excluded me.” (Shukran Decl., ¶ 22.) Shukran declares that he has “meritorious defenses” and “[g]ranting relief will allow the dispute to be decided on its merits. I am prepared to proceed promptly and comply with all future court dates and orders.” (Shukran Decl., ¶¶ 24-25.)

Shukran’s motion asserts that he “answered and thus demonstrated an intent to defend. Plaintiff had previously addressed Harris at Unit 707 while simultaneously asserting it had taken possession and while Harris claimed he could not enter. If the November 20 trial-setting order was mailed only to that inaccessible address, Harris’s lack of actual notice was not indifference to the action; it was a foreseeable product of the lockout and the chosen service address. Harris’s declaration must establish: (1) he did not actually receive the November 20 order before trial; (2) he had no other actual notice of the December 8 date; (3) where he was living and receiving mail; (4) whether his answer listed an email or alternate address; (5) when and how he learned of trial and judgment; and (6) why the interval before filing this motion was reasonable. These facts, if proved, constitute surprise or excusable neglect causally connected to the nonappearance.” (Opp., 7:4-15.)

Pursuant to Shukran’s sworn declaration, he demonstrates that he did not have actual notice of the non-jury trial and thus was unable to defend himself and present evidence. Shukran’s application for relief is timely, and the evidence presented demonstrates that Shukran did not intentionally decline to participate in the instant action. Based on the foregoing explanation, and in light of the lack of opposition, the Court finds that relief is proper under Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b).

Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, Shukran’s motion to, inter alia, vacate judgment is GRANTED. The Court vacates the Judgment entered against Shukran on February 13, 2026, and the Court recalls and quashes the writ of possession issued February 20, 2026. The Court sets a Case Management Conference on ___________, 2026 at 10:00 a.m. in Department 508.

Shukran is ordered to provide notice of this Order.
