DEPARTMENT M LAW AND MOTION RULINGS



Case Number: 24SMCV05144    Hearing Date: July 16, 2026    Dept: M

CASE NAME:           Greenfield v. Waste Management of California, Inc., et al.

CASE NO.:                24SMCV05144

MOTION:                  Motion to Compel Independent Medical Examination

HEARING DATE:   7/16/2026

 

Legal Standard

 

Any party may obtain discovery by means of mental or physical examination of a party to the action if the mental or physical condition of that party is in controversy in the action. (CCP § 2032.020.) In a personal injury action, a defendant is permitted to demand one physical examination of the plaintiff as long as the examination does not include “painful, protracted, or intrusive” procedures and is conducted within 75 miles of the plaintiff’s residence. (CCP §2032.220(a).) A defendant must show good cause for leave to conduct a physical examination more than 75 miles from the plaintiff’s residence. (CCP §2032.220(a), (b); Carpenter v. Superior Court (2006) 141 Cal.App.4th 249, 259.) The demand must specify the date, time, manner, conditions, scope, and nature of the examination, as well as the identity and the specialty, if any, of the physician who will perform the exam. (CCP § 2032.220(c).) Any demand shall be scheduled for a date that is at least 30 days after service of the demand, but the court may shorten this time upon motion.  (CCP § 2032.220(d).) Within 20 days after service of the demand the party to whom the demand is directed shall respond to the demand by a written statement that the party will comply as stated, comply as modified, or refuse. (CCP § 2032.230(a).)

 

            A separate statement is required to compel a “medical examination over objection.” (CRC Rule 3.1345(a)(6).)

 

ANALYSIS

 

Defendant Waste Management of California Inc. moves for an order compelling Plaintiff Nicole Greenfield to appear in Los Angeles County, California for an Orthopedic Independent Medical Examination (“IME”).

 

Defendant explains that this matter arises from an auto v. pedestrian collision on December 15, 2022, at Capri Apartments, located at 13953 Panay Way, Marina Del Rey, CA. Plaintiff alleges that she suffered injuries from the accident resulting in lower back pain, pain in both feet, left wrist pain, left forearm pain, a miscarriage, L4-S1 disc bulge, bilateral foot pain, back spasms with tenderness, reduced lumbar range of motion, lumbar facet arthropathy, lumbar strain/sprain, myofascial pain, bilateral lower extremity numbness and tingling, reduction in lumbar sensation, Qi and blood stagnation, post-traumatic Bi syndrome, tenderness in the bilateral suboccipital, tenderness in the bilateral trapezius muscles, moderate muscle spasms in the cervical spine, and tenderness in the cervical joints. Plaintiff further claims that her pain is exacerbated by basic activities of daily living such as sitting for extended periods of time, exercise, standing for extended periods of time, getting in and out of bed, and moving from sitting to standing. Plaintiff also claims to have suffered emotional injuries such as increased anxiety, depression and heightened stress levels due to her inability to sit and stand for prolonged periods resulting in an inability to work. To properly investigate and evaluate the nature and extent of her alleged injuries, Defendant must obtain an independent orthopedic evaluation. Defendant has retained Dr. Arthur Kreitenberg, M.D., a board-certified orthopedic surgeon to conduct the examination.

 

Defendant seeks an IME greater than 75 miles from Plaintiff’s residence in East Meadow New York. Defendant argues that good cause exists to order Plaintiff appear in Los Angeles for an examination, because Plaintiff elected to file this suit in Los Angeles, and thus should have to have the IME in Los Angeles. Defendant also asserts that permitting Dr. Kreitenberg to conduct the IME in Los Angeles will ensure that their preferred expert is more likely to be available at trial. Moreover, Defendant claims that it is not familiar with a qualified orthopedic specialists available near Plaintiff’s residence. (JSM Decl., ¶ 5, Ex. 2.) Defendant represents that it is willing to reimburse reasonable travel expenses to and from Los Angeles for purposes of the examination, as required by the statute.

 

Defendant fails to show good cause for taking the IME greater than 75 miles from Plaintiff’s residence. While Defendant contends that Plaintiff chose Los Angeles as the forum for this action, Defendant does not dispute the subject accident occurred in this county, or that any alternative venue would have been proper. Defendants do not make any showing they have attempted to retain an expert within 75 miles of Plaintiff’s residence. Defendant does not provide evidence showing that there are no qualified experts in New York City, which is within 75 miles of Plaintiff’s residence. Defendant do not explain why a New York orthopedic specialist would be unqualified or would not be available to testify in California for trial. Thus, Defendant does not demonstrate any prejudice requiring them to retain an expert to conduct an IME in New York. Conversely, the record suggests that it will be physically taxing on Plaintiff to travel to Los Angeles for an IME.

 

Accordingly, the motion is DENIED.

Case Number: 24SMCV2913    Hearing Date: July 16, 2026    Dept: M

CASE NAME:           Barba, v. Krieger

CASE NO.:                24SMCV2913

MOTION:                  Motion for Terminating Sanctions

HEARING DATE:   7/16/2026

 

Legal Standard

 

If a party fails to obey a court order compelling it to provide a discovery response, “the court may make those orders that are just, including the imposition of an issue sanction, an evidence sanction, or a terminating sanction . . . In lieu of or in addition to this sanction, the court may impose a monetary sanction . . . .” (CCP §§ 2030.290(c), 2030.300(e), 2031.300(c), 2031.320(c).) Misuse of the discovery process, which includes disobeying a court order to provide discovery, is conduct subject to sanctions. (CCP § 2023.010(g).) Possible sanctions are:

 

(a) [A] monetary sanction ordering that one engaging in the misuse of the discovery process, or any attorney advising that conduct, or both pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney's fees, incurred by anyone as a result of that conduct….

 

(b) [A]n issue sanction ordering that designated facts shall be taken as established in the action in accordance with the claim of the party adversely affected by the misuse of the discovery process. The court may also impose an issue sanction by an order prohibiting any party engaging in the misuse of the discovery process from supporting or opposing designated claims or defenses.

 

(c) [A]n evidence sanction by an order prohibiting any party engaging in the misuse of the discovery process from introducing designated matters in evidence.

 

(d) [A] terminating sanction by one of the following orders:

 

(1) An order striking out the pleadings or parts of the pleadings of any party engaging in the misuse of the discovery process.

 

(2) An order staying further proceedings by that party until an order for discovery is obeyed.

 

(3) An order dismissing the action, or any part of the action, of that party.

 

(4) An order rendering a judgment by default against that party.

 

(e) [A] contempt sanction by an order treating the misuse of the discovery process as a contempt of court.

 

(CCP § 2023.030.)

 

The party seeking to impose sanctions need only show the failure to obey earlier discovery orders. (Puritan Ins. Co. v. Superior Court (1985) 171 Cal.App.3d 877, 884 [interpreting former statute dealing with “refusal” to comply].) However, numerous cases hold that severe sanctions (i.e., terminating or evidentiary sanctions) for failure to comply with a court order are allowed only where the failure was willful. (R.S. Creative, Inc. v. Creative Cotton, Ltd. (1999) 75 Cal.App.4th 486, 495; Vallbona v. Springer (1996) 43 Cal.App.4th 1525, 1545; Biles v. Exxon Mobil Corp. (2004) 124 Cal.App.4th 1315, 1327.) The burden of proof then shifts to the party seeking to avoid sanctions to establish a satisfactory excuse for his or her conduct. (Corns v. Miller (1986) 181 Cal.App.3d 195, 201.)

 

Analysis

 

Defendant Estate of Michael Krieger moves for terminating sanctions, specifically an order dismissing Plaintiff Antonio Barba’s action for her alleged unreasonable and unjustified refusal to obey her discovery obligations and the Court’s discovery orders.

 

Defendant demonstrates that Plaintiff willfully disobeyed this Court’s discovery order. On February 11, 2026, the Court ordered Plaintiff to provide verified responses to Defendant’s Form Interrogatories, Set One, Special Interrogatories, Set One, and Request for Production of Documents, Set One, without objections and to pay monetary sanctions of $1,496.00. Despite notice of this order, Plaintiff has not provided responses to the subject discovery requests. Therefore, Plaintiff has willfully failed to comply with the February 11, 2026, discovery order. Since Plaintiff has failed to pay the monetary sanctions as ordered, lesser sanctions would not be effective to compel Plaintiff’s compliance and more severe sanctions are warranted.

 

The Court is therefore inclined to issue the requested terminating sanctions. Before issuing that sanction, however, the Court will afford Plaintiff one final opportunity to obey the February 11, 2026, discovery order. Plaintiff must file proof of service of substantially code-compliant responses, without objection, to the subject discovery requests on or by July 27, 2026. If Plaintiff fails to do so, the Court will impose the terminating sanctions as requested and dismiss Plaintiff’s action.

 


Case Number: 25SMCV03661    Hearing Date: July 16, 2026    Dept: M

CASE NAMES:         Serene Corp. v. Rene’s Cultivar LLC;

                                    Adamian Family Trust v. Rene’s Cultivar LLC, et al.

CASE NOS.:              25SMCV03661/25SMCV04823

MOTION:                  Motion to Relate Cases

HEARING DATE:   7/22/2026

 

Legal Standard

 

California Rules of Court (“CRC”), rule 3.300 requires both parties to file notices of related case where two or more cases are related within the meaning of the rule. (CRC Rule 3.300(b), (h).) Cases are related when they (1) involve the same parties and are based on the same or similar claims, (2) arise from the same or substantially identical transactions, incidents, or events requiring the determination of the same or substantially identical questions of law or fact, (3) involve claims against, title to, possession of, or damages to the same property, or (4) are likely for other reasons to require substantial duplication of judicial resources if heard by different judges. (CRC Rule 3.300(a).) “The Los Angeles Superior Court has hundreds of bench officers and courtrooms. When for tactical reasons one side files an action related to an existing case, both sides must file this routine notice. The notice goes to the trial judge in the low-numbered case, who then can decide how to handle the parallel litigation. The notice allows the trial court to promote efficiency, to avoid duplicative effort, to combat judge-shopping, and to minimize the prospect of conflicting results. The trial court system relies on this mechanism to detect and to cure the problem of parallel litigation within its jurisdiction.” (Harris v. Rojas (2021) 66 Cal.App.5th 817, 820.) 

 

Los Angeles Superior Court Local Rule 3.3(f)(3) states in pertinent part: “In the event that the judge designated under California Rules of Court, rule 3.300(h)(1)(A)(B)(C) to make the decision, does not order related any of the cases set forth in the Notice of Related Cases, any party may file a motion to have the cases related. …If the cases are all pending in one district that is other than the Central District, the motion shall be heard by the Supervising Judge of that district. The motion must be served on each party in every case listed in the Notice of Related Cases, with proof of service attached[.]”

 

ANALYSIS

 

Defendants/Cross-Complainants Rene’s Cultivar LLC and Nicholas Jammal move for an order that Serene Corp v. Rene’s Cultivar LLC, et al., Case No. 25SMCV03661 (the “Serene Action”) and Adamian Family Trust v. Rene’s Cultivar LLC, et al., Case No. 25SMCV04823 (the “Adamian Action”) be deemed related and assigned to a single department. Defendants assert that the two actions (i) involve the same or similar parties and are based on the same or similar claims; (ii) arise from the same or substantially identical transactions, occurrences, or events requiring determination of the same or substantially identical questions of law or fact; (iii) involve claims concerning the same property; (iv) are likely to require substantial duplication of judicial resources if heard by different judges; and (v) present a substantial risk of inconsistent results if conducted separately.

 

Plaintiffs first object on procedural grounds arguing that this motion violates CCP section 1008. Defendants had filed a notice of related case in both actions, and the Court denied relation on January 27, 2026. Generally, such a ruling would preclude re-litigation of the issue. (CCP § 1008.) However, Local Rule 3.3(f)(3) provides a grounds for a noticed motion after a judge declines to relate cases set forth in notices of related case.

 

Initially, the two cases were not related. The Serene Complaint concerns a secured promissory note and related security agreement which Defendants allegedly failed to repay. The Security Agreement granted Serene a security interest in all of Defendants’ assets, including Cannabis licenses and regulatory approvals; (b) California commercial cannabis licenses; (c) Accounts, inventory, equipment, fixtures, and tools of the trade; and (d) All property associated with cannabis operations. On the other hand, the Adamian Complaint concerns Defendants’ breach of a distinct commercial lease for real property located at 11314 W. Pico Blvd., including Defendants’ failure to pay rent, abandoning the property, and causing damage to the property. While both of the actions generally involve Defendants’ cannabis business and their alleged failure to repay debts, there was no material connection between the two actions as defined by Rule 3.300. The two complaints did not involve the same parties and were based on different contractual obligations/transactions. The two actions did not involve title to, possession of, or damages to the same property. Thus, it was not facially apparent that the two actions would resolve substantially identical questions of law or fact, or require substantial duplication of judicial resources.

 

            Defendants argue that the two actions arise from the same integrated transaction and

overlapping course of conduct. Defendants explain that on December 20, 2023, they entered into an Asset Purchase Agreement with Herbalcure Corporation (the “APA”). (Lawrence Decl., ¶ 2, Ex. 1.) The APA transferred the Phase 1 Adult-Use and Medicinal cultivation, manufacturing, and distribution approvals under the Los Angeles Department of Cannabis Regulation (“DCR”) application record C12-18-0000191-TEMP (the “APA License”). (Id.) Cross-Defendant Peter Tejera executed the APA on behalf of Herbalcure as its CEO. (Id., Ex. 1, at 33.)

 

On February 24, 2024, Cross-Complainants executed a Senior Secured Promissory Note in favor of Serene Corp in the principal amount of $175,000 (the “Promissory Note”). (Id., ¶ 3, Ex. 2.) The Promissory Note was signed by Cross-Defendant Peter Tejera on behalf of Serene. (Id., Ex. 2, at 11.) The Promissory Note is secured by a Security Agreement executed the same day, also signed by Tejera as Serene’s CEO. (Id., ¶ 4, Ex. 3.) The Security Agreement is subject to the terms and provisions in the APA, and identifies the local approval and authorization associated with Record No. C12-18-0000191-TEMP (and related Accela records) and any California commercial cannabis licenses as the collateral. (Id., Ex. 3, §§ A(a), K(a).)

 

In March 2024, Tejera solicited Defendants to lease the premises at 11314 W. Pico Boulevard in Los Angeles for cannabis manufacturing, distribution, and cultivation operations as contemplated by the APA. (Id., Ex. 11 ¶ 34.) On April 20, 2024, the Adamian Family Trust entered into a Standard Industrial/Commercial Multi-Tenant Lease with Rene’s Cultivar LLC for that property (the “Lease”). (Lawrence Decl. ¶ 5, Ex. 4.) Throughout the Lease term, Tejera served as the sole point of contact with the landlord. (Id., Ex., 11 at ¶ 34.)

 

            Both cross-complaints center around the acts of Tejera, who allegedly acted as an agent for Serene and the Adamians. The cross-complaints allege that the overall transaction had a commercial objective, and impose interdependent performance obligations. For example, the cross-complaint in the Adamian action alleges that Tejera, acting in multiple capacities as (a) seller of cannabis licensing rights through Herbalcure Corporation, (b) CEO, CFO, and Secretary of Herbalcure Corporation, and authorized agent of Herbalcure in connection with the Lease; and (c) Landlord’s agent for the Premises, made systematic misrepresentations that induced Cross-Complainants to execute the subject agreements, invest over $160,000 in improvements and pay regulatory fees. In the cross-complaint in the Serene Action, Defendants similarly allege alter-ego/agency between Serene Corp, Herbalcure, and Tejera to the APA, Promissory Note, and Security Agreement, and claim fraud-in-the-inducement and declaratory-relief claims that turn on the same misrepresentations. As Plaintiffs recognize, both cross-complaints involve the same parties, the same or similar causes of action, and the same remedies between Defendants, Serene, Herbalcure, Tejera, and the Adamians. Plaintiffs even accuse the pending cross-complaints of being duplicative and violating abatement principles. Thus, the two cross-actions involve the same parties and are based on the same or similar claims, the actions allegedly arise from the same alleged overall transaction which will require the determination of the same or substantially identical questions of law or fact. Thus, the actions will require substantial duplication of judicial resources if heard by different judges.

 

Plaintiffs complain that relating the cases will somehow prejudice them. However, Plaintiffs only identify prejudice from consolidating the actions. This motion is not for consolidation. Merely relating the cases will simply place the two cases in the same department, which would not result in any meaningful prejudice.

 

Accordingly, the two actions are deemed related. 25SMCV03661 is the lead case. 25SMCV04823 is reassigned to Department N for all purposes. All hearings currently set in case 25SMCV04823 are advanced and vacated.  A Case Management Conference is set for August 27, 2026, at 8:30 a.m. in Department N.

Case Number: 26SMCV01409    Hearing Date: July 16, 2026    Dept: M

CASE NAME:           Fisher, et al., v. 212 S. Carmelina Ave. LLC, et al.

CASE NO.:                26SMCV01409

MOTION:                  Writ of Attachment

HEARING DATE:    7/16/2026

 

 

Legal Standard

 

An application for a writ of attachment shall be executed under oath and must include: (1) a statement showing that the attachment is sought to secure the recovery on a claim upon which an attachment may be issued; (2) a statement of the amount to be secured by the attachment; (3) a statement that the attachment is not sought for a purpose other than the recovery on the claim upon which the attachment is based; (4) a statement that the applicant has no information or belief that the claim is discharged or that the prosecution of the action is stayed in a proceeding under the Bankruptcy Act (11 U.S.C. section 101 et seq.); and (5) a description of the property to be attached under the writ of attachment and a statement that the plaintiff is informed and believes that such property is subject to attachment.  (CCP § 484.020.)

 

The application shall be supported by an affidavit showing that the plaintiff on the facts presented will be entitled to a judgment on the claim. (CCP § 484.030.) The Court shall issue a right to attach order if the Court finds all of the following: (1) The claim upon which the attachment is based is one upon which an attachment may be issued. (2) The plaintiff has established the probable validity of the claim upon which the attachment is based. (3) The attachment is not sought for a purpose other than the recovery on the claim upon which the attachment is based. (4) The amount to be secured by the attachment is greater than zero. (CCP § 484.090.)

 

“A claim has ‘probable validity’ where it is more likely than not that the plaintiff will obtain a judgment against the defendant on that claim.” (CCP § 481.190.) In determining the probable validity of a claim where the defendant makes an appearance, the court must consider the relative merits of the positions of the respective parties and make a determination of the probable outcome of the litigation.” (Loeb & Loeb v. Beverly Glen Music, Inc. (1985) 166 Cal.App.3d 1110, 1120; see¿Epstein v. Abrams (1997) 57 Cal.App.4th 1159, 1168 [attachment law strictly construed].)

 

ANALYSIS

 

Plaintiff Damon R. Fisher and Elizabeth C. Fisher seek a writ of attachment against Defendant 212 S. Carmelina Avenue, LLC, (“Seller”) in the amount of $1,300,000.00.

 

Evidentiary Issues

 

            Seller’s objections are SUSTAINED as to nos. 21-22, 27-38, 43-44, 48, 50, 55-56, and OVERRULED as to the remainder.

 

Plaintiffs’ objections to the H. Klien declaration are SUSTAINED as no. 3. Plaintiffs’ objections to the M. Klien declaration are SUSTAINED as no. 3. The remainder of Plaintiffs’ objections are OVERRULED.

 

Probable Validity

 

            In support of the probable validity of their claims, Plaintiffs explain that on February 14, 2025, Plaintiffs entered into a written Residential Purchase Agreement and related addenda (collectively, the “Agreement”) with Seller for the purchase of the residence to be constructed at 212 S. Carmelina Avenue, Los Angeles, California 90049 (the “Property”) with a purchase price of $12,000,000. (Fisher Decl. ¶ 2.) Plaintiffs paid a deposit of $1,200,000 to Seller that same day. (Id., ¶ 3.) The Agreement provides that if completion did not occur by December 31, 2025 (the “Outside Date”), Plaintiffs could terminate by written notice, whereupon Seller was obligated to promptly return the deposit with interest. (Id.) It is undisputed that completion did not occur by the Outside Date. (Id., ¶ 4.) On January 8, 2026, Plaintiffs gave written notice of termination and demanded return of the Deposit with interest. (Id., ¶ 5.) Seller failed and refused to return the Deposit or pay any interest. (Id. ¶ 6.) Seller is a single purpose entity for the

purpose of constructing a single family residence on the Property, and selling it. (CC ¶ 7.) Without turning to the merits of the cross-claims, this evidence presents a contract claim for a

fixed amount of $1.2 million.

 

Plaintiffs note that the instant controversy is covered by an arbitration agreement. CCP section 1281.8(b) provides that a party to an arbitration agreement may apply to the court for provisional relief in connection with an arbitrable controversy, but only upon the ground that the award “may be rendered ineffectual without provisional relief.”  The requirements of CCP section 1281.8 are in addition to the other elements of proof for provisional relief. (Woolley v. Embassy Suites, Inc., (1991) 227 Cal.App.3d 1520, 1529.) The “ineffectual” element exists to ensure that the court does not invade the province of the arbitrator. (California Retail Portfolio Fund GMBH & Co. v. Hopkins Real Estate Group, (2011) 193 Cal.App.4th 849, 856.) Thus, the court is only empowered to grant provisional relief where the arbitrator’s award may be inadequate to make the moving party whole. (Id.) In the context of attachment, the irreparable harm requirement for ex parte attachment provides guidance on the issue of, and is similar to, ineffectual relief under section 1281.8. (Id. at 859.) An award may be “ineffectual” if defendant is insolvent or is unable to otherwise pay damages that might be awarded. (Ibid.)

 

            Plaintiffs failed to demonstrate that an eventual arbitration award would be rendered ineffectual without the sought attachment order. Plaintiff does not present strong evidence of Seller’s insolvency, or that Seller will be unable to pay the amount sought. Plaintiff only presents evidence that principal of Seller, Hal Klein, told him on multiple occasions, that: (1) Klein knows he owes Plaintiffs the full Deposit plus interest; (2) he is presently unable to pay; and (3) he intends to repay Plaintiffs if and when Fountainhead sells certain of its properties, including the Property at 212 S. Carmelina Avenue. (Fisher Decl. ¶ 15.) Further, Plaintiffs present evidence that the deposit was diverted to other Fountainhead expenses and was never used for this project, and that Seller and Fountainhead are “significantly cash-constrained.” (Id., ¶¶ 12-13.) This bare and vague admission that Seller is cash constrained does not strongly support an inference that Seller is insolvent. Even if Seller is cash constrained, the evidence shows that Seller still owns the subject property which may be sold to pay any future award. Plaintiffs present no substantial evidence that, absent attachment, the Seller will sell the Property and render itself insolvent or unable to pay its debt by transferring or other dissipating the proceeds before conclusion of this action. Thus, the record does not support a finding that, absent attachment, an award will be rendered ineffectual.

 

Accordingly, the writ is DENIED.
