TENTATIVE RULING

 

Defendant’s motion for order is moot as to the request for a terminating sanctions and the request that Aguilar appear for deposition. The motion was filed July 2, 2026. On September 10, 2026 Plaintiff filed opposition.  On September 16, 2026, Cambridge Farms filed its reply.

 

The motion is not moot with respect to Defendant’s claim for attorney’s fees. The hearing on Defendant’s motion is continued as to the request for attorney’s fees and costs.

 

BACKGROUND

 

This is a wage-and-hour action.

 

Here, Defendant seeks an order “imposing terminating sanctions against Plaintiff Victor Ariel Herrera Aguilar” or, alternatively, ordering Aguilar “to appear for deposition examination on a date certain[.]”  (Notice of Motion, p. 2.)

 

Defendant additionally seeks an award of attorney fees and costs against Aguilar in the amount of $4,487.50.  (See ibid.)

 

APPLICABLE LAW

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.010(g) “makes ‘disobeying a court order to provide discovery’ a ‘misuse of the discovery process.’”  (Weil & Brown, Cal. Practice Guide: Civ. Procedure Before Trial (The Rutter Group 2026) ¶ 8:2146, emphasis in original.)

 

“Once a party or witness has been ordered to attend a deposition, or to answer discovery, or to produce documents, more severe sanctions are available for continued refusal to make discovery.”  (Id. at ¶ 8:2145, emphasis in original.)

 

“However, numerous cases hold that severe sanctions (i.e., evidence or terminating sanctions) for failure to comply with a court order are allowed only where the failure was willful.”  (Id. at ¶ 8:2147.)

 

For a terminating sanction, the court may order any of the following against the disobeying party:

 

* “Striking that party’s pleadings or parts thereof[.]”  (Id. at ¶ 8:2185, emphasis in original.)

 

* “Staying further proceedings by that party until the order is obeyed[.]”  (Ibid., emphasis in original.)

 

* “Dismissing that party’s action, or any part thereof[.]”  (Ibid., emphasis in original.)

 

* “Entering default judgment against that party.”  (Ibid., emphasis in original.)

 

DISCUSSION

 

Arguments

 

Defendant

 

Defendant contends the Court should either grant terminating sanctions or set a new deposition date because:

 

* On May 22, 2026, the Court ordered Aguilar to “sit for deposition examination on June 24, 2026.”  (Motion, p. 2; see also 5/22/26 Minute Order, p. 1.)

 

* Aguilar failed to appear for deposition on June 24th.  (See Motion, pp. 2-4.)

 

* To date, Aguilar’s counsel “has not provided a proposed date for []  Aguilar’s deposition  examination.”  (Id. at p. 4; see also id. at pp. 5-7.)

 

Defendant also contends the Court should award attorney fees and costs because Aguilar “[m]isuse[d] [] the discovery process” by failing to comply with the May 22nd order.  (Id. at p. 7; see also id. at p. 8.)

 

Aguilar

 

Aguilar asserts in opposition that the “motion is moot.”  (Opposition, p. 1 [noting that “Aguilar’s . . . deposition was conducted and completed on August 13, 2026”]; see also id. at pp. 2-4.)

 

Aguilar claims the request for attorney fees and costs should be denied because:

 

* Aguilar’s failure to appear on June 24th “was not an instance of intentional obstruction of discovery or abuse[.]”  (Id. at p. 4 [stating that Aguilar’s “cell phone was stolen in June 2026”]; see also id. at p. 5.)

 

* Defendant failed to file a meet-and-confer declaration.  (See id. at pp. 5-8.)

 

* The requested amount is unsupported.  (See id. at pp. 9-10.)

 

Defendant’s Reply

 

In Reply, Defendant does not press the request for terminating sanctions. The Aguilar deposition took place on August 13, 2026, but, Defendant maintains, was not completed.  Plaintiff produced no documents and apparently did not look for any. Some of Defendant’s questions that did not call for privileged communications were objected to with instructions not to Answer. 

 

Analysis

 

Aguilar’s deposition took place on August 13, 2026. (see Davis Decl., ¶ 45), The motion is partially moot with respect to his court-ordered appearance. Terminating sanctions are not warranted and apparently, based on the Reply, no longer sought. A separate motion is needed to secure compliance with a duty to answer questions and produce requested materials. It appears that Defendant has reserved October 19, 2026 for a hearing on a motion to compel Aguilar to complete his deposition, produce requested documents, and for monetary sanctions. As of September 22, 2026, the motion is not in the Court’s docket.

    

Defendant’s request for attorney fees and costs is not moot.  As noted above, on May 22nd, the Court ordered Aguilar to be deposed on June 24th.  (See 5/22/26 Minute Order, p. 1.)  On June 3rd, apparently before Aguilar’s phone was stolen, Aguilar’s counsel’s office spoke with Aguilar “to confirm his deposition date[.]”  (Davis Decl., ¶ 37.)  Yet, despite notice, Aguilar failed to appear.  His disobedience of the May 22nd order caused Defendant to incur expenses that it should not have had to incur.  Therefore, the Court finds that this portion of the motion is not moot.

 

The Court intends to award attorney fees and costs to Defendant.  A short continuance is appropriate, though, to allow Defendant’s counsel to submit billing records showing the exact fees and costs incurred in connection with this motion.  Mr. Qualls’s July 2, 2026 declaration merely states that an additional $2,825 in attorneys’ fees, and an anticipated $1,662.50 more for the reply were requested as part of this motion. (Qualls Decl. ¶ 17.)  But no further explanation is given.  This documentation is inadequate. (Taylor v. County of Los Angeles (2020) 50 Cal.App.5th 205, 207 [lack of contemporaneous time records justify reduction in claimed fees; “contemporaneous time records are the best evidence of lawyers’ hourly work… Lawyers know this better than anyone”].) The court will set a schedule for submission of an attorney fee declaration with appropriate exhibits and Plaintiff’s response.  

 

A final point.  The Court rejects Plaintiff’s “meet and confer” argument.  Defendant’s counsel’s declaration suffices to show adequate meet-and-confer efforts to resolve this matter.  (See Qualls Decl., ¶¶ 3-15.)

C.     Attorney Fees and Costs

            Class Counsel requests an award of $175,833.33 in fees and $5,792 in costs. (Memo ISO Attorneys’ Fees at 1:3-6.) The Settlement Agreement provides for up to $175,833.33 (33 1/3 %) for attorney fees and $10,000 in costs (¶XIV.A).

“Courts recognize two methods for calculating attorney fees in civil class actions: the lodestar/multiplier method and the percentage of recovery method.”  (Wershba v. Apple Computer, Inc. (2001) 91 Cal.App.4th 224, 254.)  Here, class counsel request attorney fees using the percentage method, as crosschecked by lodestar. (Memo ISO Attorneys’ Fees at pp. 1-12.)

In common fund cases, the Court may employ a percentage of the benefit method, as cross-checked against the lodestar. (Laffitte v. Robert Half Int’l, Inc. (2016) 1 Cal.5th 480, 503.) The fee request represents one-third of the gross settlement amount, which is the average generally awarded in class actions.  (See In re Consumer Privacy Cases (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 545, 558, fn. 13 [“Empirical studies show that, regardless whether the percentage method or the lodestar method is used, fee awards in class actions average around one-third of the recovery.”].)

D.    Incentive Award

The class representative, Jerman Contreras, seeks an enhancement payment of $5,000 for his contributions to the action. (Memo ISO Attorneys’ Fees at 14:4-6.)    

In connection with the final fairness hearing, named Plaintiffs must submit declarations attesting to why they should be entitled to an enhancement award in the proposed amount.  The named Plaintiffs must explain why they “should be compensated for the expense or risk he has incurred in conferring a benefit on other members of the class.”  (Clark v. American Residential Services LLC (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 785, 806.)  Trial courts should not sanction enhancement awards of thousands of dollars with “nothing more than pro forma claims as to ‘countless’ hours expended, ‘potential stigma’ and ‘potential risk.’ Significantly more specificity, in the form of quantification of time and effort expended on the litigation, and in the form of reasoned explanation of financial or other risks incurred by the named plaintiffs, is required in order for the trial court to conclude that an enhancement was ‘necessary to induce [the named plaintiff] to participate in the suit . . . .’”  (Id. at 806-807, italics and ellipsis in original.)

Plaintiff represents that his contributions to this litigation include: meeting with his attorneys on multiple occasions to review pleadings, discovery, sample time and pay records, as well as to review and execute declarations in support of the Class’ claims and to execute multiple versions of the Class settlement, reviewing personal employment records, attending the mediation in person, and reviewing the settlement. He estimates spending 40-50 hours on the case. (Declaration of Jerman Contreras ISO Final, ¶¶5-10.)

Based on the above, as well as the benefits obtained on behalf of the class, the recommendation is to grant the enhancement payment in the amount of $5,000 to Plaintiff.

 

E.     Settlement Administration Costs

            The settlement administrator, Atticus, is requesting $8,800 for the costs of settlement administration. (Linton Decl., ¶15.) This is less than the estimated cost of $10,000 provided for in the settlement agreement (¶VIII) and disclosed to class members in the Notice, to which there were no objections. (Linton Decl., ¶14, Exhibit A.) Based on the above, the recommendation is to award costs in the requested amount of $8,800.

BACKGROUND

 

This is a class action arising from a data security incident. Plaintiffs Ali Concepcion and Lisa Robert-McCharen sue Defendant Deanco Healthcare, LLC (“Deanco” or “Defendant”). Plaintiffs seek to represent a class of individuals whose Personally Identifying Information may have been compromised in the data breach that is the subject of the Notice of Security Breach that was sent by Defendant to Plaintiffs and Class Members on or around November 21, 2023 (the "Data Security Incident").

            On or around November 21, 2023, Deanco issued a Notice of Security Incident acknowledging that it was the victim of a cyberattack perpetrated against it on or around May 1, 2023. Thereafter, Deanco launched an investigation and determined that an unauthorized person accessed information on its systems on or around May 1, 2023. The investigation resulted in the determination that files containing individuals’ Private Information had been potentially accessed in the Data Incident, including address, date of birth, social security number, driver’s license number, financial account information, health insurance plan member ID, claims data and clinical information about care with Deanco. The Data Incident affected approximately 269,847 individuals, including Plaintiffs.

On November 30, 2023, Plaintiff Ali Concepcion filed a class action complaint, Case No. 23STCV29292, alleging claims for: (1) Negligence; (2) Violation of Cal. Civ. Code 1798.82 (Customer Records Act); (3) Breach of Implied Contract; (4) Violation of the California Confidentiality of Medical Information Act; (5) Violation of California’s Unfair Competition Law (“UCL”); and (6) Unjust Enrichment. Plaintiff Lisa Robert-McCharen alleged (1) Negligence; (2) Breach of Implied Contract; (3) Violation of the California Confidentiality of Medical Information Act; (4) Violation of California’s Unfair Competition Law – Unlawful Business Practice; (5) Violation of California’s Unfair Competition Law – Unfair Business Practice; and (6) Violation of Cal. Civ. Code 1798.82 (Customer Records Act).

On December 1, 2023, Plaintiff Lisa Robert-McCharen filed a putative class action against Defendant, Case No. 23STCV29517. On March 29, 2024, the class actions filed by Representative Plaintiffs Concepcion and Robert-McCharen were consolidated into the Concepcion action.

On April 2, 2024, the parties informed the Court that they were in the process of selecting a mediator and the Court ordered the parties to participate in a mediation session by July 1, 2024. The parties began settlement negotiations directly for several weeks and ultimately reached an agreement without a formal mediation. The terms of settlement were finalized in the long-form Class Action Settlement Agreement and Release (“Settlement Agreement”), a copy of which was filed with the Court on December 20, 2024.

On March 9, 2026, after the parties filed several rounds of additional briefing and revisions to the Settlement Agreement to address concerns raised by the Court, preliminary approval of the settlement was granted. All references below are to the agreement attached as Exhibit 2 to the Second Further Supplemental Declaration of Daniel Srourian filed January 30, 2026.

C.     Attorney Fees and Costs

D.    Incentive Awards

The class representatives, Ali Concepcion and Lisa Robert-McCharen, seek enhancement payments of $2,000 each for their contributions to the action. (Memo ISO Plaintiffs’ Motion for Service Awards, Attorney’s Fees, and Expenses at 3:4.)   

In connection with the final fairness hearing, named Plaintiffs must submit declarations attesting to why they should be entitled to an enhancement award in the proposed amount.  The named Plaintiffs must explain why they “should be compensated for the expense or risk he has incurred in conferring a benefit on other members of the class.”  (Clark v. American Residential Services LLC (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 785, 806.)  Trial courts should not sanction enhancement awards of thousands of dollars with “nothing more than pro forma claims as to ‘countless’ hours expended, ‘potential stigma’ and ‘potential risk.’ Significantly more specificity, in the form of quantification of time and effort expended on the litigation, and in the form of reasoned explanation of financial or other risks incurred by the named plaintiffs, is required in order for the trial court to conclude that an enhancement was ‘necessary to induce [the named plaintiff] to participate in the suit . . . .’”  (Id. at 806-807, italics and ellipsis in original.)

Each Plaintiff represents that his or her contributions to this litigation include: assisting in the investigation of the case, maintaining contact with counsel, reviewing the pleadings, answering counsel's questions, communicating with counsel during the settlement negotiations, and reviewing the terms of the Settlement Agreement. (Declarations of Ali Concepcion and Lisa Robert-McCharen filed ISO Prelim Approval.)

Based on the above, as well as the benefits obtained on behalf of the class, the recommendation is to grant the enhancement payments in the amount of $2,000 to each Plaintiff.

4.     May conditional class certification be granted?

1.     Standards

A detailed analysis of the elements required for class certification is not required, but it is advisable to review each element when a class is being conditionally certified (Amchem Products, Inc. v. Winsor (1997) 521 U.S. 620, 622-627.)  The trial court can appropriately utilize a different standard to determine the propriety of a settlement class as opposed to a litigation class certification.  Specifically, a lesser standard of scrutiny is used for settlement cases.  (Dunk at 1807, fn 19.)  Finally, the Court is under no “ironclad requirement” to conduct an evidentiary hearing to consider whether the prerequisites for class certification have been satisfied. (Wershba at 240.)

2.     Analysis

a.     Numerosity.  There are approximately 102 class members. (MPA ISO Prelim, 22:9-14.) This element is met. 

b.     Ascertainability.  The proposed class is defined above.  The class definition is “precise, objective and presently ascertainable.”  (Sevidal v. Target Corp. (2010) 189 Cal.App.4th 905, 919.) The class members are identifiable from Defendant’s records. (MPA ISO Prelim, 22:9-14.)

c.      Community of interest.  “The community of interest requirement involves three factors: ‘(1) predominant common questions of law or fact; (2) class representatives with claims or defenses typical of the class; and (3) class representatives who can adequately represent the class.’”  (Linder v. Thrifty Oil Co. (2000) 23 Cal.4th 429, 435.)

As to commonality, Counsel contends that common legal and factual questions exist, such as (a) whether individuals working for Defendant were subject to unlawful wage-and-hour policies and/or practices under the California Labor Code; (b) whether these violations triggered derivative claims for penalties; and (c) whether Defendants’ violations were knowing or willful.  (MPA ISO Prelim, 22:18-25.) 

As to typicality, Counsel contends that Plaintiff’s claims are typical of the Class Members because they were subject to Defendants’ policies and practices described above in Plaintiff’s analysis of the claims and defenses, which were consistently applied class-wide to all Employees. (MPA ISO Prelim, 23:3-7.) 

As to adequacy, Plaintiff asserts that he understood his duties as a class representative, has been willing to undergo the risk of litigation, and has no conflict of interest with the other Class Members. (MPA ISO Prelim, 23:9-24; Declaration of Plaintiff Luis Herminio Vanegas Lopez, passim.)

d.     Adequacy of class counsel.  As indicated above, Class Counsel has shown experience in class action litigation, including wage and hour class actions.

e.     Superiority.  Given the relatively small size of the individual claims, a class action appears to be superior to separate actions by the class members.

 

      CONCLUSION: The class may be conditionally certified since the prerequisites of class certification have been satisfied.

6.     Attorney fees and costs

CRC rule 3.769(b) states: “Any agreement, express or implied, that has been entered into with respect to the payment of attorney fees or the submission of an application for the approval of attorney fees must be set forth in full in any application for approval of the dismissal or settlement of an action that has been certified as a class action.”

            Ultimately, the award of attorney fees is made by the court at the fairness hearing, using the lodestar method with a multiplier, if appropriate.  (PLCM Group, Inc. v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095-1096; Ramos v. Countrywide Home Loans, Inc. (2000) 82 Cal.App.4th 615, 625-626; Ketchum III v. Moses (2000) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132-1136.)  Despite any agreement by the parties to the contrary, “the court ha[s] an independent right and responsibility to review the attorney fee provision of the settlement agreement and award only so much as it determined reasonable.” (Garabedian v. Los Angeles Cellular Telephone Company (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 123, 128.)

            The question of whether Class Counsel is entitled to $28,333.33 (33 1/3%) in attorney fees will be addressed at the fairness hearing when class counsel brings a noticed motion for attorney fees.  Class counsel must provide the court with billing information so that it can properly apply the lodestar method and must indicate what multiplier (if applicable) is being sought as to each counsel.    

            Class Counsel should also be prepared to justify the costs sought (capped at $15,000) by detailing how they were incurred.

 

7.     Incentive Award to Class Representative

The Settlement Agreement provides for an enhancement award of $7,500 to Named Plaintiff.

In connection with the final fairness hearing, the named Plaintiff must submit a declaration attesting to why he should be entitled to an enhancement award in the proposed amount.  The named Plaintiff must explain why he “should be compensated for the expense or risk she has incurred in conferring a benefit on other members of the class.”  (Clark v. American Residential Services LLC (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 785, 806.)  Trial courts should not sanction enhancement awards of thousands of dollars with “nothing more than pro forma claims as to ‘countless’ hours expended, ‘potential stigma’ and ‘potential risk.’ Significantly more specificity, in the form of quantification of time and effort expended on the litigation, and in the form of reasoned explanation of financial or other risks incurred by the named plaintiffs, is required in order for the trial court to conclude that an enhancement was ‘necessary to induce [the named plaintiff] to participate in the suit . . . .’”  (Id. at 806-807, italics and ellipsis in original.)

     The Court will decide the issue of the enhancement award at the time of final approval.

¿ Do not include language indicating that class members may only be heard at final approval if they have complied with all objection procedures or that they must use specific language to request exclusion, or, if a specific procedure is sought, explain why it is necessary.  In general, the court will hear from any class member who attends the final approval hearing and asks to speak regarding his or her objection.

 

·       Ensure the Notice reflects that class members do not need to use specific language to opt-out.

 

·       Ensure the Notice reflects that class members may orally object at the final approval hearing regardless of submitting a written objection.

 

F. Cy Pres Distribution

 

¿ Indicate the length of time from issuance for which settlement checks will remain valid.

 

¿ Identify the fund to which uncashed checks will be directed and detail the steps that will be taken to ensure compliance with Code of Civil Procedure section 384. The Court’s Omnibus Trailer Bill of 2018 replaced the language of the prior statutory distribution scheme under Code of Civil Procedure, section 384 with a requirement that the Court re-open judgments following the final distribution of funds to include the cy pres in the judgment and to include the unclaimed amount, plus an unspecified amount of interest. Such information should be actively contemplated/provided for within the current terms of the settlement.

 

¿ Explain why the cy pres distribution fills the purposes of the lawsuit or is otherwise appropriate. (State of California v. Levi Strauss & Co. (1986) 41 Cal.3d 460, 472; In re Microsoft I-V Cases (2006) 135 Cal.App.4th 706, 722; Nachshin v. AOL, Inc. (9th Cir. 2011) 663 F.3d 1034, 1038–1041; Dennis v. Kellogg Co. (9th Cir. 2012) 697 F.3d 858, 865; Code Civ. Proc., § 384.)

 

¿ Provide declarations by all parties and counsel disclosing any interest or involvement (or lack thereof) in the governance or work of the cy pres recipient.
