DEPARTMENT F43 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 25CHCV03708    Hearing Date: September 22, 2026    Dept: F43

Dept. F43

Hearing Date: 09-22-26

Case # 25CHCV03708, Ohler Corp. v. City Electric Supply Co., Inc., et al.

Trial Date: 05-21-29

 

DEMURRER

 

MOVING PARTY:  Defendant Generac Power Systems, Inc.

RESPONDING PARTY:  Plaintiff Ohler Corporation

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Order sustaining demurrer to the complaint’s First and Second Causes of Action.

 

RULING:  Demurrer is sustained to the First Cause of Action with leave to amend and to the Second Cause of Action without leave to amend.

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

 

On October 16, 2025, plaintiff Ohler Corporation (Plaintiff) filed this action against several defendants, seeking damages resulting from a failed generator Plaintiff purchased at defendant John McCraw Electric Company’s suggestion.  The complaint asserts five causes of action:  (1) breach of express warranty of merchantability; (2) breach of implied warranty of merchantability; (3) breach of implied warranty of fitness; (4) negligence; and (5) breach of contract.  Plaintiff has identified three defendants:  City Electric Supply Company, Inc., John McCraw Electric Company, LLC (McGraw Electric), and Generac Power Systems, Inc., previously Doe 1.  City Electric filed an answer on December 10, 2025, and McCraw Electric filed an amended answer on January 27, 2026.  Each defendant has also filed a cross-complaint.

 

Plaintiff owns a restaurant called Backwoods Inn in Santa Clarita, CA.  (Compl. ¶ 2.)  McGraw Electric operates a licensed electrician business, acting as an agent of defendant City Electric Supply Company, Inc.  (Id. ¶¶ 4-5, 7.)  In December of 2023, Plaintiff notified McGraw Electric’s employees that it lost thousands of dollars in food spoilage and had unscheduled closures due to rolling blackouts as a result of actions taken by its electric utility, Southern California Edison.  (Id. ¶ 8.)  To mitigate the losses, McGraw Electric’s employees recommended that Plaintiff acquire a standalone electric generator (a Generac RGO 4845ANAC) and an accompanying Generac RTSW400A3, for a cost, before installation, of $23,861.57.  (Id. ¶ 9.)  The generator were supposed to be sufficient capacity to provide electricity to the Restaurant, to allow Plaintiff’s restaurant to remain open, to operate its refrigerators, and to avoid closures and food spoilage.  (Id. ¶ 10.)  Plaintiff relied on these representations and purchased both items on February of 2024, and paid $80,000 to install the items.  (Id. ¶¶ 11-12.)  Due to the generator’s inadequacies, Plaintiff had to close the restaurant on several occasions during power outages and throw away spoiled food due to lack of refrigeration.  (Compl. ¶ 13.)  This resulted in damages of tens of thousands of dollars in spoiled food and tens of thousands of dollars in lost profits from unscheduled closures.  Moreover, Plaintiff was forced to call McGraw Electric to fix items which were not suitable for their intended purpose.  (Id. ¶ 13.)

 

On August 19, 2026, defendant Generac Power Systems, Inc. (Generac) filed a demurrer to the Complaint’s first and second causes of action.  Plaintiff filed an untimely opposition[1] on September 11, 2026, and Generac replied on September 15, 2026.

 

 

MEET AND CONFER

 

Before filing a demurrer, the parties must meet and confer “in person, by telephone, or by video conference.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a).)  The moving party must file and serve a meet and confer declaration stating either: (1) the means by which the parties met and conferred, that the parties did not reach an agreement resolving the issues raised in the demurrer; or (2) that the party who filed the pleading subject to the demurrer failed to respond to the meet and confer request or failed to meet and confer in good faith.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 430.41, subd. (a)(3).)

 

Counsel met and conferred by telephone on July 29, 2026, but did not come to an agreement.  (Declaration of Kathrine A. Hight ¶ 2.)  Thus, the “meet and confer” requirement is met.

 

 

ANALYSIS

 

A.  Demurrer

 

A party may respond to a pleading against it by demurrer based on one or more of eight enumerated grounds, including that “the pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action” and is uncertain, meaning “ambiguous and unintelligible.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subds. (e), (f).)  The purpose of a demurrer is to challenge the sufficiency of a pleading “by raising questions of law.”  (Postley v. Harvey (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 280, 286.)  The grounds for demurring must be apparent from either the face of the complaint or a matter of which the court may take judicial notice.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.30, subd. (a); see also Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)  “In the construction of a pleading, for the purpose of determining its effect, its allegations must be liberally construed, with a view to substantial justice between the parties.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 452.)  The court “treat[s] the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law[.]”  (Berkley v. Dowds (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 518, 525.)  In applying these standards, the court liberally construes the complaint to determine whether a cause of action has been stated.  (Picton v. Anderson Union High School Dist. (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 726, 733.)

 

California courts must “give the complaint a reasonable interpretation, reading it as a whole and its parts in context,” rather than reading each cause of action in complete isolation.  (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)  However, the “reading it as a whole” standard does not automatically import facts from a general statement of facts section into individual causes of action that make no reference to those facts.  Each cause of action must be supported by ultimate facts.  (Doheny Park Terrace Homeowners Ass’n, Inc. v. Truck Ins. Exchange (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 1076, 1085; see also Thompson v. Spitzer (2023) 90 Cal.App.5th 436, 452.)  When a cause of action contains only a bare recitation of legal elements, it presents nothing but legal conclusions.  The complaint may avoid the “legal conclusions” trap by expressly incorporating factual allegations in prior sections or causes of action into each subsequent cause of action to save repetition.  (See Kajima Eng’g & Construction, Inc. v. City of Los Angeles (2002) 95 Cal.App.4th 921, 931-932;  see also Doheny Park Terrace Homeowners Ass’n, Inc., supra, 132 Cal.App.4th at p. 1096.)  The court then reads the later causes of action as if they contain the incorporated allegations.

 

 

            1.  First Cause of Action:  Breach of Express Warranty of Merchantability

 

Defendant Generac contends the First Cause of Action is uncertain as to Generac and that Plaintiff fails to allege Generac was a seller of the generator, what kinds of assurances Generac allegedly made to Plaintiff before the generator was purchased, and no allegations stating the issue with the generator.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff contends the allegations are sufficient to establish the requirements for a breach of express warranty claim.  According to Plaintiff, whether affirmations are attributable to agents, distributors, or manufacturers in marketing the product is a factual question which is not resolved on a demurrer.

 

“Any affirmation of fact or promise made by the seller to the buyer which relates to the goods and becomes part of the basis of the bargain creates an express warranty that the goods shall conform to the affirmation or promise.  Any description of the goods which is made part of the basis of the bargain creates an express warranty that the goods shall conform to the description.”  (Cal. U. Com. Code, § 2313, subd. (1)(a)-(b).)  “[T]o prevail on a breach of express warranty claim, the plaintiff must prove (1) the seller’s statements constitute an ‘affirmation of fact or promise’ or a ‘description of the goods’; (2) the statement was ‘part of the basis of the bargain’; and (3) the warranty was breached.”  (Weinstat v. Dentsply Int’l, Inc. (2010) 180 Cal.App.4th 1213, 1227 [citing Cal. U. Com. Code, § 2313, subd. (1)(a)-(b); Keith v. Buchanon (1985) 173 Cal.App.3d 13, 20].)

 

As an initial matter, Plaintiff’s allegations concerning defendant Generac are limited.  Plaintiff has identified defendant Doe 1 as defendant Generac and alleges the doe defendants are responsible in some manner for the conduct and alleged in the complaint and for Plaintiff’s damages.  (Compl. ¶ 6.)  These are the only allegations which are specific to Generac as a Doe defendant.

 

Elsewhere in the complaint, Plaintiff combines Generac’s alleged conduct with all other defendants.  Plaintiff claims that during negotiations, all defendants reassured Plaintiff the generator was adequate for its purpose, the defendants breached their express warranty because the generator was inadequate, and the generator was unable to supply sufficient electricity to keep the Restaurant open and the refrigerators to operate.  (Compl. ¶¶ 15-16.)  Plaintiff discovered the breach in March of 2024 and notified McGraw Electric of the breach.  (Id. ¶ 17.)  As a result, Plaintiff did not receive goods as warranted by Defendants and suffered damages of $200,000 (purchase and installation, closing the restaurant, and replacement of spoiled food).  (Id. ¶¶ 16, 18-19.)

 

However, Plaintiff’s allegations do not demonstrate that Generac was involved in negotiations for the generator.  (See Compl. ¶¶ 9-13.)  Plaintiff’s non-conclusory allegations concern interactions with McGraw Electric.  Although the Complaint’s allegations and the attached quote for the generator identify the generator as “Generac,” nothing in Plaintiff’s allegations indicate Generac made any warranties to Plaintiff or directly sold the generator to Plaintiff.  Nor does Plaintiff allege it notified Generac of issues with the generator.  (But see Greenman v. Yuba Power Products, Inc. (1963) 59 Cal.2d 57, 60-64 [Greenman exception not required notice to remote manufacturer]; Cal. U. Com. Code, § 2607(3).)  Plaintiff also fails to allege Generac sold the generator, City Electric and McGraw Electric are Generac’s agents, and the particular defect in the generator.

 

Thus, the Complaint is uncertain as to Generac’s role in Plaintiff’s contentions, and Plaintiff’s allegations at most demonstrate liability about McGraw Electric and City Electric.

 

Plaintiff claims it can amend the Complaint to attach or quote Generac’s model specifications, written assurances, labeling, or marketing incorporated into the transaction, and to allege notice directed to Generac.  (Opp. at p. 4:3-6.)  In response, Generac claims a product description or marketing material is insufficient to demonstrate “assurance to an end consumer that the product is appropriate for that consumer’s needs.”  (Reply at pp. 5:26-6:5.)

 

The court agrees that alleging model specifications alone is insufficient to cure the defects.  However, Plaintiff must allege facts demonstrating written assurances by Generac to Plaintiff, pursuant to Uniform Commercial Code section 2313, the existence of an actual defect covered by the warranty, and that the particular defect was a substantial factor in causing Plaintiff’s damages.  The court also notes Generac’s concerns as to allowing Plaintiff to include further allegations which conflict with or completely replace the allegations concerning Plaintiff’s reliance on McGraw Electric’s conduct and the terms of the oral agreement with McGraw Electric.  (Demurrer at pp. 12:10-13:1.)  But the purpose of Plaintiff’s amendment is solely to provide further details concerning Generac’s conduct and the role Generac’s model specifications and written assurances played in Plaintiff accepting McGraw Electric’s proposal of the generator at issue.  These allegations do not undermine Plaintiff’s claims and allegations concerning McGraw Electric but clarify the extent of Generac’s liability.

 

Therefore, the court sustains defendant Generac’s demurrer to the First Cause of action with leave to amend.

 

 

            2.  Second Cause of Action:  Breach of Implied Warranty of Merchantability

 

Next, Generac contends the breach of implied warranty claim is uncertain as to Generac and that the claim fails because the allegations are alleged against defendants as a whole without stating any separate allegations of relationship, action, or failure to act by the Doe defendants.  Thus, Plaintiff cannot allege vertical privity.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff contends privity is alleged through the sale of Generac’s goods through McGraw Electric and City Electric.  Plaintiff asserts that “[w]hether Generac’s warranty obligations extend through its distribution chain to Plaintiff, whether Plaintiff was a third-party beneficiary of Generac’s implied warranties, or whether manufacturer-issued warranties and authorized service involvement create the requisite relationship are fact questions that cannot be disposed of on demurrer.”

 

To establish a breach of an implied warranty of merchantability, plaintiff must allege that (1) a product was purchased from defendant; (2) at the time of purchase, defendant knew, or had reason to know, that plaintiff intended to use the product for a particular purpose; (3) at the time of purchase, defendant knew, or had reason to know, that plaintiff was relying on defendant’s skill and judgment to select or furnish a product that was suitable for the particular purpose; (4) plaintiff justifiably relied on defendant’s skill and judgment;  (5) The product was not suitable for the particular purpose; (6) plaintiff took reasonable steps to notify defendant within a reasonable time that the product was not suitable; (7) plaintiff was harmed; and (8) The failure to be suitable was a substantial factor in causing plaintiff's harm.  (Cal. U. Comm. Code, § 2315. See also American Suzuki Motor Corp. v. Superior Court (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 1291, 1295 [“Unlike express warranties, which are basically contractual in nature, the implied warranty of merchantability arises by operation of law.”].)

 

As with the First Cause of Action, Plaintiff’s Second Cause of Action lumps all defendants together but fails to state any specific conduct as to defendant Generac.  Plaintiff alleges are defendants were merchants with respect to goods of the kind which were sold to Plaintiff, and there was in the sale to Plaintiff an implied warranty that those goods were merchantable.  (Compl. ¶ 21.)  According to Plaintiff, defendants breached that warranty implied in the contract for the sale of goods because the generator was not fit for the ordinary purposes for which it was to be used and did not conform to the promises made by the defendants.  (Id. ¶ 22.)  As a result, Plaintiff did not receive goods as impliedly warranted by defendants and suffered damages of $200,000.  (Id. ¶¶ 23-24.)

 

But Plaintiff alleges the subject generator was purchased from defendant City Electric, not defendant Generac.  (Compl. ¶ 11.)

 

Privity of contract is a prerequisite for recovery on a theory of breach of implied warranties of fitness and merchantability.  (Blanco v. Baxter Healthcare Corp. (2008) 158 Cal.App.4th 1039, 1059.)  “[T]here is no privity between the original seller and a subsequent purchaser who is in no way a party to the original sale.’” (Blanco v. Baxter Healthcare Corp. (2008) 158 Cal.App.4th 1039, 1059, citations omitted.)  To demonstrate vertical privity with a manufacturer, plaintiff must allege that the buyer and manufacturer were parties to the sales contract or that an exception applies. (Cardinal Health 301, Inc. v. Tyco Electronics Corp. (2008) 169 Cal.App.4th 116, 138-141 [discussing vertical privity and the “direct dealings” exception].)

 

The Complaint alleges no vertical privity between Generac (the alleged manufacturer) and Plaintiff (the buyer) or facts demonstrating an exception applies.  Plaintiff alleges no direct interaction with Generac, that Generac directly sold the generator, or that Plaintiff had a direct contractual nexus with Generac.  At most, the Complaint and the attached quote demonstrate McGraw Electric and Plaintiff had an oral agreement under which McGraw Electric would recommended the subject generator which Plaintiff purchased and had installed.  Although Plaintiff’s allegations indicate Generac manufactured the generator, Plaintiff alleges no direct dealings with Generac or contractual privity.

 

Plaintiff claims leave to amend should be granted to allege facts concerning “Generac’s warranties accompanying the goods, direct manufacturer communications, authorized service involvement, and Plaintiff’s third-party beneficiary status as the intended end-user of the specifically-identified Generac equipment supplied for Plaintiff's restaurant.”  (Opp. at p. 4:25-28.)  Although such an amendment was valid for the express warranty claim (marketing materials), the implied warranty claim as currently alleged and the later identification of Doe 1 as Generac suggests that Plaintiff had few if any direct interactions with Generac beyond its written assurances and marketing materials before purchasing the generator and suffering damages.  If Plaintiff had direct dealings with Generac, as the proposed amendment suggests, it is unclear why Generac was not originally named as a defendant in this matter.  As for the third-party beneficiary exception, Plaintiff fails to provide facts demonstrating it was the intended third-party beneficiary of any contract between McGraw Electric or City Electric and Generac.  Nor does Plaintiff allege any such arrangement existed.  Finally, Plaintiff does not mention any facts demonstrating an agency relationship existed between Generac, McGraw Electric, or City Electric.

 

Therefore, the court sustains defendant Generac’s demurrer to the Second Cause of action without leave to amend.

 

 

CONCLUSION and ORDER

 

Demurrer is sustained to the First Cause of Action with leave to amend and to the Second Cause of Action without leave to amend.

 

Plaintiff may file and serve an amended complaint within thirty (30) days of the issuing of this order.

 

Defendant Generac Power Systems, Inc. to give notice.

Case Number: 25CHCV04349    Hearing Date: September 22, 2026    Dept: F43

Dept. F43

Hearing Date: 09-22-26

Case # 25CHCV04349, Urbn US Retail, LLC v. Centennial VTC, LLC

Trial Date: 06-04-29

 

MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES TO REQUESTS FOR ADMISSION

 

MOVING PARTY:  Defendant/Cross-Complainant Centennial VTC, LLC

RESPONDING PARTY:  Plaintiff/Cross-Defendant Urbn US Retail, LLC

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Order compelling plaintiff/cross-defendant to serve further objection-free responses to defendant/cross-complainant’s first set of requests for admission numbers 1 through 36 and genuineness of documents number 1-4 within ten (10) days of the court’s order.

 

RULING:  Motion is granted in part and denied in part.  Urbn’s request for sanctions is denied.

 

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

 

On December 5, 2025, plaintiff Urbn US Retail, LLC (Urbn) filed this action, contending defendant Centennial VTC, LLC (Centennial) breached two written retail lease agreements.  Urbn leases retail space from Centennial at the Valencia Town Center mall for its brands Urban Outfitters and Anthropologie.  The lease agreements were executed with third party Valencia Town Center Venture, L.P. on October 21, 2020 and January 23, 2020, respectively.  On September 1, 2023, Centennial purchased the leased spaces and assumed the leases as landlord pursuant to Section 46 of the lease agreements.  According to Section 17 of the lease agreements, Urban made the tenant improvements, opened both stores, and has performed all contractual obligations required of it.  However, Centennial has failed and refused to pay for the tenant improvements (Tenant Improvement Allowances), as required under each lease, and Urbn seeks $3,222,329.16, as of November 2025.  Centennial filed an answer on February 9, 2026.

 

On February 10, 2026, Centennial filed a cross-complaint against Urbn, asserting breaches of the lease agreements and declaratory relief.  Centennial alleges Urbn did not seek to enforce the lease agreements with respect to the original landlord’s delinquent failure to pay for the improvements until August 1, 2024 (Anthropologie Lease) and March 1, 2025 (Urban Outfitters Lease), when Urban stopped paying rent.  However, when Centennial assumed the obligations of the original landlord, it did not assume the obligation to pay the Tenant Improvement Allowances.  On May 30, 2025, Urbn filed an action against Centennial in the U.S. District Court for the Central District of California based on failure to pay the Tenant Improvement Allowances.  The action was subsequently dismissed due to lack of subject matter jurisdiction.  As such, Urbn is obligated to pay a total of $968,745.21 in past due rent.  Urbn filed an answer on March 17, 2026.

 

On May 1, 2026, Centennial electronically served Urbn with its first set of requests for admissions.  (Declaration of Julia B. Glazer ¶ 2 [36 requests for admission and 4 requests for admission of genuineness of documents].)  Responses were due June 2, 2026.  On May 25, 2026, Urban’s counsel, Holly Heffner, requested an extension to June 30, 2026, due to being out of the country from June 1 through 18, 2026.  (Id., Exh. A at p. 3.)  After speaking by telephone, counsel agreed to a 10-day extension, June 12, 2026.  (Id., Exh. A at p. 2.)  No responses were served by June 12, 2026.

 

On June 18, 2026, Centennial’s counsel stated that no motion to compel would be filed if Urbn served verified, objection-free responses by close of business on June 19, 2026.  (Glazer Decl., Exh. A at p. 2.)  If verified responses containing objections were served by close of business on June 19, 2026, Centennial would file a motion to deem the matters in the requests for admission as admitted and seek sanctions.  (Id., Exh. A at p. 2.)  Urbn’s counsel responded that same day, stating there was a miscommunication on their end, that it was impossible to meet the June 19, 2026, deadline, and requested an extension to serve the responses by end of day on Tuesday, June 23, 2026.  (Id., Exh. A at p. 1.)  Centennial’s counsel responded “yes,” agreeing to extend the deadline to June 23, 2026.  (Id., Exh. A at p. 1.)  On June 23, 2026, Urbn electronically served its responses which included admissions, denials, and objections.  (Id., Exh. B.)

 

On June 24, 2026, Centennial’s counsel called Urbn’s counsel and a left a voicemail concerning issues with the responses but did not receive a call back.  (Glazer Decl., Exh. D at pp. 5-6.)  On June 25, 2026, Centennial served a “meet and confer” letter, representing that after the deadline passed its counsel had notified Urbn’s counsel that the matters in the requests for admission were deemed admitted by operation of law but would allow Urbn to avoid a motion to deem the requests admitted if Urbn served objection-free responses by end of day on June 19, 2026.  (Id., Exh. C at pp. 1-2.)  As such, Centennial insisted the responses were untimely, that Urbn had waived its objections, and that the substantive responses contradicted Urbn’s own pleadings.  (Id., Exh. C at pp. 2-4.)  Centennial requested that Urbn serve amended objection-free responses with straightforward denials within five days, June 30, 2026.  Urbn’s counsel emailed a response to the “meet and confer” letter on June 30, 2026, discussing its counsel’s interpretation of the agreement.  (Id., Exh. D at pp. 1-5.)  No amended responses were served.

 

On August 10, 2026, Centennial filed the instant motion to compel Urbn’s further objection-free responses to requests for admissions number 1-36 and request for genuineness of documents numbers 1-4 pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 2033.290 and 2033.280, subdivision (a).)  Centennial filed its separate statement on August 11, 2026, but filed an amended declaration on August 12, 2026.  The court does not consider the amended declaration because it was filed after the August 11, 2026 deadline.  The court notes that Centennial did not serve Urbn with its moving papers and amended declaration until August 12, 2026, after the August 11, 2026, deadline.  Because Urbn was able to file a timely opposition and response to Centennial’s separate statement, the court still considers the motion.

 

Urbn filed an opposition on September 9, 2026, and a notice errata on September 17, 2026.

 

Centennial filed a reply on September 15, 2026.

 

 

 

MEET AND CONFER

 

Motions to compel further responses to requests for admission must include a declaration stating facts showing a “reasonable and good faith attempt” to resolve the issues mentioned in the motion before filing.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2016.040, 2033.290, subd. (b)(1).)  In Department F43, meet and confer means in person or via phone.  A letter or email does not suffice.  (Department F43 Courtroom Information, p. 2.)

 

The moving papers indicate Centennial’s counsel called Urbn’s counsel on June 24, 2026, and sent a follow-up letter discussing issues with Urbn’s responses.  However, counsel did not come to an agreement concerning whether the responses should include objections.

 

 

ANALYSIS

 

A.  Compel Further Responses to Requests for Admissions and the Genuineness of Documents

 

Where responses to requests for admission have been served but the demanding party believes the responses are evasive or incomplete or an objection lacks merit, the demanding party may move for an order compelling a further response.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.290, subd. (a).)  The demanding party must serve the motion within 45 days after service of verified responses in question, or any verified supplemental responses, or any specific later date which the parties agreed to in writing.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.290, subd. (c).)  Where a motion to compel further challenges both objections and factual responses, the 45-day deadline begins to run from service of verifications.  (Golf & Tennis Pro Shop, Inc. v. Superior Court (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 127, 134-137.)  Otherwise, the requesting party waives the right to compel further responses to requests for admission.  (See Sperber v. Robinson (1994) 26 Cal.App.4th 736, 745; Sexton v. Superior Court (1997) 48 Cal.App.4th 1403, 1410.)

 

1.  Although Urbn automatically waived all objections by operation of law, by serving responses after the agreed upon June 12, 2026, deadline extension, Centennial did not file a motion to deem the matters in the requests for admission as true and admitted before Urbn served its late responses.

 

Centennial contends the objections are without merit because they were waived by operation of law when Urbn served the responses after the June 12, 2026, deadline extension and asserts the parties agreed to objection-free responses thereafter.  (Mot. at pp. 5:19-7:15 [citing Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280].)

 

“If a party to whom requests for admission are directed fails to serve a timely response, the following rules apply:  (a) The party to whom the requests for admission are directed waives any objection to the requests, including one based on privilege or on the protection for work product[.]  The court, on motion, may relieve that party from this waiver on its determination that both of the following conditions are satisfied:  (1) The party has subsequently served a response that is in substantial compliance with Sections 2033.210, 2033.220, and 2033.230 [and]

(2) The party’s failure to serve a timely response was the result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (a)(1)-(2).)

 

Based on Urbn’s failure to serve responses by the June 12, 2026, deadline, Urbn automatically waived all objections by operation of law.  The language in Centennial’s June 18, 2026, email, represents that Centennial would file a motion to deem the matters in the requests for admission as admitted if Urbn served verified responses with objections.  (Glazer Decl., Exh. A at p. 2.)  However, Centennial never filed such a motion, and Urbn subsequently served responses containing both objections and substantive responses before Centennial filed its motion, with the exception of Request Number 36, which is an objection-only response.

 

Although asserting waived objections in untimely responses to requests for admission does not prevent substantial compliance with Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.220, subdivision (c), no motion to deem admitted was filed.  (See Katayma v. Continental Investment Grp. (2024) 105 Cal.App.5th 898, 909.)  By waiting until after Urbn served its responses to file the instant motion, Centennial must rely on the distinct framework in Section 2033.290 to address the objections.  (See St. Mary v. Superior Court (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 762, 782-784.)  Centennial cannot rely on Section 2033.280’s objection waiver to demonstrate the objections lack merit.  Thus, the motion fails to the extent it seeks relief based on waiver of objection

 

            2.  Centennial’s motion to compel further is granted in part.

 

Centennial seeks further responses to request for admission numbers 1 through 36 and request for genuineness of documents numbers 1 through 4, contending Urbn’s objections lack merit.  (Mot. at pp. 7:17-10:10 [citing Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.220, subs. (a), (b)(1)].)

 

The responding party must answer each request for admission separately with (1) an admission, (2) a denial, or (3) a statement claiming an inability to admit or deny and explaining the reasons why.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.220, subd. (b).)  If the responding party gives lack of information or knowledge as a reason for failure to admit all or part of a request, the responding party must state in the answer that “a reasonable inquiry concerning the matter in the particular request has been made, and that the information known or readily obtainable is insufficient to enable that party to admit the matter.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.220, subd. (c).)  The responding party has a reasonable duty to investigate the facts before answering items that fall outside the party’s personal knowledge.  (Bloxham v. Saldinger (2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 729, 752.)

 

 

Request for Admission Nos. 1 and 2

 

Request numbers 1 and 2 ask whether the original landlord under the Anthropologie and Urban Outfitters leases, paid Urbn any portion of the Tenant Improvement Allowance prior to September 1, 2023.

 

In response, Urbn objects based on overbreadth, unreasonable, undue burden, oppression and using prefatory definitions to seek privileged documents; vague, ambiguous, and unintelligible; overly broad, uncertain, and included unidentified third parties; seeking irrelevant information not reasonably calculated to lead to discovery of admissible evidence; and lacking foundation.

 

Each of these objections lack merit because the request includes definitions defining specific parties and documents, the request is relevant to determining whether Centennial had a valid basis for not paying rent (Urbn and the prior landlord did not pay Centennial the tenant improvement allowance), and the requests simply ask whether Urbn has personal knowledge about specific payments.  Thus, the court overrules each of these objections.

 

As for the attorney-client privilege and attorney-work product doctrine, the court is unclear how the requests seek information concerning confidential attorney-client communications or attorney legal reasoning, through processes, or strategy.  Thus, the court also overrules these objections.

 

Finally, Urbn provides the following improper substantive response to each request:  “Admit Valencia Town Center Venture, L.P. did not pay the Antropologie TIA; deny Valencia Town Center Venture, L.P. was obligated to pay prior to September 1, 2023.” and “Admit Valencia Town Center Venture, L.P. did not pay the Urban Outfitters TIA; deny Valencia Town Center Venture, L.P. was obligated to pay prior to September 1, 2023.”

 

The “denial” in each response is unnecessary because the request did not ask Urbn to admit or deny that Valencia Town Center Venture, L.P. was obligated to pay prior to September 1, 2023.

 

Thus, the court grants Centennial’s motion to compel further as to the Requests for Admission Nos. 1 and 2.

 

                        Request for Admission Nos. 3 and 4

 

Request number 3 asks Urbn to admit the first half of the Anthropologie tenant improvement allowance ($786,620) became due on or before July 2, 2020.  Request number 4 asks Urbn to admit the second half of the Anthropologie tenant improvement allowance ($786,620) became due on or before December 5, 2020.

 

Urbn objects to both requests due to overbreadth, unreasonable, undue burden, oppression and using prefatory definitions to seek privileged documents; seeking irrelevant information not reasonably calculated to lead to discovery of admissible evidence; lacking foundation; and attorney-client privilege and attorney work-production doctrine.  Urbn also denies both requests.
