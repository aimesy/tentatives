Legal Standard

 

            “The party moving for summary judgment bears the burden of persuasion that there is no triable issue of material fact and that he is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.”¿(Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co.¿(2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850 (Aguilar).) A triable issue of material fact exists if the evidence would allow a reasonable trier of fact to find the underlying fact in favor of the party opposing the motion in accordance with the applicable standard of proof. (Ibid.)¿ 

¿ 

            “When deciding whether to grant summary judgment, the court must consider all of the evidence set forth in the papers (except evidence to which the court has sustained an objection), as well as all reasonable inferences that may be drawn from that evidence, in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment.”¿(Avivi v. Centro Medico Urgente Medical Center¿(2008) 159 Cal. App. 4th 463, 467; Code Civ. Proc. § 437c, subd. (c).)

Analysis

 

A.    Defendant’s Motion for Summary Judgment Against Caiden

 

                        Preliminary Issues

 

            As an initial matter, there are three significant defects in Defendant’s motion against Caiden. First, the notice of motion does not identify the issues that Defendant is basing its motion for summary adjudication on. Second, the separate statement does not identify all three causes of action in Caiden’s operative pleading or the issues on which Defendant moves on its basis for summary adjudication.

 

             A “fundamental principle of summary judgment law that was disregarded—that the procedural rules required by statute must be strictly applied.” (California-American Water Co. v. Marina Coast Water Dist. (2022) 86 Cal.App.5th 1272, 1296.)

 

            ““It is elemental that a notice of motion must state in writing the ‘grounds upon which it will be made.’ [Citations.] Only the ground specified in the notice of motion may be considered by the trial court. [Citations.] This rule has been held to be especially true in the case of motions for summary adjudication of issues. [Citation.]” [Citation.] The Gonzales court continued, “The language in Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (f) makes it clear that a motion for summary adjudication cannot be considered by the court unless the party bringing the motion duly gives notice that summary adjudication is being sought. [Citations.] ‘If a party desires adjudication of particular issues or subissues, that party must make its intentions clear in the motion....’ [Citation.] There is a sound reason for this rule: ‘... the opposing party may have decided to raise only one triable issue of fact in order to defeat the motion, without intending to concede the other issues. It would be unfair to grant a summary adjudication order unless the opposing party was on notice that an issue-by-issue adjudication might be ordered if summary judgment was denied.’ [Citation.]” (Jimenez v. Protective Life Ins. Co. (1992) 8 Cal.App.4th 528, 534 (citations omitted).) “If summary adjudication is sought, whether separately or as an alternative to the motion for summary judgment, the specific cause of action, affirmative defense, claims for damages, or issues of duty must be stated specifically in the notice of motion and be repeated, verbatim, in the separate statement of undisputed material facts.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1350(b).)

 

            Defendant’s notice of motion does not identify the issues on which it bases its motion for summary adjudication. The separate statement is also defective as it only identifies two headings labeled as “A. There are issues of Fact with Respect to the Causes of Action for Negligence” and “B. There Are No Triable Issues of Fact with Respect to the Cause of Action for Premises Liability.” (Separate Statement, 2:6-7, 20:5-6.) Defendant’s notice of motion and separate statement are defective regarding its motion for summary adjudication. Thus, the Court will only address Defendant’s motion for summary judgment and not summary adjudication for failing to comply with the procedural requirements identified above.

 

                        Merits of Defendant’s Motion for Summary Judgment

 

            Defendant argues that it is entitled to summary judgment because there are no triable issues of fact with respect to the first, second, and third causes of action. The Court finds that Defendant’s moving papers and supporting evidence does not support such claim.

 

            Defendant’s notice of motion states that its motion against Caiden is as to his operative Complaint that was “filed on or about February 2, 2022.” (Notice of Motion, i:3-8; Defendant’s Separate Statement, 1:4-8.) Defendant also affirms its arguments within the memorandum of authorities referring to Caiden’s Complaint, and includes the Complaint in its moving papers. (Mot., 3:24-4:11, 6:25-27; Ex. C.) However, the operative Complaint in Caiden’s case is the Second Amended Complaint filed on August 19, 2024. (August 19, 2024, Caiden’s SAC.)

 

            The pleadings delimit the issues to be considered on a motion for summary judgment. [Citation.]” (Laabs v. City of Victorville (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 1242, 1253, as modified on denial of reh'g (July 7, 2008).) “Accordingly, the burden of a defendant moving for summary judgment only requires that he or she negate plaintiff's theories of liability as alleged in the complaint; that is, a moving party need not refute liability on some theoretical possibility not included in the pleadings.” (Hutton v. Fidelity National Title Co. (2013) 213 Cal.App.4th 486, 493, as modified on denial of reh'g (Feb. 22, 2013).)

 

            Defendant attempts to cure this defect in reply by arguing that the reply was made as to “each Cause of Action in the operative Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”) filed by Plaintiff [Caiden] . . . on February 2, 2022.” (Reply, 1:4-7.) However, this does not cure the defect. Caiden’s initial Complaint alleged three causes of action for: (1) negligence—wrongful death; (2) survival—wrongful death; and (3) premises liability—wrongful death. While the SAC alleges three causes of action for: (1) negligence—wrongful death; (2) wrongful death—premises liability; and (3) negligent hiring/retention/supervision training. It is unequivocally clear to the Court that Defendant made its motion for summary judgment against Caiden as to the Complaint and not the SAC, and has failed to address the allegations made in the SAC. Defendant does not address the third cause of action for negligent hiring/retention/supervision training. Because the Court is only considering the merits of Defendant’s motion for summary judgment, Defendant has not met its burden to prevail on its motion because it did not address Caiden’s third cause of action for negligent hiring/retention/supervision training.

 

            Moreover, as to the premises liability cause of action, the SAC does not make allegations against Defendant for owning the property or controlling the property where the Subject Incident occurred. The SAC alleges that Defendant was involved in organizing, promoting, selecting and coordinating artists, implementing safety measures, controlling access to restricted backstage areas, maintaining backstage, and providing reasonable security measures for invited artists and guests. (SAC, ¶¶ 78-81.) Defendant does not address these other issues in its moving papers.

 

            Defendant has not established that there is no triable issue of material fact as to all three causes of action in the SAC. Because Defendant failed to meet its burden, the burden does not shift to Caiden.

 

            Therefore, the Court denies Defendant’s motion for summary judgment against Caiden. The Court also denies Defendant’s motion for summary adjudication against Caiden.

 

B.     Defendant’s Motion for Summary Judgment Against Devante Plaintiffs

Similar to its motion against Caiden, in its motion against Devante Plaintiffs, the notice of motion and separate statement do not identify the issues that Defendant is addressing in its motion for summary adjudication.

 

            The Court reincorporates the legal doctrine it included in this ruling for Defendant’s motion against Caiden. Defendant’s motion against Devante Plaintiffs’ is defective in the same way as its motion against Caiden. The notice of motion fails to identify the issues as the basis for the motion for summary adjudication. (See Notice of Motion.) As a result, Defendant’s separate statement also lacks the issues listed for the Court to determine what material facts go to each issue for its motion for summary adjudication. Thus, the Court will only address Defendant’s motion for summary judgment and not summary adjudication for failing to comply with the procedural requirements.

 

                        Merits of Defendant’s Motion for Summary Judgment

 

            Defendant contends that it is entitled to summary judgment because Plaintiff cannot present any evidence that: (1) Defendant owed a duty to Decedent to ensure his safety; (2) Defendant’s acts or omissions were the proximate cause of Decedent’s death; and (3) Defendant was responsible for selecting or supervising any security services that provided any security at the Music Festival. (Mot., 5:1-4.)

 

                        First Cause of Action – Negligence

 

            “‘To succeed in a negligence action, the plaintiff must show that (1) the defendant owed the plaintiff a legal duty, (2) the defendant breached the duty, and (3) the breach proximately or legally caused (4) the plaintiff's damages or injuries.’” (Thomas v. Stenberg (2012) 206 Cal.App.4th 654, 662.) “As a general rule, each person has a duty to exercise reasonable care to avoid causing injury to others. [Citation.] However, a person who has not created a peril generally has no duty to take affirmative action to protect against it, and a person generally has no duty to protect another from the conduct of third parties.” (University of Southern California v. Superior Court (2018) 30 Cal.App.5th 429, 440.) “A defendant cannot be held liable for the defective or dangerous condition of property which it did not own, possess, or control. Where the absence of ownership, possession, or control has been unequivocally established, summary judgment is proper.” (Isaacs v. Huntington Memorial Hospital (1985) 38 Cal.3d 112, 134 (Isaacs).) “Whether such a duty exists is a question of law to be determined on a case-by-case basis.” (Id. at p. 124.)

 

            Thus, “‘[a] tenant ordinarily is not liable for injuries to his invitees occurring outside the leased premises on common passageways over which he has no control. [Citations.] Responsibility in such cases rests on the owner, who has the right of control and the duty to maintain that part of the premises in a safe condition. It is clear, however, that if the tenant exercises control over a common passageway outside the leased premises, he may become liable to his business invitees if he fails to warn them of a dangerous condition existing thereon.’” [Citation.] The “‘crucial element is control.’” (Moses v. Roger-McKeever (2023) 91 Cal.App.5th 172, 179 (citation omitted).)

 

            Defendant argues that it did not owe Decedent a duty because “it simply assisted Defendant C3 ultimately in booking various artists for the Music Festival.” (Mot., 6:2-4.) However, Defendant’s evidence does not support that conclusion.

 

            “The party moving for summary judgment bears an initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing of the nonexistence of any triable issue of material fact; if he carries his burden of production, he causes a shift, and the opposing party is then subjected to a burden of production of his own to make a prima facie showing of the existence of a triable issue of material fact.” (Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at p. 850.)

 

            “In order to meet the test of sufficiency and thus place the proceeding in the posture where, in the absence of sufficient counter-affidavits, the trial court would be required to grant the motion for summary judgment, the affidavit or affidavits of the moving party must satisfy three requirements: (1) They must contain facts sufficient to entitle the moving party to a judgment, i.e., facts establishing every element necessary to sustain a judgment in his favor; (2) such facts must be set forth with particularity, i.e., all the requisite evidentiary facts must be stated, and not the ultimate facts or conclusions of law; and (3) the affiant must show that if sworn as a witness he can testify competently to the evidentiary facts contained in his affidavit.”
(Kramer v. Barnes (1963) 212 Cal.App.2d 440, 446 (Kramer).) “The declarations are deficient in that they contain in part only conclusions.” (Colby v. Schwartz (1978) 78 Cal.App.3d 885, 888.)

 

            Defendant’s separate statement primarily relies on Drieslien’s declaration. Several paragraphs of Drieslien’s declaration are stricken for evidentiary objections, but the declaration is also defective. There is a lack of facts to establish that Defendant did not owe a duty to Decedent, and the role Defendant had in the events leading up to and at the Music Festival remains a triable issue of material fact. Drieslien’s declaration attests to more than his knowledge of the facts of the events; he repeatedly states his role, his involvement, and what other people employed by Defendant did not do. (See Drieslien’s Decl.) Romero’s declaration alone does not support Defendant’s argument that no triable issue of material fact exists regarding the material issue of whether Defendant did or did not owe Decedent a duty.

 

            Moreover, the holding in USC’s motion for summary judgment has no effect on Defendant's motion for summary judgment. USC met its burden to prevail on summary judgment. Defendant must present evidence to support that it is also entitled to summary judgment; it cannot rely on another party’s outcome as evidence for the purpose of summary judgment.

 

            Thus, Defendant does not meet its initial burden to establish that no triable issue of material fact exists regarding Devante Plaintiff’s claims against it. Because Defendant did not meet its burden, the burden does not shift to Devante Plaintiffs. As addressed in this ruling, due to Defendant’s defective notice of motion and separate statement the Court is only addressing Defendant’s motion for summary judgment. 

 

            Therefore, the Court denies Defendant’s motion for summary judgment. The Court also denies Defendant’s motion for summary adjudication.

Case Name:                 Garcia v. Ramirez, et al.

Case No.:                    24STCV02208 

Matter:                        Motion for Summary Judgment

Moving Party:             Defendants Marco Ramirez and Maria Ramirez

Responding Party:       Plaintiff Rebecca Garcia

 

Tentative Ruling:      Defendants Marco Ramirez and Maria Ramirez’s motion for summary judgment is denied.

 

 

            Plaintiff Rebecca Garcia filed this action against Defendants Marco Ramirez, Maria Ramirez (collectively, “Ramirez Defendants”), Erika Coronado, and Does 1 through 20. The Complaint alleges three causes of action for: (1) strict liability pursuant to Civil Code section 3342; (2) negligence; and (3) strict liability (dog with known dangerous propensities).

 

                 Ramirez Defendants move for summary judgment. Plaintiff opposes.

 

Legal Standard

 

            “The party moving for summary judgment bears the burden of persuasion that there is no triable issue of material fact and that he is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.”¿(Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co.¿(2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850.) A triable issue of material fact exists if the evidence would allow a reasonable trier of fact to find the underlying fact in favor of the party opposing the motion in accordance with the applicable standard of proof. (Ibid.)¿ 

¿ 

            “When deciding whether to grant summary judgment, the court must consider all of the evidence set forth in the papers (except evidence to which the court has sustained an objection), as well as all reasonable inferences that may be drawn from that evidence, in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment.”¿(Avivi v. Centro Medico Urgente Medical Center¿(2008) 159 Cal. App. 4th 463, 467; Code Civ. Proc. § 437c, subd. (c).) 

 

Analysis

 

            Ramirez Defendants move for summary judgment against Plaintiff Rebecca Garcia’s Complaint. Defendants contend that Plaintiff’s causes of action against them have no merit because there are no triable issues of material fact that Defendants owned the Subject Dog or had knowledge of any previous dangerous propensities of the Subject Dog, and under California law did not owe Plaintiff a duty.

 

            Preliminary Matters

 

            As an initial matter, the Court notes that the notice of motion states only that Defendants seek summary judgment and does not, in the alternative, state a motion for summary adjudication. (See Notice of Motion.)

 

            A “fundamental principle of summary judgment law that was disregarded—that the procedural rules required by statute must be strictly applied.” (California-American Water Co. v. Marina Coast Water Dist. (2022) 86 Cal.App.5th 1272, 1296.)

 

            ““It is elemental that a notice of motion must state in writing the ‘grounds upon which it will be made.’ [Citations.] Only the ground specified in the notice of motion may be considered by the trial court. [Citations.] This rule has been held to be especially true in the case of motions for summary adjudication of issues. [Citation.]” [Citation.] The Gonzales court continued, “The language in Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (f) makes it clear that a motion for summary adjudication cannot be considered by the court unless the party bringing the motion duly gives notice that summary adjudication is being sought. [Citations.] ‘If a party desires adjudication of particular issues or subissues, that party must make its intentions clear in the motion....’ [Citation.] There is a sound reason for this rule: ‘... the opposing party may have decided to raise only one triable issue of fact in order to defeat the motion, without intending to concede the other issues. It would be unfair to grant a summary adjudication order unless the opposing party was on notice that an issue-by-issue adjudication might be ordered if summary judgment was denied.’ [Citation.]” (Jimenez v. Protective Life Ins. Co. (1992) 8 Cal.App.4th 528, 534 (citations omitted).) “If summary adjudication is sought, whether separately or as an alternative to the motion for summary judgment, the specific cause of action, affirmative defense, claims for damages, or issues of duty must be stated specifically in the notice of motion and be repeated, verbatim, in the separate statement of undisputed material facts.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1350(b).)

 

             Because Defendants’ notice of motion does not also seek summary adjudication, as long as Defendants do not meet their burden on any cause of action, Defendants’ motion for summary judgment must be denied.

 

            Merits of Defendants’ Motion for Summary Judgment

 

            Defendants contend that each and every cause of action have no merit. The evidence before the Court does not support Defendants’ argument.

 

            Defendants argue that, as landowners under Civil Code section 3342, they do not owe Plaintiff a duty because they do not own the dog. While under a strict liability theory, there may be truth to this claim if it were undisputed that Defendants are not the owners of the Subject Dog. But under a negligence theory, Defendants do not present evidence to support that there is no triable issue of material fact regarding whether they owed Plaintiff a duty. Moreover, the Court notes that Defendants presented only eight material facts in support of their motion. (See Separate Statement.)

 

           The undisputed facts in the present motion are that on April 2, 2022, Plaintiff alleges that while delivering a package to Defendants’ home, she was bitten by a dog (“Subject Dog”) (“Subject Incident”). (Undisputed Material Fact (“UMF”) 1, 2)  Defendants are the owners of the Subject Premises, 2510 E. Gleason Avenue, Los Angeles, California 90033 (“Subject Premises”), where the Subject Incident occurred. (UMF 3) On April 2, 2022, Erica Coronado brought the Subject Dog to the Subject Premises for a visit. (UMF 6) Furthermore, the Subject Dog has never bitten any person or attempted to bite any person or dog prior to the Subject Incident. (UMF 7.) However, Defendants' Separate Statement only has one material facts regarding  whether Defendants knew that the Subject Dog had dangerous and vicious propensities. (See UMF 8)  Defendants do not meet their burden of production to show that they did not, in fact, have knowledge of the Subject Dog’s dangerous propensities.

 

            Furthermore, as to the negligence cause of action, the evidence before the Court does not support Defendants’ argument that Plaintiff’s claim against it is without merit.

 

            “Under California law, a landlord owes a duty of care to his tenant's invitees to prevent injury from the tenant's vicious dog when the landlord has “actual knowledge” of the dog's vicious nature in time to protect against the dangerous condition on his property.” (Yuzon v. Collins (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 149, 152.) “A common element in these exceptions is that either at or after the time possession is given to the tenant the landlord retains or acquires a recognizable degree of control over the dangerous condition with a concomitant right and power to obviate the condition and prevent the injury. In these situations, the law imposes on the landlord a duty to use ordinary care to eliminate the condition with resulting liability for injuries caused by his failure so to act.” (Uccello v. Laudenslayer (1975) 44 Cal.App.3d 504, 511.)

 

            Defendants rely on Yuzcon and Uccello to argue that they are not liable. However, this argument is without merit because the separate statement lacks evidence that on the day of the Subject Incident the alleged owner of the Subject Dog, Erica Coronado, was a tenant. (See UMF 4-6; Defendants Ex. B., 18:1-5, 27:1-7.) The produced evidence supports the finding that after 2020 Coronado was no longer a tenant at the Subject Property. (See UMF 4; Defendants Ex. C, 24:14-18; 46:5-7.) Thus, Ucello and Yuzcon do not support Defendants’ argument that the negligence claim against them is without merit. Defendants do not make further argument to argue that there is no triable issue of material fact regarding whether they owed Plaintiff a duty of care under a negligence theory as owners of the Subject Property.

 

            Thus, Defendants fail to meet their burden of proof as to the negligence cause of action. Because Defendants fail to produce admissible evidence that no triable issue of material fact exists as to the negligence cause of action, the burden does not shift to Plaintiff. Because the burden does not shift and Defendants only filed their motion as a motion for summary judgment, their entire motion fails. The Court will not address the merits of Defendants’ arguments as to the remaining causes of action. 

 

            Conclusion

 

            Defendants Marco Ramirez and Maria Ramirez’s motion for summary judgment is denied.

Judge James I. Montgomery

Department 516

Hearing Date:             July 9, 2026   

Case Name:               Navarrette v. Better Mortgage Corporation

Case No.:                   26STCV12157

Matter:                        Application to be Admitted Pro Hac Vice

Moving Party:             Better Mortgage Corporation

Responding Party:      None

 

Tentative Ruling:      The Applications to be admitted pro hac vice are denied. 

 

 

            Defendant Better Mortgage Corporation applies for two orders granting

Michael J. Lufkin and Carly E. Hazelip admission to appear pro hac vice on its behalf. The

applications are unopposed.

 

Legal Standard

 

            An attorney in good standing in another jurisdiction may apply to appear as counsel pro hac vice in the State of California by filing a verified application together with proof of service by mail of a copy of the application and notice of hearing on all parties who have appeared in the case and on the State Bar of California at its San Francisco office, with payment of a $600.00 fee. The attorney cannot be a resident of California, employed in the state, or regularly engage in substantial business, professional, or other activities in the state. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(a)(1)-(3), (c)(1).)  

¿ 

            The written application must state: (1) the applicant’s residence and office address; (2) the courts to which the applicant has been admitted to practice and the dates of admission; (3) that the applicant is a member in good standing in those courts; (4) that the applicant is not currently suspended or disbarred in any court; (5) the title of each court and cause in which the applicant has filed an application to appear as counsel pro hac vice in this state in the preceding two years, the date of each application, and whether or not it was granted; and (6) the name, address, and telephone number of the active member of the State Bar of California who is attorney of record in the local action. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(d)(1)-(6).)¿ 

 

Analysis

 

            Both Michael J. Lufkin’s and Carly E. Hazelip’s applications contain the required information under California Rules of Court, rule 9.40(d).

 

            However, both applications are defective because there is no indication that Defendant has paid the respective application fees. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(e).) Both memorandums mention a $50 fee, but it is unclear what it relates to, as the fee for each application is $600. (Hazelip Mot., 3:16-18; Lufkin Mot., 3:16-18.)

 

            Thus, without any certainty that both Michael J. Lufkin and Carly E. Hazelip have paid the required fees, the Court denies both applications.

 

Conclusion

           

            The Court denies the Applications of counsel, Michael J. Lufkin and Carly E. Hazelip, to appear on behalf of Defendant Better Mortgage Corporation.

 

 

 


Case Number: 26STCV13825    Hearing Date: July 9, 2026    Dept: 516

Judge James I. Montgomery

Department 516

Hearing Date:             July 9, 2026   

Case Name:               Urbina v. Mobilitas Insurance Company, et al.

Case No.:                   26STCV13825      Matter:                        Motion to Compel Arbitration

Moving Party:              Petitioner Juan Carlos Garcia Urbina

Responding Party:       Respondent Mobilitas Insurance Company

 

Tentative Ruling:      Petitioner Juan Carlos Garcia Urbina’s motion is continued until August 20, 2026.

 

 

            On April 30, 2026, Petitioner Juan Carlos Garcia Urbina (“Petitioner”) filed his petition against Respondents Mobilitas Insurance Company (“Mobilitas”) and United States Fire Insurance Company (“Fire Insurance”).

 

            Petitioner moves for the Court to compel a joint arbitration proceeding with Mobilitas and Fire Insurance. Mobilitas opposes. Fire Insurance did not file an opposition.

 

            The Court notes that, while Petitioner has reserved the hearing on his motion as a motion to compel arbitration, it is, in fact, a motion to consolidate arbitration proceedings with Mobilitas and Fire Insurance.

 

Legal Standard

 

            Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.3 states that:

 

A party to an arbitration agreement may petition the court to consolidate separate arbitration proceedings, and the court may order consolidation of separate arbitration proceedings when:

            (1) Separate arbitration agreements or proceedings exist between the same parties; or one party is a party to a separate arbitration agreement or proceeding with a third party; and

             (2) The disputes arise from the same transactions or series of related transactions; and

            (3) There is common issue or issues of law or fact creating the possibility of conflicting rulings by more than one arbitrator or panel of arbitrators.

 

If all of the applicable arbitration agreements name the same arbitrator, arbitration panel, or arbitration tribunal, the court, if it orders consolidation, shall order all matters to be heard before the arbitrator, panel, or tribunal agreed to by the parties. If the applicable arbitration agreements name separate arbitrators, panels, or tribunals, the court, if it orders consolidation, shall, in the absence of an agreed method of selection by all parties to the consolidated arbitration, appoint an arbitrator in accord with the procedures set forth in Section 1281.6.

In the event that the arbitration agreements in consolidated proceedings contain inconsistent provisions, the court shall resolve such conflicts and determine the rights and duties of the various parties to achieve substantial justice under all the circumstances.

The court may exercise its discretion under this section to deny consolidation of separate arbitration proceedings or to consolidate separate arbitration proceedings only as to certain issues, leaving other issues to be resolved in separate proceedings.

This section shall not be applicable to an agreement to arbitrate disputes as to the professional negligence of a health care provider made pursuant to Section 1295.

(Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.3.)

 

            Insurance Code section 11580.2 establishes certain rules governing uninsured and underinsured motor vehicle insurance policies: “The policy or an endorsement added thereto shall provide that the determination as to whether the insured shall be legally entitled to recover damages, and if so entitled, the amount thereof, shall be made by agreement between the insured and the insurer or, in the event of disagreement, by arbitration. The arbitration shall be conducted by a single neutral arbitrator. An award or a judgment confirming an award shall not be conclusive on any party in any action or proceeding between (i) the insured, his or her insurer, his or her legal representative, or his or her heirs and (ii) the uninsured motorist to recover damages arising out of the accident upon which the award is based.” (Ins. Code, § 11580.2, subd. (f).) The provisions of section 11580.2 are deemed to be a part of every uninsured motorist policy. (See Quintano v. Mercury Casualty Co. (1995) 11 Cal.4th 1049, 1053; Harford Fire Insurance Co. v. Macri (1992) 4 Cal.4th 318, 324; Travelers Indemnity Co. v. Kowalski (1965) 233 Cal.App.2d 607, 609.)

           

Analysis

             Preliminary Issue

             A preliminary issue is that Petitioner served his motions on Fire Insurance and Mobilitas  electronically, which is improper. (See Proof of Service.) The Court’s records show that neither entity has consented to electronic service. Per California Rules of Court, rule 2.251(b), electronic service on a party is valid only with the party's express consent. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.251(b).)

 

            Mobilitas did file an opposition, but Fire Insurance did not. The Court will address the merits of Petitioner’s motion, and if at the hearing Fire Insurance submits to the tentative, the tentative will be adopted. Otherwise, the Court will continue the hearing on the motion to allow proper service pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1005, subd. (b). (Code Civ. Proc., § 1005, subd. (b).)

 

            Motion to Consolidate

 

             Petitioner moves to consolidate the arbitration proceedings with Mobilitas and Fire Insurance into a single proceeding.

 

            In opposition, Mobilitas contends that it does not oppose the consolidation of arbitration, but requests that the Court to “make it clear that contractually UNITED STATES FIRE/BLUESTAR will pay any award for medical specials, and MOBILITAS will pay any award for general damages to avoid double recovery. (Opp., 4:19-21.) Mobilitas argues that pursuant to Business and Professions Code section 7455, subdivision (e), “[a]ny benefits provided to an app-based driver under subdivision (a) or (b) of this section shall be considered amounts payable under a worker's compensation law or disability benefit for the purpose of determining amounts payable under any insurance provided under Article 2 (commencing with Section 11580) of Chapter 1 of Part 3 of Division 2 of the Insurance Code.” (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 7455.) The Court will not specify which costs Mobilitas and Fire Insurance are responsible for, as that determination is based on the agreements with each entity. The Mobilitas Agreement states that:

 

9. Same Element of Loss Where the law permits, we will not pay for any amounts recoverable under this policy by a “Rideshare Driver” who has received, or is entitled to, payment under any: a. Occupational accident insurance; or b. Driver injury protection insurance.

(Mot., Ex. A, p. 28.)

            While the Fire Insurance policy specifies:

 

Certificate DEFINITIONS This document, which is a legal contract between Policyholder and Us to provide coverage for Occupational Accidents.

(Petition, Fire Insurance Policy, p. 4.)

 

            Thus, the Court will not specify what each entity can cover because the parties are limited to what each policy states.

 

            Also, while Petitioner and Mobilitas agree to a joint arbitration, Petitioner is required to satisfy the elements under Code of Civil Procedure, section 1281.3, to compel Fire Insurance to a consolidated arbitration.

 

            First, Mobilitas is required to arbitrate Petitioner’s claims pursuant to Insurance Code section 11580.2. While the Fire Insurance policy contains the following arbitration provision:

 

This Arbitration provision permanently bars the institution of any individual or class action lawsuit brought by the Covered Person or the Recipient of Payment. With this binding Arbitration provision, the Covered Person, for himself or herself or the Recipient of Payment is waiving the right to a trial by jury.

(Petition, Fire Insurance Policy, p. 20.)

 

            Also, the disputes regarding Mobilitas and Fire Insurance relate to the same accident Petitioner suffered on December 22, 2023. At the time of the incident, Petitioner was covered by both policies.

 

            Third, consolidation is required to prevent inconsistent rulings, as Mobilitas and Fire Insurance dispute who is responsible and for how much Petitioner is entitled to from each entity. The Court finds that consolidation achieves substantial justice and does not prejudice Mobilitas or Fire Insurance. (See Parker v. McCaw (2005) 125 Cal.App.4th 1494, 1505.)

 

            Thus, all three elements are satisfied to grant consolidation of the separate  arbitration proceedings pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.3.

 

            Appointment of Arbitrator

 

            Here, neither Mobilitas's nor Fire Insurance’s agreements specify a method to select an arbitrator. Mobilitas’s policy only states that “[t]he arbitration shall be conducted by a single neutral arbitrator.” (Mot., Ex. A, p. 33.)

 

            Code Civil Procedure section 1281.6 provides that “[i]f the arbitration agreement provides a method of appointing an arbitrator, that method shall be followed. If the arbitration agreement does not provide a method for appointing an arbitrator, the parties to the agreement who seek arbitration and against whom arbitration is sought may agree on a method of appointing an arbitrator and that method shall be followed. In the absence of an agreed method, or if the agreed method fails or for any reason cannot be followed, or when an arbitrator appointed fails to act and his or her successor has not been appointed, the court, on petition of a party to the arbitration agreement, shall appoint the arbitrator.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 1281.6.) Upon such a petition, the court shall nominate five potential arbitrators from lists of people supplied jointly by the parties or obtained from a governmental agency concerned with arbitration or private disinterested association concerned with arbitration. (Ibid.) The parties shall then, within five days of receipt of the nominations, jointly select an arbitrator; if the parties fail to do so, the court shall select an arbitrator from among the nominees. (Ibid.)  

 

            The parties dispute as to whether they have engaged in good faith in selecting an arbitrator. Both Mobilitas and Petitioner contend that they have agreed to retain Hon. Robert Moss (Ret.) to conduct the arbitration proceedings. However, there is no evidence that Fire Insurance has agreed to the selection of the Hon. Robert Moss. The Court is inclined to continue the hearing on the appointment of an arbitrator to allow the parties to select an arbitrator before the Court proceeds with the selection process.

 

            Petitioner’s Timing Request

 

            Petitioner does not provide the Court with any legal authority that it has to require arbitration to be completed within 90 days. At this time, the Court denies Petitioner’s request.

 

Conclusion

 

            Petitioner Juan Carlos Garcia Urbina’s motion to consolidate arbitration proceedings continued until August 20, 2026.

 

            Petitioner’s request to require arbitration to be completed within 90 days is denied.
