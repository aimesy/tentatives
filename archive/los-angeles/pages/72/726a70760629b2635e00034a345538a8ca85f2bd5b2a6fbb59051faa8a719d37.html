Superior Court of California

KANG HWA KO,

Plaintiff,

vs.

LAN CHEN, et al.

Defendants.

Case No.:

25STCV06000

Hearing Date:

August 4, 2026

ORDER GRANTING MOTION FOR DETERMINATION OF GOOD FAITH SETTLEMENT

 

I.           BACKGROUND

Plaintiffs Kang Hwa Ko, Gi Hwan Ko, and Tanya Yim Son were on a property owned by Defendants Alexandre Cazazian (“Alexandre”) and Luiza Cazazian (“Luiza”) when a vehicle, owned and operated by Defendants Lan Chen (“Lan”) and Peng-Shen Chen (“Peng-Shen”) drove into the building, causing injury to Plaintiffs. Plaintiffs sued Defendants for five causes of action, including motor vehicle tort and premises liability.

Pursuant to the Cross-Complaint filed on May 28, 2025, Alexandre and Luiza sued Peng-Shen and Lan for indemnity, apportionment of fault, and declaratory relief.

Pursuant to the Cross-Complaint filed on March 24, 2025, Alexandre, on behalf of the Cazazian Trust (“Trust”), sued Peng-Shen and Lan for indemnity, apportionment of fault, and declaratory relief.

Pursuant to the Cross-Complaint filed on April 7, 2026, Peng-Shen and Lan sued Alezandre, Luiza, and Trust for indemnity, apportionment of fault, comparative fault, and declaratory relief.

On March 19, 2026, Peng-Shen and Lan (“Moving Defendants”) filed the instant motion for determination of good faith settlement between Moving Defendants and Kang Hwa Ko and Gi Hwan Ko (“Kos”). Luiza, Alexandre, and the Trust (“Opposing Defendants”) filed an opposition. Moving Defendants filed a reply.

 

II.         LEGAL STANDARD

California Code of Civil Procedure section 877.6, subdivision (a)(2),[1] states that “a settling party may give notice of settlement to all parties and to the court, together with an application for determination of good faith settlement and a proposed order. The application shall indicate the settling parties, and the basis, terms, and amount of the settlement. The notice, application, and proposed order shall be given by certified mail, return receipt requested, or by personal service. Proof of service shall be filed with the court. Within 25 days of the mailing of the notice, application, and proposed order, or within 20 days of personal service, a nonsettling party may file a notice of motion to contest the good faith of the settlement. If none of the nonsettling parties files a motion within 25 days of mailing of the notice, application, and proposed order, or within 20 days of personal service, the court may approve the settlement. The notice by a nonsettling party shall be given in the manner provided in subdivision (b) of Section 1005. However, this paragraph shall not apply to settlements in which a confidentiality agreement has been entered into regarding the case or the terms of the settlement.” The statute further clarifies that the party asserting the lack of good faith shall have the burden of proof on that issue.[2]  (§ 877.6, subd. (d).)

Section 877 provides: “[w]here a release, dismissal with or without prejudice, or a covenant not to sue or not to enforce judgment is given in good faith before verdict or judgment to one or more of a number of tortfeasors claimed to be liable for the same tort, or to one or more other co-obligors mutually subject to contribution rights, it shall have the following effect: (a) It shall not discharge any other such party from liability unless its terms so provide, but it shall reduce the claims against the others in the amount stipulated by the release, the dismissal or the covenant, or in the amount of the consideration paid for it, whichever is the greater. (b) It shall discharge the party to whom it is given from all liability for any contribution to any other parties.”

In Tech-Bilt, Inc. v. Woodward-Clyde & Associates (1985) 38 Cal.3d 488, 499, the California Supreme Court identified the following nonexclusive factors courts are to consider in determining if a settlement is in good faith under section 877.6: “a rough approximation of plaintiffs’ total recovery and the settlor's proportionate liability, the amount paid in settlement, the allocation of settlement proceeds among plaintiffs, and a recognition that a settlor should pay less in settlement than he would if he were found liable after a trial.  Other relevant considerations include the financial conditions and insurance policy limits of settling defendants, as well as the existence of collusion, fraud, or tortious conduct aimed to injure the interests of nonsettling defendants.”

           

III.       DISCUSSION

Moving Defendants request the Court finds the settlement between Moving Defendants and the Kos in the amount of $225,000.00 was entered into in good faith and dismiss any and all pending claims filed against Moving Defendants in this action, as well as all actual or potential claims against Moving Defendants, for equitable causes of action arising out of the subject matter of this action.

When determining if a settlement is in good faith pursuant to section 877.6, the Court reviews the nonexclusive factors outlined in Tech-Bilt, Inc. v. Woodward-Clyde & Associates (1985) 38 Cal.3d 488, 499. These factors include: 1) the plaintiff’s total recovery in comparison to the settlor’s proportionate liability; 2) the amount paid in settlement; 3) the allocation of settlement proceeds among plaintiffs (if there are multiple); 4) a recognition that a settlor should pay less in settlement than if found liable at trial; 5) financial conditions and insurance policy limits of the settlor; and 6) collusion, fraud, or tortious conduct aimed to injure the interests of non-settling defendants. (Ibid.) The evaluation of whether a settlement was made in good faith is required to “be made on the basis of information available at the time of settlement.”  (Ibid.) 

A.         Recovery, Proportionate Liability, and Amount Paid in Settlement

Moving Defendants and the Kos settled this claim in July 2025, with Moving Defendants agreeing to pay the Kos $225,000.00, in return for the Kos dismissing, with prejudice, and releasing Moving Defendants from all future claims related to this matter. (Mot., Hill Decl. ¶ 2.)

Gi’s claims were entirely based upon emotional distress pursuant to loss of consortium, and unsupported by any medical documentation. (Id. at ¶¶ 5-6, Ex A-B.) Kang’s claims were based upon injuries to head, neck, back, arms, hands, shoulders, knees, pelvis, legs, chest, and ankles, as well as anxiety, depression, nervousness, nausea, blurred vision, limping and incontinence. (Id. at ¶ 7, Ex. C.) At the time of settlement, Kang claimed $223,675.94 in medical costs. (Id. at ¶ 8, Ex. D.) Kang also claimed $53,978.00 in property damages that had already been paid. (Id. at ¶ 7, Ex. C.) There were no claims for loss of earnings or other damages. Therefore, uncontested economic damages totaled $223,675.94.

Even assuming Moving Defendants are liable for 100% of the Kos injuries, Moving Defendants’ payment of $225,000.00 is within ‘the ballpark’ of a reasonable payment for economic damages known at the time of settlement. The Court evaluates settlements within the context of information available at the time of settlement. (See Tech-Bilt, Inc., supra, 38 Cal.3d at 499.) Parties are not obligated to go above and beyond to research potential liability of other parties or damages not claimed by a plaintiff; this would dissuade parties from entering into swift settlements.

Opposing Defendants argue that the special damages at least total $245,055.25, as of the time of filing an opposition. (Opp., Cruz Decl. ¶ 6, Ex. C.) The Court notes Opposing Defendants rely upon Kang’s form interrogatory responses served in April 2026, approximately 9 months after settlement was reached. Therefore, this number is irrelevant for purposes of determining this motion.

As outlined in Civil Code section 1431.2, subdivision (a), in any action for personal injury, the liability of each defendant for non-economic damages is several and not joint. Other parties, like Alexandre, will not bear the burden for any non-economic damages not stemming from their own contribution. Non-economic damages are also not utilized to determine the reasonableness of settlement.

Based upon all the evidence, this factor supports the finding that the settlement was made in good faith. The Court notes that Opposing Defendants do not contest any factors other than proportionate share of liability. However, in the interest of addressing all potential concerns, the Court evaluates the additional Tech-Bilt factors.

 

B.          Allocation of Settlement   / Financial Considerations

The $225,000.00 in settlement offered to the Kos is part of a global settlement for $500,000.00, between Moving Defendants and other parties involved in the incident. (Mot., Hill Decl.¶ 2.) This was offered pursuant to Moving Defendants insurance policy limits, which covers $250,000.00 per person, and $500,000.00 for each accident. (Id., at ¶ 9, Ex. E.) As the amount is proportionate to Moving Defendants’s liability, and almost exhausts Moving Defendants policy limits, this is indicative of the settlement being made in good faith. 

C.          Collusion or Fraud  

There is no evidence to suggest collusion, fraud, or tortious conduct aimed to injure the rights of non-settling parties. Settlement was reached via arms-length negotiation between counsel via a mediator. (Id. at ¶ 2.) This is indicative of the settlement being made in good faith.

_________

The facts establish that this settlement was entered into in good faith based upon the information available at the time of settlement. Opposing Defendants have not provided any evidence to establish a lack of good faith. Therefore, the Court grants the motion.

 

V.         CONCLUSION

Defendants Lan Chen and Peng-Shen Chen’s application for determination of good faith settlement is GRANTED.

Defendants Alexandre Cazazian, the Cazazian Trust, and Luiza Cazazian’s motion to contest the good faith of the settlement is DENIED.

The Court dismisses all pending and future claims against Lan and Peng-Shen by the parties represented by counsel served with this motion (to the extent those claims arise from the facts giving rise to this case), including cross-complaints for equitable indemnity.

Lan and Peng-Shen are ordered to give notice.

BRENDA KINNERY, et al.,

vs.

GENERAL MOTORS, LLC

Defendant.

Case No.:

25STCV09500

Hearing Date:

August 4, 2026

ORDER DENYING MOTION TO STAY THE PROCEEDINGS

 

I.           BACKGROUND

Plaintiffs Brenda Kinnery (“Brenda”) and Craig Kinnery (“Craig”) entered into a warranty contract with Defendant General Motors, LLC (“GM”) regarding a 2016 Cadillac XTS (“subject vehicle”). The subject vehicle allegedly has defects and nonconformities making the car effectively worthless. GM allegedly failed to replace the vehicle or reimburse Plaintiffs. Plaintiffs sued GM for five causes of action, including violations of the Song-Beverly act.

On June 22, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the instant motion to stay the proceedings. GM filed an opposition.

 

II.         LEGAL STANDARD

A court ordinarily has inherent power, in its discretion, to stay proceedings when such a stay will accommodate the ends of justice. (OTO, L.L.C. v. Kho (2019) 8 Cal.5th 111, 141.) The power to stay proceedings is incidental to the power inherent in every court to control the disposition of the cause on its docket with the economy of time and effort for itself, for counsel, and for litigants. (Id.) Trial courts generally have inherent power to stay proceedings in the interest of justice and to promote judicial efficiency. (Freiberg v. City of Mission Viejo (1995) 33 Cal.App.4th 1484, 1489.) 

 

III.       DISCUSSION

Plaintiffs request the court stay the proceedings indefinitely pending the Second District Court of Appeal’s resolution of Los Angeles Superior Court case no. B353231, Price v. General Motors. Plaintiffs argue that the appellate court is currently considering whether a manufacturer's post-filing election into the Song-Beverly Act’s new procedural regime can retroactively time-bar a complaint already on file when the new statutes of repose and limitations took effect. Plaintiffs argue this is potentially relevant to this case and may result in a later revision or reversal upon the appellate court issuing a ruling.

The Court first notes Plaintiffs’ moving papers are entirely based upon three exhibits submitted with Plaintiffs’ Declaration of Jacob Lister, all of which are court documents. Lister has not provided sufficient foundation to warrant acceptance of these exhibits or otherwise requested judicial notice of these documents. Therefore, it is improper of the Court to consider these exhibits absent a showing of good cause, pursuant to California Rules of Court rule 3.1306.

Even if the Court was to consider the attached exhibits, the Court does not find good cause to grant an indefinite stay. Generally speaking, trial courts have the power to issue stays to promote judicial efficiency and in the interest of justice. (Freiberg v. City of Mission Viejo (1995) 33 Cal.App.4th 1484, 1489.) There is no indication as to when the Court of Appeals will issue a ruling on Price, or if it will have any impact on this case; it is not within the interest of fairness or economy to identify delay because a case might impact a ruling at some unknown time. As Plaintiffs noted in their moving papers, if the Price ruling does impact the case, Plaintiffs may move to revise or reverse any contradictory ruling in this case. Therefore, there is also no indication of prejudice to Plaintiffs in the Court denying the request.

The Court denies the motion.

 

IV.        CONCLUSION

Plaintiffs Brenda Kinnery and Craig Kinnery’s motion to stay the proceedings is DENIED.

GM is ordered to give notice.

Case Number: 26STCV00160    Hearing Date: August 4, 2026    Dept: 730

Superior Court of California

County of Los Angeles

Department 730

 

JONATHAN BECERRA,

Plaintiff,

vs.

BERGEN SHIPPERS CORP, et al.,

Defendants.

Case No.:

26STCV00160

Hearing Date:

August 4, 2026

ORDER DENYING MOTION TO COMPEL ARBITRATION

 

I.           BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Jonathan Becerra (“Becerra”) was wrongfully terminated from his employment with Defendants Bergen Shippers Corp (“BSC”) and Bergen Logistics (“BL”) after Becerra made official complaints regarding workplace safety. Becerra sued Defendants for nine causes of action, including wrongful termination and failure to abide by Labor Code requirements.

On June 22, 2026, BSC filed the instant motion to compel arbitration and stay litigation. Becerra filed an opposition. BSC filed a reply brief.

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

            Under both the Federal Arbitration Act and California law, arbitration agreements are valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, except on such grounds that exist at law or equity for voiding a contract. (Winter v. Window Fashions Professions, Inc. (2008) 166 Cal.App.4th 943, 947.) The party moving to compel arbitration must establish the existence of a written arbitration agreement between the parties. (Code Civ. Proc. § 1281.2[1].) This is usually done by presenting a copy of the signed, written agreement to the court. “A petition to compel arbitration or to stay proceedings pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 1281.2 and 1281.4 must state, in addition to other required allegations, the provisions of the written agreement and the paragraph that provides for arbitration. The provisions must be stated verbatim or a copy must be physically or electronically attached to the petition and incorporated by reference.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1330.) The moving party must also establish the other party’s refusal to arbitrate the controversy. (Code Civ. Proc. § 1281.2.) The filing of a lawsuit against the moving party for a controversy clearly within the scope of the arbitration agreement affirmatively establishes the other party’s refusal to arbitrate the controversy. (Hyundai Amco America, Inc. v. S3H, Inc. (2014) 232 Cal.App.4th 572, 577.)

“Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.2 requires a trial court to grant a petition to compel arbitration ‘if the court determines that an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists.’”¿(Avery v. Integrated Healthcare Holdings, Inc. (2013) 218 Cal.App.4th 50, 59 (Avery) (quoting CCP § 1281.2).) Accordingly, “when presented with a petition to compel arbitration, the court’s first task is to determine whether the parties have in fact agreed to arbitrate the dispute.”¿ (Ibid.)¿A petition to compel arbitration is in essence a suit in equity to compel specific performance of a contract.¿ (Id. at 71.)¿As with any other specific performance claim, “a party seeking to enforce an arbitration agreement must show the agreement’s terms are sufficiently definite to enable the court to know what it is to enforce.” (Ibid. [internal citations omitted].)¿ “Only the valid and binding agreement of the parties, including all material terms well-defined and clearly expressed, may be ordered specifically performed.”¿(Ibid.) An arbitration agreement “must be so interpreted as to give effect to the mutual intention of the parties as it existed at the time of contracting, so far as the same is ascertainable and lawful.”¿(Civ. Code § 1636.)¿The language of the contract governs its interpretation if it is clear and explicit. (Civ. Code § 1368.) If uncertainty exists, “the language of a contract should be interpreted most strongly against the party who caused the uncertainty to exist.”¿(Civ. Code § 1654.)

The party seeking to compel arbitration bears the burden of proving the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by the preponderance of the evidence. (Engalla v. Permanente Medical Group, Inc. (1997) 15 Cal.4th 951, 972.) It would then be plaintiff’s burden, in opposing the motion, to prove by a preponderance of the evidence any fact necessary to her opposition. (Ibid.) “In these summary proceedings the trial court sits as a trier of fact, weighing all the affidavits, declarations, and other documentary evidence, as well as oral testimony received at the court’s discretion, to reach a final determination. (Ibid.)

           

III.       DISCUSSION

The Court first notes this motion is not barred by section 1008(b) as it is not a motion for reconsideration. The previous motion to compel arbitration was denied without prejudice, providing opportunity for BSC to file a new motion to compel arbitration.

BSC argues this action is subject to arbitration as Becerra entered into a valid arbitration agreement with BSC, agreeing to resolve any disputes arising from Becerra’s employment via binding arbitration. For the Court to grant a motion to compel arbitration, the moving party must demonstrate the existence of a valid arbitration agreement and the other party’s refusal to arbitrate the controversy. (§ 1281.2.)

A.         Existence of Arbitration Agreement

BSC provided provide a copy of a prima facie valid arbitration agreement between BL and Becerra. It is undisputed Becerra and BL signed and executed an arbitration agreement on September 23, 2024. (Mot., Ramos Decl. ¶ 5, Ex. A.) The agreement, in pertinent part, provides as follows:

"As an express condition of Bergen Logistics considering, you for hire, hiring you or continuing to employ you, you and the Company and your and its representatives, successors and assigns, agree that:

(i) all … disputes, controversies, and claims of any nature … arising out of, involving, affecting, or related in any way to your recruit, hiring, employment, compensation, and/or termination by or from Company, the conditions of your employment or any act or omission of Company or Company’s others employees shall be resolved exclusively by final and binding arbitration before the American Arbitration Association and in accordance with the applicable rules of the American Arbitration Association. This Agreement covers all employment matters, including but not limited to matters directly or indirectly related to wrongful termination, breach of contract, discrimination, harassment, retaliation (in the whistleblower or any other context), denial of family or medical leave rights, denial of employment or retirement benefits, and any other violation of state, federal, or common law …” (Ibid.)

Even though the subject arbitration agreement only mentions BL, BSC provided evidence demonstrating BL, at all relevant times, was registered as fictious business name for BSC. (Mot., Bryan Decl. ¶ 3, Ex. B.) All of Becerra’s claims arise from his employment relationship with BL or BSC, falling within the scope of the agreement. Therefore, the evidence demonstrates the existence of a binding arbitration agreement between BSC and Becerra that would apply to this action.

From February 18, 2026, through March 4, 2026, parties met and conferred regarding the arbitration agreement. Becerra refused to attend arbitration, stating the agreement was unconscionable. (Mot., Klein Decl. ¶ 4., Ex. C.)  As Becerra opposes this motion and filed this action despite being on notice of the arbitration agreement, Becerra has refused to arbitrate. (See Hyundai Amco America, Inc. v. S3H, Inc. (2014) 232 Cal.App.4th 572, 577, in which the filing of a lawsuit against the moving party for a controversy clearly within the scope of the arbitration agreement affirmatively established the other party’s refusal to arbitrate the controversy.)

BSC has met its burden of demonstrating the existence of an arbitration agreement between parties and that this action is with the scope of the arbitration agreement. BSC has also demonstrated a refusal by Becerra to arbitrate claims. BSC has met its initial burden, which now shifts to Becerra.

B. Unconscionability

Becerra argues the arbitration agreement is unenforceable, as it is procedurally and substantively unconscionable. Commonly formulated, unconscionability refers to the “absence of meaningful choice on the part of one of the parties together with the contract terms which are unreasonably favorable to the other party. [Citations.]” (Sonic-Calabasas A, Inc. v. Moreno (2013) 57 Cal.4th 1109, 1145.) Unconscionability has “both a procedural and a substantive element, the former focusing on oppression or surprise due to unequal bargaining power, the latter on overly harsh or one-sided results. (Sanchez v. Valencia Holding Company, LLC (2015) 61 Cal.4th 899, 910.) “But they need not be present in the same degree. . . . [T]he more substantively oppressive the contract term, the less evidence of procedural unconscionability is required to come to the conclusion that the term is unenforceable, and vice versa.” (Armendariz v. Foundation Health Psychcare Services, Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 83, 114.)

1.          Procedural Unconscionability

“Procedural unconscionability pertains to the making of the agreement and requires oppression or surprise. [Citations.] ‘ “ ‘Oppression occurs where a contract involves lack of negotiation and meaningful choice, surprise where the allegedly unconscionable provision is hidden within a prolix printed form.’ ” ’ ” [Citation]“ ‘[T]here are degrees of procedural unconscionability. At one end of the spectrum are contracts that have been freely negotiated by roughly equal parties, in which there is no procedural unconscionability.... Contracts of adhesion that involve surprise or other sharp practices lie on the other end of the spectrum.’ ” [Citation].” (Hasty v. American Automobile Assn. etc. (2023) 98 Cal.App.5th 1041, 1055.)

Becerra first argues the arbitration agreement is procedurally unconscionable as the agreement was presented on a “take it or leave it basis.” Generally speaking, if a court finds the subject contract is a contract of adhesion, the court also finds the contract procedurally unconscionable. (Armendariz v. Foundation Health Psychcare Services, Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 83, 113.) “An adhesive contract is standardized, generally on a preprinted form, and offered by the party with superior bargaining power ‘on a take-it-or-leave-it basis.’ ” (OTO, L.L.C. v. Kho (2019) 8 Cal.5th 111, 126.)

Becerra argues the agreement was a standardized contract, presented to Becerra as a mandatory condition of his employment by his employer, a party with superior bargaining power. Courts have generally held stock pre-employment arbitration contracts are contracts of adhesion, and therefore procedurally unconscionable, as employers have greater bargaining power due to economic pressure exerted by employers on “all but the most sought-after employees,” as “few employees are in a position to refuse a job because of an arbitration requirement.” (Armendariz, supra at 24 Cal. 4th at pg. 115.) Becerra was only notified of this letter after completing the application and interview process via his employment offer letter, which did not attach a copy of the agreement. (Opp., Becerra Decl. ¶¶ 5-6, Ex.1-2.) The agreement was presented for the first time on Becerra’s first day of employment as “an express condition of [BL] considering [Becerra] for hire, hiring [Becerra] or continuing to employ [Becerra].” (Mot., Ramos Decl. ¶¶ 4-5, Ex. A.)  Said situation warrants the same analysis as that in Armendariz, as the party with superior bargaining power presented a stock arbitration agreement Becerra had no choice but to sign in order to begin his employment. By delaying knowledge of the existence and content of the arbitration agreement until late in the hiring process, Becerra may have been meaningfully denied from pursuing other employment that did not require an arbitration clause.

In response, BSC argues that an arbitration agreement being established as a contract of adhesion, alone, is insufficient to demonstrate procedural unconscionability. While it is true procedural unconscionability takes into account all factors, contracts of adhesion lie on the side of the spectrum tending towards unconscionability. BSC also argues that BSC gave notice to Becerra, in Becerra’s offer letter, of the arbitration agreement. As noted previously, only offering notice without any additional context at the point in which an offer is extended is also indicative of procedural unconscionability, as Becerra may not have diligently pursued this position if aware of the requirement.

Becerra next argues the proposed arbitration rules, which Becerra was not provided prior to signing the agreements, create a substantial degree of surprise and oppression. Failure to attach a copy of the arbitration rules may indicate procedural unconscionability when the rules allegedly hid essential terms through obfuscation or legal language. (Nguyen v. Applied Med. Resources Corp. (2016) 4 Cal.App.5th 232, 248-249; OTO, surpra 8 Cal.5th at 126-128.)

The facts show Becerra was never provided with a copy of the rules. The arbitration agreement stated arbitration will be administered pursuant to the applicable rules of the American Arbitration Association (“AAA”). (Mot., Ramos Decl. ¶ 5, Ex. A.) The agreement does not specify what set of rules apply. These rules were also not attached to the arbitration agreement or otherwise provided by BSC. (Opp., Becerra Decl. ¶ 10.) It is unclear if the rules hid essential terms, thus the Court cannot further evaluate if failure to attach is indicative of procedural unconscionability.

While failing to attach the arbitration rules is only procedurally unconscionable when those rules themselves are unfair, the Court may still consider failure to include the rules when considering general oppression to Becerra. (Baltazar v. Forever 21, Inc. (2016) 62 Cal.4th 1237, 1246.) When determining oppression to a party to a contract, courts consider: “(1) the amount of time the party is given to consider the proposed contract; (2) the amount and type of pressure exerted on the party to sign the proposed contract; (3) the length of the proposed contract and the length and complexity of the challenged provision; (4) the education and experience of the party; and (5) whether the party's review of the proposed contract was aided by an attorney.” (OTO, surpra 8 Cal.5th at p. 126-127)

By failing to 1) put Becerra on notice of the existence of the arbitration agreement until the offer letter; 2) produce a copy of the arbitration agreement until the first day of Becerra’s employment; and 3) attach a copy of the arbitration rules, the Court finds very little time was given to Becerra to properly consider the proposed contract. As Becerra was only provided the contract upon his first day of work, Becerra could not have meaningfully contacted an attorney to review the contract prior to employment, despite that being a condition identified by Becerra’s signature in the contract. (See Mot., Ramos Decl. ¶ 5, Ex. A.; “YOU ACKNOWLEDGE AND AGREE THAT YOU HAVE READ THIS AGREEMENT AND HAVE HAD SUFFICIENT TIME TO STUDY AND CONSIDER IT AND TO CONSULT WITH COUNSEL OF YOUR CHOICE.”)

In total, the Court finds the evidence demonstrates the arbitration agreement is substantially procedurally unconscionable, as Becerra was presented with a contract of adhesion, necessary to employment, and only put on notice as to its existence at the end of the hiring process. As Becerra has shown the agreement is, at least, in part procedurally unconscionable, the Court reviews the substantive unconscionability of the agreement.

2.          Substantive Unconscionability

“ ‘Substantive unconscionability examines the fairness of a contract's terms.’ [Citation] The substantive element of the unconscionability analysis “looks to the actual terms of the parties’ agreement to ‘ensure[ ] that contracts, particularly contracts of adhesion, do not impose terms that have been variously described as “ ‘ “overly harsh” ’ ” [citation], “ ‘unduly oppressive’ ” [citation], “ ‘so one-sided as to “shock the conscience” ’ ” [citation], or “unfairly one-sided.” ’ [Citation.] These formulations ‘all mean the same thing.’ [Citation.] Substantive unconscionability ‘ “is concerned not with ‘a simple old-fashioned bad bargain’ [citation], but with terms that are ‘unreasonably favorable to the more powerful party.’ ” ’ [Citation.] ‘The substantive component of unconscionability looks to whether the contract allocates the risks of the bargain in an objectively unreasonable or unexpected manner.’ [Citation.] While private arbitration may resolve disputes faster and cheaper than judicial proceedings, it ‘ “may also become an instrument of injustice imposed on a ‘take it or leave it’ basis.” ’ [Citation.] ‘ “The courts must distinguish the former from the latter, to ensure that private arbitration systems resolve disputes not only with speed and economy but also with fairness.” ’ ” (Hasty, supra 98 Cal.App.5th at pg. 1058-1059.)

Becerra first argues the agreement is substantively unconscionable as it contains an explicit waiver of right to punitive and exemplary damages. An arbitration agreement may not limit statutorily imposed remedies such as punitive damages or attorney fees. (Armendariz supra 24 Cal.4th at pg. 103.) The agreement specifically provides, “BY SIGNING THIS AGREEMENT YOU AND COMPANY ARE WAIVING ANY RIGHT, STATUTORY OR OTHERWISE, TO A TRIAL BY JURY AND TO PUNITIVE AND EXEMPLARY DAMAGES.” (Mot., Ramos Decl. ¶ 5, Ex. A.) This portion of the agreement is prima facie unenforceable and substantively unconscionable.

BSC agrees this portion is unenforceable and should be severed. The AAA rules otherwise provide for “any remedy or relief that would have been available to the parties had the matter been heard in court, as stated in Rule 39(d), Award,” which would allow Becerra to obtain punitive damages, pursuant to the binding arbitration rules. (Mot., Klein Decl., ¶5, Exhibit C, Rule 46.) Therefore, while this is indicative of substantive unconscionability, the Court will not use this as a basis to deny arbitration absent other showings of substantive unconscionability.

Becerra next argues the arbitration agreement is substantially unconscionable because it lacks mutuality. “When only the weaker party's claims are subject to arbitration, and there is no reasonable justification for that lack of symmetry, the agreement lacks the requisite degree of mutuality.” (Hasty, supra 98 Cal.App.5th at pg. 1059.) Becerra specifically argues that the agreement does not encapsulate claims arising from Non-Disclosure, Non-Competition, and Non-Solicitation Agreements, which are generally claims solely brought by employing corporations. (Mot., Ramos Decl. ¶ 5, Ex. A.) These generally encapsulated claims based upon intellectual property violations, unfair competition, and the use of trade secrets or other confidential information.

Mercuro v. Superior Court (2002) 96 Cal.App.4th 167 is analogous. In Mercuro, the court held that the fact the arbitration agreement did not compel parties to bring claims the employer was most likely to bring against its employees was substantively unconscionable. (Id. at 175-176.) The Mercuro court specifically identified “claims for injunctive and/or other equitable relief for intellectual property violations, unfair competition and/or the use and/or unauthorized disclosure of trade secrets or confidential information....” as claims likely to be bought solely by the employer. (Id. at 176.) These are substantially similar to claims arising from Non-Disclosure, Non-Competition, and Non-Solicitation Agreements.

BSC argues the agreement is not unconscionable because the exceptions apply both ways, in contrast to the intellectual property claims in Mercuro, which could only be meaningfully brought to court by the employer. However, this still does not address that a majority of the encapsulated claims would solely be brought by BSC. “An employer may not impose a system of arbitration on an employee that seeks to maximize the advantages and minimize the disadvantages of arbitration for itself at the employee's expense,” without reasonable justification for the arrangement other than the mere desire to maximize the employer’s advantage. (Armendariz, supra at 24 Cal. 4th at pg. 120.) This need not include every potential claim, but mere the majority or more likely claims. In the absence of justification, the court assumes the agreement is unconscionable. (Ibid.)

The mere fact Becerra has the right to bring non-disclosure, non-competition, and non-solicitation claims against BSC does not change the fact BSC is far more likely to bring these claims against Becerra than Becerra will bring these claims against BSC. Use of more generalized terminology does not defeat the intent or effect of the provisions of the arbitration agreement. BSC provides no explanation as to why these particular claims are carved out, indicating to the Court that it was likely done to allow BSC the benefit of forum choice.

Becerra then argues that the arbitration agreement unfairly imposes a confidentiality requirement on Becerra. The agreement states: “You shall not disclose, and shall keep strictly confidential, the contents of any testimony given or documents exchange or the evidence or contests of any award issued by the arbitrator unless disclosure is required by law.” (Mot., Ramos Decl. ¶ 5, Ex. A.) No such requirement is imposed upon BSC.

General confidentiality agreements have been found to be substantively unconscionable. For example, in Murrey v. Superior Court (2023) 87 Cal.App.5th 1223, 1254, the court found a confidentiality provision as to publication of the arbitration award was substantively unconscionable because it served no purpose other than to benefit the employer, as it disallowed future employees from taking advantage of findings in past arbitrations. A one-sided confidentiality requirement clearly benefits BSC even further than a typical confidentiality clause, indicating substantive unconscionability.

Becerra also argues the agreement is overbroad in scope, as it includes “any act or omission of Company or Company’s other employees.” (Mot., Ramos Decl. ¶ 5, Ex. A.) Such broad provisions without limitation are substantively unconscionable. (Cook v. University of Southern California (2024) 102 Cal.App.5th 312, 32.) BSC argues this is untrue, as the agreement is limited to claims relating to Becerra’s employment relationship. While this may have been BSC’s intent, the agreement does not have any explicit limitations as to scope. It allows “all (past, present and future) disputes...arising out of...any act or omissions of Company or Company’s employees,” to be subject to arbitration. (Mot., Ramos Decl. ¶ 5, Ex. A.) This cuts in favor of finding substantive unconscionability.

Although the agreement does not specifically outline Becerra’s costs, the AAA rules provide that Becerra’s total filing fees are capped at $350.00; this is not indicative of Becerra being forced to bear any type of expense Becerra would not be forced to bear if bringing the action in court. (Armendariz, supra, 24 Cal.4th at pg. 110–111.) Therefore, this does not indicate substantive unconscionability.

The Court finds, in total, the agreement is moderately substantively unconscionable. Large portions of the substantive unconscionability cannot be severed, such as the scope, lack of mutuality, and confidentiality provisions. As the agreement is both moderately procedurally and substantively unconscionable, the Court finds enforcement of the agreement is precluded by unconscionability. Therefore, the Court denies the motion.

 

V.        CONCLUSION

Defendant Bergen Shippers Corp’s Motion to Compel Arbitration is DENIED.

Plaintiff Jonathan Becerra is ordered to give notice.

Case Number: 26STCV01524    Hearing Date: August 4, 2026    Dept: 730

Superior Court of California

County of Los Angeles

Department 730

 

BEVERLY ANNE FAUCHER,

Plaintiff,

vs.

METRO-GOLDWYN-MAYER STUDIOS, INC., et al.,

Defendants.

Case No.:

26STCV01524

Hearing Date:

August 4, 2026

ORDER SUSTAINING DEMURRER TO COMPLAINT

 

I.           BACKGROUND

Employing Defendants allegedly placed Christopher Ottinger (“Ottinger”) in charge of Plaintiff Beverly Anne Faucher’s (“Faucher”) division and Ottinger allegedly began placing unrealistic demands and deadlines on Faucher. Faucher advised Ottinger and other parties these expectations were unrealistic. In response, Ottinger allegedly retaliated against Faucher by micro-managing her, making discriminatory/hurtful comments against Faucher and favoring younger male employees. Faucher reported this behavior to HR; following this, Faucher was investigated and eventually terminated for alleged bullying and misconduct. Faucher sued Defendants Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer Studios Inc. (“MGM”) and Amazon Studios LLC (“Amazon”) for seven causes of action, including intentional infliction of emotional distress (“IIED”) and negligent infliction of emotional distress (“NIED”).

On March 20, 2026, Defendants filed the instant demurrer to the sixth and seventh causes of action for IIED and NIED. Faucher filed an opposition. Defendants filed a reply brief.

 

II.         LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10[1] states: “The party against whom a complaint or cross-complaint has been filed may object, by demurrer or answer as provided in Section 430.30, to the pleading on any one or more of the following grounds: (a) The court has no jurisdiction of the subject of the cause of action alleged in the pleading; (b) The person who filed the pleading does not have the legal capacity to sue; (c) There is another action pending between the same parties on the same cause of action; (d) There is a defect or misjoinder of parties; (e) The pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action; (f) The pleading is uncertain. As used in this subdivision, ‘uncertain’ includes ambiguous and unintelligible; and (g) In an action founded upon a contract, it cannot be ascertained from the pleading whether the contract is written, is oral, or is implied by conduct.”

As a general matter, in a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice.  (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)  “A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.”  (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.)  As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations.  (Ibid.)  The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action.  (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment.  (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.)  The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully.  (Ibid.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.)  However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.”  (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245).

           

III.       DISCUSSION

Defendants demur to the causes of action for IIED and NIED, on the basis Faucher failed to allege facts to constitute causes of action against Defendants.

A.         IIED

Defendants argue the IIED claims should be subject to demurrer as Faucher does not allege Defendants engaged in extreme and outrageous behavior. A cause of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress requires: (1) extreme and outrageous conduct by the defendant with the intention of causing, or reckless disregard of the probability of causing, emotional distress; (2) the plaintiff's suffering severe or extreme emotional distress; and (3) actual and proximate causation of the emotional distress by the defendant's outrageous conduct. (Hughes v. Pair (2009) 46 Cal.4th 1035, 1050-1051 [citations and quotations omitted].) A defendant’s conduct is “outrageous” when it is so extreme as to exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated in a civilized community. (Ibid.) The defendant must either intend his or her conduct to inflict injury or have engaged in it with the realization that injury will result. Liability for intentional infliction of emotional distress does not extend to mere insults, indignities, threats, annoyances, petty oppressions, or other trivialities. (Ibid.) 

Faucher’s complaint alleged that Defendants subjected Faucher to conduct that exceeded the bounds of ordinary employment disputes, including “public humiliation, discriminatory intimidation, and retaliation against a senior executive for protected activity.” (Complaint ¶ 124.) Specifically, Faucher alleged that Ottinger, as Faucher’s supervisor, micromanaged Faucher, publicly suggested he preferred speaking with younger male employees, publicly and aggressively insulted Faucher in front of peers and superiors, and called Faucher “Bev the Bottleneck,” (Complaint ¶¶ 47, 51, 53.) Faucher also alleged Defendants began a pretextual investigation into Faucher for alleged bullying and misconduct, eventually used to terminate Faucher without offering any sort of performance improvement plan. (Complaint ¶¶ 61-62.)

Extreme and outrageous conduct must be more than “mere insults, indignities, threats, annoyances, petty oppressions or other trivialities,[] but only to conduct so extreme and outrageous ‘as to go beyond all possible bounds of decency, and to be regarded as atrocious, and utterly intolerable in a civilized community.’ ” (Alcorn v. Anbro Engineering, Inc. (1970) 2 Cal.3d 493, 499 fn.5.) A supervisor’s actions relating to terminations, criticism of work practices, and negotiations are not within the realm of extreme and/or outrageous conduct, even if improper motive is alleged. (Janken v. GM Hughes Elecs. (1996) 46 Cal. App. 4th 55, 80.)

Faucher has not alleged any facts that are within the realm of extreme or outrageous behavior. Ottinger’s disparaging comments fall within the realm of mere insults, indignities, and petty oppressions. Managerial decisions, such as micromanaging, investigation, and termination, are not issues for IIED claims but discrimination suits. (Ibid.)

The Court also notes Faucher’s complaint does not include allegations supporting Faucher suffering from severe or extreme emotional distress. The “mere allegation that [a plaintiff] suffered severe emotional distress, without facts indicating the nature or extent of any mental suffering incurred as a result of the defendant's alleged outrageous conduct, fail[s] to state a cause of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress.” (Pitman v. City of Oakland (1988) 197 Cal. App.3d 1037, 1047.) Here, Faucher provided no facts to support any allegations regarding emotional distress, beyond alleging “[Faucher] suffered severe emotional distress.” (Complaint ¶ 133.) There is no description as to the nature or extent of Faucher’s suffering.

Faucher has insufficiently pled essential facts to bring this cause of action. Therefore, it is subject to demurrer.

B.          NIED

Defendants argue the NIED claim should similarly be subject to demurrer, as Faucher has not pled the existence of any duty or breach of duty. In California, a cause of action for NIED is not distinct from a cause of action for negligence, beyond the fact a NIED claim only alleges emotional damages. Therefore, the same elements as a negligence claim apply: a duty of care, breach of duty, proximate cause between breach and injury, and damages to a plaintiff. (Ahern v. Dillenback (1991) 1 Cal.App.4th 36, 42.)

Defendants argue Faucher has not alleged the existence of a duty to Faucher, outside of her employment relationship, nor that the employment relationship would impose a duty to maintain her emotional condition. The Court disagrees. Faucher generally alleged the existence of a duty to Faucher. “In California, the general rule is that all persons have a duty ‘ “to use ordinary care to prevent others being injured as the result of their conduct. …” (John B. v. Superior Court (2006) 38 Cal.4th 1177, 1188-89.) The allegation of a general duty invokes the general duty role, imposing a duty on all to prevent others being injured a result of their conduct.

Similarly, Faucher has sufficiently alleged breaches of duty by outlining offensive and harmful acts taken by Defendants’ supervising employees and staff. Because of this offensive conduct, Faucher suffered emotional distress. Claims for negligence or NIED do not require specific information as to the depth and severity of emotional distress suffered.

However, Defendants also argue the demurrer should be sustained as the NIED cause of action is based upon the same facts as the IIED cause of action, and intentional conduct cannot give rise to a negligence cause of action. While Faucher is entitled to plead alternative theories of liability, Court have held that an employer’s supervisory conduct is inherently intentional, including issuing termination, criticism of work practices, and decisions to investigate or supervise. (Semore v. Pool (1990) 217 Cal. App. 3d 1087, 1105.) As all alleged actions could only be intentional, and not negligent, Faucher has not presented a viable cause of action for negligent infliction of emotional distress. (Ibid.)

Therefore, the Court sustains the demurrer in full.

C.          Worker’s Compensation Exclusivity

Defendants also argue the exclusive remedy for the IIED and NIED causes of action are workers’ compensation. (See Miklosy v. Regents of University of California (2008) 44 Cal.4th 876, 902.) “So long as the basic conditions of compensation are otherwise satisfied [citation], and the employer’s conduct neither contravenes fundamental public policy [citation] nor exceeds the risks inherent in the employment relationship [citation], an employee’s emotional distress injuries are subsumed under the exclusive remedy provisions of workers’ compensation.” (Livitsanos v. Superior Court (1992) 2 Cal.4th 744, 754.) However, here, both of the relevant causes of action arise out of alleged sex and age discrimination, which contravenes public policy. These claims are not barred by workers’ compensation exclusivity.

D.         Leave to Amend

The burden is on a plaintiff to show in what manner he can amend the complaint, and how that amendment will change the legal effect of the pleading. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349; Hendy v. Losse (1991) 54 Cal.3d 723, 742.) Faucher requests leave to amend, arguing discovery and investigation may allow Faucher to develop grounds to justify a timely amendment. Although the Court would prefer Faucher provide some information as potential evidence, the Court notes this is the first demurrer, concerning the original complaint. The Court grants leave to amend in the interest of resolving this issue on the merits and allowing Faucher another opportunity to present an actionable complaint.

 

IV.        CONCLUSION

Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer Studios Inc. and Amazon Studios LLC’s demurrer to the SAC is SUSTAINED, with 30 days leave to amend.

Defendants are ordered to give notice.
