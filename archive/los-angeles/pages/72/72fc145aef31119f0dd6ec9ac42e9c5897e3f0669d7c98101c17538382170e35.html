DEPARTMENT 516 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 24STCV13674    Hearing Date: July 21, 2026    Dept: 516

Hearing Date:             July 21, 2026

Case Name:                 Wilson v. McDonald’s Corporation, et al.

Case No.:                    24STCV13674

Matter:                        Motion to Continue Trial

Moving Party:             Defendants McDonald’s Corporation, DRS Hospitality, LLC, and Monica

                                    Gonzalez

Responding Party:       None

 

Tentative Ruling:      Defendants McDonald’s Corporation, DRS Hospitality, LLC, and Monica Gonzalez’s motion to continue trial is denied.

 

 

Plaintiff Donald Wilson (“Plaintiff”) filed this action against Defendants McDonald’s Corporation, DRS Hospitality, LLC, Monica Gonzalez (collectively “Defendants”), Warner Safe Guard, Inc., and Does 1 through 25. The Complaint alleges three causes of action for: (1) premises liability; (2) negligence; and (3) negligent hiring and supervision.

 

Defendants move for the Court to continue the trial date and related dates. Plaintiff did not file an opposition.

 

Discussion

 

Defendants move for the Court to continue the trial and related trial dates pursuant to California Rules of Court 3.1332.

 

California Rules of Court expressly permit a trial date to be continued upon a showing of “good cause.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1332(c).) Trial continuances are “disfavored,” and “[t]he court may grant a continuance only on an affirmative showing of good cause requiring the continuance.” (Ibid.)  

 

Rule 3.1332(c) lists seven circumstances “that may indicate good cause,” and Rule 3.1332(d), lists additional factors the trial court may consider. The trial court must consider all relevant facts and circumstances surrounding the continuance, including: “[t]he proximity of the trial date”; “[t]he length of the continuance requested”; “[t]he availability of alternative means to address the problem that gave rise to the motion or application for a continuance”; “[t]he prejudice that parties or witnesses will suffer as a result of the continuance”; and “[w]hether the interests of justice are best served by a continuance, by the trial of the matter, or by imposing conditions on the continuance” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1332(d)(1)-(4), (10).)

 

In support of their motion, Defendants move for a trial continuance because they have not deposed Plaintiff. Defendants argue that a continuance is warranted because, after they depose Plaintiff, there is a possibility of engaging in mediation. Defendants contend that they have previously served five notices for Plaintiff’s deposition for July 15, 2025, August 12, 2025, November 11, 2025, February 9, 2026, and June 10, 2026. (Lascola Decl., ¶ 5.) Defendants assert that the scheduled deposition for July 15, 2025, was continued by Defendants, but that Plaintiff has not appeared for his deposition. (Ibid.) However, the Court does not find good cause to continue the trial based on Defendants' lack of diligence in deposing Plaintiff. To date, Defendants have not filed a motion to compel the deposition of Plaintiff. Also, Defendants do not provide the Court with any reasoning as to why they did not pursue the remedies available pursuant to the Civil Discovery Act. Defendant argues that a secondary result of their inability to depose Plaintiff will be that any “potential expert witness discovery” will be incomplete because Plaintiff’s deposition will be taken three and a half months before trial if the deposition is conducted on July 24, 2026. (Lascola Decl., ¶ 7.) However, as addressed above, Defendants did not seek the available relief and do not present further explanation as to why all discovery cannot be completed before trial.

 

Defendants also argue that a trial continuance is warranted so the parties can engage in meaningful settlement discussions. However, this argument does not warrant a trial continuance under California Rules of Court 3.1332(c)-(d). Plaintiff filed this action on May 31, 2024, and trial is still several months away.

 

Defendants have failed to demonstrate good cause and thus, Defendants’ motion to continue trial is denied.

 

Conclusion

 

            Defendants McDonald’s Corporation, DRS Hospitality, LLC, and Monica Gonzalez’s motion to continue the trial date and related dates is denied.


Case Number: 25STCV00473    Hearing Date: July 21, 2026    Dept: 516

Hearing Date:             July 21, 2026

Case Name:                 Bagler v. RBD California Restaurants Limited

Case No.:                    25STCV00473

Matter:                        Motions to Quash Deposition Subpoena for Production of Business Records

Moving Party:             Plaintiff Autumn Bagler

Responding Party:       Defendant RBD California Restaurants Limited

 

Tentative Ruling:      Plaintiff Autumn Bagler’s motion is granted in part and denied in part.

 

 

            Plaintiff Autumn Bagler (“Plaintiff”) filed her action against RBD California Restaurants Limited (“RBD”), KFC Corporation (“KFC”), Gilberto Mendez (“Mendez”), and Does 1 through 20. Plaintiff’s First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) alleges 11 causes of action for: (1) disability discrimination in violation of the Fair Employment and Housing Act (“FEHA”); (2) sex discrimination in violation of FEHA; (3) sexual harassment in violation of FEHA; (4) failure to engage in interactive process in violation of FEHA; (5) failure to accommodate in violation of FEHA; (6) failure to prevent discrimination in violation of FEHA; (7) retaliation in violation of FEHA; (8) interference in violation of Pregnancy Disability Leave Law; (9) interference with rights under California Family Rights Act; (10) wrongful discharge in violation of public policy; and (11) defamation.

 

            Plaintiff moves to quash the subpoena for production of business records served on the Los Angeles County Department of Children and Family Services (“DCFS”). RBD opposes.

 

Legal Standard

 

“If a subpoena requires the attendance of a witness or the production of books, documents, electronically stored information, or other things before a court, or at the trial of an issue therein, or at the taking of a deposition, the court, upon motion reasonably made by any person described in subdivision (b), or upon the court's own motion after giving counsel notice and an opportunity to be heard, may make an order quashing the subpoena entirely, modifying it, or directing compliance with it upon those terms or conditions as the court shall declare, including protective orders. In addition, the court may make any other order as may be appropriate to protect the person from unreasonable or oppressive demands, including unreasonable violations of the right of privacy of the person.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.1, subd. (a).) 

           

Analysis

 

            Preliminary Matter

 

            California Rules of Court, rule 3.1345, requires that motions to quash the production of documents include a separate statement. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1345(a).) “The separate statement must be full and complete so that no person is required to review any other document in order to determine the full request and the full response. Material must not be incorporated into the separate statement by reference.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1345 (c).)

 

           Here, Plaintiff submitted a separate statement, but RBD did not. RBD’s failure to submit a separate statement is, by itself, grounds not to consider RBD’s opposition. However, the Court will exercise its discretion and will consider the procedurally defective opposition.

 

            Motion to Quash

 

            Plaintiff moves to quash RBD’s deposition subpoena served on DCFS on the basis that the records are statutorily protected, it infringes on Plaintiff’s child’s privacy, the requested documents are not relevant to the case, and the request is overbroad. Plaintiff also requests that the Court impose monetary sanctions against RBD and counsel of record in the amount of $3,200.

 

            RBD’s subpoena makes one categorical request for documents from DCFS for:

 

Any and all case records, current judgments and court orders, statement of collections and distribution, declaration of support payment history showing all payments made or missed, National Medical Support Notice information on health insurance enrollment for minor child, detailed breakdown of how agency calculated support amount, and guideline calculations, records of communication between agency and other parent, copies, of other parent’s financial records, including all paystubs, tax returns and child care expense receipts, other parent’s full information, current address, social security number and employment information, proof of paternity such as birth certificate, and any existing custody orders.

 

(Gutierrez Decl., Ex. A (emphasis added).)

 

            “Juvenile delinquency case files and a living child’s juvenile dependency case files may be obtained or inspected, and information from the file may be disclosed, only in accordance with sections 827, 827.12, and 828. The file may not be obtained or inspected by civil or criminal subpoena, and the information from the file may not be disclosed by testimony without a juvenile court order.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 5.552(a).)

 

            RBD argues that California Welfare and Institutions Code section 827 is inapplicable because the documents sought are not juvenile delinquency case files or Plaintiff’s juvenile dependency records. The Court agrees that the records requested do not fall under the protections of California Welfare and Institutions Code section 827.

 

            Plaintiff argues that California Welfare and Institutions Code section 10850 bars the production of the requested documents. The Court agrees that Welfare and Institutions Code section 10850 does apply. The Court also notes that RBD did not address the statute's application. Welfare and Institutions Code section 10850 protects the confidentiality of all applications and records relating to “any form of public social services.” (Welf. & Inst. Code, § 10850, subd. (a).)

 

The California Constitution protects individuals' right to privacy. (Cal. Const., Art. I, § 1.) “Protection of informational privacy is the provision's central concern.” (Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 552.) “Invasion of a privacy interest is not a violation of the state constitutional right to privacy if the invasion is justified by a competing interest.” (Hill v. National Collegiate Athletic Assn. (1994) 7 Cal.4th 1, 38.) A statutory invasion of privacy requires a legally protected privacy interest, a reasonable expectation of privacy, and a serious invasion of that privacy interest. (Id. at pp. 35-37.)

 

Here, all three elements are met. As established above, the records held by DCFS are protected documents. Also, the information sought regarding non-party Quinn McGee (“McGee”), assuming he is the father of Plaintiff’s child, is entitled to privacy as well. RBD is asking for McGee’s “current address, social security number and employment information, proof of paternity such as a birth certificate, and any existing custody orders.” (Gutierrez Decl., Ex. A (emphasis added).) Plaintiff’s child is also not a party to the action; thus, all requested information in relation to the child is also protected. Moreover, Plaintiff, Plaintiff’s child, and McGee have a reasonable expectation of privacy that, if any records exist, their records held by DCFS will not be disclosed. Finally, the release of the documents is a serious invasion of privacy. RBD is asking the Court not to uphold the protections of Welfare and Institutions Code section 10850.

 

Thus, the production of the requested documents is an invasion of privacy. The Court declines to address the remaining arguments because granting Plaintiff’s motion to quash is warranted.

 

Sanctions

 

            Plaintiff requests that the Court impose monetary sanctions in the amount of $3,200 against RBD and RBD’s counsel of record pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1987.2. The Court finds that sanctions are warranted, but the amount requested is not reasonable.

 

             Thus, the Court will impose sanctions against RBD and counsel of record in the reduced amount of $2,000.

 

Conclusion

 

            Plaintiff Autumn Bagler’s motion to quash is granted.

 

            Sanctions are granted against Defendant RBD California Restaurants Limited and counsel of record in the reduced amount of $2,000. RBD shall pay sanctions by August 20, 2026.

           

 


Case Number: 25STCV32392    Hearing Date: July 21, 2026    Dept: 516

Hearing Date:             July 21, 2026

Case Name:                 Wright v. Volkswagen Group of America, Inc.

Case No.:                    25STCV32392

Motion:                       Motion to be Relieved as Counsel

Moving Party:             Counsel for Plaintiff

Opposing Party:          None

 

Tentative Ruling:      The Motion to be Relieved as Counsel is denied.     

 

            Plaintiff’s firm –Prestige Legal Solutions, P.C.– seeks to be relieved as counsel of record for Plaintiff Candace Michele Wright. No opposition was filed.      

 

Discussion

 

            An attorney is entitled to withdraw upon the consent of the client, or without that consent if approved by the court. (Code Civ. Proc., § 284; Ramirez v. Sturdevant (1994) 21 Cal.App.4th 904, 915.) In the latter case, counsel must make a motion to be relieved as attorney of record.  The motion must be made using mandatory forms: Notice of Motion and Motion to be Relieved as Counsel – Civil (MC-051), Declaration (MC-052), and Proposed Order (MC-053). (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362, subd. (a), (b), (e).)

 

            The declaration accompanying the motion to be relieved as counsel must state “in general terms and without compromising the confidentiality of the attorney-client relationship why a motion under Code of Civil Procedure section 284(2) is brought instead of filing a consent under Code of Civil Procedure section 284(1).” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362, subd. (b).)

 

            The notice to the client must be delivered by personal service, electronic means, or mail. If it is done via mail under Code of Civil Procedure section 1013, it must be accompanied by a declaration confirming the service address to be the most current residence or that it is the client’s last known address, and the attorney has been unable to locate a more current address. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362, subd. (d)(1)(A)-(B).) If the notice is served on the client by electronic service under Code of Civil Procedure section 1010.6 and rule 2.251, it must be accompanied by a declaration stating that the electronic service address is the client's current electronic service address. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362, subd. (d)(2).)

 

            Here, the firm Prestige Legal Solutions, P.C. has complied with the Rules of Court by filing forms MC-051,  MC-052, and MC-053.  

 

Michelle Yang (“Yang”), on behalf of  Prestige Legal Solutions, P.C. states that there has been a breakdown of the attorney-client relationship. The Court finds that this alone is not sufficient information to determine whether the motion should be granted.

 

There is also ambiguity in relation to whether Yang has confirmed Plaintiff’s address to be served. Yang declares that the breakdown between the Plaintiff and representation is due to “numerous attempts to communicate reasonably with Plaintiff through written correspondence.” (Yang Decl., ¶ 3.) But Plaintiff’s firm contends that it confirmed Plaintiff’s address over telephone. (MC-052)

 

              Thus, the Court denies Prestige Legal Solutions, P.C.’s motion to be relieved as counsel.
