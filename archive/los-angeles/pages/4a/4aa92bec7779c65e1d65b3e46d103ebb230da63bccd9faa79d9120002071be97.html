[TENTATIVE] RULING ON PETITION FOR WRIT OF MANDATE

 

Petitioners Jennifer Chrisman and Carey Chrisman (collectively, “Petitioners”) petition for a writ of administrative mandate as to respondent County of Los Angeles (“Respondent”) directing it to set aside its decision to approve a final map (“Final Map”) for Tract 45465-04 (“Tract”).

The petition for writ of mandate is denied.

I.                Factual Background

On October 5, 1988, Respondent’s Regional Planning Commission (“Commission”) approved Vesting Tentative Tract Map No. 45465 for the subdivision of a 443-acre lot into 81 single family residential lots, subject to conditions (“Conditions”) including, among others, provision of proof of off-site rights to build a debris basin and storm drain system (AR 104-05) and securing title or interest to permit any off-site improvements (AR 104, 130, 143). (AR 116-47.)

On May 7, 2021, Petitioners executed an agreement to purchase the property located at 26763 Mulholland Highway, Calabasas, CA (“Property”). (AR 113.) During escrow, seller Arthur A. Alisi Family Survivor’s Trusts Established on December 21, 1995 (“Seller”) trustees Justin Freer (“Freer”) and Jaime Varley (“Varley”) executed a Deed Restrictions Grant of Construction and Easement Rights (“Grant of Rights”) to developer Spectrum Development, Inc. (“Spectrum”), allowing Spectrum to satisfy the Conditions. (SAR 1-28.)

Thereafter, Petitioners filed suit seeking, inter alia, to quiet title and compel specific performance, case no. 21STCV31211 (“Title Action”). (AR 19-33.) Some of the Title Action claims were submitted to mandatory binding arbitration between only Petitioners and Freer and Varley (for Seller) under the Property purchase agreement, and the arbitrator’s Partial Final Arbitration Award (“Award”) (SAR 29-67) concluded Petitioners had a right to specific performance to receive title to the Property subject to determination in the Title Action. (SAR 60, 62).

On June 13, 2024, Petitioners objected to processing an approval of the Final Map on the basis that subdivider Malibu Valley Partners, LLC (“Valley Partners”) had not secured sufficient title or interest in the Property and so could not satisfy the Conditions. (SAR 68-174.)

Respondent replied, stating approval of the Final Map was “contingent upon the developer complying with the conditions of approval” and noting that “[o]ne of the listed requirements of the Drainage Concept was that the developer show proof of having necessary off-site rights to build a debris basin and storm drain system” through the Property, and concluding that that condition was not met. (AR 104-05).

On December 16, 2024, Respondent approved the Final Map at public hearing (“Decision”). (AR 93-103.)

III.           Standard of Review

Code of Civil Procedure (“CCP”) section 1094.5 is the administrative mandamus provision that structures the procedure for judicial review of adjudicatory decisions rendered by administrative agencies. (Topanga Ass’n for a Scenic Community v. County of Los Angeles (1974) 11 Cal.3d 506, 514-15) [“Topanga”].) Section 1094.5 does not specify which cases are subject to independent review, leaving that issue to the courts. (Fukuda v. City of Angels (1999) 20 Cal.4th 805, 811 [“Fukuda”].) In cases reviewing decisions that affect a vested, fundamental right, the trial court exercises independent judgment on the evidence. (Bixby v. Pierno (1971) 4 Cal.3d 130, 143 [“Bixby”]; see CCP § 1094.5(c).)

“Substantial evidence” is relevant evidence that a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion (California Youth Authority v. State Personnel Board (2002) 104 Cal.App.4th 575, 585 [“California Youth Authority”]) or evidence of ponderable legal significance, which is reasonable in nature, credible and of solid value (Mohilef v. Janovici (1996) 51 Cal.App.4th 267, 305, n. 28). Substantial evidence can be the opinion of a single expert (Coastal Southwest Dev. Corp. v. Coastal Zone Conservation Comm’n (1976) 55 Cal.App.3d 525, 532), or opinions in a staff report (Griffin Dev. Co. v. City of Oxnard (1985) 39 Cal.3d 256, 261). 

The court considers all evidence in the administrative record, including evidence that detracts from evidence supporting the agency’s decision. (California Youth Authority, supra, 104 Cal.App.4th at p. 585.) The court must uphold the decision unless it concludes, based on the evidence before the agency, a reasonable person could not reach the conclusion reached by the administrative agency.¿(Harris v. City of Costa Mesa¿(1994) 25 Cal.App.4th 963, 969 [“Harris”].) Where “reasonable persons may differ,” the courts will not disturb the judgment of the administrative agency. (Breakzone Billiards v. City of Torrance (2000) 81 Cal.App.4th 1205, 1246.) The court does not weigh evidence or decide who has the better argument and must resolve reasonable doubts in favor of the findings and decision. (Topanga, supra, 11 Cal.3d at p. 514.)

An agency is presumed to have regularly performed its official duties (Evid. Code § 664), and the petitioner therefore has the burden of proof on mandamus. (Steele v. Los Angeles County Civil Service Commission (1958) 166 Cal.App.2d 129, 137.) “[T]he burden of proof falls upon the party attacking the administrative decision to demonstrate wherein the proceedings were unfair, in excess of jurisdiction or showed prejudicial abuse of discretion.” (Alford v. Pierno, (1972) 27 Cal.App.3d 682, 691.)

IV.           Analysis

Petitioners seek administrative mandamus to set aside Respondent’s Decision to approve the Final Map on the grounds that (1) Respondent could not lawfully approve the final Map without compliance with the Conditions; (2) any reliance on the Grant of Rights violates the Award; and (3) Respondent failed to bridge the analytical gap between the evidence and its findings.

While Petitioners argue the matter under the standard for administrative mandamus, the action turns on matters of law.[3] While the courts generally afford significant deference to agency decisions in mandamus proceedings, the court reviews de novo rulings on questions of law. (Sieg v. Fogt (2020) 55 Cal.App.5th 77, 88.)

1.               Respondent Could Lawfully Approve the Final Map

Petitioners argue the Respondent abused its discretion in its Decision to approve the Final Map because Valley Partners had not complied with all Conditions of the tentative map. Specifically, Petitioners argue Valley Partners has not secured off-site title rights to build an off-site debris basin and storm drain system, which would require rights to build on the Property. Petitioners further contend that Respondent itself acknowledged noncompliance when DPW stated, in July 2024, that it could not recommend approval because the developer had not demonstrated sufficient off-site rights.

In opposition, Respondent argues that the Final Map substantially complied with the Conditions, and thus Respondent lawfully approved, and was required to approve under Government Code section 66462.5, the Final Map.[4] Respondent contends that section 66462.5(a) directly prohibits a local agency from refusing or postponing approval of a final map on the basis of missing off-site title rights when the improvements at issue are “off-site improvements” governed by the statute, and that any unresolved title dispute can be addressed through an agreement to complete the improvements at a later time.

In reply, Petitioners argue Government Code section 66462.5 does not apply, because that section only applies to “public improvements,” and the drainage system is not a public improvement because it is not “streets, sidewalks, sewer and/or water lines, street lights, etc.” (Reply at 2.) Thus, they reason, the County must deny a final map that does not conform to the tentative map and its conditions. Petitioners again rely on DPW’s July 2024 correspondence as evidence that Respondent recognized that section 66462.5(a)’s mandatory approval rule does not apply.

To resolve these competing interpretations, the Court first clarifies what constitutes an “improvement” and, by extension, a “public improvement” under the Subdivision Map Act. The threshold question before the Court is whether the drainage system is a “public improvement” subject to Government Code section 66462.5. The Court concludes that it is.

Petitioners correctly note that “‘[i]mprovement plan,’” within the meaning of the Subdivision Map Act, “means the plan for public improvement as described in Sections 66418 and 66419.” (Reply at 2.) Government Code section 66419, in turn, provides definitions for the relevant statute as follows:

(a) ‘Improvement’ refers to any street work and utilities to be installed, or agreed to be installed, by the subdivider on the land to be used for public or private streets, highways, ways, and easements, as are necessary for the general use of the lot owners in the subdivision and local neighborhood traffic and drainage needs as a condition precedent to the approval and acceptance of the final map thereof.

(b) ‘Improvement’ also refers to any other specific improvements or types of improvements, the installation of which, either by the subdivider, by public agencies, by private utilities, by any other entity approved by the local agency, or by a combination thereof, is necessary to ensure consistency with, or implementation of, the general plan or any applicable specific plan.

(Gov. Code § 66419.)

This language is broad. It expressly includes “easements,” as are needed for lot owners’ general use and “local neighborhood traffic and drainage needs.” Subdivision drainage systems, including storm drain facilities and debris basins that serve several lots and address neighborhood drainage demands, fall within subsection (a) because they are “necessary for the general use of the lot owners” and meet “local neighborhood…drainage needs.”

Nothing in the statute limits subsection (a)’s reach to the specific categories that Petitioners list (“streets, sidewalks, sewer and/or water lines, street lights, etc.”). Nor does the statute draw a distinction between improvements located within the subdivision boundary and those located on adjacent parcels, so long as they serve subdivision drainage (or traffic) needs.

Thus, while Petitioners highlight that the improvements at issue do not fall neatly into their selected examples, the statutory definition is much broader and encompasses the drainage improvements at issue in this case. There is no further or more restrictive definition provided for a “public improvement,” and as described, these improvements are facially “public” in character. The Court therefore rejects Petitioners’ argument that section 66462.5(a) applies only when improvements fall within a narrow category that the statute itself does not appear to require.

Accordingly, Government Code section 66462.5 applies, and Respondent was prohibited from postponing or refusing approval of the Final Map based on the off-site rights and drainage system Conditions, but instead was required, if other Conditions were met, to require Valley Partners to enter into an agreement to complete the improvements at such time as sufficient interest is acquired. (Gov. Code § 66462.5.)

2.               Respondent Need Not Rely on the Grant of Rights

Petitioners argue that Respondent cannot rely on the Grant of Rights to show compliance with the Conditions.

As discussed above, Respondent was required to approve the Final Map even if Spectrum had not acquired sufficient off-site rights, as long as there was otherwise substantial compliance with the Conditions. (Gov. Code § 66462.5.) Therefore, any reliance on the Grant of Rights to meet that Condition, whether valid or not, is immaterial. Because the County’s approval was compelled by statute, the Court need not determine whether the Grant of Rights is valid, enforceable, or capable of satisfying Tentative Map Conditions in the absence of section 66462.5(a).

Accordingly, this is not grounds to overturn the Decision.

3.               Respondent Need Not Find Spectrum Had Off-Site Rights

Petitioner argues that the Decision failed to bridge the analytic gap between the evidence and the ultimate conclusion that the Final Map was in substantial compliance.

The Court does not reach the question, because the answer would not impact the final resolution of this matter. As discussed above, Respondent could not deny or postpone the Final Map on the basis that Spectrum did not have the off-site rights. (Gov. Code § 66462.5.) Thus, even assuming Respondent’s findings were incomplete or would otherwise warrant closer scrutiny under Topanga, those issues are not material where section 66462.5(a) independently requires approval.

V.              Conclusion

Respondent could not legally deny or postpone the Final Map based solely on the lack of off-site rights, even though such was a Condition of the tentative map. Any insufficiencies in any showing or finding that Spectrum had such rights are immaterial. Because Petitioners’ remaining arguments depend on an interpretation of the Subdivision Map Act that the Court does not adopt, and because section 66462.5(a) required approval once other Conditions were satisfied, Petitioners have not demonstrated a basis to disturb the Decision.

The petition for administrative mandamus is therefore DENIED. Pursuant to Local Rule 3.231(n), respondent County of Los Angeles shall prepare, serve, and ultimately file a proposed judgment.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: DEMURRER

 

Respondents Amy J. Bodek (“Bodek”), Michelle Lynch (“Lynch”), and Los Angeles County Planning Department (“Planning”) (collectively, “Respondent”) demur to plaintiff James J. Maksimuk’s (“Petitioner”) verified petition for writ of mandate and complaint for declaratory relief, injunctive relief, and damages (“Petition”).

The demurrer is sustained without leave to amend.

I.                Factual Allegations

Petitioner alleges as follows. Petitioner owns and resides at property located 10160 Escondido Cyn Rd, Santa Clarita, CA 91390 (“Property”). (Pet., ¶ 4.) The Property is zoned A-1-2, an agricultural zoning that allows single-family residences. (Pet. at 5.)

On April 3, 2025, Respondent rejected Petitioner’s plan to subdivide the Property (plan no. RPA2025001608, “Plan”) under the provisions of Senate Bill 9 (“SB 9”) which provide for ministerial approval. (Pet. at 5.) Respondent rejected the Plan because the Property is not zoned as residential and because Accessory Dwelling Units (“ADUs”) are only allowed where there is an existing primary residential use. (Pet. at 5.)

On April 4, 2025, Petitioner replied to Respondent, arguing that the primary purpose of the zone in question is single family residence, and so SB 9 applies.

On April 6, 2025, Respondent maintained its position, stating the primary purpose of A-1 zoning is not residential.

II.             Procedural History

Petitioner filed the petition against Respondent on April 7, 2026. There is no proof of service on file.

On May 26, 2026, Respondent demurred to the Petition. On June 17, 2026, Petitioner filed a “Declaration for: Petitioner’s Opposition to Demurrer to Petition for Writ of Mandate and Complaint for Damages; Memorandum of Points and Authorities”.

On June 18, 2026, the Court denied Petitioner’s ex parte application for trial preference without prejudice to Petitioner to file a properly noticed motion for trial preference.

On July 16, 2026, Respondent filed its reply. Respondent’s reply suggests Petitioner served Respondent with a referenced opposition memorandum; however, Petitioner did not file any documents in opposition aside from the above-stated declaration.

On July 23, 2026, the Court heard Respondent’s demurrer. Petitioner represented that he served an opposition on Respondent. Respondent represented that it received the opposition. The Court permitted Petitioner’s opposition to be filed after the original hearing so that it could be considered in full. The Court therefore continued the hearing and ordered Petitioner to file within two days the opposition he served on Respondent.

On July 24, 2026, Petitioner filed his opposition.

The Court has considered all documents filed and properly before it.

III.           Legal Standard

A demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the pleading alone and will be sustained where the pleading is defective on its face.

A demurrer may be asserted on any one or more of the following grounds: (a) The court has no jurisdiction of the subject of the cause of action alleged in the pleading; (b) The person who filed the pleading does not have legal capacity to sue; (c) There is another action pending between the same parties on the same cause of action; (d) There is a defect or misjoinder of parties; (e) The pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action; (f) The pleading is uncertain (“uncertain” includes ambiguous and unintelligible); (g) In an action founded upon a contract, it cannot be ascertained from the pleading whether the contract is written, is oral, or is implied by conduct; (h) No certificate was filed as required by CCP section 411.35 or (i) by CCP section 411.36. (CCP §430.10.) Accordingly, a demurrer tests the sufficiency of a pleading, and the grounds for a demurrer must appear on the face of the pleading or from judicially noticeable matters. (CCP §430.30(a); Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) The face of the pleading includes attachments and incorporations by reference (Frantz v. Blackwell (1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 91, 94); it does not include inadmissible hearsay. (Day v. Sharp, (1975) 50 Cal.App.3d 904, 914.)

The sole issue on demurrer for failure to state a cause of action is whether the facts pleaded, if true, would entitle the plaintiff to relief. (Garcetti v. Superior Court (1996) 49 Cal.App.4th 1533, 1547 [“Garcetti”]; Limandri v. Judkins (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 326, 339.) The question of plaintiff’s ability to prove the allegations of the complaint or the possible difficulty in making such proof does not concern the reviewing court. (Quelimane Co. v. Stewart Title Guaranty Co., (1998) 19 Cal.4th 26, 47.) The ultimate facts alleged in the complaint must be deemed true, as well as all facts that may be implied or inferred from those expressly alleged. (Marshall v. Gibson, Dunn & Crutcher, (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 1397, 1403 [“Marshall”].) Nevertheless, this rule does not apply to allegations expressing mere conclusions of law, or allegations contradicted by the exhibits to the complaint or by matters of which judicial notice may be taken. (Vance v. Villa Park Mobilehome Estates, (1995) 36 Cal.App.4th 698, 709 [“Vance”].)

These principles guide the Court’s analysis of Respondent’s demurrer, below.

IV.           Analysis

Respondent demurs to the Petition in its entirety for failure to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action on the basis that it is time-barred. Respondent further demurs to each cause of action. For the first cause of action, titled “Want of Ordinary Care / Negligence, CA Code 815.2 (a), 815.6, 820 (a),” Respondent demurs on the basis that it fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. For the second cause of action, titled “Dereliction of Duty,” Respondent demurs on the basis that there is no such cause of action. For the third cause of action, untitled but evidently for traditional mandamus, Respondent demurs on the basis that it fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action.

1.               The Petition Is Untimely

Respondent argues the entire Petition is untimely under the 90-day statute of limitations established by Government Code section 66499.37.

This section provides, in part:

“Any action or proceeding to attack, review, set aside, void, or annul the decision of an advisory agency, appeal board, or legislative body concerning a subdivision, or of any of the proceedings, acts, or determinations taken, done, or made prior to the decision, or to determine the reasonableness, legality, or validity of any condition attached thereto, including, but not limited to, the approval of a tentative map or final map, shall not be maintained by any person unless the action or proceeding is commenced and service of summons effected within 90 days after the date of the decision. Thereafter all persons are barred from any action or proceeding or any defense of invalidity or unreasonableness of the decision or of the proceedings, acts, or determinations.” (Gov. Code § 66499.37.)

Because the Petition seeks to set aside Respondent’s denial of a subdivision request under SB 9, it falls squarely within Government Code section 66499.37. Regardless of characterization, all legal theories in the Petition depend on this Court finding that the decision to deny the Plan was improper. (See generally, Pet.) In other words, each cause of action in the Petition requires the Court to review Respondent’s decision concerning the subdivision Plan. Therefore, the 90-day statute of limitations applies.

Respondent denied the Plan on April 3, 2025. (Pet. at 5.) Consequently, the limitations period ended 90 days after April 3, 2025, or July 2, 2025. Petitioner filed the Petition on April 7, 2026, over a year after the original denial and nine months after the end of the applicable limitations period.

In opposition, Petitioner raises two primary arguments.

First, Petitioner argues that Respondent did not issue a “formal administrative denial after processing,” but rather “‘voided’ the online submission via the [online] portal on the exact day it was submitted.” (Opp. at 9.) Consequently, Petitioner contends there was no decision for the purposes of Government Code section 66499.37.

Petitioner presents no authority or argument to explain why voiding his application did not constitute a decision for the purposes of Government Code section 66499.37. Moreover, his own Petition characterized this action as Respondent rejecting the Plan. (Pet. at 5.) Respondent voided the application and transmitted to Petitioner an email explaining that the application was voided on the basis that the Property is not legally eligible for the SB 9 subdivision process. (Pet. at 5, Ex. C.) Therefore, the voiding was clearly a decision concerning a subdivision, and at a minimum was part of “any of the proceedings, acts, or determinations taken, done or made prior to the decision.”  (Gov. Code § 66499.37.) Consequently, the Government Code section 66499.37 limitations period applies and the petition is time-barred.

Second, Petitioner argues the petition is not about a subdivision dispute, but what he characterizes as Respondents’ alleged “ultra vires” action of imposing conditions outside of SB 9. (Opp. at 10.) However, the Petition seeks a writ of mandate directing Respondent to vacate its denial of the Plan on the basis that the denial was wrongful. (Pet. at 9.) While Petitioner alleges the wrongfulness of the decision stems from constitutional violations and statutory interpretation, the action remains an action to set aside a decision on a subdivision under SB 9 and thus remains subject to the limitations period set forth in Government Code section 66499.37. (Gov. Code § 66499.37.)

Accordingly, the entire Petition is barred by the statute of limitations and, even assuming the truth of all well-pleaded facts, the limitations defect cannot be cured by amendment.

2.               Each Cause of Action Independently Fails

Even if the Court were to conclude that the Petition is not barred by the applicable statute of limitations, the Court would still sustain the demurrer because each cause of action independently fails as a matter of law for the reasons set forth below. These independent defects exist even assuming all allegations are taken as true.

a.                The First Cause of Action Fails

Petitioner’s first cause of action is titled “Want of Ordinary Care / Negligence, CA Code 815.2 (a), 815.6, 820 (a)”. (Pet. at 10.) Petitioner alleges Respondent improperly denied Petitioner’s Plan. (See Pet. at 12-13.) Petitioner alleges this denial proximately harmed Petitioner, causing him damages by preventing him from constructing ADUs and renting them for profit. (See Pet. at 17-18.) Petitioner alleges Respondent’s denial constitutes a breach of the duty of care on the basis that Respondent was required to “review the applicable statutory text” and “act on [Petitioner’s] request in ministerial capacity as stipulated in SB-9.” (Pet. at 18.) Petitioner cites Government Code sections 815.2 (providing for vicarious liability on the part of public entities for employee liability caused by acts or omissions within the scope of employment) and 820, subdivision (a), (providing that public employees are liable for injury to the same extent as private persons, subject to statutory exceptions), as the basis for the claim.[1]

Respondent argues this cause of action fails because, under Government Code sections 818.4 and 821.2, Respondents Planning, Bodek and Lynch are a public entity and public employees not liable for injury caused by denial or failure to issue approval for matters which they are authorized to determine.

Under Government Code section 815.2, a public entity is liable for acts or omissions by their employees committed during the scope of employment, “if the act or omission would, apart from this section, have given rise to a cause of action against that employee or his personal representative.” (Gov. Code § 815.2.)

Under Government Code section 820, subdivision (a), a public employee is liable for injury to the same extent as a private person “[e]xcept as otherwise provided by statute”. (Gov. Code § 820(a).)

The Government Code also provides that public entities (Gov. Code § 818.4) and public employees (Gov. Code § 821.2) “[are] not liable for an injury caused by [the] issuance, denial, suspension or revocation of, or by [the] failure or refusal to issue, deny, suspend or revoke, any permit, license, certificate, approval, order, or similar authorization where [they are] authorized by enactment to determine whether or not such authorization should be issued, denied, suspended or revoked.” (Gov. Code §§ 818.4, 821.2). Consequently, if Respondent is authorized by enactment to determine whether the Plan should have been approved or denied, Respondent is not liable for any injury caused by that denial.

While Petitioner alleges the Plan met all objective criteria and so should have been approved, Respondent is undisputedly authorized by enactment to determine whether the Plan should have been approved or denied, albeit ministerially.[2] (See, e.g., Pet., at 12.)

Therefore, by operation of Government Code section 818.4 and 821.2, Respondents Planning, Bodek, and Lynch are not liable for any injury caused by denial of the Plan. Because public entity vicarious liability under Government Code section 815.2 only applies where the public employees are themselves liable, respondent Planning is also not subject to liability through that section.

Accordingly, the first cause of action fails as a matter of law. This conclusion is based solely on the statutory immunity applicable to Respondent’s decision and does not reflect any evaluation of the merits of Petitioner’s disagreement with that decision. As all liability for the alleged actions is precluded by statute, and because immunity applies regardless of Petitioner’s characterization of Respondent’s decision, leave to amend would not be appropriate.

b.               The Second Cause of Action Fails

Petitioner’s second cause of action is titled “Dereliction of Duty.” (Pet. at 21.) Petitioner alleges Respondent violated Government Code section 19572, subdivision (d), “Inexcusable neglect of duty.”[3] (Pet. at 21.)

Respondent argues Government Code section 19572 does not provide a private right of action, and generally no cause of action exists for “Dereliction of Duty.” (Opp. at 11.)

The Court agrees. Government Code section 19572 provides an enumerated list of grounds constituting cause to discipline an employee under the State Civil Service Rules. (Gov. Code § 19572; see Gov. Code §§ 18500-19799.) Subdivision (d) of this section provides that “[i]nexcusable neglect of duty” constitutes one such grounds. (Gov. Code § 19572(d).) Nothing in this section or the Civil Service Rules more broadly provides that grounds to discipline a public employee may result in a private right of action or liability to a private party.

Accordingly, the second cause of action fails as a matter of law. No amendment could create a private right of action where none exists under statute; therefore, leave to amend is not appropriate.[4]

c.                The Third Cause of Action Fails

Petitioner’s untitled third cause of action does not expressly seek relief under CCP section 1085 (or any other section of the CCP). However, this cause of action alleges a ministerial duty (Pet. at 23), a beneficial interest (Pet. at 23), breach of that duty (Pet. at 24), exhaustion of available administrative remedies (or alternatively futility) (Pet. at 24), and otherwise no adequate remedy at law (Pet. at 24).[5] This is the substance of a traditional mandamus claim. (Pomona Police Officers’ Assn. v. City of Pomona (1997) 58 Cal.App.4th 578, 583-84 [“¿‘Generally, a writ will lie when there is no plain, speedy, and adequate alternative remedy; the respondent has a duty to perform; and the petitioner has a clear and beneficial right to performance.’¿”].) Petitioner also identifies the Petition as, in part, “for Writ of Mandate (CCP §§ 1085, 1094.5).” (Pet. at 1.) Petitioner alleges, as described in more detail ante, that Respondent had a ministerial duty to approve his Plan to subdivide his Property under SB 9, but instead Respondent denied the Plan. (Pet. at 23-24.)

Respondent argues that Respondent did not violate its ministerial duty, and that Petitioner has adequate remedy at law.

i.          Respondent Did Not Violate Any Identified Ministerial Duty

Respondent argues that Government Code sections 65852.21 and 66411.7 (codifying SB 9) do not require Respondent to ministerially approve Petitioner’s Plan. Specifically, Respondent contends that the Property is not within a single-family residential zone.[6]

Government Code section 65852.21, subdivision (a), provides, in part, that “[a] proposed housing development containing no more than two residential units within a single-family residential zone shall be considered ministerially . . . .” (Gov. Code § 65852.21(a) [emphasis added].) Likewise, Government Code section 66411.7, subdivision (a), provides that “[n]otwithstanding any other provision of this division and any local law, a local agency shall ministerially approve, as set forth in this section, a parcel map for an urban lot split only if the local agency determines that the parcel map for the urban lot split meets all the following requirements: . . . The parcel is located within a single-family residential zone.” (Gov. Code § 66411.7(a).)

Petitioner relies on the California Department of Housing and Community Development’s SB 9 Fact Sheet (2022) (“Fact Sheet”) for the proposition that “[a] zone where single-family residential use is a legal, permitted use is considered a single-family residential zone for purposes of SB 9.” (Pet. at 5.) Respondent notes that this same guidance document specifically excludes agricultural zones. The 2022 Fact Sheet is not the current version of the fact sheet and neither party provides any version of the Fact Sheet or asks the Court to take judicial notice of it.

Regardless, Petitioner agrees the Property is zoned A-1 for agricultural use. (Pet. at 5.) This is not a single-family residential zone. Consequently, SB 9 does not apply. SB¿9 applies only where a parcel is located in a single-family residential zone. The Property’s A-1-2 designation is an agricultural zone as a matter of law, and the Court cannot, through mandamus, redefine that zoning classification.

Based on the foregoing, Petitioner does not allege facts showing Respondent violated any identified ministerial duty. Accordingly, the third cause of action fails as a matter of law and leave to amend is not appropriate. Given this conclusion, the Court need not reach any further arguments. The Court expresses no view on any policy concerns raised by Petitioner, and rules only on the legal sufficiency of the pleading.

 

V.              Conclusion

The entire Petition is untimely, and the individual causes of action also fail. The demurrer is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.
