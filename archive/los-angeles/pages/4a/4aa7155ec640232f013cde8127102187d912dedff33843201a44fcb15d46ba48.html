NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: Hearing on Defendant’s Motion to Reclassify Case from Unlimited Civil to Limited Civil Jurisdiction

Defendant Cultureedit, LLC’s Motion to Reclassify is denied.

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Tom of Finland Foundation, Inc. (Plaintiff), a public benefit corporation, together with seven wholesale suppliers, filed this action against Defendant Cultureedit, LLC (Defendant). Plaintiff alleges that it licensed Defendant to operate an online store using Plaintiff’s trademarks and intellectual property, that Defendant failed to pay royalties owed under the license agreement, and that Defendant retained the store’s proceeds and misappropriated Plaintiff’s intellectual property and internet domain.

The operative Second Amended Complaint (SAC) alleges six causes of action: (1) breach of contract; (2) breach of the duty of loyalty; (3) violation of the Unfair Competition Law; (4) conversion; (5) fraud; and (6) an accounting. Plaintiff seeks compensatory damages, an accounting, disgorgement, declaratory relief, injunctive relief, and punitive damages.

The Court previously sustained Defendant’s demurrer to the third and fifth causes of action with leave to amend and overruled the demurrer on the ground of misjoinder. The seven wholesale suppliers were later dismissed or removed from the action, leaving Plaintiff as the only remaining plaintiff.

Defendant filed a Motion to Reclassify. Plaintiff filed a Notice of Non-Opposition.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure section 403.040 governs a motion to reclassify a civil action. A defendant may move for reclassification within the time allowed to respond to the initial pleading, and the court shall grant the motion if the case has been classified in an incorrect jurisdictional classification. (Code Civ. Proc., § 403.040, subd. (a).) When a party moves for reclassification after that time, the court shall grant the motion only if both the case is incorrectly classified and the moving party shows good cause for not seeking reclassification earlier. (Code Civ. Proc., § 403.040, subd. (b).)

An action is a limited civil case only if all applicable conditions are satisfied, including that the amount in controversy, exclusive of attorney’s fees, interest, and costs, does not exceed thirty-five thousand dollars ($35,000), and that the relief sought is of a type that may be granted in a limited civil case. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 85, 86, subd. (a)(1).)

“[A] matter may be reclassified as a limited civil action ‘when (i) the absence of jurisdiction is apparent before trial from the complaint, petition, or related documents, or (ii) during the course of pretrial litigation, it becomes clear that the matter will “necessarily” result in a verdict below the superior court’s jurisdictional amount ….’” (Ytuarte v. Superior Court (2005) 129 Cal.App.4th 266, 276-277 (Ytuarte), quoting Walker v. Superior Court (1991) 53 Cal.3d 257, 262 (Walker).)

“The court may believe it highly unlikely that plaintiff will recover the amount demanded, but this is not enough to defeat jurisdiction, unless it appears to a legal certainty that plaintiff cannot recover the amount which he has demanded.” (Walker, supra, 53 Cal.3d at p. 270.) The standard evaluates the amount fairly in controversy and is not an adjudication of the merits of the claim. (Ytuarte, supra, 129 Cal.App.4th at p. 277.) It “is not met when it appears a verdict within the unlimited court’s jurisdiction is ‘possible.’” (Ibid.)

The high standard reflects the circumscribed procedures and recovery available in the limited civil courts. (Ytuarte, supra, 129 Cal.App.4th at p. 278.) A plaintiff in a limited civil action may not obtain a permanent injunction and has fewer rights to declaratory relief than a litigant in an unlimited case. (Id. at p. 275; see Code Civ. Proc., § 86, subds. (a)(7), (a)(8).)

 

ANALYSIS

Because Defendant moved to reclassify after the time to respond to the initial pleading, Defendant must show both that the case is incorrectly classified and good cause for the delay. (Code Civ. Proc., § 403.040, subd. (b).) Plaintiff’s non-opposition does not establish that the case is incorrectly classified.

I. The Amount in Controversy

Defendant argues that, with the wholesale suppliers no longer parties, Plaintiff’s remaining claims cannot yield a recovery exceeding $35,000 to a legal certainty.

Defendant’s showing addresses the contract claim. Under the license agreement, Plaintiff is entitled to royalties only on the net proceeds of Custom Products sold through the online store, and Defendant’s evidence is that total net proceeds for those products since 2017 were $25,739.82, yielding a maximum royalty of approximately $2,573.98, further reduced by a $15,000 advance. (Andreasson Decl. ¶¶ 6-9.) Taken alone, the contract claim falls below the jurisdictional threshold.

Plaintiff’s recovery, however, is not confined to contract royalties. The SAC pleads conversion of Plaintiff’s intellectual property and internet domain, breach of the duty of loyalty, fraud, and disgorgement of the store proceeds Defendant allegedly retained, and it seeks punitive damages on the conversion and fraud causes of action. The SAC fixes no amount for these claims, and the amount in controversy includes the recovery sought on them, including punitive damages. (Code Civ. Proc., § 85, subd. (a).)

Defendant responds that these claims cannot produce a meaningful recovery because the agreement forecloses the loyalty claim, the Unfair Competition Law affords no damages, the conversion claim is preempted, and the fraud claim states no coherent basis for recovery. Those are contentions about the merits. On a motion to reclassify, the Court evaluates the amount fairly in controversy and does not adjudicate the merits of the claims. (Ytuarte, supra, 129 Cal.App.4th at p. 277.) The test is whether a jurisdictionally sufficient verdict is possible, not whether it is probable. (Maldonado v. Superior Court (1996) 45 Cal.App.4th 397, 402.)

Because Plaintiff’s conversion, fraud, disgorgement, and punitive damages claims seek unliquidated amounts that the SAC does not cap, and because the Court may not resolve the merits of those claims on this motion, it does not appear to a legal certainty that Plaintiff’s recovery will necessarily be $35,000 or less. A verdict within the unlimited court’s jurisdiction remains possible. (Ytuarte, supra, 129 Cal.App.4th at p. 277.)

II. The Relief Sought

Independent of the amount in controversy, the relief Plaintiff seeks is not of a type available in a limited civil case. An action qualifies as a limited civil case only if the relief sought is of a type that may be granted in such a case. (Code Civ. Proc., § 85.)

The SAC seeks permanent injunctive relief: an order compelling Defendant to return the internet domain, to cease using Plaintiff’s intellectual property, and to refrain from representing any association with Plaintiff. A plaintiff in a limited civil action may not obtain a permanent injunction. (Ytuarte, supra, 129 Cal.App.4th at p. 275.) Section 86, subdivision (a)(8) authorizes a limited civil court to issue only a temporary restraining order or preliminary injunction necessary to preserve the property or rights of a party to a case that is otherwise a limited civil case. It does not authorize the permanent injunctive relief Plaintiff seeks.

Defendant contends that the requested relief is authorized under section 86, subdivision (a)(8). That subdivision applies to provisional relief ancillary to a case that already qualifies as limited, not to a permanent injunction sought as ultimate relief. Plaintiff also seeks declaratory relief, which a limited civil court may grant only by cross-complaint or after nonbinding attorney-client fee arbitration. (Code Civ. Proc., § 86, subd. (a)(7).) Neither circumstance is present.

Because the SAC seeks relief that a limited civil court cannot grant, the action is not correctly classified as limited on this ground as well. For the reasons stated above, the case is not incorrectly classified, and the Court does not reach the question of whether Defendant showed good cause for failing to seek reclassification earlier.

Accordingly, the Motion is denied.

 

CONCLUSION

Defendant Cultureedit, LLC’s Motion to Reclassify is denied.


Case Number: 24STCV11590    Hearing Date: July 17, 2026    Dept: 515

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: Hearing on Petitions of Petitioner Daniel Onofre for Approval of Compromise of Claim of Minors Angie Delilah Onofre, Daniel Onofre Jr., and Zachary Isaiah Marquez

 

Petitioner’s Petitions for Minor’s Compromise are granted.

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiffs Daniel Onofre, Maria Mazariegos Marquez, Daniel Onofre Jr., Angie Delilah Onofre, and Zachary Isaiah Marquez (collectively, “Plaintiffs”), tenants of an apartment building, filed this action against Salvador Munoz and Ana M. Hernandez, as trustee of the Ana Maria Hernandez Trust (collectively, “Defendants”).

The causes of action are: (1) breach of the implied warranty of habitability; (2) breach of the statutory warranty of habitability; (3) breach of the covenant of quiet enjoyment; (4) negligence; (5) violation of Civil Code section 1942.4; (6) private nuisance; (7) violation of the City of Los Angeles Tenant Anti-Harassment Ordinance; and (8) violation of the Unfair Competition Law.

Three of the Plaintiffs are minors: Angie Delilah Onofre, Daniel Onofre Jr., and Zachary Isaiah Marquez (collectively, “Minors”). Each Minor appears through a guardian ad litem, Daniel Onofre (Petitioner). The parties settled the action for a global sum of $375,000, allocated among the five Plaintiffs. As guardian ad litem, Petitioner filed three substantively identical petitions requesting the Court’s approval of the compromise of each Minor’s claim.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

Courts must decide whether to approve a compromise by determining whether petitioners, such as guardians, are acting in the best interests of minors. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 372, subd. (a); Scruton v. Korean Air Lines Co. (1995) 39 Cal.App.4th 1596, 1603-1607.) “[T]he protective role the court generally assumes in cases involving minors, [is] a role to assure that whatever is done is in the minor’s best interests…. [I]ts primary concern is whether the compromise is sufficient to provide for the minor’s injuries, care and treatment.” (Goldberg v. Superior Court (1994) 23 Cal.App.4th 1378, 1382.) Judicial discretion in approving petitions for minors’ compromises, and in ordering distributions, must be exercised in the best interests of minors, on a case-by-case basis tailored to the circumstances. (Christensen v. Superior Court (1987) 193 Cal.App.3d 139, 142-144.)

A petition for court approval of a compromise under Code of Civil Procedure section 372 must comply with California Rules of Court, rules 7.950, 7.951, and 7.952. (See Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1384(a).) Provisions of the Probate Code and the Code of Civil Procedure apply to compromises of minors’ claims. (See Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.950.)

Payment of attorney’s fees on a minor’s behalf must be approved as reasonable by the court. (Niles v. City of San Rafael (1974) 42 Cal.App.3d 230, 244.) “The trial court itself must develop and resolve any counterarguments on behalf of the minor, lest the attorney receive an excessive award of fees.” (Gonzalez v. Chen (2011) 197 Cal.App.4th 881, 887.) Unless the court has approved the fee agreement in advance, the court must use a reasonable fee standard in approving attorney’s fees payable from money paid or to be paid for the benefit of a minor. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.955(a)(1).) The court must consider the terms of the agreement between the attorney and the minor’s representative and evaluate the agreement based on the facts and circumstances existing when the agreement was made. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.955(a)(2).) Rule 7.955(b) sets out nonexclusive factors bearing on the reasonableness of the fee, and the petition must include a declaration from the attorney addressing the applicable factors. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.955(c).)

 

ANALYSIS

I. Settlement

In seeking approval of the settlement, Petitioner represents that he made a careful and diligent inquiry and investigation into the facts and circumstances of the incident, the responsibility for it, and the nature and extent of each Minor’s injuries. (Petitions ¶ 9.) The Minors resided with their family in one unit of an apartment building that suffered from uninhabitable conditions, including rodent, cockroach, and bedbug infestations. Each Minor’s claim is for bodily injury alone, consisting of insect bites and allergy symptoms treated with over-the-counter medication, from which each Minor has fully recovered without treatment by a physician. (Petitions ¶¶ 5-8 & Attach. 13a.)

Each Minor is to receive a gross recovery of $20,000. The Court finds that this amount is fair, reasonable, and in each Minor’s best interest, and is consistent with amounts this Court has approved in comparable cases involving minors who lived in uninhabitable conditions, fully recovered, and required no medical treatment. Accordingly, the Court approves the compromise of each Minor’s claim.

II. Disposition of Funds

The net balance available to each Minor after costs and attorney fees is $14,722.73. Probate Code section 3611, subdivision (b), authorizes the Court to order that the remaining balance of a minor’s settlement be deposited in an insured account in a financial institution in this state, subject to withdrawal only upon the authorization of the court. (See also Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.953.) Petitioner requests that each Minor’s net recovery be deposited in a blocked account at JP Morgan Chase Bank, subject to withdrawal only on order of the Court, until the Minor reaches the age of majority. The Court finds this disposition to be in each Minor’s best interest and approves it.

III. Attorney’s Fees

Counsel requests attorney’s fees of $4,907.57 for each Minor, representing twenty-five percent of the Minor’s recovery after deduction of costs, under a written contingency fee agreement. (Attach. 17a.) The petitions include the declaration of counsel required by California Rules of Court, rule 7.955(c). (Attach. 13a.) The declaration addresses the applicable factors under rule 7.955(b): the nature of each Minor’s injuries; the time and labor involved, estimated at twenty-five hours per Minor; counsel’s experience and reputation in habitability litigation; the contingent nature of the representation and the risk of no recovery; counsel’s advancement of costs; and the informed consent of the guardian ad litem to the fee. The requested fee equals approximately $196 per hour. The Court finds the requested fee reasonable. Accordingly, the Court approves attorney’s fees of $4,907.57 and costs of $369.70 as to each Minor.

 

CONCLUSION

The Petitions are granted. The Court approves the compromise of each Minor’s claim in the gross amount of $20,000, approves attorney’s fees of $4,907.57 and costs of $369.70 as to each Minor, and orders that the net balance of $14,722.73 for each Minor be deposited in a blocked account subject to withdrawal only upon further order of the Court.


Case Number: 24STCV23198    Hearing Date: July 17, 2026    Dept: 515

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: Hearing on Defendants’ Motion for Summary Judgment or, Alternatively, Summary Adjudication.

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Ernest N. Ramirez (Plaintiff) filed this action against Defendants Ford Motor Company (Ford) and BP Ford of Long Beach (the Dealership) (collectively, “Defendants”), alleging that the new 2022 Ford Maverick Plaintiff purchased (the Vehicle) developed defects that Ford failed to repair after a reasonable number of attempts and that the Dealership negligently repaired the Vehicle.

The Complaint alleges five causes of action: (1) Violation of Civil Code Section 1793.2, Subdivision (D); (2) Violation of Civil Code Section 1793.2, Subdivision (B); (3) Violation of Civil Code Section 1793.2, Subdivision (A)(3); (4) Breach of the Implied Warranty of Merchantability, each asserted against Ford Under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act; and (5) Negligent Repair, asserted against the Dealership.

Defendants filed a Motion for Summary Judgment or, Alternatively, Summary Adjudication. Plaintiff filed an Opposition.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

A defendant moving for summary judgment must show that one or more elements of a cause of action cannot be established or that there is a complete defense to it. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) The motion must be granted if the papers show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c).)

A defendant meets its initial burden by presenting evidence, and not by simply arguing that the plaintiff lacks evidence. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 854 (Aguilar).) A moving defendant may also rely on a plaintiff’s factually devoid discovery responses to shift the burden. (Union Bank v. Superior Court (1995) 31 Cal.App.4th 573, 590.) If the defendant carries that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) The Court strictly construes the moving party’s evidence and liberally construes the opposing party’s evidence, resolving any doubts in favor of the opposing party. (Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at p. 843.)

A motion for summary adjudication proceeds under the same standard and may be granted only if it completely disposes of a cause of action, an affirmative defense, a claim for damages, or an issue of duty. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (f)(1).)

 

ANALYSIS

I. Defendants’ Objection to the Untimely Opposition

An opposition to a motion for summary judgment must be served and filed not less than 20 days before the hearing unless the Court for good cause orders otherwise. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (b)(2).) Plaintiff served and filed the Opposition after that deadline. Defendants object on that ground and, in the alternative, request a continuance to file a reply.

The Court has broad discretion to refuse to consider papers filed after the deadline without a prior order finding good cause. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1300(d); Bozzi v. Nordstrom, Inc. (2010) 186 Cal.App.4th 755, 765.) The Court exercises its discretion to consider the opposition. The policy favoring disposition on the merits supports consideration, and the evidence submitted with the Opposition consists of excerpts of Plaintiff’s deposition, which Defendants’ counsel took. The request for a continuance is denied.

Accordingly, the objection is overruled, and the Court considers the Opposition.

II. Violation of Civil Code Section 1793.2, Subdivision (d)

To prevail on a claim under section 1793.2, subdivision (d), a plaintiff must prove that (1) the vehicle had a nonconformity covered by the express warranty that substantially impaired its use, value, or safety; (2) the vehicle was presented to an authorized representative of the manufacturer for repair; and (3) the manufacturer or its representative did not repair the nonconformity after a reasonable number of repair attempts. (Donlen v. Ford Motor Co. (2013) 217 Cal.App.4th 138, 152.) The replacement or restitution remedy is triggered only after a reasonable number of attempts, and a single repair opportunity does not suffice. (Silvio v. Ford Motor Co. (2003) 109 Cal.App.4th 1205, 1208.) The reasonableness of the number of repair attempts is a question of fact to be determined in light of the circumstances, subject to the minimum that there be more than one opportunity to repair the nonconformity. (Robertson v. Fleetwood Travel Trailers of California, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 785, 799.)

Ford’s evidence shows that Plaintiff presented the Vehicle three times before filing suit, that the steering vibration for which Plaintiff presented the Vehicle was resolved, and that Plaintiff’s discovery responses direct Ford to a document production containing no evidence of an unrepaired nonconformity. (Scott Decl., ¶¶ 4, 5, 8, 9, Ex. 3, Ex. 4, Ex. 7 at 24:21-24, 30:18-21, 32:4-33:9, 33:10-36:18, 38:16-19, 40:24-41:5, Ex. 8.) That showing shifts the burden to Plaintiff.

Plaintiff’s deposition testimony raises a triable issue. Plaintiff testified that the Vehicle exhibits transmission hesitation, described as a delay or failure in gear shifting on acceleration, and recurring brake problems, and that both conditions remained unresolved as of the deposition despite presentation of the Vehicle for repair on multiple occasions. (Fuchs Decl. ¶¶ 9-13, Ex. 1 at 24:25-25:10, 25:11-17, 33:4-9, 39:1-19.) Viewing this evidence and the reasonable inferences from it in the light most favorable to Plaintiff, a trier of fact could find that the Vehicle had a nonconformity covered by the express warranty that Ford failed to repair after a reasonable number of attempts. Whether the number of attempts was reasonable is itself a question of fact. (Robertson, supra, 144 Cal.App.4th at p. 799.)

Accordingly, summary adjudication of the first cause of action is denied.

III. Violation of Civil Code Section 1793.2, Subdivision (b)

Section 1793.2, subdivision (b), requires that, where warranty service or repair is necessary, repairs be commenced within a reasonable time and, unless the buyer agrees otherwise in writing, that the vehicle be serviced or repaired to conform to the applicable warranties within 30 days, subject to extension for delay beyond the manufacturer’s control. (Civ. Code, § 1793.2, subd. (b).) The obligation is to conform the vehicle to the warranty within 30 days; the duration of any single repair visit does not establish compliance.

Ford contends that no repair visit exceeded 30 days. (Mot. at p. 13:9-14; Scott Decl., ¶ 4, Ex. 3.) That showing does not negate the element. Plaintiff testified that he reported a malfunctioning rearview camera, was told the necessary part was unavailable, and waited approximately three to four months for the repair, and that the transmission and brake defects were never conformed to warranty. (Fuchs Decl. ¶ 9, Ex. 1 at 33:4-9, 39:1-19; ¶¶ 12-13, Ex. 1 at 24:25-25:10, 25:11-17.) That evidence raises a triable issue as to whether Ford serviced or repaired the Vehicle to conform to the applicable warranties within 30 days.

Ford separately asks the Court to adjudicate that the replacement or restitution remedy is unavailable under this cause of action. (Mot. at p. 13:25-p. 14:10.) A request to limit the remedy available on a cause of action does not completely dispose of a cause of action, an affirmative defense, a claim for damages, or an issue of duty, and is not a proper subject of summary adjudication. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (f)(1).)

Accordingly, summary adjudication of the second cause of action is denied.

IV. Violation of Civil Code Section 1793.2, Subdivision (a)(3)

Section 1793.2, subdivision (a)(3), requires the manufacturer to make available to its authorized service and repair facilities sufficient service literature and replacement parts to effect repairs during the express warranty period. (Civ. Code, § 1793.2, subd. (a)(3).)

Ford relies on Plaintiff’s factually devoid discovery responses to shift the burden. (Mot. at p. 14:11-p. 15:2; Scott Decl., ¶¶ 4, 5, Exs. 3, 4.) Plaintiff testified, however, that after he reported the rearview camera malfunction, Ford’s authorized facility was unable to obtain the necessary replacement part for approximately three to four months. (Fuchs Decl. ¶ 9, Ex. 1 at 33:4-9, 39:1-19.) Drawing all inferences in Plaintiff’s favor, a trier of fact could find that Ford failed to make sufficient replacement parts available to its authorized facility during the warranty period.

Accordingly, summary adjudication of the third cause of action is denied.

V. Breach of the Implied Warranty of Merchantability

The implied warranty of merchantability accompanies every retail sale of consumer goods and requires, among other things, that the goods be fit for the ordinary purpose for which they are used; in the case of a vehicle, the warranty is breached only if the vehicle manifests a defect so basic that it is unfit for its ordinary purpose of providing transportation, and the warranty provides for a minimum level of quality. (Civ. Code, §§ 1791.1, 1792; American Suzuki Motor Corp. v. Superior Court (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 1291, 1296.) The duration of the implied warranty is coextensive with the express warranty but in no event more than one year following the sale. (Civ. Code, § 1791.1, subd. (c).) That durational provision limits the period during which the warranty is in force; it does not require that a latent defect be discovered, or that the vehicle be presented for repair, within that period. A latent defect existing at the time of sale renders the vehicle unmerchantable by the existence of the defect, not by its later discovery. (Mexia v. Rinker Boat Co., Inc. (2009) 174 Cal.App.4th 1297, 1304-1305.)

Ford contends that Plaintiff cannot establish a breach because the Vehicle was not presented for warranty repair within the one-year implied warranty period. (Mot. at p. 15:24-28.) That contention misapprehends the governing standard. Because a latent defect existing at the time of sale may breach the implied warranty even though it manifests later, Ford’s showing that the Vehicle was not presented within the first year neither negates the existence of a latent defect nor establishes that Plaintiff cannot prove a breach. Ford has not carried its initial burden as to this cause of action. Plaintiff’s testimony that the transmission and brake defects recur and remain unresolved further supports a reasonable inference that the defects were latent in the Vehicle at the time of sale, and whether the Vehicle was merchantable is a question of fact. (Fuchs Decl. ¶¶ 12-13, Ex. 1 at 24:25-25:10, 25:11-17.)

Accordingly, summary adjudication of the fourth cause of action is denied.

VI. Negligent Repair

The economic loss rule bars recovery in tort for purely economic losses, meaning financial harm unaccompanied by personal injury or damage to property other than the product itself. (Sheen v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2022) 12 Cal.5th 905, 922.) As between contracting parties, the rule bars a tort claim arising from the contractual relationship unless the defendant violated a duty independent of the contract or engaged in intentional conduct such as fraud. (Robinson Helicopter Co., Inc. v. Dana Corp. (2004) 34 Cal.4th 979, 988-990.)

The Dealership’s evidence establishes, without dispute, that Plaintiff alleges no personal injury and no damage to property other than the Vehicle itself and seeks only economic losses. (Scott Decl., ¶¶ 2, 7, Ex. 1, Ex. 6, Nos. 6.1, 7.1, 8.1, 9.1.) The negligent repair cause of action arises from the same warranty repair relationship that underlies Plaintiff’s statutory claims and identifies no duty independent of that relationship and no intentional conduct. (Mot. at p. 17:6-16; Scott Decl., ¶ 2, Ex. 1, ¶¶ 11, 45, 46, 48.) Plaintiff did not address this cause of action in the Opposition and has not raised a triable issue. The economic loss rule bars the negligent repair claim.

Accordingly, summary judgment is granted as to the dealership. Because summary adjudication is denied as to the first through fourth causes of action, summary judgment is denied as to Ford.

 

CONCLUSION

Defendants’ Motion for Summary Judgment is granted as to the Dealership and denied as to Ford. Defendants’ alternative Motion for Summary Adjudication is moot as to the fifth cause of action and is otherwise denied.


Case Number: 25STCV01155    Hearing Date: July 17, 2026    Dept: 515

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: Hearing on Motion for Attorney Fees

 

Plaintiff’s Motion for Attorney Fees is granted in the reduced amount of $13,958.01.

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Scott Anthony Harris (Plaintiff) filed this Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act (Civ. Code, § 1790 et seq.) action against General Motors LLC (Defendant), alleging that he purchased a vehicle manufactured by Defendant that subsequently manifested defects.

On September 9, 2025, the parties agreed to settle the action, with Plaintiff as the prevailing party for purposes of seeking attorney fees and costs. The Court dismissed the action on December 29, 2025.

Plaintiff filed a Motion for Attorney Fees. Defendant filed an Opposition.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

A prevailing party is entitled to recover costs as a matter of right. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1032, subds. (a)(4), (b).) Attorney’s fees are also recoverable as costs when authorized by contract, statute, or law. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (a)(10).) The moving party bears the burden of establishing entitlement to attorney fees. (Christian Research Institute v. Alnor (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 1315, 1320 (Alnor).) Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d) provides that a buyer who prevails in an action under that section, “shall be allowed by the court to recover as a part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and the prosecution of such action.”

 

ANALYSIS

Plaintiff seeks $26,002.51 in total fees and costs based on $19,860.00 in attorney fees, a 0.2 multiplier ($3,972.00), and $2,170.51 in costs and expenses.

I. Timeliness

Defendant argues that the motion should be denied outright as untimely.

A motion claiming attorney fees for services rendered through the rendition of judgment must be served and filed within the time for filing a notice of appeal. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1702(b)(1).) That time is the earlier of 60 days after service of notice of entry of judgment or 180 days after entry of judgment. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 8.104(a)(1).) Rule 3.1702 does not require an appealable order or judgment; a voluntary dismissal that concludes the litigation and triggers a right to fees starts the clock. (Hatlevig v. General Motors LLC (2026) 118 Cal.App.5th 644 (Hatlevig).) In Hatlevig, the plaintiff’s counsel reported a settlement with Defendant to the trial court, which ordered a dismissal to be filed within 45 days, and the clerk later mailed notice that the case would be deemed dismissed on a date certain unless a dismissal was filed or good cause shown. Neither occurred. The Court of Appeal held that the case stood dismissed on the date fixed in the clerk’s notice, and that a fee motion served nearly two months after 180 days had run from that date was untimely. (Id. at p. 650.)

Defendant asserts that this action was dismissed on October 21, 2025. (Opp. at p. 3:25-26.) It was not. Plaintiff filed a Notice of Settlement of the Entire Case on October 21, 2025. (Pengilley Decl. ¶ 64.) The next day, the Court placed the matter in a conditional settlement stay and set an order to show cause regarding dismissal. (Id. at ¶ 65.) The Court dismissed the action on December 29, 2025. (Ibid.) One hundred eighty days later was June 27, 2026, and Plaintiff served and filed this motion on June 1, 2026. The 60-day period never began, because nothing in the record shows that the clerk or a party served notice of entry of the dismissal. (See Cal. Rules of Court, rule 8.104(a)(1)(A)-(B).)

Notably, Hatlevig did not concern a conditional settlement. (Hatlevig, supra, 118 Cal.App.5th at p. 648.) Thus, under California Rules of Court, rule 3.18385(b), the court was required to dismiss the case 45 days after it received the notice of settlement. In contrast here, Plaintiff’s Notice of Settlement of the Entire Case indicated a conditional settlement and, pursuant to rule 3.18385(c)(1), specified the date by which the dismissal was to be filed, December 12, 2025. Absent a showing of good cause, the Court was required to dismiss within 45 days of this later date (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.18385(c)(2)), as indeed, it did. Accordingly, the motion is timely.

II. Lodestar

Courts begin this inquiry “with the ‘lodestar,’ i.e., the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate.” (PLCM Group v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095.) From there, the “lodestar figure may then be adjusted [according to a multiplier enhancement] based on consideration of factors specific to the case, in order to fix the fee at the fair market value for the legal services provided.” (Ibid.) Relevant multiplier factors include “(1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, [and] (4) the contingent nature of the fee award.” (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132 (Ketchum).)

A. Hourly Rate

Plaintiff requests $545 per hour in 2025 and $575 per hour in 2026 for Stephanie Pengilley; $675 per hour for Victor Block,; $500 per hour for Armig Khodanian and Daniel Law; $450 per hour for Andrea Plata; $400 per hour for David Derderian; 325 per hour for Ellie Comaschi; and $125 per hour for paralegals, legal assistants, and case managers. (Pengilley Decl. ¶¶ 3-16, Ex. A.)

“In determining hourly rates, the court must look to the ‘prevailing market rates in the relevant community.’ [Citation.] … In making its calculation, the court should also consider the experience, skill, and reputation of the attorney requesting fees. [Citation.] The court may rely on its own knowledge and familiarity with the legal market in setting a reasonable hourly rate. [Citation.] ‘Affidavits of the plaintiffs’ attorney and other attorneys regarding prevailing fees in the community, and rate determinations in other cases, particularly those setting a rate for the plaintiffs’ attorney, are satisfactory evidence of the prevailing market rate.’ [Citation.]’’” (Heritage Pacific Financial, LLC v. Monroy (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 972, 1009.)

Because lemon law cases are typically brought on a contingency basis, the Court considers that factor in determining the reasonable hourly rate. (See Horsford v. Board of Trustees of California State University (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 359, 395 [“The contingency adjustment may be made at the lodestar phase of the court’s calculation or by applying a multiplier to the noncontingency lodestar calculation (but not both).”].) The Court finds, based on Pengilley’s declaration and the Court’s own experience, that the requested attorney hourly rates are reasonable for attorneys with their experience, working on a contingency basis. The $125 rate for paralegals is also reasonable.

B. Hours Billed

Next, the Court considers whether the 31.0 hours Plaintiff’s counsel billed are reasonable. Plaintiff separately seeks a flat fee of $4,500.00 for services rendered before the Complaint. (Pengilley Decl. ¶¶ 20-21, Ex. A.)

“‘In challenging attorney fees as excessive because too many hours of work are claimed, it is the burden of the challenging party to point to the specific items challenged, with a sufficient argument and citations to the evidence. General arguments that fees claimed are excessive, duplicative, or unrelated do not suffice.’” (Lunada Biomedical v. Nunez (2014) 230 Cal.App.4th 459, 488.)

1. Flat Fee for Pre-Complaint Work

Defendant argues that the $4,500.00 flat fee is not compensable, relying on Dominguez v. American Suzuki Motor Corp. (2008) 160 Cal.App.4th 53, 58. (Opp. at p. 5:4-17.) Dominguez addressed a manufacturer that offered to repurchase the vehicle before the complaint was filed, after which the plaintiff sued only to recover a civil penalty and fees. That is not what happened here, and, generally, a party may recover statutory attorney’s fees for work performed before the complaint. (See Hogar Dulce Hogar v. Community Development Com. of City of Escondido (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 1358, 1370 [noting “pre-complaint attorney fees have been permitted”].)

However, the flat fee is only partially supported. Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d), permits an award of fees “based on actual time expended.” Counsel keeps no record of the time spent before the Complaint. She declares that it “would be impractical to keep track of billables during the intake stage,” and that the flat rate “is equal to nine hours of time at a rate of $500.” (Pengilley Decl. ¶ 21.) That figure is a derivation from the fee, not a record of hours, and no timekeeper is identified for the nine hours. The single entry also bundles the preparation of the Attorney-Client Retainer Agreement, which is the firm’s own business, together with work that would otherwise be compensable, and there is no basis in the record to separate them. (Pengilley Decl., Ex. A.) Where a fee applicant keeps no record of the time expended, the Court may reduce the award to account for that failure rather than strike the hours altogether. (Alnor, supra, 165 Cal.App.4th at p. 1320 [the court “may properly reduce compensation on account of any failure to maintain appropriate time records”].) The Court credits half of the asserted nine hours as reasonably expended on compensable pre-Complaint work and deducts the remainder, a reduction of $2,250.00.

2. Deposition of Defendant’s Person Most Qualified

Victor Block billed 5.5 hours ($3,712.50) to prepare for, take, and summarize the deposition of Defendant’s person most qualified. Defendant asks the Court to allow 2.5 hours. (Opp. at pp. 5:18-6:2.)

The initial deposition of a manufacturer’s person most qualified may not exceed two hours and is limited to six enumerated topics. (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26, subds. (c), (i).) Plaintiff billed 2.3 hours for an examination that the Legislature capped at 2, and the Court allows 2.0 hours for it. Preparation of 2.6 hours is excessive for an examination so confined, particularly for an attorney with his experience in lemon law matters. (Pengilley Decl. ¶ 11.) The Court allows 1.5 hours of preparation. The 0.6 hours Block spent summarizing the testimony is not clerical and is billable. The Court deducts 1.4 hours ($945.00).

3. Deposition of Plaintiff

Daniel Law billed 2.8 hours ($1,400.00) to prepare Plaintiff for his deposition and to defend it. Defendant asks for a fifty percent reduction because the entry is block billed. (Opp. at p. 6:4-13.) “Blockbilling, while not objectionable per se in our view, exacerbates [s] the vagueness of counsel’s fee request…” (Alnor, supra, 165 Cal.App.4th at p. 1325.) Here, the entry describes related tasks performed on a single day, and 2.8 hours to meet a client, prepare him to testify, and defend his deposition is reasonable. The Court makes no deduction.

4. Written Discovery

Andrea Plata billed 2.0 hours ($900.00) to draft form interrogatories, special interrogatories, requests for admission, requests for production, a supporting declaration, and a deposition notice. Defendant asks the Court to allow 0.5 hours. (Opp. at p. 6:14-19.) The requests Plaintiff served are substantially identical to those Plaintiff’s counsel served in another action against Defendant. (Kay Decl. ¶ 8, Exs. A, B.) Counsel acknowledges that the firm bills only for the time spent modifying and updating its forms. (Pengilley Decl. ¶ 19.) Given the templated nature of this work, the Court reduces the time to 1.0 hour, a deduction of 1.0 hour ($450.00).

5. Opposition to Motion to Compel Initial Disclosures

Plata billed 1.9 hours ($855.00) to draft the opposition to Defendant’s motion to compel Plaintiff’s initial disclosures and 1.8 hours ($810.00) to attend the hearing. Defendant asks the Court to allow 1.0 hour. (Opp. at pp. 6:21-7:2.) Defendant’s motion succeeded, sanctions were issued, and the Court set an order to show cause regarding Plaintiff’s repeated noncompliance with the disclosure requirements. (Pengilley Decl. ¶¶ 61-62.) Time spent unsuccessfully resisting a motion made necessary by Plaintiff’s own failure to produce the documents Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26 requires was not reasonably incurred. The Court adopts Defendant’s proposed reduction of 2.7 hours ($1,215.00).

6. Fee Motion and Anticipated Time

Comaschi billed 4.6 hours ($1,495.00), and Pengilley billed 1.9 hours ($1,092.50) to prepare this motion, and Pengilley anticipates 5.0 additional hours ($2,875.00) to review the Opposition, prepare a reply, and attend the hearing. Defendant argues that this time is excessive. (Opp. at p. 7:4-13.)

The Court agrees. The motion is templated. The declaration filed in support of it bears the caption of a different case, before a different judge, in a different department. (Pengilley Decl., p. 1.) The motion refers throughout to “Plaintiffs,” though there is only one. A routine fee motion, reply, and hearing like this should not take more than 5 hours in total. The Court allows 2.5 hours for Comaschi ($812.50) and 2.5 hours for Pengilley ($1,437.50), a deduction of 6.5 hours ($3,212.50).

III. Multiplier

A trial court may award a lodestar multiplier under Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d). (Robertson v. Fleetwood Travel Trailers of California, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 785, 821.) Relevant multiplier factors include “(1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, [and] (4) the contingent nature of the fee award.” (Ketchum, supra, 24 Cal.4th at p. 1132.) “[W]hen determining the appropriate enhancement, a trial court should not consider these factors to the extent they are already encompassed within the lodestar.” (Id. at p. 1138.)

This case is not unusually complex and is typical of the cases that Plaintiff’s counsel handles. Moreover, the contingent nature of the work is already reflected in the hourly rate. Therefore, the Court declines to award a multiplier.

IV. Costs and Expenses

Plaintiff additionally requests $2,170.51 in costs and expenses: $475.25 in filing and motion fees, $155.75 in jury fees, $1,284.65 in deposition costs, $37.50 for service of process, $96.00 for electronic filing and service, and $121.36 in other costs, consisting of a court appearance service and this action’s one-twenty-second share of a mediator’s fee for a session covering twenty-two matters. (Pengilley Decl., Exs. B, C.) The Legislature used the word “expenses” in section 1794, subdivision (d), to reach out-of-pocket items that the statutory definition of costs does not capture. (Jensen v. BMW of North America, Inc. (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 112, 137-138.)

“If items on their face appear to be proper charges, the verified memorandum of costs is prima facie evidence of their propriety, and the burden is on the party seeking to tax costs to show they were not reasonable or necessary.” (Jones v. Dumrichob (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 1258, 1266, disapproved on other grounds in Rodriguez v. FCA US LLC (2024) 17 Cal.5th 189, 205.) Statements in a memorandum of points and authorities and in counsel’s declaration do not rebut that showing. (Ibid.)

Defendant objects to item 4, deposition costs of $1,284.65, contending that the deposition exceeded the two-hour statutory limit “by over an hour (or about 40%)” and asking the Court to strike $513.86. (Opp. at p. 9:9-13.) Defendant submits no transcript, no reporter’s certificate, and no evidence of the examination’s length; its counsel’s declaration states only that Plaintiff’s counsel took the deposition on June 3, 2025. (Kay Decl. ¶ 7.) The only evidence of duration is Plaintiff’s invoice, which reflects 2.3 hours. The objected-to charge is for the original transcript of the deponent, not for the reporter’s time. (Pengilley Decl., Ex. C.) Defendant does not explain why the price of a transcript would fall by forty percent had the examination closed eighteen minutes sooner. Defendant has not carried its burden. Defendant also identifies a single objectionable item worth $513.86 and then asks for a reduction of $636.86. (Opp. at p. 9:15.) The Court awards the full $2,170.51 in costs and expenses.

 

CONCLUSION

Plaintiff’s Motion for Attorney Fees is granted and fees and costs are awarded in the reduced amount of $13,958.01.


Case Number: 26STCV03097    Hearing Date: July 17, 2026    Dept: 515

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: Hearing on Defendants’ Demurrer to Plaintiffs’ Complaint and Motion to Strike Portions of Plaintiffs’ Complaint

Defendants’ Demurrer to the Complaint is overruled. Defendants’ Motion to Strike is denied.

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiffs Hendry Holloway, Ivers Holloway, Vance Holloway, Ashford Grantham, and Garrick Grantham Holloway, a minor, by and through his guardian ad litem, Ivers Holloway (collectively, Plaintiffs), filed this action against 427 Ivory LP and Zephyr Jetstream Company LLC (collectively, “Defendants”), the owners and managers of the residential property where Plaintiffs resided (the Subject Property). Plaintiffs allege that Defendants failed to remediate substandard and dangerous conditions at the Subject Property throughout their tenancy, including a persistent cockroach infestation, recurring mold, repeated ceiling leaks and water intrusion, two incidents in which a shower door shattered on a Plaintiff, exposed electrical outlets, and defective flooring, and that Plaintiffs were forced to vacate the Subject Property as a result.

The causes of action in the Complaint are: (1) Breach of Contract; (2) Breach of the Implied Warranty of Habitability; (3) Nuisance; (4) Negligence; (5) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress; (6) Wrongful Eviction; and (7) Violation of the Los Angeles Tenant Anti-Harassment Ordinance.

Defendants filed a Demurrer with Motion to Strike portions of the Complaint. Plaintiff did not file an Opposition.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (Wilson v. Transit Authority of City of Sacramento (1962) 199 Cal.App.2d 716, 720-21.) In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) “‘A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or the facts alleged.’ [Citation.]” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Services (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, courts assume the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Ibid.) However, they do not accept as true deductions, contentions, or conclusions of law or fact. (Stonehouse Homes LLC v. City of Sierra Madre (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 531, 538.)

“The court may, upon a motion made pursuant to Section 435, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper: [¶] (a) [s]trike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading….” (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) “The grounds for a motion to strike shall appear on the face of the challenged pleading or from any matter of which the court is required to take judicial notice.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437, subd. (a).)

 

ANALYSIS

I. Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress

Defendants demur to the fifth cause of action on the ground that the Complaint alleges only a habitability dispute and does not plead conduct sufficiently extreme and outrageous to support a claim for intentional infliction of emotional distress.

To state a claim for intentional infliction of emotional distress, a plaintiff must allege (1) extreme and outrageous conduct by the defendant with the intention of causing, or reckless disregard of the probability of causing, emotional distress; (2) the plaintiff’s suffering severe or extreme emotional distress; and (3) actual and proximate causation of the emotional distress by the defendant’s outrageous conduct. (Christensen v. Superior Court (1991) 54 Cal.3d 868, 903.) Conduct is outrageous when it is so extreme as to exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated in a civilized community. (Ibid.) Whether conduct is outrageous is usually a question of fact. (So v. Shin (2013) 212 Cal.App.4th 652, 672 (So).)

A tenant may state a claim for intentional infliction of emotional distress against a landlord. In Stoiber v. Honeychuck (1980) 101 Cal.App.3d 903, a tenant sued the owners and managers of her rented premises for damages arising from dilapidated and unsafe conditions, alleging that the defendants’ failure to correct the defective conditions was knowing, intentional, and willful. (Id. at pp. 911-913.) The Court of Appeal held that the availability of a remedy for breach of the implied warranty of habitability does not preclude a tenant from suing the landlord for intentional infliction of emotional distress where the landlord’s acts are extreme and outrageous and result in severe mental distress, and that the tenant’s allegations of a knowing and willful failure to repair were sufficient to meet the test of outrageous conduct. (Id. at pp. 921-922.)

Here, the Complaint does not rest on a conclusory characterization of Defendants’ conduct. Plaintiffs allege that they repeatedly notified Defendants of specific dangerous conditions throughout their tenancy and that Defendants knowingly refused to remediate them. Plaintiffs allege that Defendants refused to abate a cockroach infestation for years (Compl. ¶ 19); that Defendants responded to visible mold by painting over it and thereafter repeatedly refused to remediate it (Compl. ¶ 20); that Defendants ignored recurring ceiling leaks, including one so severe that emergency services were required and the living room was rendered unusable for approximately five months without alternative housing (Compl. ¶ 21); that a shower door shattered on two Plaintiffs on separate occasions after Plaintiffs had repeatedly warned Defendants that the shower door railings were unstable and unsafe (Compl. ¶ 22); that Defendants left electrical outlets exposed and unremedied (Compl. ¶ 23); and that Defendants knew the flooring was defective and unsafe yet refused to repair it (Compl. ¶ 21). Plaintiffs further allege that these conditions caused them severe emotional distress and forced them, including a minor child, to vacate the Subject Property. (Compl. ¶¶ 24, 31-34.)

These allegations describe a knowing and willful refusal to remediate dangerous conditions over a period of years, not the isolated failure to repair characteristic of an ordinary habitability dispute. As in Stoiber, Plaintiffs allege a knowing, intentional, and willful failure to correct defective and dangerous conditions resulting in severe mental distress. Whether that conduct is sufficiently outrageous is a question of fact that cannot be resolved on demurrer. (So, supra, 212 Cal.App.4th at p. 672.) Defendants’ reliance on Hughes v. Pair (2009) 46 Cal.4th 1035, in which the defendant’s isolated offensive remarks were held not outrageous as a matter of law (id. at pp. 1050-1051), does not aid them, because the Complaint alleges a sustained course of conduct rather than a discrete offensive statement.

Accordingly, the Demurrer to the fifth cause of action is overruled.

II. Violation of the Los Angeles Tenant Anti-Harassment Ordinance

Defendants demur to the seventh cause of action on three grounds: that the Los Angeles Tenant Anti-Harassment Ordinance reaches only intentional harassment undertaken to drive a tenant from the property, and that a failure to make repairs cannot constitute harassment; that Plaintiffs have not alleged conduct directed at them specifically, absent any allegation that other tenants’ complaints were treated differently; and that, because the ordinance carries criminal penalties, Plaintiffs must plead more than negligence.

The Los Angeles Tenant Anti-Harassment Ordinance defines tenant harassment as “a landlord’s knowing and willful course of conduct directed at a specific tenant or tenants that causes detriment and harm, and that serves no lawful purpose.” (L.A. Mun. Code, § 45.33.) The ordinance enumerates categories of conduct constituting harassment, including the failure to perform and timely complete necessary repairs and maintenance required by federal, state, county, or local housing, health, or safety laws. (Ibid.)

Defendants’ premise that the ordinance reaches only conduct undertaken to drive a tenant from the property is incorrect. The ordinance contains no such requirement. It enumerates a landlord’s failure to timely perform legally required repairs and maintenance as a form of harassment, requiring that the conduct be a knowing and willful course of conduct directed at a specific tenant, causing detriment and harm, and serving no lawful purpose. (L.A. Mun. Code, § 45.33.) Nor must Plaintiffs allege that Defendants treated other tenants differently. The ordinance only requires that the conduct be directed at an identified tenant, not that the tenant have been singled out from others left unaffected. Defendants are correct that the ordinance requires more than negligence, as it applies only to a knowing and willful course of conduct. However, that higher standard is met here.

The Complaint alleges that the Subject Property was subject to the ordinance and that Defendants violated it by refusing to perform requested repairs. (Compl. ¶¶ 43-44.) Read with the incorporated allegations, the Complaint alleges that Plaintiffs repeatedly notified Defendants of specific substandard conditions in their unit and repeatedly requested repairs, and that Defendants refused to remediate those conditions over the course of the tenancy. (Compl. ¶¶ 17-24.) It alleges, for example, that Defendants responded to Plaintiffs’ mold complaints by painting over the mold rather than abating it, and that Defendants refused to repair the shower door railings after Plaintiffs reported them as unstable and after the door twice shattered on occupants. (Compl. ¶¶ 20, 22.) Those allegations describe conduct directed at Plaintiffs, because the refusals were responsive to Plaintiffs’ own notices and operated on the specific conditions of Plaintiffs’ tenancy, rather than an undifferentiated neglect aimed at no identified tenant. They further describe a knowing and willful course of conduct because Defendants refused to act despite repeated notice over a period of years, and, taken as true, they support the inference that the refusals served no lawful purpose. The alleged failure to remediate conditions rendering the unit substandard under Civil Code section 1941.1 and Health and Safety Code section 17920.3 falls within the ordinance’s enumerated category of a failure to timely perform necessary repairs and maintenance required by law. (L.A. Mun. Code, § 45.33.) The allegations are sufficient to state a claim. Accordingly, the demurrer to the seventh cause of action is overruled.

 

MOTION TO STRIKE

Defendants move to strike Plaintiffs’ allegations and prayer for punitive damages (Complaint, ¶¶ 35, 41, and Prayer ¶ 3), the allegations comprising the fifth cause of action (Complaint, ¶¶ 31-34), a portion of the seventh cause of action (Complaint, ¶ 44), and the prayer for statutory damages (Prayer ¶ 4), on the grounds that the punitive damages allegations are unsupported by specific facts and that the nuisance cause of action is duplicative of the negligence cause of action.

I. Punitive Damages

A claim for punitive damages must be supported by specific factual allegations of oppression, fraud, or malice; conclusory characterizations of a defendant’s conduct are insufficient. (Civ. Code, § 3294; Smith v. Superior Court (1992) 10 Cal.App.4th 1033, 1041-1042.) To state a prima facie claim for punitive damages, a complaint must allege the elements set forth in Civil Code section 3294. (College Hospital Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 721.) “Malice” means conduct intended to cause injury to the plaintiff, or despicable conduct carried on with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others. (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (c)(1).) “Oppression” means despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person’s rights. (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (c)(2).) Something more than the commission of a tort is required; there must be circumstances of aggravation or outrage, such as spite or malice, or a conscious and deliberate disregard of the interests of others. (Taylor v. Superior Court (1979) 24 Cal.3d 890, 894-895.)

The punitive damages allegations are not subject to being stricken. A tenant’s action against the owners of residential property for failure to remedy defective living conditions may support a claim for punitive damages. (Stoiber v. Honeychuck, supra, 101 Cal.App.3d at p. 921; Rivera v. Sassoon (1995) 39 Cal.App.4th 1045, 1046.) As set forth above, the Complaint alleges that Defendants had actual, repeated notice of dangerous conditions at the Subject Property, including a shower door that had already shattered on a Plaintiff and flooring known to be defective, and that Defendants nonetheless refused to remediate those conditions over a period of years. (Compl. ¶¶ 17-24.) Read as a whole, those allegations supply the specific facts of despicable conduct carried on with a willful and conscious disregard of Plaintiffs’ safety that Civil Code section 3294 requires. The punitive damages allegations do not rest solely on the statutory labels in paragraphs 35 and 41, but on the incorporated factual allegations that Defendants knew of specific dangerous conditions and refused to remediate them over a period of years. Defendants’ authorities, which address pleadings that merely recite the statutory language without supporting facts, are therefore inapposite. Accordingly, the Motion is denied as to the punitive damages allegations.

II. Statutory Damages

Defendants move to strike the prayer for statutory damages on the same theory they advance against the punitive damages allegations, contending that the Complaint pleads only conclusory conduct and therefore fails to allege facts sufficient to support a statutory penalty. The premise is mistaken. A prayer for a statutory penalty is subject to being stricken only when the cause of action that authorizes the penalty is not adequately pleaded. The prayer for statutory damages is supported by the seventh cause of action, which authorizes a statutory penalty. (L.A. Mun. Code, § 45.35.) The penalty the ordinance authorizes turns on a violation of the ordinance, not on the malice, oppression, or fraud that Civil Code section 3294 requires for punitive damages, so Defendants’ reliance on the punitive damages standard is misplaced. For the reasons stated above, in overruling the demurrer to the seventh cause of action, the Complaint adequately alleges a violation of the ordinance, and well-pleaded facts therefore support the prayer for statutory damages. The related request to strike the portion of paragraph 44 alleging that Defendants intentionally disturbed a tenant’s peace and quiet fails for the same reason. Accordingly, the Motion is denied as to the prayer for statutory damages.

III. Nuisance

The Motion also challenges the nuisance cause of action as duplicative of the negligence cause of action. Duplicativeness is not a proper basis for a motion to strike. The Court may strike only “irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a).) The predecessor statute authorized striking redundant matter, but that authority was eliminated, and redundancy, “if it justifies any judicial intervention at all, is ordinarily dealt with most economically at trial, or on a dispositive motion such as summary judgment.” (Blickman Turkus, LP v. MF Downtown Sunnyvale, LLC (2008) 162 Cal.App.4th 858, 890; see Civ. Code, § 3537 [“Superfluity does not vitiate”].) Accordingly, the Motion is denied as to the nuisance cause of action.

Accordingly, the Motion to Strike is denied.

 

CONCLUSION

Defendants’ Demurrer to the Complaint is overruled. Defendants’ Motion to Strike is denied. Defendants have 20 days to file an answer.
