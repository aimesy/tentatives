Case Number: 22STCV11915    Hearing Date: July 30, 2026    Dept: S27

1.     Background Facts

This is a premises liability case. Plaintiff Gary Dean Swisher (“Plaintiff”) alleges that he was riding his bicycle on the sidewalk owned, controlled, or maintained by Defendant the People of the State of California when his bicycle struck an uneven, raised portion of the sidewalk, causing him to be thrown to the ground and suffer severe injuries. (FAC, Prem.L-1.)

On April 7, 2022, Plaintiff filed this action against Defendants California Department of Transportation, City of Los Angeles, Kaiser Permanente Ventures, LLC, McCarthy Building Companies, Inc., and Does 1 to 40. On April 3, 3036, Plaintiff filed a First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) to name the People of the State of California, acting by and through the Department of Transportation.

On April 21, 2026, Defendant the People of the State of California, acting by and through the Department of Transportation, erroneously sued as California Department of Transportation (“Defendant”) filed this Motion to Compel Compliance with Deposition Subpoena and Request for Order Awarding Monetary Sanctions Against Deponent in the Sum of $2,600.00 (“Motion”).

No opposition has been filed.

 

2.     Legal Standard

“If a subpoena requires the attendance of a witness or the production of books, documents, electronically stored information, or other things before a court, or at the trial of an issue therein, or at the taking of a deposition, the court, upon motion reasonably made by any person described in subdivision (b), or upon the court’s own motion after giving counsel notice and an opportunity to be heard, may make an order quashing the subpoena entirely, modifying it, or directing compliance with it upon those terms or conditions as the court shall declare, including protective orders.”¿(Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.1, subd. (a).)¿“In addition, the court may make any other order as may be appropriate to protect the person from unreasonable or oppressive demands, including unreasonable violations of the right of privacy of the person.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.1, subd. (a).)¿A party to an action may bring a motion¿pursuant to¿Code Civ. Proc.¿§ 1987.1(a).¿(Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.1, subd. (b)(1).)¿¿

“In California, discovery may be obtained from a nonparty through an oral deposition, a written deposition, or a deposition for the production of business records and things.”¿(Board of Registered Nursing v. Superior Court of Orange County¿(2021) 59 Cal.App.5th 1011, 1030.)¿“To pursue the deposition of a nonparty, a party must generally serve a deposition subpoena.”¿(Ibid.)

“A deposition subpoena may command any of the following . . . (a) [o]nly¿the attendance and the testimony of the deponent . . . (b) [o]nly¿the production of business records for copying . . . (c) [t]he attendance and the testimony of the deponent, as well as the production of business records, other documents, electronically stored information, and tangible things¿. . . .”¿(Code Civ. Proc., § 2020.020, subd. (a)-(c).)¿¿¿¿¿

“For discovery purposes, information is relevant if it might reasonably assist a party in evaluating¿the case,¿preparing¿for trial, or¿facilitating¿settlement.”¿(Gonzalez v. Superior Court (1995) 33 Cal.App.4th 1539, 1546 [italics in original].)¿Generally, all¿unprivileged information that is relevant to the subject matter of the action is discoverable if it would itself be admissible evidence at trial or if it appears¿reasonably calculated¿to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence.¿(Schnabel v. Superior Court¿(1993) 5 Cal.4th 704, 711.)

 

3.     Discussion

Defendant moves for an order compelling Dr. Majid Molaie, M.D. (“Deponent”) to appear and produce documents pursuant to a deposition subpoena served on or about February 19, 2026. Deponent was identified and designated as Plaintiff’s non-retained expert neurologist. (Rhoads Decl., ¶ 10.) Defendant’s subpoena requests (1) any and all medical and office records and reports from the first date of treatment to the present; (2) all billing records and all records of payment and/or discount regarding any billing from the first date of treatment to present; and (3) any and all radiological films, images, and documents from the first date of treatment to present. (Id. at Ex. 2, Attachment 3.)

A.    Compliance with Subpoena

Defendant contends to have electronically served Deponent with a deposition subpoena for personal appearance and production of documents and things on February 19, 2026. (Id. at ¶ 4, Ex. 1.) Defendant contends to have emailed and called Deponent’s office several times before March 20, 2026, to confirm Deponent’s appearance for the deposition. (Id. at ¶ 5.) Defendant contends that Defendant’s office never received an objection, motion for protective order, or motion to quash from Deponent or any party. (Id. at ¶ 6.) On March 20, 2026, Deponent failed to appear or produce documents. (Id. at ¶ 7.) Defendant tried again to email Deponent, but to this date Deponent has not contacted Defendant. (Id. at ¶¶ 8-9.)

The Court notes that “written notice and all moving papers supporting a motion to compel an answer to a deposition question or to compel production of a document or tangible thing from a nonparty deponent must be personally served on the nonparty deponent unless the nonparty deponent agrees to accept service by mail or electronic service at an address or electronic service address specified on the deposition record.” (Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1346.) The proof of service attached to the Motion indicates that the Motion was served on Deponent via mail and email. While  Defendant indicates that it spoke with Deponent’s receptionist and was told that Deponent would accept service by email  (Rhoads Decl., ¶ 3.), given that the default method of service is by personal service, simply having a receptionist state that email service would be accepted is not sufficient for this Court. 

As to the subpoenaed documents and things, Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.450 requires:

(1) The motion shall set forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the production for inspection of any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing described in the deposition notice.

(2) The motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040, or, when the deponent fails to attend the deposition and produce the documents, electronically stored information, or things described in the deposition notice, by a declaration stating that the petitioner has contacted the deponent to inquire about the nonappearance.

(Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subd. (b).)

            Here, Defendant has included a declaration that Defendant has contacted Deponent to inquire about the nonappearance. (See Rhoads Decl.) However, Defendant has not set forth any specific facts showing good cause justifying the production for inspection of any document or thing described in the deposition notice. Defendant has merely stated that Deponent was identified as a non-retained expert neurologist for Plaintiff. (Rhoads Decl., ¶ 10.) Defendant has not provided any facts as to why the production of the documents is justified or relevant. Accordingly, the Motion is denied as to the subpoenaed documents.

B.    Sanctions

Defendant requests the Deponent pay $500 in sanctions plus $1,500 in attorney fees incurred for bringing this motion. Given the court’s ruling this request for $500 in sanctions is denied.

Moreover, because Defendant’s Motion was not successful as to the subpoenaed documents and the Deponent has not unsuccessfully opposed the Motion, the Court will not impose sanctions in the amount of $1,500.

4.     Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, the Court DENIES the Motion to Compel Compliance as to Deponent’s appearance at a deposition. The Court DENIES the Motion to Compel Compliance as to the request to compel the requested documents.

Defendant is ordered to give notice.

Case Number: 22STCV20519    Hearing Date: July 30, 2026    Dept: S27

1.     Background Facts

Plaintiff, Jennifer Small, filed this suit against numerous defendants for damages arising out of an assault by a third party at Plaintiff’s apartment building.  The operative First Amended Complaint asserted causes of action for (1) negligence, (2) breach of contract, (3) breach of implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, and (4) breach of implied warranty of habitability.

 

The case proceeded to trial by jury, which concluded with a special verdict on 3/17/26 in favor of Plaintiff.  

 

However, on 5/21/26, the Court granted Defendants’ motions for new trial and judgment notwithstanding the verdict, and on 6/1/26, entered Judgment in favor of Defendants.

 

            On 6/26/26, Defendants filed the instant motion to strike the Memorandum of Costs that Plaintiff filed on 6/12/26.  The motion is unopposed.

           

2.     Motion to Tax Costs

a.     Relief Sought

Strike the entire Memorandum of Costs that Plaintiff filed on 6/12/16.

 

b.     Analysis

Code of Civil Procedure “Section 1032 defines prevailing party for purposes of an award of litigation costs.  It reads: ‘(a) As used in this section, unless the context clearly requires otherwise: [¶] ... [¶] 4) “Prevailing party” includes the party with a net monetary recovery, a defendant in whose favor a dismissal is entered, a defendant where neither plaintiff nor defendant obtains any relief, and a defendant as against those plaintiffs who do not recover any relief against that defendant.  When any party recovers other than monetary relief and in situations other than as specified, the ‘prevailing party’ shall be as determined by the court, and under those circumstances, the court, in its discretion, may allow costs or not and, if allowed may apportion costs between the parties on the same or adverse sides pursuant to rules adopted under Section 1034.’  (§ 1032, subd. (a)(4).)”  (Wakefield v. Bohlin (2006) 145 Cal.App.4th 963, 972–973, disapproved on other grounds in Goodman v. Lozano (2010) 47 Cal.4th 1327.)

 

A prevailing party who claims costs in a civil action must serve and file a memorandum of costs.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1700(a)(1).)  “Unless objection is made to the entire cost memorandum, [a] motion to strike or tax costs must refer to each item objected to by the same number and appear in the same order as the corresponding cost item claimed on the memorandum of costs and must state why the item is objectionable.”  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1700(a)(2).)

 

Here, Defendants move to strike Plaintiff’s entire Memorandum of Costs, arguing that Plaintiff is no longer the prevailing party because the Court vacated the jury verdict and entered Judgment in Defendants’ favor.  The Court agrees, and Plaintiff has not filed an opposition arguing otherwise.

           

            Therefore, the motion to strike the Memorandum of Costs filed on 6/12/26 is GRANTED.

 

Defendants are ordered to give notice.

Case Number: 24LBCV00078    Hearing Date: July 30, 2026    Dept: S27

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff, Daniel Diamantides -Abel filed this action against Defendants, City of Long Beach, County of Los Angeles, and State of California for damages arising out of a fall that occurred when Plaintiff was riding in the bike lane and went over a damaged road, causing him to fly and hit his face on the concrete.

Plaintiff filed the complaint on 1/16/24. Plaintiff dismissed the County on 2/28/24. Plaintiff dismissed the State on 3/22/24.

On 10/01/24, the City filed a Notice of Permanent Channeling Injunction and Stay of Tort Litigation. The City indicated the lawsuit against it was barred by Bird Global Inc. and its related entities’ filing of bankruptcy proceedings; the City indicated the lawsuit was a barred claim raised by a barred person against one of the insurance settlement released parties per the confirmation order, which includes all municipalities that are being sued for damages arising out of the use, etc., of the debtors’ bird scooters.

On 5/14/25, the Court held a status conference re: bankruptcy. The Court, finding no party to this action is a party to any bankruptcy proceeding, lifted the stay.

On May 5, 2026, the court granted the motion of Plaintiff’s counsel be relieved. The court indicated it would dismiss the entire case if Plaintiff either failed to retain new counsel, or failed to appear for the next hearing (e.g. Case Management Conference continued from June 12, 2026 to July 30, 2026).

 

DISCUSSION

Applicable Law

“An action may be dismissed in any of the following instances: (1) With or without prejudice, upon written request of the plaintiff to the clerk, filed with papers in the case, or by oral or written request to the court at any time before the actual commencement of trial, upon payment of the costs, if any. (Code Civ. Proc., § 581, subd. (b)(1).)

 

Analysis

City of Long Beach established its inclusion in the Chapter 11 Bankruptcy Court Second Amended Joint Chapter 11 Plaint of Liquidation. The plan specifically includes all municipalities where Bird scooter products were provided to riders/users. The Bankruptcy Court Liquidation Plan creates a system whereby any claims arising from use of a Bird scooter against a municipality will require submission of a claim to an administrator (e.g. the “Channeling Injunction.”) Plaintiff admitted to utilizing a Bird scooter at the time of the incident. [Declaration of Michael Swift.]

The unchallenged motion relies on a finding that all claims involving Bird scooters renders the subject action moot, due to said “channeling injunction,” without actually addressing the substantive underlying complaint for premises liability and negligence based on that portion of the damaged road at the time. In fact, the Bird scooter manufacturing company has never been a party to this action.

Regardless, at the time of the May 5, 2026, order allowing counsel to be relieved as Plaintiff’s attorney, the court instructed Plaintiff to appear and/or find new counsel. Given the lack of any opposition by Plaintiff the court finds in its discretion that Plaintiff is not prosecuting this matter. 

CONCLUSION

The unopposed motion for dismiss is GRANTED due to the failure of Plaintiff to continue prosecuting the action.

The entire action is dismissed.

Case Number: 24LBCV01082    Hearing Date: July 30, 2026    Dept: S27

1.     Background

This is a lemon law case. On May 22, 2024, Plaintiffs Darius Pazdel and Jessarae M. Pazdel (“Plaintiffs”) filed this action against Defendant American Honda Motor Company, Inc. (“Defendant”). On or about March 28, 2023, Plaintiffs entered into a warranty contract with Defendant regarding a 2022 Honda Accord (“Vehicle”). (Compl., ¶ 6.) Defects and nonconformities to warranty manifested themselves and the value of the Vehicle was worthless and/or de minims. (Id. at ¶¶ 11-13.)

On March 20, 2026, Plaintiffs filed this Motion for Attorneys’ Fees, Costs, and Expenses (“Motion”).

On July 17, 2026, Defendant filed an opposition.

On July 23, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a reply with objections.

 

2.     Requests for Judicial Notice

Plaintiffs submit 33 requests for judicial notice of orders, minute orders, and tentative rulings of attorneys' fees from other California cases. However, Plaintiffs do not attach the relevant documents anywhere to the Motion to furnish the court with sufficient information to enable it to take judicial notice of the matter. Accordingly, the Court denies the requests for judicial notice. (Evid. Code, § 453.)

 

3.     Objections

Plaintiffs submit 2 objections to the Declaration of Travis Manfredi in support of Defendant’s opposition. The Court overrules both objections.

 

4.     Motion for Attorneys’ Fees

a.     Parties’ Positions

Plaintiffs seek to recover attorneys’ fees incurred in this litigation in the total amount of $77,279.30, consisting of $48,410.00 in attorney fees; (2) 1.35 multiplier enhancement of $16,943.50; (3) $7,925.80 in costs and expenses; and (4) $4,000 for Plaintiffs’ counsel to review Defendant’s opposition, draft the reply, and attend the motion.

In opposition, Defendant argues this case does not warrant an upward multiplier, that the fee should be reduced to account for inefficiency of staffing twenty-nine different individuals for this case, and because Plaintiffs overestimated the time required for future tasks.

 

b.     Entitlement to Fees

Defendant does not oppose the motion as to the issue of entitlement of fees. It is undisputed, then, that Plaintiffs are the prevailing party and, as such, are entitled to an award of attorneys’ fees pursuant to Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d) and the parties’ settlement.

 

c.     Reasonableness of Fees

The Court turns to the issue of reasonableness of the fees sought. “[T]rial courts have broad discretion in determining the amount of a reasonable attorney's fee award. This determination is necessarily ad hoc and must be resolved on the particular circumstances of each case.” (Meister v. Regents of University of California (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 437, 452.) “Trial judges are entrusted with this discretionary determination because they are in the best position to assess the value of the professional services rendered in their courts.” (Christian Research Institute v. Alnor (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 1315, 1321.)

“[O]nce a party has established he or she is entitled to fees, the lodestar method is generally presumed to be the starting point in analyzing the appropriate amount of attorney fees. Under this method, a court first calculates the number of hours reasonably spent multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate for each billing professional, and then may adjust the amount based on various relevant factors to ensure the fee reflects the fair market value [of the attorney services] for the particular action. (K.I. v. Wagner (2014) 225 Cal.App.4th 1412, 1425 [quotations and citations omitted].)

i.              Reasonableness of Hourly Rates

“In determining hourly rates, the court must look to the prevailing market rates in the relevant community. The rates of comparable attorneys in the forum district are usually used. In making its calculation, the court should also consider the experience, skill, and reputation of the attorney requesting fees. The court may rely on its own knowledge and familiarity with the legal market in setting a reasonable hourly rate.” (Heritage Pacific Financial, LLC v. Monroy (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 972, 1009 [internal quotations and citations omitted].)

Plaintiffs contend that their hourly rates range from $195.00 for law clerks to $695.00 for senior partners. Defendant does not oppose these hourly rates. Accordingly, the Court will not reduce the hourly rates.

 

ii.             Reasonableness of Time Incurred

“[T]he verified time statements of the attorneys, as officers of the court, are entitled to credence in the absence of a clear indication the records are erroneous.” (Horsford v. Board of Trustees of California State University (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 359, 396.) “In challenging attorney fees as excessive because too many hours of work are claimed, it is the burden of the challenging party to point to the specific items challenged, with a sufficient argument and citations to the evidence. General arguments that fees claimed are excessive, duplicative, or unrelated do not suffice.” (Premier Medical Management Systems, Inc. v. California Ins. Guarantee Assn. (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 550, 564.)

That said, “[a] fee request that appears unreasonably inflated is a special circumstance permitting the trial court to reduce the award or deny one altogether.” (Serrano v. Unruh (1982) 36 Cal.3d 621, 635). The court may “make across-the-board percentage cuts either in the number of hours claimed or in the final lodestar figure” in consumer law cases “when a voluminous fee application is made.” (Warren v. Kia Motors America, Inc. (2018) 30 Cal.App.5th 24, 41 [quotations and citation omitted].)

Plaintiffs contend to have spent 109.6 hours prosecuting this action. (Chandy Decl., Ex. 1.) Plaintiffs argue that counsel was efficient and did not result in excessive or duplicative billing.

In opposition, Defendant argues that use of an excessive number of attorneys to staff a case leads to inherent inefficiency and justifies a reduction. Defendant argues that the 29 different individuals billing on a straightforward lemon law case is nearly three times as many as the Court of Appeals has found to be highly inefficient. Defendant argues that staffing eight different people to propound and respond to discovery, having partners do the work of associates, and having twenty-two individuals do 40% of the work was inefficient. (See Chandy Decl., Ex. 1.)  Defendant requests the Court to reduce the lodestar by 20% or $9,682. Additionally, Defendant contends that the Plaintiffs overestimated the amount of time required to respond to this motion.

Plaintiffs contend to have staffed 29 individuals for this matter. (Chandy, Decl., Section D.) To justify the reasonableness and efficiency of this group, Plaintiff provides non-binding federal law cases. The Court is not persuaded that Plaintiffs’ 29-person staff was efficient.

Defendant has provided authority and examples of how Plaintiffs’ 29-person staff proved to be inefficient. In Mikhaeilpoor v. BMW of North America, LLC (2020) 48 Cal.App.5th 240, the court found that staffing 10 attorneys on the lemon law case was “astonishing” and supported the trial court’s finding that plaintiff’s counsel failed to act efficiently. As the trial court was in the best position to evaluate the professional services rendered before it, it was not an abuse of discretion to reduce the request for attorneys’ fees. (Mikhaeilpoor v. BMW of North America, LLC (2020) 48 Cal.App.5th 240, 256.)

Here, given the billing entries and the evidence of inefficiency demonstrated by Defendant, the Court will reduce the amount of attorneys’ fees by 20% to account for the inefficiency of staffing 29 different individuals on a simple lemon law case. Thus, the attorneys’ fees are reduced by $9,682.

Next, the Court finds that the $4,000 requested as an estimation of time to be spent responding to and attending the hearing for this Motion is unreasonable. Nowhere in the Motion or declarations do Plaintiffs justify the amount requested by explaining which of the 29 individuals’ billing rates will be applied or how many hours they intend to spend on this Motion. Accordingly, the Court will not award the $4,000 requested.

Accordingly, attorneys’ fees are reduced by $13,683.

 

d.     Multiplier

While the lodestar reflects the basic fee for comparable legal services in the community, it may be adjusted based on various factors, including “(1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, and the skill displayed in presenting them; (2) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys; (3) the contingent nature of the fee award” and (4) the success achieved. (Serrano v. Priest (1977) 20 Cal.3d 25, 49.)

Nonetheless, the court must not consider extraordinary skill and the other Serrano factors to the extent these are already included with the lodestar. (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1138-1139.) “[A] trial court should award a multiplier for exceptional representation only when the quality of representation far exceeds the quality of representation that would have been provided by an attorney of comparable skill and experience billing at the hourly rate used in the lodestar calculation. Otherwise, the fee award will result in unfair double counting and be unreasonable.” (Id. at 1139.)

Plaintiffs request a lodestar 1.35 multiplier enhancement of $16,943.50. The Court finds that under the circumstances of this case, a lodestar multiplier is not appropriate. Nothing before the Court indicates that the case presented novel issues or that the quality of representation far exceeded the quality of representation that would have been provided by attorneys of comparable skill and experience billing at the same rates. Further, in terms of the contingency basis, the Court finds that such consideration and risk is already included within the lodestar amount.

Accordingly, the Court declines to award a lodestar multiplier.

 

e.     Costs

Allowable costs “shall be reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation rather than merely convenient or beneficial to its preparation.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (c)(2).) Any items not specifically mentioned by statute “may be allowed or denied in the court's discretion.” (Id., subd. (c)(4).) The Beverly-Song Act allows a successful plaintiff to recover both “costs” and “expenses.” (See Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).)

“If the items appear to be proper charges the verified memorandum is prima facie evidence that the costs, expenses and services therein listed were necessarily incurred by the defendant [citations], and the burden of showing that an item is not properly chargeable or is unreasonable is upon the [objecting party].' [Citation.]” (Nelson v. Anderson (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 111, 131.)

Plaintiff requests a total of $7,925.80 in costs. The cost items appear to be proper, and there was no opposition challenging any particular costs. Therefore, the Court awards Plaintiff the requested amount without any reductions.

 

5.     Conclusion

The Court reduces the attorneys’ fees by $13,683 and does not apply the multiplier for $16,943.50. The Court awards $7,925.80 in costs and expenses.

 Accordingly, the Court GRANTS the Motion for Attorneys’ Fees, Costs, and Expenses in the reduced amount of $46,652.80.

Plaintiffs are ordered to give notice.

Case Number: 25LBCV00347    Hearing Date: July 30, 2026    Dept: S27

1.     Background Facts

This is a negligence action arising out of a slip and fall incident that occurred on or about September 2, 2023, wherein Plaintiff Maria Franco (“Plaintiff”) sustained injuries and damages. On February 11, 2025, Plaintiff filed the operative Complaint against Defendants 7-Eleven Store #20679; 7-Eleven, Inc.; Asha Investments, Inc. d/b/a 7-Eleven; and DOES 1 through 100 (collectively, “Defendants”), asserting a sole cause of action for Premises Liability – Negligence.

On March 17, 2025, Defendant 7-Eleven, Inc. filed its Answer to the Complaint and Cross-Complaint against ROES 1 through 100, asserting causes of action for: (1) Equitable Indemnity and (2) Declaratory Relief.

On June 2, 2025, default was entered against Defendant Asha Investments, Inc. d/b/a 7-Eleven. On July 30, 2025, default was entered against Defendant 7-Eleven Store #20679.

On February 11, 2025, Plaintiff filed the instant Request for Default Judgment against Defendants Asha Investments, Inc. d/b/a 7-Eleven and Defendant 7-Eleven Store #20679 (collectively, “Defaulting Defendants”).

2.     Legal Standard

California Code of Civil Procedure Section 585 permits entry of a default judgment after a party has failed to timely respond or appear. (Code Civ. Proc., § 585.) “A party seeking a default judgment on declarations must use mandatory Request for Entry of Default (Application to Enter Default) (form CIV-100), unless the action is subject to the Fair Debt Buying Practices Act, Civil Code section 1788.50 et seq., in which case the party must use mandatory Request for Entry of Default (Fair Debt Buying Practices Act) (form CIV-105).” (Cal. Rules of Ct., rule 3.1800, subd. (a).) The default judgment packet must include: 1) a brief summary of the case; (2) declarations or other admissible evidence in support of the judgment requested; (3) interest computations as necessary; (4) a memorandum of costs and disbursements; (5) declaration of nonmilitary status; (6) a proposed form of judgment; (7) a dismissal of all parties against whom judgment is not sought or an application for separate judgment under CCP § 579, supported by a showing of grounds for each judgment; (8) exhibits as necessary; and (9) a request for attorney’s fees if allowed by statute or by the agreement of the parties. (Id.)

“The relief granted to the plaintiff, if there is no answer, cannot exceed that demanded in the complaint, in the statement required by Section 425.11, or in the statement provided for by Section 425.115….”(Code Civ. Proc., § 580, subd. (a).) “Section 580 is strictly construed ‘in accordance with its plain language’….” (Airs Aromatics, LLC v. CBL Data Recovery Technologies, Inc. (2018) 23 Cal.App.5th 1013, 1018.) Therefore, “[w]hen a trial court, in a default judgment, has entered a judgment for a type of relief not requested in the complaint the judgment is void.” (Finney v. Gomez (2003) 111 Cal.App.4th 527, 550.)

 

 

3.     Discussion

Plaintiff seems to move for default judgment against Defaulting Defendants in the total amount of $180,000.00 consisting of: (1) $90,514.93 in special damages and (2) $109,485.07 in general damages less $20,000.00 in credits acknowledged. (See CIV-100, Item 2(a)-(b).) However, the following defects preclude granting default judgment at this time:

·         Item 2(a) of the CIV-100 form indicates that the demand of the complaint was $200,000.00, however, the complaint did not specify a demand amount;

·         There is no proposed judgment form, ideally on JUD-100 form, filed with the default judgment packet per California Rules of Court, rule 3.1800, subd. (a)(6); and

·         Plaintiff’s counsel states that the Statement of Damages (“SOD”) attached as Exhibit F to the declaration in support of default judgment was served on Defaulting Defendants but the proof of service accompanying the SOD is blank. (Saboorian Decl., ¶11, Ex. F.) Furthermore, no proof of service regarding the SOD is filed separately with the Court. As such, the default judgment packet fails to demonstrate when and how the SOD was served on Defaulting Defendants in compliance with Code of Civil Procedure Section 425.11, subdivision (c).

4.     Conclusion

Case Number: 25LBCV01845    Hearing Date: July 30, 2026    Dept: S27

1.     Background Facts

This is a lemon law case. On or about March 15, 2019, Plaintiffs Brett Roman and Evelyn Roman (“Plaintiffs”) entered into a warranty contract with Defendant FCA US, LLC (“FCA”) regarding a 2019 Jeep Grand Cherokee (“Vehicle”) which was manufactured and/or distributed by FCA. (Compl., ¶ 7.) Defects and nonconformities to warranty manifested themselves, including engine defects, transmission defects, and electrical defects. (Id. at ¶ 12.) Plaintiffs allege that FCA knew prior to Plaintiffs’ purchase of the Vehicle that the 2019 Jeep Grand Cherokee vehicles equipped with the 3.6L engine had defects. (Id. at ¶ 16.)

On June 25, 2025, Plaintiffs filed a Complaint against Defendants FCA, Glenn E. Thomas Dodge Chrysler Jeep Ram, and Does 1 through 10 alleging (1) Violation of Subdivision (D) of Civil Code Section 1793.2; (2) Violation of Subdivision (B) of Civil Code Section 1793.2; (3) Violation of Subdivision (A)(3) of Civil Code Section 1793.2; (4) Breach of the Implied Warranty of Merchantability; (5) Negligent Repair; and (6) Fraudulent Inducement Concealment.

On April 28, 2026, Plaintiffs filed this Motion to Compel Initial Disclosures Pursuant to California Code of Civil Procedure § 871.26 and Request for Monetary Sanctions (“Motion”).

On July 17, 2026, FCA filed an opposition.

No reply has been filed.

 

2.     Legal Standard

“Within 60 days after the filing of the answer or other responsive pleading, all parties shall, without awaiting a discovery request, provide to all other parties an initial disclosure and documents pursuant to subdivisions (f), (g), and (h).” (Civ. Proc. Code, § 871.26, subd. (b).) “The defendant or manufacturer shall provide the following documents to all other parties pursuant to the timelines prescribed in subdivision (b): … (6) Motor vehicle information reports, including build documentation, component information, and delivery details. (7) Entire warranty transaction history for the motor vehicle. (8) Listing of required field actions applicable to the motor vehicle. (9) Published technical service bulletins (”TSBs”)1 for the same make, model, and year reasonably related to the nonconformities pertaining to the motor vehicle. (10) Published information service bulletins (“ISBs”) for the same make, model, and year reasonably related to the nonconformities pertaining to the motor vehicle… (12) Warranty policies and procedure manuals. (13) Service manuals reasonably related to the nonconformities pertaining to the motor vehicle….” (Id. at § 871.26, subd. (h).)

“Unless the party failing to comply with this section shows good cause, notwithstanding any other law and in addition to any other sanctions imposed pursuant to this chapter, a court shall impose sanctions as follows:

(1) A one-thousand-five-hundred-dollar ($1,500) sanction against the plaintiff's attorney or two-thousand-five-hundred-dollar ($2,500) sanction against the defense attorney respectively, paid within 15 business days for failure to comply with the document production requirements as prescribed in subdivision (b).

(2) A one-thousand-five-hundred-dollar ($1,500) sanction against the plaintiff's attorney or two-thousand-five-hundred-dollar ($2,500) sanction against the defense attorney respectively, paid within 15 business days for failure to comply with the provisions relating to depositions as prescribed in subdivision (c).”

(Id. at § 871.26, subd. (j).)

 

3.     Discussion

Plaintiffs move for an order compelling FCA to produce all documents required under section 871.26(h)(6)-(10), and (12)-(13) and an order imposing mandatory sanctions against FCA and its counsel for $2,500.

Plaintiffs contend that FCA has failed to produce (1) the Warranty Policies and Procedure Manual (WPPM); (2) Pre-Delivery Inspection (PDI) records showing what defects FCA knew about the vehicle’s condition at the time of sale; (3) the entire warranty transaction history with missing diagnostic trouble code (DTC) results and software version information; (4) field action and recall documentation; (5) applicable technical service bulletins (TSBs) and information service bulletins (ISBs); and (6) service manuals for systems the dealer diagnosed and repaired.

In opposition, FCA argues that FCA complied with its section 871.26 obligations by serving Initial Document Production on the statutory deadline of December 5, 2025. FCA argues that Plaintiff seeks an order compelling broad categories of documents beyond the statute’s limited initial-disclosure framework. To the extent that Plaintiffs believe additional documents exists outside the scope of section 871.26, Plaintiffs may serve discovery requests.

Plaintiffs do not contend that the FCA failed to serve initial disclosure production in its entirety. (Rucker Decl., ¶ 10.) Plaintiffs merely contend that the FCA’s production is incomplete. (Id. at ¶ 11.) FCA served Plaintiffs with Initial Production of Documents on December 5, 2025, consisting of over 1,000 pages of materials including repair records, warranty claim records, sales brochures, sales documents, vehicle information detail report, glove box documents, the Monroney label, Technical Service Bulletins (TSBs), and recalls applicable to the Vehicle. (Wang Decl., ¶ 8.) On April 13, 2026, FCA produced more documents in an abundance of caution and provided a written Initial Disclosure Pleading. (Id. at ¶ 10.) Plaintiffs admit to having received the document production. (Rucker Decl., ¶ 10.) Thus, the Court finds that FCA complied with its initial disclosure obligations. To the extent Plaintiffs seek further or additional information, Plaintiffs should do so through other discovery methods, such as interrogatories or requests for production. Thus, the Motion is denied.

Since the Court finds FCA to have complied with section 871.26 by serving the required documents in their initial disclosures, the Court also denies Plaintiffs’ request for sanctions.

 

4.     Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, the Court DENIES Plaintiffs’ Motion to Compel Initial Disclosures Pursuant to California Code of Civil Procedure § 871.26 and Request for Monetary Sanctions.

Moving Plaintiffs are ordered to give notice.
