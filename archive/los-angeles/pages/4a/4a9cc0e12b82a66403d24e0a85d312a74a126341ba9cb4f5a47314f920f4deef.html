DEFENDANT CUSTOM FOODS LLC’S MOTION TO STRIKE PORTIONS OF PLAINTIFF’S COMPLAINT

 

Background

Plaintiff Margarita Lazcano (“Plaintiff”) filed this action on August 15, 2023 against Defendant Custom Foods LLC (“Defendant”). On February 18, 2025, Plaintiff filed the operative third amended complaint (“TAC”), alleging causes of action for (1) age discrimination in violation of FEHA, (2) wrongful termination of employment in violation of public policy, and (3) failure to prevent discrimination, harassment, and retaliation in violation of FEHA.

On December 6, 2023, the Court found “that the following cases, 23STCV26344 and 23STCV19406, are related within the meaning of California Rules of Court, rule 3.300(a). 23STCV19406 is the lead case.” (Minute Order 12/6/23.)

Defendant moves to strike portions of Plaintiff’s complaint relating to punitive damages. Plaintiff opposes. Defendant did not reply.

///

///

Legal Standard

A court may “[s]trikeout any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading” or “[s]trike out all or any part of any pleading “not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court.¿” (¿¿Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subds. (a)-(b)¿¿.) “¿The grounds for a motion to strike shall appear on the face of the challenged pleading or from any matter of which the court is required to take judicial notice.¿” (¿¿Code Civ. Proc., § 437¿¿.)

“Where the defect raised by a motion to strike or by demurrer is reasonably capable of cure, leave to amend is routinely and liberally granted to give the plaintiff a chance to cure the defect in question. [Citations.] A pleading may be stricken only upon terms the court deems proper [citation], that is, terms that are just. [Citations.] It is generally an abuse of discretion to deny leave to amend, because the drastic step of denial of the opportunity to correct the curable defect effectively terminates the pleader’s action.” ((Velez v. Smith (2006) 142 Cal.App.4th 1154, 1174-1175 [internal quotations omitted].)

Request for Judicial Notice

The Court grants Plaintiff’s request for judicial notice of “RJN” 1, 2, 3, and 4.

Discussion

Defendant moves to strike multiple portions of Plaintiff’s TAC that relate to punitive damages.

A.    Timeliness

As a preliminary matter, Defendant contends that the instant motion is timely, whereas Plaintiff disagrees. Plaintiff asserts that “Defendant failed to file a motion to strike Plaintiff’s claim for punitive damages, which were alleged in her initial Complaint, First Amended Complaint, and Second Amended Complaint. None of Plaintiff’s prior pleadings contained new or different causes of action from the prior complaints and only clarified the legal theory upon which Plaintiff is entitled to recovery.” (Opp., 3:25-4:2.)

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 435, subdivision (b)(1), “[a]ny party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading may serve and file a notice of motion to strike the whole or any part thereof.” The timeframe to respond to a pleading is 30 days from when the pleading is served. ((See Id., § 412.20, subd. (a)(3).)

Both parties cite to Code of Civil Procedure section 435.5, subdivision (b), which states that “[a] party moving to strike a pleading that has been amended after a motion to strike an earlier version of the pleading was granted shall not move to strike any portion of the pleadings on grounds that could have been raised by a motion to strike as to the earlier version of the pleading.” However, this specific code section refers to pleadings that were amended after a motion to strike was previously granted. Here, the Court does not see how this is applicable, given that this is the first motion to strike filed by Defendant. Additionally, Defendant filed the instant motion to strike on March 7, 2025, and Plaintiff filed her TAC on February 18, 2025. Accordingly, Defendant filed the motion to strike fewer than thirty days after the operative pleading was filed and, thus, is timely.

B.     Punitive Damages

Defendant moves to strike on the basis that “Plaintiff’s allegations of malice and oppression do not rise to the level of culpability required by Civil Code Section 3294.” (Mot., 1:4-5.) Defendant further contends that “Plaintiff dedicated a portion of the TAC to the recovery of punitive damages and seeks punitive damages against Defendant on the second and third causes of action. However, Plaintiff’s punitive damage allegations do not conform to California law.” (Mot., 2:14-16.)

Punitive damages may be recovered upon a proper showing of malice, fraud, or oppression. ((Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (a).) A motion to strike may lie where the facts alleged do not rise to¿the level of “malice, oppression¿or fraud” required to support a punitive damages award. (¿¿Turman v. Turning Point of Central California, Inc. (2010) 191 Cal.App.4th 53, 63-64¿¿.) “¿‘Malice’ means conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.¿” (¿¿Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (c)(1)¿¿.) “¿‘Oppression’ means despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person’s rights.¿” (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (c)(2)¿.) “¿‘Fraud’ means an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known to the defendant with the intention on the part of the defendant of thereby depriving a person of property or legal rights or otherwise causing injury.¿” (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (c)(3)¿.)

Conclusory allegations, devoid of any factual assertions, are insufficient to support a conclusion that parties acted with oppression, fraud, or malice. ((Smith v. Superior Court (1992) 10 Cal.App.4th 1033, 1042.) To justify punitive damages on this basis, a “plaintiff must establish that the defendant was aware of the probable dangerous consequences of [their] conduct, and that [they] willfully and deliberately failed to avoid those consequences.” (Id., at pp. 895-896.) California law follows a heightened pleading standard for punitive damages—vague and conclusory allegations are insufficient. ((G.D. Searle & Co. v. Superior Court (1975) 49 Cal.App.3d 22, 29.) The trial court shall strike an allegation of punitive damages on motion unless the plaintiff pleaded ultimate supporting facts. ((Clauson v. Superior Court (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1253, 1255.)

Civil Code section 3294, subdivision (b) states that “[a]n employer shall not be liable for damages pursuant to subdivision (a), based upon acts of an employee of the employer, unless the employer had advance knowledge of the unfitness of the employee and employed him or her with a conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others or authorized or ratified the wrongful conduct for which the damages are awarded or was personally guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice. With respect to a corporate employer, the advance knowledge and conscious disregard, authorization, ratification or act of oppression, fraud, or malice must be on the part of an officer, director, or managing agent of the corporation.” “‘Managing agents’ are employees who ‘exercise[ ] substantial discretionary authority over decisions that ultimately determine corporate policy.’ . . . ‘[T]o demonstrate that an employee is a true managing agent under [Civil Code] section 3294, subdivision (b), a plaintiff seeking punitive damages would have to show that the employee exercised substantial discretionary authority over significant aspects of a corporation’s business.’” (Davis v. Kiewit Pacific Co. (2013) 220 Cal.App.4th 358, 366 [emphasis in original].)

Defendant contends that “Plaintiff’s blanket allegations do not meet the high threshold pleading burden for punitive damages because they lack specific facts.” (Mot., 9:16-17.) However, Plaintiff asserts that, inter alia, she “has properly alleged a claim for punitive damages. Plaintiff alleges that Defendant terminated her employment on the basis of their age. (Third Amended Complaint (‘TAC’) ¶¶ 11-14.) Plaintiff further alleged Defendant not only terminated her but also terminated several employees over the age of forty (40) and hired younger employees to replace them. (TAC ¶¶ 13-14.) Plaintiff further alleged Defendant did so with the intent to cause injury to them or with reckless disregard of their rights, and that Defendant’s conduct was despicable and committed with willful and conscious disregard of Plaintiff’s rights. (TAC ¶ 18.) Moreover, Plaintiff alleged Defendant engaged in a discriminatory employment practice/selection policy which disproportionately affected employees over the age of forty (40). (TAC ¶ 28.) This employment practice/selection policy was done with a conscious disregard for Plaintiff’s rights to be free from discrimination in the workplace.” (Opp., 5:16-27.)

As Plaintiff asserted above, her TAC alleges the elements of punitive damages and meets the pleading standard. Plaintiff demonstrates malice and oppression when she “alleges that Defendants had an employment practice/selection policy that wrongfully discriminated against her on the basis of her age in violation of the FEHA. Specifically, Plaintiff alleges that Defendants, her former employer, established a selection policy that targeted employees over the age of forty (40) and selected them for termination. This resulted [in] Defendant’s termination of Plaintiff and several of her co-workers who were over the age of forty (40) on April 14, 2023.” (TAC, ¶ 28.) Pleading that Defendant has a policy that wrongfully terminates employees and discriminates against a protected class demonstrates malice and oppression, since the policy disregards Plaintiff’s rights, resulting in injury and hardship. Additionally, Plaintiff pleads intent because not only does she plead that the policy is intentional, but Plaintiff also pleads that “[d]uring the course of Plaintiff’s employment, Defendants failed to prevent their employees from engaging in intentional actions that resulted in Plaintiff’s being treated less favorably because of Plaintiff’s protected status (i.e., her age).” (TAC, ¶ 38.) Accordingly, Plaintiff pleads malice and oppression.

Defendant also noted in its motion that “Plaintiff cannot recover punitive damages absent factual allegations supporting the conclusion that Defendant, through its officers, directors or managing agents, acted with oppression, fraud or malice” (Mot., 2:23-25), and, here, “Plaintiff has not pled facts showing[] advance knowledge and conscious disregard, authorization, ratification or act of oppression, fraud or malice by an officer, director, or managing agent of the Custom Foods.” (Mot., 1:5-7.)

Plaintiff does not address this argument in her opposition. However, the Court notes that Plaintiff pleads authorization on the part of Defendant’s officers. Plaintiff’s TAC alleges that “[a]ll defendants compelled, coerced, aided, and/or abetted the discrimination, retaliation, and harassment alleged in this Complaint, … [and] one or more of the defendants was the agent or employee, and/or acted under the control or supervision, of one or more of the remaining defendants and, in committing the acts alleged, acted within the course and scope of such agency and employment and/or is or are otherwise liable for plaintiff’s damages.” (TAC, ¶ 4.) Even more pertinent, Plaintiff pleads that “Defendant Custom Foods’ CEO Randy Newbold (‘Randy’) and Defendant Custom Foods’ Human Resources Representative, Stephanie Cortez (‘Stephanie’) called a meeting with Plaintiff and six (6) of Plaintiff’s coworkers at Defendant Custom Foods’ warehouse.” (TAC, ¶ 11.) Plaintiff pleads that “[d]uring the meeting, Randy and Stephanie informed Plaintiff and her six (6) coworkers that they were being terminated, effective immediately. When Plaintiff asked Stephanie to confirm the reason for her termination, Stephanie stated that Defendant Custom Foods did not have enough work for Plaintiff and her six (6) coworkers.” (TAC, ¶ 12.) Plaintiff meets the pleading standard for pleading punitive damages against a corporate defendant because she specifically states that Randy Newbold, Defendant’s Chief Executive Officer, terminated her. Plaintiff’s allegations regarding Defendant’s CEO presence at the meeting and assistance in firing her are sufficient to demonstrate that an officer authorized her termination under the pretext of insufficient work. Accordingly, Plaintiff satisfies the required elements to plead punitive damages against a corporate defendant.

Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, Defendant’s motion to strike punitive damages is DENIED.

Defendant is ordered to give notice of this Order.

PLAINTIFF JACK CHENG’S MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES FROM YI & YI CO. LTD TO REQUEST FOR PRODUCTION OF DOCUMENTS AND REQUEST FOR MONETARY SANCTIONS

 

Background   

Plaintiff Jack Cheng, an individual, doing business as Law Offices of Cheng & Associates, filed this action on April 1, 2025, against Defendants Chunchao Zhou (“Zhou”) and Yi and Yi Co. Ltd. (“Yi & Yi”) (jointly, “Defendants”). The complaint alleges twelve causes of action for (1) defamation per se; (2) libel; (3) libel per se; (4) civil harassment – CCP § 527.6; (5) account stated; (6) violation of Civil Code § 1719 – dishonored check; (7) fraud – tendered and cancelled check; (8) rescission; (9) intentional infliction of emotional distress; (10) injunctive relief; (11) trade libel; and (12) invasion of privacy.

Plaintiff Jack Cheng (“Plaintiff”) now moves for an order compelling Yi & Yi to provide further verified responses, without objection, and to produce all documents responsive to Plaintiff’s request for production of documents, Nos. 1, 4, 6-22 and 31. Plaintiff also moves for sanctions in the amount of $5,460.00. Zhou filed an opposition on behalf of Yi & Yi. Plaintiff replied.

Legal Standard

Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.310, subdivision (a) provides that “[o]n receipt of a response to a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling, the demanding party may move for an order compelling further response to the demand if the demanding party deems that any of the following apply: (1)¿A statement¿of compliance with the demand is incomplete. (2)¿A representation of inability to comply is inadequate, incomplete, or evasive. (3)¿An objection in the response is without merit or too general.” A motion under subdivision (a) shall “set forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought by the demand,” and “shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration…” (¿Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (b)¿.)

“To establish good cause, a discovery proponent must identify a disputed fact that is of consequence in the action and explain how the discovery sought will tend in reason to prove or disprove that fact or lead to other evidence that will tend to prove or disprove the fact.”¿(Digital Music News LLC v. Superior Court (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 216, 224 (disapproved on other grounds by Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531).)¿If a timely motion to compel is filed, the burden is on the responding party to justify any objection or failure to fully answer. (Coy v. Superior Court of Contra Costa County (1962) 58 Cal.2d 210, 220-221 [when discussing a motion to compel interrogatories, the court stated that the moving party “had the burden of showing facts from which the trial court might find that the interrogatories were interposed for improper purposes.”].)

“The court may impose a monetary sanction ordering that one engaging in the misuse of the discovery process, or any attorney advising that conduct, or both pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney’s fees, incurred by anyone as a result of that conduct.” ((Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030, subd. (a).) Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.310 subdivision (h) states that “the court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel further response to a demand, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.”

Discussion

A.    Procedural Issues

As a preliminary matter, the Court notes that Zhou filed an opposition to the instant motion, even though the motion is against Yi & Yi. However, this is improper. “A corporation has the capacity to bring a lawsuit because it has all the powers of a natural person in carrying out its business. [Citation.] However, under a long-standing common law rule of procedure, a corporation, unlike a natural person, cannot represent itself before courts of record in propria persona, nor can it represent itself through a corporate officer, director or other employee who is not an attorney. It must be represented by licensed counsel in proceedings before courts of record.” ((CLD Construction, Inc. v. City of San Ramon (2004) 120 Cal.App.4th 1141, 1145.)

Plaintiff discusses this in his reply papers, asserting that “[t]here appears to be a problem with Zhou’s acceptance that he and Yi & Yi are legally different (which in part is the basis for the alter ego claims in this case). This is not the first time he has filed papers or served discovery responses in his own name when a response was due from Yi & Yi. Once could be a misunderstanding. But the repetitive nature of this, even when advised by Plaintiff’s counsel and this Court that Zhou cannot act on behalf of Yi & Yi, demonstrate willful disregard for the law.” (Reply, 2:16-20.) “Accordingly, Plaintiff requests that this Court strike and disregard the Opposition filed on June 22, 2026 by Zhou.” (Reply, 2:25-26.)

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 436, subdivision (b), the Court has the power to “[s]trike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state.” Given that Zhou’s opposition to the instant motion is not allowed under the law, the Court strikes the opposition.

B.     Requests for Production

Plaintiff moves for an order compelling Yi & Yi to provide further responses, without objections, to his requests for production of documents and to produce all documents responsive to Nos. 1, 4, 6-22 and 31.

On September 3, 2025, Plaintiff’s counsel “served Plaintiff’s Requests for Production of Documents, Set One, on Defendant Yi & Yi.” (Marchetti Decl., ¶ 3; Ex. A.) Plaintiff’s counsel attests that Yi & Yi “served written responses to the Requests for Production. Although Defendant nominally responded, the responses were substantively defective. The responses consisted largely of boilerplate objections, conclusory statements of nonproduction, improper assertions of privilege, and refusals to produce documents despite admissions that responsive documents exist,” which “failed to comply with the statutory requirements of Code of Civil Procedure sections 2031.210 through 2031.240. In numerous instances, Defendant failed to provide a complete statement of compliance under section 2031.220, failed to provide a legally sufficient statement of inability to comply under section 2031.230, and failed to support privilege objections with the factual showing or privilege log required by section 2031.240(c).” (Marchetti Decl., ¶¶ 4-5; Ex. B.)

On October 22, 2025, Counsel sent a detailed meet and confer to Yi & Yi, “identifying the specific defects in Defendant’s responses, citing the applicable statutory provisions, and requesting that Defendant serve verified, code-compliant further responses and produce responsive documents,” but “[d]espite these efforts, Defendant failed and refused to cure the deficiencies. Defendant did not withdraw its improper objections, did not provide meaningful supplemental responses, and did not produce the documents demanded.” (Marchetti Decl., ¶¶ 6, 8; Ex. C.) Plaintiff’s counsel explains that “[o]n December 2, 2026, Yi & Yi’s attorney Howard Gan acknowledged that further responses were due, but advised that he was not receiving cooperation from his client such that he lacked the ability to provide further responses and that was part of the reason he was moving to be relieved as counsel for Yi & Yi. He agreed to extend the deadline for a motion to compel further responses to February 28, 2026.” (Marchetti Decl., ¶ 9; Exs. B-C.) Counsel states that “[a]fter Mr. Gan was relieved from further representing Yi & Yi, on February 5, 2026, I sent a letter directly to Yi & Yi about the failure to respond to discovery… As of the filing of this motion nearly three weeks later, there have still been no responses and no promise to respond.” (Marchetti Decl., ¶ 10; Ex. D.) In Plaintiff’s reply papers filed on July 14, 2026, Plaintiff again confirmed that “[n]o further responses nor responsive documents have been received and all issues identified in the moving papers remain unaddressed and outstanding.” (Reply, 2:27-28.)

In support of the instant motion, Plaintiff asserts, “[i]n multiple instances, Defendant has claimed inability to comply without providing the statutorily required showing of a diligent search. In others, Defendant has invoked constitutional privacy protections that do not apply to corporate entities and has asserted an accountant-client privilege that does not exist under California law.” (Mot., 3:14-17.)

Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.240 outlines the procedure for objecting to a request for production of documents, namely: “(a) If only part of an item or category of item in a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling is objectionable, the response shall contain a statement of compliance, or a representation of inability to comply with respect to the remainder of that item or category. (b) If the responding party objects to the demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling of an item or category of item, the response shall do both of the following: (1) Identify with particularity any document, tangible thing, land, or electronically stored information falling within any category of item in the demand to which an objection is being made. (2) Set forth clearly the extent of, and the specific ground for, the objection. If an objection is based on a claim of privilege, the particular privilege invoked shall be stated. If an objection is based on a claim that the information sought is protected work product…, that claim shall be expressly asserted. (c)(1) If an objection is based on a claim of privilege or a claim that the information sought is protected work product, the response shall provide sufficient factual information for other parties to evaluate the merits of that claim, including, if necessary, a privilege log.”

Plaintiff first asserts that Yi & Yi’s “[b]lanket objections are not merely insufficient—they are invalid. The Court should strike them and order substantive responses.” (Mot., 5:14-15.)

Plaintiff also contends that the relevance objections are unreasonable because “Defendant repeatedly declares that entire categories of documents—tax records, payroll records, financial transfers, import documentation, and communications—are ‘irrelevant.’ That position reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of California discovery law. However, these documents are directly relevant to the issues in this case. Zhou’s publications include allegedly destroyed his importing business, Yi & Yi and forced him to shut down his company. Those statements were not casual remarks; they were central to Zhou’s public narrative that Plaintiff caused him financial devastation. Accordingly, the discovery requests at issue go directly to the core elements of falsity and damages in this defamation action.” (Mot., 5:18-25.)

Plaintiff further explains that “[w]here a defendant affirmatively claims that his business was harmed or destroyed as a result of a plaintiff’s conduct, financial records, tax returns, banking records, customer records, and operational documents are not peripheral — they are central evidence.” (Mot., 5:27-6:2.) Plaintiff specifically outlines that the discovery is not speculative, not harassing, and not overbroad, because “[i]t goes to: 1. The falsity of Zhou’s statements that Plaintiff destroyed his business; 2. The falsity of his claim that he was coerced into settlement; 3. The truth or falsity of his self-characterization as financially destitute; 4. His motive and intent in publishing defamatory statements; and 5. Evidence supporting actual malice and punitive damages.” (Mot., 7:13-18.)

Plaintiff also points out Yi & Yi’s “categorical reliance on ‘privacy’ to avoid production of corporate records,” asserting that this is “legally indefensible,” because privacy under the California Constitution is not absolute and “[w]holesale refusal to produce documents is not an appropriate response.” (Mot., 8:15-18.)

Plaintiff also asserts that Yi & Yi’s “refus[al] to produce communications with its CPA and related tax preparation documents on the ground that such communications are ‘protected’ … is incorrect as a matter of law.” (Mot., 8:20-22.) Plaintiff avers that “[i]nvoking a nonexistent privilege further underscores the lack of substantial justification for Defendant’s objections.” (Mot., 9:1-2.)

Plaintiff also asserts that Yi & Yi fails to comply with Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.230 because “[i]n numerous responses, Defendant asserts that ‘no responsive documents exist” or that “no documents will be produced.’ Yet Defendant provides no description of any search conducted, no identification of custodians consulted, no explanation of systems searched, and no statement as to whether documents ever existed… A conclusory statement that documents do not exist does not satisfy the statute.” (Mot., 9:5-12.)

Lastly, Plaintiff avers that “[p]erhaps most egregiously, Defendant admits that payments were made, communications occurred, checks were issued, and CPA services were retained—yet refuses to produce the underlying documents. Section 2031.220 mandates that when a party agrees to comply, it must produce all responsive documents in its possession, custody, or control. A party cannot simultaneously acknowledge that documents exist and refuse to produce them based on generic objections.” (Mot., 9:16-21.)

Plaintiff’s motion “set[s] forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought by the demand.” (¿Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (b)¿.) Plaintiff also includes a separate statement with the requests for production, Yi & Yi’s objections and denials, and Plaintiff’s responses to these. Moreover, the Court reviewed the Separate Statement and finds Yi & Yi’s responses deficient. “Where the question is specific and explicit, an answer which supplies only a portion of the information sought is wholly insufficient. Likewise, a party may not provide deftly worded conclusionary answers designed to evade a series of explicit questions.” ((Deyo v. Kilbourne (1978) 84 Cal.App.3d 771, 783.)

For example, request for production No. 12 states: “Produce all DOCUMENTS relating to transfers of money made by Yi & Yi Co. Ltd to the Bank. of China or to Guangzhou Yi & Yi Leather Co., Ltd. from 2021 through 2023.” (Sep. Statement, 13:17-18.) Yi & Yi’s response says “Defendants reiterate and incorporate their General Objections. This request seeks information concerning international fund transfers, bank accounts, and commercial trade secrets, all of which constitute highly sensitive financial and privacy-protected information under the California Constitution and applicable privacy laws. Moreover, the request has no relevance to the defamation claims at issue, is outside the scope of permissible discovery, and constitutes improper harassment and an overreaching inquiry into unrelated corporate matters. Accordingly, Defendants will not produce any documents in response to this request.” (Sep. Statement, 13:21-27.) The Court finds that this answer is representative of the other discovery responses by Yi & Yi. Given that Yi & Yi failed to give proper responses and did not produce any responsive documents, Yi & Yi is not in compliance with Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.240, and the motion to compel further responses is necessary. Plaintiff demonstrates good cause because not only does he highlight the deficiency of Yi & Yi’s responses, but he also explains to the Court why the information sought by Yi & Yi is directly relevant to his case. Plaintiff underscores the importance of obtaining financial records and information from Yi & Yi because the requests directly relate to defamatory statements made about him. For request No. 12, Plaintiff asserts that production is necessary because, inter alia, “[i]f Zhou was wiring millions of dollars abroad to accounts under his exclusive control while simultaneously publishing statements that he was financially devastated and forced into settlement due to lack of resources, that contradiction is powerful evidence of falsity. It demonstrates not merely inaccuracy but knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for the truth— hallmarks of actual malice in a defamation case.” (Sep. Statement, 14:18-22.)

Based on the foregoing, and in light of the lack of opposition, Plaintiff demonstrates that his requests are relevant to the issues raised in the complaint and to the claims Yi & Yi placed at issue in the case. Plaintiff shows a need for further responses because Yi & Yi either provides improper objections or does not produce relevant documents. Accordingly, the Court grants Plaintiff’s request to compel further responses, without objection except that the objection to tax returns and tax preparation documents is sustained and the Court does not compel production of such documents. Otherwise, Yi & Yi is ordered to produce all other documents responsive to Plaintiff’s request for production of documents.

Plaintiff asserts that “[u]nder section 2031.310(h), the Court shall impose monetary sanctions unless the party acted with substantial justification. Defendant’s positions directly contradict settled California law. There is no substantial justification. The objections are legally inapplicable. The blanket objections are invalid. The privilege assertions are unsupported. The inability statements are deficient. Sanctions are mandatory.” (Mot., 10:9-13.)

            Background

On December 22, 2025, Plaintiff Susan Almadova (“Plaintiff”) sued Defendants Milon Pluas LLP, dba Worker’s Rights Legal Group, Angel D. Pluas, Joshua Milon, Christopher J. DeClue (collectively, “Attorney Defendants”), and William Hougland (“Hougland”), alleging one cause of action for malicious prosecution.

On March 13, 2026, Attorney Defendants filed a special motion to strike the sole cause of action in the complaint. Plaintiff opposes. Attorney Defendants replied.

Request for Judicial Notice

            The Court grants Plaintiff’s request for judicial notice of Exhibits 1, 4, 5, 6, 13, 14, and 15.

Evidentiary Objections

The Court rules on Plaintiff’s evidentiary objections as follows:

Objection Nos. 1, 2: sustained

Legal Standard

            A party may file a special motion to strike to dismiss entire complaints, specific causes of action, or portions of a cause of action when the party believes the lawsuit is a strategic attempt to infringe upon their “valid exercise of the constitutional rights of freedom of speech and petition for the redress of grievances.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (a).) The anti-SLAPP statute (strategic lawsuits against public participation) protects parties from frivolous lawsuits: “you have a right not to be dragged through the courts because you exercised your constitutional rights.” ((People ex rel. Lockyer v. Brar (2004) 115 Cal.App.4th 1315, 1317.) The legislature intended this statute to be construed broadly. (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (a).)

Anti-SLAPP actions must be filed within 60 days of service of the operative complaint and stays discovery until after disposition of the motion. (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subds. (f), (g).)

Anti-SLAPP actions employ a two-step analysis to determine the merits. (Navellier v. Sletten (2002) 29 Cal. 4th 82, 88 (Navellier).) In the first prong, the defendant must make a threshold showing that the challenged cause of action arises from protected activity. (Ibid.) “A defendant meets this burden by demonstrating that the act underlying the plaintiff’s cause fits one of the [following] categories:” (Ibid.)

 

(1) any written or oral statement or writing made before a legislative, executive, or judicial proceeding, or any other official proceeding authorized by law,

(2) any written or oral statement or writing made in connection with an issue under consideration or review by a legislative, executive, or judicial body, or any other official proceeding authorized by law,

(3) any written or oral statement or writing made in a place open to the public or a public forum in connection with an issue of public interest, or

(4) any other conduct in furtherance of the exercise of the constitutional right of petition or the constitutional right of free speech in connection with a public issue or an issue of public interest.

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (e).) If the court finds the defendant made the requisite showing, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to demonstrate the probability that their cause(s) of action will succeed. (Code Civ. Proc. § 425.16, subd. (b)(1); Navellier, supra, at p. 88-89.)

The second step employs a “summary-judgment-like procedure,” because the court will not weigh evidence or resolve conflicting factual claims. (Sweetwater Union High School District v. Gilbane Building Co. (2019) 6 Cal.5th 931, 940 (Sweetwater).) The court’s inquiry is limited to whether the plaintiff “stated a legally sufficient claim and made a prima facie factual showing sufficient to sustain a favorable judgment.” (Ibid.) This burden is minimal; however, a plaintiff cannot solely rely on their complaint—even if verified. (Ibid.) Instead, they must provide competent evidence, which the court accepts as true. (Ibid.) The court will only evaluate the defendant’s showing “to determine if it defeats the plaintiff’s claim as a matter of law.” (Ibid.)

“Only a cause of action that satisfies both prongs of the anti-SLAPP statute—i.e., that arises from protected speech or petitioning and lacks even minimal merit—is a SLAPP, subject to being stricken under the statute.” (Navellier, supra, at p. 89.)

If a defendant prevails on their anti-SLAPP motion, they are entitled to attorney fees and costs as a matter of right. (Code Civ. Proc. § 425.16, subd. (c)(1).) However, if the anti-SLAPP motion is deemed frivolous or intended to cause unnecessary delay, the court will award the prevailing plaintiff reasonable fees, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 128.5. (Ibid.)

Discussion

            Attorney Defendants move to strike the sole cause of action alleged in Plaintiff’s complaint, which alleges malicious prosecution.

A.    Prong One: Protected Activity

The Anti-SLAPP Statute protects “¿(1) any written or oral statement or writing made before a legislative, executive, or judicial proceeding, or any other official proceeding authorized by law, (2) any written or oral statement or writing made in connection with an issue under consideration or review by a legislative, executive, or judicial body, or any other official proceeding authorized by law, (3) any written or oral statement or writing made in a place open to the public or a public forum in connection with an issue of public interest, or (4) any other conduct in furtherance of the exercise of the constitutional right of petition or the constitutional right of free speech in connection with a public issue or an issue of public interest.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (e).)

Attorney Defendants assert that “Ms. Almadova’s malicious prosecution claims are based solely on WRLG’s activities as counsel for Mr. Hougland in the Underlying Lawsuit. Ms. Almadova does not allege that WRLG acted outside of this context. Regardless of whether the Underlying Lawsuit was maintained maliciously, the act of filing pleadings in a civil action on behalf of a client is per se protected petitioning activity under the Anti-SLAPP statute.” (Mot., 12:10-14.) Plaintiff does not appear to dispute this.

In the complaint, it states, “[i]n February 2024, Defendants commenced retaliatory litigation against Ms. Almadova in the Superior Court of California, County of Orange (Hougland v. Cannon Cochran Management Services, Inc. and Susan Almadova -- later removed to the United States District Court for the Central District of California, Southern Division, USDC Case No. 8:24-00532 JWH ADS), asserting two frivolous claims: violation of California Labor Code section 1102.5 (Whistleblower Retaliation) and Age Harassment in violation of the Fair Employment and Housing Act (‘FEHA’) (the ‘Hougland Matter’). The Hougland Matter asserted the same two claims and eight additional claims against CCMSI.” (Compl., ¶ 4.) The complaint alleges that “Defendants, willfully disregarding the fact that Mr. Hougland could not identify a single instance in which Ms. Almadova engaged in any discriminatory or harassing conduct or speech (much less a pattern), drafted and filed a public document falsely accusing Ms. Almadova of ‘a pattern and practice of harassing [Mr. Hougland],’ ‘engaging in offensive conduct,’ and ‘acting in a hostile and abusive manner’ toward him.” (Compl., ¶ 6.)

Because Plaintiff’s complaint solely and directly arises from Attorney Defendants’ litigation of and involvement in the Hougland Matter, Plaintiff’s complaint is predicated on protected petitioning activity. Thus, the cause of action arises out of protected activity, and prong one is satisfied.

B.     Prong Two: Probability of Success on the Merits

Attorney Defendants demonstrated that the cause of action arises out of protected activity, so the burden shifts to Plaintiff to show that “each challenged claim based on protected activity is legally sufficient and factually substantiated.” ((Baral v. Schnitt (2016) 1 Cal.5th 376, 396.) Affidavits, declarations, and their equivalents may be considered in evaluating an anti-SLAPP motion, so long as the evidence is admissible or reasonably capable of being admitted at trial. (Sweetwater, supra, 6 Cal.5th at p. 949.)

“[I]n order to establish a cause of action for malicious prosecution of either a criminal or civil proceeding, a plaintiff must demonstrate ‘that the prior action (1) was commenced by or at the direction of the defendant and was pursued to a legal termination in his, plaintiff’s, favor [citations]; (2) was brought without probable cause [citations]; and (3) was initiated with malice.’” ((Van Audenhove v. Perry (2017) 11 Cal.App.5th 915, 919.)

a.      Favorable Termination

“A favorable termination does not occur merely because a party complained against has prevailed in an underlying action. While the fact he has prevailed is an ingredient of a favorable termination, such termination must further reflect on his innocence of the alleged wrongful conduct. If the termination does not relate to the merits—reflecting on neither innocence of nor responsibility for the alleged misconduct—the termination is not favorable in the sense it would support a subsequent action for malicious prosecution. [W]hen the underlying action is terminated in some manner other than by a judgment on the merits, the court examines the record to see if the disposition reflects the opinion of the court or the prosecuting party that the action would not succeed.” (Sycamore Ridge Apartments, LLC v. Naumann (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 1385, 1399 (Sycamore Ridge) [internal quotations and citations omitted].)

            Attorney Defendants’ motion asserts that Plaintiff “cannot plead malicious prosecution as to the Whistleblower claim because her dismissal from that cause of action was obtained through a stipulation, in which Ms. Almadova agreed to a waiver of fees and costs.” (Mot., 13:17-19.) Attorney Defendants concede that “[u]nlike the Whistleblower claim, WRLG does not dispute that the underlying age harassment claim was terminated in Ms. Almadova’s favor. However, WRLG strongly contends Ms. Almadova still cannot show the Underlying Lawsuit was brought without probable cause or with malice.” (Mot., 14:13-15.)

            Plaintiff asserts that “[w]hile admitting the age harassment claim was terminated on the merits, the Attorney Defendants misleadingly portray their dismissal of Hougland’s whistleblower retaliation claim as a non-merits-based compromise in exchange for Almadova’s waiver of fees and costs and argue that a negotiated dismissal is not a ‘favorable termination’ for purposes of a malicious prosecution claim. What the Attorney Defendants fail to disclose is that the stipulation merely gave effect to their unilateral decision to abandon their whistleblower retaliation claim by filing a Notice of Non-Opposition to Almadova’s Motion to Dismiss – a decision made before any discussion of a stipulation (with or without a fee and cost waiver).” (Opp., 12:9-16.)

            Plaintiff provides supporting evidence for how the motion to dismiss stipulation arose. Plaintiff provides the declaration from her counsel, Jeffrey M. Cohon, who “personally handled the defense of the Hougland Action since CCMSI and Ms. Almadova first made an appearance in the District Court.” (Cohon Decl., ¶ 2.) Cohon attests that he “sent a detailed meet and confer letter to the Attorney Defendants advising them that their claim for whistleblower retaliation against Ms. Almadova pursuant to Labor Code section 1102.5 was improper because the whistleblower statute did not provide for individual liability. I also provided legal authority supporting this position, including judicial decisions from the Central District of California specifically holding that supervisors cannot – as a matter of law – be liable for an alleged violation of the whistleblower statute.” (Cohon Decl., ¶ 3, Ex. 2.) Cohon explains that during a meeting with Mr. DeClue, counsel of record for the Hougland Matter, “Mr. DeClue could not identify a single case holding that an individual supervisor could be liable under Labor Code section 1102.5. Instead, Mr. DeClue inaccurately claimed that the whistleblower statute had been amended to provide for liability of a supervisor. I asked Mr. DeClue for any authority to support his position that the statute had been amended to provide for individual liability, but he did not provide it to me.” (Cohon Decl., ¶ 4, Ex. 3.) The email from Mr. DeClue provides that “I reviewed your position regarding individual liability for Labor Code 1102.5 claims. I appreciate your diligence. However, I sense that we will agree to disagree. Individual liability has been a grey area for the Court for years. However, in 2023, the legislature made it very clear by amending the statute to clarify that individuals can be named in Labor Code 1102.5 claims.” (Cohon Decl., Ex. 3, p. 48.) In response, on March 25, 2024, Cohon stated that “we respectfully disagree with your contention that in 2023 the California Legislature amended Labor Code section 1102.5 to provide for individual liability. For your review, we attach the 2023 amendment to Labor Code section 1102.5. The handful of amendments to section 1102.5 address other issues entirely. Interestingly, this past week, another federal district court adopted the reasoning in Sean Dawson (and other cases) and dismissed the plaintiff’s individual whistleblower claim against his supervisor. Jack Arne Friedman V. Brooke Jenkins 2024 WL 1182878, at *6 (N.D. Cal. Mar. 19, 2024). We know that you said in your last email that the parties will have to agree to disagree, but we are writing to revisit this issue one last time before we file a motion to dismiss this claim.” (Cohon Decl., Ex. 3, p. 46.)

Cohon attests that Mr. DeClue did not respond to his March 25, 2024 communications, so he “had no choice but to file the Motion to Dismiss seeking dismissal of the Labor Code section 1102.5 claim alleged against Ms. Almadova, which I did on April 12, 2024.” (Cohon Decl., ¶ 8, Ex. 4.) Cohon explains that, in response to the motion, Attorney Defendants filed a non-opposition, and “[o]n April 26, 2024, in the interest of cooperation and so that the Court would not have to waste time working up the unopposed motion, and because this dismissal was early in the litigation, Ms. Almadova thereafter stipulated pursuant to Fed. R. Civ. P. 41(a)(2) to allow Mr. Hougland to dismiss his whistleblower claim against her. Importantly, up to that point, the Attorney Defendants failed to provide me with any authority to support a possible opposition to Ms. Almadova’s Motion to Dismiss, despite refusing to dismiss Plaintiff’s whistleblower claim prior to forcing Ms. Almadova to file her dismissal motion.” (Cohon Decl., ¶¶ 9-10, Exs. 5-6.)

However, as Attorney Defendants point out, a negotiated dismissal or compromise “does not constitute a ‘favorable termination.’” ((Ludwig v. Superior Court (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 8, 29.) Which, they contend, “is exactly what occurred here. Ms. Almadova agreed to dismissal of the Whistleblower claim in exchange for a waiver of fees and costs in April 2024. A fee-and-cost waiver constitutes consideration sufficient to show compromise, even where no money is paid, and it defeats favorable termination as a matter of law.” (Reply, 5:1-4.)

Plaintiff asserts that “the Attorney Defendants’ decision to not oppose Almadova’s motion to dismiss that claim on the merits is a concession that their claim ‘would not succeed’ and lacked legal probable cause. The proposal to file a stipulation of dismissal, solely to avoid an unnecessary hearing on an unopposed motion, arose after the non-opposition.” (Opp., 12:26-13:2.) Plaintiff contends that “[b]y electing not to oppose Almadova’s motion, ‘the inference is unavoidable that the decision to abandon [that claim] was made independently’ of whether Almadova would later stipulate to a waiver of fees and costs to formalize the dismissal more expeditiously.” (Opp., 12:16-19, citing Roche v. Hyde (2020) 51 Cal.App.5th 757, 792.)

“[W]hen the underlying action is terminated in some manner other than by a judgment on the merits, the court examines the record to see if the disposition reflects the opinion of the court or the prosecuting party that the action would not succeed.” (Sycamore Ridge, supra, 157 Cal.App.4th at p. 1399 [internal quotations omitted].) The record demonstrates that Attorney Defendants conceded the motion to dismiss by filing a non-opposition, before the stipulation was ever discussed or executed. The inference can thus be made that Attorney Defendants’ decision to abandon the whistleblower cause of action occurred before the parties stipulated to dismiss the cause of action. (See Roche v. Hyde, supra, 51 Cal.App.5th at p. 792.)

Given the notice of non-opposition filed by Attorney Defendants, the legal arguments that Plaintiff’s counsel provided when meeting and conferring with Attorney Defendants, and Attorney Defendants’ failure to provide a cogent response, the Court finds that the record supports that the termination was favorable to Plaintiff. Accordingly, Plaintiff demonstrates a probability of success for the first element of malicious prosecution.

b.      Probable Cause

“[A]n attorney may be held liable for malicious prosecution for continuing to prosecute a lawsuit discovered to lack probable cause.” ((Zamos v. Stroud (2004) 32 Cal.4th 958, 970.) “‘An action is deemed to have been pursued without probable cause if it was not legally tenable when viewed in an objective manner as of the time the action was initiated or while it was being prosecuted.’ The test is whether, on the basis of facts then known, any reasonable attorney would have believed that instituting or maintaining the prior action was tenable.” ((Citizens of Humanity, LLC v. Hass (2020) 46 Cal.App.5th 589, 599 (Citizens).) “Probable cause, moreover, must exist for every cause of action advanced in the underlying action. ‘[A]n action for malicious prosecution lies when but one of alternate theories of recovery is maliciously asserted....’” ((Soukup v. Law Offices of Herbert Hafif (2006) 39 Cal.4th 260, 292.) Granting an anti-SLAPP is not proper “when there is a dispute as to the state of the defendant’s knowledge and the existence of probable cause turns on resolution of that dispute, [because] there becomes a fact question that must be resolved before the court can determine the legal question of probable cause.” (Citizens, supra, 46 Cal.App.5th at pp. 599-600.)

1.      Whistleblower Retaliation

Under Labor Code section 1102.5, employers may not obstruct an employee from disclosing information about “a violation of state or federal statute, or a violation of or noncompliance with a local, state, or federal rule or regulation.” (Lab. Code, § 1102.5, subd. (a).) Also, employers may not retaliate against employees who disclose such information or who “refus[e] to participate in an activity that would result in a violation of federal or state law, rule, or regulation.” (Lab. Code, § 1102.5, at subds. (b)-(c).)

To state a prima facie case for whistleblower retaliation, “a plaintiff must show that she engaged in protected activity, that she was thereafter subjected to adverse employment action by her employer, and there was a causal link between the two.” (Morgan v. Regents of Univ. of California (2000) 88 Cal.App.4th 52, 69.) “The retaliatory motive is proved by showing that plaintiff engaged in protected activities, that his employer was aware of the protected activities, and that the adverse action followed within a relatively short time thereafter. [Citation.] The causal link may be established by an inference derived from circumstantial evidence, such as the employer’s knowledge that the [employee] engaged in protected activities and the proximity in time between the protected action and allegedly retaliatory employment decision.” (Id. at pp. 69-70 [internal quotations omitted].)

Plaintiff contends that “[t]he whistleblower retaliation claim against Almadova lacked both legal and factual probable cause from inception. Just two weeks before the filing of the Hougland Complaint, yet another federal district court held that Labor Code section 1102.5 does not permit an alleged whistleblower to recover money damages from an individual who retaliated against him on behalf of an employer… In light of this authority, no reasonable attorney could have believed Hougland had a viable whistleblower retaliation claim against Almadova. Nevertheless, when provided with this authority, and more, the Attorney Defendants forced Almadova to file a motion to dismiss – only to not oppose it.” (Opp., 16:16-25.)

The District Court noted when it granted Almadova’s Motion for Summary Judgment that “it is evident from the record that Hougland’s Motion to Dismiss is an attempt to circumvent an adverse ruling on Defendants’ Motion for Summary Judgment. Even when it was blatantly clear to Hougland that his claim against Almadova was fruitless, he continued to pursue that claim until he was confronted with Defendants’ Motion for Summary Judgment. Such an abuse of Rule 41(a) is inappropriate.” (Cohon Decl., ¶ 41, Ex. 14, pp. 212-213.)

            Based on the information and analysis of the Favorable Termination section, supra, Plaintiff establishes that she has a probability of success for lack of probable cause for the whistleblower cause of action, because Plaintiff’s counsel directed Attorney Defendants to adverse authority, could not substantiate authority in support of their position, and still maintained the cause of action until Plaintiff filed the motion to dismiss. Additionally, as the District Court addressed in its decision on the motion for summary judgment, the cause of action for whistleblower retaliation against Plaintiff was “fruitless.”

2.      Age Harassment

For a harassment cause of action, a plaintiff must “show that (1) he was a member of a protected class; (2) he was subjected to unwelcome [] harassment; (3) the harassment was based on [membership in the protected class]; (4) the harassment unreasonably interfered with his work performance by creating an intimidating, hostile, or offensive work environment; and (5) the [employer] is liable for the harassment.” (¿Thompson v. City of Monrovia (2010) 186 Cal.App.4th 860, 876¿; see also ¿Dickson v. Burke Williams, Inc. (2015) 234 Cal.App.4th 1307, 1313¿ [“¿One of the elements of a harassment claim … is that the harassment be sufficiently severe or pervasive so as to alter the conditions of employment and create an abusive working environment.¿”].) The elements of severe or pervasive harassment unreasonably interfering with work performance are assessed from the perspective of a reasonable person of the protected class of the plaintiff. (Id. at p. 877.)

Under Government Code section 12932 subdivision (a), “harassment creates a hostile, offensive, oppressive, or intimidating work environment and deprives victims of their statutory right to work in a place free of discrimination when the harassing conduct sufficiently offends, humiliates, distresses, or intrudes upon its victim, so as to disrupt the victim’s emotional tranquility in the workplace, affect the victim’s ability to perform the job as usual, or otherwise interfere with and undermine the victim’s personal sense of well-being.” “A single incident of harassing conduct is sufficient to create a triable issue regarding the existence of a hostile work environment if the harassing conduct has unreasonably interfered with the plaintiff’s work performance or created an intimidating, hostile, or offensive working environment.” (Gov. Code, § 12923, subd. (b).) “The existence of a hostile work environment depends upon the totality of the circumstances and a discriminatory remark, even if not made directly in the context of an employment decision or uttered by a nondecisionmaker, may be relevant, circumstantial evidence of discrimination.” (Gov. Code, § 12923, subd. (c).)

Here, Attorney Defendants assert that they “had probable cause to file the underlying age harassment cause of action based on its initial understanding of the facts and the representations made by Mr. Hougland at the outset of the Underlying Lawsuit. (DeClue Decl., ¶¶ 50–53, 56–58.) Mr. DeClue was provided with pertinent information regarding Ms. Almadova’s pattern of hostility and animosity toward Mr. Hougland, and he formed a good-faith belief—under the objective probable cause standard—that Ms. Almadova was the person responsible for the age-related comments made to Mr. Hougland during his termination meeting. (Id. ¶ 56.) In other words, based on the evidence available at the time, WRLG reasonably believed Ms. Almadova was culpable for the age harassment alleged.” (Mot., 16:20-27.)

            DeClue’s declaration specifically states that he “considered the following in naming Almadova for age harassment: Mr. Hougland was 67 years old at the time of his termination; Ms. Almadova treated Ms. Hougland with hostility from the time she began managing him; CCMSI had no problems with Mr. Hougland until Ms. Almadova supervised him; Ms. Almadova took actions that led to Mr. Hougland’s termination; in Mr. Hougland’s termination meeting, he was told that he is not getting any younger and that he should retire.” (DeClue Decl., ¶ 51.) Plaintiff’s opposition points out that “[n]oticeably absent are any facts to satisfy the actual elements of an age harassment claim, namely, that Hougland was subjected to ‘verbal or physical conduct of an age-related nature, that the conduct was unwelcome, and that the conduct was sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of [his] employment and create an abusive work environment.’” (Opp., 14:23-26 [emphasis omitted].) “Being unfriendly, causing someone’s termination, and suggesting retirement during the meeting when they are being terminated is not age harassment – as the District Court found when it granted Almadova’s Motion for Summary Judgment.” (Opp., 14:27-15:1; see also Cohon Decl., ¶ 41, Ex. 14, p. 218 [“The record is devoid of facts that support a harassment claim against Almadova. It is undisputed that Hougland could not recall any discriminatory statements or conduct by Almadova regarding his age, nor any person’s age. Hougland agrees that his age harassment claim against Almadova cannot be substantiated, due to a lack of evidence.”].)

            Plaintiff asserts that “the factors concocted by the Attorney Defendants are directly contradicted by the Hougland Complaint. The Attorney Defendants alleged in the Hougland Complaint that Almadova’s hostility began after Hougland (supposedly) complained about one of her directives in May 2023, (Cohon Decl., Ex. 1 at ¶ 24) not immediately after she was appointed his supervisor in November 2022 (DeClue Decl., ¶ 51).” (Opp., 15:5-9.) As noted by Plaintiff, the Hougland complaint states that Hougland “complained to Defendants, including Defendant Almodova” and “[s]hortly after reporting CCMSI’s violations, and thereafter complaining about and/or opposing Defendant’s illegal business practices, … Plaintiff’s superiors, including Defendant Almodova, began to treat him with hostility.” (Cohon Decl., Ex. 1, ¶¶ 23-24.) Plaintiff avers that “[t]he Attorney Defendants’ attempt to now modify the allegations of the Hougland Complaint to imply ageism rather than retaliation (when there was neither) is itself evidence that the facts as alleged did not support probable cause.” (Opp., 15:9-11.) When an attorney “actively advocated the changed story and abandoned the facts as pleaded in the complaint[,] [i]t is probable a jury could find [the attorney] knowingly prosecuted a false claim.” ((Golden State Seafood, Inc. v. Schloss (2020) 53 Cal.App.5th 21, 37.)

Plaintiff points to Attorney Defendants’ lack of evidence about ongoing and pervasive age harassment before prosecuting the Hougland lawsuit to support the lack of probable cause. Plaintiff also presents evidence in Hougland’s complaint that the harassment complained of by Hougland arose after he complained to management, but DeClue’s declaration in support states that “Ms. Almadova treated Ms. Hougland with hostility from the time she began managing him.” (DeClue Decl., ¶ 51.) This directly conflicts with the Hougland complaint. Moreover, as stated above, in the District Court’s order for the motion for summary judgment, the Court stated that the “record is devoid of facts that support a harassment claim against Almadova.” (Cohon Decl., ¶ 41, Ex. 14, p. 218.) This is probative evidence that Attorney Defendants pursued a meritless cause of action.

Accordingly, Plaintiff presents competent evidence that Attorney Defendants pursued the age harassment cause of action despite lacking probable cause.

c.       Malice

“The malice element of the malicious prosecution tort goes to the defendant’s subjective intent in initiating the prior action. For purposes of a malicious prosecution claim, malice is not limited to actual hostility or ill will toward the plaintiff. Rather, malice is present when proceedings are instituted primarily for an improper purpose. Suits with the hallmark of an improper purpose include, but are not necessarily limited to, those in which: ... (1) the person initiating them does not believe that his claim may be held valid; (2) the proceedings are begun primarily because of hostility or ill will; (3) the proceedings are initiated solely for the purpose of depriving the person against whom they are initiated of a beneficial use of his property; (4) the proceedings are initiated for the purpose of forcing a settlement which has no relation to the merits of the claim. (Sycamore Ridge Apartments, supra, 157 Cal.App.4th at p. 1407 [internal quotations and citations omitted].) “[E]vidence that an attorney failed to conduct an adequate investigation before filing a lawsuit supports a finding of malice.” (Citizens, supra, 46 Cal.App.5th at p. 607 [internal citation omitted].)

Plaintiff discusses that “the Attorney Defendants argue that they reasonably (but mistakenly) believed they had probable cause for the age harassment claim until they ‘learned’ – during their own client’s deposition – that Almadova was not the ‘female in management’ who mentioned retirement during Hougland’s termination (Mtn. at 20.) However, they clearly knew this months before Hougland’s March 2025 deposition – evidenced by the facts that in August 2024 they served written discovery but did not ask about age harassment, and they deposed Almadova in January 2025 and did not ask her a single question relating to age harassment.” (Opp., 15:20-26; see Cohon Decl., ¶ 13 [“The first deposition taken in the case was of Ms. Almadova, taken on January 29, 2025. Even though Mr. Hougland’s third cause of action for age harassment alleged that Ms. Almadova displayed ‘a pattern and practice of harassing [Mr. Hougland],’ and ‘engag[ed] in offensive conduct’ and/or ‘act[ed] in a hostile and abusive manner,’ Mr. Hougland’s counsel never asked Ms. Almadova if she made any specific age-based statements to Mr. Hougland or whether she engaged in any conduct that might be perceived as age-related.”].)

In DeClue’s declaration, he attests that “[b]efore the employment lawsuit was filed, I conducted a reasonable inquiry into the facts of this matter, which included interviewing Mr. Hougland, meeting and conferring with Mr. Rogal, and reviewing the limited documents available to me. Many of the documents were not available to me because CCMSI failed to produce Mr. Hougland’s personnel file. Based on my understanding of the facts, I believed that Ms. Almadova was the person responsible for causing Mr. Hougland’s termination. Also, based on the facts presented to me, including the representations made by Mr. Hougland and Mr. Rogal, I had a reasonable belief that Mr. Hougland had viable grounds to bring an age harassment claim against Ms. Almadova.” (DeClue Decl., ¶ 53.) DeClue further declares that “Mr. Hougland relayed to me that during his termination meeting, a female in management was on the call and made age-related comments. At the time, I believed that Mr. Hougland was referring to Ms. Almadova since she was the person who harbored animosity towards Mr. Hougland and caused his termination. I later learned that it was Ms. Rhonda Stuebe that was on the phone at the meeting and made comments about Mr. Hougland retiring, my belief was made in good faith based on this context.” (DeClue Decl., ¶ 56.) Attorney Defendants’ motion asserts that “Mr. DeClue only learned of the identity issue through deposition testimony more than a year after filing and promptly sought to correct it. (DeClue Decl. ¶¶ 53, 54, 56.) A reasonable error corrected once discovered is not evidence of malice.” (Mot., 20:3-5.)

However, Plaintiff’s opposition asserts that “[t]he Attorney Defendants subjected Almadova to a year of frivolous litigation based on an assumption. When Hougland supposedly told the Attorney Defendants that a ‘female in management’ suggested retirement, they had a duty to ask the obvious follow-up question: ‘Do you know who she was?’ Instead, with shocking indifference (i.e., malice), they simply assumed he meant Almadova despite the clear indicator that he did not, i.e., he did not use her name. Maintaining the charade of the Attorney Defendants’ mistake-based defense further requires that they never asked Hougland who participated in his termination meeting, a rather basic investigatory fact to have been overlooked in an employment case hinging on termination.” (Opp., 18:3-10.)

As noted above, Plaintiff contends that the Attorney Defendants knew Plaintiff was not the female in management that suggested retirement by January 29, 2025, the date of Almadova’s deposition, because they did not ask Plaintiff “a single question about alleged age-based statements,” and “[d]espite this, they still refused to dismiss her when faced with evidence in support of her summary judgment motion in March 2025, and ultimately waited until April 4, 2025 to propose dismissal without prejudice and with a waiver of fees and costs. (Id. at ¶¶ 17-35, Ex, 11.) This was not a serious offer.” (Opp., 18:11-16.)

A finding of malice is supported when there is “evidence that an attorney failed to conduct an adequate investigation before filing a lawsuit.” (Citizens, supra, 46 Cal.App.5th at p. 607 [internal citation omitted].) In Citizens, the Court of Appeal found “sufficient evidence of malice,” in part, because the attorneys “conducted little investigation into [plaintiff’s] claims, even when significant questions were raised regarding the validity of her claims.” (Id. at pp. 607-608.) The court noted that “there [wa]s enough evidence here that satisfies [the plaintiff’s] burden with respect to this element,” and that even when “circumstances do not conclusively establish malice, they [may be] sufficient to allow the issue to go to the trier of fact for resolution.” (Id. at p. 608.) From the evidence presented by Plaintiff and DeClue’s own declaration, Attorney Defendants did not conduct an investigation into the comment allegedly made by Plaintiff before initiating the lawsuit. Accordingly, Plaintiff presents competent evidence that Attorney Defendants acted maliciously.

Based on the foregoing, Plaintiff made a prima facie factual showing she meets the elements of a malicious prosecution cause of action and that there is a probability she will succeed. (See Sweetwater, supra, 6 Cal.5th at p. 940.)

Conclusion

Attorney Defendants’ motion to strike the sole cause of action of the complaint is DENIED.
