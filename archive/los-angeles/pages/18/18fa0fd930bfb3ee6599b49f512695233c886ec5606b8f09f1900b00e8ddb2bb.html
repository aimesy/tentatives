Case Number: 24CHCV01514    Hearing Date: August 3, 2026    Dept: F49

Dept. F49

Date: 8/3/26

Case Name: Sadiqa K. Harris; Djuan C. Smith vs Southern California Regional Rail Authority dba Metrolink; Leslie O’Neil Holloway; State of California; County of Los Angeles; City of Santa Clarita; and Does 1-50

Case No. 24CHCV01514

 

LOS ANGELES SUPERIOR COURT

NORTH VALLEY DISTRICT

DEPARTMENT F49

 

AUGUST 3, 2026

 

MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES TO REQUESTS FOR PRODUCTION

Los Angeles Superior Court Case No. 24CHCV01514

 

Motion filed: 2/17/26

 

MOVING PARTY: Plaintiff Sadiqa K. Harris

RESPONDING PARTY: Defendant Leslie Holloway

NOTICE: OK

 

RELIEF REQUESTED: An order from this Court compelling Defendant Leslie Holloway to serve further verified responses and produce responsive documents to Plaintiff Sadiqa K. Harris’ Request for Production of Documents, Set One, Nos. 11-12 and 21-23.

 

TENTATIVE RULING: The motion is GRANTED.

 

BACKGROUND

 

On April 23, 2024, Plaintiffs Sadiqa Harris (“Harris”) and Djuan Smith (“Smith”) (collectively, “Plaintiffs”) initiated this action seeking damages arising from the death of their son, Maseeh Muhammad (“Muhammad”). Muhammad was walking his dog on the sidewalk near railroad tracks in Santa Clarita, California, when he was allegedly struck and killed by a Metrolink train unsafely operated by Defendant Leslie Holloway (“Holloway”). The Complaint alleges the following four causes of action against Holloway, Southern California Regional Rail Authority dba Metrolink (“Metrolink”), the State of California (the “State”), the County of Los Angeles (the “County”), the City of Santa Clarita (the “City”), and Does 1-50 (collectively, “Defendants”): (1) Wrongful Death (against all Defendants), (2) Negligence (against all Defendants), (3) Negligent Hiring and Supervision (against all Defendants), and (4) Dangerous Condition of Public Property (against Metrolink, the State, the County, the City, and Does 1-50). At the request of Plaintiffs, the County, the City, and the State have been dismissed from the action.

 

On March 28, 2025 and September 22, 2025, Metrolink and Holloway filed their respective Answers.

 

On February 17, 2026, Harris filed the instant motion to compel Holloway’s further responses to Harris’ Request for Production of Documents, Set One (the “Motion”).

 

On July 21, 2026, Holloway filed an Opposition.

 

On July 27, 2026, Harris filed a Reply.

 

ANALYSIS

 

“On receipt of a response to a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling, the demanding party may move for an order compelling further response to the demand if the demanding party deems that any of the following apply: (1) A statement of compliance with the demand is incomplete. (2) A representation of inability to comply is inadequate, incomplete, or evasive. (3) An objection is without merit or too general.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (a).)

 

A.    Procedural Requirements

 

(1)   Timeliness of Motion

 

A party must bring a motion to compel further responses to requests for production within 45 days after the responding party serves verified responses or supplemental verified responses. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (c).) Parties may also agree in writing to a specific later date by which to file a motion to compel further responses. (Ibid.) If a motion to compel further responses is not timely made, the propounding party waives any right to compel a further response. (Ibid.)

 

Although not “‘jurisdictional’ in the fundamental sense, [the time limit] is … ‘jurisdictional’ in the sense that it renders the court without authority to rule on motions to compel other than to deny them.” (Sexton v. Superior Court (1997) 58 Cal.App.4th 1403, 1410.)

 

Holloway served further verified responses to Harris’ Request for Production of Documents, Set One on January 30, 2026 via email. (Whitehead Decl., ¶ 15, Exh. D, Proof of Service.) The deadline to bring a motion to compel further responses was thus March 18, 2026. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 1010.6, 2031.310.) The Motion, filed on February 17, 2026, was accordingly timely.

 

(2)   Meet and Confer

 

A motion to compel further responses to a demand for production must be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(2).) “A meet and confer declaration in support of a motion shall state facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt, either in person, by telephone, or by videoconference, to informally resolve each issue presented by the motion.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2016.040.)

 

Plaintiffs’ counsel attests to exchanging meet and confer letters to Holloway’s counsel regarding deficiencies in both the initial and further verified responses. (Whitehead Decl., ¶¶ 10-13, 18-19, Exhs. B, C, E.) Email communications do not generally satisfy the in-person or telephonic meet and confer requirement of the Discovery Act. Nevertheless, given that the parties continued to meet and confer after the Motion was filed in an effort to resolve the matter informally (see Opp’n, at p. 2), the Court finds that the purpose of the meet and confer requirement has been fulfilled.

 

(3)   Separate Statement

 

A motion to compel further responses to a demand for production must be accompanied by a separate statement. (Cal. Rules of Court, rules 3.1345, subd. (a)(3).) “A separate statement is a separate document filed and served with the discovery motion that provides all the information necessary to understand each discovery request and all the responses to it that are at issue.” (Id., rule 3.1345, subd. (c).)

 

Here, Harris has filed a separate statement concurrently with the Motion. The separate statement identifies each request at issue, stating the text of the request and the initial and further responses and providing a reason why further response should be compelled. (See generally Harris’ Separate Statement (“Pl. SS”).) The Court accordingly finds that Harris has satisfied the requirements of California Rules of Court, rule 3.1345, subdivision (c).

 

B.     Motion to Compel Responses to Requests for Production

 

The party to whom a demand for production has been made must respond separately to each demand by any of the following: (1) a statement that the party will comply with the demand by the date set; (2) “[a] representation that the party lacks the ability to comply with the particular demand;” or (3) an objection to the demand. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.210, subd. (a).)

 

A statement of compliance with a particular demand must state whether compliance will be made in whole or in part, and “that all documents or things in the demanded category that are in the possession, custody, or control of that party and to which no objection is being made will be included in the production.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.220.)

 

“A representation of inability to comply with [a] particular demand … shall affirm that a diligent search and a reasonable inquiry has been made in an effort to comply with that demand. This statement shall also specify whether the inability to comply is because the particular item or category has never existed, has been destroyed, has been lost, misplaced, or stolen, or has never been, or is no longer, in the possession, custody, or control of the responding party.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.230.) The statement must also set forth the name and address of any person or organization known or believed to have possession, custody, or control of that item or category of item. (Ibid.)

 

If the responding party objects to the demand, the response must: (1) identify with particularity any document or item falling within the category of the demand to which an objection is being made; and (2) clearly set forth the extent of and specific ground for the objection. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.240, subd. (b).) “If an objection is based on a claim of privilege, the particular privilege invoked shall be stated.” (Id., § 2031.240, subd. (b)(2).)

 

(1)   The Responses at Issue

 

The Motion seeks to compel further responses to Request for Production of Documents, Set One, Nos. 11-12 and 21-23. (Mot., at p. 3.) In his Opposition, however, Holloway indicates that he obtained additional responsive documents and supplemented his document production, leaving only Request No. 22 at issue. In Reply, Harris does not contend that Holloway’s supplemental production is insufficient as to the remaining requests, directing argument solely to Request No. 22. The Court’s ruling is accordingly limited to Request No. 22.

 

Request No. 22 seeks “All DOCUMENTS PERTAINING to the speed at which YOU were operating train #269 on September 4, 2023.” (Pl. SS, at p. 8.)

 

In addition to asserting objections based on overbreadth, confidentiality, and attorney-client privilege and work product protection, Holloway’s further response, served on July 14, 2026, states: “Responding Party will comply with this request and identifies records that are in the possession custody and/or control of Metrolink, SCRRA Bates #909-910 and his deposition testimony. Responding Party also identifies Southern California Regional Rail Authority dba Metrolink, who may be contacted through counsel, and the Los Angeles Sheriff’s Department, as entities who may also have documents responsive to this request.” (Pl. SS, at p. 9.)

 

Harris contends that the foregoing response remains deficient because it does not state whether Bates Nos. 909-910 represent complete production of responsive materials or whether additional responsive documents exist. (Reply, at pp. 3-4.) Harris argues that a complete response would confirm: (1) that all responsive documents in Holloway’s possession, custody, or control have been produced, (2) that Bates Nos. 909-910 constitute a complete responsive production, (3) whether additional documents exist, and (4) what software is necessary to access any native Event Data Recorder files. (Id., at p. 6.)

 

Holloway argues that his response fully complies with the Discovery Act and that Harris seeks relief beyond both what is requested and what is required by the Discovery Act. (Opp’n, at p. 7.) The Court notes that the moving papers seek to compel Holloway to: (1) describe the documents contained in the production and how they relate to the speed of the train, (2) state whether he reviewed Event Data Recorder files, (3) indicate whether he has any personal notes, logs, or records regarding the train’s speed, and (4) confirm that the train was travelling at 50 miles per hour or provide evidence to the contrary. (Pl. SS, at p. 9.) While the Court would agree that Code of Civil Procedure sections 2031.210 et seq. do not require such detailed description of the production being made, Harris’ Reply dispenses with these requests and seeks only confirmation that full compliance with Request No. 22 has been made.

 

As to the response, it purports to be a statement of compliance. A statement of compliance must state whether production “will be allowed in whole or in part, and that all documents or things in the demanded category that are in the possession, custody, or control of that party … will be included in the production.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.220.) Holloway’s response does not so state, and is accordingly incomplete insofar as it does not verify that Bates Nos. 909-910 and Holloway’s deposition testimony constitute all responsive documents within Holloway’s possession, custody, or control.

 

(2)   Good Cause for Further Response

 

In addition to identifying the insufficiency of responses to a demand for production, a motion to compel further response “shall set forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought by the demand.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(1).) Good cause may be shown by a fact-specific showing of relevance. (Kirkland v. Superior Court (2002) 95 Cal.App.4th 92, 98.) Information is relevant to the subject matter if it “might reasonably assist a party in evaluating the case, preparing for trial, or facilitating settlement.” (Lipton v. Superior Court (1996) 48 Cal.App.4th 1599, 1611, emphasis omitted.)

 

The parties do not dispute the relevance of the requested production to the litigation, which is apparent from the nature of the claims. The Court therefore finds that good cause justifying further response exists.

 

Moreover, the Discovery Act contemplates that electronically stored information be produced in reasonably usable form. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.280, subd. (e).) It is accordingly reasonable for Plaintiff to seek verified confirmation of what software is required to read the Event Data Recorder files produced.

 

Based on the foregoing, the Court GRANTS the Motion insofar as it seeks confirmation: (1) that full production of the documents in Holloway’s possession, custody, or control has been made in response to Request No. 22, and (2) of what software is required to read the Event Data Recorder material produced.

 

CONCLUSION

 

The Court GRANTS Plaintiff Sadiqa K. Harris’ motion to compel further responses, consistent with the foregoing ruling. Defendant Leslie Holloway is ordered to serve further verified responses to Harris’ Requests for Productions of Documents, Set One, No. 22 within 20 days.

Case Number: 24CHCV03039    Hearing Date: August 3, 2026    Dept: F49

Dept. F49

Date: 8/3/26

Case Name: Richard Jones, Olivia Chan-Jones vs. Chad M. Schott, Brooke K. Schott, and Does 1-50

Case No. 24CHCV03039

 

LOS ANGELES SUPERIOR COURT

NORTH VALLEY DISTRICT

DEPARTMENT F49

 

AUGUST 3, 2026

 

MOTION TO SET ASIDE ORDER DEEMING MATTERS ADMITTED

Los Angeles Superior Court Case No. 24CHCV03039

 

Motion filed: 2/9/26

 

MOVING PARTY: Plaintiffs Richard Jones and Olivia Chan-Jones

RESPONDING PARTY: Defendants Chad M. Schott and Brooke K. Schott

NOTICE: OK

 

RELIEF REQUESTED: An order from this Court setting aside the Court’s February 2, 2026 order deeming Defendant Chad M. Schott’s Requests for Admission, Set One served on each Plaintiff admitted.

 

TENTATIVE RULING: The motion is GRANTED.

 

BACKGROUND

 

This action arises from an alleged breach of an oral agreement between the parties regarding the sale of property located at 24400 Cavaliani Place, Valencia, CA 91355-6054 (the “Subject Property”).

 

On August 23, 2024, Plaintiffs Richard Jones (“Richard”) and Olivia Chan-Jones (“Olivia”) (collectively, “Plaintiffs”) filed the Complaint against Defendants Chad M. Schott (“Chad”), Brooke K. Schott (“Brooke”) (collectively, “Defendants”), and Does 1 through 50, alleging the following causes of action: (1) breach of contract, (2) fraud, and (3) declaratory relief. Subsequently, on January 9, 2025, Defendants filed their joint Answer to the Complaint.

 

On the same day, January 9, 2025, Defendants filed a Cross-Complaint against Plaintiffs and Does 1 to 50, alleging the following causes of action: (1) breach of contract, (2) breach of implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, (3) negligent misrepresentation, (4) unjust enrichment, (5) fraud in the inducement, and (6) declaratory relief. Subsequently, on April 17, 2025, the Court sustained in part Plaintiffs’ demurrer to the Cross-Complaint as to the second and third causes of action, with leave to amend. On May 28, 2025, Plaintiffs filed their joint Answer to the Cross-Complaint.

 

On February 2, 2026, the Court granted Defendants’ motion to deem the matters specified in Chad’s Requests for Admission (“RFAs”), Set One, propounded on both Plaintiffs, admitted. (Minute Order, Feb. 2, 2026, at pp. 13-14.) 

 

On February 9, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the instant motion to set aside the portion of the Court’s February 2, 2026 order deeming matters admitted (the “Motion”).

 

On July 20, 2026, Defendants filed an Opposition.

 

On July 27, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a Reply.

 

ANALYSIS

 

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b), “[t]he court may, upon any terms as may be just, relieve a party or the party’s legal representative from a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against the party through the party’s mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect.” An application for relief must be made within a reasonable time, not exceeding six months, after the order. (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b).)

 

A.    Motion to Set Aside Order Deeming Matters Admitted

 

Plaintiffs seek to set aside the portion of the Court’s February 2, 2026 order deeming the truth of the matters specified in Chad’s RFAs, Set One. In that Order, the Court found that although Plaintiffs’ counsel represented that he had served responses to the requests on behalf of both Plaintiffs by email and mail, the Court had insufficient basis to determine that substantially compliant responses were served before the hearing in the absence of proof of service or a copy of the responses. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (c) [court must make the order deeming matters admitted unless it finds that the responding party has served a substantially compliant proposed response before the hearing].) At the hearing on the motions to deem the requests admitted, Defendants acknowledged receiving an email with attachments from Plaintiffs’ counsel, but claimed that electronic service was improper and indicated that they had not reviewed the attachments. (C. Schott Decl., Exh. B.) As Plaintiffs note, the ruling turned on evidentiary issues.

 

Plaintiffs’ counsel, Brad Barnholtz (“Barnholtz”), asserts that it was his excusable neglect in failing to submit a copy of the responses and the proof of service that resulted in the adverse ruling. (Barnholtz Decl., ¶¶ 4-5.) Defendants argue that there was no excusable error because the Order was entered after months of Plaintiffs’ non-compliance with discovery obligations and that in any case, Plaintiffs do not establish proper service before the hearing, making relief from the Order unavailable. (Opp’n, at pp. 8-14.)

 

(1)     The Adverse Order Was the Result of Counsel’s Error.

 

Defendants contend that Barnholtz’s averment of error isolates a single mistake that does not account for Plaintiffs’ months-long failure to respond to the requests. (Opp’n, at pp. 9-10.) Defendants reason that the adverse ruling resulted, in the first instance, from Plaintiffs’ default in responding to discovery. (Id., at p. 9.)

 

While the motion to deem the matters specified in the requests would have been averted by service of timely responses, Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.280, subdivision (c) allows a responding party to avoid deemed admissions by serving responses before hearing on the motion. This provision and related case law recognize that deemed admissions have drastic litigation consequences that should not be applied lightly. (See St. Mary v. Super. Ct. (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 762, 783-784.)

 

Service of substantially compliant responses would have precluded the Court from granting the motion and deeming the RFAs admitted. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (c).) Had evidence been submitted to the Court that the responses were served and that they were substantially compliant with the Discovery Act, the Court accordingly would have denied the motion. It is thus the failure to submit such evidence that resulted in the adverse ruling.

 

(2)     The Error Was Excusable.

 

In determining whether the failure to submit evidence that the responses were served was excusable, the relevant inquiry is whether a reasonably prudent person under the same circumstances might have made the same error. (Hearn v. Howard (2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 1193, 1206.) A wholly inexcusable mistake will not justify relief. (Ibid.)

 

Courts construe the provisions of section 473 liberally, pursuant to a policy favoring disposition of actions on their merits. (Zamora v. Clayborn Contracting Group, Inc. (2002) 28 Cal.4th 249, 256.) Where a party moves promptly for relief and no prejudice would result, only slight evidence is required to justify set-aside. (Rogalski v. Nabers Cadillac (1992) 11 Cal.App.4th 816, 819-820.) Any doubts in applying discretionary relief are accordingly resolved in favor of the moving party. (Id., at p. 819.)

 

Here, Barnholtz attests that he was retained only days before the hearing on the motion to deem the RFAs admitted, that several discovery motions remained outstanding at that point, and that he promptly met with Plaintiffs to prepare verified responses to the RFAs so that he could effectuate service before the hearing. (Barnholtz Decl., ¶ 3.) He attests that he served the responses both electronically and via mail on February 1, 2026, the day before the hearing. (Ibid.) Barnholtz further states that he mistakenly did not file the responses or proof of service thereof or proffer them at the hearing. (Id., ¶ 4.) The responses, with proof of service electronically and by mail on February 1, 2026, are attached to Plaintiffs’ counsel’s declaration. (Id., Exh. B.)

 

Defendants contend that Barnholtz’s failure to file the responses and relevant proofs was inexcusable because he admitted in email correspondence with Defendants that he reviewed the Court’s tentative ruling regarding the motion and accordingly understood that the motion would be granted if substantially compliant responses were not served. (Opp’n, at p. 12.) Defendants suggest that a reasonably prudent attorney would have tendered the responses and the proof of service to the Court at the hearing or requested leave to file them rather than focusing on whether electronic service of the responses was adequate. (Id., at p. 13.)

 

As Plaintiffs assert in their Reply, this argument identifies another error in reasoning committed by Barnholtz—the assumption that email pursuant to a Notice of Electronic Service Contact Update was sufficient to effectuate service of the responses. (Reply, at p. 7.)

 

Defendants acknowledge that Barnholtz was substituted into this matter as Plaintiffs’ counsel on January 30, 2026, one court day before the hearing on the motion to deem Chad’s RFAs admitted. Barnholtz was accordingly working to resolve several outstanding discovery matters within severe time constraints, and mistakes in filing and in interpretation of filed notices might, under the circumstances, have been made by any reasonably prudent attorney. The Court thus finds that the errors resulting in the deemed admissions were excusable.

 

(3)     Plaintiffs Timely Moved for Relief.

 

Plaintiffs moved for relief just one week after the February 2, 2026 Order was entered, well within the six-month deadline prescribed by statute and promptly after notice of the order was given. The Court therefore finds that the motion was made within a reasonable time.

 

(4)     Defendants Do Not Rebut Plaintiffs’ Evidence of Service.

 

Defendants make much of the fact that their recollection of the hearing does not correspond to the Court’s findings in its February 2, 2026 Order and to the factual premise of the Motion. Specifically, Defendants assert that, although the ruling indicates that Barnholtz represented at the hearing that he had served the responses by electronic service and via mail, Barnholtz actually made no such representation and it was Defendants who stated that mail service was represented. (Opp’n, at pp. 5-6; B. Schott Decl., ¶¶ 7-8; C. Schott Decl., ¶ 9.) Defendants contend that Barnholtz’s representation of service by mail only arose after the hearing, and imply that no responses were served by an authorized method of service before the hearing. (Opp’n, at p. 8.)

 

As to what matter was discussed at the hearing, Defendants effectively ask the Court to reverse its previous factual findings without evidence that those findings were erroneous. In the absence of evidence otherwise, the Court has no basis to find that Barnholtz did not represent that he made service by mail at the February 2, 2026 hearing.

 

As to whether service was effectuated before the hearing, Barnholtz submits proof of service of the responses by mail, declaring under penalty of perjury that the responses were served on February 1, 2026 by depositing them with the United States Postal Service in Valencia, California. (Barnholtz Decl., Exh. B.) Service by mail is complete at the time of deposit. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1013, subd. (a).) Defendants provide no evidence that the responses were not mailed as indicated.

 

Moreover, the foregoing argument presumes that service before the hearing is required for relief. It is not. All that is required by section 473, subdivision (b) is mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect. Case law and the Discovery Act’s withdrawal provision support a liberal grant of relief even where substantially compliant responses are not served before the hearing on a motion to deem RFAs admitted. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.300, subd. (b) [withdrawal of admission available if made as the result of mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect]; Wilcox v. Birtwhistle (1999) 21 Cal.4th 973, 983 [allowing party to withdraw from deemed admissions to prevent windfall to requesting party].)

 

Given that the Court has found that Barnholtz’s erroneous reliance on email service was excusable, mail-service of responses before the hearing, although adequately shown, is not necessary to the determination of the Motion.

 

(5)     The Responses Are Substantially Compliant.

 

            Upon review of the responses attached to Barnholtz’s declaration, they are verified by Plaintiffs and contain both admissions and denials, including explanatory remarks, without asserting any waived objections. (See generally Barnholtz Decl., Exh. B.) The Court finds the responses to be substantially compliant with the Discovery Act. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2033.210 et seq.)

 

Based on the foregoing, the Court GRANTS the Motion. The portion of the February 2, 2026 Order deeming the matters specified in Chad’s RFAs, Set One admitted by Plaintiffs is hereby vacated.

 

(6)     Defendants’ Request for Fees and Costs Is Unsupported.

 

Defendants ask that the Court, if it grants relief, award Defendants their reasonable attorney fees and costs incurred in opposing the Motion. (Opp’n, at p. 14.) Plaintiffs concede that this would be appropriate, but assert that the request for relief is unsupported. (Reply, at p. 10.)

 

Defendants contend that they consulted with limited-scope representation counsel regarding the Motion and preparation of their Opposition, incurring consultation fees in the amount of $850.00, and $50.00 in filing fees, mailing expenses, and printing costs. (B. Schott Decl., ¶ 14.) No further support for the request is included, such as a declaration from counsel of the fee calculation or verified itemization of the costs incurred. Plaintiffs note that, while two Notices of Limited Scope Representation were filed on July 17, 2026, those notices define the scope of representation as including only deposition proceedings and related preparation and attendance, and do not include any motion consultation. (Suppl. Barnholtz Decl., Exhs. A, B.) The Court thus has insufficient basis to award fees and costs.

 

Defendants’ request for fees and costs is accordingly DENIED.

 

CONCLUSION

 

The Court GRANTS Plaintiffs Richard Jones and Olivia Chan-Jones’ motion to set aside the portion of the Court’s February 2, 2026 Order deeming the matters specified in Defendant Chad M. Schott’s Requests for Admission, Set One admitted.

 

The Court DENIES Defendants Chad M. Schott and Brooke K. Schott’s request for attorney fees and costs.

Case Name: Shawn Sanderfer vs. Equity Lifestyle Properties, Inc.; Thousand Trails Management Services, Inc.; MHC Property Management, L.P.; MHC TT, L.P.; and Does 1-25

Case No. 24CHCV03232

 

LOS ANGELES SUPERIOR COURT

NORTH VALLEY DISTRICT

DEPARTMENT F49

 

AUGUST 3, 2026

 

MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES TO SPECIAL INTERROGATORIES; REQUEST FOR SANCTIONS

Los Angeles Superior Court Case No. 24CHCV03232

 

Motions filed: 9/25/25

 

MOVING PARTY: Plaintiff Shawn Sanderfer

RESPONDING PARTY: Defendant Thousand Trails Management Services, Inc.

NOTICE: OK

 

RELIEF REQUESTED: An order from this Court compelling Defendant Thousand Trails Management Services, Inc. to provide further responses to Plaintiff’s Special Interrogatories, Set One; and awarding sanctions in the amount of $3,000.00.

 

TENTATIVE RULING: The Motion is GRANTED IN PART. The request for sanctions is GRANTED IN PART.

 

BACKGROUND

 

This action arises from personal injuries Plaintiff Shawn Sanderfer (“Plaintiff”) sustained on September 10, 2022, allegedly due to a dangerous condition at Defendants’ Thousand Trails Soledad Canyon RV resort (the “Soledad Canyon Property”). 

 

On September 9, 2024, Plaintiff filed a Complaint against Defendants Equity Lifestyle Properties, Inc. (“Equity Lifestyle”), Thousand Trails Management Services, Inc. (“TTMSI”), MHC Property Management, L.P. (“MHC Property”), MHC TT, L.P. (“MHC TT”) (collectively, “Defendants”), and Does 1 through 25, alleging four causes of action: (1) negligence, (2) premises liability, (3) intentional infliction of emotional distress, and (4) negligent infliction of emotional distress. On November 22, 2024, Defendants filed their joint Answer to the Complaint.

 

On September 25, 2025, Plaintiff filed the instant Motion to Compel Further Responses to Special Interrogatories, Set One (the “Motion”).

 

On July 21, 2026, TTMSI filed an Opposition.

 

No reply papers have been filed.

 

ANALYSIS

 

“On receipt of a response to interrogatories, the propounding party may move for an order compelling a further response if the propounding party deems that any of the following apply: (1) An answer to a particular interrogatory is evasive or incomplete. (2) An exercise of the option to produce documents under Section 2030.230 is unwarranted or the required specification of those documents is inadequate. (3) An objection to an interrogatory is without merit or too general.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (a).)

 

A.    Procedural Requirements

 

(1)   Meet and Confer

 

A motion to compel further responses to interrogatories must be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (b)(1).) A meet and confer declaration must “state facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt, either in person, by telephone, or by videoconference, to informally resolve each issue presented by the motion.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2016.040.)

 

Plaintiff’s counsel attests to exchanging several meet-and-confer letters with Defendants’ counsel between July 15, 2025 and August 28, 2025 and engaging in an extended meet-and-confer conference on August 21, 2025. (Miller Decl., ¶¶ 10-20.) The Court finds Plaintiff’s meet-and-confer effort to be satisfactory.

 

(2)   Separate Statement

 

“Any motion involving the content of a discovery request or the responses to such a request must be accompanied by a separate statement. The motions that require a separate statement include a motion … (2) To compel further responses to interrogatories.” (Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1345, subd. (a).) The separate statement must “provide[] all information necessary to understand … all the responses … that are at issue” and “be full and complete so that no person is required to review any other document in order to determine the full request and the full response.” (Id., Rule 3.1345, subd. (c).)

 

Plaintiff has filed a separate statement concurrently with the Motion, identifying each request at issue, stating the text of the interrogatory and response, and providing a basis for compelling further response. (See generally, Plaintiff’s Separate Statement (“Pl. SS”).)

 

The Court finds that Plaintiff has complied with the requirements of California Rules of Court, rule 3.1345.)

 

B.     Motion to Compel Further Responses to Special Interrogatories, Set One

 

“Each answer in a response to interrogatories shall be as complete and straightforward as the information reasonably available to the responding party permits.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.220, subd. (a).) “If an interrogatory cannot be answered completely, it shall be answered to the extent possible.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.220, subd. (b).) “If the responding party does not have personal knowledge sufficient to respond fully to an interrogatory, that party shall so state, but shall make a reasonable and good faith effort to obtain the information by inquiry to other natural persons or organizations, except where the information is equally available to the propounding party.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.220, subd. (c).)

 

“As a general matter, the statutory scheme imposes no obligation on a party propounding interrogatories to establish good cause or prove up the merits of any underlying claims.” (Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 550.) A litigant “is entitled to demand answers to its interrogatories, as a matter of right, and without a prior showing, unless the party on whom those interrogatories are served objects and shows cause why the questions are not within the purview of the code section.” (Id., at p. 541, citing West Pico Furniture Co. v. Superior Court (1961) 56 Cal.2d 407, 422.)

 

TTMSI served initial responses to Plaintiff’s Special Interrogatories, Set One on June 18, 2025. (Miller Decl., ¶ 9.) After meeting and conferring regarding the responses, TTMSI served supplemental responses on August 20, 2025 and further supplemental responses on August 29, 2025, but never supplemented the responses presently at issue. (Id., ¶¶ 15-19.) Plaintiff contends that the responses to Special Interrogatory Nos. 3-8, 20-21, and 25 are evasive and incomplete. (Mot., at pp. 2-3.)

 

(1)   Special Interrogatories, Set One, Nos. 3-8

 

Special Interrogatories Nos. 3 through 8 ask TTMSI to “DESCRIBE IN DETAIL any RELATIONSHIP” between it and: (1) MHC Property (Nos. 3-4), (2) MHC TT (Nos. 5-6), and (3) Equity Lifestyle (Nos. 7-8), both generally and as the relationships relate to the Soledad Canyon Property. (Pl. SS, at pp. 1-8.)

 

(a)   Responses to Special Interrogatory Nos. 3-8

 

TTMSI’s responses to Special Interrogatory Nos. 3-8 make the following general objection to each of these interrogatories: “Objection, the interrogatory is vague and ambiguous with respect to the term[] ‘relationship.’” (Pl. SS, at pp. 1-9.)

 

TTMSI also provides the following responsive information:

 

Response to Special Interrogatory Nos. 3-6: “There is no relationship between Responding Party and Thousand Trails. Thousand Trails does not own or manage the property where the subject incident took place. Thousand Trails Management Services, Inc., is a separate entity that engages in third-party management of properties it doesn’t own, like US Forest Service properties. It does not play a part in the subject property.” (Pl. SS, at p. 1-7.)

 

Response to Special Interrogatory Nos. 7: “Thousand Trails is an indirect subsidiary of ELP.” (Pl. SS, at p. 7.)

 

Response to Special Interrogatory No. 8: “There is no relationship between Thousand Trails and ELP that relate to the subject property. Thousand Trails Management Services, Inc., is a separate entity that engages in third-party management of properties it doesn’t own, like US Forest Service properties. It does not play a part in the subject property.” (Pl. SS, at p. 9.)

 

Plaintiff argues that these responses are incomplete because “DESCRIBE IN DETAIL” and “RELATIONSHIP” are defined such that TTMSI must provide information concerning shared personnel, shared offices and resources, financial flows, formal or informal agreements between the entities, and payment of one entity’s obligations by another. (Mot., at p. 7.)

 

In its Opposition, TTMSI asserts that it served supplemental responses on February 24, 2026 and July 21, 2026 that name all of its officers and directors and describe in further detail the space and personnel overlap between the entities. (Opp’n, at p. 4.) However, TTMSI admittedly does not include any information regarding the financial relationship of the entities, arguing that such information is not within the scope of permissible discovery. (Opp’n, at p. 5.)

 

As the responses do not fully address the inquiry of the foregoing interrogatories, the Court finds them to be incomplete. The Court next considers TTMSI’s objections to the interrogatories.

 

(b)   Disclosure of Financial Relationships

 

TTMSI contends that the interrogatories are overbroad in their request for corporate financial information. (Opp’n, at p. 5.)

 

First, TTMSI cites Civil Code section 3295 to argue that Plaintiff’s request for financial information is procedurally improper. (Opp’n, at p. 6.) Civil Code section 3295 permits the court, for good cause, to grant a protective order requiring that a plaintiff present a prima facie case of the defendant’s liability for exemplary damages before obtaining discovery about that defendant’s financial condition. (Civ. Code, §§ 3294, 3295.) The interrogatories at issue, however, do not seek information about TTMSI’s financial condition. They seek information relevant to shared financial management of the entities to determine whether there is common governance. Section 3295 accordingly does not apply.

 

TTMSI also admits that financial flows, inter-company payments, and financial agreements are “classic veil-piercing discovery,” but asserts that veil-piercing discovery is unjustified until Plaintiff first makes a factual showing supporting his alter-ego liability theories. (Opp’n, at pp. 6-7.) However, the cases TTMSI cites regarding alter-ego liability do not support limiting discovery in this way. Hennessy’s Tavern, Inc. v. American Air Filter Co. (1988) 204 Cal.App.3d 1351 (Hennessy’s) addressed when alter-ego issues may be raised (in the pleadings, at trial, at a hearing to determine the true identity of the judgment debtor, or in a subsequent enforcement action) and when the alter-ego defendant must be served after first being added as a new defendant in an amended complaint. (Hennessy’s, at pp. 1358-1360.) And Greenspan v. LADT, LLC (2010) 191 Cal.App.4th 486 held that it was error to deny a motion to add a party as a judgment debtor on an alter-ego theory on the ground that alter-ego liability was not raised in arbitration, reasoning that requiring plaintiffs to name a corporation and all its affiliated entities in the complaint would result in unnecessary investigation into such claims without regard to the circumstances. (Greenspan, at p. 517.) Neither case requires a plaintiff to present a prima facie case of alter-ego liability in order to obtain relevant discovery.

 

Even if a reasonable basis for the assertion of an alter-ego theory were required to seek discovery of information relating to asset commingling, the Court finds that the commonality of officers and directors among the Defendants, as shown in Defendants’ supplemental responses, supplies that basis. (Medel Decl., Exhs. 2, 3.)

 

The Motion is accordingly GRANTED as to Special Interrogatory Nos. 3-8.

 

(2)   Special Interrogatories, Set One, Nos. 20-21

 

Interrogatory No. 20: “IDENTIFY YOUR current officers, including their names, titles, and dates of service.” (Pl. SS, at p. 10.)

 

Interrogatory No. 21: “IDENTIFY all of YOUR current directors.” (Pl. SS, at p. 11.)

 

Response to both: “Objection, the interrogatory is wholly overbroad, burdensome and seeks information not likely to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence. Further, the interrogatory seeks information wholly irrelevant to the subject matter. A party may obtain discovery regarding any matter not privileged, that is relevant to the subject matter involved in the pending action or the determination of any motion made in that action, if the matter either is itself admissible in evidence or appears reasonabl[y] calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence. Emerson Electric Co. v. Superior Court (1997) 16 Cal.4th 1101, 1108. Although the scope of discovery is broad it is not without limits. Objection, the interrogatory seeks private information. California courts have held that the right to privacy is not absolute, but discovery that invades privacy must be justified by a compelling interest. Merely being a board member does not, by itself, make a person’s identity or personal information discoverable. Article 1, Section 1 of the California Constitution. Responding Party is willing to meet and confer on this issue to determine the relevance of the requested private information to the subject matter.” (Pl. SS, at pp. 10-11.)

 

TTMSI asserts that its supplemental responses, served on February 24, 2026 and July 15, 2026, supply the information sought, listing the names of all of its officers, directors, and partners. (Opp’n, at p. 5.) Upon review, the supplemental responses list TTMSI’s current officers (No. 20) and directors (No. 21), and list the titles of the officers. (Medel Decl., Exh. 3, at pp. 25-27.) Dates of service are not included, however, for the officers. The supplemental responses accordingly do not supply a complete response to Special Interrogatory No. 20.

 

The Court accordingly GRANTS the Motion as to Special Interrogatory No. 20. As to Special Interrogatory No. 21, the Motion is DENIED.

 

(3)   Special Interrogatories, Set One, No. 27

 

The Court notes that the Motion is unclear about whether it seeks further response from TTMSI to Special Interrogatory No. 25 or No. 27, or both. The Notice of Motion, introduction to the accompanying memorandum, and declaration of Plaintiff’s counsel all indicate that the Motion requests an order compelling further response to Special Interrogatory No. 25, but further argument in the memorandum and the separate statement contend that it is the response to No. 27 that is deficient. (Mot., at pp. 2-3, 7; Miller Decl., ¶ 19; Pl. SS, at pp. 12-13.) Moreover, the most recent meet-and-confer correspondence between the parties appears focused on responses to the interrogatories noted in the Notice of Motion and Plaintiff’s counsel’s declaration, specifically Nos. 3-8, 20-21, and 25. (Miller Decl., Exhs. 8-10.) Confirming this confusion, TTMSI’s Opposition addresses Special Interrogatory No. 25 but not No. 27.

 

Insofar as Plaintiff seeks further response to Special Interrogatory No. 25, Plaintiff provides no argument regarding why TTMSI’s response is inadequate. The Court accordingly DENIES the Motion as to Special Interrogatory No. 25.

 

Insofar as Plaintiff really seeks further response to Special Interrogatory No. 27, the Court finds that the moving papers do not provide adequate notice to TTMSI of the relief being sought. The Court accordingly DENIES the Motion as to Special Interrogatory No. 27.

 

C.    Monetary Sanctions

 

“The court may impose a monetary sanction and order that one engaging in the misuse of the discovery process … pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney’s fees, incurred by anyone as a result of that conduct.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2023.030, subd. (a).) Misuse of the discovery process includes making an evasive response to discovery. (Id., § 2023.010, subd. (f).)

 

“The court shall impose a monetary sanction … against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel a further response to interrogatories, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (d).)

 

Plaintiff requests that the Court impose sanctions against TTMSI in the amount of $3,000.00, calculated as 6 hours’ work in connection with preparing the Motion and supporting papers, preparing a reply, and appearing on the Motion. (Miller Decl., ¶ 21.)

 

As the Court has found that TTMSI has provided incomplete responses to the interrogatories at issue without substantiating its objections, sanctions are appropriate. TTMSI contends that the amount of sanctions sought is egregious given that Plaintiff has filed four identical motions regarding these same interrogatories, one directed to each Defendant. (Opp’n, at p. 8.) This is the last of the four motions being heard, and the Court agrees that they are substantially similar such that awarding the full amount requested would result in compensation disproportionate to the reasonable amount of work incurred. The Court finds Plaintiff’s counsel’s hourly rate of $500.00 to be reasonable and finds the total and reasonable attorney fees in connection with the Motion to be $750.00 (.5 hours reasonably expended on the moving and reply papers and one hour for the appearance, at the rate of $500.00).

 

The request for sanctions is thus GRANTED IN PART.

 

CONCLUSION

 

Plaintiff’s motion to compel Defendant Thousand Trails Managements Services, Inc.’s further responses to Plaintiff’s Special Interrogatories, Set One is GRANTED IN PART. Defendant Thousand Trails Managements Services, Inc. is ordered to serve verified further responses to Plaintiff’s Special Interrogatories, Set One, Nos. 3-8 and 20 within 20 days.

 

Plaintiff’s request for monetary sanctions is GRANTED IN PART. Defendant Thousand Trails Managements Services, Inc. is ordered to pay $750.00 to Plaintiff within 20 days.
