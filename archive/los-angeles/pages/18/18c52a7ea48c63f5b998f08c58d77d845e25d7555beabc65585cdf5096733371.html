Case Number: 21SMCV01145    Hearing Date: July 16, 2026    Dept: I

This is a request to issue an out of state commission for two depositions.  The depositions, if they occur, are for two Mexican nationals residing in Mexico.  

 

The depositions are sought as to Mr. and Ms. Escamilla (not married to each other—Mr. Escamilla is Ms. Escamilla’s father).  Defense counsel states that she has been trying to contact them since 2023.  Ms. Escamilla did not respond, but Mr. Escamilla stated that he was open to a meeting.  According to the defense, he would testify that he did not know that plaintiff used a Mexican address as his address when applying for immigration status with Mexico.  This is relevant, the defense claims, because either Fuller was lying to the Mexican government (which is Fuller has already said under oath is what occurred) or he was living in Mexico and that undercuts his claim that he was in a committed relationship and living with McCourt.  After the summary judgment proceedings concluded, defense counsel renewed the attempt to talk to these two witnesses, but both ignored here communications and plaintiff refused to assist the defense, purportedly insisting on formal discovery.

 

Ms. Escamilla is now plaintiff’s wife.  Defendants believe that he was involved in a committed relationship with her at the same time that he and defendant McCourt were allegedly in an exclusive committed relationship and formed their Marvin agreement.  Defendants intend to inquire as to the timing of the relationship between plaintiff and Ms. Escamilla to disprove the relationship with Ms. McCourt.  Mr. Escamilla allegedly has knowledge as to architectural plans related to land purchased in Mexico.  The land was purchased allegedly by 18-Rabbit, an asset that plaintiff and McCourt allegedly owned as part of the Marvin arrangement.  Mr. Escamilla will also be questioned on the alleged use of his address by plaintiff when plaintiff applied for immigration status in Mexico.  Defendants contend that Mr. Escamilla might also testify that plaintiff is not actually married to Ms. Escamilla.  According to defendants, this testimony will go to the question of cohabitation as well as defendants’ theory that plaintiff has a pattern of defrauding women by claiming an exclusive and committed relationship when he has no such actual intent.  Defendants also note in reply that there is another issue.  In plaintiff’s opposition, he discloses that Ms. Escamilla caused a criminal investigation in Mexico to be opened as against defense counsel Armenta.  Defendants also contend that they want to explore the basis of such claims.

 

Frankly, the court is torn.  Were this motion brought in 2024, the court almost certainly would have granted it.  Out of state commissions are hardly uncommon and the bar is very low.  (Of course, the fact that the court issues the commission does not mean that the deposition will occur.  There are, presumably, proceedings in Mexico that would need to go forward.)  The court’s concern is that this is one of many discovery motions, and the trial is coming in November—and this court fully intends to try the case on the date now set.  Plaintiff has been contending that defendants are essentially papering him to death through discovery.  And, he claims, that this will impede his ability to be ready for trial.

 

The court’s concern is this.  There seems to be plenty of evidence concerning the alleged relationship between plaintiff and Ms. Escamilla.  Defendants already have emails, and apparently the two are married (or at least that is how it appears).  The emails well pre-date the end of the Fuller/McCourt relationship.  It is hard to see how a deposition in Mexico is critical to the case.  And as to Mr. Escamilla, it appears that this goes primarily to general credibility, and the court really does not believe that is appropriate.  Further, as to the residence address, the court’s recollection is that Fuller already admitted under oath that he lied on his application to the Mexican government in that regard.  What gives the court more pause is the criminal investigation in Mexico.  The court has seen Armenta in this case on many occasions.  It is fair to say that she is an attorney who is dedicated to representing her client to the fullest extent, as she is ethically required to do.  It is also fair to say that the court has not seen her cross any ethical lines, and certainly not to the point of something potentially criminal.  Armenta’s contention that Ms. Escamilla and plaintiff may have acted in some inappropriate way to get the criminal investigation initiated seems plausible because this court’s observation of her conduct would not give rise to any belief that she broke any laws in Mexico (or the United States, for that matter) in the conduct of this case.  But that said, allowing the depositions for that purpose seems like the tail wagging the dog.  The court questions whether any of that evidence would go to the jury given Evidence Code section 352 (assuming the defense even wanted to admit it).

 

Because of the trial’s proximity and the time that it will take actually to conduct these depositions if they go forward (in Mexico), the court is inclined to DENY the motions based to a large extent on the need to keep the trial date.  The court understands that defendants contend that these depositions will have no effect on the trial date.  And that would be true were plaintiff represented by counsel with significant resources.  But plaintiff is self-represented.  While that does not excuse him from discovery, it does play into the balance of hardships that the court must consider for this kind of discovery four months before trial and about 90 days before the discovery cut off.  On balance, the time and effort for the discovery is outweighed by the need to maintain the trial date and concentrate on other, more critical, discovery.

Case Number: 24SMCV03901    Hearing Date: July 16, 2026    Dept: I

This is an application by defendant to continue the trial.  The case was filed in August 2024.  A case management conference was held on January 22, 2025.  At that hearing, the current trial date of September 14, 2026, was set—approximately 20 months out.  A mediation completion date of March 1, 2026, was also set.  All of these dates were set with the agreement of both parties.  Defense counsel states that counsel is new to the case, having been retained in June 2026.  Counsel suggests that counsel needs more time to get up to speed, but more particularly, that there has not been sufficient time to do meaningful discovery. 

 

One problem is that the court does not really have a good slot for the case if it does not go forward on September 14, 2026, as planned.  The court is not trying to be difficult.  The court has about 1300 cases on its docket.  The court’s trial calendar assumes that 95% of the cases set for trial resolve without a continuance or the need of a trial.  That is probably not a low estimate, but it is not really high either.  That is why at the CMC the court expressly warned both parties that there would be no continuances absent extraordinary good cause—and that is reflected in the minute order of that date. 

 

There is a second reason.  The fact that discovery is not complete—and apparently has not even begun on the defense side—is hardly a good reason for a continuance.  Rather, it appears that the defense simply was not diligent during the year and a half before new counsel was retained.  (The court assumes that written discovery has since been propounded.)  A lack of diligence is not good cause to continue the trial or, for that matter, the discovery cut off.

 

The court will inquire of the parties as to the trial estimate.  Defendant’s estimate at the CMC was 2 days.  (Plaintiff had estimated 7 days.)  The court’s inclination is to DENY the motion.

Case Number: 25SMCV03160    Hearing Date: July 16, 2026    Dept: I

This is a continuation of the motion to approve the sale.  The court issued a detailed tentative at the last hearing.  However, during oral argument, plaintiffs orally in court offered to “sweeten the deal” significantly.  As a result, the court told the parties (all of them) to discuss whether the receiver believed that the new deal was such that he still wanted to proceed with the sale.  At the end of the day, the receiver and Bobs had worked out a term sheet.  Although it states that it is “non-binding,” Bobs made it clear at the hearing that it was in fact binding, and the receiver’s view was predicated on that understanding.  Based on the term sheet—which was binding but anticipated to be superseded by a long form agreement—the receiver tentatively withdrew the motion for sale.  It is fair to say that defendants were somewhat miffed.  They contended that a hastily drafted agreement ought not be approved by the court.  The court tentatively approved the agreement subject to defendants’ ability to file objections prior to this hearing, which they did.  Part of the pressure was that Hankey—the buyer who was the subject of the receiver’s motion—had given a “drop dead” date of July 10, 2026, to accept his offer or it would be deemed rescinded.  That meant that there was no time to commission a neutral valuation expert or the like.  Since then, apparently everyone has continued to negotiate.  Hankey did not rescind the offer and is still willing to bargain.  Bobs is also bargaining.  Defendants contend that there are lots of terms that are ambiguous.

 

So here is where we are.  First, if the receiver is withdrawing the motion, there is no sale before the court to approve.  It is the receiver’s motion, and, frankly, the odds that the court will approve the sale based solely on a motion by the defense is thin.  Second, this is no way to run a railroad.  Hankey is still negotiating notwithstanding his deadline.  The court is not sure that Bobs has finished negotiating.  The receiver is still negotiating.  If this were a $50,000 item, that might be ok, but this is a nine digit issue.  The court simply is not going to be pressured in this way.  Not by Bobs, not by defendants, and not by Hankey.

 

There is no basis to rule on anything today.  The record is too murky and the court does not even know what the current deals are.  As the court warned the parties, it is going on medical leave for four weeks starting Monday.  So here is what we well do.  The motion is CONTINUED until a date after the court’s return to be set today with consultation with all parties.  The court will order that anyone who wants to be heard on this motion will submit a LAST, BEST, AND FINAL offer no later than 21 days before the hearing.  There will be no sweeteners or amendments to that last, best, and final.  No later than 10 calendar days before the hearing, the receiver will file papers recommending which deal, if any, the receiver supports and the reasons therefor.  No later than 5 court days before the hearing, any party opposing the receiver’s recommendation may file papers explaining why.  Hankey is expressly invited to participate in this process.  The court will make a ruling based on the proposed deals and the receiver’s recommendation and any other papers filed.  If Hankey walks, then he does.  But at this stage, with further negotiations occurring even after last week’s hearing, the court simply does not believe that it has an adequate basis to accept or reject Bobs’ term sheet and it is not clear where the receiver even stands at this moment.

Case Number: 25SMCV06493    Hearing Date: July 16, 2026    Dept: I

This is a motion for sanctions filed by the defense.  There is no opposition.  The theory is that the prior motion to quash/demurrer was frivolous.  The court views this as a motion under section 128.5.  Assuming that proper notice was given, the motion is nonetheless DENIED.  While the court did view the motion to quash as without merit, the court is not prepared to start awarding sanctions at this time for that motion.  And as to the demurrer, while the court agrees that it was not in proper format, the party was self-represented, and the court is going to give more leeway for things like that under the circumstances.  The court is certainly not going to award 128.5 sanctions on that basis.  The motion is DENIED.

 

The court also has plaintiff’s ex parte application to shorten time to hear his motion for a protective order.  The hearing date was reserved for November 2, 2026, although it appears that the reservation has lapsed.  The application is DENIED.  First, the motion has not yet been filed.  The court is not going to start advancing dates for an unfiled motion.  Second, the court has no time to hear the substantive matter today.  Third, there has been no showing as to why it is that the motion need be heard early or outside of the regular course.

Case Number: 26SMCV03065    Hearing Date: July 16, 2026    Dept: I

This is the preliminary injunction hearing following the court’s granting of a TRO.  The court had issued a TRO on the theory that plaintiff asserted that she was being banned from the premises because she accurately reported an incident that ultimately resulted in the termination of an employee in a manner that would get to the Union representing that employee, and also because she posted what defendant contended were negative and defamatory comments about the venue.  The court could discern no harm to defendant by allowing plaintiff to return, and plaintiff had established that her time at the venue was an important part of her life.  Plaintiff also at least raised the possibility that case law did not allow for the arbitrary banning of a patron from a business.  The major case upon which plaintiff relief for that proposition is In re Cox (1970) 3 Cal.3d 205.  In that case, Justice Tobriner of our Supreme Court discussed the interplay between freedom of speech and a business owner’s ability to exclude a person from the property.  The plaintiff there was arrested at a shopping center parking lot and charged with a violation of a local ordinance for remaining on the premises after being told by the manager to leave.  Plaintiff claimed that the defendant could not arbitrarily eject him—a potential customer—from the premises because under the Unruh Act, such conduct was not allowed.  The Court agreed that a business owner could not arbitrarily exclude a person from the premises, although the Court stated that reasonable regulations were appropriate.  In particular, the Court rejected an overly narrow interpretation of the prohibited bases upon which a customer could be refused pursuant to the Unruh Act but rather concluded that the Unruh Act was meant to bar all arbitrary discrimination.  However, the Court was careful to note that a business could insist that a customer leave the premises where there was a reason.  And the Court stated that a business could promulgate reasonable deportment regulations rationally related to the services provided.  The Court noted that the business, there, was generally open to the public like a town center would be and that it provided important functions upon which the public relied.  Accordingly, the business could not, the Court held, bar people who dressed in a particular way any more than it could bar someone based on race.  Cox certainly helps plaintiff here.  But what it essentially does is shift the burden onto the defense to establish a legitimate business-related reason for excluding plaintiff rather than an arbitrary one.

 

Here, the defendant initially listed two grounds, both reasserted in this opposition.  The first is that plaintiff wrote a letter discussing potential drug use by two patrons and named them.  It was not drugs like heroin or cocaine; it was a prescription medication.  But plaintiff’s point was that the combination of those prescription drugs with the consumption of what would otherwise be a reasonable amount of alcohol made them appear to be intoxicated and led to a slip and fall, and plaintiff wanted to document that the cause was not an employee serving alcohol to an obviously intoxicated person.  Defendant contends that writing the letter and using the patrons’ full names was not proper.  The court has some misgivings.  Had plaintiff posted the full names on the internet, the court would have no problem agreeing with the defense.  But this was in a letter given to the employee in question who was potentially going to be disciplined over the incident and forwarded to the union.  (It is addressed “Union 11,” which is the relevant local.)  Defendant also contends that plaintiff verbally told hotel personnel of the incident and again used names.  This seems thin to the court.  It was a conversation that related to an incident that plaintiff saw.  One can argue whether plaintiff’s inferences and conclusions were proper, but it is not “gossip” in the sense defendant suggests, and it was not published to the world, but apparently told to hotel staff who plaintiff at least thought needed to know.  The negative posting is a bit harder.  Plaintiff apparently posted that she was told by hotel management to keep quiet about the theft of some of her belongings.  The defense says that this was an untrue statement and defamatory.  Whether a business can bar someone from the premises who makes defamatory comments about the business is a very close question.  There is a statute that bars a business from conditioning a sale on the customer agreeing not to post negative reviews about the product, but that is not at issue here.  The court notes, though, that plaintiff took down the post when defendant requested that she do so.  The court’s view is that this posting, taken down at defendant’s request, is too thin to constitute a valid reason to bar her from the premises.  The defense also complains that plaintiff over-used the hotel’s complaint line.  The court frankly does not even understand that allegation.

 

At the last hearing, the court granted the TRO, in large part based on the lack of any harm that might come to defendant.  However, this is the preliminary injunction stage.  According to the defense, after she was allowed to return to the premises, plaintiff loudly proclaimed that she is “the Queen” and that “You can’t get rid of the Queen.”  She is also accused of being disruptive at the bar and trying to entice employees to violate company policy on tipping.  The court’s concern is that plaintiff is using the injunction as a sword rather than a shield.  By proclaiming that she could not be removed, she was indicating that she was immune from other rules, which is not true.  By being disruptive, she provided the defense with a legitimate reason under Cox to remove her.  By trying to convince employees to violate the tipping policy (by taking a separate tip rather than adhere to the pooling policy), plaintiff also provided a reason to exclude her.  But having said that, the evidence the defense provides does not support the bold claims made in the brief.  As to the tipping issue, the court has no admissible evidence that this ever happened.  The court has a hearsay declaration in which the declarant claims to have been told by Turner that plaintiff had asked to tip him directly.  But that is classic hearsay.  Worse, defendants submitted a declaration by Turner, who makes no such statement, which is a red flag to the court.  The “Queen” comments were, according to plaintiff, made to Turner (who did declare that she made a “Queen” comment), but plaintiff states that is in part because she, her guest, and Turner were talking about Honeybees and that the comment was not made loudly to the world, but only to Turner and her guest and could not otherwise be heard.  Plaintiff’s companion confirms that testimony.  Turner is completely silent as to context.  There is no other evidence that plaintiff was in any way disruptive.

 

The court will need to consider this more carefully, but the court is very concerned with plaintiff’s claims that defendant is making things up.  That is especially so given that the tipping comment was made to Turner, who allegedly related it but Turner did not confirm it in his declaration (and plaintiff denies it, showing that she routinely adds a tip amount to the tab, which the court would assume is consistent with the pooling policy).  The court may need to hear from Turner directly.  He is also the person who witnessed the “Queen” comment.  The court is inclined therefore to CONTINUE the matter until the court returns from leave (meaning about a month).  The alternative would be to grant the motion on the basis that the defense evidence of new conduct appears to be suspect, but without prejudice to allowing defendant to move to dissolve the preliminary injunction based on firmer evidence.  The court prefers to continue, though, because so much briefing has already been done.  The court notes that there are a host of objections.  The court does not really see any of them as terribly consequential other than the one discussed herein.
