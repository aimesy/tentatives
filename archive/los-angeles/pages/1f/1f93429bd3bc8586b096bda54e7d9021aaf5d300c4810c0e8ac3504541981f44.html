DERRICK BRUCE,

Plaintiff,

vs.

FCA US, LLC, et al.

Defendants.

Case No.:

24STCV26593

Hearing Date:

September 16, 2026

ORDER GRANTING MOTION FOR SUMMARY ADJUDICATION

 

I.         BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Derrick Bruce (“Bruce”) entered into a warranty contract with Defendant FCA US, LLC (“FCA”) regarding a 2019 Dodge Challenger (“subject vehicle”). The subject vehicle allegedly has defects and nonconformities making the car effectively worthless. GM allegedly was aware of the subject vehicle’s defects and failed to disclose the existence of defects at all times to Bruce. Defendant Cerritos Dodge Chrysler Jeep Ram (“Cerritos”) allegedly failed to repair the vehicle. Bruce sued Defendants for six causes of action, including failure to repair and fraudulent inducement.

On May 28, 2026, FCA filed the instant motion for summary adjudication. No party filed an opposition.

III.      LEGAL STANDARD

A party may move for summary judgment “if it is contended that the action has no merit or that there is no defense to the action or proceeding.”  (Code Civ. Proc. § 437c, subd. (a).[1]) “[I]f all the evidence submitted, and all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law,” the moving party will be entitled to summary judgment. (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.) 

The moving party bears an initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing of the nonexistence of any triable issue of material fact, and if he does so, the burden shifts to the opposing party to make a prima facie showing of the existence of a triable issue of material fact. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850, citing to § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence.  (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

Neither a moving nor responding party may rely on the mere allegations or denials of its pleadings. A moving party must submit specific admissible evidence showing that the responding party cannot establish at least one element of his, her or its cause of action or defense. The responding party, to defeat the motion, must submit specific admissible evidence showing that a triable issue of material fact does exist as to that element of the cause of action or defense. (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

The “Golden Rule” on a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication is that “if [a fact] is not set forth in the separate statement, it does not exist.” (Zimmerman, Rosenfeld, Gersh & Leeds LLP v. Larson (2005) 131 Cal.App.4th 1466, 1477, citing United Community Church v. Garcin (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 327, 337.).

 

IV.       DISCUSSION

FCA requests the Court grant summary adjudication as to the second, fourth and sixth causes of action for violation of Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (b), breach of implied warranty of merchantability, and fraudulent inducement – concealment, respectively. FCA requests the second and fourth causes of action be granted summary adjudication as Bruce cannot present any triable issues of material fact, while the sixth should be subject to adjudication as it is barred by case law.

A.        Statement of Facts

On August 25, 2019, Bruce purchased the subject vehicle from Jeep Chrysler Dodge Ram Fiat of Ontario (“Ontario”). (UMF 1.) Bruce did not obtain any information about the subject vehicle via research or from FCA prior to purchase. (UMF 60, 71.) The vehicle was accompanied by a New Vehicle Limited Warranty that covered repairs needed to correct defects in material or workmanship of covered parts. (UMF 2-3.)

On August 27, 2019, Bruce presented the subject vehicle to Ontario for a detail service. (UMF 6.) From November 2019 to January 2023, Bruce brought in the subject vehicle to Cerritos 10 times for routine maintenance. (UMF 7-17.) From July 1, 2023, to October 25, 2024, Bruce brought in the subject vehicle to Cerritos for issues with the cooling system, cooling fan, and coolant loss 7 times. (UMF 18-22.) On November 5, 2024, Bruce presented the subject vehicle to Cerritos due to a concern that the vehicle struggled to start. (UMF 24.)

B.        Second Cause of Action – Violation of Civil Code Section 1793.2, Subdivision (b)

FCA first requests the Court grant summary adjudication as to the causes of action for violation of Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (b), as there is no indication that any repair did not occur within a reasonable amount of time. Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (b) provides that, when services and/or repair are necessary because goods do not conform with express warranties, repair shall be commenced within a reasonable time by the manufacturer, or within 30 days if not otherwise outlined.

The undisputed evidence shows that all repairs were completed within, at most, 9 days. (UMF 25-26.) No vehicle visits to the dealership lasted more than 30 days, the limit for reasonableness under Civil Code section 1793.2. The fact certain repairs concerned the same issue does not artificially extend the repair period. As outlined by the persuasive authority Bui v. Mercedes-Benz U.S.A., LLC (N.D. Cal. 2024) 744 F.Supp.3d 910, 913, Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (b), is only concerned with whether a single repair attempt took more than 30 days. If no single attempt took more than 30 days, as are the facts in this case, there is no basis for a Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (b) claim.

FCA has met its burden, which shifts to Bruce. As Bruce did not oppose this motion, the Court grants summary adjudication as to this cause of action.

C.        Fourth Cause of Action - Breach of Implied Warranty

FCA requests the Court grant summary adjudication as to the cause of action for breach of implied warranty as, under Civil Code section 1795.5, subdivision (c), the duration of a warranty of merchantability is limited to one year following the sale of a new consumer good. FCA argues that Bruce cannot prove the subject vehicle was unmerchantable at the time of purchase or within the following year, as there is no claim of defect during the time period or any indication of a ‘latent’ defect. Merchantability provides protection from “fundamental defects”; therefore, a vehicle need not be perfect, but only “reasonable suited for ordinary use.” (Brand v. Hyundai Motor America (2014) 226 Cal. App. 4th 1538, 1545.)

Here, FCA has provided sufficient evidence that the subject vehicle did not have any fundamental defects that made it unsuitable for ordinary use. During the year period following Bruce’s purchase, Bruce only brought the vehicle in for detail service or routine maintenance. (UMF 6-10.) Potential defects of the cooling system, the first identified issue, were not a problem until July 2023, almost four years after purchase of the vehicle. (UMF 18.)

There is no indication Bruce has any evidence to demonstrate a latent defect existed at the time of sale. Bruce did not oppose this motion with any evidence to support evidence of a latent defect, the Court grants summary adjudication as to this cause of action.

D.        Sixth Cause of Action - Fraudulent Inducement - Concealment

FCA requests the Court grant summary adjudication as to the cause of action for fraudulent inducement – concealment, as Bruce has no evidence to support the existence of the alleged ‘engine defect’, FCA’s knowledge and concealment of the ‘engine defect’, or any damage associated with the concealment. Fraud based on concealment requires a showing of the defendant concealing or suppressing a material fact, the defendant having a duty to disclose the fact, intentionality in concealment to defraud a plaintiff, justifiable reliance, and sustained damages.   (Bigler-Engler v. Breg, Inc. (2017) 7 Cal.App.5th 276, 310-311.)

Here, FCA provides sufficient evidence that Bruce does not have evidence to establish existence, knowledge, or concealment of the alleged defect. A defendant may satisfy their burden on summary judgment by showing that a claim “cannot be established” because of the lack of evidence on some essential element of the claim.  (Union Bank v. Superior Court (1995) 31 Cal.App.4th 574, 590.) In response to FCA’s discovery requests, Bruce was unable to provide any facts or documents supporting essential elements of this cause of action. (UMF 45.) As Bruce did not oppose this motion, there is no indication any evidence exists calling into dispute FCA’s knowledge or concealment of this action.

The Court grants summary adjudication as to this cause of action. The Court does not address the merits of the duty of disclosure or economic loss rule arguments, as there is already sufficient basis to grant summary adjudication.

V.        CONCLUSION

Defendant FCA US, LLC’s motion for summary adjudication is GRANTED, in full.

FCA is to give notice.

ORDER GRANTING MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT

 

I.         BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Derrick Bruce (“Bruce”) entered into a warranty contract with Defendant FCA US, LLC (“FCA”) regarding a 2019 Dodge Challenger (“subject vehicle”). The subject vehicle allegedly has defects and nonconformities making the car effectively worthless. GM allegedly was aware of the subject vehicle’s defects and failed to disclose the existence of defects at all times to Bruce. Defendant Cerritos Dodge Chrysler Jeep Ram (“Cerritos”) allegedly failed to repair the vehicle. Bruce sued Defendants for six causes of action, including negligent repair.

On May 28, 2026, Cerritos filed the instant motion for summary judgment. No party filed an opposition.

III.      LEGAL STANDARD

A party may move for summary judgment “if it is contended that the action has no merit or that there is no defense to the action or proceeding.”  (Code Civ. Proc.[1] § 437c, subd. (a).) “[I]f all the evidence submitted, and all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law,” the moving party will be entitled to summary judgment. (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.) 

The moving party bears an initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing of the nonexistence of any triable issue of material fact, and if he does so, the burden shifts to the opposing party to make a prima facie showing of the existence of a triable issue of material fact. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850, citing to § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence.  (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

Neither a moving nor responding party may rely on the mere allegations or denials of its pleadings. A moving party must submit specific admissible evidence showing that the responding party cannot establish at least one element of his, her or its cause of action or defense. The responding party, to defeat the motion, must submit specific admissible evidence showing that a triable issue of material fact does exist as to that element of the cause of action or defense. (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

The “Golden Rule” on a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication is that “if [a fact] is not set forth in the separate statement, it does not exist.” (Zimmerman, Rosenfeld, Gersh & Leeds LLP v. Larson (2005) 131 Cal.App.4th 1466, 1477, citing United Community Church v. Garcin (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 327, 337.).

 

IV.       DISCUSSION

Cerritos requests the Court grant summary judgment as to the sole cause of action asserted against Cerritos, negligent repair, as it is barred by the economic loss rule and Bruce cannot present any triable issue of material fact.

A.        Statement of Facts

On August 25, 2019, Bruce purchased the subject vehicle from Jeep Chrysler Dodge Ram Fiat of Ontario (“Ontario”). (UMF 1.) Bruce presented the vehicle for repair of a ‘warrantable concern’ to Cerritos one time during the standard powertrain warranty period and four times during Bruce’s optional mechanical protection service warranty period. (UMF 4.) During these visits, Bruce and Cerritos contracted for performance of auto repair services; services were performed pursuant to contract. (UMF 4, 8.)

No personal injury is claimed stemming from the repair, nor is there any evidence of intentional property damages. (UMF 10.)

B.        Fifth Cause of Action – Negligent Repair

The Court finds the cause of action for negligent repair is barred by the economic loss rule. “In general, there is no recovery in tort for negligently inflicted ‘purely economic losses,’ meaning financial harm unaccompanied by physical or property damage.” (Sheen v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2022) 12 Cal.5th 905, 922.) There are few exceptions to traditional contract remedy restrictions: physical injury, breach of covenant of good faith and fair dealing in insurance contracts, wrongful discharge in violation of fundamental public policy, or fraudulent inducement. (Erlich v. Menezes (1999) 21 Cal.4th 543, 551-52.)

Here, none of the exceptions apply. Bruce has not alleged any physical injury to his person or intentional damage to his car. Neither FCA nor Cerrito are insurance brokers, and therefore no potentially applicable contracts concern insurance. There is no evidence of wrongful discharge in violation of public policy, nor is there any indication of fraudulent inducement. Therefore, any alleged violation of the alleged contract cannot be a basis for seeking remedies in tort. This cause of action fails, as a a matter of law.

As negligent repair is the only cause of action asserted against Cerritos, the Court grants summary judgment.

V.        CONCLUSION

Defendant Cerritos Dodge Chrysler Jeep Ram’s motion for summary judgment is GRANTED, in full.

Cerritos is to give notice.

Case Number: 25STCV25720    Hearing Date: September 16, 2026    Dept: 730

Superior Court of California

County of Los Angeles

Department 730

 

VINELAND CAPITAL GROUP, LLC, et al.

Plaintiffs,

vs.

WILLIAM E. SHAMLIAN, et al.,

Defendants.

Case No.:

25STCV25720

Hearing Date:

September 16, 2026

ORDER SUSTAINING, IN PART, AND OVERRULLING, IN PART, DEMURRER TO COMPLAINT WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND

 

I.         BACKGROUND

Plaintiffs Vineland Capital Group, LLC (“Vineland”) and Sun Valley Holdings, LLC (“Sun Valley”) entered into a contract with Defendant California Growers League (“CGL”) for a commercial lease of 11019 Penrose Street, Sun Valley, California (the “subject property”). During the pendency of the lease, CGL allegedly made material changes to the property plans without notifying Plaintiffs, violating the contract. Plaintiffs sued Defendants for three causes of action, including fraud.

On April 29, 2026, Defendants CGL and William E. Shamlian (“Shamlian”) (collectively “Moving Defendants”) filed the instant demurrer to the FAC. Plaintiffs filed an opposition. Moving Defendants filed a reply.

II.        request for judicial notice

The Court takes judicial notice of Moving Defendants’s requested documents, pursuant to Evidence Code section 452. Exhibit 1, Moving Defendants’ served Request for Admissions, and Exhibit 2, Sun Valley’s responses to the Request for Admissions, are admissible as admissions inconsistent with the allegations of the pleading before the Court. (See Del E. Webb Corp. v. Structural Materials Co. (1981) 123 Cal.App.3d 593, 604-605 [“The court will take judicial notice of records such as admissions, answers to interrogatories, affidavits, and the like, when considering a demurrer, only where they contain statements of the plaintiff or his agent which are inconsistent with the allegations of the pleading before the court.”].) Exhibit 3, notice of ruling on the Court’s order, is proper for judicial notice under Evidence Code section 452, subdivision (d).

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747 (Hahn).) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context. In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)¿ 

“A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed. (Code of Civil Procedure §§ 430.30, 430.70[1].) “The only issue involved in a demurrer hearing is whether the complaint, as it stands, unconnected with extraneous matters, states a cause of action.” (Hahn, supra, 147 Cal.App.4th at p. 747.)

At the pleading stage, a plaintiff need only allege ultimate facts sufficient to apprise the defendant of the factual basis for the claim against him. (Semole v. Sansoucie (1972) 28 Cal. App. 3d 714, 721.) A “demurrer does not, however, admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law alleged in the pleading, or the construction of instruments pleaded, or facts impossible in law.” (S. Shore Land Co. v. Petersen (1964) 226 Cal.App.2d 725, 732 (internal citations omitted).)

III.      DISCUSSION

Moving Defendants request the Court sustain the demurrer as to Sun Valley, in full, as Sun Valley is not entitled to maintain an action or proceeding in the state of California. Moving Defendants also request the Court sustain the demurrer to the FAC, in full, as it fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action.

A.        Sun Valley

Moving Defendants request the Court sustain the demurrer as to Sun Valley as Sun Valley is not entitled to bring the subject lawsuit. Pursuant to Corporation Code section 17708.07, subdivision (a), “a foreign limited liability company transacting intrastate business in this state shall not maintain an action or proceeding in this state unless it has a certificate of registration to transact intrastate business in this state.”

Although the FAC states Sun Valley is, and all times relevant to the complaint, was a “Limited Liability Compan[y] duly formed and operating lawfully[,]” Moving Defendants provided judicially noticeable evidence to the contrary. (FAC ¶ 1.) Moving Defendants served requests for admissions on Sun Valley, requesting they admit that Sun Valley: 1) was organized under the laws of the State of New Mexico; 2) is registered with the Secretary of State of New Mexico; and 3) is not and has never been registered with the California Secretary of State. (RJN Ex. 1.) Sun Valley served verified responses, admitting each request. (RJN Ex. 2.) As this is a verified admissions that contradicts the allegations of the FAC, the Court may consider it when ruling on demurrer. (See Del E. Webb Corp. v. Structural Materials Co. (1981) 123 Cal.App.3d 593, 604-605.)

As the evidence demonstrates Sun Valley is a foreign limited liability company transacting intrastate business without a certificate of registration, Sun Valley cannot bring or maintain an action in this state. Therefore, Sun Valley’s claims are subject to demurrer. The burden is on the pleading party to show in what manner he can amend the complaint, and how that amendment will change the legal effect of the pleading. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349; Hendy v. Losse (1991) 54 Cal.3d 723, 742.) Sun Valley provides no basis upon which Sun Valley can or will cure this issue, beyond saying “the appropriate remedy would be a limited stay or abatement to permit compliance.” (Opp., pg. 5 ¶¶ 9-10.) As Sun Valley was not registered at the time of filing this action, Sun Valley was never entitled to file this action; later obtaining registration cannot cure that Sun Valley had no right to file this action.

The Court sustains the demurrer as to Sun Valley, without leave to amend.

B.        First Cause of Action - Breach of Contract

Moving Defendants request the Court sustain the demurrer against Vineland’s first cause of action for breach of contract, as Vineland did not state facts sufficient to establish a cause of action. “To prevail on a cause of action for breach of contract, the plaintiff must prove (1) the contract, (2) the plaintiff’s performance of the contract or excuse for nonperformance, (3) the defendant’s breach, and (4) the resulting damage to the plaintiff.” (Richman v. Hartley (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 1182, 1186.)

Plaintiffs assert two separate breaches of contract: breach of paragraph 18 of the subject contract, when CGL allegedly failed to disclose to Plaintiffs alterations to the premises and obtain Plaintiffs’ consent, and failure to pay rent and other fees associated with the lease, breaching the payment provisions. Paragraph 18 of the contract provides that “Tenant shall not make any alterations in or about the Premises, including installation of trade fixtures and signs, without Landlord's prior written consent, which shall not be unreasonably withheld. Any alterations to the Premises shall be done according to law and with required permits. Tenant shall give Landlord advance notice of the commencement date of any planned alteration, so that Landlord, at its option, may post a Notice of Non-Responsibility to prevent potential liens against Landlord's interest in the Premises” (FAC, Ex. A.)

Moving Defendants first argue that any allegations as to breach of paragraph 18 is barred by the provisions of the Management Services Agreement (“MSA”), which Moving Defendants allege is subject to judicial notice as an exhibit attached to the Request for Admissions (“RFA”). The Court disagrees; although the RFA is admissible for purposes of evaluating admissions contradictory to the allegations of the complaint, not all attachments to the RFA are judicially noticeable without caveat. (Supra Del E. Webb Corp., 123 Cal.App.3d 604-605.) Moving Defendants request the Court take judicial notice of a particular portion of the MSA, allowing Moving Defendants the right to make ‘leasehold improvements’, as it is allegedly contradictory to the FAC’s assertion that Moving Defendants failed to obtain Plaintiffs’ consent in advance of material changes to the property. (FAC ¶ 7.) However, the scope of a ‘Leasehold improvement’ is not specifically outlined in the MSA beyond general improvements and equipment. (RJN, Ex. 1, Ex. C.)

In taking into account potentially contradictory admission responses, ‘[t]he hearing on demurrer may not be turned into a contested evidentiary hearing through the guise of having the court take judicial notice of affidavits, declarations, depositions, and other such material which was filed on behalf of the adverse party and which purports to contradict the allegations and contentions of the plaintiff.’” (Id.) As the scope of what is included in ‘Leasehold Improvement’ is up for debate, this is not a judicially noticeable piece of evidence. The Court would be required to effectively hold a mini-evidentiary hearing to determine the applicability. Therefore, the MSA cannot be used as evidence to demonstrate the FAC is not sufficiently pled as to lack of prior consent.

Furthermore, the Court notes there is no judicially noticeable evidence to indicate that the MSA or Purchase Agreement (“PSA”) are attributable to either Plaintiff. The PSA is signed by Aram Sogomonian, CEO of Hempen, Inc., California Growers League Management, LLC (“CGLM”), and CGL. (RJN, Ex. 1, Ex. B.) The MSA is signed by CGL and CGLM. (RJN, Ex. 1, Ex. C.) There is no evidence either constitutes an agreement by Plaintiffs Vineland or Sun Valley to allow Moving Defendants to make changes.

Moving Defendants have provided no judicially noticeable evidence demonstrating Plaintiffs gave pre-existing consent for Moving Defendants to alter plans. Therefore, the allegation that Moving Defendants acted without consent, violating paragraph 18 of the subject contract, is still well pled. As Plaintiffs have articulated a cause of action for breach of contract, the Court overrules the demurrer on this cause of action. The Court does not address the argument concerning payment provisions, as that is a secondary argument on the breach of contract cause of action.

C.        Second Cause of Action – Fraud

Moving Defendants request the Court sustain the demurrer as to the second cause of action as Plaintiffs have insufficiently pled this cause of action. Plaintiffs appear to specifically be pleading under a theory of intentional misrepresentation. “The elements of fraud that will give rise to a tort action for deceit are: “ ‘(a) misrepresentation (false representation, concealment, or nondisclosure); (b) knowledge of falsity (or ‘scienter’); (c) intent to defraud, i.e., to induce reliance; (d) justifiable reliance; and (e) resulting damage.’ ” (Engalla v. Permanente Medical Group, Inc. (1997) 15 Cal.4th 951, 974.)

Plaintiffs’ FAC alleges that Defendants allege they would fully disclose, obtain permission for, and comply with the terms and conditions of the lease during in person meetings with Sogomonian on behalf of Plaintiffs in order to induce Plaintiffs to enter into the lease agreement. Defendants allegedly knew the representations were false when made and were made with intent to deceive Plaintiffs.

Moving Defendants first rely upon the text of the MSA, which, as previously addressed, is not judicially noticeable for these purposes on demurrer. Therefore, the Court does not consider this argument.

Moving Defendants next argue Plaintiffs failed to provide facts to demonstrate intent and/or justifiable reliance. The Court disagrees, and notes Moving Defendants rely upon case law addressing a final trial ruling, rather than standards on demurrer. Plaintiffs sufficiently pled Moving Defendants intent to deceive Plaintiffs and that Plaintiffs justifiably relied upon these assertions. “Justifiable reliance is ordinarily a question of fact that is not properly determined on demurrer, but ‘whether a party’s reliance was justified may be decided as a matter of law if reasonable minds can come to only one conclusion based on the facts,’ which facts include consideration of ‘the knowledge, education and experience’ of the person whose reliance is at issue.”  (Amiodarone Cases (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 1091, 1111.) Here, there is no evidence to support as a matter of law that Plaintiffs may not have justifiably relied upon the alleged fraudulent statements.

The Court overrules the demurrer to this cause of action.

D.        Violation of the UCL – Third Cause of Action

Moving Defendants finally request the Court sustain the demurrer as to the third cause of action for violation of the UCL, as the complaint does sufficiently plead facts to support fraudulent or unlawful practices. The UCL identifies unfair competition as any unlawful, unfair, or fraudulent business act or practice. (Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200.) Each category represents a separate and distinct basis for liability. (Id.; Beaver v. Tarsadia Hotels (9th Cir. 2016) 816 F.3d 1170, 1177.)

The FAC specifically alleges that Defendants actions were fraudulent and illegal, not unfair. As the Court found the section on fraud was sufficiently pled, the Court also finds the claim for violation of the UCL pursuant to fraud is also sufficiently pled. Therefore, the Court overrules the demurrer as to this cause of action.

 

IV.       CONCLUSION

Moving Defendants’ demurrer is sustained, in part, as to Sun Valley’s claims, and otherwise overruled. The Court does not grant leave to amend.

Moving Defendants are ordered to give notice.

JANE DOE,

Plaintiff,

vs.

KEST INDUSTRIES, LLC, et al.,

Defendants.

Case No.:

25STCV33104

Hearing Date:

September 16, 2026

ORDER DENYING MOTION FOR LEAVE TO AMEND COMPLAINT

 

I.         BACKGROUND

During Plaintiff Veronica Stoever’s (“Stoever”) tenancy, Stoever’s landlords, Defendants Goldrich & Kest Industries, LLC (“GK”) and Bunker Hill Apartments, LLC (“BH”) allegedly harassed, distressed, and violated the warranty of habitability with Stoever. Landlord Defendants’s attorney, Defendant Kimball, Tirey & St. John LLP (“KTS”) allegedly also harassed and denied Stoever her legal rights. Landlord defendants hired Defendant Envirocheck, Inc. (“EC”) to test Stoever’s apartment. EC found no trace of mold, despite Stoever alleging otherwise. Stoever sued Defendants for thirty-three causes of action.

On June 11, 2026, Stoever filed the instant motion for leave to amend the complaint. GK and Defendant Greystar California, Inc. (“Greystar”) filed an opposition. Stoever replied.

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (a)(1)[1], “[t]he court may, in furtherance of justice, and on any terms as may be proper, allow a party to amend any pleading or proceeding by adding or striking out the name of any party, or by correcting a mistake in the name of a party, or a mistake in any other respect; and may, upon like terms, enlarge the time for answer or demurrer. The court may likewise, in its discretion, after notice to the adverse party, allow, upon any terms as may be just, an amendment to any pleading or proceeding in other particulars; and may upon like terms allow an answer to be made after the time limited by this code.” 

A motion to amend a pleading before trial must 1) include a copy of the proposed amendment or amended pleading; 2) state what allegations in the previous pleading are to be deleted and where, by page, paragraph and line number, the deleted allegations are located; and 3) state what allegations are proposed to be added, if any, and where, by page, paragraph and line number, the allegations are located. (Cal. Rules of Court rule 3.1324, subd. (a).) It must also include a support declaration specifying 1) the effect of the amendment; 2) why the amendment is necessary and proper; 3) when the facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered; and 4) the reason why the request for amendment was not made earlier. (Id. at subd. (b).)

 

III.      DISCUSSION

Stoever’s motion for leave to amend the complaint fails to comply with the requirements of California Rules of Court rule 3.1324, as it does not identify the allegations to be added or deleted from the previous complaint, nor does it contain a declaration.

California Rules of Court rule 3.1324, subdivision (a), requires any motion to amend a pleading include a copy of the proposed amendment and state what allegations are to be added and deleted, identified by page, paragraph, and line number. While Stoever’s motion includes a copy of the proposed Second Amended Complaint, it does not identify the allegations to be added or deleted beyond generally describing the potential plaintiffs to be added. This does not satisfy the requirements of California Rules of Court rule 3.1324, subdivision (a)(2-3).

Stoever also failed to submit a proper declaration. California Rules of Court rule 3.1324, subdivision (b), requires any motion to amend a pleading include a declaration in support of the motion. Stoever submitted a document titled “DECLARATION IN SUPPORT OF MOTION FOR LEAVE TO FILE SECOND AMENDED COMPLAINT,” attached to the moving papers. However, the ‘declaration’ does not comply with statutory requirements for a declaration. Pursuant to section 2015.5, a declaration must contain a signed and dated statement that attests the declaration is certified or declared to be true under penalty of perjury. If signed within California, it must include the location of execution; if signed outside of California, the declaration must state it specifically is certified under penalty of perjury under the laws of the State of California. (Ibid.) Stoever’s declaration does not contain a wet or digital signature. It also does not certify the declaration was made under penalty of perjury. Therefore, Stoever failed to attach a declaration as required by court rules.

Stoever has failed to comply with California Rules of Court rule 3.1324’s requirements. Therefore, the Court denies the motion.

Finally, the Court notes Stoever identifies herself as a licensed attorney who is representing the 15 other potential plaintiffs on a contingency fee basis. (Mot., Stoever Decl. ¶ 11.) However, Stoever has never identified herself by state bar number on any moving papers. The Court is unsure if Stoever is a currently licensed attorney capable of representing other individuals absent this information.

IV.       CONCLUSION

Plaintiff Stoever’s motion for leave to amend is DENIED.
