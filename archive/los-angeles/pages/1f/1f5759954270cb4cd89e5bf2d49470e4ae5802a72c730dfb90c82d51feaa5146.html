Case Number: 25STCV01499    Hearing Date: September 24, 2026    Dept: 509

Jayesh Patel v. Jayesh Kumar, et al.

MOTION TO SET ASIDE DEFAULT AND VACATE DEFAULT JUDGMENT
 

MOVING PARTY: Defendant Jayesh Kumar

RESPONDING PARTY(S): Plaintiff Jayesh Patel

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

Plaintiff Jayesh Patel (Plaintiff) alleges he invested over $4 million dollars with the defendants to use for construction projects. Plaintiff alleges the defendants, including Jayesh Kumar, have failed or refused to return any money invested. This action followed.

On April 22, 2025, the court clerk entered Jayesh Kumar’s default. (See 04/22/2025 Entry of Default.)

On April 23, 2025, Jayesh Kumar filed a demurrer.

On July 9, 2025, Jayesh Kumar’s demurrer came to hearing where he was present via LACC. (See 7/9/25 Minute Order.) The Court took the demurrer off calendar and informed Jayesh Kumar directly that he was in default and he would need to file a motion to set aside the default for his demurrer to be heard. (Ibid, [“Defendant will need to file a motion to set aside the default if no stipulation is reached.”]).) This was reiterated in the notice of ruling served on Jayesh Kumar two days later. (See 7/11/25 Notice of Ruling, [“To proceed, Defendant must file a noticed motion to set aside the default and demonstrate a proper ground for relief.”]) Despite these clear instructions, no motion was filed.

On October 29, 2025, Jayesh Kumar appeared, via LACC, however, he was not present when case was called. (10/29/25 Minute Order.)

On January 20, 2026, Jayesh Kumar appeared, via LACC. In the hearing, the issue of Jayesh Kumar’s appearance in this case was addressed. (1/20/26 Minute Order.) 

Finally, on April 24, 2026, the court entered a default judgment against Jayesh Kumar in the amount of $7,193,158.96.

Jayesh Kumar now moves for relief from default and the default judgment. Plaintiff opposed.  [FN 1] 

On August 21, 2026, Jayesh Kumar continued this motion and on September 17, 2026, filed a reply. In its discretion, the Court considers this untimely reply.

TENTATIVE RULING

Jayesh Kumar’s Motion to Set Aside Default and Vacate Default Judgment is DENIED.

Plaintiff is ordered to give notice.

DISCUSSION:

Motion to Set Aside Default and Vacate Default Judgment

I. Legal Standard 
 
“The court may, upon any terms as may be just, relieve a party or his or her legal representative from a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against him or her through his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect.” (Code Civ. Proc., §¿473(b).)  
 
The mandatory relief provision of section 473(b) does not apply to self-represented parties because the statute requires an attorney’s sworn affidavit attesting to mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect, and by its terms distinguishes between the attorney and the client. (McClain v. Kissler¿(2019) 39 Cal.App.5th 399, 429.) When a party is self-represented, they are “one and the same” as their own attorney, so the mandatory relief provision is unavailable. (Ibid.) This means pro per defendants can only seek discretionary relief under section 473(b), which requires them to¿demonstrate¿excusable neglect and is reviewed under an abuse of discretion standard rather than the mandatory standard that would require the court to grant relief. (Ibid.)¿ 
 
Under discretionary relief, pro-per defendants face a higher burden because they must show their neglect was excusable,¿whereas¿represented parties seeking mandatory relief can obtain relief even for inexcusable attorney neglect (Pagnini v. Union Bank, N.A.¿(2018) 28 Cal.App.5th 298, 303.) The discretionary provision allows courts to grant relief based on mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect, but the determination rests within the trial court’s sound discretion and will not be disturbed on appeal absent a clear showing of abuse of discretion. (McClain,¿supra, 39 Cal.App.5th at p. 429.) 
 
Application for relief under Code of Civil Procedure, section¿473(b) must be made within six months of when the judgment, dismissal, order, or proceeding was taken.

II. Analysis

Jayesh Kumar moves to vacate the default judgment entered on April 24, 2026, and set aside the default entered on April 22, 2025. (Not. of Mot.) Jayesh Kumar “requests that the default and default judgment be set aside on the grounds that he did not receive notice and on the grounds of surprise and mistake.” (Mot. at p. 3:13-15.)

As an initial matter, this Court is mindful that Jayesh Kumar is in pro per. However, “pro. per. litigants are held to the same standards as attorneys.” (Kobayashi v. Superior Ct. (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 536, 543.) 

A. Jayesh Kumar’s Motion is Untimely

First, Jayesh Kumar’s motion is untimely. 

The court may, upon any terms as may be just, relieve a party or his or her legal representative from a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against him or her through his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect.  Application for this relief shall be accompanied by a copy of the answer or other pleading proposed to be filed therein, otherwise the application shall not be granted, and shall be made within a reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months, after the judgment, dismissal, order, or proceeding was taken.

 (Code Civ. Proc., §¿473, subd. (b), emphasis added.)  
 
Jayesh Kumar’s motion is untimely, as it was filed more than six months after entry of default on April 22, 2025. Instead, motion was filed on July 2, 2026, more than fourteen months after his default was entered. This Court understands that the motion was filed within 6 months from the entry of the default judgment.  Be that as it may, this motion is untimely and relief cannot be granted as to the entry of the default.

B. The Motion Would Likewise Be Denied on the Merits

As to the default judgment, and even if the Court were to consider this instant motion as timely as to the entry of default, the motion would be denied on the merits for numerous reasons..

First, not only does the record reflect Jayesh Kumar was properly served on March 10, 2025, but Jayesh Kumar was given notice, personally by this Court, no less than three times that he had defaulted in this action during his personal appearances in this action. There is no doubt that Jayesh Kumar was adequately informed of this action and his defaulted status.

Second, Jayesh Kumar was properly served with the default judgment. Jayesh Kumar was served by mail at 13940 Moorpark Street, #410, Sherman Oaks, CA 91423. (Lew Decl. ¶8.) This is the same address where he was served and is currently listed on the first page of this instant motion. Notably, Jayesh Kumar refused to consent to electronic service just days before the request for default judgment was filed, thereby necessitating service on his address. (See 1/20/26 Minute Order, [“Appearing parties do not consent to email notices.”])

Third, the issue of the one judgment rule has been mooted by the recent dismissal of all other defendants. (See 7/20/26 Requests for Dismissals.)

And finally, a judgment or order is “void” only “when the court entering that judgment lacked jurisdiction in a fundamental sense due to the entire absence of power to hear or determine the case resulting from the absence of authority over the subject matter or the parties.” (People v. N. River Ins. Co. (2020) 48 Cal.App.5th 226, 233 [citations omitted].) However, Jayesh Kumar does not challenge this Court’s authority over the subject matter or the parties.

In the end, “a default admits the material allegations of the complaint.” (Jackson v. Bank of America (1986) 188 Cal.App.3d 375, 387 [citing Ellis v. Rademacher (1899) 125 Cal. 556, 557].) Jayesh Kumar’s remaining argument arises out of a challenge to the arguments presented in Plaintiff’s complaint. However, Jayesh Kumar had ample opportunity to fully litigate this action and chose not to do so. He cannot collaterally attack the allegations at this time.

Accordingly, Defendant’s Motion to Set Aside Default and Vacate Default Judgment is DENIED.

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Case Number: 25STCV23085    Hearing Date: September 24, 2026    Dept: 509

Brianda Camacho, et al. v. Haifa Eshtiaghpour, et al.

MOTION TO SET ASIDE DEFAULT
 

MOVING PARTY: Defendants Royalty 26, Inc., and DLucky, Inc.

RESPONDING PARTY(S): Plaintiffs Brianda Camacho, Luis Camacho Rodriguez, Luis Camacho, Saray Camacho, and Emma Victory Camacho

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

On August 5, 2025, plaintiffs Brianda Camacho, Luis Camacho Rodriguez, Luis Camacho, Saray Camacho, and Emma Victory Camacho (Plaintiffs) filed a complaint for a single cause of action for fraudulent conveyance against defendants Haifa Eshtiaghpour, Royalty 26, Inc., and DLucky, Inc. (collectively, Defendants). Plaintiffs obtained a monetary judgment against defendant Royalty 26, Inc. in a previous action (Brianda Camacho, et al. v. Royalty 26 Inc., LASC Case No. 24STCV22906.). Plaintiffs now allege that defendant Royalty 26, Inc., an alter ego of Haifa Eshtiaghpour, transferred two properties to defendant DLucky, Inc. without consideration to avoid collection of the judgment

On May 4, 2026, Defendants’ counsel motion to be relieved was granted. (See 5/4/26 Minute Order.) In that hearing, the Court set an Order to Show Cause Re: Striking Answer of the Corporate Defendants for Failure to Appear with Legal Counsel for June 3, 2026. (Ibid.)

On June 3, 2026, Defendants, including corporate defendants Royalty 26, Inc., and DLucky, Inc. (Corporate Defendants) failed to appear. Corporate Defendants’ answers were stricken and their default was entered. 

On August 12, 2026, Corporate Defendants moved to set aside the default. Plaintiffs opposed. 

TENTATIVE RULING

Corporate Defendant’s Motion to Set Aside Default is DENIED.

Plaintiffs to give notice.

DISCUSSION

Motion to Set Aside Default

I. Analysis 

A. Motion to Set Aside Default

Corporate Defendants now moves to set aside the default entered against them on June 3, 2026, under Code Civil Procedure section 473(b). 

“Section 473, subdivision (b) provides for two distinct types of relief…Under the mandatory relief provision, on the other hand, upon a showing by attorney declaration of ‘mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect,’ the court shall vacate any ‘resulting default judgment or dismissal entered.’” (Leader v. Health Indus. of Am., Inc. (2001) 89 Cal.App.4th 603, 615–16.) Here, Corporate Defendants do not request mandatory relief for good reason:  Their attorneys did not do anything which supports such mandatory relief.

Instead, under the discretionary provisions of section 473(b), the court “may, upon any terms as may be just, relieve a party or his or her legal representative from a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against him or her through his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect.” (Code Civ. Pro. § 473, subd. (b).) To obtain relief under this provision, the application for relief must be made “within a reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months, after the judgment, dismissal, order, or proceeding.” (Ibid.) This Court is mindful of the policy favoring relief from default where there is no prejudice to the opposing party. (See Even Zohar Constr. & Remodeling, Inc. v. Bellaire Townhouses, LLC, (2015) 61 Cal. 4th 830, 839 [“The general underlying purpose of section 473(b) is to promote the determination of actions on their merits”]; see also Austin v. Los Angeles Unified Sch. Dist. (2016) 244 Cal. App. 4th 918, 929 [“[b]ecause the law favors disposing of cases on their merits, ‘any doubts in applying section 473 must be resolved in favor of the party seeking relief from default [citations].’”].) 

However, here, discretionary relief is also improper as the default was not entered as a result of “mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect” of the Corporate Defendants. Instead, Corporate Defendants admit they were given proper notice and “DLucky and Royalty 26 do not contend otherwise.” (Reply at p. 3:5-6.) “The relevant neglect is therefore not ignorance of the June 3 date - it is the difficulty two corporations faced in retaining replacement counsel willing to step into a case on the eve of default.” (Id. at p. 3:8-10.) However, Corporate Defendants were given reasonable time to obtain counsel and failed to do. This does not demonstrate a mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect. 

In their motion, Corporate Defendants acknowledge that a corporation may not appear in court without being represented by a licensed attorney. Corporate Defendants suggests, however, that this Court should view this deficiency as a curable defect. They cite CLD Construction, Inc. v. City of San Ramon (2004) 120 Cal.App.4th 1141 (CLD), where the appellate court reversed an order striking a corporation’s complaint under section 436. The corporation had filed the complaint in propria persona, but it had obtained counsel by the time the defendant city moved to strike the complaint. The Court of Appeal held that it was “more appropriate and just to treat a corporation’s failure to be represented by an attorney as a defect that may be corrected, on such terms as are just in the sound discretion of the court.” (CLD, supra, at p. 1149.) Because the parties were at the threshold of the lawsuit, no prejudice to the defendant could have occurred. Most notably, because CLD had retained counsel before the city appeared, its initial self-representation was “minimal, and essentially inconsequential. For all practical purposes CLD was represented by counsel before the City became a player in the action, so neither the City nor the trial court was ever in the position of having to deal with a nonattorney corporate representative.” (Id. at p. 1150.) Indeed, the record indicated to the appellate court that the substitution might have taken place even before the defendant was served. Thus, the court held, just as courts arrange for counsel when minors have attempted to represent themselves in court, a corporation “should not be foreclosed from going forward with its legal right to sue because of a defective complaint that can be readily and easily cured without prejudice to either its opponent or the court.” (Id. at p. 1152.) 

However, CLD does not help Corporate Defendants. Unlike in CLD, Corporate Defendants here seek relief from this obligation just a month before trial. This motion is not beginning the lawsuit but rather attempting to relieve Corporate Defendants of its failure to obtain counsel on the eve of trial. Indeed, coupled with the actions of Corporate Defendants’ representatives, Ms. Eshtiaghpour and Mr. Daravi, (as discussed below), the Plaintiffs here have been impacted by Corporate Defendants’ failure to obtain counsel. This is not a scenario, like CLD, where the impact of the relief results in minimal disruption to the action. Corporate Defendants effectively abandoned their duties with respect to this lawsuit until it became clear that judgment was imminent.

Finally, even if the Court was persuaded that relief was proper under the circumstances, Corporate Defendants’ actions in this matter have made any relief on “terms as may be just” impractical. (Code Civ. Pro. §473, subd. (b).) For example, Corporate Defendants have not complied with discovery in this matter. (Hermansen Decl. ¶15.) Instead, during Corporate Defendants’ default, Royalty 26’s director, Ms. Eshtiaghpour, twice failed to appear for depositions despite actual notice given to her and DLucky’s director Mr. Daravi. (Id. at ¶¶10-15.) It appears Corporate Defendants have disregarded compliance with discovery from the onset of this action. Now, on the eve of trial, there appears to be no just terms to allow Corporate Defendants to proceed in this action that would not result in an unjust windfall at the expense of the Plaintiffs. To that end, Corporate Defendants argue that the Court “may condition relief on an expedited discovery schedule, payment of reasonable costs directly attributable to the defaults, compliance by fixed dates, or other case-management terms.” (Opp. at p. 4:19-21.) However, Corporate Defendants fail to sufficiently suggest, and this Court does not find any terms that are just under the totality of the circumstances. 

Accordingly, Corporate Defendants’ Motion to Set Aside Default is DENIED. 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Case Number: 26STCP02760    Hearing Date: September 24, 2026    Dept: 509

Vivek Shah v. Intermap Technologies, Inc.

MOTION REQUIRING PETITIONER TO FURNISH SECURITY
 

MOVING PARTY: Respondent Intermap Technologies, Inc.

RESPONDING PARTY(S): Petitioner Vivek Shah

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

Petitioner Vivek Shah (Petitioner) filed this action seeking to send a dispute with defendant Intermap Technologies, Inc. (Respondent) to binding arbitration. In the underlying dispute, Petitioner asserts that Respondent violated the California Invasion of Privacy Act (CIPA). 

On August 26, 2026, Respondent filed a motion requiring Petitioner to furnish security as a vexatious litigant. Petitioner opposed.

TENTATIVE RULING:

Respondent’s Motion to Furnish Security is GRANTED. 

The amount of security in the instant action is set as $20,000.00 to be furnished within 60 days of this order. If Petitioner fails to adequately furnish said security within 60 days, the action shall be dismissed pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure, section 391.4.

An OSC Re: Dismissal for Petitioner’s Failure to Post the $20,000.00 Security is scheduled for November 30, 2026, at 8:30 a.m.

This action is otherwise stayed until 10 days after the Petitioner adequately posts the required bond.

Respondent to give notice.

DISCUSSION:

Motion Requiring Petitioner to Furnish Security

I. Judicial Notice

Respondent requests judicial notice of Exhibits A-K.

Petitioner requests judicial notice of Exhibits 1-3.

Pursuant to the parties’ requests, the Court GRANTS judicial notice of Respondent’s Exhibits A-K and Petitioner’s Exhibits 1-3. 

II. Legal Standard 
 
Code of Civil Procedure, section 391.1(a) provides: 
 
In any litigation pending in any court of this state, at any time until final judgment is entered, a defendant may move the court, upon notice and hearing, for an order requiring the plaintiff to furnish security or for an order dismissing the litigation pursuant to subdivision (b) of Section 391.3. The motion for an order requiring the plaintiff to furnish security shall be based upon the ground, and supported by a showing, that the plaintiff is a vexatious litigant and that there is not a reasonable probability that they will prevail in the litigation against the moving defendant. 
 
In determining whether the plaintiff has a reasonable probability of prevailing, the “court shall consider any evidence, written or oral, by witnesses or affidavit, as may be material to the ground of the motion.” (Code of Civ. Pro. § 391.2.) “[I]f, after hearing the evidence upon the motion, the court determines that the plaintiff is a vexatious litigant and that there is no reasonable probability that the plaintiff will prevail in the litigation against the moving defendant, the court shall order the plaintiff to furnish, for the benefit of the moving defendant, security in such amount and within such time as the court shall fix.” (Code of Civ. Pro. § 391.3(a).) 
 
Thus, “[i]n pending litigation, a defendant may have the plaintiff declared a vexatious litigant and, if the plaintiff has no reasonable probability of prevailing, ordered to furnish security. If the plaintiff fails to furnish the security, the action will be dismissed.” (Shalant v. Girardi (2011) 51 Cal.4th 1164, 1171 [citing §§ 391.1–391.6].)  
 
In moving for an order requiring a vexatious litigant to post security, the defendant has the burden of showing there is no reasonable likelihood plaintiff will prevail in the action. (Golin v. Allenby (2010) 190 Cal.App.4th 616, 640, 642.) “A court’s decision that a vexatious litigant does not have a reasonable chance of success in the action is based on an evaluative judgment in which the court weighs the evidence.” (Id. at p. 636.) 
 
III. Analysis 
 
A. Petitioner is a Vexatious Litigant
 
As a threshold issue, Petitioner has already been declared a vexatious litigant in Shah v. Crain Communications, Inc., No. 2:26-cv-03070-RGK-CTS (C.D. Cal.). (RJN Ex. A.) Therefore, this element is met. (Hupp v. Solera Oak Valley Greens Assn. (2017) 12 Cal.App.5th 1300, 1311 [“Where a plaintiff has already been declared vexatious, a defendant moving under section 391.7 need not again establish the plaintiff’s status.”])

In opposition, Petitioner argues that this action arises from different facts than the federal action. Code of Civil Procedure, section 391(a)(4) states that a vexatious litigant means any person that “[h]as previously been declared to be a vexatious litigant by any state or federal court of record in any action or proceeding based upon the same or substantially similar facts, transaction, or occurrence.” In the Federal action, “[t]he Court declares Plaintiff Vivek Shah a vexatious litigant; A Prefiling Order is hereby entered against Plaintiff for any new case filed in the Central District of California that alleges any claims arising under the California Invasion of Privacy Act (“CIPA”) or other related digital privacy claims.” (RJN Ex. A at p. 11.) This action appears entirely rooted in Petitioner’s assertion of his CIPA or digital privacy claims against a similarly situated defendant. Here, Petitioner went onto a website, identified certain features on that website, and filed an action. (Shah Decl.) These are substantially similar facts underpinning Petitioner’s actions in the Federal action which led to his vexatious litigant status. (RJN Ex. A.) The Court finds the facts of this action to be substantially similar to those in Shah v. Crain Communications, Inc., No. 2:26-cv-03070-RGK-CTS (C.D. Cal.) to find Petitioner is a vexatious litigant under Section 391(b)(4).

Further, even if Petitioner did not meet the definition of a vexatious litigant under Section 391(b)(4), considering the totality of the evidence, the Court, in its discretion, would declare Petitioner a vexatious litigant under Section 391(b)(1). Under either section, Petitioner would be declared a vexatious litigant.

As a result, the Court turns to Petitioner’s probability of prevailing in this action. 
 
B. Petitioner’s Probability of Prevailing 
 
Respondent argues it is highly likely that they, and not Petitioner, will prevail in this action. The Court agrees.

“[T]o satisfy its burden of showing that the plaintiff has no reasonable probability of prevailing, the defendant must show that the plaintiff’s recovery is foreclosed as a matter of law or that there are insufficient facts to support recovery by the plaintiff on its legal theories, even if all the plaintiff’s facts are credited.” (Deveraux v. Latham & Watkins (1995) 32 Cal.App.4th 1571, 1582-83.) Here, Petitioner’s litigation is a special proceeding seeking an order compelling arbitration. The question is therefore whether Petitioner has a reasonable probability of obtaining the judicial relief requested in his petition.

In his petition, “Petitioner Vivek Shah (“Petitioner”) brings this Petition pursuant to California Code of Civil Procedure sections 1281, 1281.2, and 1281.4, the Federal Arbitration Act, 9 U.S.C. section 2, and, conditionally, Civil Code sections 1799.201 and 1799.208, to compel Respondent Intermap Technologies, Inc. (“Respondent”) to arbitrate under Respondent’s Terms of Use.” (Petition at p. 1.) Petitioner states that “Petitioner accepts the obligation to arbitrate but primarily requests arbitration in California under California law because he resides here, accessed Respondent’s website here, elected to accept the Terms here, and alleges that the challenged violations occurred here. Petitioner initiated AAA arbitration” in California. (Petition at p. 2.)

However, the Terms and Conditions that support Petitioner’s right to arbitration plainly state that:

These Terms and your use of the Site are governed and interpreted in accordance with the laws of the State of Colorado without regard to its conflicts of law provisions. The United Nations Convention on the International Sale of Goods does not apply. Any dispute arising between the parties out of or in connection with this Agreement will be finally resolved by arbitration conducted by one arbitrator in Denver, Colorado pursuant to the International Arbitration Rules of the American Arbitration Association (“AAA”) applicable to commercial disputes.

(Shah Decl., Ex 2.) 
 
Indeed, it appears that despite seeking to enforce the Terms and Conditions to his benefit, Petitioner has unilaterally chosen a venue not supported by the same. This fact materially alters the probability of success on the petition because Petitioner has failed to demonstrate Respondent refused to arbitrate under the terms of the Terms and Conditions.

1. Civil Code, Section 1799.208 Does Not Apply

As an initial matter, the Court addresses Petitioner’s argument that the Terms and Conditions are voidable. 

Civil Code, section 1799.208 states:

(a) A seller shall not require a consumer to agree to a provision that would do either of the following:
(1) Require the consumer to arbitrate outside of California a claim arising in California.
(2) Require the consumer to arbitrate a controversy arising in California under the substantive law of a state other than California.
(b) Any provision of a contract that violates subdivision (a) is voidable by the consumer, and if a provision is rendered void at the request of the consumer, the matter shall be adjudicated in California and California law shall govern the dispute.

(Civ. Code, § 1799.208)

However, setting aside the parties’ respective arguments as to the FAA preemption, the evidence before this Court clearly demonstrates that this section plainly does not apply to this instant action regardless of any preemption.

A consumer “means a natural person who has entered into a consumer contract with a seller” and a seller “means a person who has entered into a consumer contract with a consumer.” (Civ. Code, §1799.201, subd (a) and (h).) However a consumer contract “means a writing prepared by a seller and, except as provided in subdivision (c) of Section 1799.202, signed, or to be signed, by a consumer, which provides (1) for the sale or lease of goods or services that are purchased or leased primarily for personal, family, or household purposes.” (Id. at subd. (b), emphasis added.) 

Here, the undisputed evidence is clear that Petitioner did not purchase or lease anything from Respondent. (Shah Decl.) That fact alone renders the Terms and Conditions outside the scope of the definition of a “consumer contract” and outside the scope of Civil Code, section 1799.208. Therefore, the Terms and Conditions are not voidable and Civil Code, section 1799.208 does not provide grounds for arbitration beyond the scope of the Terms and Conditions.

2. Petitioner Did Not Demand Arbitration Pursuant to the Terms and Conditions

Now, in the petition, Petitioner requests this Court send this action to arbitration pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure, sections 1281.

“[S]ection 1281.2 does require a party seeking to compel arbitration to plead and prove a prior demand for arbitration under the parties’ arbitration agreement and a refusal to arbitrate under the agreement.” (Mansouri v. Superior Court (2010) 181 Cal.App.4th 633, 640 (Mansouri).) “The only reasonable construction of the statutory language that conforms to such intent, is to require for a petition to compel arbitration the pleading and proof of (1) the parties’ written agreement to arbitrate a controversy [Citation]; (2) a request or demand by one party to the other party or parties for arbitration of such controversy pursuant to and under the terms of their written arbitration agreement; and (3) the refusal of the other party or parties to arbitrate such controversy pursuant to and under the terms of their written arbitration agreement.” (Id. at p. 641, emphasis in original.)

Here, Petitioner only demonstrated that Respondent did not proceed in arbitration in California. (Shah Decl.) There is no evidence of a demand for arbitration in Denver, Colorado pursuant to the Terms and Conditions or any rejection of such demand. (Shah Decl.) Therefore, Petitioner’s initiation of arbitration in California, and Respondent’s failure to participate in such arbitration, does not evidence a refusal to arbitrate under the Terms and Conditions and the petition seeks relief unavailable as a matter of law.

Petitioner argues that HM DG, Inc. v. Amini (2013) 219 Cal.App.4th 1100 (HM DG) is persuasive in this matter. However, HM DG and this action are dissimilar. There, plaintiff sent a demand which “states, in no uncertain terms: ‘Please accept this letter as defendants’ demand that all of the claims raised by plaintiffs in their complaint in the Action, as well as the claims to be made by defendants, be arbitrated per the ‘Arbitration Clause’ found on Page 3 of the June 24, 2010 ‘Project Proposal and Agreement’.’” (Id. at p. 1112.) No such demand letter exists here. (See Shah Decl.) 

In the end, “[s]uch demand and refusal is what requires and justifies the intervention of the court to order arbitration under the agreement. It makes no sense for this language to be read to allow a petitioner to compel arbitration on allegations and proof of a written agreement to arbitrate the controversy, but with allegations and proof that arbitration under some other statutory scheme or contract was offered and refused. The Legislature plainly intended section 1281.2 to provide a procedural device for enforcing the parties’ written arbitration agreement if one or more of the parties would not agree to such arbitration.” (Mansouri v. Superior Court, supra, 181 Cal.App.4th at p. 641.) The undisputed evidence demonstrates that Petitioner failed to demand arbitration under the Terms and Conditions. Therefore, any conduct, implied or otherwise, does not demonstrate a refusal to arbitrate under the Terms and Conditions. As a result, Petitioner has no legal grounds to demand arbitration. Therefore, the probability of obtaining the judicial relief requested in the petition is highly unlikely.

Accordingly, Respondent’s Motion to Furnish Security is GRANTED. 

C. Amount of Security

“Security” is defined as “an undertaking to assure payment, to the party for whose benefit the undertaking is required to be furnished, of the party’s reasonable expenses, including attorney’s fees and not limited to taxable costs, incurred in or in connection with a litigation instituted, caused to be instituted, or maintained or caused to be maintained by a vexatious litigant.” (Code of Civ. Pro. § 391, subd. (c).) The judge is ultimately responsible for fixing an appropriate amount of security to cover the moving party’s reasonable expenses. (Code of Civ. Pro. § 391.3, [“the court shall order the plaintiff to furnish, for the benefit of the moving defendant, security in such amount and within such time as the court shall fix.”]) 

Here, the declaration of attorney Aaron R. Goldstein declares that $75,000 would be the reasonable expenses in opposition to this petition. (Goldstein Decl. ¶8.) However, the Court finds reasonable expenses to be less than the requested amount given the likely resolution of this action as discussed above. The Court finds a reasonable security to be $20,000. 

The amount of security in the instant action is set as $20,000. If Petitioner fails to adequately furnish said security within 60 days, the action “shall be dismissed as to the defendant for whose benefit it was ordered furnished.” (Golin v. Allenby (2010) 190 Cal.App.4th 616, 634.)


D. This Matter is Stayed Until 10 Days After Security is Furnished

“Under the plain language of section 391.6, the entirety of the litigation is stayed once the defendant files a [security] motion.” (Hanna v. Little League Baseball, Inc. (2020) 53 Cal.App.5th 871, 875-76.) The automatic “stay shall remain effective until 10 days after the motion is denied or 10 days after the security is furnished. [Citation.] Section 391.6 does not carve out any exception for proceedings that may continue during that period except for the dismissal of the action under section 391.3[(b)].” (Ibid.)

This action is otherwise stayed, until 10 days after Petitioner furnishes the security.

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Any party may submit on the tentative ruling by contacting the courtroom via email at SMCDept509@lacourt.ca.gov by no later than 4:00 p.m. the day before the hearing. All interested parties must be copied on the email. It should be noted that if you submit on a tentative ruling the court will still conduct a hearing if any party appears. By submitting on the tentative you have, in essence, waived your right to be present at the hearing, and you should be aware that the court may not adopt the tentative, and may issue an order which modifies the tentative ruling in whole or in part.
