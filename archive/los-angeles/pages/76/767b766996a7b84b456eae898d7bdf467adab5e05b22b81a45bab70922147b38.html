DEPARTMENT 224 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 20STCV02913    Hearing Date: September 17, 2026    Dept: 224

Carter v. Zuma

TENTATIVE RULING

The motion is GRANTED IN PART and DENIED IN PART. The Court orders the inspection completed, on a modified protocol and subject to the temporal and privilege limitations set forth below, and orders production of the log data captured on July 21, 2026.

Both parties' requests for monetary sanctions are DENIED.

ANALYSIS

Introduction

On January 23, 2020, Plaintiff David Carter filed the Complaint in this action.

On March 16, 2023, Defendant Yuan Shi aka Henry Shi moved for leave to file a Cross-Complaint against Carter alleging, among other claims, invasion of privacy and violations of the Stored Communications Act, 18 U.S.C. section 2701 et seq., arising out of Carter's access to the email account henry@zumavc.com and its associated Google Drive.

On January 23, 2026, Shi served Request for Inspection No. 177, seeking inspection of the email account and Google Drive, including all files stored therein and their activity and viewer history logs.

On February 27, 2026, Carter served a response objecting on grounds of relevance, overbreadth, burden, and privilege, and refusing any inspection.

On May 15, 2026, Shi filed a Motion to Compel Inspection of Email Account and Google Drive.

On May 21, 2026 and June 5, 2026, the Court held Informal Discovery Conferences addressing the inspection. The June 5, 2026 minute order states that the parties agree to access to non-privileged metadata on the Google Workspace drive, and that two experts and counsel are allowed to be present during download.

On July 21, 2026, the parties convened the inspection remotely. Carter, acting as

Global Administrator, exported four sets of account-level log events to files saved on his own computer. The examination ended without the collection of any file metadata after Carter's counsel declined to permit a Google Workspace data export.

On July 27, 2026, Shi proposed an alternative collection method using Axiom by Magnet Forensics. Carter did not respond.

On August 21, 2026, Shi filed the instant Amended Motion to Compel Inspection of Email Account and Google Drive and Request for Sanctions, which amends and supersedes the May 15, 2026 motion.

On September 3, 2026, Carter filed an opposition.

On September 10, 2026 a reply was filed as well as objection to improper reply evidence.

Meet and Confer

The Supplemental Declaration of Alexander H. Hu executed on August 21, 2026, sets forth the meet and confer efforts of counsel for the Defendant/Cross-Complainant, and counsel's attempts to resolve the discovery dispute informally through written correspondence, at two Informal Discovery Conferences, and at the July 21, 2026 examination itself. The Court finds that the declaration satisfies the requirement that the parties "meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by videoconference, to informally resolve each issue presented by the motion."

Legal Standard

“A party may demand that any other party produce . . . a document that is in the possession, custody, or control of the party on whom the demand is made.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.010(b).) The demanding party may move for an order compelling further response to the demand if the demanding party deems that (1) a statement of compliance with the demand is incomplete, (2) a representation of inability to comply is inadequate, incomplete, or evasive, or (3) an objection in the response is without merit or too general. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310(a).) “The motion shall set forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought by the demand,” and “[t]he motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310(b).)

Unlike with requests to compel further interrogatories, a moving party seeking to compel further responses for requests for production must show good cause along with satisfaction of the meet and confer requirement. (CCP§ 2031.310(b).)

To prevail, a party moving for an order compelling further responses to a document production demand must first offer specific facts demonstrating “good cause justifying the discovery sought by the demand.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(1).) This burden “is met simply by a fact-specific showing of relevance.” (TBG Ins. Servs. Corp. v. Superior Court (2002) 96 Cal.App.4th 443, 448.) If “good cause” is shown by the moving party, the burden shifts to the responding party to justify any objections made to document disclosure. (Kirkland v. Superior Court (2002) 95 Cal.App.4th 92, 98.)

“The party to whom a demand for inspection, copying, or testing, or sampling has been directed shall respond separately to each item or category of item by any of the following: (1) A statement that the party will comply with the particular demand... A statement that the party ... will comply with the particular demand shall state that the production will be allowed either in whole or in part, and that all documents or things in the demanded category that are in possession, custody, or control of that party and to which no objection is being made will be included in the production. (2) A representation that the party lacks the ability to comply with the demand... A representation of inability to comply with the particular demand ... shall affirm that a diligent search and a reasonable inquiry has been made in an effort to comply with that demand. This statement shall also specify whether the inability to comply is because the particular item or category has never existed, has been destroyed, has been lost, misplaced, or stolen, or has never been, or is no longer, in possession, custody, or control of the responding party. The statement shall set for the name and address of any natural person or organization known or believed by that party to have possession, custody, or control of that item or category of items. (3) An objection to the particular demand... If only part of an item of category of item in a demand... is objectionable, the response shall contain a statement of compliance or a representation of inability to comply with respect to the remainder of that item or category. If the responding party objects to the demand... the response shall do both of the following: [a] Identify with particularity any document or tangible thing... falling within any category of item in the demand to which an objection is being made, [b] Set forth clearly the extent of, and the specific ground for the objection.... If an objection is based on a claim of privilege or a claim that the information sought is protected work product, the response shall provide sufficient factual information for other parties to evaluate the merits of that claim, including, if necessary, a privilege log.” (California Code of Civil Procedure §§ 2031.210, 2031.220, 2031.230, and 2031.240)

Good Cause

Shi has established good cause. Carter's verified responses admit that he accessed the Account from November 2019 forward, ran keyword searches, downloaded and saved emails and files to his personal Drive and computer, sent emails from the Account, changed the credentials, and altered document permissions — while stating under penalty of perjury that he recalls none of the dates, search terms, or specific permission changes and has produced no records of them. Each cross-claim, and Carter's consent and litigation privilege defenses, turn on those facts.

Good cause has been established.

Discussion

Request No. 177

Request for Inspection No. 177 seeks inspection of the henry@zumavc.com email account and associated Google Drive, "including all files stored therein and their activity and viewer history logs." Carter objected on relevance, overbreadth, burden, and privilege grounds. Shi moved to compel on May 15, 2026.

Following IDCs on May 21 and June 5, 2026, the parties agreed to a metadata inspection. The Court's June 5 minute order recites that "[t]he parties agree to access to non-privileged metadata on the Google Workspace drive" and that "[t]wo experts and counsel are allowed to be present during download."

The inspection convened by Zoom on July 21, 2026. Carter, acting as Global Administrator, exported four sets of account-level log events (67 user log entries, 1,059 Drive log entries, 34 OAuth entries, 14 takeout entries) to CSV files saved on his own computer. Queries of the admin data action log and Vault log returned nothing. When Dr. Greenfield then asked Carter to perform a Google Workspace data export so that file-level metadata could be extracted account-wide, Carter's counsel refused and the session ended. The exported log files have never been produced, and no privilege log has been served.

On July 27, 2026, Shi proposed an alternative method using Magnet Forensics' Axiom, with the collected file contents wiped after metadata extraction. Carter did not respond.

Carter objects that Request No. 177 specified no date, time, place, or manner, and that the Court cannot compel discovery broader than the demand or conscript him into performing the inspection.

The objections are without merit. The specification objection was not raised in Carter's response and is waived. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.240(b)(2).) It is in any event cured by Attachment A, which supplies the date, time, place, participants, manner, and the express no-alteration statement, and the Court independently may set the terms of the inspection it orders. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2031.060(d)-(e), 2031.310(e)-(f).)

Independently, the parties twice agreed to an inspection, addressed it at two IDCs, and actually convened and partially performed it.

Carter's reliance on Volkswagen of America, Inc. v. Superior Court (2006) 139 Cal.App.4th 1481 and Brown v. Superior Court (1985) 168 Cal.App.3d 489 is misplaced. Those cases bar an order compelling discovery broader than the demand. The relief sought here – non-privileged metadata – is materially narrower than Request No. 177, which on its face reached the files themselves.

Carter's principal objection conflates two categories of data. The log events he exported reflect account-level activity; they do not contain the metadata of the individual emails and files showing when and how each item was accessed or modified. Both fall within Request No. 177. The download is a step in the collection, not a production. Axiom collects the Account into a secure forensic container, the raw metadata is separated from the contents without anyone reviewing them, the metadata goes to Carter's counsel for privilege review, and the contents are wiped. Nothing in this order permits Shi, his counsel, or Maryman to receive, review, or retain the file contents.

Nor does the record support the contention that a data export was never contemplated. The June 5, 2026 minute order refers to counsel and experts being present "during download." Carter raised no objection for six weeks and objected first mid-session on July 21. The Court does not read the June 5 minute order as having adjudicated the method of collection.

As to privacy and privilege, these objections are addressed by the protocol. Carter elects who performs the collection. If Maryman performs it, Carter changes the password before and after, credentials are shared with no one, and Carter and his counsel observe every step by screen share, including the wipe. All material is tendered to Carter's counsel first for privilege review, and Shi receives only non-privileged metadata with a privilege log.

Carter's remaining objection regarding temporal scope has merit. The demand is not reasonably limited in time and would reach approximately eleven years, when his own pre-litigation access spanned two months. Shi offers no theory under which metadata reflecting his own use between 2015 and November 2019 bears on the cross-claims.

Further response is ordered, including a privilege log for any documents withheld on grounds of privilege. Production, however, is limited to metadata reflecting events occurring on or after November 20, 2019.

Sanctions

The Court must impose a monetary sanction against a party or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel a further response, unless the Court finds that the party subject to sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances would make the sanction unjust. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300, subd. (d), 2031.310, subd. (h).)

Both requests are denied. The record reflects genuine ambiguity about what was agreed. The Court declines to find that the motion was made or opposed without substantial justification. (§§ 2031.310(h), 2023.020.)

Conclusion

The motion is GRANTED IN PART and DENIED IN PART. The Court orders the inspection completed, on a modified protocol and subject to the temporal and privilege limitations set forth below, and orders production of the log data captured on July 21, 2026.

Both parties' requests for monetary sanctions are DENIED.

Case Number: 23STCV28338    Hearing Date: September 17, 2026    Dept: 224

Mandy C. Mena et al., v. Adobe Communities

TENTATIVE RULING

The Motion for Court Approval of the Parties' PAGA Settlement is CONTINUED for Moving Party to reconcile the amount allocated to PAGA Penalties with the terms of the Settlement Agreement.

The Motion hearing and Order to Show Cause Re Dismissal for Settlement are continued to November 16, 2026 at 9:00 a.m.

ANALYSIS

Introduction

On October 20, 2022, Plaintiff Mena filed a Representative Action Complaint in Los Angeles Superior Court, Case No. 22STCV33949, alleging a single cause of action for civil penalties under PAGA predicated on Labor Code sections 201, 202, 203, 226(a), 226.7, 510, 512, 1194, 1194.2, 1199, and 2802.

On November 20, 2023, Plaintiffs Zamora and Perez filed a Representative Action Complaint in this action, Case No. 23STCV28338, alleging the same single PAGA cause of action.

On February 6, 2024, Defendant moved to compel arbitration. The Court granted the motion on July 25, 2024, and Plaintiffs Zamora and Perez separately initiated arbitration before ADR Services, Inc. on August 13, 2024.

On June 25, 2025, the parties participated in an all-day mediation before Kevin Barnes and reached a tentative settlement.

On February 10, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a Consolidated First Amended Representative Action Complaint consolidating the Mena and Zamora/Perez actions. The First Amended Complaint is the operative pleading.

On July 2, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the instant Motion for Approval of Representative Action Settlement pursuant to Labor Code section 2699(l)(2).

Legal Standard

Labor Code § 2699(l)(2) states: “The superior court shall review and approve any settlement of any civil action filed pursuant to this part [Labor Code Private Attorneys General Act of 2004 (“PAGA”)]. The proposed settlement shall be submitted to the [Labor and Workforce Development Agency (“LWDA”)] at the same time that it is submitted to the court.” Any settlement of any civil action filed under PAGA must be “fair and adequate in view of the purposes and policies of the statute.” (Flores v. Starwood Hotels & Resorts Worldwide, Inc. (C.D. Cal. 2017) 253 F.Supp.3d 1074, 1077, quoting O’Connor v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (N.D. Cal. 2016) 201 F.Supp.3d 1110, 1135.)

Seventy-five percent of all PAGA penalties must be paid to the LWDA. (See Lab. Code, § 2699(i) [“Except as provided in subdivision (j), civil penalties recovered by aggrieved employees shall be distributed as follows: 75 percent to the Labor and Workforce Development Agency for enforcement of labor laws and education of employers and employees about their rights and responsibilities under this code, to be continuously appropriated to supplement and not supplant the funding to the agency for those purposes; and 25 percent to the aggrieved employees.”].) Courts have discretion to approve settlements that do not allocate any penalty amount.

(Nordstrom Com. Cases (2010) 186 Cal.App.4th 576, 589). Labor Code § 2699(g)(1) provides, in relevant part: “Any employee who prevails in any action shall be entitled to an award of reasonable attorney’s fees and costs . . . .” (Lab. Code, § 2699(g)(1).)

Discussion

As an initial matter, Plaintiffs have presented evidence the parties complied with Labor Code section 2699(l)(2) by submitting a copy of the Settlement and instant motion to the LWDA at the same time they were submitted to the Court. Proof of submission and a copy of the email from the LWDA confirming receipt is filed with the Court. (Kingsley Decl. ¶149, Exh. 6.)

With respect to the distribution of the PAGA settlement, the Court cannot determine whether the parties’ agreement is proper at this juncture. After engaging in informal discovery and participating in an all-day mediation on June 25, 2025, with mediator Kevin Barnes, Plaintiffs and Defendant were able to reach a settlement of Plaintiffs' PAGA claims. (Kingsley Decl. ¶19; Settlement Agreement ¶2.4.) Pursuant to the Settlement Agreement, the parties agreed that Defendant will pay a Gross Settlement Amount of $210,000.00. (Kingsley Decl. ¶22, Exh. 1, Settlement Agreement ¶¶1.10, 3.1.)

The parties have agreed to a proposed distribution of the Gross Settlement Amount as follows: (1) $69,972.00 for attorney's fees, (2) $18,125.81 for litigation costs, (3) $4,750.00 for settlement administration costs, (4) $15,000.00 in enhancement payments to the three named Plaintiffs, and (5) the Net Settlement Amount — i.e., the amount remaining from the Gross Settlement Amount after deducting attorney's fees, litigation costs, settlement administration costs, and the enhancement payments — for PAGA penalties, wherein 75% would be paid to the LWDA and 25% would be paid to all aggrieved employees. (Kingsley Decl. ¶23, Exh. 1, Settlement Agreement ¶¶1.15, 3.2.)

The Court is unable to reconcile the amount identified as PAGA Penalties with the remaining terms of the Settlement. Plaintiffs seek approval of $115,152.19 in PAGA Penalties, with $86,364.14 to the LWDA and $28,788.05 to the aggrieved employees. (Kingsley Decl. ¶23.) The Settlement Agreement itself recites PAGA Penalties of $112,278.00, with $84,208.50 to the LWDA and $28,069.50 to the aggrieved employees. (Settlement Agreement ¶3.2.4.) Neither figure follows from the Agreement's own definition of the Net Settlement Amount, which is the Gross Settlement Amount less the Enhancement Payment, the PAGA Counsel Fees

Payment, the PAGA Counsel Litigation Expenses Payment, and the Administration Expenses Payment. (Settlement Agreement ¶1.15.) Applying that definition to the amounts requested — $210,000.00 less $69,972.00 in fees, $18,125.81 in costs, $4,750.00 in administration expenses, and $15,000.00 in enhancement payments — yields $102,152.19, not $115,152.19. The requested figure appears to overstate the Net Settlement Amount by $13,000.00, which is the difference between the $15,000.00 in enhancement payments the parties have agreed to and the $2,000.00 that appears to have been deducted in arriving at the $112,278.00 figure recited in paragraph 3.2.4 of the Agreement. Because the amount of PAGA Penalties determines the sums payable to the LWDA and to the aggrieved employees, this discrepancy must be resolved before the Court can approve the Settlement.

With respect to the civil penalties, the proposed distribution complies in form with Labor Code section 2699(i), which requires 75 percent of the amount to be paid to the LWDA and 25 percent of the amount to be paid to the aggrieved employees, which consists of all non-exempt employees who worked for Defendant at any time during the PAGA Period, defined as August 16, 2021 through and including August 25, 2025. (Settlement Agreement ¶¶1.19, 1.23.) Defendant estimates there are approximately 253 aggrieved employees who worked a total of 10,789 pay periods during the PAGA Period. (Settlement Agreement ¶4.1.) Each aggrieved employee will receive a pro rata share of the 25% allocation based on the number of pay periods worked. (Kingsley Decl. ¶25; Settlement Agreement ¶3.2.4.1.)

The Court finds the release under the PAGA Settlement Agreement is fair and reasonable under the statute. The release as to the aggrieved employees is appropriately limited to claims for civil penalties under PAGA that were alleged, or could have been alleged, based on the facts and circumstances identified in Plaintiffs' PAGA Notices of August 15, 2022 and September 13, 2023 and in the complaints filed in this Action, arising during the PAGA Period, including claims for alleged failure to pay all wages including overtime; failure to provide meal periods and rest breaks; failure to provide accurate wage statements; failure to reimburse business expenses; and failure to timely pay wages upon termination, under Labor Code sections 201–203, 226(a), 226.7, 510, 512, 1194, 1194.2, 1199, 2802, and the applicable I.W.C. Wage Orders. (Settlement Agreement ¶¶1.28, 5.2.) The aggrieved employees do not release any other individual claims. The broader general release set forth in paragraph 5.1 applies only to the three named Plaintiffs, who have separately settled their individual claims and who have each submitted declarations acknowledging the terms of the Settlement. (Settlement Agreement ¶¶3.2.1.)

With respect to the enhancement payments, the Settlement provides for a payment of $5,000.00 to each of the three named Plaintiffs, for a total of $15,000.00. (Settlement Agreement ¶3.2.1; Kingsley Decl. ¶145.) Each Plaintiff has submitted a declaration describing his or her participation in the litigation, including retaining counsel, gathering documents, providing information regarding employment conditions, maintaining regular contact with counsel, attending a full day of mediation, and reviewing the settlement documents, and each estimates having spent approximately 10 to 15 hours on this matter. (Mena Decl. ¶¶5-7; Zamora Decl. ¶¶5-7; Perez Decl. ¶¶5-7.) Incentive awards are appropriate to compensate representative plaintiffs for work performed and for the financial and reputational risk undertaken in bringing the action. (Munoz v. BCI Coca-Cola Bottling Co. of Los Angeles (2010) 186 Cal.App.4th 399, 412.) Subject to the reconciliation discussed above, the Court would find the requested enhancement payments reasonable.

With respect to attorney's fees, Plaintiffs' counsel has provided the hourly rates and experience of each attorney and the approximate amount of time each attorney spent prosecuting and resolving this matter. (Kingsley Decl. ¶¶116-133.) Counsel calculates an aggregate lodestar of $50,622.00, representing 72.50 hours of attorney time, which includes 52.50 hours incurred through the filing of this Motion and 20 anticipated future hours. (Kingsley Decl. ¶132.) The requested fee of $69,972.00 represents approximately one-third of the Gross Settlement Amount and reflects a multiplier of 1.38. (Kingsley Decl. ¶134.) After accounting for the time spent, counsel's experience, the contingent nature of the representation, and the results achieved, the Court finds the requested $69,972.00 in attorney's fees is reasonable and proper in this case.

Regarding the $18,125.81 requested in litigation costs, a costs ledger itemizing the expenses incurred is included with counsel's declaration. (Kingsley Decl. ¶¶138–144, Exh. 5.) This is sufficient to justify the $18,125.81 requested in litigation costs, which is below the $21,000.00 cap provided in the Settlement Agreement.

With respect to the settlement administration costs, the Settlement allocates $4,750.00 in payment for the costs of the Settlement Administrator, Phoenix Class Action Administration Solutions ("Settlement Administrator"), associated with administration of the Settlement. (Settlement Agreement ¶3.2.3.) Plaintiffs have submitted a declaration from the Settlement Administrator attaching a "will not exceed" quote in the amount of $4,750.00 and attesting to its qualifications, its data security procedures, its errors and omissions and cyber insurance coverage, and the absence of any conflict of interest. (Lawrence Decl. ¶¶4-22, Exhs. A, B.) Based on the above, the Court would award settlement administration costs in the amount of $4,750.00.

Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, the Motion for Court Approval of the Parties' PAGA Settlement is CONTINUED for Moving Party to reconcile the amount allocated to PAGA Penalties with the terms of the Settlement Agreement.

The Motion hearing and Order to Show Cause Re Dismissal for Settlement are continued to November 16, 2026 at 9:00 a.m.

Case Number: 25STCV01976    Hearing Date: September 17, 2026    Dept: 224

Hogan v. General Motors, LLC

TENTATIVE RULING

The demurrer is OVERRULED in part and SUSTAINED with LEAVE TO AMEND in part. Plaintiffs shall file their Amended Complaint within 20 days.

The Case Management Conference is continued to February 18, 2027 at 8:30 a.m.

ANALYSIS

Introduction

On January 24, 2025, Plaintiff filed a Complaint arising out of Plaintiff’s warranty contract with Defendant General Motors LLC (“GM”) regarding a 2023 Cadillac XT5, VIN 1GYKNCR42PZ174962 (“Subject Vehicle”).

On March 3, 2026 Plaintiffs filed a First Amended Complaint.

On April 21, 2026 Defendant filed the instant Demurrer to Plaintiff’s FAC.

No opposition has been filed.

Meet and Confer

Before filing a demurrer or a motion to strike, the demurring or moving party is required to meet and confer with the party who filed the pleading demurred to or the pleading that is subject to the motion to strike for the purposes of determining whether an agreement can be reached through a filing of an amended pleading that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer. (CCP §§ 430.41)

The Declaration of Sarah Perez, executed on April 21, 2026, sets forth Defendant’s counsel meet and confer efforts and attempts to resolve the disputes informally by telephone. The Court finds that the parties conferred as required.

Legal Standard

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action.¿ (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)¿When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context.¿ (Wilson v. Transit Authority of City of Sacramento (1962) 199 Cal.App.2d 716, 720-21.)¿In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice.¿(Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)¿“A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not on the evidence or facts alleged.” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Id.) The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action. (Hahn, supra, 147 Cal.App.4th at 747.)

Fifth Cause of Action: Fraudulent Inducement – Concealment

Duty to Disclose

Defendant argues that Plaintiffs have not pled a transactional relationship between GM and Plaintiff which gives rise to a duty to disclose.

“There are¿‘four circumstances in which nondisclosure or concealment may constitute actionable fraud: (1) when the defendant is in a fiduciary relationship with the plaintiff; (2) when the defendant had exclusive knowledge of material facts not known to the plaintiff; (3) when the defendant actively conceals a material fact from the plaintiff; and (4) when the defendant makes partial representations but also suppresses some material facts.’”¿¿(Id.¿at p. 311.)¿

“[O]ther¿than the first instance, in which there must be a fiduciary relationship between the parties, the other three circumstances in which nondisclosure may be actionable presuppose¿the existence of some other relationship between the plaintiff and defendant in which a duty¿to disclose can arise¿. . .¿A relationship between the parties is present¿[only]¿if there is some sort of¿transaction¿between the parties.¿ (Hoffman v. 162 North Wolfe LLC¿(2014)¿228 Cal.App.4th¿1178, 1187;¿see¿Nissan Motor Acceptance Cases¿(2021) 63 Cal.App.5th¿793¿.)¿¿“Such a transaction must necessarily arise from direct dealings between the plaintiff and the defendant; it cannot arise between the defendant and the public at large.”¿¿(Bigler-Engler, supra,¿(2017) 7 Cal.App.5th¿at¿312.)¿

Here, Plaintiffs have alleged that Defendant issued warranties directly on Plaintiffs’ vehicle, and the Subject Vehicle was purchased at GM’s authorized retail dealership Three-Way Chevrolet Co., in Bakersfield, California (FAC, ¶¶ 6-7.) Thus, a transactional relationship exists pursuant to Dhital, wherein the court found that the plaintiffs' allegations were sufficient to establish a relationship requiring the manufacturer to disclose known defects. There, plaintiffs bought the car from a Nissan dealership, and Nissan backed the car with an express warranty. The court noted that Nissan's authorized dealerships were its agents for the purposes of selling Nissan vehicles to consumers (Dhital v. Nissan North America, Inc., 84 Cal.App.5th 828 (2022)).

Further, the claim is based on both Defendant’s superior knowledge and Defendant’s knowing and intentional concealment (Id. ¶¶ 79-86). Plaintiffs have alleged Defendant had exclusive knowledge of, failed to disclose and concealed the existence of the transmission defect in order to ensure Plaintiff’s purchase of the Subject Vehicle. (Id.)

A duty to disclose has been pled.

Defendant’s demurrer is OVERRULED on this ground.

The Economic Loss Rule

Defendant further argues that the Economic Loss Rule bars Plaintiffs’ fifth cause of action.

The economic loss rule posits that a purchaser of a product that does not live up to the buyer’s expectations can only recover in contract and not tort, “unless [the purchaser] can demonstrate harm above and beyond a broken contractual promise.” (Food Safety Net Services v. Eco Safe Systems USA, Inc. (2012) 209 Cal.App.4th 1118, 1130.) A tort claim for fraud must be sufficiently independent from a breach of contract claim for which the plaintiff suffered economic loss. (Robinson Helicopter Co., Inc. v. Dana Corp. (2004) 34 Cal.4th 979, 991.)

The Court of Appeal in Dhital addressed Nissan’s assertion that, under Robinson Helicopter, the Buyer’s cause of action is barred pursuant to the Economic Loss Rule because the cause of action concerns fraudulent concealment (as opposed to affirmative misrepresentations). The Court ultimately held that “[u]nder California law, the economic loss rule does not bar plaintiffs' claim here for fraudulent inducement by concealment. Fraudulent inducement claims fall within an exception to the economic loss rule recognized by our Supreme Court, and plaintiffs allege fraudulent conduct that is independent of Nissan's alleged warranty breaches.” (Dhital v. Nissan N. Am., Inc. (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 828, 843, citing Robinson Helicopter, supra 34 Cal.4th at 989–90.)

In Rattagan v. Uber Techs. (2024) Inc., 17 Cal. 5th 1 (Rattagan), the California Supreme Court addressed the question left unanswered in Robinson Helicopter regarding the applicability of the economic loss rule in the context of a fraudulent concealment claim, holding that “an independent fraudulent concealment tort may arise during an ongoing contractual relationship.” (Id. at p. 19 [emphasis original].)

Framed more specifically, the court stated:

“Can a plaintiff assert an independent claim of fraudulent concealment in the performance of a contract? The answer to this question is also yes. A plaintiff may assert a tort claim for fraudulent concealment based on conduct occurring in the course of a contractual relationship, if the elements of the cause of action can be established independently of the parties' contractual rights and obligations and the tortious conduct exposes the plaintiff to a risk of harm beyond the reasonable contemplation of the parties when they entered into the agreement.”

(Rattagan, supra, 17 Cal. 5th 1 at p. 38.)

In other words, as long as the plaintiff can establish all the required elements of the cause of action independently of the parties’ contractual rights and obligations and can demonstrate an exposure to risks of harm beyond those reasonably expected as a result of a contractual breach, then a cause of action for concealment may lie. (Ibid.) As stated in Rattagan, “it has long been the rule that where a contract is secured by fraudulent representations, the injured party may elect to affirm the contract and sue for the fraud.” (Id. at p. 41.)

Plaintiffs’ fifth cause of action alleges Defendant was aware and knew that the 9-speed transmission in the Vehicle was defective but failed to disclose this fact to Plaintiffs prior to, at the time of sale, and thereafter (FAC ¶¶ 82-84), thereby alleging a claim for fraudulent concealment rather than affirmative representations. (See Dhital at p. 841 [“independence is present in the case of fraudulent inducement (whether it is achieved by intentional concealment or by intentional affirmative misrepresentations), because a defendant's conduct in fraudulently inducing someone to enter a contract is separate from the defendant's later breach of the contract or warranty provisions that were agreed to.”; finding the economic loss rule did not preclude vehicle buyers’ fraudulent inducement-concealment claim against the vehicle manufacturer, where the buyers’ allegations concerned fraudulent conduct before the sale, which was therefore separate from the manufacturer’s performance under the contractual or warranty provisions.)].

Here, the Court is guided by Dhital and concludes Plaintiffs’ fifth cause of action is not barred by the Economic Loss Rule. As stated in Dhital, “the economic loss rule does not bar plaintiffs’ claim . . . for fraudulent inducement by concealment.” (Dhital, supra, at p. 9.)

As such, the economic loss rule does not bar Plaintiffs’ fraud cause of action as a matter of law.

The demurrer is OVERRULED on this ground.

Specificity

However, Plaintiffs must still plead the circumstances of fraudulent concealment with particularity. (Rattagan v. Uber Techs., Inc. (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1, 43–44.)

“‘The required elements for fraudulent concealment are (1) concealment or suppression of a material fact; (2) by a defendant with a duty to disclose the fact to the plaintiff; (3) the defendant intended to defraud the plaintiff by intentionally concealing or suppressing the fact; (4) the plaintiff was unaware of the fact and would not have acted as he or she did if he or she had known of the concealed or suppressed fact; and (5) plaintiff sustained damage as a result of the concealment or suppression of the fact. [Citation.]’ [Citation.]” (Hambrick v. Healthcare Partners Medical Group, Inc. (2015) 238 Cal.App.4th 124, 162.)

Ordinarily, “fraud must be pleaded specifically; general and conclusory allegations do not suffice.” (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 645.). “This particularity requirement necessitates pleading facts which show how, where, to whom, and by what means” the alleged fraud occurred. (Id.) The purpose of the particularity requirement is to “separate meritorious and nonmeritorious cases, if possible in advance of trial.” (Small v. Fritz Companies, Inc. (2003) 30 Cal.4th 167, 184.)¿¿¿¿

California courts apply the same specificity standard to evaluate the factual underpinnings of a fraudulent concealment claim at the pleading stage, even though the focus of inquiry shifts to the unique elements of the claim. (Rattagan v. Uber Techs., Inc., 17 Cal. 5th 1, 43–44, 553 P.3d 1213, 1240 (2024) citing Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 347, 134 Cal.Rptr. 375, 556 P.2d 737; Boschma v. Home Loan Center, Inc. (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 230, 248, 129 Cal.Rptr.3d 874; Cansino v. Bank of America (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 1462, 1472, 169 Cal.Rptr.3d 619.) In Rattagan, the court held that it had to determine whether the plaintiff alleged a sufficient factual basis for establishing a duty of disclosure on the part of the defendant independent of the parties' contract. If the duty allegedly arose by virtue of the parties' relationship and defendant's exclusive knowledge or access to certain facts, as the plaintiff alleged there, the Court found that the complaint also had to include specific allegations establishing all the required elements, including (1) the content of the omitted facts, (2) defendant's awareness of the materiality of those facts, (3) the inaccessibility of the facts to plaintiff, (4) the general point at which the omitted facts should or could have been revealed, and (5) justifiable and actual reliance, either through action or forbearance, based on the defendant's omission. “[M]ere conclusionary allegations that the omissions were intentional and for the purpose of defrauding and deceiving plaintiff[ ] ... are insufficient for the foregoing purposes.” (Goodman, at p. 347, 134 Cal.Rptr. 375, 556 P.2d 737.) (Rattagan v. Uber Techs., Inc., 17 Cal. 5th 1, 43–44, 553 P.3d 1213, 1240 (2024)).

Defendant argues that Plaintiffs have not pled this cause of action with the requisite specificity. Namely, Defendant argues that Plaintiffs (1) failed to allege the contents of the omitted facts; (2) failed to present allegations establishing that GM knew of those “facts” and concealed them at the time of purchase; (3) failed to identify by name any person who made any alleged omissions when they purchased the Subject Vehicle; (4) failed to establish the manner in which Plaintiffs learned about the “behaviors” they attribute to GM; and (5) failed to allege the advertisements materials where GM could have disclosed the allegedly omitted “facts” that Plaintiffs reviewed and relied upon in purchasing the Subject Vehicle.

GM relies on the general rule for pleading fraud against a corporation, which requires a plaintiff to allege the identity of the speaker, their authority, to whom they spoke, what they said, and when it was said. (See, e.g., Tarmann v. State Farm Mut. Auto Ins. Co. (1991) 2 Cal.App.4th 153, 157.) However, this rule is inapplicable to a claim of fraudulent concealment. (Alfaro v. Community Housing Improvement System & Planning Association, Inc. (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1356, 1384 (“How does one show ‘how’ and ‘by what means’ something didn’t happen, or ‘when’ it never happened, or ‘where’ it never happened?”).) Instead, such details, in the context of a fraudulent concealment claim, “are properly the subject of discovery, not demurrer.” (Id. at 1384-85.)

Here, the Court agrees that Plaintiff does not plead the fraudulent concealment claim with the requisite particularity, though only for reason number five. As discussed above, the Tarmann factors GM contends Plaintiffs omitted – the identity of the speaker, their authority, to whom they spoke, and what was said and when – do not govern a claim sounding in concealment rather than affirmative misrepresentation. (Alfaro, supra, 171 Cal.App.4th at 1384.) The claim is instead deficient because the FAC fails to plead the factors required for a concealment claim under Rattagan. Although Plaintiff alleges that GM had superior knowledge of the defect through pre-production and post-production testing data; early consumer complaints about the Transmission Defect; aggregate warranty data; testing conducted in response to these complaints; as well as warranty repair and part replacements data, (FAC ¶ 79) the pleading does not specify when, where, or how GM’s alleged omissions occurred, nor does it identify which advertisements, marketing materials, or representations Plaintiffs reviewed, when they reviewed them, or how those materials were prepared or disseminated by GM as opposed to the dealership. Further, Plaintiffs do not allege the general point at which disclosure should or could have been made.

Further, while Plaintiffs allege that GM’s concealment induced them to purchase the vehicle and that they would not have done so had they known of the defect (FAC ¶ 85), the FAC does not allege any facts establishing Defendant’s intent to defraud.

The demurrer is SUSTAINED on this ground, with leave to amend.

Conclusion

The demurrer is SUSTAINED with leave to amend. Plaintiffs shall file their Amended Complaint within 20 days.

Case Number: 25STCV05703    Hearing Date: September 17, 2026    Dept: 224

Espitia v. FCA, US LLC

TENTATIVE RULING

The Court DENIES as moot Plaintiff's Motion to Compel Initial Disclosures Pursuant to California Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26.

The Court GRANTS Plaintiff's Request for Monetary Sanctions in the amount of $2,500.00 against Defendant FCA US LLC's counsel of record, payable to Plaintiff's counsel within 15 business days of this Order.

Motion to Compel Initial Disclosures

Plaintiff moves for an order compelling Defendant FCA US LLC to produce all documents required under section 871.26(h)(6)-(10) and (12)-(13) and imposing monetary sanctions against Defendant's counsel of record in the amount of $2,500.00.

Untimely Opposition

Defendant's opposition was due September 3, 2026 and was filed September 10, 2026. Defense counsel declares that he contracted Influenza A shortly before the deadline, that his condition deteriorated to the point of a severe potassium deficiency requiring emergency treatment and hospitalization on September 9, 2026, and that he filed the opposition the day after his discharge, on the same day he received Plaintiff's notice of non-opposition. (Rivera Decl., ¶¶ 3-12.) The Court finds good cause to exercise its discretion and considers the late-filed opposition.

Legal Standards

"Within 60 days after the filing of the answer or other responsive pleading, all parties shall, without awaiting a discovery request, provide to all other parties an initial disclosure and documents pursuant to subdivisions (f), (g), and (h)." (Civ. Proc. Code, § 871.26, subd. (b).) "The defendant or manufacturer shall provide the following documents to all other parties pursuant to the timelines prescribed in subdivision (b): … (6) Motor vehicle information reports, including build documentation, component information, and delivery details. (7) Entire warranty transaction history for the motor vehicle. (8) Listing of required field actions applicable to the motor vehicle. (9) Published technical service bulletins ("TSBs") for the same make, model, and year reasonably related to the nonconformities pertaining to the motor vehicle. (10) Published information service bulletins ("ISBs") for the same make, model, and year reasonably related to the nonconformities pertaining to the motor vehicle… (12) Warranty policies and procedure manuals. (13) Service manuals reasonably related to the nonconformities pertaining to the motor vehicle…." (Id. at § 871.26, subd. (h).)

"Unless the party failing to comply with this section shows good cause, notwithstanding any other law and in addition to any other sanctions imposed pursuant to this chapter, a court shall impose sanctions as follows:

(1) A one-thousand-five-hundred-dollar ($1,500) sanction against the plaintiff's attorney or two-thousand-five-hundred-dollar ($2,500) sanction against the defense attorney respectively, paid within 15 business days for failure to comply with the document production requirements as prescribed in subdivision (b).

(2) A one-thousand-five-hundred-dollar ($1,500) sanction against the plaintiff's attorney or two-thousand-five-hundred-dollar ($2,500) sanction against the defense attorney respectively, paid within 15 business days for failure to comply with the provisions relating to depositions as prescribed in subdivision (c)."

(Id. at § 871.26, subd. (j).)

Section 871.26 is not of general application. It "only applies to a civil action seeking restitution or replacement of a motor vehicle pursuant to Section 871.20" and "only applies to a civil action filed on or after January 1, 2025." (Civ. Proc. Code, § 871.26, subds. (a), (l).) Senate Bill 26 (Stats. 2025, ch. 1), effective April 2, 2025, further conditioned the chapter on a manufacturer's election. Absent an election under section 871.29, "Sections 871.20 to 871.28, inclusive, shall not apply." (Civ. Proc. Code, § 871.29, subd. (b).) Section 871.30 created a one-time 30-day window for a manufacturer to elect as to vehicles sold new in 2025 and all prior years and provides that, absent that election, the chapter does not apply, "including actions already filed between January 1, 2025 and the effective date of the act adding this section." (Id. at § 871.30, subd. (c).)

Discussion

Plaintiff does not contend that Defendant failed to serve an initial disclosure production in its entirety. Defendant served a preliminary production of more than 1,000 pages on June 23, 2025, comprising the glove box documents, sales brochure, repair order detail report, vehicle information detail report, warranty claims records, Monroney label, and applicable TSBs and recalls. (Rivera Decl., ¶ 13, Ex. A.) Defendant declares that it conducted a diligent search and located no service contract claims records, Customer Assistance Inquiry Records, or Cherwell report. (Ibid.)

Defendant withheld four categories pending entry of a protective order: the FCA US Dealer Policy Manual, the Service Manual for the Subject Vehicle, the FCA US Warranty Administration Manual, and the policies and procedures of its Customer Assistance Center regarding customer complaints and requests for refund or replacement. On June 10, 2026, following execution of the Stipulation and Protective Order, Defendant served its complete production including those categories. (Rivera Decl., ¶ 16, Ex. B.) The Stipulation and Protective Order was entered June 15, 2026.

The withheld categories are the categories this Motion seeks to compel. Because Defendant has produced them, there is nothing left for the Court to order, and the Motion is denied as moot.

Sanctions

That Defendant ultimately produced does not answer whether it complied with subdivision (b). Subdivision (b) prescribes both what must be produced and when. Defendant's preliminary production on June 23, 2025 was timely. Its production of the four withheld categories on June 10, 2026 was not; it came approximately eleven months after the statutory deadline and nearly five months after this Motion was filed. Late compliance with subdivision (b) is noncompliance, and the sanction prescribed by subdivision (j)(1) attaches unless Defendant shows good cause.

Defendant has not made that showing. Section 871.26 does not abrogate a manufacturer's right to seek protection for trade secrets or confidential commercial information, and the Court does not suggest otherwise. But the mechanism for obtaining that protection is a noticed motion under section 2031.060, subdivision (a), which requires a party to move "promptly." Defendant never moved. It instead conditioned a mandatory, self-executing statutory obligation on the other side's agreement to a stipulation, and withheld the documents for eleven months while that agreement remained unexecuted. A party may not unilaterally suspend a statutory disclosure deadline pending a stipulation it has no power to compel.

The record also does not support Defendant's account of the delay. Defendant attributes the delay to Plaintiff's "refusal to agree to an SPO." But the Stipulation and Protective Order filed in this action was executed by Plaintiff's counsel on September 18, 2025 and served on Defendant's counsel that same day. Defendant's counsel did not countersign until June 9, 2026. Defendant produced the confidential categories the following day. On this record, the roughly nine-month gap between Plaintiff's signature and Defendant's is attributable to Defendant, not Plaintiff.

Defendant's timely production of the remaining categories is relevant but not dispositive. Substantial compliance with some categories of subdivision (h) does not excuse noncompliance with others. (See Civ. Proc. Code, § 871.26, subd. (j)(1).)

The Court therefore imposes the mandatory $2,500 sanction against Defendant's counsel of record under section 871.26, subdivision (j)(1), payable within 15 business days. The Court declines to require reporting to the State Bar under subdivision (j)(5).

Sanctions

Subdivision (b) prescribes both what must be produced and when. Where a party produces after the 60-day deadline, it has not complied with subdivision (b), and the $2,500 sanction prescribed by subdivision (j)(1) attaches against the defense attorney unless that party "shows good cause." (Civ. Proc. Code, § 871.26, subd. (j).)

Defendant's preliminary production on June 23, 2025 was timely. However, its production of the four withheld categories on June 10, 2026 came approximately eleven months after the statutory deadline and nearly five months after this Motion was filed. Defendant never moved for a protective order. Defendant attributes the delay to Plaintiff's refusal to agree to a protective order, but the Stipulation and Protective Order filed in this action was executed by Plaintiff's counsel on September 18, 2025 and served on Defendant's counsel that same day; Defendant's counsel did not countersign until June 9, 2026, and produced the following day. Timely compliance as to other categories of subdivision (h) does not excuse noncompliance as to these.

Good cause not having been shown, the Court imposes the $2,500 sanction against Defendant's counsel of record under section 871.26, subdivision (j)(1), payable within 15 business days.

Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, the Court DENIES as moot Plaintiff's Motion to Compel Initial Disclosures Pursuant to California Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26.

The Court GRANTS Plaintiff's Request for Monetary Sanctions in the amount of $2,500.00 against Defendant FCA US LLC's counsel of record, payable to Plaintiff's counsel within 15 business days of this Order.

Case Number: 25STCV11833    Hearing Date: September 17, 2026    Dept: 224

Topanga Oasis v. State of California

TENTATIVE RULING

Plaintiff’s Motion for Relief from Waiver of Jury Trial is GRANTED. Trial set for October 11, 2027 in this matter, will be by jury.

ANALYSIS

Introduction

On April 23, 2025, Plaintiff Topanga Oasis, LLC filed the Complaint in this action.

On January 29, 2026, Plaintiff filed the First Amended Complaint.

On January 6, 2026, the Court held the initial Case Management Conference. The Court's minute order of that date states that "Jury fees are not posted by any party in compliance with C.C.P. Section 631 at the time of case management conference hearing” and the Court set trial for October 11, 2027 as a non-jury trial.

Later that same day, Plaintiff filed its Notice of Posting Jury Fees, reflecting payment of the $150 nonrefundable advance jury fee.

On January 27, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant Motion.

No opposition has been filed.

Legal Standard

A party must pay jury fees no later than 365 calendar days after filing of the initial complaint. (CCP § 631, subds. (b) and (c).) The failure to post jury fees constitutes a waiver of that right. (CCP § 631(f)(5)&(6).)

California Code of Civil Procedure section¿631, subdivision (g) provides courts with discretion to allow a jury trial despite there being a waiver of trial by jury.¿ “A trial court abuses its discretion as a matter of law when [] relief has been denied where there has been no prejudice¿to the other party or to the court from an inadvertent waiver.”¿ (Tesoro del Valle Master Homeowners Assn. v. Griffin¿(2011) 200 Cal.App.4th 619,¿638.)¿ Policy favors the court using its discretion to proceed with a jury trial. (Bishop v. Anderson (1980) 101 Cal.App.3d 821, 823.)

“Where the right to jury is threatened, the crucial focus is whether any prejudice will be suffered by any party or the court if a motion for relief from waiver is granted. [Citations.] A trial court abuses its discretion as a matter of law when ‘…relief has been denied where there has been no prejudice to the other party or to the court from an inadvertent waiver. [Citations.]’” (Wharton v. Superior Court (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 100, 104.) “The mere fact that trial will be by jury is not prejudice per se.” (Johnson-Stovall v. Superior Court (1993) 17 Cal.App.4th 808, 811 [prejudice not shown by need to prepare motions in limine, enlarged exhibits, and jury instructions unless inadequate time also shown].) Rather, the prejudice that must be shown is “prejudice from the granting of relief from waiver not prejudice from the jury trial.” (Winston v. Superior Court (1987) 196 Cal.App.3d 600, 602, 603 [fact that it takes longer to try a jury case is not prejudice from granting relief from waiver].)

Discussion

Although captioned as a "Motion for Jury Trial," the motion is in substance a motion for relief from waiver of jury trial under Code of Civil Procedure section 631, subdivision (g), and the Court treats it as such. Plaintiff also invokes section 473(b), but the Court's discretion under section 631(g) is sufficient to resolve the Motion, and relief under section 631(g) requires a lesser showing than section 473(b).

The Court's January 6, 2026 minute order found that jury fees were not posted by any party in compliance with section 631 as of the time of the Case Management Conference, and set the matter for a non-jury trial. (Collins Decl. ¶ 3, Ex. B.)

The Court notes that section 631, subdivision (c) provides that where the initial case management conference is scheduled less than 365 days after the filing of the initial complaint, jury fees are "due on or before the date scheduled for the initial case management conference in the action." (CCP § 631(c).) The initial Case Management Conference here was held on January 6, 2026, and Plaintiff posted its $150 nonrefundable advance jury fee that same day. (Collins Decl. ¶ 9, Ex. C.)

Any waiver was inadvertent. Plaintiff demanded a jury trial in its Complaint, demonstrating its intent from the outset to try this matter to a jury. (Collins Decl. ¶¶ 2, 4, Ex. A.) Counsel declares that the failure to calendar and complete the fee payment before the conference was the result of an administrative oversight in his office and was not intentional or deliberate. (Collins Decl. ¶¶ 5-7.)

Plaintiff also acted promptly. Counsel posted the fees the same day he learned at the conference that the matter had been set for a non-jury trial, and filed this Motion three weeks later. (Collins Decl. ¶¶ 8-9, Ex. C.)

No prejudice will result from granting relief. Trial is set for October 11, 2027 – well over a year from the filing and hearing of this Motion – leaving the parties ample time to prepare for a jury trial and eliminating any possibility of unfair surprise. (Collins Decl. ¶ 10.) Discovery remains ongoing, and no party has substantially altered its trial preparation in reliance on a bench trial. (Collins Decl. ¶ 11.) The mere fact that trial will be by jury is not prejudice per se. (Johnson-Stovall, supra, 17 Cal.App.4th at p. 811.) Rather, the prejudice that must be shown is "prejudice from the granting of relief from waiver not prejudice from the jury trial." (Winston, supra, 196 Cal.App.3d at pp. 602-603.)

The Motion is unopposed, and no party has identified any prejudice arising from proceeding to a jury trial. Given the strong policy favoring resolution of doubts in favor of preserving the constitutional right to a jury trial (Bishop, supra, 101

Cal.App.3d at p. 823), relief is warranted.

Accordingly, Plaintiff's Motion for Relief from Waiver of Jury Trial is GRANTED. The trial in this matter, currently set for October 11, 2027, is designated as a jury trial.

Case Number: 25STCV19633    Hearing Date: September 17, 2026    Dept: 224

Houcheime v. FCA, US LLC

TENTATIVE RULING

The demurrer is SUSTAINED in part, with 20 DAYS leave to amend. The Motion to Strike is GRANTED, with 20 DAYS leave to amend.

The Case Management Conference is CONTINUED to February 18, 2026 at 8:30 a.m.

ANALYSIS

Introduction

On June 30, 2025, Plaintiff filed a Complaint arising out of Plaintiff’s warranty contract with Defendant FCA US, LLC (“FCA”) regarding a 2023 Jeep Wrangler, VIN 1C4JJXP67PW536083 (“Subject Vehicle”).

On November 5, 2025 Defendants filed the instant Demurrer and Motion to Strike to Plaintiff’s Complaint.

No opposition has been filed.

Meet and Confer

Before filing a demurrer or a motion to strike, the demurring or moving party is required to meet and confer with the party who filed the pleading demurred to or the pleading that is subject to the motion to strike for the purposes of determining whether an agreement can be reached through a filing of an amended pleading that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer. (CCP §§ 430.41)

The Declaration of Arya Shirani, executed on November 5, 2025, sets forth Defendant’s counsel meet and confer efforts and attempts to resolve the disputes informally by telephone. The Court finds that the parties conferred as required by Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41

Legal Standard

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action.¿ (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)¿When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context.¿ (Wilson v. Transit Authority of City of Sacramento (1962) 199 Cal.App.2d 716, 720-21.)¿In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice.¿(Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)¿“A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not on the evidence or facts alleged.” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Id.) The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action. (Hahn, supra, 147 Cal.App.4th at 747.)

Fifth Cause of Action: Negligent Repair

Defendants demur to the fifth cause of action in the Complaint for Negligent Repair against Stevens Creek Chrysler Jeep Dodge (“Stevens”) on the grounds that the Economic Loss Rule bars Plaintiff’s claim.

To plead a claim for negligent repair, a plaintiff must allege the elements of negligence. “Ordinarily, negligence may be pleaded in general terms and the plaintiff need not specify the precise act or omission alleged to constitute the breach of duty.” (Lopez v. S. Cal. Rapid Transit Dist. (1985) 40 Cal.3d 780, 795; Crouse v. Brobeck (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1509, 1532 (a plaintiff need only state what occurred, and generally that the acts were negligently done, but “need not

state the specific act or omission constituting negligent conduct.”)) Likewise, causation may be alleged “succinctly and generally, unless the pled facts ‘do not naturally give rise to an inference of causation…’” (Bockrath v. Aldrich Chemical Co., Inc.¿(1999) 21 Cal.4th 71, 78, internal citations omitted.)

In order to plead around the economic loss rule, a party must plead the existence of a duty that arises independent of any contractual duty and independent injury, other than economic loss, that arises from the breach of that duty. (Robinson Helicopter, supra, 34 Cal.4th at 991. 988-91.) “In general, there is no recovery in tort for negligently inflicted ‘purely economic losses,’ meaning financial harm unaccompanied by physical or property damage.” (Sheen v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2022) 12 Cal.5th 905, 922.)

Here, Plaintiff’s negligent repair claim is brought against Stevens (not FCA). Plaintiff allegedly delivered the Vehicle to Stevens for repairs “on at least one occasion.” (Complaint, ¶ 60.) Plaintiff generally alleges that Stevens “owed a duty to Plaintiff to use ordinary care and skill in storage, preparation, and repair” of the Vehicle “in accordance with industry standards.” (Id., ¶ 61.) Indeed, “[a]ny individual, partnership, corporation, association, or other legal relationship which engages in the business of providing service or repair to new or used consumer goods has a duty to the purchaser to perform those services in a good and workmanlike manner.” (Civ. Code, § 1796.5.) Thus, Plaintiff has sufficiently pled an independent duty.¿

However, Plaintiff’s negligent repair claim is devoid of factual detail clarifying how Stevens acted negligently. Plaintiff alleges Stevens breached its duty to Plaintiff “by failing to properly store, prepare and repair [the Vehicle] in accordance with industry standards.” (Id., ¶ 62.) Plaintiff further alleges Stevens’ “negligent breach of its duties owed to Plaintiff was a proximate cause of Plaintiff’s damages.” (Id., ¶ 63.) Plaintiff must at least include facts indicating what is alleged to have occurred with enough factual detail to put Stevens on notice of the claim brought against it. For example, the Complaint is vague and uncertain as to the nature or type of storage and/or repairs that Stevens was supposedly hired to perform on the Vehicle, and fails to allege how Stevens’ conduct deviated from industry norms. Additionally, the Complaint does not allege any facts indicating what type of damages were sustained by Plaintiff. Without more factual detail as to what Stevens allegedly did or failed to do, it cannot be inferred that its negligent acts or omissions caused Plaintiff to suffer a particular injury, economic or otherwise. Thus, while the Economic Loss Rule may not necessarily bar Plaintiff’s Negligent Repair claim, Plaintiff has not adequately pled his cause of action for negligent repair.¿

The demurrer to the fifth cause of action is SUSTAINED with leave to amend.

Sixth Cause of Action: Fraudulent Inducement – Concealment

Duty to Disclose

Defendant argues that Plaintiff has not pled a transactional relationship between FCA and Plaintiff which gives rise to a duty to disclose.

The California Supreme Court “has described the necessary relationship giving rise to a duty to disclose as a ‘transaction’ between the plaintiff and defendant.” (BiglerEngler v. Breg, Inc. (2017) 7 Cal.App.5th 276, 311.) Specifically, “[i]n¿transactions¿which do not involve fiduciary or confidential relations, a cause of action for non-disclosure of material facts may arise in at least three instances: (1) the defendant makes representations but does not disclose facts which materially qualify the facts disclosed, or which render his disclosure likely to mislead; (2)¿the facts are known or accessible only to defendant, and defendant knows they are not known to or reasonably discoverable by the plaintiff; (3) the defendant actively conceals discovery from the plaintiff.” (Ibid.)¿For example, “a duty to disclose may arise from the relationship between seller and buyer, employer and prospective employee, doctor and patient, or parties entering into any kind of contractual agreement.” (LiMandri v. Judkins (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 326, 336.)¿

Here, the Court is not persuaded that FCA’s written warranty, without more, is sufficient to impose a duty to disclose upon FCA where Plaintiff has not alleged facts indicating any “direct dealings” between the parties. (Complaint, ¶ 7.) “Under California law, manufacturer warranties that accompany the sale of a vehicle without regard to the substantive terms of the sale contract between the buyer and the dealer are independent of the sale contract.” (Davis v. Nissan North America, Inc. (2024) 100 Cal.App.5th 825, 837, emphasis added.)

While courts do consider the existence of an express warranty in evaluating the duty to disclose, Plaintiff has not set forth any legal authority to indicate that a written warranty, standing alone, constitutes the type of “direct dealings” necessary for such a duty, particularly where there are no facts alleging Plaintiff purchased the Vehicle from an FCA-authorized dealership or other FCA sales representatives, or that FCA’s agents directly solicited business from Plaintiff. (Cf. Dhital¿v. Nissan North America, Inc.,¿(2022)¿84 Cal.App.5th 828, 844 (holding a transactional relationship is sufficiently pled for the purposes of a demurrer where “plaintiffs alleged that they bought the car from a Nissan dealership, that Nissan backed the car with an express warranty, and that Nissan’s authorized dealerships are its agents for purposes of the sale of Nissan vehicles to consumers.”))

At present, the Complaint is completely devoid of facts clarifying the circumstances through which Plaintiff acquired the Vehicle. Plaintiff must allege additional facts to support the existence of a transactional relationship with FCA.

A duty to disclose has not been pled.

The demurrer is SUSTAINED with leave to amend on this ground.

Specificity

Assuming, arguendo, that Plaintiff had successfully pled a transactional relationship with FCA, Plaintiff must still plead the circumstances of fraudulent concealment with particularity. (Rattagan v. Uber Techs., Inc. (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1, 43–44.)

Defendant argues that Plaintiff has not pled this cause of action with the requisite specificity. Namely, Defendant argues that Plaintiff (1) failed to identify by name any person who made any alleged omissions when he purchased the Subject Vehicle; (2) failed to allege the general point at which the omitted facts should or could have been revealed; and (3) failed to allege the materials that Plaintiff reviewed and relied upon in purchasing the Subject Vehicle.

Defendant relies on the general rule for pleading fraud against a corporation, which requires a plaintiff to allege the identity of the speaker, their authority, to whom they spoke, what they said, and when it was said. (See, e.g., Tarmann v. State Farm Mut. Auto Ins. Co. (1991) 2 Cal.App.4th 153, 157.) However, this rule is inapplicable to a claim of fraudulent concealment. (Alfaro v. Community Housing Improvement System & Planning Association, Inc. (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1356, 1384 (“How does one show ‘how’ and ‘by what means’ something didn’t happen, or ‘when’ it never happened, or ‘where’ it never happened?”).) Instead, such details, in the context of a fraudulent concealment claim, “are properly the subject of discovery, not demurrer.” (Id. at 1384-85.)

Here, the Court agrees that Plaintiff does not plead the fraudulent concealment claim with the requisite particularity, though only for reasons two and three. As discussed above, the Tarmann factors FCA contends Plaintiff omitted – the identity of the speaker, their authority, to whom they spoke, and what was said and when – do not govern a claim sounding in concealment rather than affirmative misrepresentation. (Alfaro, supra, 171 Cal.App.4th at 1384.) The claim is instead deficient because the Complaint fails to plead the factors required for a concealment claim under Rattagan. Although Plaintiff alleges that FCA had superior knowledge of the defect through pre-production and post-production testing data; early consumer complaints about the Transmission Defect; aggregate warranty data; testing conducted in response to these complaints; as well as warranty repair and part replacements data, (Complaint ¶ 69) the pleading does not identify which advertisements, marketing materials, or representations Plaintiff reviewed, when he reviewed them, or how those materials were prepared or disseminated by FCA as opposed to the dealership. Further, Plaintiff does not allege the general point at which disclosure should or could have been made.

Further, while Plaintiff alleges that FCA’s concealment induced them to purchase the vehicle and that they would not have done so had they known of the defect (Complaint ¶ 70), the Complaint does not allege any facts establishing Defendant’s intent to defraud.

The demurrer is SUSTAINED in part, on this ground, with leave to amend.

Motion to Strike

Defendants move to strike Plaintiff’s prayer for punitive damages from the Complaint on the grounds the Complaint fails to state facts sufficient to support the recovery of punitive damages.

Civil Code § 3294(a) authorizes the recovery of punitive damages in non-contract cases “where it is proven by clear and convincing evidence that the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice…

(1) “Malice” means conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.

(2) “Oppression” means despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person’s rights.

(3) “Fraud” means an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known to the defendant with the intention on the party of the defendant of thereby depriving a person of property or legal rights or otherwise causing injury.

(Civ. Code § 3294(c)(1)-(3).)

As discussed above, the Court finds that Plaintiff’s fraud-based claim is not sufficiently pled to survive general demurrer. The Court also found that Plaintiff’s negligent repair claim is devoid of factual detail in support of its negligence allegations against Stevens.

Accordingly, the Court GRANTS Defendant’s Motion to Strike Plaintiff’s punitive damage request based on Plaintiff’s Fraudulent Concealment claim and Negligent Repair claim. The Court has allowed Plaintiff leave to amend to address the issues in the fifth and sixth causes of action and punitive damages may be awarded upon properly pled fraud and negligent repair claims. (See Civil Code § 3294). If Plaintiff amends the fifth and sixth causes of action, Plaintiff may plead punitive damages upon amendment.

Defendant’s Motion to Strike Punitive Damages from Plaintiff’s Complaint is GRANTED as to the fifth and sixth causes of action for Negligent Repair and Fraudulent Inducement – Concealment, with leave to amend.

Conclusion

The demurrer is SUSTAINED in part, with 20 DAYS leave to amend. The Motion to Strike is GRANTED, with 20 DAYS leave to amend.
