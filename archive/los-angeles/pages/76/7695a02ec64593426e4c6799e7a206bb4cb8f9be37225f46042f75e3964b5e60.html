DEPARTMENT 515 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 25STCV28722    Hearing Date: August 11, 2026    Dept: 515

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: Hearing on Motion to Compel Arbitration

The Motion to Compel Arbitration filed by Parkhouse Tire Service, Inc. is granted. The action is stayed pending completion of the arbitration.

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Jose Martinez filed this action against Parkhouse Tire Service, Inc. (Parkhouse Tire), alleging that Parkhouse Tire terminated his employment in retaliation for his complaints about unsafe vehicle lifts and unpaid wages.

The causes of action are: (1) Violation of Labor Code Section 1102.5; (2) Violation of Labor Code Sections 6310 and 6311; (3) Violation of Labor Code Section 232.5; (4) Violation of Labor Code Section 98.6; (5) Wrongful Termination in Violation of Public Policy; (6) Failure to Pay Minimum or Contractual Wages; (7) Failure to Pay Overtime Wages; (8) Failure to Reimburse Necessary Expenses; (9) Failure to Pay Wages Due at Time of Discharge; (10) Failure to Provide Accurate Wage Statements; and (11) Violation of Business and Professions Code Section 17200 et seq.

Parkhouse Tire filed a Motion to Compel Arbitration. Plaintiff filed an Opposition.

EVIDENTIARY OBJECTIONS

Plaintiff invokes the rule that new evidence is not permitted with reply papers. (Opp., p. 6, fn. 2.) A trial court has discretion whether to accept evidence offered for the first time in reply. (Jack v. Ring LLC (2023) 91 Cal.App.5th 1186, 1210-1211.) The supplemental declarations of Brian Parkhouse and Robert H. Rose lay the foundation for evidence Parkhouse Tire submitted with the Motion and answer objections Plaintiff first raised in his Opposition. The Court considers them for that purpose and relies on them where cited below.

Objection 1. Overruled. Foundation established. Parkhouse Tire’s Director of Human Resources and each of Parkhouse Tire’s shop managers report to Brian Parkhouse. (Supp. Parkhouse Decl. ¶ 2.) An officer responsible for those functions may testify to the company’s ordinary practice of presenting the Arbitration Agreement to new hires and placing the signed document in the personnel file. Paragraph 4 describes that practice from the declarant’s knowledge and is not offered as a business record. It contains no statement that Plaintiff was given as much time as he believed he needed.

Objection 2. Overruled. Brian Parkhouse is responsible for Parkhouse Tire’s policies and practices governing the maintenance of employee personnel records. (Supp. Parkhouse Decl. ¶ 2.) He is therefore the custodian of those records. A custodian may testify to the identity of the signed Agreement kept in Plaintiff’s file, to the date that document bears, and to his act of signing it. The Court relies on paragraph 5 for those three things and for no account of the circumstances under which Plaintiff signed. (Supp. Parkhouse Decl. ¶ 5.) Plaintiff’s signature also appears on the face of Exhibit A. (Parkhouse Decl. ¶ 5, Ex. A.)

LEGAL STANDARD

“On petition of a party to an arbitration agreement alleging the existence of a written agreement to arbitrate a controversy and that a party to the agreement refuses to arbitrate that controversy, the court shall order the petitioner and the respondent to arbitrate the controversy if it determines that an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists….” (Code Civ. Proc. § 1281.2.) “The party seeking arbitration bears the burden of proving the existence of an arbitration agreement, and the party opposing arbitration bears the burden of proving any defense, such as unconscionability.” (Pinnacle Museum Tower Assn. v. Pinnacle Market Development (US), LLC (2012) 55 Cal.4th 223, 236.) A party meets its initial burden simply by reciting the terms of the governing provision, or by attaching a copy of the provisions. (Sprunk v. Prisma LLC (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 785, 793.) Once the petitioner meets its burden, “the burden shifts to the party opposing the motion to compel, who may present any challenges to the enforcement of the agreement and evidence in support of those challenges.” (Baker v. Italian Maple Holdings, LLC (2017) 13 Cal.App.5th 1152, 1160.)

ANALYSIS

I. Existence of the Agreement

A. Initial Burden

Parkhouse Tire argues that Plaintiff signed the Arbitration Agreement upon his hire and is bound by its terms. Parkhouse Tire provides the declaration of Brian Parkhouse, who attaches the executed Arbitration Agreement bearing Plaintiff’s handwritten signature. (Parkhouse Decl. ¶ 5, Ex. A.) Plaintiff does not dispute that the signature is his. He testifies that he was instructed to sign the onboarding documents and that he reviewed Exhibit A after the Motion was filed. (Martinez Decl. ¶¶ 3-4, 6.) An individual can recognize his own handwritten signature. Where a signatory to an arbitration agreement does not deny that a handwritten signature is his, his failure to recall signing creates no factual dispute about the signature’s authenticity. (Iyere v. Wise Auto Group (2023) 87 Cal.App.5th 747, 757.)

Plaintiff argues that the three-day interval between his signature and the employer’s countersignature leaves it doubtful that the parties ever formed an agreement. (Opp., p. 3:18-21.) Plaintiff signed on October 17, 2023, and Brian Parkhouse countersigned for Parkhouse Tire on October 20, 2023. (Parkhouse Decl. ¶ 5, Ex. A; Supp. Parkhouse Decl. ¶ 5.) Nothing in the Agreement conditions formation on simultaneous execution. Both parties signed, and neither withdrew before the other did.

B. Fraud in the Execution

Plaintiff argues that no agreement was formed because Parkhouse Tire procured his signature by fraud in the execution. (Opp., pp. 6:10-7:6.) A contract is void on that ground where “the fraud goes to the inception or execution of the agreement, so that the promisor is deceived as to the nature of his act, and actually does not know what he is signing.” (Rosenthal, supra, 14 Cal.4th at p. 415.) The party asserting the defense must show that apparent assent “is negated by fraud so fundamental that [he was] deceived as to the basic character of the document[] [he] signed and had no reasonable opportunity to learn the truth.” (Id. at p. 425.) Misrepresentation alone does not suffice. “[I]f a party, with such reasonable opportunity, fails to learn the nature of the document he or she signs, such ‘negligence’ precludes a finding the contract is void for fraud in the execution.” (Id. at p. 423.)

Here, Plaintiff identifies no misrepresentation. His declaration states that no one at Parkhouse Tire mentioned arbitration, explained what arbitration is, or told him that the Agreement waived his right to a jury trial. (Martinez Decl. ¶¶ 7-9.) Silence about a contract term is not deception about the character of the document. Rosenthal declined to extend even a broker’s fiduciary obligations to alerting a customer “to the existence of an arbitration clause or explaining its meaning and effect.” (Rosenthal, supra, 14 Cal.4th at p. 425.) It follows then that Parkhouse Tire, which did not owe Plaintiff a fiduciary duty, was likewise under no obligation to warn Plaintiff about the nature of the Arbitration Agreement.

Nor was Plaintiff denied a reasonable opportunity to learn what he was signing. He testifies that several documents could not be taken home and had to be signed immediately, and that no one told him he could wait and consult a lawyer. (Martinez Decl. ¶¶ 4, 10.) He does not testify that he asked for time and was refused, that anyone prevented him from reading the document, or that anyone told him that it was anything other than an Arbitration Agreement. A signer’s account of feeling rushed adds nothing to the showing without evidence that the other party said or did something to hurry him. (Rosenthal, supra, 14 Cal.4th at p. 424, fn. 12.) The document is captioned “Employment Arbitration Agreement.” Its first sentence states that the parties agree to submit claims “to final and binding arbitration before a neutral arbitrator, and not to any court or during a trial by a jury or in any other forum.” (Parkhouse Decl. , Ex. A, p. 119.)

Plaintiff relies on Najarro v. Superior Court (2021) 70 Cal.App.5th 871. That case is readily distinguishable from this case. In Najarro, two of the employees there were told that the agreements were unimportant, or that the documents concerned only insurance, and were not given sufficient time to read what they signed. The court held that this showed at most fraud in the inducement, because neither employee claimed an inability to read the language of the agreement and each was left to review the documents on his own. (Id. at pp. 889-890.) The employees who did establish the defense were physically prevented from reading the document, or could read neither English nor Spanish and were told to sign anyway. (Id. at pp. 887, 890-891.) Here, Plaintiff provides no evidence that anyone said anything to him about the Agreement or did anything to prevent him from reviewing the Agreement.

The Court therefore concludes that Plaintiff has not established fraud in the execution, and that the parties formed a written agreement to arbitrate.

C. Covered Claims

The Agreement covers “any and all claims or controversies relating to EMPLOYEE’S hiring by, employment with, or the termination of employment from COMPANY.” It names among covered claims those arising under the California Labor Code, those alleging retaliation, and those alleging wrongful discharge. (Parkhouse Decl. , Ex. A, pp. 119-120.) Each of the eleven causes of action arises from Plaintiff’s employment and its termination, and the Agreement therefore covers each of them.

II. Waiver

Plaintiff argues that Parkhouse Tire waived arbitration by litigating for eight months before serving its Motion. (Opp., pp. 7:7-10:20.) A party opposing enforcement “must prove by clear and convincing evidence that the waiving party knew of the contractual right and intentionally relinquished or abandoned it.” (Quach v. California Commerce Club, Inc. (2024) 16 Cal.5th 562, 584 (Quach).) Prejudice is no longer an element. (Id. at p. 585, fn. 5.) The inquiry looks to the words and conduct of the party seeking arbitration. (Ibid.)

Parkhouse Tire’s knowledge is undisputed. Parkhouse Tire held the signed Agreement from October 2023 forward, and it invoked the same form agreement twenty-three days after service in a separate action by another employee. (Parkhouse Decl. ¶ 5, Ex. A; Valdez Decl. ¶¶ 4-5, Exs. 2-3.) Plaintiff offers that conduct as circumstantial evidence of an intent to abandon the right here. How quickly a defendant sought arbitration against a different plaintiff asserting different claims says little about whether it abandoned the right in this case.

Parkhouse Tire pleaded the Agreement as its eighth affirmative defense on November 24, 2025. (Rose Decl. ¶ 3; Supp. Rose Decl., Ex. E, p. 18.) It said nothing further about arbitration until March 9, 2026, when it served discovery responses objecting that Plaintiff had chosen an improper forum. (Rose Decl. ¶ 4; Supp. Rose Decl. ¶ 2.) This delay is not, by itself, clear and convincing evidence of waiver. Waiver requires conduct inconsistent with an intent to enforce, and Plaintiff identifies no litigation step Parkhouse Tire took during those months beyond answering the Complaint, obtaining a two-week extension, and serving written responses to discovery Plaintiff propounded. (Opp., p. 8:16-25.) Responding to discovery an opponent serves is not an election of the judicial forum.

On March 25, 2026, Parkhouse Tire sent Plaintiff’s counsel the signed Agreement and a draft stipulation to arbitrate, and filed a Case Management Statement stating that arbitration is the appropriate forum. (Rose Decl. ¶¶ 5-6, Ex. B.) At the Case Management Conference on April 8, 2026, the Court directed the parties to meet and confer before Parkhouse Tire filed its motion. (Rose Decl. ¶ 7.) Parkhouse Tire renewed the request on April 13, 2026, and Plaintiff’s counsel declined on April 17, 2026, and again on May 22, 2026. (Rose Decl. ¶¶ 8-9, 11, Exs. C, D.) Parkhouse Tire served the Motion on June 3, 2026, twelve days after the final refusal. (Valdez Decl. ¶ 14.) A defendant that files its motion on the schedule the Court set has not abandoned the right.

The defendant in Quach propounded three sets of written discovery, took the plaintiff’s deposition for a full day, and requested a jury trial before moving to compel thirteen months into the case. (Quach, supra, 16 Cal.5th at pp. 586-587.) Parkhouse Tire engaged in none of that conduct.

Plaintiff also points to Parkhouse Tire’s response of “No” to Form Interrogatory No. 200.3, which asks whether any agreement governed the employment relationship. (Opp., p. 10:7-10.) The same set of responses opens with a general objection, applicable to every request, quoting the Agreement and asserting that Plaintiff had proceeded in an improper forum. (Supp. Rose Decl. ¶ 2, Ex. E, p. 2.) The responses to Nos. 200.1, 200.4, and 216.1 identify the Arbitration Agreement. (Supp. Rose Decl. ¶ 2; Ex. E, pp. 3-4, 18.) Read with those responses, the answer to No. 200.3 addresses the terms of employment and not the forum for disputes. Plaintiff reads a further reference in the responses to a protective order with the Court or the arbitrator as showing the same ambiguity. (Opp., p. 10:5-7.) A phrase contemplating either forum, in a set of responses that objects throughout on the ground that Plaintiff had chosen the wrong one, is not clear and convincing evidence of an intent to abandon the right.

Accordingly, the Court concludes that Parkhouse Tire did not waive its right to compel arbitration.

III. Unconscionability

Plaintiff argues that the Agreement is unconscionable and unenforceable. (Opp., pp. 10:21-15:25.) Unconscionability has both a procedural and a substantive element. (Ramirez v. Charter Communications, Inc. (2024) 16 Cal.5th 478, 492 (Ramirez).) Both must be present for a contract to be unenforceable, though not to the same degree, and they are weighed on a sliding scale under which the more substantively oppressive the term, the less evidence of procedural unconscionability is required, and the converse. (Armendariz v. Foundation Health Psychcare Services, Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 83, 114 (Armendariz).) The burden of proving unconscionability is Plaintiff’s. (Ramirez, supra, 16 Cal.5th at p. 492.)

A. Procedural Unconscionability

Procedural unconscionability turns on oppression and surprise arising from unequal bargaining power, and oppression considers the time given, the pressure to sign, the contract’s length and complexity, the employee’s education and experience, and whether the employee had legal assistance. (OTO, L.L.C. v. Kho (2019) 8 Cal.5th 111, 126-127.)

Adhesion contracts in the employment context, offered to employees on a take-it-or-leave-it basis, typically contain some aspects of procedural unconscionability, but that adhesive aspect is not dispositive. (Serpa v. California Surety Investigations, Inc. (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 695, 704.) Where “there is no other indication of oppression or surprise, ‘the degree of procedural unconscionability of an adhesion agreement is low, and the agreement will be enforceable unless the degree of substantive unconscionability is high.’” (Ibid.)

Plaintiff argues that the Agreement is adhesive and was imposed oppressively. (Opp., pp. 11:5-12:4.) He testifies that Parkhouse Tire presented it among a set of onboarding documents he had to sign to begin work. (Martinez Decl. ¶ 4.) Parkhouse Tire does not dispute that the Arbitration Agreement was a condition of employment. Therefore, the Court finds that there was at least a low level of procedural unconscionability.

Here, Plaintiff shows more than bare adhesion. Kho names the time a party is given to consider the contract and the pressure exerted on him to sign it among the circumstances relevant to oppression. Parkhouse Tire offers evidence that new hires are allowed to review the Arbitration Agreement and to ask questions about it. (Parkhouse Decl. ¶ 4.) That evidence of ordinary practice bears on what occurred at Plaintiff’s onboarding. Plaintiff does not testify that he asked for more time and was refused, that he could not read English, or that this document in particular was rushed past him. His testimony that he received no copy of the Agreement remains uncontradicted.

Plaintiff argues surprise on two grounds. He argues first that the Agreement was inserted into an employee handbook exceeding 100 pages. (Opp., p. 11:28-12:2.) However, his declaration describes no handbook and does not describe the number of pages of documents he had to sign. (Martinez Decl. ¶ 4.) He additionally argues that the jury waiver is buried in a prolix paragraph. (Opp., p. 3:12-16.) The Agreement states the waiver in its first sentence and repeats it in a paragraph printed in capital letters. (Parkhouse Decl. , Ex. A, pp. 119, 122.) Exhibit A is a six-page document captioned as an arbitration agreement, paginated 119 through 124, and marked “SIGN AND RETURN” at the top and bottom of every page. (Parkhouse Decl. , Ex. A, pp. 119-124.) Therefore, the Court does not find any additional surprise.

Based on the adhesive nature of the contract and the pressure to sign it immediately, the Court concludes there is a moderate level of procedural unconscionability.

B. Substantive Unconscionability

A term must be overly harsh, unduly oppressive, or unreasonably favorable before it is unconscionable. (Sanchez v. Valencia Holding Co., LLC (2015) 61 Cal.4th 899, 910.) “Not all one-sided contract provisions are unconscionable; hence the various intensifiers in our formulations.” (Id. at p. 911.) “The ultimate issue in every case is whether the terms of the contract are sufficiently unfair, in view of all relevant circumstances, that a court should withhold enforcement.” (Id. at p. 912.)

1.      Scope

First, Plaintiff argues that the Agreement covers claims unrelated to his employment because it extends to disputes with Parkhouse Tire’s “parent entities, subsidiary entities, owners, directors, officers, managers, associates, and agents.” (Opp., pp. 12:13-13:12.) He relies on Cook v. University of Southern California (2024) 102 Cal.App.5th 312 (Cook).

Cook held unconscionable an agreement requiring arbitration of “all claims, whether or not arising out of Employee’s University employment, remuneration or termination,” against the university “or any of its related entities … or its or their officers, trustees, administrators, employees or agents, in their capacity as such or otherwise.” (Id. at p. 317.) That breadth was intolerable because of the many ways a claim against USC, completely unrelated to employment, could arise, one illustration being a botched surgery at a USC hospital 15 years later. (Id. at p. 318.)

Here, the first granting sentence commits to arbitration claims “relating to EMPLOYEE’S hiring by, employment with, or the termination of employment from COMPANY.” It places the parenthetical naming Parkhouse Tire’s affiliates, owners, officers, managers, and agents inside that limitation. An officer, manager, or agent may invoke the Agreement only with respect to a dispute arising from the hiring, employment, or termination. The Cook agreement carried no comparable limitation. It reached claims against the university’s personnel, whether those claims arose out of employment and whether those persons were acting in a capacity connected to the employee.

The subsequent sentence of the Agreement does not enlarge that grant. It provides that the Agreement applies to claims “whether found in contract or tort (including personal injury) or arise under a federal, State or local statute.” It then lists five categories. Every one of those modifiers sorts claims by legal theory. The sentence forecloses an argument that arbitration covers only contract claims, or only statutory ones. It says nothing about subject matter. It runs between “PARTIES,” a term the first sentence defines in the same breath as the employment limitation.

The enumeration confirms the reading. The fourth category is “[a]ny other claims or controversies of any sort relating to EMPLOYEE’S hiring by, employment with or the termination of EMPLOYEE’S employment with COMPANY.” A drafter enlarging the grant beyond the employment would not make an employment-bounded catchall the residual item in the list it introduces. The fifth category, naming claims for theft, embezzlement, unfair competition, and breach of contract, is the employer’s side of the same grant. An employee’s opportunity to commit theft or embezzlement against his employer, or to compete unfairly with it, arises from the employment. (Parkhouse Decl. , Ex. A, pp. 119-120.)

The Agreement therefore reaches claims relating to Plaintiff’s hiring, his employment, and its termination, and no others.

Even on Plaintiff’s reading of the second sentence, the scope is not unconscionable on this record. Unconscionability is highly dependent on context. (Sanchez, supra, 61 Cal.4th at pp. 911.) The exposure that made the Cook agreement intolerable arose from the university’s hospital operations. (Cook, supra, 102 Cal.App.5th at p. 318.) A comparable exposure was absent in Ayala-Ventura v. Superior Court (2026) 119 Cal.App.5th 241, where the employer provided commercial janitorial services and the employee offered no similar show of potential claims. (Id. at p. 257.) Parkhouse Tire restores and services commercial truck and heavy equipment tires for business customers. (Parkhouse Decl. ¶ 2.) As in Ayala-Ventura, Plaintiff likewise offers no examples of claims unrelated to his employment that he might assert against Parkhouse Tire.

On either reading, the Court concludes that Plaintiff has not shown the Agreement’s scope to be substantively unconscionable.

2.      Mutuality

Next, Plaintiff argues that the Agreement lacks mutuality because Parkhouse Tire’s affiliates, officers, and agents may compel him to arbitrate while assuming no reciprocal obligation, and that Parkhouse Tire offers no justification for the asymmetry. (Opp., p. 13:15-27.) Unconscionability “turns not only on a ‘one-sided’ result, but also on an absence of ‘justification’ for it.” (Armendariz, supra, 24 Cal.4th at pp. 117-118.) An employer imposing a one-sided term must show “at least some reasonable justification for such one-sidedness based on ‘business realities.’” (Id. at p. 117.)

Cook held that an agreement conferring a significant benefit on the university’s related entities without a reciprocal benefit to the employee lacked mutuality, and the related entities in that agreement were not bound to arbitrate. (Cook, supra, 102 Cal.App.5th at pp. 319, 328.) Importantly, the employee in Cook was bound to arbitrate claims against the university’s officers and employees “in their capacity as such or otherwise,” and to arbitrate them whether or not they arose out of the employment. (Cook, supra, 102 Cal.App.5th at p. 317.)

The breadth of the Cook arbitration agreement meant that it extended to any claim brought against the related entities, even those that had nothing to do with the plaintiff’s employment. By contrast, here, the parenthetical falls within the employment limitation. An officer, manager, or agent may invoke the Agreement only as to a dispute arising from the hiring, employment, or termination that Plaintiff has already agreed to arbitrate with Parkhouse Tire. The parenthetical adds persons who may compel arbitration; it adds no subject matter to what Plaintiff gave up.

It was on this basis that the court in Ayala-Ventura distinguished Cook. (Ayala-Ventura v. Superior Court (2026) 119 Cal.App.5th 241, 258.) The court further rejected the argument that an employer’s failure to require its employees and agents to arbitrate their claims against an employee results in a lack of mutuality because those employees and agents were not a party to the agreement. (Id. at p. 259.) Armendariz conditions its demand for a business-realities justification on a term that is one-sided as between the contracting parties. Plaintiff has not shown a term of that kind.

Plaintiff also attacks the clause preserving each party’s right to seek “appropriate interim injunctive relief … before or while arbitration proceedings are pending.” (Opp., pp. 13:28-14:15; Parkhouse Decl. , Ex. A, p. 122.) He argues that the clause is mutual in form and one-sided in operation because an employer is more likely than an employee to seek injunctive relief in court. A provision that “does no more than recite the procedural protections already secured by section 1281.8(b)” is not unfairly one-sided. (Baltazar v. Forever 21, Inc. (2016) 62 Cal.4th 1237, 1247-1248.) Plaintiff’s authority condemns a carve-out that broadly authorized the employer to seek any type of injunctive relief in court while confining the employee to arbitration. (Carbajal v. CWPSC, Inc. (2016) 245 Cal.App.4th 227, 249-250.) This clause runs to either party and is confined to interim relief pending arbitration. It enumerates no category of claim, so it exempts none of the confidentiality and non-solicitation disputes an employer would be the one to bring.

3.      Venue

Finally, Plaintiff argues that setting the arbitration in San Diego County is unconscionable because he worked in Bell Gardens and every event underlying his claims occurred in Los Angeles County. (Opp., pp. 14:16-15:4.) The Agreement provides that “[t]he arbitration shall be held in San Diego County or some other location mutually agreed upon by the PARTIES.” (Parkhouse Decl. , Ex. A, p. 121.)

Plaintiff opens the argument with Verdugo v. Alliantgroup, L.P. (2015) 237 Cal.App.4th 141, 147 (Verdugo). A party opposing a forum selection clause ordinarily bears the burden of showing that enforcement would be unreasonable or unfair. That burden is reversed where the underlying claims rest on unwaivable statutory rights, and the party seeking enforcement must then show that enforcement will not diminish those rights. (Ibid.) Verdugo reversed the burden because a forum selection clause could otherwise send a California employee to a forum that may not apply California law, and the clause there designated Harris County, Texas, alongside a Texas choice-of-law provision. (Id. at pp. 146-147.) This Agreement places the arbitration in California, designates the California Arbitration Act as the governing procedure unless the Federal Arbitration Act applies, and directs the arbitrator to construe any covered dispute under applicable California law. (Parkhouse Decl. , Ex. A, pp. 121-122.) Enforcement diminishes no unwaivable Labor Code right, and Parkhouse Tire has met the burden Verdugo assigns it.

Here, Plaintiff offers no evidence that traveling to San Diego County would burden him. Murrey v. Superior Court (2023) 87 Cal.App.5th 1223, on which Plaintiff relies, addressed an agreement that designated no location at all and left the choice to an unidentified dispute resolution organization, an omission the court treated as surprise bearing on procedural unconscionability. (Id. at pp. 1238, 1247.) The employee there had no assurance the arbitration would be held near her home or workplace, and the court observed that the term might have been reasonable had the employer designated a location near the workplace. (Id. at p. 1247.) This Agreement designates a county within California. Plaintiff worked in Bell Gardens, which lies in Los Angeles County. The designated forum is in the same region of the state as the workplace and is reachable by car within a few hours. (The Court takes judicial notice of the relative distance of these locations under Evid. Code, § 452, subd. (h).) While potentially inconvenient, the designated forum is not so far away as to be overly harsh or unduly oppressive to Plaintiff or unreasonably favorable to Parkhouse Tire. The Court concludes that the Arbitration Agreement is not unconscionable. Accordingly, the Motion to Compel Arbitration is granted, and the action is stayed. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.4.)

CONCLUSION

The Motion to Compel Arbitration filed by Parkhouse Tire Service, Inc. is granted. The action is stayed pending completion of the arbitration.

Case Number: 26STCV01794    Hearing Date: August 11, 2026    Dept: 515

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: Hearing on Motion to Compel Arbitration.

The Motion to Compel Arbitration is granted. The action is stayed pending completion of arbitration. The request for dismissal is denied.

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Hector Solorio-Espinoza (Plaintiff) filed this action against DSV Air & Sea Inc. (DSV) and Antoine Belote (Belote), named in the Complaint as Antoine Melote (collectively, Defendants), alleging that Defendants refused to accommodate his disability, harassed and retaliated against him, and terminated his employment because of his disability.

The causes of action are: (1) Discrimination on the Basis of Disability or Medical Condition; (2) Failure to Accommodate Disability; (3) Failure to Engage in a Good Faith Interactive Process; (4) Harassment; (5) Failure to Take All Steps Necessary to Stop Discrimination and Harassment from Occurring; (6) Retaliation; (7) Wrongful Termination in Violation of Public Policy; and (8) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress.

Defendants filed a Motion to Compel Arbitration. Plaintiff filed an Opposition.

LEGAL STANDARD

A written agreement to submit a controversy to arbitration is “valid, enforceable and irrevocable, save upon such grounds as exist for the revocation of any contract.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.) The court “shall order the petitioner and the respondent to arbitrate the controversy if it determines that an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists,” unless the right to compel arbitration has been waived or grounds exist for rescission. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2, subds. (a), (b).)

The moving party must prove the agreement’s existence by a preponderance of the evidence, and the party opposing the motion must prove any defense to enforcement. (Gamboa v. Northeast Community Clinic (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 158, 164-165.) A party invoking an exemption “bear[s] the burden to demonstrate that the exemption applies.” (Betancourt v. Transportation Brokerage Specialists, Inc. (2021) 62 Cal.App.5th 552, 559.) On a motion to compel arbitration, the court “sits as a trier of fact, weighing all the affidavits, declarations, and other documentary evidence, as well as oral testimony received at the court’s discretion, to reach a final determination.” (Engalla v. Permanente Medical Group, Inc. (1997) 15 Cal.4th 951, 972.)

ANALYSIS

I. Existence and Scope

Defendants argue that Plaintiff agreed to arbitrate every dispute arising from his employment when he executed DSV’s Dispute Resolution Agreement during onboarding. (Mot., pp. 2-3.)

Defendants provide the declaration of Shané Jackson, which attaches two arbitration agreements: DISPUTE RESOLUTION AGREEMENT - CA (CA Agreement), executed August 8, 2025, and DISPUTE RESOLUTION AGREEMENT - Non CA (Non-CA Agreement), executed August 14, 2025. (Jackson Decl., ¶¶ 17, 21, Exs. A, C.) Jackson describes the second signing as a re-execution of the same documents, but the Non-CA Agreement contains different terms and names a different company, “DSV Solutions, LLC (name change to DSV Contract Logistics, LLC pending).” (Jackson Decl., ¶ 20, Ex. C, ¶ 1.) The later document did not displace the earlier one. DSV Air & Sea Inc. is not a party to it, and its paragraph 12 requires a writing signed by all parties specifically stating an intent to revoke or modify. (Jackson Decl., Ex. C, ¶ 12.) The operative agreement is the CA Agreement.

Paragraph 1 obligates both parties to arbitrate all disputes arising out of or related in any way to Plaintiff’s employment, and it names wrongful termination, discrimination, harassment, and retaliation among the disputes covered. (Jackson Decl., Ex. A, ¶ 1.) Each cause of action arises from conduct during the employment or from its termination. (Compl., ¶¶ 14-22, 56.)

Paragraph 4 assigns disputes over validity, scope, enforceability, and arbitrability to a court, so the questions Plaintiff raises are for the Court to decide. (Jackson Decl., Ex. A, ¶ 4.) Plaintiff does not deny signing the CA Agreement; he describes the circumstances in which he signed. (Solorio Espinoza Decl., ¶¶ 13-21.)

II. Transportation Worker Exemption

Plaintiff argues that his employment contract falls within section 1 of the Federal Arbitration Act (FAA), which exempts “contracts of employment of seamen, railroad employees, or any other class of workers engaged in foreign or interstate commerce.” (9 U.S.C. § 1.) The question is decided before the Act’s enforcement provisions may be applied. (New Prime Inc. v. Oliveira (2019) 586 U.S. 105, 111.)

The Court first defines the class by reference to the actual work its members as a whole typically carry out, then asks whether that class is engaged in foreign or interstate commerce. (Southwest Airlines Co. v. Saxon (2022) 596 U.S. 450, 455-456 (Saxon).) The worker “must at least play a direct and ‘necessary role in the free flow of goods’ across borders,” and must be actively “engaged in transportation” of those goods through the channels of foreign or interstate commerce. (Id. at p. 458.) The statute does not direct courts to the employer’s industry; the question is what the worker does. (Bissonnette v. LePage Bakeries Park St., LLC (2024) 601 U.S. 246, 254, 256.) A transportation worker “must play some role in the actual transportation of goods.” (Vela v. Harbor Rail Services of California, Inc. (2026) 120 Cal.App.5th 353, 366.)

A. The Relevant Class

Section 1 asks what a class of workers typically does, and Plaintiff bears the burden of establishing it. Here, the record contains two accounts of the Warehouse Supervisor position, one from Plaintiff and one from the manager who supervised him, Mohammed Dayekh.

Plaintiff states that he spent his entire shift on the warehouse floor; that he supervised the employees who loaded and unloaded freight and those who screened shipments with K-9 units before departure; that he directed the order in which outbound trucks were loaded; that he inspected outbound loads before departure and inspected damaged inbound freight; and that he could stop a truck from leaving until an improper load was corrected. (Solorio Espinoza Decl., ¶¶ 2-10.) Dayekh states that the role required Plaintiff to supervise exclusively the workers preparing deliveries for transport from the warehouse, that Plaintiff performed no physical loading or unloading, and that he supervised the building of cargo units, monitored build and load times through the warehouse management system, and handled coaching, discipline, and scheduling. (Dayekh Decl., ¶ 10.)

Plaintiff describes what his position required throughout every shift, and Dayekh describes what “[t]he role of Warehouse Supervisor required,” framing the duties as attributes of the job. (Solorio Espinoza Decl., ¶¶ 3, 10; Dayekh Decl., ¶ 10.) Both accounts therefore describe the position itself. Defendants offer no evidence that any other warehouse supervisor at DSV performed the position differently. Weighing the two accounts, the Court finds that warehouse supervisors at DSV typically direct the sequence in which outbound freight is loaded, inspect the loads, and decide whether a loaded truck may leave. Dayekh’s additional description of scheduling and personnel duties does not contradict any of those functions.

B. Engagement in Commerce

Here, trucks arrived and departed continuously through the workday, and the shipping paperwork and labels Plaintiff observed identified destinations throughout the United States and abroad. (Solorio Espinoza Decl., ¶¶ 5, 11.) DSV ships goods throughout the United States and internationally by air, sea, and roadway. (Jackson Decl., ¶ 2.)

In Saxon, the plaintiff was a ramp supervisor who trained and supervised the ramp agents who physically loaded and unloaded baggage, airmail, and freight on and off airplanes traveling across the country, and who frequently stepped in and did that loading work herself. (Saxon, supra, (2022) 596 U.S.at p. 454.) The Supreme Court held that she belonged to a “class of workers engaged in foreign or interstate commerce” exempt from the FAA under section 1 (9 U.S.C. § 1), reasoning that one who loads cargo onto a plane bound for interstate transit is directly involved in the transportation of that cargo. ( Saxon, supra, 596 U.S. at pp. 453-454, 458-459.) Because the ramp supervisors frequently loaded cargo themselves, the Supreme Court declined to decide whether supervision of cargo loading alone would exempt a class. (Saxon, supra, 596 U.S. at p. 456, fn. 1.)

The governing test asks whether the worker’s relationship to the movement of goods is close enough that the work “plays a tangible and meaningful role in their progress through the channels of interstate commerce.” (Ortiz v. Randstad Inhouse Services, LLC (9th Cir. 2024) 95 F.4th 1152, 1160.) Physical contact with the goods is not always required. The Ninth Circuit held that a fuel technician who places fuel in an airplane used for foreign and interstate commerce is a transportation worker, reasoning that refueling was a vital component of the aircraft’s ability to engage in the interstate and foreign transportation of goods and was therefore “so closely related to interstate and foreign commerce as to be in practical effect part of it.” (Lopez v. Aircraft Service International, Inc. (9th Cir. 2024) 107 F.4th 1096, 1101.)

Similarly, the Third Circuit exempted a field services supervisor who oversaw 30-35 drivers at a package transportation and delivery company and monitored their performance to ensure efficient deliveries. (Palcko v. Airborne Express, Inc. (3d Cir. 2004) 372 F.3d 588, 590.) Her “direct supervision of package shipments” made her work “‘so closely related [to interstate and foreign commerce] as to be in practical effect part of it.’” (Id. at p. 593, quoting Tenney Engineering, Inc. v. United Electrical Radio & Machine Workers of America (3d Cir. 1953) 207 F.2d 450, 452.)

Here, as in Palcko, Plaintiff’s class, warehouse supervisors, satisfies the test because its members decide when the goods are released. The goods do not begin the interstate leg of their journey until the warehouse supervisor authorizes it. Loading cargo onto a vehicle bound for interstate transit is itself commerce (Saxon, supra, 596 U.S. at pp. 457-458), and a worker who determines when that loading is complete is actively engaging in that interstate commerce.

Defendants also argue that Plaintiff’s evidence does not identify the origin, destination, customer, and transportation status of any particular shipment he handled. (Reply, p. 3:22-24.) Section 1 asks about a class, identified by the work its members typically perform, and it calls for no accounting of individual packages. Defendants further argue that the labels Plaintiff observed do not show that a material portion of the class’s work concerned interstate freight. (Reply, p. 4:7-15.) However, Defendants fail to refute Plaintiff’s declaration stating that he regularly observed paperwork and labels showing that the goods were being shipped out of state and internationally. (Solorio Espinoza Decl., ¶ 10.) The Court concludes that Plaintiff has carried his burden of showing that he is part of an exempt class and that the FAA does not govern the Agreement.

III. The Exemption’s Effect

Defendants request in the alternative that the Court compel arbitration under the California Arbitration Act (CAA). (Mot., p. 7.) The exemption’s effect “‘is merely to leave the arbitrability of disputes in the excluded categories as if the [Federal] Arbitration Act had never been enacted.’” (Palcko, supra, 372 F.3d at p. 596, quoting Mason-Dixon Lines, Inc. v. Local Union No. 560 (3d Cir. 1971) 443 F.2d 807, 809.) The exemption does not itself defeat enforcement of the Agreement. Where the FAA does not govern, a California court assesses the agreement under the CAA and enforces it absent a valid defense. (Ruiz v. Sysco Food Services (2004) 122 Cal.App.4th 520, 538-539.)

The Court notes that the Legislature has recently addressed the enforceability of agreements exempt from the FAA under the CAA. Assembly Bill 2155, which the Governor signed on June 30, 2026, adds a subdivision (b) to Code of Civil Procedure section 1281 providing that a written arbitration agreement “is not enforceable under this section to the extent the agreement is not enforceable under the Federal Arbitration Act.” (Stats. 2026, ch. 46.) The amendment takes effect January 1, 2027. It is not in effect on the date of this hearing and does not govern this motion.

IV. Unconscionability

Plaintiff argues that the Agreement is unconscionable and unenforceable. Unconscionability is a general contract defense, and the party asserting it bears the burden of proving it. (Sanchez v. Valencia Holding Co., LLC (2015) 61 Cal.4th 899, 911; see Civ. Code, § 1670.5, subd. (a).) “Both procedural and substantive elements must be present to conclude a term is unconscionable, but these required elements need not be present to the same degree. [Citation.] Courts apply a sliding scale analysis under which ‘the more substantively oppressive [a] term, the less evidence of procedural unconscionability is required to come to the conclusion that the term is unenforceable, and vice versa.’ [Citation.]” (Ramirez v. Charter Communications, Inc. (2024) 16 Cal.5th 478, 493.)

A. Procedural Unconscionability

Procedural unconscionability turns on oppression and surprise arising from unequal bargaining power, and oppression considers the time given, the pressure to sign, the contract’s length and complexity, the employee’s education and experience, and whether the employee had legal assistance. (OTO, L.L.C. v. Kho (2019) 8 Cal.5th 111, 126-127.)

Plaintiff argues that DSV imposed the Agreement on him in English, his secondary language, during a compressed onboarding process, without translation or explanation.

1. Adhesion

Adhesion contracts in the employment context, offered to employees on a take-it-or-leave-it basis, typically contain some aspects of procedural unconscionability, but that adhesive aspect is not dispositive. (Serpa v. California Surety Investigations, Inc. (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 695, 704.) Where “there is no other indication of oppression or surprise, ‘the degree of procedural unconscionability of an adhesion agreement is low, and the agreement will be enforceable unless the degree of substantive unconscionability is high.’” (Ibid.)

Here, DSV drafted the Agreement and presented it through a mandatory onboarding process at the end of a year-long transition following its acquisition of the business. (Compl., ¶¶ 17, 19; Jackson Decl., ¶¶ 3, 8, 12.) No one told Plaintiff that signing was optional, and he understood it to be a condition of continued employment. (Solorio Espinoza Decl., ¶ 20.) The Agreement is adhesive, which establishes at least a low degree of oppression. (Ramirez, supra, 16 Cal.5th at p. 492.)

2. Tight Deadline

Plaintiff states that he was required to sign more than five onboarding documents before a deadline while performing his regular duties. He states that he did not feel he had sufficient time to read each one. (Solorio Espinoza Decl., ¶ 14.) Plaintiff does not state that he asked for more time, that anyone refused, or when the deadline fell. He attributes computer access problems to employees in general but does not say he personally experienced them. (Ibid.). Jackson states that the documents were sent via an email link, that no time limit applied once the review of documents began, and that an employee could return to the link later to finish the review. (Jackson Decl., ¶¶ 8, 12-13.) Plaintiff provides no evidence that DSV said or did anything requiring him to finish signing documents without having an accurate opportunity to review them. Accordingly, the Court concludes that Plaintiff has failed to show additional surprise or oppression based on inadequate time to review the CA Agreement.

3. Lack of Translation

Next, Plaintiff argues that DSV failed to provide him with a Spanish translation of the CA Agreement despite his limited English language ability.

“Generally, a party may not avoid enforcement of an arbitration provision because the party has limited proficiency in the English language. If a party does not speak or understand English sufficiently to comprehend a contract in English, it is incumbent upon the party to have it read or explained to him or her.” (Caballero v. Premier Care Simi Valley LLC (2021) 69 Cal.App.5th 512, 518-519.) However, an arbitration provision is procedurally unconscionable where it “was neither provided in a Spanish-language copy nor explained to respondents who did not understand written English.” (Penilla v. Westmont Corp. (2016) 3 Cal.App.5th 205, 209.)

Plaintiff does not claim that he does not understand written English. Instead, he declares that Spanish is his primary language, that “[a]lthough I understand some conversational English, I do not understand legal terminology written in English,” and that he “read portions of the onboarding documents, including some of the titles, but ... did not understand the legal language contained in the documents.” (Solorio Espinoza Decl., ¶¶ 15-16.) This is consistent with the evidence, which shows that Plaintiff has a working command of English. He communicated with his manager in English daily, in person and in writing. He responded in English to an instruction to issue a corrective action and to an email among several supervisors about warehouse release procedures, and he passed two English safety quizzes. (Dayekh Decl., ¶¶ 3-9, Exs. E-H.)

The record does not show that Plaintiff requested that the contract be read or explained to him, that he sought time to consult a lawyer, or that he told anyone he did not understand what he was signing. Paragraph 11 recites that the signer “had time to read this agreement and ask the Company’s representative any questions I had about the agreement before signing this agreement.” (Jackson Decl., Ex. A, ¶ 11.) At a minimum, that sentence cannot be reasonably understood to constitute “legal terminology.”

Difficulty with legal language bears on the analysis, but only where the contract is drafted to be needlessly difficult to understand. Surprise is present when an agreement’s meaning is difficult to ascertain, as when “‘the allegedly unconscionable provision is hidden within a prolix printed form.’” (Fuentes v. Empire Nissan, Inc. (2026) 19 Cal.5th 93, 104.) An agreement’s “complex sentences replete with legal jargon and statutory references” may present “a substantial barrier to understanding its terms.” (Id. at pp. 104-105.) The agreement in Fuentes was a single paragraph of roughly 900 words containing a 214-word sentence, naming six statutes and citing code sections without explanation, printed in a font the trial court called visually impenetrable. (Id. at p. 105.) This Agreement is two pages. Its provisions are separately numbered, its title states its subject, and the consequence of signing appears in capital letters immediately above the signature line. (Jackson Decl., Ex. A, p. 2.) Plaintiff fails to identify any provisions which are hidden, buried, or impenetrable. What Plaintiff describes is the ordinary unfamiliarity of contract language. A unilateral lack of understanding is not a ground for revoking a contract. (Brookwood v. Bank of America (1996) 45 Cal.App.4th 1667, 1674.)

Because this record shows no further indication of oppression or surprise, the Court concludes that the degree of procedural unconscionability is low.

B. Substantive Unconscionability

Plaintiff argues that paragraph 5 is one-sided because it requires the employee, and only the employee, to deliver a written statement of any claim to DSV’s Legal Department before bringing that claim to arbitration. (Opp., p. 15:13-22; Jackson Decl., Ex. A, ¶ 5.)

A term must be overly harsh, unduly oppressive, or unreasonably favorable before it is unconscionable. (Sanchez, supra, 61 Cal.4th at pp. 910.) “Not all one-sided contract provisions are unconscionable; hence the various intensifiers in our formulations.” (Id. at p. 911.) “The ultimate issue in every case is whether the terms of the contract are sufficiently unfair, in view of all relevant circumstances, that a court should withhold enforcement.” (Id. at p. 912.)

Nyulassy v. Lockheed Martin Corp. (2004) 120 Cal.App.4th 1267 treated a prearbitration submission requirement as a ground of substantive unconscionability where the obligation to arbitrate bound only the employee. The employee’s duty to arbitrate there arose solely from his objection to a Lockheed Martin personnel decision, and he had to carry that objection through successive levels of his supervisory chain of command before he could arbitrate. (Id. at pp. 1273-1274, 1282.) Requiring the employee to submit to “an employer-controlled dispute resolution mechanism (i.e., one without a neutral mediator)” suggested that the employer would receive a “free peek” at the employee’s case, and an advantage if the employee later demanded arbitration. (Id. at pp. 1282-1283.) Carmona applied Nyulassy to a prearbitration disclosure obligation running against employees alone. The “unilateral ‘free peek’ provision” there contributed to the agreement’s substantive unconscionability, which already lacked mutuality in three other respects. (Carmona, supra, 226 Cal. App.4th at pp. 89-90.)

Subsequent cases have rejected free peek challenges to comparable clauses to the one in the CA Agreement. In Serpa, the agreement provided that a dispute the parties could not resolve “through informal internal efforts” would go to binding arbitration. (Serpa, supra, 215 Cal.App.4th at p. 710.) “Informal internal efforts” was not defined in the agreement, and the court read the clause as requiring only informal notice of a grievance. (Ibid.) Distinguishing the one-sided arbitration agreement in Nyulassy, the court held that “a requirement that internal grievance procedures be exhausted before proceeding to arbitration is both reasonable and laudable in an agreement containing a mutual obligation to arbitrate. It plainly does not ‘shock the conscience’ so as to vitiate the arbitration agreement” (Ibid.)

Here, paragraph 1 subjects both parties’ employment-related claims to arbitration and waives both parties’ rights to a court and a jury. (Jackson Decl., Ex. A, ¶ 1.) Paragraph 6 requires the parties to select a neutral arbitrator at the outset of the arbitration. (Jackson Decl., Ex. A, ¶ 6.) The Agreement is therefore neither unilateral nor employer-controlled in the way Nyulassy describes. It also carries none of the further one-sided terms that surrounded the disclosure obligation in Carmona. Moreover, Paragraph 5 does not require DSV to approve the claim or go through any internal procedures before commencing arbitration. Nor does it require Plaintiff to submit more information in his claim than he would include in any notice of arbitration. Indeed, paragraph 5 does not even specify an amount of time that Plaintiff must submit the written claim before commencing arbitration. Therefore, the value of any “free peek” is entirely within Plaintiff’s control by including no more information in the written claim than in the notice of arbitration and providing no significant gap between the written claim and serving notice. Combined with the fact that, unlike Nyulassy, the arbitration itself is bilateral, paragraph 5’s lack of mutuality at most suggests a low degree of substantive unconscionability.

Because there is only a low level of procedural unconscionability, even if the Court concluded that paragraph 5 contains a low degree of substantive unconscionability, that would not render the provision unconscionable. (See Serpa, supra, 215 Cal.App.4th at p. 704.) Accordingly, the Court concludes that the CA Agreement is not unconscionable.

V. Third Party Beneficiary

Defendants argue that Belote, a nonsignatory, may enforce the Agreement as an intended beneficiary. (Mot., pp. 11-12.) A person who is not a party to a contract may enforce it if that person is likely to benefit from the contract, if a motivating purpose of the contracting parties was to provide the benefit, and if enforcement by the third party is consistent with the objectives of the contract and the reasonable expectations of the contracting parties. (Goonewardene v. ADP, LLC (2019) 6 Cal.5th 817, 829-830; see Civ. Code, § 1559.)

Here, paragraph 3 extends Plaintiff’s agreement to arbitrate to claims he might bring against the officers, managers, employees, and agents of DSV and its affiliates. (Jackson Decl., Ex. A, ¶ 3.) The Complaint alleges that Belote was a general manager employed by DSV and that he supervised Plaintiff. It pleads harassment based on disability against all Defendants and names Belote among those who harassed Plaintiff. (Compl., ¶¶ 6, 20, 88, 90.) Paragraph 3 names claims of that kind. The parties included paragraph 3 to extend the benefit of the Agreement to DSV’s managers, and permitting Belote to enforce the promise accords with an agreement whose object is to keep employment disputes out of court. Plaintiff’s contrary argument depends on the premise that no enforceable agreement exists, which the Court has already rejected.

VI. Stay or Dismissal

Defendants request dismissal of the action or, alternatively, a stay pending arbitration. (Mot., pp. 12-13.)

Once a court has ordered arbitration of a controversy at issue in a pending action, it “shall, upon motion of a party to such action or proceeding, stay the action or proceeding until an arbitration is had in accordance with the order to arbitrate or until such earlier time as the court specifies.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.4.)

Here, the Court orders every cause of action to arbitration, so Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.4 requires a stay. Retaining the action on the Court’s docket preserves jurisdiction to address a petition to confirm, correct, or vacate any award. The action is stayed, and the request for dismissal is denied.

CONCLUSION

The Motion to Compel Arbitration is granted. The action is stayed pending completion of arbitration. The request for dismissal is denied.
