MOTION FOR TERMINATING SANCTIONS

Hearing Date: 7/17/26¿ 

CASE NO./NAME: 21BBCV00971 Amy R. Amira, Trustee of The Cynthia L. Amira Family Trust v. CMM, LLP et al.

Moving Party: Plaintiff Amy R. Amira

Responding Party: Unopposed

Notice: Insufficient

Ruling: DENY

 

NOTICE

The Court is not requesting oral argument on this matter.  The Court is guided by California Rules of Court, Rule 3.1308(a)(1) whereby notice of intent to appear is requested.  Unless the Court directs argument in the Tentative Ruling, no argument is requested and any party seeking argument should notify all other parties and the court by 4:00 p.m. on the court day before the hearing of the party’s intention to appear and argue.  The tentative ruling will become the ruling of the court if no argument is received.  

Notice may be given either by email at [email protected] or by telephone at (818) 260-8422.

 

BACKGROUND

This is a real estate investment dispute case. On May 23, 2025, Plaintiff Amy R. Amira, as Trustee of The Cynthia L. Amira Family Trust (Plaintiff) filed the operative Third Amended Complaint asserting twelve causes of action against nine defendants: (1) CMM, LLP (CMM), (2) Gregory A. Mogab (Mogab), (3) OMG Construction Management, LLC (OMG), (4) Agustin Gonzalez (Gonzalez), (5) Anthony Olmedo (Olmedo), (6) Home Trend Developers, Inc. (Home Trend), (7) Ira J. Boren (Boren), (8) Robert Schreiber (Schreiber) and (9) derivatively and nominally defendant Hazelhurst Place Properties, L.P. (“Hazelhurst) (collectively, Defendants). Plaintiff accuses Defendants of orchestrating a fraudulent investment scheme and taking Plaintiff’s investment for personal use and expenses.

On April 30, 2025, Plaintiff filed the instant motion for terminating sanctions against defendant Olmedo only, based on Olmedo’s repeated failures to comply with discovery and the Court’s prior orders. Olmedo has not filed an opposition as of July 16, 2026.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure § 2023.030 gives the court the discretion to impose sanctions against anyone engaging in a misuse of the discovery process. Misuse of the discovery process includes failure to respond to an authorized method of discovery or disobeying a court order to provide discovery. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.010, subds. (d), (g).) A court may impose escalating sanctions where a party disobeys a discovery order. (Code Civ. Proc. § 2025.450(h).) A court may impose terminating sanctions by striking pleadings of the party engaged in misuse of discovery or entering default judgment. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030, subd. (d).) A violation of a discovery order is sufficient for the imposition of terminating sanctions. (Collison & Kaplan v. Hartunian (1994) 21 Cal.App.4th 1611, 1620.) Terminating sanctions are appropriate when a party persists in disobeying the court’s orders. (Deyo v. Kilbourne (1978) 84 Cal.App.3d 771, 795-796.)

The court should consider the totality of the circumstances, including conduct of the party to determine if the actions were willful, the detriment to the propounding party, and the number of formal and informal attempts to obtain discovery. (Lang v. Hochman (2000) 77 Cal.App.4th 1225, 1246.) If a lesser sanction fails to curb abuse, a greater sanction is warranted. (Van Sickle v. Gilbert (2011) 196 Cal.App.4th 1495, 1516.) However, “the unsuccessful imposition of a lesser sanction is not an absolute prerequisite to the utilization of the ultimate sanction.” (Deyo, supra, 84 Cal.App.3d at p. 787.) Before any sanctions may be imposed the court must make an express finding that there has been a willful failure of the party to serve the required answers. (Fairfield v. Superior Court for Los Angeles County (1966) 246 Cal.App.2d 113, 118.) Lack of diligence may be deemed willful where the party understood its obligation, had the ability to comply, and failed to comply. (Deyo, supra, 84 Cal.App.3d at p. 787; Fred Howland Co. v. Superior Court of Los Angeles County (1966) 244 Cal.App.2d 605, 610-611.) The party who failed to comply with discovery obligations has the burden of showing that the failure was not willful. (Deyo, supra, 84 Cal.App.3d at p. 788; Cornwall v. Santa Monica Dairy Co. (1977) 66 Cal.App.3d 250; Evid. Code, §§ 500, 605.)

A terminating sanction is a “drastic measure which should be employed with caution.” (Deyo, 84 Cal.App.3d at p. 793.) “A decision to order terminating sanctions should not be made lightly. But where a violation is willful, preceded by a history of abuse, and the evidence shows that less severe sanctions would not produce compliance with the discovery rules, the trial court is justified in imposing the ultimate sanction.” (Mileikowsky v. Tenet Healthsystem (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 262, 279-280.) 

 
DISCUSSION

Plaintiff argues that Olmedo, who represents himself pro per, has chosen not to participate in this case. (Mot., p. 3.) Plaintiff argues that Olmedo abruptly terminated his previous deposition and refused to reschedule or appear pursuant to court order. (Ibid.) Plaintiff argues that it is clear Olmedo has willfully disregarded his discovery obligations and Court orders. (Ibid.) Plaintiff argues that terminating sanctions are needed to mitigate the prejudice caused by Olmedo’s conduct and disregard for important discovery procedures. (Mot., p. 5.) Plaintiff argues that Olmedo is a central player in the alleged fraudulent scheme and allowing Olmedo to appear at trial after willfully ignoring Court orders and avoiding discovery would be wholly unfair. (Ibid.)

Here, the Court finds that Olmedo’s violations were willful. On December 4, 2024, Olmedo and Plaintiff’s counsel logged into a virtual deposition session where Olmedo appeared briefly on video. (Markevitch Decl., ¶ 4.) Plaintiff’s counsel declares that Olmedo appeared to be walking around outside and from the outset let Plaintiff’s counsel know he was only available for two hours, even though Plaintiff’s counsel advised Olmedo the deposition would take up to seven hours. (Markevitch Decl., ¶¶ 4-5.) Plaintiff’s counsel declares that Olmedo then suddenly disconnected from the deposition without warning and did so again a few more times before communicating to Plaintiff’s counsel that he was going to look for a Starbucks to establish a better connection. (Markevitch Decl., ¶¶ 5-7; Exh. 2.) In the email communication, Olmedo reveals he did not go look for a Starbucks as he had indicated prior and did not feel he would have time to talk anyway. (Markevitch Decl., Exh. 2, p. 2.)

On March 10, 2025, the Court granted Plaintiff’s unopposed motion to compel the deposition of Olmedo. (Robinson Decl., Exh. 4.) The Court imposed sanctions of $2,185.00 on Olmedo. (Ibid.) On March 14, 2025, Plaintiff sent Olmedo a copy of the Court’s order via email, to no response. (Robinson Decl., Exh. 5.) On March 24, 2025, Plaintiff electronically served Olmedo a second amended deposition notice setting the deposition for March 31, 2025. (Robinson Decl., Exh. 6.) Olmedo failed to appear at the deposition and did not file any objections. (Robinson Decl., Exh. 7.) This was Olmedo’s second non-appearance after Plaintiff’s counsel deemed Olmedo’s first deposition a non-appearance due to its quick and abrupt termination. (Markevitch Decl., Exh. 3.) On March 31, 2025, Plaintiff reached out to Olmedo via email to inquire about his non-appearance in attempt to reschedule and Olmedo did not respond. (Robinson Decl., Exh. 8.)  On April 18, 2025, Plaintiff once more reached out to Olmedo via email to inquire about the deposition, Olmedo did not respond. (Robinson Decl., Exh. 9.)

Since December of 2024, Olmedo has refused to appear for a Court ordered deposition, disregarded the discovery process, and failed to respond to any communications from Plaintiff’s counsel. (Robinson Decl., ¶ 8.) Thus, the Court finds a willful abandonment of the litigation process.

The burden then shifts to Olmedo to show the failures were not willful. (Deyo v. Kilbourne (1978) 84 Cal.App.3d 771, 788; Cornwall v. Santa Monica Dairy Co. (1977) 66 Cal.App.3d 250; Evid. Code, §§ 500, 605.) Olmedo filed no opposition and therefore made no showing that the repeated failures to appear were not willful.

However, the Court finds service inadequate. In civil cases involving both represented and self-represented parties, each self-represented party or other person is to file, serve, and be served with documents by non-electronic means unless the self-represented party or other person affirmatively agrees otherwise. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.253(b)(3).) A self-represented party may express consent to electronic service by either (1) serving a notice on all parties and filing the notice with the court, or (2) manifesting affirmative through electronic means with the court and concurrently providing the party’s electronic address. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1010.6(c)(3).) The act of electronic filing shall not be construed as express consent. (Ibid.) Here, the Court finds no record of Olmedo, who is in pro per, consenting to electronic service. An email communication wherein Olmedo acknowledges he is without counsel in response to the notice of deposition is not explicit consent to electronic notice. (See Markevitch Decl., Exh. 2, pp. 4-5.) Thus, Olmedo has not properly been given notice, and his repeated failures to appear are not considered willful.

For this reason, the motion for terminating sanctions is DENIED.

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Plaintiff Amy R. Amira’s motion for terminating sanctions is DENIED.

Plaintiff shall provide proper notice of this order.

MOTION TO DISMISS AND EXPUNGE LIS PENDENS

Hearing Date: 7/17/26

CASE NO./NAME: 23BBCV00340 Michele A. Santopietro v. Sandra D. Santopietro et al.

Moving Party: Specially Appearing Defendants Juvelyn Palomique and Stephen Harrison

Responding Party: Plaintiff Michele A. Santopietro

Notice: Sufficient 

Ruling: GRANT

 

NOTICE

The Court is not requesting oral argument on this matter.  The Court is guided by California Rules of Court, Rule 3.1308(a)(1) whereby notice of intent to appear is requested.  Unless the Court directs argument in the Tentative Ruling, no argument is requested and any party seeking argument should notify all other parties and the court by 4:00 p.m. on the court day before the hearing of the party’s intention to appear and argue.  The tentative ruling will become the ruling of the court if no argument is received.  

Notice may be given either by email at [email protected] or by telephone at (818) 260-8422.

BACKGROUND

This is a real property dispute. On June 27, 2007, plaintiff Michelle A Santopietro (Plaintiff) and defendant Sandra D. Santopietro (Defendant), daughter and mother, acquired the property located at 554 E. San Jose Ave., #204, Burbank, California 9150 (the Property) as joint tenants. On October 8, 2008, Plaintiff conveyed her share of the interest in the Property to Defendant. Specially appearing defendants, and bona fide purchasers, Juvelyn Palomique and Stephen Harrison (Movants) later purchased the Property from Defendant.

On February 10, 2023, Plaintiff filed a Complaint alleging two causes of action for (1) quiet title and (2) fraud against Defendant and Movants concerning the Property. Plaintiff alleged that her property interest was conveyed to Defendant without her knowledge, and the subsequent sale of the Property to Movants was wrongful. On February 17, 2023, Plaintiff filed an amended Notice of Pendency of Action with the Court and recorded it in the Los Angeles County Recorder’s Office.

On April 5, 2024, the Court dismissed Plaintiff’s first cause of action for quiet title against Defendant. On October 21, 2024, the Court granted summary judgment in favor of Defendant, and against Plaintiff. On December 19, 2024, Plaintiff filed a notice of appeal of the judgment. On July 1, 2025, the Second Appellate District entered an order dismissing the appeal.

On January 16, 2026, Movants filed the instant motion to Expunge Lis Pendens requesting an order dismissing Movants from the action, expunging the Notice of Pendency of Action (Lis Pendens) and awarding attorney’s fees and costs. Movants argue that they have never been served with the Complaint, which was filed nearly three years ago, but Plaintiff has not dismissed the case against them. Moreover, Movants argue that the judgement entered against Plaintiff defeats any claim to the Property.

On May 11, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition. On May 15, 2026, Movants filed a reply.

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

            Plaintiff requests judicial notice of Exhibits: (A) Complaint in this matter (filed February 10, 2023); (B) First Notice of Pendency of Action (filed February 16, 2023, but not recorded); (C) second Notice of Pendency (filed and recorded February 17, 2023); (D) Proof of Service of Summons and Complaint (filed June 21, 2023); (E) Minute Order (dated July 10, 2023); (F) Order re: Motion to Dismiss the First Cause of Action in the Verified Complaint Filed by Plaintiff (dated April 5, 2024); (G) Order re: Motion for Summary Judgment or Alternatively, for Summary Adjudication (filed October 18, 2024); (H) Judgment by Court in favor of Sandra D. Santopietro (entered October 21, 2024); (I) Plaintiff’s Notice of Appeal of the Judgment (filed December 19, 2024) and (J) Court of Appeal, Second Appellate District’s Order dismissing the Appeal (filed July 1, 2025). The request is granted. (Evid. Code § 452(d).)

MOTION TO DISMISS

Legal Standard

Code of Civil Procedure section 583.410 authorizes the Court, in its discretion, to dismiss an action for delay in prosecution. Section 583.420(a)(1) expressly provides that the Court may dismiss an action if service is not made within two years after the action commenced against the defendant. The two-year period is measured from the date the complaint is filed. 

An unexcused failure to serve a summons within the two-year period constitutes prima facie grounds for dismissal. In such a case, there is no requirement for an affirmative showing of prejudice. (Scarzella v. DeMers (1993) 17 Cal.App.4th 1762, 1768–1769.) The standard for excusing delay in service is more stringent than that for delay in bringing a case to trial, and any claimed impossibility, impracticability, or futility must be strictly construed. (Id. at 1770.) To avoid dismissal, the plaintiff must show that he exercised reasonable diligence throughout the entire two-year period. (A. Groppe & Sons Glass Co., Inc. v. Fireman's Fund Ins. Co. (1991) 232 Cal.App.3d 220, 225.)

Discussion

Movants argue that it has been well over two years since the filing of the Complaint, and Plaintiff has not served them. (Mot., p. 7.) Moreover, Movants argue it has nearly been three years since the filing of the Complaint and Plaintiff has not brought the case to trial against Movants. (Ibid.)

Plaintiff does not substantively address Movants’ arguments for dismissal in her opposition. (See Opp.)

            Here, the Complaint was filed on February 10, 2023. (See RJN, Exh. A.) As of July 14, 2026, no proof of service for service of the Complaint has been filed with the Court regarding Movants. (Lazaran Decl., ¶ 6.) Thus, it has been more than two years since the action commenced, and Movants have not been served. The statutory period has expired.

            Accordingly, the court exercises its discretion and dismisses the action for failure to prosecute.

MOTION TO EXPUNGE LIS PENDENS

Legal Standard

Code of Civil Procedure section 405.30 states: “At any time after notice of pendency of action has been recorded, any party, or any nonparty with an interest in the real property affected thereby, may apply to the court in which the action is pending to expunge the notice. . . . Evidence or declarations may be filed with the motion to expunge the notice. The court may permit evidence to be received in the form of oral testimony, and may make any orders it deems just to provide for discovery by any party affected by a motion to expunge the notice. The claimant shall have the burden of proof under Sections 405.31 and 405.32.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 405.30.)

“In proceedings under this chapter, the court shall order the notice expunged if the court finds that the pleading on which the notice is based does not contain a real property claim.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 405.31.)

“[T]he court shall order that the notice be expunged if the court finds that the claimant has not established by a preponderance of the evidence the probable validity of the real property claim. The court shall not order an undertaking to be given as a condition of expunging the notice if the court finds the claimant has not established the probable validity of the real property claim.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 405.32.)

Because the claimant has the burden of proof, in opposing the motion to expunge the lis pendens, they must demonstrate the following by a preponderance of the evidence: (1) the action affects title to or right of possession of the real property described in the notice; (2) in so far as the said notice is concerned, the party recording the notice has commenced the action for a proper purpose and in good faith; and (3) the probable validity of the real property claim. (Hunting World, Inc. v. Superior Ct. (1994) 22 Cal.App.4th 67, 70; see also Nunn v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A. (2021) 64 Cal.App.5th 346, 363 [“The party opposing a motion to expunge a lis pendens has the burden to show that a real property claim has been alleged and has probable validity based upon a preponderance of the evidence.”].)

“A claim has ‘probable validity’ where it is more likely than not that the plaintiff will obtain a judgment against the defendant on that claim.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 481.190.)      

Discussion

Movants argue that the dismissal order, MSJ, and judgment, all in favor of Defendant, effectively preclude Plaintiff from demonstrating the probable validity of a real property claim against Movants. (Mot., p. 9.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that if the Court were to expunge lis pendens, Movants “will have the door opened wide to commit more crimes” and have “the opportunity to flee the country to avoid prosecution”. (Opp. p. 14.)  

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 405.30, the claimant who filed the lis pendens has the burden of proof. Here, Plaintiff filed the lis pendens, and thus, Plaintiff has the burden of proof. (RJN, Exhibit C; Lazaran Decl., ¶ 3.) Here, the Court dismissed Plaintiff’s first cause of action for quiet title against Defendant and summary judgment was entered in favor of Defendant, against Plaintiff, disposing of the cause of action for fraud. (RJN, Exh. F-G.) The Court concluded that no fraud occurred regarding the 2008 deed in which Plaintiff granted Defendant her share of interest in the Property. (Ibid.) Since Plaintiff’s claim for quiet title was dismissed and the Court did not find fraud in execution of the 2008 deed, Defendant’s conveyance to Movants in 2022 was not wrongful. Moreover, Plaintiff’s opposition to the instant motion contains no legal authority and is made up of several criminal accusations against Movants and the Court. Thus, Plaintiff has not provided evidence to demonstrate probable validity of the real property claim by a preponderance of the evidence. Plaintiff has not met her burden under the statute.  

Accordingly, the motion to expunge lis pendens is granted. 

Attorney’s Fees and Costs

“The court shall direct that the party prevailing on any motion under this chapter be awarded the reasonable attorney’s fees and costs of making or opposing the motion unless the court finds that the other party acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of attorney's fees and costs unjust.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 405.38.) 

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 405.38, Movants request attorney’s fees in the amount of $2,750 and costs in the amount of $60.00. (Mot., p. 11.) This amount encompasses six hours in drafting and editing the instant motion and five hours for review of an opposition, preparation of a reply, and appearing at the hearing at a rate of $250/hour. (Lazaran Decl., ¶ 7.) Movants counsel also requests $60 in costs for electronic filing. (Ibid.)  

The Court finds that the 11.0 hours billed for preparing the motion is excessive. In granting the instant motion, the Court finds it reasonable to award Movants $2,060, reflecting 8.0 hours at a rate of $250/hour and $60 in costs. 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Specially Appearing Defendants Juvelyn Palomique and Stephen Harrison’s motion to Dismiss and motion to Expunge Lis Pendens is GRANTED. Attorney’s fees and costs are awarded in the amount of $2,060.

Specially Appearing Defendants Juvelyn Palomique and Stephen Harrison shall provide notice of this order.

NOTICE

The Court is not requesting oral argument on this matter.  The Court is guided by California Rules of Court, Rule 3.1308(a)(1) whereby notice of intent to appear is requested.  Unless the Court directs argument in the Tentative Ruling, no argument is requested and any party seeking argument should notify all other parties and the court by 4:00 p.m. on the court day before the hearing of the party’s intention to appear and argue.  The tentative ruling will become the ruling of the court if no argument is received.

BACKGROUND

On August 12, 2024, Plaintiff Joseph Isagulyan (“Plaintiff”) filed this action against Defendant Ralphs Grocery Company (“Defendant”) and Does 1 to 20 for injuries sustained in a slip and fall incident at Defendant’s property.  The Complaint alleges a single cause of action for premises liability. 

On July 16, 2025, Defendant served Form Interrogatories, Set One, Special Interrogatories, Set One, and Requests for Production of Documents, Set One on Plaintiff. (Woodruff Decl., ¶ 5.)  Having received no responses to their discovery requests, Defendant proceeded to file the instant motions compelling Plaintiff to provide responses.  Defendant also requests monetary sanctions in the amount of $1,410.00 per motion. 

LEGAL STANDARD

Where a party¿fails to¿serve¿timely¿responses to discovery requests, the court may make an order compelling responses.¿¿(Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.290, 2031.300;¿Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pacific Healthcare Consultants¿(2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, 403.)¿¿ A party that¿fails to¿serve¿timely¿responses waives any objections to the request, including ones based on privilege or the protection of attorney work product.¿¿(Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.290, subd. (a), 2031.300, subd. (a).)¿ Unlike a motion to compel¿further¿responses, a motion to compel responses is not subject to a 45-day time limit and the propounding¿party has no meet and confer obligations.¿¿(Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc., supra, 148 Cal.App.4th at p. 404.)¿ 

If a party to whom a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling is directed¿fails to¿serve¿a timely¿response to it, the party making the demand may move for an order compelling response to the demand. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300, subd. (b).) In addition, the failure of the responding party to serve¿timely¿responses “waives any objection to the demand, including one based on privilege or on the protection for work product . . .” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300, subd. (a).)¿ 

 

DISCUSSION                                                                                              

A.    Motions to Compel

Defendant served Form Interrogatories, Set One, Special Interrogatories, Set One, and Request for Production of Documents, Set One on July 16, 2025. (Woodruff Decl., ¶ 5.)  Responses were due August 19, 2025.  (Woodruff Decl., ¶ 6.)  Plaintiff obtained an extension to respond by September 1, 2025.  (Woodruff Decl., ¶ 7.)  Defendant sent a meet and confer letter on May 1, 2026, which Plaintiff has not responded to.  (Woodruff Decl., ¶ 8.) As of the filing of the instant motions on May 20, 2026, Plaintiff has not served responses to Defendant’s discovery requests. (Woodruff Decl., ¶ 10.)

As Defendant properly served the discovery requests, and Plaintiff failed to serve responses, the Court finds Defendant entitled to orders directing Plaintiff to provide responses to Defendant’s discovery requests. 

Accordingly, Defendant’s motions to compel responses to Form Interrogatories, Set One, Special Interrogatories, Set One, and Requests for Production of Documents, Set One, are GRANTED. 

 

B.    Monetary Sanctions

Defendant requests monetary sanctions in the amount of $1,410.00 per motion, consisting of two hours preparing the motion, an anticipated two hours to review Plaintiff’s opposition and prepare a reply, one hour preparing for the hearing, and one hour attending the hearing at an hourly rate of $225.00, as well as a $60.00 filing fee.  (Woodruff Decl., ¶¶ 12-13.) 

Where the court grants a motion to compel responses, sanctions shall be imposed against the party who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel, unless the party acted with substantial justification or the sanction¿would¿otherwise be unjust.¿¿(Code¿Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.290, subd. (c), 2031.300, subd. (c).)¿

The Court finds two hours preparing each motion excessive, as the motions are straightforward and similar to each other.  Additionally, no opposition has been filed for any of the three motions.  Finally, the Court finds two hours to prepare for and attend the hearing to be excessive for motions of this scope.  The Court reduces the sanctions request to a reasonable total amount of $855.00, consisting of two hours for preparing all three motions and one hour for preparing for and attending the hearing at the hourly rate of $225.00, plus a $60.00 filing fee for each motion. 

Defendant’s request for sanctions is GRANTED in the total amount of $855.00, against Plaintiff and counsel, jointly and severally. 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Defendant’s motions to compel responses to Form Interrogatories, Set One, Special Interrogatories, Set One, and Requests for Production, Set One are GRANTED.

Defendant’s request for monetary sanction is GRANTED in the total amount of $855.00, against Plaintiff and counsel, jointly and severally.

BACKGROUND

This is a personal wrongful death and survival action matter, arising from a vehicle collision. Attorney Mark J. Nagle (“Counsel”) represents Plaintiff Lacey Brewer. Counsel moves to be relieved as counsel, citing an irremediable breakdown in the attorney-client relationship. No opposition has been filed.

LEGAL STANDARD

            The Court has discretion to allow an attorney to withdraw, and such a motion should be granted provided that there is no prejudice to the client and it does not disrupt the orderly process of justice. (See Ramirez v. Sturdevant (1994) 21 Cal.App.4th 904, 915; People v. Prince (1968) 268 Cal.App.2d 398, 403-407.)

A motion to be relieved as counsel must be made on Judicial Council Form MC-051 (Notice of Motion and Motion), MC-052 (Declaration), and MC-053 (Proposed Order). (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362, subds. (a), (c), (e).) The requisite forms must be served “on the client and on all parties that have appeared in the case.” (Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1362, subd. (d).)

DISCUSSION           

Counsel has submitted completed MC-051, MC-052, and MC-053 forms in compliance with the rule 3.1362 requirements. 

Counsel submitted a declaration stating there has been an irreconcilable breakdown in attorney-client relationship and communications. (MC-052.)  Counsel asserts the client has been served with copies of the motion papers, and a proof of service has been filed, reflecting service via mail was made on Plaintiff. (MC-052; POS 6/26/26.) Defendant has not yet appeared in this case.  Thus, Counsel has complied with the service requirements of rule 3.1362. 

Accordingly, Counsel’s motion to be relieved as counsel is GRANTED. 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Counsel’s motion to be relieved as counsel is GRANTED.

Ruling: SUSTAINED in part and OVERRULED in part

BACKGROUND

This is a property dispute action. Plaintiff Garen Avetisyan (Plaintiff) is the former spouse of defendant Nektar Avetisyan (Defendant), the two were divorced in May of 2014. This dispute concerns a Toluca Lake residence (the Property), which Plaintiff alleges Defendant wrongfully transferred into her trust, and then later sold.  Plaintiff alleges that he funded the initial purchase of the property and Defendant has appropriated the proceeds of the sale.

On December 4, 2025, Plaintiffs filed the First Amended Complaint (FAC) asserting ten causes of action for (1) breach of contract; (2) breach of implied in fact contract; (3) constructive trust; (4) unjust enrichment; (5) resulting trust; (6) constructive fraud; (7) conversion; (8) fraud (intentional misrepresentation or concealment); (9) accounting; and (10) money had and received. On June 10, 2026, Defendant filed the instant demurrer to all ten causes of action for failure to state a claim and alleges the FAC is a sham pleading and uncertain. On July 6, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition. On July 10, 2026, Defendant filed a reply.

LEGAL STANDARD

            “[A] demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the allegations in a complaint.” (Lewis v. Safeway, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 385, 388.) A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. (See Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994 [in ruling on a demurrer, a court may not consider declarations, matters not subject to judicial notice, or documents not accepted for the truth of their contents].) For purposes of ruling on a demurrer, all facts pleaded in a complaint are assumed to be true, but the reviewing court does not assume the truth of conclusions of law. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hosp. Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 967.)

Further, the court may, upon motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a).) The court may also strike all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (b).) The grounds for a motion to strike are that the pleading has irrelevant, false, or improper matter, or has not been drawn or filed in conformity with laws. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437.)

Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (See Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349 [court shall not “sustain a demurrer without leave to amend if there is any reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment”]; Kong v. City of Hawaiian Gardens Redevelopment Agency (2002) 108 Cal.App.4th 1028, 1037 [“A demurrer should not be sustained without leave to amend if the complaint, liberally construed, can state a cause of action under any theory or if there is a reasonable possibility the defect can be cured by amendment.”]; Vaccaro v. Kaiman (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 761, 768 [“When the defect which justifies striking a complaint is capable of cure, the court should allow leave to amend.”].) The burden is on the complainant to show the Court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

MEET AND CONFER

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41 requires that before the filing of a demurrer the moving party “shall meet and confer in person or by telephone” with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer.  (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41(a).)  The parties are to meet and confer at least five days before the date the responsive pleading is due. (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41(a)(2).)  Thereafter, the moving party shall file and serve a declaration detailing their meet and confer efforts. (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41(a)(3).) 

Defendant’s counsel declares they sent a meet and confer letter to Plaintiff’s counsel on June 6, 2026, asking Plaintiff to dismiss the action. (Karapetyan Decl., ¶ 3.) Defendant’s counsel declares that they followed up with Plaintiff’s counsel via telephone on June 8, 2026, and Plaintiff refused to dismiss the FAC. (Ibid; see also Decl. of Demurring or Moving Party Regarding Meet and Confer.) Plaintiff does not address meet and confer efforts.

Although one letter and phone call asking opposing party to dismiss the action outright does not sufficiently exhaust meet and confer efforts, the Court cannot overrule a demurrer based on an insufficient meet and confer.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41(a)(3).). The Court will review the demurrer on its merits but admonishes Defendant to make more of an effort to meet and confer in the future.

DISCUSSION           

Defendant demurs to each cause of action asserted by Plaintiff in the FAC for failure to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. (Dem., pp. 2-3.) Defendant also argues the entire FAC is a sham pleading and is uncertain regarding Defendant’s trustee capacity. (Ibid.)

Sham Pleading Doctrine

A court has the authority to strike sham pleadings (i.e. those not filed in conformity with a prior ruling. (Code of Civ. Proc. §436.)  A party may seek to attack an alleged sham pleading via a motion to strike or courts have ruled, via a demurrer. (Ricard v. Grobstein, Goldman, Stevenson Siegel, LeVine & Mangel (1992), 6 Cal.App.4th 157, 162.)

"Under the sham pleading doctrine, allegations in an original pleading that rendered it vulnerable to demurrer or other attack cannot simply be omitted without explanation…. The purpose of the doctrine is to enable the courts to prevent an abuse of process…. The doctrine is not intended to prevent honest complainants from correcting erroneous allegations or to prevent the correction of ambiguous facts…." (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 751.)

“If a party files an amended complaint and attempts to avoid the defects of the original complaint by either omitting facts which made the previous complaint defective or by adding facts inconsistent with those of previous pleadings, the court may take judicial notice of prior pleadings and may disregard any inconsistent allegations. (Deveny v. Entropin, Inc. (2006) 139 Cal.App.4th 408, 425.) 

            Defendant argues that the original Complaint and FAC directly contradict each other in a material way. (Dem., p. 6.) Defendant argues that the original complaint alleges that Plaintiff “subsequently took residence at the Property, alongside Defendant” and that in 2024, Plaintiff “decided to vacate the property” while continuing to financially support the mortgage and living expenses. (Ibid.) Defendant argues that in contrast, the FAC alleges that Plaintiff “never took residence at the Property alongside Defendant”. (Ibid.) Moreover, Defendant argues that the FAC also alleges that Plaintiff “funded the home they resided in”. (Dem., p. 7.) Defendant argues that this swap is incredibly contradictory and Plaintiff offers no explanation is provided which explains swapping “one factual universe for another”. (Dem., p. 6.)

            Plaintiff argues that an explanation is offered in the amended pleading to satisfy the doctrine’s requirements and leave to amend must be granted. (Opp., p. 8.) In reply Defendant argues that this explanation is sufficient. (Reply, p. 2.)

            Here, the Complaint alleges that Plaintiff lived at the Property until 2024 and then decided to move out while continuing to financially support the home. (Complaint, ¶¶ 27-28.) However, the FAC alleges that Plaintiff never resided at the Property while also referring to the Property as the home Plaintiff and Defendant resided in. (FAC, ¶¶ 12, 68.) Plaintiff’s counsel explains in his declaration that the discrepancy reflects a drafting error made in the initial pleading, which was corrected to conform to the facts Plaintiff relayed. (Bayburtski Decl., ¶ 3.)

            Since Plaintiff has offered an explanation for the discrepancy, the sham pleading doctrine is inapplicable here.

Uncertainty

“A demurrer for uncertainty is not intended to reach the failure to incorporate sufficient facts in the pleading but is directed at the uncertainty existing in the allegations actually made”. (Butler v. Sequeira (1950) 100 Cal.App.2d 143, 145-146.) Moreover, demurrers for uncertainty are disfavored and will only be sustained where the pleading is so ambiguous and unclear that the defendant cannot reasonably respond, i.e., he or she cannot reasonably determine what issues must be admitted or denied, or what counts or claims are directed against him or her. (Khoury v. Maly’s of Calif. Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.)

Defendant argues that the FAC is uncertain as to Defendant’s trustee status because Plaintiff states the claim against Defendant in her capacity as trustee, but the substantive allegations concern alleged personal promises. (Dem., p. 7.)  

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that Plaintiff may sue Defendant as an individual and in her capacity as trustee of her own revocable trust because his claim is one of a creditor and a trustee is personally liable for obligations arising from ownership or control of trust property. (Opp., pp. 16-17.) Defendant does not address this argument in his reply.

Here, the FAC is not uncertain. While Plaintiff does not specify Defendant’s capacity in each cause of action, Plaintiff identifies Defendant as an individual being sued in her capacity as a trustee. (FAC, ¶ 4.) Moreover, the FAC is not so ambiguous or unclear that Defendant cannot reasonably respond.

Thus, demurrer is OVERRULED as to uncertainty

First Cause of Action: Breach of Contract

To state a cause of action for breach of contract, Plaintiff must be able to establish “(1) the existence of the contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance, (3) defendant’s breach, and (4) the resulting damages to the plaintiff.” (Oasis West Realty, LLC v. Goldman (2011) 51 Cal.4th 811, 821.) 

If a breach of contract claim “is based on alleged breach of a written contract, the terms must be set out verbatim in the body of the complaint or a copy of the written agreement must be attached and incorporated by reference”. (Harris v. Rudin, Richman & Appel (1999) 74 Cal.App.4th 299, 307.) In some circumstances, a plaintiff may also “plead the legal effect of the contract rather than its precise language.” (Construction Protective Services, Inc. v. TIG Specialty Ins. Co. (2002) 29 Cal.4th 189, 198-199.) 

            Defendant argues that Plaintiff fails to state whether the contract is written, oral, or implied and fails to plead facts which support formation of a valid contract. (Dem., p. 9.) Defendant argues that Plaintiff only alleges that a contract was formed because of an “understanding”. (Ibid.) Moreover, Plaintiff argues that the nature of the “understanding”, ownership interest in real property, falls within the statute of frauds which mandates a writing. (Ibid.) Moreover, Defendant argues Plaintiff fails to allege any sufficient consideration for the purported contract. (Dem., p. 10.)

            In opposition, Plaintiff argues that the Court has the discretion to compel specific performance in cases of part performance and to prevent fraud. (Opp., pp. 8-9.)  Plaintiff argues that equitable estoppel can overcome the requirements of the statute of frauds. (Opp., p. 9.) In reply, Defendant reiterates the statute of frauds argument. (Reply, pp. 3-5.)

Here, Plaintiff does not state in the pleadings whether the alleged contract was written, oral, or implied. Plaintiff only makes the conclusory allegation that a valid contract existed. (FAC, ¶ 23.) Under Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10 subdivision (g), a party may demurrer to a pleading with an action founded upon a contract when it cannot be ascertained from the pleading whether the contract is written, is oral, or is implied by conduct. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10 (g).) Moreover, under the statute of frauds, a contract for interest in real property, or for an amount over $100,000, is invalid unless it is in writing. (Civ. Code, § 1624 (a)(3), (a)(7).) Plaintiff is alleging breach of contract for interest in a property and for an amount over $100,000 with no purported written contract attached. Thus, Plaintiff has failed to state facts to establish the existence of a valid contract. Since Plaintiff has amended the complaint and did not supply the court facts surrounding the purported agreement or provided a written valid contract which would satisfy the statute of frauds, leave to amend will not be granted.

Accordingly, Defendants’ demurrer as to the first cause of action for breach of contract is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.

Second Cause of Action: Breach of Implied-in-Fact Contract

An implied-in-fact contract has the same essential elements as an express contract: mutual assent and consideration. (Chandler v. Roach (1957) 156 Cal.App.2d 435, 440.) To show breach, Plaintiff must be able to establish “(1) the existence of the contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance, (3) defendant’s breach, and (4) the resulting damages to the plaintiff.” (Oasis West Realty, supra, 51 Cal.4th at 821.)

Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s cause of action for breach of implied-in-fact contract fails for all the reasons set forth above. (Dem., p. 10.) Moreover, Defendant argues that Plaintiff fails to plead the agreed upon rate of the contract which the court has said is required for a breach of implied contract claim. (Dem., p. 11.) Defendant argues that the purported contract is not a contract at all because it is only supported by unilateral expectations and is not supported by mutual assent. (Ibid.)

As stated above, Plaintiff argues that the Court has the discretion to compel specific performance in cases of part performance and to prevent fraud. (Opp., pp. 8-9.) In reply, Defendant reiterates the lack of allegations to support an argument of mutual assent. (Reply, p. 5.)

Here, Plaintiff alleges an implied-in-fact contract formed by the conduct of the parties. However, Plaintiff fails to allege facts which establish conduct to suggest Defendant agreed to “not take any actions to undermine Plaintiff’s financial interests in the property”. (FAC, ¶¶ 32-33.) This purported agreement for conduct is not subject to the statute of frauds, however Plaintiff has pled no facts describing the conduct which created the alleged agreement. Thus, plaintiff has failed to plead facts which establish the existence of a contract through conduct.

Accordingly, Defendants’ demurrer as to the second cause of action for breach of implied-in-fact contract is SUSTAINED with leave to amend.

Third Cause of Action: Constructive Trust

            “A constructive trust is an involuntary equitable trust created by operation of law as a remedy to compel the transfer of property from the person wrongfully holding it to the rightful owner. The essence of the theory of constructive trust is to prevent unjust enrichment and to prevent a person from taking advantage of his or her own wrongdoing”. (Campbell v. Superior Court (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 904, 920, citations omitted.) “[A] constructive trust may only be imposed where the following three conditions are satisfied: (1) the existence of a res (property or some interest in property); (2) the right of a complaining party to that res; and (3) some wrongful acquisition or detention of the res by another party who is not entitled to it.” (Ibid., italics omitted.) 

Constructive trust is not a cause of action but merely a remedy to compel a person not justly entitled to property to transfer it to another who is entitled to it. (Davies v. Krasna (1975) 14 Cal.3d 502, 515.)

Defendant argues that a Plaintiff cannot state a claim for constructive trust because it is not a cause of action, but an equitable remedy. (Dem., p. 12.) Defendant argues that even if the court were to indulge in the count as a remedy in the prayer section, Plaintiff cannot show that he had a right to the Property or that Defendant wrongfully acquired to Property. (Dem., pp. 12-13.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that one who gains a thing by fraud is an involuntary trustee who can be subject to a constructive trust. (Opp., p. 10.) In reply, Defendant reiterates that a constructive trust is not a cause of action. (Reply, p. 6.)

Here, Plaintiff has pled sufficient facts to constitute a prayer for a constructive trust. (FAC, ¶ 39-45.)  Plaintiff may seek a constructive trust as a remedy as it relates to other underlying causes of action. However, a constructive trust is not an independent cause of action in California. (Batt v. City and County of San Francisco (2007) 155 Cal.App.4th 65, 82.) Thus, the Plaintiff fails to state a legitimate cause of action.

Accordingly, Defendants’ demurrer as to the third cause of action for a constructive trust is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.

Fourth Cause of Action: Unjust Enrichment or Restitution

“The elements for a claim of unjust enrichment are receipt of a benefit and unjust retention of the benefit at the expense of another. The theory of unjust enrichment requires one who acquires a benefit which may not justly be retained, to return either the thing or its equivalent to the aggrieved party so as not to be unjustly enriched.” (Lyles v. Sangadeo-Patel (2014) 225 Cal.App.4th 759, 769, quotation marks and citations omitted.)  Notably, “[u]njust enrichment is not a cause of action”; it is simply “a restitution claim.” (Hill v. Roll International Corp. (2011) 195 Cal.App.4th 1295, 1307; see also Melchior v. New Line Productions, Inc. (2003) 106 Cal.App.4th 779, 793 [“there is no cause of action in California for unjust enrichment”].)

Defendant argues that Plaintiff cannot state a claim for unjust enrichment because it is not a standalone cause of action, but a remedy. (Dem., 14.) Defendant argues that even if the court were to construe the cause of action as a quasi-contract theory, it fails because Plaintiff provided funds to purchase property and that is what the funds were used for. (Ibid.)

In opposition, Plaintiff concedes that unjust enrichment is not a cause of action but argues that it is an enforceable quasi-contract remedy. (Opp., pp. 11-12.) In reply, Defendant reiterates that restitution is not a cause of action and Plaintiff’s quasi-contract claim fails because capital was obtained by unidentified third parties, and the money is not his. (Reply, p. 7.)  

Here, Plaintiff has pled an underlying action for fraudulent inducement and incorporated those allegations into the claim of unjust enrichment.  (FAC, ¶¶ 46-54.) Plaintiff may seek restitution as it relates to that underlying claim.  However, it is settled that unjust enrichment is not an independent cause of action in California. (Hill v. Roll Internat. Corp. (2011) 195 Cal.App.4th 1295, 1307.) Moreover, Plaintiff has not sought relief under a quasi-contract theory. Thus, the Plaintiff fails to state a legitimate cause of action.

Accordingly, Defendants’ demurrer as to the fourth cause of action for unjust enrichment or restitution is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.

Fifth Cause of Action: Resulting Trust

A resulting trust arises when property is transferred under circumstances showing that the transferee was not intended to take the beneficial interest; because the beneficial interest was not effectively disposed of it remains in or returns to the transferor. (Lonely Maiden Productions, LLC v. GoldenTree Asset Management, LP (2011) 201 Cal.App.4th 368, 386; Lloyds Bank Cal. v. Wells Fargo Bank (1986) 187 Cal.App.3d 1038, 1042.) A claimant must prove facts establishing a beneficial interest by clear and convincing evidence. [Gomez v. Cecena (1940) 15 Cal.2d 363, 366- 367]

In Stansfield v. Starkey (1990) 220 Cal.App.3d 59, the court found that the trial court acted within its discretion by sustaining a demurrer without leave to amend as to causes of action for resulting trust and constructive trust, which are legal remedies. (Stansfield v. Starkey (1990) 220 Cal.App.3d 59, 76.)

Defendant argues that Plaintiff cannot state a claim for a resulting trust because it is not a cause of action, but a remedy. (Dem., p. 15.) Moreover, Defendant argues that she was the intended beneficiary of the Property at the time title was acquired. (Dem., pp. 15-16.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that “when a transfer of real property is made to one person and the consideration therefor is paid by or for another, a trust is presumed to result in favor of the person by or for whom such payment is made”. (Opp., p. 10.) In reply, Defendant argues that even if Plaintiff were to seek relief in the form of a resulting trust it would fail because Plaintiff does not identify what portion of the purchase price he funded and how much came from unidentified friends and investors. (Reply, p. 5.)   

Here, Plaintiff has pled an underlying action for fraudulent inducement relating to the transfer of the Property and incorporated those allegations into the claim for a resulting trust.  (FAC, ¶¶ 46-54.)  The Plaintiff may seek a resulting trust as it relates to that underlying claim.  However, a resulting trust is not an independent cause of action in California. (Stansfield, supra, 220 Cal.App.3d at 76.) Thus, the Plaintiff fails to state a legitimate cause of action.

Accordingly, Defendants’ demurrer as to the fifth cause of action for resulting trust is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.

Sixth Cause of Action: Constructive Fraud

            The necessary elements for constructive fraud are: (1) a fiduciary, confidential or special relationship;¿(2)¿breach (e.g., nondisclosure); (3)¿intent to deceive;¿(4)¿reliance;¿(5) causation; and¿(6)¿damages.¿¿(Younan¿v. Equifax Inc.¿(1980) 111 Cal.App.3d 498, 516-517, fn. 14.)

Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s claim of constructive fraud fails because Plaintiff fails to plead a fiduciary relationship between Defendant and Plaintiff and he is not a beneficiary to Defendant’s estate. (Dem., pp. 17-18.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that the co-investor relationship of the parties amounts to a fiduciary relationship. (Opp., p. 16.). In reply, Defendant reiterates that Plaintiff fails to plead sufficient facts. (Reply, p. 8.)

Here, Plaintiff has stated facts to allege the existence of a fiduciary relationship. Plaintiff alleges that Defendant owed a fiduciary duty to Plaintiff arising from the financial trust placed in her by Plaintiff who funded the purchase of the home. (FAC, ¶ 68.) Thus, Plaintiff has alleged a fiduciary business relationship. Although Defendant challenges the validity of the fiduciary relationship, at the demurrer stage the Court is only evaluating the sufficiency of the pleadings. (Lewis, supra, 235 Cal.App.4th at 388.) Thus, Plaintiff has pled facts sufficient to state a cause of action for constructive fraud.

 Accordingly, Defendants’ demurrer as to the sixth cause of action for constructive fraud is OVERRULED.

Seventh Cause of Action: Conversion

“Conversion is the wrongful exercise of dominion over the property of another. The elements of a conversion claim are: (1) the plaintiff’s ownership or right to possession of the property; (2) the defendant’s conversion by a wrongful act or disposition of property rights; and (3) damages.” (Lee v. Hanley (2015) 61 Cal.4th 1225, 1240.) 

Defendant argues that Plaintiff cannot state a claim for conversion because conversion applies to personal property, not real property. (Dem., p. 19.) Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s conversion theory targets the Property, but real property is not chattel that can be converted. (Ibid.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that money can be the subject of a conversion claim where a specific identifiable sum is involved. (Opp., p. 13.) Plaintiff argues that the proceeds from the house constitute a specific identifiable sum. (Ibid.) In reply, Defendant argues that the money rule is irrelevant because Plaintiff pled conversion of real property in the FAC. (Reply, pp. 7-8.)

Here, the FAC alleges Defendant “without authorization assumed and exercised control over Plaintiff’s property”. (FAC, ¶ 75.) While Plaintiff attempts to reconcile the conversion claim in their opposition, the FAC alleges conversion of real property. (FAC, ¶¶ 74-82.) The FAC does not focus on money as the subject of the conversion claim. (Ibid.) Thus, plaintiff has failed to state a cause of action for conversion.

Accordingly, Defendants’ demurrer as to the seventh cause of action for conversion is SUSTAINED with leave to amend.

Eighth Cause of Action: Fraud (Intentional Misrepresentation or Concealment)

            “The elements of fraud,” including a cause of action for fraudulent inducement, “are (a) a misrepresentation (false representation, concealment, or nondisclosure); (b) scienter or knowledge of its falsity; (c) intent to induce reliance; (d) justifiable reliance; and (e) resulting damage.” (Hinesley v. Oakshade Town Ctr. (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 289, 294.) The facts constituting the alleged fraud must be alleged factually and specifically as to every element of fraud, as the policy of “liberal construction” of the pleadings will not ordinarily be invoked. (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 645.)

            Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s fraud claim lacks the requisite specificity. (Dem., p. 20.) Defendant argues that Plaintiff does not allege facts which support promissory fraud and only makes conclusory allegations of a broken promise. (Ibid.) Moreover, Defendant argues that Plaintiff does not allege reliance or damages with requisite specificity. (Dem., p. 21.)

            In opposition, Plaintiff restates the elements of a fraud action and argues that an intent not to perform supports a fraud action. (Opp., pp. 14-15.) In reply, Defendant reiterates that Plaintiff fails to plead sufficient facts. (Reply, p. 8.)

            Here, Plaintiff has failed to plead fraud with the requisite specificity. Plaintiff’s allegations are conclusory. (FAC, ¶¶ 83-92.) For example, regarding the elements of knowledge of falsity and intent to induce reliance, Plaintiff merely states “[a]t the time Defendant made these representations, she knew they were false” and “Defendant intended that Plaintiff rely on these representations. (FAC, ¶¶ 87-88.) These statements are not supported with the specific allegations required of a fraud pleading. (Lazar, supra, 12 Cal.4th at 645.) Thus, Plaintiff has failed to state a cause of action for fraud.

Accordingly, Defendants’ demurrer as to the eighth cause of action for fraud is SUSTAINED with leave to amend.

Ninth Cause of Action: Accounting

“A cause of action for an accounting requires a showing that a relationship exists between the plaintiff and defendant that requires an accounting, and that some balance is due the plaintiff that can only be ascertained by an accounting. An action for accounting is not available where the plaintiff alleges the right to recover a sum certain or a sum that can be made certain by calculation.” (Teselle v. McLoughlin (2009) 173 Cal.App.4th 156, 179, citations and paragraph break omitted.) 

Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s accounting cause of action is insufficient because Plaintiff does not allege a relationship with Defendant which requires an accounting. (Dem., pp. 21-22.) Moreover, Defendant argues that accounting is not required here, where Plaintiff has alleged a sum certain in damages of “at least $1,400,000, representing the financial contributions and support provided to the property”. (Dem., p. 23.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that the co-investor relationship of the parties and the complexity of the financial transactions support the cause of action. (Opp., p. 16.) In reply, Defendant argues that Plaintiff fails to plead a sufficient fiduciary relationship. (Reply, p. 9.)

Here, Plaintiff reiterates the existence of a fiduciary relationship. (FAC, ¶ 94.) Plaintiff alleges that Defendant owed a fiduciary duty to Plaintiff arising from the financial arrangements they entered regarding the Property. (Ibid.) Thus, Plaintiff has alleged a fiduciary business relationship requiring an accounting. Moreover, while Plaintiff makes a prayer for damages of $1,400,000 at minimum, Plaintiff prays for an amount in damages to be determined at trial. (FAC, p. 15, ¶ D.) Thus, Plaintiff has pled facts sufficient to state a cause of action for accounting.

Accordingly, Defendants’ demurrer as to the ninth cause of action for accounting is OVERRULED.

Tenth Cause of Action: Money Had and Received

The required elements of a common count claim are “(1) the statement of indebtedness in a certain sum, (2) the consideration, i.e., goods sold, work done, etc., and (3) nonpayment. A cause of action for money had and received is stated if it is alleged the defendant is indebted to the plaintiff in a certain sum for money had and received by the defendant for the use of the plaintiff.” (Farmers Insurance Exchange v. Zerin (1997) 53 Cal.App.4th 445, 460, citation and quotation marks omitted.) 

Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s claim for “money had and received” fails because Plaintiff the money in dispute was used for its intended purpose, purchase of property. (Dem., p. 24.) Defendant argues that the money was not loaned or given to Defendant with the understanding that it had to be returned. (Ibid.) Moreover, Defendant argues that the money in dispute does not entirely belong to Plaintiff if he obtained loans and investments for the property from friends and investors as he alleges. (Ibid.) Defendant further argues that the pleaded amount is incoherent and the retention of the money in dispute was lawful. (Dem., p. 25.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that this cause of action is available when a sum of money is owed to another in equity and good conscious. (Opp., p. 16.) Plaintiff argues that money had and received is not subject to the heightened pleading requirements of fraud. (Ibid.) In reply, Defendant argues that Plaintiff does not identify a specific sum “owed” by Plaintiff considering the admission that Defendant took proceeds from friends and investors for the purchase of the Property. (Reply, p. 9.)

Here, Plaintiff has failed to state a sum certain as required for a common counts claim. (Farmers Insurance Exchange, supra, 53 Cal.App.4th at 460.) While Plaintiff makes a prayer for damages of $1,400,000 at minimum, Plaintiff prays for an amount in damages to be determined at trial. (FAC, p. 15, ¶ D.) Thus, Plaintiff has failed to state a cause of action for money had and received, or common counts. Plaintiff cannot maintain the cause of action for accounting and money had and received based on the same sum of money because they have directly contradicting elements. Thus, Plaintiff cannot successfully amend this cause of action.  

Accordingly, Defendants’ demurrer as to the eighth cause of action for fraud is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Defendant Nektar Avetisyan’s demurrer to Plaintiff’s First Amended Complaint is SUSTAINED in part and OVERRULED in part.

LEGAL STANDARD

            Motions to compel further responses must always be accompanied by a meet-and confer-declaration (per Code Civ. Proc., § 2016.040) demonstrating a “reasonable and good faith attempt at an informal resolution of each issue presented by the motion.”  (Id. at §§ 2030.300, subd. (b), 2031.310, subd. (b)(2), 2033.290, subd. (b).) They must also be accompanied by a separate statement containing the requests and the responses, verbatim, as well as reasons why a further response is warranted. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1345, subd. (a).) The separate statement must also be complete in itself; no extrinsic materials may be incorporated by reference.  (Id. at rule 3.1345, subd. (c).) 

 

Special Interrogatories

 Pursuant to Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.030(a)(1), a party may propound to another party “[t]hirty-five specially prepared interrogatories that are relevant to the subject matter of the action.” Further special interrogatories, beyond the thirty-five allowed by right, may be propounded if the party “attaches a supporting declaration” describing why further special interrogatories are warranted based on “[¶] [t]he complexity or the quantity of the existing and potential issues in the particular case.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.040, subd. (a)(1).)

The propounding party may move for an order compelling further responses to the special interrogatories if the propounding party deems that (1) an answer to a particular interrogatory is evasive or incomplete, (2) an exercise of the option to produce documents in lieu of a response is unwarranted, or (3) an objection to an interrogatory is without merit or too general. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300, subd. (a)(1)-(3).) Such a motion compelling further responses must be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Code Civ. Proc. § 2016.040 and a separate statement document providing a concise explanation of all information necessary to understand each discovery request and all the responses to it that are at issue. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (b)(1); Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1345.)

 

Requests for Admission

California law requires a responding party to respond to each propounded request for admission with either a substantive answer or an objection to the particular request. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.210, subd. (b).) Each substantive answer must: “(1)¿Admit so much of the matter involved in the request as is true, either as expressed in the request itself or as reasonably and clearly qualified by the responding party; (2)¿Deny so much of the matter involved in the request as is untrue; (3)¿Specify so much of the matter involved in the request as to the truth of which the responding party lacks sufficient information or knowledge.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.220, subd. (b).) “If a responding party gives lack of information or knowledge as a reason for a failure to admit all or part of a request for admission, that party shall state in the answer that a reasonable inquiry concerning the matter in the particular request has been made, and that the information known or readily obtainable is insufficient to enable that party to admit the matter.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.220, subd. (c).)¿    

            A propounding party may move for an order compelling further responses to requests for admission if any of the following apply: “(1) An answer to a particular request is evasive or incomplete; or (2) An objection to a particular request is without merit or too general.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.290, subd. (a).)

The Court is not requesting oral argument on this matter.  The Court is guided by California Rules of Court, Rule 3.1308(a)(1) whereby notice of intent to appear is requested.  Unless the Court directs argument in the Tentative Ruling, no argument is requested and any party seeking argument should notify all other parties and the court by 4:00 p.m. on the court day before the hearing of the party’s intention to appear and argue.  The tentative ruling will become the ruling of the court if no argument is received.

BACKGROUND

This is an elder abuse case.  On December 20, 2025, Plaintiff Harvey Olen Quinn (“Plaintiff”) served a Notice of Taking Deposition of the Person Most Knowledgeable of Defendant Valley Post Acute and Rehab, LLC (“Defendant”).  Plaintiff filed a motion to compel Defendant’s PMK deposition on January 13, 2026.  On March 27, 2026, this court granted Plaintiff’s motion, ordering Defendant to produce its PMK for deposition and to produce the requested documents within 20 days of the order.

Plaintiff now moves for an order imposing issue, evidence, and monetary sanctions against Defendant and its counsel of record, jointly and severally, in the amount of $5,000.00 for Defendant’s violation of the March 27, 2026, Court Order compelling Defendant to produce its PMK for deposition and to produce the requested documents by April 16, 2026.

Defendant has not filed an opposition.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

            Under Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.300, “[i]f a party … then fails to obey the order compelling a response, the court may make those orders that are just, including the imposition of an issue sanction, an evidence sanction, or a terminating sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010). In lieu of or in addition to that sanction, the court may impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing¿with Section 2023.010).” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (c).)¿¿¿ 

The “court, after notice to any affected party, person, or attorney, and after an opportunity for hearing, may impose” monetary, issue, evidence, or terminating sanctions “against anyone engaging in conduct that is a misuse of the discovery process[.]” (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2023.030; 2023.030, subds.¿(a)-(d).)¿Failing to¿respond¿or¿submit¿to an authorized method of discovery and disobeying a court order to provide discovery are misuses of the discovery process.¿ (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.010, subds. (d) and (g).)¿¿ 

“The trial court has broad discretion in selecting discovery sanctions, subject to reversal only for abuse.¿¿The trial court should consider both the conduct being sanctioned and its effect on the party seeking discovery and, in choosing a sanction, should ‘attempt¿to tailor the sanction to the harm caused by the withheld discovery.’”¿¿(Doppes¿v. Bentley Motors, Inc.¿(2009) 174 Cal.App.4th¿967, 992 [citations omitted].)¿¿

 

DISCUSSION                                                                                              

Plaintiff seeks issue, evidentiary, and/or monetary sanctions against Defendant for their failure to comply with the Court’s March 27, 2026, Court Order compelling Defendant to produce its PMK for deposition and to produce the requested documents. (Mot., p. 2.)  In the alternative, Plaintiff moves the court to order Defendant to produce all documents in its care responsive to the request for production contained within the original Notice of Deposition and without objection. (Mot., p. 4.)

Evidentiary Sanctions

Plaintiff moves the Court to order the defense be precluded from presenting any evidence as to the following issues:

(a)   That Defendant had sufficient staff to meet the needs of its residents during the residency of Harvey Quinn;

(b)   That Defendant did not know their staff who provided nursing services were unfit to perform their job duties;

(c)   Defendant’s managing agents did not know of the unfitness of their staff who provided nursing services to Plaintiff; and

(d)   Such actions by Defendant were not the proximate cause of Harvey Quinn suffering from an avoidable, open coccyx pressure sore while a resident of the facility.

(Mot., p. 4.)

When a party violates a discovery order, a trial court may prohibit the violating party from introducing evidence at trial that contradicts the opponent’s claims or supports the violating party’s defenses.  (Juarez v. Boy Scouts of America (2000) 81 Cal.App.4th 377, 387.) 

Evidentiary sanctions may be imposed only after a motion to compel is made and granted and the party to be sanctioned has failed to comply with that order.  (Karlsson v. Ford Motor Co. (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 1202, 1214.)  However, violation of a discovery order is not a prerequisite to evidentiary sanctions when the offending party has engaged in a pattern of willful discovery abuse that causes the unavailability of evidence.  (Id. at p. 1215.) 

In Young v. Rosenthal (1989) 212 Cal.App.3d 96, a party was precluded from testifying after refusing to cooperate with the taking of his deposition.  As a result of having failed to comply with the trial court’s discovery order only once, the trial court granted a motion for issue and evidence sanctions ordering that certain facts be deemed established according to Plaintiff’s claims and that severe evidentiary limitations be placed on the defendant at trial.  (Young v. Rosenthal (1989) 212 Cal.App.3d 96, 104-108.) 

Here, Plaintiff argues that Defendant has not complied with the March 27, 2026, Minute Order Compelling Defendant to produce its PMK for deposition and to all produce requested documents within twenty days of the order. (See 3/27/26 Minute Order.)   The deposition and document production were therefore to occur by April 16, 2026.  On May 1, 2026, the deposition of Defendant’s PMK occurred, however Defendant did not provide a response to Topic of Inquiry Number 3 and failed to produce all responsive documents. (Garcia Decl., ¶ 9.)  On May 26, 2026, a further deposition occurred, however Defendant again failed to produce all responsive documents. (Garcia Decl., ¶ 11.)  Defendant has thus failed to comply with the Court’s order.

The Court finds that Defendant has engaged in conduct that is a misuse of discovery by disobeying the Court’s March 27, 2026, Court Order.  Defendant did not serve objections to the notice of taking deposition and failed to appear at the noticed deposition. (See 3/27/26 Minute Order.)  Over four months after the motion to compel deposition was granted, Defendant still has not produced the requested documents. (Garcia Decl., ¶ 12.)  Defendant has shown willful and serious abuse of the discovery process and has not opposed this motion or other discovery-based motions filed by Plaintiff in this action.

Moreover, this misuse of discovery is not an isolated incident.  The Court previously granted and imposed monetary sanctions against Defendant after Plaintiff filed a Motion to Compel Further Responses to several discovery requests. (See 2/6/26 Minute Order.)  An evidentiary sanction is necessary to compel compliance because Defendant has shown monetary sanctions alone are insufficient to ensure Defendant complies with this Court’s orders.

Therefore, Defendant is precluded from introducing evidence as to the following:

(a)   That Defendant had sufficient staff to meet the needs of its residents during the residency of Harvey Quinn;

(b)   That Defendant did not know their staff who provided nursing services were unfit to perform their job duties;

(c)   Defendant’s managing agents did not know of the unfitness of their staff who provided nursing services to Plaintiff; and

(d)   Such actions by Defendant were not the proximate cause of Harvey Quinn suffering from an avoidable, open coccyx pressure sore while a resident of the facility.

 

Issue Sanctions

Plaintiff moves the Court for an order determining that Plaintiff has proven, by clear and convincing evidence, the following as to Defendant:

(a)   Defendant failed to have sufficient staff to meet the needs of its residents during the residence of Harvey Quinn on an ongoing basis in violation of 22 Code of Regulations section 72329;

(b)   Defendant knew their staff were unfit to perform their job duties;

(c)   That in doing so, Defendant employed their staff with the knowing disregard for the rights and safety of Harvey Quinn; and

(d)   Such an action by Defendant was the proximate cause of Harvey Quinn suffering from an avoidable open coccyx pressure sore.

In light of this Court’s ruling on Plaintiff’s request for evidentiary sanctions, Plaintiff’s request in the alternative for issue sanctions is denied.

Monetary Sanctions

In addition to or in lieu of any other sanction, the court may order the disobedient party or counsel responsible or both to pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney fees, incurred as a result of the failure to obey (including fees on the sanctions motion).  (Code Civ. Proc. § 2023.030, subd. (a).)

Plaintiff also seeks monetary sanctions be imposed against Defendant and its counsel in the amount of $5,000.00. Plaintiff’s counsel, Mr. Garcia, declares that a paralegal spent 1.5 hours drafting the instant motion at an hourly rate of $150.00, and that Mr. Garcia himself has spent 2 hours researching and finalizing the motion and anticipates another 4 hours considering the opposition, preparing a reply, and attending the hearing at an hourly billing rate of $1,000.00. (Garcia Decl., ¶ 15.) Further, Mr. Garcia declares spending 2 hours preparing for and taking the second deposition and anticipates spending 2 hours taking the third deposition. (Garcia Decl., ¶ 15.) Mr. Garcia declares spending in excess of $750.00 in court reporter fees for the second deposition and anticipates the same for a third deposition. (Garcia Decl., ¶ 15.)  Finally, Mr. Garcia declares incurring over $750.00 in fees as to a court reporter at the hearing on this motion and a $60.00 filing fee. (Garcia Decl., ¶ 15.)  Nevertheless, Plaintiff limits the reimbursement request to $5,000.00. (Garcia Decl., ¶ 15.)

Here, the Court will exercise its discretion and reduce the monetary sanctions as follows: 1 hour for drafting at a paralegal rate of $150 per hour for a total of $150, plus .5 hours reviewing the motion at an attorney rate of $1,000 per hour for a total of $500, plus 2 hours spent preparing for and taking the second deposition at an attorney rate of $1,000 for a total of $2,000, plus the $60 filing fee for the instant motion for a total of $60, and a report fee of $750 for each court-reporter fee actually incurred for a total of $1,500. This results in a total of $4,210 in monetary sanctions against Defendant and their counsel.

However, in light of certain arguments presented by Defense counsel concerning his health-related reasons for the delays in responding, the Court finds that a further reduction in the sanctions amount is warranted. Accordingly, the Court reduces the sanctions to $2,500.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Plaintiff’s motion for evidentiary sanctions is GRANTED. 

Plaintiff’s motion for issue sanctions is DENIED.

Plaintiff’s motion for monetary sanctions is GRANTED in the amount of $2,500.00.

BACKGROUND

This is a lemon law case. On November 24, 2023, plaintiff Luis Alberto Lopez (Plaintiff) leased a 2024 Mercedes-Benz CLA250C (Vehicle) from the dealership Calstar Motors Inc. (Dealership). On October 21, 2025, Plaintiff filed the operative Complaint against Mercedez-Benz USA, LLC (Defendant) alleging two causes of action for (1) Violation of Song-Beverly Act – Breach of Express Warranty and (2) Violation of Song-Beverly Act – Breach of Implied Warranty.

On February 6, 2026, Defendant filed the instant motion to compel arbitration and stay action. Plaintiff filed an opposition on July 2, 2026. Defendant filed a reply on July 10, 2026.

LEGAL STANDARD

            Parties may be compelled to arbitrate a dispute upon the court finding that: (1) there was a valid agreement to arbitrate between the parties; and (2) said agreement covers the controversy or controversies in the parties’ dispute.¿ (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2; Omar v. Ralphs Grocery Co. (2004)¿118 Cal.App.4th 955, 961.)

DISCUSSION           

Written Arbitration Agreement Between Parties Exists

Under both the Federal Arbitration Act and California law, arbitration agreements are valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, except on such grounds that exist at law or equity for voiding a contract. (Winter v. Window Fashions Professions, Inc. (2008) 166 Cal.App.4th 943, 947.) The party moving to compel arbitration must establish the existence of a written arbitration agreement between the parties. (Code of Civ. Proc. § 1281.2.) This is usually done by presenting a copy of the signed, written agreement to the court. “A petition to compel arbitration or to stay proceedings pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 1281.2 and 1281.4 must state, in addition to other required allegations, the provisions of the written agreement and the paragraph that provides for arbitration. The provisions must be stated verbatim, or a copy must be physically or electronically attached to the petition and incorporated by reference”. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1330.) The moving party must also establish the other party’s refusal to arbitrate the controversy. (Code of Civ. Proc. § 1281.2.) The filing of a lawsuit against the moving party for a controversy clearly within the scope of the arbitration agreement affirmatively establishes the other party’s refusal to arbitrate the controversy. (Hyundai Amco America, Inc. v. S3H, Inc. (2014) 232 Cal.App.4th 572, 577.)

Defendant argues that they have established an arbitration agreement exists by providing a copy of the lease agreement containing the arbitration provisions. (Mot., p.  3.) Plaintiff does not dispute the existence of an arbitration agreement; however, Plaintiff argues that Defendant is not a contracting party to the agreement and therefore lacks any privity with Plaintiff. (Opp., p. 6.)

Here, the lease agreement provided by Defendant contains a clearly identified arbitration clause labeled “Important Arbitration Disclosures”. (Ameripour Decl., Exh. 2, p. 4.) The signature page also stipulates that the Lessee, Plaintiff, acknowledges they have “received and read all 10 pages of [the] lease carefully and agree to all of its terms, INCLUDING THE IMPORTANT ARBITRATION DISCLOSURES ON PAGE 4 AND 5” (Ameripour Decl., Exh. 2, p. 6.) The Court notes that the signature page features a dated electronic signature by Plaintiff. (Ibid.) The arbitration clause itself lists Defendant as a “Third-Party Beneficiary”. (Ameripour Decl., Exh. 2, p. 4.) The clause further states that “any claim or dispute, whether in contract, tort or otherwise…between you and us…or the vehicle distributor, including Mercedes-Benz USA LLC (each a "Third Party Beneficiary"), which arises out of or relates to…this lease, or any resulting transaction or relationship arising out of this lease…shall, at the election of either you, us, or a Third Party Beneficiary, be resolved by a neutral, binding arbitration and not by a court action”. (Ibid.) Neither party disputes that the arbitration agreement in the lease agreement is governed by the Federal Arbitration Act (FAA). (Ibid.)

Accordingly, the Court finds that Defendant has established the existence of an arbitration agreement.

Arbitration Agreement Enforcement as Intended Third-Party Beneficiary

In ruling on a motion to compel arbitration, the court must first determine whether the parties actually agreed to arbitrate the dispute, and general principles of California contract law help guide the court in making this determination. (Mendez v. Mid-Wilshire Health Care Center (2013) 220 Cal.App.4th 534, 541.) A petition to compel arbitration is a suit in equity to compel specific performance of a contract. (Frog Creek Partners, LLC. v. Vance Brown, Inc. (2012) 206 Cal.App.4th 515, 532.). Arbitration is a matter of contract, and a party cannot be required to arbitrate a dispute he has not agreed to submit. (Villacreses v. Molinari (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 1223, 1230.)

Defendant argues that they are an intended third-party beneficiary to the agreement and can enforce it pursuant to the clause which identifies them as a Third-Party Beneficiary. (Motion, p. 5; see also Ameripour Decl., Exh. 2, p. 4.)

Plaintiff argues that Defendant mistakenly relies on an equitable estoppel theory to create standing for them to enforce the arbitration agreement found in the lease. (Opp., p.  4.) Plaintiff argues that Defendant’s argument is flawed because Plaintiff is not attempting to enforce the lease with the Dealership against Defendant, since Defendant is not a signatory, and they cannot be forced to arbitrate with a non-signatory. (Opp. pp. 4-5.) Further, Plaintiff argues that their Complaint does not reference or rely on the lease whatsoever, Plaintiff instead relies on statutory obligations of manufacturers found in the song Beverly Act. (Opp., p. 6.) Moreover, Plaintiff argues that Defendant cannot show that they are a valid Third-Party Beneficiary because Defendant cannot establish the Goonewardene factors consistent with the three-prong test adopted by the California Supreme Court. (Opp., pp. 9-14.)

In reply, Defendant reiterates that they are a named and intended third-party beneficiary with the ability to enforce arbitration. (Reply, p. 1.) Further, Defendant argues that they satisfy the Goonewardene factors because a Federal Court has concluded that “MBUSA is entitled to compel [Plaintiff’s] claims against it to arbitration”. (Reply, p. 3; Durkin v. Mercedes-Benz USA LLC (N.D.Cal. July 15, 2025, No. 25-cv-03064-WHO) 2025 U.S.Dist.LEXIS 134943, at *6.) It is worth noting that decisions of federal courts in matters of state law are not binding on state courts, they may only serve persuasive purposes. (Brakke v. Economic Concepts, Inc. (2013) 213 Cal.App.4th 761, 770.)

Here, Defendant is a named Third-Party Beneficiary to the arbitration agreement, where power to enforce the provision is explicitly provided to Third-Party Beneficiaries. (Ameripour Decl., Exh. 2, p. 4.) However, the Court finds that Defendant does not satisfy the standards set for establishing status as a Third-Party Beneficiary set forth in Goonewardene.

Under Goonewardene, a non-signatory must establish they are a valid third-party beneficiary by satisfying the following factors established by the California Supreme Court: (1) whether the third party would in fact benefit from the contract, but also (2) whether a motivating purpose of the contracting parties was to provide a benefit to the third party, and (3) whether permitting a third party to bring its own breach of contract action against a contracting party is consistent with the objectives of the contract and the reasonable expectations of the contracting parties. (Goonewardene v. ADP, LLC (2019) 6 Cal.5th 817, 830.)

On the first prong, while Defendant would certainly benefit from an arbitration clause, Defendant does not benefit from the sales contract between Plaintiff and the Dealership. The purpose of the contract which contains the arbitration clause is to lease a vehicle, the Dealership benefits from that sale. (See generally Ameripour Decl., Exh. 2.) Moreover, the financial beneficiary of payments on the lease is Mercedes-Benz Financial Services, not Defendant, as they are separate entities. (Ameripour Decl., Exh. 2, p. 2.) In either case, Defendant does not benefit from the contract. 

On the second prong, the motivation of the parties to the contract was to lease a vehicle, not to minimize litigation costs of a non-signatory. The Court in Ford Motor similarly rejected the argument that the arbitration clause was an objective of the sales contract. (Ford Motor Warranty Cases (2025) 89 Cal.App.5th 1324, 1338-1339.) (“The manifest intent of the parties was to buy, sell and finance a car.”)

Since Defendant cannot satisfy two of three prongs, Defendant cannot satisfy all the Goonewardene factors and does not have standing as a Third-Party Beneficiary to move for enforcement of the valid arbitration agreement.

Additionally, although Defendant is named as a Third-Party Beneficiary who may elect to arbitrate an issue, they are not a signatory. A non-signatory may compel arbitration only “when the causes of action against the non-signatory are intimately founded in and intertwined with the underlying contractual obligations”. (Goldman v. KPMG, LLP (2009) 173 Cal.App.4th 209, 217-218; Opp., p. 4.) Here, Plaintiff is pursuing action against Defendant pursuant to the Song-Beverly Act, not any contractual obligations imposed by the lease. The Court in Ford Motor similarly found that plaintiff’s claims under the Song-Beverly Act were not founded or intertwined with the contract because “the substantive terms of the sale contracts relate to sale and financing and nothing more”. (Ford Motor Warranty Cases, supra, 89 Cal.App.5th at1335.)

Accordingly, Defendant’s motion to compel arbitration is DENIED

Stay of Proceedings

Under 9 U.S.C. § 3, when a court finds that a dispute is subject to arbitration and a party requests a stay, the court “shall” stay the action until arbitration has been completed. California law is in accord. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 1281.2, 1281.4.) 

Defendant’s request to stay the proceeding is DENIED because the motion to compel arbitration is denied.

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Defendant Mercedez-Benz USA, LLC’s motion to compel arbitration and stay proceedings is DENIED.

Defendant Mercedez-Benz USA, LLC shall provide notice of this order.

BACKGROUND

This is a medical negligence case. On or about June 14, 2022, Plaintiff Shu Yun He (“Plaintiff”) had an initial consultation with Defendant Thomas G. Harris (“Defendant”) for a mild posterior tibial tendon tear with scar tissue in her right ankle and subtalar joint. (FAC, ¶ 4.) Defendant agreed to perform a limited, joint-preserving procedure consisting of posterior tibial tendon repair via arthroscopy and removal of scar tissue of ankle joint and subtalar joint via arthroscopy. (Id. at ¶ 8.) After the consultation, Defendant informed Plaintiff that the surgical plan was changed to include fusion and osteotomy, which Plaintiff rejected. (Id. at ¶¶ 9, 11.) On August 9, 2022, Defendant suggested fusion might be required and Plaintiff again rejected. (Id. at ¶¶ 12, 13.)  Plaintiff alleges that Defendant failed to disclose material risks such as removal of relatively healthy cartilage, joint destruction, nerve injury, and deviation from a joint-preserving procedure. (Id. at ¶ 14.) Immediately before surgery, Plaintiff requested preservation of cartilage, which Defendant did not respond to. (Id. at ¶ 15.)

On August 10, 2022, Defendant performed the surgery. (Id. at ¶ 16.) Immediately upon waking, Plaintiff alleges experiencing and reporting extreme and abnormal pain, stabbing sensations, and numbness in the lateral subtalar region. (Id. at ¶ 17.) Defendant represented that the cartilage of the ankle joint was healthy, that the lateral side of the subtalar joint had minimal arthritis, the left side of the subtalar joint had a mild coalition, and pains were normal. (Id. at ¶ 19.) Plaintiff relied on Defendant’s representations. (Id. at ¶ 21.) Plaintiff observed large open incisions inconsistent with standard arthroscopy. (Id. at ¶ 25.) On or about September 6, 2024, Plaintiff was diagnosed by Dr. Bradley Lamm (“Lamm”) with end-stage arthritis of ankle joint and subtalar joint and informed that her sural nerve had been irreparably damaged. (Id. at ¶ 30.) Lamm advised no surgical option exists to restore the joints and nerve. (Id. at ¶ 31.) On November 18, 2024, Plaintiff first suspected wrongdoing when Dr. William Adams performed imaging which revealed previously preserved cartilage, multiple regions of cartilage had been removed, and rapid structural collapse. (Id. at ¶¶ 32-35.) On October 1, 2025, Dr. Jeffery McAlister confirmed severe arthritis of the ankle joint and subtalar joint, extensive joint destruction, and irreparable joint damage. (Id. at ¶¶ 37-38.) On October 30, 2025, Dr. Eman Elmi concluded Plaintiff is permanently disabled. (Id. at ¶ 41.) Plaintiff alleges the conditions were not present before surgery. (Id. at ¶ 44.)

On November 7, 2025, Plaintiff filed a Complaint. On April 13, 2026, Plaintiff filed a First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) alleging (1) medical negligence and (2) lack of informed consent.

On May 6, 2026, Plaintiff filed the Motion to Quash. On May 27, 2026, Plaintiff filed an Amended Notice and Motion to Quash or Limit Deposition Subpoenas for Production of Business Records (Medical Records) (“Motion”).

On July 2, 2026, Defendant filed an opposition.

No reply has been filed.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

            Civil Code of Procedure section 1987.1 states that “[i]f a subpoena requires the attendance of a witness or the production of books, documents, electronically stored information, or other things before a court, or at the trial of an issue therein, or at the taking of a deposition, the court, upon motion reasonably made by any person described in subdivision (b), or upon the court’s own motion after giving counsel notice and an opportunity to be heard, may make an order quashing the subpoena entirely, modifying it, or directing compliance with it upon those terms or conditions as the court shall declare, including protective orders.”¿ (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.1, subd. (a).)¿ “In addition, the court may make any other order as may be appropriate to protect the person from unreasonable or oppressive demands, including unreasonable violations of the right of privacy of the person.”¿ (Ibid.)¿ Subdivision (b) provides that a party may make a motion pursuant to subdivision (a).¿ (Id. at § 1987.1, subd. (b).)¿

As a general rule, all unprivileged information that is relevant to the subject matter of the action is discoverable if it would itself be admissible evidence at trial or if it appears reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2017.010; Schnabel v. Superior Court (1993) 5 Cal.4th 704, 711.) When the information sought to be discovered impacts a person’s constitutional right to privacy, limited protections come into play for that person. (Shaffer v. Superior Court (1995) 33 Cal.App.4th 993, 999.) The protections cover both a person’s personal and financial matters. (Ibid.) The court must balance competing rights — the right of a litigant to discover relevant facts and the right of an individual to maintain reasonable privacy — in determining whether the information is discoverable. (Ibid.)¿

¿           For discovery purposes, information is relevant if it might reasonably assist a party in evaluating the case, preparing for trial, or facilitating settlement. (Gonzalez v. Superior Court (1995) 33 Cal.App.4th 1539, 1546.)¿

 

MEET AND CONFER REQUIREMENT

California Code of Civil Procedure section¿2025.410(c) provides that a motion to quash a deposition notice must be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration.¿¿Similarly, a motion to quash a subpoena for personal records, including medical records, must be accompanied by a declaration showing a “reasonable and good faith attempt at informal resolution of the dispute” between the party requesting the records and the “consumer” or “employee” whose records are involved or counsel for such person.¿ (Code Civ. Proc., §§1985.3, subd. (g), 1985.6, subd. (f)(4).)

Plaintiff’s declaration demonstrates an attempt to informally resolve the matter prior to the filing of this Motion. (He Decl., ¶ 4.) However, the parties were unable to resolve the matter. (Id. at ¶ 6.) The Court finds that Plaintiff has satisfied the meet and confer requirement.¿

 

DISCUSSION                                                                                              

            Plaintiff moves to quash the deposition subpoenas for production of business records directed to Sutter Physicians Services, Palo Alto Medical Foundation, Palo Alto Medical Foundation X-Rays, Bradley Lamm, D.P.M., Jeffery MacAllister, D.P.M., and Eman Elmi, D.P.M. In the alternative, Plaintiff requests the Court limit the scope of the subpoenas or issue a protective order. Plaintiff requests that the Court limit the subpoenas to records concerning Plaintiff’s right ankle, foot, and subtalar joint for the period of August 2021 through November 7, 2025. Additionally, Plaintiff requests withholding or redacting insurance-related information.

            Plaintiff argues that Defendant’s subpoenas are overbroad and violate Plaintiff’s right to privacy because they seek “any and all” medical records without meaningful limitation. Plaintiff argues that, because privacy rights are implicated, the burden has shifted to Defendant to demonstrate a compelling need and narrow tailoring. Plaintiff contends that Defendant has made no particularized showing of need and proposed no meaningful limitation. Defendant has failed to identify a single specific record, provider, or condition necessary to its defense. Plaintiff contends that Defendant has not explained what it expects to find from records five or ten years ago. If earlier records were not necessary for the surgeon to decide to operate, then they are not necessary to evaluate the decision to operate. Plaintiff contends that seeking records from 10 years ago is a fishing expedition. Plaintiff argues that the subpoenas would invade Plaintiff’s privacy by disclosing medical information regarding matters entirely unrelated to the 2022 surgery. Additionally, Plaintiff contends the benefits of allowing the subpoenas are nonexistent, while the burden of intrusion is substantial. Plaintiff also argues that the subpoenas seek information beyond the scope of matters in controversy, which include (1) the condition of Plaintiff’s right ankle and related structures immediately prior to the surgery; (2) the surgical procedure itself; and (3) the resulting post-operative complications, including cartilage damage and nerve injury. Since the subpoenas request any and all records, they encompass non-relevant information such as general medicine, urology, women’s health and reproductive care, and unrelated treatments. Also, the Plaintiff’s claims for pain, disability, emotional distress, and medical expenses do not create a wholesale waiver of privacy. Plaintiff contends the subpoenas are duplicative and include unrelated providers and medical histories.

            In opposition, Defendant argues Plaintiff has placed her medical condition at issue in this litigation by seeking damages for permanent disability, severe pain, loss of mobility, and emotional distress. Defendant argues that Defendant is entitled to discover whether or not Plaintiff had pre-existing injuries which might negate or mitigate the damages Plaintiff is seeking. Defendant contends that limiting Defendant to only obtain records from one year preceding her treatment is unreasonable. Defendant contends that medical records show Plaintiff was complaining of pain for more than five years before she first sought treatment with Defendant. Defendant argues it is not targeting sensitive personal information but seeking to ascertain the truth and the full picture of Plaintiff’s prior medical condition relating to the injuries at issue in this litigation. Defendant contends that the generality of the claims create the possibility that features of her other medical and psychological history will be directly relevant to the determination of emotional distress. The subpoenas are directly relevant, essential to a fair resolution, and no more intrusive than necessary.

           

            While a plaintiff is not obligated to sacrifice all privacy to seek redress for a specific physical, mental, or emotional injury, “they may not withhold information which relates to any physical or mental condition which they have put in issue by bringing this lawsuit.”  (Britt v. Superior Court (1978) 20 Cal.3d 844, 864; City & County of San Francisco v. Superior Court (1951) 37 Cal.2d 227, 232.) A plaintiff suing for personal injuries waives the physician-patient privilege to some extent, but this does not make discoverable all of a plaintiff’s lifetime medical history.  (Britt, supra, 20 Cal.3d at 863-64.) 

            Here, Defendant’s subpoenas directed to Palo Alto Medical Foundation, Sutter Health, and other providers identified seek “any and all” medical records. The Court agrees that the subpoenas are not limited in scope as to time or the relevant injuries suffered by the Plaintiff in this action. While some of Plaintiff’s prior medical history may be relevant to this action to determine if Plaintiff suffered from preexisting conditions, issues, or pain in certain body parts prior to the subject incident, discovery of the entirety of Plaintiff’s medical records, including billing and insurance information, is unreasonably broad in scope. Thus, the Court will limit the scope of the subpoenas to records related to Plaintiff’s right ankle, foot, subtalar joint, posterior tibial tendon, sural nerve, and related conditions, as requested by Plaintiff.

Additionally, the Court agrees the time scope of the subpoenas should be limited. The subject surgery occurred on August 10, 2022. (FAC, ¶ 16.) Plaintiff suggests the subpoenas should be limited to a period of time beginning in August 2021. The Court does not agree with Plaintiff that medical records prior to August 2021 are unlikely to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence. As such, the Court denies the time limitation suggested by Plaintiff beginning in August 2021. Balancing the intrusion into Plaintiff’s medical records as a whole against the need for discovery, the Court will limit the subpoenas in scope of time to 5 years prior to the injury to the present.

Accordingly, the motion is granted in part and denied in part.

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Plaintiff’s Motion to Quash, or in the Alternative, to Limit Deposition Subpoenas for Production of Business Records (Medical Records) is granted in part and denied in part. The motion is denied in part to the extent that Plaintiff sought to quash the subpoenas in their entirety and as to Plaintiff’s suggested time limitation on the subpoenas. The Motion is granted in part such that the Court will limit the subpoenas scope of time to 5 years prior to the subject surgery and to records relating to Plaintiff’s right ankle, foot, subtalar joint, posterior tibial tendon, sural nerve, and related conditions.

CASE NO./NAME: 25NNCV09364/ The Estate of Cosmo Silverman, et. al v. Campbell Hall, et. al.

Moving Party: Plaintiffs The Estate of Cosmo Silverman, Adam Silverman, and Louise Bonnet

Responding Party: Defendant Campbell Hall

Notice: Sufficient

Ruling: GRANT

 

NOTICE

The Court is not requesting oral argument on this matter.  The Court is guided by California Rules of Court, Rule 3.1308(a)(1) whereby notice of intent to appear is requested.  Unless the Court directs argument in the Tentative Ruling, no argument is requested and any party seeking argument should notify all other parties and the court by 4:00 p.m. on the court day before the hearing of the party’s intention to appear and argue.  The tentative ruling will become the ruling of the court if no argument is received.  

Notice may be given either by email at [email protected] or by telephone at (818) 260-8422.

BACKGROUND

This is a wrongful death and premises liability case. On June 4, 2025, fifteen-year-old Campbell Hall student Cosmo Silverman (“Cosmo”) was run over and killed in the school’s parking lot. (Compl., ¶ 1.) On December 30, 2025, Plaintiffs Adam Silverman, Louise Bonnet, and The Estate of Cosmo Silverman, by and through its Successors-in-Interest Adam Silverman and Louise Bonnet (collectively, “Plaintiffs”), filed a Complaint alleging (1) wrongful death-negligence and (2) premises liability against Defendant Campbell Hall (“Defendant”) and Does 1 through 10. Plaintiffs allege that had the school acted on prior complaints about the dangerous design and operation of its Triangle Parking Lot, Cosmo would still be alive. (Ibid.) Campbell Hall made safety improvements to its parking lot after Cosmo’s death. (Ibid.)

On February 9, 2026, Plaintiffs filed this Motion for Monetary Sanctions in the amount of $732.70 for Defendant’s failure to return notice and acknowledgement of receipt of summons and complaint.

On July 10, 2026, Defendant filed an opposition.

On July 10, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a reply.

 

 

 

LEGAL STANDARD

            A summons may be served by mail as provided in this section. A copy of the summons and of the complaint shall be mailed (by first-class mail or airmail, postage prepaid) to the person to be served, together with two copies of the notice and acknowledgment provided for in subdivision (b) and a return envelope, postage prepaid, addressed to the sender. (Civ. Proc. Code, § 415.30, subd. (a).) If the person to whom a copy of the summons and of the complaint are mailed pursuant to this section fails to complete and return the acknowledgment form set forth in subdivision (b) within 20 days from the date of such mailing, the party to whom the summons was mailed shall be liable for reasonable expenses thereafter incurred in serving or attempting to serve the party by another method permitted by this chapter, and, except for good cause shown, the court in which the action is pending, upon motion, with or without notice, shall award the party such expenses whether or not he is otherwise entitled to recover his costs in the action. (Id. at Civ. Proc. Code, § 415.30, subd. (d); Thierfeldt v. Marin Hosp. Dist. (1973) 35 Cal.App.3d 186, 199 [“Code of Civil Procedure section 415.30, effective July 1, 1970, which authorizes service of summons by mail, expressly predicates the efficacy of such service upon the execution and return of an acknowledgment of service. If the party addressed fails to do so, there is no effective service, he merely becomes liable for the reasonable expenses of service in a more conventional manner.”]) A notice or acknowledgment of receipt in form approved by the Judicial Council is deemed to comply with this section. (Civ. Proc. Code, § 415.30, subd. (e).)

 

DISCUSSION                                                                                              

            Plaintiffs argue they are entitled to recover the costs associated with serving Defendant and request an order sanctioning Defendant for reasonable expenses incurred in effecting that personal service. Plaintiffs argue that insurance defense counsel missed the January 26, 2026, statutory deadline to return the executed acknowledgment of receipt without explanation or a request for an extension. Plaintiffs contend that on December 30, 2025, Patrick Stockalper (“Stockalper”), insurance defense attorney for Defendant, confirmed he had authority to accept service in this lawsuit and Plaintiffs filed the complaint that day. Then, Plaintiffs served Stockalper with a Notice and Acknowledgement of Receipt. Plaintiffs argue that the executed acknowledgement was due back to Plaintiff’s counsel on January 26, 2026. Stockalper did not return the executed Acknowledgement, nor request an extension. Plaintiffs informed Stockalper that they intended to serve the summons and complaint by personal service and by U.S. Mail on Defendant and its registered agent. Plaintiffs contend service was completed on January 28, 2026, at a cost of $732.70.  

            In opposition, Defendant argues that Plaintiffs cannot establish the required causal connection. Defendant contends that section 415.30(d) permits recovery of expenses only “thereafter incurred” because of the failure to return the acknowledgment form, and argues that there should be a direct casual link between the statutory noncompliance and the claimed costs. Defendant argues that Plaintiffs’ decision to personally serve Defendant was not “caused by” the failure to return the acknowledgment form because Plaintiff’s own evidence establishes that Defendant’s counsel had already confirmed acceptance of service. Defendant argues that the costs were not compelled by necessity but instead were voluntarily incurred after Plaintiffs elected to proceed with duplicative service efforts. Additionally, the Court retains discretion to deny the fees for good cause. Good cause exists here because (1) Defendant did not evade service or delay notice; (2) counsel expressly agreed to accept service; (3) Plaintiffs suffered no prejudice; and (4) the claimed expenses were not caused by any obstruction or refusal of service. Finally, Defendant argues that the amount sought is unreasonable in light of the circumstances.

            In reply, Plaintiffs argue that Defendant’s representation that one will accept service is not the same as actually returning the statutory Notice and Acknowledgement. Plaintiffs also argue that Defendant has not shown good cause. Plaintiffs argue that whether Defendant evaded service is irrelevant. Counsel’s representation that he would accept service makes his failure inexcusable, not excusable. Because Defendant failed to return the Acknowledgment, Plaintiff incurred the expense of effecting service by another method. Finally, the amount requested is reasonable and undisputed as it reflects the documented cost of two substituted service efforts. Defendant does not identify an excessive charge, offer contrary evidence, or dispute that Plaintiffs paid the costs.

            Here, Plaintiffs demonstrated that Stockalper confirmed authority to accept service and then served Stockalper on January 5, 2026. (Glassman Decl., ¶ 2, Ex. 2.) Accordingly, Acknowledgement was due within 20 days. Stockalper did not comply with the statute or request an extension. (Id. at ¶ 3, Ex. 3.) Plaintiffs then served Defendant by personal service and by U.S. Mail which cost $478.00 and $254.70, totaling $732.70. (Id. at ¶ 3, Ex. 4, Ex. 5.) Because Defendant failed to comply with the statute, the Court finds that Plaintiff is entitled to the reasonable expenses thereafter incurred in serving the party by another method.

            Moreover, the Court is not persuaded by Defendant’s argument that there is no direct causation or that good cause exists not to grant the motion. Defendant’s proffered “good cause” does not demonstrate a good reason exists for Defendant’s failure to perform the statutory requirement. Instead, the Court agrees that Defendant’s failure to comply with the statute directly caused Plaintiffs to incur expenses of serving Defendant in a different method. That Stockalper agreed to accept service and did not evade service is not the same as complying with the statute.

            Finally, the Court finds the amount requested to be reasonable. Plaintiffs have presented invoices that demonstrate that service cost a total of $732.70. (Glassman Decl., ¶ 3, Ex. 4, Ex. 5.) Moreover, Defendants did not present any evidence that the amount was excessive or incorrect in opposition.

            Accordingly, the motion is granted.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Plaintiffs’ Motion for Monetary Sanctions in the amount of $732.70 for Defendant’s failure to return notice and acknowledgement of receipt of summons and complaint is granted.

BACKGROUND

(“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Defendants Robert Angel and Linda Angel (collectively “Defendants”), alleging five causes of action for: (1) Foreclosure of Mechanic’s Lien; (2) Breach of Contract; (3) Open Book Account; (4) Account Stated; and (5) Reasonable Value of Goods and Services.

LEGAL STANDARD

            After a mechanic’s lien has been recorded, “[t]he owner of property or the owner of any interest in property subject to a claim of lien may petition the court for an order to release the property from the claim of lien if the claimant has not commenced an action to enforce the lien within the time provided in Section 8460.”  (Civ. Code, § 8480, subd. (a).)   

            A claimant must commence an action to enforce a lien within 90 days of recording the lien. (Civ. Code, § 8460, subd. (a).)  Section 8460 further provides that “[i]f the claimant does not commence an action to enforce the lien within that time, the claim of lien expires and is unenforceable.” (Civ. Code, § 8460, subd. (a).) Section 8460 also provides that the 90-day time limit to commence an action to enforce a lien does not apply if there was an agreement to extend credit and a notice of that fact was recorded within 90 days after recordation of the claim of lien or more than 90 days after recordation of the claim of lien but before a purchaser or encumbrancer for value and in good faith acquires rights in the property.  (Civ. Code, § 8460, subd. (b).)   

DISCUSSION                                                                                              

Defendants petition for release of the mechanic’s lien against the Subject Property, on the basis the lien is unenforceable, void, or voidable due to the lien being asserted against the wrong property.  (Pet., ¶ 13.)

On July 8, 2026, Elie Haddad, the President of One Stop Shop, Inc., filed a declaration with proof of the release of the mechanic’s lien on the Subject Property. (Haddad Decl., ¶ 2, Ex. A.) Therefore, the Court finds the Petition is MOOT.

Defendants also request reasonable attorneys’ fees under Civil Code section 8488, subdivision (b), in demanding said release from Plaintiff and in bringing the Petition in an amount to be determined by motion after entry of judgment. (Pet., ¶ 13.)  The Court instructs Defendants to file a noticed motion on attorneys’ fees.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Defendants’ Petition for Order Releasing Property from Mechanic’s Lien is DENIED as MOOT.
