MOTION

 

This case arises from a dispute between a property owner and LADWP over water intrusion allegedly caused by a public project. 

 

The operative Third Amended Complaint (“TAC”) brought by Plaintiff BSE IKG LLC (“Plaintiff”) against Defendants Arya Chalon, LLC (“Arya”); Ardeshir “Ardie” Tavangarian (“Tavangarian”); Ronald Mark Beckenfeld, as trustee of the Ronald Mark Beckenfeld Living Trust U/D/T May 18, 2022 (“Beckenfeld”); City of Los Angeles Department of Water and Power (“LADWP”); Norman Reiss, as a trustee of the Reiss Family Trust dated May 12, 2006 (“Norman”); and Jennifer L. Reiss, as a trustee of the Reiss Family Trust dated May 12, 2006 (“Jennifer”) (together, “Defendants”) alleges seven causes of action as follows:

 

1.     Inverse Condemnation against LADWP

2.     Dangerous Condition of Public Property against LADWP

3.     Nuisance against LADWP

4.     Trespass against LADWP

5.     Negligence against the Somera-Chantilly Defendants

6.     Nuisance against the Somera-Chantilly Defendants

7.     Trespass against the Somera-Chantilly Defendants

 

Plaintiff owns real property on Chantilly Road; Arya, which is controlled by Tavangarian, owns various properties on Somera Road, upslope of Plaintiff’s property; Beckenfeld owns a property on Somera road upslope of and adjacent to Plaintiff’s property; and the Reiss Defendants own a property located on Chantilly Road next to Plaintiff’s property.

 

On July 2, 2026, Arya cross-complained against Plaintiff, Norman, Jennifer, and Beckenfeld, alleging three causes of action for (1) nuisance; (2) trespass; and (3) negligence.

 

Beckenfeld now moves to strike the punitive damages allegations from Arya’s Cross-Complaint.  Arya opposes the motion.

 

ANALYSIS

 

1.     MOTION TO STRIKE

 

Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading, may serve and file a motion to strike the whole pleading or any part thereof.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 435, subd. (b)(1); Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1322, subd. (b).)  On a motion to strike, the court may: (1) strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or (2) strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of California, a court rule, or an order of the court.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a)-(b); Stafford v. Shultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782.) 

 

In ruling on a motion to strike punitive damages, “judges read allegations of a pleading subject to a motion to strike as a whole, all parts in their context, and assume their truth.”  (Clauson v. Superior Court (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1253, 1255.)  To state a prima facie claim for punitive damages, a plaintiff must allege the elements set forth in the punitive damages statute, Civil Code section 3294.  (College Hosp., Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 721.)  Per Civil Code section 3294, a plaintiff must allege that the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice.  (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (a).)   As set forth in the Civil Code,

 

(1) “Malice” means conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.  (2) “Oppression” means despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person's rights.  (3) “Fraud” means an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known to the defendant with the intention on the part of the defendant of thereby depriving a person of property or legal rights or otherwise causing injury.

 

(Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (c)(1)-(3), emphasis added.) 

 

Further, a plaintiff must assert facts with specificity to support a conclusion that a defendant acted with oppression, fraud or malice.  To wit, there is a heightened pleading requirement regarding a claim for punitive damages.  (See Smith v. Superior Court (1992) 10 Cal.App.4th 1033, 1041-1042.)  “When nondeliberate injury is charged, allegations that the defendant’s conduct was wrongful, willful, wanton, reckless or unlawful do not support a claim for exemplary damages; such allegations do not charge malice.  When a defendant must produce evidence in defense of an exemplary damage claim, fairness demands that he receive adequate notice of the kind of conduct charged against him.” (G. D. Searle & Co. v. Superior Court (1975) 49 Cal.App.3d 22, 29 [cleaned up].)  In Anschutz Entertainment Group, Inc. v. Snepp, the Court of Appeal noted that the plaintiffs’ assertions related to their claim for punitive damages were “insufficient to meet the specific pleading requirement.”  (Anschutz Entertainment Group, Inc. v. Snepp (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 598, 643 [plaintiffs alleged “the conduct of Defendants was intentional, and done willfully, maliciously, with ill will towards Plaintiffs, and with conscious disregard for Plaintiff's rights. Plaintiff's injuries were exacerbated by the malicious conduct of Defendants. Defendants' conduct justifies an award of exemplary and punitive damages”]; see also Grieves v. Superior Court (1984) 157 Cal.App.3d 159, 166 [“The mere allegation an intentional tort was committed is not sufficient to warrant an award of punitive damages.  Not only must there be circumstances of oppression, fraud, or malice, but facts must be alleged in the pleading to support such a claim”].) 

 

As to Beckenfeld, Cross-Complainant alleges:

 

3. [A]t all times relevant herein, Beckenfeld was the owner of the real property located at 962 Somera Road, Los Angeles, California 90077 (the “Beckenfeld Property”). . . .

 

11. The Arya Properties neighbor the Beckenfeld Property, which is adjacent to the Arya Properties. The Beckenfeld Property is located within a designated Grading Area as established by the City of Los Angeles. Grading and earthwork within a designated Grading Area require permits from the City of Los Angeles Department of Building and Safety.

 

14. All of the properties referenced herein exist upon a hillside and are on or adjacent to an ancient landslide.

 

15. In or about May 2024, a landslide within the ancient landslide activated, damaging the Arya Properties, including because the property and earth surrounding the Arya Properties has subsided, exposing and damaging the subterranean and stabilizing structures at the Arya Properties as well as other aspects of the Arya Properties, and because earth and other debris has invaded the Arya Properties.

 

16. Cross-Complainant is informed and believes and based thereon alleges that prior to May 2024, Beckenfeld caused unpermitted changes to be made to the rear yard of the Beckenfeld Property, including the construction of retaining walls creating terraces, a faux pond and stream, a putting green, a greenhouse, and other unpermitted improvements. Moreover, Beckenfeld caused unpermitted grading to be performed at the Beckenfeld Property in conjunction with construction of the rear yard improvements. This grading was performed within a designated Grading Area without the required permits from the City of Los Angeles. Further and pursuant to improvements caused by Beckenfeld, storm water from the roof and lower pads at the Beckenfeld Property was conducted to three approximately 1,750-gallon above-grade storm water collection tanks on a level pad east of and below the southeast corner of the residence. Overflow from those tanks flowed into a below-grade vault with sump pumps, and water was pumped to Somera Road via subsurface pipes. That storm water collection and management system was located within what is now the active landslide. Upon information and belief, the pipe system leading to and from the collection tanks and sump vault at 962 Somera Road was compromised and leaking prior to the May 2024 landslide, thereby contributing to the saturation of soils and the activation of the ancient landslide. Beckenfeld also performed other earthwork and work affecting the hillside. The earthwork and other improvements caused by Beckenfeld contributed to the May 2024 landslide.

 

20. Following the May 2024 landslide and the initiation of this action, Beckenfeld performed new activities in May of 2026 and caused grading and substantial earth movement to be undertaken at the Beckenfeld Property, including grading, export of dirt, demolition work, and the moving of truckloads of boulders. This work was performed after any previously issued permits had expired and without obtaining new permits required for work within the designated Grading Area. Accordingly, Beckenfeld has caused conditions at the head of the active landslide to be altered without geotechnical input or required permits from the City of Los Angeles Department of Building and Safety or any other governmental authority.

 

21. Beckenfeld also caused to be moved and removed his previously installed unpermitted improvements and water storage tanks, and the grade at the Beckenfeld Property is being further altered without permits or geotechnical oversight. Such actions constitute an effort to hide and obscure evidence related to the May 2024 landslide. Such actions also contribute to the instability of the hillside.

Cross-Complainant is informed and believes and based thereon alleges that Beckenfeld has also caused the former storm water collection system at 962 Somera Road to be dismantled without replacement. It is imperative that an operational storm water collection and management system be established at 962 Somera Road before the next rain event to prevent further damage to the Arya Properties and other neighboring properties.

On or about June 4, 2026, Cross-Complainant’s counsel sent a demand letter to Beckenfeld’s counsel demanding immediate cessation of all unpermitted grading and earthwork at the Beckenfeld Property and preservation of all evidence relevant to the condition of the hillside and the cause and extent of the ongoing landslide. Despite receiving notice of the dangerous conditions through the June 4, 2026 demand letter as well as by the May 2024 landslide and the initiation of this action, among other things Beckenfeld has caused unpermitted grading and earth movement to continue at the Beckenfeld Property, including the moving of truckloads of boulders within the designated Grading Area without the required permits or geotechnical oversight. The ongoing unpermitted grading and earthwork that Beckenfeld has caused to be undertaken at the head of the active landslide threatens to cause further landslide movement and damage to the Arya Properties.

 

24. Beckenfeld knew or should have known that causing unpermitted grading and substantial earth movement to be undertaken at the head of an active landslide, within a designated Grading Area, without geotechnical oversight or required permits, would endanger neighboring properties including the Arya Properties. Beckenfeld was placed on notice of the dangerous consequences of continued unpermitted work after the May 2024 landslide and the filing of this action and the letter from Cross-Complainant's counsel, and yet persisted in his dangerous actions. The geological conditions at the Arya Properties have degraded because of Beckenfeld's continued dangerous actions.

 

25. Beckenfeld’s conduct as alleged herein was despicable, and willful, oppressive, and malicious within the meaning of California Civil Code Section 3294.

 

26. Beckenfeld acted with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights and safety of Cross-Complainant and other neighboring property owners. His decision to cause substantial grading and earth movement to be performed—including moving truckloads of boulders—within a designated Grading Area at the head of an active landslide, without permits or geotechnical oversight, and in defiance of demands to cease such activity and after notice of the harmful effects of his conduct, constitutes malice and oppression under California Civil Code Section 3294. Beckenfeld was and is aware of the probable dangerous consequences of his conduct and has deliberately failed to avoid those consequences.

 

27. Cross-Complainant is therefore entitled to an award of punitive damages in an amount sufficient to punish Beckenfeld and deter similar conduct in the future.

29. Cross-Complainant has no adequate remedy at law for the injuries caused by Beckenfeld's wrongful acts in that, among other things, monetary compensation would not adequately compensate Cross-Complaint for being deprived of the use and enjoyment of the Arya Properties, as set forth above. Accordingly, Cross-Complainant is entitled to injunctive relief compelling Beckenfeld to cease all unpermitted grading and earthwork at the Beckenfeld Property.

 

(Cross-Complaint at ¶¶ 3, 11, 14-16, 20-29.)

 

            As the Court previously explained, allegations made “on information and belief” are generally insufficient to satisfy heightened pleading requirements “unless the facts upon which the belief is founded are stated in the pleading.”  (Dowling v. Spring Valley. Water Co. (1917) 174 Cal.218, 221.) 

 

            Here, Arya does not indicate the basis for the belief that the work done to the Beckenfeld property was unpermitted and lacking geotechnical oversight, nor does Arya allege who, what, when, or how this construction work was done.  Further, Arya does not allege the basis for Arya’s allegation that Beckenfeld knew or should have known the construction work was destabilizing the common hillside prior to the May 2024 landslide that caused Arya’s damages. 

 

            Therefore, Arya has not alleged oppression, fraud, or malice with requisite specificity to support a claim for punitive damages against Beckenfeld.

 

2.     LEAVE TO AMEND

 

A plaintiff has the burden of showing in what manner the complaint could be amended and how the amendment would change the legal effect of the complaint, i.e., state a cause of action. (See The Inland Oversight Committee v. City of San Bernardino (2018) 27 Cal.App.5th 771, 779; PGA West Residential Assn., Inc. v. Hulven Int'l, Inc. (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 156, 189.) A plaintiff must not only state the legal basis for the amendment, but also the factual allegations sufficient to state a cause of action or claim. (See PGA West Residential Assn., Inc. v. Hulven Int'l, Inc., supra, 14 Cal.App.5th at p. 189.) Moreover, a plaintiff does not meet his or her burden by merely stating in the opposition to a demurrer or motion to strike that “if the Court finds the operative complaint deficient, plaintiff respectfully requests leave to amend.” (See Major Clients Agency v Diemer (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1116, 1133; Graham v. Bank of America (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 594, 618 [asserting an abstract right to amend does not satisfy the burden].)

 

Here, because Arya does not specify the specific facts Arya intends to add to the cross-complaint to cure the deficiencies above, the Court denies Arya’s request for leave to amend. 

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

 

For the reasons stated, the Court grants Beckenfeld’s motion to strike without leave to amend and without prejudice. 

 

Further, the Court orders Beckenfeld to file and serve an Answer to the Cross-Complaint on or before September 28, 2026. 

 

Further, the Court will enter the proposed Order lodged on August 10, 2026 in conformity with the ruling.   

 

Beckenfeld shall provide notice of the Court’s ruling/Order, and file the notice with a proof of service forthwith.

DEPARTMENT          207

CASE NUMBER        25SMCP00513

MOTION                    Amend Judgment

MOVING PARTY      Petitioner Ami Shah

OPPOSING PARTY   Respondent Debora A. Gregory

 

MOTION

 

This case arises from a petition to confirm an arbitration award. 

 

On September 30, 2025, Petitioner Ami Shah as assignee of Assure Home Therapy’s claims (“Petitioner”) filed the case-initiating Petition to Confirm Arbitration Award against Respondent Debra Gregory (Caring Like Family Inc. DBA: Like Family Home Health).

 

On April 8, 2026, the Court granted the petition in part as to Caring Like Family Inc. (“Caring”), but denied the petition as to Caring’s principal, Debora Gregory (“Gregory”) on the grounds that Gregory was not a respondent in the underlying arbitration and the arbitration award was only against Caring.  Thereafter, the Court entered the Judgment on April 8, 2026 in conformity with its ruling. 

 

Petitioner now moves to amend the judgment pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 187 on the ground that Gregory is an alter ego of Caring.  Gregory opposes the motion.  Petitioner filed an untimely reply, which the Court did not initially consider, but now does, in light of the continued hearing.

 

Petitioner argued at the initial hearing that Chase bank produced documents pursuant to a subpoena the night before, which are necessary to bolster Petitioner’s motion.  The Court continued the hearing to allow Petitioner to submit supplemental briefing.  Petitioner filed a supplemental brief, Respondent filed a supplemental opposition, and Petitioner filed a supplemental reply.  Petitioner also submitted a supplemental reply declaration, to which Respondent objected.

 

Regarding Petitioner’s Supplemental Reply Declaration and exhibits, the Court does not generally consider reply evidence, as it deprives the opposing party of a fair opportunity to respond, in violation of their due process rights.  (San Diego Watercrafts, Inc. v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2002) 102 Cal.App.4th 308, 316 [“due process requires a party be fully advised of the issues to be addressed and be given adequate notice of what facts it must rebut in order to prevail”]; see also Wall Street Network Ltd. v. New York Times Co. (2008) 164 Cal.App.4th 1171.)  Therefore, the Court does not consider Petitioner’s Supplemental Reply Declaration or the exhibits attached thereto.

ANALYSIS

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 187 provides: “When jurisdiction is, by the Constitution or this Code, or by any other statute, conferred on a Court or judicial officer, all the means necessary to carry it into effect are also given; and in the exercise of this jurisdiction, if the course of proceeding be not specifically pointed out by this Code or the statute, any suitable process or mode of proceeding may be adopted which may appear most conformable to the spirit of this Code.” 

 

“Code of Civil Procedure section 187 authorizes a trial court to amend a judgment to add a judgment debtor who is found to be an alter ego of a corporate defendant.”  (Misik v. D'Arco (2011) 197 Cal.App.4th 1065, 1069.)  “[A] plaintiff's failure to allege the alter ego doctrine in the underlying lawsuit does not preclude a motion to amend the judgment.”  (Ibid.)

 

To establish alter ego liability, Plaintiff must show (1) such a unity of interest and ownership between the corporations that no separation actually exists, and (2) an inequitable result if the acts in question are treated as those of the corporation alone.  (Leek v. Cooper (2011) 194 Cal.App.4th 399, 417.)  Several factors are considered in determining whether the alter ego doctrine applies, including:

 

(1)   commingling of funds and other assets, failure to segregate funds of the separate entities, and the unauthorized diversion of corporate funds or assets to other than corporate uses;

 

(2)   the treatment by an individual of the assets of the corporation as his own;

 

(3)   the failure to obtain authority to issue stock or to subscribe to or issue the same;

 

(4)   the holding out by an individual that he is personally liable for the debts of the corporation;

 

(5)   the failure to maintain minutes or adequate corporate records;

 

(6)   sole ownership of all of the stock in a corporation by one individual or the members of a family;

 

(7)   the failure to adequately capitalize a corporation;

 

(8)   the total absence of corporate assets, and undercapitalization;

 

(9)   the use of a corporation as a mere shell, instrumentality or conduit for a single venture or the business of an individual or another corporation;

 

(10)         the concealment and misrepresentation of the identity of the responsible ownership, management and financial interest, or concealment of personal business activities;

 

(11)         the disregard of legal formalities and the failure to maintain arm's length relationships among related entities;

 

(12)         the use of the corporate entity to procure labor, services or merchandise for another person or entity;

 

(13)         the diversion of assets from a corporation by or to a stockholder or other person or entity, to the detriment of creditors, or the manipulation of assets and liabilities between entities so as to concentrate the assets in one and the liabilities in another;

 

(14)         the contracting with another with intent to avoid performance by use of a corporate entity as a shield against personal liability, or the use of a corporation as a subterfuge of illegal transactions; and

 

(15)         the formation and use of a corporation to transfer to it the existing liability of another person or entity.

 

(Id. at pp. 417–418.)


            In support of the motion, Petitioner advances the Declaration of Ami Shah, which as demonstrated by the Court’s evidentiary rulings above, primarily contains inadmissible legal arguments and statements which lack foundation.

 

The gist of Petitioner’s argument for which Petitioner has laid an adequate foundation is that Gregory is the sole shareholder, officer, and agent for service of process for Caring, which Gregory runs out of her personal residence, and Caring was suspended by the Franchise Tax Board as of April 2, 2024.  Thus, on August 19, 2024 when Petitioner and Caring entered into the underlying agreement at the heart of their arbitration dispute, and Caring became indebted to Petitioner for approximately $50,000, Caring was already suspended and disqualified from operating as a legal entity.  As such, Petitioner argues, Caring was effectively a sole proprietorship at the time the agreement was made.   

 

But, at best, Petitioner has demonstrated (1) sole ownership and (2) failure to adequately capitalize the corporation. 

 

The Court does not find the fact that Gregory ran the business out of her personal residence alone sufficient to demonstrate improper comingling, the treatment by Gregory of Caring as though its assets were her own personal assets, lack of corporate formalities, or that Gregory used Caring to procure goods or services for another person or entity.  To wit, in opposition, Gregory provides a declaration clarifying that she maintains her own separate bank account, segregated from the bank account for Caring, she has not used Caring’s funds for her own purposes, and the parties’ agreement was for Petitioner to provide physical and occupational therapy services to Caring’s patients.  (Gregory Decl. ¶¶ 4, 11.)  There is no evidence that Petitioner performed services for Gregory personally.

 

Further, the fact that Petitioner personally viewed the company’s records in Gregory’s home demonstrates that such records were being kept.  (See also Gregory Decl. ¶ 9 [Caring “has followed corporate formalities”].)  And the fact that Gregory’s name is all over the officially filed corporate records demonstrates Gregory did not conceal or misrepresent her identity as the company’s owner. 

 

Further, Petitioner’s attempt to use alter ego to bring Gregory into the litigation in her personal capacity further demonstrates that Gregory did not personally guaranty the debts of the corporation.  (See also Gregory Decl. ¶ 4 [“I did not personally guarantee the obligations of CLFI under the Agreement”].)

 

Although Petitioner characterizes Caring as a mere shell or instrumentality, that Caring was created as a shield to avoid performance and liability, or that Caring was created to transfer Gregory’s liability, Petitioner has not presented any evidence supporting these arguments.  The mere fact that Caring was solely owned by Gregory and ultimately became unprofitable to the point where it could not satisfy its debts does not, without more, demonstrate that Caring and Gregory are alter egos of each other.

 

In supplemental briefing, however, Petitioner has provided bank records demonstrating 139 transfers between Gregory’s personal bank account and the corporate bank accounts, including six transfers to the entity bank accounts which Petitioner contends (and Gregory confirms in paragraph 13 of her supplemental opposition declaration) are Medicare reimbursements, which were followed by transfers of nearly all of that money into Gregory’s personal bank account in the fourth quarter of 2025.

 

Gregory has characterized those transactions as providing capital for the companies and then subsequently taking capital draws:

 

7. At all times, I have maintained my own bank account separate from the bank account maintained by CLFI. At all relevant times, my funds have been segregated from the bank account of CLFI. I have not treated the assets of CLFI as my own or used CLFI' s funds for my own personal purposes.

 

9. One of the principal assertions of Shah in her Supplemental Memorandum concerns $69,757.12 net, allegedly transferred from CLFI to me. Shah contends that between February 7, 2025 and January 2, 2026, CLFI transferred $209,349.83 to me, and I transferred $139,592.71 back to CLFI through 89 separate transactions. The $69,757.12 being highlighted is therefore the mathematical net of substantial two-way financial activity between the accounts, rather than $69,757.12 simply being transferred from the corporation to me.

 

10. There is also broader financial history surrounding these isolated transactions that cannot necessarily be determined from the bank statements alone or Shah's own analysis. During this period, I was capitalizing CLFI, including, among other things, providing personal funding and using personal credit to assist CLFI and related hospice companies in meeting their financial obligations because of the limited direct lending opportunities available to the businesses. Transfers between myself and the businesses occurred in the context of this ongoing funding activity. As with any small company, I took draws to the extent available for my services on behalf of CLFI.

 

12. This is also relevant to the credit card payments identified by Shah. As with many small companies, I was forced to use personal credit as a source of funding for the business and to meet business expense obligations to keep CLFI operating, including, among other things, obtaining medical supplies and paying subscriptions, such as business telephone services. Accordingly, the fact that an account or credit card bears my individual name does not, by itself, establish whether the underlying expenditure was personal or business related. That determination would require consideration of each of the underlying transactions and supporting records.

 

13. Shah's supplemental filing also focuses on six (6) Medicare reimbursements received by CLFI between September 29 and December 18, 2025, totaling only $24,168.56. The timing of a Medicare deposit followed by a transfer does not, standing alone, establish the purpose or character of that transfer. Further, viewed over the period being analyzed, that amount of Medicare reimbursement is not, by itself sufficient to sustain the operating requirements of a business such as CLFI, including paying payroll, vendors, contractors and other ongoing business operating obligations.

 

14. There is also an important distinction regarding Caring Like Family Hospice, which was not a party to an agreement with Claimant or a party to the Arbitration. Hospice is, and at all times herein mentioned was, a separate business with its own bank accounts. Shah identifies $16,685 transferred from Hospice to CLFI and $5,750 transferred from CLFI to Hospice, resulting in a net $10,935 flowing into CLFI. The Hospice transactions are separate from the transactions between CLFI and me.

 

15. Shah also contends that a McLaren vehicle, which I drove, was somehow improperly leased through the company. Such vehicle was used for business purposes, including, among other things, visiting patients and for marketing purposes.

 

(Supp Gregory Decl. ¶¶ 7, 9-10, 12-15.)

 

            The Court does not find Gregory credible.  To start, Gregory does not adequately explain why Caring continued to enter into contracts, like the one with Petitioner, after Caring was suspended by the Francise Tax Board in April 2024, other than to indicate that the suspension was an “oversight.”  (See Original Gregory Decl. ¶ 10.)

 

            Further, the fact that there were limited lending opportunities available to the businesses, causing Gregory to start capitalizing the business with her own personal funds demonstrates that the lines began to blur between Gregory in her individual capacity and the entities, with Gregory making personal loans to the entities.

 

            Yet, Gregory apparently did not bear the risk of those loans, as she continued to take draws, including of Medicare reimbursements, in the fourth quarter of 2025, when the business was clearly failing.  Those draws suggest Gregory was improperly draining the business of capital for her own personal benefit, at the expense of the company’s creditors, like Petitioner.

 

            Even further, Gregory has not adequately explained why a luxury sports car is necessary or appropriate to visit patients or to market her home health aid or hospice companies.  While the Court acknowledges that Gregory’s hospice company is not a party to this action, the comingling of her personal funds with the hospice company demonstrates a pattern and practice of blurring the lines between her entities and personal finances.

 

            Thus, Petitioner has adequately demonstrated that Gregory comingled both the debts and profits of Caring with her own personal finances, used the company to finance a luxury sports vehicle for herself, and took “draws” to deplete the company of assets as it was failing, and Gregory has not adequately demonstrated that any of the above was reasonable under the circumstances.

 

CONCLUSION

 

            Finding Gregory is an alter ego of Caring, the Court grants Petitioner’s motion to amend the judgment to add Gregory in her personal capacity as an alter ego for Caring.   

 

Further, the Court orders Petition to lodge a proposed Order and proposed Amended Judgment in conformity with the ruling on or before September 28, 2026.  Any objections to the proposed Order or Judgment shall be filed and served on or before October 8, 2026. 

 

Petitioner shall provide notice of the Court’s ruling/Order, and file the notice with a proof of service forthwith.

MOTION

Protective Order

MOVING PARTY

Plaintiff Susan Neuberg

OPPOSING PARTY

Defendant Costco Wholesale Corporation

 

BACKGROUND

 

On April 3, 2025, Plaintiff Susan Neuberg (“Plaintiff”) filed suit against Defendants Costco Wholesale Corporation and Carl Barrio (“Defendants”) alleging two causes of action for premises liability and negligent hiring. 

 

On June 4, 2026, the Court granted Plaintiff’s motion for a trial preference, and set the trial for September 28, 2026.

 

Plaintiff now moves for a protective order to quash Costco’s demand for expert witness exchange and for monetary sanctions.  Costco opposes the motion and requests monetary sanctions.

 

ANALYSIS   

 

I.                Motion for Protective Order

 

“A demand for an exchange of information concerning expert trial witnesses shall be in writing and shall identify, below the title of the case, the party making the demand. The demand shall state that it is being made under this chapter.  The demand shall specify the date for the exchange of lists of expert trial witnesses, expert witness declarations, and any demanded production of writings. The specified date of exchange shall be 50 days before the initial trial date, or 20 days after service of the demand, whichever is closer to the trial date, unless the court, on motion and a showing of good cause, orders an earlier or later date of exchange.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2034.230, subds. (a)-(b).)

 

“A party who has been served with a demand to exchange information concerning expert trial witnesses may promptly move for a protective order. This motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.  The court, for good cause shown, may make any order that justice requires to protect any party from unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, oppression, or undue burden and expense. The protective order may include, but is not limited to, one or more of the following directions:  (1) That the demand be quashed because it was not timely served.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2034.250, subds. (a)-(b).) 

 

Here, on June 4, 2026, the Court granted Plaintiff’s motion for a trial preference and scheduled the case for trial on September 28, 2026.  Fifty days before the September 28, 2026 trial is Sunday, August 9. 

 

On July 20, 2026, Defendants electronically served the Demand for Expert Witness Exchange, specifying an exchange date of Monday, August 10, which Plaintiff objected to on August 10.

 

Plaintiff argues that because August 9 falls on a Sunday, Costco improperly rounded the date forward to August 10, which is technically less than 50 days before trial, in violation of the statute.  Further, Plaintiff argues, that because the demand was served electronically, an additional 2 court days should have been added to account for electronic service.

 

In Staub v. Kiley (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 1437, 1447, the Court of Appeal explained that where there is not an unreasonable delay, gamesmanship, or a comprehensive attempt to thwart legitimate and necessary discovery, the exclusion of such evidence is not justified. 

Similarly, here, the Court does not find Costco’s conduct, which at best amounts to a slight miscalculation of the rounding rules for the dates, constitutes gamesmanship or is otherwise an “unreasonable” failure to comply with the expert designation rules, warranting the exclusion of Defendants’ expert witnesses.  (See Staub, supra, 226 Cal.App.4th at p. 1447 [“The record here does not support a determination that plaintiffs so unreasonably failed to timely disclose their experts that exclusion of all expert testimony was warranted”].)

 

To the contrary, any gamesmanship here lies with Plaintiff, who, after successfully obtaining a preference trial now appears to be employing “gotcha” litigation tactics to try to deprive Defendants’ of their expert witnesses over a matter of three days.

 

II.             Sanctions

 

The parties each seek monetary sanctions against the other in connection with the motion and opposition.  Because the Court denies Plaintiff’s motion, it similarly denies Plaintiff’s request for sanctions.

 

Costco requests sanctions on the grounds that, while Plaintiff met and conferred, Plaintiff failed to adequately meet and confer in good faith prior to filing the motion, which amounts to a misuse of the discovery process. 

 

The Court does not find monetary sanctions warranted on the record before it.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

 

            For the foregoing reasons, the Court denies Plaintiff’s motion for a protective order quashing Defendants’ demand for an exchange of expert information under Code of Civil Procedure section 2034.230. 

 

Further, the Court denies both parties’ requests for monetary sanctions.

 

Further, the Court admonishes both parties to work collaboratively in good faith toward preparing this case for trial on the merits and to refrain from any further gamesmanship tactics which may delay the start of the trial, notwithstanding the trial preference. 

 

Plaintiff shall provide notice of the Court’s ruling and file the notice with a proof of service forthwith.

MOTION

Motion to Quash Deposition Subpoena for Production of Business Records to WFG National Title Insurance Co.

MOVING PARTY

Defendant Debora A. Gregory, individually and as a Trustee of Debora Ann Gregory Living Trust dated June 9, 2011, as amended

OPPOSING PARTY

Plaintiff Ami Shah

 

MOTION

 

This case arises from a dispute concerning an outstanding debt. 

 

On May 14, 2026, Plaintiff Ami Shah (“Plaintiff”) filed suit against Defendants Debora A. Gregory, individually and as Trustee of the Debora Ann Gregory Living Trust dated June 9, 2011, as amended (“Gregory”) and ZZ Enterprises, Inc. (“ZZ”).  The operative First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) was filed on August 11, 2026 against the same Defendants and alleges three causes of action for (1) avoidance of voidable transfer and obligation – actual intent); (2) avoidance and recovery of voidable transfers – actual intent; and (3) declaratory relief.

 

Gregory now moves to quash a deposition subpoena for production of business records served on WFG National Title Insurance Co.  Plaintiff opposes the motion and Gregory replies.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

 

“In California, discovery may be obtained from a nonparty through an oral deposition, a written deposition, or a deposition for the production of business records and things. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2020.010, subd. (a).)” (Board of Registered Nursing v. Superior Court of Orange County (2021) 59 Cal.App.5th 1011, 1030 (hereafter Board of Registered Nursing).) “To pursue the deposition of a nonparty, a party must generally serve a deposition subpoena.” (Ibid.).)

 

“‘A deposition subpoena that commands only the production of business records for copying shall designate the business records to be produced either by specifically describing each individual item or by reasonably particularizing each category of item, and shall specify the form in which any electronically stored information is to be produced, if a particular form is desired.’ [Citation.]” (Board of Registered Nursing, supra, 59 Cal.App.5th at p. 1030.)

 

If a subpoena requires the production of documents, the court may quash the subpoena entirely or modify it. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.1, subd. (a).)

 

EVIDENTIARY OBJECTIONS

 

            The Court rules as follows on Gregory’s evidentiary objections:

 

1.     Overruled

2.     Overruled

3.     Overruled

4.     Overruled

 

ANALYSIS

 

I.                Motion to Quash

 

            Defendant moves to quash the deposition subpoena on the following grounds:

 

(1) the notice to consumer was improperly served only by email to Behzad Nahai, Esq. and Jeffrey Lewiston, Esq. of Nahai Law Group, who are not attorneys of record for Gregory as Gregory has not yet appeared in this matter and who are not authorized to accept service of process on Gregory’s behalf;

 

(2) the financial records sought violate Gregory’s right to privacy and there has been no protective order entered in this case; and

 

(3) the documents sought are not relevant or likely to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence

 

            Taking Defendant’s first argument, Code of Civil Procedure, Section 1985.3 requires that service be made “To the consumer personally, or at his or her last known address, […] or, if he or she is a party, to his or her attorney of record.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1985.3, subd. (b)(1), emphasis added.)

 

            Although Gregory is a party to this action, Gregory does not yet have an attorney of record in this action, as Plaintiff has not demonstrated proper service of the summons and complaint on Gregory.

 

            On May 20, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Proof of Service indicating Plaintiff mailed Gregory a copy of the summons and complaint with a notice and acknowledgment of receipt on May 14, 2026, but there is no indication that Gregory signed and returned the notice.

 

            On May 29, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Proof of Service indicating the summons and complaint “by first class mail” at an address in Marina Del Rey, California.  But other than a notice of acknowledgment and receipt, service by first-class mail is generally only appropriate for service on a person outside the State of California.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 415.40.)

 

            On May 29, 2026, Plaintiff filed a proof of service of the summons and complaint via electronic service to attorneys Nahai and Lewiston.  But absent a Court order, electronic service of the summons and complaint is generally not an appropriate means of serving the summons and complaint.  Further, electronic service is generally not appropriate unless a party has expressly agrees to it.

 

            Thus, there is no evidence that Gregory has been properly served with a copy of the summons and complaint.  As such, Gregory had not yet appeared and did not have counsel when the deposition subpoena was purportedly served.

 

Gregory also advances the Declaration of Jeffrey Lewiston, which provides:

 

1. I am an attorney associated with of Nahai Law Group, a professional corporation ("NLG") attorneys for specially appearing defendant Debora A. Gregory, individually and as Trustee of the Deborah Gregory Living Trust dated June 9, 2011, as Amended ("Gregory") as to this the subject Motion to Quash Deposition Subpoena for Production of Business Records (the "Subpoena") to WFG National Title Insurance Co. (the "Witness"), issued by attorney John Sullivan ("Sullivan") on behalf of plaintiff Ami Shah ("Plaintiff') and served by JPL Process Service, LLC (the "Deposition Officer") in the above-entitled action (the "Action"). As to the facts contained in this declaration, I know them to be true of my own knowledge or I have gained knowledge from the records and files of Nahai Law Group which are maintained in the ordinary course of the business of Nahai Law Group. If called upon to testify, I would and could testify competently to the facts set forth herein.

 

2. On June 2, 2026, at 12:04 a.m., Sullivan emailed to Behzad Nahai, Esq. of Nahai Law Group and myself, a Notice of Consumer to Debora Gregory, and the Subpoena. Attached hereto as Exhibit "A" and incorporated herein by reference is a true and correct copy of such email, attaching the Notice to Consumer and the Subpoena. As reflected by Sullivan's proof of service attached to the Notice to Consumer, Plaintiff did not serve Gregory personally or otherwise with such Notice.

 

3. Gregory has not been served with the Summons and Complaint in this Action and she has not generally appeared in the Action. In May 2026, Gregory retained NLG to specially appear in the Action to represent Gregory solely in connection with objecting to the Notice of Pendency of Action filed in the Action. In support of such objections, Gregory filed a Declaration in this Action on May 22, 2026 (the "Gregory Deel") which is attached hereto as Exhibit "B" for the Court's convenience and incorporated herein by reference.

 

4. NLG has not consented in writing, or otherwise, to accept service of process, including, without limitation, the Notice to Consumer on behalf of Gregory.

 

5. I met and conferred with Plaintiff’s counsel Sullivan, by writing him the letter attached hereto as Exhibit "C" and incorporated herein by reference (the "Demand Letter").  Sullivan did not respond to the Demand Letter and neither Plaintiff nor Sullivan have voluntarily withdrawn the Subpoena. Furthermore, no protective order has been entered in the Action.

 

(Lewiston Decl. ¶¶ 1-5.)   Further, the proof of service attached to the deposition subpoena indicates only that Lewiston and Nahai were served electronically.  (See Ex. A to Lewiston Decl.)  Therefore, the Court finds that the Notice to Consumer was not properly served on Gregory.

 

            In opposition, Plaintiff argues only that Gregory had actual notice of it, by virtue of her timely objection to it.  But actual notice is not the standard.  Rather, the statute requires personal service, unless the person is a party to the action, in which case service may be effectuated on her counsel of record.  Because Gregory does not yet have counsel of record in this matter, Plaintiff’s attempted service on Gregory’s counsel was insufficient.

 

            Plaintiff also argues that Gregory was additionally served with the Subpoena by mail.  (Sullivan Decl. ¶ 3 and Ex. 2.)  But that does not cure the deficiency of failing to personally serve the notice to consumer on a party who does not yet have counsel of record.

 

II.             Sanctions

In ruling on a motion to quash, “the court may in its discretion award the amount of the reasonable expenses incurred in making or opposing the motion, including reasonable attorney's fees, if the court finds the motion was made or opposed in bad faith or without substantial justification or that one or more of the requirements of the subpoena was oppressive.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.2, subd. (a).)  “Unreasonable or oppressive demands” include “unreasonable violations of the right of privacy of the person.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.1, subd. (a).)

 

Gregory seeks monetary sanctions against Plaintiff and her attorney of record, John W. Sullivan, jointly and severally in the amount of $5,100.

 

The Court agrees that because the Notice to Consumer was not properly served on Gregory and Plaintiff made no legal arguments to the contrary in opposition, that the motion to quash was opposed without substantial justification, warranting monetary sanctions.  Notwithstanding, the Court does not find $5,100 to be reasonable.

 

As such, the Court grants in part Gregory’s request for monetary sanctions in the amount of $1,500, representing four (4) hours at Lewiston’s hourly rate of $375.

CONCLUSION AND ORDERS

 

The Court grants in part Plaintiff’s motion, finding the Notice to Consumer was not properly served.  Accordingly, the Court quashes the subpoena. 

 

Further, finding Plaintiff opposed the motion without substantial justification, the Court awards Gregory monetary sanctions against Plaintiff and Plaintiff’s attorney of record, John W. Sullivan, jointly and severally, in the amount of $1,500.00, payable to Gregory, by and through Gregory’s counsel, specially appearing, within thirty (30) days of notice of the Court’s order, unless otherwise agreed to by the parties in writing.

 

Further, the Court orders Gregory to lodge a proposed Order in conformity with the ruling on or before September 23, 2026.

MOTION

Motion to Compel Arbitration

MOVING PARTIES

Defendants Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC and Fletcher Jones Motor Cars, Inc. dba Mercedes-Benz of Beverly Hills

OPPOSING PARTY

none

 

MOTION

 

This case arises from allegations that Defendant leased a faulty vehicle to Plaintiff and failed to repair or replace it, in violation of the Song-Beverly Act. 

 

On July 7, 2026, Plaintiff Jaklin Nemanebrahim (“Plaintiff”) filed suit against Defendants Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC (“Mercedes”) and Fletcher Jones Motor Cars, Inc. dba Mercedes-Benz of Beverly Hills (“Fletcher”), alleging three causes of action for violations of the Song-Beverly Act. 

 

Defendants now move to compel Plaintiff’s claims to arbitration and to stay the action pending the outcome of arbitration proceedings. The motion is unopposed.

 

LEGAL STANDARDS – MOTION TO COMPEL ARBITRATION

 

            “[T]he advantages of arbitration include a presumptively less costly, more expeditious manner of resolving disputes.  It follows a party to a valid arbitration agreement has a contractual right to have its dispute with another party to the contract resolved quickly and inexpensively.”  (Henry v. Alcove Investment, Inc. (1991) 233 Cal.App.3d 94, 99–100 [cleaned up].)  Thus, “on petition of a party to an arbitration agreement alleging the existence of a written agreement to arbitrate a controversy and that a party to the agreement refuses to arbitrate that controversy, the court shall order the petitioner and the respondent to arbitrate the controversy if it determines that an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2; see also

EFund Capital Partners v. Pless (2007) 150 Cal.App.4th 1311, 1320 [the language in section 1281.2 compelling arbitration is mandatory].) The right to compel arbitration exists unless the court finds that the right has been waived by a party’s conduct, other grounds exist for revocation of the agreement, or where a pending court action arising out of the same transaction creates the possibility of conflicting rulings on a common issue of law or fact.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2, subds. (a)-(c).)   

 

            “On a petition to compel arbitration, the trial court must first determine whether an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists.  Because the existence of the agreement is a statutory prerequisite to granting the petition, the petitioner bears the burden of proving its existence by a preponderance of the evidence.  The party seeking arbitration can meet its initial burden by attaching to the petition a copy of the arbitration agreement purporting to bear the respondent's signature.”  (Bannister v. Marinidence Opco, LLC (2021) 64 Cal.App.5th 541, 543-544 [cleaned up].)  The party seeking to compel arbitration must also “plead and prove a prior demand for arbitration and a refusal to arbitrate under the agreement.”  (Mansouri v. Superior Court (2010) 181 Cal.App.4th 633, 640-641.) 

 

            And while the moving party on a motion to compel arbitration “bears the burden of proving the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by a preponderance of the evidence, [a] party opposing the petition bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence any fact necessary to its defense.  The trial court sits as the trier of fact, weighing all the affidavits, declarations, and other documentary evidence, and any oral testimony the court may receive at its discretion, to reach a final determination.”  (Ruiz v. Moss Bros. Auto Group, Inc. (2014) 232 Cal.App.4th 836, 842 [cleaned up]; see also Pinnacle Museum Tower Assn. v. Pinnacle Market Development (US), LLC (2012) 55 Cal.4th 223, 236 [“The party seeking arbitration bears the burden of proving the existence of an arbitration agreement, and the party opposing arbitration bears the burden of proving any defense, such as unconscionability”].) 

 

ANALYSIS

 

In support of the motion, Defendants advance the Lease Agreement between Plaintiff as Lessee and “Mercedes Benz of Beverly Hills” as Lessor.  The Lease Agreement defines “we,” “us,” and “our,” as referring to Mercedes Benz of Beverly Hills as lessor and, after the lease is assigned, to Mercedes-Benz Vehicle Trust, or its successors and assigns.  (Ex. 1 to Allison Decl. at p. 1.)  Page 4 of the Lease contains the following arbitration provision:

 

Any claim or dispute, whether in contract, tort or otherwise (including any dispute over the interpretation, scope, or validity of this lease, arbitration section or the arbitrability of any sue), between you and us or any of Our employees, agents, successors, assigns, or the vehicle distributor, including Mercedes Benz USA LLC (each a “Third Party Beneficiary”), which arises out of or relates to a credit application, this lease, or any resulting transaction or relationship arising out of this lease (including any such relationship with third parties who do not sign this contract) shall, at the election of either you, us, or a Third Party Beneficiary, be resolved by a neutral, binding arbitration and not by a court action. Any claim or dispute is to be arbitrated on an individual basis and not as a class action. The arbitration shall be administered by the American Arbitration Association, or by any other organization that you may choose, subject to our or a Third Party Beneficiary’s approval.  You may get a copy of the rules of the American Arbitration Association by visiting its website at www.adr.org.    

 

(Ex. 1 to Allison Decl. at p. 4 [emphasis added].)

 

            Thus, Defendants have has demonstrated the existence of a valid and binding agreement to arbitrate which Fletcher has standing to compel as a signatory and Mercedes has standing to compel as an expressly-named Third Party Beneficiary of the Lease Agreement.
