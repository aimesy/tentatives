      I.          Background

 

The instant case arises from alleged personal injuries and damages sustained by Plaintiffs Bianca Urrutia, Anthony Casas, Anthony Casas, Jr., and Serenity Casas (collectively, “Plaintiffs”) after staying at the Best Western Plus Desert Poppy Inn on September 28, 2021, that is owned, operated, and managed by Defendant Bains Hospitality, LLC (“Defendant”), and located at 2038 W. Ave I, Lancaster, CA 93536 (“Motel”).

 

On August 28, 2025, Defendant filed a Demurrer to the First Amended Complaint with Motion to Strike, specifically demurring to the first, third, fourth, fifth, sixth, and seventh causes of action for failure to sufficiently plead facts to sustain the claims. The Court sustained the Demurrer with leave to amend and granted the Motion to Strike as to the punitive damages demand and denied otherwise on November 18, 2025.

 

On December 22, 2025, Plaintiffs filed their Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”) against Defendants Bains Hospitality LLC, Best Western Plus Desert Poppy Inn, Manny Bains, and Does 1 through 20, alleging seven causes of action: (1) battery; (2) negligence; (3) intentional infliction of emotional distress; (4) fraudulent concealment; (5) private nuisance; (6) public nuisance; and (7) breach of contract. 

 

On February 18, 2026, Defendant filed the operative Demurrer to the SAC with Motion to Strike, specifically demurring to the first, third, fourth, fifth, sixth, and seventh causes of action for failure to sufficiently plead facts to sustain the claims.

 

No opposition has been filed.[1]

 

   II.          Analysis

 

Standard for Demurrer –The grounds for a demurrer must appear on the face of the pleading or from judicially noticeable matters.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.30, subd. (a); Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)  Concerning the legal sufficiency of a pleading, the sole issue on demurrer is whether the facts pleaded, if true, state a valid cause of action – i.e., if the complaint pleaded facts that would entitle the plaintiff to relief.  (LiMandri v. Judkins (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 326, 339.)

 

A general demurrer admits the truth of all factual, material allegations properly pled in the challenged pleading, regardless of possible difficulties of proof.  (Blank, supra, 39 Cal.3d at p. 318.)  Thus, no matter how unlikely or improbable, plaintiff’s allegations must be accepted as true for the purpose of ruling on the demurrer.  (Del E. Webb Corp. v. Structural Materials Co. (1981) 123 Cal.App.3d 593, 604.)  Nevertheless, this rule does not apply to allegations expressing mere conclusions of law, or allegations contradicted by the exhibits to the complaint or by matters of which judicial notice may be taken.  (Vance v. Villa Park Mobilehome Estates (1995) 36 Cal.App.4th 698, 709.)  A general demurrer does not admit contentions, deductions, or conclusions of fact or law alleged in the complaint; facts impossible in law; or allegations contrary to facts of which a court may take judicial notice.  (Blank, supra, 39 Cal.3d at p. 318.)

 

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (e), the party against whom a complaint has been filed may object by demurrer to the pleading on the grounds that the pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action.  It is an abuse of discretion to sustain a demurrer if there is a reasonable probability that the defect can be cured by amendment.  (Schifando v. City of Los Angeles (2003) 31 Cal.4th 1074, 1082, as modified (Dec. 23, 2003).)

 

The Court may, upon a motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a).) The Court may also strike all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the Court. (Id., § 436, subd. (b).) The grounds for a motion to strike are that the pleading has irrelevant, false or improper matter, or has not been drawn or filed in conformity with laws. (Id., § 436.) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (Id., § 437.)

 

“A failure to oppose a motion may be deemed a consent to the granting of the motion.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 8.54(c).)

 

When a demurrer is sustained or a motion to strike is granted, the Court determines whether there is a reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment. (Blank, supra, 39 Cal.3d at p. 318.) When a plaintiff “has pleaded the general set of facts upon which his cause of action is based,” the court should give the plaintiff an opportunity to amend his complaint, since plaintiff should not “be deprived of his right to maintain his action on the ground that his pleadings were defective for lack of particulars.” (Reed v. Norman (1957) 152 Cal.App.2d 892, 900.)

First Cause of Action (Battery) –

Defendant demurs to the first cause of action on the grounds that the SAC fails to state sufficient facts to sustain a claim for battery. Defendant specifically argues that the SAC does not allege facts establishing intentionality.

 

“The essential elements of a cause of action for battery are: (1) defendant touched plaintiff, or caused plaintiff to be touched, with the intent to harm or offend plaintiff; (2) plaintiff did not consent to the touching; (3) plaintiff was harmed or offended by defendant’s conduct; and (4) a reasonable person in plaintiff’s position would have been offended by the touching.”  (So v. Shin (2013) 212 Cal.App.4th 652, 668-69.)

 

The Court sustained the demurrer to the FAC as to this cause of action because it found Defendant’s failure to identify an infestation of bedbugs did not rise to the level of intentionality required, and Plaintiffs failed to allege any facts showing that Defendant actually knew of the alleged infestation other than making conclusory allegations.

 

In the SAC, Plaintiffs contend that Defendants “intentionally subjected Plaintiffs to harmful and offensive bodily contact by affirmatively assigning Plaintiffs to and renting a guest room at the [motel] despite knowledge of bedbug complaints and bedbug conditions at the property, with knowledge that bedbug bites were substantially certain to occur as a result of that assignment.” (SAC ¶ 50.) Plaintiffs allege that Defendant was aware of bedbug infestations at the motel both prior to and on the date of the alleged incident, pointing to public records. (SAC ¶¶ 43, 48.)

 

Second, Plaintiffs argue that Defendants’ prior knowledge of bedbug complaints and conditions at the motel, their failure to warn Plaintiffs of the possibility of bedbugs, and their knowledge that harmful or offensive contact was substantially certain to occur satisfy the intent element. (SAC ¶¶ 51, 54.) Plaintiffs argue that Defendants’ failure to “properly and thoroughly inspect and clean the room assigned to Plaintiffs to ensure it was free from bedbugs prior to renting the room,” “to adequately treat and eliminate bedbug conditions,” “to implement reasonable inspection protocols to detect and prevent bedbug infestations,” “to properly train housekeeping and maintenance staff regarding bedbug detection and remediation,” to warn Plaintiffs, and the assignment to a room despite knowledge of previous complaints give rise to offensive contact. (SAC ¶ 53.)

 

Lastly, Plaintiffs allege that they did not consent to the offensive contact, as evidenced by their complaints to the motel staff, and a reasonable person would not consent to being bitten by bedbugs. (SAC ¶ 58.)

 

Defendant contends that the SAC does not sufficiently plead material facts establishing Defendant’s “intent to do the harmful act.” (Mot., at p. 2.)  Upon review of the SAC, the Court agrees. For battery, “’[t]he pivotal question [as to mens rea] is whether the defendant intended to commit an act likely to result in such physical force.’” (People v. Thurston (1999) 71 Cal.App.4th 1050, 1054, quoting People v. Colantuono (1994) 7 Cal.4th 206, 217-218.) Plaintiffs have failed to plead facts establishing that Defendant or its employees caused Plaintiffs to be touched with the intent to harm or offend Plaintiffs. The SAC maintains similar allegations as the FAC and fails to provide further facts to step over the bar for the intent element. Plaintiffs also fail to support their conclusory assertion that Defendant or its employees knew that their specific room was infested with bedbugs. Plaintiffs allege that public records from the Department of Public Health for the County of Los Angeles relating to the motel reflect that complaints had been filed concerning the presence of bedbugs in 2021 and 2022 (SAC ¶ 48), but the allegation fails to state whether these complaints resulted in Defendant’s agents and/or employees having actual knowledge of an infestation in Plaintiff’s room prior to their stay. Without this knowledge, an alleged failure to protect Plaintiffs from bed bugs does not constitute a battery under California law.

 

Accordingly, the Court SUSTAINS the demurrer as to the first cause of action.

Third Cause of Action (Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress) –

Defendant demurs to the third cause of action on the grounds that the SAC fails to state sufficient facts to sustain a claim for intentional infliction of emotional distress. Defendant specifically argues that the SAC does not allege facts establishing that Defendant engaged in “extreme and outrageous conduct” with an intent to cause emotional distress.

 

“The elements of a prima facie case for the tort of intentional infliction of emotional distress are: (1) extreme and outrageous conduct by the defendant with the intention of causing, or reckless disregard of the probability of causing, emotional distress; (2) the plaintiff’s suffering severe or extreme emotional distress; and (3) actual and proximate causation of the emotional distress by the defendant’s outrageous conduct. Conduct to be outrageous must be so extreme as to exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated in a civilized community.”  (Wilson v. Hynek (2012) 207 Cal.App.4th 999, 1009, cleaned up.)

 

The Court sustained the demurrer to the FAC as to this cause of action because it found Defendant’s conduct did not rise to outrageous or exceeding that of what is tolerated in a civilized community and Plaintiffs did not provide evidence showing Defendant was aware of any alleged infestation or demonstrated a conscious disregard for Plaintiffs’ wellbeing.

 

In the SAC, Plaintiffs contend that Defendants “knowingly and deliberately chose to rent and assign Plaintiffs to a guest room at the [motel] despite knowledge of bedbug complaints and bedbug conditions at the property, which conduct caused Plaintiffs physical injury and severe emotional distress. Defendants’ conduct exceeds all bounds of that usually tolerated in a civilized community, as paying hotel guests reasonably expect to be provided a safe and habitable room free from insect infestations and to be warned of known dangerous conditions.” (SAC ¶ 81.)

 

Plaintiffs argue that Defendants’ “conscious disregard of bedbug complaints and bedbug conditions at the Subject Hotel,” “intentional failure to disclose the presence or risk of bedbugs to Plaintiffs prior to or at check-in,” “affirmative decision to rent and assign Plaintiffs to a guest room despite knowledge of bedbug conditions,” “failure to take reasonable measures to inspect, remediate, or remove affected rooms from service prior to Plaintiffs’ stay,” “tolerance of practices that allowed bedbug conditions to persist within guest rooms,” “failure to implement or enforce adequate policies and procedures to protect guests from exposure to bedbugs,” and “conscious indifference to the high probability that guests assigned to affected rooms would suffer physical injury and severe emotional distress” amount to intentional, conscious, and outrageous conduct. (SAC ¶ 82.)

 

As to the second element, Plaintiffs argue that Defendants “knew or should have known that intentionally assigning hotel guests to rooms affected by bedbug conditions and concealing such conditions would result in severe emotional distress, yet consciously disregarded that risk.” (SAC ¶ 88.) Lastly, Plaintiffs allege that as a direct and proximate result of Defendant’s actions, they suffered severe emotional distress, leading to “serious and permanent injuries.” (SAC ¶ 90.)

 

Defendant contends that the SAC does not sufficiently plead material facts establishing Defendant engaged in “extreme and outrageous conduct” with an intent to cause emotional distress. (Mot., at p. 4.)  Upon review of the SAC, the Court agrees. First, Plaintiffs fail to allege any conduct by Defendant that rises to “outrageous” or “extreme.” A reasonable person would not find Defendant’s alleged failure to disclose the presence or risk of bedbugs prior to check-in as exceeding the bounds of civilized society. Plaintiffs also make conclusory allegations that Defendant was aware of an alleged infestation in their room based on public records as mentioned above without providing details about the complaints, if they were made prior to this incident, or if Defendant or its employees were aware of said complaints. Thus, Plaintiffs also fail to allege any extreme or outrageous conduct by Defendant caused Plaintiffs’ emotional distress. Plaintiffs also do not plead with specificity what the resulting emotional distress is other than a general allegation of severe distress that has caused serious injuries. And as mentioned above, the SAC maintains similar allegations as the FAC and fails to provide further facts to step over the bar for the intent element.

 

Accordingly, the Court SUSTAINS the demurrer as to the third cause of action.

Fourth Cause of Action (Fraudulent Concealment) –

Defendant demurs to the fourth cause of action on the grounds that the SAC fails to state sufficient facts to sustain a claim for fraudulent concealment. Defendant specifically argues that the SAC does not allege facts with the requisite specificity establishing that Defendant intentionally concealed a material fact and/or intended to defraud Plaintiffs.

 

“The elements of fraud are (a) a misrepresentation (false representation, concealment, or nondisclosure); (b) scienter or knowledge of its falsity; (c) intent to induce reliance; (d) justifiable reliance; and (e) resulting damage.” (Hinesley v. Oakshade Town Ctr. (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 289, 294.) The facts constituting the alleged fraud must be alleged factually and specifically as to every element of fraud, as the policy of “liberal construction” of the pleadings will not ordinarily be invoked. (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 645.) To properly allege fraud against a corporation, the plaintiffs must plead the names of the persons allegedly making the false representations, their authority to speak, to whom they spoke, what they said or wrote, and when it was said or written. (Tarmann v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. (1991) 2 Cal.App.4th 153, 157.)

 

The Court sustained the demurrer to the FAC as to this cause of action because it found Plaintiff has not sufficiently pled that Defendants knew of any alleged infestation and such there cannot be intent.

 

In the SAC, Plaintiffs contend that Defendant possessed superior knowledge regarding the existence of bedbugs, which was material to Plaintiffs’ decision to rent the room, and thus had a duty to disclose. (SAC ¶¶ 94-95, 99.) Second, Plaintiffs argue that Defendant “intentionally concealed and failed to disclose the existence of bedbug conditions at the time of check-in and room assignment, with the intent to induce Plaintiffs to rent and occupy the guest room and to obtain payment for their stay.” (SAC ¶ 100.) Third, Plaintiffs argue that they could not have reasonably known about the bedbugs and reasonably relied on Defendants to disclose unsafe conditions. (SAC ¶ 103.) As a result, Plaintiffs suffered physical and psychological injuries. (SAC ¶ 105.)

 

The Court notes the following changes between the Compliant, FAC, and SAC:

·       Complaint Paragraph 94 [“As evidenced by prior similar incidents and reviews posted on various online platforms by prior guests and invitees, Defendants, and DOES 1 through 20, inclusive, were aware of the presence of the infestation of bedbugs, and the like, to be present in the rooms at their Subject Hotel.”] is not in either the FAC or SAC

·       FAC Paragraph 96 [“Defendants, and DOES 1 through 20, inclusive, had knowledge and were on notice of the existing bedbug infestation at the Subject Hotel as evidenced by the extensive online reviews by other prior guests which indicates presence of said infestation and conditions present at the Subject Hotel. Given this existing knowledge of the unsanitary conditions and infestation, Defendants, and DOES 1 through 20, inclusive, failed to inform Plaintiffs of said conditions existing in the room to which they were assigned. Defendants, and DOES 1 through 20, inclusive, failed to make any representations to the Plaintiffs regarding this infestation present at the Subject Hotel. In fact, Plaintiffs were unaware of the said conditions until after being after being assigned to the room at the Subject Hotel.”] was removed from the SAC.

·       SAC Paragraph 98 [“Any general or anecdotal information that may have existed in publicly available online reviews did not disclose the specific, material facts known to Defendants, including the existence of bedbug conditions affecting guest rooms, and did not place Plaintiffs on notice of the concealed conditions in the exact room assigned to them.”] is not found in the FAC or Complaint.

 

"Under the sham-pleading doctrine, admissions in an original complaint that has been superseded by an amended pleading remain within the court's cognizance and the alteration of such statements by amendment designed to conceal fundamental vulnerabilities in a plaintiff's case will not be accepted." (Lockton v. O'Rourke (2010) 184 Cal.App.4th 1051, 1061) As the Court stated in the ruling on the demurrer to the FAC where this issue was once again noted, while inconsistent theories of recovery are permitted, a pleader cannot blow hot and cold as to the facts positively stated, regardless of whether the pleading is verified. (Manti v. Gunari (1970) 5 Cal. App. 3d 442, 449, 450) In reference to the antagonistic allegations in the single cause of action the court is required to take as true the averments in the complaint which bear most strongly against the pleader. (Manti, supra, 5 Cal. App. 3d at 450.) In other words, “the general rule [. . .] is that material factual allegations in a verified pleading that are omitted in a subsequent amended pleading without adequate explanation will be considered by the court in ruling on a demurrer to the later pleading.” (Shoemaker v. Myers (1990) 52 Cal.3d 1, 12.)

 

Just as was found in the previous order, the Court believes that the statement that “Plaintiffs did not know, and could not reasonably have known, of the concealed bedbug conditions at the time of check-in and reasonably relied on Defendants to disclose any known conditions rendering the room unsafe or uninhabitable[]” contradicts the facts alleged in the Complaint, the FAC, as well as the current SAC. (SAC ¶ 103.) Per Plaintiffs’ own allegations, the online reviews provided Plaintiffs with a way of knowing that there was an alleged bedbug issue prior to renting the hotel room. (SAC ¶ 27.)

 

The Court next turns to the arguments in the Demurrer.

 

 

 

Defendant contends that the SAC does not sufficiently plead material facts with the requisite specificity establishing that Defendant intentionally concealed a material fact and/or intended to defraud Plaintiffs. (Mot., at pgs. 4-6.)  Upon review of the SAC, the Court agrees. Plaintiff does not allege with particularity or specificity any false statements Defendant or Defendant’s employees made to Plaintiff or when these false statements were made or by whom. Plaintiffs make general allegations about a duty to disclose the alleged bedbug conditions by the front desk personnel and hotel management when checking in. As has been established above, however, Plaintiffs have not sufficiently pled that Defendants knew of any alleged infestation. Thus, due to the lack of sufficiently pled facts regarding the alleged misrepresentation, Plaintiffs do not sufficiently plead the statements were false or that Defendant and its employees knew the misrepresentations were false and intended to defraud or induce Plaintiffs’ reliance. Plaintiffs also do not plead with specificity regarding the resulting damages other than a general allegation about physical and psychological injuries.

 

Accordingly, the Court SUSTAINS the demurrer as to the fourth cause of action.

 

The Court also notes that Plaintiffs watered down the mentions to online reviews and alleged more general claims "Under the sham-pleading doctrine, admissions in an original complaint that has been superseded by an amended pleading remain within the court's cognizance and the alteration of such statements by amendment designed to conceal fundamental vulnerabilities in a plaintiff's case will not be accepted." (Lockton v. O'Rourke (2010) 184 Cal.App.4th 1051, 1061)

Fifth and Sixth Causes of Action (Private & Public Nuisance) –

Defendant demurs to the fifth and sixth causes of action on the grounds that the SAC fails to state sufficient facts to sustain a claim for private and public nuisance. Defendant specifically argues that the SAC does not allege material facts establishing that Defendant specifically and unreasonably caused Plaintiffs to suffer substantial harm.

 

Pursuant to Civil Code section 3480, “[a] public nuisance is one which affects at the same time an entire community or neighborhood, or any considerable number of persons, although the extent of the annoyance or damage inflicted upon individuals may be unequal.” “A private person may maintain an action for a public nuisance, if it is specially injurious to himself, but not otherwise.” (Civ. Code § 3493.) “Every nuisance not included in the definition of the last section is private.” (Civ. Code § 3481.)

 

To establish an action for private nuisance, (1) “the plaintiff must prove an interference with his use and enjoyment of his property”; (2) “the invasion of the plaintiff’s interest in the use and enjoyment of the land must be substantial, that is, that it causes the plaintiff to suffer substantial actual damage”; (3) “the interference with the protected interest must not only be substantial, but it must also be unreasonable, i.e., it must be of such a nature, duration, or amount as to constitute unreasonable interference with the use and enjoyment of the land.”  (Mendez v. Rancho Valencia Resort Partners, LLC (2016) 3 Cal.App.5th 248, 262-263, cleaned up.) Public nuisances are “substantial and unreasonable” “offenses against, or interferences with, the exercise of rights common to the public.”  (County of Santa Clara v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2006) 137 Cal.App.4th 292, 305.)

 

The Court sustained the demurrer to the FAC as to the public nuisance cause of action because it found that Plaintiffs failed to present any precedence of the infestation spreading from their room to the community at large, especially at the same point in time. As to the private nuisance claim, the Court found that because the underlying facts for both the Negligence and Nuisance claims are the same, bedbugs, under California precedent the nuisance is a negligence claim.

 

In the SAC, Plaintiffs contend that the presence of bedbugs constitutes a nuisance pursuant to Civil Code section 3479, et seq., because the conditions “were injurious to Plaintiffs’ health and safety, offensive to Plaintiffs’ senses, and substantially interfered with Plaintiffs’ comfortable enjoyment and use of the guest room.” (SAC ¶¶ 110, 112.) Plaintiffs argue that Defendant negligently and intentionally caused and maintained unsanitary and uninhabitable conditions at the motel, interfering with Plaintiffs’ right to safely occupy and enjoy the motel room and making the room unfit for its intended purpose. (SAC ¶¶ 113, 122.) As a result, Plaintiffs suffered damages, emotional distress, and property damage. (SAC ¶ 116.)

 

Specifically, as to the public nuisance claim, Plaintiffs argue that Defendant owned, operated, and maintained the motel as a “public accommodation,” the nuisance affected a considerable number of people due to the contagious nature of bedbugs and the transitory nature of motel guests, and  Plaintiffs suffered special injury because they were exposed to bedbug bites. (SAC ¶¶ 119, 123, & 125.)

 

Defendant contends that the SAC does not allege material facts establishing that Defendant specifically and unreasonably caused Plaintiffs to suffer substantial harm. (Mot., at pgs. 6-7.)  Upon review of the SAC, the Court agrees. First, “[a]lthough ‘any interest sufficient to be dignified as a property right’ will support an action based on a private nuisance, and this includes within its purview a tenancy for a term, such right does not inure in favor of a licensee, lodger or employee. [citation omitted]” (Venuto v. Owens-Corning Fiberglas Corp. (1971) 22 Cal.App.3d 116, 125.) A regular motel guest (not a long-stay one) is usually a licensee; Plaintiffs have not established a sufficient property interest. Plaintiffs specifically allege that they entered into a lodging contract. (SAC ¶ 129.) Also, as noted in this Court’s previous tentative, under California case precedent there cannot be separate claims for negligence and private nuisance when they rely on the same facts; where both causes of action are brought in reliance on the same facts, the nuisance is a negligence claim. (El Escorial Owners’ Assn. v. DLC Plastering, Inc. (2007) 154 Cal.App.4th 1337, 1349.) Here, the underlying facts for both the Negligence and Private Nuisance claims are the same (i.e., bedbugs).

 

Second, as to public nuisance, while Plaintiffs allege that they suffered a special injury (bedbug bites), they have not sufficiently established on the SAC’s face that the bedbug condition affected a considerable number of people and that their injury was qualitatively different from the general risk the general public would face from a bedbug infestation. In addition, while Plaintiffs state that there is a potential to spread infestation from their room, throughout the hotel, and to the community at large, they fail to present any precedence of this occurring in their pleadings other than conclusory statements or that it affected the community at large at the same point in time.

 

Accordingly, the Court SUSTAINS the demurrer as to the fifth and sixth causes of action.

Seventh Cause of Action (Breach of Contract) –

Defendant demurs to the seventh cause of action on the grounds that the SAC fails to state sufficient facts to sustain a claim for breach of contract. Defendant specifically argues that the SAC a most has pled the existence of a contract and Plaintiffs’ performance, but does not allege material facts establishing that Defendant breached the written contract for the hotel room.

 

To state a cause of action for breach of contract, Plaintiffs must be able to establish “(1) the existence of the contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance, (3) defendant’s breach, and (4) the resulting damages to the plaintiff.”  (Oasis West Realty, LLC v. Goldman (2011) 51 Cal.4th 811, 821.)

 

If a breach of contract claim “is based on alleged breach of a written contract, the terms must be set out verbatim in the body of the complaint or a copy of the written agreement must be attached and incorporated by reference.”  (Harris v. Rudin, Richman & Appel (1999) 74 Cal.App.4th 299, 307.)  In some circumstances, a plaintiff may also “plead the legal effect of the contract rather than its precise language.”  (Construction Protective Services, Inc. v. TIG Specialty Ins. Co. (2002) 29 Cal.4th 189, 198-99.)

 

The Court sustained the demurrer to the FAC as to this cause of action because it found Plaintiff has not attached a copy of the written contract or provided the terms, instead broadly presenting satisfaction of the elements.

 

 

In the SAC, Plaintiffs contend that the parties entered into a lodging contract that included “implied-in-fact terms arising from the nature of the transaction and Defendants’ operation of a hotel open to the public, including the provision of a clean, sanitary, and habitable guest room fit for overnight lodging, and a room free from dangerous and unsanitary conditions, including bedbug conditions, at the time Plaintiffs occupied the room.” (SAC ¶¶ 129-130.) Second, Plaintiffs state that they performed all material obligations, like paying for the room and complying with hotel policies. (SAC ¶ 131.) Third, Plaintiffs argue that Defendant breached the contract by failing to provide a room that complied with the implied-in-fact terms, like providing a sanitary and inhabitable room. (SAC ¶ 132.) As a result, Plaintiffs suffered damages, like the loss of the benefit of the bargain, out-of-pocket expenses, and personal property damage. (SAC ¶ 133.)

 

Defendant contends that the SAC does not allege material facts establishing that Defendant breached the written contract for the hotel room. (Mot., at pgs. 7-8.)  Upon review of the SAC, the Court agrees. Plaintiffs do not attach the written contract to the SAC, nor does the SAC provide the terms. Plaintiffs only provide general allegations of implied-in-fact terms arising from a hotel’s general transactional nature and state Defendant breached these terms “by providing a room that was unsanitary, uninhabitable, and affected by bedbug conditions.” (SAC ¶ 132.) Thus, it is not clear on the face of the SAC whether the Defendant breached the contract because the terms are uncertain.

 

Accordingly, the Court SUSTAINS the demurrer as to the seventh cause of action.

First, the Court notes that Defendant incorrectly identifies Paragraph 26 in the first bullet, when the referenced language is in Paragraph 27. This immaterial mistake is likely a Scrivener’s error, so the Court turns to the merits.

 

Based on the Court’s analysis above in the demurrer, Plaintiffs have not alleged facts showing that there has been willful disregard for Plaintiffs, despicable conduct subjecting one to cruelty, or intentional deceit. The Court sustained the demurrer as to the underlying causes of action. Therefore, the demand for punitive damages is improper.

 

Accordingly, the Motion to Strike is GRANTED in part as to the demands for punitive damages and otherwise DENIED.

      I.          Background

 

The instant Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act (“Song Beverly”) case arises from alleged nonconformities and defects to warranty in Plaintiff’s 2017 Ram 1500, (hereafter "Vehicle"), which was manufactured and/or distributed by Defendant FCA US, LLC (“FCA”).

 

On January 12, 2024, Plaintiff filed their Complaint against FCA and Does 1 through 10, alleging four causes of action: (1) violation of Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (d); (2) violation of Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (b); (3) violation of Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (a)(3); and (4) breach of the implied warranty of merchantability.

 

On February 13, 2024, FCA filed its answer.

 

On January 21, 2026, the Court held the initial Final Status Conference. As pre-trial documents were not completed, the Court continued the Final Status Conference to the date of the Jury Trial, January 30, 2026. Counsel for both parties appeared at the January 21, 2026 FSC.

 

At the continued FSC and Jury Trial of January 30, 2026, counsel for both parties appeared, but counsel for FSC stated that trial counsel was not available due to engagement in another trial. The Court continued the FSC and Jury Trial to February 6, 2026, advising the parties that trial would begin on February 6. Farshid John Fakourfar appeared for Plaintiff.

 

On February 6, 2026, the matter was called for the continued final status conference and the first day of jury trial. Counsel for Defendant appeared, but neither Plaintiff nor Plaintiff’s counsel appeared. Upon the motion of Defendant, the Court dismissed Plaintiff's complaint without prejudice pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 581(b)(5).

 

On March 2, 2026, Plaintiff filed the operative motion to set aside dismissal. 

 

On June 23, 2026, FCA filed an opposition.

 

No reply has been filed.

 

   II.          Analysis

 

Standard for Motion to Set Aside – Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b) contains two distinct provisions: one discretionary and one mandatory. Under the discretionary provision, the court “may, upon any terms as may be just, relieve a party or the party’s legal representative from a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against the party through the party’s mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect,” provided the application is made within a reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months. Under the mandatory provision, if the trial court finds the dismissal was caused by the attorney’s mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect, it must vacate any “resulting default judgment or dismissal entered against the attorney’s client…whenever an application for relief is made no more than six months after entry of judgment, is in proper form, and is accompanied by an attorney’s sworn affidavit attesting to the attorney’s mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect.” The mandatory provision, however, has been construed narrowly: it reaches only those dismissals that are the procedural equivalent of a default. (Noceti v. Whorton (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 1062, 1065–1066.)

 

-----

Discussion

 

Plaintiff’s Counsel’s Mistake, Inadvertence, Error, or Excusable Neglect –

Plaintiff argues that the mandatory provision under Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b), applies here to set aside dismissal, because Plaintiff’s counsel failed to appear at the jury trial because of mistake, inadvertence, error, or excusable neglect. Plaintiff alleges he will be severely prejudiced if the dismissal is not set aside. (Mot., at p. 5.)

 

Plaintiff’s counsel states that “[t]hrough miscommunication, inadvertence, mistake, and error, SLP’s scheduling department did not reschedule the continued FSC to February 06, 2026, for attorney Fakourfar to appear.” (Fakourfar Decl., ¶ 4.)  Thus, Plaintiff’s counsel did not appear at the hearing. (Fakourfar Decl., ¶ 5.)

 

In opposition, FCA argues that the mandatory provision does not apply here because Plaintiff did not default or abandon the case. (Opp., at pg. 8.) More importantly, counsel was aware of the February 6, 2026 trial date because he appeared at the January 30, 2026 Final Status Conference during which the Court continued the matter. (Id.) FCA contends that the mandatory provision is limited to dismissals that are procedurally equivalent to a default—that is, situations in which a party effectively abandons the litigation by failing to participate at all—citing Comunidad en Accion v. Los Angeles City Council (2013) 219 Cal.App.4th 1116, 1132, Gee v. Estate of Jewett (2017) 6 Cal.App.5th 477, 493–494, and Pagarigan v. Aetna U.S. Healthcare of California, Inc. (2007) 158 Cal.App.4th 38, 45. (Opp., at pp. 7–8.) FCA argues that Pagarigan held the mandatory provision inapplicable where a dismissal results from counsel’s failure to appear at a required proceeding, because such conduct is not equivalent to a defendant’s failure to respond to a complaint. (Opp., at p. 8, citing Pagarigan, supra, 158 Cal.App.4th at p. 45.)

 

FCA also argues that even under the discretionary provision, the conduct does not equate to “excusable neglect.” First, FCA argues that the neglect is not an insolated mistake, but rather a common occurrence with Plaintiff’s counsel in various other matters, and counsel’s appearance at the January 30, 2026 FSC during which the Court continued the matter to February 6, 2026 means the failure to appear was inexcusable neglect. (Opp., at pg. 9.) Second, FCA argues that a reasonably prudent person would not have made the same mistake would have made sure the February 6, 2026 hearing was calendared after the January 30 FSC hearing. (Opp., at pg. 10.) Third, FCA contends that counsel was not otherwise diligent because the “record reflects multiple continuances of the FSC, including continuances attributable to the conduct or circumstances of Plaintiff’s own counsel’s firm.” (Id.)

 

Fourth, FCA argues that the motion is procedurally deficient because Plaintiff has failed to file a proposed pleading or answer, even though FCA acknowledges that this case did not arise from a pleading default. (Opp., at pg. 11.) The motion also references Rabiya Tirmizi’s declaration, but the declaration is submitted by Farshid John Fakourfar. (Id.) Lastly, FCA contends that the balancing of interests weighs against granting because FCA has been defending this action for over two years, and the dismissal “brought a legitimate and appropriate end to proceedings that Plaintiff’s counsel had repeatedly failed to advance in a timely and organized manner.” (Id.) Granting would “reward Plaintiff’s lack of diligence while imposing additional and unnecessary litigation burdens on Defendant.” (Id.)

 

Although Plaintiff’s memorandum recites the standards governing both forms of relief, its argument invokes only the mandatory provision, contending that “[t]he mandatory provisions to grant relief and set aside the dismissal applies in this case.” (Mot., at p. 5.) Out of an abundance of caution, the Court addresses both the mandatory and the discretionary provisions.

 

The mandatory provision does not apply here. As numerous  authorities explain, a “dismissal” within the meaning of the mandatory provision is one that is the procedural equivalent of a default—i.e., one entered because the plaintiff’s attorney failed to respond to or oppose a dismissal motion. (Peltier v. McCloud River R.R. Co. (1995) 34 Cal.App.4th 1809; Bernasconi Commercial Real Estate v. St. Joseph’s Regional Healthcare System (1997) 57 Cal.App.4th 1078; Pagarigan v. Aetna U.S. Healthcare of California, Inc. (2007) 158 Cal.App.4th 38, 45.) Noceti v. Whorton (2014) 224 Cal. App. 4th 1062 is directly on point. There,  plaintiffs failed to appear for trial because their attorney miscalendared the trial date; the trial court rendered judgment in favor of defendant. The Court of Appeal held that the judgment following the failure of plaintiffs to appear for trial was not a “dismissal” for which mandatory relief is available. (Noceti, supra, 224 Cal.App.4th at pp. 1064–1067.)  Noceti explained the difference:

 

The issue here is whether the judgment entered against plaintiffs is tantamount to a “dismissal” under section 473(b)'s mandatory relief provision.

Several decisions, including many from this court, have interpreted narrowly the word “dismissal” in section 473(b)'s mandatory provision—as applying only when a plaintiff's attorney fails to respond to a dismissal motion. The basic reasons for this narrow construction are threefold:

First, the language of section 473(b), including the statute's structure and the dichotomous nature of the relief it affords, discretionary versus mandatory: The discretionary provision applies to “a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against” a party, while the mandatory provision applies only to a “default entered by the [court] clerk,” or a resulting “default judgment or dismissal.” Such a “default” means only a defendant's failure to answer a complaint and a “default judgment” means only a judgment entered after such failure; so, too, then, “dismissal” must be the procedural equivalent of such a default—i.e., a plaintiff failing to respond to a dismissal motion;

Second, section 473(b)'s history: The Legislature, as a matter of fairness, amended the mandatory provision of section 473(b) to add “dismissal,” so as to put plaintiffs on equal footing with defendants who are defaulted for failing to respond to an action (see Historical and Statutory Notes, 15 West's Ann.Code Civ. Proc. (2014 supp.) foll. § 473, p. 25);

And, third, the need to harmonize section 473(b)'s mandatory relief with the discretionary dismissal delay statutes (Code Civ. Proc., § 583.410 et seq.): Since nearly every discretionary dismissal—for example, failure to timely serve a complaint, or failure to timely bring a case to trial—is caused by the mistake, inadvertence or neglect of the plaintiff's attorney, a broad reading of section 473(b)'s mandatory relief provision involving dismissals would absurdly abrogate the discretionary dismissal delay statutes by implication.

 

(Noceti, 224 Cal. App. 4th 1062, 1065–66.)

 

 

The same reasoning obtains here. FCA’s trial counsel appeared on February 6, 2026, prepared to proceed with the scheduled trial (Abramova Decl., ¶ 5). Upon the request of Defendant, the Court dismissed the action was dismissed because Plaintiff did not appear when the case was called for trial. This  supposedly was the product of a calendaring failure at counsel’s firm and not because counsel failed to oppose a dismissal motion. Plaintiff’s own authority acknowledges that the mandatory provision “is limited to dismissals that are procedurally equivalent to defaults” (Mot., at p. 5), and Plaintiff’s conclusory assertion that “[t]he dismissal of this action had the effect of a default” (ibid.) is supported by no analysis and is contrary to the record. The mandatory provision is inapplicable, and therefore counsel’s affidavit of fault cannot compel relief.

 

Moreover, even if the mandatory provision could apply, neither the motion nor Fakourfar’s declaration provides enough information to sufficiently show that it was the attorney’s mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect that caused the dismissal. First, the declaration is primarily not from a first-person point of view: Paragraphs 3 & 4 reference Attorney Fakourfar rather than I, seeming to be passages copied and pasted from other declarations.  (Fakourfar Decl.) As a result, the administrative mistake is not attributed to counsel, but rather reads as miscommunication, inadvertence, mistake, and error from SLP’s scheduling department. (Fakourfar Decl., ¶ 4.) The Court further notes that the memorandum relies on “the declaration of Rabiya Tirmizi” (Mot., at p. 5), but no such declaration was filed; the motion is supported only by the declaration of attorney Fakourfar.

 

Nor is Plaintiff entitled to relief under the discretionary provision, which requires a showing that the dismissal resulted from mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect—“that neglect which might have been the act of a reasonably prudent person under the same circumstances.” (Baratti v. Baratti (1952) 109 Cal.App.2d 917, 921.) In evaluating whether counsel’s error is excusable, courts consider the nature of the mistake or neglect and whether counsel was otherwise diligent in investigating and pursuing the claim. (Bettencourt v. Los Rios Community College Dist. (1986) 42 Cal.3d 270, 276; Tackett v. City of Huntington Beach (1994) 22 Cal.App.4th 60, 65.) Here, attorney Fakourfar personally appeared at the January 30, 2026 Final Status Conference and Trial date, at which the Court continued the final status conference and trial to February 6, 2026. When the Court continued the trial on January 30 it advised the parties that the trial would proceed on the new date of February 6. The Court’s minute order of January 30 reflected the new trial date of February 6, 2026. Plaintiff’s Counsel therefore had first-hand knowledge of the February 6, 2026 trial date. A reasonably prudent attorney who personally attends the hearing at which his client’s trial is continued confirms that the new date is calendared, rather than assuming that his firm’s scheduling department will independently learn of and calendar it. Counsel’s declaration describes no calendaring system, no effort to communicate the continued date to the scheduling department, and no precaution that failed despite reasonable diligence; it states only that the scheduling department “did not reschedule” the matter for his appearance. (Fakourfar Decl., ¶ 4.) (See Hearn v. Howard (2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 1193, 1206 [neglect is not excusable where it results from counsel’s failure to follow established office procedures or to exercise reasonable diligence in supervising subordinates].) Although the law strongly favors resolution of disputes on the merits, that policy does not compel relief where, as here, “inexcusable neglect is clear.” (Elston v. City of Turlock (1985) 38 Cal.3d 227, 235.)

 

Finally, the Court notes the lack of candor in Plaintiff’s attempted showing of excusable neglect. The Fakourfar declaration repeatedly states that counsel failed to appear for the Final Status Conference of February 6, 2026. (Fakourfar Decl., ¶ ¶ 4-5.) But it wasn’t just the status conference that counsel failed to appear for, it was also, and primarily, a jury trial that counsel failed to appear for on February 6. That is why the Court granted Defendant’s request to dismiss.

 

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s Motion to Set Aside Dismissal is DENIED.

 

-----

 

 III.          Conclusion

 

Plaintiff’s Motion to Set Aside Dismissal is DENIED.

 III.          Legal Standard

Standard for Leave to Amend a Complaint – Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (a)(1) provides, in relevant part: “The court may, in furtherance of justice, and on any terms as may be proper, allow a party to amend any pleading or proceeding by adding or striking out the name of any party, or by correcting a mistake in the name of a party, or a mistake in any other respect; and may, upon like terms, enlarge the time for answer or demurrer. The court may likewise, in its discretion, after notice to the adverse party, allow, upon any terms as may be just, an amendment to any pleading or proceeding in other particulars; and may upon like terms allow an answer to be made after the time limited by this code.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (a)(1).) This discretion should be exercised liberally in favor of amendments, for “judicial policy favors resolution of all disputed matters in the same lawsuit.” (Kittredge Sports Co. v. Superior Court (1989) 213 Cal.App.3d 1045, 1047.)

“The trial court has discretion to permit or deny the amendment of the complaint, but instances justifying the court's denial of leave to amend are rare.” (Armenta ex rel. City of Burbank v. Mueller Co.¿(2006) 142¿Cal.App.4th¿636, 642.)¿ However, “[a]lthough courts are bound to apply a policy of great liberality in permitting amendments to the complaint at any stage of the proceedings, up to and including trial [Citations], this policy should be applied only ‘[w]here no prejudice is shown to the adverse party . . .’ [Citation.] A different result is indicated ‘[w]here¿inexcusable delay and probable prejudice to the opposing party’ is shown. [Citation.]” (Magpali¿v. Farmers Group, Inc.¿(1996) 48¿Cal.App.4th¿471, 487.)

Under rule 3.1324, subdivision (a) of the California Rules of Court, a motion to amend a pleading shall (1) include a copy of the proposed amendment or amended pleading, which must be serially numbered to differentiate it from previous pleadings or amendments; (2) state what allegations in the previous pleading are proposed to be deleted, if any, and where, by page, paragraph and line number, the deleted allegations are located; and (3) state what allegations are proposed to be added to the previous pleading, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the additional allegations are located. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1324(a).)

Under subdivision (b), a separate declaration must accompany the motion and must specify (1) the effect of the amendment; (2) why the amendment is necessary and proper; (3) when the facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered; and (4) the reasons why the request for amendment was not made earlier.    II.          Analysis

 

Standard for Motion to Compel Arbitration – Under California and federal law, public policy favors arbitration as an efficient and less expensive means of resolving private disputes. (Moncharsh v. Heily & Blasé (1992) 3 Cal.4th 1, 8-9 (Moncharsh); AT&T Mobility LLC v. Concepcion (2011) 563 U.S. 333, 339.) Accordingly, whether an agreement is governed by the California Arbitration Act or the Federal Arbitration Act, courts resolve doubt about an arbitration agreement’s scope in favor of arbitration. (Moncharsh, supra, 3 Cal.4th at p. 9; Comedy Club, Inc. v. Improv West Associates (9th Cir. 2009) 553 F.3d 1277, 1284; see also Engalla v. Permanente Med. Grp., Inc. (1997) 15 Cal.4th 951, 971-72 [“California law incorporates many of the basic policy objectives contained in the Federal Arbitration Act, including a presumption in favor of arbitrability [citation] and a requirement that an arbitration agreement must be enforced on the basis of state law standards that apply to contracts in general [citation].”].) “[U]nder both the FAA and California law, ‘arbitration agreements are valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, save upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract.’” (Higgins v. Superior Court (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 1238, 1247.)

 

The party seeking to compel arbitration bears the burden of proving the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by the preponderance of the evidence.  (Engalla, supra, 15 Cal.4th 951, 972.)  A petition to compel arbitration must allege both a “written agreement to arbitrate” the controversy, and that a party to that agreement “refuses to arbitrate” the controversy. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2.) It then becomes plaintiff’s burden, in opposing the motion, to prove by a preponderance of the evidence any fact necessary to her opposition. (Ibid.)

 

“Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.2 requires a trial court to grant a petition to compel arbitration ‘if the court determines that an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists.’”  (Avery v. Integrated Healthcare Holdings, Inc. (2013) 218 Cal.App.4th 50, 59 (Avery), quoting Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2).) Accordingly, “when presented with a petition to compel arbitration, the court’s first task is to determine whether the parties have in fact agreed to arbitrate the dispute.” (Id., at p. 59.) Generally, on a petition to compel arbitration, the court must grant the petition unless it finds either (1) no written agreement to arbitrate exists; (2) the right to compel arbitration has been waived; (3) grounds exist for revocation of the agreement; or (4) litigation is pending that may render the arbitration unnecessary or create conflicting rulings on common issues. (Ibid.; see also Condee v. Longwood Management Corp. (2001) 88 Cal.App.4th 215, 218-219.) “In these summary proceedings the trial court sits as a trier of fact, weighing all the affidavits, declarations, and other documentary evidence, as well as oral testimony received at the court’s discretion, to reach a final determination.” (Avery, supra, 218 Cal.App.4th at p. 59; accord Rosenthal v. Great Western Financial Securities Corp. (1996) 14 Cal.4th 394, 413.)

 

 

-----

 

Discussion

 

Existence of an Arbitration Agreement –

Mirage advances the declaration of Isaiah Navarro, the Admissions Assistant at Mirage who has familiarity with the admission process and the documents executed by residents or their representatives upon admission, including the Arbitration Provision in contention, and the facility’s practices/procedures regarding record maintenance. (Navarro Decl., ¶¶ 1-2.) I specifically recall meeting the Plaintiff and reviewing these documents with him.” (Navarro Decl., ¶ 10.) (Navarro Decl., ¶ 11.)

FAA applies & Code of Civil Procedure Section 1281.2, subdivision (c) does not bar the Petition –

Plaintiff argues that the Court should deny arbitration under Code of Civil Procedure Section 1281.2, subdivision (c) because the other named Defendants in this action are not signatories to the arbitration agreement and all of Plaintiff’s claims arise from the same transaction. (Opp., at p. 4.) Plaintiff cites to Cronus Investments, Inc. v. Concierge Services (2005) 35 Cal.4th 376, 393 & Avila v. Southern California Specialty Care, Inc. (2018) 20 Cal.App.5th 835, 844-845, but those cases are distinguishable from the facts here.

 

In Cronus, the Supreme Court explicitly held:

“Our opinion does not preclude parties to an arbitration agreement to expressly designate that any arbitration proceeding should move forward under the FAA's procedural provisions rather than under state procedural law. We simply hold that the language of the arbitration clause in this case, calling for the application of the FAA “if it would be applicable,” should not be read to preclude the application of 1281.2(c), because it does not conflict with the applicable provisions of the FAA and does not undermine or frustrate the FAA's substantive policy favoring arbitration.”

(Cronus, supra, 35 Cal.4th at p. 394.) The Supreme Court did not denounce the express contractual waiver of Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.2, subdivision (c) and the express designation for an arbitration proceeding to move forward under the FAA.

 

In Avila, the Court reiterated that “[t]he procedural aspects of the FAA do not apply in state court absent an express provision in the arbitration agreement,” which was missing in that case since the agreement did not “mention the FAA at all, although it [did] mention California law several times.” (Avila, supra, 20 Cal.App.5th at 840.)  

 

The Court of Appeals in Valencia v. Smyth (2010) 185 Cal.App.4th 153, 177 noted that “under the Rosenthal–Cronus–DIRECTV trilogy, the FAA's procedural provisions do not apply in state court unless the parties expressly adopt them. The trilogy makes clear that the question … is whether the parties expressly incorporated the FAA's procedural provisions into their agreements.” In Valencia, unlike here, California’s procedural provisions were applied because the agreement expressly incorporated California law and procedural provisions and did not expressly adopt the FAA’s procedural provisions. (Valencia, supra, 185 Cal.App.4th at pg. 178.) The Court of Appeals has reiterated multiple times that “’the FAA's procedural rules may apply if the parties expressly agree they do or if the CAA's procedural rules are preempted.’” (Wright v. WellQuest Elk Grove, LLC (2026) 119 Cal.App.5th 267, 279, quoting Quach v. California Commerce Club, Inc. (2024) 16 Cal.5th 562, 582.)

 

Here, the Agreement explicitly states in paragraph 7.2 that:

 

“As a result, the parties agree that this Agreement is to be governed by the Federal Arbitration Act (9 U.S.C. §§1-16) and the procedural rules set forth in the Federal Arbitration Act shall govern any and all arbitration proceedings, including any petition to compel arbitration.”

(Williams Decl., Exh. B, pg. 3.)

 

Paragraph 7.3 states that:

 

“The parties agree that California Code of Civil Procedure §1281.2(c) is excluded from this Agreement as the parties mutually desire to have any and all disputes submitted to binding arbitration. The parties do not want any claims or related lawsuits not subject to arbitration to impede any and all other claims from being ordered to binding arbitration.”

(Id.)

Therefore, based on the above quoted language, it is clear that the parties expressly incorporated the FAA's procedural provisions into their agreements and expressly waived Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.2, subdivision (c).

 

Accordingly, the Court finds that the FAA’s procedural provisions apply here, and Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.3, subdivision (c) does not bar because the agreement expressly waives application of the section.

Enforceability of the Delegation Clause –

Mirage argues that Article 4.1 of the Agreement expressly delegates enforceability questions, like unconscionability, to the arbitration and Plaintiff fails to challenge the provision itself in opposition; thus, as a baseline, the unconscionability argument is invalid here. (Reply., at pg. 6.)

 

Article 4.1 of the Agreement states:

 

“The arbitrator, and not any federal, state, or local court or agency, shall resolve all disputes, including without limitation, any disputes regarding the making, execution, enforceability, voidability, revocability, unconscionability, severability, scope, arbitrability, interpretation, waiver, duress, preemption or any other defense to the validity or enforceability of this Arbitration Agreement, as well as resolve the Parties’ underlying disputes, as it is the Parties’ intent to completely avoid the court system and resolve disputes without any judge or jury.”

 

(Williams Decl., Exh. B, at pg. 2.)

 

The United States Supreme Court held in Rent-A-Center, West, Inc. v. Jackson (2010) 561 U.S. 63, 72-73 that “unless [Plaintiff] challenged the delegation provision specifically, we must treat it as valid under § 2, and must enforce it under §§ 3 and 4, leaving any challenge to the validity of the Agreement as a whole for the arbitrator;” thus, Defendant’s argument that the arbitrator must decide the enforceability challenge was “uncontested” because Plaintiff only challenged the validity of the arbitration agreement as a whole, and not the delegation provision. In Malone v. Superior Court (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 1551, 1559-1560, the Court of Appeals, referencing Rent-A-Center, articulated that:

 

“when a party is claiming that an arbitration agreement is unenforceable, it is important to determine whether the party is making a specific challenge to the enforceability of the delegation clause or is simply arguing that the agreement as a whole is unenforceable. If the party's challenge is directed to the agreement as a whole--even if it applies equally to the delegation clause--the delegation clause is severed out and enforced; thus, the arbitrator, not the court, will determine whether the agreement is enforceable. In contrast, if the party is making a specific challenge to the delegation clause, the court must determine whether the delegation clause itself may be enforced (and can only delegate the general issue of enforceability to the arbitrator if it first determines the delegation clause is enforceable).

 

Here, Plaintiff has two different challenges that could be called “scope” challenges. The first is that the dispute falls outside the scope of disputes that the parties agreed to arbitrate. This also is often phrased as an arbitrability challenge, in that the dispute is not subject to arbitration. (See Opposition, at 5-6.) However phased, that challenge is plainly reserved for the arbitrator under the delegation clause. Plaintiff’s second “scope” challenge is that the “Other Claims” provision is impermissibly broad in the types of claims and possible claimants it applies to, and is thus unconscionable. (Opposition, at 6.) But the parties also agreed that questions of unconscionability also are reserved for the arbitrator. What Plaintiff has not done is challenge the delegation clause itself as unconscionable or otherwise unenforceable. Under Rent-A-Center, the delegation clause is a severable agreement from the arbitration agreement, and therefore the challenge must be directed to that agreement in particular for the court to find the delegation agreement unenforceable.

 

As the moving party has shown the existence of an agreement to delegate within an arbitration agreement, and the Plaintiff has not challenged the validity of that agreement, the delegation agreement must be enforced. Plaintiff’s challenges to the arbitrability of this dispute and to the unconscionability of the broader arbitration agreement are to be determined by the arbitrator in the first instance. Of course, should the arbitrator determine that the broader arbitration agreement is not enforceable or does not apply to this dispute, then Plaintiff’s claims would be referred back to this Court for adjudication.

 

Accordingly, the Court GRANTS the Motion to Compel Arbitration.[1]

   II.          Preliminary Procedural Issues

 

Timeliness of Clearview’s Demurrer – A responsive pleading to the complaint is due within 30 days of service of the plaintiff’s moving papers. (Code Civ. Proc., §412.20, subd. (a).) However, “[i]f the parties are not able to meet and confer at least 5 days before the date the responsive pleading is due, the demurring party shall be granted an automatic 30-day extension of time within which to file a responsive pleading, by filing and serving, on or before the date on which a demurrer would be due, a declaration stating under penalty of perjury that a good faith attempt to meet and confer was made and explaining the reasons why the parties could not meet and confer. The 30-day extension shall commence from the date the responsive pleading was previously due, and the demurring party shall not be subject to default during the period of the extension.” (Code Civ. Proc., §430.41, subd. (a)(2).)

 

Here, Clearview’s demurrer was due on or before February 11, 2026. However, on February 6, 2026, Clearview filed a declaration of demurring party in support of automatic extension, stating Clearview intends to file a demurrer, motion to strike, or motion for judgment on the pleadings, but is unable to do so prior to meeting and conferring with Plaintiff. Clearview’s counsel, Bradley T. Weeks, stated he had been unable to meet and confer despite good faith attempts, including calling Plaintiff’s counsel, David Yadidi on January 30 and February 3, 2026, and leaving a message requesting a call regarding the issues to be raised on demurrer. Weeks also stated counsel scheduled a meet and confer call for February 6, 2026 by email, but Yadidi was again unavailable when Weeks called. This was the first automatic extension in this case.

 

Thus, Clearview’s declaration in support of an automatic extension is procedurally proper, and the demurrer became due on or before March 13, 2026. Accordingly, Clearview’s demurrer is timely.

 

Meet and Confer Requirement – Before filing a demurrer, the demurring party is required to meet and confer at least five days before the date the responsive pleading is due, in person, by telephone, or by video conference, with the party who filed the pleading demurred to for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a).) The demurring party shall file and serve with the demurrer a declaration stating the means by which the parties met and conferred and that the parties did not reach an agreement to resolve the issues raised, or that the party who filed the pleading demurred to failed to respond or otherwise failed to meet and confer in good faith. (Ibid.)

 

Here, Clearview has satisfied the meet and confer requirement. Clearview’s counsel, Weeks, submitted his declaration, in which he sets forth the facts as outlined above regarding his good faith meet and confer efforts. On three dates, January 30, 2026, February 3, 2026, and February 6, 2025, Weeks called Yadidi, who was unavailable.

 

Accordingly, the Court finds the meet and confer requirement is satisfied.

 

 III.          Request for Judicial Notice

 

Clearview requests that the Court take judicial notice of seven documents: (1) Deed from Aram Movslysyan to Ashot Nikoghosyan, (2) Deed of Trust by Ashot Nikoghosyan, (3) Assignment Deed of Trust, (4) Substitution of Trustee, (5) Notice of Default, (6) Notice of Trustee’s Sale, and (7) Trustee’s Deed. (RJN, p. 2:19-27; Weeks RJN Decl., ¶¶ 4-10.)

 

California Evidence Code section 452 permits judicial notice being taken of facts and propositions not reasonably subject to dispute and capable of immediate and accurate determination by resort to sources of reasonably indisputable accuracy. (Evid. Code., § 452, subd. (h).) California Evidence Code section 453 mandates a court take judicial notice of a matter specified in section 452 if a party so requests, gives the adverse party sufficient notice through pleadings or otherwise, and furnishes the court with sufficient information to enable it to take judicial notice. (Evid. Code, § 453, subd. (a), (b).)

 

A Court may properly take judicial notice of recorded documents. (Md. Casualty Co. v. Reeder (1990) 221 Cal.App.3d 961, 977; Evans v. California Trailer Court, Inc. (1994) 28 Cal.App.4th 540, 549 (overruled in part on other grounds) [courts may take judicial notice of recorded deeds].) The Court may take judicial notice “not only of the existence and recordation of recorded documents but also of a variety of matters that can be deduced from the documents.” (Fontenot v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 256, 265 (overruled in part on other grounds).) In Poseidon Development, Inc. v. Woodland Lane Estates, LLC (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 1106, “the court affirmed the trial court’s taking of judicial notice in sustaining a demurrer of the parties, dates, and legal consequences of a series of recorded documents relating to a real estate transaction. [Citation.] Although the court recognized that it would have been improper to take judicial notice of the truth of statements of fact recited within the documents, the trial court was permitted to take judicial notice of the legal effect of the documents' language when that effect was clear. [Citation.]” (Fontenot, supra, 198 Cal.App.4th at 265.)

 

However, while the Court may take judicial notice of the legal effect of a recorded document where it is clear and not subject to reasonable dispute, the Court may not take judicial notice of factual matters stated therein. (Poseidon, supra, 152 Cal.App.4th 1106, 1117; see also Yvanova v. New Century Mortgage Corp. (2016) 62 Cal.4th 919, 943, fn. 1 [notice of recorded documents are subject to mandatory judicial notice on proper request of a party, but not of disputed or disputable facts stated therein]; Herrera v. Deutsche Bank National Trust Co (2011) 196 Cal.App.4th 1366, 1375 [judicial notice of hearsay statements subject to reasonable dispute is improper]; Panterra GP, Inc. v. Superior Court (2022) 74 Cal.App.5th 697, 709 [“[t]he hearing on demurrer may not be turned into a contested evidentiary hearing through the guise of having the court take judicial notice of documents whose truthfulness or proper interpretation are disputable.”].) Thus, the Court is permitted to take judicial notice of the existence of recorded documents, undisputable facts contained therein, and the undisputable legal effect of said documents at the demurrer stage, but may not take judicial notice of factual assertions contained therein.

 

Here, Clearview has provided to the Court exhibits one and two, both of which were attached to the operative complaint. Clearview has also provided certified copies of exhibits three through seven. All of these exhibits have been recorded with the Los Angeles County Recorder’s Office. (Weeks RJN Decl., ¶ 3.) Furthermore, a valid proof of service has been filed and Clearview has provided sufficient notice of the request to Plaintiff through its moving papers.

 

Accordingly, Clearview’s request for judicial notice is GRANTED, and the Court will consider the information contained and effects of these documents only to the extent permitted as described above. The Court preliminarily notes that the lack of an opposition does not permit the Court to assume Plaintiff’s position is an admission or concession of any fact stated therein, and any disputable facts are not subject to judicial notice.

 IV.          Legal Standard

 

Standard for Demurrer – A party against whom a complaint has been filed may object by demurrer to the pleading on the ground that the pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. (Cal. Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (e).) A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations reasonably, liberally, and in context. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

As a general matter, in a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) “A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Ibid.) Courts must “treat the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Blank, supra, 39 Cal.3d at 318.) The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action. (Hahn, supra, 147 Cal.App.4th at 747.)

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Id.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.) The threshold for meeting this burden is low, and “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245).

    V.          Discussion

 

Application – Clearview individually demurrers to Plaintiff’s third and fourth causes of action on the grounds that each fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action pursuant to California Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (e). (Statement of Demurrers, ¶ 1-2.)

 

a.     Third Cause of Action – Declaratory Relief

 

Plaintiff’s third cause of action is based on allegations that foreclosure proceedings commenced by Data Mortgage through America West were invalid and ineffective because neither had legal standing to commence foreclosure proceedings against the Property. (Compl., ¶ 33.) Specifically, Plaintiff claims that GenHome was the proper and rightful party to commence foreclosure proceedings, not Data Mortgage, and that America West was not the trustee with authority to exercise powers of the trustee named in the GenHome Trust Deed. (Ibid.) Plaintiff alleges that no substitution of trustee was filed or recorded prior to or at the time the Notice of Default was recorded. (Compl., p.4:22-25.) Plaintiff’s third cause of action seeks a judicial determination that the notices recorded by Data Mortgage through America West, and the Trustee’s Deed Upon Sale recorded by Clearview, are invalid and ineffective. (Id. at ¶ 34.)

 

Clearview argues that there is no controversy as to the third cause of action because both the beneficiary and trustee under a deed of trust may initiate foreclosure proceedings, and at the time of initiation of foreclosure proceedings, the beneficiary was Data Mortgage Inc. (Demurrer, p. 4:22-5:4.)

 

As discussed above, the Court may take judicial notice of the legal effect of a recorded document when the legal effect is not itself, and is not based upon, statements or contentions of fact therein which are reasonably subject to dispute. In support of its argument, Clearview first provides the original Trust Deed between Plaintiff as trustor, GenHome has lender, Stewart Title as trustee, and MERS as beneficiary. (Weeks RJN Decl., ¶ 5, Ex. 2, p. 1-2.) The Trust Deed is executed and notarized with Plaintiff’s signature, and Plaintiff’s complaint affirms the validity of the Trust Deed provided. (Compl., ¶ 11-13.) The Court finds that the legal effect of the Trust Deed is not subject to reasonable dispute, and as such, the legal consequences of the Trust Deed are judicially noticed to the extent that it establishes Plaintiff as trustor, GenHome has lender, Stewart Title as trustee, and MERS as beneficiary.

 

Clearview claims on July 9, 2024, MERS assigned its beneficial interest under the Trust Deed to Data Mortgage. (Weeks RJN Decl., ¶ 6, Ex. 3, p. 2.) The certified copy of the Assignment of Trust Deed establishes that it was recorded on July 9, 2024, and states, in relevant part, “FOR VALUE RECEIVED, the undersigned assignor (“Assignor”): Mortgage Electronic Registration Systems, Inc. [MERS], as beneficiary, as nominee for GenHome Mortgage Corporation, its successors and assigns, whose address is P.O. Box 2026 Flint, MI 48501-2026, does hereby grant, bargain, assign, transfer and convey to the following assignee (“Assignee”): Data Mortgage, Inc., DBA Essex Mortgage whose address is 1417 North Magnolia Road, Ocala, FL 34475. All of Assignor’s right, title, and interest under a certain Mortgage, dated August 29, 2022, made and executed by Ashot Nikoghosyan, to [MERS], as nominee for [GenHome], its successors and assigns, recorded on September 2, 2022 in Instrument Number 20220873797, Los Angeles County, California.” The Assignment is also signed and notarized by John Jones, identified as Assistant Secretary for MERS as beneficiary, as nominee for GenHome.

 

“A recorded assignment of note and deed of trust vests in the assignee all of the rights, interests of the beneficiary [citation] including authority to exercise any power of sale given the beneficiary [citation.].” (Strike v. Trans-West Discount Corp. (1979) 92 Cal.App.3d 735, 744.) The legal effects of this assignment are clear and not subject to reasonable dispute, nor based on statements or allegations of fact subject to reasonable dispute. Accordingly, the Court takes judicial notice of the date and time of recordation, the parties involved in the assignment, and the legal effect of the assignment in assigning MERS’s beneficial interest under the trust deed to Data Mortgage, effective on July 9, 2024.

 

Clearview asserts that on August 26, 2024, Data Mortgage substituted America West as trustee under the deed of trust. (Weeks RJN Decl., ¶ 7, Ex. 4, p. 4.) The certified copy of the Substitution of Trustee establishes that it was recorded on August 26, 2024, and lists MERS as the original beneficiary as nominee for GenHome, Data Mortgage as the current beneficiary, and Stewart Title as the original trustee. (Id at p. 3.) The Substitution of Trustee states, in relevant part, “The beneficiary, pursuant to the terms of and authority granted under the deed of trust, hereby substitutes America West Lender Services, LLC, whose address is 5404 Cypress Center Drive, Suite 300, Tampa, FL 33609, as successor trustee under the deed of trust with all powers of the original trustee.” (Id at p. 4.) The document is dated August 23, 2024, signed by Gwenna Sturdivant, who is identified as Assistant Secretary of Data Mortgage, and is notarized.

 

California Civil Code section 2934a, subdivision (a)(1)(A) states that a trustee under a trust deed upon real property may be substituted by the recording, in the county in which the property is located, of a substitution, executed and acknowledged by all beneficiaries under the trust, or their successors in interest, and the substitution shall be effective notwithstanding any contrary provision in any trust deed executed on or after January 1, 1968. Here, a substitution was recorded in Los Angeles County, in which the subject Property is located, and is executed by Data Mortgage, the beneficiary. The legal effects of this substitution are clear and not subject to reasonable dispute, nor based on statements or allegations of fact subject to reasonable dispute. Accordingly, the Court takes judicial notice of the date and time of recordation, the parties involved in the substitution, and the legal effect of the document in legally substituting America West as the successor trustee under the Trust Deed with all powers of the original trustee.

 

Clearview also notes the Notice of Default, which was recorded by America West on August 29, 2024. (Weeks RJN Decl., ¶ 8, Ex. 5, p.2.) The document states the date and time the Notice was recorded and states that America West “is either the original trustee, the duly appointed substitute trustee, or acting as agent for the trustee or beneficiary under a Deed of Trust dated 08/29/2022, executed by: Ashot Nikoghosyan, an unmarried man, as Trustor(s) to secure certain obligations in favor of [MERS], as beneficiary, as nominee for [GenHome] its successors and assigns as Beneficiary, recorded 09/02/2022.” (Weeks RJN Decl., ¶ 8, Ex. 5, p. 3.) The Court notes the existence of the recorded Notice, the date and time of the recordation, the parties involved, and the legal effect to the extent that it establishes that a Notice of Default was recorded by America West as trustee in favor of all assigns as beneficiary and against Plaintiff.

 

The three steps to complete a nonjudicial foreclosure of real property are, in chronology: (1) recording and service of the notice of default, providing the borrower no less than three months to reinstate the loan, (2) setting the sale date of the real property and providing notice of the sale within 90 days of the recording of default, and (3) placing the property up for auction. (Cal. Civ. Code, § 2924, subd. (a), § 2924b, § 2924g.)

 

Here, the foreclosure proceedings were initiated on or around August 29, 2024, when America West recorded the Notice of Default. On this date, the Court finds that it is undisputable that under the Trust Deed, Plaintiff was the trustor, America West was the trustee, and Data Mortgage was beneficiary. On July 11, 2025, America West issued and recorded a Notice of Trustee’s sale as required. (Weeks RJN Decl., ¶ 9, Ex. 6.) The Property was sold at auction on or around August 19, 2025, to Clearview and a Trustee’s Deed Upon Sale was issued by America West on September 9, 2025, and recorded on September 12, 2025. (Weeks RJN Decl., ¶ 9, Ex. 6; Compl., ¶ 20.)

 

Here, taking all of Plaintiff’s factual allegations in the complaint as true, it is clear that the claims are explicitly contradicted by judicially noticed recorded documents, establishing the appropriate parties that could initiate foreclosure at the time it commenced were America West as trustee and Data Mortgage as beneficiary. The legal effects of these documents, the assignment to Data Mortgage and the substitution of America West, are not subject to reasonable dispute. Furthermore, the judicially noticeable documents clearly delineate the rights and authorities to initiate foreclosure were legally transferred to Data Mortgage and America West through their respective assignment and substitution.

 

Plaintiff’s causes of action against Clearview are predicated on his claim that neither Data Mortgage nor America West had the authority to initiate foreclosure proceedings. Both a beneficiary and a substituted trustee have the authority to initiate the foreclosure process. (Cal. Civ. Code, § 2924, subd. (a)(6).) Thus, as substituted trustee, America West had the authority to initiate foreclosure proceedings on the Property, Clearview had the right to bid on the Property and subsequently purchase the Property from America West, and the claims upon which Plaintiff asserts his third cause of action for declaratory relief fail as a matter of law. Plaintiff sets forth no separate or additional allegations sufficient to maintain this cause of action for declaratory relief against Clearview as purchaser.

 

Accordingly, Clearview’s demurrer to Plaintiff’s third cause of action is SUSTAINED.

 

                                                  i.     Leave to Amend

 

As set forth above, leave to be amend must only be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment, and the plaintiff bears the burden of showing the court the pleading can be amended successfully. (Goodman, supra, 18 Cal.3d 335, 348; Lewis, supra, 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.)

 

Here, Plaintiff did not file any opposition to the present motion or provide any argument as to how successful amendment is reasonably possible. Furthermore, “[a] trial court may properly sustain a general demurrer to a declaratory relief action without leave to amend when, as here, the controversy presented can be determined as a matter of law” or when a plaintiff seeks a declaration of rights to which he is legally not entitled. (City of Fresno v. Cal. Highway Com. (1981) 118 Cal. App. 3d 687, 699; Childhelp, Inc. v. City of Los Angeles (2023) 91 Cal.App.5th 224, 236.) In Brown v. Deutsche Bank National Trust Co. (2016) 247 Cal.App.4th 275, the Court found that the trial court did not err in sustaining a demurrer without leave to amend where the plaintiff failed to sufficiently allege the company that initiated foreclosure proceedings lacked authority to do so. (Brown, supra, 247 Cal.App.4th at 280.) The Brown Court noted in its ruling that the plaintiff’s briefing failed to clearly identify the allegations relied upon in support of her claim that the initiating company lacked authority to initiate foreclosure proceedings, and that her allegations relied on to support her theory were contradicted by judicially noticed documents establishing said authority. (Id at 282.)

 

As in Brown, Plaintiff has failed to sufficiently identify the allegations relied upon to support his claim that America West did not have the authority to initiate foreclosure on the Property. Plaintiff’s claims that America West was not a trustee, that no substitution of trustee had been filed prior to initiation of proceedings, and that Data Mortgage was not a beneficiary are directly contradicted by undisputable, judicially noticeable evidence.

 

“The fundamental basis of declaratory relief is the existence of an actual, present controversy over a proper subject.” (City of Cotati v. Cashman (2002) 29 Cal.4th 69, 79.) Here, as has been established, America West had a legal right to initiate foreclosure proceedings, Plaintiff has set forth no additional arguments to support his third cause of action, and there is no actual, present controversy over a disputable subject, fact, or allegation.

 

Accordingly, Clearview’s demurrer to Plaintiff’s third cause of action is sustained without leave to amend.

 

b.     Fourth Cause of Action -- Injunction

 

Plaintiff’s fourth cause of action alleges Data Mortgage and Clearview deceitfully and fraudulently schemed to deprive Plaintiff of his right, title, and interest in the Property by posting a Notice to Quit and demanding Plaintiff and his tenants move out of the Property within three days. (Compl., ¶ 37.) Plaintiff thus requests that Defendants be enjoined or otherwise restrained from proceeding with attempts to evict Plaintiff and his tenants. (Compl., ¶ 38-39.)

 

In California, the law is clear that “[i]njunctive relief is a remedy and not, in itself, a cause of action, and a cause of action must exist before injunctive relief may be granted.” (Shell Oil Co. v. Richter (1942) 52 Cal.App.2d 164, 168.) Sustaining a demurrer as to a cause of action for injunctive relief is proper, as an injunction is not a cause of action. (Allen v. City of Sacramento (2015) 234 Cal.App.4th 41, 48, 65.)

 

Even taking the above contentions as true, Plaintiff’s fourth cause of action requests only a form of relief and does not assert an individual cause of action.

 

Accordingly, Clearview’s demurrer to Plaintiff’s fourth cause of action is SUSTAINED.

 

                                                  i.     Leave to Amend

 

As set forth above, leave to be amend must only be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment, and the plaintiff bears the burden of showing the court the pleading can be amended successfully. (Goodman, supra, 18 Cal.3d 335, 348; Lewis, supra, 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.)

 

Here, Plaintiff did not file any opposition to the present motion or provide any argument as to how successful amendment is reasonably possible. The fourth cause of action only requests a form of relief, and the Court finds no reasonable possibility of amendment to cure this defect.

 

Accordingly, Clearview’s demurrer to Plaintiff’s fourth cause of action is sustained without leave to amend.

 VI.          Conclusion

 

Defendant Clearview Real Estate Holdings, LLC’s unopposed Demurrer to Plaintiff’s Third and Fourth Causes of Action is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.

      I.          Background

 

This is a breach of contract case arising out of the sale of real property.

 

On November 24, 2025, Plaintiffs Perri Alise Bach and Ricky Roger (together Plaintiffs), acting as Co-Trustees of the Richard Bruce Thomas and Mary Elizabeth Thomas Family Living Trust, filed their complaint against Defendant Ersell Reid (Defendant), asserting four causes of action for (1) breach of contract, (2) breach of oral contract, (3) common count for quantum meruit), and (4) declaratory relief.

 

Specifically, Plaintiffs allege that on June 28, 2024, Plaintiffs, by and through their predecessors in interest, and Defendant entered into a written contract wherein Defendant agreed to purchase the subject real property with a closing deadline of August 27, 2024. (Compl., ¶ 7.) Plaintiffs claim Defendant failed and refused to close, resulting in her material breach of the Agreement, and that Defendant refused all efforts to mediate or arbitrate the dispute. (Id. at ¶ 10-11.)

 

On January 27, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a request for entry of default against Defendant.

 

On January 29, 2026, Plaintiffs’ request was rejected for procedural deficiencies in the proof of service of summons.

 

On February 4, 2026, Plaintiffs filed an amended proof of service, and another request for entry of default against Defendant. Default was entered the same day against Defendant.

 

On February 13, 2026, Defendant specially appeared and filed an ex parte application for an order setting aside entry of default on the grounds that the summons were not properly served, that she had no notice of the pending action, and that the Court lacked personal jurisdiction.

 

On February 17, 2026, the Court granted Defendant’s ex parte request and ordered the default entered on February 4, 2026 as to Defendant vacated. The Court made no finding that there was improper service or that the Court lacked personal jurisdiction. The Court determined setting aside the default was in the interests of justice as Defendant had appeared in the matter and stated she was prepared to litigate it.

 

On April 30, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a request for entry of default against Defendant. Default was entered on the same day.

 

On May 11, 2026, default judgment was entered in favor of Plaintiffs and against Defendant.

 

On May 18, 2026, Defendant reserved a hearing date for July 23, 2026 for hearing of a motion to set aside/vacate default and default judgment.

 

On May 19, 2026, Defendant filed an ex parte application for a temporary restraining order, stay of enforcement of default judgment, and an order preserving the status quo pending hearing on Defendant’s motion to set aside default and default judgment.

 

On May 20, 2026, the Court denied Defendant’s ex parte application, but advanced the reserved hearing date for Defendant’s motion to vacate entry of default and default judgment to July 7, 2026 and stayed enforcement of the judgment through the July 7, 2026 hearing.

 

On June 25, 2026, Defendant filed her motion to set aside/vacate entry of default and default judgment, her proposed verified answer, and her proposed cross-complaint.

 

On June 30, 2026, Plaintiffs filed their opposition.

   II.          Preliminary Procedural Issues

 

Timeliness of Defendant’s Motion – The Court notes that Defendant’s motion I untimely. While Defendant originally reserved July 23, 2026 as the hearing date for her motion to vacate or set aside the default and default judgment, the Court advanced this hearing date on May 20, 2026. Defendant was provided with notice. (See Certificate of Mailing dated May 20, 2026.) All moving and supporting papers must be served and filed at least 16 court days before the hearing.

 

Accordingly, Defendant’s motion was due on or before June 11. 2026. The proof of service attached to Defendant’s motion indicates that Jan Scott served Defendant’s moving papers to Plaintiffs’ counsel’s both via mail to counsel’s business address, and by electronic service, on June 22, 2026. This notice is well beyond the date the present motion was due.

 

However, notwithstanding the untimely filed motion, given Plaintiffs’ substantive opposition and in the interests of resolving the underlying matter, the Court considers the motion on the merits.

 

 III.          Legal Standard

Standard for Motion to Set Aside Default and Default Judgment – Per Code of Civil Procedure section 473(b), a court “may, upon any terms as may be just, relieve a party or his or her legal representative from a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against him or her through his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect.”

The general underlying purpose of Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b) is to promote the determination of actions on their merits. (Even Zohar Const. & Remodeling, Inc. v. Bellaire Townhouses, LLC (2015) 61 Cal.4th 830, 839.) However, the court only has broad discretion to vacate the entry of default, default judgment, or a dismissal where the moving party timely establishes a proper ground for relief. (Cruz v. Fagor America, Inc. (2007) 146 Cal.App.4th 488, 495.) The moving party bears the burden of establishing that the default was the result of excusable neglect. (Marcotte v. Municipal Court (1976) 64 Cal.App.3d 235, 239.)

California Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (d) permits the Court to set aside any void judgment or order.

The party must seek such relief  “within a reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months, after the judgment, dismissal, order, or proceeding was taken.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b).)  

-----

 

 IV.          Discussion

 

Application – Defendant moves the Court for an order setting aside the default and default judgment entered against defendant on the grounds of excusable neglect, pursuant to California Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivisions (b) and (d), and equitable relief. “The burden of establishing excusable neglect is upon the party seeking relief who must prove it by a preponderance of the evidence.” (Iott v. Franklin (1988) 206 Cal. App. 3d 521, 528, citing Elms v. Elms (1946) 72 Cal. App. 2d 508, 519.)

 

Specifically, Defendant contends her neglect resulting in default was the product of a string of circumstances which establish excusable neglect in failing to respond in the present action. Defendant contends she was hospitalized for two weeks, beginning on or around April 13, 2026 and lasting until “late April” (Defendant Decl. ¶  4)the end of April, rendering her physically and emotionally unable to handle legal matters. (Motion, p. 4:13-14.) On April 23, 2026, Defendant claims she learned of her aunt’s passing. (Motion, p. 4:15-17, Ex. A.) Defendant laims that after she was discharged from the hospital, she traveled to New Jersey for funeral and family matters on May 1, returning around May 11, 2026. (Defendant Decl., ¶ 4-5, Ex. B, C.) Finally, during her time in New Jersey, Defendant states she was otherwise occupied attending to familial and administrative matters, including applying for a replacement Social Security Card on May 4, 2026. (Id. at ¶ 6, Ex. D.)

 

The Court finds that Defendant has not established proper ground for relief. First, Defendant was unequivocally aware not only of the pending litigation, but also of the consequences of failure to timely respond to the summons, as evidenced by entry of default against Defendant and her subsequent motion for relief in February of this year. While the entry of default was vacated in the first instance, as a result of those prior proceedings, Defendant clearly understands the legal effect of default and was thereby on sufficient notice of her obligations as a participant in this matter to respond within 30 days upon proper service. The Court notes that at no time does Defendant state in her Declaration that she was unaware of the filing date for her responsive pleading.

 

Second, Defendant executed a Notice and Acknowledgement of the summons, complaint, and relevant papers on March 26, 2026. (Rose-McCaslin Decl., ¶ 5, Ex. A.) There is no question, and Defendant does not dispute, that she was properly served. Thus, Defendant had until Monday, April 27, 2026 to file a responsive pleading and appear in this matter to avoid default. Defendant provides no explanation as to why no responsive pleading was filed between March 26th and April 13th. As to her hospitalization, Defendant states only that it began April 13th and ended in “late April.” (Defendant Decl., ¶ 4.) Indeed, while Defendant is very precise about certain dates – such as when she entered the hospital, when her aunt died, when she applied for a Social Security card – she omits the end date for her hospitalization. Her own declaration suggests it was before her response date of April 27. (“During the relevant period leading to the default judgment, I was hospitalized for approximately two weeks.  Thereafter, my aunt passed away on April 23, 2026.” (Defendant’s Decl., ¶  12-13 (emphasis added).) In any event, default was not entered until April 30, and Defendant has not explained why she was prevented from filing her responsive pleading before that date, or from contacting Plaintiff’s counsel to seek a brief extension of time to respond. Regarding the hospitalization itself, Defendant provides no supporting documentation or explanation as to why her condition would prevent her from submitting a responsive pleading. Other circumstances Defendant points to took place after she was already in default.

 

Third, the reasons given by Defendant are not satisfactory. As discussed, there is no evidence of Defendant’s hospitalization during the final days in which she was permitted to file a responsive pleading. Her hospitalization also does not excuse her lack of response in the weeks prior, in the time after her discharge (again, a date Defendant does not provide), or why her condition prevented her from attending to this matter while hospitalized or from contacting opposing counsel for an extension. While the Court recognizes the hardship that undoubtably arises in losing a family member, the Court cannot find that this is sufficient to find excusable neglect, particularly in light of Defendant’s evidence that she was actively attending to other personal administrative matters, such as filing for a new Social Security card while in New Jersey. Additionally, whether Defendant was in California or New Jersey, she had the ability and means to monitor the active litigation pending against her, or otherwise contact Plaintiffs’ counsel and request an extension of time and explain her hardships. She did not do so.

 

Finally, Defendant failed to exercise diligence in avoiding a second entry of default in this matter. Plaintiffs state that at no point before Defendant’s filing of her ex parte application for relief did she contact them to request an extension or explain her inability to respond. (Rose-McCaslin Decl., ¶ 18.) Defendant failed to act when Plaintiffs served her with their request for entry of default on April 29, 2026, after she was apparently released from the hospital and in California. (Rose-McCaslin Decl., ¶ 14.) Defendant took no action when Plaintiff’s request for default judgment was served on May 5, 2026. (Rose-McCaslin Decl., ¶ 15.) Defendant took no action when Plaintiffs’ notice of entry of judgment was served on May 13, 2026, after she had returned from California. (Rose-McCaslin Decl., ¶ 18.) And what is missing from all of this is any statement from Defendant that she did not know her responsive pleading date.

 

As to Defendant’s request for equitable relief, the Court has the inherent authority to vacate a default and default judgment on equitable grounds such as extrinsic fraud or extrinsic mistake. (See Pulte Homes Corporation v. Williams Mechanical, Inc. (2016) 2 Cal.App.5th 267, 275.) However, once “a default judgment has been obtained, equitable relief may only be given in exceptional circumstances.” (Rappleyea v. Campbell (1994) 8 Cal.4th 975, 981.) There are three essential requirements to obtain relief on this ground: (1) a meritorious defense; (2) diligence in seeking relief once the default or default judgment is discovered; and (3) a satisfactory excuse for not presenting a defense to the original action. (Rappleyea, supra, 8 Cal.4th at 982.) The statutory time limits on relief under California Code of Civil Procedure sections 473, subdivision (b) and 473.5 do not apply, but once the purported extrinsic fraud or mistake is discovered, a party is expected to proceed diligently to seek relief. This requirement is inextricably intertwined with prejudice to the plaintiff. (Id., at 983-984.) 

 

The Court finds that Defendant has failed to establish all three requirements exist here. As to the first requirement establishing a meritorious defense, the defendant need only make a minimal showing and does not have to guarantee success or demonstrate with certainty that a different result would be obtained if the default judgment is vacated. (Mechling v. Asbestos Defendants (2018) 29 Cal.App.5th 1241, 1246.) The moving defendant only needs to “‘show facts indicating a sufficiently meritorious claim to entitle [it] to a fair adversary hearing.’” (Ibid (citations omitted).)

 

Here, Defendant sets forth a number of defenses against Plaintiffs, including Plaintiffs’ lack of standing, failure to establish authority to act on the trust’s behalf, failure to establish enforceable contractual rights, unenforceability of the liquidated damages provision within the contract, breach of escrow, and disputes as to the enforceability of the arbitration and alternate dispute resolutions provisions within the contract. (Motion, p. 5:8-19.) Defendant has offered as evidence communications between Defendant and Plaintiffs regarding Plaintiffs’ authority to act on behalf of the trust and standing to bring suit, requests for identification of the parties, requested operative agreements, and correspondence and filings relating to arbitration proceedings. (Defendant Decl., Ex. E-F, J-K.) These documents largely repeat Defendant’s allegations. For the reasons stated in the Opposition papers, the Court does not fined that the materials “show facts indicating a sufficiently meritorious claim.”

 

But even assuming that Defendant had made such a showing, the Court finds that that Defendant has neither exercised diligence in seeking relief once the default and default judgment were entered, nor a satisfactory excuse for not presenting her defense to the original action. Further, Defendant does not allege any extrinsic mistake or fraud in this matter. Thus, Defendant is not entitled to equitable relief from the default judgment entered against her, and this Court declines to exercise its equitable discretion to vacate the default judgment.

 

As to Defendant’s request for relief pursuant to California Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (d), Defendant makes no showing that the default judgment is in any way void, and the Court fails to find merit in this request.

 

Accordingly, Defendant’s motion to set aside default and the default judgment is DENIED.

Defendant Ersell Reid’s Motion to Set Aside Default and Default Judgment is DENIED. The stay in the enforcement of judgment shall be lifted upon the filing of this Order.
