DEPARTMENT 316 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 25STCV16399    Hearing Date: September 17, 2026    Dept: 316

BACKGROUND

On June 5, 2025, Plaintiff Sumalai Sapphaithoon filed a complaint against Defendants Ampaunchit Wiwatniwong, Nittaya Bivens, Somsri Lertsmitivanta, Arisa Wiwatniwong, and DOES 1-10. The complaint alleges that Defendants owe Plaintiff $1,405,671.39 based on a judgment in a prior case and are attempting to transfer assets to avoid paying the judgment.  

On October 24, 2025, Defendant Nittaya Bivens (hereinafter referred to as “Defendant”) filed a Motion to Quash Service of Summons.

On April 7, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Proof of Publication as it relates to Defendant.

On April 28, 2026, the Court issued a Minute Order denying Defendant’s Motion to Quash.

On May 11, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Notice of Ruling on Defendant’s Motion to Quash.

On May 20, 2026, Defendant filed a Declaration of Demurring or Moving Party in Support of Automatic Extension.

On May 27, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Request for Entry of Default against Defendant. This request was rejected for this stated reason – “Other - Declaration of Demurring or moving party in support of automatic extension filed on 5/20/26.”

On June 23, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Motion for Entry of Default.

On June 24, 2026, Defendant filed a demurrer to the complaint and motion to strike.

On September 4, 2026, Defendant filed an opposition.

On September 10, 2026, Plaintiff filed a reply.

 

TENTATIVE RULING

Plaintiff’s Motion for Entry of Default is DENIED.

LEGAL STANDARD

When a party is served by publication, service is considered complete on the 28th day after first publication. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 415.50, subd. (c); Gov. Code, § 6064.) After the 28th day, the defendant must file a responsive pleading within 30 days. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 412.20, subd. (a)(3).) If a party fails to respond by the 30th day, then the clerk shall enter the default of the defendant. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 585, subd. (c).)

When a defendant files a motion to quash service of summons, “[t]he service and filing of the notice shall extend the defendant’s time to plead until 15 days after service upon him or her of a written notice of entry of an order denying his or her motion, except that for good cause shown the court may extend the defendant’s time to plead for an additional period not exceeding 20 days.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 418.10, subd. (b).) “No default may be entered against the defendant before expiration of his or her time to plead, and no motion under this section. . . .” (Code Civ. Proc., § 418.10, subd. (d).)

DISCUSSION

The Court finds that the clerk’s rejection of Plaintiff’s request for entry of default was proper because Defendant had timely filed a responsive pleading before her deadline to respond had expired. Therefore, the Court will not enter default against Defendant.

Defendant was served with a written notice of entry of an order denying her motion to quash on May 11, 2026. Accordingly, Defendant had until May 26, 2026, to file a file a responsive pleading. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 418.10, subd. (b).) Defendant filed her Declaration of Demurring or Moving Party in Support of Automatic Extension on May 20, 2026, six days before her deadline to respond. Therefore, Defendant timely filed the extension declaration. For this reason, default shall not be entered on the ground that Defendant untimely filed a responsive pleading. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 418.10, subd. (d).)

Plaintiff argues that the Court should ignore the motion to quash and timelines stemming therefrom and find that Defendant failed to respond within the time required by service by publication, which Plaintiff undertook while the motion to quash the purported personal service was pending. The Court does not find a basis to ignore the motion to quash, the Court’s ruling on the motion or the response timing that flows from that ruling. Further, following Plaintiff’s approach would contravene the policy in favor of deciding cases on the merits and of relief from default. It would also likely only produce a default that would be subject to a motion to vacate whether based on excusable neglect in relying the timing of the then pending motion to quash or attorney mistake.

Plaintiff also seeks to rely on alleged deficiencies in Defendant’s extension declaration. (See Reply, p. 2:1-10.) Specifically, Plaintiff contends that Defendant’s meet and confer attempt was made less than five days before their responsive pleading was due. (See Reply, p. 2:7-10.) However, Defendant’s meet and confer attempt was on May 7, 2026, 19 days before her responsive pleading was due on May 26, 2026. Therefore, this contention is unavailing. Plaintiff also contends that Defendant’s meet and confer efforts were defective. (See Reply, p. 2:2-6.) However, this motion is not the proper mechanism by which to judge the effectiveness of Defendant’s meet and confer efforts. Said issue should be addressed in opposition to the demurrer itself. Rather, this motion is meant to determine whether Defendant timely filed all relevant documents and if default was improperly denied. Given that Defendant filed her extension declaration before her deadline to respond, the clerk’s decision to reject Plaintiff’s default request was proper. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 418.10, subd. (d).)

CONCLUSION

For these foregoing reasons, Plaintiff’s Motion for Entry of Default is DENIED.

Case Number: 26STCV15769    Hearing Date: September 17, 2026    Dept: 316

BACKGROUND

This case is a quiet title action.

On May 18, 2026, Somsri Lertsmitivanta filed a complaint against Sumalai Sapphaithoon. The complaint alleges six causes of action.

On June 10, 2026, Sapphaithoon (hereinafter referred to as “Cross-Complainant”) filed a cross-complaint against Lertsmitivanta and Ampaunchit Wiwatniwong (hereinafter collectively referred to as “Cross-Defendants”).

On June 30, 2026, the Court issued a Minute Order relating this action with another currently pending action (i.e., Case No. 25STCV16399).

On July 30, 2026, Cross-Defendants filed a Special Motion to Strike Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16 (Anti-SLAPP).

On August 17, 2026, Cross-Complainant filed an opposition.

On September 4, 2026, Cross-Defendants filed a reply brief in support of the motion.

TENTATIVE RULING

Cross-Defendants’ Special Motion to Strike Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16 is DENIED.

LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16, subdivision (a) provides that “[a] cause of action against a person¿arising from any act of that person in¿furtherance of the person’s right of petition¿or free speech under the United States¿Constitution or the California Constitution in¿connection with a public issue shall be¿subject to a special motion to strike, unless¿the court determines that the plaintiff has¿established that there is a probability that¿the plaintiff will prevail on the claim.”   

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

Cross-Defendants request the Court take judicial notice of four exhibits: (1) the Verified Complaint filed May 18, 2026, in this action, Case No. 26STCV15769; (2) the Cross-Complaint filed June 10, 2026, in this action; (3) the Complaint for Avoidance of Voidable Transfers filed June 5, 2025, in Sapphaithoon v. Wiwatniwong, et al., Case No. 25STCV16399; and (4) the Register of actions in Case No. 25STCV16399 showing that the first-filed avoidance action remains pending.

The Court shall take judicial notice of the filing of these exhibits and the status of Case No. 25STCV16399 as records of this Court. (See Evid. Code, § 452, subd. (d)(1).)

DISCUSSION

I.                “Arising From” Protected Activity

The Court finds that the cross-complaint does not arise from Cross-Defendants engaging in protected activity pursuant to their right of petition or free speech. 

In determining whether to grant these motions, the court must engage in a two-step process. (See Simpson Strong-Tie Co., Inc. v. Gore (2010) 49 Cal.4th 12, 21.) First, the moving party must meet the threshold burden of showing that the opposing party’s cause of action arises from the moving party engaging in protected activity pursuant to their right of petition or free speech. (See ibid. [citing Code Civ Proc., § 425.16, subd. (b)(1)].)

“A cause of action ‘arising from’ defendant’s litigation activity may appropriately be the subject of a¿section 425.16¿motion¿to strike. ‘Any act’ includes communicative conduct such as the filing, funding, and prosecution of a civil action.” (Rusheen v. Cohen (2006) 37 Cal.4th 1048, 1056 [citations omitted].) Furthermore, “any written or oral statement or writing made in connection with an issue under consideration or review by a legislative, executive, or judicial body, or any other official proceeding authorized by law” is also protected. (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (e).) 

Once the moving party establishes that they engaged in protected activity, they must show that the opposing party’s cause of action arises from the moving party engaging in the protected activity. (See Simpson Strong-Tie Co., Inc. v. Gore (2010) 49 Cal.4th 12, 21.) A cause of action arises from protected activity only when that activity underlies or forms the basis for the claim, not when it merely supplies evidence or context. (See Park v. Board of Trustees of Calif. State Univ.¿(2017) 2 Cal.5th 1057, 1063.) Therefore, a cause of action will be struck when the protected activity “itself¿is the wrong complained of.” (Id. at 1060.) When ruling on this motion, courts “should consider the elements of the challenged claim and what actions by the defendant supply those elements and consequently form the basis for liability.” (Ibid.)

Here, in 2025, Cross-Complainant filed an action against Cross-Defendants to void the transfer of the subject property (i.e., Case No. 25STCV16399). (See RJN, Exh. 3.) That action remains pending. (See RJN, Exh. 4.) Thereafter, Cross-Defendant Lertsmitivanta commenced the present action against Cross-Complainant to obtain quiet title of the subject property (i.e., Case No. 26STCV15769). (See RJN, Exh. 1.) Thereafter, Cross-Complainant filed the cross-complaint against Cross-Defendants seeking the avoidance of transfers. (See RJN, Exh. 2.) In the cross-complaint, Cross-Complainant briefly references 2025 action, Cross-Defendant Lertsmitivanta’s complaint in this action (i.e., Case No. 26STCV15769), and the default judgment entered against Cross-Defendant Chan and in favor of Cross-Complainant in the underlying financial elder abuse action and appeal of that judgment. (See RJN, Exh. 2, ¶¶ 24-28.)

Cross-Defendants contend that Cross-Complainant’s action is prohibited because it arises from protected judicial or litigation activity. (See Motion, p. 4:6-10.)  

The Court disagrees with Cross-Defendants’ position. The purpose of the cross-complaint is to prevent the transfer of the subject property so that it can be used to satisfy Cross-Complainant’s judgment in the sum of $1,405,671.39. (See RJN, Exh. 2, Relief ¶¶ 1-6.) The Court finds no basis for Cross-Defendants’ contention that the cross-complaint is intended to punish them for filing this present action (i.e., 26STCV15769) or any other litigation activity. (See Motion, p. 5:19-24.) Furthermore, Cross-Complainant’s reference to this present action (i.e., 26STCV15769), their other pending action (i.e., Case No. 25STCV16399) and the default judgment in the underlying elder abuse matter entered against Cross-Defendant Chan simply provides context for the cross-claims. (See Opp’n, p. 8:6-10 [citing Baral v. Schnitt (2016) 1 Cal.5th 376, 394].) The cross-complaint does not seek to establish a basis of liability against Cross-Defendants based on the complaint or any other litigation activity. Rather, it effectively restates the request made by Cross-Complainant in the Complaint in Case No. 25STCV16399. To the extent the complaint in that matter and the cross-complaint in this matter are duplicative, that is not a basis to strike under Section 425.16. In sum, the cross-complaint does not arise from protected judicial or litigation activity, but from the 2024 property transfer.

Cross-Defendants cite Navellier v. Sletten (2002) 29 Cal.4th 82 as support for their position. However, reliance on this case is unpersuasive because the claims at issue there directly stemmed from defendant filing a counterclaim in federal court. (See id. at p. 90 [“Sletten is being sued because of the affirmative counterclaims he filed in federal court. In fact, but for the federal lawsuit and Sletten’s alleged actions taken in connection with that litigation, plaintiffs’ present claims would have no basis. This action therefore falls squarely within the ambit of the anti-SLAPP statute's ‘arising from’ prong.”].) Here, the basis of the cross-complaint is not Cross-Defendants’ litigation activity or Cross-Defendant Lertsmitivanta filing suit. Rather, as stated above, it is the contested transfer of the subject property that was made in 2024. (See RJN, Exh. 2, Relief ¶¶ 1-6.) Therefore, the present case is readily distinguishable from Navellier.

The Court finds that the cross-complaint does not arise from protected judicial or litigation activity. Therefore, motion does not meet the first prong under Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Cross-Defendants’ Special Motion to Strike Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16 is DENIED.

Cross-Defendants are ordered to file and serve an answer to the Cross-Complaint within twenty days of the Court’s ruling.
