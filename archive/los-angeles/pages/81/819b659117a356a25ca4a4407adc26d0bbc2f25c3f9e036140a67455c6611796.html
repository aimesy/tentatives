II.             ANALYSIS

A.              Legal Standard

A party must respond to interrogatories within 30 days after service. (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.260(a).)   As is set forth by Code of Civil Procedure § 2030.220, a responding party’s must respond to special interrogatories as follows:

(a) Each answer in a response to interrogatories shall be as complete and straightforward as the information reasonably available to the responding party permits.

 

(b) If an interrogatory cannot be answered completely, it shall be answered to the extent possible.

 

(c) If the responding party does not have personal knowledge sufficient to respond fully to an interrogatory, that party shall so state, but shall make a reasonable and good faith effort to obtain the information by inquiry to other natural persons or organizations, except where the information is equally available to the propounding party.

(Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.220.)

The propounding party may bring motions to compel further responses to interrogatories if it believes (1) the responses received are evasive, or (2) incomplete, or (3) if the objections raised are meritless or too general. (Id., §§ 2030.300(a).) The propounding party must serve notice of a motion to compel further responses to interrogatories within 45 days of the service of the verified responses, or any supplemental verified response, unless the parties agree in writing to extend the deadline to a specific later date. (Id., § 2030.300(c).)¿¿ 

A motion to compel further responses to interrogatories shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration and a separate statement, or concise outline of the discovery request and each response in dispute. (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.300(b).) A respondent has the burden to justify objections in response to a motion filed to compel further responses. (Fairmont Ins. Co. v. Superior Court (2000) 22 Cal.4th 245, 255 (“Fairmont”).)

B.              Meet and Confer

A motion to compel further discovery responses must be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure § 2016.040. (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.300.) “A meet and confer declaration in support of a motion shall state facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt, either in person, by telephone, or by videoconference, to informally resolve each issue presented by the motion.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2016.040.)

 

Plaintiffs’ counsel Suraj Teppara (“Teppara”) attests that, after the Court’s April 23, 2026 Order compelling further responses to Plaintiffs’ Special Interrogatories, Set One, Allied served supplemental responses on May 14, 2026. (Teppara Decl. ISO Sergio Mot., ¶¶ 3-4, Exh. B.)  On June 4, 2026, Teppara served a meet and confer letter to Alliance’s counsel. (Id., ¶ 7, Exh. C.) On June 10, 2026, Teppara attests that he met and conferred telephonically with Alliance’s counsel and followed up with an email the next day. (Id., ¶ 8.) The parties were unable to come to a resolution. (Id., ¶¶ 8-9.)

The Court finds that Plaintiffs have satisfied the statutory meet and confer requirements.

 

C.              Discussion 

Plaintiffs move the Court to compel Alliance’s further responses to Special Interrogatories Nos. 13, 16, 17, 18, 24, 28-32 and 40-64.  Alliance opposes, arguing that it has fully complied with the Court’s April 23, 2026 Order.

 

1.               Defendant Tinisha Nelson

At the outset, the Court addresses Defendant Tinisha Nelson’s status as Alliance’s insured.  To various of the contested interrogatories, Alliance responds as follows:

Responding Party does not have access to this information because Tinisha Nelson was not a named insured under the insurance policy in effect at the time of the subject accident, and she has not cooperated with Responding Party in connection with the subject incident or this lawsuit despite numerous attempts by Responding Party.

 

Instead, the insurance policy in effect at the time of the subject incident contained only Morris Thomas as a named insured, with no additional insureds listed.  As a result, Responding Party is not in possession, custody or control of any business records that contain any information that would permit it to respond to this interrogatory.

(See, e.g., Sergio Separate Statement (“SSS”), p. 2:12-19.)

Alliance’s response is inadequate and its justification for the inadequacy is in direct contravention with its previous representations.  Alliance moved to intervene in this matter on behalf of Defendants Thomas and Nelson, asserting that it “may be required to satisfy a judgment against Defendants” and that it sought leave to intervene on behalf of both Defendants “to defend [Alliance’s] interests as well as those of its insured.”  (See Motion for Leave to Intervene, filed 1/2/25, p. 3:8-10, p. 5:17-18.)  Alliance further stated in its Motion for Leave to Intervene that it “has a direct interest in the lawsuit because the lability of its insured are at issue.” (Id., p. 6:22.)[3] Relying upon Alliance’s representation that both Thomas and Nelson were its insureds, the Court granted Alliance leave to intervene on their behalf. (See Minute Order, 2/4/25 (Granting Alliance leave to intervene on behalf of Thomas and Nelson).)  And Alliance’s Answer-in-Intervention states that “[p]ursuant to Court Order Intervenor ALLIANCE UNITED INSURANCE COMPANY . . . hereby intervenes in this action as the insurance carrier for Defendants TINISHA MARION NELSON and MORRIS THOMAS (“Defendants” or “Insureds”) as insured are named Defendants in the Complaint . . .” (See Answer-in-Intervention, filed 2/6/25, p. 1:24-27.)  Alliance cannot seek leave to intervene on Nelson’s behalf claiming that Nelson is an insured only to later profess an inability to provide discovery responses because Nelson is not an insured.

Furthermore, and to the extent that Alliance’s qualifies its response by stating that Nelson “was not a named insured under the insurance policy in effect at the time of the subject accident,” that Nelson was not a named insured at the time of the accident has no bearing upon whether Alliance has in its possession, custody or control documents from when Nelson was an insured (which the Court presumes Nelson must have been given Alliance’s aforementioned representations) that might provide the information sought. Not only that, but within its Motion for Leave to Intervene Alliance’s counsel makes attestations as to having telephoned, texted, emailed and hired a private investigator to locate Nelson. (See Carter Decl. ISO Motion for Leave to Intervene, ¶¶ 4-12; see also Ngo Decl., ¶ 6.)  Alliance’s current protestations as to its inability to provide information as to Nelson, such as even a cellular phone number (see SROG No. 13), therefore rings hollow.

In short, Nelson was an insured at some point as evidenced by Alliance’s presence in this suit as an intervenor on behalf of Nelson, by Alliance’s representations made in the course of seeking to intervene, and by the Answer-in-Intervention filed by Alliance.  Alliance is required to provide a response that is “as complete and straightforward as the information reasonably available to the responding party permits.”  (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.220(a).)  Alliance’s blanket responses that it has no business records because Nelson was not an insured at the time of the incident are insufficient.
 

2.               Whether the Further Responses Provided Were Code Compliant

 

As has been noted twice above, an answer to an interrogatory must be “as complete and straightforward as the information reasonably available to the responding party permits.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.220(a); Collin v. CalPortland Co. (2014) 228 Cal.Appp.4th 582, 590.)  Where an interrogatory cannot be answered completely, it must be answered to the extent possible. (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.220(b).)   In its April 23, 2026 Order, the Court has already found that the interrogatories at issue are those for which further responses are required because the information might be available from Alliance’s business records. (See Minute Order, 4/23/26.)

 

In addition to the analysis set forth above as to Nelson’s status as an insured, the Court does not find that Alliance’s responses are code compliant. Alliance’s protestation that records available via an industry database(s) are not in Alliance’s possession, custody or control is at odds with the express language of the Code of Civil Procedure, which requires  that in providing its further responses Alliance must “make a reasonable and good faith effort to obtain the information by inquiry to other natural persons or organizations, except where the information is equally available to the propounding party.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.220(c)(emphasis added).)   And Alliance’s contention that providing such information would require it to attest to the veracity of information based upon third party records which may contain “layered hearsay issues” is not quite correct.  Where the information is available from a third party, Alliance may comply with the Code of Civil Procedure by responding to the interrogatory and simply indicating that the information was obtained from a third-party source and therefore Alliance cannot attest to its veracity.[4]

 

The above preliminary considerations are made as to SROGs 13, 16, 24, 28-32, and 40-64. For each, Alliance provides the same response as set forth above and, for each, the analysis set forth above applies.  Alliance is ordered to provide further responses to SROGs 13, 16, 24, 28-32, and 40-64.

As to the remaining at-issue SROGs, Nos. 17 and 18, SROG 17 asks Alliance to identify each person it contends is legally responsible for Plaintiff’s damages.  And SROG 18 asks Alliance to state all facts in support of its contention that any identified person is legally responsible for Plaintiff’s damages.  Alliance argues that response to both interrogatories is that each calls for an improper legal conclusion.

An interrogatory may relate to whether a party is making a particular legal contention and may relate to the documents on which the legal contention is based. An interrogatory is not objectionable because the answer to it involves an opinion or contention that would be based on legal theories developed in anticipation of litigation or in preparation for trial. (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.010; Rifkind v. Superior Court (1994) 22 Cal.App.4th 1255, 1261-62 (legal contentions and opinions are discoverable by interrogatory).)

Further responses are required.

3.               Requests for Sanctions

 

The court shall impose a monetary sanction against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel a response unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust. (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.300(d).)  As is noted above, Plaintiffs seek a total of $2,134.80 against Alliance and its counsel of record for each Discovery Motion. In its oppositions, Alliance contends there is no justification for an award of sanctions against it and requests monetary sanctions of $500 per motion against Plaintiffs “as a consequence for unsuccessfully making this motion, without substantial justification.” (Opp. to Sergio SROGs Mot., p. 6:13-25.)

 

For Sergio’s SROGs motion, Plaintiffs seek a total of $1,883.80, consisting of: (1) 6 hours researching and drafting the motion; (2) an anticipated 2 additional hours reviewing any opposition, drafting a reply and attending the hearing, all billed at the rate of $300/hr; and (3) $83.70 in filing fees. (Teppara Decl. ISO Sergio SROGs Mot. ¶¶ 10-12.)  For Lizeth, Godin and Karol’s motions, Plaintiffs seek only $83.70 in filing fees for each motion. (Lizeth Mot., ¶ 12; Godin Mot., ¶ 12; Karol Mot., ¶ 12.)

 

The Court finds that sanctions are warranted given the above analysis regarding Alliance’s responses as contrasted to its representations in seeking to intervene.  The Court further finds that Plaintiff’s cited rate is reasonable and the hours spent are also reasonable as Plaintiffs have acknowledged the duplicative nature of the Discovery Motions and have reduced their requested fees accordingly.

 

Plaintiffs’ requests for sanctions are GRANTED in the amount of $2,134.80.  

III.           CONCLUSION

Plaintiffs’ four Motions to Compel Further Responses to Special Interrogatories, Sets One are GRANTED.

Plaintiffs’ Requests for Sanctions are GRANTED in the amount of $2,134.80.  Alliance’s requests for sanctions are DENIED.

Further responses are ordered served no later than August 14, 2026.  The awarded sanctions are ordered to be paid to Plaintiffs’ counsel no later than August 31, 2026.

II.             ANALYSIS

A.              Legal Standard

Code of Civil Procedure § 2023.030 gives the court the discretion to impose sanctions against anyone engaging in a misuse of the discovery process. Misuse of the discovery process includes failure to respond to an authorized method of discovery or disobeying a court order to provide discovery. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.010(d), (g).)   Where a party fails to obey an order regarding discovery, “the court may make those orders that are just, including the imposition of an issue sanction, an evidence sanction, or a terminating sanction.” (Code Civ. Proc. §§ 2030.290(c), 2023.010(c); R.S. Creative, Inc. v. Creative Cotton, Ltd. (1999) 75 Cal.App.4th 486, 495 (“R.S. Creative”).) Terminating sanctions are appropriate when a party persists in disobeying a court’s orders. (Deyo v. Kilbourne (1978) 84 Cal.App.3d 771, 795-796 (“Deyo”); see also Code Civ. Proc. § 2023.030(d)(A court may impose terminating sanctions by striking pleadings of the party engaged in misuse of discovery or entering default judgment).) 

 

However, a terminating sanction is a “drastic measure which should be employed with caution.” (Deyo supra, 84 Cal. App. 3d at 793.) “A decision to order terminating sanctions should not be made lightly. But where a violation is willful, preceded by a history of abuse, and the evidence shows that less severe sanctions would not produce compliance with the discovery rules, the trial court is justified in imposing the ultimate sanction.” (Mileikowsky v. Tenet Healthsystem (2005) 128 Cal. App. 4th 262, 279-280.)

 

Courts generally recognize two factual prerequisites to the imposition of non-monetary sanctions for violation of court ordered discovery, including issue or evidence sanctions: (1) a failure to comply with a court order, and (2) such failure must be willful. (See R.S. Creative, supra, 75 Cal.App.4th at p. 496; Biles v. Exxon Mobil Corp. (2004) 124 Cal.App.4th 1315, 1327 (“Biles”); Liberty Mutual Fire Ins. Co. v. LcL Administrators, Inc. (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 1093, 1102, citing Vallbona v. Springer (1996) 43 Cal.App.4th 1525, 1545; Deyo, supra, 84 Cal.App.3d at pp. 787-88 (“Lack of diligence may be deemed willful in the sense that the party understood his obligation, had the ability to comply, and failed to comply. [Citation.] … A conscious or intentional failure to act, as distinguished from accidental or involuntary non-compliance, is sufficient to invoke a penalty.”) (internal citations omitted).)

The court should consider the totality of the circumstances, including conduct of the party to determine if the actions were willful, the detriment to the propounding party, and the number of formal and informal attempts to obtain discovery. (Lang v. Hochman (2000) 77 Cal.App.4th 1225, 1246.)  If a lesser sanction fails to curb abuse, a greater sanction is warranted. (Van Sickle v. Gilbert (2011) 196 Cal.App.4th 1495, 1516.) However, “the unsuccessful imposition of a lesser sanction is not an absolute prerequisite to the utilization of the ultimate sanction.” (Deyo, supra, 84 Cal.App.3d at p. 787.) Before any sanctions may be imposed the court must make an express finding that there has been a willful failure of the party to comply with the discovery obligation. (Fairfield v. Superior Court for Los Angeles County (1966) 246 Cal.App.2d 113, 118.) Lack of diligence may be deemed willful where the party understood its obligation, had the ability to comply, and failed to comply. (Deyo, supra, 84 Cal.App.3d at p. 787; Fred Howland Co. v. Superior Court of Los Angeles County (1966) 244 Cal.App.2d 605, 610-611.)  The party who failed to comply with discovery obligations has the burden of showing that the failure was not willful. (Deyo, supra, 84 Cal.App.3d at p. 788; Cornwall v. Santa Monica Dairy Co. (1977) 66 Cal.App.3d 250; Evid. Code §§ 500, 605.)

B.              Discussion

LAUSD moves the Court for terminating sanctions for Rivera’s willful failure to comply with his discovery obligations and for Rivera’s failure to appear for deposition after the Court granted LAUSD’s Ex Parte Application to compel Plaintiff’s deposition.  In the alternative, LAUSD requests the Court impose issue or evidentiary sanctions against Rivera.

In support of its motion, LAUSD sets forth the multiple times it has unsuccessfully noticed Plaintiff’s deposition, amounting to eight times Plaintiff has either objected to being deposed or failed to appear for deposition.  (See Martinez Decl., ¶¶ 3-10, 12, 18, Exhs. A-H, M.)  Included in LAUSD’s narrative are two times when Plaintiff did not serve any objections to the deposition date but simply failed to appear, first on March 17, 2026 and again on June 1, 2026. (Id., ¶¶ 13, 19, Exhs. H, N.)  LAUSD also contends Plaintiff has now twice failed to comply with the Court’s order to appear at deposition (see id., ¶¶ 13, 20) and accordingly requests the Court specifically order Plaintiff’s action against Defendant dismissed with prejudice, arguing that Plaintiff’s failure to participate in this litigation has resulted in unnecessary expenditure of time and resources and that Plaintiff’s failure was a willful and deliberate violation of a Court Order.

Rivera does not oppose.

When deciding whether to impose terminating sanctions, courts generally weigh three factors: (1) whether the party subject to the sanction acted willfully, (2) the detriment to the party seeking discovery, and (3) the number of formal and informal unsuccessful attempts to obtain discovery. (Creed-21 v. City of Wildomar (2017) 18 Cal.App.5th 690, 702; Lang v. Hochman (2000) 77 Cal.App.4th 1225, 1246.)

Although the Court previously found the factual circumstances presented in the March 23 Terminating Sanctions Motion did not suffice to warrant terminating sanctions, the circumstances have changed. Rivera’s initial failure to appear at the Court ordered deposition was not due to his own actions, but his failure to attend the June 1, 2026 deposition is in violation of the Court’s Order and there appear to be no mitigating circumstances.  The Court ordered Rivera to appear for his deposition on June 1, 2026 (see Minute Order, 5/22/26) and Rivera was given notice of the Court’s Order (see Notice of Ruling, filed 5/22/26; see also Martinez Decl., ¶ 18, Exh. M), but Rivera did not sit for his deposition on June 1 as was ordered. (Martinez Decl., ¶ 19, Exh. N.)  Compounding Rivera’s failure to appear for his deposition is the lengthy history of his prior failures to appear for the seven previously noticed depositions (making the last failure to appear his eighth). (See Martinez Decl., ¶¶ 3-10, 12, 18, Exhs. A-H, M.)  Upon review of the Court’s records and LAUSD’s supporting evidence, the Court finds that Rivera: (1) understood both his obligation to sit for a deposition in general and his obligation to comply with the Court’s Order to appear for his deposition; (2) had the ability to comply with the Court’s Order; and (3) willfully failed to comply.  The Court further finds that Rivera’s willful failure to comply with his discovery obligations and the Court’s Order renders LAUSD unable to properly evaluate Rivera’s claims and meaningfully present a defense.  Given that the Court’s earlier orders compelling Rivera’s compliance and imposing monetary sanctions have had no effect on Rivera’s willingness to sit for his deposition, the Court finds that lesser sanctions will not curb Rivera’s discovery abuse.

III.           CONCLUSION

In accordance with the above, LAUSD’s Motion for Terminating Sanctions is GRANTED.  Rivera’s Complaint is ordered dismissed with prejudice.

I.                BACKGROUND

This is a habitability, premises liability and nuisance action. Plaintiff Dinisha Eskridge alleges that she leases the residential real property located at 11216 S. Figueroa St., Unit # 6, Los Angeles, CA 90061 (the “Property”) from Defendant CRCD Apartments, LLC (“CRCD”).  Plaintiff alleges that the Property suffers from a severe bedbug infestation and that CRCD knew of the infestation but concealed the infestation and failed to warn Plaintiff prior to Plaintiff leasing the Property.  Plaintiff claims CRCD’s failure to maintain the Property in a habitable condition caused her to suffer physical and mental injuries. 

On April 10, 2026, Plaintiff filed this action against CRCD and Does 1 through 50, inclusive, alleging causes of action for: (1) Breach of Implied Warranty of Habitability (Violation of Civil Code §1941.1; Health & Safety Code 17920.3); (2) Breach of Implied Warranty of Habitability (Violation of Civil Code §1942.4); (3) Negligence - Premises Liability/Failure to Warn – Negligence Per Se; (4) Private Nuisance; (5) Public Nuisance; (6) Breach of Contract; (7) Breach of Covenant of Quiet Enjoyment; (8) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress; (9) Fraudulent Concealment; (10) Unfair Business Practices (Violation of Business & Professions Code §17200, et seq.).

 

            On May 19, 2026, CRCD filed the instant Demurrer to Plaintiff’s Complaint and Motion to Strike Portions of Plaintiff’s Complaint.

 

            On July 21, 2026, Plaintiff filed her Oppositions to CRCD’s Demurrer and Motion to Strike.

            On July 28, 2026, CRCD filed its Replies.

II.             ANALYSIS

A.            Legal Standard

1.               Demurrer

 

A party may demur to a complaint on the grounds that it “does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.10(e).) A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)

 

A party may also demur to a complaint¿on the grounds that it “is uncertain. As used in this subdivision, ‘uncertain’ includes ambiguous and unintelligible.” However, “[a] demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures. [Citation.]” (Khoury v. Maly’s of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.)

 

When considering demurrers, courts accept all well pleaded facts as true. (Fox v. JAMDAT Mobile, Inc. (2010) 185 Cal.App.4th 1068, 1078.) “A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed.” (SKF Farms v. Superior Court (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 902, 905.) “The only issue involved in a demurrer hearing is whether the complaint, as it stands, unconnected with extraneous matters, states a cause of action.” (Hahn, supra, 147 Cal.App.4th at p. 747.)¿On demurrer, a court does “not accept contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Simonyan v. Nationwide Insurance Company of America (2022) 78 Cal.App.5th 889, 895.)¿¿

 

Although courts construe pleadings liberally, sufficient facts must be alleged to support the allegations pled to survive a demurrer. (Rakestraw v. California Physicians' Serv. (2000) 81 Cal.App.4th 39, 43.) Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349.) The burden is on the party who filed the pleading subject to demurrer to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Ibid.)

 

2.               Motion to Strike

            Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading, may serve and file a notice of motion to strike the whole or any part of the pleading. (Code Civ. Proc. § 435(b)(1).) The court may, upon a motion or at any time in its discretion and upon terms it deems proper: (1) strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or (2) strike out all or any part of the pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of California, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Code Civ. Proc. § 436(a), (b); Stafford v. Schultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782.) 

            The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleadings or by way of judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc. § 437.) Accordingly, the Court does not consider any extrinsic evidence, including that contained in affidavits, declarations, or deposition transcripts concurrently filed with the moving or opposing papers. (See Garcia v. Sterling (1985) 176 Cal.App.3d 17, 21.) 

            In ruling on a motion to strike, the allegations in the complaint are considered in context and presumed to be true: “[J]udges read allegations of a pleading subject to a motion to strike as a whole, all parts in their context, and assume their truth.” (Clauson v. Superior Court (Pedus Services, Inc.) (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1253, 1255.) 

B.              Meet and Confer

Before filing a demurrer, the demurring party is required to meet and confer “in person, by telephone, or by video conference with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to the demurrer for the purposes of determining whether an agreement can be reached through a filing of an amended pleading that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41(a).)

 

Counsel for Defendant, Herbert Hayden (“Hayden”), attests to sending a meet and confer letter to Plaintiff’s counsel by email and mail. (Hayden Decl., ¶¶ 4-6, Exh A.) Hayden also attests to inviting Plaintiff’s counsel to meet and confer by telephone or videoconference. (Id., at ¶ 7.) Counsel attests that Plaintiff’s counsel has not responded to the meet and confer letter or contacted Defendant’s counsel. (Id., at ¶ 8.)

 

The Court finds that CRCD has not satisfied the statutory meet and confer requirement.  However, the Court’s determination that the meet and confer process was insufficient is not grounds to overrule or sustain CRCD’s Demurrer.  (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41(a)(4).)   Accordingly the Court proceeds to analyze CRCD’s Demurrer on its merits.  The parties are cautioned, however, of their obligation to comply with statutory requirements, including meet and confer requirements.

C.              Discussion

1.               Demurrer

            CRCD demurs to Plaintiff’s Second, Third, Fifth, Eighth, Ninth, and Tenth Causes of Action on the grounds that each fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action and/or is uncertain.

i.                 Second Cause of Action for Breach of Implied Warranty of Habitat ability (Violation of Civil Code § 1942.4)

            Section 1942.4(a)[1] provides that “[a] landlord of a dwelling may not demand rent, collect rent, issue a notice of a rent increase, or issue a three-day notice to pay rent or quit . . . if all of the following conditions exist prior to the landlord’s demand or notice: (1) [t]he dwelling substantially lacks any of the affirmative standard characteristics listed in Section 1941.1 or violates Section 17920.10 of the Health and Safety Code, or is deemed and declared substandard as set forth in Section 17920.3 of the Health and Safety Code because conditions listed in that section exist to an extent that endangers the life, limb, health, property, safety, or welfare of the public or the occupants of the dwelling[;] (2) [a] public officer or employee who is responsible for the enforcement of any housing law, after inspecting the premises, has notified the landlord or the landlord’s agent in writing of his or her obligations to abate the nuisance or repair the substandard conditions[;] (3) [t]he conditions have existed and have not been abated 35 days beyond the date of service of the notice specified in paragraph (2) and the delay is without good cause . . . [; and] (4) [t]he conditions were not caused by an act or omission of the tenant or lessee in violation of Section 1929 or 1941.2.” (Civ. Code § 1942.4(a).)

CRCD argues Plaintiff’s Complaint does not plead all elements of a Section 1942.4(a)  cause of action because Plaintiff fails to allege that any public officer or employee inspected the property and issued a written notice to CRCD and also fails to allege and that thirty-five days have passed since the issuance of any such written notice.

 In opposition, Plaintiff argues that the Complaint alleges that she filed a complaint with the County of Los Angeles Health Department and notified Defendant, but the bed bug infestation was not resolved for an extended period, which would exceed 35 days. Plaintiff thus argues that she has alleged each element of Civil Code §1941.1 and information such as the name of the public officer will require a discovery process for Plaintiff to obtain that information.

            Plaintiff alleges she has complained multiple times to Defendant about the uninhabitable conditions of the subject unit since 2022 and continuing through 2026, that she has filed a complaint with the County of Los Angeles Health Department and notified Defendant to abate the bed bug infestation, but the infestation was not resolved in the subject unit for “an extended period.” (Complaint ¶¶ 70, 72, 74.) Plaintiff’s allegations are insufficient.

First, Plaintiff’s allegation that she “filed a complaint with County of Los Angeles Health Department [“LAHD”] and notified Defendants to abate the bed bug infestation” (see Complaint, ¶ 74) does not suffice to allege the specific statutory elements set forth in Section 1942.4; to state a cause of action, Plaintiff must allege that a “public employee or officer [] notified [CRCD] in writing of [its] obligation to abate the nuisance or repair the substandard conditions.” (Civ. Code § 1942.4(a)(2).)  Nowhere in her Complaint does Plaintiff allege that a public officer or employee notified CRCD in writing of its obligations to abate the alleged nuisance after inspecting the premises. Plaintiff’s contention that “the detail[s] of the written notice, name of the public officer is within the Defendant’s control and possession, which will require a discovery process for Plaintiff to obtain said information” (see Opp., p. 24-26) is both an overstatement and a misstatement.  Plaintiff overstates what she is required to plead.  Plaintiff is not required to plead the specific name of the public officer or the details of the written notice.  Plaintiff is simply required to plead that there was an inspection and CRCD received written notice, which pleading may be made on information and belief.[2] And that such information is solely within CRCD’s purview appears to be a misstatement; California law requires notice be given to the landlord with a  copy to the tenants in a residential building when there has been a violation affecting the tenants’ health and safety. (See Health & Safety Code § 17980(d)(1)(B)(“[T]enants in a residential building shall be provided copies [of an] order of the code enforcement agency issued after inspection of the premises declaring the dwelling to be in violation of a provision described in subdivision (a)[,]” which subdivision includes maintenance of a building in violation of an order or notice for which a landlord is normally given reasonable time to correct).)

 As to timing, the Court finds that Plaintiff’s allegations are insufficient as well.  Section 1942.4 requires that the conditions existed and were not abated for 35 days “beyond the date of service of the [written notice for a public officer or employee notifying CRCD of its obligation to abate the alleged nuisance].” (Civ. Code § 1942.4(a)(3).)   While the Court may read Plaintiff’s allegation that the alleged condition existed for an “extended period” and infer that the alleged condition existed for years, any such inference does not suffice to address the pleading requirement here.  The only relevant time period is 35 days after CRCD received written notice to abate from a public agency.  Without an allegation that the relevant time period has been established or an alleged date that CRCD received any written notice, Plaintiff’s pleadings are insufficient.

            CRCD’s Demurrer to Plaintiff’s Second Cause of Action is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

ii.               Third Cause of Action for Negligence – Premises Liability/Failure to Warn – Negligence Per Se

            CRCD demurs to Plaintiffs third cause of action on the ground that negligence per se is an evidentiary doctrine, not substantive law, and does not itself create a private right of action. CRCD further argues Plaintiff’s third cause of action is uncertain because it cannot meaningfully respond to a claim that conflates an evidentiary doctrine with substantive theories of liability.

            In opposition, Plaintiff argues that the third cause of action for negligence arises from premises liability, failure to warn, and negligence per se, and Plaintiff argues the Complaint adequately pleads negligence.

            The fundamental elements of negligence are (1) duty, (2) breach, (3) causation, and (4) injury. (Thomas v. Steinberg (2012) 206 Cal.App.4th 654, 662.)   Negligence per se is not a cause of action; it is a theory that affects the burden of proof in an underlying negligence action. (See California Service Station etc. Assn. v. American Home Assurance Co. (1998) 62 Cal.App.4th 1166, 1178 [“[a]n underlying claim of negligence must be viable before the [negligence per se] presumption ... can be employed”].)  Where a plaintiff claims a defendant is negligent per se, he or she simply invokes a pre-established statutory standard of conduct. (Ramirez v. Plough, Inc. (1993) 6 Cal.4th 539, 547. Put differently, the plaintiff “‘borrows’ statutes to prove duty of care and standard of care.” (Johnson v. Honeywell Internat., Inc. (2009) 179 Cal.App.4th 549, 558.) 

            CRCD’s demurrer to Plaintiff’s negligence per se claim is SUSTAINED.  It is not a distinct cause of action.  To the extent CRCD’s demurrer asserts that Plaintiff’s Third Cause of Action is uncertain because it “comingles” negligence and premises liability, the Court does not find CRCD’s argument persuasive.  The elements of a cause of action for premises liability are the same as those for negligence: duty, breach, causation, and damages. (Ortega v. Kmart Corp. (2001) 26 Cal.4th 1200, 1205; see Civ. Code § 1714(a).)  Both theories of liability are negligence-based and both have the same elements; the Court finds no uncertainty.

            In accordance with the above, CRCD’s Demurrer to Plaintiff’s the Third Cause of Action is OVERRULED as to Negligence/Premises Liability and SUSTAINED as to Negligence Per Se.

iii.             Fifth Cause of Action for Public Nuisance

            “A public nuisance is one which affects at the same time an entire community or neighborhood, or any considerable number of persons, although the extent of the annoyance of damage inflicted upon individuals may be unequal.”  (Civ. Code § 3480; see Birke v. Oakwood Worldwide (2009) 169 Cal.App.4th 1540, 1548.)  “A private person may maintain an action for a public nuisance, if it is specially injurious to himself, but not otherwise.”  (Civ. Code § 3493.) 

"In determining whether something is a public nuisance, the focus must be upon whether an entire neighborhood or community or at least a considerable number of persons are affected in the manner and by the factors that make the thing a nuisance under Civil Code section 3479. [Citations.] In other words, a private nuisance does not become a public nuisance merely because the public may be said to be affected in some tangential manner rather than specifically in the manner set forth in Civil Code section 3479." (Beck Development Co. v. Southern Pacific Transportation Co. (1996) 44 Cal.App.4th 1160, 1209.)

CRCD demurs to Plaintiff’s Fifth Cause of Action on the grounds that Plaintiff fails to allege a public nuisance because the Complaint only alleges that the bed bugs were present in Plaintiff’s unit and another unit but it does not allege that the bed bugs infested neighboring residences or otherwise affected the surrounding community.  CRCD also argues that Plaintiff has not alleged she suffered a different kind of harm, only that she suffered a different degree of harm.

            Plaintiff argues in opposition that her Fifth Cause of Action is viable because she alleges to have suffered a different type of harm (financial harm) in additional to a similar harm suffered by the public at large and the public at large in this case includes all guests that visit the subject property that can transfer the bed bugs to the rest of the community at large.

            As to any effect on the public, in her Fifth Cause of Action Plaintiff alleges “[t]he uninhabitable conditions (bed bug infestations) at the Subject Property and Subject Unit, as described herein constitutes a public nuisance . . . in that these defective and uninhabitable conditions (bed bug infestations) are injurious to the health and safety of Plaintiff and the public at large, indecent and offenses to the senses of Plaintiff and the public at large . . .” (Complaint, ¶ 102.)  The nuisance has a deleterious effect on the public at large while at the same time specially injuring Plaintiff . . .” (Id., ¶ 103.) These allegations are conclusory and speculative; Plaintiff alleges nothing more than that the bedbugs at issue here are a public nuisance (a legal conclusion) and they have a deleterious effect on the public (a factual conclusion and speculation).  The Court is not required to accept as true conclusions of fact or law when ruling upon a demurrer. (See Fox v. JAMDAT Mobile, Inc. (2010) 185 Cal.App.4th 1068, 1078 (A judge does not assume the truth of pleaded deductions, contentions, or conclusions of law or fact).) And Plaintiff’s argument made in opposition that all guests that visit the subject property could transfer the bed bugs from the subject property to the rest of the community at large (see Opp, p. 5:16-19) is not an allegation made in the Complaint.  Plaintiff does not adequately allege a public nuisance.

As to damages, a “plaintiff cannot recover damages for a public nuisance, so far as the injury is to the public only, but if he has suffered damage peculiar to himself”.  (Grigsby v. Clear Lake Water Works Co. (1870) 40 Cal. 396, 406.)  This idea has been consistently repeated throughout California case law.  (See Frost v. Los Angeles (1919) 181 Cal. 22, 24-25 (“To entitle a party to sue to enjoin a public nuisance he must allege and prove facts showing that it causes special injury to himself in person or property, and of a character different in kind from that suffered by the general public”); see also County of Santa Clara v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2006) 137 Cal. App. 4th 292, 307 fn. 6 (“no mention of a “special injury” to the City, which is necessary for a plaintiff to recover damages in a public nuisance action).) Here, Plaintiff alleges she “suffered harm that was different form the type of harm suffered by the public at large in that Plaintiff has suffered economic harm (paying to reside at the bed bug-infested [sic] property).” (Complaint, ¶ 103.)   Plaintiff’s rent, however, is a contractual obligation resulting from her leasing the Property. (See Complaint, ¶ 17.)  While Plaintiff’s may be entitled to recover a portion of her rent paid subject to a determination of whether the Property was uninhabitable and for how long, it remains that Plaintiff’s rent is not an economic damage caused by the bedbugs.  As currently pleaded, the danger to the public (if any) caused by a bed bug infestation is the same injury complained of by Plaintiff, i.e., exposure to bed bugs, including being bitten, and suffering financial harm for disposing infested items and paying rent despite a bed bug infestation.

            CRCD’s Demurrer to Plaintiff’s Fifth Cause of Action is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

iv.              Eighth Cause of Action for Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress

The essential elements for intentional infliction of emotional distress (“IIED”) are: (1) outrageous conduct; (2) an intent to cause or a reckless disregard of the possibility of causing emotional distress; (3) severe or extreme emotional distress; and (4) actual and proximate cause of the emotional distress by the outrageous conduct.  (Symonds v. Mercury Sav. & Loan Ass’n (1990) 225 Cal.App.3d 1458,1468.)  Outrageous conduct is conduct that “exceed[s] all bounds usually tolerated by decent society, of a nature which is especially calculated to cause, and does cause, mental distress of a very serious kind.”  (Ochoa v. Superior Court (1985) 39 Cal.3d 159, 166, fn. 5.)          

CRCD demurs to Plaintiff’s Eighth Cause of Action on the ground that Plaintiff fails to allege “extreme and outrageous” conduct, citing Stoiber v. Honeychuck (1980) 101 Cal.App.3d 903 (“Stoiber”) for its contention that, to state a cause of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress in a habitability situation, there must be additional abusive conduct by the landlord in the form of actual knowledge and active concealment.  CRCD argues that the Complaint only alleges “garden variety negligence in property management” in its allegations that Defendant knew of bed bugs, was repeatedly notified, and failed to abate, but the Complaint does not allege intentional harassment or deliberate exposure to known danger and therefore the Complaint fails to allege sufficient “extreme and outrageous” conduct.

            Also citing Stoiber and additionally citing Burnett v. Chimney Sweep (2004) 123 Cal.App.4th 1057 (“Burnett’), Plaintiff argues Defendant’s malicious failure to correct the alleged bedbug infestation amounts suffices as a basis for her cause of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress. Plaintiff argues that a landlord’s knowing, intentional and willful failure to correct a defective condition may constitute “extreme and outrageous” conduct sufficient to sustain a cause of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress and contends that the specificity of her allegations suffices to allege such conduct by CDCR.   

            Plaintiff alleges that she moved into the Premises in November 2021 and noticed bedbugs in approximately 2022.  (Complaint, ¶ 17.)  Albeit on information and belief, Plaintiff alleges that CRCD was actually and constructively aware of the bedbug issue prior to Plaintiff moving in. (Id., ¶ 22, 131.)  In 2022, 2023, 2024, 2025 and 2026 Plaintiff alleges she notified CRCD’s managers and managing agents of the problem, but CRCD has failed to remedy the problem. (Id., ¶¶ 17-19, 131.)  In addition to notifying CRCD, Plaintiff further filed a complaint with LAHD. (Id., ¶ 74.)  Despite Plaintiff’s complaints to CRCD and LAHD and despite the condition of the unit, Plaintiff has continued paying rent and CRCD has continued accepting rent. (Id., ¶¶ 24, 71.)  And despite Plaintiffs’’ years-long pattern of complaints, the bedbug issue continues and affects not only Plaintiff’s unit but a second unit as well (at least). (Id., ¶¶ 134, 135.) Construing Plaintiff’s allegations as true, as the Court must at this procedural juncture, the facts as alleged are that CRCD knew of and concealed a severe bedbug infestation, allowed Plaintiff to move into the Property (which was infested with bedbugs), and has since refused to remedy the bedbug problem but has continued to demand and collect rent.  Although Plaintiff’s description of CRCD’s’ actions as “intentional and willful” are conclusory, it can be inferred from the four-year span of the allegations and the allegations that the bedbug issue affects more than on unit (both if taken as true) that CRCD was undoubtedly aware of the problem and chose not to act. On these facts it cannot be said that such conduct is not extreme and outrageous as a matter of law such that CRCD’s demurrer should be sustained.  Whether these circumstance amount to “extreme and outrageous” conduct is a factual issue for a determination by the trier of fact rather than at the demurrer stage.

            CRCD’s Demurrer to Plaintiff’s Eighth Cause of Action is OVERRULED.

v.                Ninth Cause of Action for Fraudulent Concealment

            CRCD demurs to Plaintiff’s Eight Cause of Action, arguing that Plaintiff fails to plead fraudulent concealment with the heightened specificity required when pleading fraud against a corporation.  In opposition, Plaintiff argues that the Complaint sufficiently pleads fraud based on concealment because the facts lie more within Defendant’s knowledge than Plaintiff’s.

            In general, the facts constituting fraud must be alleged factually and specifically as to every element of fraud, as the policy of “liberal construction” of the pleadings will not be invoked. (Lazar v. Superior Court (1995) 12 Cal.4th 631, 645 (“Lazar”).) And to properly allege fraud against a corporation, a plaintiff must plead the names of the person allegedly making the false representations, their authority to speak, to whom they spoke, what they said or wrote, and when it was said or written. (Tarmann v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. (1991) 2 Cal.App.4th 153, 157 (“Tarmann”).)¿ 

However, the specificity requirement is “relaxed when it is apparent from the allegations that the defendant necessarily possesses knowledge of the facts.” (Quelimane Co. v. Steward Title Guaranty Co. (1998) 19 Cal.4th 26, 27.) Further, the pleading rule for corporations is inapplicable to a claim of fraudulent concealment. (Alfaro v. Community Housing Improvement System & Planning Association, Inc. (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1356, 1384 (“How does one show ‘how’ and ‘by what means’ something didn’t happen, or ‘when’ it never happened, or ‘where’ it never happened?”).)¿ 

The Supreme Court’s recent decision in Rattagan re-affirms and clarifies this rule; although it held that the same specificity pleading standard for affirmative fraud applies to fraudulent concealment claims, it clarified that “the focus of inquiry shifts to the unique elements of the claim. [Citations.]” (Rattagan, supra, 17 Cal.5th at p. 43.) Thus, in a concealment claim based on a defendant’s duty to disclose arising from the defendant’s exclusive knowledge, the complaint must specifically allege “(1) the content of the omitted facts, (2) defendant’s awareness of the materiality of those facts, (3) the inaccessibility of the facts to plaintiff, (4) the general point at which the omitted facts should or could have been revealed, and (5) justifiable and actual reliance, either through action or forbearance, based on the defendant’s omission.” (Id. at p. 43-44.)

            Here, Plaintiff alleges: (1) the content of the omitted facts (that there was a bedbug infestation at the unit rented by Plaintiff)(see Complaint, ¶ 146); (2) CRCD’s awareness of the materiality of the omitted fact (that the bedbug infestation “posed a danger to [Plaintiff’s] physical health and well-being”)(Id., ¶ 145); (3) the inaccessibility of those facts to Plaintiff (Id., ¶ 146, 148 (Plaintiff did not know of the infestation and could not have discovered it); and (5) justifiable and actual reliance (Plaintiff rented the unit)(Id., ¶ 17.)    Plaintiff’s allegations are sufficient to maintain her cause of action for fraudulent concealment.

            CRCD’s Demurrer to Plaintiff’s Ninth Cause of Action for Fraudulent Concealment is OVERRULED.

vi.              Tenth Cause of Action for Violation of Business & Professions Code § 17200[3]

            CRCD demurs to Plaintiff’s Tenth Cause of Action on the ground that the Complaint includes by reference all preceding allegations, including the demand for compensatory, general, special and punitive damages which are not recoverable via a Business & Professions Code § 17200 cause of action.  CRCD further argue that “[t]he Prayer for Relief, which Plaintiff seeks on “all causes of action,” demands compensatory damages, general and special damages, and punitive damages” again which are not recoverable via the instant cause of action. (Mot., p. 13:23-25.)

            Plaintiff argues she seeks restitution for the damages she had to pay out of pocket as a result of the bed bug infestation and for rent paid to Defendant during the time the property was uninhabitable due to the bed bug infestation. Plaintiff argues the Complaint alleges the acts and omissions that show Defendants engaged in unfair competition within the meaning of Business and Professions Code §17200 thus entitled she to injunctive and restitution relief as provided by California Business and Professions Code §17203.

            To state a cause of action for Unfair Business Practices, a plaintiff must allege (1) Defendant has engaged in more than one unlawful, unfair, or fraudulent transaction, including unfair, deceptive, untrue or misleading advertising; (2) Plaintiff's right to restitution, if any. Damages are not recoverable; (3) Plaintiff's right to injunctive relief, if any. (Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200 et seq.; Dean Witter Reynolds, Inc. v. Superior Court (1989) 211 Cal.App.3d 758, 773-774.)  “By proscribing ‘any unlawful’ business practice, ‘[Business and Professions Code] section 17200 “borrows” violations of other laws and treats them as unlawful practices’ that the [UCL] makes independently actionable.” (Cel-Tech Communications, Inc. v. Los Angeles Cellular Telephone Co. (1999) 20 Cal.4th 163, 180 (“Cel-Tech”).)  “An unlawful business practice under [Business and Professions Code] section 17200 is ‘an act or practice, committed pursuant to business activity, that is at the same time forbidden by law. [Citation.]’ ” (Progressive West Ins. Co. v. Superior Court (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 263, 287.) “Virtually any law—federal, state or local—can serve as a predicate for an action under Business and Professions Code section 17200. [Citation.]” (Ticconi v. Blue Shield of California Life & Health Ins. Co. (2008) 160 Cal.App.4th 528, 539.)  A landlord’s violation of housing codes can be the basis of a Business & Professions Code § 17200 cause of action.  (See e.g. City & County of S.F. v. Sainez (2000) 77 Cal.App.4th 1302, 1308 (Code violations constituted unfair business practices).)

            Here, Plaintiff alleges Defendants were unjustly enriched at Plaintiff’s expense and Plaintiff is entitled to restitution, and Plaintiff has alleged Defendant’s violation of housing codes. (Complaint ¶¶ 158, 160.) Plaintiff has sufficiently pleaded her Tenth Cause of Action.  That Plaintiff’s Complaint is worded such that it would appear she seeks damages not awardable pursuant to her cause of action for violation of Business & Professions Code § 17200 does not render it subject to demurrer for failure to state a cause of action.  Plaintiff states a cause of action, including damages which are recoverable.

            CRD’s Demurrer is OVERRULED as to Plaintiff’s Tenth Cause of Action.

2.               Motion to Strike

i.                 Punitive Damages

CRCD moves to strike Plaintiff’s allegations seeking punitive damages, arguing that the allegations are not supported by specific facts establishing oppression, fraud or malice or the corporate ratification element required by Civil Code § 3294(b).  In essence, CRCD seeks to strike all allegations that it acted with malice or ill intent because said allegations are conclusory.  Though conclusory allegations can be stricken, they will generally not be stricken when sufficiently supported by factual allegations in the complaint. (See, e.g., Perkins v. Sup. Ct. (1981) 117 CA3d 1, 6.) Additionally, the line between ultimate facts and conclusions of law is often unclear and the language sought to be stricken must be read not in isolation but in the context of the entire Complaint. (Id.)  Here, the Court has overruled CRCD’s demurrer to causes of action which allow punitive damages; accordingly, the Court finds that some factual basis is alleged to support Plaintiff’s allegations of intentional and malicious conduct.

As to the corporate ratification allegations, CRCD moves to strike on the ground Plaintiff does not allege that its “advance knowledge and conscious disregard, authorization, ratification or act of oppression, fraud, or malice [was] on the part of an officer, director, or managing agent.” (See Civ. Code § 3294(b).) First, Civil Code § 3294(b) does not set forth the allegations Plaintiff must make to state a cause of action seeking punitive damages against a corporation, it sets forth what Plaintiff must prove in order to recover punitive damages against a corporation after “it is proven by clear and convincing evidence that the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice.” (Id., §3294(a).)  Accordingly, Plaintiff’s Complaint is drawn in conformity with the laws of California as to the causes of action for which CRCD’s Demurrers have been overruled.

CRCD’s Motion to Strike Plaintiff’s punitive damages allegations and prayer is DENIED.

ii.               Private Attorney General Allegations

CRCD moves to strike Plaintiff’s allegations and prayer seeking attorney fees pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure § 1021.5, arguing that such fees are awardable only by post-judgment motion, are not a substantive cause of action or item of pleaded relief, and do not apply to an individual habitability action. Plaintiff argues in opposition that attorney fees may be awarded pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure § 1021.5 because Plaintiff has pleaded a significant benefit to the public, the necessity and financial burden of private enforcement here makes an award of fees appropriate, and justice requires that any fees not be paid out of Plaintiff’s recovery.

First, unsupported attorneys' fees allegations need not be stricken pursuant to a motion to strike, since later discovery may reveal a basis for their recovery. (Camenisch v. Superior Court (1996) 44 Cal.App.4th 1689, 1699.)   And “t]here is no requirement that the intent to seek attorney fees under section 1021.5 must be pleaded in the underlying action. [Citation.] Such fees are not part of the underlying cause of action, but are incidents to the cause and are properly awarded after entry of a … judgment … .” (Snatchko v. Westfield LLC (2010) 187 Cal.App.4th 469, 497 (“Snatchko”)(quoting Washburn v. City of Berkeley (1987) 195 Cal.App.3d 578, 583).)  Indeed, because there is no pleading requirement, at least one Court of Appeal has held that it is error to strike attorney fees sought pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure § 1021.5. (See Snatchko, supra, 187 Cal.App.4th at p. 497 (“As there was no requirement they be pled at all, the trial court erred in striking Snatchko's prayer for attorney fees based on a failure to adequately plead their basis, and Snatchko's failure to reallege his request for fees in his first amended complaint does not waive or forfeit his ability to seek them at the conclusion of this case”).)  Accordingly, the Court determines that – even were it inclined to issue such an order - an order striking Plaintiff’s Code of Civil Procedure § 1021.5 allegations would be an exercise in futility; such claims need not be pleaded, may be sought even if not pleaded, and may be sought after having been stricken.

The Court declines to make a futile order.  CRCD’s arguments as to whether Code of Civil Procedure § 1021.5 fees are warranted in this matter are more properly addressed when and if a motion is made, at which point the Court will be more equipped to determine if Plaintiff is the successful party and if “an important right affecting the public interest” was vindicated via the instant action, along with the other criteria set forth in Code of Civil Procedure § 1021.5.

CRCD’s Motion to Strike Plaintiff’s Private Attorney General allegations and prayer is DENIED.

iii.             Injunctive Relief

CRCD moves to strike Plaintiff’s prayer for injunctive relief, arguing that “Plaintiff has not pled the elements necessary to support equitable injunctive relief.” (Mot., p. 5:5.)

The Court has overruled CRCD’s Demurrer to Plaintiff’s Tenth Cause of Action for Violation of Business & Professions Code § 17200, which cause of action has as a remedy injunctive relief.  Accordingly, the Court finds that, contrary to CRCD’s assertion, Plaintiff has pleaded the necessary elements to support injunctive relief.

CRCD’s Motion to Strike Plaintiff’s prayer for injunctive relief is DENIED.

III.           CONCLUSION

CRCD’s Demurrers to Plaintiff’s Eighth, Ninth and Tenth Causes of Action are OVERRULED.

CRCD’s Demurrer to Plaintiff’s Third Cause of Action is SUSTAINED IN PART and DENIED IN PART.

CRCD’s Demurrers to Plaintiff’s Second and Fifth Causes of Action are SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.  

Where leave to amend has been granted, Plaintiff’s First Amended Complaint is ordered to be filed and served no later than September 4, 2026.

CRCD’s Motion to Strike is DENIED.
