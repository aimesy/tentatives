DEPARTMENT I LAW AND MOTION RULINGS

If the parties wish to submit on the tentative ruling and avoid a court appearance on the matter, the moving party must contact the opposing party and all other parties who have appeared in the action and confirm that each will submit on the tentative ruling. Please call the court no later than 4:30 p.m. on the court day before the hearing, leave a message with the court clerk at (310) 260-3629 advising her that all parties will submit on the tentative ruling and waive hearing, and finally, serve notice of the Court's ruling on all parties entitled to receive service. If any party declines to submit on the tentative ruling, then no telephone call is necessary, and all parties should appear at the hearing.

Case Number: 24SMCV02488    Hearing Date: June 26, 2026    Dept: I

This is a motion to compel further.  The underlying case is a construction dispute.  Defendant propounded written discovery and claims that the responses to special interrogatories 5 and 6 are not sufficient.  Interrogatory 5 seeks identification of the subcontractor’s supervisor and interrogatory 6 is to set forth the amount billed for that subcontractor.  Defendant also complains about requests for production 8 and 18.  RFP 8 seeks all communications between plaintiff and anyone else concerning the property, and RFP 18 is for accounting books and records regarding the project.  The court notes that plaintiff agreed to provide responsive documents, but objected that the requests are vague and ambiguous.  In opposition, plaintiff says that further responses should be done and served before the hearing.  It has been hard, they claim, to get older documents, but that ought to be done by now.

 

As to the interrogatories, the court tends to agree with defendant.  The response is essentially to refer to interrogatory 1, but the response to interrogatory 1 does not give the information.  Therefore, the motion is GRANTED as to the special interrogatories.  Verified supplemental responses without objection are to be provided within 30 days.  As to the RFP, the motion would seem to be MOOT, although plaintiff will need to say whether any documents are being withheld on the basis of the objection and, if plaintiff is standing on “vague and ambiguous,” plaintiff will need to set forth how it is defining the offending terms for purposes of the production.

Case Number: 24SMCV03557    Hearing Date: June 26, 2026    Dept: I

These are two motions to compel further responses.  They were filed by defendant.  Defendant contends that it served discovery on December 18, 2025, and additional discovery on January 6, 2026.  Defendant states that plaintiff asked for an extension on the discovery.  Defendant states that it agreed to extend time on the original discovery to February 2, 2026, but plaintiff wanted more additional time.  The court ultimately gave plaintiff relief on an ex parte application but ordered the discovery responses to be served within 30 days of February 3, 2026.  Responses were purportedly served on March 5, 2026, but defendant states that they were objection only responses and does not agree that they were timely served.  Defendant sent a meet and confer letter to plaintiff.  Plaintiff apparently left a message and defendant agreed to meet and confer, but according to the defense, plaintiff never called back and never met and conferred.  In opposition, plaintiff states that the motions are procedurally improper because they the two relating to interrogatories should not be brought together.  Plaintiff further states that he served proper responses on March 5, 2026, so to the extent defendant contends that plaintiff failed to comply with the court’s order, the defense is wrong.  Accordingly, plaintiff argues, the proper motion is a motion to compel further, not a motion to compel.  And now a motion to compel would be time barred because the 45 day period to bring such a motion has passed.  Further, plaintiff says, the responses were proper.  Finally, plaintiff states that it has offered to serve supplemental responses so as to incorporate the general objections into each response.  And plaintiff seeks sanctions.

 

In reply, defendant states that that the most recent responses were in fact not served until May 29, 2026, and plaintiff still has not acknowledged defendant’s offer to meet and confer.  More troubling, defendant states that it has a technology expert who has checked the emails received by defendant to see if there was an email from plaintiff on March 5, 2026, and that no such email was found.  According to defendant, plaintiff’s election to provide only a PDF of the metadata is not helpful because such a document is easy to manipulate.  Defendant suggests that native files be produced by everyone, including metadata, so the court can see who the liar is.  Further, defendant contends that the court should impose an evidentiary sanction because it is automatic.

 

The parties need to stop this.  The court is not going to hold a trial as to who is manipulating metadata.  The court does not need to do so.  The court has reviewed the discovery responses.  Assuming, without deciding, that they were served on March 5, 2026 (and therefore timely), there remains a problem.  They are, as defendant states, objection only responses.  There is nothing improper about such responses—if, but only if, each objection is well taken such that the question cannot be answered.  However, the objections here, even assuming proper format, were not of that ilk.  They are in patent bad faith.  For example, RFP 1 is for documents showing each request plaintiff made to review HOA records and the response.  The response was an incorporation of the general objections.  It also included the assertion that the request was overly broad (it wasn’t) was unintelligible by using the phrase “breached the Governing Documents” (which was not in this RFP), and was unduly burdensome and oppressive (it wasn’t).  There was an objection that defendant had not shown a “compelling need” for the discovery or that there was a less intrusive means to get it (which is not a valid objection).  Plaintiff went on to object because it required him to make a compilation (it didn’t) and that it requested information that could not be known pending further discovery (it didn’t) and that plaintiff was still doing discovery and might find something later and might (or might not) supplement (which makes no sense).  That objection is essentially repeated for each RFP.  Special Interrogatory 1 is to identify every communication plaintiff made to request a review of the HOA’s records.  Plaintiff responded by objecting that defendant already had the information so the request was unduly burdensome and that the used of defined terms made the request unintelligible.  The burdensomeness objection is ill taken.  Even if defendant ought to have those records, the defendant is entitled to know what plaintiff has to be sure that there are no surprises at trial.  And the use of defined terms did not make the interrogatories unintelligible.  There is also an overbreadth objection because the interrogatory was not bounded as to time or scope and was ambiguous and that this was not calculated to lead to admissible evidence.  That is also improper.  The interrogatories by their subject matter were bounded.  Those objections were essentially the same for every interrogatory.

 

The bad faith is obvious and patent.  The court had given plaintiff the extension sought on the theory that plaintiff would act in good faith.  The court was apparently wrong about that and the court confesses to have being misled by the plaintiff into granting the request.  Had the court known that plaintiff planned on abusing the order that the court had given, the court would have denied the emergency application for more time.  Indeed, the application was made in bad faith because no additional time was needed to submit bad faith responses like the ones at issue.

 

Accordingly, the motions are GRANTED.  Plaintiff will provide verified responses without objection within 10 court days.  Documents will be provided at the same time.  The court is aware that 10 court days is short.  That is deliberate.  Plaintiff is playing games with the defense and games with the court.  The request by defendant for sanctions is GRANTED in the amount of $2480.00 for both motions combined.  It is payable within 30 days to defense counsel.  The court very much hopes that plaintiff will turn over a new leaf in this case and litigate properly.

Case Number: 25SMCV00592    Hearing Date: June 26, 2026    Dept: I

This is an interesting motion.  Plaintiff sued defendant claiming injuries.  Defendant demanded that plaintiff submit to a medical examination.  Plaintiff agreed and, pursuant to CCP section 2032.610, demanded a detailed report as to the findings of the examination.  That was provided (although there was some motion practice about the level of detail).  Plaintiff now wants to depose the expert conducting the examination, Dr. Sadun.  Plaintiff served a deposition notice to which defendant objected claiming that at present, Dr. Sadun is an expert and can be deposed only under the expert discovery statutes.  In other words, defendant agrees that Dr. Sadun can be deposed eventually and that there is no work product protection that can be asserted as to the examination, but not yet.  Plaintiff argues that Dr. Sadun is now a percipient witness to the extent of the examination and can be deposed just like any other percipient witness.  It is an interesting question, and the court has found no case directly on point.  Both sides refer to Kennedy v. Lucky Stores (1998) 64 Cal.App.4th 674, but it does not really answer the question.  There, the plaintiff had submitted to an examination and the examiner had been designated as an expert.  However, before the expert was deposed, the defense withdrew the designation and re-designated the expert as a consulting expert.  When plaintiff sought to depose the expert, the defense objected.  The Court of Appeal allowed the deposition to go forward.  Even though there is no general right to depose a consulting expert, the court found that section 2032.610 contemplated that a deposition be allowed to take place and that the normal protections afforded to a consulting expert would not pertain.

 

The court tends to think that plaintiff has the right of it in terms of entitlement to depose Dr. Sadun.  It could well be that defendant never intends to designate Dr. Sadun as an expert at all.  If that is the case, then there is no right under the expert discovery statutes to depose Dr. Sadun at all either, but under Kennedy there is plainly a right to depose Dr. Sadun even so.  As the Kennedy court found, it is section 2032.610 that provides for that right.  Moreover, defendant’s reading leads to a problem.  There is no work product protection generally with regard to a testifying expert.  Accordingly, if section 2032.610 were meant to apply only in the expert discovery phase, in most cases the work product language would mean nothing anyway.  On balance, the court believes that Dr. Sadun can be deposed and that plaintiff need not wait for the expert discovery phase to do so.  But that is not quite the end of it.  There is the tactical oddity of plaintiff potentially getting two depositions of this person: one now, and one during expert discovery.  That might not be a real problem; plaintiff states that counsel “does not seek to depose Dr. Sadun as an expert witness.”  But that is ambiguous.  It could mean that plaintiff is foregoing the opportunity to take another deposition of Dr. Sadun even if Dr. Sadun is later designated as an expert.  But it could also mean that counsel does not intend to ask those sorts of questions at this deposition but reserves the right to do so later.  If the former, well and good; the court would be inclined to grant the motion.  But if the latter, the court is not sure.  Depositions are an imposition (not to mention expensive), and it seems tactically unfair to give plaintiff essentially two cracks at this person.  The court will inquire.

 

The bottom line is that the court believes that the statute does not force plaintiff to wait for the expert designation to depose Dr. Sadun.  But the court also believes that (like any human witness), there should be one deposition of Dr. Sadun, not two.  Because the issue seems to the court to be novel, the request for sanctions is DENIED.

 

The court notes that this would be an interesting question for appellate court resolution.  The court would not take it amiss were either party to seek writ review of this question.  (Not that the court would ever take it amiss; it is just that this question might be more writworthy than others.)  The court notes that the trial is not until about a year from now.

Case Number: 25SMCV05046    Hearing Date: June 26, 2026    Dept: I

This is a motion to compel further responses.  Essentially, plaintiff claims to have been wrongfully terminated and sued her employer.  Her employer offered her additional compensation if she would sign a release of all claims, including FEHA claims.  The release included the statutory protections for such waivers.  Plaintiff initially refused.  However, she contends that she came to be in dire financial straits and ultimately agreed to sign the release in return for the money.  Plaintiff does not contend that she did not have adequate time (to the extent required by law) to review the document or to seek advice of counsel, and plaintiff does not contend that she revoked the agreement in the way set forth in the statute.  Defendant paid plaintiff the money owed, but plaintiff has sued.  Defendant’s principal argument at this stage is the release, and defendant has brought a summary judgment motion to that effect.  Plaintiff contends that she was under economic duress when she signed the contract, and therefore the contract cannot be enforced.  The substance of that debate will, presumably, be hashed out during the summary judgment proceedings; the court is not opining now.

 

What is at issue here is discovery that plaintiff propounded to defendant dealing with the circumstances of her termination and job performance—that is, the merits.  Defendant objects to that discovery on the basis that, until the MSJ is heard, it is premature.  Trial is not until December 2027, so there is plenty of time, defendant argues, for that discovery to go forward if the MSJ—currently set for July 14, 2026—is denied.  Plaintiff contends that this is relevant even to that motion.

 

The court has at least some sympathy for defendant’s argument.  If the release is valid, the whole point of it is to forestall litigation—like this—and the expense of it.  It undermines the value of the release if defendant has to submit to a full barrage of discovery anyway.  But the court has sympathy for plaintiff’s argument, too.  Plaintiff needs to defeat the MSJ or she is out of court.  It is understandable that she wants discovery.

 

The right way to have done this would have been for defendant to have sought a protective order upon being served with the discovery.  First, that would have put the burden where it belongs—on the defense.  There is nothing in the Discovery Act that gives the defense the right to self help in this way.  If it believes that the discovery is improper because of the MSJ, the right thing to do is to ask the court to limit discovery to that topic and to set the early MSJ hearing date.  Plaintiff would then have had the chance to oppose, and then we could have proceeded in an orderly fashion—either way.  But defendant did not do that.  Rather, defendant unilaterally refused to provide responses.  And the time to oppose the MSJ has almost come and gone.  Which puts us in this awkward situation.

 

One option is for the court to grant the motion because there is no basis in the Code to deny it.  One problem with that choice is that by the time plaintiff gets the discovery, the time to oppose the motion will have run, so the motion will need to go off calendar, which the court hates to do.  Another option would be to deny the motion, but to infer that any documents that exist out there would support plaintiff’s case.  If plaintiff can show that even hypothetically (but reasonably) there is a document that would make out her case, that would be enough to defeat the motion.  After all, to win on an MSJ, defendant needs to show that plaintiff does not have now and will not have later any evidence that could create a triable issue of fact.  Normally, that is shown by a paucity of evidence in opposition, but where the paucity is due to the defendant’s own conduct, the inference would go the other way.

 

What the court will do is this.  The court will inquire as to which interrogatories plaintiff believes might be relevant to the MSJ.  The court will also hear from the defense, but the court will resolve all doubts in plaintiff’s favor.  If there is any argument that any of the documents might rationally and reasonably be pertinent to opposing that motion, the court will grant the motion and likely continue the MSJ for some number of months.  On the other hand, if plaintiff cannot articulate even a plausible reason why this information might be pertinent to opposing the MSJ, the court would be inclined to deny the motion.  One way to think about it is this.  Assume that the discovery would conclusively show that defendant did terminate plaintiff’s employment for discriminatory reasons.  Defendant’s position is that it does not matter for the motion because those torts were released.  For purposes of the motion only, the court assumes defendant is conceding that point, arguendo.  So, the question for plaintiff is what she hopes to discover that would defeat the motion beyond proof that defendant fired her for unlawful reasons.  That is the question to which the court seeks plaintiff’s guidance.

 

However the motion comes out, the request for sanctions is DENIED.

 


Case Number: SC126806    Hearing Date: June 26, 2026    Dept: I

This is a motion to compel Jane Doe’s deposition.  The waters became muddy due to some conduct by Doe (“Doe” refers to Jane Doe only unless otherwise stated).  There is a trial set in this case for early October, which is soon, but not in the context of a case that is over a decade old.  However, very recently, Doe dismissed her claims against almost all of the other parties.  What remains of all the cases but one are fairly discrete claims by May Doe and claims by a trust that purports to own a unit in the condominium development.  There are also various other claims and cross complaints, but all of them relate to ownership of that unit.  When the court saw that, the court had assumed that, for whatever reasons, Doe had elected not to go forward with the litigation as to the various torts she had alleged and pursued for so long.  But at the hearing two days ago, the court was informed that its understanding was wrong.  In fact, one case had been stayed.  The reason it had been stayed was that it was duplicative of the main case—SC126806—and therefore added nothing.  In hindsight, the court would have been better off just consolidating the two.  But in either event, it was stayed, and Doe did not dismiss her claims in that case.  Accordingly, as the court learned, this was something of self-help by Doe.  By dismissing her claims in the case that was set for trial, she believed she had bought a unilateral extension of that case until some later point.  She asserts that she was not trying to play games, but rather that for health issues—her own and her mother’s—she simply could not focus on the case at present. 

 

At Wednesday’s hearing, the court was surprised by this.  The right way to have done this would have been by way of a motion to bifurcate the case or to continue the trial.  That would have allowed all parties to be heard and the court to have fashioned some remedy that made sense for all concerned.  The court does not want to jeopardize Doe’s health.  But at the same time, defendants have rights as well.  They have been fighting this case for over a decade and state that they want their day in court to clear their name.  True, some of the reason for the two cases (and the delay) is that the Legislature enacted various revival laws that revived what would otherwise have been time-barred claims.  Rather than assert that the original case was now revived, Doe elected to file a new case, thereby complicating an already too complex procedural quagmire.  In any event, the self-help aspect has thrown this case into even more disarray.  To be clear, the court is not faulting Doe for filing the 2024 case; it is faulting her for engaging in self-help, thereby making an already difficult situation worse.

 

At issue here is defendant’s motion to compel plaintiff’s deposition.  There is no doubt but that the deposition is essential—especially if Doe’s tort claims against Olson and others are alive and kicking.  When the deposition notice was served (which was March 2, 2026), Doe said that the proposed date was not good because she was ill but that she would provide an alternative date.  However, as time passed, no date was provided.  So a new notice was sent out.  Doe said that her attorney (presumably for purposes of the deposition only, as she is generally self-represented) was not available on that date.  She also stated that she believed that the depositions she had earlier noticed should be finished first.  The defense disagreed, especially because the case in which Doe had noticed depositions did not really implicate many of the issues raised in her case.  Plaintiff still did not provide a deposition date.  The court notes that one issue had been the lack of an agreed upon confidentiality order, but that has since been resolved.  That led to the instant motion to compel.  Plaintiff, presumably thinking that the motion would go off calendar due to her dismissal of the claims in SC126806, did not oppose the motion, although she claims to want to oppose it.

 

The court is inclined to hear the motion, and to GRANT it.  Plaintiff elected to engage in self-help rather than do this in an orderly fashion in which all parties could be heard.  That was her choice, but it was an unfortunate one and she must bear the consequences of it.  This case has been pending for a long, long time.  It is plain to the court that plaintiff needs to be deposed.  She is the principal witness on her side for most of the events of which she complains.  The trial is in four months.  There is no basis at this time to delay further.  The protective order is in place; plaintiff can designate portions of the transcript as confidential or highly confidential as is appropriate.  The court will also say that to the extent that one of plaintiff’s concerns is the distress of having some of those she claims engaged in tortious conduct in the room, the court would agree and require that those individuals ought to attend remotely in such a way that they cannot be seen nor heard by plaintiff, nor heard by the court reporter.  (They can, of course, communicate with their counsel.)  They should be able to see plaintiff, however.  To pick the date, the court will order as follows.  No later than Thursday, July 2, 2026, at noon, plaintiff (or her deposition counsel) will email Olson’s counsel with five dates all of which are no earlier than July 13, 2026, nor later than August 10, 2026.  They will be weekdays.  No later than Monday, July 6, 2026, at noon, Olson’s counsel will email plaintiff choosing one of the five dates provided.  On the designated day, the deposition will begin at 9:30 am and conclude at 5:00 pm.  There will be a one hour lunch break and one 15 minute break in the morning and another in the afternoon.  If the deposition does not conclude, it will continue from day to day until concluded, weekends and holidays excepted.  The foregoing is not meant to be a judicial order that the presumptive time limit may be exceeded, but neither is the court ruling that circumstances would not justify exceeding it.  This will be the one and only deposition of Doe in the entire constellation of cases, so defense counsel (and plaintiffs’ counsel, for that matter) ought to coordinate.  The foregoing procedure may be changed without leave of court based on any written agreement by all parties.

 

The request for sanctions is DENIED.

 

If plaintiff wants to seek reconsideration of this order based on medical evidence—meaning from a treating physician under oath and with sufficient detail to be something that the opposition and the court can evaluate meaningfully—she is given leave to do so.

 

The court also has an ex parte application by Olson to advance the hearing date on his motion for summary judgment against May Doe.  The motion was filed on June 11, 2026.  That is a timely filed motion in the sense that it gives May Doe the full 81 days of notice and could have been set more than 30 days before the trial, although not by much.  The earliest hearing date that would comply with the statute would be September 3, which is 32 days before the trial.  Even so, it was timely and the court is duty-bound to hear it.  The question in the court’s mind is what is it that is going to be tried on October 5?  Right now, it is not clear that Jane Doe’s case is what will be tried.  That leaves the property issue and May Doe’s case.  In either situation, though, the court is inclined to DENY the application but to exercise its discretion to hear the motion less than 30 days before trial.  While the court cannot reduce the statutory notice period, it can elect to hear the motion less than 30 days before the trial.  The court notes that this solution addresses the points raised in May Doe’s opposition papers.
