Case Number: 20STCV47735    Hearing Date: August 3, 2026    Dept: 406

 

ROSALINA HARRIS,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

COUNTY OF LOS ANGELES, et al.,

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  20STCV47735

  Hearing Date:  August 3, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

defendants’ motion for summary judgment or adjudication (CRS# 5481)

 

 

I. BACKGROUND

            a. Plaintiff’s Action Against the County (20STCV47735)

On December 14, 2020, Plaintiff Rosalina Harris filed this action against Defendants County of Los Angeles and Lana Lee Choi. Plaintiff filed the operative First Amended Complaint on March 29, 2021. The complaint asserts (1) breach of contract, (2) breach of the duty of good faith and fair dealing, (3) municipal liability, (4) professional negligence, (5) breach of fiduciary duty, and (6) negligent infliction of emotional distress.

Plaintiff is a police officer who was sued in federal district court and held liable for the wrongful arrest of her neighbor, Crystal Holmes. Plaintiff alleges that the County failed to adequately defend and indemnify her in accordance with the parties’ agreement. Plaintiff alleges that her personal assets were placed at risk and subjected to enforcement even though she was led to believe otherwise.

This action has been resolved.

 

b. Plaintiff’s Action Against the Attorneys (21STCV32323)

On August 31, 2021, Plaintiff filed an action against Defendants Andrew Pongracz and Seki, Nishimura & Watase, LLP, alleging (1) professional negligence and (2) breach of fiduciary duty. These defendants were attorneys selected by the County to represent Plaintiff in the Holmes action. Plaintiff alleges that Defendants did not adequately represent her interests and failed to raise various defenses, including qualified immunity.

The two actions have been consolidated, with this case (20STCV47735) as the lead case.

            c. The Instant Motion

            On October 30, 2025, Defendants Pongracz and Seki, Nishimura & Watase, LLP filed the instant motion for summary judgment or adjudication. Plaintiff filed an opposition on February 20, 2026. Defendants filed a reply on March 2, 2026.

II. LEGAL STANDARD

The function of a motion for summary judgment or adjudication is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party cannot show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) Code of Civil Procedure section 437c(c) “requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.)

“[T]he party moving for summary judgment bears an initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing of the nonexistence of any triable issue of material fact; if he carries his burden of production, he causes a shift, and the opposing party is then subjected to a burden of production of his own to make a prima facie showing of the existence of a triable issue of material fact.” (Leyva v. Garcia (2018) 20 Cal.App.5th 1095, 1101.) To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce “substantial responsive evidence.” (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.) Courts “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.” (Dore v. Arnold Worldwide, Inc. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 384, 389.)

IV. DISCUSSION

            a. Professional Negligence

            Defendants argue that they cannot be liable for legal malpractice because they met the standard of care and did not cause Plaintiff’s injuries. Plaintiff’s legal malpractice claim is based on Defendants’ alleged failure to use reasonable care (Compl. ¶ 26(c)), properly analyze facts and law (id., ¶ 26(f)), treat Plaintiff fairly and reasonably (id., ¶ 26(e)), and timely raise defenses (id., ¶ 26(g)).

                        1. Defendants’ Actions

            Defendants argue that “Pongracz kept in constant contact with Plaintiff about each step of the Holmes Lawsuit. (Pongracz Deck, ¶¶ 6, 8, 24.) Pongracz discussed discovery, prepped Harris for her deposition, worked with Harris to find alternate forms of liability coverage in the event LA County chose not to pay. (Pongracz Decl., ¶¶ 6-9.) Plaintiff has not produced any evidence supporting the conclusory allegation that she was not treated fairly, and without evidence, there is no connection to the alleged injury.” (Mtn. 20:9-14.) Defendants point out that they succeeded in getting the claims against Plaintiff’s husband and daughter dismissed on summary judgment, leaving only the Section 1983 civil rights claim against Plaintiff.

            However, this does not foreclose a triable issue over whether Defendants satisfied the duty of care. Defendants simply restate their actions in the Holmes case and then, with no citation to legal authority or expert testimony, conclude that they met the standard of care as a matter of law. Not so. What actions meet the standard of care under the particular circumstances is a question for the trier of fact. Moreover, as discussed below, Plaintiff’s experts conclude that Defendants did not meet the standard of care, thus raising a triable issue.

                        2. Punitive Damages

            Defendants failed to secure indemnity from the County for punitive damages. (Plntf.’s Ex. 9 at 130:1-131:3.) According to Plaintiff’s experts, this conduct was below the standard of care. (Langsfeld Decl. ¶¶13-14, ¶¶ 21-22.) Defendants argue that the Defense Agreement reserved the County’s right not to indemnify for punitive damages. (Sposato Decl., Ex. B.) However, this reservation of rights did not preclude Defendants from seeking indemnity. (See Casselman Decl. ¶ 50 [the County “could chose to pay, but was not required to do so”]; Langsfeld Decl. ¶ 12 [“while the Los Angeles County did not have a statutory duty to automatically indemnify Rosalina Harris for any punitive damages judgment in the Holmes v. Harris case, that does not mean that the County of Los Angeles would absolutely not indemnify Ms. Harris. In my experience and career I have obtained the approval of public entities, including the County of Los Angeles to pay for punitive damages awarded against their agents or employees”].) A reasonable trier of fact may find that indemnity was potentially available, and that Defendants’ failure to pursue it was negligent. 

Moreover, Defendants did not clearly inform the court that the County would not indemnify the $1.5 million in punitive damages. Had Defendants done so, the amount may have been reduced or vacated based on Plaintiff’s inability to pay. (Casselman Decl. ¶¶ 50-51.) Defendants acknowledge that ability to pay is one of the factors considered in the remittitur analysis. (Reply 13:19-22.) Defendants further acknowledge that “the district court treated Harris’s ability to pay as ‘a neutral factor’ because of the indemnity uncertainty.” (Ibid.; Plntf.’s Ex. 6 [“Harris’ ability to pay is a neutral factor because it is unknown whether the County will indemnify her”].) Thus, it is possible that had Defendants accurately informed the court that the County would not be indemnifying for punitive damages, the court would have considered ability to pay as a more significant factor and reversed or reduced the award.

                        3. Personal Assets

            Plaintiff alleges that Defendants told her “the Holmes claims were not a big deal and that I didn’t need to worry about Holmes’ claims. Andrew Pongracz told me that my personal assets were not at risk as a result of Homes’ claims, but in reality Holmes has taken my home, cars and other assets I spent my life accumulating. Andrew told me that Holmes’ suit was about revenge against me by a neighbor and that I wouldn’t be homeless or lose my home or assets.” (Plntf.’s Ex. 8, p. 10.) Plaintiff’s experts aver that this was also a breach of Defendants’ fiduciary duty and below the standard of care. (Casselman Decl. ¶¶ 37-38; Langsfeld Decl. ¶ 26.)

Defendants argue that Plaintiff fails to establish causation because she has no evidence that she would have done anything differently (e.g., retain a different attorney, pursue a different litigation strategy) had Pongracz told her the truth. However, it is reasonable to infer that Plaintiff could have taken a different course of action to reduce her exposure had she been accurately informed of the risks. It is for the trier of fact to determine whether Pongracz made the alleged statement and whether it made a difference in the Holmes case.     

                        4. Probable Cause

            Plaintiff also claims that Defendants decided to focus solely on proving that Plaintiff had probable cause to arrest Holmes. (Plntf.’s Ex. 3 at 70:12-19, 125:17-127:10.) As a result, Defendants did not introduce evidence of Holmes’ actions preceding the arrest and also dismissed the need for evidence to rebut Holmes’ attacks on Plaintiff’s credibility and character. (Ex. 3 at 125:17-127:10.) Defendants allegedly told Plaintiff that it did not matter if the jury did not like her, because they could prove probable cause for the arrest. (Ibid.) Plaintiff’s experts both agree that this exclusive reliance on probable cause harmed Plaintiff’s defense and fell below the standard of care. (Casselman ¶ 36; Langsfeld Decl. ¶ 25.)   

                        5. Qualified Immunity

“Government officials enjoy qualified immunity from civil damages unless their conduct violates ‘clearly established statutory or constitutional rights of which a reasonable person would have known.’” (Jeffers v. Gomez (9th Cir. 2001) 267 F.3d 895, 910.) Determining the applicability of qualified immunity involves two inquiries: (1) whether the law governing the state official’s conduct is clearly established; and (2) whether a reasonable state official would have believed his conduct was lawful. (Ibid.)

Here, it is undisputed that the court rejected the qualified immunity defense as waived because Defendants failed to timely raise it. (Plntf.’s Ex. 6.) Plaintiff’s experts opine that Defendants did not meet the standard of care because they failed to move for dismissal based on qualified immunity prior to trial. (Casselman Decl. ¶¶ 21-25; Langsfeld Decl. ¶ 11.) Defendants argue that the defense would have failed even if Defendants had timely raised it. Specifically, Defendants contend that a reasonable official in Plaintiff’s position would not have believed Plaintiff’s conduct was lawful because the jury found that Plaintiff lacked probable cause for arresting Holmes and that Plaintiff acted with malice, oppression, or reckless disregard. (See Jeffers, supra, 267 F.3d at p. 910; United States v. Lopez (9th Cir. 2007) 482 F.3d 1067, 1072.)  

However, even assuming Defendants are not liable for failing to raise the qualified immunity defense, the legal malpractice claim is based on other allegations of misconduct. As discussed above, there are triable issues as to whether Defendants met the standard of care in the way they conducted the case, apart from the qualified immunity issue. In other words, Defendants’ focus on qualified immunity fails to dispose of the entire cause of action. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 437c(f)(1).)

In sum, triable issues exist as to whether Defendants are liable for professional negligence.

b. Fiduciary Duty

Defendants argue that they did not breach their fiduciary duties by favoring the County or letting the County dictate litigation strategy. However, the breach of fiduciary duty claim is based on more than that. As discussed above, there are triable issues with regards to whether Defendants’ conduct in the Holmes action met the standard of care. Therefore, triable issues exist as to whether Defendants are liable for breach of fiduciary duty.

V. CONCLUSION

            Defendants Andrew Pongracz’s and Seki, Nishimura & Watase, LLP’s motion for summary judgment or adjudication is DENIED.

 

 


Case Number: 24STCV18864    Hearing Date: August 3, 2026    Dept: 406

 

KARLA LEIVA,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

AIDS HEALTHCARE FOUNDATION,

                        Defendant.

 

  Case No.:  24STCV18864

  Hearing Date:  August 3, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

defendant’s motion to compel production of unredacted documents (CRS# 4747)

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On July 26, 2024, Plaintiff Karla Leiva filed this action against Defendant Aids Healthcare Foundation for employment discrimination and retaliation. Plaintiff alleges that she was terminated while on disability leave after protesting and reporting Defendant’s illegal activities, including unsafe or uninhabitable conditions.

            On June 16, 2026, Defendant filed the instant motion to compel Plaintiff to produce unredacted versions of two emails. Plaintiff filed an opposition on July 21, 2026. Defendant filed a reply on July 27, 2026.

DISCUSSION

            “If a privilege is claimed on the ground that the matter sought to be disclosed is a communication made in confidence in the course of the lawyer-client . . . relationship . . . the communication is presumed to have been made in confidence and the opponent of the claim of privilege has the burden of proof to establish that the communication was not confidential.” (Evid. Code, § 917(a).) “The party claiming the privilege has the burden of establishing the preliminary facts necessary to support its exercise, i.e., a communication made in the course of an attorney-client relationship.” (Citizens for Ceres v. Superior Court (2013) 217 Cal.App.4th 889, 911.) “Once that party establishes facts necessary to support a prima facie claim of privilege, the communication is presumed to have been made in confidence and the opponent of the claim of privilege has the burden of proof to establish the communication was not confidential or that the privilege does not for other reasons apply.” (Ibid.)

This motion concerns documents produced in response to Defendant’s requests for production. (Eisenberg Decl. ¶¶ 4-6.) Plaintiff produced 2,242 pages on April 28, 2026. (Id., ¶ 9.) Within that production, Plaintiff redacted parts of an email chain at pages 044 and 047. (Id., Ex. 7.) On May 20, 2026, Plaintiff served a privilege log indicating that the redacted emails were attorney-client communications between Plaintiff and her former attorney, Annette Morasch (now Annette Harings), relating to legal representation. (Id., Ex. 9.) Plaintiff and Harings further aver in their declarations that the emails were attorney-client communications. (Levia Decl. ¶ 3; Harings Decl. ¶ 5.)

Plaintiff has established the preliminary facts supporting a presumption that the communications are privileged. That is, Plaintiff has established that the redactions contain “communication[s] made in the course of an attorney-client relationship.” (See Citizens for Ceres, supra, 217 Cal.App.4th at p. 911.) Thus, the burden shifts to Defendant to demonstrate that the communications are not privileged. (Ibid.) Defendant argues that the emails are not privileged under Holmes v. Petrovich Development Co., LLC (2011) 191 Cal.App.4th 1047. In Holmes, the court held that section 917(b) did not protect attorney-client communications where the plaintiff “us[ed] the company's computer to communicate with her lawyer, knowing the communications violated company computer policy and could be discovered by her employer due to company monitoring of e-mail usage.” (Id. at p. 1051.)

            According to Defendant, Plaintiff forwarded work emails to her personal lawyer, Harings. (Mtn. 3:13-14.) The underlying emails have not been redacted. (Eisenberg Decl., Ex. 7.) Only the portions where Plaintiff apparently forwarded the emails to Harings have been redacted. (Ibid.) Defendant contends that “[t]he forwarding naturally most likely would have been done from Leiva’s AHF email account.” (Mtn. at 3:14-15.) However, this is mere speculation. Defendant acknowledges that the forwarding could also have been done from a personal account and admits that it “cannot know for sure.” (Mtn. at p. 3, fn. 2.) Defendant has no evidence that the emails were sent from Plaintiff’s work computer or work email.

Holmes is distinguishable because the issue in that case was the employer’s introduction of emails that it had already discovered on its own computers or servers. (Holmes, supra, 191 Cal.App.4th at pp. 1051-52.) The employer had access to the emails because its policy stated that emails sent on its systems were not private and could be monitored. (Ibid.) Here, by contrast, Defendant is moving to compel Plaintiff to produce documents in her possession. If the emails were sent from Defendant’s internal systems, as in Holmes, Defendant would already possess them, because it has a similar access policy. (See Eisenberg Decl., Ex. 1, 4.) Instead, Defendant cannot verify emails purportedly sent from its own system and even admits that Plaintiff may have used a personal email. This cuts against Defendant’s contention that the emails were sent from its servers or computers. Holmes does not permit production of presumptively privileged material based on this kind of speculation.

            Defendant also argues that “[g]iven Leiva’s extreme hostility toward AHF and desire to see AHF harmed, it seems more likely that Leiva was secretly helping AHF’s adversaries in litigation, rather than communicating with lawyers on Leiva’s own legal matter.” (Mtn. at 4:8-11.) This is further speculation. Plaintiff provided a privilege log, and both Plaintiff and Harings unequivocally aver under oath that the emails were attorney-client communications. Defendant provides no evidence to the contrary other than its subjective “suspicion.” (See Mtn. at pp. 3-4.)

In sum, Defendant has not met its burden of showing that the challenged materials are not privileged.

CONCLUSION

            Defendant’s motion to compel production of unredacted documents is DENIED. Sanctions are denied as the parties acted with substantial justification.

“If a party filing a response to a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling . . . thereafter fails to permit the inspection, copying, testing, or sampling in accordance with that party’s statement of compliance, the demanding party may move for an order compelling compliance.” (Code Civ. Case Number: 25NWLC50455    Hearing Date: August 3, 2026    Dept: 406

 

AMERICAN CONTRACTORS INDEMNITY COMPANY,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

EDWARD ALLEN BORIO, et al.,

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  25NWLC50455

  Hearing Date:  August 3, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

cross-defendant american contractors indemnity company’s demurrer and motion to strike

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On September 4, 2025, American Contractors Indemnity Company (ACIC) filed this action against Edward Allen Borio (Borio) and Borio Construction for breach of contract. ACIC issued a contractors license bond containing an indemnity agreement. Borio and Borio Construction allegedly failed to reimburse ACIC for claims against the bond.

            On May 20, 2026, Borio filed a cross-complaint against ACIC for (1) breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing and (2) tortious interference with business relations. The cross-complaint alleges that ACIC paid out a claim against the bond in bad faith with no investigation and then pursued Borio for repayment, driving him out of business.

            On June 23, 2026, ACIC filed the instant demurrer and motion to strike against the cross-complaint.

LEGAL STANDARD

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (Taylor v. City of Los Angeles Dept. of Water and Power (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 1216, 1228.) In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or by proper judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.30(a).) A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. (SKF Farms v. Superior Court (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 902, 905.) “California law emphasizes ultimate fact pleading (with some exceptions, notably for fraud and related torts) ‘in ordinary and concise language,’ and the test for adequacy is not absolute but ‘whether the pleading as a whole apprises the adversary of the factual basis of the claim.’” (Lim v. The.TV Corp. Internat. (2002) 99 Cal.App.4th 684, 690.)

MEET AND CONFER

Before filing a demurrer or a motion to strike, the demurring or moving party is required to meet and confer with the party who filed the pleading demurred to or the pleading that is subject to the motion to strike for the purposes of determining whether an agreement can be reached through a filing of an amended pleading that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 430.41, 435.5.) The Court finds that Cross-Defendant has satisfied the meet and confer requirement. (See Buchanan Decl.)

DISCUSSION

I. Demurrer

            a. Standing

            ACIC argues that Borio has no standing to pursue the cross-complaint because the cross-complaint seeks damages incurred by Borio Construction. However, the facts in the cross-complaint describe harm to Borio. The contract which ACIC relies on in its complaint and the instant demurrer names Borio individually as the indemnitor. (Compl., Ex. A.) ACIC is pursuing Borio individually for indemnity. Thus, ACIC’s alleged actions—overpaying a claim in bad faith and then pursuing repayment—would have damaged Borio individually. Borio has established his standing for pleading purposes.  

            b. Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing

            “The covenant of good faith and fair dealing, implied by law in every contract, exists merely to prevent one contracting party from unfairly frustrating the other party’s right to receive the benefits of the agreement actually made.” (Guz v. Bechtel National, Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 317, 349-350.) “The covenant of good faith and fair dealing imposes obligations on the contracting parties separate and apart from those consensually agreed to.” (Bodenhamer v. Superior Court (1987) 192 Cal.App.3d 1472, 1477.) “The obligations imposed by the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing are not those set out in the terms of the contract itself, but rather are obligations imposed by law.” (Id. at p. 1478.)

Nonetheless, “the scope of conduct prohibited by the covenant of good faith is circumscribed by the purposes and express terms of the contract.” (Avidity Partners, LLC v. State of California (2013) 221 Cal.App.4th 1180, 1204.) “The implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing does not impose substantive terms and conditions beyond those to which the parties actually agreed.” (Ibid.) “It cannot impose substantive duties or limits on the contracting parties beyond those incorporated in the specific terms of their agreement.” (Ibid.) 

            ACIC argues that it cannot be liable because the contract expressly gives ACIC “sole and absolute discretion, to determine whether any claims shall be paid, compromised, adjusted, defended, prosecuted or appealed.” (Compl., Ex. A.) ACIC argues that the cross-complaint contravenes the express terms of the contract by asserting that ACIC “owed an unspecified, extra-contractual duty not to settle the underlying bond claim.” (Dem. 5:2-3.)

            However, “‘a discretionary power must be exercised in good faith.’” (California Dental Assn. v. Delta Dental of California (2025) 115 Cal.App.5th 142, 155, quoting Third Story Music, Inc. v. Waits (1995) 41 Cal.App.4th 798, 804.) “‘[W]here a contract confers on one party a discretionary power affecting the rights of the other, a duty is imposed to exercise that discretion in good faith and in accordance with fair dealing.’” (Bevis v. Terrace View Partners, LP (2019) 33 Cal.App.5th 230, 253, quoting Automatic Vending Co. v. Wisdom (1960) 182 Cal.App.2d 354, 358.) For example, in Arntz Contracting Co. v. St. Paul Fire & Marine Ins. Co. (1996) 47 Cal.App.4th 464, 482, an indemnity agreement gave the surety, St. Paul, the right to “take such action as it might deem necessary or proper to obtain its release from any and all liability under the said bond or bonds” and to “settle claims arising under the bonds and to manage completion of the project.” The court of appeal held that “St. Paul was given broad discretion in managing completion of the project, requiring its exercise of good faith.” (Ibid.) “Nothing in the Indemnity Agreement grants St. Paul unconditional power to incur expenses in completing the project, regardless of St. Paul's good faith belief in its actions.” (Ibid.)

Here, the cross-complaint alleges that ACIC acted in bad faith by blindly paying out the claim without an investigation and without considering the outstanding amounts owed to Borio by the homeowners, resulting in overpayment. ACIC then pursued Borio for repayment of the excessive amount. The allegations sufficiently establish that ACIC exercised its discretion in bad faith. The discretion “to determine whether any claims shall be paid” (Compl., Ex. A) does not necessarily confer the right to pay any amount, no matter how excessive, with no rational justification. The obligation to conduct a fair investigation before settling claims is arguably consistent with “the purposes and express terms of the contract.” (See Avidity Partners, LLC, supra, 221 Cal.App.4th at p. 1204.) Therefore, the implied covenant claim has been adequately pled.

c. Interference with Business Relations

The second cause of action is for “Tortious Interference with Business Relations and Total Business Destruction.” The elements of a claim for intentional interference with prospective economic advantage are: (1) an economic relationship between the plaintiff and some third party, with the probability of future economic benefit to the plaintiff; (2) the defendant's knowledge of the relationship; (3) intentional acts on the part of the defendant designed to disrupt the relationship; (4) actual disruption of the relationship; and (5) economic harm to the plaintiff proximately caused by the acts of the defendant. (Korea Supply Co. v. Lockheed Martin Corp. (2003) 29 Cal.4th 1134, 1153.)

The cross-complaint fails to identify any business relationship which was interfered with. Instead, the cross-complaint only alleges generally that Borio was driven out of business and incurred other expenses “due to the Surety’s abandonment.” This is insufficient to maintain a claim for interference with business relations. There is no independent claim for “total business destruction.” Therefore, the second cause of action fails as a matter of law.

II. Motion to Strike

“In an action for the breach of an obligation not arising from contract, where it is proven by clear and convincing evidence that the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice, the plaintiff, in addition to the actual damages, may recover damages for the sake of example and by way of punishing the defendant.” (Civ. Code, § 3294(a).)

Here, the only surviving claim in the cross-complaint is the first cause of action for breach of the implied covenant, which is a contract claim. Because punitive damages are unavailable in contract claims, the punitive damage allegations must be stricken.

CONCLUSION

            Cross-Defendant ACIC’s demurrer to the cross-complaint is SUSTAINED as to the second cause of action without leave to amend. The motion to strike punitive damages is GRANTED without leave to amend.

           


Case Number: 25STCV24684    Hearing Date: August 3, 2026    Dept: 406

 

MANUEL JOHNSON,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

LOS ANGELES COUNTY METROPOLITAN TRANSPORTATION AUTHORITY, et al.,

                       

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  25STCV24684

  Hearing Date:  August 3, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

los angeles county metropolitan transportation authority’s motions to compel responses to discovery (CRS# 1622, 5386, 9658)

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On August 21, 2025, Plaintiff Manuel Johnson filed this action against Defendants Los Angeles County Metropolitan Transportation Authority (MTA), Rene Denise Honer (Honer), and Lawrence Wilson (Wilson). Plaintiff filed the operative First Amended Complaint on January 28, 2026, asserting causes of action for (1) negligence and (2) violation of Government Code sections 815.2(a) and 820(a).

            The lawsuit arises out of an auto collision between a bus and another vehicle that occurred in November 2024. Plaintiff was a passenger in the bus operated by MTA, Honer was driving the bus, and Wilson was driving the other vehicle. The complaint alleges that Honer drove on the wrong side of the road, while Wilson made an unsafe turn, leading to the collision.

            On January 15, 2026, Wilson filed a cross-complaint against MTA and Honer for indemnity, declaratory relief, and apportionment.

            On March 27, 2026, MTA and Honer filed a cross-complaint against Wilson for indemnity, declaratory relief, and apportionment.

            On April 17, 2026, the Court entered dismissal of the underlying complaint against Wilson with prejudice pursuant to Plaintiff’s request.

            On May 11, 2026, the Court entered dismissal of Wilson’s cross-complaint without prejudice pursuant to Wilson’s request.

            On June 18, 2026, MTA filed the instant three motions to compel Wilson’s responses to interrogatories and requests for production.

DISCUSSION

            MTA served Wilson with form interrogatories, special interrogatories, and requests for production on April 1, 2026. After MTA granted an extension, Wilson served unverified responses on May 18, 2026, consisting solely of objections. MTA moves to compel verified responses without objection.

Ordinarily, unverified responses are tantamount to no response at all. (See Appleton v. Super. Ct. (1988) 206 Cal.App.3d 632, 636.) However, responses containing solely objections need not be verified. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.250(a); 2031.250(a).) Because Wilson’s responses contained only objections, they were properly unverified. And because Wilson served responses, there is no basis for a motion to compel.

 To the extent MTA takes issue with the substance of the responses, including objections, MTA should have filed motions to compel further. (See Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300, 2031.310.) MTA did not do so, nor did it file the requisite separate statement. (See Cal. Rules of Ct., Rule 3.1345(a).) MTA’s argument that meet and confer was not required further indicates that MTA only filed motions to compel in the first instance. (See Mtn. 4:23-28; Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300(b)(1), 2031.310(b)(2).)

CONCLUSION

            Los Angeles County Metropolitan Transportation Authority’s motions to compel responses are DENIED.


Case Number: 26STCV09040    Hearing Date: August 3, 2026    Dept: 406

 

LANCE SOARES INCORPORATED, et al.,

                        Plaintiffs,

            v.

 

CITY OF SANTA CLARITA,

                        Defendant.

 

  Case No.:  26STCV09040

  Hearing Date:  August 3, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

defendant’s demurrer to complaint

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On March 19, 2026, Lance Soares Incorporated (LSI) and Lance Soares (Soares) (collectively, Plaintiffs) filed this action against the City of Santa Clarita (the City). Plaintiffs filed the operative First Amended Complaint on May 18, 2026.

            Plaintiffs are currently being sued by their employees for failure to pay prevailing wages. The employees worked as street sweepers pursuant to LSI’s contract with the City. Plaintiffs filed this action against the City for indemnity in the event Plaintiffs are held liable to the employees, because the City represented to Plaintiffs that the contract did not involve a “public work” and was therefore not subject to prevailing wage laws. The FAC asserts claims under Labor Code sections 1726 and 1781, as well as a claim for equitable indemnity.

            On June 22, 2026, the City filed the instant demurrer to the complaint. Plaintiffs filed an opposition on July 21, 2026. The City filed a reply on July 29, 2026.

 

 

LEGAL STANDARD

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (Taylor v. City of Los Angeles Dept. of Water and Power (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 1216, 1228.) In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or by proper judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.30(a).) A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. (SKF Farms v. Superior Court (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 902, 905.) “California law emphasizes ultimate fact pleading (with some exceptions, notably for fraud and related torts) ‘in ordinary and concise language,’ and the test for adequacy is not absolute but ‘whether the pleading as a whole apprises the adversary of the factual basis of the claim.’” (Lim v. The.TV Corp. Internat. (2002) 99 Cal.App.4th 684, 690.)

MEET AND CONFER

Before filing a demurrer or a motion to strike, the demurring or moving party is required to meet and confer with the party who filed the pleading demurred to or the pleading that is subject to the motion to strike for the purposes of determining whether an agreement can be reached through a filing of an amended pleading that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 430.41, 435.5.) The Court finds that Defendant has satisfied the meet and confer requirement. (See Chee Decl.)

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

            The City’s request for judicial notice of Exhibits A through E is granted.

 

 

 

 

DISCUSSION

I. Timeliness

            a. Statutory Indemnity

Under the Government Claims Act (GCA), certain claims must be presented to a public entity before a plaintiff may file a civil action for damages. (Gov. Code, § 945.4.) A plaintiff must present his claim no later than one year after the cause of action accrues. (Id., § 911.2(a).) “A cause of action accrues ‘when [it] is complete with all of its elements’—those elements being wrongdoing, harm, and causation.” (Pooshs v. Philip Morris USA, Inc. (2011) 51 Cal.4th 788, 797.) “[O]rdinarily, the statute of limitations runs from ‘the occurrence of the last element essential to the cause of action.’” (Aryeh v. Canon Business Solutions, Inc. (2013) 55 Cal.4th 1185, 1191.)

Here, the FAC alleges that Plaintiffs presented a government claim for the first cause of action on September 28, 2025 and another government claim for the second and third causes of action on March 26, 2026. (FAC ¶¶ 24-25.) The City argues that the claims were untimely because LSI presented them more than one year after the causes of action accrued. The City argues that LSI knew or should have known that it might be subject to liability for prevailing wages no later than October 2021, when the Department of Industrial Relations (DIR) issued a determination in a different case that a street sweeping contract involved a public work and was therefore subject to prevailing wage laws. (See Def.’s RJN, Ex. 1.) Alternatively, the City argues that LSI was on notice by July 2023, when its new contract with the City came into effect requiring the payment of prevailing wages, which should have raised suspicion about the City’s prior representation that the project was not subject to prevailing wage laws. At the latest, the City argues, LSI was on notice by the time its employees filed their lawsuits in October and November 2023.

            However, a cause of action does not accrue until it is complete with all of its elements, including damages. (Pooshs, supra, 51 Cal.4th at p. 797.) Therefore, “a cause of action for indemnity does not accrue until the indemnitee suffers loss through payment of an adverse judgment or settlement.” (City of San Diego v. U.S. Gypsum Co. (1994) 30 Cal.App.4th 575, 587.) Here, Plaintiffs’ claims under Labor Code sections 1726 and 1781 seek statutory indemnity. (See Nabors Corporate Services, Inc. v. City of Long Beach (2025) 108 Cal.App.5th 540, 554.) However, Plaintiffs have not paid an adverse judgment or settlement in the cases brought by their employees. Therefore, the statutory causes of action have not accrued.

The statutory language also supports this result. The statutes permit recovery of “the difference between the wages actually paid to an employee and the wages that were required to be paid to an employee under this chapter, any penalties required to be paid under this chapter,” or “increased costs incurred by the contractor” as a result of the failure to identify a project as a public work. (Lab. Code, §§ 1726(c), 1781(a)(1).) LSI arguably has not yet incurred these costs, as there has been no adverse judgment or settlement in the underlying lawsuits.

The City cites Fox v. Ethicon Endo-Surgery, Inc. (2005) 35 Cal.4th 797, 807-08 for the proposition that a cause of action accrues when the plaintiff “has reason to discover” a factual basis for the claim. However, LSI had no reason to discover the factual basis for its indemnity claim before the claim was complete with all of its elements. An indemnity claim is complete when the plaintiff suffers damages, i.e., the underlying liability. (City of San Diego, supra, 30 Cal.App.4th at p. 587.) The claim must be complete before the plaintiff can be charged with knowledge of it. The discovery rule does not accelerate accrual based on suspicion of a possible future injury.

            Even if accrual depended on LSI’s actual or constructive knowledge of its potential liability, it is a question of fact whether and when LSI obtained such knowledge. The City’s arguments rely on facts external to the complaint. The City’s theory requires the Court to make the following assumptions at the pleading stage: (1) LSI knew or had reason to know about the DIR’s 2021 determination in an unrelated case and should have known that this other determination was binding on LSI’s own contract with the City; (2) the new July 2023 contract with the City demonstrated that the City misrepresented the project’s status as a public work prior to that date, putting LSI on notice that it would be held liable for prevailing wages; and (3) LSI knew or should have known, as soon as the employees’ lawsuits were filed, that the underlying claims had merit and that LSI would be held liable for prevailing wages.

The Court can make no such assumptions based on any of the pleadings or judicially noticeable materials. Therefore, it cannot be determined at this stage that the statutory claims accrued on the dates proffered by the City. Instead, the allegations in the complaint support a reasonable inference that the claims accrued no earlier than July 2025, when the Court summarily adjudicated that LSI had a duty to pay prevailing wages. (See FAC ¶ 23.) As a result, the claims under Labor Code sections 1726 and 1781 are timely.

b. Equitable Indemnity

The Government Code provides an exception to the ordinary accrual rule in the case of equitable indemnity against a public entity: “the date upon which a cause of action for equitable indemnity or partial equitable indemnity accrues shall be the date upon which a defendant is served with the complaint giving rise to the defendant’s claim for equitable indemnity or partial equitable indemnity against the public entity.” (Gov. Code, § 901.)

Here, the employees’ lawsuits were served to Plaintiffs in October and November 2023. Those are the complaints for which Plaintiffs seek equitable indemnity. Plaintiffs identify no other complaint for which equitable indemnity is sought. Section 901 unequivocally provides that the limitations period runs from the date of service of the complaint, not from the date that liability is determined. Therefore, the limitations period under section 901 began running in October and November 2023. Plaintiffs presented their equitable indemnity claim to the City in March 2026, more than one year later. (FAC ¶ 25.) Thus, the equitable indemnity claim is time-barred.

II. Immunity

            The City argues that it is immune from liability under Government Code section 818.8: “A public entity is not liable for an injury caused by misrepresentation by an employee of the public entity, whether or not such misrepresentation be negligent or intentional.” However, a specific statutory provision governs over a general one if the two are inconsistent. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1859.) Government Code section 818.8 provides general immunity for misrepresentations, but Labor Code sections 1726 and 1781 specifically provide for liability against an “awarding body” in relation to prevailing wages. An “awarding body” is defined as a “department, board, authority, officer or agent awarding a contract for public work,” which includes public entities such as the City. (See Lab. Code, § 1722.) Thus, to the extent the provisions are inconsistent, the Labor Code provisions would control.

            The City correctly notes that under the Government Code, the liability provisions are subordinate to the immunity provisions. However, the liability at issue here stems from the Labor Code. Government Code section 815(b) provides that “[t]he liability of a public entity established by this part (commencing with Section 814) is subject to any immunity of the public entity provided by statute, including this part.” (Gov. Code, § 815(b).) Labor Code sections 1726 and 1781 are not “established by this part” within the meaning of Government Code section 815(b). Therefore, the immunities established by the Government Code do not necessarily trump the liabilities created by the Labor Code.

            The caselaw cited by the City confirms this conclusion. For example, the court in Tokeshi v. Cal. (1990) 217 Cal.App.3d 999, 1005 acknowledged that the legislative purpose behind Government Code section 818.8 is to “exempt[] the state from tort liability for ‘misrepresentation.’” The Legislature “must have had in mind those areas in which private defendants typically face liability for ‘misrepresentation.’” (Ibid.) The liability established under Labor Code sections 1726 and 1781 falls beyond this description, i.e., it does not involve a “misrepresentation” as contemplated in Government Code section 818.8. 

            The City relies on Tokeshi for the additional proposition that Government Code section 818.8 provides immunity for misrepresentations even if the claim is framed as a statutory violation. (See Tokeshi, supra, 217 Cal.App.3d at p. 1008.) However, the claims here are not framed as statutory violations. The City is not being accused of violating Labor Code sections 1726 and 1781. Those statutes simply authorize an action for indemnity in specific situations concerning public works and prevailing wages. Therefore, Tokeshi is distinguishable.

Ultimately, the City cites no authority suggesting that Government Code section 818.8 immunizes public entities from liability under Labor Code sections 1726 and 1781. The City’s theory would provide immunity in every case where the awarding body is a public entity, which would render sections 1726 and 1781 largely ineffectual. This could not have been the Legislature’s intent. Therefore, Government Code section 818.8 does not preclude liability in this case.  

CONCLUSION

            Defendant City of Santa Clarita’s demurrer is SUSTAINED without leave to amend as to the third cause of action and OVERRULED in all other respects.

 


Case Number: BC538187    Hearing Date: August 3, 2026    Dept: 406

 

ROBERT SCOTT SHTOFMAN,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

BERNICE CHINYERE IVOKO, et al.,

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  BC538187

  Hearing Date:  August 3, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

plaintiff’s motion to compel further responses

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On March 4, 2014, Plaintiff Robert Scott Shtofman filed this action for unpaid legal fees against Defendants Bernice Chinyere Ivoko, et al.

            On April 27, 2018, the Court issued a judgment on discovery sanctions, in favor of Plaintiff, in the total amount of $21,883.71.

            On January 11, 2019, the Court issued a judgment in favor of Plaintiff following a jury trial, in the amount of $271,600.

            On January 28, 2019, Defendant Ivoko filed a notice of appeal.

            On January 30, 2019, Joseph Ivoko, Michelle Uzowuru, and Michael Uzowuru filed declarations as personal sureties for the undertaking on appeal.

            On April 28, 2021, the Court of Appeal affirmed the judgments.

            On July 1, 2021, the remittitur was issued, finalizing the appellate decision.

            On June 22, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion to compel judgment debtor George Omoko’s further responses to post-judgment interrogatories. Omoko filed an opposition on July 22, 2026. Plaintiff filed a reply on July 27, 2026.

LEGAL STANDARD

Upon receiving responses to its discovery requests, the propounding party may move for an order compelling further responses if the responses are incomplete or evasive, or objections are without merit or too general. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300(a), 2031.310(a), 2033.290(a).)

DISCUSSION

I. Timeliness

The deadline for serving a motion to compel further responses is 45 days after receipt of the responses. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300(c).) The deadline is extended by two court days if responses are served electronically. (Id., § 1010.6(a)(3)(B).) If the deadline falls on a weekend or holiday, the deadline is extended to the next court day. (Id., § 2016.060.) The service deadline is “mandatory and jurisdictional.” (Sexton v. Superior Court (1997) 58 Cal.App.4th 1403, 1410.)

            Here, Omoko electronically served his responses to the interrogatories on May 3, 2026. (Chaskin Decl. ¶ 14.) Accounting for the 45-day deadline plus two court days for electronic service results in a date of Friday, June 19, 2026. Because that day was a holiday, the deadline extends to the next court day, which was Monday, June 22, 2026, the day the motion was filed. Therefore, the motion is timely.            

II. Merits

A party “is entitled to demand answers to its interrogatories, as a matter of right . . . [and] the burden of justifying any objection and failure to respond remains at all times with the party resisting an interrogatory.” (Williams v. Sup. Ct. (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 541.)

Here, Omoko objected to each SROG and either provided no information or incomplete information. Plaintiff’s motion is accompanied by a detailed separate statement setting forth the substance of each SROG, the relevance of each SROG, Omoko’s responses to each SROG, and the reasons why the responses are defective. Omoko’s opposition fails to address the points made by Plaintiff and merely asserts in conclusory fashion that the responses “are legally valid and were accompanied by substantive responses where appropriate.” (Opp. 9:1-5.) This is insufficient to satisfy Omoko’s burden of justifying his objections and refusal to respond. (See Williams, supra, 3 Cal.5th at p. 541.) Therefore, further responses are warranted.

III. Sanctions

            Sanctions are warranted as Omoko has presented no substantial justification for his failure to provide proper responses. However, Plaintiff’s counsel demands an excessive recovery of $8,860 ($800 x 11 hrs. + $60). (See Chaskin Decl. ¶ 21.) The Court reduces the award to $2,310 ($450 x 5 hrs. + $60). 

CONCLUSION

            Plaintiff’s motion to compel further responses to post-judgment special interrogatories is GRANTED. Judgment debtor George Omoko shall provide further responses within 20 days of today. The Court sanctions Omoko in the amount of $2,310, to be paid within 30 days of today.
