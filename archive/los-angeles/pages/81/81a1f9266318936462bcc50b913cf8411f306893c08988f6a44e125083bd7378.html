Plaintiff Michael B. Smith brought this suit against his former employer, Defendant Michael Kors Inc., alleging race discrimination in his employment and a subsequent unlawful layoff.

On March 26, 2026, the Court granted a motion for judgment on the pleadings filed by Defendant, which disposed of the then-operative March 19, 2024, Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”). Plaintiff filed a motion for reconsideration on March 30, 2026, which was opposed by Defendant, and was ultimately denied by the Court on May 20, 2026. The Court entered judgment in Defendant’s favor that same day.

Now before the Court is Plaintiff’s motion to set aside/vacate the May 20, 2026, judgment on the grounds that another action affecting the outcome of the proceedings before this Court is purportedly presently pending before the Compton, California branch of the Los Angeles Superior Court as 22CMCV00753, Smith v. Michael Kors Inc. (the “Santa Monica action”). (See Motion, p. 2, ¶ 4.)

The motion is DENIED, with prejudice.

Plaintiff’s motion is noticed as being premised on Code of Civil Procedure sections 473, subdivision (d), and 1008, subdivisions (a), (b), and (e). (Motion, pp. 1-2.) Both premises fail.

As argued in Defendant’s opposition, there is no applicable provision of Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (d), here. (See Opposition pp. 11-12.) Plaintiff is asking that the Court vacate a final judgment based on the purported pendency of the Santa Monica action. This is not a valid ground for relief under section 473, subdivision (d), which is limited to clerical errors and void judgments and orders, including those voided by extrinsic fraud or mistake, neither of which Plaintiff’s paper show. (See Kramer v. Traditional Escrow, Inc. (2020) 56 Cal.App.5th 13, 29 [discussing § 473, subd. (d)].)

Similarly, none of the grounds for party-requested reconsideration—new or different facts, circumstances, or law (Code Civ. Proc., § 1008, subd. (a))—appear here, as the requested relief is based on the purported pendency of the Santa Monica action rather than some fact, circumstance, or new law relating to this action.

Last, the Court takes judicial notice of Exhibit A attached to Defendant’s opposition, which shows a June 18, 2026, minute order for the Santa Monica action, in which the court in that case denied a similar motion by Plaintiff in this case to vacate or set aside the judgment in that case, which was entered on November 6, 2025. (Opposition, Request for Judicial Notice, Ex. A, pp. 6-7.)

Plaintiff’s motion is accordingly DENIED. No further motions seeking identical relief shall be entertained absent a proper statutory basis.

a.     Pleadings

Plaintiff Katherine Keller (“Plaintiff”) sues Defendants Jeanette Ann Bisno (“Jeanette”) and Robert H. Bisno (“Robert”) (collectively, the “Bisnos”), as well as a number of companies and entities related to the Bisnos pursuant to a February 20, 2024, Complaint alleging claims of (1) Malicious Prosecution and (2) Violation of the California Bane Act.

The claims arise from allegations that, following Defendant Jeanette Ann Bisno’s battery of Plaintiff on December 12, 2018—which resulted in Jeanette’s arrest that same day and later criminal prosecution—Jeanette and her husband, Defendant Robert H. Bisno, undertook a series of actions intended to intimidate and harass Plaintiff. Specifically, the Complaint alleges that on December 20, 2018, Defendants filed Los Angeles Superior Court Case No. 18STCV09206 asserting claims for assault, battery, intentional infliction of emotional distress, false imprisonment, and false arrest against unnamed Doe defendants, all arising from the December 12 incident. Moreover, Robert, acting as Jeanette’s attorney in her criminal battery case, received Plaintiff’s name and contact information through criminal discovery and, in violation of Penal Code section 1054.2, disclosed that information to Jeanette and the other Defendants, enabling them to amend the 18STCV09206 complaint the very next day to name Plaintiff. The first cause of action is based on Defendants’ malicious prosecution of Case No. 18STCV09206, and the second cause of action is based on Robert’s unlawful disclosure of Plaintiff’s information obtained from the criminal case.

c.     Legal Standard

The court may, upon a motion or at any time in its discretion and upon terms it deems proper: (a) strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or (b) strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of California, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Code Civ. Proc. § 436, subds. (a), (b); Stafford v. Shultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782 [“Matter in a pleading which is not essential to the claim is surplusage; probative facts are surplusage and may be stricken out or disregarded”].)

For the purposes of a motion to strike pursuant to Sections 435 to 437 of the Code of Civil Procedure, the term “pleading” generally means a demurrer, answer, complaint, or cross-complaint, (Code Civ. Proc., § 435, subd. (a)), and an immaterial allegation or irrelevant matter in a pleading entails (1) an allegation that is not essential to the statement of a claim or defense, (2) an allegation that is neither pertinent to nor supported by an otherwise sufficient claim or defense, or (3) a demand for judgment requesting relief not supported by the allegations of the complaint or cross-complaint (Code Civ. Proc., § 431.10, subds. (b)(1)-(3), (c)).

In analyzing a motion to strike, California courts “accept as true the well-pleaded allegations” in the challenged pleadings, as well as “well-pleaded allegations admitted in” the responsive “answer.” (Atwell Island Water Dist. v. Atwell Island Water District (2020) 45 Cal.App.5th 624, 628 (Atwell); Clauson v. Superior Court (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1253, 1255 [California courts “read allegations of a pleading subject to a motion to strike as a whole, all parts in their context, and assume their truth”].)

    1. Discussion

After review, the Court finds in favor of Plaintiff.

                                          i.    Reverse Veil Piercing

The Court does not credit JAJ3’s argument that alter ego cannot be stated as against an LLC through a “reverse” piercing of the veil from a person to an LLC.

“Reverse veil piercing is similar to traditional veil piercing in that when the ends of justice so require, a court will disregard the separation between an individual and a business entity. [Citation.] But the two serve unique purposes and are used in different contexts. Rather than seeking to hold an individual responsible for the acts of an entity, reverse veil piercing seeks to satisfy the debt of an individual through the assets of an entity of which the individual is an insider.” (Curci Investments, LLC v. Baldwin (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 214, 221 (“Curci”).)

The Fourth Appellate District has held that a third-party creditor cannot pierce the corporate veil to reach corporate assets in order to satisfy a shareholder’s personal liability. In Postal Instant Press, Inc. v. Kaswa Corp. (2008) 162 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1522-1524 (“Postal Instant Press”), the court explained that traditional veil-piercing is inappropriate in this context because the abuse of the corporate form does not exist when the judgment debtor is the shareholder; the shareholder did not incur the debt through misuse of the corporation and is not using the corporate form to escape personal liability. (See ibid.) Instead, outside reverse veil-piercing concerns a different problem—where a shareholder transfers personal assets to the corporation to shield those assets from collection by the shareholder’s creditor. (See ibid.) The court noted that this type of wrongdoing can be addressed through existing judgment-collection procedures that protect creditors when innocent shareholders and creditors will not be harmed, legal remedies are inadequate, and the traditional alter-ego requirements are satisfied. (See ibid.) Under these procedures, a judgment creditor may enforce the judgment against the shareholder’s assets, including the shareholder’s corporate shares, and once the creditor acquires those shares, it obtains whatever rights the shareholder held in the corporation. (See ibid.)

In contrast, in Curci, the Fourth Appellate District held that a third-party creditor may pierce the veil of a limited liability company. (Curci, supra, 14 Cal.App.5th at p. 224.) Specifically, the court reasoned that the concerns underlying Postal Instant Press—which rejected reverse veil-piercing for corporations—do not apply in the LLC context, where: the facts in Curci differed because the judgment debtors were spouses who owned 100% of the LLC and who themselves owed the debt the creditor sought to collect, making the LLC simply their vehicle for shielding assets; a creditor has significantly fewer remedies against an LLC member than against a corporate shareholder insofar as a creditor can directly acquire a shareholder’s stock and step into the shareholder’s rights (such as voting, receiving dividends, or selling shares), but against an LLC member, a creditor may obtain only a charging order against distributions and cannot acquire any ownership interest in the LLC; and the structure of an LLC allows a judgment debtor to maintain full control over distributions, including directing payments to third parties rather than to themselves, effectively enabling the debtor to avoid personal liability by preventing any distributions that could be reached by the creditor. (See ibid.)

Here, under the framework of Postal Instant Press and Cursi, the Court determines that as an LLC, the reserve alter ego doctrine may be stated against JAJ3.

                                         ii.    Sufficiency of Allegations of Alter Ego

Moreover, the Court also determines that the Complaint sufficiently alleges alter ego.

Two requirements must be met to invoke the alter ego doctrine: (1) “[T]here must be such a unity of interest and ownership between the corporation and its equitable owner that the separate personalities of the corporation and the shareholder do not in reality exist”; and (2) “there must be an inequitable result if the acts in question are treated as those of the corporation alone.” (Sonora Diamond Corp. v. Superior Court (2000) 83 Cal.App.4th 523, 538 (Sonora).)

Here, the Complaint alleges alter ego liability against Jeanette A. Bisno, Robert H. Bisno, and the entity Defendants. (Complaint, ¶¶ 18-23.) It asserts a unity of interest and ownership such that any separateness between the individuals and the entities had ceased to exist, and that Jeanette and Robert completely controlled, dominated, and operated the entities for their own purposes; that the Bisnos comingled funds and assets, disregarded legal formalities, failed to maintain arm’s-length relationships, used a common office and employees, treated the entities as mere shells or conduits, manipulated assets and liabilities among the entities, and used them to shield themselves from personal obligations; and that every action taken by Jeanette was funded by the LLCs, demonstrating that the entities’ finances and her own were indistinguishable. (See ibid.)

Such allegations are comparable to allegations found sufficient at the pleadings stage by California courts. For example, the court of appeal has held that unity of interest is sufficiently alleged where “[the plaintiff] alleged that [the individual defendant] dominated and controlled [the entity defendant]; that a unity of interest and ownership existed between [the individual defendant] and [the entity defendant]; that [the entity defendant] was a mere shell and conduit for [the individual defendant]’s affairs; that [the entity defendant] was inadequately capitalized; that [the entity defendant] failed to abide by the formalities of corporate existence; [and] that [the individual defendant] used [the entity defendant] assets as her own …” (Rutherford Holdings, LLC v. Plaza Del Rey (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 221, 235-236 (Rutherford); see also First Western Bank & Trust Co. v. Bookasta (1968) 267 Cal.App.2d 910, 915-916 (First Western) [unity of interest sufficiently pleaded through allegations that “that the individuals, especially including E. H. Bookasta, ‘dominated’ the affairs of the corporation; that a ‘unity of interest and ownership’ existed between respondent and the corporation; that the corporation is a ‘mere shell and naked framework’ for individual manipulations; that its income was diverted to the use of the individuals and respondent; that the corporation was, in effect, inadequately capitalized; that the corporation failed to issue stock and to abide by the formalities of corporate existence; [and] that the corporation is and has been insolvent …”].)

Moreover, the harms alleged in the Complaint also provide a sufficient basis to determine that the Complaint contemplates injustice if alter ego liability is not applied here, e.g., by alleging that Defendants’ conduct in the plural consciously disregarded Plaintiff’s rights and subjected Plaintiff to cruel and unjust hardship. (See, e.g., Complaint, ¶ 117 [allegation in punitive damages context]; see also Shaoxing County Huayue Import & Export v. Bhaumik (2011) 191 Cal.App.4th 1189, 1198 [Question is “whether justice and equity are best accomplished in a particular case, and fraud defeated, by disregarding the separate nature of the corporate form as to the claims in that case”].)

Last, the Court briefly notes that this Court need not agree with the reasoning of the June 18, 2026, order. (Cf. Wrightson v. Dougherty (1936) 5 Cal.2d 257, 265 [demurrer was overruled in one department, judgment on pleadings was later granted in another department].)

e.     Conclusion

Accordingly, Defendant JAJ3, LLC’s demurrer is OVERRULED.

a.     Legal Standard on Petitions to Approve Minors’ Compromise

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 372, any compromise of claim or action or disposition of proceeds of judgment made for a minor or adult with a disability must be approved by the Court. (See also Prob. Code § 3600, subd. (b) [a compromise or covenant for a disputed claim or damages, money, or other property of a minor or person who lacks legal capacity is valid only after it has been approved by the superior court].) A petition for court approval of a compromise of a minor or disabled adult’s compromise or judgment of a pending action or proceeding to which this person is a party must be verified by the petitioner and must contain a full disclosure of all information that has any bearing upon the reasonableness of the compromise, covenant, settlement, or disposition. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.950; see also Cal. Rules of Court, rules 7.950.5-7.955.) The petition is generally submitted on a completed Petition for Approval of Compromise of Claim or Action or Disposition of Proceeds of Judgment for Minor or Person with a Disability (Judicial Council form MC-350). (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.950.) If the Court is satisfied that the compromise, covenant, settlement, or disposition is in the best interest of the person, then the Court should approve the same. (See Pearson v. Superior Court (2012) 202 Cal.App.4th 1333, 1338.)

  1. Motion to Seal

a.     Substantive Requirements

                              i.        Legal Standard

“The public has a First Amendment right of access to civil litigation documents filed in court and used at trial or submitted as a basis for adjudication.” (Savaglio v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. (2007) 149 Cal.App.4th 588, 596 (Savaglio), citing NBC Subsidiary (KNBC-TV), Inc. v. Superior Court (1999) 20 Cal.4th 1178, 1208-1209, fn. 25.)

Therefore, before a trial court orders a record sealed, it must hold a hearing and make express findings set forth in California Rules of Court, rule 2.550, subdivision (d): (1) there exists an overriding interest that overcomes the right of public access to the record; (2) the overriding interest supports sealing the record; (3) a substantial probability exists that the overriding interest will be prejudiced if the record is not sealed; (4) the proposed sealing is narrowly tailored; and (5) no less restrictive means exist to achieve the overriding interest. (Savaglio, supra, at p. 596; Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.550, subd. (d).) A court’s order allowing documents to be filed under seal must contain express factual findings establishing these five factors. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.550(d).)

                             ii.        Discussion

After review, the Court DENIES Berg’s motion, without prejudice.

1.     Overriding Interest

Berg’s motion to seal seeks to seal copies of or references to a 2006 confidential settlement agreement between Livingston and other parties, as related to sexual harassment allegations by Livingston at employment at ICM Partners.

The initial and critical step in sealing a record is showing an overriding interest in sealing the record that overcomes the right of public access to that record. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.2550(d)(1).)

Here, Berg’s motion argues as follows regarding overriding interest:

Privacy interests and binding contractual obligations not to disclose information provide the requisite “overriding interest” that justifies sealing a document and its associated information. NBC Subsidiary (KNBCTV), Inc. v. Superior Court, 20 Cal.4th 1178, 1222 n. 46 (1999) (approvingly citing Publicker Indus., Inc. v. Cohen, 733 F.2d 1059, 1073 (3d Cir. 1984) (recognizing such an overriding interest where “a binding contractual obligation not to disclose certain information which to the court seems innocuous but newsworthy,” as “in that situation unbridled disclosure of the nature of the controversy would deprive the litigant of his right to enforce a legal obligation”)); cf. Universal City Studios, Inc. v. Superior Ct., 110 Cal. App. 4th 1273, 1283 (2003) (agreeing “that [a party’s] contractual obligation not to disclose can constitute an overriding interest within the meaning of rule 243.1(d)” but holding no prejudice from disclosure had been shown in that case).

The overriding interests here are concrete and substantial. The 2006 Agreement expressly requires Ms. Livingston to keep confidential the Agreement’s terms and substance, including the settlement payment amount, and the underlying facts relating to her allegations against ICM and Mr. Sadoff. The TACC squarely violates this obligation by attaching the 2006 Agreement as an exhibit, disclosing its terms and underlying circumstances, and setting forth allegations concerning Mr. Sadoff, ICM’s internal complaint process, and the mediation process that resulted in the 2006 Agreement.

(Motion, pp. 4-5.)

However, Berg’s recitation of law is not correct.

It is true that a contractual obligation not to disclose the terms of a settlement agreement may constitute an overriding interest supporting the sealing of certain records. (Universal City Studios, Inc. v. Superior Court (2003) 110 Cal.App.4th 1273, 1284 (Universal City Studios).) Indeed, in NBC Subsidiary (KNBCTV), Inc. v. Superior Court, 20 Cal.4th 1178 (“NBC”), our Supreme Court cited Publicker Industries, Inc. v. Cohen (3d Cir. 1984) 733 F.2d 1059 (Publicker) for the proposition that “enforcement of binding contractual obligations not to disclose” may constitute an overriding interest. (NBC, supra, 20 Cal.4th at p. 1223, fn. 46.) In turn, Publicker held an “overriding interest” may include circumstances where the content of information may be critical, such as where trade secrets are involved, or where certain information is protected by the attorney-client privilege. (Publicker, supra, 733 F.2d at p. 1073.) The court further opined: “A similar situation would be presented where there is a binding contractual obligation not to disclose certain information which to the court seems innocuous but newsworthy; in that situation unbridled disclosure of the nature of the controversy would deprive the litigant of his right to enforce a legal obligation.” (Id. at pp. 1073–1074.)

However, and critical to the motion before the Court, under Publicker, “more than a mere agreement of the parties [is required] to seal documents filed in a public courtroom.” (Universal City Studios, supra, 110 Cal.App.4th at p. 1281.) There must be “a specific showing of serious injury” from disclosure. (Id. at p. 1282.) Universal City Studios noted no appellate court decision had construed Publicker to permit a record to be filed under seal “merely upon the agreement of the parties without a specific showing of serious injury.” (Universal City Studios, supra, at p. 1282; see Huffy Corp. v. Superior Court (2003) 112 Cal.App.4th 97, 106; McNair v. National Collegiate Athletic Assn. (2015) 234 Cal.App.4th 25, 35–36.) Thus, the “[m]ere deprivation of the right to enforce a contractual obligation is not, without an additional showing of serious harm, sufficient to override the public's right of access to the courts.” (Lederman v. Prudential Life Ins. Co. of America, Inc. (2006) 385 N.J. Super. 307, 318; see Publicker, supra, 733 F.2d at p. 1071 [party seeking to seal records must show disclosure will work a clearly defined and serious injury]; see also Universal City Studios, supra, 110 Cal.App.4th at pp. 1283–1284 [contractual obligation not to disclose terms of settlement agreement could be an “overriding interest,” however, defendant did not show substantial prejudice to its business interests would occur if settlement were not sealed].)

Here, the moving papers do not argue “serious injury,” instead limiting themselves to arguing “prejudice,” which is the third of five elements in a motion to seal. (Motion, pp. 5-6.) A review of the prejudice grounds shows arguments that (1) prejudice arises from the bare-faced public availability of the 2006 agreement in the record because the agreement was meant to be kept confidential and (2) third-party privacy rights are implicated, favoring denial.

The first point fails because, if this Court were to accept that the record must be sealed strictly based on the confidentiality provisions of the 2006 agreement, then this Court would be in direct contradiction of Universal Studios City. (Universal City Studios, supra, at p. 1282 [noting that no appellate court decision had construed Publicker to permit a record to be filed under seal “merely upon the agreement of the parties without a specific showing of serious injury”].)

The Court notes that confidentiality provisions contained in settlement agreements do not become unenforceable merely because litigation later arises involving the same subject matter. Rather, the confidentiality obligation may constitute an overriding interest if the moving party demonstrates with particularized facts that disclosure would result in a clearly defined and serious injury beyond the mere inability to enforce the agreement. Here, however, the moving papers contain no declaration identifying any specific reputational, business, privacy, or other concrete harm likely to result from disclosure of the challenged materials.

However, the second point carries merit regarding third-party privacy rights, which cannot be waived other than by those individuals or entities, and not by other persons or entities. (See Boler v. Superior Court (1987) 201 Cal.App.3d 467, 472, fn.1.)

Accordingly, the Court determines that an overriding interest here exists, but only as to third-party information, e.g., names, birth dates, addresses, phone numbers, email addresses, and Social Security number.

2.     Remaining Factors

The remaining factors for sealing a record are (2) the overriding interest supports sealing the record; (3) a substantial probability exists that the overriding interest will be prejudiced if the record is not sealed; (4) the proposed sealing is narrowly tailored; and (5) no less restrictive means exist to achieve the overriding interest.

Here, the overriding interest in protecting third-party privacy supports sealing the record as to third-party information, and a substantial probability exists that such interests will be prejudiced by the ongoing public nature of the record.

However, Berg’s motion, as filed, is overbroad as to whether the proposed sealing is narrowly tailored and whether less restrictive means exist to achieve the overriding interest. This is because the motion to seal does nothing to indicate which portions of the record are proposed to be sealed, e.g., which paragraphs in the TACC or which clauses in the 2006 agreement are meant to be sealed in relation to third-party privacy rights. If the Court were to accept the current recommendation, the Court would need to seal the entire TACC and 2006 agreement, all without any guidance as to which information therein is being challenged as inappropriately disclosed, and even though conceivably not all contents therein would be subject to an overriding interest supporting sealing of the record.

Less restrictive alternatives were readily available. For example, the moving party could have proposed narrowly tailored redactions directed only to identifying information or specific confidential settlement provisions rather than requesting wholesale sealing of the pleadings and exhibits. Such a proposal would have better satisfied Rule 2.550(d)(4) and (5).

An explanation for this shortcoming appears in counsel’s declaration: “At the time of this filing, the TACC has not yet been entered on the Court’s public docket, and I cannot verify whether the 2006 Agreement has been filed publicly or is under seal, nor whether Ms. Livingston has excerpted only portions of it or filed it in full. Accordingly, Mr. Berg submits the complete 2006 Agreement under seal for the Court’s review.” (Motion, Dugdale Decl., ¶ 2.) This explanation, however, does nothing to help Berg’s motion and in fact accomplishes the opposite by highlighting the fact that Berg filed his motion acting on imperfect information that caused him to request overbroad relief.

Berg’s motion thus fails to carry his burden, foreclosing relief.

b.     Conclusion

Cross-Defendant Jeffrey Berg’s motion to seal is accordingly DENIED, without prejudice.

a.     Meet and Confer

The Court determines that sufficient meet and confer efforts preceded the filing of this demurrer as required by the Code. (Demurrer, Dugdale Decl., ¶ 3, Ex. B; see Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(3)(A).)

b.     Legal Standard

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747; see Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (e).)

To sufficiently allege a cause of action, a complaint must allege all the ultimate facts—that is, the facts needed to establish each element of the cause of action pleaded. (Committee on Children’s Television, Inc. v. General Foods Corp. (1983) 35 Cal.3d 197, 212, superseded by statute as stated in Branick v. Downey Savings & Loan Assn. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 235, 242.) “[E]ach evidentiary fact that might eventually form part of the plaintiff’s proof need not be alleged.” (C.A. v. William S. Hart Union High School Dist. (2012) 53 Cal.4th 861, 872.)

In testing the sufficiency of the cause of action, the demurrer admits the truth of all material facts properly pleaded. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 966-67.) Courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (Taylor v. City of Los Angeles Dept. of Water and Power (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 1216, 1228, disapproved on other grounds, Jones v. Lodge at Torrey Pines Partnership (2008) 42 Cal.4th 1158, 1162.) A demurrer, however, “does not admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Daar v. Yellow Cab Co. (1967) 67 Cal.2d 695, 713.)

A demurrer may only be used to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) The face of the complaint includes exhibits attached to the complaint. (Frantz v. Blackwell (1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 91, 94.) If facts appearing in the exhibits contradict those alleged, the facts in the exhibits take precedence. (Holland v. Morse Diesel Intern., Inc. (2001) 86 Cal.App.4th 1443, 1447, superseded by statute on other grounds as stated in White v. Cridlebaugh (2009) 178 Cal.App.4th 506, 521.)

c.     Discussion

The TACC’s sixth cause of action alleges Negligent Supervision / Enabling Abuse against Berg, which arises from allegations surrounding the 2006 harassment alleged to have been experienced by Livingston and Berg’s failure to supervise Sadoff in 2006 and Gonzalez in the 2020s.

Berg demurrers to the TACC’s sixth cause of action on the grounds that the claim is barred either by an executed and applicable release from Livingston in the 2006 agreement or by the claim’s statute of limitations, which began to run in 2006. Berg otherwise argues that the claim fails to allege essential elements of the cause of action, including in part on the basis that the TACC fails to allege an underlying employment relationship as to Gonzalez. (See Demurrer, pp. 3-4.)

As to the limitations period argument, Livingston argues that on demurrer, the proposition that a claim has run its limitations period must be clear on the face of the pleadings, which Livingston argues is not the case here. (Opposition, pp. 3-4.) Livingston otherwise argues that she has sufficiently alleged this claim against Berg as based on conduct by Sadoff and Gonzalez. (Opposition, p. 4.)

In reply, Berg reiterates the position that the limitations period began to run in 2006 and argues that no continuing violations doctrine applies here. Specifically, Berg argues that the TACC’s only allegations against Berg beyond 2006 involve an innocuous question regarding whether Livingston had violated the 2006 agreement, and that the allegations against David Gonzalez (alleged as Berg’s former assistant) involve conduct in the 2020s that bears no alleged relationship to the 2006 allegations. (Reply, pp. 9-10.)

After review, the Court finds in favor of Berg.

Negligent supervision as subject to a two-year statute of limitations. (Code Civ. Proc., § 335.1.) Here, Berg is correct in highlighting that the TACC clearly establishes when the negligent supervision relating to Sadoff and the sexual harassment allegations is alleged to have taken place: “In 2006, while employed at ICM Partners under then-CEO Jeffrey Berg, she was subjected to persistent, disgusting sexual harassment and professional retaliation by Hal Sadoff.” (TACC, p. 2.) Assuming that this harassing conduct took place on December 31, 2006—the last day of 2006 and most favorable reading of the allegations—then on the face of the pleadings, the limitations period ran as of December 31, 2008.

Nor do the allegations support application of the continuing violation doctrine. The TACC alleges discrete acts of negligent supervision occurring in 2006. Later allegations concerning separate conduct by other individuals decades afterward do not revive a claim predicated upon supervisory decisions allegedly made in 2006. Absent allegations of continuing supervisory authority or continuing wrongful conduct by Berg within the limitations period, the statute of limitations is not tolled.

Separately, as argued by the demurrer (p. 5, lines 22-25), the TACC also pleads no viable negligent supervision in relation to conduct by David Gonzalez because the TACC clearly alleges that at the time that Gonzalez allegedly harassed Livingston in the 2020s, Gonzalez was a former employee of Berg’s. (TACC, p. 35 [“The full video surveillance recording capturing David Gonzales --- former assistant to Jeffrey Berg and long-term collaborator --- walking outside Cross-Complainant's residence, and scrutinizing it, in a manner consistent with surveillance.”].) This is fatal where negligent supervision applies only where “an employer … ‘knew or should have known that hiring the employee created a particular risk or hazard and that particular harm materializes.’” (Phillips v. TLC Plumbing, Inc. (2009) 172 Cal.App.4th 1133, 1139, emphasis added.)

Accordingly, Berg has met his burden on demurrer as to the sixth cause of action.

Leave to amend is denied because the defects identified above appear incapable of cure. The statute of limitations bar appears from the face of the pleading, and the TACC affirmatively alleges that David was a former employee rather than an employee subject to Berg's supervision. Livingston has not identified, either in the opposition or at meet-and-confer, any additional facts that could cure these legal deficiencies. Accordingly, the Court finds no reasonable possibility that amendment would state a viable cause of action.

d.     Conclusion

Cross-Defendant Jeffrey Berg’s demurrer is SUSTAINED, without leave to amend.

3.     Motion for Reconsideration

a.     Law Governing Reconsideration

The legislative intent was to restrict motions for reconsideration to circumstances where a party offers the court some fact or circumstance not previously considered, and some valid reason for not offering it earlier.  Gilberd v. AC Transit (1995) 32 Cal.App.4th 1494, 1500.  The burden under §1008 “is comparable to that of a party seeking a new trial on the ground of newly discovered evidence: the information must be such that the moving party could not, with reasonable diligence, have discovered or produced it at the trial.”  New York Times Co. v. Superior Court (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 206, 212-213.

 

A party seeking reconsideration of a prior order based on “new or different facts, circumstances or law” must provide a satisfactory explanation for failing to present the information at the first hearing; i.e., a showing of reasonable diligence.  Garcia v. Hejmadi (1997) 58 Cal.App.4th 674, 690.  A motion for reconsideration is properly denied where based on evidence that could have been presented in connection with the original motion. Morris v. AGFA Corp. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 1452, 1460.

 

Ruling on a motion for reconsideration is a two-step process.  The Court must first determine if there are new or different facts, circumstances, of law that support reconsidering the prior decision.  Once the Court finds such facts, circumstances, or law exist, the Court then revisits the prior decision and decides whether or not to change the decision.  Corns v. Miller (1986) 181 Cal.App.3d 195, 202.

 

b.     New or Different Facts, Circumstances, or Law

The threshold issue on this motion is whether Plaintiff presents new or different facts, circumstances, or law that could not have been presented in connection with the original motion to strike.  Plaintiff supports the motion with his own declaration, wherein he avers that he attempted to obtain information from Samsung but the requests went unfulfilled for “an extended period” and he received responsive information “recently.”  He declares an email from EV West was not available at the time of the original hearing but is now available.  He declares creating a timeline took effort and analysis of documents that were not organized at the time of the original hearing.  He declares he could not obtain physical evidence of unopened safety equipment until he learned of the industry standards from EV West.

 

With respect to the timeline, all dates were in the exclusive knowledge of Plaintiff, and any failure to create the timeline was a result of Plaintiff’s choice not to do so, not any “new or different” facts or circumstances.  Plaintiff mentions an email from EV West that was “not available” at the time of the original hearing, but does not attach the email or provide any specific information concerning when he contacted EV West, how long EV West took to respond, when the email from EV West was sent, etc.  With respect to Samsung, Plaintiff uses the word “recently,” but does not clarify whether this was before or after his opposition to the motion to strike was due.

 

c.     Merits of Motion

For purposes of this ruling, the Court will find that Plaintiff made an adequate showing of new or different facts or circumstances, despite the fact that, for the reasons detailed above, the showing is quite weak.  The Court must then move on to the second prong of the inquiry, which is whether the original ruling should stand or be altered.

 

Defendants argue a motion to strike punitive damages challenges only the face of the pleading, and therefore nothing stated by Plaintiff could possibly alter the outcome of the ruling.  Clearly this is true with respect to the portion of the ruling granting the motion to strike.  It is not, however, true with respect to the portion of the ruling denying leave to amend.  The Court routinely considers declarations and evidence when determining whether to grant leave to amend.

 

Plaintiff has not provided documentary evidence with his motion, and therefore the Court can only rely on Plaintiff’s declaration concerning what the documentary evidence shows.  The crux of the motion and Plaintiff’s declaration in support of the motion is that Defendants disregarded industry standards and battery safety requirements when repairing Plaintiff’s camper, and their decisions in this regard caused the fire that forms the basis of the lawsuit.  Plaintiff also declares that certain component parts that were necessary to the proper repair of his camper were found, unopened, in his van at the time of the fire, which confirms Defendants’ failure to repair the camper in a safe manner. 

 

The Court finds all of the above is evidence of negligence, but not of malice, fraud, or oppression, as defined by Civil Code §3294 and relevant case law.  Plaintiff has not provided any evidence of fraud or oppression.  “Malice” is defined as “conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.”   §3294(c)(1), College Hospital Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 725.  Absent an intent to injure the plaintiff, the conduct must be “despicable,” which is defined as “base, vile, or contemptible.”  Id. at 725.

 

Clearly various aspects of the declaration support the conclusion that Defendants acted with conscious disregard of Plaintiff’s safety.  There is, however, nothing in Plaintiff’s declaration that supports a finding that Defendants acted in a manner that was despicable (base, vile, or contemptible), as opposed to merely negligent. 

 

d.     Conclusion

Thus, even if the Court were to find new or different facts exist such that the ruling should be reconsidered, the Court would reaffirm its earlier ruling granting the motion to strike without leave to amend.

  1. Motion to Compel Deposition and Request for Sanctions

a.     Motion to Compel Deposition

                              i.        Legal Standard

After a deposition notice has been properly served, if the party or designated deponent does not serve a valid objection pursuant to section 2025.410 and then fails to appear, fails to proceed with the deposition, or fails to produce the documents or other materials identified in the notice, the noticing party may move for a court order compelling the deponent to appear, testify, and produce the requested materials. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subd. (a).)

Section 2025.450 contains no time limit for a motion to compel where a party fails to attend the deposition or produce any documents after valid service of the deposition notice and no objections have been made. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subds. (a)-(i)(2).)

If the motion seeks to compel production of documents, the motion must be accompanied by a showing of “good cause,” i.e., declarations containing specific facts justifying inspection of the documents described in the notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subd. (b)(1); see Code Civ. Proc., § 2016.040.)

No “meet and confer” is required where the deponent “fails to attend the deposition and produce the documents … described in the deposition notice.” In such cases, all that is required is a declaration by the moving party that the party has contacted the deponent “to inquire about the nonappearance.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subd. (b)(2).)

                             ii.        Discussion

After review, the Court finds in favor of Defendant YMCA, in part.

1.     Compelling Deposition

Defendant YMCA properly served deposition notices on Plaintiff on May 15, 2025, and April 17, 2026, both via service on counsel. (Motion, Valenti Decl., ¶¶ 6, 12, Exs. 1, 5.)

Plaintiff failed to raise valid objections pursuant to section 2025.410, where section 2025.410 contemplates objections for failure to comply with the requirements of Code of Civil Procedure sections 2025.210 to 2025.295. (See Motion, Valenti Decl., ¶¶ 7, 13, Exs. 2, 6 [Plaintiff’s written objections to deposition notices, failing to show objections pursuant to sections 2025.210 to 2025.295].)

Plaintiff did not appear for her noticed depositions (Motion, Valenti Decl., ¶¶ 6-15, Exs. 1-8), nor has she appeared for a deposition to date. (See Reply, Valenti Decl., ¶ 14-17.)

And Defendant YMCA provides extensive evidence to show it followed up with Plaintiff regarding her non-appearance. (See Motion, Valenti Decl., ¶¶ 6-17.)

As a result, an order compelling Plaintiff’s deposition is properly supported by the papers.

The Court briefly notes that it finds no merit to the opposition argument that Defendant YMCA’s motion is mooted by Plaintiff’s mere offering of deposition dates. (Opposition pp. 6-7.) Unless a deposition has actually gone forward, the relief sought in Defendant YMCA’s motion remains a live controversy.

Section 2025.450 does not require traditional meet and confer efforts before filing a motion based on a party’s failure to appear.  Rather, the noticing party needs only contact the deponent regarding the nonappearance.  (Code Civ. Pros., section 2025.450, subd. (b)(2)(Opposition p. 6.)

2.     Compelling Production of Documents in Deposition Notice

Separately, Plaintiff did not produce documents in response to the deposition notices, only serving substantive objections. (See Motion, Valenti Decl., ¶¶ 7, 13, Exs. 2, 6.)

However, the declaration of counsel attached to Defendant YMCA’s motion fails to advance specific facts justifying inspection of the documents described in the notice. (See Motion, Valenti Decl., ¶¶ 1-21; see also Reply, Valenti Decl., ¶¶ 3-19, Exs. 1-4 [discussing production generally but not establishing facts supporting relief].)

Consequently, relief as to production of documents is not supported by the papers.

                            iii.        Conclusion

Accordingly, Defendant Young Men’s Christian Association of Greater Long Beach’s motion is:

(1)   GRANTED as to compelling the deposition of Plaintiff; and

(2)   DENIED as to compelling production of documents pursuant to deposition notice.

                              i.        Legal Standard

If a motion pursuant to section 2025.450, subdivision (a) is granted, the court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) in favor of the party who noticed the deposition and against the deponent or the party with whom the deponent is affiliated, unless the court finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subd. (g)(1).)

The court may award sanctions under the Discovery Act in favor of a party who files a motion to compel discovery, even though no opposition to the motion was filed, or opposition to the motion was withdrawn, or the requested discovery was provided to the moving party after the motion was filed. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1348, subd. (a).)

Defendant YMCA also seeks $4,650.00 in sanctions against Plaintiff for failure to attend deposition, with the monetary amount sought in sanctions comprising hours spent by counsel in relation to attempting to depose Plaintiff.

(Opposition, p. 7.)

  1. Background Facts

Plaintiff Andrew Marquez sues Defendant General Motors, LLC and Does 1 through 20 pursuant to a May 15, 2026, Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”) alleging statutory and common law claims arising from Plaintiff’s lease of a 2024 Chevrolet Blazer manufactured, sold, distributed, and/or imported by Defendant.

On May 12, 2026, the Court granted an opposed, March 23, 2026, motion by Plaintiff to compel the deposition of putative General Motors employee, Kelsea Smith, which General Motors objected to on various grounds, including that Kelsea Smith was not at that time a General Motors employee, a representation which the Court did not credit.

Now before the Court is General Motors’ motion for reconsideration of the Court’s May 12, 2026, Order.

  1. Motion for Reconsideration
    1. Preliminary Matter – Different Judicial Officer Ruling on Present Motion than Prior Motion

“Generally, one trial judge may not review the ruling of another trial judge because the superior court, although comprised of many judges, is a single court. [Citation.] This principle, however, does not apply where the original judge is ‘unavailable.’ [Citation.]” (Geddes v. Superior Court (2005) 126 Cal.App.5th 417, 426.)

Here, the May 12, 2026, order was made by the Honorable Mel Red Recana, a retired judge serving temporarily as a bench officer for the Los Angeles Superior Court, i.e., a judge “unavailable” for the present motion’s purposes.

Accordingly, this Court under its current judicial officer proceeds with an analysis of the motion for reconsideration.

b.     Legal Standard

A motion for reconsideration is used to ask the court to modify, amend, or revoke its earlier order on a prior motion to the court because of new or different facts, circumstances, or law. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1008, subd. (a).) Such an order may involve an interim or final order (see Code Civ. Proc., § 1008, subds. (e), (h)), where an interim order is an intermediate ruling of some kind that requires further proceedings before the suit can be resolved, and where a final order is an order that finally disposes of the suit (see People v. DeLouize (2004) 32 Cal.4th 1223, 1231 [differentiating between interim and final orders]).

A motion for reconsideration may be brought by a party or by the court on its own motion. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 1008, subds. (a), (c).) The grounds for the motion vary depending on whether a party or the Court is making the motion. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 1008, subds. (a), (c), (e).)

A party may move for reconsideration based on: (1) new or different facts, (Code Civ. Proc., § 1008, subd. (a); see e.g., In re Marriage of LaMoure (2013) 221 Cal.App.4th 1463, 1473 [reconsideration motion granted on new evidence]); (2) new or different circumstances, (Code Civ. Proc., § 1008, subd. (a)); and (3) new or different law, (Code Civ. Proc., § 1008, subd. (a); Baldwin v. Home Sav. of Am. (1997) 59 Cal.App.4th 1192, 1196.) However, a party cannot move for reconsideration based on the court’s erroneous order, i.e., the court’s misinterpretation of facts or law. (See Jones v. P.S. Dev. Co. (2008) 166 Cal.App.4th 707, 724, disapproved on other grounds by Reid v. Google, Inc. (2010) 50 Cal.4th 512, 531-532, 532 n. 4; Gilbert v. AC Transit (1995) 32 Cal.App.4th 1494, 1500.) Instead, a party can suggest to the court that it should reconsider its erroneous order on its own motion so long as the suggestion is not made ex parte. (Le Francois v. Goel (2005) 35 Cal.4th 1094, 1108; see e.g., In re Marriage of Spector (2018) 24 Cal.App.5th 201, 214-15 [emails husband sent to court about erroneous order were not ex parte because wife was copied on all emails].)

    1. Discussion

After review, the Court finds in favor of Plaintiff.

As argued in Plaintiff’s opposition, all General Motors’ bases for relief involve facts that were or could have been before Judge Recana on May 12, 2026. General Motors’ motion’s arguments section is broken into three pieces: the first subsection addressing an alleged improper shift of the burden of proof by Judge Recana vis-à-vis whether General Motors employed Ms. Smith as of May 12, 2026; the second arguing that Judge Recana misstated General Motors’ opposition and moreover did not allow General Motors to clarify that misunderstanding; and the third arguing that this Court has no authority to compel General Motors to produce a non-affiliated witness or to pay sanctions for failure to produce Ms. Smith. (Motion, pp. 5-6.) None of these grounds state new or different facts, law, or circumstances that were not available on May 12, 2026, nor do the moving papers point to any authority that supports an interpretation of these grounds as new or different facts, law, or circumstances. General Motors thus in essence seeks a second bite at the apple, submitting evidence purporting to show new facts, but really, attempting to re-argue the point of whether Kelsea Smith in fact was a General Motors employee when the Court made its May 12, 2026, order.

The Court thus reads General Motors’ motion as one for reconsideration insofar as the moving party requests that the Court reconsider its erroneous order by way of the present noticed motion rather than presenting new or different facts, law, or circumstances.

However, the Court declines to exercise its discretion to reconsider the May 12, 2026, order, which the Court determines is supported by the record, as set forth in the order.

The Court closes with two brief points.

First, over Plaintiff’s opposition objections, the Court is satisfied with the form of the declarations filed with the moving papers, which set forth the basis for relief as required in Code of Civil Procedure section 1008 and Branner v. Regents of the Univ. of Cal. (2009) 175 Cap.App.4th 1043 (Branner). (See Code Civ. Proc., §§ 1008, subd. (a) [motion must include affidavit], 2015.5 [motion may use declaration instead of affidavit]; cf. Branner, supra, at p. 1048 [motion was invalid because it did not include either an affidavit or a declaration].)

Second, Plaintiff’s opposition devotes several pages to arguing why General Motors’ filing of its motion constitutes conduct supporting sanctions pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 128.7. (Opposition, pp. 6-8.) The Court is not convinced. As discussed above, the Court reads the motion for reconsideration as a request by a party, not made ex parte, but rather, by noticed motion, for reconsideration based on the Court’s own discretion.

d.     Conclusion

In conclusion, Defendant General Motors, LLC’s motion for reconsideration is DENIED.

Defendant General Motors, LLC is ordered to give notice.

  1. Background Facts

Plaintiff Joanna Patrice sues Defendants HSR Moorpark, LLC, HSR Management, Inc., and Does 1-50 pursuant to an October 1, 2025, First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) alleging statutory and common law claims arising from allegations that Defendants allowed persistent water intrusion, mold, and asbestos hazards to remain unaddressed in her unit, responded to her repair requests with threats and retaliation, caused her significant health issues and property contamination, reduced required housing services, and discriminated against her based on her sex and medical condition.

Now before the Court are Plaintiff Joanna Patrice’s opposed (1) motion to compel compliance with court orders, (2) request for issue sanctions and evidentiary sanctions, and (3) request for monetary sanctions.

  1. Motion to Compel Compliance with Court Orders

a.     Preliminary Note re: Nature of Motion

 A trial court may construe a motion bearing one label as a different type of motion. (Austin v. Los Angeles Unified School Dist. (2016) 244 Cal.App.4th 918, 930.) “‘The nature of a motion is determined by the nature of the relief sought, not by the label attached to it. The law is not a mere game of words. … The principle that a trial court may consider a motion regardless of the label placed on it by a party is consistent with the court’s inherent authority to manage and control its docket.’ [Citation.]” (Ibid.)

In its opposition to the motion to compel compliance with Court orders, Defendant HSR Moorpark argues that Plaintiff’s motion is procedurally improperly for being noticed pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.450. (See, e.g., Opposition, pp. 7-8.)

This is correct insofar as it goes. However, it is clear from the face of the record and the nature of the relief sought that Plaintiff is seeking to enforce this Court’s April 21, 2026, order mandating that Defendants produce subpoenaed documents concerning incidents of water intrusion, mold and remediation in the subject property. (See Motion, pp. 3, 4-5.)

Consequently, the Court reads this motion as being brought pursuant to a counterpart of the statute pursuant to which Plaintiff brought this motion, i.e., Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.480 as opposed to section 2025.450.

b.     Legal Standard

Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.480 authorizes a motion to compel when a deponent fails to answer a question or produce documents, electronically stored information, or tangible things identified in a deposition notice or subpoena, and subdivision (a) expressly provides that the party seeking discovery may request an order compelling such an answer or production. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.480, subd. (a).)

If a deponent fails to obey an order issued under this section, that failure may be treated as contempt of court, and when the disobedient deponent is a party—or an officer, director, managing agent, or employee of a party—the court may issue any just orders against that party or its affiliated party, including imposing an issue sanction, an evidence sanction, or a terminating sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010). (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.480, subd. (k).)

c.     Discussion

Plaintiff has filed a motion to enforce this Court’s April 21, 2026, order. That order resolved a motion filed by Plaintiff to compel HSR Moorpark to comply with 28 subpoenas for records. The subpoenas sought records copies of each unit’s lease agreement, work orders for repairs, and estimates and bills for remediation or repairs for water intrusion, mold, “etc.” for each apartment in Plaintiff’s building. The Court granted the motion in part, ordering production of documents involving water intrusion and mold, while limiting the relief only to Units 1 and 2 and the unit directly across from Plaintiff, and requiring redaction of private third-party information. (Apr. 21, 2026, Minutes, pp. 1-2, 4.)

In its July 22, 2026, opposition, HSR Moorpark recognizes its non-compliance with this Court’s order but argues that because the face of the Court’s order failed to impose a deadline for when production needed to be effected, HSR Moorpark is not in breach of the Court’s order. HSR Moorpark otherwise contends that it has diligently worked on gathering the court-ordered production but that the process has been complicated by counsel’s trial calendar, and that HSR Moorpark expects to produce documents within 14 days of filing the opposition. (Opposition, p. 8, citing Opposition, Arzoo Decl., ¶¶ 9-13.)

The reply fails to show that HSR Moorpark complied with its discovery obligations prior to July 24, 2026.

After review, the Court finds in favor of Plaintiff.

Simply, HSR Moorpark equivocates on whether a compliance period was set by the Court to openly disregard compliance with the April 21, 2026, order. Nothing in the record before the Court shows that HSR Moorpark has remedied its shortcomings in failing to produce the ordered documents over the past three and a half months.

Failure to comply may result in the imposition of sanctions, monetary or otherwise.

 

  1. Request for Issue and Evidence Sanctions

A review of Plaintiff’s moving papers shows, as argued by HSR Moorpark (Opposition, pp. 4-5), that Plaintiff failed to file a separate statement in support of her motion.

California Rules of Court, rule 3.1345(a)(7) specifically requires a separate statement in a motion for “issue or evidentiary sanctions.” The purpose of that separate statement is to streamline adjudication of discovery motions. (Mills v. U.S. Bank (2008) 166 Cal.App.4th 871, 893 (Mills).) The failure to file a separate statement with a motion when required may result in denial. (See, e.g., ibid. at p. 893 [failure to include separate statement as required by Rules of Court justified trial court’s discretionary denial of discovery motion].) As stated by the court of appeal regarding separate statements in the summary judgment context:  “The burdens imposed upon the trial court … will be eased considerably if the court insists, as it should, on strict compliance with (the required separate statement),” because the court’s ability to focus on and articulate the evidentiary basis for its ruling will be found in the separate statement. (Young v. Superior Court (1986) 179 Cal.App.3d 28, 31-32.)

Here, while the Court could exercise its discretion to rule on Plaintiff’s motion for issue and evidence sanctions, the burden on the Court to do so would be high insofar as Plaintiff’s moving papers insufficiently elaborate on the form and scope of relief sought. Otherwise stated, the moving papers do not offer even a concise outline of the discovery requests and each response in dispute, which may act in lieu of a separate statement and clarify for the court the issues to be addressed in the issue and evidence sanctions context. (See Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1345(b)(2).)

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s motion for issue and evidence sanctions is DENIED.

 

  1. Request for Monetary Sanctions

a.     Legal Standard

In lieu of or in addition to issue, evidence, and terminating sanctions, the court may also impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 against a disobedient party-deponent or any party with whom the deponent is affiliated. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subd. (k).)

b.     Discussion

After review, the Court finds in favor of HSR Moorpark.

As argued in Defendant’s opposition, the notice of motion and moving papers fail to identify against whom sanctions are sought (Opposition, pp. 8-9)—e.g., HSR Moorpark and/or counsel—which violates Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.040, requiring that a notice of motion identify every person, party, and attorney against whom sanctions are sought and moreover specify the type of sanction sought.

c.     Legal Standard

Plaintiff’s request for monetary sanctions is accordingly DENIED.

On February 4, 2026, Plaintiff filed for and the Clerk entered an entry of default against Teresa Sandoval.

Now before the Court is Defendant Teresa Sandoval’s unopposed (1) motion to set aside entry of default and (2) motion to quash service of summons, both based on the argument that service of summons was effected at an address bearing no relation to Teresa Sandoval at the time of service, for which reason she had no notice of this action and an entry of default was secured against her.

 

  1. Motion to Set Aside Default

a.     Legal Standard

California courts are empowered pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 473 to relieve a party “upon any terms as may be just … from a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against him or her through his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b).)

Section 473 consists of two distinct parts: “a discretionary provision, which applies permissively, and a mandatory provision, which applies as of right.” (Minick v. City of Petaluma (2016) 3 Cal.App.5th 15, 25-26.) Relief is discretionary unless the application is accompanied by an “attorney affidavit of fault” supporting mandatory relief, and such discretionary relief must be based on a showing of “mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b); see Lorenz v. Commercial Acceptance Ins. Co. (1995) 40 Cal.App.4th 981, 989.)

A party moving for relief on the basis of “mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect” must show specific facts demonstrating that one of these conditions was met. (Hopkins & Carley v. Gens (2011) 200 Cal.App.4th 1401, 1410 [defendant’s burden is to demonstrate “that due to some mistake, either of fact or of law, of himself or of his counsel, or through some inadvertence, surprise or neglect which may properly be considered excusable, the judgment or order from which he seeks relief should be reversed” (emphasis in original; internal quotes omitted)].)

Discretionary relief must be sought within a reasonable time and in no case exceeding six months after entry of the default, judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding. (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b).)

b.     Discussion

After review, the Court finds sufficient grounds to grant relief.

Defendant Teresa Sandoval has filed a motion for relief from the February 4, 2026, entry of default against her. (See Motion, ¶¶ 2-3 at pp. 2-3.)

Service of the motion was properly effected on counsel via email. (Compare Motion, Proof of Service [service on counsel at [email protected]], with Complaint, 1:1-4 [counsel information, with [email protected] email address].)

Relief is properly sought within six months of entry of default, Defendant having filed her motion for relief on May 15, 2026, within six months of February 4, 2026.

And Defendant Teresa Sandoval provides a sworn declaration to show entry of default was entered against her by mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect: Service of summons was effected at 12854 Morning Avenue, Downey, California 90242, on November 14, 2025, on which date Plaintiff resided at 6042 Orange Avenue, Cypress, California 90630, for which reason Defendant Teresa Sandoval received no notice of this action, and her failure to respond is excusable. (Motion, Sandoval Decl., ¶¶ 1-6.)

The record fails to reflect any opposition to this motion by Plaintiff to contest Defendant Teresa Sandoval’s position regarding service at the correct address.

c.     Conclusion

Defendant Teresa A. Sandoval’s motion to set aside entry of default is accordingly GRANTED.

The Court VACATES the February 4, 2026, entry of default against Defendant Teresa A. Sandoval.

 

  1. Motion to Quash Service of Summons

a.     Legal Standard

 

A motion to quash can be brought on the ground that the court lacks personal jurisdiction over the defendant, e.g., because service was improper. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 418.10, subd. (a)(1); see, e.g., Tresway Aero, Inc. v. Superior Court (1971) 5 Cal.3d 431, 433 [defendant filed motion to quash on ground that service on corporation was defective because it did not comply with Code Civ. Proc., § 410, now § 412.30]; County of Riverside v. Superior Court (1997) 54 Cal.App.4th 443, 446 [defendant filed motion to quash on ground that service was defective because summons did not specify date for answering and appearing].)

The defendant should attach evidence to support its motion. (School Dist. of Okaloosa Cty. v. Superior Court (1997) 58 Cal.App.4th 1126, 1131 [defendant must present some admissible evidence in form of affidavits or declarations to place issue of lack of jurisdiction before court]; see, e.g., Aquila, Inc. v. Superior Court (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 556, 563 [defendant supported motion to quash with declarations].) The defendant does not have to provide supporting evidence, however, until the plaintiff shows that the court has personal jurisdiction over the defendant. (See Floveyor Int’l v. Superior Court (1997) 59 Cal.App.4th 789, 793-794.) Stated otherwise, the defendant can file a motion to quash without supporting evidence and then do nothing until the plaintiff has provided evidence that the court has personal jurisdiction. (See ibid. [“‘A defendant who takes the position that the service of summons as made upon him did not bring him within the jurisdiction of the court’[] may serve and file a notice of motion to quash the service,’” where “‘the effect of such a notice is to place upon the plaintiff the burden of proving the facts that did give the court jurisdiction … [or] the facts requisite to a[] [showing of] effective service,’” citations omitted].)

b.     Discussion

Defendant Teresa Sandoval brings a motion to quash service of summons for failure to comply with the Code of Civil Procedure insofar as Defendant was served at a residential address where she did not live on the date of service. (See Motion, ¶ 1 at p. 2.)

Defendant supports this position with a declaration from Defendant Teresa Sandoval herself, discussed above, indicating that service of summons was effected at 12854 Morning Avenue, Downey, California 90242, on November 14, 2025, on which date Plaintiff resided at 6042 Orange Avenue, Cypress, California 90630, for which reason service does not conform with the Code of Civil Procedure’s requirements for service of summons. (See, e.g., Ibid.)

The record fails to reflect any opposition to this motion by Plaintiff to contest Defendant Teresa Sandoval’s position regarding service at the correct address, where, as stated ante, after a defendant makes a motion to quash, the burden is on the plaintiff to prove by a preponderance of the evidence facts justifying the exercise of jurisdiction over the defendant. (Ziller Elecs. Lab. GmbH v. Superior Court (1988) 206 Cal.App.3d 1222, 1232-1233.)

c.     Conclusion

Defendant Teresa A. Sandoval’s motion to quash service of summons is accordingly GRANTED.

  1. Background Facts

a.     Motion Before the Court

Now before the Court are Plaintiff’s opposed motions to quash seven subpoenas duces tecum seeking Plaintiff’s employment records [CRS # 4154] and therapy and medical records [CRS # 8958].)

b.     Pleadings

As contextual background, the Court summarizes the pleadings.

Plaintiff Marti’an Hughes (“Plaintiff”) sues Defendants Jean-Jacques Elbaz, D.D.S., M.S. (“Elbaz”), The Beverly Hills Center for Advanced Dental Implants & Periodontology (the “Beverly Hills Center”), and Does 1-100 pursuant to a December 3, 2025, Complaint alleging claims for (1) Sexual Harassment, (2) Failure to Prevent Harassment, (3) Retaliation under FEHA, (4) Whistleblower Retaliation under Labor Code § 1102.5, (5) Wrongful Constructive Termination in Violation of Public Policy, (6) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress, (7) Failure to Pay Minimum Wages, (8) Unpaid Overtime, (9) Failure to Provide Accurate Wage Statements, and (10) Failure to Pay All Wages Due Upon Separation.

The claims arise from the following allegations. Hughes, an employee of Defendants’, was subjected to repeated sexual harassment by a long-standing patient and by Elbaz himself, including unwanted sexual comments, a physical assault, and receipt of a pornographic image, all of which she allegedly reported and Defendants failed to address. Instead, Defendants retaliated after Hughes’s complaints and moreover retaliated against Hughes after she refused to retroactively alter patient records, isolating Hughes, reducing her duties, removing benefits, and worsening her working conditions. These conditions became intolerable, forcing Hughes’s constructive resignation on June 2, 2025. Defendants otherwise are alleged to have engaged in wage-and-hour violations, including unpaid off-the-clock work, inaccurate wage statements, and failure to pay all wages owed at separation.

In turn, Defendant and Cross-Complainant Jean-Jacques Elbaz, D.D.S., M.S., a Professional Dental Corporation dba The Beverly Hills Center for Advanced Dental Implants & Periodontology (“Cross-Complainant”), brings a January 29, 2026 Cross-Complaint against Plaintiff and Cross-Defendant Marti’an Hughes (“Hughes”) and Roes 1–20 alleging a single cause of action for Violations of California Penal Code § 502.

The claims arise from allegations that Elbaz and his dental practice allege that Hughes intentionally accessed and manipulated Beverly Hills Center’s computer systems without authorization shortly before resigning, deleting operational files and wiping browser data in a manner that concealed her activity. They further claim she entered the restricted server room on her last day, after which the network crashed due to a physically disconnected cable, abruptly halting all patient operations. The Practice states the crash required emergency IT remediation, forensic investigation, and substantial internal labor to restore functionality and identify missing or damaged files. It seeks damages for lost business, operational disruption, and continuing investigative and restoration costs.

    1. Meet and Confer

The Court briefly notes that no meet and confer efforts need precede the filing of a motion to quash pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1985.3, subdivision (g) (permitting motion by consumer pursuant to § 1987.1 to quash deposition subpoena for consumer records), 1985.6, subdivision (f)(1) (motion by employee pursuant to § 1987.1  to quash deposition subpoena for employment records), or 1987.1, subdivision (a) (motion to quash a subpoena for attendance and/or production at a deposition). (See Code Civ. Proc., § 1985, et seq.)

b.     Preliminary Considerations

                                          i.    Separate Statement

The Court declines to deny the present motion for failure to attach a separate statement as required by California Rules of Court, rule 3.1345. First, the election is discretionary. (Mills v. U.S. Bank (2008) 166 Cal.App.4th 871, 893 [a court’s denial of a motion for failure to attach a separate statement is discretionary].) Second, Plaintiff’s papers provide a sufficient outline of the party’s position for the hearing to move forward, as shown by the fact that Defendants were able to draft a separate statement roughly summarizing Plaintiff’s position on each motion to quash. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1345(b)(2) [court may permit concise outline may serve in lieu of separate statement].)

                                         ii.    Request for Judicial Notice

The Court declines to take judicial notice of prior orders by Judge Kim in other actions vis-à-vis whether a separate statement is required in support of motions to quash subpoenas duces tecum. No such notice is required for disposition of the matters before the Court.

c.     Legal Standard

A motion to quash a deposition subpoena or notice is used to strike, modify, or impose conditions on a subpoena that is procedurally or substantively defective. (See Code Civ. Proc., §§ 1985.3, subd. (g), 1985.6, subd. (f)(1),¿1987.1, subd. (a), 2025.410, subd. (c).)

A motion to quash may be made by (1) any party to the action, (2) the subpoenaed witness, (3) a person whose consumer, governmental, or employment records are sought, (4) a person whose “personally identifying information” is subpoenaed in context of free-speech activities, or (5) the court on its own motion after notice and hearing. (See Code Civ. Proc., §¿1987.1(b)(1)–(5), (a); see also id. at §§ 1985.3, subd. (g), 1985.6, subd. (f)(1).)

Grounds for such a motion include: errors or irregularities in the deposition notice (Code Civ. Proc., §¿2025.410, subds. (a), (c)); requests for information outside the scope of discovery or non-relevant material (Catholic Mut. Relief Soc’y v. Superior Court (2007) 42 Cal.4th 358, 365; Cadiz Land Co. v. Rail Cycle, L.P. (2000) 83 Cal.App.4th 74, 122-123; unreasonable or oppressive burdens (McClatchy Newspapers v. Sup. Ct. (1945) 26¿Cal.2d¿386,¿391); invasion of privacy interests (Fett v. Medical Bd. (2016) 245¿Cal.App.4th¿211,¿213); or violations of privilege (Monarch Healthcare v. Superior Court (2000) 78 Cal.App.4th 1282, 1290.)

    1. Discussion

After review, the Court finds in favor of Plaintiff, in part, and in favor of Defendants, in part.

                                          i.    Duplicative Discovery

The Court initially notes that it does not find any merit to Plaintiff’s argument that Defendants fail to use less intrusive means to seek the information sought by the subject subpoenas. (4154 Motion, p. 12; 8958 Motion, p. 12.) Multiple forms of discovery may be sought as to the same subject absent a showing under Code of Civil Procedure section 2019.030, subdivisions (a)(1)-(2). (See also Carter v. Superior Court (1990) 218 Cal.App.3d 994, 997 [discovery as to the same subject permitted in different forms—§ 2031 production requests and deposition production requests—even if duplicative, absent § 2019.030 showing].)

                                         ii.    Alleged Gamesmanship and Character Evidence Issues

The Court briefly notes that while providing helpful context, Plaintiff’s argument that Defendants are seeking wide-sweeping information related to Plaintiff while failing to comply with their own discovery obligations is not a basis to grant or deny the present motion. (4154 Motion, pp. 12-13; 8958 Motion, p. 13.)

Separately, the Court notes Plaintiff’s concerns that production of disciplinary records simply goes toward character evidence, which the Court weighs in making its final determination. (4154 Motion, pp. 13-14.)

                                        iii.    Right to Privacy, Overbreadth, and Subpoena Modification

The Court turns to Plaintiff’s main two arguments in favor of relief—the right to privacy and overbreadth—as well as Plaintiff’s request for subpoena modifications. (4154 Motion, pp. 12-13, 14-15; 8958 Motion, pp. 5-12, 13-14.)

Under the California Constitution, a person has a right to privacy. (Cal. Cons., art. I, § 1; Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 552 (Williams); County of L.A. v. Los Angeles Cty. Empl. Relations Comm’n (2013) 56 Cal.4th 905, 926; see, e.g., In re Ins. Installment Fee Cases (2012) 211 Cal.App.4th 1395, 1428 [A person has a right to prevent disclosure of information about personal finances, including information about bank accounts, assets, and debts]; Mathews v. Becerra (2019) 8 Cal.5th 756, 782 [A person has a right to prevent disclosure of the person’s medical history and to prevent an intrusion into bodily autonomy, to wit, information of psychiatric treatment].)

Critically, however, the right to privacy is qualified, not absolute. (John B. Superior Court (2006) 38 Cal.4th 1177, 1199 (John B.).) A party can waive its right to privacy, such as by raising a claim or defense in a suit that puts the personal information at issue. (See, e.g., ibid. at p. 1199 [by putting his own medical condition at issue, defendant substantially lowered expectation of privacy]; see also Wegner, Fairbank, Epstein & Chernow, California Practice Guide: Civil Trials & Evidence (The Rutter Group) Ch. 8-E ¶ 8:2728 [a party who claims emotional distress from sexual harassment may waive her right to privacy as to her present mental or emotional condition].)

If the case involves an obvious invasion of a privacy interest that is fundamental to the holder’s personal autonomy, a compelling interest or need must be present to overcome the privacy interest. (Williams, supra, 3 Cal.5th at p. 552.) To show a compelling need, the discovering party must demonstrate that the information sought is directly relevant and essential to the fair resolution of the legal proceeding. (Alch v. Superior Court (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 1412, 1425; Britt v. Superior Court (1978) 20 Cal.3d 844, 859 [same].)

If the privacy interest is less central or in bona fide dispute, a general balancing test must be used. (Lewis v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 561, 572.) Under this test, the invasion of privacy interest does not violate the state constitutional right to privacy if the invasion is justified by a legitimate interest. (Lewis v. Superior Court, supra, 3 Cal.5th at p. 573; Hill v. NCAA, supra, 7 Cal.4th at p. 38.) The general balancing test applies to most privacy interests. (Lewis v. Superior Court, supra, 3 Cal.5th at p. 573; Hernandez v. Hillsides, Inc. (2009) 47 Cal.4th 272, 288.)

Here, Plaintiff’s Complaint puts damages at issue (e.g., wrongful termination, lost wages, and mitigation) and moreover puts emotional distress at issue. (See, e.g., ¶¶ 94-105 [emotional distress at ¶ 101, within wrongful termination context].) Thus, records from past and present employers, as well as medical records relating to Plaintiff’s emotional distress over the years, are, to a point, relevant to a fair resolution of this action, which would satisfy even the compelling interest standard. For example, if confounding sources of emotional distress exist, or if evidence shows damages have been mitigated, those pieces of evidence are directly relevant to a resolution of Plaintiff’s claims and to Defendants’ defenses.

However, at the heart of the question is whether the subpoenas go too far irrespective of whether they can be pared down to comply with the Code.

The Court’s review answers in the affirmative, both with respect to privacy and with respect to overbreadth. However, the Court also determines that the subpoenas can be properly modified by this Court to state requests properly tailored to lead to admissible evidence or matter that appears reasonably calculated to lead to admissible evidence. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.1, subd. (a) [court may modify subpoena upon motion]; see Code Civ. Proc., § 2017.010 [scope of discovery].)

    1. Request for Monetary Sanctions in Opposition

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 1987.2, subdivision (a), the court retains discretion to award “reasonable expenses” — including attorney’s fees — incurred in making or opposing a motion. Such an award may be made if the court determines that the motion was presented in bad faith, without substantial justification, or that a particular subpoena requirement was oppressive.

Defendants’ opposition papers seek an award of monetary sanctions against Plaintiff for bringing this motion without justification among other things.

The Court DENIES those requests. Simply, the Court’s reasoning above shows both parties acted with substantial justification in making and opposing the motions to quash subpoenas duces tecum.

Plaintiff Mark Williams sues the Honorable Randolph Hammock, Judge of the Superior Court, pursuant to an April 27, 2026, Complaint alleging claims for deprivation of rights under color of law pursuant to 42 United States Code section 1983.

Plaintiff’s Complaint arises from allegations that, acting under color of law, Judge Hammock “refused to adjudicate his authority” when directly questioned and nevertheless “proceeded to order” dismissals of cases filed by Plaintiff, and that Hammock acted “outside lawful judicial authority without jurisdiction,” thereby denying Williams due process by exercising judicial power without first establishing the constitutional authority required to do so. Based thereon, Plaintiff asserts deprivation of constitutional rights to due process, impartial adjudication, and lawful exercise of judicial authority under 42 U.S.C. § 1983. (Complaint, pp. 2-4 at § III.)

Now before the Court is Judge Hammond’s properly noticed yet unopposed demurrer to Plaintiff’s Complaint, including on the grounds of judicial immunity. (Compare Complaint, p. 1, with Demurrer, Proof of Service [service at same postal office box address].)

After review, the Court finds in favor of Judge Hammond.

The United States Supreme Court established the rule that judges are immune from civil suits arising out of the exercise of their judicial functions. (Mireles v. Waco (1991) 502 U.S. 9, 11.) Moreover, in California, judicial officers are absolutely immune from liability for the performance of their judicial duties. (Howard v. Drapkin (1990) 222 Cal.App.3d 843, 851; Frost v. Geernaert (1988) 200 Cal.App.3d 1104; Oppenheimer v. Ashburn (1959) 173 Cal.App.2d 624, 629-630.) The test for judicial immunity is set forth in Stump v. Sparkman (1973) 435 U.S. 349, 362: “[T]he factors determining whether an act by a judge is a ‘judicial’ one relate to the nature of the act itself, i.e., whether it is a function normally performed by a judge, and to the expectations of the parties, i.e., whether they dealt with the judge in his judicial capacity.”

Here, the allegations in the Complaint fall squarely into that of a person dealing with a judge in his judicial capacity. For example, the Complaint alleges deprivation of rights through Judge Hammond’s “refus[al] to adjudicate his authority” when directly questioned and “proceed[ing] to order” dismissals of cases filed by Plaintiff. (Complaint, pp. 2-4 at § III.)

Defendant, the Honorable Randolph Hammock’s demurrer is accordingly SUSTAINED, without leave to amend.

Defendant, the Honorable Randolph Hammock, is ordered to give notice.
