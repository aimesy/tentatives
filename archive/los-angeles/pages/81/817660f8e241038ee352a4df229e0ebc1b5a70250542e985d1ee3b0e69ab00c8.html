APPLICATION

Admitted Pro Hac Vice

APPLICANT

Scott Browning

 

            Scott Browning of Husch Blackwell LLP applies to be admitted pro hac vice as counsel for Defendants Unto Infinity, LLC; Siri Singh Sahib of Sikh Dharma; Siri Singh Sahib Corp.; Sikh Dharma International; 3HO Foundation International; and Kundalini Research Institute.  The application is unopposed.          

 

ANALYSIS

 

            California Rules of Court rule 9.40 provides that an attorney in good standing in another jurisdiction may apply to appear as counsel pro hac vice in the State of California by filing a verified application together with proof of service by mail of a copy of the application and notice of hearing on all parties who have appeared in the case and on the State Bar of California at its San Francisco office, with payment of the application fee, so long as that attorney is not a resident of the State of California, and is not regularly engaged in substantial business, professional, or other activities in the State of California. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40.)

 

The application must state: (1) the applicant’s residence and office addresses; (2) the courts to which the applicant has been admitted to practice and the dates of admission; (3) that the applicant is a member of good standing in those courts; (4) that the applicant is not currently suspended or disbarred in any court; (5) the title of each court and cause in which the applicant has filed an application to appear as counsel pro hac vice in this state in the preceding two years, the date of each application, and whether or not it was granted; and (6) the name, address, and telephone number of the active member of the State Bar of California who is attorney of record in the local action. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(d).)

 

            The Verified Application provides all this information.  Further, the Declaration of Paloma I. Acosta indicates that the requisite fee has been paid.    

 

The proof of service indicates counsel for all partes were served electronically and the State Bar was served both electronically and by mail at its San Francisco office, in accordance with California Rules of Court, Rule 9.40(c), which provides:  “A person desiring to appear as counsel pro hac vice in a superior court must file with the court a verified application together with proof of service by mail in accordance with Code of Civil Procedure section 1013a of a copy of the application and of the notice of hearing of the application on all parties who have appeared in the cause and on the State Bar of California at its San Francisco office. The notice of hearing must be given at the time prescribed in Code of Civil Procedure section 1005 unless the court has prescribed a shorter period.”   (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(c)(1), emphasis added.) 

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

 

Finding Counsel has satisfied all procedural and substantive requirements, the Court grants Counsel’s unopposed verified application to appear pro hac vice in this action. 

 

Further, the Court will enter the proposed Order lodged on June 3, 2026 in conformity with the ruling.

 

Counsel shall provide notice of the Court’s ruling and file the notice with a proof of service forthwith.

            Curtis Greene of Barnes & Thornburg, LLP, applies to be admitted pro hac vice as counsel for Cross-Defendants Quanex Building Products Corporation and Quanex IG Systems, Inc.  The application is unopposed.     

 

ANALYSIS

 

            California Rules of Court rule 9.40 provides that an attorney in good standing in another jurisdiction may apply to appear as counsel pro hac vice in the State of California by filing a verified application together with proof of service by mail of a copy of the application and notice of hearing on all parties who have appeared in the case and on the State Bar of California at its San Francisco office, with payment of the application fee, so long as that attorney is not a resident of the State of California, and is not regularly engaged in substantial business, professional, or other activities in the State of California. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40.)

 

The application must state: (1) the applicant’s residence and office addresses; (2) the courts to which the applicant has been admitted to practice and the dates of admission; (3) that the applicant is a member of good standing in those courts; (4) that the applicant is not currently suspended or disbarred in any court; (5) the title of each court and cause in which the applicant has filed an application to appear as counsel pro hac vice in this state in the preceding two years, the date of each application, and whether or not it was granted; and (6) the name, address, and telephone number of the active member of the State Bar of California who is attorney of record in the local action. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(d).)

 

            The Verified Application provides most this information, and indicates that the requisite application fee has been paid.  

 

            However, instead of indicating the title of each court and cause in which the applicant has filed an application to appear as pro hac vice in this state in the preceding two years, the date of each application, and whether or not such application as granted, Counsel simply indicates the cases in which he has been admitted to practice in California in the preceding two years.  That does not satisfy the requirement. 

 

The proof of service indicates counsel for all partes were served electronically.  However, there is no proof of service demonstrating service on the State Bar by mail at its San Francisco office, as required by California Rules of Court, Rule 9.40(c), which provides:  “A person desiring to appear as counsel pro hac vice in a superior court must file with the court a verified application together with proof of service by mail in accordance with Code of Civil Procedure section 1013a of a copy of the application and of the notice of hearing of the application on all parties who have appeared in the cause and on the State Bar of California at its San Francisco office. The notice of hearing must be given at the time prescribed in Code of Civil Procedure section 1005 unless the court has prescribed a shorter period.”   (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(c)(1), emphasis added.) 

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

The Court denies the application without prejudice as procedurally defective. 

 

Counsel shall provide notice of the Court’s ruling and file the notice with a proof of service forthwith.

Defendant opposes the motion and Plaintiff replies.

LEGAL STANDARDS – MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT/ADJUDICATION

 

“[T]he party moving for summary judgment bears the burden of persuasion that there is no triable issue of material fact and that he is entitled to judgment as a matter of law[.] There is a triable issue of material fact if, and only if, the evidence would allow a reasonable trier of fact to find the underlying fact in favor of the party opposing the motion in accordance with the applicable standard of proof.” ¿(Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850 (hereafter Aguilar).) ¿“[T]he party moving for summary judgment bears an initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing of the nonexistence of any triable issue of material fact; if he carries his burden of production, he causes a shift, and the opposing party is then subjected to a burden of production of his own to make a prima facie showing of the existence of a triable issue of material fact.” ¿(Ibid.; Smith v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 1463, 1474 [summary judgment standards held by Aguilar apply to summary adjudication motions].) 

 

Further, “the trial court may not weigh the evidence in the manner of a factfinder to determine whose version is more likely true.  Nor may the trial court grant summary judgment based on the court's evaluation of credibility.”  (Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th. at p. 840 [cleaned up]; see also Weiss v. People ex rel. Department of Transportation (2020) 9 Cal.5th 840, 864 [“Courts deciding motions for summary judgment or summary adjudication may not weigh the evidence but must instead view it in the light most favorable to the opposing party and draw all reasonable inferences in favor of that party”].) 

 

A party may move for summary adjudication as to one or more causes of action, affirmative defenses, claims for damages, or issues of duty if that party contends that there is no merit to the cause of action, defense, or claim for damages, or if the party contends that there is no duty owed.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (f)(1).)  “A motion for summary adjudication shall be granted only if it completely disposes of a cause of action, an affirmative defense, a claim for damages, or an issue of duty.”  (Ibid.)  A cause of action has no merit if: (1) one or more elements of the cause of action cannot be separately established, even if that element is separately pleaded, or (2) a defendant establishes an affirmative defense to that cause of action.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (n); Union Bank v. Superior Court (1995) 31 Cal.App.4th 573, 583.)  Once the defendant has shown that a cause of action has no merit, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to show that a triable issue of material fact exists as to that cause of action.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (o)(2); Union Bank v. Superior Court, supra, 31 Cal.App.4th at p. 583.)  Additionally, in line with Aguilar, “[o]n a motion for summary adjudication, the trial court has no discretion to exercise.  If a triable issue of material fact exists as to the challenged causes of action, the motion must be denied. If there is no triable issue of fact, the motion must be granted.”  (Fisherman's Wharf Bay Cruise Corp. v. Superior Court (2003) 114 Cal.App.4th 309, 320.) 

 

DISCUSSION

 

1.     ISSUE 1: Defendant’s Breach of Guaranty

 

“To prevail on a cause of action for breach of contract, the plaintiff must prove (1) the contract, (2) the plaintiff's performance of the contract or excuse for nonperformance, (3) the defendant's breach, and (4) the resulting damage to the plaintiff.”  (Richman v. Hartley (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 1182, 1186.) 

 

a.     Plaintiff’s Evidence

 

In support of the motion, Plaintiff advances the following evidence:

 

·       Plaintiff is the owner and landlord of the real property at issue (UMF No. 1.)

 

·       Plaintiff and Lessee, Karma CPAS, Inc. (“Karma”) entered into a commercial lease agreement and first amendment to the lease agreement for the premises on February 8, 2021.  (UMF No. 2.)

 

·       Defendant is president of Karma and guarantor of the lease.  (UMF No. 3.)

 

·       The lease provides as follows:

 

o   Section 1.4: Lessor makes no representation or warranty concerning the suitability of the Leased Premises to conduct Lessee’s business

 

o   Section 14.1: “Common Area” includes the “parking areas.”

 

o   Section 14.3: Lessor has the unqualified right to increase or reduce the “Common Area” and to rearrange parking spaces, driveways, and building improvements on the “Common Area.”

 

o   Section 14.5: The “Common Area” is under Lessor’s exclusive control/management and Lessee agrees to abide and conform with rules/regulations adopted thereto.

 

o   Section 14.8: Lessor covenants that the “Common Area” shall be available for the non-exclusive use by the Lessee during the term of the Lease, subject to the rules/regulations.

 

o   Section 34.6: There are no oral agreements between the parties and the Lease supersedes and cancels any previous negotiations or representations of any kind whatsoever.

 

o   Section 34.7: The Lease constitutes the entire agreement between Lessor and Lessee and may only be amended by written agreement.

 

o   “Amendments” at Section 12: Lessee shall have a 180-day contingency period from lease execution to obtain Permits for its use.  (UMF No. 4.)

 

·       A Second Amendment to the Lease, executed by Plaintiff and Karma on September 17, 2021, provides as follows:

 

o   Section 12 of the “Amendments,” referencing the contingency is removed and no additional contingencies remain.  (UMF Nos. 5-6)

 

·       A Third Amendment to the Lease, executed by Plaintiff and Karma on September 1, 2022, provides as follows:

 

o   Lessor shall not grant any type of concession to Lessee whatsoever, including a tenant improvement allowance.  The original space (Suites 18537-18539) and the additional space (Suite 18541) were delivered to Lessee and Lessee accepted its current condition.  All demolition and improvements for Lessee’s use shall be at Lessee’s sole cost and expense.  (UMF Nos. 7-8.)

 

·       Defendant executed a Guaranty of Lease on February 8, 2021, which provides:

 

o   Guarantor unconditionally and irrevocably guaranties the prompt payment by Tenant of all rentals and all other sums payable by Tenant under the Lease.

 

o   Guarantor’s liability under the Guaranty shall continue until all rents due under the lease have been paid in full.

 

o   Guarantor agreed that if a default shall at any time occur in the payment of any sums due under the Lease by Tenant…Guarantor shall and will forthwith upon demand pay such sums.

 

o   In the event of any dispute or litigation regarding the enforcement or validity of this Guaranty, Guarantor shall be obligated to pay all charges, costs and expenses (including, without limitation, reasonable attorneys’ fees) incurred by Landlord

 

o   The Guaranty shall constitute the entire agreement between each Guarantor and the Landlord with respect to the subject matter thereof.  (UMF Nos. 9-10.)

 

·       Plaintiff performed the covenants required on its part under the terms of the Lease, except those that were excused by Karma’s breaches.  (UMF No. 11.)

 

·       Karma defaulted under the Lease by failing to pay the rent and common area/NNN charges when due under the lease from May 2022 through July 2023.  (UMF No. 12.)

 

·       On October 4, 2023, Plaintiff filed an unlawful detainer action and ultimately obtained a judgment of possession for the premises against Karma, which was enforced by the Los Angeles County Sheriff’s Department on February 13, 2024.  (UMF Nos. 13-14.)

 

·       Plaintiff’s Aged Delinquency Report dated March 25, 2024 demonstrates a total amount owed of $344,711.25, representing (1) unpaid rent in the amount of $198,547.80 and (2) common area/NNN charges of $146,163.45.  (UMF No. 15.)

 

·       To date, neither Karma nor Defendant have paid the outstanding amounts owed.  (UMF No. 16.)

 

Thus, Plaintiff has met its initial prima facie burdens of production and persuasion to demonstrate (1) the Guaranty/agreement; (2) Defendant’s breach; (3) Plaintiff’s performance under the agreement, except to the extent excused by Defendant’s breach; and (4) resulting damages.

 

b.     Defendant’s Evidence

 

Defendant does not dispute the existence or terms of the Lease Agreement, Amendments, or Guaranty, the fact that Karma was evicted through unlawful detainer proceedings, or the outstanding balance remains unpaid.  Defendant contends that Plaintiff made material misrepresentations and omissions during the course of negotiations and/or breached the Lease Agreement by failing to provide premises that conform to the parties’ agreement with respect to the parking; water, sewer and gas lines; and zoning. 

 

Defendant also contests the outstanding amount owed, arguing that, to the extent any rent payments are still outstanding, they should be offset by Defendant’s own damages incurred and that Plaintiff failed to adequately mitigate its damages.

 

In support, Defendant advances his own declaration, which provides as follows:

 

5. Karma intended to operate the Initial Space as an adult day health care facility. From the outset, adequate parking was critical to the viability of that use. The facility needed sufficient parking for licensing purposes and to support staff, clients, transportation activity, and occupancy levels necessary to operate profitably. Without sufficient parking, the facility could not lawfully or economically function as intended.

 

6. Prior to execution of the Lease, Plaintiff’s broker and/or agent provided me with a parking analysis for the shopping center. That document represented, among other things, that thirty-two (32) parking spaces were allocated to Karma’s leased premises and that the shopping center had approximately 600 total parking spaces, with approximately 590 spaces required. Exhibit C to Defendant’s Appendix of Exhibits is a true and correct copy of that parking analysis.

 

7. I relied on the parking analysis and the representation that thirty-two parking spaces were allocated to the Premises in deciding to proceed with the transaction, execute the Lease, and personally execute the Guaranty. At the time, Karma needed at least that level of parking to obtain licensing and justify opening the facility and to seek the occupancy level needed to make the location economically workable.

 

8. Before Karma could begin operating, the City of Santa Clarita required a Minor Use Permit (“MUP”) as well as built out engineered plans. After months of work and delay, on or about September 2, 2021, the City approved MUP 21-006 for the Initial Space. Exhibit B to Defendant’s Appendix of Exhibits is a true and correct copy of the MUP approval and conditions of approval.

 

9. The City’s approval materially differed from what had been represented to me before lease execution. Although I had been shown a parking analysis allocating 32 spaces to Karma, the City’s approved MUP reflected only 18 available spaces could be allocated for the Initial Space and limited the operation accordingly and to a maximum occupancy of 66. It was calculated one dedicated space to each of our initial 6 employees and one space dedicated to every 5 visitors equaling a maximum of 60 daily participants, bringing the total maximum occupancy to 66. This was highly significant because it materially affected the permitted occupancy and the economic viability of the business.

 

10. Based on the City’s parking restrictions, the Initial Space could not support the level of occupancy Karma needed. Karma required a substantially higher occupancy level to break even, and its goal was ultimately to secure occupancy for up to 200 clients. The parking limitation was therefore not minor or academic. It directly affected whether the business could operate as planned.

 

11. After reporting the issue to the brokers and building management, they attempted to add a few spaces to the existing limited parking. Added spaces were rejected by the MUP inspectors as not legal and contradicting with fire department regulation thus not fruitful. In order to obtain building permit and begin the construction, we were moved to agree with the issued permit to build with the low approved occupancy by the MUP and seek ways to increase the occupancy while constructing.

 

12. After further efforts with the City, and after discovering a City provision relating to a designated pick-up and drop-off space which improved the rating from 5 to 10 people per each parking space. Newly discovered city rules increased the occupancy to 126, dedicating 6 spaces to 6 employees and 12x10=120 people totaling 126, revised plans were submitted in an effort to improve the permitted occupancy. Eventually, following additional delay and permitting work, an adjacent approximately 1,200 square foot space became available showing 5 assigned parking spaces, and Karma agreed to expand into that space (the “Expansion Space”) even though it had no need for the space. It agreed to secure the space in order to obtain enough additional 50 occupancy by 5-10=50 parking for licensing purposes and operation of a viable business.

 

13. The Expansion Space had most recently been occupied by an escrow company. The Expansion Space was originally part of the space we rented and both spaces were occupied by a church entirely. Karma agreed to take that additional space solely because the expansion was expected to help secure more parking allocation and a larger occupancy load.

 

14. A second MUP process followed for the expansion. On or about July 12, 2023, the City approved MUP 23-006 relating to the expanded operation. Exhibit D and Exhibit E to Defendant’s Appendix of Exhibits are true and correct copies of the MUP documents and related conditions of approval. Exhibit D is the original MUP approval approving the space addition to the original space and keep approving the original 66 maximum occupancy. The MUP calculation method became clear for me by the city inspector. Each existing business on their records was given an allocation of parking based on the type of reported business, then the addition of total assigned parking to all existing business was taken out of the total available parking spaces in the shopping center arriving at 18 spaces left over. Thus, the added space would be assigned with 0 spaces and not five as the rental agreement. I obtained the list of all recorded existing business from MUP.

 

15. I found multiple business in the shopping center that were not matching the MUP list, I presented pictures of the replacing business and presented to MUP in order to recalculate the total parking allocation based on actual current actual existing business. They removed old businesses and added replaced ones and allocated parking to new business types the appropriate number of parking spaces and ended up with 27 unassigned spaces. They then updated the original July 12,2023 approval with the higher and matching our business objectives. Exhibit E. They were surprised that so many new tenants were allowed to rent and do business without MUP. They also added that the Escrow company was never permitted to do business as well as there was no record of any building permit to remodel. I did not need to add the additional space nor I would have all the delays if the shopping center had followed the city regulation and had required every new tenant to obtain MUP approval. MUP would have approved 27 spaces with the original approval.

 

16. The expanded approval ultimately reflected a total of 27 parking spaces for the expanded facility, not the 32 spaces that had originally been represented to me for the Initial Space alone before execution of the Lease and Guaranty. This discrepancy was significant and confirmed that the pre-lease parking representations were inaccurate and materially misleading.

 

17. In addition to the parking problem, Karma encountered serious utility and infrastructure defects that were not properly disclosed to me before I executed the Lease and Guaranty.

 

18. As part of the approved operational plans, Karma required a dedicated two-inch water line. No such dedicated line existed. Instead, a smaller line ran through the Initial Space and also supplied an adjacent restaurant. This meant the space did not have the dedicated water infrastructure required for the intended use.

 

19. The water-line situation created a practical impossibility. If Karma were to use that line as needed for the adult day care operation, it would affect water service to neighboring space or spaces. In other words, Karma could either lack adequate water service for its own operation or use the existing line at the expense of another tenant. That was not a workable condition for the intended use.

 

20. Karma also encountered serious sewer-line problems. When work was undertaken relating to the sewer connection, it was discovered that the sewer line serving the Initial Space was shared with an adjacent clinic and that the existing cast iron line was in poor condition and, in relevant part, unusable. This was another material condition affecting the lawful and practical use of the premises.

 

21. Karma also learned that neither the Initial Space nor the Expansion Space had a dedicated gas meter for Karma’s use. A gas line existed, there was a gas meter directly outside behind our space with gas line from it traveling up and entering our space, but it also served an adjacent restaurant. This created the same fundamental problem as the water line: the infrastructure necessary for Karma’s intended use was not independently available to Karma as represented or expected. Even if adequate for both businesses, it made it impossible to measure each user volume.

 

22. These utility conditions were not trivial construction inconveniences. They materially affected whether the premises could function as an adult day health care facility at all, and whether the space could lawfully and safely operate as approved.

 

23. I also later learned of serious zoning and permitting issues relating to the Expansion Space. As noted above, the space had previously been used as part of a church. I learned that Plaintiff had not properly obtained the necessary zoning variance, entitlement, or other approval to convert that space into the use that had followed, and then to lease it for Karma’s expanded operation. Per department of building and safety plan checker, after careful review of architectural and various engineering plans, he had approved and issued permit to build the original space. But the addition of Expansion Space could not be approved. Due to the separating wall, being designed as a shear wall and is rated for two hours of fire protection. He added, the existing opening by a six foot by eight-foot double door that is currently connecting the two sides has compromised the safety codes and immediately ordered to stop all construction. Until the separating wall has been restored to the original condition without any connecting passage.

 

24. I further discovered that, in connection with the prior conversion of the Expansion Space, the Expansion Space was originally part of the adjacent restaurant. The restaurant including Expansion Space were considered as one building and our rented space next to it was considered a separate building and identified as such by the fire department. The wall separating the entire original restaurant and our rented original space had to have two hours rating to satisfy fire safety separating two buildings and required to be a shear wall for the stability of the building to satisfy the building and safety department. The wall between the Expansion Space and our original rented space had been compromised or removed without proper permitting. This created an illegal and unsafe condition. City of Santa Clarita department of building and safety had stopped the build out and needed a resolution of the compromised wall. Not only did they want a cure, but they also wanted to rebuild the separating wall entirely and follow the current city codes. Rebuilding and closing the entire wall would make the Expansion Space unreachable. After multiple meetings with the city plan checker, architect and engineers we resolved it by engineering the new wall with large footings, rebuilding the wall and adding shear wall and only leaving a connecting door measuring three feet by 6 feet two-hour fire rated door. This was time consuming, costly and tiring. Finally, after many months we obtained building permit to begin completing the build out.

 

25. That firewall and shear wall issue had substantial consequences. Karma could not simply occupy and use the Expansion Space while that condition remained unresolved. It had to wait for further direction and approval, and the condition materially interfered with the use of the premises.

 

26. Had I known, before signing the Lease and Guaranty, that (a) the represented parking allocation was inaccurate, (b) the center in fact lacked the amount of parking shown in the parking study, (c) the premises lacked the dedicated utility infrastructure necessary for the intended use, (d) the Expansion Space had zoning and permitting defects, and (e) the fire-rated demising wall had been compromised or removed without proper permit compliance, I would not have caused Karma to enter into the transaction on the terms offered, and I would not have personally executed the Guaranty.

 

27. As part of this process, Karma retained JAC Architecture and Property Management to conduct an independent review regarding parking. JAC confirmed that the shopping center’s actual parking count was far lower than what had been represented in the pre-lease parking analysis.

 

28. Specifically, JAC confirmed that the center had approximately 368 spaces, not the approximately 600 spaces provided and 590 required reflected in the original parking analysis. The shortfall was so significant, nearly forty percent of the parking spaces represented in the prelease parking analysis, that it materially affected whether the shopping center had sufficient parking to support the uses Plaintiff authorized in the Lease. Exhibit F to Defendant’s Appendix of Exhibits is a true and correct copy of the email reflecting that confirmation.

 

29. The JAC confirmation was highly significant because it showed that the center-wide parking numbers reflected in the pre-lease parking study were materially inaccurate. It also confirmed that the allocation of parking to Karma that had been shown to me before execution was not grounded in the actual parking conditions at the center.

 

30. The parking study was not something I regarded as incidental or non-binding sales talk. It was provided during the transaction and directly concerned the core operational requirement for the type of business Karma intended to operate. I relied on it as part of the basis for moving forward with the Lease and Guaranty.

 

31. Likewise, the utility, zoning, and firewall and shear wall issues were not matters I understood or agreed to assume as unknown conditions existing at the outset. These were material conditions that affected whether the premises were suitable, lawful, and practically capable of supporting the intended use.

 

32. Throughout the permitting and build-out process, Karma spent substantial time, money, and effort attempting to make the premises usable for the adult day health care operation. The obstacles described above caused significant delay, disruption, additional expense, and loss of anticipated business opportunity.

 

33. The conditions described above also prevented the premises from being delivered and made available in the condition bargained for and required for lawful and workable occupancy at the commencement of the tenancy.

 

34. Plaintiff nevertheless never assisted or participated in any way to find a cure for his misrepresentation but continued to demand full performance and payment under the Lease and later sought to enforce the Guaranty, despite the existence of the above-described conditions and despite the fact that those conditions materially impaired the intended use of the premises. The business could not operate without these issues being addressed by the landlord.

 

35. The statements in this declaration are based on my personal knowledge, my direct participation in the transaction and subsequent events, and my review of the documents attached to Defendant’s Appendix of Exhibits, each of which I received, created, paid, relied upon, or otherwise became personally familiar with in connection with the Lease, the Guaranty, the permitting process, or the operation of Karma’s proposed facility.

 

36. On or about December 10, 2024 I filed a cross-complaint in this action setting for my claim against the Plaintiff including their misrepresentations that has caused hundreds of thousands of Dollars in damages to me and my business.

 

[…]

 

43. Adult day health care facility is one of the most highly regulated businesses in California. It requires strict adherence to the various codes including but not limited to zoning, structure, parking and every other aspect of legal requirements for commercial occupancy. Karma as tenant was not able to timely secure permit for the space because it was prevented from doing so by the Plaintiff Landlord that misrepresented the parking allocation in the center, allowing zoning and permitting defects in the Expansion Space, compromising the fire-rated demising wall without proper permit compliance as well as lacking dedicated utility infrastructure necessary for the intended use. Due the myriad violations of the Plaintiff over the years and complete disregard of proper permitting process, the Defendant was prevented by the City to move forward with the project until such time the Landlord addressed its previous violations. Plaintiff however persisted in its refusal to take any action to rectify its previous wrongdoing.

 

(Abrishamchian Decl. ¶¶ 5-36, 43.)

 

            Defendant has met his burden of production to create triable issues of material fact as to whether Plaintiff misrepresented aspects of the premises to induce Defendant to guaranty the lease and/or provided nonconforming premises in violation of the parties’ agreement. 

 

In reply, Plaintiff argues that because the Lease Agreement disclaims any representation or warranty concerning the suitability of the leased premises, that the Lease supersedes and cancels any previous representations, and contains an integration clause, it exculpates Plaintiff from Defendant’s accusations, pursuant to the Parol Evidence Rule.

 

“Under the parol evidence rule, extrinsic evidence is not admissible to contradict express terms in a written contract or to explain what the agreement was.”  (Cerritos Valley Bank v. Stirling (2000) 81 Cal.App.4th 1108, 1115–1116.)  Thus, Plaintiff argues, Defendant cannot present any evidence contradicting these plain terms of the Agreement, pursuant to the rule.

 

But the Parol Evidence Rule does not bar the introduction of evidence regarding fraud.  (Riverisland Cold Storage, Inc. v. Fresno-Madera Production Credit Assn. (2013) 55 Cal.4th 1169, 1182.)  Thus, a Plaintiff may not generally use the parol evidence rule as a shield to insulate itself from liability for making material misrepresentations about the premises by merely including generic exculpatory clauses within the lease.

 

Here, the Parol Evidence Rule does not bar evidence that Plaintiff materially misrepresented the number of parking spaces available to Karma within the common areas to the premises, and/or concealed the zoning, firewall, and/or plumbing issues in order to induce Plaintiff to sign the guaranty.  Further, the alleged fraud may entitle Defendant to an offset, reducing or eliminating any outstanding rent owed.  These issues are for a fact finder to assess. 

 

As such, the Court denies summary adjudication of Issue 1.

 

2.     ISSUES 2-3: Plaintiff’s Second Cause of Action for Open Book Account and Third Cause of Action for Account Stated

 

In order to prevail on a cause of action for an open book account, Plaintiff must establish (1) Plaintiff and Defendants had financial transactions with each other; (2) Plaintiff, in the regular course of business, kept a written or electronic account of the debits and credits involved in the transactions; (3) Defendants owe Plaintiff money on the account; and (4) the amount of money owed.  (CACI No. 372.)

 

The elements of an “Account Stated” cause of action are (1) at the time of the statement, an indebtedness from one party to the other existed (2) a balance was then struck and agreed to be the correct sum owing from the debtor to the creditor; and (3) the debtor expressly or impliedly promised to pay to the creditor the amount thus determined to be owing.  (Truestone, Inc. v. Simi West Industrial Park II (1984) 163 Cal.App.3d 715, 725.)  The second and third elements can be established where an account is sent to the debtor, and the debtor does not object within a reasonable time.  Trafton v. Youngblood (1968) 69 Cal.2d 17, 25; Atkinson v. Golden Gate Tile Co. (1913) 21 Cal.App. 168, 171.) 

 

Here, as discussed above, because there are triable issues of material fact as to whether the amount Defendant owes Plaintiff should be reduced, in whole or in part, the Court similarly denies summary adjudication of Issues 2-3.

 

3.     ISSUES 4-27: Defendant’s Affirmative Defenses

 

Plaintiff moves for summary judgment as to each of Defendant’s twenty-four affirmative defenses on the ground that “Defendant has failed to meet the burden of proof which has shifted to him.”

 

a.     ISSUE 4—Affirmative Defense No. 1: Failure to State a Cause of Action

 

As to the first cause of action for breach of written guaranty, the FAC alleges as follows:

 

13. Defendant executed and delivered to Plaintiff a Guaranty of Lease agreement dated as of February 8, 2021 (“Guaranty”), a true and correct copy of which is attached as Exhibit 4 and incorporated herein.

 

14. Pursuant to the terms of the Guaranty, Defendant guaranteed the prompt payment by Lessee of all rentals and other sums payable under the Lease and performance of the terms and conditions of the Lease.

 

15. Despite demand, Defendant failed to pay the sums due and owing to Plaintiff under the Lease.

 

16. There is now due and owing to Plaintiff from Defendant, as of February 6, 2024, the sum of not less than $344,711.25, together interest at the contract rate and late fees plus other fees/charges in accordance with the Lease, all according to proof at time of trial or entry of judgment.

 

17. The Guaranty provides that Defendant agreed to pay reasonable attorneys' fees incurred in the enforcement thereof. Plaintiff has employed the law firm of Buchalter, A Professional Corporation, for the purpose of instituting and prosecuting this action.

 

18. Plaintiff has performed all the promises, covenants and conditions it agreed to perform in accordance with the terms of the Lease and Guaranty, except for those promises, covenants and conditions excused by the acts or omissions of Lessee or Defendant.

 

(FAC ¶¶ 13-18.)

 

            As to the second cause of action, the FAC alleges:

 

20. Within four years last past, Defendant became indebted to Plaintiff in the sum of $344,711.25 for a balance due on a book account for money due.

 

21. No part of said sum has been paid, although demand for payment has been made, and there is now due, owing and unpaid from said defendant to Plaintiff the sum of $344,711.25, plus interest at the legal rate according to proof at time of trial or entry of judgment.

 

(FAC ¶¶ 20-21.)  Thus, Plaintiff has adequately stated a cause of action for open book account.

 

            As to the third cause of action, the FAC alleges:

 

23. Within the last four years, an account was stated in writing by and between Plaintiff and Defendant wherein it was agreed that said defendant was indebted to Plaintiff in the sum of $344,711.25.

 

24. Although payment of the balance has been demanded, there is now due, owing and unpaid from said defendant to Plaintiff the sum of $344,711.25, plus interest at the legal rate according to proof at time of trial or entry of judgment.

 

(FAC ¶¶ 23-24.)  Therefore, Plaintiff has adequately stated a cause of action for account stated.

 

Although Defendant has met his burden of production to create triable issues of material fact as to whether Plaintiff will ultimately prevail on merits of its causes of action, Defendant has not demonstrated that Plaintiff failed to state a cause of action.

 

Therefore, the Court grants summary adjudication as to Issue 4, Defendant’s first affirmative defense for failure to state a cause of action.

 

b.     ISSUE 5—Affirmative Defense No. 2: Statute of Frauds

 

The statute of frauds requires that certain types of contracts be in writing in order to be valid.  (See Civ. Code, § 1624.) 

 

Here, Defendant has not created any triable issues of material fact as to the existence of the written lease agreements, amendments, or guaranty that control the instant dispute.  Because all purported contracts at issue are in writing, they necessarily comply with the requirement of statute of frauds that certain agreements be in writing. 

 

Therefore, the Court grants summary adjudication of Issue 5.

 

c.      ISSUES 6 and 17—Affirmative Defense No. 3 and 14: Good Faith

 

An affirmative defense for Good Faith requires (1) that defendant took property from plaintiff in good faith and (2) that defendant took the property for a reasonably equivalent value.

 

Here, as discussed above, because Defendant has created triable issues of material fact as to the value of the premises as delivered and/or whether any offset should be applied to any outstanding amounts owed, the Court denies summary adjudication of Issues 6 and 17.

 

d.     ISSUES 7 & 23—Affirmative Defenses Nos. 4 & 20: Failure to Mitigate & Avoidable Consequences

 

            Defendant has advanced evidence that Plaintiff failed to mitigate its damages by refusing to work with the City to cure its own prior zoning and safety issues     that delayed and prevented Plaintiff from operating its business out of the leased space.  Therefore, the Court denies summary adjudication of Issues 7 and 23.

 

e.      ISSUE 8—Affirmative Defense No. 5: No Damages

 

As discussed above, there are triable issues of material fact as to whether Plaintiff has incurred any recoverable damages or whether the contract is unenforceable as having been fraudulently induced, or whether an appropriate offset by Defendant’s own damages eliminate Plaintiff’s recoupable damages.  As such, the Court denies summary adjudication of Issue 8.

 

f.      ISSUE 9—Affirmative Defense No. 6: Consent

 

Plaintiff has advanced evidence that it did not consent to Defendant’s failure to pay the outstanding rent owed.  Specifically, Plaintiff brought a successful unlawful detainer action to evict Karma for nonpayment of rent and has now brought the instant action to recoup the outstanding rent amounts owed. 

 

Defendant has not presented any evidence that Plaintiff consented to Defendant’s nonpayment of rent at any point in time.

 

Therefore, the Court grants summary adjudication of Issue 9.

 

g.     ISSUES 10-11 & 20—Affirmative Defenses No. 7-8 & 17: Estoppel, Unclean Hands & Fraud

 

As discussed above, there are triable issues of material fact as to whether Plaintiff has unclean hands and/or should be estopped from recouping any more rent payments on the grounds that Plaintiff (1) fraudulently made material misrepresentations about the premises; (2) concealed and/or omitted material defects regarding the firewall, plumbing, and parking; and/or (3) delivered materially nonconforming premises to Defendant.

 

Therefore, the Court denies summary adjudication of Issues 10, 11, and 20.

 

h.     ISSUE 12—Affirmative Defense No. 9: Waiver

 

There is no evidence in the record that Plaintiff waived its claims.  Therefore, the Court grants summary adjudication of Issue 12.

 

i.       ISSUE 13—Affirmative Defense Nos. 10: No Causation

 

As discussed above, there are triable issues of material fact as to whether Plaintiff caused its own damages, in whole or in part, by making material misrepresentations/omissions about the premises, delivering nonconforming premises, and/or failing to mitigate zoning and safety issues with the City that predated Defendant’s lease.

 

Therefore, the Court denies summary adjudication of Issue 13.

 

j.      ISSUE 14—Affirmative Defense No. 11: Offset

 

As discussed above, there are triable issues of material fact as to whether Plaintiff’s damages, if any, should be offset by the damages Defendant incurred.  Therefore, the Court denies summary adjudication of Issue 14.

 

k.     ISSUE 15—Affirmative Defense No. 12: Excusal

 

As discussed above, there are triable issues of material fact as to whether Defendant is excused from having to pay the rent amounts owed, due to Plaintiff’s delivery of nonconforming premises.  Therefore, the Court denies summary adjudication of Issue 15.

 

l.       ISSUE 16—Affirmative Defense No. 13: Plaintiff’s Bad Faith

 

As discussed above, there are triable issues of material fact as to whether Plaintiff made fraudulent misrepresentations, concealments, and/or omissions, which would demonstrate Plaintiff’s bad faith.  Therefore, the Court denies summary adjudication of Issues 16 and 18.

 

m.   ISSUE 18—Affirmative Defense No. 15: Malicious Prosecution

 

As discussed above, Plaintiff has met its initial prima facie burden of production and persuasion to demonstrate (1) the existence of the parties’ contractual agreements; (2) Plaintiff’s performance or excuse for performance; (3) Defendant’s breach; and (4) Plaintiff’s damages. 

 

Although Defendant has created triable issues of material fact, Defendant has not advanced any evidence demonstrating that Plaintiff filed the instant case for the purposes of harassment or malicious prosecution.

 

Therefore, the Court grants summary adjudication of Issue 18.

 

n.     ISSUES 19 & 21—Affirmative Defenses Nos. 16 & 18: Superseding/Intervening Cause and Indemnity/Comparative Fault

 

Defendant has not advanced any evidence that the actions of any third parties caused or contributed to Plaintiff’s damages.  Therefore, the Court grants summary adjudication of Issues 19 and 21.

 

o.     ISSUE 22—Affirmative Defense No. 19: No Jurisdiction

 

Although the evidence demonstrates the premises in question are located in the City of Santa Clarita, and therefore could theoretically be brought in a different venue, Defendant has not advanced any evidence that this Court lacks jurisdiction over the parties or subject matter. 

 

Therefore, the Court grants summary adjudication of Issue 22.

 

p.     ISSUE 24—Affirmative Defense No. 21: Failure to Exhaust Administrative Remedies

 

Defendant has not advanced any evidence demonstrating that Plaintiff failed to exhaust administrative remedies.

 

Therefore, the Court grants summary adjudication of Issue 24.

 

q.     ISSUE 25—Affirmative Defense No. 22: Arbitration

 

There is no evidence in the record that any of the agreements at issue contain a mandatory arbitration clause. 

 

Therefore, the Court grants summary adjudication of Issue 25.

 

r.      ISSUE 26—Affirmative Defense No. 23: After-Acquired Evidence

 

The doctrine of after-acquired evidence provides for an equitable defense related to the concept of “unclean hands” in employment cases where an employer being sued for wrongful termination learns after the fact that the employee committed misconduct that would have led to their termination in any event.  (See Finegan v. County of Los Angeles (2001) 91 Cal.App.4th 1, 7.)

 

Defendant has not provided any legal authority that the after-acquired evidence doctrine applies to contexts outside of employment disputes, nor has Defendant advanced any evidence demonstrating that this is an employment dispute, or that Plaintiff acquired any such evidence after the fact.

 

Therefore, the Court grants summary adjudication of Issue 26.

 

s.      ISSUE 27—Affirmative Defense No. 24: Promissory Estoppel

 

As discussed above, Defendant has advanced sufficient evidence that Plaintiff made promises about the parking availability upon which Defendant relied to his detriment, such that there are triable issues of material fact as to whether promissory estoppel should bar Plaintiff from denying such an agreement/promise was made.

 

Therefore, the Court denies summary adjudication of Issue 27.

 

4.     ISSUE 28: Defendant’s First Cause of Action for Breach of Contract

 

As discussed above, there are triable issues of material fact as to whether Plaintiff breached the Lease Agreement by delivering nonconforming premises with inadequate parking, water, sewer, and gas lines, that was properly permitted and zoned as a commercial space, with a functional and code-compliant firewall. 

 

Therefore, the Court denies summary adjudication of Issue 28.

 

5.     ISSUES 29-30: Defendant’s Second Cause of Action for Breach of the Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing and Third Cause of Action for Fraudulent Concealment

 

“Implied in every contract is a covenant of good faith and fair dealing. The implied covenant prevents one side from unfairly frustrating the other's right to receive the benefits of the agreement actually made. The covenant does not impose substantive terms beyond those of the contract. A plaintiff claiming breach must allege the defendant's wrongful conduct was contrary to the contract's purpose and the parties’ legitimate expectations.”  (Cordoba Corp. v. City of Industry (2023) 87 Cal.App.5th 145, 156.)

 

Here, as discussed above, there are triable issues of material fact as to whether Plaintiff acted in bad faith by materially misrepresenting, omitting, and/or fraudulently concealing facts about the deficiencies in the available parking, water, sewer, and gas lines, zoning, and fire wall.

 

Therefore, the Court denies summary adjudication of Issues 29 and 30.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

 

For the reasons stated, the Court grants in part and denies in part Plaintiff’s Motion for Summary Adjudication. 

 

Finding no triable issues of material fact, the Court grants Plaintiff’s motion for summary adjudication of Issues 4, 5, 9, 12, 18, 19, 21, 22, 24, 25, and 26, pertaining to Defendant’s affirmative defenses no. 1, 2, 6, 9, 15, 16, 18, 19, 21, 22, and 24.

 

Finding triable issues of material fact, the Court denies summary adjudication of the remaining Issues 1-3, 6-8, 10-11, 13-17, 20, 23, and 27-30.

 

Having denied summary adjudication of the issues identified above, the Court denies Plaintiff’s Motion for Summary Judgment.

 

Further, the Court will enter the proposed Order lodged on January 12, 2026 in conformity with the ruling. 

 

Plaintiff shall provide notice of the Court’s ruling and file the notice with a proof of service forthwith.

MOTION

 

            Thomas H. Scully of Law Offices of Thomas Scully (“Counsel”), counsel for Defendant and Cross-Complainant Luubao N Nguyen and Defendant REML, LLC, moves to be relieved as counsel, citing a breakdown of the attorney-client relationship.  The motion is unopposed.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 284 provides “[t]he attorney in an action or special proceeding may be changed at any time before or after judgment or final determination as follows: 1. Upon the consent of both client and attorney, filed with the clerk, or entered in the minutes; 2. Upon the order of the court, upon the application of either client or attorney, after notice from one to the other.”

 

Procedural Requirements

 

California Rules of Court, rule 3.1362, requires:

 

(1) the motion must be made on form MC-051; (subd. (a));

 

(2) it must be accompanied by a declaration on form MC-052 stating why the motion is brought under Code of Civil Procedure section 284(2) instead of a consent brought under section 284(1); (subd. (c));

 

(3) a proposed order on form MC-053 must be lodged with the court, specifying all hearing dates scheduled in the action or proceeding, including the date of trial, if known; (subd. (e)); and

 

(4) The documents must be served on the client and on all parties that have appeared in the case. (subd. (d).)

 

If the notice is served by mail or electronic service, it must be accompanied by a declaration indicating that the address served is the current address, or in the case of service by mail, that it was served on the last known address and a more current address could not be located after reasonable efforts within 30 days before filing the motion.  (Ibid.)  The court may delay the effective date of the order relieving counsel until proof of service of a copy of the signed order on the client has been filed with the court.”  (Ibid.) 

 

Substantive Requirements

 

Rules of Professional Conduct, rule 1.16(a) outlines the reasons a lawyer must withdraw from representation of a client:

 

(1)   the client is bringing an action, conducting a defense, asserting a position in litigation, or taking an appeal, without probable cause and for the purpose of harassing or maliciously injuring any person;

 

(2)   the representation will result in violation of the Rules of Professional Conduct or the State Bar Act;

 

(3)   the lawyer’s mental or physical condition renders it unreasonably difficult to carry out the representation effectively; or

 

(4)   the client discharges the lawyer.

 

Rules of Professional Conduct, rule 1.16(b) outlines the reasons a lawyer may withdraw from representation of a client:

 

(1)   the client insists upon presenting a claim or defense in litigation, or asserting a position or making a demand in a non-litigation matter, that is not warranted under existing law and cannot be supported by good faith argument for an extension, modification, or reversal of existing law;

 

(2)   the client either seeks to pursue a criminal or fraudulent course of conduct or has used the lawyer’s services to advance a course of conduct that the lawyer reasonably believes was a crime or fraud;

 

(3)   the client insists that the lawyer pursue a course of conduct that is criminal or fraudulent;

 

(4)   the client by other conduct renders it unreasonably difficult for the lawyer to carry out the representation effectively;

 

(5)   the client breaches a material term of an agreement with, or obligation, to the lawyer relating to the representation, and the lawyer has given the client a reasonable warning after the breach that the lawyer will withdraw unless the client fulfills the agreement or performs the obligation;

 

(6)   the client knowingly and freely assents to termination of the representation;

 

(7)   the inability to work with co-counsel indicates that the best interests of the client likely will be served by withdrawal;

 

(8)   the lawyer’s mental or physical condition renders it difficult for the lawyer to carry out the representation effectively;

 

(9)   a continuation of the representation is likely to result in a violation of these rules or the State Bar Act; or

 

(10)         the lawyer believes in good faith in a proceeding pending before a tribunal that the tribunal will find the existence of other good cause for withdrawal.

 

DISCUSSION

 

            Counsel has filed forms MC-051, MC-052, and MC-053.  The attorney declaration (MC-052) indicates that the motion was filed instead of filing a consent because:

 

Irreconcilable differences have arisen between Defendants and Cross-Complainants Luubao Nguyen and his single-member entity, REML, LLC., after the mediation effort ended without a resolution. Communications have broken down between counsel, Mr. Scully, and the client, Luubao Nguyen, who represents both himself, as a natural person, and of his entity, REML, LLC. regarding trial preparations, performance under the written fee agreement, and other matters, necessitating the substitution of trial counsel. However, Luubao Nguyen has not yet arranged for new counsel or substituted Mr. Scully out, and in the event that cannot be accomplished in a timely manner, Mr. Scully requests that he be simply relieved as counsel of record prior to trial.

 

(MC-052 at ¶ 2.)  As such, the Court finds that the motion complies with the Rules of Professional Conduct, rule 1.16(b)(4).) 

 

            However, Counsel has not filed any proof of service demonstrating proper service of the forms on the client and on the parties who have appeared in the matter.  Therefore, the Court finds the motion to be procedurally improper.

ANALYSIS

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 187 provides:   “When jurisdiction is, by the Constitution or this Code, or by any other statute, conferred on a Court or judicial officer, all the means necessary to carry it into effect are also given; and in the exercise of this jurisdiction, if the course of proceeding be not specifically pointed out by this Code or the statute, any suitable process or mode of proceeding may be adopted which may appear most conformable to the spirit of this Code.” 

 

“Code of Civil Procedure section 187 authorizes a trial court to amend a judgment to add a judgment debtor who is found to be an alter ego of a corporate defendant.”  (Misik v. D'Arco (2011) 197 Cal.App.4th 1065, 1069.)  “[A] plaintiff's failure to allege the alter ego doctrine in the underlying lawsuit does not preclude a motion to amend the judgment.”  (Ibid.)

 

To establish alter ego liability, Plaintiff must show (1) such a unity of interest and ownership between the corporations that no separation actually exists, and (2) an inequitable result if the acts in question are treated as those of the corporation alone.  (Leek v. Cooper (2011) 194 Cal.App.4th 399, 417.)  Several factors are considered in determining whether the alter ego doctrine applies, including:

 

(1)   commingling of funds and other assets, failure to segregate funds of the separate entities, and the unauthorized diversion of corporate funds or assets to other than corporate uses;

 

(2)   the treatment by an individual of the assets of the corporation as his own;

 

(3)   the failure to obtain authority to issue stock or to subscribe to or issue the same;

 

(4)   the holding out by an individual that he is personally liable for the debts of the corporation;

 

(5)   the failure to maintain minutes or adequate corporate records;

 

(6)   sole ownership of all of the stock in a corporation by one individual or the members of a family;

 

(7)   the failure to adequately capitalize a corporation;

 

(8)   the total absence of corporate assets, and undercapitalization;

 

(9)   the use of a corporation as a mere shell, instrumentality or conduit for a single venture or the business of an individual or another corporation;

 

(10)         the concealment and misrepresentation of the identity of the responsible ownership, management and financial interest, or concealment of personal business activities;

 

(11)         the disregard of legal formalities and the failure to maintain arm's length relationships among related entities;

 

(12)         the use of the corporate entity to procure labor, services or merchandise for another person or entity;

 

(13)         the diversion of assets from a corporation by or to a stockholder or other person or entity, to the detriment of creditors, or the manipulation of assets and liabilities between entities so as to concentrate the assets in one and the liabilities in another;

 

(14)         the contracting with another with intent to avoid performance by use of a corporate entity as a shield against personal liability, or the use of a corporation as a subterfuge of illegal transactions; and

 

(15)         the formation and use of a corporation to transfer to it the existing liability of another person or entity.

 

(Id. at pp. 417–418.)


            In support of the motion, Petitioner advances the Declaration of Ami Shah, which as demonstrated by the Court’s evidentiary rulings above, primarily contains inadmissible legal arguments and statements which lack foundation.

 

The gist of Petitioner’s argument for which Petitioner has laid an adequate foundation is that Gregory is the sole shareholder, officer, and agent for service of process for Caring, which Gregory runs out of her personal residence, and Caring was suspended by the Franchise Tax Board as of April 2, 2024.  Thus, on August 19, 2024 when Petitioner and Caring entered into the underlying agreement at the heart of their arbitration dispute, and Caring became indebted to Petitioner for approximately $50,000, Caring was already suspended and disqualified from operating as a legal entity.  As such, Petitioner argues, Caring was effectively a sole proprietorship at the time the agreement was made.   

 

But, at best, Petitioner has demonstrated (1) sole ownership and (2) failure to adequately capitalize the corporation. 

 

The Court does not find the fact that Gregory ran the business out of her personal residence alone sufficient to demonstrate improper comingling; the treatment by Gregory of Caring as though its assets were her own personal assets; lack of corporate formalities; or that Gregory used Caring to procure goods or services for another person or entity. 

 

To wit, in opposition, Gregory provides a declaration clarifying that she maintains her own separate bank account, segregated from the bank account for Caring; she has not used Caring’s funds for her own purposes; and the parties’ agreement was for Petitioner to provide physical and occupational therapy services to Caring’s patients.  (Gregory Decl. ¶¶ 4, 11.)  There is no evidence that Petitioner performed services for Gregory personally.

 

Further, the fact that Petitioner personally viewed the Caring’s records in Gregory’s home demonstrates that corporate records were being maintained.  (See also Gregory Decl. ¶ 9 [Caring “has followed corporate formalities”].)  And the fact that Gregory’s name is all over the officially filed corporate records demonstrates Gregory did not conceal or misrepresent her identity as the company’s owner. 

 

Petitioner’s attempt to use the alter ego doctrine to bring Gregory into the litigation in her personal capacity further demonstrates that Gregory did not personally guaranty the debts of the corporation.  (See also Gregory Decl. ¶ 4 [“I did not personally guarantee the obligations of CLFI under the Agreement”].)

 

Although Petitioner characterizes Caring as a mere shell or instrumentality, that Caring was created as a shield to avoid performance and liability, or that Caring was created to transfer Gregory’s liability, Petitioner has not presented sufficient, competent evidence supporting those arguments.

 

The mere fact that Caring was solely owned by Gregory and ultimately became unprofitable to the point where it could not satisfy its debts does not, without more, demonstrate that Caring and Gregory are alter egos of each other.

CONCLUSION

 

            The Court denies Petitioner’s motion to amend the judgment to add Gregory in her personal capacity as an alter ego for Caring.

Motion to Strike Portions of Complaint

BACKGROUND

 

            This case arises from a dispute between adjacent neighbors concerning an easement. 

 

            On April 7, 2026, Plaintiffs Pamela Hieronymi and Mark Johnson (“Plaintiffs”) brought suit against Defendants Erik Chin (“Chin”) and Eighteen Cypress, LLC (“Eighteen Cypress”) (together, “Defendants”) alleging seven causes of action for (1) breach of contract; (2) breach of contract – specific performance; (3) interference with easement; (4) nuisance; (5) trespass; (6) breach of implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing; and (7) declaratory relief.

 

            On June 1, 2026, Eighteen Cypress cross-complained against Plaintiffs for (1) interference with easement; (2) declaratory relief; (3) trespass; and (4) nuisance.

 

            Defendants now move to strike the first, second, and sixth causes of action, allegations about the easement, and the request for attorneys’ fees.  Plaintiffs oppose the motion and Defendants reply.

 

MOTION TO STRIKE

 

Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading, may serve and file a motion to strike the whole pleading or any part thereof.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 435, subd. (b)(1); Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1322, subd. (b).)  On a motion to strike, the court may: (1) strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or (2) strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of California, a court rule, or an order of the court.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a)-(b); Stafford v. Shultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782.) 

 

Defendants move to strike Plaintiff’s first, second, and sixth causes of action for breach of contract, breach of implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, and declaratory relief; the request for attorneys’ fees; as well as the following allegations pertaining to the Easement in question, on the grounds that those causes of action, allegations, and damages are not drawn in conformity with the laws of California because they are premised upon the Easement contract, to which Defendants were not a party, and therefore the contract cannot be enforced as to them:

 

·       “Via paragraph 7 of the Easement, the Parties also agreed to ‘execute such other documents and to take such other actions as may reasonably be necessary to further the purposes of this [Easement] Agreement.’”  (Complaint ¶ 16.)

 

·       “despite the Easement’s requirement (expressed in paragraph 7 thereto) that the Parties ‘execute such other documents and to take such other actions as may reasonably be necessary to further the purposes of this [Easement] Agreement.’”  (Complaint ¶ 31.)

 

·       “Defendants have also failed to comply with the Easement’s terms by refusing to meet, confer and/or discuss other documents or actions as may reasonably be necessary to further the purposes of the Easement as expressly agreed by the Parties.”  (Complaint ¶ 39.)

 

·       “Defendants have also failed to comply with the Easement’s terms by refusing to meet, confer and/or discuss other documents or actions as may reasonably be necessary to further the purposes of the Easement as expressly agreed by the Parties.”  (Complaint ¶ 45.)

 

The crux of Defendants’ argument is that Plaintiffs and the former owners of Defendants’ property entered into an Amended Easement Agreement.  Although that Easement Agreement provides that the easements shall run with the land and benefit/encumber successor owners of the properties (Ex. A to Complaint at p. 5, ¶ 4), Defendants argue the Easement Agreement does not contain express language binding successors to the other terms of the Agreement, notably the attorney fee provision.

 

But Defendants’ arguments go to the merits of the underlying claims and dispute about the easement, not whether the Complaint was drawn in conformity with the laws of California. For pleading purposes, the Court finds that Plaintiffs’ complaint is adequately pled.     

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

 

For the reasons stated, the Court denies Defendants’ motion to strike in its entirety.
