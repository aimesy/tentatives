DEPARTMENT 309

 

TENTATIVE RULING

 

ACKEE BAMBOO JAMAICAN CUISINE LLC

 

         vs.

 

COMMUNITY BUILD, INC.     

 

 Case No.: 25STCV33207 

 

 

 

 Hearing Date: September 24, 2026

 

Defendant’s demurrer is SUSTAINED, WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND, as to the sixth and seventh causes of action, but OVERRULED as to the remaining claims. Defendant’s motion to strike is denied.

 

On 11/13/2025, Plaintiff Ackee Bamboo Jamaican Cuisine (Plaintiff) initiated this action against Defendant Community Build, Inc. (Defendant). On 4/17/2026, Plaintiff filed a first amended complaint (FAC), alleging: (1) breach of contract; (2) breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing; (3) breach of covenant of quiet enjoyment; (4) constructive eviction; (5) negligence; (6) private nuisance; (7) common count; (8) unfair competition. 

 

            On 5/19/2026, Defendant demurred to the FAC’s second, third, fourth, sixth, seventh, and eighth causes of action. Defendant also moves to strike portions of the FAC.

 

Discussion

 

            Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s second, third, sixth, seventh, and eighth causes of action are insufficiently pled and run afoul of the sham pleadings doctrine.

 

            In support, Defendant contends that the original Complaint alleged that Ackee Bamboo LLC leased the Premises and entered into the Lease with Community Build. Now, the FAC alleges that Delroy Beckford and Marlene Sinclair Beckford—not Ackee Bamboo Jamaican Cuisine LLC—entered into the Lease and were the "lessees and tenants of record.”

 

            Indeed, the parties do not dispute that the Lease expressly identifies only Delroy Beckford and Marlene Sinclair Beckford as "Lessee” and states that no other person or entity shall be deemed Lessee under the Lease. However, Plaintiff alleges that Plaintiff Ackee Bamboo Jamaican Cuisine LLC (Ackee Bamboo LLC) was the entity through which the Individual Plaintiffs operated the restaurant; that Community Build knew Ackee Bamboo LLC was operating the restaurant at the Premises; that Community Build accepted rent and performance from Ackee Bamboo LLC; and that Community Build never objected to its occupancy or operation of the restaurant. (FAC ¶¶ 2–3.)

 

            As such, the Court agrees that Plaintiff’s new allegations do not run afoul of the sham pleadings doctrine, but rather were made to correct an erroneous or imprecise characterization of the parties’ respective roles, rather than invent a new contractual relationship or omit a fact that makes the action incapable of being stated truthful. (JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A. v. Ward (2019) 33 Cal.App.5th 678, 690.)

 

            Moreover, the Court finds that, in light of the allegations, whether or not Ackee Bamboo LLC has standing to pursue lease-based claims is a factual determination not appropriately made at the pleadings stage. (Spinks v. Equity Residential Briarwood Apartments (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1004, 1032.)

 

            The Court finds the remaining claims sufficiently pled at the pleadings stage, with the exception of the nuisance and common count claims. Plaintiff’s nuisance claim is redundant of the contract claims, and the common count claim/money had and received is not applicable here as Plaintiff does not allege facts which could show a total failure of consideration. (Rutherford Holdings, LLC v. Plaza Del Rey (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 221, 230.)

 

            Based on the foregoing, Defendant’s demurrer is sustained, without leave to amend, as to the sixth and seventh causes of action, but overruled as to the remaining claims. Defendant’s motion to strike is denied.

 

It is so ordered.

Case Number: 26STCV08102    Hearing Date: September 24, 2026    Dept: 309

Superior Court of California

County of Los Angeles

 

DEPARTMENT 309

 

TENTATIVE RULING

 

FRANK DIAZ

 

         vs.

 

AEROTEK, INC., et al.

 

 Case No.: 26STCV08102

 

 

 

 Hearing Date: September 24, 2026

 

 

Defendant’s motion to compel arbitration is DENIED.

 

            On 3/13/2026, Plaintiff Frank Diaz (Plaintiff) filed suit against Aerotek, Inc., Duluth Services Inc. fka Duluth Environmental Services, Inc. and dba Vest Tube LLC and Vest, and Stacie Miles, alleging: (1) disability discrimination; (2) failure to reasonably accommodate; (3) failure to engage; (4) failure to provide; (5) retaliation; (6) failure to prevent; (7) wrongful termination; (8) retaliation; and (9) defamation.

 

            On 7/2/2026, Defendants Aerotek, Inc. and Duluth Services, Inc. (collectively, Defendants) moved to compel arbitration and stay this action.

 

Discussion

 

I.                   Defendants’ Burden

 

The party moving to compel arbitration “bears the burden of proving [the] existence [of an arbitration agreement] by a preponderance of the evidence.” (Rosenthal v. Great Western Fin. Securities Corp. (1996) 14 Cal.4th 394, 413.) The moving party also bears the burden of demonstrating that the claims fall within the scope of the arbitration agreement. (Omar v. Ralphs Grocery Co. (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 955, 961.)

 

            Defendants submitted evidence that Plaintiff was assigned to work at Vest Tube, LLC (Vest) as a General Production Worker from approximately 8/20/2024 to 3/5/2025, and entered into 13 arbitration agreements throughout that time. (Collum Decl. Exhs. 1-13)

 

            Defendants also submitted evidence that Plaintiff’s claims fall within the scope of the agreements. (Id.)

 

            However, in opposition, Plaintiff argues that the Federal Arbitration Act (FAA) expressly exempts from its coverage all contracts of employment involving “any other class of workers engaged in” foreign or interstate commerce. (9 U.S.C. § 1.) Plaintiff contends that throughout his employment, he “performed work directly and necessarily connected to the shipping, handling, movement, and delivery of goods in interstate and international commerce. His assignments included working as a lead shipping and receiving clerk for Bird Rides, Inc., a material handler for BDP International, Inc., an international logistics company, and a general production worker for Vest, where he manufactured, bundled, transported, staged, and prepared steel products for shipment.” (Opp., 1: 7-8.)

 

The FAA expressly does not apply “to contracts of employment of seamen, railroad employees, or any other class of workers engaged in foreign or interstate commerce.” (9 USC § 1; see Rittmann v. Amazon.com, Inc. (9th Cir. 2020) 971 F3d 904, 914-915 (exclusion covers package delivery drivers who operate wholly within the state when packages originate out of state). The U.S. Supreme Court has interpreted the exclusion to apply only to “transportation workers.” All other employment contracts affecting commerce are subject to the FAA. The section one exception specifically names two classes of exempt workers, seamen and railroad employees, and the final exempt group, “workers engaged in commerce,” must be defined in light of the narrowness of the other two exemptions. (Circuit City Stores, Inc. v. Adams (2001) 532 US 105, 118, 121 S.Ct. 1302, 1311; see Muller v. Roy Miller Freight Lines, LLC (2019) 34 CA5th 1056, 1062.)

 

The term “transportation workers” has been defined as those workers who at least play a “necessary role in the free flow of goods” across borders. (Circuit City Stores, Inc. v. Adams (2001) 532 US 105, 121; see also Southwest Airlines Co. v. Saxon (2022) 596 US 450, 457 (“any class of workers directly involved in transporting goods across state or international borders falls within § 1's exemption” (finding airplane cargo loaders are such a class); Harden v. Roadway Package Systems, Inc. (9th Cir. 2001) 249 F3d 1137, 1140 (driver delivering packages throughout U.S. was “worker engaged in interstate commerce” exempt from FAA); Hill v. Rent-A-Center, Inc. (11th Cir. 2005) 398 F3d 1286, 1288-1290 (emphasis of FAA was on class of workers in transportation industry rather than workers who incidentally transported goods interstate as part of their job in otherwise unregulated industry).

 

            Plaintiff contends that because he is a transportation worker exempt from the FAA, the FAA does not preempt California Labor Code section 432.6, which prohibits employers from requiring employees to waive rights, forums, or procedures concerning violations of the FEHA and Labor Code as a condition of employment.

 

A transportation worker need not work in the transportation industry to fall within the exemption from the FAA.” Bissonnette v. LePage Bakeries Park St., LLC (2024) 601 US 246, 256, 144 S.Ct. 905, 913—“any exempt worker must at least play a direct and necessary role in the free flow of goods across borders” (internal quotes omitted)].

 

In Ortiz v. Randstad Inhouse Services, LLC (9th Cir. 2024) 95 F.4th 1152., the Ninth Circuit analyzed whether the plaintiff played a “direct and necessary role” in the transportation of goods. In Ortiz, the plaintiff was a warehouse worker whose job duties were (1) “picking up the packages and transporting them to the warehouse racks to organize them,” (2) “transport[ing] the packages to the picking section of the warehouse,” (3) “assisting Pickers in obtaining packages so they could be shipped out,” and (4) “assist[ing] the Outflow Department to prepare packages to leave the warehouse for their final destination.” Id. at 1158. The Court held that these job duties were sufficient to qualify the warehouse worker under the FAA’s transportation worker exemption. Id. at 1162; see also, e.g. Lopez v. Aircraft Service Int'l, Inc. (9th Cir. 2024) 107 F4th 1096, 1103 (holding that a fuel technician placing fuel in plane used for foreign and interstate commerce fell within exemption even though the technician had no hands-on contact with goods or cargo).

 

Again, those who do not cross state lines nevertheless qualify as “transportation workers” provided the goods they transport originate out of state such that they participate “in the continuation of the movement of interstate goods to their destinations.” Nieto v. Fresno Beverage Co., Inc. (2019) 33 Cal.App.5th 274, 281-284; see also Betancourt v. Transportation Brokerage Specialists, Inc. (2021) 62 Cal.App.5th 552, 559-561 (“last-mile delivery drivers” from Amazon warehouse qualify as “transportation workers”); Rittmann v. Amazon.com, Inc. (9th Cir. 2020) 971 F3d 904, 907, 919 (same); Muller v. Roy Miller Freight Lines, LLC (2019) 34 Cal.App.5th 1056, 1062.

 

After review, the Court agrees that the FAA’s Section 1 exemption applies here because Plaintiff was a transportation worker who performed duties that were directly and necessarily connected to the movement of goods in interstate and international commerce. Plaintiff’s employment assignments demonstrate that his work was not merely incidental to the movement of goods. Rather, throughout each of the relevant assignments, Plaintiff performed duties that placed him directly within the stream of shipping, handling, and delivery of merchandise. (See Opp., 5:13-6:21.)

 

Given that Plaintiff falls within Section 1’s transportation-worker exemption, the FAA does not apply to his employment agreement. As a result, the FAA does not preempt California’s public policy against the enforcement of arbitration agreements under the California Labor Code section 432.6.

 

California Labor Code § 432.6 prohibits employers from requiring current and prospective employees to "waive any right, forum, or procedure" for a violation of the Fair Employment and Housing Act (FEHA) and the California Labor Code. Lab. C. § 432.6(a); see Chamber of Commerce of United States v. Bonta, 13 F.4th 766,771 (9th Cir. 2021) (finding that section 432.6's enforcement mechanisms, including civil and criminal penalties, were preempted by the FAA, but regulation of employers' pre-agreement conduct was not).

 

Given Plaintiff’s claims, which arise out of FEHA, the Court finds that the arbitration is unenforceable here in light of Labor Code section 432.6.

 

Based on the foregoing, Defendant’s motion to compel arbitration is denied.

 

It is so ordered.
