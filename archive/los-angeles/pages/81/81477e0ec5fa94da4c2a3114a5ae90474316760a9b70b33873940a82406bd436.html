LEGAL STANDARD:

 

C.C.P. § 187 states: “When jurisdiction is, by the Constitution or this Code, or by any other statute, conferred on a Court or judicial officer, all the means necessary to carry it into effect are also given; and in the exercise of this jurisdiction, if the course of proceeding be not specifically pointed out by this Code or the statute, any suitable process or mode of proceeding may be adopted which may appear most conformable to the spirit of this Code.”

 

Section 187 “grants to every court power to use all means to carry its jurisdiction into effect, even if those means are not specifically pointed out in the code. [Citation.] It is now settled that ‘... the authority of the court will be exercised to impose liability under a judgment upon the alter ego who has had control of the litigation.’ [Citation.]”  (Jack Farenbaugh & Son v. Belmont Construction, Inc. (1987) 194 Cal.App.3d 1023, 1028–1029 [citation and footnote omitted]; see Greenspan v. LADT, LLC (2010) 191 Cal.App.4th 486, 508 [“Judgments may be amended to add additional judgment debtors on the ground that a person or entity is the alter ego of the original judgment debtor….”].)  This is an equitable procedure based on the theory that the Court is not amending the judgment to add a new defendant but is merely inserting the correct name of the real defendant.  (McClellan v. Northridge Park Townhome Owners Association (2001) 89 Cal. App. 4th 746, 752 [permitting a plaintiff to add the individual who was the alter ego of a judgment debtor corporation].)  This procedure permits the Court to make the judgment “speak the truth” as to the true names of the defendant.  (Jack Farenbaugh, supra, 194 Cal.App.3d at 1029.)

 

“The ability under section 187 to amend a judgment to add a defendant, thereby imposing liability on the new defendant without trial, requires both (1) that the new party be the alter ego of the old party and (2) that the new party had controlled the litigation, thereby having had the opportunity to litigate, in order to satisfy due process concerns.” (Toho-Towa Co., Ltd. v. Morgan Creek Productions, Inc. (2013) 217 Cal.App.4th 1096, 1106.) 

 

“In California, two conditions must be met before the alter ego doctrine will be invoked. First, there must be such a unity of interest and ownership between the corporation and its equitable owner that the separate personalities of the corporation and the shareholder do not in reality exist. Second, there must be an inequitable result if the acts in question are treated as those of the corporation alone. [Citation.]” (Cam-Carson, LLC v. Carson Reclamation Authority (2022) 82 Cal.App.5th 535, 549 [internal quotation marks and citations omitted].)  “Alter ego liability is not limited to the parent-subsidiary corporate relationship; rather, under the single-enterprise rule, liability can [also] be found between sister [or affiliated] companies.”  (Id. at 550.) 

 

“[A] successor company has liability for a predecessor's actions if: (1) the successor expressly or impliedly agrees to assume the subject liabilities […], (2) the transaction amounts to a consolidation or merger of the successor and the predecessor, (3) the successor is a mere continuation of the predecessor, or (4) the transfer of assets to the successor is for the fraudulent purpose of escaping liability for the predecessor's debts.”  (CenterPoint Energy, Inc. v. Superior Court (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 1101, 1120.)

 

ANALYSIS:

 

Plaintiff seeks to amend the Judgment—originally entered against Elit Auto Group, Inc. (“EAG”) and Karlen Malkhasyan—to include non-party Elit Los Angeles (“ELA”) as the alter ego or successor in interest of EAG. 

 

Plaintiff’s counsel, Daren M. Schlecter, states that the California Secretary of State records show that EAG was registered on January 25, 2016, Karlen Malkhasyan is the owner and manager of EAG, EAG is designated as a “Retail Automotive Dealership,” and EAG is operated at 19420 Business Center Dr., Suite 100 in Northridge.  (Schlecter Decl., ¶¶6-7, Exs. A-B [EAG’s March 10, 2021 and May 18, 2022 Secretary of State filings].)  EAG is currently inactive and was suspended by the Franchise Tax Board in 2024.  (Id., ¶8, Ex. C.) 

 

Mr. Schlecter states that on April 20, 2020, ELA was created by Vahag Malkhasyan, Edgar Antonyan was identified as the CEO, its address was 19420 Business Center Dr., Suite 100 (the address later switched to 14310 Victory Blvd. in Van Nuys in 2026), and ELA was designated as a “Retail Automotive Dealership.”  (Id., ¶¶10-11, Exs. D-E [ELA’s April 2, 2020 and February 16, 2026 Secretary of State filings].)  Mr. Schlecter states that according to LinkedIn, Karlen Malkhasyan identifies himself as ELA’s CEO.  (Id., ¶¶12-13, Ex. F.) 

 

On February 26, 2026, Plaintiff’s counsel served on Zelle (a mobile application to transfer funds between bank accounts) a Subpoena for Judgment Debtor Karlen Malkhasyan’s transactional history and received responsive documents on March 16, 2026, showing transactions between Karlen Malkhasyan and ELA from 2022 to the present.  (Id., ¶¶14-17, Ex. G.)  Mr. Schlecter states that the logos for EAG and ELA are identical in color, shape, and usage to promote both auto businesses.  (Id., ¶¶18-19, Ex. H.)  (The Court notes that the EAG logo appeared to be comprised solely of a red circle with a tilted “E,” whereas the ELA logo includes the same image but with “ELIT LOS ANGELES” written to the right of the circle image.) 

 

Plaintiff argues that based on when the loan agreement was entered (March 4, 2020), ELA was formed (April 2, 2020), Defendants defaulted (June 2020), and Judgment was entered (August 25, 2021), it believes that Karlen Malkhasyan created ELA to evade payment to Plaintiff as he continues to operate a new entity.  (Schlecter Decl., ¶¶2-4, 10, 20-22.) 

 

Plaintiff argues that the Court should apply the alter ego doctrine between EAG and ELA because: (1) Karlen Malkhasyan controls both EAG and ELA; (2) the interest between EAG and ELA are identical as they have the same/similar names, are in the same industry/line of business, have the same logo, and are/were located at the same address; (3) an inequitable result would occur if ELA was not added as an alter ego because Defendants could avoid payment to Plaintiff and continue to operate their business under a new name (even if the Secretary of State documents indicate a different CEO, which Plaintiff asserts was Karlen Malkhasyan’s attempt to evade service and enforcement of the Judgment); and (4) Plaintiff did not discover the extent of the relationship between EAG and ELA until after ELA was created in April 2020 and Plaintiff began further investigation.  (Mot. at pp.6-7.)  It also argues that successor liability applies for the same reasons.  (See id. at pp.7-8.)

 

The Court continued the hearing on the motion (initially scheduled for June 26, 2026), requesting further briefing.  The Court previously found that Plaintiff had partially upheld its burden in showing alter ego liability and/or successor company liability by establishing that an inequitable result would occur if ELA were treated as a separate corporation from Defendants, but Plaintiff had not fully established that there is a unity of interest between the EAG, ELA, and Karlen Malkhasyan.  Specifically, the Court noted that: “There may be some similarities between the entities and the timing of ELA’s formation may be suspect, but without more, the Court cannot ascertain whether there is such a unity of interest where there is no separation between Defendants and ELA.  Similarly, with respect to ELA being the successor in interest to EAG, Plaintiff has not shown that ELA expressly or impliedly agreed to assume EAG’s liabilities, their transactions that amounted to a consolidation or merger of the entities, or that there were indeed any transfer of assets to avoid judgment.”  (June 26, 2026 Minute Order at pp.4-5.) 

 

In its supplemental brief, Plaintiff provides the California Secretary of State filings showing that Malkhasyan still controls EAG; EAG was suspended by the FTB in 2024; Malkhasyan was CEO/CFO, a director, and agent for service of process for ELA by the December 17, 2024 filing; and ELA omitted Malkhasyan by the February 20, 2025 filing.  (Pl.’s Further Brief at Exs. 1, 4-6.)  (Some of these documents were already submitted with the motion papers.) 

 

Plaintiff also provides EAG and Malkhasyan’s bank statements showing that ELA made multiple transfers (deposits and withdrawals) from $5.00 to $20.00 from February 2023 to August 2023 (Pl.’s Further Brief at Exs. 2-3.)  It provides numerous Zelle transactions showing transfers between Malkhasyan and ELA from August 23, 2022 to March 16, 2026.  (Id., Ex. 7.)  

 

Plaintiff argues that considering the evidence together, Plaintiff can show that Malkhasayan owned and controlled EAG and ELA since 2024, Malkhasyan slowly moved funds/assets from EAG to ELA from and after 2023, and Malkhasyan caused EAG’s status to be FTB suspended while controlling and operating ELA.  Plaintiff contends that Malkhasyan was “surreptitiously” removed from ELA’s corporate filings, but the records show that he has systemic transfers between Malkhasyan and ELA. 

 

Plaintiff’s evidence shows a commingling of funds and assets between Malkhasyan, EAG, and ELA and that while Malkhasyan removed himself from ELA’s corporate filings, he still regularly receives from and/or transfers funds to ELA.   The Court finds that based on Plaintiff’s showing, it has established a unity of interest between EAG, ELA, and Malkhasyan such that the Court will include ELA as the alter ego of EAG. 

 

Plaintiff’s motion and supplemental brief were served on EAG, Karlen Malkhasyan, and ELA.  Despite the continuance and two opportunities to oppose the motion, neither Defendants nor ELA opposed the motion or provided any contrary evidence that EAG and ELA are separate entities, are not alter egos of the other, or are not successor corporations. 

 

For these reasons, the motion is granted.

ALLEGATIONS: 

 

A.     Complaint

 

Yofiel Bibi, a minor, by and through his Guardian Ad Litem, Sharon Bibi (“Plaintiff”) alleges that on September 11, 2023 at approximately 8:00 p.m., he was riding a non-electric scooter at the Chandler Boulevard and Bellaire Avenue intersection in Valley Village.  As Plaintiff turned left, he was struck by a bus operated by Defendant Los Angeles County Metropolitan Transportation Authority (“LACMTA”).  Plaintiff asserts that he submitted government claims with Defendants City of Los Angeles (“City”) and County of Los Angeles (“County”), but City and County each denied his claims.

Code, § 815.2(a)); (3) Negligence – Premises Liability; (4) General Negligence; and (5) Dangerous Condition of Public Property (Gov. Code, § 835).  On April 6, 2026, Plaintiff named Woods Maintenance Services, Inc. (Doe 1, “Woods”).

LEGAL STANDARD:

 

“Before, during, or after a deposition, any party, any deponent, or any other affected natural person or organization may promptly move for a protective order. The motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.”  (C.C.P. § 2025.420(a).)  “The court, for good cause shown, may make any order that justice requires to protect any party, deponent, or other natural person or organization from unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression, or undue burden and expense,” including that the deposition not be taken at all.  (C.C.P. § 2025.420(b)(1).)

 

EVIDENTIARY OBJECTIONS:

 

With the opposition, Plaintiff submitted objections to the declarations of Alberto Vazquez, LACMTA’s Manager, General Liability Claims). 

 

·         Mr. Vazquez’s Declaration in support of the motion directed at Gary Ross: Objection Nos. 1, 2, 4, and 5 to paragraphs 7, 8, 10, and 14 (improperly cited as paragraph 12 in the evidentiary objection) are sustained.  Objection No. 3 to paragraph 9 is overruled.

 

·         Mr. Vazquez’s Declaration in support of the motion directed at Pete Mellon: Objection Nos. 1, 2, 4, and 5 to paragraphs 7, 8, 10, and 13 (improperly cited as paragraph 12 in the evidentiary objections) are sustained.  Objection No. 3 to paragraph 9 is overruled.

 

ANALYSIS:

 

LACMTA moves for protective orders regarding the depositions of Gary Ross and Pete Mellon. 

 

A.     Gary Ross

 

On May 22, 2026, the Court: (1) granted Plaintiff’s motion to compel LACMTA’s PMQ’s deposition; and (2) denied in part Plaintiff’s motion to compel LACMTA’s employees as to Jose Saucedo, and granted the motion in part as to the remaining 11 witnesses (Mr. Gomez, Lewis Simms, T. Simmon, Gary Ross, Manager Williams, Roman Alarcon, Mr. DeJoseph, Alfonso Munoz, Deckar Alexander, Sam Hooper, and Derek Brown).  On June 26, 2026, the Court denied LACMTA’s Motion for Reconsideration of the Court’s Ruling on Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel Deposition of LACMTA’s Employees. 

 

LACMTA moves for a protective order of Mr. Ross’s deposition—an accident investigator whose involvement LACMTA claims arose solely from its post-incident investigation undertaken at the direction of County Counsel, LACMTA’s legal team, and LACMTA’s Risk Management Department, and in anticipation of litigation.  It argues Mr. Ross’s deposition should not be taken at all because he is not a percipient witness, any knowledge was acquired solely through post-incident investigations upon counsel’s direction and in anticipation of litigation, the testimony is privileged, Plaintiff’s re-noticing of Mr. Ross’s deposition after prior objection and withdrawal is harassing and unduly burdensome, and Plaintiff should not be able to use Woods’ improperly produced materials to obtain further discovery.  LACMTA argues Mr. Ross’s testimony is subject to the attorney-client privilege and work product doctrine and that Plaintiff should not be permitted to use privileged material improperly produced by a third party as a roadmap to identify, target, and depose LACMTA’s privileged investigative witnesses (including Mr. Ross and Mr. Mellon). 

 

LACMTA argues that Plaintiff’s efforts to depose Mr. Ross are not isolated to ordinary discovery, but to documents improperly produced by Woods despite LACMTA’s privilege objections.  (Citron Decl., Ex. 15 [May 3, 2026 Email].)  In the May 3, 2026 email, Plaintiff’s counsel Tom Schultz stated, “Based on the limited documents produced by Woods (a handful of emails/documents, invoices, and an unsigned contract and one of the modification, number 9), I know the below documents exist and pursuant to your responses you stated you would provide them going back to 2019: … Please also provide deposition dates for the following MTA witnesses: Maral Minassian, Gary Ross, Steve Flores, Sharif Ford, Peter Mellon and provide a date when we can expect the above documents ….”  (Id.)  In response, LACMTA’s counsel Mr. Citron stated that they would not be producing the four individuals as they were part of the investigation team post-incident.  (Id., Ex. 16.)  Mr. Schultz responded that: (1) Ms. Minassian was the project administrator on contract with Woods, she was on countless invoices with Woods produced from its subpoena on Woods, and Woods produced emails showing Ms. Minassian regarding photos taken on the night of the incident, etc.; and (2) Mr. Mellon was on a spreadsheet produced by Woods, such that Plaintiff wanted to depose him on all prior incidents.  (Id., Ex. 17.)

 

LACMTA provided a privilege log to Plaintiff’s RPDs, indicating that certain documents were privileged, including some documents provided by Woods.  (Citron Decl., Ex. 1 [RPD Requests], Ex. 2 [Privilege Log].)  Thereafter, LACMTA sent an amended privilege log.  (Id., Ex. 5 [Amended Privilege Log.)  Relevant to the four motions, the amended privilege log among other documents, lists as privileged documents: (1) an email thread with Gary Ross, Steve Flores, Maral Minasian, Raymond Lopez, and Carlos Martinez from September 13 to November 6, 2023; (2) emails and photos of the hedges by Maral Minasian on April 25, 2024; (3) Incident Report dated May 9, 2024 by Maral Minasian; (4) Incident Report dated July 3, 2024 by Peter Mellon; and (5) email dated October 14, 2025 by Maral Minasian. 

 

The Court denies the motion for protective order as to Mr. Ross.

 

The Court already granted Plaintiff’s opposed motion to compel the deposition of LACMTA’s employees, of which Mr. Ross was included in the list.  As summarized above, the Court also denied LACMTA’s motion for reconsideration on that order, thereby allowing Mr. Ross’s deposition to proceed.  The Court notes that producing these employees for their depositions does not mean that LACMTA and its counsel are barred from raising objections at the deposition.  If there are legitimate objections based on the attorney-client privilege and/or attorney work product doctrine, then those objections may be raised at the time of the depositions.”  (June 26, 2026 Minute Order at p.6.)  The Court also noted that even though Mr. Ross was not listed in the Third Amended Deposition Notice (which listed only five employees), this did not amount to a waiver of the remaining six employees as ordered in the Court’s May 22, 2026 order.  The Court declines to reconsider the rulings on Plaintiff’s motion to compel the employee depositions and LACMTA’s motion for reconsideration for a third time. 

 

Next, while LACMTA argues that Plaintiff relies on privileged documents produced by Woods, Plaintiff’s request to depose Mr. Ross predated Woods’ production of documents.  Plaintiff noticed Mr. Ross’s deposition on January 30, 2026 and thereafter filed a motion to compel the depositions of LACTMA’s employees on March 2, 2026.  It was months thereafter on May 3, 2026 that Mr. Schultz emailed LACMTA that it was in receipt of documents from Woods.

 

Finally, LACMTA provided Mr. Vazquez’s declaration in support of the motion.  However, his statements and opinions regarding Mr. Ross are based on the best of his knowledge or upon information and belief.  Mr. Vazquez states that Mr. Ross’s involvement arose from his participation of the investigation and “[t]o his knowledge, Mr. Ross’s knowledge … is therefore derived from post-incident investigative work,” which based on his experience, role, and understanding, would be undertaken at the direction of counsel and in anticipation of litigation.  (Vazquez Decl., ¶¶7-10.) 

 

As discussed above, the Court has sustained a portion of Plaintiff’s evidentiary objections to Mr. Vasquez’s declaration.  Even if considered, his statements are in part legal conclusions that Mr. Ross’s investigative work was privileged.  Furthermore, he has not stated whether he is County Counsel and, as counsel, directed Mr. Ross to conduct the post-incident investigation in anticipation of litigation, such that the Court cannot ascertain whether the attorney-client privilege and attorney work product apply.  This is better explored during the deposition where Mr. Ross may provide his account on whether he was directed by counsel to undertake the post-incident investigation, whether this was done in anticipation of litigation, etc.

 

The motion for a protective order regarding Mr. Ross’s deposition is denied. 

 

B.     Peter Mellon

 

LACMTA moves for a protective order so that Mr. Mellon’s deposition not occur at all, arguing that he is not a percipient witness, his involvement arose solely from LACMTA’s post-incident investigation undertaken at counsel’s direction, and Plaintiff cannot use improperly produced documents by Woods despite LACMTA’s privilege objections to depose him.  LACMTA’s arguments rely in large part on the same arguments made in the motion concerning Mr. Ross.

 

LACMTA relies on Scripps Health v. Superior Court (2003) 109 Cal.App.4th 529, arguing that those documents were prepared in anticipation of litigation and, therefore, privileged. 

 

In Scripps Health, the hospital withheld producing five “occurrence reports” based on the attorney-client and work product privileges, stating in its privilege log that they were prepared in anticipation of litigation.  The reports stated in bold letters on the top: “CONFIDENTIAL: Not part of medical record / Do not photocopy” and sought information about the occurrence and asked for an evaluation of the significance level of the occurrence in terms of the potential for claims or litigation.  (Scripps Health, supra, 109 Cal.App.4th at 532.)  The trial court granted the plaintiff’s motion for production of the reports, finding that the reports were administrative in nature, were not necessarily forwarded to counsel or an insurer, and were not necessarily prepared for the purpose of preparing to defend the hospital in the event of a lawsuit.  (Id.)  The Court of Appeal reversed, finding the dominant purpose of the reports was for transmittal to an attorney in the course of the professional attorney-client relationship where the hospital expected confidentiality.  (Id. at 534.) 

 

“Where … a corporate employer requires that its employees make a report, the privilege of that report is determined by the employer's purpose in requiring the report. [Citation.] When the corporate employer has more than one purpose in requiring the report, the dominant purpose will control.”  (Id. at 533.)  The Court of Appeal found that even if the reports were not forwarded to counsel or an insurer, this distinction was illusory because the hospital was self-insured and maintained in-house counsel, required completion of confidential occurrence reports for the purpose of attorney review, and intended the reports to be confidential.  (Id. at 534.)  Such facts were supported by the hospital’s corporate in-house counsel’s declaration regarding the confidentiality of reports under its risk management plan pursuant to the legal department’s directive, which was derived so that it could be self-insured.  (Id. at 534-535.)  The Court of Appeal found: “Here, the reports were ‘primarily created for the purpose of attorney review whether or not litigation is actually threatened at the time a report is made.’ Based on Scripps's self-insured status, the reports were also used by the risk managers as de facto insurance claims handlers. These facts, which plaintiffs did not dispute, compel a finding that the occurrence reports are attorney-client privileged communications.”  (Id. at 535.)

 

The Court finds Scripps Health distinguishable.  Scripps Health involved the production of documents that were marked confidential and supported by in-house counsel’s declaration stating that they were prepared in anticipation of litigation and for the purpose of attorney review.  In contrast, this motion involves whether or not Mr. Mellon’s deposition should go forward.  A review of the Notice of Deposition of Defendant Los Angeles County Metropolitan Transpiration Authority’s Witnesses only seeks to depose various witnesses and is not accompanied by a request for production of documents.  (See Mot. at Ex. 11.) 

 

Furthermore, while LACTMA may have cited certain documents in its privilege log (and that Woods may have inadvertently produced to Plaintiff), the Court is unaware of whether those documents were marked confidential, whether they had language stating that the incident reports, photographs, and emails were for attorney review or done in anticipation of litigation.  While such language on the documents themselves would not be controlling, the Court notes that LACTMA also has not provided a declaration from County Counsel detailing protocols and instructions to LACMTA employees regarding post-incident investigations and reports for the purpose of establishing the attorney-client and work product privileges. 

 

At this point, the Court declines to impose a protective order preventing Mr. Mellon’s deposition.  As previously stated, if the deposition goes forward, LACMTA’s counsel is not barred from raising substantiated privilege objections to specific questions.  Furthermore, Plaintiff argues that Mr. Mellon may have unprivileged knowledge regarding vegetation conditions at the subject location, which are central to Plaintiff’s dangerous condition and negligence claims.  (Opp. at p.2.)  Thus, his knowledge is not isolated solely to post-incident investigation conduct. 

 

The motion for a protective order regarding Ms. Mellon’s deposition is denied.

ALLEGATIONS: 

 

Complete Merchant Services Inc. (“Plaintiff”) alleges that Signature Payments, LLC (“Defendant”) operates as an Independent Sales Organization (“ISO”) and performs third-party agent services on behalf of sponsoring banks.  Plaintiff operated as an independent contractor sales agent for Defendant, referring prospective merchants to Defendant for payment processing services.  On February 23, 2023, the parties entered into a three-year Agent Agreement, which included Section 7’s Compensation of Agent term obligating Defendant to pay Plaintiff for all compensation under the schedule. 

 

On February 26, 2023, Plaintiff claims it submitted Digica, Inc. as a merchant and that on July 19, 2023, Defendant advised it would migrate Digica’s account to a new backend service provider.  Plaintiff alleges that after the change, Defendant ceased paying residuals to Plaintiff on Digica’s account and refused to pay Plaintiff its portion of residual shares.  Plaintiff asserts that Defendant moves merchant processing activity to a new merchant identification number (“MID”) to circumvent bank investigation.

 

The Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”), filed September 23, 2024, alleges: (1) Breach of Contract; (2) Breach of the Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing; (3) Conversion; (4) Money Had and Received; (5) Violation of Penal Code § 496; and (6) Unfair Competition.

LEGAL STANDARD:

 

A.     Motion to Compel Further Deposition Answers

 

C.C.P. § 2025.460(e) states: “If a deponent fails to answer any question or to produce any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing under the deponent's control that is specified in the deposition notice or a deposition subpoena, the party seeking that answer or production may adjourn the deposition or complete the examination on other matters without waiving the right at a later time to move for an order compelling that answer or production under Section 2025.480.”

 

C.C.P. § 2025.480 states, “If a deponent fails to answer any question or to produce any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing under the deponent's control that is specified in the deposition notice or a deposition subpoena, the party seeking discovery may move the court for an order compelling that answer or production.”  (C.C.P. § 2025.480(a).)  The motion shall be made no later than 60 days after the completion of the deposition record and shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under section 2016.040.  (C.C.P. § 2025.480(b).)  “If the court determines that the answer or production sought is subject to discovery, it shall order that the answer be given or the production be made on the resumption of the deposition.”  (C.C.P. § 2025.480(i).) 

 

B.     Motion for Issue and Evidence Sanctions

 

Under C.C.P. § 2023.030, the Court may impose issue sanctions ordering that designated facts be taken as established in the action in accordance with the claim of the party adversely affected by the misuse of the discovery process.  (C.C.P. § 2023.030(b).)  The Court may impose an evidence sanction by an order prohibiting any party engaging in the misuse of the discovery process from introducing designated matters in evidence.  (C.C.P. § 2023.030(c).)  Any motion for issue or evidentiary sanctions must be accompanied by a separate statement.  (CRC Rule 3.1345(a)(7).) 

 

Evidence or issue sanctions may be imposed only after parties violated discovery orders compelling further responses, except in exceptional circumstances, including where there was sufficiently egregious misconduct regarding a failure to respond to discovery, or a prior discovery order would be futile. (New Albertsons, Inc. v. Sup. Ct. (2008) 168 Cal.App.4th 1403, 1426.)  Two prerequisites for the imposition of non-monetary sanctions are: (1) there must be a failure to comply; and (2) the failure must be willful.  (Vallbona v. Springer (1996) 43 Cal.App.4th 1525, 1545.)  The party requesting sanctions under C.C.P. §§ 2023.030 and 2023.010(g) must establish that the other party has failed to comply or disobeyed a court order to provide discovery.  (C.C.P. § 2023.010(g); Miranda v. 21st Century Ins. Co. (2004) 117 Cal.App.4th 913, 929 [first requirement of failure to comply].)  To avoid sanctions, the burden of proving that a discovery violation was not willful is on the party on whom the discovery was served. (Cornwall v. Santa Monica Dairy Co. (1977) 66 Cal.App.3d 250, 252- 253.)

Plaintiff argues further deposition responses are necessary because Mr. Johnson was designated as Defendant’s PMK but came to the deposition unprepared to answer discovery verification questions, when the parties discussed and stipulated that this would fall within the scope of deposition topics. 

 

In opposition, Defendant argues that the motion should be denied as to these questions because Mr. Johnson was identified as a fact witness and not as a PMQ, such that there was no requirement that a fact witness have knowledge about certain issues in preparation for his deposition (in contrast to C.C.P. § 2025.230 requiring a PMQ to familiarize themselves with noticed topics).  Defendant claims that while Mr. Johnson’s statutory and legal obligations are clear, it is also aware that the obligations are counterbalanced by Mr. Johnson being identified as someone who could verify Defendant’s RPD responses and that Plaintiff is entitled to ask questions regarding information and sources of information that Mr. Johnson consulted to reach said conclusion.  (Opp. at p.7.)  Defendant requests that given the limited scope of the specific issue, Mr. Johnson should not be compelled to sit for a second deposition, but the Court should instead order Plaintiff to propound written discovery as a more cost-effective and efficient method so that Mr. Johnson can retrace his steps to verify responses. 

 

While the Court understands Defendant’s arguments regarding fact witnesses versus PMQs, prior to Mr. Johnson’s June 11, 2026 deposition, the parties were aware of the scope of Mr. Johnson’s deposition as ordered by the Court.  As discussed in the Court’s May 14, 2026 IDC Minute Order, the subject matters for Mr. Johnson’s deposition would be limited to those matters for which he was identified as a person with knowledge in Defendant’s discovery responses or verified responses to RFAs.  At the July 1, 2026 IDC, the Court noted that Mr. Johnson’s inability to answer questions and instructions not to answer questions, appeared to be inconsistent with him being identified as a person with knowledge concerning facts as set forth in the discovery responses.  Thus, Defendant was aware of its obligations to produce Mr. Johnson for deposition for the specific purpose of answering questions regarding discovery verification. 

 

The Court declines Defendant’s alternate proposal that Plaintiff should propound written discovery on this topic.  As such, the motion to compel Mr. Johnson’s further deposition answers is granted as to Question Nos. 1-4.

N [Def.’s Third Suppl. Responses to FROG, Set One], Ex. O [Def.’s Suppl. Objections and Responses to SROG, Set Two].)  

 

Plaintiff argues that the supplemental FROG responses do not remedy the discovery abuse at issue because: (1) Defendant’s in-house counsel Brian Turley previously described Sofia Munoz as a lower level sales associate who would not be privy to the events that occurred (Dahlberg Reply Decl., Ex. 14 [1/12/24 Letter]); (2) Meredith Baraja and Joshua Cobian were already deposed but each testified under oath that they lacked personal knowledge of the relevant facts (Dahlberg Decl., Ex. 6 [Barajas Depo. at pp.131-132]; Dahlberg Reply Decl., Ex. 15 [Cobian Depo.]); and (3) Defendant did not provide any contact information for the remaining former employees or attorneys, including Mr. Turley (id.).  Thus, Plaintiff argues that a further deposition be allowed so that Plaintiff may seek further deposition answers and ask follow-up questions.  The Court will allow the further deposition of Mr. Johnson regarding Question No. 9.  The basic facts of who was interviewed are not privileged, nor are questions regarding witness or contact information of those who have knowledge of the case.

MOTION FOR ISSUE AND EVIDENCE SANCTIONS ANALYSIS:

 

Plaintiff moves for issue and evidence sanctions against Defendant for its disobedience of the Court’s January 24, 2025 order with respect to RPD Nos. 1-5 and 16.

 

The RPDs at issue seek:

 

·         RPD No. 1: “All DOCUMENTS that REFER, or RELATE to DIGICA, including, but not limited to, all applications, change forms, SIGNATURE’S internal notes, SIGNATURE’s internal COMMUNICATIONS, from the time DIGICA was initially boarded through the day of YOUR response to this Request.”

 

·         RPD No. 2: “All COMMUNICATIONS with DIGICA.”

 

·         RPD No. 2(2): “All COMMUNICATIONS that REFER or RELATE to DIGICA, which shall include but not be limited to communications with CMS and communications among SIGNATURE’S internal personnel regarding DIGICA.”

 

·         RPD No. 3: “All DOCUMENTS that REFER or RELATE to CMS.”

 

·         RPD No. 4: “All COMMUNICATIONS that REFER or RELATE to CMS, including internal COMMUNICATIONS.”

 

·         RPD No. 5: “All DOCUMENTS that EVIDENCE, REFER, or RELATE to DIGICA’S processing activity, including monthly gross numbers, processing fees, and referral/residual fees paid out to referring agents/ISO/third parties.”

 

·         RPD No. 16: “All DOCUMENTS that EVIDENCE, REFER, or RELATE to YOUR defenses in this Action.”

 

(See Pl.’s Separate Statement.) 

 

Plaintiff seeks an order to: (1) deem established the facts that the withheld discovery would have otherwise shown and (2) preclude Defendant from offering documents or defenses beyond those it already disclosed in response to RPD No. 16.  Plaintiff argues that:   

 

As summarized above, the Court granted Plaintiff’s motion to compel further responses to the RPD on January 24, 2025, ordering responses by February 24, 2025.  In its order, the Court stated that Defendant argued the motion was moot as it will cure all deficiencies by supplementing its responses, but the Court found that Defendant had not demonstrated that it rendered any supplemental responses nor that the responses cured the deficiencies which form the basis of Plaintiff’s motions.  (Jan. 24, 2025 Minute Order at p.4.)  The Court found that Defendant also provided no further argument in support of its objections to the requests and thus necessarily failed to uphold its burden in justifying the objections, thereby ordering further responses.  (Id. at pp.4-5.) 

 

In its August 11, 2025 Third Supplemental Responses to RPD Nos. 1-5, 8-9, and 16, Defendant objected to the RPDs at issue and responded that subject to and notwithstanding the foregoing and previously asserted objections, it would produce all non-privileged documents within its possession, custody, or control and identified the documents produced.  (Dahlberg Decl., Ex. 7.)  In its August 20, 2025 Fourth Supplemental Responses to RPD No. 5, Defendant against stated that subject to and notwithstanding the previously asserted objections, it would produce all non-privileged documents and identified the documents produced.  (Id., Ex. 8.) 

 

On June 16, 2026, defense counsel Michael Kushner emailed Ms. Dahlberg, stating that Plaintiff was misinterpreting the Court’s order because the Court ordered further responses by February 24, 2025, which Defendant complied with as Plaintiff did not move nor did the Court explicitly issue a waiver of Defendant’s initial objections for any supplemental response.  (Id., Ex. 9.)  At the July 1, 2026 IDC, the Court advised that Defendant’s interpretation and subsequent noncompliance were inconsistent with the Court’s ruling and advised Plaintiff’s counsel the matter cannot be resolved at an IDC and must proceed via motion requesting evidentiary and/or issue sanctions. 

 

In opposition, Defendant states: “While Signature’s initial interpretation of the Order was misguided, the reading was reasonable under the circumstances and not willful.”  (Opp. at p.1.)  It argues that because the Court’s order did not expressly waive Defendant’s objections for supplemental responses, it understood the order to mean its initial objections were insufficient to justify Defendant’s prior discovery conduct but was unclear as to whether Defendant’s privilege objections were waived.  (Id.)  It argues that once it was apprised of its misinterpretation at the July 1, 2026 IDC, the parties met and conferred on July 2, 2026 where Defendant stated its intention to produce responsive documents, but Plaintiff filed this motion.  On July 27, 2026, Defendant served its Fifth Supplemental Responses, producing 3,757 pages of responsive, non-privilege documents and a privilege log.  (Kushner Decl., Ex. H.)  A review of the supplemental responses shows that Defendant again asserted objections, asserted new objections based on the attorney-client and attorney work product privileges (stating it would file an ex parte motion to request to be relieved from potential waivers of those objections), and stated that subject to and without waiving its privilege objections, it refers and cites to previously produced documents, as well as additional non-privilege documents. 

 

In reply, Plaintiff argues that Defendant waited until the opposition due date to serve responses—documents Plaintiff contends Defendant had within its possession but withheld over the past two years of litigation—and Defendant has not produced documents included on its privilege log.  Further, Plaintiff argues that Defendant’s internal email retention policy retain emails for 90 day unless archived (Opp. at p.9) amounts to spoliation of evidence.  Plaintiff argues it is not asserting that Defendant waived its privilege objections but that it is improperly withholding and redacting documents that were never privileged to begin with.  (Reply at p.10.) 

 

Despite the Court’s January 24, 2025 order for further responses, the parties’ many informal attempts to resolve this issue, and the IDCs held by this Court, Defendant continues to provide responses with objections.  As discussed in its January 24, 2025 order, Defendant had failed to substantiate any of its objections.  Though not explicitly stated, such a finding leads to the logical conclusion that the objections were overruled or waived, such that further responses without objections should have been provided.  The Court reiterated its position at the July 1, 2026 IDC, wherein it stated that Defendant’s position in its further responses was inconsistent with the Court’s ruling. 

 

Defendant contends it has now understood the Court’s order and provided further responses, but Defendant continues to assert objections and provide responses subject to those objections and previously asserted objections.  The time to rely on those previously asserted objections has long since passed.  Furthermore, Defendant’s newly asserted objections based on the attorney-client and work product privileges are long past due.  Plaintiff served the RPDs on June 3, 2024 and now, nearly two years later, Defendant seeks to assert new objections when the Court already ordered further responses by February 24, 2025. 

 

There has been a pattern of discovery delays by Defendant in its failure to provide non-evasive, objection-free further responses, its withholding of documents, and its failure to timely and fully comply with the Court’s order.  Despite the Court’s order and IDCs, Defendant continued to withhold documents and now seeks to assert new objections.  However, the Court also recognizes that Defendant has attempted to comply with its discovery obligations by producing additional documents.  If Plaintiff finds that the supplemental documents are still lacking (either by failing to produce certain emails over 90-days old or non-privileged documents), then Plaintiff may file a motion to compel further responses to the specific RPDs and/or the disputed documents identified in the privilege log at issue. Plaintiff may contact the Courtroom to have any such hearing specially set without an ex parte and then can give notice as to the date for hearing.  In the alternative, Plaintiff may seek to continue this motion, include supplemental briefing concerning documents and other responses recently received to expedite compliance.

 

The motion for issue and evidence sanctions is denied without prejudice.

 

A.    Sanctions

 

“The court may impose a monetary sanction ordering that one engaging in the misuse of the discovery process, or any attorney advising that conduct, or both pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney's fees, incurred by anyone as a result of that conduct. The court may also impose this sanction on one unsuccessfully asserting that another has engaged in the misuse of the discovery process, or on any attorney who advised that assertion, or on both. If a monetary sanction is authorized by any provision of this title, the court shall impose that sanction unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.”  (C.C.P. § 2023.030(a).)

 

Under California law, a discovery order cannot go further than is necessary to accomplish the purpose of discovery.  (Newland v. Superior Court (1995) 40 Cal. App. 4th 608, 613.)  The purpose of discovery sanctions is to prevent abuse of the discovery process and correct the problem presented.  (McGinty v. Superior Court (1994) 26 Cal. App. 4th 204, 210.) 

 

Plaintiff seeks $14,111.25 in sanctions against Defendant to reimburse Plaintiff for attorney’s fees and costs incurred in connection with this motion (= [15.75 hours on this motion] + [2 anticipated hours for the reply and to attend the hearing] at $795/hour).  (Dahlberg Decl., ¶¶24-27.)  Sanctions were not sought against defense counsel. 

 

As a lesser sanction to issue or evidentiary sanctions, the Court will award sanctions of 17 hours at $500 an hour for a total of $8,500; however, this shall be stayed provided compliance occurs, no further discovery violations take place during the pendency of the case and all other monetary sanctions imposed are timely paid.

LEGAL STANDARD:

 

If a party to whom interrogatories are directed fails to serve a timely response, the propounding party may move for an order compelling response and for a monetary sanction.  (C.C.P. § 2030.290(b).)  The statute contains no time limit for a motion to compel where no responses have been served.  All that need be shown in the moving papers is that a set of interrogatories was properly served on the opposing party, that the time to respond has expired, and that no response of any kind has been served.  (See Leach v. Superior Court (1980) 111 Cal. App. 3d 902, 905-906.) 

 

Where there has been no timely response to a demand to produce documents, the demanding party may seek an order compelling a response.  (C.C.P. § 2031.300(b).)  Failure to timely respond waives all objections, including privilege and work product.  (C.C.P. § 2031.300(a).)  Thus, unless the party to whom the demand was directed obtains relief from waiver, he or she cannot raise objections to the documents demanded. There is no deadline for a motion to compel responses.  Likewise, for failure to respond, the moving party need not attempt to resolve the matter outside court before filing the motion.

B.     Sanctions

 

The Court has discretion to impose a monetary sanction against a party engaging in the misuse of the discovery process, or any attorney advising that conduct. (C.C.P. § 2023.030(a).) Sanctions are only mandatory as against a party who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel a response to interrogatories or RPD, unless the Court finds they acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust. (C.C.P. §§ 2030.290(c) & 2031.300(c).) Regardless, it is customary to grant sanctions where a party has filed a motion to compel, and the other party fails to file an opposition. (C.R.C. Rule 3.1348(a).) 

 

Defendants request $1,124.70 in sanctions against Plaintiff and his attorney (= [2 hours for the motion + [2 anticipated hours to review/reply to the opposition and attend the hearing] at $260/hour + $84.70 in filing fees).  (Mikucki Decl., ¶10.)  Plaintiff, when bringing a lawsuit, must understand the concomitant discovery responsibilities and obligations. If they are unwilling to assent to these, then perhaps a lawsuit is not ultimately in Plaintiff’s best interest.  "[P]retrial discovery promotes the 'historically important state interest of facilitating the ascertainment of truth....'" (Davies v. Superior Court (1984) 36 Cal.3d 291) "The policy of the state is that the parties to a lawsuit 'shall cooperate.' Period. Full stop." (Lasalle v. Vogel (2019) 36 Cal.App.5th 127, 130).  A defendant should not be forced to incur the stress of a lawsuit and have that compounded by the expense of pursuing basic discovery.  The request is granted in the reasonable amount of $604.70.  The Court finds that this amount is reasonable as this is a straightforward, unopposed motion to compel initial responses.

ALLEGATIONS: 

 

Manuel Ontaneda (“Plaintiff”) alleges that on February 12, 2023, he rode his bicycle in front of Defendant Jesus Plascencia’s (“Plascencia”) property when two dogs owned by Plascencia and Defendant Bryan Yepes Centeno’s (“Centeno”) came out towards Plaintiff.  Plaintiff alleges the first dog jumped in front of the bicycle, while the second dog viciously bit Plaintiff’s lower left leg.

MOTIONS ON CALENDAR:

 

There are two discovery motions on calendar.  On April 7, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Motion to Compel Defendant Plascencia’s (“Defendant”) Further Responses to Request for Production of Documents, Set One (“RPD”).  On April 10, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Motion to Compel Defendant’s Further Responses to Special Interrogatories, Set One (“SROG”).  On July 23, 2026, Defendant filed opposition.  On July 28, 2026, Plaintiff filed a reply.

 

LEGAL STANDARD:

 

A.     Motion to Compel Further Responses to Interrogatories

 

A motion to compel further interrogatory responses may be brought based on: (1) answers that are evasive or incomplete, (2)¿an unwarranted or insufficiently specific exercise of an option to produce documents in lieu of a substantive response, or (3) unmerited or overly generalized objections.  (C.C.P. §¿2030.300(a).)   The motion must be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration.  (C.C.P. § 2030.300(b)(1).)  The motion must be made within 45 days of service of the verified response or supplemental verified response, or on/before any specific late date to which the parties have agreed in writing.  (C.C.P. § 2030.300(c).) 

 

The responding party has the burden of justifying the objections thereto. (Coy v. Sup.Ct. (1962) 58 Cal.2d 210, 220-21.)

 

B.     Motion to Compel Further Responses to RPD

 

A motion to compel further RPD responses may be brought based on: (1) incomplete statements of compliance; (2) inadequate, evasive, or incomplete claims of inability to comply; or (3) unmerited or overly generalized objections.  (C.C.P. § 2031.310(a).) 

s

A motion to compel further production must set forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought by the inspection demand and be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration. (C.C.P. § 2031.310(b)(1).)  “Good cause” is shown by “a disputed fact that is of consequence in the action and the discovery sought will tend in reason to prove or disprove that fact or lead to other evidence that will tend to prove or disprove the fact.”  (Digital Music News LLC v Superior Court (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 216, 224.)  The motion must be made within 45 days of service of the verified response or supplemental verified response, or on/before any specific late date to which the parties have agreed in writing.  (C.C.P. § 2031.310(c).) 

 

If the moving party has shown good cause for the requests for production, the burden is on the objecting party to justify the objections. (Kirkland v. Superior Court of Los Angeles County (2002) 95 Cal. App.4th 92, 98.) 

 

ANALYSIS: 

 

A.     SROGs

 

Plaintiff seeks Defendant’s further responses to SROG Nos. 8 and 36-44. 

 

1.      Timeliness

 

Preliminarily, Defendant argues that the SROG motion was untimely filed because Plaintiff’s deadline was April 7, 2026 and the SROG motion was served on April 7, 2026 but untimely filed on April 10, 2026. 

 

In reply, Plaintiff argues that Defendant’s verification is invalid because the SROG responses are signed by defense counsel Warren M. Ellis and dated February 12, 2026, but Defendant signed the verification on February 5, 2026, such that Defendant could not attest the truth of responses that did not yet exist.  Plaintiff argues without a valid verification, the 45-day clock did not commence.

 

Indeed, the verification is questionable as it appears to have been executed by Defendant on February 5, 2026 before responses were finalized by defense counsel on February 12, 2026.  It is unclear if verifications were prepared in advance of the finalized responses, or if there was a typographical error.  As there are questions regarding the validity of the SROG responses’ verification, the Court will deem the motion timely filed because the 45-day time limitation did not commence where Defendant did not provide a properly dated verification.

 

The Court will consider the substantive merits of the motion.

 

2.      Merits of Motion

 

SROG No. 8 asks Defendant to state the facts upon which he bases his contention that any injuries suffered were the result of Plaintiff’s negligence or actions of unknown third parties.  Defendant objected that discovery was ongoing, the SROG contained subparts or was compound, the SROG sought premature information, the SROG constituted a “continuing” interrogatory, the information was protected by the attorney-client privilege and attorney work product doctrine, and the SROG sought irrelevant information.  Without waiving objections, Defendant responded by citing photos of Plaintiff’s injuries, the canines, and the location of the incident; Azusa Police Department Incident Report No. 23-04732; Plaintiff’s billing and medical records; and Plaintiff’s employment records. 

 

Defendant’s objection that discovery is ongoing is not a valid objection.  Defendant has an obligation to investigate and make a reasonable and good faith effort to obtain the information as requested in the inquiry.  (See C.C.P. § 2030.220.) 

 

Next, Defendant argues that he complied by referring to documents.  However, C.C.P. § 2030.230 states:

 

If the answer to an interrogatory would necessitate the preparation or the making of a compilation, abstract, audit, or summary of or from the documents of the party to whom the interrogatory is directed, and if the burden or expense of preparing or making it would be substantially the same for the party propounding the interrogatory as for the responding party, it is a sufficient answer to that interrogatory to refer to this section and to specify the writings from which the answer may be derived or ascertained. This specification shall be in sufficient detail to permit the propounding party to locate and to identify, as readily as the responding party can, the documents from which the answer may be ascertained. The responding party shall then afford to the propounding party a reasonable opportunity to examine, audit, or inspect these documents and to make copies, compilations, abstracts, or summaries of them.

 

(C.C.P. § 2030.230.)  Defendant has not substantiated his reliance on section 2030.230 as he has not stated that responding to SROG No. 8 would necessitate the preparation of making of a compilation, etc. and that responding to the SROG would be burdensome or expensive otherwise.  Furthermore, based on the documents described in Defendant’s answer, the listed documents are not responsive to whether the injuries were suffered as a result of Plaintiff’s or an unknown third party’s actions. 

 

Finally, Defendant has not substantiated his other objections or claim of privilege.  (See Opp. Separate Statement at pp.3-4.)  As such, the remaining objections are overruled.  The motion is granted as to SROG No. 8.

 

SROG Nos. 36-44 are as follows:

 

·         SROG No. 36 asks Defendant to identify each document or record concerning any fencing/gating or planned guardrails enclosing his subject property as of the date of the incident.  

 

·         SROG No. 37 asks whether there were any other dogs on Defendant’s subject property on the date of the incident.

 

·         SROG No. 38 asks for the number of dogs at the subject property on the date of the incident.

 

·         SROG No. 39 asks Defendant to identify each document/record concerning any other dogs on his property at any time before the incident. 

·         SROG No. 40 asks Defendant whether he had any knowledge of any other dogs on his property exhibiting vicious tendencies prior to the incident.

 

·         SROG No. 41 asks whether Defendant received complaints concerning the subject dogs exhibiting vicious tendencies towards other dogs at his property prior to the incident.

 

·         SROG No. 42 asks whether Defendant had knowledge of any other dog bite incidents at the subject property prior to the incident.

 

·         SROG No. 43 asks Defendant to identify each document/record concerning any incidents involving other dogs on his property at any time before the incident.

 

·         SROG No. 44 asks whether he was aware of other dogs leaving his property prior to the incident.

 

Defendant responded/objected to these SROGs in the same manner.  Defendant objected that discovery was ongoing, the SROGs contained subparts or was compound, the SROGs sought premature information, the SROGs constituted a “continuing” interrogatory, the information was protected by the attorney-client privilege and attorney work product doctrine, the SROGs sought irrelevant information, and Plaintiff served more than 35 SROGs in violation of C.C.P. § 2030.030 without submitted a section 2030.050 declaration making the SROGs excessive in number. 

 

Based on Defendant’s arguments in opposition, the only objection he relies upon is that these SROGs are excessive in number.  (Defendant argues that Plaintiff propounded 47 SROGS, or 12 over the statutory limit; however, the number of SROGs is actually 44, or nine over the statutory limit.)

 

C.C.P. § 2030.030 states that a party may propound 35 SROGs.  If more than 35 are propounded,  a party need only respond to the first 35 SROGs unless a declaration pursuant to section 2030.050 has been made.  Section 2030.050’s subpart (8) requires the following statement: “This number of questions is warranted under Section 2030.040 of the Code of Civil Procedure because __________. (Here state each factor described in Section 2030.040 that is relied on, as well as the reasons why any factor relied on is applicable to the instant lawsuit.).” 

 

C.C.P. § 2030.040 states:

 

(a) Subject to the right of the responding party to seek a protective order under Section 2030.090, any party who attaches a supporting declaration as described in Section 2030.050 may propound a greater number of specially prepared interrogatories to another party if this greater number is warranted because of any of the following:

 

(1) The complexity or the quantity of the existing and potential issues in the particular case.

 

(2) The financial burden on a party entailed in conducting the discovery by oral deposition.

 

(3) The expedience of using this method of discovery to provide to the responding party the opportunity to conduct an inquiry, investigation, or search of files or records to supply the information sought.

 

(b) If the responding party seeks a protective order on the ground that the number of specially prepared interrogatories is unwarranted, the propounding party shall have the burden of justifying the number of these interrogatories.

 

(C.C.P. § 2030.040; see Weil & Brown, The Rutter Guide, Cal. Prac. Guide Civ. Pro. Before Trial (June 2026 Update) Ch. 8F-3, § 8:956 [stating, “The statute authorizes more than 35 interrogatories when accompanied by a ‘declaration of necessity,’ ‘[s]ubject to the right of the responding party to seek a protective order.’ [CCP § 2030.040(a) (emphasis added)] [¶] The clear implication is that the responding party cannot simply object to more than 35 interrogatories. Rather, the responding party must seek a protective order (within the time and in the manner required by CCP § 2030.090(a); [citation].).”].)  

 

C.C.P. § 2030.090 states in relevant part:

 

(a) When interrogatories have been propounded, the responding party, and any other party or affected natural person or organization may promptly move for a protective order. This motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.

 

(b) The court, for good cause shown, may make any order that justice requires to protect any party or other natural person or organization from unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression, or undue burden and expense. This protective order may include, but is not limited to, one or more of the following directions: …

 

(2) That, contrary to the representations made in a declaration submitted under Section 2030.050, the number of specially prepared interrogatories is unwarranted.

 

(C.C.P. § 2030.090(a), (b)(2).) 

 

A review of Plaintiff’s SROG requests confirms it included a Declaration for Additional Discovery.  (See SROG Mot. at Ex. A.)   In the declaration, counsel Harout Messrelian stated that the number of SROGs was warranted because “the quantity of existing and potential factual and legal issues in this particular case. The number of special interrogatories is also necessary to ensure that each request is not compound, conjunctive or disjunctive.”  Defendant disagrees with this rationale, arguing that counsel’s declaration fails to adequately provide a reason for the additional SROGs.  The Court disagrees and concludes that Mr. Messrelian’s declaration sufficiently explains the need for an additional nine SROGs. 

 

Furthermore, as stated in C.C.P. § 2030.040(a), a party attaching a supporting section 2030.050 declaration may propound a greater number of SROGs, subject to the right of the responding party to seek a protective order pursuant to section 2030.090.  Here, Defendant chose simply not to respond rather than seeking a protective order.  Defendant simply objected to the excessive number of SROGs which is not the proper means to address excessive SROGs.  The Court overrules this objection and will allow the additional nine SROGs to proceed. 

 

The motion is granted as to SROG Nos. 36-44.

However, as the Court grants the motion to compel Defendant’s further responses to SROG Nos. 35, 39, and 43, the Court too orders Defendant to provide further responses to RPD Nos. 9-11.  The motion is granted as to RPD Nos. 9-11.

A.    Complaint

 

W.M. Klorman Construction Corporation (“Plaintiff” or “Klorman”) alleges it solicited and relied on a subcontractor bid and subsequent Best and Final Offer (“BAFO”) from American Ironworks Manufacturing, Inc. (“Defendant” or “AIM”) for the metal works scope of a construction project at the VA Medical Center in Long Beach. After Plaintiff was awarded the prime contract, it accepted Defendant’s BAFO and worked for months on project planning, Requests for Information, and submittal packages.  However, after nearly a year of performance and after Plaintiff accepted all requested commercial changes, Defendant abruptly raised new objections, refused to proceed, and ultimately walked away from the project, forcing Plaintiff to secure a replacement subcontractor at a significantly higher cost. 

 

The Complaint, filed June 10, 2025, alleges: (1) Promissory Estoppel; (2) Breach of Contract; and (3) Breach of the Implied Covenant of Good Faith and fair Dealing.

 

B.     Operative Cross-Complaint

 

On March 30, 2026, Defendant AIM filed a Second Amended Cross-Complaint (“SAXC”) against Plaintiff Klorman for: (1) Deceit; (2) Reasonable Value of Goods and Services Rendered; and (3) Declaratory Relief.

 

AIM alleges that in 2024, Klorman provided specifications and asked AIM to provide a bid for certain metal works on the VALB Parking Structure Project in Long Beach.  (SAXC, ¶5.)  AIM prepared and submitted a bid to Klorman, Klorman made changes to the specifications regarding galvanizing, and AIM provided Klorman a further revised bid which excluded galvanizing, metalizing, and powder coating unless noticed in scope.  (Id., ¶6.)  After further discussions, AIM provided third and fourth revised proposals with the same exclusions.  (Id., ¶7.)  Despite numerous communications regarding a possible subcontract on the project, AIM contends that William M. Klorman (CEO and President of Klorman) refused to cooperate with AIM in providing it with a subcontract that matched AIM’s revised proposals.  (Id., ¶8.)  Due to Klorman’s failure to address AIM’s concerns regarding galvanizing, surface preparations regarding steel, and bonding, the parties did not enter a contract.  (Id., ¶9.)  AIM alleges that it spent hours reviewing Klorman’s bid package, preparing and revising the proposals, and communicating with Klorman, thereby incurring modeling and engineering costs, submittal costs, and material purchase and fabrication and delivery costs.  (Id.) 

 

MOTIONS ON CALENDAR:

 

On April 29, 2026, Plaintiff Klorman filed a Demurrer and Motion to Strike Defendant’s SAXC.  On July 27, 2026, Defendant AIM filed opposition.  On July 31, 2026, Plaintiff Klorman filed replies.

 

LEGAL STANDARD:

 

A.     Demurrer

 

The grounds for a demurrer must appear on the face of the pleading or from judicially noticeable matters. (C.C.P. § 430.30(a); Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal. 3d 311, 318.) A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) The only issue involved in a demurrer hearing is whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Id.)

 

A demurrer assumes the truth of all factual, material allegations properly pled in the challenged pleading. (Blank, supra, 39 Cal. 3d at p. 318.) No matter how unlikely or improbable, the plaintiff’s allegations must be accepted as true for the purpose of ruling on the demurrer. (Del E. Webb Corp. v. Structural Materials Co. (1981) 123 Cal.  App. 3d 593, 604.) But this does not include contentions; deductions; conclusions of fact or law alleged in the complaint; facts impossible in law; or allegations contrary to facts of which a court may take judicial notice.  (Blank, supra, 39 Cal. 3d at 318.)

 

Pursuant to C.C.P. §§ 430.10(e) and (f), the party against whom a complaint has been filed may demur to the pleading on the grounds that the pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action, or that the pleading is uncertain, ambiguous and/or unintelligible. It is an abuse of discretion to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend if there is a reasonable probability that the defect can be cured by amendment. (Schifando v. City of Los Angeles (2003) 31 Cal. 4th 1074, 1082.)

 

B.     Motion to Strike

 

Motions to strike are used to reach defects or objections to pleadings that are not challengeable by demurrer, such as words, phrases, and prayers for damages. (See C.C.P. §§ 435, 436, and 437.) The proper procedure to attack false allegations in a pleading is a motion to strike. (C.C.P. § 436(a).) In granting a motion to strike made under C.C.P. § 435, “[t]he court may, upon a motion made pursuant to Section 435 [notice of motion to strike whole or part of complaint], or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper: (a) Strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading.” (C.C.P. § 436(a).) Irrelevant matters include immaterial allegations that are not essential to the claim or those not pertinent to or supported by an otherwise sufficient claim. (C.C.P. § 431.10.)

 

The court may also “[s]trike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court.” (C.C.P. § 436(b).)

 

MEET AND CONFER:

 

C.C.P. §§ 430.41(a) and 435.5(a) require that the moving party meet and confer with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to the demurrer and/or motion to strike. Upon review, the Court finds the meet and confer requirements were met. (See Schiedermayer Demurrer Decl., ¶¶3-5, Ex. 1; Schiedermayer MTS Decl., ¶¶3-5, Ex. 1.) 

 

ANALYSIS RE DEMURRER:

 

Plaintiff Klorman demurs to the First, Second, and Third Causes of Action in Defendant’s SAXC.   

 

A.    First Cause of Action for Deceit – Sustained with Leave to Amend

 

The elements for fraud are: (1) a representation, usually of fact, which is false; (2) knowledge of its falsity; (3) intent to defraud; (4) justifiable reliance upon the misrepresentation; and (5) damage resulting from that justifiable reliance.  (Stansfield v. Starkey (1990) 220 Cal. App. 3d 59, 72-73.)  This cause of action is a tort of deceit and the facts constituting each element must be alleged with particularity; the claim cannot be saved by referring to the policy favoring liberal construction of pleadings.  (Committee on Children's Television, Inc. v. General Foods Corp. (1983) 35 Cal.3d 197, 216.)  Since the claim must be pleaded with particularity, the cause of action based on misrepresentations must allege facts showing how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the misrepresentations were tendered.  (Stansfield v. Starkey (1990) 220 Cal.App.3d 59, 73.)

 

In the First Cause of Action, AIM alleges that by inviting AIM to present a bid on the project, “Klorman represented that if [AIM] would provide a bid based on the specifications provided by Klorman to [AIM], [AIM] would have an opportunity to be awarded the metal works scope of work as a subcontractor to Klorman for the Project.”  (SAXC, ¶11.)  AIM alleges Klorman’s representations were false as Klorman and Mr. Klorman had no intention of contracting AIM based on AIM’s provided specifications, but intended to add requirements regarding galvanizing and surface preparations regarding steel that were not in the specifications provided to AIM.  (Id., ¶12.)  AIM alleges the parties sent multiple emails whereby AIM did not agree to the additional scope, while Mr. Klorman stated AIM would be bound to galvanize without additional compensation.  (Id.)  AIM contends Klorman knew from the beginning of the bid process that AIM would not provide galvanizing work unless specifically noted in scope, but Klorman “deceitfully” pressed AIM to sign a subcontract for the project where AIM would be required to perform galvanizing for no extra compensation, but required AIM to post a surety bond.  (Id.)  AIM alleges that Klorman, through Mr. Klorman, represented AIM would have the opportunity to be awarded the metal works scope of work subcontract if it provided a bid, but this was false, Klorman intended to deceive AIM and induce AIM to spend time and costs to submit the bid, and Klorman knew it would not provide a subcontract to AIM that would match AIM’s bid.  (Id., ¶13.)  AIM alleges it was ignorant of its falsity and was induced to spend time and incur costs to submit a bid in reliance on Klorman and Mr. Klorman’s misrepresentations.  (Id., ¶14.)  AIM claims damages in the amount of $96,068.75 for the lost bid.  (Id., ¶15.)

 

Klorman demurs to the First Cause of Action, arguing that AIM has not alleged specific facts supporting each Fraud element.

 

AIM has not alleged the misrepresentation element with the requisite particularity.  Paragraph 11 only alleges the nature of the misrepresentation, but fails to allege specific facts including how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the misrepresentations were tendered. 

 

To the extent AIM relies on the parties’ emails regarding the work/scope specifications and revised bids, the February 23 and 24, 2025 emails show that Mr. Klorman required a revised bid in order to reissue the subcontract for immediate execution, but Dan Boss of AIM disagreed stating they would not be adding galvanizing.  (SAXC, ¶12, Exs. G-H.)  The emails do not show any actionable misrepresentations.  Furthermore, the proposals attached are dated March 19, 2024 (Ex. A), May 2, 2024 (Ex. B), May 7, 2024 (Ex. C), June 5, 2024 (Ex. D), and June 6, 2024 (Ex. E).  Thus, the February 2025 emails post-date the four proposals, such that the emails fail to show how AIM relied on the emails if categorized as misrepresentations.  Thus, the Fraud elements have not been met with respect to the emails. 

 

The allegations regarding the element of knowledge of falsity also lacks specificity.  AIM alleges that Klorman intended from the beginning of the bid process not to award the subcontract to AIM unless it included galvanizing work and post a surety bond, but these allegations are conclusory and are not supported by specific facts.  While AIM provides the emails are dated months after the proposals, which does not indicate that Klorman had knowledge of the falsity of its representations or intended from the beginning not to accept AIM’s bid. 

 

As these elements have not been adequately alleged, the remaining Fraud elements are lacking.  Thus, the demurrer to the First Cause of Action is sustained with leave to amend.

 

B.     Second Cause of Action for Reasonable Value of Goods and Services Rendered – Sustained with Leave to Amend

 

“To recover on a claim for the reasonable value of services under a quantum meruit theory, a plaintiff must establish both that he or she was acting pursuant to either an express or implied request for services from the defendant and that the services rendered were intended to and did benefit the defendant.”  (Ochs v. PacifiCare of California (2004) 115 Cal.App.4th 782, 794.)  “The theory of quasi-contractual recovery is that one party has accepted and retained a benefit with full appreciation of the facts, under circumstances making it inequitable for him to retain the benefit without payment of its reasonable value.”  (Day v. Alta Bates Medical Center (2002) 98 Cal.App.4th 243, 248.)  “[W]hen the services are rendered by the plaintiff to a third person, the courts have required that there be a specific request therefor from the defendant: [C]ompensation for a party's performance should be paid by the person whose request induced the performance.” (Id. at 249 [internal quotation marks and citations omitted].)

 

In the Second Cause of Action, AIM alleges that Klorman became indebted to AIM in the amount of at least $96,068.75 for work, labor, services, and materials rendered by AIM to Klorman at Klorman’s special request.  (SAXC, ¶17.)  AIM contends Klorman asked AIM to provide a bid for the project’s metal works portion and AIM spent substantial time and effort preparing the bid and revisions, which Klorman changed at the time of presenting Klorman’s presentation of a subcontract to AIM to sign.  (Id., ¶18.)  AIM claims it incurred shop labor costs for the project, as WIM fabricated, loaded, and delivered metal embeds to the project site and fabricated bollards for the project at Klorman’s request.  (Id.)  AIM alleges it did not sign a subcontract due to the mismatch between the specifications bid upon, AIM’s bid presented to Klorman, and the subcontract tendered to AIM for signature.  (Id., ¶19.)  AIM is informed and believes that its bid documents were of significant value to Klorman and assisted Klorman in retaining another subcontractor for the metal works scope of the project, such that Klorman was unjustly enriched by AIM’s bid documents and fabrication and delivery of metal embeds.  (Id.) 

 

Based on the SAXC’s allegations, the Second Cause of Action fails to allege an express or implied request for services from Klorman.  At most, the allegations and attached exhibits, taken as true, show that Klorman sought a subcontract bid for the project from AIM but the parties disagreed on the scope of work.  While AIM alleges that Klorman requested the fabrication, loading, and delivery of metal embeds and the fabrication of bollards for the project, the allegations have no further supporting facts that this was an express request, or that Klorman impliedly requested such services during the parties’ bid proposal/subcontract discussions and negotiations.  Furthermore, as pointed out by Klorman in its demurrer, while AIM provides a Shipping List of the materials (dated December 18 and 19, 2024) (SAXC, Ex. F), there are no allegations that Klorman accepted, received, or used the materials and how it benefitted.  (Dem. at p.12.)

 

The demurer to the Second Cause of Action is sustained with leave to amend.

 

C.     Third Cause of Action for Declaratory Relief – Sustained with Leave to Amend

 

A cause of action for declaratory relief is a remedy created by C.C.P. § 1060 and it is pleaded if it: (1) sets forth facts showing the existence of an actual controversy relating to the legal rights and duties of the respective parties and (2) requests that the rights and duties be adjudged.  (City of Tiburon v. Northwestern Pac. R.R. Co. (1970) 4 Cal.App.3d 160, 170; see C.C.P. § 1060 [identifying the remedy of declaratory relief].)  “‘[A] request for declaratory relief will not create a cause of action that otherwise does not exist.’ Rather, ‘an actual, present controversy must be pleaded specifically’ and ‘the facts of the respective claims concerning the [underlying] subject must be given.’” (City of Cotati v. Cashman (2002) 29 Cal.4th 69, 80 [internal quotation marks and citations omitted].) 

 

In the Third Cause of Action, AIM alleges that an actual controversy has arisen and exists between AIM and Klorman because AIM contends it is entitled to compensation from Klorman for its time and effort in bidding for the project, fabricating and delivering materials, and for lost profits if it was able to subcontract with Klorman for the project, and that it was within its rights to refuse to sign the subcontract when the parties’ specification did not match.  (SAXC, ¶21.)  AIM seeks a final declaration and determination regarding whether the subcontract Klorman provided AIM matched the specifications provided to AIM upon which AIM submitted its bids and whether the subcontract matched the bid provided by Aim to Klorman.  (Id., ¶22.)  AIM also seeks a declaration and determination regarding whether AIM is entitled to compensation from Klorman for the time Klorman caused AIM to waste and the costs incurred to bid and fabricate/deliver materials, when Klorman knew it would never provide a subcontract that matched AIM’s bid documents.  (Id.) 

 

As alleged, AIM seeks a declaration to redress past wrongs.  “Declaratory relief generally operates prospectively to declare future rights, rather than to redress past wrongs” and is a remedy in the interests of “... preventative justice, to declare rights rather than execute them.”  (Jolley v. Chase Home Finance, LLC (2013) 213 Cal.App.4th 872, 909.)  As currently stated, the declaratory relief claim fails to allege sufficient facts to amount to an actionable cause of action. 

 

Thus, the demurrer to the Third Cause of Action is sustained with leave to amend.

ANALYSIS RE DEMURRER:

 

Plaintiff demurs to the First, Second, Third, and Fourth Causes of Action in Defendant’s FAXC.   

 

A.    First Cause of Action for Defamation/Filing a False Report – Sustained with Leave to Amend

 

The elements of defamation are: (1) publication that is (2) false, (3) defamatory, (4) unprivileged, and (5) has a natural tendency to injure or cause special damage.  (Wong v. Tai Jing (2010) 189 Cal.App.4th 1354, 1369.)

 

In the First Cause of Action, Defendant alleges that Defendant never met Plaintiff who was the landlord.  (FAXC, ¶16.)  Defendant alleges she only intended to construct the water division system within the property line of the CMU wall and attempted to contact Plaintiff’s tenant in a calm manner instead of threats as claimed by Plaintiff.  (Id., ¶¶17-18.)  Defendant alleges she never entered beyond the front porch of Plaintiff’s Property while delivering notes and that the water division system does not exceed nor show proof of water leakage.  (Id., ¶¶19-20.)  Defendant alleges that she left the water division system unfinished after the unresolved title of the property line.  (Id., ¶¶21.)  Defendant contends Plaintiff and its tenant failed to act and comply with the necessary requirements for Defendant to resolve the measurement of the property lines. (Id., ¶22.) 

 

The allegations fail to allege the elements of a defamation claim.  Defendant has not alleged what statement Plaintiff made/wrote, whether the publication was false and defamatory, and whether it had a natural tendency to injure or cause Defendant damages.  In opposition, Defendant argues that Plaintiff published false, damaging statements about Defendant’s property use to neighborhood residents, tenants, and third parties to impair her reputation in the community.  (Opp. at p.6.)  However, these allegations were not alleged in the FAXC and thus constitute extrinsic facts/arguments that cannot be considered to determine the sufficiency of the cross-complaint.   

 

As the defamation elements have not been alleged in the FAXC, the demurrer to the First Cause of Action is sustained with leave to amend. 

 

B.     Second Cause of Action for Coercion – Sustained with Leave to Amend

 

“[A] threat to take legal action is not unlawful unless the party making the threat knows the falsity of his claim.”  (Odorizzi v. Bloomfield School Dist. (1966) 246 Cal.App.2d 123, 128.)  “[I]t does not constitute duress or coercion to threaten to do that which a party has a legal right to do.”  (Marshall v. Packard-Bell Co. (1951) 106 Cal.App.2d 770, 774.) 

 

In the Second Cause of Action, Defendant alleges that from February 27 to March 3, 2026, during her trip, Defendant’s daughter received multiple copies of the demurrer and motion to strike the answer.  (FAXC, ¶¶23-24.)  She alleges that Plaintiff’s counsel used a forceful and aggressive tone to rush Defendant’s response on the same day she was returning to the United States.  (Id., ¶25.) 

 

Plaintiff argues that this cause of action is uncertain and that there is no standalone cause of action for “coercion.”  The Court agrees that Defendant’s allegations do not amount to a cause of action as currently stated and that the allegations are therefore uncertain.  (The Court notes that Plaintiff’s demurrer also addresses duress and the Tom Bane Civil Rights Act under Civil Code, § 52.1, but as the nature of this cause of action is uncertain, such arguments need not be considered at this time.) 

 

The demurer to the Second Cause of Action is sustained with leave to amend.  However, upon amendment, Defendant should consider whether she has sufficient supporting facts to allege coercion or a related type of claim.

 

C.     Third Cause of Action for Harassment – Sustained with Leave to Amend

 

In the Third Cause of Action, Defendant alleges that she asked Plaintiff’s counsel to stop the repeated harassment via duplicated demurrers/motions to strike through email, but Plaintiff’s counsel only responded with two more copies of the former, proving Plaintiff’s actions of harassment via attorney and disrespecting Defendant’s request to leave her alone to recover.  (FAXC, ¶27.)

 

C.C.P. § 527.6(a)(1) states that a person who has suffered harassment may seek a temporary restraining order and an order after hearing prohibiting harassment.  “Harassment” is defined as “unlawful violence, a credible threat of violence, or a knowing and willful course of conduct directed at a specific person that seriously alarms, annoys, or harasses the person, and that serves no legitimate purpose. The course of conduct must be that which would cause a reasonable person to suffer substantial emotional distress, and must actually cause substantial emotional distress to the petitioner.”  (C.C.P. § 527.6(b)(3).)

 

Defendant has not alleged a viable claim for harassment against Plaintiff and/or her counsel (who is not a party to this action).  At best, Defendant alleges that Plaintiff/Plaintiff’s counsel caused pleadings to be served on Defendant, which is a routine procedure in a litigation.  Defendant has not alleged unlawful violence, a credible threat of violence, or a course of conduct that seriously alarms, annoys, or harasses a person and that serves no legitimate purpose. 

 

Thus, the demurrer to the Third Cause of Action is sustained with leave to amend.

 

D.    Fourth Cause of Action for Mental/Emotional Damage – Sustained with Leave to Amend

 

The elements of intentional infliction of emotional distress are: (1) outrageous conduct by the defendant; (2) the defendant’s intention of causing or reckless disregard of the probability of causing emotional distress; (3) the plaintiff suffered severe or extreme emotional distress; and (4) the plaintiff’s injuries were actually and proximately caused by the defendant’s outrageous conduct.  (Vasquez v. Franklin Mgmt. Real Estate Fund, Inc. (2013) 222 Cal.App.4th 819, 832.)  In order to avoid a demurrer, the plaintiff must allege with great specificity, the acts which she believes are so extreme as to exceed all bounds of behavior usually tolerated in a civilized community.  (Id.)

 

Outrageous conduct must be so extreme as to exceed all bounds of behavior usually tolerated in a civilized community.  (Christensen v. Superior Court (1991) 54 Cal.3d 868, 903.)  In addition, the outrageous conduct must be of a nature which is especially calculated to cause, and does cause, mental distress of a very serious kind.  (Id.)  “Although emotional distress may consist of any highly unpleasant mental reaction such as fright, grief, shame, humiliation, embarrassment, anger, chagrin, disappointment or worry [citation], to make out a claim, the plaintiff must prove that emotional distress was severe and not trivial or transient.”  (Wong v. Tai Jing (2010) 189 Cal.App.4th 1354, 1376.)  Such distress must be “of such substantial quality or enduring quality that no reasonable person in civilized society should be expected to endure it.”  (Potter v. Firestone Tire & Rubber Co. (1993) 6 Cal.4th 965, 1004.)


In the Fourth Cause of Action, Defendant alleges that Plaintiff’s actions have negatively affected Defendant and her family (especially her daughter), and caused them to suffer anxiety, stress, and paranoia, and to experience loss of sleep and focus.  (FAXC, ¶¶28-30.)  She alleges she experienced depression and anxiety, requiring anti-depressants, and her daughter has suffered insomnia and anxiety, requiring therapy and prescriptions.  (Id., ¶¶31-32.) 

 

There is no cause of action for mental/emotional damage.  However, to the extent Defendant is alleging an IIED claim, Defendant has not alleged sufficient facts regarding what conduct Plaintiff engaged in that would be considered extreme and outrageous that exceeds the bounds of behavior usually tolerated in a civilized community. As this element has not been alleged, Defendant has not alleged sufficient facts supporting an IIED cause of action.  The demurrer to the Fourth Cause of Action is sustained with leave to amend.
