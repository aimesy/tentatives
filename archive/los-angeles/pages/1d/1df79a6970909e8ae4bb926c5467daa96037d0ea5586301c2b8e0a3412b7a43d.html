DEPARTMENT 413 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 21STCV44716    Hearing Date: July 15, 2026    Dept: 413

TENTATIVE RULING

HEARING DATE:                 July 15, 2026

CASE NUMBER:                   21STCV44716

CASE NAME:                        Library Rights Company (UK) LTD v. Initial Entertainment Group, Inc., et al.

MOVING PARTY:                 Defendant/Cross-Complainant, CP IEG Holdings LP

OPPOSING PARTY:             Plaintiff/Cross-Defendant, Library Rights Company (UK) LTD

PROCEEDING:                      Motion to compel further deposition

RULING SUMMARY:          CP IEG’s Motion to Compel Further Deposition of Ray Reyes is denied. CP IEG’s request for sanctions against Reyes and his counsel is denied.  Cross-Defendants’ request for sanctions is also denied. 

 

Background

 

On December 7, 2021, Plaintiff Library Rights Company (UK) LTD. (“LRC”) filed this action against Defendants Initial Entertainment Group, Inc., GK Films, LLC, Graham King, and GKF Distribution, LLC, Content Partners, LLC and CP IEG Holdings LP (“CP IEG”) for breach of contract and accounting of gross proceeds from three films “Ali,” “Gangs of New York,” and “Traffic” (collectively, “Pictures”).¿

 

¿¿On June 21, 2024, Plaintiff filed the operative Third Amended Complaint alleging four causes of action for (1) Breach of Contract, (2) Breach of the Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing, (3) Common Count, and (4) Intentional Interference with Contract.¿The Court notes that Defendant GFK was renamed in the TAC as GFK Distribution, LP. The Court refers to GFK Distribution LP as “GFK.” 

 

Defendant/Cross-Complainant CP IEG filed a Cross-Complaint against LRC, Ray Reyes (“Reyes”), and Roes 1 to 10 (collectively, “Cross-Defendants”). On March 5,2024, Cross-Complainant CP IEG filed the operative First Amended Cross-Complaint (“FACC”). The FACC asserts three causes of action for (1) Fraud, (2) Negligent Misrepresentation, and (3) Declaratory Relief.¿¿¿

 

On May 7, 2026, the deposition of LRC’s representative and cross-defendant Rey Reyes took place. 

 

On June 12, 2026, CP IEG filed the motion to compel further deposition of Rey Reyes on the grounds that Reyes repeatedly refused to answer “straightforward yes-or-no questions” during his deposition, providing evasive and non-responsive answers that constitute a misuse of the discovery process. (Mot., p. 1.)  On July 1, 2026, Cross-Defendants filed their opposition.  On July 8, 2026, CP IEG filed its Reply.

 

motion name

 

I.         Legal Standard

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.480, provides:¿¿

 

(a)   If a deponent fails to answer any question or to produce any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing under the deponent’s control that is specified in the deposition notice or a deposition subpoena, the party seeking discovery may move the court for an order compelling that answer or production.

 

(b)   This motion shall be made no later than 60 days after the completion of the record of the deposition and shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.

 

…

 

(i)    If the court determines that the answer or production sought is subject to discovery, it shall order that the answer be given or the production be made on the resumption of the deposition.

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.480.)

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.460(e) provides:

 

If a deponent fails to answer any question … the party seeking that answer or production may adjourn the deposition or complete the examination on other matters without waiving the right at a later time to move for an order compelling that answer or production under Section 2025.480.

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.460(e).)

 

II.        Discussion

 

a.     Timeliness

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.480(b), provides that a motion to compel further deposition “shall be made no later than 60 days after the completion of the record of the deposition.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.480(b).)

 

Here, the deposition took place on May 7, 2026. (Leeker Decl., ¶ 2.)  The deadline for CP IEG to file a motion to compel further deposition was July 6, 2026.  CP IEG filed the instant motion on June 12, 2026.  Therefore, the motion is timely under Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.480(b).

 

b.     Meet and Confer

 

CP IEG filed a meet and confer declaration concurrently with the motion.  CP IEG sent counsel for Cross-Defendants a meet and confer letter by email on May 14, 2026, regarding Reyes’ evasive responses during the deposition on May 7, 2026. (Leeker Decl., ¶ 3, Ex. B.)  CP IEG also requested a further deposition of Reyes. (Ibid.)

 

Counsel for Cross-Defendants twice requested additional authorities supporting CP IEG’s position. (Leeker Decl., ¶ 3, Ex. B.)  On June 4, 2026, counsel for Cross-Defendants stated their refusal to make Reyes available for further deposition. (Ibid.)

 

CP IEG has satisfied the meet and confer requirements under Code of Civil Procedure, section 2016.040.

 

c.      Arguments

 

CP IEG argues that Reyes should be ordered to appear for further deposition to provide code compliant responses to questions related to threshold issues in this action. (Mot., p. 9.)  The questions Reyes refused to answer were related to whether Reyes was telling the truth when he made a statement, or whether he had certain knowledge as to identified issues, specifically, (i) whether Reyes was being truthful when he made certain prior statements and representations; (ii) whether Reyes was aware as to whether LRC had other assets than those identified in the question, and (iii) whether Reyes could confirm that he was not representing in the 2015 Sales Presentation that LRC’s alleged rights to profit participation in the three pictures at issue in this dispute were being offered for sale. ((Id., at pp. 9-10.)  According to CP IEG, these questions called for a yes or no answer, which Reyes did not provide.

 

CP IEG further argues that Reyes’ responses, which CP IEG contends were evasive, should be treated no differently than outright refusals to answer. (Deyo v. Kilbourne (1978) 84 Cal.App.3d 771, 783; see Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.010(f) ["Making an evasive response to interrogatory, inspection demand, or deposition question" is defined as a misuse of the discovery process.]) 

 

Cross-Defendants, in opposition, contend that Reyes provided substantive, good-faith answers to each question and that Reyes merely “declined to adopt [CP IEG]’s preferred framing and instead answered truthfully, in context, and with appropriate qualifications.” (Opp., p. 1.) 

 

Cross-Defendants argue that CP IEG is misconstruing Code of Civil Procedure, section 2025.480(a). (Opp., p. 4.)  According to Cross-Defendants, a motion to compel on these grounds is meant to address a deponent’s refusal or failure to answer, not to police the form of answers given. (See Trail v. Cornwell (1984) 161 Cal.App.3d 477, 486 [There is no basis to grant motion to compel where deponent did not “fail[] or refuse[]” to answer questions.].)

 

Cross-Defendants also cite Evidence Code section 766, arguing it provides that if a question cannot fairly be answered with a simple “yes” or “no,” the witness is entitled to provide a complete and accurate answer, which may necessarily be a narrative. (Opp., p. 4.)  However, statute does not so provide; it states that “[a] witness must give responsive answers to questions, and answers that are not responsive shall be stricken on motion from any party.” (Evid. Code, § 766.)

 

Cross-Defendants further argue that under Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.290(a), a deposition is limited to seven hours of testimony. (Opp., 4.)  Cross-Defendants admit that CP IEG did not exhaust all seven hours at the May 7, 2026 deposition but counsel for CP IEG stated he had “no further questions” at the conclusion of the deposition. (Ibid.)  However, California law does not require the deposing party to reserve their right to further deposition before concluding the original deposition.  The statute simply provides that if a deponent fails to answer any question during a deposition, the party seeking discovery may move the court for an order compelling that answer, provided the motion is made no later than 60 days after completion of the deposition record. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.480.)

 

In Reply, CP IEG argues that the deposition questions posed to Reyes did not require elaboration. (Reply, p. 3.)  CP IEG contends that the questions were propounded for the sole, legitimate purpose of receiving clear, unambiguous answers to critical questions concerning the 2015 Sales Presentation, which is a key issue in the instant action. (Ibid.)

 

As there is contention as to the adequacy of Reyes’ responses to four specific questions, the Court shall evaluate each question and Reyes’ responses to determine if they are indeed evasive or should be otherwise treated as a failure to answer.

 

d.     Questions at Issue:

 

1)     “Was it your intention to communicate truthful information to all the prospective investors under this 2015 sales presentation for LRC?” (Leeker Decl., Ex. A., pp. 312:21-318:16.)

 

Reyes responded to Question 1 with a narrative.  CP IEG rephrased the question two more times before indicating that Reyes’ responses were non-responsive and should be stricken.  While a large portion of Reyes’ responses were narrative and non-responsive, Reyes more directly answered the question with the following responses:

 

My intention was to give the prospective buyer information regarding the prospective acquisition that was being offered to them.

 

(Leeker Decl. Ex. A, pp. 315:10-12.)

 

I gave them information about a complicated asset and it gave them basic information that they would have to go and verify on their own.

 

(Id., pp. 315:24-25.)

 

The Court determines that Reyes did not refuse to answer, even if the answer included additional narrative.  Reyes sufficiently answered the question posed, and the motion is denied as to Question 1.

 

2)     “Is it correct that your intent in revising these boxes on page 9 was to communicate truthful information?” (Leeker Decl., Ex. A., pp. 402:18-403:20.)

 

Reyes responded to Question 2 with a narrative, but within it were the following statements that do address the question posed:

 

My intent in the edits that I made in the draft sales presentation were—was to make the presentation better, to make it seem like a more attractive opportunity that a prospective buyer might want to come and take a look at.

 

(Leeker Decl. Ex. A, pp. 402:22-25.)

 

[W]hat I put in here and my comments were meant to introduce information regarding and characterize the events that happened over the course of this timeline . . . it is not a complete statement and it is not intended to be a full statement.

 

(Id., pp. 403:7-14.)

 

Upon reviewing Reyes’ responses, the Court finds that Reyes did not refuse to answer, even if the answer included additional narrative.  Reyes sufficiently answered the question posed, and the motion is denied as to Question 2.

 

3)     “So is it correct your testimony is that you cannot testify today as to whether you have knowledge that LRC owns assets of Intermedia other than the 80 film titles, the 59 development titles, the three pictures that are the subject of this lawsuit -- ALI, TRAFFIC and GANGS OF NEW YORK -- or the project IF ONLY? That’s correct, you can’t answer that question as to whether it owns those -- any other film titles?” (Leeker Decl., Ex. A., pp. 373:6-374:9.)

 

Reyes responds to Question 3 by arguing that he is being “pigeonholed” into a particular response. (Leeker Decl., Ex. A., p. 373:16-19.)  Specifically, he states that “[he]’s not aware of anything else. [He] may be aware. [He] may – it may have assets.” (Leeker Decl., Ex. A, pp. 373:16-19.)  When the question is posed again by CP IEG, Reyes further states that he cannot answer the question and then ultimately refuses to answer. (Id., pp. 373:24, 374:9.) 

 

Upon reviewing Reyes’ responses, the Court finds that Reyes provide a responsive substantive response to the Question 3, even if the answer was he was not aware.  Based on his testimony, Reyes’ statement refusing to answer appears to have been in frustration after already stating he was not aware.  Reyes sufficiently answered the question posed and the motion is denied as to Question 3.

 

4)     “But you did not state in any way that those four movies were part of the Intermedia library that was being offered for sale at this time, correct?” (Leeker Decl., Ex. A., pp. 393:24-397:20.) 

 

Reyes again responds with an explanation that he cannot properly answer the question in the way it was posed by CP IEG. (Leeker Decl., Ex. A., p. 394:3-10.)  CP IEG rephrased the question several times, which elicited the following responses from Reyes:

 

It’s just meant to highlight the quality of film that . . . Intermedia in its heyday were involved in.

 

(Leeker Decl., Ex. A, pp. 393:10-12.)

 

I was—yes. I wanted to point out that a one-time subsidiary of Intermedia—IEG— produced some quality work.

 

(Id., pp. 393:21-23.)

 

[A]t the time this 2015 sales presentation was prepared, I wasn’t aware of the participation interests that we’re discussing.

 

(Id., pp. 394:21-23.)

 

[T]his is not a complete statement of the rights that were being offered by LRC in the sale.

 

(Id., pp. 396:19-21.)

 

Upon reviewing Reyes’ responses, the Court finds that Reyes did not refuse to answer, even if his responses were not as direct as desired by CP IEG.  Reyes sufficiently answered the question posed, and the motion is denied as to Question 4.

 

e.      Sanctions

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.480(j) requires that the court impose monetary sanctions against any party or attorney who unsuccessfully opposes a motion to compel further deposition testimony, unless the court finds that the opposing party acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of sanctions unjust. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.480(j).)

 

Both parties request sanctions against the other under Code of Civil Procedure, section 2025.480(j).  CP IEG requests sanctions in the amount of $9,000 against Reyes and his counsel. (Mot., p. 11.)  Cross-Defendants request sanctions against CP IEG but requests the amount be established by declaration.  (Opp., p. 12.)

 

The Court denies both requests for sanctions.  The dispute over whether Reyes’ responses were sufficient or whether further responses should be compelled was a good faith dispute as to which neither side acted without substantial justification or otherwise engaged in discovery abuse. 

 

Conclusion

 

CP IEG’s Motion to Compel Further Deposition of Ray Reyes is denied.  CP IEG’s request for sanctions against Reyes and his counsel under Code of Civil Procedure, section 2025.480(j) is denied.  LRC and Ray Reyes’ request for sanctions against CP IEG under Code of Civil Procedure, section 2025.480(j) is denied.

Case Number: 22STCV08889    Hearing Date: July 15, 2026    Dept: 413

TENTATIVE RULING

HEARING DATE:                 July 15, 2026

CASE NUMBER:                   22STCV08889

CASE NAME:                        Lee Donald Taicher v. Aroma Taicher, et al.

MOVING PARTY:                 Defendant Linh Huong Ton

OPPOSING PARTY:             Plaintiff Lee Donald Taicher

PROCEEDING:                      motion to quash service of summons

RULING SUMMARY:          Defendant’s Motion to Quash is denied.  Defendant is ordered to file an Answer to the Complaint within 10 days.  The Court continues the Case Management Conference to, and issues and sets an Order to Show Cause re filing of Answer or Default of Defendant for, ________________, 2026, at 8:30AM.                                 

Background

 

In this fraud and financial elder abuse action, Plaintiff Lee Donald Taicher (“Plaintiff”) alleges the following, among other things. After Plaintiff met Defendant Aroma Taicher aka Huong Thi Thanh Nguyen in Vietnam, the defendant misled him into believing that she loved him to induce him to help her immigrate to the United States and marry her. However, after marriage, the defendant ignored and mistreated him except when she wanted his money to buy expensive things. The defendant also took advantage of his desire for love, affection, and a wholesome family by convincing him to give $500,000 to the defendant’s daughter, named defendant Linh Huong Ton, so that the latter could obtain an EB-5 investor visa to come to the United States and give birth to her baby here, claiming that all four (including the daughter’s baby) could live together as a family in the United States. However, the defendants failed to keep their promises and subsequently used his money to secretly buy a house in Florida, where they intend to move.

 

On April 26, 2022, Plaintiff filed the operative First Amended Complaint (“FAC”), alleging the following causes of action: (1) Fraud-Intentional Misrepresentation (against all defendants), (2) Fraud-False Promise (against all defendants), (3) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress (against all defendants), (4) Negligence (against all defendants), (5) Breach of Fiduciary Duty (against all defendants), (6) Financial Abuse of an Elder (against all defendants), and (7) Breach of Promissory Note (only against Aroma Taicher).

 

On April 14, 2022, Plaintiff filed Proof of Service of Summons, stating that on April 14, 2022, Defendant Linh Huong Ton (“Defendant”) was served through substituted service at a West Hollywood, California address.

 

On November 21, 2022, Plaintiff filed an application for an order allowing Plaintiff to serve Defendant Linh Huong Ton (“Defendant”) by publication.

 

On December 12, 2022, the Court denied the application for publication.

 

On January 31, 2023, Plaintiff filed a Proof of Service of Summons, stating that Defendant was personally served with copies of the Summons and Complaint at a Florida address.

 

On May 27, 2023, Defendant filed a motion to quash service of the summons, arguing that she has never been a resident of California. However, that motion was never called for hearing in this case or the related cases (i.e., family law case 21STFL13780 and probate case 22STPB03579).

 

On July 7, 2023, Defendant’s counsel of record in this case filed a substitution of attorney, withdrawing from the case. Defendant is currently self-represented.

 

On April 7, 2025, Plaintiff dismissed the first through sixth causes of action as to Aroma Taicher only.

 

On April 1, 2026, Defendant filed the “Amended Motion to Quash Service of Summons,” and Plaintiff filed a timely opposition.

 

I.         Legal Standard

 

A defendant, on or before the last day to plead or within any further time that the court may for good cause allow, may serve and file a notice of motion to quash service of summons on the ground of lack of jurisdiction of the court over her. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 418.10, subd. (a); 412.20, subd. (a)(3) [stating that a summons must state that a defendant has 30 days after service to file a response].)

“In the absence of a voluntary submission to the authority of the court, compliance with the statutes governing service of process is essential to establish that court’s personal jurisdiction over a defendant.  [Code Civ. Proc.] § 410.50.”  (Dill v. Berquist Construction Co. (1994) 24 Cal.App.4th 1426, 1439 (Dill) [footnote omitted].)  

“When a defendant challenges [personal] jurisdiction by bringing a motion to quash, the burden is on the plaintiff to prove the existence of jurisdiction by proving, inter alia, the facts requisite to an effective service.”  (Id. at pp. 1439–1440.) 

“However, strict compliance is not required.  In deciding whether service was valid, the statutory provisions regarding service of process should be liberally construed to effectuate service and uphold the jurisdiction of the court if actual notice has been received by the defendant.  [Citation.]  Thus, substantial compliance is sufficient.”  (Id. at pp. 1436-1437.)

II.        Discussion

 

Defendant’s amended motion to quash included only a notice of motion and her declaration; no memorandum of points and authorities was submitted.  The motion argues that the Court lacks personal jurisdiction over her for two reasons.

 

First, the purported substituted service on April 14, 2022, is void because Defendant vacated the California address in 2021, before the alleged service. (Notice of Motion, p. 2:11-17.)

 

Second, the “Publication on January 13, 2023,” is void for failure to file the mandatory affidavit of diligence and Defendant “was a discoverable non-resident of California.” (Notice of Motion, at p. 2:7-9; see also Declaration of Linh Huong Ton, ¶ 6 [“I am informed and believed that Plaintiff obtained an Order for Publication December 2022 without filing the mandatory affidavit of diligence required by CCP § 415.50. Had Plaintiff performed a basic search of public records or a skip trace, my Florida residency would have been discoverable”].)

 

Defendant’s arguments that service was inadequate are unpersuasive.  Even if the 2022 substituted service were improper, Plaintiff subsequently filed a Proof of Service of Summons, on January 31, 2023, stating that Defendant was personally served on January 14, 2023, at 2536 Galliano Cir., Winter Park, FL 32792. In her Reply, Defendant did not dispute personal service.  Instead, she implied that the service was improper by pointing out that the service “omitted the ADR materials required by the California Rules of Court (Exhibit B-4).”  (Reply, p. 8:20-21; see Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.221(c) [“In all general civil cases, the plaintiff must serve a copy of the ADR information package on each defendant together with the complaint”].) Defendant cited no legal authority to support her argument that failure to serve ADR information renders the Court without personal jurisdiction where the defendant was personally served with the summons and complaint.

 

Moreover, Defendant had 30 days from the personal service on January 14, 2023, to move to quash service on the ground that Plaintiff failed to serve the ADR information. Here, Defendant did not file a motion to quash service within that period or seek a court order granting her an extension of time to April 1, 2026, to file the motion.

 

Defendant’s arguments that service by publication was inadequate are also unpersuasive. The only application for publication filed in this case was the November 21, 2022 application, which the Court denied. Therefore, an “Order for Publication” was never entered.

 

Conclusion

Defendant’s Motion to Quash is denied.  Defendant is ordered to file an Answer to the Complaint within 10 days.  The Court continues the Case Management Conference to, and issues and sets an Order to Show Cause re filing of Answer or Default of Defendant for, ________________, 2026, at 8:30AM.

Case Number: 25STCV29212    Hearing Date: July 15, 2026    Dept: 413

TENTATIVE RULING

HEARING DATE:                 Wednesday, July 15, 2026

CASE NUMBER:                   25STCV29212

CASE NAME:                        Perez v. Simplified Labor Staffing Solutions, Inc., et al.                       

MOVING PARTY:                 Defendants Simplified Labor Staffing Solutions, Inc., Maersk Logistics & Services USA Inc., and Performance Team Logistics LLC

OPPOSING PARTY:             Plaintiff Carlos Perez

PROCEEDING:                      PETITION TO COMPEL ARBITRATION

RULING SUMMARY:          The Petition to Compel Arbitration and Joinder to Petition to Compel Arbitration are denied.

Background

 

            This is an employment discrimination action arising out of Plaintiff Carlos Perez’s (“Plaintiff”) employment as a Forklift Driver at Defendants Simplified Labor Staffing Solutions, Inc. (“SLS”); Performance Team Logistics LLC (“PTL”); Maersk Logistics & Services, Inc. (“Maersk”) erroneously sued as A.P. Moller – Maersk Inc.; and DOES 1 through 20 (collectively, “Defendants”) warehouse and logistics facility.

 

On April 9, 2026, Plaintiff filed the operative First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) against Defendants, asserting causes of action for (1) Discrimination in Violation of FEHA; (2) Failure to Prevent Discrimination (FEHA); (3) Retaliation in Violation of FEHA; (4) Harassment in Violation of FEHA; (5) Failure to Prevent Harassment in Violation of FEHA; (6) Labor Code, § 98.6; (7) Labor Code, § 1102.5; (8) Labor Code, § 6310; and (9) Wrongful Termination in Violation of Public Policy. The FAC alleges that Plaintiff was forced to resign from his employment because of a hostile work environment based on sexual harassment, physical violence and intimidation, unsafe working conditions, and retaliation following Plaintiff engaging in protected activity.

 

On April 17, 2026, SLS and Maersk filed the Amended Petition to Compel Arbitration. On June 22, 2026, PTL and Maersk Warehousing & Distribution Services USA LLC[1] filed a Joinder to the Petition to Compel Arbitration.

 

motion TO COMPEL ARBITRATION

 

I.                Legal Standard

 

Generally, on a petition to compel arbitration, the court must grant the petition unless it finds either (1) no written agreement to arbitrate exists; (2) the right to compel arbitration has been waived; (3) grounds exist for revocation of the agreement; or (4) litigation is pending that may render the arbitration unnecessary or create conflicting rulings on common issues. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2; see also Condee v. Longwood Management Corp. (2001) 88 Cal.App.4th 215, 218-219.)  

 

“California has a strong public policy in favor of arbitration and any doubts regarding the arbitrability of a dispute are resolved in favor of arbitration.” (Coast Plaza Doctors Hospital v. Blue Cross of California (2000) 83 Cal.App.4th 677, 686.) “This strong policy has resulted in the general rule that arbitration should be upheld unless it can be said with assurance that an arbitration clause is not susceptible to an interpretation covering the asserted dispute.” (Ibid. [internal quotations omitted].) This is in accord with the liberal federal policy favoring arbitration agreements under the Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”), which governs all agreements to arbitrate in contracts “involving interstate commerce.” (9 U.S.C. § 2, et seq.) “Thus, under both the FAA and California law, ‘arbitration agreements are valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, save upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract.’” (Higgins v. Superior Court (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 1238, 1247, citing Armendariz v. Foundation Health Psychcare Services, Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 83, 98.)

 

II.             Discussion

 

SLS, Maersk, and PTL (hereinafter, “Defendants”) move for an order compelling Plaintiff to submit his claims against them to binding arbitration on the grounds that: (1) Plaintiff signed a written arbitration agreement; (2) Plaintiff refused to submit his claims to arbitration pursuant to the agreement; and (3) PTL is a joint employer with SLS and Maersk, making Plaintiff’s claims against PTL subject to arbitration as well.

 

Applicability of the FAA

 

The Federal Arbitration Act (the “FAA”) applies in both federal and state courts to contracts evidencing a transaction involving interstate commerce.  (9 U.S.C. §§ 1–2; Southland Corp. v. Keating (1984) 465 U.S. 1, 12.)  The FAA preempts conflicting state law. (Preston v. Ferrer (2008) 552 U.S. 346, 353; see also Mastick v. TD Ameritrade, Inc. (2012) 209 Cal.App.4th 1258, 1263.) The party that contends the FAA applies bears the burden to demonstrate that the arbitration agreement is in a “‘contract evidencing a transaction involving commerce’ . . ..”  (Woolls v. Super. Ct. (2005) 127 Cal.App.4th 197, 211.)

 

“[T]he FAA’s procedural provisions do not apply in state court unless the parties expressly adopt them.” (Valencia v. Smyth (2010) 185 Cal.App.4th 153, 177.) Essentially, “[a]arbitration under the [FAA] i[s] a matter of consent, not coercion, and the parties are generally free to structure their arbitration agreements as they see fit. Just as they may limit by contract the issues which they will arbitrate, [Citation], so too may they specify by contract the rules under which the arbitration will be conducted.” (Volt Information Sciences, Inc. v. Board of Trustees of Leland Stanford Junior University (1989) 489 U.S. 468, 469.)

 

“As an initial matter, we note that Golden State’s briefing before us contends that section 1281.98, whether preempted or not, ‘do[es] not apply because the parties unambiguously agreed [in the contract] to be bound by the FAA and JAMS’ rules.’ ” (Hohenshelt v. Superior Court (2025) 18 Cal.5th 310, 326.) “Golden State did not raise this argument in the trial court or the Court of Appeal, and neither court addressed it. Moreover, whether the parties did, or whether they may, opt out of the CAA is distinct from the issue on which Golden State sought our review — namely, ‘whether sections 1281.97 et seq. are preempted.’ ” (Id. at 327.) “Accordingly, we decline to address the belated contention that the CAA does not apply here.” (Id.)

 

Initially, Defendants argued that the FAA applies to the arbitration agreement at issue. (Mot. at 10:27-11:5.) Specifically, Defendants stated that SLS is a temporary staffing services company doing business in California and other States; has numerous office locations throughout Southern California and other States; and supplies labor and staffing to customers in California and those based outside of California. (L. Perez Decl., ¶4.) This demonstrates that Defendants are engaged in interstate commerce. However, Plaintiff asserts that he is exempt from the FAA because as a Forklift Driver he drove the forklift, loaded and unloaded, sorted, and moved goods (FAC, ¶12), thus he is a “transportation worker” per 9 U.S.C. Section 1. (Opp. at 5:24-6:2; see also Rittmann v. Amazon.com, Inc. (9th Cir. 2020) 971 F.3d 904, 915 [“Accordingly, we conclude that § 1 exempts transportation workers who are engaged in the movement of goods in interstate commerce, even if they do not cross state lines.”].)

 

In their reply brief, Defendants concede that Plaintiff is exempt from the FAA based on his status as a “transportation worker.” (Reply at 8:10-25.) As such, Defendants now contend that Plaintiff cannot invoke the Ending Forced Arbitration of Sexual Assault and Sexual Harassment Act (“EFAA”) to invalidate the arbitration agreement at issue. (Id.) The Court disagrees.

 

“[A]t the election of the person alleging conduct constituting a sexual harassment dispute or sexual assault dispute, or the named representative of a class or in a collective action alleging such conduct, no predispute arbitration agreement or predispute joint-action waiver shall be valid or enforceable with respect to a case which is filed under Federal, Tribal, or State law and relates to the sexual assault dispute or the sexual harassment dispute.” (Murrey v. Superior Ct. (2023) 87 Cal.App.5th 1223, 1234 [discussing the applicability of the Ending Forced Arbitration Act (“EFAA)]; see also Yost v. Everyrealm, Inc. (2023) 657 F.Supp.3d 563, 585 [plaintiff must plead plausible allegations of sexual harassment to invoke the EFAA].) 

 

“FEHA’s prohibition against sexual harassment includes protection from a broad range of conduct, ranging from expressly or impliedly conditioning employment benefits on submission to or tolerance of unwelcome sexual advances, to the creation of a work environment that is hostile or abusive on the basis of sex.” (Lewis v. City of Benicia (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 1519, 1524 [internal quotations omitted].) 

 

Here, the FAC pleads allegations of sexual harassment under FEHA against Defendants. Plaintiff alleges, among other things, the following:  In or about January 2022, defendants hired Plaintiff as a Forklift Driver to work at their warehouse and logistics facility located 8610 Atlantic Avenue, South Gate, CA 90280 (the “Facility”) until January 1, 2024. (FAC, ¶ 11.) During Plaintiff’s employment, a coworker named Dwayne a/k/a “Slin,” repeatedly demanded that Plaintiff translate unwelcome sexual comments and sexual language into Spanish directed at two female coworkers, which he refused. (Id., ¶ 14.) Following Plaintiff’s refusal, Slin became verbally abusive, aggressive, and engaged in physically intimidating and violent behavior toward Plaintiff in the workplace. (Id., ¶¶ 16-17.) Slin would throw and hit Plaintiff with boxes inside the shipping containers. () Plaintiff complained to Defendants’ management about the hostile work environment, sexual harassment, physical violence and intimidation. (Id., ¶ 19.) Defendants’ management knew or should have known of the sexual harassment, hostile work environment, and physical violence and intimidation but failed to investigate Plaintiff’s complaints or take any corrective action. (Id., ¶¶ 21-24.) On or about January 1, 2024, Plaintiff was forced to resign because the working conditions had become intolerable. (Id., ¶ 25.)

 

No California court has addressed whether a Plaintiff who is exempt from the FAA as a transportation worker may invoke – or, as Defendants argue, is precluded from invoking – the EFAA.  An argument can be made that the EFAA uses the phrase “this chapter,” when discussing the scope of its applicability. (9 U.S.C., § 402, subd. (b).) As such, a party seeking to compel arbitration could argue that a Plaintiff exempt from the FAA cannot invoke the EFAA to preclude arbitration their claims. On the other hand, the EFAA’s applicability is not limited to cases under the FAA because it expressly references cases filed under “Federal, Tribal, or State law.” (9 U.S.C., § 402, subd. (a).) It is undisputed that Plaintiff brings state FEHA and Labor Code violation claims against Defendants.

 

Based on legislative purpose of the EFAA to restore court access for sexual harassment and/or sexual assault survivors, California and federal courts have found the California Arbitration Act (“CAA”) to be preempted by the EFAA. (E.g., Turner v. Tesla, Inc. (N.D. Cal. 2023) 686 F.Supp.3d 917, 925 [“The EFAA, in contrast to the FAA, demonstrates Congress’s preference that plaintiffs alleging sexual harassment be permitted to be heard in court and not be compelled to arbitration.”]; Casey v. Superior Court (2025) 108 Cal.App.5th 575, 584–585 [“The EFAA’s purpose is plainly obstructed by an attempt to use state law to force a person who is alleging sexual harassment to arbitrate their dispute.”].) Therefore, Defendants assertion that the CAA does not contain any specific “carve-outs” against arbitrating FEHA claims is unavailing.

 

Compelling Plaintiff to arbitrate his claims, which are based on sexual harassment would run afoul of the purpose of the EFAA and California case law.  The Court determines that the EFAA precludes arbitration in this case

 

Conclusion

 

For the reasons explained above, Defendants Simplified Labor Staffing Solutions, Inc., Maersk Logistics & Services USA Inc., and Performance Team Logistics LLC’s Petition to Compel Arbitration and Joinder to Petition to Compel Arbitration are denied.
