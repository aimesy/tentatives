Motion to Compel Further Response to Requests for Admission; Request for Sanctions

Hearing Date: 9/4/26

CASE NO.: 24BBCV00019

CASE NAME: Richard Eugene Lund v. Edwin Arieal Subuyujzuleta

Moving Party: Plaintiff Richard Eugene Lund

Responding Party: Defendant Edwin Arieal Subuyujzuleta

Notice: Sufficient

Ruling: DENIED.

 

NOTICE

The Court is not requesting oral argument on this matter/motion.  The Court is guided by California Rules of Court, Rule 3.1308(a)(1) whereby notice of intent to appear is requested.  Unless the Court directs argument in the Tentative Ruling, no argument is requested AND ANY PARTY SEEKING ARGUMENT SHOULD NOTIFY ALL OTHER PARTIES AND THE COURT BY 4:00 P.M. ON THE COURT DAY BEFORE THE HEARING of the party’s intention to argue as to this matter/motion.  The tentative ruling will become the ruling of the court if no argument is received.   Notice may be given either by email at BurDeptB@LACourt.org or by telephone at (818) 260-8422.

Parties must appear on all other matters scheduled for this case.

BACKGROUND

This is a personal injury action stemming from a vehicle accident. On January 3, 2024, Plaintiff Richard Eugene Lund (“Plaintiff”) filed the complaint against Defendant Edwin Arieal Subuyujzuleta (“Defendant”).

On July 7, 2026, Plaintiff filed a motion to compel further response Requests for Admission (RFAs) (Res ID: 5950). On July 8, 2026, the Court denied the motion. (See 7/8/26 Minute Order.) Although the motion was denied, the matter appears on calendar, Plaintiff has not moved for relief from this order. Accordingly, the order DENYING that motion stands.

On July 22, 2026, Plaintiff subsequently filed the instant motion seeking an order compelling further response of the same set of RFAs (Res ID: 4729). On August 24, 2026, Defendant filed opposition. On August 26, 2026, Plaintiff filed a reply.

LEGAL STANDARD

            “Unless notice of this motion is given within 45 days of the service of the verified response, or any supplemental verified response, or on or before any specific later date to which the propounding party and the responding party have agreed in writing,” the trial court lacks jurisdiction to order further responses to interrogatories and/or requests for production of documents.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.290(c); see Vidal Sassoon v. Superior Court (1983) 147 Cal.App.3d 681, 685 (45-day deadline to file motion to compel further is mandatory and jurisdictional).)

Compel Further Response to Requests for Admission

            “On receipt of a response to requests for admissions, the party requesting admissions may move for an order compelling a further response if that party deems that either or both of the following apply: (1) an answer to a particular request is evasive or incomplete, (2) an objection to a particular request is without merit or too general.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.290 (a).)

            A motion under subdivision (a) shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040 and a separate statement, required under the California Rules of Court, or a concise outline of the discovery request and each response in dispute. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.290 (b).)

 

DISCUSSION                                                                                              

Timeliness

            Here, counsel declares that supplemental responses to propounded RFAs were provided on July 13, 2026. (Halpern Decl., ¶ 6.) The instant motion was filed on July 20, 2026, 7 days after the issuance of supplemental responses. Thus, the motion is timely and conforms with the 45-day statutory deadline.

Meet and Confer

            A motion to compel further responses must be accompanied by a¿meet¿and confer declaration “showing a reasonable and good faith attempt, either in person, by telephone, or by videoconference, to informally resolve each issue presented by the motion.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2016.040, subd. (a); § 2030.300, subd. (b)(2).)

            Here, Plaintiff declares that they sent a meet and confer letter on July 15, 2026, in an effort to discuss and cure deficiencies. (Halpern Decl., ¶ 7; Halpern Add. Decl. ¶ 5.) However, Defendant argues that no meet-and-confer declaration was included in the motion as required under statute. (Opp., 3.) In reply, Plaintiff argues that the record contradicts Defendant’s argument that the meet and confer requirement was not met. (Reply, p. 3.) Plaintiff reiterates that the parties met and conferred to discuss amended responses, but Defendant did not deliver them. (Reply, p. 3.) 

Plaintiff supplied the court with a declaration detailing meet and confer efforts. Accordingly, the requirement is met.

Separate Statement

California Rules of Court, rule 3.1345 requires a separate statement to be filed in connection with a motion to compel further. Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.290(b)(2) allows a moving party to file a concise outline detailing each discovery response to be compelled further in lieu of the separate statement. Here, Plaintiff did not file a separate statement or concise outline in lieu of separate statement detailing why RFA Nos. 5, 13, 15, 16, 17, 19, 20, 23– 26, 28–32, 34–35, 37–38, 40–41 require further response.

Defendant argues that the motion should be denied for this reason, citing applicable statutory requirements. (Opp., p. 2.) In reply, Plaintiff argues that the moving papers identify the specific RFAs in dispute and reasons why further responses are warranted in compliance with the rules. (Reply, p. 3.)

Here, Plaintiff does not provide the court with a concise outline detailing each discovery response to be compelled further. Rather, Plaintiff makes general allegations. For example, Plaintiff argues that “many of these RFAs address facts that are squarely within Defendant’s personal knowledge” but does not specify which RFAs they are referencing. (Mot., p. 8.) Further, Plaintiff alleges that where deposition testimony has placed subject matter of RFAs squarely within Defendant’s knowledge and Defendant subsequently asserts inability to admit or deny them, but Plaintiff does not identify which specific RFAs they are referencing. While these allegations fall under headings grouping several RFAs, Plaintiff makes allegations of insufficiency pertaining to only some of the RFAs within that subgroup and fails to identify which ones.

Accordingly, this requirement is not satisfied, and the motion is DENIED on procedural grounds.

Sanctions

The Court shall impose sanction on the party, the person, or the attorney whose failure to serve a timely response to the request necessitated the motion to compel responses to interrogatories or RPD. (See Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2031.300(c), 2030.290(c); see also Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1348(a) [the court can award sanctions under the Discovery Act in favor of a party seeking to compel discovery even though no opposition was filed, the opposition was withdrawn, or the requested discovery was provided to the moving party after the motion was filed].)

“A request for a sanction shall, in the notice of motion, identify every person, party, and attorney against whom the sanction is sought, and specify the type of sanction sought. The notice of motion shall be supported by a memorandum of points and authorities and accompanied by a declaration setting forth facts supporting the amount of any monetary sanction sought.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.040.)

Plaintiff request imposition of sanctions in the amount of $3,000 against Defendant. (Mot., pp. 9-10.) Here, the Court will not award sanctions as the motion to compel further has been denied.

            Accordingly, request for sanctions is DENIED.

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Defendant’s’ Motion to Compel Further Response to Requests for Admission (Res ID: 4729 is DENIED.

Defendant’s Motion to Compel Further Response to Requests for Admission (Res ID: 5950 is DENIED.

Defendant’s request for sanctions is DENIED.

MOVING PARTY shall provide notice.

ON THE COURT DAY BEFORE THE HEARING of the party’s intention to argue as to this matter/motion.  The tentative ruling will become the ruling of the court if no argument is received.   Notice may be given either by email at BurDeptB@LACourt.org or by telephone at (818) 260-8422.

BACKGROUND

This is a negligence and breach of contract action. On February 5, 2026, Golden Gate Realty & Investments Inc. (Plaintiff) brought this action against defendant Toluca Village Owners Association (Defendant) due to water damage sustained to Plaintiff’s property in violation of the HOA agreements in place.

On July 22, 2026, Defendant filed the instant motions to compel initial responses to Form Interrogatories (Res ID: 3321), Special Interrogatories (Res ID: 9078), and Requests for Production of Documents (RPDs) (Res ID: 8966) served on Plaintiff.

No oppositions have been filed as of August 31, 2026. 

 

LEGAL STANDARD

A party must respond to interrogatories within 30 days after service. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.260, subd. (a).) If a party to whom interrogatories are directed does not provide timely responses, the requesting party may move for an order compelling responses to the discovery. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (b).) The party also waives the right to make any objections, including one based on privilege or work-product protection. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (a).) There is no time limit for a motion to compel responses to interrogatories other than the cut-off on hearing discovery motions 15 days before trial. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2024.020, subd. (a), 2030.290.) No meet and confer efforts are required before filing a motion to compel responses to the discovery. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290; Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pacific Healthcare Consultants (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, 411.)

A party must respond to requests for production of documents within 30 days after service. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.260, subd. (a).) If a party to whom requests for production of documents are directed does not provide timely responses, the requesting party may move for an order compelling responses to the discovery. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300, subd. (b).) The party also waives the right to make any objections, including one based on privilege or work-product protection. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300, subd. (a).) There is no time limit for a motion to compel responses to production of documents other than the cut-off on hearing discovery motions 15 days before trial. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2024.020, subd. (a), 2031.300.) No meet and confer efforts are required before filing a motion to compel responses to the discovery. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300; Sinaiko, supra, 148 Cal.App.4th at 411.)

 

DISCUSSION           

Motion to Compel Initial Responses

Defendant contends that Plaintiff failed to timely respond to Form Interrogatories, Special Interrogatories, and RPDs on or by the statutory deadline. (Mot., p. 3.)

Here, Defendant’s counsel served Plaintiff with the Form Interrogatories, Special Interrogatories, and RPDs by electronic service on November 25, 2025. (Mot. Compel Form Interrog., Koletsky Decl., ¶ 2; Mot. Compel Special Interrog., Koletsky Decl., ¶ 2; Mot., Compel RPDs, Koletsky Decl., ¶ 2.) Defendant contends that initial responses were due December 30, 2025. (Koletsky Decl., ¶ 3.) Defendant’s counsel declares that several extensions were given to Plaintiff in an effort to avoid filing these motions, but Plaintiff again failed to respond. (Koletsky Decl., ¶¶ 4-5.) On May 26, 2026, Defendant’s counsel served Plaintiff with a meet and confer letter instructing responses to be served by June 1, 2026. (Koletsky Decl., ¶ 5.) On June 3, 2026, Plaintiff emailed a link supposedly containing responses however Defendant could not access the link. (Koletsky Decl., ¶ 6.) On July 6, 2026, Defendant’s counsel met with Plaintiff’s counsel via telephone to discuss discovery responses, and Plaintiff agreed to provide discovery responses by July 24, 2026. (Koletsky Decl., ¶ 7.) As of July 22, 2026, Defendant declares that Plaintiff has not provided verified responses. (Koletsky Decl., ¶ 8.)

Since Plaintiff was properly served and failed to respond to discovery requests by the statutory deadline, Defendant is entitled to an order compelling Plaintiff to provide responses without objections to Form Interrogatories, Special Interrogatories, and RPDs.

Accordingly, the motion to compel initial responses is GRANTED.

Sanctions

The Court shall impose sanction on the party, the person, or the attorney whose failure to serve a timely response to the request necessitated the motion to compel responses to interrogatories or RPD. (See Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2031.300(c), 2030.290(c); see also Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1348(a) [the court can award sanctions under the Discovery Act in favor of a party seeking to compel discovery even though no opposition was filed, the opposition was withdrawn, or the requested discovery was provided to the moving party after the motion was filed].)

“A request for a sanction shall, in the notice of motion, identify every person, party, and attorney against whom the sanction is sought, and specify the type of sanction sought. The notice of motion shall be supported by a memorandum of points and authorities and accompanied by a declaration setting forth facts supporting the amount of any monetary sanction sought.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.040.)

Plaintiff’s failure to comply with discovery requests necessitated the motion, and the conduct is subject to sanctions. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280(c); see also Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1348(a).) Moreover, The Court finds Defendant’s Notices of Motion comply with Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.040.

Defendant’s counsel requests sanctions in the amount of $2,959.95 consisting of $986.65 for each motion. (Koletsky Decl., ¶ 9.) Defendant has filed a total of 3 motions to compel. For each motion, Defendant seeks attorney’s fees of $925 plus $184.95 ($61.65 x 12) in filing costs. (Ibid.) Requested attorney’s fees reflect 2.5 hours spent researching, preparing, and drafting this motion and 1.25 hours anticipated analyzing any opposition and drafting a reply at a rate of $185 per hour for each motion. (Ibid.)

The Court, in its discretion, will only award $555 to reflect three hours at a rate of $185 for all three motions filed against Plaintiff plus $180 filing fees for each motion ($60 x 3). All motions are substantially similar and straight forward.

Accordingly, the Court GRANTS Defendant’s request for imposition of sanctions against Defendant in the amount of $735.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Defendant Toluca Village Owners Association’s motions to compel initial responses to form interrogatories (Res ID: 3321), special interrogatories (Res ID: 9078), and RPDs (Res ID: 8966) are GRANTED.

Defendant Toluca Village Owners Association’s request for sanctions is GRANTED in the amount of $735, to be paid by Plaintiff to Defendant within 30 days from this ruling.

LEGAL STANDARD

Court approval is required for all settlements of a minor’s claim or that of a person lacking the capacity to make decisions.  (Prob. Code, §§ 2504, 3500, 3600 et seq.; Code Civ. Proc., § 372; see Pearson v. Superior Court (2012) 202 Cal.App.4th 1333, 1337.)

“[T]he protective role the court generally assumes in cases involving minors, [is] a role to assure that whatever is done is in the minor’s best interests . . . .  [I]ts primary concern is whether the compromise is sufficient to provide for the minor’s injuries, care and treatment.”  (Goldberg v. Superior Court (1994) 23 Cal.App.4th 1378, 1382.) 

A petition for court approval of a compromise or covenant not to sue under Code of Civil Procedure section 372 must comply with California Rules of Court Rules 7.950, 7.951, and 7.952.  The petition must be verified by the petitioner and contain a full disclosure of all information that has “any bearing upon the reasonableness” of the compromise or the covenant.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.950.)  The person compromising the claim on behalf of the minor or person who lacks capacity, and the represented person, must attend the hearing on compromise of the claim unless the court for good cause dispenses with their personal appearance.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.952(a).)

An order for deposit of funds of a minor or person lacking decision-making capacity and a petition for the withdrawal of such funds must comply with California Rules of Court Rules 7.953 and 7.954.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1384; see also Super. Ct. L.A. County, Local Rules, rules 4.115-4.118.)

Rules of Court, rules 7.950, 7.950.5, 7.591 and 7.952].) Petitioner uses Judicial Council form MC-350 for the petitions and presents the Court with proposed orders approving compromise of the disputed claim.

The Petition is thus procedurally proper.

Substantive Requirements

The petitions show that Minor Claimants Vahagn Asaduriyan and Nairi Asaduriyan settled with Defendant for total amount of $31,000 each.

Vahagn Asaduriyan

The Court notes the terms and conditions of the annuity submitted with the petition expired, at latest, March 2, 2026. (Vahagn Pet., Attachment 18(b)(3).)

The Court finds the settlements are fair, reasonable and in the best interests of Minor Claimant.  Further, the Court finds that the declaration of Marine Davtyan is sufficient to support the request for attorney’s fees in the amount of $7,750 (representing 25 percent of the gross $31,000 settlement).  (Vahagn Pet., Attachment 13a, Davtyan Decl., ¶¶ 15-17.)

However, the Court has not been given current and accurate terms and conditions of the annuity.

The Court notes the terms and conditions of the annuity submitted with the petition expired, at latest, March 2, 2026. (Nairi Pet., Attachment 18(b)(3).)

Hearing Date: 9/4/26

CASE NO.: 25NNCV00448

Moving Party: Plaintiff Talmadge Morning Jr.

Responding Party: Unopposed

Notice: Sufficient

NOTICE

The Court is not requesting oral argument on this matter/motion.  The Court is guided by California Rules of Court, Rule 3.1308(a)(1) whereby notice of intent to appear is requested.  Unless the Court directs argument in the Tentative Ruling, no argument is requested and ANY PARTY SEEKING ARGUMENT SHOULD NOTIFY ALL OTHER PARTIES AND THE COURT BY 4:00 P.M. ON THE COURT DAY BEFORE THE HEARING of the party’s intention to argue as to this matter/motion.  The tentative ruling will become the ruling of the court if no argument is received.   Notice may be given either by email at BurDeptB@LACourt.org or by telephone at (818) 260-8422.

Parties must appear on all other matters scheduled for this case.

BACKGROUND

This is a property dispute partition action. The subject property, located at 629 E. Jackson Street, Pasadena, CA 91104 (the “Property”), is currently co-owned in equal one-third interests by plaintiff Talmadge Morning Jr. (Plaintiff) and Staci Morning and Kimberly Morning (collectively, Defendants). The Property has an estimated value of $1,250,000.00.

On January 26, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion seeking interim relief and a preliminary injunction. No opposition has been filed as of September 1, 2026.

 

 

LEGAL STANDARD

            Pursuant to California Code of Civil Procedure § 872.120, in a partition action the Court “may hear and determine all motions, reports, and accounts and may make any decrees and orders necessary or incidental to carrying out the purposes of this title and to effectuating its decrees and orders”. (Code Civ. Proc., § 872.120.)

            The court may issue temporary restraining orders and injunctions, with or without bond, for the purpose of: (a) preventing waste; (b) protecting the property or title thereto; (c) restraining unlawful interference with a partition of the property ordered by the court. (Code Civ. Proc., § 872.130.)

The purpose of a preliminary injunction is to preserve the¿status quo¿and prevent irreparable harm pending a trial on the merits.¿(White v. Davis¿(2003) 30 Cal.4th 528, 554.) To obtain a¿preliminary injunction, a moving party must show: (1) they would likely suffer an irreparable injury for which damages would be an inadequate remedy if the injunction were denied; and (2) there exists a responsible probability that she will prevail on the merits of her claims.¿(Huong¿Que, Inc. v. Luu¿(2007) 150 Cal.App.4th 400, 408.)

 

DISCUSSION                                                                                              

Plaintiff seeks an order (1) prohibiting any new occupants from residing at the subject property without the written consent of all co-owners and court approval; (2) requiring that all rental agreements for the subject property be in writing, signed by all co-owners, and filed with the Court; (3) requiring monthly reporting of all rent collected and expenses incurred with supporting documentation; (4) reallocating property expenses proportionally based on each co-owner’s percentage ownership interest; (5) appointing a qualified referee with full management authority over the subject property, including leasing, maintenance, and financial decisions, with Court oversight; (6) establishing emergency procedures authorizing the referee to spend up to $5,000 for emergency repairs with immediate Court notification; and (7) implementing the requested relief within 30 days with monthly Court review. (Mot., p. 2.)

Plaintiff asks the Court to exercise its broad authority under the Partition of Real Property Act to grant interim relief to prevent waste, deterioration, and mismanagement of the subject property pending final resolution of this partition action and issue a preliminary injunction. (Mot., p. 3.) Plaintiff argues that the property is currently being mismanaged by Defendants and they have allowed their relatives who are unauthorized occupants to reside at the Property without Plaintiff’s consent or any rental agreements in place. (Ibid.) Further, Plaintiff argues the property has resulting physical deterioration, safety violations, and code violations. (Ibid.) Plaintiff maintains that without Court intervention, the property will continue to deteriorate resulting in diminution of value and potential liability for co-owners.

However, although Plaintiff states they satisfy a likelihood of success on the merits, Plaintiff has not made an argument showing the Court that there is a likelihood of success here. Thus, Plaintiff has not satisfied both elements required to implement a preliminary injunction.

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s motion is DENIED.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Plaintiff Talmadge Morning’s motion for preliminary injunction is DENIED.

BACKGROUND

This is a breach of contract action. On March 24, 2025, plaintiff Maria Allahverdian filed a Complaint against defendant Estes Express Lines Corporation for incomplete and damaged delivery of a Chandelier. Plaintiff asserted eight causes of action including both breach of contract and tort claims.

On May 27, 2026, Defendant filed the instant demurrer. Defendant also references a motion to strike which was not filed with the Court.

On August 24, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition to the demurrer and motion to strike. On August 28, 2026, Defendant filed a reply.

The Court will only substantively address the demurrer, as moving papers regarding the motion to strike were not filed with the Court.

 

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

            Plaintiff requests judicial notice of Exhibit: (1) Document: Order Granting Plaintiff’s Motion to Remand and Denying Defendant’s Motion to Dismiss. The request is granted.  (Evid. Code, §452(d).)

 

LEGAL STANDARD

            “[A] demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the allegations in a complaint.” (Lewis v. Safeway, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 385, 388.) A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. (See Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994 [in ruling on a demurrer, a court may not consider declarations, matters not subject to judicial notice, or documents not accepted for the truth of their contents].) For purposes of ruling on a demurrer, all facts pleaded in a complaint are assumed to be true, but the reviewing court does not assume the truth of conclusions of law. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hosp. Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 967.)

Further, the court may, upon motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a).) The court may also strike all, or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (b).) The grounds for a motion to strike are that the pleading has irrelevant, false, or improper matter, or has not been drawn or filed in conformity with laws. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437.)

Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (See Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349 [court shall not “sustain a demurrer without leave to amend if there is any reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment”]; Kong v. City of Hawaiian Gardens Redevelopment Agency (2002) 108 Cal.App.4th 1028, 1037 [“A demurrer should not be sustained without leave to amend if the complaint, liberally construed, can state a cause of action under any theory or if there is a reasonable possibility the defect can be cured by amendment.”]; Vaccaro v. Kaiman (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 761, 768 [“When the defect which justifies striking a complaint is capable of cure, the court should allow leave to amend.”].) The burden is on the complainant to show the Court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

 

DISCUSSION

Meet and Confer

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41 requires that before the filing of a demurrer the moving party “shall meet and confer in person or by telephone” with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer.  (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41(a).)  The parties are to meet and confer at least five days before the date the responsive pleading is due. (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41(a)(2).)  Thereafter, the moving party shall file and serve a declaration detailing their meet and confer efforts. (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41(a)(3).) 

Defendant’s counsel declares they sent Plaintiff’s counsel a meet and confer letter on April 15, 2026, and subsequently met and conferred on April 23, 2026, via telephone but were unable to come to an agreement. (Lucas Decl., ¶¶ 2-4.) Plaintiff’s counsel confirms compliance with meet and confer requirements. (Allahverdian Decl., ¶ 4.)

Based on declarations of parties’ counsel, the parties have complied with meet and confer requirements.

Demurrer       

Defendant demurs to the first cause of action for conversion, second cause of action for negligence, fourth cause of action for breach of implied covenant, fifth cause of action for unfair business practices, sixth cause of action for fraudulent deceit, seventh cause of action for intentional misrepresentation (fraud), and eighth cause of action for negligent misrepresentation. (Dem., pp. 2-3.) Defendant demurrers on the grounds that (1) Plaintiff’s claims are preempted by federal law because they directly relate to Defendant’s services as a motor carrier, and (2) they are barred by California’s economic loss rule. (Memo., p. 2.)

FAAAA Affirmative Defense

The Federal Aviation Administration Authorization Act (“FAAAA”) provides that states may not enact or enforce any law related to a price, route, or service of any motor carrier, broker, or freight forwarder with respect to the transportation of property. (49 U.S.C. § 14501(c)(1).) However, this provision does not apply to the intrastate transportation of household goods. (49 U.S.C. § 14501 (c)(2)(B).)

Defendant argues that courts have consistently held that claims based on delivery timing, handling, damage, and claims processing are preempted because they seek to impose state-law requirements on cross-country motor carriers. (Memo., p. 5.) Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s claims fall squarely within the FAAAA preemption because defendant agreed to transport and deliver goods, failed to timely deliver the shipment, the goods were delivered in a damaged condition, and defendant allegedly denied or mishandled the claim. (Ibid.) Defendant also argues that no exceptions apply. (Memo., p. 6.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that nothing under the FAAAA completely bars their claims, and any demurrer based on an affirmative defense can only properly be sustained if the claim is barred, whereas here it is not. (Opp., p. 6.) Further, Defendant argues that the FAAAA does not preempt their claims because preemption does not reach state actions that only affect carrier services in a tenuous, remote, or peripheral manner. (Opp., p. 7.) Plaintiff argues that preemption under the FAAAA does not apply to household goods, and the chandelier in the instant case is a household item, purchased and delivered to a private residence. (Opp., p. 8.) Lastly, Plaintiff argues that a federal court already ruled on the applicability of preemption and remanded the case back to State Court, holding that state law claims were not completely preempted. (Ibid.)

In reply, Defendant reiterates that Plaintiff’s claims fall squarely within the FAAAA because they directly relate to a motor carrier delivering the chandelier. (Reply, p. 2.) Further, Defendant argues that the Federal Court only addressed complete preemption and not ordinary preemption as an affirmative defense. (Reply, pp. 2-3.) Lastly, Defendant argues that Plaintiff cannot establish the household goods exception by simply stating the chandelier was meant for residential use. (Reply, p. 4.)

A demurrer based on an affirmative defense "cannot properly be sustained where the action might be barred by the defense, but is not necessarily barred."(CrossTalk Prods., Inc. v. Jacobson, (1998) 65 Cal. App. 4th 631.) As previously stated, an exception to preemption under the FAAAA is interstate transportation of household goods. (49 U.S.C. § 14501 (c)(2)(B).) Defendant’s affirmative defense requires a factual inquiry which cannot be determined on demurrer. Further, on the face of the pleadings, this affirmative defense does not lead the Court to conclude Plaintiff has failed to state a claim, and that this affirmative defense completely bars all of Plaintiff’s allegations.

Accordingly, Defendant’s demurrer to the first, second, fourth, fifth, sixth, seventh, and eighth causes of action on the grounds of affirmative defense is OVERRULED.

Economic Loss Rule

There is no recovery in tort for purely economic loss “unless two conditions are satisfied.” (Rattagan v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1, 20.) A plaintiff must show that defendant's conduct “violated a duty that is independent of the duties and rights assumed by the parties when they entered the contract; and second, defendant's conduct must have caused injury to persons or property that was not reasonably contemplated by the parties when the contract was formed.” (Rattagan, supra, 17 Cal.5th at pp. 20-21.)

Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s tort claims, including the first, second, sixth, seventh, and eighth causes of action, fail under the economic loss rule. (Memo., p. 6.) Further, Defendant argues that Plaintiff has not alleged duty or loss outside economic loss resulting from duties that arose under contract. (Ibid.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that the economic loss rule does not bar recovery because the case involved property damage, not purely economic loss. (Opp., p. 9.) Further, Defendant argues that pursuant to Rattagan, the economic loss rule does not bar claims of fraud, and that conversion is based on an absolute duty arising outside of the contract. (Opp., p. 9-10.)

In reply, Defendant argues that Rattagan actually held that an intentional fraud claim arising during a contractual relationship may proceed only if its elements can be established independently of the parties’ contractual rights and obligations, and if the conduct exposed the plaintiff to a risk of harm beyond the parties’ reasonable contemplation at formation. (Reply, p. 5; Rattagan, supra, 17 Cal.5th at 38-39, 44.) Further, Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s fraud claims are insufficiently pled as they allege only a broken future contractual promise not intentional misrepresentation of an existing fact. (Reply, pp. 5-6.)

Here, Plaintiff’s first cause of action for conversion does not assert duty arising outside the contractual agreement and is based on economic loss suffered as a result of the alleged breach. (Comp., ¶¶ 34-39.) Plaintiff’s second cause of action for negligence specifically asserts duty arising from contract and damages incurred as a result of the alleged breach. (Comp., ¶¶ 40-43.) Thus, Plaintiff’s causes of action for conversion and negligence are barred by the economic loss rule.

With regard to fraud under the economic loss rule, “[a] plaintiff may assert a tort claim for fraudulent concealment based on conduct occurring in the course of a contractual relationship, if the elements of the cause of action can be established independently of the parties’ contractual rights and obligations and the tortious conduct exposes the plaintiff to a risk of harm beyond the reasonable contemplation of the parties when they entered into the agreement.” (Rattagan, supra, 17 Cal.5th at, 38.) “The elements of fraud are (a) a misrepresentation (false representation, concealment, or nondisclosure); (b) scienter or knowledge of its falsity; (c) intent to induce reliance; (d) justifiable reliance; and (e) resulting damage.” (Hinesley v. Oakshade Town Ctr. (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 289, 294.) The facts constituting the alleged fraud must be alleged factually and specifically as to every element of fraud, as the policy of “liberal construction” of the pleadings will not ordinarily be invoked. (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 645.)

Here, Plaintiff cites damages incurred as a result of the damaged chandelier, which was within reasonable contemplation of the parties when they entered the agreement, for the sixth, seventh, and eighth causes of action for fraudulent deceit, fraud, and negligent misrepresentation respectively. Moreover, Plaintiff alleges justifiable reliance as it relates to the contractual obligations of Defendant to deliver the Chandelier. Thus, Plaintiff’s fraud claims are not established independent of the parties contractual obligations and are barred by the economic loss rule.

Accordingly, Defendant’s demurrer to the first, second, sixth, seventh, and eighth causes of action on the grounds of the economic loss rule is SUSTAINED with leave to amend.

Motion to Strike

From Plaintiff’s opposition, and Defendant’s reply, the court gathers that Defendant intends to move to strike Plaintiff’s prayer for punitive damages. However, moving papers regarding the motion to strike were not filed with the Court.

Accordingly, the Court will DENY Defendant’s motion to strike.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Defendant’s demurrer as to the first, second, sixth, seventh, and eighth causes of action on the grounds of the economic loss rule is SUSTAINED with leave to amend.

Defendant’s demurrer as to the fourth and fifth causes of action is OVERRULED.

Plaintiff’s motion to strike punitive damages is DENIED.

BACKGROUND

On September 4, 2025, plaintiff Virgel Standfill (Plaintiff) filed a First Amended Complaint against defendants Southern California Edison Company (Defendant) and Edison International alleging their electrical meter (the Meter) caused an explosion which severely harmed Plaintiff.  

On May 15, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion seeking Court order compelling destructive testing of the Meter at issue. On August 24, 2026, Defendant filed an opposition. On August 28, 2026, Plaintiff filed a reply.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

“Any party may obtain discovery within the scope delimited by Chapter 2 (commencing with Section 2017.010), and subject to the restrictions set forth in Chapter 5 (commencing with Section 2019.010), by inspecting, copying, testing, or sampling documents, tangible things, land or other property, and electronically stored information in the possession, custody, or control of any other party to the action”. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.010(a).)

“Unless otherwise limited by order of the court in accordance with this title, any party may obtain discovery regarding any matter”, not privileged, that is (1) relevant to the subject matter involved in the pending action and (2) appears reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2017.010.)

“A party may demand that any other party produce and permit the party making the demand, or someone acting on the demanding party’s behalf, to inspect and to photograph, test, or sample any tangible things that are in the possession, custody, or control of the party on whom the demand is made”. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.010(c).)

The court shall restrict the frequency or extent of use of a discovery method provided in Section 2019.010 if it determines that…the selected method of discovery is unduly burdensome or expensive, taking into account the needs of the case, the amount in controversy, and the importance of the issues at stake in the litigation. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2019.030(a)(2).)

 

DISCUSSION           

Motion to Compel Destructive Test

            Plaintiff seeks a motion compelling Defendant to produce an electrical meter (the Meter) which exploded, severely burning Plaintiff. (Mot., p. 5.) Plaintiff argues that good cause exists for compelling production of the meter for destructive testing because the meter is central evidence and internal examination of the meter directly relates to the causation and/or origin of the incident and potential defect of the meter. (Ibid.) Whether internal components show electrical failure, arcing, heat damage, terminal deformation, conductor failure, contamination, debris paths, melting, or other failure evidence, Plaintiff argues it is directly relevant to their claims and Defendant’s defenses. (Ibid.) Plaintiff argues that alternative inspections, i.e. visual inspection or CT imaging, are useful but do not allow full direct inspection of debris patterns and all the internal parts. (Ibid.) Plaintiff argues that Destructive testing of devices involved in electrical incidents is commonplace, and Courts recognize that destructive testing is an appropriate discovery tool in cases involving unique physical evidence. (Mot., pp. 4-5.) Plaintiff anticipates Defendant will argue that the proposed testing is not essential or properly tailored; and Plaintiff argues that the destructive testing is tailored to seek the cause of the explosion and Plaintiff need not prove the test is essential, only that it is relevant and reasonably calculated. (Mot., pp. 6-7.)

            In opposition, Defendant argues that the demand for destructive testing is unnecessary and redundant since Plaintiff has already conducted two nondestructive examinations and the test is not essential to resolve a genuinely disputed material issue. (Opp., p. 2.) Defendant argues that the cause of the explosion was an electrical panel, not the meter. (Ibid.) Further, Defendant argues that destructive testing will permanently destroy unique and central evidence resulting in prejudice by not allowing Defendant to subsequently examine the Meter. (Ibid.) Defendant then turns to argue against Plaintiff’s purported expert, Dr. Palmer, who advocated for destructive testing. (Mot., p. 6.) Defendant argues that their expert, Dr. Pena, has far more experience and maintains that CT scan provided enough information to aid in the causation inquiry without causing the significant prejudice of destructive testing. (Mot., pp. 6-7.) Defendant also points out that the electrical panel has not been tested. (Mot., p. 7.) Thus, Defendant argues Plaintiff has not shown good cause for destructive testing. (Mot., p. 9.)

            In reply, Plaintiff reiterates that the standard is relevance and good cause, not a showing that the method of testing is essential and the last resort. (Reply, p. 1.) Plaintiff also disputes Defendant’s argument that the CT scan can provide everything the destructive test can provide. (Ibid.) Lastly, Plaintiff argues that Defendant’s attack on Dr. Palmer’s credibility is legally irrelevant since it is based on an opinion provided on another case eight years ago based on a completely different set of facts and circumstances. (Reply, p. 5.) In that case, parties sought destructive testing of a drill when the explosion occurred in conduction system called a “bus duct” and did not originate in the drill. (Ibid.) Further, Plaintiff argues credibility and qualifications of experts are for the jury to decide, not a basis to deny discovery. (Ibid.)

            Here, the Court is inclined to agree with Plaintiff. The court finds testing relevant to the subject matter of the action. The Court finds that physical examination of the Meter’s internal pieces and patterns is relevant to the cause of the explosion because it is disputed where the explosion originated from. While Defendant primarily denies that the explosion originated from the Meter, Defendant also maintains that the Meter is central evidence. (Opp., p. 2.) The cause of the explosion is still at issue, and a test examining the internal workings of the Meter accused of causing the fire is directly relevant.

Further, the Court finds that the destructive testing appears reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence. Dr. Palmer describes, investigating soot deposits, thermal discoloration, and physical results of the explosion not apparent on CT scan would be beneficial to the factual inquiry. (Palmer Decl., ¶ 15.) Dr. Pena confirms CT scans can create a 3-D physical rendering including structures, wall thickness, and possible defects. (Pena Decl., ¶ 6.) However, the Court finds that evidence including soot deposits and thermal discoloration are helpful to the inquiry. Further, the Court finds Defendant’s attempt to attack Dr. Palmer’s position unfounded as it was based on an entirely different case with unique and different facts.

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s motion to compel destructive testing is GRANTED.

Proc., § 2031.310(h).)

“A request for a sanction shall, in the notice of motion, identify every person, party, and attorney against whom the sanction is sought, and specify the type of sanction sought. The notice of motion shall be supported by a memorandum of points and authorities and accompanied by a declaration setting forth facts supporting the amount of any monetary sanction sought.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.040.)

Defendants’ failure to comply with discovery requests necessitated the motion, and the conduct is subject to sanctions. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280(c); see also Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1348(a).) Moreover, The Court finds Plaintiff’s Notice of Motion complies with Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.040.

Here, the Court in its discretion will reduce sanctions to $1,000, reflecting 2 hours at a rate of $500 per hour.

Accordingly, the Court GRANTS Plaintiff’s request for imposition of sanctions against Defendant in the amount of $1,000.

BACKGROUND

This is a breach of contract case. On June 23, 2025, plaintiff Golden Bank, N.A. (Plaintiff) filed a Complaint against defendants Charioton International, LLC, Feifei Lu, and Yanpeng Zhang (collectively, Defendants) for defaulting on a loan agreement. On February 9, 2026, default judgment was entered in favor of Plaintiff and against all Defendants.

On March 2, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion seeking payment of attorney’s fees and costs pursuant to the loan agreement and as a matter of law. No opposition has been filed as of August 31, 2026.  

 

LEGAL STANDARD

A prevailing party is entitled to recover costs, including attorneys’ fees, as a matter of right. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 1032(a)(4), 1032(b), 1033.5.)¿¿

The fee setting inquiry in California ordinarily begins with the “lodestar” method, i.e., the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate. A computation of time spent on a case and the reasonable value of that time is fundamental to a determination of an appropriate attorneys’ fee award. The lodestar figure may then be adjusted, based on consideration of factors specific to the case, in order to fix the fee at the fair market value for the legal services provided. (Serrano v. Priest (1977) 20 Cal.3d 25, 49.)¿ Such an approach anchors the trial court’s analysis to an objective determination of the value of the attorney’s services, ensuring that the amount awarded is not arbitrary. (Id. at 48, n.23.) After the trial court has performed the lodestar calculations, it shall consider whether the total award calculated under all of the circumstances of the case is more than a reasonable amount and, if so, shall reduce the section 1717 award so that it is a reasonable figure.¿ (PLCM Group v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095-96.)¿¿

The factors considered in determining the modification of the lodestar include the nature and difficulty of the litigation, the amount of money involved, the skill required and employed to handle the case, the attention given, the success or failure, and other circumstances in the case. (EnPalm, LLC v. Teitler Family Trust (2008) 162 Cal. App. 4th 770, 774 (emphasis in original).) A negative modifier was appropriate when duplicative work had been performed. (Thayer v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2001) 92 Cal.App.4th 819.)¿¿

“The reasonable hourly rate is that prevailing in the community for similar work.” (PLCM Group v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095.) “The experienced trial judge is the best judge of the value of professional services rendered in [her] court.” (Ibid.) “A trial court assessing attorney fees begins with a touchstone or lodestar figure, based on the ‘careful compilation of the time spent and reasonable hourly compensation of each attorney ... involved in the presentation of the case.” (Christian Research Institute v. Alnor (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 1315, 1321.) “The reasonableness of attorney fees is within the discretion of the trial court, to be determined from a consideration of such as the nature of the litigation, the complexity of the issues, the experience and expertise of counsel and the amount of time involved. The court may also consider whether the amount requested is based upon unnecessary or duplicative work.” (Wilkerson v. Sullivan (2002) 99 Cal.App.4th 443, 448.)

 

DISCUSSION                                                                                              

Plaintiff seeks to recover attorney fees and reimbursement costs from Defendants pursuant to the loan agreement and as a matter of law, as Plaintiff has won its claims. (Mot., p. 1.) Plaintiff seeks a total amount of $16,905.96 consisting of (1) $13,140.96 in attorney’s fees pursuant to local rule 3.214, and (2) additional attorney’s fees at a lodestar figure of $3,765 for bringing the instant motion. (Ibid.) Plaintiff does not seek a multiplier or costs.

Prevailing Party

Plaintiff argues they are entitled to attorney’s fees because they have been deemed the prevailing party and judgment was entered in Plaintiff’s favor. (Mot., p. 3.) Plaintiff argues that the terms of the loan agreement at issue provides that the Lender (Plaintiff) be awarded attorney’s fees in connection with the collection of the loan. (Mot., p. 2.)

The Court finds that Plaintiff is entitled to recover attorney’s fees and costs from Defendants pursuant to Civil Code section 1717 and the default judgment.  (Civ. Code, § 1717(a) [“In any action on a contract, where the contract specifically provides that attorney’s fees and costs, which are incurred to enforce that contract, shall be awarded either to one of the parties or to the prevailing party, then the party who is determined to be the party prevailing on the contract…shall be entitled to reasonable attorney’s fees in addition to other costs”].) The loan agreement at issue does provide for collection of attorney’s fees and costs in connection with enforcement, i.e. collection, of the loan. (Comp., Exh. 1, ¶4.12.)

Accordingly, Plaintiff is the prevailing party and entitled to recovery of attorney’s fees.

Local Rule, 3.214

             Plaintiff argues that they are entitled to fees in the amount of $13,140.96. (Mot., p. 4.) Plaintiff reasons that that because they have been awarded default judgment and damages of $1,125,095.89, they are entitled to an award of $2,890 plus 1% of the excess over $100,000 pursuant to Los Angeles Local Rules, Rule 3.214. (Ibid.)

            Pursuant to Rule 3.214(a) table for contract disputes ending in default, a prevailing party awarded judgment over $100,000 is entitled to $2,890 plus 1% of the excess over $100,000. (Local Rules, Rule 3.214(a).)

            Here, 1% of the excess judgment over $100,000 is $10,250.96. Thus, pursuant to Local Rules, Plaintiff is entitled to $13,140.96 ($10,250.96 + $2,890).

Lodestar

In addition to local rule fees, Plaintiff seeks attorney’s fees for the instant motion at a lodestar figure of $3,765.

Hourly Rates

Plaintiff seeks an hourly rate for the following individuals at: (1) $750/hour for attorney Yong; (2) $195/hour for unnamed paralegal. (Yong Decl., ¶ 9.) Plaintiff’s primary support for Counsel’s billing rate is their experience in the field. (Yong Decl., ¶¶ 10-12.)

The Court has considered the declarations. (Kaufman Decl., ¶¶ 4, 6-9, 13; Hunt Decl., ¶¶ 7-9.) Based on the Court’s experience and in light of the straightforward nature of the motion, the Court finds it appropriate to reduce the requested fees as follows: 1) $500/hour for attorney Yong; (2) $195/hour for unnamed paralegal.

Hours Expended

Plaintiff seeks a lodestar figure of $3,765.00 ((2.5 hours x $750 rate = $1,875 for attorney Yong) + (2.0 hours x $195 rate = $390 for paralegal)) for a total of 4.5 hours of work. (Yong Decl., ¶ 13.)

Plaintiff does not provide billing records and does not provide a great deal of argument surrounding the requested fees. Due to the straightforward nature of Plaintiff’s motion and the absence of an opposition, the Court will reduce requested fees to $570.00 ((0.5 hour x $750 attorney rate) + (1 hour x 195 paralegal rate)).

Accordingly, the Court finds a lodestar figure of $570 reasonable.

Summary

Therefore, Plaintiff is awarded attorney’s fees in the amount of $13,140.96 pursuant to local rules and $570 for the instant motion for a total sum of $13,710.96. Accordingly, Plaintiff’s Motion for Attorney’s Fees and Costs is GRANTED.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Plaintiff Golden Bank, N.A.’s motion for attorney’s fees is GRANTED

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

LEGAL STANDARD

The court may, upon motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a).) The court may also strike all, or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (b).) The grounds for a motion to strike are that the pleading has irrelevant, false, or improper matter, or has not been drawn or filed in conformity with laws. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) DISCUSSION                                                                                              

Defendants move to strike punitive damages as to Defendants Elliman and Douglas. (Mot., p. 2.) Defendants also move to strike allegations regarding unrelated litigation of Defendants. (Ibid.)

Punitive Damages

Punitive damages may be recovered upon a proper showing of malice, fraud, or oppression. (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (a).) “Fraud” is an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known by defendant, with intent to deprive a person of property, rights or otherwise cause injury. (Turman v. Turning Point of Cent. Cal., Inc. (2010) 191 Cal.App.4th 53, 63.) Conclusory allegations, devoid of any factual assertions, are insufficient to support a conclusion that parties acted with oppression, fraud or malice. (Smith v. Superior Court (1992) 10 Cal.App.4th 1033, 1042.)

“An employer shall not be liable for damages pursuant to subdivision (a), based upon acts of an employee of the employer, unless the employer had advance knowledge of the unfitness of the employee and employed him or her with a conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others or authorized or ratified the wrongful conduct for which the damages are awarded or was personally guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice. With respect to a corporate employer, the advance knowledge and conscious disregard, authorization, ratification or act of oppression, fraud, or malice must be on the part of an officer, director, or managing agent of the corporation.” (Civ. Code, § 3294(b).)

Defendants move to strike prayer for punitive damages at FAC paragraph 37, page 12, line 6 through 7 and page 14, line 3. (Mot., p. 4.) Defendants argue that the FAC fails to plead ratification or authorization of an agent to justify seeking punitive damages against Defendants corporation. (Memo., p. 5.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that the FAC properly alleges Defendants ratified and authorized the conduct of their employee who allegedly acted with malice, oppression, and fraudulently. (Opp., pp. 9-10.) Further, Plaintiff argues that the Court already found prayer for punitive damages based on the alleged conduct of Defendants’ employee was proper. (Opp., pp. 7-8.)

In reply, Defendants argue that the FAC responded to previous demurrer and motion to strike by adding statutory language to make general allegations and does not plead operative facts. (Reply, p. 5.)

Here, The FAC alleges that employee of Defendants, against whom the court found prayer for punitive damages was proper, was an officer, director, and/or managing agent of Elliman, and that an officer, director, and/or managing agent had actual knowledge of Carswell’s malicious, oppressive, and fraudulent conduct, and authorized it, ratified it after learning of it, and/or permitted it through corporate policies, practices, incentive structures, or failures of supervision. (FAC, ¶ 37; Exh. A, p. 7.) At the demurrer stage this allegation is enough to survive a motion to strike. The fact that Plaintiff uses language tracking statute does not alone give rise to striking the allegations. The parties are reminded that Plaintiff must prove these allegations in order to actually recover.

Accordingly, Defendants’ motion to strike prayer for punitive damages is DENIED.

Irrelevant and Improper Matter

The court may…at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436 (a).)

“An immaterial allegation in a pleading is any of the following: (1) an allegation that is not essential to the statement of a claim or defense; (2) an allegation that is neither pertinent to nor supported by an otherwise sufficient claim or defense; (3) a demand for judgment requesting relief not supported by the allegations of the complaint or cross-complaint.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 431.10(b).) An “immaterial allegation” means “irrelevant matter” as that term is used in Section 436. (Code Civ. Proc., § 431.10(c).)

Defendants move to strike paragraph 24 of the FAC, page 7, line 24 through page 8, line 6, concerning unrelated litigation between Carswell and Elliman. (Mot., p. 4.) Defendants argue that these allegations serve no purpose other than to invite generalized negative inferences about Defendants based on unrelated litigation and separate disputes. (Memo., p. 8.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that their allegations are relevant because the description of Defendants is consistent with Plaintiff’s allegations that an officer, director, and/or managing agent of Elliman authorized, ratified and/or permitted Carswell’s wrongful conduct through Elliman’s “corporate policies, practices, incentive structures, or failures of supervision.” (Opp., p. 14.) Further, Plaintiff argues that the allegations involving prior lawsuits are judicial admissions made by Defendant Carswell in his publicly filed complaint against Defendant Elliman. (Opp., p. 13.)

In reply, Defendants argue that judicial admissions made in unrelated cases may become evidence but are not binding judicial admissions. (Reply, p. 10.) Further, Defendants argue that the allegations do not support ratification or authorizing conduct as it relates to seeking punitive damages. (Ibid.) Defendants argue that “profit-oriented corporate culture” does not mean Defendants encouraged brokers to misrepresent to buyers. (Ibid.)

Here, the statements at issue are offered to support the allegation that Defendant promoted a corporate culture that encouraged profits over people. (FAC, ¶ 24.) Plaintiff alleges that these statements support the cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty against Defendant Elliman, a corporation, by showing that this misconduct is a corporate practice, rather than an isolated incident attributable to one employee. (Ibid.) While these statements do not directly support allegations that Defendants ratified and authorized the conduct of their employee, the statements do support allegations of intentional breach of fiduciary duty. These statements are part of a previous judicial record and not made for improper purpose.

Accordingly, Defendants motion to strike improper matter is DENIED.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Defendants Douglas Elliman of California, Inc. and Ernie Carswell’s motion to strike is DENIED.

BACKGROUND

This is a personal injury action. On October 8, 2025, plaintiff Huanming Cen (Plaintiff) filed a Complaint against defendant Jun Sheng Liang (Defendant) arising out of a motor vehicle accident that occurred on November 22, 2024.

On November 19, 2025, Plaintiff served Defendant with discovery requests including form interrogatories, special interrogatories, requests for admission, and requests for production of documents (collectively, Discovery Requests). After two extensions, responses to Discovery Requests were due on January 23, 2026. Defendant did not respond on or by the deadline.

On February 26, 2026, Defendant filed the instant motion seeking relief from waiver of objection to Discovery Requests. As of September 1, 2026, no opposition has been filed.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

            “If a party to whom a¿demand¿for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling¿fails to serve a timely response” the party waives any objection to the requests, including one based on privilege. (Code Civ. Proc. § 2031.300.) However, a party may be relieved of that waiver if “(1) [t]he party has subsequently served a response that is in substantial compliance with [the Discovery Act]” and “(2) [t]he party’s failure to serve a timely response was the result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect.” (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2033.290(a)(1)-(2), 2031.300(a)(1)-(2), 2033.280(a)(1)-(2).) Substantial compliance means actual compliance with respect to the substance essential to every reasonable¿objective¿of the statute. (St. Mary v. Superior Court¿(2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 762, 779.)¿However, substantial compliance should not be understood as requiring actual compliance with every specific statutory requirement. (Ibid.)

 

DISCUSSION                                                                                              

Defendant seeks relief from waiver of objection to Discovery Requests due to subsequent substantial compliance. (Mot., p. 5.) Defendant argues that they served verified code-compliance responses one month after the agreed deadline to respond, in good faith. (Ibid.) Further, Defendant argues relief should be granted because failure to provide timely response was the result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect. (Mot., p. 6.) Defense counsel argues that through their paralegal, they obtained two extensions from Plaintiff and due to a language barrier, a third extension was sought. (Mot., p. 7.) However, due to an administrative breakdown and improper calendaring, Defense counsel argues that they were unaware that a third extension was not granted. (Ibid.) Defense counsel further maintains that they were under the impression that responses had been prepared and served. (Ibid.) Defense counsel argues that they only recently discovered the error because they were involved in trial but upon discovery, promptly met and conferred with Plaintiff and subsequently served responses. (Ibid.) Defense maintains that withholding response was not intentional, deliberate, or purposeful, and Plaintiff will suffer no prejudice as a result of the short delay. (Ibid.)

            Here, Defense counsel declares that on February 26, 2026, they served responses to Plaintiff’s Discovery Requests. (Hovsepian Decl., ¶ 16; Exh. 5-8.) Defense counsel served verified responses and produced all responsive documents one month after discovering the missed deadline. (Ibid.) Defendant served substantive responses and included only privilege-based objections. (Hovsepian Decl., ¶ 13.)

Further, Defense counsel declares that they were heavily engaged in trial preparation in Sahagun v. Samz, (Los Angeles Superior Court Case No. 24STLC05270) which was originally set for January 20, 2026, and continued to February 3, 2026, before it was resolved in a last-minute settlement. (Hovsepian Decl., ¶ 9.) These circumstances constitute excusable neglect under established California law, which recognizes that calendaring errors and administrative oversights may warrant relief. (Renteria v. Juvenile Justice, Department of Corrections & Rehabilitation (2006) 135 Cal.App.4th 903, 911.) (concluding that an attorney’s reliance on mistakenly calendared dates can constitute excusable neglect). Moreover, upon learning of the missed deadline, Defense counsel acted diligently and served complete responses. This quick remediation and Plaintiff’s failure to show prejudice further supports the finding of excusable neglect

Thus, Defendant has subsequently served substantially compliant responses and shown that their failure to serve a timely response was the result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect.

Accordingly, Defendant’s motion for relief from waiver of objection is GRANTED.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Defendant Jun Sheng Liang’s motion for relief from waiver of objection is GRANTED.

BACKGROUND

This is a personal injury action arising from a motor vehicle accident that occurred on December 14, 2022, with an uninsured driver. Defendant Mahmood Arzanipour (Defendant) settled the matter with the other motorists, and on March 18, 2024, sent a demand letter demanding arbitration to his own insurance company, plaintiff Mobilitas Insurance Company of Arizona (Plaintiff).

On July 13, 2026, Plaintiff filed a petition for an Uninsured Motorist Action.

            On June 3, 2026, Plaintiff sent an amended notice of deposition of Person Most Qualified (PMQ) to Defendant along with a deposition subpoena and letter to Dr. Yaghoobian, concerning billing for Defendant’s purported medical costs.

            On June 12, 2026, the deposition of Dr Yaghoobian took place.

On July 22, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion to compel further deposition of Defendant’s PMQ. On August 26, 2026, Non-Party Dr. Yaghoobian filed an opposition and Defendant separately filed joinder of that opposition. On August 27, 2026, Plaintiff filed a reply.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

            Under Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.480, if a deponent fails to answer any question or to produce any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing under the deponent’s control that is specified in the deposition notice or a deposition subpoena, the party seeking discovery may move the court for an order compelling that answer or production. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.480(a).) A motion to compel further responses to a deposition subpoena for production of business records must be made within 60 days after the completion of the record of the deposition and be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.480(b).) If the court determines that the answer or production sought is subject to discovery, it shall order that the answer be given or the production be made on the resumption of the deposition. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.480(i).)   

 

DISCUSSION                                                                                              

Timeliness

            Here, the deposition of Dr. Yaghoobian took place on June 12, 2026. (Mot., Radusic Decl., ¶ 7.) The instant motion was filed by Plaintiff on July 22, 2026, 40 days after the deposition. Thus, the motion is timely and conforms with the 60-day statutory deadline.

Separate Statement

California Rules of Court, rule 3.1345 requires a separate statement for motions to compel further responses. Plaintiff filed a separate statement in compliance with rule 3.1345(a) for the noticed motion. Accordingly, this requirement is satisfied

Meet and Confer

            A motion to compel further responses must be accompanied by a¿meet¿and confer declaration “showing a reasonable and good faith attempt, either in person, by telephone, or by videoconference, to informally resolve each issue presented by the motion.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2016.040, subd. (a); § 2030.300, subd. (b)(2).)

            Here, Plaintiff declares that they have attempted to engage in meet and confer efforts by sending a meet and confer letter to Dr. Yaghoobian on June 24, 2026. (Radusic Decl., ¶ 21.) However, Plaintiff declares that Dr. Yaghoobian has not responded, necessitating this motion. (Radusic Decl., ¶ 22.) Responding party Dr. Yaghoobian, does not acknowledge having engaged in meet and confer efforts.

One attempt at meeting and conferring is not compliant with good faith efforts. The parties are admonished to comply with meet and confer requirements in the future; however, the Court will address the motions on its merits.

Compel Further Deposition   

            Plaintiff moves the Court to compel request for further deposition and production of documents of Arash Yaghoobian, M.D. person most qualified. (Mot., pp. 2-3.) Plaintiff argues that Dr. Yaghoobian stated that he was the PMQ for the topics listed in the amended deposition notice and then refused to answer them. (Mot., p. 9.) Plaintiff argues that Dr. Yaghoobian coded his bills, created the bills, and provided them to the parties however, later claimed he did not know requested information including the factors taken into consideration when deciding to issue a lien, average amount he recovers in other Personal Injury lien cases and other cases, if he had sent patients to collections in the past, and if he was paid for procedures at issue. (Ibid.) Plaintiff argues that the information sought is relevant and of reasonable value because they seek information related to Defendant’s medical billing. (Mot., p. 10.)

            In opposition, Dr. Yaghoobian and Defendant (collectively, Respondents) argue that the topics and documents requests are overbroad, burdensome, and seek proprietary business practices and nonparty information beyond what is needed for this matter. (Opp., p. 3.) Respondents argue that Plaintiff seeks five years of cross-payer reimbursement data, fee schedules, lien factoring arrangements, collection histories, and communications. (Ibid.)  Further, Respondents argue that Plaintiff seeks generalized business analytics that a treating physician PMQ is not required to provide. (Opp., p. 6.)

            In reply, Plaintiff argues that the requests are not overburdensome, and if Dr Yaghoobian truly was not the PMQ regarding the questions asked, he should not have purported to be. (Reply, pp. 3-5.)

Here, Topics 1-6 inquire about general billing practices typical of Dr. Yaghoobian’s practice as they relate to lawsuits. (Sep. Statement, pp. 1-16.) Further, Questions 1-19 seek general billing information and practices of Dr. Yaghoobian’s practice. (Sep. Statement, pp. 16-60.) In response to Plaintiff’s separate statement detailing why further response is sought as to specific topics of deposition, Respondent Dr. Yaghoobian argues that “witnesses respond to questions, not topics”. (See Response to Separate Statement.) Further, Dr. Yaghoobian answered nearly every question (Questions 1-19) with “I don’t know” in a seemingly combative manner despite declaring himself the person most knowledgeable on all billing practices at his office. (Yaghoobian Decl., ¶ 9.) The court does not find these topics or categories overbroad and find them relevant to the action.

Plaintiff also requests Production of Documents (RPDs) pursuant to the deposition. RPDs 1-4, 7-9, and 17-20 seek documents relied upon for the deposition, general fee schedules, and the instant action. RPDs 5-6 seek communications with Defendant’s attorney’s regarding the action. RPDs 10-15, and 21-23 seek all records and billing documents for patients of the same or similar circumstances of Defendant and general lien patient documents for the past 5 years. RPD 24 asks Dr. Yaghoobian to specify where the documents requested in the RPDs above might be if not in his possession. The Court finds documents requested in RPDs, and 17-20 relevant to the instant action and not overbroad as they are tailored to the Defendant’s action. However, the Court finds that RPDs 5-6 request privileged information from communication with Defense counsel and finds that RPDs 10-15 and 21-23 are overbroad in scope as they seek information about other patients and their billing and all documents relating to lien patients for the last 5 years.

Accordingly, the motion to compel further response to deposition as to topics 1-6, questions 1-19, and RPDs 1-4, 7-9, and 17-20 is GRANTED. However, the motion to compel further response to deposition as it relates to RPDs 5-6, 10-16, and 21-23 is DENIED.
