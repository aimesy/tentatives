LEGAL STANDARD

 

            The propounding party may file motions to compel further responses to requests for production if it believes the responses received are (1) evasive, (2) incomplete, or (3) if the objections raised are meritless or overly general. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (a

The burden is on the moving party to “set forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought by the demand.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(1).)  This burden “is met simply by a fact-specific showing of relevance.” (TBG Ins. Servs. Corp. v. Superior Court (2002) 96 Cal.App.4th 443, 448.) In response to a motion to compel further responses, the respondent bears the burden of justifying any objections. (Fairmont Ins. Co. v. Superior Court (2000) 22 Cal.4th 245, 255.)

 

DISCUSSION

 

Meet and Confer

 

A motion to compel must be “accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.” (Code Civ. Proc., 2031.310 subd. (a).) Section 2016.040 states that “a meet and confer declaration in support of a motion shall state facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt at an informal resolution of each issue presented by the motion.” (Code Civ. Proc., 2016.040.)

 

Here, the parties exchanged detailed meet and confer letters regarding the subject discovery. Therefore, the Court finds that Plaintiff has satisfied the meet and confer requirements.

 

Plaintiff requests that the Court order further responses to special interrogatories nos. 47-75.

 

Privacy

 

Defendant argues that the Court should deny Plaintiffs' Motion to Compel Further Responses because the interrogatories seek the identities of nonparty patients at Defendant Kindred South Bay and their responsible parties without justifying an invasion of those individuals' constitutionally protected right to privacy. Defendant contends that this information constitutes protected health information of nonparty individuals who possess a legally protected privacy interest and a reasonable expectation of privacy, and that Plaintiffs fail the two-part inquiry the California Supreme Court articulated for privacy claims. (Hill v. National Collegiate Athletic Assn. (1994) 7 Cal.4th 1.)

 

Defendant contends that the first element is met because third-party patient information carries clear protection under both federal and state law, including the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act and the Confidentiality of Medical Information Act. (Bugarin v. ChartOne, Inc. (2006) 135 Cal.App.4th 1558, 1561.) Defendant reasons that disclosing a responsible party's identity requires access to a patient's medical information for nonmedical purposes, which the law forbids absent a lawful purpose related to medical care.

 

Defendant also argues that the second element is met because patients and their responsible parties reasonably expect that medical record information will not be disclosed or used improperly. (Hill, supra, 7 Cal.4th 1, 36, 37.) Defendant emphasizes that medical patients have a bona fide interest in the confidentiality of information that may include symptoms, family history, diagnoses, and treatment, and that the mere identification of a patient reveals that the patient required care. (County of Los Angeles v. Superior Court (2021) 65 Cal.App.5th 621, 641.)

 

Defendant argues that the third element is met because an inquiry into medical records is a serious invasion of privacy that goes to the core of concerns about the privacy of an individual's information. (Grafilo v. Soorani (2019) 41 Cal.App.5th 497, 507.) Defendant contends the interrogatories are overly expansive, spanning a two-to-three-year period beyond Decedent's admission, and that discovery is not justified merely because it might lead to admissible evidence when it intrudes on constitutionally protected areas. (Board of Trustees v. Superior Court (1981) 119 Cal.App.3d 516, 525.)

 

Defendant further argues that Plaintiffs cannot demonstrate any countervailing interest sufficient to justify the discovery, because absent showings of direct relevance, compelling need, and unavailability of alternative sources, the privacy interest prevails. (County of Los Angeles, supra, 65 Cal.App.5th 621, 653.) On relevance, Defendant argues that its claims require expert testimony establishing that its own conduct caused Decedent's injury within a reasonable medical probability, so information about the care of other patients is irrelevant and inadmissible to prove negligence. (Sanchez v. Kern Emergency Medical Transportation Corp. (2017) 8 Cal.App.5th 146, 153.)

            In opposition, Plaintiff argues that the Court should compel further responses to Special Interrogatories because the names and contact information of responsible parties are not protected medical information under HIPAA or the CMIA. Plaintiff contends that witness identification is central to discovery, that the names and addresses of third parties are not particularly sensitive and are not medical records, and that such disclosure is routine and does not violate constitutional rights.  Plaintiff contends that responsible parties are not patients, that their identities exist independently of any patient's medical condition, and that HIPAA and the CMIA protect health information rather than the bare contact information of a non-patient. (45 C.F.R. § 160.103; Civ. Code, § 56.05.) Plaintiff adds that the requests seeking patient identity, such as Special Interrogatory no. 56, seek identity rather than records or treatment details.

           

Plaintiff further argues that Defendant's reliance on the medical-records privacy cases is misplaced because each involved compelled production of records or patient identity that itself revealed the underlying medical condition, unlike the basic witness information sought here. (County of Los Angeles v. Superior Court (2021) 65 Cal.App.5th 621, 627, 628.)

 

Last, Plaintiff argues that the interrogatories are narrowly tailored and that Defendant made no evidentiary showing of burden or of a viable less-intrusive alternative. Plaintiff contends the calibrated remedy for third-party contact-information privacy is an opt-out notice and a protective order rather than withholding, that a burden objection requires an evidentiary showing Defendant did not make, and that discovery is not denied merely because responding requires work. (Belaire-West Landscape, Inc. v. Superior Court (2007) 149 Cal.App.4th 554.)  Last, Plaintiff states that Defendant's less-intrusive-means argument is circular because the witnesses cannot be deposed until their identities are disclosed.

 

            “The disclosure of the names and addresses of potential witnesses is a routine and essential part of pretrial discovery.” (Puerto v. Superior Court (2008) 158 Cal.App.4th 1242, 1249-1250.) “There is no justification for concluding disclosure of contact information, after affording affected individuals the opportunity to opt out, would entail a serious invasion of privacy.” (Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 555.)

 

Here, the discovery concerns elderly care patient information, not employment information as in Williams and Puerto. While most of the special interrogatories do not connect any medical information to the requested contact information, special interrogatories nos. 48, 52, 53, 56, 57, 61, 62, 63, 66, 67, request the contact information of patients who filed a complaint alleging failure in wound care, skin deterioration, or pressure injuries. Thus, these requests connect complaints, and thus patient names and contact information, to medical events. However, they are not a wholesale request for production of medical records as in County of Los Angeles, supra, 65 Cal.App.5th 621. (Id. at 62.) Consequently, the Court finds that under a protective order and following an opt-out mechanism similar to the one seen in Belaire-West Landscape, Inc. v. Superior Court (2007) 149 Cal.App.4th 554, the subject special interrogatories do not entail a serious invasion of privacy. (Id. at 556.) Last, the Court finds that the requests are relevant and not overbroad.

 

            Therefore, the Court grants the Motion to Compel Further Responses to Special Interrogatories pursuant to a protective order and a Belaire-West opt-out mechanism.

 


            Sanctions

 

            Plaintiff requests that the Court impose sanctions on Defendant in the amount of $3,560. However, given the privacy concerns requiring a protective order and an opt-out mechanism, the Court finds that Defendant has substantial justification for opposing the instant motion.

 

            Therefore, the Court denies the request for sanctions.

LEGAL STANDARD

 

The Federal Arbitration Act (the “FAA”) applies in both federal and state courts to contracts evidencing a transaction involving interstate commerce. (9 U.S.C. §§ 1-2; Southland Corp. v. Keating (1984) 465 U.S. 1, 12.) The FAA preempts conflicting state law. (Preston v. Ferrer (2008) 552 U.S. 346, 353.) The party that contends the FAA applies bears the burden to demonstrate that the arbitration agreement is in a “contract evidencing a transaction involving commerce.” (Woolls v. Super. Ct. (2005) 127 Cal.App.4th 197, 211.) 

 

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.2, “[o]n petition of a party to an arbitration agreement alleging the existence of a written agreement to arbitrate a controversy and that a party thereto refuses to arbitrate such controversy, the court shall order the petitioner and the respondent to arbitrate the controversy if it determines that an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists unless it determines that: (a) The right to compel arbitration has been waived by the petitioner; or (b)¿Grounds exist for the revocation of the agreement.”¿

Existence of Valid Arbitration Agreement and Claims Covered by Arbitration Clause¿ 

 

“[T]he petitioner bears the burden of proving the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by the preponderance of the evidence . . . .” (Giuliano v. Inland Empire Personnel, Inc. (2007) 149 Cal.App.4th 1276, 1284.) “In determining whether an arbitration agreement applies to a specific dispute, the court may examine only the agreement itself and the complaint filed by the party refusing arbitration [citation]. The court should attempt to give effect to the parties’ intentions, in light of the usual and ordinary meaning of the contractual language and the circumstances under which the agreement was made.” (Weeks v. Crow (1980) 113 Cal.App.3d 350, 353.) “To determine whether a contractual arbitration clause requires arbitration of a particular controversy, the controversy is first identified, and the issue is whether that controversy is within the scope of the contractual arbitration clause.” (Titolo v. Cano (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 310, 316.) “Doubts as to whether an arbitration clause applies to a particular dispute are to be resolved in favor of sending the parties to arbitration. The court should order them to arbitrate unless it is clear that the arbitration clause cannot be interpreted to cover the dispute.” (California Correctional Peace Officers Ass’n v. State (2006) 142 Cal.App.4th 198, 205.) 

 

“[A] party opposing the petition bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence any fact necessary to its defense. [Citation.] In these summary proceedings, the trial court sits as a trier of fact, weighing all the affidavits, declarations, and other documentary evidence, as well as oral testimony received at the court’s discretion, to reach a final determination.” (Giuliano, supra, 149 Cal.App.4th at p. 1284.)

 

“The burden of persuasion is always on the moving party to prove the existence of an arbitration agreement with the opposing party by a preponderance of the evidence: Because the existence of the agreement is a statutory prerequisite to granting the [motion or] petition, the [party seeking arbitration] bears the burden of proving its existence by a preponderance of the evidence.” (Gamboa v. Northeast Community Clinic (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 158, 165.) However, the burden is a three-step framework. “First, the moving party bears the burden of producing prima facie evidence of a written agreement to arbitrate the controversy. The moving party can meet its initial burden by attaching to the [motion or] petition a copy of the arbitration agreement purporting to bear the [opposing party's] signature. (Ibid.) “For this step, it is not necessary to follow the normal procedures of document authentication.”( Ibid.)

 

“If the moving party meets its initial prima facie burden and the opposing party disputes the agreement, then in the second step, the opposing party bears the burden of producing evidence to challenge the authenticity of the agreement. (The opposing party can do this in several ways. For example, the opposing party may testify under oath or declare under penalty of perjury that the party never saw or does not remember seeing the agreement, or that the party never signed or does not remember signing the agreement.” (Ibid.)

 

Last, “if the opposing party meets its burden of producing evidence, then in the third step, the moving party must establish with admissible evidence a valid arbitration agreement between the parties.” (Ibid.)

 

            In opposition, Plaintiff argues that the documentary evidence submitted with the Opposition shows that Plaintiff did not disregard the contractual arbitration process that Defendant now asks the Court to compel, but instead repeatedly attempted to invoke it. Plaintiff contends that Plaintiff first served a written Pre-Arbitration Notice of Dispute, which Defendant acknowledged. After the notice period expired without resolution, Plaintiff attempted to initiate arbitration through FairClaims, the administrator designated by Defendant, by requesting the required signup link, submitting follow-up requests, and notifying Defendant of Plaintiff's intent to proceed. When FairClaims advised that arbitration could not proceed unless Defendant participated, Plaintiff followed those instructions and continued communicating with both FairClaims and DefendantArbitration while preserving Plaintiff's legal rights.

 

Plaintiff further notes that, months before filing the present Motion to Compel Arbitration, Defendant Turo's Associate General Counsel, Matthew Brown, advised Plaintiff that Defendant Turo, Inc.'s next step would be to compel arbitration, a course Defendant Turo, Inc. ultimately pursued. On this record, Plaintiff requests that the Court consider the complete documentary record, deny Defendant Turo, Inc.'s Motion to Compel Arbitration, and grant such other and further relief as the Court deems just and proper.

 

Here, Defendant has provided its terms of service, which include an arbitration agreement. Thus, Defendant has satisfied its initial burden. In response, Plaintiff has not provided a declaration or any other type of evidence showing that the parties did not enter into an agreement. Furthermore, Plaintiff’s opposition does not provide any case law allowing the Court to deny the instant motion.

 

Therefore, the Court grants the Motion to Compel Arbitration.
