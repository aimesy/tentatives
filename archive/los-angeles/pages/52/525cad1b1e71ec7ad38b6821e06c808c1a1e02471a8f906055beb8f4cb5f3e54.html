MICHAEL VASQUEZ V. GENERAL
MOTORS LLC

CASE NO.:  24NWCV04857

HEARING:  08/06/2026 @ 9:30 a.m.

 

#11

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Defendant General Motors LLC’s
demurrer is OVERRULED.

 

Defendant General Motors LLC’s
to strike is DENIED.

 

Defendant General Motors LLC shall
file and serve an Answer to the Second Amended Complaint within 30 days of
notice of this ruling.

 

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

Defendant General Motors LLC
(Defendant) demurs to the Second Amended Complaint (SAC) filed by Plaintiff Michael
Vasquez (Plaintiff).  Defendant also
moves to strike the prayer for punitive damages.

 

Background

 

This is a lemon law action.  On January 8, 2026, Plaintiff filed the
operative SAC against Defendant and Does 1 through 10, inclusive, asserting
five causes of action: (1) Violation of Subdivision (d) of Civil Code Section
1793.2; (2) Violation of Subdivision (b) of Civil Code Section 1793.2; (3)
Violation of Subdivision (a)(3) of Civil Code Section 1793.2; (4) Breach of the
Implied Warranty of Merchantability; and (5) Fraudulent Inducement—Concealment.

 

Legal Standard

 

As a general matter, in a
demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading
or via proper judicial notice. ¿ (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004)
116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)  “A demurrer
tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.”  (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs.
(2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315 (E-Fab).)  As such, the court assumes the truth of the
complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Id.)  The only issue a demurrer is concerned with
is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action.  (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th
740, 747.)

 

Where a demurrer is sustained,
leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of
successful amendment.  (Goodman v.
Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) 
The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be
amended successfully.  (Id.; Lewis v.
YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.)  However, “[i]f there is any reasonable
possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to
sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” 
(Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240,
245). 

 

Plaintiff is only required to
allege ultimate facts, not evidentiary facts. 
(See Committee on Children's Television, Inc. v. General Foods Corp. (1983)
35 Cal.3d 197, 212 [“the complaint should set forth the ultimate facts
constituting the cause of action, not the evidence by which plaintiff proposes
to prove those facts”); 1 Cal. Affirmative Def. § 10:2 (2d ed.) [allegations of
agency, course and scope of employment, etc. qualify as ultimate facts].)  Plaintiff’s allegations must be accepted as
true on demurrer.  (See Yvanova v. New
Century Mortgage Corp. (2016) 62 Cal.4th 919, 924 [“For purposes of
reviewing a demurrer, we accept the truth of material facts properly pleaded in
the operative complaint”].)

 

Meet and Confer

 

Code of Civil Procedure section
430.41, subdivision (a) requires the moving party to meet and confer “in
person, by video conference, or by telephone” in an attempt to resolve a
demurrer’s deficiencies five days before the responsive pleading is due.  (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41(a)(2).)

 

The Court finds that Defendant
has fulfilled the meet and confer requirement. 
(Quezada Decl., ¶ 2.)

 

Analysis

 

          Fifth
Cause of Action

 

Defendant demurs to the fifth
cause of action on grounds that the claim (1) is barred by the three-year
statute of limitations for fraud; (2) fails to plead fraud with the requisite
specificity; and (3) fails to allege a transactional relationship between the
parties giving rise to a duty to disclose.

 

The Court will address each
argument in turn.

 

                     Statute
of Limitations

 

A three-year limitations period
applies to fraud claims.  (Code Civ.
Proc. § 338, subd. (d).)

 

Plaintiff argues the Fifth Cause
of Action is timely because the delayed discovery rule postpones accrual until
the plaintiff discovers, or reasonably should have discovered, the facts
constituting the fraud.  (Opp., pp. 1-6.)
 Plaintiff contends the SAC now cures the
deficiency previously identified by the Court by alleging specific repair
dates, that Defendant repeatedly represented the Subject Vehicle had been
repaired or was operating as designed, and that Plaintiff had no reasonable
means of discovering Defendant's alleged concealment because Defendant
continued to conceal the Transmission Defect throughout the repair process.  (Ibid.)  Plaintiff further argues that whether he
exercised reasonable diligence and when he should have discovered the alleged
fraud are factual questions that cannot be resolved on demurrer.  (Ibid.)

 

Here, Plaintiff alleges he
purchased the Subject Vehicle on June 10, 2019 and presented it to Defendant's
authorized repair facilities on approximately seven occasions between September
19, 2019 and November 20, 2023.  (SAC, ¶¶
6, 14-18.)  Plaintiff alleges that,
during each repair visit, Defendant represented that the Vehicle had been
repaired and was operating as designed while continuing to conceal the alleged
Transmission Defect.  (Id., ¶¶
14-18.)  Plaintiff further alleges that
he had no way of uncovering Defendant's alleged deception because Defendant
performed diagnostics and repairs, repeatedly represented that nothing was
wrong with the Vehicle or that it had been repaired, and that Plaintiff
diligently presented the Vehicle for repair whenever issues arose.  (Id., ¶ 18.)

 

Defendant argues the claim
nevertheless remains time-barred because Plaintiff purchased the Subject
Vehicle on June 10, 2019 but did not file this action until December 13, 2024.  (Demurrer, pp. 8-10; Reply, pp. 1-3.)  Defendant contends Plaintiff's own allegations
establish that the defects manifested during the warranty period and that
Plaintiff therefore knew, or reasonably should have known, of the alleged fraud
no later than the repair visits.  (Ibid.)
 Defendant further argues the delayed
discovery rule is unavailable because the SAC still fails to plead facts
demonstrating that Plaintiff could not have discovered the alleged fraud
through reasonable diligence, and that neither the repair doctrine nor
equitable tolling applies.  (Ibid.)

 

Under the delayed discovery rule,
a cause of action accrues at the time when the cause of action is complete with
all its elements.  (Fox v. Ethicon
Endo-Surgery, Inc. (2005) 35 Cal.4th 797, 806 807.)  The test for when the statute of limitations
on a claim starts to run under the delayed discovery rule is “whether the
plaintiff has information of circumstances sufficient to put a reasonable
person on inquiry, or has the opportunity to obtain knowledge from sources open
to his or her investigation.”  (McGee
v. Weinberg (1979) 97 Cal.App.3d 798, 803.)  “In order to invoke this special defense to
the statute of limitations, the plaintiff must specifically plead facts which
show (1) the time and manner of discovery and (2) the inability to have made
earlier discovery despite reasonable diligence.”  (Saliter v. Pierce Brothers Mortuaries
(1978) 81 Cal.App.3d 292, 300.)  “The
burden is on the plaintiff to show diligence, and conclusory allegations will
not withstand demurrer.”  (E-Fab,
supra, 153 Cal.App.4th at 1319 (internal quotations omitted).)

 

Here, the SAC alleges specific
repair dates spanning from September 19, 2019 through November 20, 2023 and
alleges that Defendant repeatedly represented the Vehicle had been repaired
while continuing to conceal the alleged Transmission Defect.  Plaintiff further alleges that he exercised
reasonable diligence by presenting the Vehicle for repairs whenever issues
manifested and that Defendant's repeated assurances prevented him from
discovering the alleged concealment sooner.  At the pleading stage, these allegations are
sufficient to invoke the delayed discovery rule.

 

Accordingly, the Court finds
Plaintiff has sufficiently alleged delayed discovery for purposes of surviving
demurrer. Defendant's demurrer on statute of limitations grounds is therefore
OVERRULED.

 

                    Lack of specificity

 

“[T]he elements of an action for
fraud and deceit based on concealment are: (1) the defendant must have
concealed or suppressed a material fact, (2) the defendant must have been under
a duty to disclose the fact to the plaintiff, (3) the defendant must have
intentionally concealed or suppressed the fact with the intent to defraud the
plaintiff, (4) the plaintiff must have been unaware of the fact and would not
have acted as he did if he had known of the concealed or suppressed fact, and
(5) as a result of the concealment or suppression of the fact, the plaintiff
must have sustained damage.”  (Lovejoy
v. AT&T Corp. (2004) 119 Cal.App.4th 151, 157-158.)¿

 

“Every element of a cause of
action for fraud must be alleged in the proper manner and the facts
constituting the fraud must be alleged with sufficient specificity to allow [a]
defendant to understand fully the nature of the charge made.”  (Tarmann v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.
(1991) 2 Cal.App.4th 153, 157.)  When it
comes to a fraud action against a corporation, the plaintiff must generally
allege the names of the persons who made the allegedly fraudulent
representations, their authority to speak, to whom they spoke, what they said
or wrote, and when it was said or written. 
(Ibid.)

 

However, the rule for pleading
fraud against a corporation is inapplicable to a claim of fraudulent
concealment.  (Alfaro v. Community
Housing Improvement System & Planning Association, Inc.¿(2009) 171
Cal.App.4th 1356, 1384 [“How does one show ‘how’ and ‘by what means’
something¿didn’t¿happen, or ‘when’ it never happened, or ‘where’ it never
happened?”].)  Instead, such details, in
the context of a fraudulent concealment claim, “are properly the subject of
discovery, not demurrer.”  (Id. at
1384-85.)  Moreover, the specificity
requirement is “relaxed when it is apparent from the allegations that the
defendant necessarily possesses knowledge of the facts.”  (Quelimane¿Co. v. Steward Title Guaranty
Co.¿(1998) 19 Cal.4th 26, 27.)¿¿

 

Defendant argues the Fifth Cause
of Action still fails to plead fraud with the requisite specificity because
Plaintiff does not identify the individual who concealed the alleged defect,
the specific representations made, or facts establishing Defendant's intent to
induce Plaintiff's reliance.  (Demurrer,
pp. 10-11.)  Defendant further contends
the allegations remain conclusory and merely recite the elements of fraudulent
concealment.  (Reply, pp. 4-5.)

 

Plaintiff argues the SAC now
adequately pleads fraudulent concealment by alleging Defendant knew of the
Transmission Defect before the sale through numerous internal sources,
concealed that defect before and after the sale, intended Plaintiff to rely on the
omission, and that Plaintiff would not have purchased the Subject Vehicle had
the defect been disclosed.  (Opp., pp.
6-9.)

 

The Court finds that information
regarding the names of the persons who made the allegedly fraudulent
representations, their authority to speak, to whom they spoke, what they said
or wrote, and when it was said or written is not required to plead a claim for
fraudulent concealment.  Rather, a
fraudulent concealment claim concerns a defendant's failure to disclose
information it had a duty to disclose.  Accordingly,
it would be nonsensical to require Plaintiff to allege affirmative
representations where the gravamen of the claim is that the pertinent
information was never disclosed.

 

Here, Plaintiff alleges Defendant
knew the Subject Vehicle's 8-speed Transmission was defective before Plaintiff
purchased the Vehicle, acquired that knowledge through internal testing,
warranty data, consumer complaints, dealer reports, and other internal sources,
failed to disclose the alleged defect despite possessing superior knowledge,
and that Plaintiff would not have purchased the Vehicle had the defect been
disclosed.  (SAC, ¶¶ 69-80.)  Plaintiff further alleges Defendant
intentionally concealed the defect and that its officers, directors, or
managing agents authorized or ratified the marketing materials and product
disclosures that omitted the alleged defect.  (Id., ¶¶ 74, 77-80.)

 

The Court finds these allegations
adequately apprise Defendant of the nature of Plaintiff's fraudulent
concealment claim.  Defendant's demurrer
on this ground is therefore OVERRULED.

 

                     Duty
to Disclose

 

There are four circumstances in
which nondisclosure or concealment may constitute actionable fraud: (1) when
the defendant is in a fiduciary relationship with the plaintiff; (2) when the
defendant had exclusive knowledge of material facts not known to the plaintiff;
(3) when the defendant actively conceals a material fact from the plaintiff; or
(4) when the defendant makes partial representations but also suppresses some
material fact.”  (LiMandri v. Judkins¿(1997)
52 Cal.App.4th 326, 336.)  A duty
to¿disclose¿may also arise when a defendant¿possesses¿or exerts control over
material facts not readily available to the plaintiff.  (Jones v. ConocoPhillips Co.¿(2011) 198
Cal.App.4th 1198, 1199.)¿¿¿¿

 

A vendor has a duty to disclose
material facts not only to immediate purchasers, but also to subsequent
purchasers when the vendor has reason to expect the product will be resold.  (OCM Principal Opportunities Fund v. CIBC
World Markets Corp. (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 835, 859-860.)  The duty is limited to circumstances in which
the seller has reason to expect the alleged fraud will be passed on to
subsequent purchasers.  (Geernaert v.
Mitchell (1995) 31 Cal.App.4th 601, 607-608.)  Mere foreseeability is insufficient.  (Id.¿[citing Restatement (Second) of
Torts § 305¿cmt. d (1977)].)¿  In
determining whether the seller had “reason to expect” that the fraud will be
passed onto subsequent purchasers, courts generally consider two factors: “(1)
the extent of the seller's knowledge of resale to a particular person or class
of persons and (2) the likelihood that the particular misrepresentation (or
concealment) would be passed on to them.”  (Id.¿at 608.)¿

 

Here, Plaintiff alleges he
purchased the Subject Vehicle from Defendant's authorized dealership and
entered into a warranty agreement with Defendant.  (SAC, ¶¶ 6-12.)  Plaintiff further alleges Defendant knew of
the alleged Transmission Defect before the sale, acquired that knowledge
through internal testing, warranty data, dealer reports, consumer complaints,
and other internal sources unavailable to consumers, and intentionally
concealed the defect from purchasers.  (Id.,
¶¶ 70-80.)  These allegations are
sufficient to establish Defendant had reason to expect the alleged concealment
would be passed on to Plaintiff as a subsequent purchaser and that Defendant
possessed exclusive knowledge of material facts not reasonably available to
Plaintiff.

 

Accordingly, Plaintiff has
sufficiently alleged Defendant owed a duty to disclose. Defendant's demurrer to
the Fifth Cause of Action is OVERRULED.

 

Motion to Strike

 

Code of Civil Procedure, section
436 permits trial courts to strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper
matter inserted in a pleading:

 

“The court may, upon a motion
made pursuant to Section 435, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms
it deems proper:

 

(a) Strike out any irrelevant,
false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading.

(b) Strike out all or any part of
any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a
court rule, or an order of the court.”

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 436.)

 

As discussed above, Plaintiff has
sufficiently stated a cause of action for fraudulent inducement by concealment.
 Accordingly, Plaintiff's request for
punitive damages is properly predicated on the fraud claim.  (See Bowser v. Ford Motor Co. (2022) 78
Cal.App.5th 587, 627 [affirming compensatory damages and punitive damages on a
fraud claim arising from a Song-Beverly action]; Covert v. FCA USA, LLC
(2022) 73 Cal.App.5th 821, 828, fn. 3 [recognizing that punitive damages are
recoverable, if at all, on a fraudulent concealment claim rather than under the
Song-Beverly Act].)

 

Accordingly, Defendant’s motion
to strike is DENIED.

 

Conclusion

 

Based on the foregoing, Defendant
General Motors LLC’s demurrer is OVERRULED.

 

Defendant General Motors LLC’s
motion to strike is DENIED.

 

Defendant General Motors LLC shall
file and serve an Answer to the Second Amended Complaint within 30 days
of notice of this ruling.

 

 

































































































































































































































 

Case Number: 25NWCV02367    Hearing Date: August 6, 2026    Dept: D

LETICIA MAGDALENA RODRIGUEZ, ET AL. V. GENESIS MOTOR AMERICA, LLC

CASE NO.: 25NWCV02367

HEARING:  08/06/2026 @ 9:30 a.m.

 

#12

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Plaintiffs’ motion to vacate order compelling arbitration and to lift stay is DENIED.

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

Plaintiffs Leticia Magdalena Rodriguez and Irma Ortiz Rodriguez (collectively, Plaintiffs) move for an order vacating the Court’s September 2025 Order compelling arbitration and lifting the stay in this matter.

 

Background

 

On June 30, 2025, Plaintiffs filed this lemon law action against Defendant Genesis Motor America, LLC (Defendant) and Does 1-20. 

 

On September 11, 2025, the Court granted Defendant Genesis Motor America, LLC’s (Defendant) motion to compel binding arbitration and stay action.

 

On June 22, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the instant motion.

 

On July 24, 2026, Defendant filed an opposition.

 

On July 29, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a reply.

 

Legal Standard

 

A trial court possesses "some amount of discretion to lift a stay imposed pending arbitration prior to the completion of an ordered arbitration."  (Aronow v. Superior Court (2022) 76 Cal.App.5th 865, 876.).  However, this discretion is "to lift a stay prior to the completion of arbitration only under circumstances in which lifting the stay would not frustrate the arbitrator's jurisdiction."  (MKJA, Inc. v. 123 Fit Franchising, LLC (2011) 191 Cal.App.4th 643, 660.)

 

In Quach v. California Commerce Club, Inc. (2024) 16 Cal.5th 562 (Quach), the California Supreme Court held that waiver of arbitration rights is governed by ordinary contract principles. To establish waiver, the party opposing arbitration must demonstrate by clear and convincing evidence that the opposing party was aware of its contractual right to arbitrate and deliberately chose to relinquish it.  (Id. at 584.).  The dispositive inquiry is whether the party's conduct is "so inconsistent with an intent to enforce the agreement that relinquishment may be inferred."  (Id. at 585.)  Prejudice to the opposing party is not required.  (Id., citing Morgan v. Sundance, Inc. (2022) 596 U.S. 411, 416.)  The standard is demanding.  Waiver requires a showing that it is "highly probable" the right was intentionally abandoned.  (Quach, supra, 16 Cal.5th at 584.)

 

When parties cannot agree on arbitrator selection, California law provides a specific remedy through section 1281.6, which states that "in the absence of an agreed method, or if the agreed method fails or for any reason cannot be followed, or when an arbitrator appointed fails to act and his or her successor has not been appointed, the court, on petition of a party to the arbitration agreement, shall appoint the arbitrator."  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.6.)  The proper remedy for a party facing delays in arbitrator selection is to petition the court for appointment of an arbitrator under section 1281.6, not to seek lifting of the arbitration stay.  (Engalla v. Permanente Medical Group, Inc. (1997) 15 Cal.4th 951, 981 (Engalla).)

 

A party that "imposes and administers its own arbitration program, that fraudulently misrepresents the speed of arbitrator selection process so as to induce reliance, and that in fact engages in conduct forcing substantial delay, may not then compel arbitration by contending that the other party failed to resort to the court by filing a petition for court-appointed arbitrator."  (Engalla, supra, 15 Cal.4th at 981.)  However, an "arbitration agreement is not breached when delay in selection of arbitrators is result of reasonable disagreements over arbitrator selection."  (Id. at 980.)  Courts examine whether there is "strong evidence that, despite a high degree of diligence on the part of the [plaintiff's] counsel in attempting to obtain the timely appointment of arbitrators, [defendant] lacked either reasonable diligence or good faith, or both, in cooperating on these timely appointments."  (Ibid.)

 

Analysis

 

Plaintiffs argue that Defendant waived and/or forfeited its right to arbitrate by failing to timely initiate and diligently prosecute arbitration following the Court’s September 2025 order compelling arbitration.

 

Specifically, Plaintiffs argue that following the Court’s order, “Defendant neither commenced arbitration nor agreed to the JAMS arbitration, which Plaintiffs commenced, which Defendant was agreeable to in its Lease Agreement.”  (Motion, p. 5.)  Plaintiffs rely on Sobremonte v. Superior Court (1998) 61 Cal.App.4th 980, 992, which identified several factors historically considered in determining waiver, including whether a party acted inconsistently with the right to arbitrate and whether delay prejudiced the opposing party.  However, under Quach, the ultimate inquiry is whether Defendant’s conduct demonstrates a voluntary and intentional relinquishment of its contractual right to arbitrate.  (Quach, supra, 16 Cal.5th at 584-585.)

 

As evidence of waiver, Plaintiffs submit evidence that: (1) following the Court’s order, Defendant “took no meaningful steps to advance the arbitral process,” allowing the matter to remain inactive for approximately eight months; (2) Plaintiffs themselves filed a demand for arbitration with JAMS in November 2025; (3) Plaintiffs selected JAMS because Defendant had identified JAMS as an approved arbitration forum; (4) after approximately seven months of inactivity, Defendant’s counsel declined to proceed with the JAMS arbitration on April 23, 2026; and (5) Defendant did not independently commence arbitration after securing the stay and refused to participate in the JAMS arbitration initiated by Plaintiffs, ultimately resulting in the closure of the JAMS matter.  (Ryu Decl., ¶¶ 3-7.)

 

However, the Court finds that Plaintiffs’ own evidence does not establish by clear and convincing evidence that Defendant intentionally relinquished its contractual right to arbitrate.  Rather, Plaintiffs’ evidence demonstrates that the parties disagreed over the proper arbitration forum.  (Quach, supra, 16 Cal.5th at 584-585.)  Exhibit 4 consists of email communications reflecting Defendant’s position that arbitration should proceed before AAA pursuant to the Connected Services Agreement (CSA) rather than JAMS, and Defendant expressly advised that it did not consent to proceeding before JAMS.  (Ryu Decl., ¶ 6, Ex. 4.)  A disagreement regarding the proper arbitration forum is not equivalent to abandoning arbitration altogether.  Indeed, an arbitration agreement is not breached where delay in selecting an arbitrator results from reasonable disagreements concerning arbitrator selection.  (Engalla, supra, 15 Cal.4th at 980.)

 

In opposition, Defendant submits evidence that it consistently maintained arbitration should proceed before AAA pursuant to the arbitration agreement, objected to Plaintiffs’ selection of JAMS, and, after JAMS closed the matter, initiated arbitration before AAA.  (Villa Decl., ¶ 12, Exs. E-J.)  This conduct is inconsistent with an intent to relinquish arbitration and instead demonstrates Defendant’s continued effort to enforce the arbitration agreement, albeit in the forum Defendant believed the parties had agreed upon.

 

Moreover, to the extent Plaintiffs believed the parties had reached an impasse regarding arbitrator selection, the Code provides a specific statutory remedy.  Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.6 authorizes the Court to appoint an arbitrator where the parties’ agreed method fails or cannot be followed.  Plaintiffs did not pursue that remedy.  Instead, Plaintiffs seek to vacate the arbitration order entirely.  Under Engalla, a disagreement regarding arbitrator selection does not itself establish abandonment of the arbitration agreement absent evidence that the delay resulted from a lack of reasonable diligence or good faith.  (Engalla, supra, 15 Cal.4th at 980-981.)

 

The Court finds that the evidence reflects an ongoing dispute concerning the proper forum, not an intentional abandonment of arbitration.  Although arbitration has been delayed, the evidence does not establish that Defendant abandoned arbitration after obtaining the stay.  

 

Accordingly, Plaintiffs have failed to establish by clear and convincing evidence that Defendant intentionally relinquished its contractual right to arbitrate.  

 

Conclusion

 

Based on the foregoing, Plaintiffs Leticia Magdalena Rodriguez and Irma Ortiz Rodriguez’s motion to vacate order compelling arbitration and to lift stay is DENIED.
