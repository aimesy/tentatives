I.       BACKGROUND  

On March 27, 2025, Plaintiff Bassam Kallo (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Defendants General Motors, LLC (“Defendant”) and Does 1 to 10 alleging five causes of action: (1) violation of Cal. Civ. Code § 1793.2(d); (2) violation of Civ. Code § 1793.2(b); (3) violation of Civ. Code § 1793.2(a)(3); (4) breach of the implied warranty of merchantability; and (5) fraudulent inducement through concealment. Plaintiff alleges that, on or about July 7, 2017, Plaintiff entered into a warranty contract with Defendant regarding a 2017 Chevrolet Camaro (the “Vehicle”), which was allegedly revealed to have defects. (Complaint at ¶¶ 6 and 11.)

On July 16, 2025, Plaintiff filed his first amended complaint (“FAC”).

On June 22, 2026, Plaintiff filed his second amended complaint (“SAC”) alleging three causes of action: (1) violation of Civ. Code § 1793.2(d); (2) violation of Civ. Code § 1793.2(b); and (3) violation of Civ. Code § 1793.2(a)(3).

On July 6, 2026, Defendant filed the instant demurrer to Plaintiff’s SAC.

On July 27, 2026, Plaintiff filed his opposition.

On July 30, 2026, Defendant filed its reply.

 

II.          LEGAL STANDARD

Demurrer

A demurrer is a pleading that may be used to test the legal sufficiency of the factual allegations in a complaint. (Cal. Code of Civ. Proc. § 430.10.) There are two types of demurrers: general demurrers and special demurrers. (See McKenney v. Purepac Pharmaceutical Co. (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 72, 77.)

General demurrers can be used to attack pleadings for failure to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action or for lack of subject matter jurisdiction. (CCP § 430.10(e); McKenney, supra, 167 Cal.App.4th at 77.) Such demurrers can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable; evidence or extrinsic matters are not considered. (CCP §§ 430.30 and 430.70; Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318; Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) To test the sufficiency of the cause of action, the Court admits “all material facts properly pleaded” and “matters which may be judicially noticed,” but does not consider contentions, deductions, or conclusions of fact or law. [Citation].” (Blank, supra, 39 Cal.3d at 318.) The Court gives these facts “a reasonable interpretation, reading it as a whole and its parts in their context.” (Ibid.) At the pleading stage, a plaintiff need only allege ultimate facts sufficient to apprise the defendant of the factual basis for the claim against him. (Semole v. Sansoucie (1972) 28 Cal. App. 3d 714, 721.) The face of the complaint includes exhibits attached to the complaint. (Frantz v. Blackwell (1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 91, 94.) "If facts appearing in the exhibits contradict those alleged, the facts in the exhibits take precedence." (Holland v. Morse Diesel Intern., Inc. (2001) 86 Cal.App.4th 1443, 1447.)

CCP § 430.41 requires that “[b]efore filing a demurrer pursuant to this chapter, the demurring party shall meet and confer in person or by telephone with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer.” (CCP § 430.41(a).) The parties are to meet and confer at least five days before the date the responsive pleading is due.  (CCP § 430.41(a)(2).) Thereafter, the demurring party shall file and serve a declaration detailing their meet and confer efforts. (CCP § 430.41(a)(3).)

When a demurrer is sustained, the Court determines whether there is a reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) When a plaintiff “has pleaded the general set of facts upon which his cause of action is based,” the court should give the plaintiff an opportunity to amend his complaint, since plaintiff should not “be deprived of his right to maintain his action on the ground that his pleadings were defective for lack of particulars.” (Reed v. Norman (1957) 152 Cal.App.2d 892, 900.) Generally, the court will allow leave to amend on at least the first try, unless there is absolutely no possibility of overcoming the issue. (See Angie M. v. Superior Court (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 1217, 1227 [“Denial of leave to amend constitutes an abuse of discretion unless the complaint shows on its face it is incapable of amendment. (Citation.) Liberality in permitting amendment is the rule, if a fair opportunity to correct any defect has not been given."].)

 

 

III.       DISCUSSION

Meet and Confer Requirement

On June 16, 2026, defense counsel met and conferred via telephone with Plaintiff’s counsel regarding the instant demurrer. (Allison Decl. ¶ 2.) The parties were unable to reach an agreement. (Ibid.) Accordingly, Defendant satisfies the meet and confer requirement of CCP § 430.41(a).

 

Demurrer

Defendant demurs to Plaintiff’s entire SAC on the grounds that it is time-barred by the applicable statute of limitation found in CCP § 871.21. (See Demurrer at p. 1.)

CCP § 871.21 provides, in pertinent part, that an action for restitution or replacement of motor vehicle or civil penalties “shall be commenced within one year after the expiration of the applicable express warranty” or “[n]otwithstanding subdivision (a) . . . shall not be brought no later than six years after the date of original delivery of the motor vehicle.” (Civ. Code § 871.21(a) and (b).) Additionally, “[t]he time periods prescribed in subdivisions (a) and (b) shall be tolled . . . [a]s provided by tolling requirements prescribed in the [Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act].” (Civ. Code § 871.21(c)(1).)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that CCP § 871.21 does not apply to the instant action because retrospective application of new procedural statutes to eliminate potential claims is a violation of due process. (Opposition at 2:19-3:13.) Plaintiff cites Rosefield Packing Co. v. Superior Court (1935) 4 Cal.2d 120, 122-123, holding that “where the change in remedy, as for example, the shortening of a time limit provision, is made retroactive, there must be a reasonable time permitted for the party affected to avail himself of his remedy before the statute takes effect. If the statute operates to cut off the existing remedy, or within so short a time as to give the party no reasonable opportunity to exercise his remedy, then the retroactive application of it is unconstitutional as to such party.” (Ibid. [citing Rosefield Packing Co., supra, 4 Cal.2d at 122-123].) Plaintiff argues that “[h]ere, Defendant seeks to have this court apply [CCP] section 871.21 retroactively, and to require Plaintiff to have filed their case by July 2023.” (Ibid.)

In reply, Defendant argues that both the statutory language and legislative history of CCP § 871.21 demonstrates that its statute of limitations applies to any action filed after January 1, 2025. (Reply at 1:14-2:9.) However, Defendant fails to cite any specific legislative history to support this argument.

California law establishes a strong presumption that statutes operate prospectively only. (Quarry v. Doe (2012) 53 Cal.4th 945, 955.) Courts must consider “the nature and extent of the change in the law and the degree of connection between the operation of the new rule and a relevant past event” guided by “familiar considerations of fair notice, reasonable reliance, and settled expectations.” (Id. at 955-956.) Here, Plaintiff entered into the warranty at issue on July 7, 2017. (See SAC at ¶ 6.) Accordingly, if the statute of limitations found in CCP § 871.21 applied, Plaintiff would have had to bring his claim by July 7, 2023, well before CCP § 871.21 was enacted. Therefore, Defendant improperly asks the Court to apply CCP § 871.21 in a manner that violates “familiar considerations of fair notice, reasonable reliance, and settled expectations.” (Quarry, supra, 53 Cal.4th at 955-956.) As Defendant notes, if application of a new statute of limitations and/or statute of repose “raise[s] substantial concerns about the effects of the new rule on the general administration of justice, or would unfairly undermine the reasonable reliance of parties on the previously existing state of the law,” a court can refuse to apply it. (Reply at 6:1-13 [citing Newman v. Emerson Radio Corp. (1989) 48 Cal.3d 973, 983.) Here, Plaintiff should not be bound to a statutory deadline of July 2023 when the applicable deadline did not exist until January 2025. Additionally, a statute “will not be applied retroactively unless such intention clearly appears in the language of the statute itself.” (Krusesky v. Baugh (1982) 138 Cal.App.3d 562, 566.) Defendant’s argument that Plaintiff’s claims are time-barred fails.

 

IV.    CONCLUSION

Defendant’s demurrer is OVERRULED.

Defendant to file an answer within 20 days of this order.

Case Number: 25VECV06346    Hearing Date: August 7, 2026    Dept: I

25VECV06346 SOFI LENDING CORP. AS ATTORNEY-IN-FACT FOR SANTANDER BANK, NA vs BRYAN YOUNG

August 7, 2026, at 8:30 a.m.

Van Nuys Courthouse East, Dept. I

Tentative Ruling - Motion to Set Aside Default (CCP § 473)

 

 

 

I.        Background

 

On November 5, 2025, Plaintiff SoFi Lending Corp. as attorney-in-fact for Santander Bank, N.A. (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Defendant Bryan Young (“Defendant”) and Does 1 to 10 alleging one cause of action for breach of contract.

 

On May 22, 2026, Defendant filed the instant motion to set aside default.

 

To date, the motion remains unopposed.

 

II.     Motion to Set Aside Default

 

Additionally, Cal. Code of Civ. Proc. § 473.5(a) provides that “[w]hen service of a summons has not resulted in actual notice to a party in time to defend the action and a default or default judgment has been entered against him or her in the action, he or she may serve and file a notice of motion to set aside the default or default judgment and for leave to defend the action.” (CCP § 473.5(a).)

 

III.  Analysis

 

Defendant argues that he was not properly served with the summons and complaint and only became aware of this action after the Order to Show Cause on April 15, 2026. (Motion at 1:21-24.) Specifically, Defendant declares that “[a]t the time Plaintiff purportedly effected personal service of the Summons and Complaint on November 14, 2025, my residence located at 20734 Clark Street, Woodland Hills, California 91367 was occupied by a short-term guest through VRBO . . .” (Young Decl. at ¶ 3)(emphasis added.)

 

Notably, Defendant admits that service occurred at his residence. CCP § 415.20 permits service by “leaving a copy of the summons and complaint . . . at the person’s usual mailing address . . . with the person who is apparently in charge thereof . . . with a person at least 18 years of age . . .” (CCP § 415.20(a).) Here, Young admits that service was left at his residence (a usual mailing address) with someone over the age of 18. (See Young Decl. at ¶ 3.) Therefore, service was proper pursuant to CCP § 415.20, and Defendant’s motion is denied.

IV.  Conclusion

 

Based on the foregoing, Defendant’s motion to set aside default is DENIED.

25VECV06813 STANLEY WETCH, et al. vs CELTIC CAPITAL CORPORATION, A CALIFORNIA STOCK CORPORATION

August 7, 2026, at 8:30 a.m.

Van Nuys Courthouse East, Dept. I

Tentative Ruling – Motion to Compel Further Discovery

 

 

 

I.        Background

 

On December 1, 2025, Plaintiffs Stanley Wetch (“Wetch”) and La Flor de Mexico, Inc. (“La Flor”)(collectively “Plaintiffs”) filed a complaint against Celtic Capital Corporation (“Defendant”) and Does 1 to 250 alleging eight causes of action: (1) conversion; (2) breach of contract; (3) accounting; (4) declaratory relief; (5) breach of fiduciary duty; (6) breach of statutory duty; (7) violations of unfair competition law; and (8) injunctive relief.

 

On January 7, 2026, Defendant filed a cross-complaint against Plaintiffs, the United States Small Business Administration, and Does 1 to 250 alleging two causes of action: (1) interpleader; and (2) breach of guaranty.

 

On June 26, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the instant motion to compel Defendant’s further responses to Requests for Production of Documents, Set One.

 

On July 20, 2026, Defendant filed its opposition.

 

On July 22, 2026, Plaintiffs filed their reply.

 

II.     Motion to Compel Further Discovery

 

“If a party filing a response to a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling under Sections 2031.210, 2031.220, 2031.220, 2031.230, 2031.240, and 2031.280 thereafter fails to permit the inspection, copying, testing, or sampling in accordance with that party’s statement of compliance, the demanding party may move for an order compelling compliance.” (Cal. Code of Civ. Proc. § 2031.320(a).)

 

“. . . [T]he court shall impose a monetary sanction . . . against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel compliance with a demand, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (CCP § 2031.320(b).)

 

 

Evidentiary Objections

 

As a preliminary matter, the Court overrules Defendant’s August 5, 2026, evidentiary objections to allegedly new evidence submitted with Plaintiffs’ reply, specifically, various meet and confer letters dated prior to Plaintiffs’ initial motion. (See August 5, 2026, Evidentiary Objections.) Plaintiffs referenced these letters in their initial moving papers. (See Garcia July 26, 2026, Decl.) Additionally, the Garcia declaration directly addresses points raised by Defendant’s opposition. (Ibid.)

 

Analysis

 

Pursuant to CCP § 2016.040, on April 20, 2026, Plaintiffs' counsel sent defense counsel a meet and confer letter regarding the alleged deficiencies in Defendant's discovery responses. (Garcia Decl. at ¶¶ 5-6.) Plaintiffs provided various extensions for amended responses. (Id. At ¶ 7.) Following several months, Defendant failed to provide supplemental responses. (Ibid.) Accordingly, Plaintiffs satisfy the meet and confer requirement of CCP § 2016.040."

 

Plaintiffs bring this motion to compel Defendant’s further responses to Requests for Production of Documents, Set One, Nos. 1-8 and 10-14. (See Separate Statement.) These requests pertain to, inter alia, documents reflecting negotiations between Defendants and Plaintiffs concerning the Discretionary Clause at issue in the parties’ contract, communications between Defendant and “any borrower in the State of California” concerning the Discretionary Clause, all documents reflecting all income received following Defendant’s foreclosure on La Flor, all documents reflecting all expenses paid from the. Proceeds of Defendant’s foreclosure on La Flor, all documents reflecting accountings Defendant has provided to La Flor following Defendant’s foreclosure, “[a]ll account records, including registers, logs, income received, expenses incurred, payments made following [Defendant]’s foreclosure on La Flor under the Agreement,” “[a]ll DOCUMENTS or COMMUNICATIONS from any person or entity making demand for payment from the time YOU initiated foreclosure under the Agreement, to the date YOU respond to this request,” “[a]ll DOCUMENTS reflecting any demand for payment from the Small Business Administration related to YOUR foreclosure under the Agreement,” “[f]or the period between January 1, 2022, and December 1, 2025, copies of all Loan and Security Agreements entered into between YOU and any borrower in the State of California and wherein the Agreements contained the Discretionary Clause,” and more. (See Separate Statement.)

 

Plaintiffs’ motion is denied as to Request No. 1 as Plaintiffs is also in possession of documents that “reflect negotiations” between themselves and Defendant. (See Separate Statement.)

 

Plaintiffs’ motion is denied as to Request Nos. 2-3 and 12-13 since the requests are vague and overbroad and loan information of other non-parties is unlikely to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence that is not protected by privacy rights. (See Grisworld v. State of Connecticut (1965) 381 U.S. 479, 484 [disclosure of private financial information of non-parties may be shielded from discovery; right of privacy guaranteed by the U.S. Constitution].)

 

Plaintiffs’ motion is denied as to Requests Nos. 4-5 and 7 as “all income,” “all expenses,” and “[a]ll accounting” following Defendant’s foreclosure on La Flor is impermissibly overbroad and vague. (See Separate Statement.)

 

Plaintiffs’ motion is denied as to Request No. 6 since Plaintiffs should also have, in their possession, “accountings [Defendant] provided to La Flor . . .” (See Separate Statement.)

 

Plaintiffs’ motion is denied as to Request No. 8 since “[a]ll DOCUMENTS or COMMUNICATIONS from any person or entity making demand for payment” is impermissibly overbroad and vague. (See Separate Statement.)

 

Plaintiffs’ motion is granted as to Request No. 10 since documents reflecting any demand(s) for payment from the Small Business Administration are directly relevant to Defendant’s cross-complaint against the Smal Business Administration. (See Separate Statement.)

 

Plaintiffs’ motion is denied as to Request No. 11 since “any written demand for wage payments” is impermissibly overbroad and vague. (See Separate Statement.)

 

Plaintiffs’ motion is granted as to Requests No. 14.  Documents reflecting any subordinate security interests to the surplus proceeds are directly relevant to both Plaintiffs’ claims and Defendant’s cross-complaint. (See Separate Statement.)

 

Sanctions

 

As a final matter, Defendant’s request for sanctions on the grounds that Plaintiffs’ motion was premature for failure to engage in meet and confer is denied since the Court finds that Plaintiffs sufficiently met and conferred regarding the allegedly deficient responses. (See Opposition at 11:8-12.)

 

III.  Conclusion

 

Based on the foregoing, Plaintiffs’ motion is GRANTED in part and DENIED in part.

 

Plaintiffs’ motion is denied as to Requests Nos. 1-8 and 11-13.

 

Plaintiffs’ motion is granted as to Requests Nos. 10 and 14.

 

Defendant is to serve supplemental, verified, objection-free responses to Plaintiffs’ Requests for Production of Documents Nos. 10 and 14 within 20 days of this order.

 

Moving party to give notice.
