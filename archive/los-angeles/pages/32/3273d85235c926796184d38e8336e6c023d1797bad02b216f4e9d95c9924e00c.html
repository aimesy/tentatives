[TENTATIVE] ORDER DENYING MOTION TO DISMISS THE CROSS-COMPLAINT

 

I.         BACKGROUND

Defendant Elizabeth Valdez (“Valdez”) allegedly undertook a retaliatory harassment campaign against Plaintiff Saeed Masjedi (“Masjedi”), her tenant, after Masjedi reported a plumbing problem. Masjedi sued Valdez for intentional tort and statutory liability.

On February 2, 2024, Valdez filed a Cross-Complaint against Masjedi for three causes of action, alleging Masjedi harassed Valdez.

On May 15, 2026, Masjedi filed the instant motion to dismiss the cross-complaint. Valdez filed an opposition.

 

II.        JUDICIAL NOTICE

The Court takes judicial notice of all judicial records on file in this case, pursuant to Masjedi’s request.

 

III.      LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure section 418.10, subdivision (a)(1),[1] holds that a defendant, on or before the last day of his or her time to pleading or within any further time that the court may for good cause allow, may serve and file a notice of motion to quash service of summons on the lack of the court’s jurisdiction over defendant.

           

III.      DISCUSSION

A.        Request for Relief under Section 415.20.

Masjedi first requests the Court dismiss the Cross-Complaint pursuant to section 415.20, on the basis Valdez’s process-server failed to first attempt to serve Masjedi personally before resorting to substitute service. Section 415.20 provides that if a copy of the summons and complaint cannot be personally delivered via reasonable diligence, substitute service may be effectuated. (§ 415.20, subd. (b). However, section 415.20 does not provide an enforcement mechanism; section 418.10 does. Section 418.10 allows a defendant to bring a motion to quash service on the lack of jurisdiction, due to improper service of the complaint, within the time to plead or, within any further time that the court may for good cause allow.

Valdez served Masjedi with the complaint via substitute service on March 21, 2024. Pursuant to section 415.20, subdivision (b), service of summons via substitute service is deemed complete on the 10th day after mailing; the deadline to respond is 30 days after completed service. Therefore, any motion to quash was due by April 30, 2024—over two years prior to Masjedi filing the motion to dismiss.

The Court will only consider a motion to quash after this point upon a finding of good cause. Here, the Court does not find good cause. Masjedi has been on notice as to the existence of this Cross-Complaint since at least May 23, 2024, when Masjedi filed his first “Motion to Dismiss” the Cross-Complaint. At that time, Masjedi moved to dismiss solely on the basis of section 425.16 and Civil Code section 47, subdivision (b). After the Court denied the motion, Masjedi moved to vacate the Court’s ruling under sections 663 and 473. The Court also denied this motion, finding Masjedi was attempting to relitigate previously decided issues. Masjedi then appealed the Court’s ruling. The Court of Appeal denied the appeal and affirmed the Court’s order.

Following resolution of appeal, Masjedi filed a motion to “Amend and Conform” the Cross-Complaint under section 128, subdivision (a)(3). (See 12/30/25 “Motion for an Order to Amend and Conform”.) This motion argued, in part, the substituted service was invalid and the Court needed to dismiss the Cross-Complaint. The Court denied the motion, finding Masjedi was attempting to relitigate the previously denied motion to dismiss, without basis.

Here, it is clear that Masjedi was served with the Cross-Complaint in such a manner that Masjedi was able to actively litigate the Cross-Complaint over the course of two years. Only after Masjedi’s other motions to dismiss were repeatedly rejected did Masjedi seek to dismiss the Cross-Complaint on these grounds. It is clear to the Court that Masjedi has appeared before this Court and agreed to the Court adjudicating this matter. Therefore, the Court does not find good cause to evaluate or potentially grant a motion to quash service.

B.        All Other Requests

Masjedi otherwise moves under section 663, 473, and 1008, none of which are applicable here.

Masjedi has already moved for the requested relief pursuant to section 663 and 473 via Masjedi’s July 3, 2024, improperly titled Motion to Set Aside/Vacate Default. Masjedi requested the Court vacate the June 18, 2024, Court order denying the motion to dismiss the Cross-Complaint and replace it with an order dismissing the Cross-Complaint, on the basis the Court reached the wrong conclusion due to the substitute service issue. The Court found the motion without merit under both section 473 or the more appropriate 1008, and totally inapplicable to section 663. (See 8/28/24 ‘Minute Order’.)

The Court rules the same here. Masjedi has not provided valid argument to support relief under any of the request statutes. Masjedi is merely attempting to relitigate previously addressed issues without any new law or facts to support the request, as required under section 1008.

The Court denies the motion, with prejudice. Further filing of motions on the same grounds will be subject to sanction.

 

IV.       CONCLUSION

Plaintiff Saeed Masjedi’s motion to dismiss the cross-complaint is DENIED, with prejudice.

Valdez is ordered to give notice.

Case Number: 24STCV11151    Hearing Date: August 12, 2026    Dept: 730

Superior Court of California

County of Los Angeles

Department 730

 

BREANA M RUCKER, et al.,

Plaintiffs,

vs.

FCA US, LLC,

Defendant.

Case No.:

24STCV11151

Hearing Date:

August 12, 2026

[TENTATIVE] ORDER DEEMING MOTION TO DEEM ADMISSIONS ADMITTED MOOT

 

I.         BACKGROUND

Plaintiffs Breana M. Rucker (“Breana”) and Hubert L. Rucker Jr. (“Hubert”) entered into a warranty contract with Defendant FCA US, LLC (“FCA”) regarding a 2020 Jeep Wrangler (“subject vehicle”). The subject vehicle has alleged defects and nonconformities making the car effectively worthless. FCA allegedly failed to replace the vehicle or reimburse Plaintiffs after a reasonable number of repair attempts. Plaintiffs sued FCA, alleging four causes of action including violations of the Song-Beverly Act.

On July 2, 2026, FCA filed the instant motion to deem the Request for Admissions, Set One, admitted against Plaintiffs. FCA also requested the court impose sanctions of $1,660.00 against Plaintiffs. Plaintiffs filed an opposition. FCA replied.

 

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.030,[1] subdivision (a), provides that “[t]he court may impose a monetary sanction ordering that one engaging in the misuse of the discovery process, or any attorney advising that conduct, or both pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney's fees, incurred by anyone as a result of that conduct.” According to section 2023.010, subdivision (d), misuse of the discovery process includes “failing to respond or to submit to an authorized method of discovery.”

Under section 2033.280, if a party fails to serve a timely response to a request for admissions, the following rules apply:

“(a) The party to whom the requests for admission are directed waives any objection to the requests, including one based on privilege or on the protection for work product under Chapter 4 (commencing with Section 2018.010). The court, on motion, may relieve that party from this waiver on its determination that both of the following conditions are satisfied:

(1) The party has subsequently served a response that is in substantial compliance with Sections 2033.210, 2033.220, and 2033.230.

(2) The party’s failure to serve a timely response was the result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect.

(b) The requesting party may move for an order that the genuineness of any documents and the truth of any matters specified in the requests be deemed admitted, as well as for a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010).

(c) The court shall make this order, unless it finds that the party to whom the requests for admission have been directed has served, before the hearing on the motion, a proposed response to the requests for admission that is in substantial compliance with Section 2033.220. It is mandatory that the court impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) on the party or attorney, or both, whose failure to serve a timely response to requests for admission necessitated this motion.”

           

III.      DISCUSSION

A.        Motions to Deem Admitted

FCA requests the Court deem Requests for Admissions, Set One, admitted against Plaintiffs. Under section 2033.280, subdivision (b), a party may move to deem the truth of any matters specified if the responding party fails to submit timely responses to a request for admissions. A timely response is either within 30 days after service of the demand or within the extended timeframe agreed upon by parties. (§ 2033.250, subd. (a).)

FCA served the Request for Admissions on August 5, 2025. (Mot., Willette Decl. ¶ 2, Ex. A.) Responses were initially due September 8, 2025. (Id. at ¶ 5.) Plaintiffs did not serve responses by this date. (Id. at ¶ 6.) On November 24, 2025, and June 22, 2026, counsel for FCA requested overdue responses (Id. at ¶¶ 7-8.) Plaintiffs did not respond to this request or serve responses prior to the filing of this motion. (Id. at ¶ 10.)

On July 17, 2026, Plaintiffs served substantially code-compliant responses to the Request for Admissions. (Opp., Washington Decl. ¶ 9, Ex. 2.) Although objections were included in the responses, these objections were waived due to the failure to respond prior to the timeline, or otherwise request an extension, and are without merit. However, as Plaintiffs served substantially code-compliant responses prior to the hearing on this motion, the Court deems the motion MOOT. (§ 2033.280, subd. (c).)

B.        Sanctions

FCA is entitled to sanctions pursuant to section 2033.280, subdivision (c), as Plaintiffs’ failure to serve timely responses to the Request for Admissions necessitated this motion.

FCA requests sanctions of $1,660.00, based upon $1,600.00 in attorney’s fees and 1 $60.00 filing fee (Mot., Willette Decl. ¶ 7.) No information was provided as to calculation of attorney’s work time. As no information was provided, the Court instead relies upon its own expertise and awards reasonable sanctions of $310.00, based upon 1 hour of attorney’s work at a reasonable rate of $250.00 per hour and 1 filing fee.

The Court also notes this motion concerns two separate pieces of discovery—one Request for Admissions for each plaintiff. Combining discovery motions allows the moving party to avoid paying the requisite filing fees. Filing fees are jurisdictional and it is mandatory for court clerks to demand and receive them. (See Duran v. St. Luke’s Hospital (2003) 114 Cal.App.4th 457, 460.) Here FCA paid a single filing fee for what should have been two separate motions. FCA is ordered to pay the Court one additional $60.00 filing fee.

 

IV.       CONCLUSION

Defendant FCA US, LLC’s motion to deem FCA’s Request for Admissions, Set One, admitted against Plaintiffs is MOOT.

FCA’s request for sanctions is GRANTED. Plaintiffs and Plaintiffs’ counsel are ordered to pay $310.00 in sanctions to FCA within 30 days of the hearing on the motion.

FCA is ordered to pay the Court one additional $60.00 filing fee.

FCA is ordered to give notice.

Case Number: 25STCV24357    Hearing Date: August 12, 2026    Dept: 730

Superior Court of California

County of Los Angeles

Department 730

 

SIMONROY DELFIERRO QUINTO,

Plaintiff,

vs.

RYAN AUDREY GOLDSMITH, et al.,

Defendant.

Case No.:

25STCV24357

Hearing Date:

August 12, 2026

ORDER GRANTING MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT

 

I.           BACKGROUND

Defendant Ryan Audley Goldsmith (“Goldsmith”) was allegedly driving his vehicle at an excessive speed, within the course and scope of his employment with Defendant The Hype Agency, LLC (“Hype”), while Plaintiff Simonroy Delfierro Quinto (“Quinto”) was making a left turn in his own vehicle, resulting in a collision. Quinto sues Defendants for three causes of action, including negligence.

On May 8, Hype filed the instant motion for summary judgment, or, in the alternative, summary adjudication. Quinto filed an opposition. Hype replied.

III.       LEGAL STANDARD

A party may move for summary judgment “if it is contended that the action has no merit or that there is no defense to the action or proceeding.”  (Code Civ. Proc. § 437c, subd. (a).[1]) “[I]f all the evidence submitted, and all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law,” the moving party will be entitled to summary judgment. (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.) 

The moving party bears an initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing of the nonexistence of any triable issue of material fact, and if he does so, the burden shifts to the opposing party to make a prima facie showing of the existence of a triable issue of material fact. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850, citing to § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence.  (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

Neither a moving nor responding party may rely on the mere allegations or denials of its pleadings. A moving party must submit specific admissible evidence showing that the responding party cannot establish at least one element of his, her or its cause of action or defense. The responding party, to defeat the motion, must submit specific admissible evidence showing that a triable issue of material fact does exist as to that element of the cause of action or defense. (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

The “Golden Rule” on a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication is that “if [a fact] is not set forth in the separate statement, it does not exist.” (Zimmerman, Rosenfeld, Gersh & Leeds LLP v. Larson (2005) 131 Cal.App.4th 1466, 1477, citing United Community Church v. Garcin (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 327, 337.).

 

III.       DISCUSSION

Hype requests the Court enter summary judgment as Goldsmith was not acting within the course and scope of his employment at the time of the incident, pursuant to the Going-and-Coming rule.

A.         Statement of Facts

At the time of the incident, Goldsmith worked as a brand ambassador for Hype, a nationwide staffing agency. (UMF 1.) On the date of the incident, Goldsmith was specifically assigned to work as a temporary W2 employee for promotional events, including representing AMEX at the Crypto.com Arena. (UMF 2.)  His job duties included supporting the Guest Services team by distributing wristbands for promotions, educating customers about the updated promotion, alleviating customer complaints, and reporting back to the AMEX team after each shift. (UMF 3.)

Hype did not hire Goldsmith as a driver. (UMF 4.) Goldsmith was not provided a vehicle or required to drive his personal vehicle. (UMF 4-5.) Hype did not reimburse Goldsmith for commute time or expenses to and from work. (UMF 6.)

On October 26, 2024, Goldsmith and Quinto were involved in a motor vehicle incident while Goldsmith was on his way to work at the Crypto.com Arena. (UMF 8.) Goldsmith was not performing work or any other errand for Hype at the time of the incident. (UMF 10.)

Quinto sued Hype for negligence and negligent hiring, supervision, and retention, based upon an assertion that Hype, as Goldsmith’s employer, was vicariously liable for Goldsmith’s negligence, and by improperly allowing Goldsmith to operate the vehicle.

B.          Vicarious Liability

Hype requests the Court grant summary judgment as to the cause of action for negligence via vicarious liability, as Goldsmith was not acting within the course and scope of his employment at the time of the incident. Vicarious liability only applies to employers when their employees have committed a tort within the scope of their employment. (Pierson v. Helmerich & Payne Internat. Drilling Co. (2016) 4 Cal.App.5th 608, 608.)

Hype argues, under the “going-and-coming” rule, Hype is not vicariously liable. Under the going-and-coming rule, an employee is generally not acting within the scope of employment while commuting to and from the workplace unless said employee is on a special “errand” or “mission” for the employer. (Jeewarat v.Warner Brothers Entertainment, Inc. (2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 427, 435-436.)

For cases involving temporary employees of temporary employment agencies, such as this, Henderson v. Adia Services, Inc. (1986) 182 Cal.App.3d 1069 is instructive. In Henderson, a temporary employee for a temporary employment agency rear-ended a plaintiff while driving the employee’s own vehicle to a temporary job assigned by the agency. (Id. at 1071.) The Court found the going-and-coming rule applied, as the only distinction between Henderson and a typical going-and-coming rule case was the fact the defendant employee did not report to the same location each day he worked. (Id. at 1077.) Therefore, absent evidence of Goldsmith’s commute constituting a “special errand” or a “special mission,” such as returning tools from the job, attending an employment social function beneficial to the employer, or being en-route to a service call, Hype is potentially barred from liability under the going-and-coming rule. (Ibid.)

Here, the facts show Goldsmith was not performing a special mission or errand for Hype at the time of the incident. Goldsmith was driving to his temporary work assignment, as required by his employment with Hype. He was providing no other benefit to Hype. Hype satisfied the initial establishment that Goldsmith was not performing a special mission or errand.

Hype also presented evidence demonstrating Goldsmith’s vehicle was not required for purposes of his employment. The going-and-coming rule is inapplicable “in cases where the employer requires an employee to furnish a vehicle of transportation on the job.” (Ducey v. Argo Sales Co. (1979) 25 Cal.3d 707, 721.) Even if not expressly stated, the going-and-coming rule can apply when an employee is impliedly required to provide a vehicle. (Huntsinger v. Glass Containers Corp. (1972) 22 Cal.App.3d 803, 809.)

The mere fact an employer could foresee an employee would use their personal automobile to reach a job site is irrelevant for purposes of determining whether there was an express or implied requirement to utilize a personal vehicle. (Henderson, supra, 182 Cal.App.3d at 1076.) The mere fact an employee may find it convenient or even essential to use an automobile to travel to and from a place of employment does not, alone, establish requirement. (Id. at 1078.) Generally, requirement to use a personal vehicle is only established if a party is required to use their vehicle, within the scope of their employment, outside of merely traveling to and from a single job site. (See Moradi v. Marsh USA, Inc. (2013) 219 Cal.App.4th 886, 890.) It also may be established if an employee is compensated for their travel time or expenses. (Hinman v. Westinghouse Elect. Co. (1970) 2 Cal.3d 956, 960.)

There are no facts here to establish Hype impliedly or expressly required Goldsmith to own and utilize his own vehicle for purposes of his employment. Goldsmith was not hired as a driver. His daily tasks did not require any amount of travel time beyond arriving at the location of his temporary assignment. Hype did not compensate Goldsmith for travel time or expenses in any capacity.

Quinto solely relies upon the fact Hype required brand ambassadors to have “reliable transportation to and from the event” to establish Hype required Goldsmith to own and utilize his own vehicle. (AMF 5.) Brand ambassadors were expected to be timely, and for that reason, Goldsmith generally only accepted assignments within a defined geographic radius. (AMF 2, 12.) However, there is no evidence to suggest Hype ever required Goldsmith to have and use his own vehicle; so long as Goldsmith arrived on time, there were no limitations on Goldsmith’s mode of transportation. The mere fact Goldsmith may have found utilizing his own vehicle convenient is not indicative of any implied requirement. (Henderson, supra, 182 Cal.App.3d at 1078.)

No other exception to the going-and-coming rule applies. For example, Hype’s business did not create an inherent foreseeable risk to others that would make imposing liability fair. (See Bussard v. Minimed, Inc., (2003) 105 Cal.App.4th 798 [employer was held liable for an employee’s motor vehicle accident because the employee was sickened by the employer’s improperly used pesticides, and was therefore unable to drive safely].) There is no alleged risk that arises from Goldsmith working as a brand ambassador resulting in injury to Quinto.

There are no material facts in dispute that Hype did not derive any benefit outside those derived by commute trips made by ordinary members of the work force and that the going-and-coming rule applies, with no applicable exceptions. All causes of action against Hype stemming from vicarious liability are subject to summary judgment.

C.          Direct Negligence

Hype argues Hype cannot be directly liable to Quinto via the cause of action for negligent supervision, hiring, and retention, as Hype had no duty to Quinto. Quito argues that Hype is liable for Goldsmith’s negligence under a respondeat superior theory, does not identify a duty that Hype owed to Quito, but argues that the Rowland factors weigh in favor of a duty.

In determining whether a duty exists for a negligence cause of action, the Court employs a two-part framework. (Brown v. USA Taekwondo (2021) 11 Cal. 5th 204, 211.) First, it determines whether there is a duty of care or special relationship, and if so, whether the Rowland factors weighed in favor of limiting or eliminating this duty. (See ibid. [“The court thus asked, first, whether Brown had adequately alleged a special relationship between the parties that gave rise to a legal duty to protect, and second, whether the Rowland factors weighed in favor of limiting or eliminating this duty”; “We conclude the two-part framework the Court of Appeal applied in this case accurately reflects the law as stated in this court's precedents”].) The Rowland factors include: 1) foreseeability of harm to the plaintiff; 2) degree of certainty plaintiff suffered injury; 3) closeness of connection between the defendant’s conduct and injury suffered; 4) moral blame attached to the defendant’s conduct; 5) policy of preventing future harm; 6) burden on defendant and consequences to the community in imposing a duty of care; and 7) availability, cost, and prevalence of insurance for risk involved. (Rowland v. Christian (1968) 69 Cal.2d 108.)

Here, Quito has failed to identify a duty that Hype owed to Quito.  Accordingly, the duty analysis fails on the first prong of the analysis, and Hype is not liable to Quito directly for negligence.

 

IV.        CONCLUSION

Defendant The Hype Agency, LLC’s motion for summary judgment is GRANTED.

Hype is to give notice.
