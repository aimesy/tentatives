Tentative Ruling - Motion to Enforce Settlement

 

 

 

I.        Motion to Enforce Settlement

 

On November 27, 2024, Plaintiffs Martin Coopwood (“Coopwood”) and Jocelyn Calderon (“Calderon”)(collectively “Plaintiffs”) filed a complaint against Defendants FCA US LLC (“Defendant”) and Does 1 to 10 alleging three causes of action: (1) breach of express warranty obligations under the Song-Beverly Warranty Act; (2) breach of implied warranty of merchantability under the Song-Beverly Warranty Act; and (3) violation of the Song-Beverly Act Section 1793.2.

 

On January 2, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a notice of settlement.

 

On June 4, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion to enforce settlement.

 

To date, the motion remains unopposed.

 

II.     Requested Relief

 

Cal. Code of Civ. Proc. § 664.6 provides, in pertinent part, as follows: “If parties to pending litigation stipulate, in a writing signed by the parties outside the presence of the court or orally before the court, for settlement of the case, or part thereof, the court, upon motion, may enter judgment pursuant to the terms of the settlement.”

 

In hearing a section 664.6 motion, the trial court may receive evidence, determine disputed facts, and enter terms of a settlement agreement as a judgment. (Bowers v. Raymond J. Lucia Companies, Inc. (2012) 206 Cal.App.4th 724, 732.) The Court may also receive oral testimony in addition to declarations. (Kohn v. Jaymar-Ruby, Inc. (1994) 23 Cal.App.4th 1530, 1533.)

 

The Court may interpret the terms and conditions of the settlement (Fiore v. Alvord (1985) 182 Cal.App.3d 561, 566), but the Court may not create material terms of a settlement, as opposed to deciding what terms the parties themselves have previously agreed upon. (Weddington Productions, Inc. v. Flick (1998) 60 Cal.App.4th 793, 810.)

 

Strict compliance with the statutory requirements is necessary before a court can enforce a settlement under this statute. (Sully-Miller Contracting Co. v. Gledson/Cashman Construction, Inc. (2002) 103 Cal.App.4th 30, 37.) The party seeking to enforce a settlement “must first establish the agreement at issue was set forth ‘in a writing signed by the parties’ (section 664.6) or was made orally before the court.” (Harris v. Rudin, Richman & Appel (1999) 74 Cal.App.4th 299, 304.)

 

The settlement must include the signatures of the parties seeking to enforce the agreement, and against whom enforcement is sought. (J.B.B. Investment Partners, Ltd. v. Fair (2014) 232 Cal.App.4th 974, 985.) “[A] writing is signed by a party if it is signed by . . . [a]n attorney who represents the party.” (CCP § 664.6(b)(2).)

 

III.  Analysis

 

Plaintiffs allege that, on January 5, 2025, the parties executed a Settlement Agreement and Release for execution (hereinafter the “Settlement Agreement”). (Mijares Decl. at ¶ 4; Exh. 1 [signed Settlement Agreement].) Pursuant to the Settlement Agreement, Defendant stated that it would issue a settlement check in the amount of $85,000.00 within 90 days of execution. (Id. at ¶ 7; see Exh. 1.) A year later, on January 5, 2026, Plaintiffs’ counsel contacted defense counsel to inquire on the status of the settlement check. (Id. at ¶ 6; Exh. 2 [email correspondence].) Defense counsel did not respond, despite multiple follow-up correspondences on January 27, February 13, and March 10, 2026. (Ibid.)

 

Defendant does not oppose the motion. Plaintiffs provide a copy of the parties’ written and signed settlement agreement pursuant to CCP § 664.6. Accordingly, Plaintiffs’ motion is granted.

 

IV.  Conclusion

 

Based on the foregoing, Plaintiffs’ motion to enforce settlement is GRANTED.

I.       BACKGROUND  

            On July 9, 2025, Plaintiffs Eduardo Argueta (“Argueta”) and Karen Melgar (“Melgar”)(collectively “Plaintiffs”) filed a complaint against Defendant Marina Pink (“Defendant”) and Does 1 to 100 alleging five causes of action: (1) negligence; (2) conversion; (3) defamation per se; (4) labor code violations; and (5) breach of contract.

            On May 19, 2026, Defendant filed the instant demurrer to and motion to strike portions of Plaintiffs’ complaint.

            To date, the demurrer and motion to strike remain unopposed.

 

 

II.          LEGAL STANDARDS

Demurrer

            A demurrer is a pleading that may be used to test the legal sufficiency of the factual allegations in a complaint. (Cal. Code of Civ. Proc. § 430.10.) There are two types of demurrers: general demurrers and special demurrers. (See McKenney v. Purepac Pharmaceutical Co. (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 72, 77.)

            General demurrers can be used to attack pleadings for failure to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action or for lack of subject matter jurisdiction. (CCP § 430.10(e); McKenney, supra, 167 Cal.App.4th at 77.) Such demurrers can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable; evidence or extrinsic matters are not considered. (CCP §§ 430.30 and 430.70; Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318; Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) To test the sufficiency of the cause of action, the Court admits “all material facts properly pleaded” and “matters which may be judicially noticed,” but does not consider contentions, deductions, or conclusions of fact or law. [Citation].” (Blank, supra, 39 Cal.3d at 318.) The Court gives these facts “a reasonable interpretation, reading it as a whole and its parts in their context.” (Ibid.) At the pleading stage, a plaintiff need only allege ultimate facts sufficient to apprise the defendant of the factual basis for the claim against him. (Semole v. Sansoucie (1972) 28 Cal. App. 3d 714, 721.) The face of the complaint includes exhibits attached to the complaint. (Frantz v. Blackwell (1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 91, 94.) "If facts appearing in the exhibits contradict those alleged, the facts in the exhibits take precedence." (Holland v. Morse Diesel Intern., Inc. (2001) 86 Cal.App.4th 1443, 1447.)

            CCP § 430.41 requires that “[b]efore filing a demurrer pursuant to this chapter, the demurring party shall meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer.” (CCP § 430.41(a).) The parties are to meet and confer at least five days before the date the responsive pleading is due. (CCP § 430.41(a)(2).) Thereafter, the demurring party shall file and serve a declaration detailing their meet and confer efforts. (CCP § 430.41(a)(3).)

            When a demurrer is sustained, the Court determines whether there is a reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment. (Blank, supra, 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) When a plaintiff “has pleaded the general set of facts upon which his cause of action is based,” the court should give the plaintiff an opportunity to amend his complaint, since plaintiff should not “be deprived of his right to maintain his action on the ground that his pleadings were defective for lack of particulars.” (Reed v. Norman (1957) 152 Cal.App.2d 892, 900.) Generally, the court will allow leave to amend on at least the first try, unless there is absolutely no possibility of overcoming the issue. (See Angie M. v. Superior Court (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 1217, 1227 [“Denial of leave to amend constitutes an abuse of discretion unless the complaint shows on its face it is incapable of amendment. (Citation.) Liberality in permitting amendment is the rule, if a fair opportunity to correct any defect has not been given."].)

 

Motion to Strike

            The court may, upon a motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (Cal. Code of Civ. Proc. § 436(a).) The court may also strike all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Id. at (b).) The grounds for a motion to strike are that the pleading has irrelevant, false or improper matter, or has not been drawn or filed in conformity with laws. (Ibid.) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (CCP § 437.)

Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Vaccaro v. Kaiman (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 761, 768 [“When the defect which justifies striking a complaint is capable of cure, the court should allow leave to amend.”].) The burden is on the complainant to show the Court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Blank, supra, 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

Before filing a motion to strike, the moving party shall meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to the motion to strike. (CCP § 435.5(a).)

 

III.       DISCUSSION

Meet and Confer

             Defense counsel states that she emailed and called Plaintiffs’ counsel multiple times in connection with the instant demurrer and motion to strike but did not receive a response. (Pink Decl. at ¶¶ 2-5; Exh. A [email correspondence].) Accordingly, the Court finds that Defendant satisfies the meet and confer requirements of CCP §§ 430.41(a) and 435.5(a).

 

Demurrer

            Defendant demurs to Plaintiff’s entire complaint on the grounds that plaintiff fails to state facts sufficient to constitute each cause of action. (See Demurrer at pp. 2-3.)

 

First Cause of Action – Negligence

            To successfully plead a cause of action for negligence, a plaintiff must allege ultimate facts showing that (1) defendant owed plaintiff a legal duty to use due care; (2) defendant breached that duty; (3) the breach of duty caused plaintiff harm; and (4) plaintiff suffered damages as a result. (County of Santa Clara v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2006) 137 Cal.App.4th 292, 318; Ladd v. County of San Mateo (1996) 12 Cal.4th 913, 917.) “Negligence is an unintentional tort, a failure to exercise the degree of care in a given situation . . .” (Donnelly v. Southern Pac. Co. (1941) 18 Cal.2d 863, 869.) “Ordinarily, negligence may be pleaded in general terms and the plaintiff need not specify the precise act or omission alleged to constitute the breach of duty.” (Lopez v. S. Cal. Rapid Transit Dist. (1985) 40 Cal.3d 780, 795.) To allege negligence claims, plaintiffs need only state what occurred, and generally that the acts were negligently done, but “need not state the specific act or omission constituting negligent conduct.” (Crouse v. Brobeck (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1509, 1532.)

            Here, Plaintiffs plead that, starting in early 2023, Argueta was an employee of Defendant, performing construction and repair work at her home and another nearby property. (Complaint at ¶ 6.) Plaintiffs allege that, on many days, Argueta worked more than eight hours; some days, Argueta worked more than twelve hours; some weeks, Argueta worked all seven days. (Id. at ¶ 7.) “In the later part of 2024, [Defendant] also made a separate contract with ARGUETA to perform a discrete job, involving construction work, for a separate contractual payment of $6,500.” (Id. at ¶ 9.) “ARGUETA provided his own tools for that work, which he left at the jobsite, [Defendant]’s home, each night with [Defendant]’s consent and agreement.” (Id. at ¶ 10.) “On a night in mid-November 2024, after ARGUETA was done with his work and gone for the day, some of his tools were taken from [Defendant]’s house and were never returned. [Defendant] claimed the tools were stolen and that the identity of the thieves was unknown. The value of ARGUETA’s tools that were taken was about $7,600.00. Some other personal property which did not belong to ARGUETA may also have been removed from [Defendant]’s home.” (Id. at ¶ 11.) “[Defendant] made a claim to her insurer for the lost property, including ARGUETA’s tools. To support that claim, [Defendant] requested that ARGUETA provide her with receipts from his purchases for some of the tools. ARGUETA did provide [Defendant] with original receipts from some of his tools.” (Id. at ¶ 12.) “ARGUETA is informed and believes and thereon alleges that [Defendant] received a payment from her insurer for the lost personal property, including some amount for the value of ARGUETA’s tools.” (Complaint at ¶ 13.) “[Defendant] did not pay any part of that insurance money to ARGUETA, but retained all of it for herself, including the part of the payment that was for ARGUETA’s tools.” (Id. at ¶ 14.) “ARGUETA did not learn about the insurance payment immediately, and did not know [Defendant] had money which belonged to him.” (Id. at ¶ 15.) “After the November 2024 incident, [Defendant] requested that ARGUETA continue working there, which he did.” (Id. at ¶ 16.) “ARGUETA continued his daily work at [Defendant]’s home and also finished the separate contracted work for [Defendant].” (Id. at ¶ 17.) “Although ARGUETA worked at [Defendant]’s request, [Defendant] failed and refused to pay ARGUETA wages for at least 12 days of work.” (Id. at ¶ 18.) “[Defendant] also failed to pay ARGUETA the agreed price for his separate contract job which he had completed.” (Id. at ¶ 19.)

            “Starting sometime after the November 2024 incident at [Defendant]’s home, [Defendant] stated to various people (outside of law enforcement personnel) that ARGUETA, with the assistance of MELGAR, had been stealing from [Defendant] in at least three ways. First, [Defendant] stated that ARGUETA and MELGAR had been using [Defendant]’s credit card to purchase tools which he then kept for himself and claimed as his own. Second, [Defendant] stated that ARGUETA and MELGAR had been stealing money from [Defendant]’s company which was designated for payroll. Third, [Defendant] stated that ARGUETA and/or MELGAR was responsible for, or complicit in, the removal of property from her house in November 2024. [Defendant] did not have any non-speculative basis for any of those accusations, and none of those accusations about ARGUETA or MELGAR were true.” (Complaint at ¶ 20.) “[Defendant] made those same accusations about ARGUETA and MELGAR to the Los Angeles Police Department. As a result, on the morning of January 4, 2025, LAPD officers raided ARGUETA and MELGAR’s apartment home, and arrested ARGUETA.” (Id. at ¶ 21.) “The LAPD officers searched the apartment and found no evidence to support [Defendant]’s claims, or showing any criminal conduct. However, based on [Defendant]’s accusations, ARGUETA was held in jail from January 4 until January 7, 2025, when he was released without any charges ever being filed.” (Id. at ¶ 22.) “ARGUETA and MELGAR do not make any claim against [Defendant] now based on her false reporting to the LAPD or the results of those false reports.” (Id. at ¶ 23.) “[Defendant] was present at ARGUETA and MELGAR’s apartment building on January 4, 2025, while the LAPD officers were conducting their raid, search and arrest of ARGUETA. During that raid, [Defendant] repeated her statements that ARGUETA and MELGAR had been stealing from her and her company, to ARGUETA and MELGAR’s friends and neighbors.” (Id. at ¶ 24.)

            Finally, Plaintiffs allege that “[b]y allowing ARGUETA to leave his valuable tools at the worksite, [Defendants] assumed responsibility to secure the worksite and the tools reasonably, to avoid theft or damage.” (Complaint at ¶ 29.) “[Defendants] failed to exercise reasonable care to secure the worksite and ARGUETA’s tools, which allowed those tools to be taken in November 2024.” (Id. at ¶ 30.) “As a direct and proximate result of [Defendants’] negligence, Plaintiffs lost tools valued at approximately $7,600, according to proof. Plaintiffs also incurred additional losses and damages due to the loss of ARGUETA’s tools and used for his work, and due to the time and additional cost of replacement, subject to proof.” (Id. at ¶ 31.)

            Based on the foregoing, Plaintiffs successfully plead negligence. Specifically, Plaintiffs plead that Defendant owed Plaintiffs a duty of care to reasonably secure Plaintiffs’ valuables, that Defendant breached that duty, that the breach of this duty caused Plaintiffs’ harm by the loss of Plaintiffs’ valuables, and that Plaintiffs suffered damages as a result.

            In her demurrer, Defendant argues that Plaintiffs must have alleged a special relationship to plead a duty of care to Plaintiffs pursuant to Rowland v. Christian (1968) 69 Cal.2d 108, 113. (See Demurrer at 5:13-19.) However, Rowland considered a motion for summary judgment and not a demurrer; additionally, Rowland was subsequently vacated. (See generally Rowland, supra, 69 Cal.2d at 113.) To allege negligence at the pleading stage, plaintiffs need only state what occurred, and generally that the acts were negligently done, but “need not state the specific act or omission constituting negligent conduct.” (Crouse, supra, 67 Cal.App.4th at 1532.)

            Accordingly, Defendant’s demurrer to Plaintiffs’ first cause of action for negligence is overruled.

 

Second Cause of Action – Conversion

            Next, to successfully plead a cause of action for conversion, a plaintiff must allege ultimate facts showing (1) the plaintiff’s ownership or right to possession of personal property; (2) defendant’s disposition of the property inconsistent with plaintiff’s rights; and (3) resulting damages. (PCO, Inc. v. Christensen, Miller, Fink, Jacobs, Glaser, Weil & Shapiro, LLP (2007) 150 Cal.App.4th 384, 395-397; Fischer v. Machado (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 1069, 1072.) “A general allegation that the defendant ‘converted the property to his or her own use’ suffices to allege conversion . . .” (Weiss v. Marcus (1975) 51 Cal.App.3d 590, 599.) An action for conversion “rests neither in the knowledge nor the intent of the defendant.” (Burlesci v. Petersen (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 1062, 1066.)

            Based on the foregoing, Plaintiffs successfully allege a cause of action for conversion. Specifically, Plaintiffs allege ownership of tools, valued at approximately $7,600, Defendant’s disposition of the property through their disappearance, and resulting damages. (See discussion of Complaint supra at 5:15-8:4.)

            In her demurrer, Defendant provides a bare argument that “[t]hese allegations fail to establish a conversion cause of action, notwithstanding that the allegations are not true.” (Demurrer at 7:3-6.) However, Defendant fails to elaborate or further support her argument.

            Accordingly, Defendant’s demurrer to Plaintiff’s second cause of action for conversion is overruled.

 

Third Cause of Action – Defamation Per Se

            Next, to successfully plead a cause of action for defamation per se, a plaintiff must allege ultimate facts showing (1) the intentional publication by defendant; (2) of statement of fact; (3) that is false; (4) defamatory; (5) unprivileged; and (6) has a natural tendency to injure or that causes special damages. (Taus v. Loftus (2007) 40 Cal.4th 683, 720; Smith v. Maldonado (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 637, 645.) Pleaders are not required to allege to whom matters were published, but instead alleging a matter was “published” is sufficient. (Farr v. Bramblett (1955) 132 Cal.App.2d 36, 46.) “Publication need not be to the public or a large group; communication to a single individual is sufficient.” (Ringler Associates Inc. v. Maryland Cas. Co. (2000) 80 Cal.App.4th 1165, 1179.)

            See discussion of Complaint supra at 5:15-8:4. Plaintiffs also plead that Defendant made the “false statements” that Plaintiffs stole from Defendant’s property “without knowing whether they were true and without regard for the truth of those statements.” (Complaint at ¶ 45.) “When [Defendant] made those false statements, she knew or reasonably should have known that her statements would be injurious and damaging to Plaintiffs.” (Id. at ¶ 46.)

            Based on the foregoing, Plaintiffs successfully plead a cause of action for defamation per se. Specifically, Plaintiffs plead that Defendant made intentional, false statements to Plaintiffs’ neighbors that Plaintiffs stole from Defendant, that such communications were unprivileged, and that have a natural tendency to injure Plaintiffs’ reputation. (See Taus, supra, 40 Cal.4th at 720; see also discussion of Complaint supra at 5:15-8:4; see also Complaint at ¶¶ 45-46.)

            Defendant makes various arguments in her demurrer. First, Defendant argues that such statements were privileged since they were made “at the same time and place as the arrest.” (Demurrer at 10:12-20; see also Demurrer generally at 7:7-12:10.) However, Plaintiffs specifically plead that their defamation claim does not arise from statements made to the police; instead, Plaintiffs’ cause of action arises from the statements made to third parties, such as Plaintiffs’ neighbors. (See Complaint at ¶ 42 [“(Defendant) made those statements to individuals who were not involved in law enforcement, out of the presence of law enforcement personnel, and not as part of any official report to law enforcement or any official proceeding authorized by law”].) Additionally, arguments that the statements actually were privileged are for a trier of fact to decide upon the merits and not applicable at the pleading stage.

            Based on the foregoing, Defendant’s demurrer to Plaintiffs’ third cause of action for defamation per se is overruled.

 

Fourth Cause of Action – Labor Code Wage Violations

            Next, to plead a minimum wage violation, a plaintiff must allege that (1) the plaintiff was an employee covered by the applicable Industrial Welfare Commission Wage Order; (2) the employer paid wages at a rate below the applicable minimum wage; and (3) the amount of unpaid wages owed. (See Cal. Lab. Code § 1197.) Additionally, to plead an overtime violation, a plaintiff must allege that (1) the plaintiff was a nonexempt employee; (2) the plaintiff worked hours in excess of the applicable daily or weekly thresholds; and (3) the employer failed to pay the required overtime rate. (See Lab. Code § 510.)

            See discussion of Complaint supra at 5:15-8:24. While Plaintiffs plead that Argueta performed “construction and repair work” for Defendant (see Complaint at ¶¶ 6-7), some which were “discrete job[s]” pursuant to separate contracts (see Complaint at ¶ 9), Plaintiffs fail to plead that Argueta was an employee covered by the applicable Industrial Welfare Wage Order or that Argueta was a nonexempt employee. (See Lab. Code §§ 510 and 1197; see also S.G. Borello & Sons, Inc. v. Department of Industrial Relations (1989) 48 Cal.3d 341.)

            Based on the foregoing, Defendant’s demurrer to Plaintiffs’ fourth cause of action for labor code wage violations is sustained with leave to amend.

 

Fifth Cause of Action – Breach of Contract

            Finally, to successfully plead a cause of action for breach of contract, a plaintiff must allege ultimate facts showing (1) the existence of a contract; (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance; (3) defendant’s breach or anticipatory breach; and (4) resulting damages. (Wall Street Network, Ltd. v. N.Y. Times Co. (2008) 164 Cal.App.4th 1171, 1178; Armstrong Petroleum Corp. v. Tri-Valley Oil & Gas Co (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 1375, 1391.) “The plaintiff need only allege the ultimate fact of the making of the contract by the defendant and himself.” (Pfaff v. Fair-Hispley, Inc. (1965) 232 Cal.App.2d 274, 279.) “Whether parties have reached a contractual agreement, and on what terms, are questions for the fact finder when conflicting versions of the parties’ negotiations require a determination of credibility.” (Hebberd-Kulow Enterprises, Inc. v. Kelomar, Inc. (2013) 218 Cal.App.4th 272, 283.) “In an action based on a written contract, the plaintiff may plead the legal effect of the contract rather than its precise language.” (Ochs v. PacifiCare of Cal. (2004) 115 Cal.App.4th 782, 795.) Pleading a contract by legal effect involves alleging the relevant terms in substance. (McKell v. Washington Mutual, Inc. (2006) 142 Cal.App.4th 1457, 1489.)

            See discussion of Complaint supra at 5:15-8:24. Based on the foregoing, Plaintiffs successfully plead a cause of action for breach of contract. Specifically, Plaintiffs plead that Argueta and Defendant made an “oral contract, which was later memorialized through text messages, for ARGUETA to perform construction work for [Defendant] which was separate and discrete from ARGUETA’s usual daily work. ARGUETA and [Defendant] agreed that the price would be $6,500.” (Complaint at ¶ 57.) “ARGUETA completed all the construction work which the parties had agreed to, and [DEFENDANT] accepted ARGUETA’s work.” (Id. at ¶ 58.) “[Defendant] failed and refused to pay ARGUETA the agreed price for his contract work, without any valid excuse.” (Id. at ¶ 59.)

            In her demurrer, Defendant argues that the oral contract is barred by the statute of frauds because it was “not to be performed within a year from the making thereof.” (See Demurrer at 13:20-14:6.) However, Defendant ignores that the Complaint also alleges that the work was contracted for and commenced in “the latter part of 2024” (see Complaint at ¶ 9) and was completed by January 2025. (Id. at ¶¶ 17-21.) Accordingly, Defendant’s argument fails.

            Based on the foregoing, Defendant’s demurrer to Plaintiff’s fifth cause of action for breach of contract is overruled.

 

Motion to Strike

            The Court now turns to Defendant’s motion to strike. Defendant moves to strike Plaintiffs’ requests for punitive damages and associated language. (See Motion at p. 2.)

            As discussed supra, Plaintiffs successfully allege causes of action for negligence, conversion, defamation, and breach of contract. These allegations support a showing that Defendant acted with “oppression, fraud, or malice” pursuant to Cal. Civ. Code § 3294(a), particularly as it relates to Defendant’s allegedly false allegations of theft. Therefore, Defendant’s request to strike punitive damages is denied.

            Notwithstanding the foregoing, as discussed supra, Plaintiffs failed to successfully plead a cause of action for labor code violations. Accordingly, Plaintiffs’ request for attorney’s fees, and related language, are struck from Plaintiff’s complaint.

 

Leave to Amend

            Generally, the court will allow leave to amend on at least the first try, unless there is absolutely no possibility of overcoming the issue. (See Angie M., supra, 37 Cal.App.4th at 1227 [“Denial of leave to amend constitutes an abuse of discretion unless the complaint shows on its face it is incapable of amendment. (Citation.) Liberality in permitting amendment is the rule, if a fair opportunity to correct any defect has not been given."].)

            Accordingly, Plaintiffs are given leave to amend their fourth cause of action for labor code violations and subsequent request for attorney’s fees.

 

IV.    CONCLUSION

Defendant’s demurrer to Plaintiffs’ first, second, third, and fifth causes of action for negligence, conversion, defamation per se, and breach of contract, respectively, are OVERRULED.

Defendant’s demurrer to Plaintiffs’ fourth cause of action for labor code violations is SUSTAINED with leave to amend.

Defendant’s motion to strike Plaintiffs’ request for punitive damages is DENIED.

Defendant’s motion to strike Plaintiffs’ request for attorney’s fees is GRANTED with leave to amend.

Plaintiffs to file an amended complaint within 20 days of this order.

I.       BACKGROUND  

            On May 20, 2014, Plaintiffs Yevgenya Shevtsov and Tatyana Shevtsov, heir and successor-in-interest to Nadezhda Babitskaya (“Plaintiffs”) filed a complaint against Country Villa Wilshire Healthcare Center, Country Villa Health Services, Cedars-Sinai Medical Center, R&T Enterprises, SR&DR Operating Company, LLC, Abraham Ishaaya, Michael Borrochim, William Stanford, Fanny Rodriguez, Bradley Rosen, Eleanor Lavretsky, Ignor Shnaider, Gadson Johnson, Niceto Lopez, Marina Vaysburd, Jay Gladstein, Jon Kea, Marina Kovalevsky, Carolyn Bell, Shaun Miller, Demi Penera, Lourdes Uy, and Eurlyn Villamor (collectively “Defendants”) alleging two causes of action: (1) intentional tort; and (2) fraud.

            From July 26, 2019, to January 16, 2024, the action was stayed as a result of Defendant Country Villa Wilshire Healthcare Center’s filing for relief under the US Bankruptcy code.

            On January 16, 2026, Plaintiffs filed their first amended complaint (“FAC”) against Defendants and West Hollywood Healthcare & Wellness Center, LP alleging nine causes of action: (1) wrongful death; (2) elder abuse and neglect; (3) battery; (4) civil conspiracy to commit elder abuse and battery; (5) fraud; (6) constructive fraud; (7) fraud by misrepresentation; (8) SNF Patient Rights’ violations; and (9) willful misconduct.

            On April 23, 2026, Defendant Cedars-Sinai Medical Center (“Cedars-Sinai”) filed the instant demurrer to and motion to strike portions of Plaintiffs’ FAC.

            On May 13, 2026, Plaintiffs filed their opposition.

            On May 19, 2026, Cedars-Sinai filed its reply.

 

II.          LEGAL STANDARDS

Demurrer

            A demurrer is a pleading that may be used to test the legal sufficiency of the factual allegations in a complaint. (Cal. Code of Civ. Proc. § 430.10.) There are two types of demurrers: general demurrers and special demurrers. (See McKenney v. Purepac Pharmaceutical Co. (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 72, 77.)

            General demurrers can be used to attack pleadings for failure to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action or for lack of subject matter jurisdiction. (CCP § 430.10(e); McKenney, supra, 167 Cal.App.4th at 77.) Such demurrers can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable; evidence or extrinsic matters are not considered. (CCP §§ 430.30 and 430.70; Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318; Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) To test the sufficiency of the cause of action, the Court admits “all material facts properly pleaded” and “matters which may be judicially noticed,” but does not consider contentions, deductions, or conclusions of fact or law. [Citation].” (Blank, supra, 39 Cal.3d at 318.) The Court gives these facts “a reasonable interpretation, reading it as a whole and its parts in their context.” (Ibid.) At the pleading stage, a plaintiff need only allege ultimate facts sufficient to apprise the defendant of the factual basis for the claim against him. (Semole v. Sansoucie (1972) 28 Cal. App. 3d 714, 721.) The face of the complaint includes exhibits attached to the complaint. (Frantz v. Blackwell (1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 91, 94.) "If facts appearing in the exhibits contradict those alleged, the facts in the exhibits take precedence." (Holland v. Morse Diesel Intern., Inc. (2001) 86 Cal.App.4th 1443, 1447.)

            CCP § 430.41 requires that “[b]efore filing a demurrer pursuant to this chapter, the demurring party shall meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer.” (CCP § 430.41(a).) The parties are to meet and confer at least five days before the date the responsive pleading is due. (CCP § 430.41(a)(2).) Thereafter, the demurring party shall file and serve a declaration detailing their meet and confer efforts. (CCP § 430.41(a)(3).)

            When a demurrer is sustained, the Court determines whether there is a reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment. (Blank, supra, 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) When a plaintiff “has pleaded the general set of facts upon which his cause of action is based,” the court should give the plaintiff an opportunity to amend his complaint, since plaintiff should not “be deprived of his right to maintain his action on the ground that his pleadings were defective for lack of particulars.” (Reed v. Norman (1957) 152 Cal.App.2d 892, 900.) Generally, the court will allow leave to amend on at least the first try, unless there is absolutely no possibility of overcoming the issue. (See Angie M. v. Superior Court (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 1217, 1227 [“Denial of leave to amend constitutes an abuse of discretion unless the complaint shows on its face it is incapable of amendment. (Citation.) Liberality in permitting amendment is the rule, if a fair opportunity to correct any defect has not been given."].)

 

Motion to Strike

            The court may, upon a motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (Cal. Code of Civ. Proc. § 436(a).) The court may also strike all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Id. at (b).) The grounds for a motion to strike are that the pleading has irrelevant, false or improper matter, or has not been drawn or filed in conformity with laws. (Ibid.) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (CCP § 437.)

Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Vaccaro v. Kaiman (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 761, 768 [“When the defect which justifies striking a complaint is capable of cure, the court should allow leave to amend.”].) The burden is on the complainant to show the Court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Blank, supra, 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

Before filing a motion to strike, the moving party shall meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to the motion to strike. (CCP § 435.5(a).)

 

III.       DISCUSSION

Meet and Confer

             Defense counsel declares that, on April 9, 2026, the parties met and conferred regarding the instant demurrer and motion to strike via telephone. (Sabzerou Decl. ¶ 5.) The parties were unable to reach an agreement. (Ibid.) Accordingly, the Court finds that Cedar-Sinai satisfies the meet and confer requirements of CCP §§ 430.41(a) and 435.5(a).

 

Demurrer

            Cedars-Sinai demurs to Plaintiffs’ entire complaint on the grounds that (1) Plaintiffs’ claims are time-barred and (2) Plaintiffs fail to state sufficient facts to constitute a cause of action. (See Demurrer at pp. 2-3.)

 

Time-Barred

            First, the Court addresses Cedars-Sinai’s argument that Plaintiff’s complaint is time-barred. Cedars-Sinai argues that Plaintiffs’ claims are barred by the applicable statute of limitations for professional negligence pursuant to CCP § 340.5, which provides that a claim is barred “one year after the plaintiff discovers, or through the use of reasonable diligence should have discovered, the injury.” (CCP § 340.5.) “Under this statute, injury is not synonymous with wrongful act; rather, injury is a word of art which refers to the damaging effect of the wrongful act and not the act itself. [Citations.]” (Zambrano v. Dorough (1986) 179 Cal.App.3d 169, 172.) Notably, when determining the applicable running period for the statute of limitations, California courts consider “substance over the form” of the complaint, requiring courts to examine the nature of the professional relationship and whether the injury resulted from the provision of medical services. (Flores v. Presbyterian Intercommunity Hospital (2016) 63 Cal.4th 75.)

            Here, Plaintiffs’ original complaint was filed on May 20, 2014, and Plaintiffs’ FAC was filed on April 23, 2026. (See Complaints.) Notably, an amended complaint relates back to the original complaint when resting on the same general set of facts as the original complaint. (San Diego Gas & Electric Co. v. Superior Court (2007) 146 Cal.App.4th 1545.) Here, Plaintiffs’ complaints, while providing a limited set of facts, both arise from the allegedly wrongful death of Nadezhda Babitskaya on May 21, 2012, “[a]s a proximate result of continuing abuse and neglect.” (See Complaints.) Accordingly, Plaintiffs’ 2026 FAC relates back to their original complaint filed on May 20, 2014. Therefore, Plaintiffs’ complaint against Cedars-Sinai for professional negligence violates the one-year statute of limitations set by CCP § 340.5.

            In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that Cedars-Sinai ignores the language of CCP § 340.5 which provides a three-year statute of limitations after the date of injury. (See Opposition at 3:6-27.) However, CCP § 340.5 provides specifically that “[i]n an action for injury or death against a health care provider based upon such person’s alleged professional negligence, the time for commencement of action shall be three years after the date of injury or one year after the plaintiff discovers, or through the use of reasonable diligence should have discovered, the injury, whichever occurs first.” (CCP § 340.5)(emphasis added.) Here, considering Plaintiffs’ claims arise from the alleged wrongful death of Nadezha Babitskaya arising from “repeated infections requiring frequent hospitalization” (see Complaints), Plaintiffs’ own complaint admits that Plaintiffs knew or should have known of the injury by the date of Babitskaya’s death on May 21, 2012. Accordingly, the applicable statute of limitations is one year, pursuant to CCP § 340.5, and began to run on May 21, 2012.

            Plaintiffs also argue that “none of the claims in the FAC arise from professional negligence.” (Opposition at 3:24-27.) However, as discussed supra, California courts, when applying CCP § 340.5, consider “substance over the form” of the complaint, requiring courts to examine the nature of the professional relationship and whether the injury resulted from the provision of medical services. (Flores, supra, 63 Cal.4th at 75.) Here, Plaintiffs allege the following against Cedars-Sinai, a hospital:

“As a proximate result of continuing abuse and neglect of the deceased . . . while she was under the care and custody of . . . Cedars-Sinai Medical Center, Mrs. Babitskaya died on May 21, 2012 . . . Instead of providing necessary treatment and proper nutrition and hydration to Mrs. Babitskaya, defendants engaged in misuse of anti-psychotic and pain medications which they knew were unnecessary and harmful to the deceased. In addition, they failed to provide the required hygiene to her. Because of the neglect, she developed numerous pressure ulcers and suffered from repeated infections requiring frequent hospitalizations. Due to severe overmedication and untreated infection, Mrs. Babitskaya became unable to eat or drink, suffered cardiac arrest with subsequent coma and died due to denial of proper treatment and nutrition by Cedars-Sinai defendants.”

            (See Complaints.)

            Based on the foregoing, while Plaintiffs do not individually name a cause of action for professional negligence, the Court considers the complaint’s substance over form, which clearly alleges a professional relationship between Cedars-Sinai and Mrs. Babitskaya in the form of a medical caretaker. Accordingly, Plaintiffs’ argument fails.

            As a final matter, while Plaintiffs argue that the demurrer exceeds the length permitted in Cal. Rules of Court Rule 3.1113(d), the Court may frequently consider filings which fail to conform with the CRC.

            Based on the foregoing, Plaintiffs’ complaint is time-barred, and Cedars-Sinai’s demurrer to Plaintiffs’ entire complaint is sustained without leave to amend. (See Six4Three, LLC v. Facebook, Inc. (2025) 109 Cal.App.5th 635.)

 

Motion to Strike

            The Court sustains Cedars-Sinai’s demurrer to Plaintiffs’ complaint in its entirety without leave to amend. Accordingly, the motion to strike is moot.

 

IV.    CONCLUSION

Cedars-Sinai’s demurrer to Plaintiffs’ entire complaint is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.

Cedars-Sinai’s motion to strike is MOOT.

Cedars-Sinai is dismissed from the action with prejudice.

Cedars-Sinai to give notice.

III.       DISCUSSION

Meet and Confer

             Defense counsel declares that, in April 2026, he attempted to meet and confer telephonically with Plaintiffs on numerous occasions. (Hunter Decl. ¶¶ 6-9.) Plaintiffs did not respond to defense counsel’s attempts. (Ibid.) Accordingly, the Court finds that SR & DR satisfies the meet and confer requirements of CCP §§ 430.41(a) and 435.5(a).

            Notably, Plaintiffs argue that defense counsel “sabotaged” the meet and confer process because defense counsel offered some dates to meet and confer that were on the weekend. (See Opposition at p. 3.) However, defense counsel also provided various weekdays to meet and confer. (Ibid.) Plaintiffs failed to respond to those dates. (See Hunter Decl.) Accordingly, the Court finds Plaintiffs’ argument in this regard without merit.

 

Demurrer

            SR & DR demurs to Plaintiffs’ entire complaint on the grounds that the complaint is uncertain and Plaintiffs fail to state facts sufficient to constitute each cause of action. (See Demurrer at pp. 2-3.)

 

Standing

            First, SR & DR argues that Plaintiffs lack standing to bring survival claims as wrongful death heirs. (See Demurrer at 3:3-23.) Specifically, SR & DR argues that “[w]rongful death beneficiaries can bring a cause of action for wrongful death based on alleged medical negligence, but not a separate cause of action for medical negligence.” (Ibid. [citing CCP § 377.60].)

            CCP § 377.30 provides that a cause of action that survives the death of a person passes to the decedent’s successor in interest and is enforceable by the “decedent’s personal representative or, if none, by the decedent’s successor in interest.” (CCP § 377.30.) The damages recoverable by a personal representative or successor in interest on a decedent’s cause of action are limited by statute to “the loss or damage that the decedent sustained or incurred before death, including any penalties or punitive or exemplary damages that the decedent would have been entitled to recover had the decedent lived, and do not include damages for pain, suffering, or disfigurement.” (See CCP §§ 377.20 and 377.34.) To bring a survivor action, the complaint must include an affidavit or declaration under penalty of perjury providing the alleged successor-in-interest’s relationship, duty, contract, or fiduciary duty to the decedent. (See CCP § 377.60.)

            Here, Plaintiffs fail to provide any such declaration. Accordingly, pursuant to CCP § 377.60, Plaintiffs lack standing to bring their second through ninth causes of action.

            Based on the foregoing, SR & DR’s demurrer to Plaintiffs’ second, third, fourth, fifth, sixth, seventh, eighth, and ninth causes of action is sustained.

 

Wrongful Death

            Next, SR & DR demurs to Plaintiffs’ first cause of action for wrongful death on the grounds that Plaintiffs fail to plead the cause of action with the requisite specificity. (Demurrer at 5:11-21.) Wrongful death claims are statutory in nature, thereby requiring specificity in pleading. (See Lopez v. Southern California Rapid Transit District (1985) 40 Cal.3d 780, 795.) Here, Plaintiffs fail to provide sufficient facts as to what led to the death of Nadezhda Babitskaya to meet this heightened pleading standard. (See generally FAC).

            Based on the foregoing, SR & DR’s demurrer to Plaintiffs’ first cause of action for wrongful death is sustained.

 

Motion to Strike

            Next, SR & DR moves to strike Plaintiffs’ requests for punitive damages, prejudgment interest and attorney’s fees. (See Motion at p. 2.) As discussed supra, Plaintiffs fail to adequately plead any of their claims, thereby failing to plead facts warranting punitive damages, prejudgment interest, or attorney’s fees.

 

Leave to Amend

            When a demurrer is sustained, the Court determines whether there is a reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment. (Blank, supra, 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) When a plaintiff “has pleaded the general set of facts upon which his cause of action is based,” the court should give the plaintiff an opportunity to amend his complaint, since plaintiff should not “be deprived of his right to maintain his action on the ground that his pleadings were defective for lack of particulars.” (Reed, supra, 152 Cal.App.2d at 900.) Generally, the court will allow leave to amend on at least the first try, unless there is absolutely no possibility of overcoming the issue. (See Angie M., supra, 37 Cal.App.4th at 1227.)

            Here, Plaintiffs’ original complaint, filed in 2014, and Plaintiffs’ FAC, filed nearly twelve years later, both fail to plead any specific facts giving rise to liability. Therefore, the Court sustains SR & DR’s demurrer to Plaintiff’s entire complaint without leave to amend. The Court also grants SR & DR’s motion to strike without leave to amend.

 

IV.    CONCLUSION

SR & DR’s demurrer to Plaintiffs’ entire complaint is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.

SR & DR’s motion to strike is GRANTED without leave to amend.

Defendant SR & DR Operating Company, LLC is dismissed from the action with prejudice.

SR & DR Operating Company to give notice.
