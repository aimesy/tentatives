Case Number: 23STCV28393    Hearing Date: September 21, 2026    Dept: 406

 

LOS ANGELES SMSA LIMITED PARTNERSHIP dba VERIZON WIRELESS,

                       

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

JESUS GARCIA, et al.,

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  23STCV28393

  Hearing Date:  September 21, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

cross-complainant majestic enterprises, lp’s motions to compel further responses (CRS# 0009, 4205)  

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On November 20, 2023, Plaintiff Los Angeles SMSA Limited Partnership (Verizon) filed this action against Defendants Jesus Garcia, Marlene Garcia, Spectrum Cellular Management LLC, and Michael Flores. Verizon filed the operative First Amended Complaint on January 23, 2024, adding Defendants Doe 1 (as the personal representative of the Estate of Jesus Garcia), Majestic Enterprises LP, and Jesmar Properties Inc. The FAC asserts causes of action for (1) breach of contract, (2) breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, (3) forcible detainer, (4) specific performance, and (5) declaratory relief.

            The dispute arises from a lease between Verizon (lessee) and Jesus Garcia and Marlene Garcia (lessor) for the operation of a wireless communications facility (the Facility). In 2010, Jesus Garcia and Marlene Garcia quitclaimed their interest to Majestic Enterprises LP (Majestic), which became the landlord, although Jesus Garcia (and now his estate) and Marlene Garcia remained liable for breaches of the agreement. Verizon alleges that in 2021, Majestic began improperly restricting Verizon’s access to the premises and prevented Verizon from fully operating the Facility. In particular, Verizon’s contractors were prevented from entering the premises to perform necessary maintenance and modifications.

            On April 15, 2024, Majestic filed a cross-complaint against Verizon, with the operative Second Amended Cross-Complaint filed on December 10, 2024. The SACC asserts causes of action for (1) breach of contract, (2) trespass, (3) private nuisance, and (4) declaratory relief. Majestic alleges that Verizon encroached onto areas of the premises beyond the scope permitted by the lease. Majestic further alleges that Verizon breached the lease by operating equipment that violates the FCC’s guidelines for Radio Frequency (RF) emissions because Verizon failed to implement the required warnings or protective measures.

            On August 21, 2026, Majestic filed the instant two motions to compel Verizon’s further responses to Requests for Production, Set Two and Form Interrogatories, Set Two. Verizon filed oppositions on September 8, 2026. Majestic filed replies on September 14, 2026.

LEGAL STANDARD

Upon receiving responses to its discovery requests, the propounding party may move for an order compelling further responses if the responses are incomplete or evasive, or objections are without merit or too general. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300(a), 2031.310(a), 2033.290(a).)

A party “is entitled to demand answers to its interrogatories, as a matter of right . . . [and] the burden of justifying any objection and failure to respond remains at all times with the party resisting an interrogatory.” (Williams v. Sup. Ct. (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 541.) By contrast, the party seeking production of documents bears the initial burden of showing good cause through a fact-specific showing of relevance. (Kirkland v. Superior Court (2002) 95 Cal.App.4th 92, 98.) Once this showing is made, the burden shifts to the responding party to justify any objections. (Ibid.)

 

 

MEET AND CONFER

A motion to compel further must be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration demonstrating an attempt to resolve the matter informally. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300(b)(1), 2031.310(b), 2033.290(b).) The Court finds that Majestic has satisfied the meet and confer requirement. (See Dack Decl.)

DISCUSSION

I. Requests for Production, Set Two (CRS# 0009)

            a. Time Period

The dispute over the RFPs centers on Verizon’s refusal to produce documents predating March 2018. Verizon has limited its production according to the Court’s November 4, 2024 order limiting Spectrum’s discovery to 7 years. Majestic notes that the November 4 order pertained to different discovery requests by a different party. However, the same reasons that warranted limiting Spectrum’s discovery apply equally to the present dispute.

            Namely, Verizon first entered into a lease for the subject premises back in 1989, 37 years ago. The lease was subsequently amended in 1999, 2008, 2010, and 2019. This is an expansive timeframe, and not everything in that time period is relevant to the present action. The pleadings indicate that the dispute at the center of this action arose in 2021. Thus, Verizon reasonably limited its responses to March 2018 onward. Verizon also indicates it is willing to produce documents dating back to November 2017. (Opp. 7:14-22.) Majestic has not demonstrated good cause for information beyond this time period.

            Majestic argues that “discovery into Verizon’s actions that pre-date November 2017 may demonstrate Verizon’s patterns and practice as it relates to RF/EME mitigation measures, its understanding of its obligations with respect to the Subject Property and the Lease Agreement, its knowledge of its obligations to comply with FCC requirements, and more.” (Reply 8:15-18.) This is a vague, speculative assertion not amounting to good cause. November 2017 already predates the dispute in this action by several years. Majestic fails to show that a more expansive timeframe is necessary to satisfy the proffered rationale for obtaining the documents.

            Majestic also argues that any request not limited in time is otherwise narrowly tailored by subject matter. Not so. For example, the RFPs seek all communications between Verizon and various individuals/entities relating to the subject property at any time. The RFPs also seek all documents relating to negotiations with the owner of the property at any time. These requests are facially overbroad and not tailored to the issues in litigation.  

Therefore, the RFPs are limited to November 2017 onward.

            b. Objections

            Majetic takes issue with Verizon’s objections. However, Verizon is entitled to preserve objections while providing a substantive response. This is not a basis for compelling further responses.

            c. Proper Labeling

            “Any documents or category of documents produced in response to a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling shall be identified with the specific request number to which the documents respond.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.280(a).) Verizon’s production must comply with this rule, but this is not a defect with the written responses. Moreover, Verizon has since produced a letter identifying which documents correspond to each RFP. Therefore, this is not a reason to compel further responses.

II. Form Interrogatories, Set Two (CRS# 4205)

            Majestic takes issue with Verizon’s response to FROG No. 17.1(b) as it pertains to RFA Nos. 9, 12, and 22. Specifically, Majestic argues that the purported facts provided in Verizon’s response are evasive and fail to explain Verizon’s denials to the RFAs.

            However, Verizon responsively provided its reason for denying each RFA. The Court cannot compel a different response just because Majestic disagrees with the proffered reason. Verizon is required to provide a responsive answer, not to provide an unassailable justification for denying the RFAs. Verizon’s answer to FROG No. 17.1(b) is responsive because it provides Verizon’s reason for denying RFA Nos. 9, 12, and 22. Whether the stated facts ultimately justify the denials is not an issue for this motion.

CONCLUSION

            Cross-Complainant Majestic Enterprises, LP’s motion to compel further responses to Requests for Production, Set Two is GRANTED in part. Document production is limited to November 2017 onward.

Majestic’s motion to compel further responses to Form Interrogatories, Set Two is DENIED.

Sanctions are denied as the parties acted with substantial justification.

NADEEM MIRZA,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

TOM MORENO, et al.,

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  25STCV03109

  Hearing Date:  September 21, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

defendant abm industry groups, llc’s motion to compel compliance with subpoena

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On February 4, 2025, Plaintiff Nadeem Mirza filed this action against Defendants Tom Moreno, ABM Parking Services, Inc., and ABM Industry Groups, LLC. The complaint asserts (1) premises liability, (2) negligent hiring, and (3) assault and battery.

            The complaint stems from an alleged incident wherein Plaintiff was assaulted by Tom Moreno while working as a security agent for Cedars Sinai Medical Center. Tom Moreno and the ABM entities allegedly own or operate the premises where Plaintiff was injured.

            On July 17, 2026, Defendant ABM Industry Groups, LLC filed the instant motion to compel compliance with three subpoenas issued to Athens Administrators. No opposition has been filed.

DISCUSSION

            The subpoenas seek records pertaining to workers compensation claims Plaintiff filed while working for Athens. The proof of service indicates service of the motion to Plaintiff and Moreno. However, Athens was not served. Therefore, the motion is denied for lack of proper service.

CONCLUSION

            Defendant ABM Industry Groups, LLC’s motion to compel compliance with subpoena is DENIED.


Case Number: 26STCV14262    Hearing Date: September 21, 2026    Dept: 406

 

HILCE LLAMAS,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

JOEY RESTAURANTS LOS ANGELES INC., et al.,

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  26STCV14262

  Hearing Date:  September 21, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

defendant joey restaurant los angeles inc.’s demurrer and motion to strike

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On May 4, 2026, Plaintiff Hilce Llamas filed this action for negligence and premises liability. The complaint stems from injuries Plaintiff sustained while she was a patron at the restaurant, Joey DTLA. Specifically, Plaintiff was struck in the eye by glass shards when a waiter dropped a glass cup.

            On August 19, 2026, Joey Restaurant Los Angeles filed the instant demurrer and motion to strike. Plaintiff filed an opposition on September 8, 2026. Defendant filed a reply on September 14, 2026.

LEGAL STANDARD

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (Taylor v. City of Los Angeles Dept. of Water and Power (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 1216, 1228.) In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or by proper judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.30, subd. (a).) A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. (SKF Farms v. Superior Court (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 902, 905.) Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed. (Ibid.) The only issue involved in a demurrer hearing is whether the complaint, as it stands, unconnected with extraneous matters, states a cause of action. (Hahn, supra, 147 Cal.App.4th at 747.)

Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading, may serve and file a notice of motion to strike the whole or any part of that pleading. (Code Civ. Proc., § 435, subd. (b).) The court may, upon a motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike (1) any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading and (2) all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Id., § 436.) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (Id., § 437.)

MEET AND CONFER

Before filing a demurrer or a motion to strike, the demurring or moving party is required to meet and confer with the party who filed the pleading demurred to or the pleading that is subject to the motion to strike for the purposes of determining whether an agreement can be reached through a filing of an amended pleading that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 430.41, 435.5.) The Court finds that Defendant has satisfied the meet and confer requirement. (See Argoud Decl.)

DISCUSSION

I. Premises Liability

            a. Sufficient Facts

            “California law requires landowners to maintain land in their possession and control in a reasonably safe condition.” (Ann M. v. Pacific Plaza Shopping Center (1993) 6 Cal.4th 666, 674.) “The elements of a cause of action for premises liability are the same as those for negligence: duty, breach, causation, and damages.” (Castellon v. U.S. Bancorp (2013) 220 Cal.App.4th 994, 998.) “Ordinarily, negligence may be pleaded in general terms and the plaintiff need not specify the precise act or omission alleged to constitute the breach of duty.” (Lopez v. Southern Cal. Rapid Transit Dist. (1985) 40 Cal.3d 780, 795.)    

            Here, the complaint alleges that “the premises, glass cup, serving/bussing equipment, pathways of travel, flooring, seating arrangement, service routes, and lighting, were in a dangerous, defective, and/or unsafe condition.” The complaint further alleges that “[t]hese unsafe conditions, jointly and individually, created an unreasonable risk of harm to patrons, including Plaintiff, and contributed to the subject incident.” Lastly, the complaint alleges that “Defendants knew or should have known that the dangerous, defective, and unsafe conditions as described herein, including the risk associated with the handling and transportation of glassware in customer areas, yet failed to take reasonable steps to correct, remedy, guard against, or warn of such conditions.”

            These allegations sufficiently describe premises liability. The complaint establishes that the condition of the premises—unsafe pathways, floors, lighting, etc.—was hazardous, especially in relation to the risk of handling glassware. This allegedly led to the incident where a waiter dropped a glass cup, sending glass shards into Plaintiff’s eye. This is adequate under the general pleading standard for negligence.

            The demurrer demands evidentiary detail not required for pleading: “the Complaint contains no factual allegations identifying which of these conditions was allegedly dangerous, how any such condition was defective, how Defendants contributed to these defects, and how the alleged defect contributed to Plaintiff's injuries.” (Dem. 6:9-12.) The existing allegations sufficiently apprise Defendant of the nature of the claim.

           

 

b. Uncertainty

            A demurrer for uncertainty is disfavored and is only granted “if the pleading is so incomprehensible that a defendant cannot reasonably respond.” (A.J. Fistes Corp. v. GDL Best Contractors, Inc. (2019) 38 Cal.App.5th 677, 695.) A complaint does not need to be a “model of clarity” to survive a demurrer because most ambiguities can be clarified through discovery. (Ibid.)

            For the reasons discussed above, the complaint is not uncertain. Defendant argues that the complaint is uncertain because it “generally refers to Defendants as a single group throughout the Complaint, making it impossible for Defendants to distinguish why and in what capacity they are being sued.” (Dem. 7:9-10.) However, Defendant cites no authority precluding a plaintiff from referring to all defendants collectively in a pleading. All of the defendants allegedly “owned, leased, occupied, operated, managed, maintained, repaired, modified, and/or controlled the premises.” This allegation must be assumed true on a demurrer and sufficiently apprises Defendants of the asserted basis for liability. The precise relationship of each defendant to the subject premises, and the precise degree of its responsibility for Plaintiff’s injuries, are factual questions to be answered on the evidence, not on the pleadings.

            c. Duplicative

            “[M]odern rules of pleading generally permit plaintiffs to ‘set forth alternative theories in varied and inconsistent counts.’” (Klein v. Chevron U.S.A., Inc. (2012) 202 Cal.App.4th 1342, 1388.) A claim is duplicative if it “adds nothing to the complaint by way of fact or theory.” (Award Metals, Inc. v. Superior Court (1991) 228 Cal.App.3d 1128, 1135.)

            Defendant argues that the premises liability claim is duplicative of the negligence claim. However, the two counts raise distinct theories of liability even if they are based on the same facts. The premises liability claim pertains to the alleged dangerous condition of the premises, while the negligence claim separately asserts negligent handling of the glass and negligent hiring. Plaintiff is entitled to assert these theories in separate counts, even if they are both theories of negligence.

            d. Additional Entities

            Defendant argues that the complaint fails to assert a basis for liability against Earls Restaurant USA Inc. and Joey Cucina Restaurants. However, these other defendants are not involved in the instant demurrer, and Defendant articulates no basis for its standing to assert arguments on their behalf.

Moreover, as discussed above, the complaint alleges that each defendant “owned, leased, occupied, operated, managed, maintained, repaired, modified, and/or controlled the premises.” This is an allegation of ultimate fact which must be assumed true on a demurrer.

II. Motion to Strike

            Defendant moves to strike all references to “JOEY DTLA” as a separate defendant because Joey DTLA is merely Defendant’s fictitious name, not a separate entity subject to a lawsuit. However, the references to Joey DTLA are proper because that is the name of the restaurant where the incident occurred. Defendant fails to identify the “irrelevant, false, or improper matter” to be stricken. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 436.)

CONCLUSION

            Defendant Joey Restaurant Los Angeles Inc.’s demurrer is OVERRULED. The motion to strike is DENIED.


Case Number: 26STCV17220    Hearing Date: September 21, 2026    Dept: 406

 

665-673 MONTEREY PASS LLC,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

BEN AND AMY, LLC, et al.,

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  26STCV17220

  Hearing Date:  September 21, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

defendants’ demurrer to complaint

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On May 29, 2026, Plaintiff 665-675 Monterey Pass LLC filed this action for breach of contract against Defendants Ben and Amy, LLC; Ben Park; and Amy Park. The dispute stems from unpaid rent. Plaintiff sues all three Defendants for breach of the lease and additionally sues the individuals Ben Park and Amy Park for breach of guaranty.

            On August 12, 2026, Defendants filed the instant demurrer to the complaint. Plaintiff filed an opposition on September 8, 2026.

LEGAL STANDARD

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (Taylor v. City of Los Angeles Dept. of Water and Power (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 1216, 1228.) In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or by proper judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.30, subd. (a).) A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. (SKF Farms v. Superior Court (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 902, 905.) Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed. (Ibid.) The only issue involved in a demurrer hearing is whether the complaint, as it stands, unconnected with extraneous matters, states a cause of action. (Hahn, supra, 147 Cal.App.4th at 747.)

MEET AND CONFER

Before filing a demurrer or a motion to strike, the demurring or moving party is required to meet and confer with the party who filed the pleading demurred to or the pleading that is subject to the motion to strike for the purposes of determining whether an agreement can be reached through a filing of an amended pleading that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 430.41, 435.5.)

Here, Defendants appear to have abandoned the meet and confer process, as their counsel promised to follow up with Plaintiff upon performing additional research, but never did. (See Kuehl Decl.) However, “[a] determination by the court that the meet and confer process was insufficient shall not be grounds to overrule or sustain a demurrer.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41(a)(4).) Therefore, the Court proceeds on the merits.

DISCUSSION

I. Breach of Lease

            To establish breach of contract, a plaintiff must show: (1) the contract existed, (2) the plaintiff’s performance of the contract or excuse for nonperformance, (3) the defendant’s breach, and (4) the resulting damage to the plaintiff. (Richman v. Hartley (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 1182, 1186.)

            Here, the complaint alleges that Plaintiff and the three defendants entered into a written lease. (Compl. ¶ 9.) Plaintiff additionally entered into a guaranty with individual defendants Ben and Amy Park. (Id., ¶ 19.) Defendants allegedly failed to pay rent and other amounts due under the lease. (Id., ¶ 20.) Plaintiff allegedly performed its obligations under the lease. (Id., ¶ 32.) Plaintiff allegedly suffered damages of at least $300,000 consisting of unpaid rent, late charges, recovery costs, restoration expenses, etc. (Id., ¶ 36.) These facts sufficiently establish a breach of contract.

            Defendants argue that “Plaintiff fails to identify which specific defendants this cause of action is pelad [sic] against.” (Dem. 5:11-12.) However, the complaint clearly asserts a breach by all three defendants. Moreover, the lease attached to the complaint indicates that all three defendants are parties to the lease. (Compl, Ex. A.) Thus, the first cause of action has been adequately pled against all three defendants.

II. Breach of Guaranty

            The second cause of action is sufficiently pled for the same reasons discussed above. Defendants argue that the claim is uncertain because “the guaranty that Plaintiff attaches to the complaint and was incorporated by reference does not show that Defendant BEN PARK or Defendant AMY PARK signed the guaranty in their individual capacity.” (Dem. 3:25-28.) According to Defendants, “the signature block of the guaranty shows that Defendant BEN PARK and Defendant AMY PARK actually signed the guaranty merely as an ‘CEO’ and ‘COO’ of the entity.” (Ibid.)

            However, the allegations and the incorporated exhibit sufficiently establish that the individuals Ben and Amy Park are proper defendants. The complaint alleges that Ben and Amy Park entered into a personal guaranty. (Compl. ¶ 19.) The guaranty attached to the complaint identifies Ben and Amy Park individually as the guarantors. (Id., Ex. B.) The fact that the signature page of the guaranty identifies the individuals’ corporate titles does not mean they signed in that capacity. It is a question of fact in what capacity Ben and Amy Park signed the guaranty. The complaint sufficiently states a cause of action against Ben and Amy Park.

CONCLUSION

            Defendants’ demurrer is OVERRULED.
