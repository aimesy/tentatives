Allstate Insurance v. Rosa Griselda Hernandez Rodriguez et al.

Background

Plaintiff Allstate Insurance Company, as subrogee of Robert Rand, alleges that on or about August 7, 2022, Defendants negligently operated, maintained, controlled, entrusted, and/or drove motor vehicles so as to cause a collision involving insured property at 559 and 561 S. Soto Street, Los Angeles, CA 90033. The complaint pleads damages of $69,919.06, plus interest and costs. The revised damages amount claimed on the CIV-100 and the JUD-100, however, is $65,333.78.

Plaintiff now seeks default judgment against Edgar Munoz Flores only. Plaintiff settled with Defendants Rosa Griselda Hernandez Rodriguez and Lupita Laura Francisco for $5,000, which was deducted from the requested judgment amount, and that those defendants were dismissed from the complaint. After subtracting the settlement credit from, and adding the costs to, the revised damages amount, the JUD-100 now seeks a total of $61,589.03.

PROOF OF SERVICE/DEFAULT: Default of Edgar Munoz Flores previously entered on 6/25/2025.

PRINCIPAL REQUESTED: $ 65,333.78.

SETTLEMENT CREDIT: -$5,000.00

COSTS: $1,255.25.

TOTAL: $ 61,589.03.

ANALYSIS:

There is no pending motion to vacate default. Thus default has been properly entered.

The named defendants against whom default judgment is not sought have been dismissed, and Does 1-15 have been dismissed.

The JC Form CIV-100 was used to request court judgment, item 2 is completed, and the declaration of nonmilitary status requirement is filled out.

A memorandum of costs is verified on the CIV-100 form.

There were two declarations in support of default judgment submitted.

The requested damages do not exceed the complaint or Statement of Damages. The complaint and Statement of Damages both use $69,919.06, while the requested revised damages are $65,333.78, less than that, before the $5,000 settlement credit and costs are applied.

Edgar Munoz Flores was served by substituted service on February 12, 2025 at 2709 E. 7th Street, Los Angeles, CA 90023, through “John Doe,” a co-occupant who refused to give his name.

Deficiencies:

At the hearing on July 8, 2026, the Court flagged for Plaintiff that Plaintiff had filed an amendment to the Complaint after default was entered. The amendment was filed October 30, 2025, correcting “Francisco Lupita” to “Lupita Laura Francisco.” While this did not alter the allegations, in Plaintiff's supplemental filing, Plaintiff will need to address whether Plaintiff served the Amended Complaint against Edgar Munoz Flores. If not, Plaintiff will need to do so and take a default on the failure to respond to the Amended Complaint if there is no response.

The JUD-100 appears to place the $1,255.25 costs amount on the attorney-fees line, while the costs line is checked but left blank. Plaintiff is not actually seeking attorney’s fees. The JUD-100 should be corrected so $1,255.25 appears as costs, not attorney fees.

The packet includes a cross-complaint by Rosa/Lupita against Edgar and others, but it appears that cross-complaint has now been dismissed as of April 9, 2026.

At the hearing the Court also advised Plaintiff, that in insurance subrogation cases, the Court requires declaration from the insured or a percipient witness establishing liability and proper identification of the defendant. The packet only has the insurer’s claims representative and attorney declarations, and the liability facts are stated as based “on information and belief.” This is not sufficient admissible evidence to obtain a judgment.

Snyder states that Allstate paid $69,919.06, then says a recent invoice review supports a revised damage amount of $65,333.78. The attached payment ledger itself, however, shows the Total Amount Paid as $68,919.06, not $69,919.06.

Conclusion

The OSC is CONTINUED for Plaintiff to remedy the deficiencies. Plaintiff shall submit its updated default judgment package 10 days before the hearing.

Declarations in response to the Orders to Show Cause are due five court days before the hearings.

Case Number: 25STCV01982    Hearing Date: July 8, 2026    Dept: 224

Ahmadi v. Wilson et al.,

TENTATIVE RULING

The motion is DENIED as MOOT as to the Wilson Family Trust Dated December 20, 2002, no default having been entered against it.

The motion is DENIED as MOOT as to Defendant Evan N. Wilson, as he has been dismissed from this action.

The Motion to Set Aside Default is DENIED as to Kathleen D. Wilson.

Introduction

On January 22, 2025, Plaintiff Asma Alhamdi filed the Complaint in this action.

On September 25, 2025, Plaintiff effected personal service of the Summons and

Complaint on Defendants Kathleen D. Wilson and Evan N. Wilson.

On February 9, 2026, the Court entered the defaults of Defendants Kathleen D. Wilson and Evan N. Wilson.

On March 27, 2026, Defendants filed the instant Motion to Set Aside the Defaults Entered on February 9, 2026.

On May 6, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition to the Motion.

On May 7, 2026, the Court entered the default of Defendant Wilson Family Trust Dated December 20, 2002.

On May 8, 2026, Defendants filed a reply.

Legal Standard

Code of Civil Procedure, section 473, subdivision (b) provides for two distinct types of relief—commonly differentiated as “discretionary” and “mandatory”—from certain prior actions or proceedings in the trial court. (Luri v. Greenwald (2003) 107 Cal.App.4th 1119, 1124 (Luri).)

The discretionary portion of section 473, subdivision (b) provides: “The court may, upon terms as may be just, relieve a party or his or her legal representative from a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against him or her through his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect. Application for this relief shall be accompanied by a copy of the answer or other pleading proposed to be filed therein, otherwise the application shall not be granted, and shall be made within a reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months, after the judgment, dismissal, order, or proceeding was taken.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b).)

The mandatory portion of section 473 requires the court to, “whenever an application for relief is made no more than six months after entry of judgment, is in proper form, and is accompanied by an attorney’s sworn affidavit attesting to his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect, vacate any (1) resulting default entered by the clerk against his or her client, and which will result in entry of a default judgment, or (2) resulting default judgment or dismissal entered against his or her client, unless the court finds that the default or dismissal was not in fact caused by the attorney’s mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect.” (Code Civ.

Proc., § 473, subd. (b).)

Personal service may be accomplished by personally delivering a copy of the summons and complaint to the person to be served. (Code of Civ. Proc., § 415.10.) If a copy of the summons and complaint cannot with reasonable diligence be personally delivered to the person being served, substitute service may be effected by leaving a copy of the summons and complaint at the person’s “dwelling house, usual place of abode, usual place of business, or usual mailing address . . . in the presence of . . . a person apparently in charge . . . and by thereafter mailing a copy of the summons and complaint by first-class mail . . . to the person to be served at the place where a copy of the summons and complaint were left.” (Code of Civ. Proc., § 415.20, subd. (b).)

Motion to Set Aside Entry of Default

As an initial matter, the motion, by its terms, seeks to set aside “the defaults . . . entered on February 9, 2026.” The Court's record reflects, however, that no default was entered against the Wilson Family Trust Dated December 20, 2002 until May 7, 2026, nearly six weeks after this motion was filed on March 27, 2026. When Defendants filed the motion, there was no default against the Trust in existence to challenge, and the motion accordingly was not, and could not have been, directed at the May 7, 2026 default. A motion cannot operate prospectively to vacate a default that had not yet been entered when the motion was filed, and neither Plaintiff's opposition nor Defendant’ reply squarely addresses the later-entered default. In these circumstances, the Court declines to address the instant motion as to the Wilson Family Trust.

As to Defendant Evan N. Wilson, the Court’s records further confirm that Evan Wilson was dismissed from this action as of May 7, 2026. Accordingly, the Court proceeds on the merits below only as to Defendant Kathleen Wilson.

Relief Pursuant to CCP § 473(b)

Defendant first moves the Court to set aside the default pursuant to CCP § 473(b) on grounds of mistake, inadvertence, surprise, and excusable neglect.

As an initial matter, application for relief pursuant to CCP § 473(b) shall be accompanied by a copy of the answer or other pleading proposed to be filed therein, otherwise the application shall not be granted, and shall be made within a reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months, after the judgment, dismissal, order, or proceeding was taken.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b).) Defendant has attached a copy of the pleading she proposes to file — a proposed Answer with a general denial and affirmative defenses (Exhibit A).

The motion is also timely. The default of Kathleen Wilson was entered on February 9, 2026, and the motion was filed on March 27, 2026, well within the six-month period for discretionary relief under section 473(b).

To the extent that Defendant requests relief pursuant to the mandatory relief provision of CCP § 473(b), Defendant must file a declaration by Counsel attesting to Counsel’s mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect. Here, no such declaration has been filed. Counsel's declaration reflects that Mr. Horn was not retained until on or about March 18, 2026, after the February 9, 2026 defaults, and does not (and could not) attribute the defaults to his own fault. Mandatory relief is therefore inapplicable.

As to discretionary relief, Kathleen D. Wilson's own evidence establishes that she received actual notice of this lawsuit on or about January 31, 2025, more than thirteen months before the defaults were entered. (Wilson Decl. ¶ 4; Wong Decl. ¶ 5.) The evidence demonstrates that Wilson learned she had been sued, confirmed the filing through her brother’s review of the docket, and then, by her account, elected to wait to be formally served rather than appear or contact Plaintiff's counsel. (Wilson Decl. ¶¶ 5-7; Wong Decl. ¶ 5-6.) She took no further action until Wong observed the defaults on or about March 4, 2026. (Wilson Decl. ¶ 8; Wong Decl. ¶ 8.)

A party who has actual knowledge of a pending action for over a year is not “unexpectedly placed to his injury without any default or negligence of his own” when a default is later entered; that is not “surprise” within the meaning of section 473(b). (Credit Managers Assn. v. National Independent Business Alliance (1984) 162 Cal.App.3d 1166, 1173.) Nor does reliance on a relative to monitor the docket transform a prolonged, informed decision to do nothing into excusable neglect. (Hopkins & Carley v. Gens (2011) 200 Cal.App.4th 1401, 1410; Shapiro v. Clark (2008) 164 Cal.App.4th 1128 [reliance must be reasonable under the circumstances].) Based on the foregoing, the Court is not inclined to afford discretionary relief pursuant to section 473(b).

The Motion to Set Aside the Entry of Default based on CCP §473(b) is DENIED.

Equitable Relief

Further, Defendant requests the Court to exercise its inherent equitable power to set aside the entry of default based on extrinsic fraud.

“In addition, the court has inherent equitable power to set aside a default judgment at any time for extrinsic fraud or mistake.” (Ibid.) “The court may grant relief under its inherent equity power if, because of the fraud of his opponent, the aggrieved party was prevented from presenting his claim or defense to the court.” (Id. at p. 750, citations omitted.) “Fraud is intrinsic and not a valid ground for setting aside a judgment when the party has been given notice of the action and has had an opportunity to present his case and to protect himself from any mistake or fraud of his adversary, but has unreasonably neglected to do so.” (Ibid.)

A party seeking equitable relief on the grounds of extrinsic fraud or mistake must show the following three elements: (1) a meritorious defense; (2) a satisfactory excuse for not presenting the defense in the first place; and (3) diligence in seeking to set aside the default judgment once discovered. (Ibid.) Extrinsic mistake “is not so much the fraud or other misconduct of one of the parties as it is the excusable neglect of the defaulting party to appear and present his claim or defense. If that neglect results in an unjust judgment, without a fair adversary hearing, the basis for equitable relief on the ground of extrinsic mistake is present.” (Mechling v. Asbestos Defendants (2018) 29 Cal.App.5th 1241, 1246.) “When a default judgment has been obtained, equitable relief may be given only in exceptional circumstances.” (Ibid., emphasis in original.)

“When evaluating a motion to set aside a default judgment on equitable grounds, the court must weigh the reasonableness of the conduct of the moving party in light of the extent of the prejudice to the responding party.” (Lasalle v. Vogel (2019) 36 Cal.App.5th 127, 139, internal quotations omitted.)

Here, Kathleen Wilson has not carried her burden on any of the three required elements, and the equities do not favor relief. Although her proposed Answer arguably provides a meritorious defense, Wilson has not shown a satisfactory excuse for failing to appear. By her own evidence, she received actual notice of this action more than thirteen months before the defaults were entered, confirmed the filing through her brother's review of the docket, and then elected to wait to be served rather than appear or contact Plaintiff's counsel. (Wilson Decl. ¶¶ 4-7; Wong Decl. ¶¶ 5-6.) A defendant's informed decision to do nothing for over a year cannot support excusable neglect that underlies extrinsic mistake, and Wilson’s reliance on a relative to monitor the docket does not render that inaction reasonable. To the extent Wilson premises the request on extrinsic fraud, she has made no showing that any conduct of Plaintiff prevented her from presenting a defense. Rather, she had notice and an opportunity to protect herself and unreasonably neglected to do so, which is not a ground for equitable relief.

Equitable relief is not warranted in these circumstances.

Relief Pursuant to CCP § 473.5

Defendant further moves to set aside the entry of default pursuant to section 473.5 of the Code of Civil Procedure.

Code of Civil Procedure, section 473.5 states in relevant part:

When service of a summons has not resulted in actual notice to a party in time to defend the action and a default or default judgment has been entered against him¿or her¿in¿the¿action, he¿or she¿may serve and file a notice of motion to set aside¿the¿default or default judgment and for leave to defend the action.¿ The¿notice of motion shall be served and filed within a reasonable time, but in no event exceeding the earlier of: (i) two years after entry of a default judgment against him¿or her; or (ii) 180 days after service on him¿or her¿of a written notice that¿the¿default or default judgment has been entered.

(Code Civ. Proc., § 473.5, subd. (a).) Additionally, the motion “shall be accompanied by an affidavit showing under oath that the party's lack of actual notice in time to defend the action was not caused by his or her avoidance of service or inexcusable neglect. The party shall serve and file with the notice a copy of the answer, motion, or other pleading proposed to be filed in the action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 473.5, subd. (b).)

Knowledge of the action from a source other than the summons does not automatically bar relief, and fragmentary awareness, such as a lis pendens that does not disclose the causes of action or a claim for punitive damages, may be too incomplete to constitute actual notice or to afford a genuine time to defend. (Olvera v. Olvera (1991) 232 Cal.App.3d 32, 40–41.)

The question is not whether Defendant knew that some lawsuit existed, but whether her non-service knowledge was complete and timely enough to give her a genuine opportunity to defend. The Court finds that it was. Here, Wilson provides that she learned on or about January 31, 2025 that this Plaintiff had sued her by name, and she promptly confirmed the case and January 22, 2025 filing date through her brother, attorney Colin Wong, who reviewed the docket. (Wilson Decl. ¶¶ 4-6; Wong Decl. ¶¶ 5-6.) Through counsel with docket access, she had the means to learn the nature of the claims at any time during the following thirteen months. That is not the incomplete awareness Olvera found insufficient. It is the timely, actionable notice that supports denial of relief.

Wilson also cannot satisfy the statute's requirement that her lack of actual notice "was not caused by . . . inexcusable neglect." (Code Civ. Proc., § 473.5, subd. (b).) Having learned she was sued and confirmed the filing through counsel, she elected to wait to be served and took no protective step for over a year. (Wilson Decl. ¶¶ 7-8; Wong Decl. ¶¶ 6, 8.) Any gap in her notice resulted from that inaction.

The Motion to Set Aside the Entry of Default based on CCP § 473.5 is DENIED.

Conclusion

The motion is DENIED as moot as to the Wilson Family Trust Dated December 20, 2002, no default having been entered against it.

The motion is DENIED as moot as to Defendant Evan N. Wilson, as he has been dismissed from this action.

The Motion to Set Aside Default is DENIED as to Kathleen D. Wilson.

Case Number: 25STCV03956    Hearing Date: July 8, 2026    Dept: 224

Delgado v Carolyn Francis Delgado

TENTATIVE RULING

Defendant’s Motion to Determine Value and Send Buyout Notice is GRANTED IN PART AND DENIED IN PART without prejudice.

Background

Plaintiff Daniel Delgado filed this complaint on February 11, 2025, against Defendant Carolyn Francis Delgado alleging a cause of action for Partition by Sale of Real Property.

On July 9, 2025, Defendant Carolyn Francis Delgado filed the instant motion to Determine Value and Send Buyout Notice.

On June 24, 2026, Plaintiff Daniel Delgado filed an opposition.

On June 30, 2026, Defendant Carolyn Francis Delgado filed a reply.

Legal Standard

Code of Civil Procedure (CCP) § 874.316 states that:

(a) “[e]xcept as otherwise provided in subdivisions (b) and (c), the court shall determine the fair market value of the property by ordering an appraisal pursuant to subdivision (d).”

(d) “If the court orders an appraisal, the court shall appoint a disinterested real estate appraiser licensed in the State of California to determine the fair market value of the property assuming sole ownership of the fee simple estate. On completion of the appraisal, the appraiser shall file a sworn or verified appraisal with the court.”

(f) “If an appraisal is filed with the court pursuant to subdivision (d), the court shall conduct a hearing to determine the fair market value of the property not sooner than 30 days after a copy of the notice of the appraisal is sent to each party under subdivision (e), whether or not an objection to the appraisal is filed under paragraph (3) of subdivision (e). In addition to the court-ordered appraisal, the court may consider any other evidence of value offered by a party.”

(g) “After a hearing under subdivision (f), but before considering the merits of the partition action, the court shall determine the fair market value of the property and send notice to the parties of the value.”

(CCP § 874.316, subds. (a), (d), (f), (g).)

“The court shall apportion the costs of partition among the parties in proportion to their interests or make such other apportionment as may be equitable.” (CCP § 874.040.)

“If any cotenant requested partition by sale, the court shall, after the determination of value under § 874.316, send notice to the parties that any cotenant except a cotenant that requested partition by sale may buy all the interests of the cotenants that requested partition by sale.” (CCP § 874.317, subd. (a).) “Not later than 45 days after the notice is sent under subdivision (a), any cotenant except a cotenant that requested partition by sale may give notice to the court that it elects to buy all the interests of the cotenants that requested partition by sale.” (CCP § 874.317, subd. (b).) “If the court sends notice to the parties under paragraph (1) or (2) of subdivision (d), the court shall set a date, not sooner than 60 days after the date the notice was sent, by which electing cotenants shall pay their apportioned price into the court.” (CCP § 874.317, subd. (e).)

“If the court does not order partition in kind under subdivision (a), the court shall order partition by sale pursuant to Section 874.320 or, if no cotenant requested partition by sale, the court shall dismiss the action.” (CCP § 874.318, subd. (b).)

Discussion

Defendant moves for an Order to (1) to appoint an appraiser to determine the fair market value of the real property located at 3629 Beck Ave. Bell, CA 90201 (the “Property”), (2) assign the costs of appraisal to Plaintiff and set a deadline for their payment, (3) set a hearing to determine the fair market value, or alternatively to order the Property to be sold, and (4) send a buyout notice with a deadline for compliance under CCP § 872.720, 872.810, 873.010, 874.316, 874.317, 874.318. (Defendant’s Mot; Defendant’s Memo.)

Defendant argues that this action is subject to the Partition of Real Property Act (“PRPA”) because this action was filed after January 1, 2023, the Property is held in tenancy in common, and there is no agreement between the parties which governs the partition. (Defendant’s Memo., 3:19-21; Defendant’s Mot.) 

Defendant requests that the Court appoint an appraiser to conduct an appraisal of the Subject Property pursuant to CCP § 874.31 6, subd. (d). (CCP § 874.316, subd. (d).) Defendant asks the Court to enter an order appointing Tom Berge to determine the Subject Property’s value, and requiring Plaintiff to pay the appraisal’s cost. Finally, Defendant requests that in the event Plaintiff fails to pay the Appraiser’s fees within 20 days of this hearing or fails to provide the Appraiser with access to the Property within 30 days of this hearing, that the Court order the Property to be sold under CCP § 874.318. (CCP § 874.318.)(Defendant’s Memo; Defendant’s Reply.)

In opposition, Plaintiff first argues that Defendant improperly names Tom Berge without any reference to his qualifications. Plaintiff also contends that the PRPA does not allow for a party to choose their appraiser and that instead, CCP § 874.316, subd. (d) directs the Court to “appoint a disinterested real estate appraiser…”(CCP § 874.316, subd. (d).) Plaintiff points out that this concern is heightened as Defendant is a “licensed California real estate agent with approximately twenty-two years of experience, with the professional knowledge and industry relationships to identify and engage an appraiser within her own network.” (Plaintiff’s Opp., 4:25-28) Plaintiff suggests that the Court instead appoint an appraisal management company ("AMC"). Plaintiff also opposes Defendant’s request that Plaintiff pay the full costs for an appraisal. Lastly, Plaintiff opposes Defendant’s request that the Court order the property to be sold if Plaintiff fails to pay her appraiser within twenty (20) days or provide access within thirty (30) days. (Plaintiff’s Opp.)

Defendant replies that she has never argued that an appraiser was mandatory. Moreover, Defendant contends that Plaintiff fails to provide evidence regarding Defendant’s alleged bias in suggesting Tom Berge, as required by Evid. Code § 500. (Evid. Code § 500.) Defendant opposes Plaintiff’s request to appoint an AMC, because of the additional costs to the parties over a traditional appraiser. Lastly, Defendant opposes Plaintiff’s request to strike Defendant’s 20/30-day deadline, claiming that Defendant is entitled to request a sale of the Property if the PRPA procedures are not complied with. (Defendant’s Mot., Defendant’s Memo., Defendant’s Reply.)

Under the PRPA, the Court finds that it is appropriate to appoint an appraiser under CCP § 874.316, subd. (a) and (d) (CCP § 874.316, subd. (a), (d).). However, due to the parties' deficient filings lacking any relevant evidence, the Court is unable to appoint an appraiser at this time. The Court orders Plaintiff to file suppelemental evidence and briefing establishing (1) the cost difference between hiring an AMC as opposed to a traditional appraiser, and (2) evidence in support of the allegations that Tom Berge is a biased appraiser.  Additionally, the Court orders Defendant to provide evidence regarding (1) Tom Berge’s qualifications to serve as a disinterested appraiser in the matter. The Court will appoint an appraiser after the gaps in evidence have been filled.

Turning to the cost of the appraisal, Defendant requests that Plaintiff cover the entirety of the cost. In ordering the appraisal, the Court follows CCP § 874.040: “the court shall apportion the costs of partition among the parties in proportion to their interests.” (CCP § 874.040.) The Court is not persuaded by Defendant’s argument that the appraisal only benefits Plaintiff and that therefore, only Plaintiff should bear the costs. Pursuant to CCP § 874.040. The Court denies Defendant’s request and orders that the costs of the appraisal be divided by the parties’ respective 50% interests and that it be paid from escrow. (CCP § 874.040.)

Finally, Defendant requested that if the Plaintiff fails to pay the Appraiser’s fees within 20 days of this hearing or fails to provide the Appraiser with access to the Property within 30 days of this hearing, that the Court order the Property to be sold under CCP § 874.318. (CCP § 874.318.) Plaintiff raises that nothing in the PRPA authorizes conditioning a cotenant's substantive rights on that cotenant's payment of another party's costs. CCP § 874.316 through 874.318 contain no such penalty. Plaintiff further contends that Defendant’s demand is self-defeating because an order selling the Property is the very relief Plaintiff requested in his complaint. Defendant contends that under CCP § 128, subd. (a)(4), the Court has the power to “compel obedience to its judgment, orders, and process…” (CCP § 128, subd. (a)(4).) The Court finds that Defendant’s position is premature, as no financial or physical obstruction by Plaintiff has occurred. Defendant can move for a court order at a later time in the event that Plaintiff fails to comply with his obligations to pay the appraiser or provide the appraiser with physical access to the Property. The Court denies Defendant’s requested 20/30-day deadline. (Plaintiff’s Opp., Defendant’s Mot., Defendant’s Memo., Defendant’s Reply.)

Accordingly, the Court orders Plaintiff to provide evidence regarding (1) the cost of hiring an AMC as opposed to a traditional appraiser, and (2) the allegations regarding Defendant’s alleged bias if Plaintiff wishes to oppose the appointment of Tom Berge. The Court also orders Defendant to supplement evidence regarding (1) Tom Berge’s qualifications to serve as a disinterested appraiser in the matter. The Court will appoint an appraiser after the gaps in evidence have been filled. The Court also orders that the costs of the appraisal be split by the parties per their respective 50% interests in the Subject Property and paid from escrow. (CCP § 874.040.) Once the appraisal is completed and filed with the Court, the Court will proceed as required under CCP § 874.316, subd. (e), (f), (g). (CCP § 874.316 subd. (e), (f), (g).) The Court otherwise DENIES the motion without prejudice.

To allow the evidence to be filed by the parties, the Court will continue the hearing set for July 8th, 2026, regarding the instant motion to September 21st, 2026, at 9:00am in Dept. 224. Supplemental filings and evidence are due no later than September 10, 2026.

Based on the foregoing, Defendant’s Motion to Determine Value and Send Buyout Notice is GRANTED IN PART AND DENIED IN PART without prejudice.

Case Number: 25STCV28000    Hearing Date: July 8, 2026    Dept: 224

PFC Holdings v. Nanula

FACTS OF THE CASE: Plaintiff alleges Defendant Richard D. Nanula executed and delivered a promissory note on or about August 16, 2018 in the original principal amount of $1,722,750.65, and that the note required repayment of the principal plus interest at 5% per year. The complaint alleges that the note was later modified by a Forbearance Agreement effective June 17, 2022, which extended the maturity date to December 31, 2023 and stated the Note's unpaid principal as $1,698,032.49. Plaintiff alleges Defendant failed to pay the outstanding balance at maturity despite demand, thereby breaching the loan documents. The complaint pleads that, as of August 31, 2025, Defendant owed $2,017,149.36, consisting of $1,698,032.49 in principal and $319,116.87 in interest, plus further interest, attorney’s fees, and costs. The JUD-100 requests a total amount of $2,061,019.92 in accordance with said further interest, attorney’s fees, and costs.

PROOF OF SERVICE/DEFAULT: Default previously entered on 11/14/2025

PRINCIPAL REQUESTED: $ 1,698,032.49

INTEREST: $ 343,485.36

ATTORNEY’S FEES: $ 18,870.32

COSTS: $631.75

TOTAL: $ 2,061,019.92

ANALYSIS:

Entry of default is not void as the plaintiff did not file an amended complaint after default was entered, and the defendant did not appear before default was entered.

There is no pending motion to vacate default.

Plaintiff has dismissed DOES 1-25, the only parties against whom a default judgment is not sought.

The JC Form CIV-100 was used to request court judgment, item 2 is completed, and the declaration of nonmilitary status requirement is filled out.

A memorandum of costs is verified on the CIV-100 form, and the costs requested are allowable under CCP §1033.5(a).

Plaintiff submitted a proposed judgment on a JUD-100 form. The JUD-100 amounts match the CIV-100.

There were two supporting declarations submitted, from Doepke and Simon respectively.

The judgment total exceeds the caption's "Demand Amount: $2,017,149.36" by about $44,000 dollars, but the excess is expressly prayed-for further interest, fees, costs.

The complaint alleges entitlement to attorney’s fees under the loan documents. Jessica Simon’s declaration calculates attorney’s fees under LASC Local Rule 3.214 based on the principal amount of $1,698,032.49: $2,890 plus $15,980.32 (1% of the excess over $100,000), for a total of $18,870.32. This matches the amount sought on the CIV-100 and JUD-100.

The complaint alleges entitlement to interest. The principal amount is fixed. An interest calculation was provided. Plaintiff used the lower contract rate rather than the 10% statutory rate.

The complaint states valid claims for breach of contract and money lent. It alleges the promissory note, the forbearance of said note, Defendant’s failure to pay at maturity, the amount of unpaid principal/interest sought, and Plaintiff’s entitlement to attorney’s fees and costs.

Deficiencies

The packet includes copies of the Note as exhibits, but the packet does not contain the original note, a lost-note declaration, or a proposed order accepting copies in lieu of originals. “California Rules of Court, rule 3.1806 applies to negotiable instruments… Rule 3.18064 requires the clerk to undertake certain obligations with respect to a specific category of judgments, namely promissory notes and other written obligations to pay money.” (HSBC Bank Nevada, N.A. v. Aguilar (2012) 205 Cal.App.4th Supp. 6, 10.) Because this action is based on a promissory note, Plaintiff must lodge the original note for cancellation or submit a declaration explaining the loss or unavailability of the original note.

Plaintiff should also address in a legal brief, whether the promissory note complies with state or local usury laws.

The Court also reserves the possibility that given the principal and damages sought on a default judgment, it may require an in-person prove-up hearing.

DISPOSITION:
