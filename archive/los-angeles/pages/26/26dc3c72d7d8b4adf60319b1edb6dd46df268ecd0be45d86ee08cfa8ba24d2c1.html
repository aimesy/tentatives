Background

On November 7, 2023, Plaintiff Kevin Ip as an individual and as trustee of the Ip Residence Trust (A) (“Ip”) filed a complaint against Defendants Yuna Chen (“Chen”), Wendy Trinh Tran (“Tran”) (jointly, “Defendants”), and all persons unknown claiming an interest in the property at issue alleging four causes of action for (1) quiet title, (2) elder abuse, (3) conversion, and (4) breach of contract.

On February 9, 2024, Defendants filed a cross-complaint against Ip and all persons unknown claiming an interest in the property at issue. On July 26, 2024, Defendants filed a first amended cross-complaint alleging (1) quiet title, (2) breach of contract and specific performance, (3) fraud, (4) declaratory relief, (5) trespass, (6) intentional infliction of emotional distress, and (7) breach of contract and specific performance. On February 28, 2025, the Court sustained Ip’s demurrer to the second cause of action without leave to amend. On July 10, 2026. Defendants filed a second amended cross-complaint (“TACC”) alleging the same causes of action as in the first amended cross-complaint, plus the addition of a fraudulent conveyance cause of action.

Ip now moves for summary adjudication as to his first and fourth causes of action for quiet title and breach of contract, respectively, as well as Defendants’ first, third, and fourth causes of action for quiet title, fraud, and declaratory relief. Defendants oppose. Ip replied.[1]

Allegations of the Complaint

The complaint alleges, inter alia, that Ip took title to the property located at 412 N. San Marino Ave., San Gabriel, California (the “Property”) pursuant to a grant deed dated December 13, 1997. (Compl., ¶¶ 1, 14.) “Ip transferred title to the Kevin Ip Family Trust dated July 19, 2007, by grant deed dated July 19, 2007. … Ip transferred title again from the Kevin Ip Family Trust dated July 19, 2007, to the Ip Residence Trust (A), by quitclaim deed dated August 9, 2023.” (Compl., ¶ 14.) Ip agreed to rent the Property to Chen for $2,000 per month.” (Compl., ¶ 16.) “On October 15, 2014, Ip and Chen entered into a California Association of Realtors (‘CAR’) Residential Lease or Month-to-Month Rental Agreement for the Property, whereby Chen agreed to lease the Property from Ip for $2,000 per month (the ‘First Lease’).” (Compl., ¶ 17.) “Ip never agreed to sell the Property to Chen and the two never entered any agreement whereby Chen would obtain any ownership interest in the Property.” (Compl., ¶ 16.) “When the First Lease terminated on October 31, 2019, Chen stayed in the Property with her daughter, Tran. Chen and Tran stopped paying rent, and has not paid Ip for more than 24 months of back rent since she has lived in the Property, convinced Ip to pay their debts and expenses, and exerted undue influence over Ip to convince him to give Tran and Chen cash, gifts, and property for their personal benefit.” (Compl., ¶ 23.) “In or around January 2023, Ip and Chen’s romantic relationship began to end.  Legal Standard

“[T]he party moving for summary judgment bears the burden of persuasion that there is no triable issue of material fact and that he is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850.) A triable issue of material fact exists if the evidence would allow a reasonable trier of fact to find the underlying fact in favor of the party opposing the motion in accordance with the applicable standard of proof. (Ibid.)

“When deciding whether to grant summary judgment, the court must consider all of the evidence set forth in the papers (except evidence to which the court has sustained an objection), as well as all reasonable inferences that may be drawn from that evidence, in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment.” (Avivi v. Centro Medico Urgente Medical Center (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 463, 467; Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c).)

A motion for summary judgment or adjudication’s function is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party cannot show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co., supra, 25 Cal.4th at p. 843.) In analyzing such motions, courts must apply a three-step analysis: “(1) identify the issues framed by the pleadings; (2) determine whether the moving party has negated the opponent’s claims; and (3) determine whether the opposition has demonstrated the existence of a triable, material factual issue.” (Hinesley v. Oakshade Town Center (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 289, 294.) Summary judgment or adjudication is proper when “all the papers submitted show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to a judgment as a matter of law.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c); see also Villa v. McFerren (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 733, 741.)

The moving party bears the initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing that there are no triable issues of material fact. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co., supra, 25 Cal.4th at p. 850.) As to each claim as framed by the complaint, the defendant moving for summary judgment or adjudication must satisfy the initial burden of proof by presenting affirmative evidence to disprove at least one essential element of the plaintiff’s cause of action, show that an element cannot be established, or to establish an affirmative defense. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2); Sanchez v. Swinerton & Walberg Co. (1996) 47 Cal.App.4th 1461, 1465.)

Once the moving defendant meets its burden for a particular cause of action, the burden shifts to the nonmoving plaintiff. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) The plaintiff may defeat the motion by producing evidence showing that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to that cause of action. (Ibid.)

On a motion for summary judgment or adjudication, the moving party’s supporting documents are strictly construed, while the nonmoving party’s documents are liberally construed. (D’Amico v. Board of Medical Examiners (1974) 11 Cal.3d 1, 24.) Any “doubts as to the propriety of granting the motion should be resolved in favor of the party opposing the motion.” (Ibid.)

Discussion

            Ip moves for summary adjudication on the first and fourth causes of action on his complaint and on the first, third, and fourth causes of action on Defendants’ TACC.

 

 

A.    Undisputed Facts

Both the complaint and cross-complaint pertain to real property located at “412 N. San Marino Ave., San Gabriel, California, 91755, APN 5366-025-002 (hereinafter the ‘Property’), more particularly described as follows: Lot 2 of Tract No: 9223, as per map recorded in Book 148 Pages 13 and 14 of Maps, in the Office of the County Recorder of Los Angeles County.” (UMF ¶¶ 1, 69; Ip Decl., ¶ 2.) The parties do not contest the existence of the December 1997 grant deed, July 2007 grant deed, and August 2023 quitclaim deed. (UMF, ¶¶ 2-4, 70-72.) Chen and her children moved into the Property in October 2014 and paid rent until mid-2017. (UMF, ¶¶ 14, 17.)

Chen did not know she was in a UPS office, instead of an escrow office, where the parties notarized a document, the purpose of which is disputed by the parties. (UMF, ¶¶ 35-37, 53-54.) Chen’s two children worked at the GNC store, which went bankrupt in 2020. (UMF, ¶¶ 45-46, 63-64.)

B.     Quiet Title

Both Ip and Defendants allege quiet title as their first cause of action in the complaint and TACC.

An action for quiet title seeks “to establish title against adverse claims to real or personal property or any interest therein.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 760.020, subd. (a).) In an action for quiet title, Plaintiff must plead (1) “[a] description of the property that is the subject of the action,” specifically the location of tangible personal property and the legal description and street address or common designation of real property, (2) “[t]he title of the plaintiff as to which a determination under this chapter is sought and the basis of the title,” (3) “[t]he adverse claims to the title of the plaintiff against which a determination is sought,” (4) “[t]he date as of which the determination is sought,” and (5) “[a] prayer for the determination of the title of the plaintiff against the adverse claims.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 761.020, subds. (a)-(e).)

Ip contends that Defendants’ cause of action is “barred by the applicable three-year statute of limitation.” (Mot., 1:26.) Ip contends that “the underlying theory of relief is defendant Chen’s claim for fraud,” which has a three-year statute of limitations. (Mot., 15:7-8.) However, Ip does not support his position for why Defendants’ quiet title’s underlying theory of relief is Ip’s alleged fraudulent activity as opposed to full performance of an agreement to purchase the property. Moreover, as discussed infra, summary adjudication for fraud based on the statute of limitations is not granted.

With respect to the elements of the quiet title cause of action, Ip attaches Defendants’ responses to special interrogatories, where Defendants concede that Ip is the Property’s owner. (Azat Decl., ¶¶ 2-3, Ex. A, 2:12, Ex. B, 2:13.) It is undisputed that Defendants did not plead in the TACC that Chen made a written purchase agreement for the Property, suggesting that there was no transfer of title promised or pending. (UMF, ¶¶ 9, 77, 133.) The discovery responses and the affirmative evidence that Chen did not make a written purchase agreement indicate that Ip meets his initial burden demonstrating that Defendants cannot establish the essential element of Chen’s title or interest in the Property.

Defendants note that although Ip is the owner of record, whether Ip is the rightful owner is disputed. Defendants present evidence that Chen offered to buy the Property for $650,000 and agreed to various terms with Ip regarding the property. (Chen Decl., ¶ 8.) The agreement between Chen and Ip was verbal, but Ip dictated the words Chen should write down in an email addressed to him to confirm the terms of the purchase agreement. (Chen Decl., ¶ 9; Ex. 1.) Chen explains that Ip dictated the words in the email and Ip “confirmed receipt and his assent verbally to” Chen. (Ibid.) The confirmation email of Chen and Ip’s agreement states, inter alia, “[p]lease help transfer the house to me within five years at the price of 680,000.” (Chen Decl., ¶ 9; Ex. 1.)

Defendants also present evidence that Ip told Chen she was signing legal documents for the purchase agreement in December of 2014, which referred to the $560,000 down payment for the Property. (Chen Decl., ¶ 11.) However, Chen instead signed an indemnification agreement. (Ibid.) Due to Chen’s limited understanding of English, she was unaware of the document’s true nature because Ip did not provide Chen with a translation or copy of the agreement. (Ibid.) Additionally, Defendants provide evidence of a chart of payments Chen sent to Ip, which includes a check payment in the amount of $560,000, made to Ip in 2014. (Chen Decl., Ex. 6.) Defendants also provide evidence that Chen paid property tax bills for the property. (Chen Decl., Exs. 2 & 3.) The presented evidence demonstrates that the true owner of the property is still in dispute.

Ip also asserts that summary adjudication is proper for quiet title because the property is held in an irrevocable trust. (Ip Decl., ¶ 5, Exs. C, I.) However, Defendants dispute the validity of the transfer. Chen “laid claim to the Property under a constructive trust due to Kevin Ip’s fraud.” (Chen Decl., ¶ 23.) Additionally, Defendants point to the lis pendens they filed on the property, which contests Ip’s title and right to the property. (Chen Decl., ¶ 23, Ex. 8.) Although Ip transferred the property to an irrevocable trust, Civil Code section 3439.04, subdivision (a)(1), provides that a “transfer made or obligation incurred by a debtor is voidable as to a creditor, whether the creditor’s claim arose before or after the transfer was made or the obligation was incurred, if the debtor made the transfer or incurred the obligation … [w]ith actual intent to hinder, delay, or defraud any creditor of the debtor.” The dispute over the validity of the transfer does not make summary adjudication proper based on the Property being held in an irrevocable trust.

Accordingly, triable issues of material fact as to the quiet title cause of action persist in both the complaint and TACC. Ip’s motion for summary adjudication is denied as to this cause of action.

C.     Breach of Contract

Ip alleges breach of contract as his fourth cause of action in the complaint. To state a cause of action for breach of contract, Plaintiff must be able to establish “(1) the existence of the contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance, (3) defendant’s breach, and (4) the resulting damages to the plaintiff.” (Oasis West Realty, LLC v. Goldman (2011) 51 Cal.4th 811, 821.)

Ip’s cause of action for breach of contract arises from the indemnification agreement the parties entered into, where Chen “agreed to indemnify, release, and hold harmless Ip from any and all future claims or liabilities that may arise from her payment of $560,000 to Ip for investment purposes.” (Compl., ¶ 56.)

Ip points to the existence of the indemnification agreement, which states that “[o]n November 5th, 2014, YUNA CHEN issued a bank check # 103 in the amount of $560,000.00. The subject instrument was made payable to Kevin Ip.” (Ip Decl., ¶ 11, Ex. F, IP000529.) The woman who notarized the agreement attests that “Yuna Chen presented herself before me on December 10, 2014, confirmed her identity to me, and executed in my presence two documents entitled ‘Indemnification.’” (Osorio Decl., ¶ 4; Ex. A.)

However, Defendants point to Chen’s declaration, which, as noted above, states that she did not read the document because it was in English, which she has very limited knowledge of and did not receive a translation of. (Chen Decl., ¶ 11.) Chen attests that Ip “claimed I had to sign legal documents for the Purchase Agreement at what he called an ‘escrow office’. I signed what I understood was purchase paperwork before a notary, which referred to the $560,000 down payment for the Property.” (Ibid.) Chen also attests that “[t]he $560,000 was solely for the purchase of the Property and I have never asked or allowed Kevin Ip to invest any money for me in any other way. I would never invest in Kevin Ip’s GNC franchise, or in the Chinese stock market, as he claims now.” (Chen Decl., ¶ 12.) Chen’s special interrogatory responses also state that the indemnification agreement was for the purchase of the property, and she did not know the true purpose of it.” (Azat Decl., Ex. A, 15:12-14.)

Defendants demonstrate a triable issue of material fact because if Ip fraudulently induced Chen to sign the indemnification agreement, then the agreement would be invalid. Under California law, “[a]ll contracts which have for their object, directly or indirectly, to exempt any one from responsibility for his own fraud, or willful injury to the person or property of another, or violation of law, whether willful or negligent, are against the policy of the law.” (Civ. Code, § 1668.) Accordingly, summary adjudication is not proper here.

 

 

D.    Fraud

            Defendants allege fraud as their third cause of action in the TACC. “The elements of fraud are (a) a misrepresentation (false representation, concealment, or nondisclosure); (b) scienter or knowledge of its falsity; (c) intent to induce reliance; (d) justifiable reliance; and (e) resulting damage.” (Hinesley v. Oakshade Town Center (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 289, 294.) “The statute of limitations for fraud is three years.” (Cansino v. Bank of America (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 1462, 1472.)

Ip contends that Defendants’ “fraud claim is barred by the th[r]ee-year statute of limitations.” (Mot., 15:26.) Ip asserts the statute of limitations defense based on three alternative timelines Chen has advanced, as follows. First, if Chen had paid rent from the move-in date of 2014 to mid-2017, made a down payment and periodic payments to purchase the Property, she would have completed her payments by January 2021. (Mot., 15:27-16:3.) Second, if Ip had transferred title to Chen five years after she moved in, according to Chen’s testimony, title would have transferred in October 2019. (Mot., 16:8-13.) Finally, if Ip had transferred title to Chen after the initial two-year rental period, which ended mid-2017, title would have transferred sometime in June 2019. (Mot., 16:14-19.) Ip maintains that “[b]y her own verified allegations and testimony, Chen’s fraud claim accrued in 2019 and is time barred” in all instances. (Mot., 17:9-10.)

Notably, Ip’s statute of limitations argument contradicts the first timeline Ip presented, where Chen completes her payments by January 2021. If Chen completed payments by January 2021, filing the cross-complaint in February 2024 does not appear to be barred by the statute of limitations because the clock begins running when Chen discovers the alleged fraud. “An action for relief on the ground of fraud … is not deemed to have accrued until the discovery, by the aggrieved party, of the facts constituting the fraud or mistake. (Code Civ. Proc., § 338, subd. (d).) Defendants’ TACC specifically pleads that “[a]fter she fully paid him for the purchase, YUNA CHEN discovered the deceit and wrongs by Kevin Ip…” (TACC, ¶ 50.) Additionally, Defendants note that the five-year deadline for Ip to transfer title to Chen was the initial projection, but the agreement changed in late 2017, and Ip agreed to convey title when the purchase price was paid. (Chen Decl., ¶ 14.) Chen began to pay the balance to purchase the Property in 2017. (Azat Decl., Ex. G, Chen Depo., 77:13-21.) Chen attests that some payments for purchase were missed during the COVID-19 pandemic. (Chen Decl., ¶ 14; Azat Decl., Ex. G, Chen Depo., 83:2-5.) Evidence that Chen did not make uninterrupted monthly payments and the changed agreement regarding when the Property would be transferred raises a triable issue of material fact as to whether the fraud cause of action is barred, since the statute of limitations did not clearly expire before filing the cross-complaint. Accordingly, the motion for summary adjudication is denied as to this cause of action.

E.     Declaratory Relief

The fourth cause of action of Defendants’ TACC seeks declaratory relief. “To qualify for declaratory relief, a party would have to demonstrate its action presented two essential elements: (1) a proper subject of declaratory relief, and (2) an actual controversy involving justiciable questions relating to the party’s rights or obligations.” (Jolley v. Chase Home Finance, LLC (2013) 213 Cal.App.4th 872, 909 [quotation marks and brackets omitted].) A cause of action for declaratory relief should not be used as a second cause of action for the determination of identical issues raised in another cause of action. (General of America Ins. Co. v. Lilly (1968) 258 Cal.App.2d 465, 470.)

The TACC states that a “judicial declaration is necessary to determine the rightful ownership and possession of the Property as between Yuna Chen and Cross-Defendants.” (TACC, ¶ 58.)

            Ip contends that “the Court should enter judgment in favor of plaintiff and against defendants on Chen’s fourth cause of action for declaratory relief because Ip holds legal title to the Property and defendants cannot show by showing clear and convincing evidence that defendants have any ownership interest in the Property.” (Mot., 17:19-22.) Although the Court granted judicial notice of the two grant deeds and one quitclaim deed, judicial notice is used only to demonstrate the existence of a document, not the content contained therein. Ip also attaches the deeds to his declaration in support of the instant motion, but as discussed above, Defendants dispute ownership. (See Ip Decl., ¶¶ 3-5; Exs. A-C.) Defendants contend that although Ip is the owner of record, Chen is the rightful owner. (Chen Decl., ¶ 36; Azat Decl., Ex. A, 2:22-24.) In other words, Chen retains an interest in the Property, and declaratory relief is not proper at this time. Accordingly, triable issues of material fact remain as to Defendants’ declaratory relief cause of action. Accordingly, the motion for summary adjudication is denied.

Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, Ip’s motion for summary adjudication of the first and fourth causes of action in his complaint and of the first, third, and fourth causes of action in Defendants’ TACC is DENIED.

Ip is ordered to provide notice of this Order.

Background

On November 13, 2025, Plaintiff Armando Contreras (“Plaintiff”) filed this action against Defendants Monique Annette Sheps, Laurie M. Sheps, and Michael Sheps (collectively, “Defendants”), alleging seventeen causes of action for (1) negligence, (2) intentional infliction of emotional distress, (3) negligent infliction of emotional distress, (4) negligence per se, (5) assault, (6) battery, (7) intentional misrepresentation, (8) negligent misrepresentation, (9) reckless endangerment, (10) Business & Professions Code § 17200, et seq., (11) common law negligent entrustment of a vehicle, (12) strict liability, (13) fraud, (14) breach of oral contract, (15) civil conspiracy to commit insurance fraud, (16) breach of covenant of good faith and fair dealing, (17) statutory negligent entrustment of a vehicle

Defendants now demur to the complaint in its entirety and move to strike portions of the complaint. Plaintiff opposes. Defendants replied.

Discussion

As an initial matter, Defendants’ counsel submits a declaration in support of both the demurrer and motion to strike, which appear to convey the same information. Defendants’ counsel’s declaration indicates, inter alia, that “I first attempted to meet and confer with Plaintiff’s counsel, Jacob Pariyeli, on October 1, 2025 via a telephone call. Plaintiff’s counsel’s response was to meet and confer via email only.” (Villasenor Decl., ¶ 3.) Defendants’ counsel further attests that “[o]n October 24, 2025, I emailed Defendant’s detailed meet and confer letter to Plaintiff’s counsel, identifying specific sections within the Complaint that are subject to demurrer, together with legal support and authorities on which each objection was based, pursuant to Code of Civil Proc. § 430.41.” (Villasenor Decl., ¶ 5.) Defendants’ counsel “received an automated message the same day, at 4:42 pm from plaintiff’s counsel’s firm indicating their involvement in a lengthy matter that would engage their office until January 2026, resulting in limited capacity to deal with other matters. I responded to this email at 4:45 the same day, offering to stipulate to re-timetable the matter of meet and confer and resolution of initial pleadings.” (Villasenor Decl., ¶ 6.) Counsel attests that “[t]here has not been any response from Plaintiff’s counsel to my meet and confer letter nor to my offer to stipulate to a request to the Court for more time to meet and confer or for Plaintiff to voluntarily amend the Complaint. I do not believe the Plaintiff intends to respond to our meet and confer, nor voluntarily amend and therefore Defendants seek the court’s intervention and ruling on Defendants’ demurrer.” (Villasenor Decl., ¶ 7.)

The Court notes that based upon Defendants’ counsel’s declaration, it does not appear that Plaintiff has met and conferred by telephone, video conference or in person. This is not good faith compliance with the code requirement to meet and confer.

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, subdivision (a), “[b]efore filing a demurrer pursuant to this chapter, the demurring party shall meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer.” In addition, “[b]efore filing a motion to strike pursuant to this chapter, the moving party shall meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to the motion to strike for the purpose of determining if an agreement can be reached that resolves the objections to be raised in the motion to strike.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 435.5, subd. (a).) Such meeting and conferring must be done in good faith by both parties with an effort to try to resolve the issues subject to the demurrer.  

In light of the foregoing, the hearing on Defendants’ demurrer and motion to strike is continued to _______________, 2026 at 2:00 p.m. in Dept. 508.¿ 

Plaintiff is ordered to meet¿and confer¿with Defendants within 10 days of the date of this order.¿If the parties are unable to resolve the pleading issues¿or if the parties are otherwise unable to meet and confer in good faith, Defendants are to¿thereafter¿file and serve¿a declaration setting forth the efforts to meet and confer in compliance with¿Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, subdivision (a)(3) and Code of Civil Procedure section 435.5, subdivision (a)(3) within 15 days of this Order.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE:

 

DEFENDANT CARLE EBERT PIEROSE’S MOTION FOR LEAVE TO FILE COMPULSORY CROSS-COMPLAINANT

 

Background

On May 29, 2024, Robert M. Cohen, dba The Law Offices of Robert M. Cohen, (“Cohen”) filed a complaint against Defendant Carle Ebert Pierose (“Pierose”), asserting four causes of action for (1) breach of written contract (fee services agreement), (2) account stated, (3) open book account, and (4) common counts.

On April 23, 2026, Pierose filed the instant motion for leave to file a compulsory cross-complaint. On May 4, 2026 Cohen filed his opposition. Pierose replied on May 7, 2026. The motion’s original hearing date was May 15, 2026, but it was continued to July 27, 2026.

On May 11, 2026, Cohen filed a petition to compel arbitration and to stay proceedings pending the outcome of the petition.

On July 15, 2026, Cohen filed an additional opposition.

Request for Judicial Notice

The Court grants Cohen’s request for judicial notice of Exhibit A.

 

Discussion

In both the original opposition and supplemental opposition, Cohen requests that the “Court pause making any ruling on Defendant’s Motion for Leave to File Cross-Complaint or any other [sic] or any other motion made by Defendant pending the court’s ruling on Plaintiff’s Petition to Compel Arbitration Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure §1281.2 and Petition Requesting the Court Stay the Pending Lawsuit, Cohen V. Pierose, Case No. 25 STCV 35339 While Arbitration Is Pending Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure §1281.4.” (Opp., 1:26-2:5.)

The Court notes that “[t]he filing of a petition to compel arbitration does not automatically stay ongoing proceedings; the party seeking arbitration must request one.” ((OTO, L.L.C. v. Kho (2019) 8 Cal.5th 111, 140.) Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.4, “[i]f an application has been made to a court of competent jurisdiction … for an order to arbitrate a controversy which is an issue involved in an action or proceeding pending before a court of this State and such application is undetermined, the court in which such action or proceeding is pending shall, upon motion of a party to such action or proceeding, stay the action or proceeding until the application for an order to arbitrate is determined…” The statute’s express language that “while such application is undetermined” necessarily means the stay is effective upon filing a proper motion, not after the motion is heard. Moreover, a “‘court ordinarily has inherent power, in its discretion, to stay proceedings when such a stay will accommodate the ends of justice.’” ((Id. at p. 141, citing People v. Bell (1984) 159 Cal.App.3d 323, 329.)

In Cohen’s petition to compel arbitration, he explicitly requests “that this Court, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure §1281.2 and Code of Civil Procedure §1281.4, order this matter to arbitration as requested by the Plaintiff in that all of the requirements mandating that the Court order a matter to arbitration are present in the case at bar. Specifically, there is a written agreement between Plaintiff and Defendant in which both parties agreed to submit the dispute to binding arbitrate as required by Code of Civil Procedure §1281.2 which provides, that there must be a written agreement giving the Court permission to order a matter to arbitration. In this case, the written agreement is the Retainer Agreement.” (Notice of Pet., 9:16-25.)

The arbitration agreement provides that controversies between the parties regarding payment of attorney fees, non-performance or breach of the agreement, and malpractice or professional negligence are subject to arbitration. (RJN, Ex. A, 7:13-21.) Pursuant to Pierose’s proposed cross-complaint, he seeks to bring four causes of action for professional negligence/ legal malpractice, breach of fiduciary duty, accounting, and declaratory relief. It appears that the arbitration provision may apply to these causes of action.

Given that Cohen filed a proper motion to stay proceedings pending the Court’s determination on the petition to compel arbitration, and the grievances in the cross-complaint may arise out of the arbitration provision, the hearing on Pierose’s motion to file a compulsory cross-complaint is stayed, pending the outcome of the petition to compel arbitration. Moreover, all litigation and discovery in this action is stayed, pending the outcome of the petition to compel arbitration, presently set for hearing on December 2, 2026. The Court will discuss advancing that hearing date with the parties.  

Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, Pierose’s motion for leave to file a cross-complaint is STAYED, pending the outcome of the petition to compel arbitration. The hearing on the petition to compel arbitration is advanced to ____________ at ______ in Dept. 508. The Court continues the hearing on the motion to file a cross-complaint to _______________ at 10:00 a.m. in Dept. 508.

Cohen is ordered to give notice of this Order.

MOTION FOR ADMISSION PRO HAC VICE OF WILLIAM D. MARLER;

 

MOTION FOR ADMISSION PRO HAC VICE OF BRIAN DAVID ERNST

 

William D. Marler and Brian David Ernst both apply for an order granting permission to appear pro hac vice in the instant action on behalf of Plaintiff, Samanta Sabaite, on behalf of her minor child J.A.K.

The applications are submitted with a Declaration of William D. Marler and a Declaration of Brian David Ernst, both made under penalty of perjury under the laws of the State of California and comply with the requirements set forth in California Rules of Court, rule 9.40(d). 

Under in California Rules of Court, rule 9.40(c)(1), “[a] person desiring to appear as counsel pro hac vice in a superior court must file with the court a verified application together with proof of service by mail in accordance with Code of Civil Procedure section 1013a of a copy of the application and of the notice of hearing of the application on all parties who have appeared in the cause and on the State Bar at its San Francisco office.” The Court notes that proof of service was filed for State Bar at its San Francisco office only. However, the instant applications were filed on June 30, 2026, and the only defendant that has appeared in the case filed its answer on July 20, 2026, so proof of service is proper.

Accordingly, the unopposed applications are GRANTED.

Plaintiff is ordered to provide notice of this Order.
