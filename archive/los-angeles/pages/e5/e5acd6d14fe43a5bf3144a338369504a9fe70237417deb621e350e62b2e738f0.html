[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: MOTION AUGMENT THE ADMINISTRATIVE RECORD 

 

Petitioner Silvana Stephanie Aparicio (“Petitioner”) seeks to augment the administrative record.[1]

The motion is denied.

I.                Background

1.               Factual Allegations

Petitioner’s petition for writ of mandate alleges, in pertinent part, the following.

On July 9, 2019, respondent Ashley Estis (“Estis”) submitted a Detention Report (“Report”). (Pet. at 4:4-5.) [2] The Report included false information, including in part that during an incident on June 17, 2019, Petitioner entered the home of her parents Alice Wong Aparicio (“Alice”) and Luis A. Aparicio (“Luis”) and began throwing objects around. (Pet. at 4:4-25, 4:11-13.)

The Department of Children and Family Services (“DCFS”), Santa Fe Springs, filed a petition to remove Petitioner’s son, Alex Aparicio (“Alex”), from Alice and Luis’s home after the June 17, 2019 incident. (Pet. at 2:16-17.) On July 18, 2019, the Juvenile Dependency Court issued a Do Not Remove Order. (Pet. at 2:1-20.) The Dependency Court further ordered Family Reunification Services on September 12, 2019. (Pet. at 2:21-22.) The case was expunged after Petitioner completed 52 weeks of parenting classes. (Pet. at 2:23-26.)

Petitioner received a letter from DCFS dated July 3, 2024 (“CACI Notification”), notifying her that the DCFS had completed an investigation and sustained an allegation against Petitioner of child abuse or severe neglect. (Pet. at 3:7.) Petitioner requested a grievance hearing on July 25, 2024 by certified mail. (Pet. at 2:7-8.)

DCFS responded with a Letter of Denial dated August 1, 2024. (Pet. at 2:8-9.) In the Letter of Denial, the DCFS aid it would not grant a grievance hearing because the matter had already been adjudicated in Juvenile Dependency Court. (Pet. at 2:12-14.)

Petitioner sent respondent Michael D. Watrobski (“Watrobski”) notarized letters from Alice and Luis dated September 28, 2024 contesting that any abuse or neglect occurred. (Pet. at 3:2-6.) Alice sent the letters again in October 2024. (Pet. at 3:18-20.) Neither correspondence received a reply. (Pet. at 3:16-17, 3:20.) Alice also called Watrobski and left a voice mail, receiving no response. (Pet. at 3:21-23.)

2.               Procedural History

On October 29, 2024, Petitioner filed this writ action against respondents Watrobski, Leonard Aguilar (“Aguilar”), and Estis (collectively, “Respondents”). On December 5, 2024, Watrobski filed his answer to the petition. On March 6, 2025, Aguilar and Estis filed their answer to the petition.

On April 17, 2025, the Court determined that Alice and Luis, as non-attorneys, cannot represent Petitioner. The Court ordered that Alice may sit with Petitioner at counsel table for emotional support only and is not permitted to make legal arguments or speak on behalf of Petitioner.

On June 26, 2025, the Court granted Petitioner’s formal June 17, 2025 request for self-representation.

On November 6, 2025, Respondents filed a Notice of Lodging of Confidential Administrative record, representing that the sealed 137-page administrative record was lodged with the Court. On November 12, 2025, Respondents filed Notice of Withdrawal of Notice of Lodging of Confidential Administrative Record as no records were lodged with the Court.

On January 15, 2026, the Court ordered that Petitioner must file any motion to augment the record by April 20, 2026. On April 27, 2026, the Court vacated the hearing on a motion to augment scheduled May 12, 2026, because Petitioner had not yet filed any motion to augment.

Later that day, Petitioner filed the instant motion to augment.

On May 12, 2026, at a Trial Setting Conference, Petitioner was admonished re proper service and notice, including as to her Motion to Augment.

On June 3, 2026, Respondents filed their opposition. On June 11, 2026, Petitioner filed her reply.

II.             Discussion

“The general rule is that a hearing on a writ of administrative mandamus is conducted solely on the record of the proceeding before the administrative agency.” (Toyota of Visalia v. New Motor Vehicle Bd. (1987) 188 Cal.App.3d 872, 881.) The court can only admit additional evidence where the party seeking its inclusion shows (1) the evidence could not have been presented to the agency in the first instance in the exercise of reasonable diligence or (2) was improperly excluded. (CCP §1094.5(e); Western States Petroleum Assn. v. Superior Court (1995) 9 Cal.4th 559, 578; Eureka Citizens for Responsible Govt. v. City of Eureka (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 357, 366.)

While Petitioner filed the motion beyond the timeline provided by the Court, the Court nevertheless exercises its discretion to consider it.

The Court denies Petitioner’s motion on the basis that (1) Petitioner has not provided the legal authority supporting her request to augment the record to include the documents she seeks to add to the administrative record, and (2) Petitioner has not clearly identified, or provided the Court, the exhibits she seeks to add. (See generally Mot.) Petitioner also failed to file a notice, a memorandum of points and authorities, and supporting evidence. Therefore, she has not complied with California Rules of Court, rules 3.1112.

For the same reasons, the Court cannot determine whether the documents Petitioner seeks to add to the record could not have been presented to the agency or were improperly excluded by the agency.

Accordingly, the motion is denied.

III.           Conclusion

The motion to augment the administrative record is DENIED.

[TENTATIVE] RULING ON PETITION FOR WRIT OF MANDATE

 

Petitioner Marva Lindsey (“Petitioner”) petitions for a writ of administrative mandate against respondent Civil Service Commission of the County of Los Angeles (“Respondent”) directing it to set aside the decision to suspend her, for five days, from her position as a Detention Services Officer (“DSO”) with the Los Angeles County (“County”) Department of Probation (“Department”) (collectively, “RPI”).

The petition for writ of mandate is denied.

I.                Factual Background

On January 23, 2019, Petitioner was on duty at Barry J. Nidorf Juvenile Hall (“BJNJH”) on the day shift, from 6:00 a.m. to 2:00 p.m., assigned to Unit W-Side 2 (“Unit W2”). (AR 383, 393-94.) A minor (Jesus C.) was housed in Unit W2. (AR 357, 377-81, 396-97.)

While under Petitioner’s supervision, Jesus C. was unruly and noncompliant. (AR 378-81.) Ultimately, Petitioner did not provide Jesus C. lunch and did not allow Jesus C. to leave his room for the remainder of her shift, including for a 90-minute period after lunch during which the general practice in Unit W2 was to allow the minors to stay out of their rooms for dayroom and recreation activities. (AR 406-420.)

Additionally and separately during the January 23, 2019 day shift, Petitioner engaged in horseplay [1] with minors Juan O. and Tony B. (AR 368, 445-46, 452-53.) Later that day, Petitioner was on PM shift duty at BJNJH from 2:00 p.m. to 10:00 p.m., assigned to the Medical Unit, and worked eight further hours after the conclusion of her day shift in Unit W2. (AR 386.) Petitioner did not complete a Special Incident Report on January 23, 2019.

On January 24, 2019, Jesus C. submitted a grievance, stating Petitioner refused him lunch because she was upset and did not allow him to eat lunch or use the restroom until the PM shift arrived. (AR 354.) The grievance was reported by Department Division IV Director Steven Olsen the same day. (AR 344-60.) On January 25, 2019, Petitioner submitted a Special Incident Report. (AR 377-81.) Petitioner did not document that she modified Jesus C.’s program in the Unit Log Book. (AR 387.)

On February 23, 2019, Petitioner was on duty at BJNJH on the PM shift, from 2:00 p.m. to 10:00 p.m., assigned to Unit W, Side 1 (“Unit W1”). (AR 660-63, 668, 683, 686, 705.) Minor Carl T. was assigned as Petitioner’s assistant and was under her direct supervision. (AR 705-06.) At about 8:00 p.m., Petitioner informed the minors that dayroom activities were over, instructing them to return to their rooms. (AR 660-63.) As Petitioner’s assistant, Carl T. was supposed to help Petitioner get the minors to their rooms and prepare for showers, but instead he began using the pay phone. (AR 660-63, 683.) Petitioner directed Carl T. to get off the phone, and when he did not, Petitioner “fired” him, directing him to return to his room and prepare for showers. (AR 705.) Carl T. again refused. (AR 708.) After repeated noncompliance, Petitioner physically struggled with Carl T., then took the phone away. (AR 683, 708-10.) Petitioner denied that this was a use of force, calling it, “for lack of a better word – horseplay.” (AR 710.) In response to this encounter, Petitioner searched Carl. T.’s room and property and confiscated Carl T.’s shampoo. (AR 660, 683, 711-13.)

On February 26, 2020, the Department notified Petitioner that, effective March 9, 2020 through March 18, 2020, she would be suspended for ten days without pay based on the above-described incidents. (AR 3.) The suspension was based on abusive institutional practices, carelessness or inattention to duties, failure to follow established rules and regulations, and failure to exercise sound judgment. (AR 4.) Specifically, the suspension was based on violation of Probation Department Policy Manual (“PDPM”) No. 102, Employee Orientation – Mission, Vision, and Core Values; PDPM No. 601, Employee Conduct – Introduction; PDPM No. 701, Discipline and Corrective Action – Introduction; PDPM 704, Discipline and Corrective Action – Letter of Suspension; PDPM No. 707, Discipline and Corrective Action – Letters of Intent and Skelly Meetings; Probation Department Detention Services Bureau Manual (“DSBM”) No. 210, Duty Statement, Detention Services Officer (DSO); DSBM No. 502, Daily Unit Operations, Daily Schedule; DSBM No. 519, Temporary Suspension of Rights; DSBM No. 607, Modified Program; DSBM No. 2322 – Horseplay; Probation Department Directive No. 1198 – Abusive Institutional Practices; Employee Handbook, Ethical and Moral Standards, C 6-7; and Los Angeles County Civil Service Rule 18.031. (AR 4-5; 133-34.)

Petitioner appealed her suspension. (AR 17-23.) On May 13, 2020, Respondent granted a hearing on Petitioner’s appeal. (AR 68.) On July 8, 2022, Respondent notified Petitioner that her appeal would be heard on August 29, 30, and 31, 2022 by Hearing Officer Jennifer Gardner (“Hearing Officer”). (AR 71.) On December 7, 2022, Respondent notified Petitioner the hearing was continued to January 24 and 26, 2023. (AR 76.)

The Hearing Officer heard Petitioner’s appeal on January 24 and 26, 2023. (AR 132.) The Hearing Officer ultimately concluded that Petitioner’s actions were reasonable and within policy, except in that she failed to document the incidents. (AR 147-49.) On September 6, 2023, Respondent announced its proposed decision to accept the Hearing Officer’s findings and recommendation. (AR 154.) The Department objected to the proposed decision. On March 13, 2024, Respondent sustained the Department’s objection in part and rejected the Hearing Officer’s recommendation to impose a letter of warning and announced a new proposed decision to impose a five-day suspension. (AR 160.) On August 7, 2024, Respondent issued Revised Conclusions of Law finding that Petitioner engaged in horseplay. (AR 158-59.) Petitioner objected. On March 5, 2025, Respondent considered and overruled Petitioner’s objections, and then approved its findings adopting its proposed decision as its final decision. (AR 182.)

III.           Standard of Review

Code of Civil Procedure (“CCP”) section 1094.5 is the administrative mandamus provision that structures the procedure for judicial review of adjudicatory decisions rendered by administrative agencies. (Topanga Ass’n for a Scenic Community v. County of Los Angeles (1974) 11 Cal.3d 506, 514-15 [“Topanga”]).) Section 1094.5 does not specify which cases are subject to independent review, leaving that issue to the courts. (Fukuda v. City of Angels (1999) 20 Cal.4th 805, 811 [“Fukuda”]). In cases reviewing decisions that affect a vested, fundamental right, the trial court exercises independent judgment on the evidence. (Bixby v. Pierno (1971) 4 Cal.3d 130, 143 [“Bixby”]; see CCP § 1094.5(c).)

The Court uses its independent judgment on matters affecting a public employee’s vested property interest in his or her employment. (Barber v. Long Beach Civil Service Comm’n (1996) 45 Cal.App.4th 652, 658.)

“In exercising its independent judgment, a trial court must afford a strong presumption of correctness concerning the administrative findings, and the party challenging the administrative decision bears the burden of convincing the court that the administrative findings are contrary to the weight of the evidence.” (Fukuda, supra, 20 Cal.4th at 817.) Unless it can be demonstrated by petitioner that the agency’s actions are not grounded upon any reasonable basis in law or any substantial basis in fact, the courts should not interfere with the agency’s discretion or substitute their wisdom for that of the agency. (Bixby, supra, 4 Cal.3d at 150-151; Bank of America v. State Water Resources Control Board (1974) 42 Cal. App.3d 198, 208.)

An agency is presumed to have regularly performed its official duties (Evid. Code § 664), and the petitioner therefore has the burden of proof on mandamus. (Steele v. Los Angeles County Civil Service Commission (1958) 166 Cal.App.2d 129, 137.) “[T]he burden of proof falls upon the party attacking the administrative decision to demonstrate wherein the proceedings were unfair, in excess of jurisdiction or showed prejudicial abuse of discretion.” (Alford v. Pierno, (1972) 27 Cal.App.3d 682, 691 [“Alford”].)

“The propriety of a penalty imposed by an administrative agency is a matter vested in the discretion of the agency, and its decision may not be disturbed unless there has been a manifest abuse of discretion.” (Williamson v. Board of Medical Quality Assurance (1990) 217 Cal.App.3d 1343, 1347.)

IV.           Request for Judicial Notice

Petitioner seeks judicial notice of County of Los Angeles Civil Service Commission Rule 4.10 (“Rule 4.10”) (RJN Ex. A). The request is granted. (Evid. Code § 452(c).)

V.              Analysis

Petitioner seeks an order setting aside Respondent’s decision to suspend her for five days. Petitioner argues that (1) the findings were not supported by the evidence because Respondent relied on hearsay; (2) the decision that Petitioner violated policy and accepted practice was contrary to the findings; and (3) the five-day suspension imposed was a manifest abuse of discretion.

1.               The Findings Were Supported by the Evidence

Petitioner argues that the findings were not supported by the evidence because Respondent’s findings were based entirely on video footage and the investigator’s interpretation of same.

Under Rule 4.10, Respondent’s hearings “need not be conducted according to technical rules relating to evidence and witness.” (Rule 4.10A.) “Any relevant evidence shall be admitted if it is the sort of evidence on which responsible persons are accustomed to rely in the conduct of serious affairs, regardless of the existence of any common law or statutory rule which might make improper the admission of such evidence or objection in civil actions.” (Ibid.) While hearsay may be admitted, hearsay alone cannot support a finding unless it would be admissible over objection in a civil action. (Rule 4.10B.)

As RPI argues (Op. at 18), a video is generally not considered hearsay, as it is not a statement made by a person. (People v. Goldsmith (2014) 59 Cal.4th 258, 274 [Evidence Code section 1200 defines hearsay as a “statement,” further defined  as an oral or written verbal expression  or nonverbal conduct of a person intended by them as a substitute for said expression; photos and video were not statements of a person within that definition]; see also People v. Lopez (2012) 55 Cal.4th 569, 583 [machine readouts or machine-generated results are not testimonial out of court statements].)

Petitioner’s argument in reply, that the complexity of the video and its need for interpretation makes the video an out of court statement, in provided without any legal authority and is incorrect. (Reply at 3-4.)  The video is not a testimonial account of what occurred. It is evidence of what occurred. (People v. Goldsmith (2014) 59 Cal.4th 258, 274; see People v. Lopez 55 Cal.4th 569, 583.)

Even assuming arguendo that the investigator’s characterizations of the video could somehow be considered hearsay, the Commission did not rely on those characterizations as the sole basis for its findings. Rule 4.10(B) prohibits reliance on hearsay alone, but the record contains ample non-hearsay evidence, including the video itself, Petitioner’s own admissions regarding both the horseplay incidents (AR 446, 710) and the failure to document (AR 434, 469), and the grievance submitted by Jesus C. (AR 354). Because the Commission relied on non-hearsay evidence sufficient to support its findings, Rule 4.10(B) is satisfied.

Accordingly, the findings were supported by the weight of the evidence.

2.               The Decision Was Supported by the Findings

Petitioner also argues Respondent’s decision was not supported by the findings. (Pet. Op. Br. 5-10.) Petitioner does not contest the conclusions that she failed to document appropriately. She likewise does not address the conclusion that she did not obtain approval from her supervisor when she kept Jesus C. in his room. (See generally Pet. Op. Br.) The only other conclusion against her was that she engaged in horseplay. (AR 158-59, 182.)[2]

Although Petitioner argues that the findings do not identify violations of the various Department policies listed in the original Letter of Suspension, Respondent’s decision rests on two categories of misconduct that the Hearing Officer’s findings of fact plainly establish. First, the Hearing Officer found that Petitioner failed to complete a Special Incident Report and failed to document her modification of Minor Jesus C.’s program in the Unit Log Book in connection with the January 23 incident (finding of fact no. 12; AR 145), and that she again failed to document the February 23 incident involving Minor Carl T. (finding of fact no.¿23; AR 147). Second, the Hearing Officer found that Petitioner engaged in horseplay with minors, including swing-punching Minor Juan O. on January 23 (finding of fact no.¿13; AR 146), and that Petitioner acknowledged that she sometimes engages in horseplay as a means of building rapport (finding of fact no.¿15; AR 146). Petitioner does not dispute these factual findings, and they are sufficient to support Respondent’s Revised Conclusions of Law that she violated Department policies governing documentation and prohibiting horseplay (AR 158-59).

Petitioner contends that the Hearing Officer’s conclusion that Petitioner used horseplay to avoid the need for a use of force was supported by the evidence and findings. On this basis, the Hearing Officer determined that Petitioner did not violate DSBM No. 2322. However, the agency decision under review in this matter is Respondent’s, not the Hearing Officer’s recommendation. That the Hearing Officer concluded differently only reflects that reasonable minds may differ. Respondent’s Revised Conclusions of Law (AR 158-59) cite the specific findings of fact on which discipline rests, which is sufficient to satisfy Topanga’s requirement that an agency provide a reasoned connection between the evidence, the findings, and the ultimate decision.

“In exercising its independent judgment, a trial court must afford a strong presumption of correctness concerning the administrative findings, and the party challenging the administrative decision bears the burden of convincing the court that the administrative findings are contrary to the weight of the evidence.” (Fukuda, supra, 20 Cal.4th at 817.) Unless it can be demonstrated by petitioner that the agency’s actions are not grounded upon any reasonable basis in law or any substantial basis in fact, the courts should not interfere with the agency’s discretion or substitute their wisdom for that of the agency. (Bixby, supra, 4 Cal.3d at 150-151; Bank of America v. State Water Resources Control Board (1974) 42 Cal. App.3d 198, 208.)

Neither party provided the language of DSBM No. 2322 that addresses horseplay. However, it is Petitioner’s burden to establish that Respondent abused its discretion in finding that she violated DSBM No. 2322. (Alford, supra¸ 27 Cal.App.3d at 691 [petitioner bears the burden to show unfairness, excess of jurisdiction, or prejudicial abuse of discretion, and must provide an adequate administrative record or else the presumption of regularity controls].) RPI asserts, and the record reflects, that DSBM No. 2322 “strictly forbids horseplay between probation staff and youth.” (Opp. at 16; AR 1023.)[3] Petitioner does not contest this. (See generally Pet. Op. Br.)

Even the Hearing Officer found Petitioner did not “initiate horseplay” but “tolerate[d] it and mitigated[d] it without unnecessary escalation.” (AR 147.) The Hearing Officer found, as finding of fact number 13, that “[o]n January 23, 2019, [Petitioner] engaged in horseplay with [Jose O.] during which she pretended to swing-punch him without making contact.” (AR 146.) As part of finding of fact number 15, the Hearing Officer found, “[Petitioner] also stated that sometimes she engages in horseplay as a means of building rapport.” Petitioner herself referred to her conduct as “horseplay” in an interview with the investigator. (AR 446, 710.)

Accordingly, Petitioner has not met her burden to show the findings do not support the conclusion that Petitioner violated DSBM No. 2322.

3.               The Five-Day Suspension Penalty Was Not an Abuse of Discretion

Petitioner contends that Respondent abused its discretion by imposing a five-day suspension. Specifically, Petitioner notes that the Hearing Officer and Respondent each found that Petitioner performed her duties well in the face of a minor engaged in threatening behavior and engaged in proper actions with the other minors. Petitioner also contends that her employment record shows she was not likely to engage in abusive action and provides substantial mitigation. She has positive performance evaluations and only one disciplinary action, which was a warning. Based on this, she notes, the Hearing Officer determined a suspension was inappropriate. Therefore, Petitioner concludes, the record does not support a five-day suspension. (Pet. Op. Br. 10-11.)

The propriety of a penalty imposed by an administrative agency is a matter in the discretion of the agency, and its decision may not be disturbed unless there has been a manifest abuse of discretion. (Lake v. Civil Service Commission (1975) 47 Cal.App.3d 224, 228 [“Lake”].) In determining whether there has been an abuse of discretion, the court must examine the extent of the harm to the public service, the circumstances surrounding the misconduct, and the likelihood that such conduct will recur. (Skelly, supra, 15 Cal.3d at 217-18.) Neither an appellate court nor a trial court is free to substitute its discretion for that of the administrative agency concerning the degree of punishment imposed. (Nightingale v. State Personnel Board (1972) 7 Cal.3d 507, 515.)

RPI argues that Respondent based its decision on the failure to document and Petitioner’s own admissions of engaging in horseplay; RPI notes that a 5-day suspension is within the Supplemental Disciplinary Guidelines for this type of misconduct. (Opp. at 19.)

In reply, Petitioner reiterates that the Hearing Officer’s conclusion reaching a different decision and penalty was supported by the evidence and findings, and Respondent failed to “distinguish the underlying bases for their revised conclusion of a 5-day suspension.” (Reply at 4.)

As noted above, this Court is reviewing Respondent’s final decision, not the Hearing Officer’s decision. An agency may adopt all factual findings made by a hearing officer and nonetheless determine that a different level of discipline is appropriate, so long as the penalty selected is reasonably supported by those findings and consistent with applicable guidelines. Respondent was therefore not required to modify the findings of fact, or to identify new factual bases, before concluding that the admitted violations (horseplay with minors and repeated failures to document significant incidents (AR 158-59)) warranted a five-day suspension.

As RPI notes (Opp. at 18) and Petitioner does not contest, Respondent was not bound by the Hearing Officer's findings or conclusions. While the parties do not point the Court to the relevant disciplinary guidelines, RPI contends, and Petitioner does not dispute, that a five-day suspension is within those guidelines. (Opp. at 19.) Based on the foregoing, the Court cannot conclude that no reasonable person could have imposed a five-day suspension for the conduct at issue.

The Commission could also reasonably conclude that Petitioner’s conduct presented significant risks to the safety and orderly operation of a juvenile detention facility. Documentation failures can compromise supervisory review, impede incident tracking, and obscure the sequence of events in volatile environments. Likewise, the Department’s prohibition on horseplay rests on undisputed testimony that such conduct blurs professional boundaries, can rapidly escalate, and may prompt intervention by other staff under the mistaken belief that a use of force incident is happening. (AR 1023.) Given the potential consequences, a five-day suspension for repeated violations in both categories falls within the bounds of reason.

Accordingly, Petitioner has not met her burden to show that a five-day suspension penalty was an abuse of discretion.

VI.           Conclusion

The weight of the record evidence supports Respondent’s findings, and its findings support its conclusions, and Petitioner has not shown the penalty imposed was an abuse of discretion. The petition for administrative mandamus is DENIED.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: MOTIONS FOR PRELIMINARY INJUNCTION 

 

Petitioner John Doe (“Petitioner”) seeks a preliminary injunction restraining respondent Regents of the University of California (“Respondent”) from disclosing documents in response to a California Public Records Act (“CPRA”) request by real party Cohen Williams, LLP (“RPI”).

Likewise, Petitioner Person Doe 1[1] (“PD1”) seeks a preliminary injunction restraining Respondent from disclosing documents in response to a similar CPRA request by RPI

Because the legal issues raised by Petitioner’s and PD1’s motions are substantially similar, the Court issues this combined ruling.

 The motions/preliminary injunction are granted.

I.                Background – Petitioner

1.               Factual Background

In June 2016, Petitioner began his employment with the University of California, Los Angeles (“UCLA”) School of Dentistry. (Petitioner Decl., ¶ 2.) Petitioner conducted research and published findings along with his colleagues and his mentor, the Associate Dean for the School of Dentistry (“Associate Dean”). IV.           Discussion

In determining whether to issue a preliminary injunction, the trial court considers two factors: (1) the reasonable probability that the plaintiff will prevail on the merits at trial (Code Civ. Proc. [“CCP”] §526(a)(1)), and (2) a balancing of the “irreparable harm” that the plaintiff is likely to sustain if the injunction is denied as compared to the harm that the defendant is likely to suffer if the court grants a preliminary injunction. (CCP §526(a)(2); 14859 Moorpark Homeowner’s Assn. v. VRT Corp. (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 1396, 1402; Pillsbury, Madison & Sutro v. Schectman (1997) 55 Cal.App.4th 1279, 1283; Davenport v. Blue Cross of California (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 435, 446; Abrams v. St. John’s Hospital (1994) 25 Cal.App.4th 628, 636.) Thus, a preliminary injunction may not issue without some showing of potential entitlement to such relief. (Doe v. Wilson (1997) 57 Cal.App.4th 296, 304.)

The purpose of a preliminary injunction is to preserve the status quo pending final resolution upon a trial. (See Scaringe v. J.C.C. Enterprises, Inc. (1988) 205 Cal.App.3d 1536; Grothe v. Cortlandt Corp. (1992) 11 Cal.App.4th 1313, 1316; Major v. Miraverde Homeowners Assn. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 618, 623.) The status quo has been defined to mean the last actual peaceable, uncontested status which preceded the pending controversy. (Voorhies v. Greene (1983) 139 Cal.App.3d 989, 995, quoting United Railroads v. Superior Court (1916) 172 Cal. 80, 87; 14859 Moorpark Homeowner’s Assn. v. VRT Corp. (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 1396. 1402.)

1.               Probability of Success

“The trial court's determination must be guided by a ‘mix’ of the potential-merit and interim-harm factors; the greater the plaintiff's showing on one, the less must be shown on the other to support an injunction.” (Butt v. State of California (1992) 4 Cal.4th 668, 678. However, “[a] trial court may not grant a preliminary injunction, regardless of the balance of interim harm, unless there is some possibility that the plaintiff would ultimately prevail on the merits of the claim.” (Ibid.)

a.                CPRA’s Investigatory Exemption

Petitioner contends that the records sought in Request No. 4 are exempt under Government Code[3] section 7923.600, subdivision (a), which exempts investigatory or security files compiled by any state or local agency for correctional, law enforcement, or licensing purposes. (§7923.600(a).)  Petitioner argues that the records are presently being used in ongoing investigations by both UCLA’s PTC and the federal ORI.

RPI responds that the exemption does not apply because UCLA prepared the report to investigate research misconduct, not for “correctional, law enforcement, or licensing purposes.” RPI cites Uribe v. Howie (1971) 19 Cal.App.3d 194 for the proposition that a record is exempt only when its primary purpose relates to those functions. (Opp. at 13.)

RPI’s reliance on Uribe is misplaced. Uribe did not adopt a rigid “primary purpose only” test. Rather, the court declined to extend the exemption to all agricultural reports merely because they could, under some circumstances, be used in disciplinary proceedings. (Uribe, supra, 19 Cal.App.3d at p. 213.) The court emphasized that the reports in that case were not primarily prepared for licensing review, and there was no evidence that any of the reports were being used for such a purpose at the time of trial. (Ibid.) Critically, Uribe expressly recognized that “if some reports were currently being used to investigate an applicator, the reports relating to that applicator would be exempt […].” (Ibid.)

Here, the record contains undisputed evidence that ORI is actively reviewing UCLA’s investigation (Petitioner Decl., ¶¶ 10–11), and that UCLA’s further investigation of the Associate Dean remains pending (Petitioner Decl., ¶ 12; Associate Dean Decl., ¶¶ 6–7). Thus, the report is both an investigatory file and one that is currently being used for enforcement review.

The remaining question is whether ORI’s review qualifies as correctional, law enforcement, or licensing activity. The Court concludes that it does. If ORI finds research misconduct, it may impose administrative sanctions, including certification requirements, suspension, or debarment, and may refer matters for civil or criminal investigation. (42 C.F.R. §§ 93.401, 93.407.) This federal oversight authority is sufficiently law enforcement adjacent to bring UCLA’s investigative report within the CPRA exemption, because UCLA’s report is currently being used as part of ORI’s enforcement review.

Accordingly, the Court finds a substantial likelihood that Petitioner will prevail on the ground that the records fall within section 7923.600(a) and are exempt from disclosure under the CPRA.

The same analysis applies to PD1, who likewise demonstrates, through uncontroverted evidence, that (1) UCLA has not concluded its investigatory process with respect to several accused individuals arising out of the same allegations (PD1 Decl., ¶¶ 22-23), and (2) ORI’s oversight review of UCLA’s research misconduct findings remains pending (PD1 Decl., ¶ 23). Under Uribe, the investigatory exemption applies not only where the primary purpose of the record is law enforcement or licensing, but also where, as here, the record is presently being used for such purposes. Because UCLA’s report is actively under ORI review, and ORI’s oversight authority includes potential corrective, compliance, and referral powers, the report is currently being used for law enforcement-adjacent purposes. Accordingly, the Court likewise finds a substantial likelihood that PD1 will prevail under Government Code section 7923.600(a).

b.               42 CFR §93.108 Confidentiality Provision

Petitioner next contends that the sought records are exempt under section 7927.705, which exempts records whose disclosure is exempted or prohibited pursuant to federal or state law. (§ 7927.705.)[4] Petitioner argues that federal law prohibits disclosure of respondent identities in research misconduct proceedings, and disclosure of the sought records would necessarily disclose Petitioner’s identity. (See 42 CFR § 93.108.)

RPI responds that 42 CFR section 93.108 is merely “aspirational,” does not categorically prohibit disclosure, and thus cannot trigger §7927.705. RPI argues that disclosure is “not always prohibited as a matter of law” under 42 CFR section 93.108, and from that concludes that section 7927.705 can never apply. (Opp. at 16.)[5]

The Court is not persuaded by RPI’s interpretation of either section 7927.705 or 42 CFR section 93.108.

Section 7927.705 states:

“Except as provided in Sections 7924.510, 7924.700, and 7929.610, this division does not require disclosure of records, the disclosure of which is exempted or prohibited pursuant to federal or state law, including, but not limited to, provisions of the Evidence Code relating to privilege.” (§ 7927.705.)

42 CFR section 93.108 states, in pertinent part:

“Disclosure of the identity of respondents and complainants in research misconduct proceedings is limited, to the extent possible, to those who need to know, consistent with a thorough, competent, objective and fair research misconduct proceeding, and as allowed by law.” (42 CFR § 93.108.)

Thus, the regulatory text creates a substantive confidentiality mandate, even if qualified. The phrase “to the extent possible” does not eliminate the prohibition; it directs institutions to maintain confidentiality unless doing so would frustrate a thorough and fair proceeding. RPI presents no evidence that protecting Petitioner’s (or PD1’s) identity is not possible here. UCLA has control over the report and can prevent disclosure entirely pending final review.

Moreover, reverse-CPRA analysis does not require an absolute, exception-free federal bar; it requires a showing that disclosure here would violate governing law. On this record, where Petitioner’s and PD1’s identities as respondents would unquestionably be revealed (Osher Decl., ¶¶ 13-14; PD1 Decl., ¶14), the Court finds that 42 CFR section 93.108 prohibits disclosure “to the extent possible.” Here, nondisclosure is possible.

Thus, at this stage, Petitioner and PD1 have demonstrated a probability of success on showing that 42 CFR section 93.108 bars disclosure of the report, and, consequently, that section 7927.705 exempts the report from disclosure under CPRA. (42 CFR § 93.108; § 7927.705.)

c.                CPRA’s Personnel Record Exemption

Petitioner argues that the records sought are exempt under section 7927.700 which exempts personnel, medical, or similar files whose disclosure would constitute an unwarranted invasion of privacy. (§ 7927.700.)[6] Petitioner contends that personnel records generally include records pertaining to grievances against an employee. (Mot. at 14, citing Associated Chino Teachers v. Chino Valley Unified School Dist. (2018) 30 Cal.App.5th 530, 539 [“Chino Teachers”], Lab. Code § 1198.5(a).) Petitioner argues that the public interest in the professional competency of a public institution is substantial, but a request for his personnel records significantly implicates his privacy rights. (Opp. at 11, citing American Federation of State Employees v. Regents of University of California (1978) 80 Cal.App.3d 913, Bakersfield City School Dist. v. Superior Court (2004) 188 Cal.App.4th 1041, BRV, Inc. v. Sup. Ct. (2006) 143 Cal.App.4th 742, 758.)

In opposition, RPI contends that when a complaint giving rise to a report is “substantial”—that is, if the complaint is upheld or discipline is imposed—then the public interest in disclosure outweighs the privacy interest of the employee. (Opp. at 16, citing Marken v. Santa Monica-Malibu Unified School District (2012) 202 Cal.App.4th 1250, 1274-75 [“Marken”].) RPI argues UCLA upheld five of the underlying allegations, and therefore the personnel records exception does not apply. (Opp. at 17.)[7]

The holding in Marken is instructive. Under Marken, disclosure of complaints and investigation reports is mandatory where the agency finds the allegations true. (Marken at 1275.) Even if the agency does not uphold the allegations, the agency’s report must be released if the agency’s file is reliable and, based on that information, the court can determine the complaint is “well founded and substantial.” (Ibid.)

RPI is correct. UCLA’s Inquiry Committee recommended formal investigation into six of seven allegations and formed an Investigation Committee. (Petitioner Decl., ¶ 6.) The Investigation Committee upheld five of those allegations. (Petitioner Decl., ¶ 8-9.) As the Court does not have UCLA’s file or report before it, the Court cannot determine whether the file is reliable or the complaint is well founded and substantial. However, UCLA upheld five of the allegations against Petitioner and mad six findings of research misconduct against PD1. (Petitioner Decl., ¶¶ 6-10; PD1 Decl., ¶ 15-16.) While it is unclear whether ORI’s potential reversal of the findings would eliminate the effect of UCLA’s findings under Marken, the fact that UCLA found against Petitioner and PD1 is a strong indicator that the complaint is well founded and substantial. Therefore, under Marken, the public’s interest to disclosure most likely outweighs Petitioner’s and PD1’s privacy interest.

Accordingly, based on the evidence before this Court, Petitioner and PD1 have not shown at this stage that the report sought under Request No. 4 is likely exempt under section 7927.700.

d.               CPRA’s Public Interest Exception

Petitioner contends that the records sought are exempt under section 7922.000 which allows any agency to withhold any record if it demonstrates that the public interest weighs against, rather than for, disclosure. (§ 7922.000.) The thrust of Petitioner’s argument is that (a) Ting will use the disclosure to attempt to improperly influence the pending investigations; and (b) Ting will use the disclosures to take revenge against Petitioner and others. (Mot. at 15.)

These arguments are not persuasive. For the former, Petitioner does not explain how public disclosure of the report will allow Ting to interfere in ORI’s review or the PTC’s investigation. For the latter, a personal dispute between Ting and his colleagues is a private, not public, interest. The section 7922.000 exception concerns only the public interests in disclosure and nondisclosure. (§ 7922.000.)

Accordingly, Petitioner is not likely to succeed under the theory that CPRA’s public interest exception under section 7922.000 does not apply.

e.                Permissive and Prohibitive CPRA Exemptions

RPI argues, in opposition to both motions, that a reverse-CPRA action can succeed only where an agency is prohibited by law from disclosing records, rather than merely permitted to withhold them. Even assuming that standard applies, Petitioner and PD1 have demonstrated a likelihood of success. As explained above, the records fall within Government Code sections 7923.600(a) and 7927.705, and, significantly, federal law independently bars disclosure here. Under 42 CFR section 93.108, the identity of respondents in research misconduct proceedings must not be disclosed “to the extent possible,” and the record shows that nondisclosure is fully possible in this instance. Because disclosure would therefore violate federal law, Petitioner and PD1 have shown a probability of success on the merits.

2.               Balance of Harms

The Court must balance the irreparable harm Petitioner and PD1 are likely to suffer if an injunction is denied against any harm RPI is likely to suffer if an injunction is granted. (CCP §526(a)(2); 14859 Moorpark Homeowner’s Assn. v. VRT Corp., supra, 63 Cal.App.4th at 1402; Pillsbury, Madison & Sutro v. Schectman, supra, 55 Cal.App.4th at 1283.) Petitioner and PD1 have demonstrated a significant privacy interest in the report, which contains UCLA’s conclusions regarding the allegations of research misconduct involving both. (Chino Teachers, supra, 30 Cal.App.5th at 541.)

RPI identifies no harm it will suffer from delaying disclosure. Its arguments focus solely on the public’s eventual right to know how UCLA addressed the allegations, which is a concern that will be fully addressed if RPI ultimately prevails on the merits. The only asserted harm is the risk that the report might never be disclosed, which is not an interim harm relevant to this balancing.

Because Petitioner and PD1 face immediate and irreversible privacy and reputational injury absent an injunction, while RPI identifies no corresponding interim harm, the balance of harms favors Petitioner and PD1.

3.               Bond

In setting the bond, the court must assume that the preliminary injunction was wrongly issued. (Abba Rubber Co. v. Seaquist (1991) 235 Cal.App.3d 1, 15.) The attorney fees necessary to successfully procure a final decision dissolving the injunction also are damages that should be included in setting the bond. (Id. at 15-16.) While Abba reasoned that the plaintiff’s likelihood of prevailing is irrelevant to setting the bond, a more recent case disagreed, stating that the greater the likelihood of the plaintiff prevailing, the less likely the preliminary injunction will have been wrongly issued, and that is a relevant factor for setting the bond. (Oiye v. Fox, (2012) 211 Cal.App.4th 1036, 1062.)

The parties do not address the issue of bond. The Court will address the issue of bond with counsel at the hearing.

V.              Conclusion

Petitioner and PD1 have shown a probability of success on the merits, and the balance of harms favors Petitioner and PD1. The motions/preliminary injunction are GRANTED.

IV.           Discussion

In determining whether to issue a preliminary injunction, the trial court considers two factors: (1) the reasonable probability that the plaintiff will prevail on the merits at trial (Code Civ. Proc. [“CCP”] §526(a)(1)), and (2) a balancing of the “irreparable harm” that the plaintiff is likely to sustain if the injunction is denied as compared to the harm that the defendant is likely to suffer if the court grants a preliminary injunction. (CCP §526(a)(2); 14859 Moorpark Homeowner’s Assn. v. VRT Corp. (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 1396, 1402; Pillsbury, Madison & Sutro v. Schectman (1997) 55 Cal.App.4th 1279, 1283; Davenport v. Blue Cross of California (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 435, 446; Abrams v. St. John’s Hospital (1994) 25 Cal.App.4th 628, 636.) Thus, a preliminary injunction may not issue without some showing of potential entitlement to such relief. (Doe v. Wilson (1997) 57 Cal.App.4th 296, 304.)

The purpose of a preliminary injunction is to preserve the status quo pending final resolution upon a trial. (See Scaringe v. J.C.C. Enterprises, Inc. (1988) 205 Cal.App.3d 1536; Grothe v. Cortlandt Corp. (1992) 11 Cal.App.4th 1313, 1316; Major v. Miraverde Homeowners Assn. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 618, 623.) The status quo has been defined to mean the last actual peaceable, uncontested status which preceded the pending controversy. (Voorhies v. Greene (1983) 139 Cal.App.3d 989, 995, quoting United Railroads v. Superior Court (1916) 172 Cal. 80, 87; 14859 Moorpark Homeowner’s Assn. v. VRT Corp. (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 1396. 1402.)

1.               Probability of Success

“The trial court's determination must be guided by a ‘mix’ of the potential-merit and interim-harm factors; the greater the plaintiff's showing on one, the less must be shown on the other to support an injunction.” (Butt v. State of California (1992) 4 Cal.4th 668, 678. However, “[a] trial court may not grant a preliminary injunction, regardless of the balance of interim harm, unless there is some possibility that the plaintiff would ultimately prevail on the merits of the claim.” (Ibid.)

a.                CPRA’s Investigatory Exemption

Petitioner contends that the records sought in Request No. 4 are exempt under Government Code[3] section 7923.600, subdivision (a), which exempts investigatory or security files compiled by any state or local agency for correctional, law enforcement, or licensing purposes. (§7923.600(a).)  Petitioner argues that the records are presently being used in ongoing investigations by both UCLA’s PTC and the federal ORI.

RPI responds that the exemption does not apply because UCLA prepared the report to investigate research misconduct, not for “correctional, law enforcement, or licensing purposes.” RPI cites Uribe v. Howie (1971) 19 Cal.App.3d 194 for the proposition that a record is exempt only when its primary purpose relates to those functions. (Opp. at 13.)

RPI’s reliance on Uribe is misplaced. Uribe did not adopt a rigid “primary purpose only” test. Rather, the court declined to extend the exemption to all agricultural reports merely because they could, under some circumstances, be used in disciplinary proceedings. (Uribe, supra, 19 Cal.App.3d at p. 213.) The court emphasized that the reports in that case were not primarily prepared for licensing review, and there was no evidence that any of the reports were being used for such a purpose at the time of trial. (Ibid.) Critically, Uribe expressly recognized that “if some reports were currently being used to investigate an applicator, the reports relating to that applicator would be exempt […].” (Ibid.)

Here, the record contains undisputed evidence that ORI is actively reviewing UCLA’s investigation (Petitioner Decl., ¶¶ 10–11), and that UCLA’s further investigation of the Associate Dean remains pending (Petitioner Decl., ¶ 12; Associate Dean Decl., ¶¶ 6–7). Thus, the report is both an investigatory file and one that is currently being used for enforcement review.

The remaining question is whether ORI’s review qualifies as correctional, law enforcement, or licensing activity. The Court concludes that it does. If ORI finds research misconduct, it may impose administrative sanctions, including certification requirements, suspension, or debarment, and may refer matters for civil or criminal investigation. (42 C.F.R. §§ 93.401, 93.407.) This federal oversight authority is sufficiently law enforcement adjacent to bring UCLA’s investigative report within the CPRA exemption, because UCLA’s report is currently being used as part of ORI’s enforcement review.

Accordingly, the Court finds a substantial likelihood that Petitioner will prevail on the ground that the records fall within section 7923.600(a) and are exempt from disclosure under the CPRA.

The same analysis applies to PD1, who likewise demonstrates, through uncontroverted evidence, that (1) UCLA has not concluded its investigatory process with respect to several accused individuals arising out of the same allegations (PD1 Decl., ¶¶ 22-23), and (2) ORI’s oversight review of UCLA’s research misconduct findings remains pending (PD1 Decl., ¶ 23). Under Uribe, the investigatory exemption applies not only where the primary purpose of the record is law enforcement or licensing, but also where, as here, the record is presently being used for such purposes. Because UCLA’s report is actively under ORI review, and ORI’s oversight authority includes potential corrective, compliance, and referral powers, the report is currently being used for law enforcement-adjacent purposes. Accordingly, the Court likewise finds a substantial likelihood that PD1 will prevail under Government Code section 7923.600(a).

b.               42 CFR §93.108 Confidentiality Provision

Petitioner next contends that the sought records are exempt under section 7927.705, which exempts records whose disclosure is exempted or prohibited pursuant to federal or state law. (§ 7927.705.)[4] Petitioner argues that federal law prohibits disclosure of respondent identities in research misconduct proceedings, and disclosure of the sought records would necessarily disclose Petitioner’s identity. (See 42 CFR § 93.108.)

RPI responds that 42 CFR section 93.108 is merely “aspirational,” does not categorically prohibit disclosure, and thus cannot trigger §7927.705. RPI argues that disclosure is “not always prohibited as a matter of law” under 42 CFR section 93.108, and from that concludes that section 7927.705 can never apply. (Opp. at 16.)[5]

The Court is not persuaded by RPI’s interpretation of either section 7927.705 or 42 CFR section 93.108.

Section 7927.705 states:

“Except as provided in Sections 7924.510, 7924.700, and 7929.610, this division does not require disclosure of records, the disclosure of which is exempted or prohibited pursuant to federal or state law, including, but not limited to, provisions of the Evidence Code relating to privilege.” (§ 7927.705.)

42 CFR section 93.108 states, in pertinent part:

“Disclosure of the identity of respondents and complainants in research misconduct proceedings is limited, to the extent possible, to those who need to know, consistent with a thorough, competent, objective and fair research misconduct proceeding, and as allowed by law.” (42 CFR § 93.108.)

Thus, the regulatory text creates a substantive confidentiality mandate, even if qualified. The phrase “to the extent possible” does not eliminate the prohibition; it directs institutions to maintain confidentiality unless doing so would frustrate a thorough and fair proceeding. RPI presents no evidence that protecting Petitioner’s (or PD1’s) identity is not possible here. UCLA has control over the report and can prevent disclosure entirely pending final review.

Moreover, reverse-CPRA analysis does not require an absolute, exception-free federal bar; it requires a showing that disclosure here would violate governing law. On this record, where Petitioner’s and PD1’s identities as respondents would unquestionably be revealed (Osher Decl., ¶¶ 13-14; PD1 Decl., ¶14), the Court finds that 42 CFR section 93.108 prohibits disclosure “to the extent possible.” Here, nondisclosure is possible.

Thus, at this stage, Petitioner and PD1 have demonstrated a probability of success on showing that 42 CFR section 93.108 bars disclosure of the report, and, consequently, that section 7927.705 exempts the report from disclosure under CPRA. (42 CFR § 93.108; § 7927.705.)

c.                CPRA’s Personnel Record Exemption

Petitioner argues that the records sought are exempt under section 7927.700 which exempts personnel, medical, or similar files whose disclosure would constitute an unwarranted invasion of privacy. (§ 7927.700.)[6] Petitioner contends that personnel records generally include records pertaining to grievances against an employee. (Mot. at 14, citing Associated Chino Teachers v. Chino Valley Unified School Dist. (2018) 30 Cal.App.5th 530, 539 [“Chino Teachers”], Lab. Code § 1198.5(a).) Petitioner argues that the public interest in the professional competency of a public institution is substantial, but a request for his personnel records significantly implicates his privacy rights. (Opp. at 11, citing American Federation of State Employees v. Regents of University of California (1978) 80 Cal.App.3d 913, Bakersfield City School Dist. v. Superior Court (2004) 188 Cal.App.4th 1041, BRV, Inc. v. Sup. Ct. (2006) 143 Cal.App.4th 742, 758.)

In opposition, RPI contends that when a complaint giving rise to a report is “substantial”—that is, if the complaint is upheld or discipline is imposed—then the public interest in disclosure outweighs the privacy interest of the employee. (Opp. at 16, citing Marken v. Santa Monica-Malibu Unified School District (2012) 202 Cal.App.4th 1250, 1274-75 [“Marken”].) RPI argues UCLA upheld five of the underlying allegations, and therefore the personnel records exception does not apply. (Opp. at 17.)[7]

The holding in Marken is instructive. Under Marken, disclosure of complaints and investigation reports is mandatory where the agency finds the allegations true. (Marken at 1275.) Even if the agency does not uphold the allegations, the agency’s report must be released if the agency’s file is reliable and, based on that information, the court can determine the complaint is “well founded and substantial.” (Ibid.)

RPI is correct. UCLA’s Inquiry Committee recommended formal investigation into six of seven allegations and formed an Investigation Committee. (Petitioner Decl., ¶ 6.) The Investigation Committee upheld five of those allegations. (Petitioner Decl., ¶ 8-9.) As the Court does not have UCLA’s file or report before it, the Court cannot determine whether the file is reliable or the complaint is well founded and substantial. However, UCLA upheld five of the allegations against Petitioner and mad six findings of research misconduct against PD1. (Petitioner Decl., ¶¶ 6-10; PD1 Decl., ¶ 15-16.) While it is unclear whether ORI’s potential reversal of the findings would eliminate the effect of UCLA’s findings under Marken, the fact that UCLA found against Petitioner and PD1 is a strong indicator that the complaint is well founded and substantial. Therefore, under Marken, the public’s interest to disclosure most likely outweighs Petitioner’s and PD1’s privacy interest.

Accordingly, based on the evidence before this Court, Petitioner and PD1 have not shown at this stage that the report sought under Request No. 4 is likely exempt under section 7927.700.

d.               CPRA’s Public Interest Exception

Petitioner contends that the records sought are exempt under section 7922.000 which allows any agency to withhold any record if it demonstrates that the public interest weighs against, rather than for, disclosure. (§ 7922.000.) The thrust of Petitioner’s argument is that (a) Ting will use the disclosure to attempt to improperly influence the pending investigations; and (b) Ting will use the disclosures to take revenge against Petitioner and others. (Mot. at 15.)

These arguments are not persuasive. For the former, Petitioner does not explain how public disclosure of the report will allow Ting to interfere in ORI’s review or the PTC’s investigation. For the latter, a personal dispute between Ting and his colleagues is a private, not public, interest. The section 7922.000 exception concerns only the public interests in disclosure and nondisclosure. (§ 7922.000.)

Accordingly, Petitioner is not likely to succeed under the theory that CPRA’s public interest exception under section 7922.000 does not apply.

e.                Permissive and Prohibitive CPRA Exemptions

RPI argues, in opposition to both motions, that a reverse-CPRA action can succeed only where an agency is prohibited by law from disclosing records, rather than merely permitted to withhold them. Even assuming that standard applies, Petitioner and PD1 have demonstrated a likelihood of success. As explained above, the records fall within Government Code sections 7923.600(a) and 7927.705, and, significantly, federal law independently bars disclosure here. Under 42 CFR section 93.108, the identity of respondents in research misconduct proceedings must not be disclosed “to the extent possible,” and the record shows that nondisclosure is fully possible in this instance. Because disclosure would therefore violate federal law, Petitioner and PD1 have shown a probability of success on the merits.

2.               Balance of Harms

The Court must balance the irreparable harm Petitioner and PD1 are likely to suffer if an injunction is denied against any harm RPI is likely to suffer if an injunction is granted. (CCP §526(a)(2); 14859 Moorpark Homeowner’s Assn. v. VRT Corp., supra, 63 Cal.App.4th at 1402; Pillsbury, Madison & Sutro v. Schectman, supra, 55 Cal.App.4th at 1283.) Petitioner and PD1 have demonstrated a significant privacy interest in the report, which contains UCLA’s conclusions regarding the allegations of research misconduct involving both. (Chino Teachers, supra, 30 Cal.App.5th at 541.)

RPI identifies no harm it will suffer from delaying disclosure. Its arguments focus solely on the public’s eventual right to know how UCLA addressed the allegations, which is a concern that will be fully addressed if RPI ultimately prevails on the merits. The only asserted harm is the risk that the report might never be disclosed, which is not an interim harm relevant to this balancing.

Because Petitioner and PD1 face immediate and irreversible privacy and reputational injury absent an injunction, while RPI identifies no corresponding interim harm, the balance of harms favors Petitioner and PD1.

3.               Bond

In setting the bond, the court must assume that the preliminary injunction was wrongly issued. (Abba Rubber Co. v. Seaquist (1991) 235 Cal.App.3d 1, 15.) The attorney fees necessary to successfully procure a final decision dissolving the injunction also are damages that should be included in setting the bond. (Id. at 15-16.) While Abba reasoned that the plaintiff’s likelihood of prevailing is irrelevant to setting the bond, a more recent case disagreed, stating that the greater the likelihood of the plaintiff prevailing, the less likely the preliminary injunction will have been wrongly issued, and that is a relevant factor for setting the bond. (Oiye v. Fox, (2012) 211 Cal.App.4th 1036, 1062.)

The parties do not address the issue of bond. The Court will address the issue of bond with counsel at the hearing.

V.              Conclusion

Petitioner and PD1 have shown a probability of success on the merits, and the balance of harms favors Petitioner and PD1. The motions/preliminary injunction are GRANTED.
