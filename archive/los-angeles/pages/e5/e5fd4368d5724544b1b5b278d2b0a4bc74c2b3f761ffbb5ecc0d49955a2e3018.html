MATTER

On May 28, 2024, Plaintiff Ceragem International, Inc. (“Plaintiff”) brought suit against Defendants Sungchil Baek (“Baek”) and Hong Min Kim (“Kim”) alleging four causes of action for (1) breach of contract; (2) fraud and deceit; (3) conversion; and (4) common counts. The proof of service indicates Baek was served with the summons and complaint by substitute service on June 3, 2024, and default was entered on August 6, 2024. Similarly, Kim was served by substitute service on May 31, 2024, and default was entered on August 6, 2024.  The Doe defendants were dismissed on March 10, 2025.

 

On March 5, 2025, Plaintiff requested default judgment as to both defendants in the amount of $224,144.97, representing special damages in the amount of $204,379 and interest in the amount of $19,765.97.

 

On February 5, 2025, Baek moved to vacate the entry of default, which the Court granted on April 2, 2025.  (See Minute Order, April 2, 2025.) 

 

After Baek’s counsel withdrew his representation and Baek failed to appear at deposition and failed to respond to discovery, Plaintiff again filed for entry of default against Baek on July 28, 2025, which was entered on July 29, 2025. 

 

At October 6, 2025, the Court held a Case Management Conference, Order to Show Cause re Default Judgment, and Informal Discovery Conference.  Baek appeared remotely at the hearing, assisted by a Korean interpreter.  In furtherance of the Court’s guidance regarding the strong public policy favoring litigating cases on the merits, Plaintiff attempted to obtain Baek’s stipulation to set aside the default, but Baek did not reply.  Notwithstanding, Plaintiff filed a motion to set aside Baek’s default, and the Court granted the unopposed motion on January 21, 2026.

 

Also on January 21, 2026, the Court analyzed the request for default judgment as to Kim only, which the Court denied without prejudice, finding Plaintiff had only substantiated damages in the amount of $5,929.

 

On February 24, 2026, the Court deemed admitted the matters specified in the RFA propounded on Baek.  And on August 11, 2026, the Court entered default against Baek.

 

Plaintiff now moves for default judgment against both Defendants in the amount of $201,892.44, comprised of $200,000 in special damages and costs in the amount of $1,892.44.

 

A.    Damages

 

             Plaintiff’s Complaint seeks $200,000 in special damages. (See Complaint ¶¶ 10, 18, 23.)  Therefore, Plaintiff does not seek damages in excess of what is pled in the Complaint.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 580, subd. (a) [“The relief granted to the plaintiff, if there is no answer, cannot exceed that demanded in the complaint”]; Levine v. Smith (2006) 145 Cal.App.4th 1131, 1136-1137 [“when recovering damages in a default judgment, the plaintiff is limited to the damages specified in the complaint”].)

            While Plaintiff has demonstrated judgment appropriate against Baek in the full amount, Plaintiff has still not demonstrated entitlement to judgment against Kim in an amount greater than the $5,929 charged to Kim’s card. 

 

            “Any matter admitted in response to a  request for admission is conclusively established against the party making the admission in the pending action[.]  […] [A]ny admission made by a party under this section is binding only on that party and is made for the purpose of the pending action only.  It is not an admission by that party for any other purpose, and it shall not be used in any manner against that party in any other proceeding.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.410, emphasis added.)  Indeed, “an agent’s deemed admissions do not bind the principal codefendant, even when the basis for the action against the principal codefendant is vicarious liability arising from the acts of the agent.  To hold otherwise would directly contradict the plain language of section 2033.410.”  (Inzunza v. Naranjo (2023) 94 Cal.App.5th 736, 746.)

 

            As such, the evidence of the matters deemed admitted as to Baek are not binding on Kim and cannot be used to establish liability on the part of Kim as to the $198,450 charged to the account ending in 3397.

 

            Therefore, the Court finds Plaintiff is entitled to judgment damages in the amount of $200,000 as to Baek, of which Kim is jointly and severally liable for only $5,929.

 

B.    Costs

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5 outlines recoverable costs to a prevailing party under Code of Civil Procedure section 1032.  The Code of Civil Procedure defines the “prevailing party” as follows:

 

[T]he party with a net monetary recovery, a defendant in whose favor a dismissal is entered, a defendant where neither plaintiff nor defendant obtains any relief, and a defendant as against those plaintiffs who do not recover any relief against that defendant. If any party recovers other than monetary relief and in situations other than as specified, the “prevailing party” shall be as determined by the court, and under those circumstances, the court, in its discretion, may allow costs or not and, if allowed, may apportion costs between the parties on the same or adverse sides pursuant to rules adopted under Section 1034.

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 1032, subd. (a)(4).)

           

            Plaintiff seeks costs in the amount of $1,892.44, comprised of filing and motion fees in the amount of $508.41, jury fees in the amount of $150, deposition costs in the amount of $912, interpreter fees in the amount of $142.08, and electronic filing/service fees in the amount of $179.95.  (See Memorandum of Costs.)  Plaintiff’s request for costs is granted as Plaintiff is the prevailing party in this action. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1032, subd. (a)(4).)

LEGAL STANDARDS – MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT/ADJUDICATION

 

“[T]he party moving for summary judgment bears the burden of persuasion that there is no triable issue of material fact and that he is entitled to judgment as a matter of law[.] There is a triable issue of material fact if, and only if, the evidence would allow a reasonable trier of fact to find the underlying fact in favor of the party opposing the motion in accordance with the applicable standard of proof.” ¿(Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850 (hereafter Aguilar).) ¿“[T]he party moving for summary judgment bears an initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing of the nonexistence of any triable issue of material fact; if he carries his burden of production, he causes a shift, and the opposing party is then subjected to a burden of production of his own to make a prima facie showing of the existence of a triable issue of material fact.” ¿(Ibid.; Smith v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 1463, 1474 [summary judgment standards held by Aguilar apply to summary adjudication motions].) 

 

Further, “the trial court may not weigh the evidence in the manner of a factfinder to determine whose version is more likely true.  Nor may the trial court grant summary judgment based on the court's evaluation of credibility.”  (Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th. at p. 840 [cleaned up]; see also Weiss v. People ex rel. Department of Transportation (2020) 9 Cal.5th 840, 864 [“Courts deciding motions for summary judgment or summary adjudication may not weigh the evidence but must instead view it in the light most favorable to the opposing party and draw all reasonable inferences in favor of that party”].) 

 

A party may move for summary adjudication as to one or more causes of action, affirmative defenses, claims for damages, or issues of duty if that party contends that there is no merit to the cause of action, defense, or claim for damages, or if the party contends that there is no duty owed.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (f)(1).)  “A motion for summary adjudication shall be granted only if it completely disposes of a cause of action, an affirmative defense, a claim for damages, or an issue of duty.”  (Ibid.)  A cause of action has no merit if: (1) one or more elements of the cause of action cannot be separately established, even if that element is separately pleaded, or (2) a defendant establishes an affirmative defense to that cause of action.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (n); Union Bank v. Superior Court (1995) 31 Cal.App.4th 573, 583.)  Once the defendant has shown that a cause of action has no merit, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to show that a triable issue of material fact exists as to that cause of action.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (o)(2); Union Bank v. Superior Court, supra, 31 Cal.App.4th at p. 583.)  Additionally, in line with Aguilar, “[o]n a motion for summary adjudication, the trial court has no discretion to exercise.  If a triable issue of material fact exists as to the challenged causes of action, the motion must be denied. If there is no triable issue of fact, the motion must be granted.”  (Fisherman's Wharf Bay Cruise Corp. v. Superior Court (2003) 114 Cal.App.4th 309, 320.)

Fidelity National Title Co. (2013) 213 Cal.App.4th 486, 493 [cleaned up]; see also Laabs v. City of Victorville (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 1242, 1258 [“The complaint limits the issues to be addressed at the motion for summary judgment. In support of the motion, Wilen advances his own declaration, paragraph 40 of which provides, “I have never touched Plaintiff, and did not pull a knife or any other weapon on Plaintiff, at the parking lot at 2323 Roscomare Road or at any other location, as alleged in the Complaint paragraph 101.”  (Wilen Decl. ¶ 40.)

 

Thus, Wilen has met his initial prima facie burdens of production and persuasion to demonstrate he did not pull a knife on Plaintiff in the parking lot of 2323 Roscomare Road, or at any other location, as alleged in paragraph 101 of the Complaint.

 

b.     Plaintiff’s Evidence 

 

In opposition, Plaintiff advances her own declaration, paragraph 9 of which provides, “In addition Wilen was attacking me with a blue silver knife on multiple occasions in the parking lot of 2323 Roscomare Road, LA CA 90077.”  (Heshmati Decl. ¶ 9.)

 

In supplemental declaration, Plaintiff also declares, “One day in 2021, the plaintiff was trying to purchase food from a food truck who she has known for years (Arturo’s) in bel air on Roscomare Road, and all of sudden a strange man ran up to me and tried to attack her screaming that she had “turned Cynthia Roan aaronson’s life upside down” and he pulled out a Blue silver knife and tried to stab and attack her.  That is how the plaintiff met […] defendant Wilen.”  (Supp. Heshmati Decl. ¶¶ 10-11.)

 

In reply, Wilen argues that if the alleged attempted stabbing occurred in 2021, it would be time barred, since there is a two-year statute of limitations for assault and battery (Code Civ. Proc., § 335.1) and Plaintiff did not file the Complaint until August 26, 2024, more than two years later.

 

While that may be so, Plaintiff declares that the alleged stabbing incident in 2021 is when Plaintiff first met Wilen, but there were multiple incidents where Wilen pulled a knife on Plaintiff in the parking lot of 2323 Roscomare Road, which is consistent with the Complaint, which alleges multiple such incidents between 2023 and 2024.

 

Thus, Plaintiff has met her burden of production to create triable issues of material fact as to Issue 2.

 

3.     ISSUE 4 – CLAIM AND ISSUE PRECLUSION

 

Wilen argues that Plaintiff’s claims are barred by claim and issue preclusion, having already been litigated and decided in Heshmati v. Wilen, Los Angeles Superior Court Case No. 24STRO00564. 

 

Collateral estoppel has five elements:

 

First, the issue sought to be precluded from relitigation must be identical to that decided in a former proceeding. Second, this issue must have been actually litigated in the former proceeding. Third, it must have been necessarily decided in the former proceeding. Fourth, the decision in the former proceeding must be final and on the merits. Finally, the party against whom preclusion is sought must be the same as, or in privity with, the party to the former proceeding.

(Lucido v. Superior Court (1990) 51 Cal.3d 335, 341.)

 

a.     Wilen’s Evidence

 

In support, Wilen advances as Exhibit 11, the trial transcript for Case No. 24STRO00564.  The standard for that hearing was the heightened “clear and convincing evidence” standard that Wilen harassed Plaintiff for no legitimate purpose.  In that hearing, the parties gave testimony about the purported stabbing incidents, before the Court ultimately determined that Plaintiff failed to meet her burden to produce clear and convincing evidence that Wilen engaged in harassment for no legitimate purpose.

 

By contrast, the default standard of proof for civil actions is preponderance of the evidence.  (See, e.g., In re Marriage of Ettefagh (2007) 150 Cal.App.4th 1578, 1585.)  Further, as discussed above, the issue on summary judgment is whether triable issues of material fact exist.  The Court is not rendering any determination on the merits of any claim or affirmative defense similar to any determinations that may follow a trial. 

 

The fact that Plaintiff failed to satisfy the heightened “clear and convincing evidence” standard in connection with the restraining order matter does not automatically mean that Plaintiff will be unable to demonstrate assault and battery by a preponderance of the evidence at trial. 

 

Therefore, the Court does not find Wilen has met his initial burdens of production and persuasion to demonstrate that Plaintiff’s claim for assault and battery is collaterally estopped by virtue of the restraining order disposition.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

 

For the reasons stated, the Court grants in part Wilen’s motion for summary adjudication.  Finding Wilen has met his initial burdens of production and persuasion to demonstrate that he was not behind the online postings or the communications underlying the first five causes of action, and that Plaintiff has failed to create any triable issues of material fact, the Court grants summary adjudication of Issue 1 as to the first five causes of action. 

 

Having granted summary adjudication of Issue 1, the Court declines to analyze Issue 3 as moot. 

 

As for Issue 2, Plaintiff has met her burden to create triable issues of material fact as to whether Wilen committed an assault and battery with a knife in 2023-2024.  As such, the Court denies summary adjudication of Issue 2 (regarding the sixth cause of action).

 

Regarding Issue 4, the Court does not find Wilen has met his initial prima facie burdens of production and persuasion to demonstrate that Plaintiff’s sixth cause of action is barred by res judicata or collateral estoppel, due to the differing standards of proof.  As such, the Court denies summary adjudication of Issue 4 (regarding the sixth cause of action).

 

Having denied summary adjudication of Issues 2 and 4 (pertaining to the sixth cause of action), the Court also denies Wilen’s motion for summary judgment.

 

Further, the Court will enter the proposed Order lodged on June 23, 2026 in conformity with the ruling.

Per Code of Civil Procedure section 639, “[w]hen the parties do not consent, the court may, upon the written motion of any party, or of its own motion, appoint a referee in the following cases pursuant to the provisions of subdivision (b) of Section 640:  . . .  [w]hen the court in any pending action determines that it is necessary for the court to appoint a referee to hear and determine any and all discovery motions and disputes relevant to discovery in the action and to report findings and make a recommendation thereon.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 639, subd. (a); see also Cal. Rules of Court, rules 3.922, et seq.) 

 

            Here, the Court finds that the appointment of a discovery referee for all discovery purposes, whose fees may be shared equally between the parties or as determined/recommended by the discovery referee, to be necessary[1] as the parties’ ongoing discovery disputes make the action more complicated and time consuming in comparison to other similar actions pending before the Court. 

 

To illustrate, the Court conducted an Informal Discovery Conference on August 13, 2026.  At the conference, the Court admonished the parties to adhere to the Discovery Act and cooperate to ensure all parties have fair access to material information. 

 

Despite the admonishment, Plaintiff Lincolnway Partners, LLC (“Plaintiff”) has filed and scheduled four discovery motions regarding 3 separate sets of Requests for Production of Documents served on Defendants Tishman Speyers Properties, Inc., 1430 Lincoln Blvd., LLC and MW Doe Street Corp. (collectively, “Defendants”) and 1 set of Form Interrogatories served on all Defendants. 

 

In summary, Plaintiff is seeking rulings regarding 174 document requests, compelling the review of hundreds of pages of papers filed as moving, opposition and reply papers by the parties, including voluminous initial and reply separate statements, and requiring the examination of hundreds of pages of documents purportedly produced by Defendants to determine whether Defendants have fulfilled their obligations under the Discovery Act.  And in relation to the document requests which span over three separate motions, Plaintiff is seeking $14,630 in monetary sanctions against Defendants.  The Court estimates that consideration of those discovery motions will consume hours of Court time per motion.[2] 

 

Further, at the Informal  Discovery Conference, counsel for Plaintiff represented that Defendants’ further responses to the Form Interrogatories were satisfactory, but Plaintiff would still seek $10,450.00 in monetary sanctions against Defendants.  Although the parties have resolved the underlying discovery dispute, the Court will still be burdened with reviewing the entirety of the papers filed in conjunction with the motion (moving, opposition and reply) to determine whether the monetary sanctions is warranted under the circumstances.  That endeavor alone will consume hours of Court time. 

 

“It is a central precept to the Civil Discovery Act of 1986 that civil discovery be essentially self-executing.   A self-executing discovery system is one that operates without judicial involvement.  Conduct frustrates the goal of a self-executing discovery system when it requires the trial court to become involved in discovery because a dispute leads a party to move for an order compelling a response.”  (Clement v. Alegre (2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 1277, 1291–1292 [cleaned up].) 

 

Here, the parties’ continued protracted and acrimonious discovery disputes, warranting Court intervention, run afoul of the central precept of the Discovery Act, which has become non-self-executing and non-self-regulating in the context of this action.  In short, despite the Court’s informal intervention to assist the parties in resolving their discovery disputes, the parties have been unable to resolve their disputes, warranting intervention by the Court through the scheduling of an Informal Discovery Conference and four discovery-related motions.  In short, the Court finds that the parties have been  unable to curb their discovery related rancor.[3]

There was no such finding in this case, only a bare conclusion parroting the words of the statute. That is not enough, particularly where, as here, it appears that any judge could resolve this discovery “dispute” in about five minutes.”  (Hood v. Superior Court (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 446, 449 [cleaned up])  Here, the Court finds that the resolution of the parties’ continuing discovery disputes have taken, and will take, longer than five minutes to resolve. 

 

[2] The burden on the Court must be measured against the Court’s impacted docket with over 1,100 pending cases, involving dozens of hearings per day and dozens of motions per week while at the same time presiding over both bench and jury trials.  (See McDonald v. FHP, Inc. (1998) 64 Cal.App.4th 1506, 1532 [“While we recognize the burden of the heavy caseloads carried by trial court judges, we must express a word of caution. The parties are entitled to the decision of the trial court with respect to each matter or issue presented by them except where they properly agree otherwise or a statute permits an appropriate reference. The apparently routine practice of referring out law and motion matters without obtaining the parties' prior written or record consent cannot be justified however helpful it might be in calendar management. Efficiency is not more important than preserving the constitutional integrity of the judicial process”].)  Here, the Court finds that the potential appointment of a referee in this action is not for “routine” discovery disputes/motions, and the Court does not have a “routine” practice of appointing referees under Section 639. 

 

[3] The court of appeal has held that “[t]he court should not make blanket orders directing all discovery motions to a discovery referee except in the unusual case where a majority of factors favoring reference are present. BACKGROUND

 

This case arises from a dispute concerning an outstanding debt.  On December 4, 2025, Plaintiff Korn Ferry (“Plaintiff”) filed suit against Defendant Jelena Tasker (“Defendant”) alleging three causes of action for breaches of three separate promissory notes.

 

Plaintiff now moves for summary judgment, or in the alternative summary adjudication of the following issues:

 

1. 2. Plaintiff’s Second Cause of Action for Breach of the 2023 Promissory Note: Korn Ferry is entitled to summary adjudication because admissible evidence is presented in support of this motion on each element of its cause of action for breach of promissory note, demonstrating that Korn Ferry is entitled to judgment on this cause of action in the amount of $45,000, plus prejudgment interest at 7.75% per annum (compounded annually) from November 7, 2025, through the date of entry of judgment, and reasonable attorneys’ fees and costs.

 

3. Plaintiff’s Third Cause of Action for Breach of the 2025 Promissory Note: Korn Ferry is entitled to summary adjudication because admissible evidence is presented on each element of its cause of action for breach of promissory note, demonstrating that Korn Ferry is entitled to judgment on this cause of action in the amount of $50,000, plus pre-judgment interest at 7.75% per annum (compounded annually) from November 7, 2025, through the date of entry of judgment, and reasonable attorneys’ fees and costs.

 

4. Tasker’s First Affirmative Defense (Unconscionability under Civil Code section 1670.5): 2 The affirmative defense lacks merit because the undisputed material facts establish that, as a matter of law, Tasker cannot demonstrate either procedural or substantive unconscionability with respect to any of the three Notes, and Korn Ferry is therefore entitled to summary adjudication.

LEGAL STANDARDS – MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT/ADJUDICATION

 

“[T]he party moving for summary judgment bears the burden of persuasion that there is no triable issue of material fact and that he is entitled to judgment as a matter of law[.] There is a triable issue of material fact if, and only if, the evidence would allow a reasonable trier of fact to find the underlying fact in favor of the party opposing the motion in accordance with the applicable standard of proof.” ¿(Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850 (hereafter Aguilar).) ¿“[T]he party moving for summary judgment bears an initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing of the nonexistence of any triable issue of material fact; if he carries his burden of production, he causes a shift, and the opposing party is then subjected to a burden of production of his own to make a prima facie showing of the existence of a triable issue of material fact.” ¿(Ibid.; Smith v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 1463, 1474 [summary judgment standards held by Aguilar apply to summary adjudication motions].) 

 

Further, “the trial court may not weigh the evidence in the manner of a factfinder to determine whose version is more likely true.  Nor may the trial court grant summary judgment based on the court's evaluation of credibility.”  (Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th. at p. 840 [cleaned up]; see also Weiss v. People ex rel. Department of Transportation (2020) 9 Cal.5th 840, 864 [“Courts deciding motions for summary judgment or summary adjudication may not weigh the evidence but must instead view it in the light most favorable to the opposing party and draw all reasonable inferences in favor of that party”].) 

 

A party may move for summary adjudication as to one or more causes of action, affirmative defenses, claims for damages, or issues of duty if that party contends that there is no merit to the cause of action, defense, or claim for damages, or if the party contends that there is no duty owed.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (f)(1).)  “A motion for summary adjudication shall be granted only if it completely disposes of a cause of action, an affirmative defense, a claim for damages, or an issue of duty.”  (Ibid.)  A cause of action has no merit if: (1) one or more elements of the cause of action cannot be separately established, even if that element is separately pleaded, or (2) a defendant establishes an affirmative defense to that cause of action.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (n); Union Bank v. Superior Court (1995) 31 Cal.App.4th 573, 583.)  Once the defendant has shown that a cause of action has no merit, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to show that a triable issue of material fact exists as to that cause of action.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (o)(2); Union Bank v. Superior Court, supra, 31 Cal.App.4th at p. 583.)  Additionally, in line with Aguilar, “[o]n a motion for summary adjudication, the trial court has no discretion to exercise.  If a triable issue of material fact exists as to the challenged causes of action, the motion must be denied. If there is no triable issue of fact, the motion must be granted.”  (Fisherman's Wharf Bay Cruise Corp. v. Superior Court (2003) 114 Cal.App.4th 309, 320.)

1.     ISSUES 1-3 THE PROMISSORY NOTES

b.     Defendant’s Evidence

 

In opposition, Defendant argues that the promissory notes were not ordinary loans, but rather salary advances, earned incrementally by continued employment, and because they were contingent upon Plaintiff’s continued employment, they are somehow not valid and enforceable repayment obligations as a matter of law.

 

But Defendant does not cite any legal authority standing for the proposition that salary advances contingent upon continued employment are somehow not valid and enforceable repayment obligations.  In fact, Defendant cites to Steinhebel v. Los Angeles Times Communications, LLC (2005) 126 Cal.App.4th 696, 704, which held that an employer may legally advance commissions to its employees prior to the completion of all conditions for payment and, by agreement, charge back any excess advance over commissions earned against any future advance should the conditions not be satisfied without violating Labor Code section 221’s prohibition on employers collecting from an employee any part of wages already paid to an employee. 

 

Defendant also argues there are triable issues of material fact as to whether the subjects of the second and third promissory notes constitute wages already earned or contingent advancements because the promissory notes conflict with the language of Defendant’s offer letter. 

 

Specifically, with respect to the second promissory note, Defendant points to the language of the employment offer, which characterizes the FY23 $75,000 amount as a “guaranteed incentive award.”  But the offer letter itself explains:

 

“This guaranteed amount will be secured by a promissory note which will be forgiven in five equal instalments on each of the first, second, third, fourth and fifth anniversaries of the payment date, provided that on each such date you remain in active, full-time employment with Korn Ferry. In order to be eligible for this guaranteed incentive award, you must maintain your full-time active employment in good standing through the July 31, 2023 payment date.”

 

Defendant seizes on the language “In order to be eligible for this guaranteed incentive award, you must maintain your full-time active employment in good standing through the July 31, 2023 payment date” to argue that because she maintained her employment through July 31, 2023, she is guaranteed entitlement to the full $75,000 amount.  The Court disagrees.  In context, it is clear from the terms of the promissory note, as articulated in the offer letter itself, that Defendant was guaranteed the first $15,000 portion of the $75,000 amount after maintaining employment through July 31, 2023, and that Defendant would earn an additional $15,000 annually in five equal installments, fully earning the entire $75,000 on her fifth anniversary.

 

            As for the third promissory note, Defendant argues that Plaintiff informed Defendant she had earned a $100,000 annual incentive award based upon her completed performance, but refused to pay Defendant her $100,000 earned incentive award outright unless Defendant agreed to sign a promissory note with respect to $50,000 on a multi-year arrangement. 

 

            In support, Defendant advances her own declaration, which provides:

 

28. On or about July 1, 2025, I participated in my annual performance review with my reporting manager, David Wise. At that time, the performance period associated with my FY25 annual incentive compensation had concluded. My FY25 Performance Review dated July 1, 2025 is included as Defendant’s Exhibit J.

 

29. During that performance review, Mr. Wise informed me that, based on my performance that fiscal year, I had earned an annual bonus of $100,000. My contemporaneous FY25 Individual Performance Dashboard reflected my performance metrics for that fiscal year. Screenshots of that dashboard taken on April 30, 2025 are included as Defendant’s Exhibit K.

 

30. During the same conversation, I was informed for the first time that Korn Ferry would not pay the portion of my earned bonus exceeding $50,000 to me as ordinary cash compensation.

 

31. I was presented with two alternatives for the remaining $50,000: a promissory-note arrangement or a deferred-compensation arrangement subject to a five-year vesting period. In my July 7, 2025 email to Mr. Wise, I confirmed that I would proceed with the promissory-note option, noting that he had “marked me already” for that option. Mr. Wise responded, “that’s the same thing I’d have done.” A true and correct copy of that exchange is included as Defendant’s Exhibit L. Mr. Wise subsequently referred in writing to the separate “non-prom note portion” and “prom note portion” of the bonus payment in his July 11, 2025 email to me, included as Defendant’s Exhibit M.

 

32. I requested that Korn Ferry pay my full $100,000 annual bonus in cash without an additional repayment, vesting, or continued-employment condition. That option was not made available to me.

 

35. After Korn Ferry declined to pay the remaining $50,000 as ordinary cash compensation and limited the available alternatives to the promissory-note or deferred-compensation structures, I executed the document identified in this action as the 2025 Note, which Plaintiff submitted as Exhibit 7 to Plaintiff’s Compendium of Evidence.

 

(Tasker Decl. ¶¶ 28-32, 35.)

 

            The documents attached as Exhibits J through M do not characterize the $100,000 as an annual bonus.  Rather, Defendant’s own declaration demonstrates that in the conversation where Mr. Wise offered Defendant the additional compensation, he informed Defendant that $50,000 would be payable outright, and $50,000 would either be payable on a deferred compensation structure or pursuant to a promissory note.

 

            Thus, even if Mr. Wise initially verbally mischaracterized the $100,000 as consisting entirely of an annual bonus, in the same meeting, he corrected himself that $50,000 is an earned bonus and the other $50,000 is an incentive bonus, to be earned over the course of years.

 

            Further, Defendant’s declaration demonstrates that Defendant was offered the choice of receiving the additional $50,000 payment either over time or up front and pursuant to a promissory note, and Defendant chose to execute the promissory note, affirmatively agreeing in writing that it was to be earned over time and repaid if Defendant’s employment did not last throughout the repayment period.  If Defendant believed she was entitled to the entirety of the $100,000, she could have demanded payment and sued Plaintiff without signing a promissory note to the contrary. 

 

For the same reason, because Defendant did have a choice in whether to receive the payments up front pursuant to a promissory note or whether to receive the payments as earned, the agreements are not procedurally unconscionable.

 

            Defendant further argues that the Notes violate California’s public policy against restraining employee mobility.  But Defendant does not cite to any binding authority standing for the proposition that advanced bonuses contingent upon continued employment are unlawful.

 

            Thus, Defendant has not met her burden of production to create any triable issues of material fact that the promissory notes are not valid and binding.

 

2.     INTEREST & ATTORNEY FEES

 

The Notes provide that amounts become due and payable upon Defendant’s voluntary resignation of employment and that any sum payable but not paid when due shall bear interest at the lesser of (a) the reference rate of Bank of America plus 0.75 percent as of the applicable due date and (b) 10%.

 

Plaintiff advances the Johnson Declaration which provides:

 

13. Korn Ferry maintains records of the applicable Bank of America reference rate. As of November 7, 2025, the Bank of America reference rate was 7.00%, which when added to the 0.75% margin yields a contract rate of 7.75% per annum, compounded annually. A true and correct copy of Korn Ferry’s record reflecting the applicable Bank of America reference rate as of November 7, 2025 is attached as Exhibit 9 to the Compendium of Evidence. On the $135,000 aggregate principal, that rate equates to $10,462.50 per year, or $28.66 per day, accruing through the date of judgment, subject to annual compounding.

 

(Johnson Decl. ¶ 13.)  

 

            Plaintiff has demonstrated entitlement to prejudgment interest on the outstanding $135,000 principal balance from Defendant’s November 7, 2025 voluntary resignation date.  The Notes also provide that Defendant shall pay reasonable attorneys’ fees and costs incurred in enforcing the Notes.  Thus, Plaintiff has demonstrated entitlement to reasonable attorneys’ fees and costs incurred in this enforcement action.  And more important, Defendant has not proffered evidence to establish triable issues of material fact regarding the interest and attorneys’ fees that may be owed based on the breach of the promissory notes.
