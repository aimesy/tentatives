Motions to Compel Discovery Responses

MOVING PARTY

Plaintiff Cal26 Property Group, LLC

OPPOSING PARTY

None

 

MOTIONS

            In two separate motions, Plaintiff Cal26 Property Group, LLC (“Plaintiff”) moved to compel responses from Defendant Gabriel Pope (“Defendant”) to Interrogatories to Enforce November 6, 2025 Order, set one (“SROG”) and Request to Inspect Writings, set two (“RPD”). 

ANALYSIS

Plaintiff served the SROG on Defendant on November 6, 2025 by email.  As such, Defendant’s responses were due by December 9, 2025.  Plaintiff served the RPD on Defendant on November 13, 2025 by email, and responses were thus due by December 16, 2025.  As of the filing date of these motions, Defendant has not responded to the discovery.  Accordingly, the motions to compel responses to the SROG and RPD are granted pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 2030.290 and 2031.300.  Defendant is ordered to serve verified responses to Plaintiff’s SROG and RPD within 30 days of service of this order.

Plaintiff seeks sanctions in connection with the motions.  The Court concludes  Defendant’s failure to timely respond to the SROG and RPD was an abuse of the discovery process and awards sanctions.  Therefore, the Court imposes sanctions against Defendant in the amount of $1,820, which represents two hours of attorney time to prepare the motions and attend the hearing at $850 per hour, plus the filing fees. 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Plaintiff’s motions to compel responses to the SROG and RPD are granted.  Defendant is to serve verified responses within 30 days of notice of this order. 

Defendant is to pay $1,820 in sanctions to Plaintiff within 30 days of notice of this order.

Plaintiff is ordered to provide notice of the Court’s ruling and to file proof of service of same.

MOTION

Motion to Compel Further Discovery Responses

MOVING PARTY

Plaintiff Cal26 Property Group, LLC

OPPOSING PARTIES

Defendant Skylar Vincent

 

MOTION

Plaintiff Cal26 Property Group, LLC (“Plaintiff”) moves to compel further responses from Defendant Skylar Vincent (“Defendant”) to Special Interrogatories, set one (“SROG”).  Defendant opposes the motion.

ANALYSIS

If the propounding party deems responses to interrogatories unsatisfactory, the propounding party may move to compel further responses.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300.) 

Defendant provided substantive responses to the SROG, including supplemental responses.  Plaintiff is dissatisfied with the responses and contends they are non-responsive.  As Defendant contends, in essence, Plaintiff challenges Defendant’s characterization of the communications Defendant identified in response to the subject interrogatories. 

The Court cannot compel Defendant to admit particular facts.  (Holguin v. Superior Court (1972) 22 Cal.App.3d 812, 820.)  Plaintiff cannot move to compel further responses because Plaintiff would prefer another response from Defendant.  Rather, Plaintiff’s remedy is to offer Defendant’s allegedly incomplete responses as admissions or as prior inconsistent statements to impeach Defendant at trial, if Defendant seeks to introduce evidence Defendant did not disclose in discovery.  (Evid. Code, §§ 1220; 1235.)

Accordingly, the motion is denied.

Plaintiff is ordered to give notice of the Court’s ruling and to file proof of service of same.

MOTIONS

Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings

MOVING PARTIES

Defendants Gabriel Pope and Skylar Vincent

OPPOSING PARTIES

Plaintiffs Cal26 Property Group, LLC and Robert Gentino

 

MOTION

Defendants Gabriel Pope and Skylar Vincent (“Defendants”) move for judgment on the pleadings on the operative first amended complaint of Plaintiffs Cal26 Property Group, LLC (“Cal26 Property Group”) and Robert Gentino (collectively, “Plaintiffs”).  Plaintiffs oppose the motion.

ANALYSIS

A motion for judgment on the pleadings has the same function as a general demurrer but may be made after the time to demur has expired. (Code Civ. Proc., § 438, subd. (f).)  “Like a demurrer, the grounds for the motion [for judgment on the pleadings] must appear on the face of the challenged pleading or from any matter of which the court is required to take judicial notice.” (Civic Partners Stockton, LLC v. Youssefi (2013) 218 Cal.App.4th 1005, 1013.)  In ruling on a motion for judgment on the pleadings, “[a]ll allegations in the complaint and matters upon which judicial notice may be taken are assumed to be true.”  (Rippon v. Bowen (2008) 160 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1313.)

Defendants argue Cal26 Property Group fails to allege it has standing.  Defendants are incorrect.  In this regard, Cal26 Property Group alleges it “is the assignee of all rights to the First through Fifth causes of action.”  (First Amended Complaint, p. 2.)  For pleading purposes, these allegations suffice.  Defendants may obtain further information regarding Plaintiffs’ claims by conducting appropriate discovery. (See, e.g.,  Khoury v. Maly’s of Cal., Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616 [“A demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures”].)

Moreover, for purposes of a judgment on the pleadings, when issues of fact are to be determined, the Court is confined to considering the four corners of the operative complaint; it must liberally construe allegations in that pleading; and it is to draw inferences favorable to the non-moving party.  In this regard, the Court notes Defendants have cited to materials, and alleged facts, which are outside of this boundary, and they have not sought judicial notice of material relative to any issue.  Thus, to consider such matters, a more developed record through discovery conducted down the line must be provided. 

Defendants also argue the litigation privilege bars the sixth, seventh, and eighth causes of action.  The litigation privilege “applies to any communication (1) made in judicial or quasi-judicial proceedings; (2) by litigants or other participants authorized by law; (3) to achieve the objects of the litigation; and (4) that have some connection or logical relation to the action.”  (Jacob B. v. County of Shasta (2007) 40 Cal.4th 948, 955, internal quotations omitted.)  The litigation privilege applies “even though the publication is made outside the courtroom and no function of the court or its officers is involved.” (Silberg v. Anderson (1990) 50 Cal.3d 205, 212.) 

As Defendants point out, the sixth, seventh, and eighth causes of action are premised on communications between Plaintiffs and Defendants in connection with this litigation.  Plaintiffs ignore this issue in opposition to the motion and thereby have waived any argument the litigation privilege does not bar these causes of action.  (See Bell v. American Title Ins. Co. (1991) 226 Cal.App.3d 1589, 1602 [failure to oppose motion constitutes waiver].)  Accordingly, the Court grants the motion for judgment on the pleadings on the sixth, seventh, and eighth causes of action without leave to amend.

Likewise, as Plaintiffs ignore Defendants’ argument Plaintiffs fail to allege the second and fourth causes of action for fraud with the requisite specificity, the Court grants the motion for judgment on the pleadings on the second and fourth causes of action without leave to amend.  (See Wilhelm v. Pray, Price, Williams & Russell (1986) 186 Cal.App.3d 1324, 1331 [“general pleading of the legal conclusion of fraud is insufficient; and . . . every element of the cause of action for fraud must be alleged in full, factually and specifically, and the policy of liberal construction of pleading will not usually be invoked to sustain a pleading that is defective in any material respect”].)

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Defendants’ motion for judgment on the pleadings as to the second, fourth, sixth, seventh, and eighth causes of action in the operative first amended complaint is granted without leave to amend.  The motion for judgment on the pleadings is otherwise denied.

Defendants are ordered to provide notice of the Court’s ruling and to file proof of service of same.

MOTION

Motion for Sanctions

MOVING PARTIES

Plaintiffs Cal26 Property Group, LLC and Robert Gentino

OPPOSING PARTY

None

 

MOTION

Plaintiffs Cal26 Property Group, LLC and Robert Gentino (“Plaintiffs”) seek sanctions against Defendants Gabriel Pope and Skylar Vincent (“Defendants”) under Code of Civil Procedure section 128.7 based on Defendants’ pending motion for judgment on the pleadings.  Defendants have not opposed the motion.  The Court previously considered this motion and, in its May 11, 2026 order, continued the matter to this date.

ANALYSIS

Code of Civil Procedure section 128.7 requires an attorney or self-represented litigant to sign all pleadings, petitions, notices of motions, and other similar papers.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 128.7, subd. (a).)  By the signature, that person certifies the “legal contentions therein are warranted by existing law or by a nonfrivolous argument for the extension, modification, or reversal of existing law or the establishment of new law.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 128.7, subd. (b)(2).)  If the Court determines an attorney or self-represented litigant has violated this requirement, the Court may impose sanctions against the attorney or self-represented litigant.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 128.7, subd. (c).)

As set forth in the Court’s order on the motion for judgment on the pleadings which is being heard concomitantly with the instant motion, the Court grants the motion for judgment on the pleadings, in part.  As such, the Court cannot impose sanctions, because Defendants’ motion does not indisputably have no merit.  (See, e.g., Plantations at Haywood 1, LLC v. Plantations at Haywood, LLC (2025) 108 Cal.App.5th 803, 816 [motion for sanctions pursuant to Code of Civil procedure section 128.7 against a party who brought an appeal is granted when the appeal “lacks any semblance of merit”].)

Accordingly, this motion for sanctions is denied.

Plaintiffs are to give notice of the Court’s ruling and to file proof of service of same.

MOTION

Motion for Summary Judgment or, in the Alternative, Summary Adjudication

MOVING PARTY

Plaintiff Creditors Adjustment Bureau, Inc.

OPPOSING PARTIES

Defendants Margarita Porras and Carlos Colin Campos

 

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Creditors Adjustment Bureau, Inc. (“Plaintiff”) moves for summary judgment against Defendants Margarita Porras (“Porras”) and Carlos Colin Campos (collectively, “Defendants”).  Defendants’ opposition was due no later than June 30, 2026.  Defendants served their opposition on July 6, 2026.  As such, the opposition is untimely.  Nonetheless, the Court exercises its discretion to consider the opposition papers, as Plaintiff replied to the merits of the opposition and thereby waived any objection to the late-filed opposition. 

LEGAL STANDARD – MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT

“[T]he party moving for summary judgment bears the burden of persuasion that there is no triable issue of material fact and that he is entitled to judgment as a matter of law[.] There is a triable issue of material fact if, and only if, the evidence would allow a reasonable trier of fact to find the underlying fact in favor of the party opposing the motion in accordance with the applicable standard of proof.”  (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850.)  “[T]he party moving for summary judgment bears an initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing of the nonexistence of any triable issue of material fact; if he carries his burden of production, he causes a shift, and the opposing party is then subjected to a burden of production of his own to make a prima facie showing of the existence of a triable issue of material fact.”  (Ibid.)  

When the plaintiff seeks summary adjudication, the plaintiff bears the burden to produce admissible evidence on each element of every cause of action at issue. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(1).)

DISCUSSION

Plaintiff asserts one cause of action for breach of promissory note against Defendants.  Plaintiff advances evidence Defendants entered into a settlement agreement with Xtra Lease LLC (“Assignor”).  The settlement agreement required Defendants to pay Assignor $5,000 by July 3, 2024 and then $1,500 per month for fifteen consecutive months and, should Defendants fail to make any payment as required, Defendants would owe the entire principal amount of $59,010.23, less any payments made, plus interest, collections costs, and attorney fees.  (Declaration of Humberto Matz, Exhibit 1.) 

Plaintiff presents evidence Defendants made the initial payment and three monthly payments and thereafter ceased making payments.  (Matz Decl., ¶ 6.)  Assignor then assigned its rights to collect on the settlement agreement to Plaintiff.  (Id., ¶ 2.)  Plaintiff also advances evidence Defendants admitted in response to Plaintiff’s discovery they failed to make payments as required pursuant to the settlement agreement, and Defendants are, therefore, liable for $59,010.23, less any payments made, plus interest, collections costs, and attorney fees.  (Declaration of Rafael Munoz, Exhibits 3, 4.)  Such evidence is sufficient to satisfy Plaintiff’s burden to show Defendants are liable to Plaintiff for breach of contract.  Thus, Plaintiff has shifted the burden to Defendants to raise triable issues of material fact as to whether Defendants are liable for breach of the promissory note.

In opposition, Defendants do not dispute the pertinent facts.  Instead, Defendants rely on Porras’s declaration in which she states, “[t]he missed installments resulted from inadvertence, not from any intent to avoid payment.”  (Declaration of Margarita Porras, ¶ 8.)  Defendants thereby admittedly missed the payments. Their intention in doing so is immaterial. 

Defendants contend the settlement agreement is not enforceable pursuant to Ridgley v. Topa Thrift & Loan Assn. (1998) 17 Cal.4th 970 (Ridgley).  In that case, the California Supreme Court held a contractual liquidated damages clause is unreasonable and hence unenforceable as a penalty, if it bears no reasonable relationship to the range of actual damages the parties anticipated would flow from a breach.  (Id. at p. 977.)  Given the facts in the underlying case, the Supreme Court noted charges for late payments of loan installments are unenforceable when they bear no reasonable relationship to the injury the creditor might suffer from late payments.  (Id. at p. 978.)  

In the instant case, however, Plaintiff presents evidence the penalty for late payments of installments due pursuant to the settlement agreement bears a precise relationship to the injury Assignor anticipated would result from late payments.  The penalty represents the exact amount to which Assignor was entitled if Assignor had not entered the settlement agreement with Defendants.  As such, it is not a liquidated damages provision, but rather “[t]he amount of damages was not ‘predetermined’ . . . [i]t was the actual amount of damages then due and owing.”  (Creditors Adjustment Bureau, Inc. v. Imani (2022) 82 Cal.App.5th 131, 135.)  Accordingly, Ridgley is not on point, as it involved consideration of a liquidated damages provision which bore no relationship to the amount of actual loss incurred. 

In essence, Assignor “agreed to accept a significantly reduced settlement amount . . . in exchange for assurances that Defendants would be able to pay that reduced amount quickly.”  (Gormley v. Gonzalez (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 72, 86.)  There is “nothing unreasonable about parties . . . agreeing to settle . . . for a steep discount, and also agreeing that if the settlement amount is not paid, judgment will be entered on the amount the parties estimated would have been recovered at trial.”  (Id. at p. 87.) 

Defendants also cite Vitatech Internat., Inc. v. Sporn (2017) 16 Cal.App.5th 796, which is likewise not on point.  In that case, the settlement agreement provided for a stipulated judgment in the amount of $303,000 if the breaching party failed to satisfy the settlement for $75,000, without any evidence $303,000 represented the likely recovery if the settled claims proceeded to trial.  (Id. at p. 811.)  Here, Defendants rely on Porras’s declaration, in which she states Defendants “consistently disputed the amounts [Assignor] claimed we owed under the lease, and we did not believe Defendants owed those sums.”  (Porras Decl., ¶ 3.) 

Defendants fail to provide any explanation for the nature of the dispute or why Defendants would not owe such sums to Assignor.  It is Defendants’ burden, not Plaintiff’s, to show any clause in the settlement agreement is unenforceable.  (See, e.g.,  Civ. Code, § 1671, subd. (b) [“ provision in a contract liquidating the damages for the breach of the contract is valid unless the party seeking to invalidate the provision establishes that the provision was unreasonable under the circumstances existing at the time the contract was made”].)  Moreover, in opposing this summary judgment motion, Defendants cannot raise triable issues of material fact by citing inferences “derived from speculation, conjecture, imagination, or guesswork.”  (Joseph E. Di Loreto, Inc. v. O'Neill (1991) 1 Cal.App.4th 149, 161.) 

Defendants’ reliance on Greentree Financial Group, Inc. v. Execute Sports, Inc. (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 495 is also misplaced.  The Court of Appeal in that case relied on the lack of evidence regarding the likelihood of success on the merits.  (Id. at pp. 499-500.)  Again, Defendants fail to advance any specific evidence they did not owe Assignor any sums under the lease.  Defendants, therefore, fail to meet their burden to show the settlement agreement is unenforceable.

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

As Defendants have failed to meet their burden to raise triable issues of material fact as to whether they are liable for breach of the promissory note, the motion for summary judgment is granted. 

Plaintiff is ordered to give notice of the Court’s ruling and to file proof of service of same.


Case Number: 25STCV09083    Hearing Date: July 20, 2026    Dept: 415

   TENTATIVE RULING

 

DEPARTMENT

415

HEARING DATE

July 20, 2026

CASE NUMBER

25STCV09083

MOTION

Special Motion to Strike

MOVING PARTY

Cross-Defendant Jonathan Paley

OPPOSING PARTIES

Cross-Complainants Marina Grasic and Jan Korbelin

 

MOTION

Plaintiff California FAIR Plan Association (“Plaintiff”) initiated this action by filing a complaint in interpleader, which named Defendants Jan Korbelin and Marina Grasic (collectively, “Defendants”) and Jonathan Paley (“Paley”) as claimants concerning the proceeds from an insurance policy.  Defendants cross-complained against Paley.  Paley filed this special motion to strike the second, third, and fourth causes of action in the cross-complaint which have been pled as civil extortion, intentional interference with contractual relations, and intentional infliction of emotional distress, respectively. 

Defendants oppose the motion.

ANALYSIS

In ruling on a defendant's special motion to strike, the trial court uses a two-step process.  First, the defendant must show the act or acts of which the plaintiff complains were taken in furtherance of the defendant's right of petition or free speech under the United States or California Constitutions in connection with a public issue.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (b)(1).)  If the defendant carries that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to demonstrate a probability of prevailing on the claim.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (b)(3).) 

In the instant case, Defendants allege Paley “did not have a legitimate claim to the insurance proceeds payouts” from Plaintiff but, nonetheless, sent a letter to Plaintiff, which accused Plaintiff of “‘aiding and abetting’ the alleged criminal activities” of Defendants (see, e.g., Cross-Complaint, ¶ 19), and Plaintiff filed this interpleader action as a result (id., ¶ 21).  Paley argues these allegations are based on statements “made in connection with an issue under consideration or review by a . . . judicial body . . . ,” and they are, therefore, subject to a special motion to strike.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (e)(2).) 

For purposes of a special motion to strike, a statement is “in connection with” litigation if it “bears some relation” to substantive issues in the litigation and is directed at persons having some interest in the litigation.  (Contemporary Services Corp. v. Staff Pro Inc. (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 1043, 1055, internal quotations omitted.)  A statement made in connection with litigation contemplated in good faith is under consideration or review by a judicial body for purposes of a special motion to strike.  (Neville v. Chudacoff (2008) 160 Cal.App.4th 1255, 1262-1264.)  As such, Paley’s letter to Plaintiff was in connection with litigation, even though Plaintiff had not yet filed this action.  Thus, Paley has met his burden to show the second, third, and fourth causes of action in Defendants’ cross-complaint falls within the special motion to strike framework.  Consequently, Paley has shifted the burden to Defendants to show a probability of prevailing on their second, third, and fourth causes of action.

In opposition, while citing Flatley v. Mauro (2006) 39 Cal.4th 299 (Flatley), Defendants argue Paley’s conduct was unlawful and therefore does not fall within a special motion to strike.  Activity the defendant concedes was illegal or the evidence before the Court demonstrates is illegal as a matter of law does not fall within the scope of a special motion to strike.  (City of Montebello v. Vasquez (2016) 1 Cal.5th 409, 424.)  However, such conduct must be criminal not just a violation of a statute or common law standard.  (See Mendoza v. ADP Screening & Selection Services, Inc. (2010) 182 Cal.App.4th 1644, 1654 [Court of Appeal made clear Flatley is not to be interpreted so that a party is provided “a tool for avoiding the application of the ant-SLAPP statute merely by showing any statutory violation”].) 

Second Cause of Action

In this regard, Defendants contend Paley’s letter to Plaintiff constituted criminal extortion.  Pursuant to Penal Code section 518, subdivision (a), as relevant to the instant action, extortion is obtaining property or other consideration from another with that person's consent, where a wrongful use of force or fear induced the consent.  Consideration is anything of value.  (Pen. Code, § 518, subd. (b).)  

As an initial matter, if Paley’s letter constituted extortion at all, it was extortion of Plaintiff, not Defendants.  Furthermore, there is no evidence before the Court Plaintiff gave up anything of value because of Paley’s letter.  Instead, Plaintiff filed this interpleader action, which did not meaningfully change Plaintiff’s position with respect to the insurance proceeds.  From Plaintiff’s perspective, Plaintiff’s interpleader of the insurance proceeds does not differ significantly from direct payment of the proceeds to Defendants.  Accordingly, Defendants fail to show Paley’s conduct was unlawful as a matter of law such that it does not fall within the special motion to strike framework.

Defendants also argue the litigation privilege does not bar their claims.  The litigation privilege “applies to any communication (1) made in judicial or quasi-judicial proceedings; (2) by litigants or other participants authorized by law; (3) to achieve the objects of the litigation; and (4) that have some connection or logical relation to the action.” (Jacob B. v. County of Shasta (2007) 40 Cal.4th 948, 955, internal quotations omitted.)  The litigation privilege applies to prelawsuit demands. (Action Apartment Assn., Inc. v. City of Santa Monica (2007) 41 Cal.4th 1232, 1241 (Action Apartment); Strawn v. Morris, Polich & Purdy, LLP (2019) 30 Cal.App.5th 1087, 1095-1096.)  Defendants argue the litigation privilege does not apply because Paley’s communications did not relate to “litigation that is contemplated in good faith and under serious consideration.”  (Action Apartment, supra, 41 Cal.4th at p. 1251.)  The litigation privilege does not attach to communications that are “merely a negotiating tactic and not a serious proposal made in good faith contemplation of going to court.”  (Edwards v. Centex Real Estate Corp. (1997) 53 Cal.App.4th 15, 35.) 

In this context, Defendants argue Paley’s letter was not offered in good faith because “he held no enforceable judgment.”  (Opposition, p. 16.)  However, Defendants misconstrue the good faith requirement.  “The good faith inquiry is not a question of whether the statement was made with a good faith belief in its truth, but rather, whether the statement was made with a good faith intention to bring a lawsuit.”  (Dickinson v. Cosby (2017) 17 Cal.App.5th 655, 683.)  Defendants advance no evidence to suggest Paley did not contemplate litigation against Plaintiff at the time Paley sent the letter.  Accordingly, Defendants fail to show the litigation privilege does not bar their claims.

Defendants also argue the litigation privilege does not apply because Paley’s letter referenced Plaintiff “aiding and abetting” Defendants.  “[T]he litigation privilege d[oes] not apply where an attorney threatens or misrepresents the existence of criminal proceedings.”  (Knoell v. Petrovich (1999) 76 Cal.App.4th 164, 170.)  According to Defendants, aiding and abetting necessarily refers to criminal proceedings.  Defendants are incorrect.  Aiding and abetting is also a basis for civil liability.  (See, e.g., Das v. Bank of America, N.A. (2010) 186 Cal.App.4th 727, 744 [court noted bank may be held liable as an aider and abettor of tort regarding elder abuse].) 

Defendants further contend their cause of action for extortion has merit.  As set forth above, Defendants are incorrect.  Defendants have not shown Paley extorted Plaintiff, let alone Defendants.  Accordingly, Defendants have not satisfied their burden by showing they can prevail on their cause of action for extortion.

Third Cause of Action

Defendants argue their cause of action for intentional interference with contract has merit.  To state this claim, Defendants must allege the existence of a valid contract between Defendants and a third party, Paley’s knowledge of the contract, Paley intentionally undertook to disrupt the contractual relationship, and the contractual relationship was disrupted to the detriment of Defendants.  (Reeves v. Hanlon (2004) 33 Cal.4th 1140, 1148.)  The cause of action for intentional interference with contract is premised on the letter Paley sent Plaintiff.  (Cross-Complaint, ¶ 54.)  As set forth above, the litigation privilege applies to the letter such that it  cannot be the basis for Defendants’ claims.  Accordingly, Defendants fail to meet their burden to show Defendants can prevail on the cause of action for intentional interference with contract.

Fourth Cause of Action

Likewise, Defendants fail to show they can prevail on their cause of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress because the claim is predicated on the letter Paley sent Plaintiff, to which the litigation privilege applies.  (Cross-Complaint, ¶ 61.) 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

Accordingly, the special motion to strike Defendants’ second, third, and fourth causes of action is granted.

Paley is ordered to provide notice of the Court’s ruling and to file proof of service of same.
