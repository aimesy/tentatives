DEPARTMENT F43 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 22CHCV00040    Hearing Date: July 13, 2026    Dept: F43

Dept. F43

Hearing Date: 07-13-26

Case # 22CHCV00040, Coates v. Interinsurance Exchange of the Automobile Club

Trial Date: None set.

 

DEMURRER

 

MOVING PARTY:  Defendant Interinsurance Exchange of The Automobile Club

RESPONDING PARTY:  No opposition was filed.

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Order sustaining demurrer to the Third Amended Complaint.

 

RULING:  Demurrer to the Third Amended Complaint’s First, Second, and Fourth Causes of Action is sustained with leave to amend.  Demurrer to the Third Cause of Action is sustained without leave to amend.

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

 

On January 18, 2022, plaintiff James Coates (Plaintiff) filed this insurance action against defendant Interinsurance Exchange of the Automobile Club (Defendant), asserting breach of contract and a bad faith insurance claim.

 

Plaintiff is self-represented.

 

On April 25, 2025, the court sustained Defendant’s demurrer to the Complaint with leave to amend.  (4/25/2025 Minute Order, at pp. 3-4.)

 

On November 17, 2025, Plaintiff filed his First Amended Complaint (FAC).  Plaintiff asserted the same causes of action with new details about when a vehicle accident occurred, messages confirming Plaintiff’s valid insurance policy, the Defendant received proof of Plaintiff’s treatment for injuries related to the accident, and the Defendant delayed paying the claim with a valid reason.

 

On January 2, 2026, Defendant filed and served its demurrer to the First Amended Complaint.  The court sustained the demurrer with leave to amend within 30 days.  (2/06/26 Minute Order.)

 

Plaintiff filed a Second Amended Complaint on March 2, 2026.

 

On May 6, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Third Amended Complaint, asserting bad faith, breach of contract, dilatory tactics, and intentional infliction of emotional distress.  Plaintiff alleges the following:  On July 20, 2019, Plaintiff was involved in a vehicle accident with an uninsured motorist.  (3AC ¶ 10.)  At the time, Plaintiff had an active automobile insurance policy with Defendant which provided uninsured motorist coverage for up to $15,000.  (Id.)  Plaintiff immediately reported the accident to Defendant the same day of the accident.  (Id. ¶ 11.)  Upon receiving notice of the accident, Defendant represented to Plaintiff that an investigation would be commenced.  (Id. ¶ 12.)  Rather than act promptly to investigate the claim and expedite settlement, Defendant did not have reasonable standards for a prompt investigation of the claim.  (Id. ¶ 13.)  Defendant settled the claim in the amount of $15,000 which was sent to Plaintiff on January 11, 2021.  (Id. ¶ 14.)  Despite this, Defendant’s unethical conduct and treatment of Plaintiff’s claim was malicious, willful, knowingly cruel, unjust, and oppressive, and showed extreme indifference to Plaintiff’s rights and safety, which entitles Plaintiff to $250,000.00 in compensatory damages.  (Id. ¶ 15.)

 

Defendant filed a demurrer on June 8, 2026.  No opposition was filed.

 

 

MEET AND CONFER

 

Before filing a demurrer, the moving party must file and serve a meet and confer declaration stating either:  (1) how the parties met and conferred and that the parties did not reach an agreement resolving the issues raised in the demurrer;  or (2) that the party who filed the pleading subject to the demurrer failed to respond to the meet and confer request or failed to meet and confer in good faith.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 430.41, subd. (a)(3).)  In Department F43, meet and confer means in person or via phone.  A letter or email does not suffice.  (Department F43 Courtroom Information, p. 2.)

 

The “meet and confer” requirement is not fully met because defense counsel only sent a letter to Plaintiff on May 22, 2026.  (Declaration of Tina M. Bhatia, ¶ 2, Exh. A.)  But Plaintiff never responded.  Regardless, the court still considers the merits of the demurrer.

 

 

ANALYSIS

 

A.  Demurrer

 

A party may respond to a pleading against it by demurrer based on one or more of eight enumerated grounds, including that “the pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action” and is uncertain, meaning “ambiguous and unintelligible.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subds. (e), (f).)  The grounds for demurring must be apparent from either the face of the complaint or a matter of which the court may take judicial notice.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.30, subd. (a); see also Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

 

The purpose of a demurrer is to challenge the sufficiency of a pleading “by raising questions of law.”  (Postley v. Harvey (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 280, 286.)  “In the construction of a pleading, for the purpose of determining its effect, its allegations must be liberally construed, with a view to substantial justice between the parties.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 452.)  The court “treat[s] the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law[.]”  (Berkley v. Dowds (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 518, 525.)  In applying these standards, the court liberally construes the complaint to determine whether a cause of action has been stated.  (Picton v. Anderson Union High School Dist. (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 726, 733.)

 

 

            1.  First Cause of Action (Bad Faith)  and Second Cause of Action (Breach of Contract)

 

Defendant contends no “bad faith” cause of action exists in California.  If Plaintiff is pleading a “breach of implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing” claim, the claim fails because Plaintiff does not state the terms of an underlying insurance policy or contract or include a copy of the contract and policy.

 

Plaintiff does not oppose.

 

Although Plaintiff titles his First Cause of Action as “Bad Faith,” Plaintiff’s allegations assert Defendant violated the “implied covenant of good faith and fair dealings.”  (3AC ¶ 21.)

 

Generally, an “implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing” claim must be supported by the existence of a contract.  (Ginsberg v. Gamson (2012) 205 Cal.App.4th 873, 896-897.)  “Because the covenant of good faith and fair dealing essentially is a contract term that aims to effectuate the contractual intentions of the parties, compensation for its breach has almost always been limited to contract rather than tort remedies.”  (Cates Construction, Inc. v. Talbot Partners (1999) 21 Cal.4th 28, 43, citations omitted.)  The elements of breach of the covenant of good faith and fair dealing are:  (1) plaintiff and defendant entered a contract;  (2) plaintiff performed all obligations under the contract or was excused from performing all obligations;  (3) defendant’s conduct prevented plaintiff from receiving the benefits under the contract;  (4) defendant did not act fairly and in good faith;  and (5) plaintiff was harmed by defendant’s conduct.  (CACI No. 325.)

 

California recognizes a common law tort cause of action for an insurer’s breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing which is commonly referred to as a “bad faith claim.  The elements of a bad faith claims handling cause of action are: (1) that the insurer has withheld benefits due under a policy, and (2) that the withholding was unreasonable or without proper cause.  (Major v. Western Home Ins. Co. (2009) 169 Cal.App.4th 1197, 1209.)  Conduct constituting bad faith handling includes denying benefits due, paying less than due, and unreasonably delayed payments due.  (Ibid.)

 

Plaintiff alleges Defendant delayed paying the $15,000 settlement until over a year after Plaintiff reported the accident and several months after receiving proof of Plaintiff’s accident-related injuries.  (3AC ¶¶ 21-22, 27.)  Plaintiff alleges he fully performed all conditions, covenants, and promises under the insurance policy but Defendant delayed settling the matter and paying Plaintiff.  (Id. ¶¶ 27-29.)

 

However, Plaintiff fails to include a copy of any insurance policy documents or to allege the terms or legal effect of the alleged insurance policy.  As the party who filed and pursued this action, Plaintiff has the burden of alleging ultimate facts to support his causes of action or attaching the relevant supporting exhibits to the complaint to support his causes of action.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 425.10, subd. (1); Burke v. Superior Court (1969) 71 Cal.2d 276, 279, fn. 4 [“[A] complaint must contain only allegations of ultimate facts as opposed to allegations of evidentiary facts or of legal conclusions or arguments.”].)

 

“In an action based on a written contract, a plaintiff may plead the legal effect of the contract rather than its precise language.”  (Construction Protective Services, Inc. v. TIG Specialty Ins. Co. (2002) 29 Cal.4th 189, 198-199.  But see Harris v. Rudin, Richman & Appel (1999) 74 Cal.App.4th 299, 307 [requiring plaintiff to attach a copy of the contract or to set out the terms verbatim in the body of the complaint]; Otworth v. Southern Pac. Transportation Co. (1985) 166 Cal.App.3d 452, 458-459.)  Pleading contracts by legal effect involves alleging the relevant terms in substance.  (McKell v. Washington Mutual, Inc. (2006) 142 Cal.App.4th 1457, 1489;  4 Witkin, Cal Procedure (6th ed. 2025) Pleading, § 527 [“legal effect” requires plaintiff to allege “the substance of [the contract’s] relevant terms.  This is more difficult, for it requires a careful analysis of the instrument, comprehensiveness in statement, and avoidance of legal conclusions”].)

 

At most, Plaintiff alleges he had an active automobile insurance policy with Defendant which provided uninsured motorist coverage for up to $15,000.  (3AC ¶ 10.)  Plaintiff provides no other information concerning the terms of the policy or insurance contract.  Without additional facts establishing the terms or legal effect of the alleged insurance policy or agreement, Plaintiff fails to allege breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing or any “bad faith” claiming handling claim.

 

Plaintiff’s breach of contract claim suffers from the same defect.  To plead breach of contract, a complaint must allege facts showing (1) the existence of a contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance, (3) defendant’s breach, and (4) resulting damages to plaintiff.  (Oasis West Realty, LLC v. Goldman (2011) 51 Cal.4th 811, 821;  Aton Center, Inc. v. United Healthcare Ins. Co. (2023) 93 Cal.App.5th 1214, 1230.)

 

Therefore, the court sustains Defendant’s demurrer to the First and Second Causes of Action.

 

The court gives Plaintiff one more opportunity to amend to address the insurance contract and policy issue.

 

            2.  Third Cause of Action: Dilatory Tactics

 

Defendant contends there is no cause of action for “dilatory tactics” recognized under California law.

 

Plaintiff does not oppose.

 

The court is perplexed by Plaintiff’s “dilatory tactics” claim.  Although California recognizes various tort claims which include delaying or frivolous conduct, no claim exists for such conduct standing on its own.  (See Sheldon Appel Co. v. Albert & Oliker (1989) 47 Cal.3d 863, 873-874 [declining to recognize dilatory litigation conduct as a standalone tort].)

 

Therefore, the court sustains Defendant’s demurrer to the Third Cause of Action without leave to amend.

 

 

            3.  Fourth Cause of Action: Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress

 

Defendant contends Plaintiff fails to allege demonstrating “outrageous” conduct.

 

Plaintiff does not oppose.

 

To plead intentional infliction of emotional distress, the complaint must allege that:  (1) defendant engaged in extreme and outrageous conduct;  (2) with the intent to cause, or with reckless disregard to the probability of causing, emotional distress;  and (3) as a result, plaintiff suffered extreme or severe emotional distress.  (Potter v. Firestone Tire & Rubber Co. (1993) 6 Cal.4th 965, 1001;  Cervantez v. J.C. Penney Co. (1979) 24 Cal.3d 579, 593.)  “[The] conduct [must be] directed at the plaintiff or occur in the presence of the plaintiff of whom the defendant is aware.  [This] factor . . . distinguishes intentional infliction of emotional distress from the negligent infliction of such injury.”  (Potter, supra, 6 Cal.4th at p. 1002, citations omitted;  see also So v. Shin (2013) 212 Cal.App.4th 652, 671 [stating conduct must be directed to the plaintiff, but malicious or evil purpose is not required].)

 

Conduct is outrageous if it is so extreme that it exceeds the bounds usually tolerated by a civilized community and is directed at the Plaintiff.  (Cervantez, supra, 24 Cal.3d at p. 593; Christensen v. Superior Court (1991) 54 Cal.3d 868, 903.)  “Behavior may be considered outrageous if a defendant (1) abuses a relation or position which gives him power to damage the plaintiff’s interest;  (2) knows the plaintiff is susceptible to injury through mental distress;  or (3) acts intentionally or unreasonably with the recognition that the acts are likely to result in illness through mental distress.”  (Pulver v. Avco Financial Services (1986) 182 Cal.App.3d 622, 637.)  The complaint must allege “with great specificity” those acts the plaintiff believes are so extreme as to exceed all bounds usually tolerated in a civilized community.  (Vasquez v. Franklin Management Real Estate Fund, Inc. (2013) 222 Cal.App.4th 819, 832.)

 

Plaintiff alleges while Defendant was processing his claim, he was going through medical treatments and was diagnosed with cancer.  (3AC ¶ 10.)  Because Defendant did not promptly process his claim, Plaintiff suffered emotional distress that contributed to the further decline in Plaintiff’s physical health.  (Id. ¶ 10.)

 

Plaintiff then alleges Defendant “acted with intent to cause emotional distress or acted with deliberate disregard of a high probability that such distress would follow.”  (3AC ¶ 11.)  During the investigative process, Defendant “imposed impediments that did not allow the plaintiff to remain unaffected by the intentional delay in the defendant’s investigative processing of the subject claim.”  (Id. ¶ 12.)  According to Plaintiff, Defendant’s impeding actions created unbearable obstacles and nuisances which led to the plaintiff’s unwarranted emotional distress and ultimately his current debilitating and declining health—extreme emotional anguish, depression, fearfulness, anxiety, stressful headaches, difficulty sleeping, and shame in an amount to be proven at trial  (Id. ¶¶ 12-13.)

 

Plaintiff fails to state the specific impediments other than “delay” or if and when Defendant learned Plaintiff was diagnosed with cancer and being treated for cancer.  Plaintiff mentions Defendant issued a $15,000 settlement check on January 11, 2021 and imposed confidentiality notices on Plaintiff after filing this action.  (3AC ¶¶ 21-22, Exhs. C, D.)  But this allegation indicates Defendant did receive the $15,000 payment.  Furthermore, a review of Plaintiff’s Exhibit D demonstrates the “confidentiality notice” is part of defense counsel’s email signature in order to comply with the California’s Rules of Professional Conduct as to attorney-client communications.  (Id., Exh. D at p. 1.)

 

This conduct does not rise to the level of despicable or outrageous conduct required to support an intentional infliction of emotional distress claim.

 

Therefore, the court sustains Defendant’s demurrer to the Fourth Cause of Action with leave to amend.

 

 

CONCLUSION and ORDER

 

Demurrer to the Third Amended Complaint’s First, Second, and Fourth Causes of Action is sustained with leave to amend.  Demurrer to the Third Cause of Action is sustained without leave to amend.

 

Plaintiff may file and serve an amended complaint within thirty (30) days of this order.

 

Defendant to give notice.


Case Number: 25CHCV02149    Hearing Date: July 13, 2026    Dept: F43

Dept. F43

Hearing Date: 07-13-26

Case # 25CHCV02149, Robleto v. Andy Gump, Inc., et al.

Trial Date: None set.

 

MOTION FOR LEAVE TO FILE A FIRST AMENDED COMPLAINT

 

MOVING PARTY:  Plaintiff Carlos Robleto

RESPONDING PARTY:  No opposition was filed.

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Order granting plaintiff leave to file a First Amended Complaint to add a premises liability cause of action.

 

RULING:  Motion is denied without prejudice.

 

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

 

This personal injury action arises from a two-vehicle collision that occurred on March 8, 2024 on a private driveway at 21380 Needham Ranch Parkway, Santa Clarita, CA.  Plaintiff Carlos Robleto (Plaintiff) alleges defendant Ricardo Cervantes, Jr. was driving a vehicle owned by defendant Andy Gump, Inc. while within the scope of his employment with defendant Andy Gump, Inc. when he collided with Plaintiff’s vehicle.  As a result, Plaintiff suffered bodily injuries and damages.  On June 20, 2025, Plaintiff filed a complaint, asserting motor vehicle and negligence.  Andy Gump, Inc. and Cervantes filed and served a joint answer on August 11, 2025.

 

On April 20, 2026, Plaintiff filed a motion for leave to file a first amended complaint to allege a premises liability claim against the owner of the private driveway, DrinkPAK, LLC.  The court denied the motion without prejudice.  (6/1/26 Minute Order.)

 

Plaintiff filed a renewed motion on June 4, 2026.  No opposition was filed.

 

 

ANALYSIS

 

A.  Granting Leave to File an Amended Pleading (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (a)(1))

 

“The court may, in furtherance of justice, and on any terms as may be proper, allow a party to amend any pleading or proceeding by adding or striking out the name of any party, or by correcting a mistake in the name of a party, or a mistake in any other respect; and may, upon like terms, enlarge the time for answer or demurrer.  The court may likewise, in its discretion, after notice to the adverse party, allow, upon any terms as may be just, an amendment to any pleading or proceeding in other particulars; and may upon like terms allow an answer to be made after the time limited by this code.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (a)(1).)

 

Judicial policy favors resolution of all disputed matters between the parties, and therefore, courts have held that “there is a strong policy in favor of liberal allowance of amendments.”  (Mesler v. Bragg Management Co. (1985) 39 Cal.3d 290, 296-297.)  “If the motion to amend is timely made and the granting of the motion will not prejudice the opposing party, it is error to refuse permission to amend and where the refusal also results in a party being deprived of the right to assert a meritorious cause of action or a meritorious defense, it is not only error but an abuse of discretion.”  (Morgan v. Superior Court (1959) 172 Cal.App.2d 527, 530, citations omitted.)

 

Motions for leave to amend must also meet certain procedural requirements.  California Rules of Court, rule 3.1324(a) requires the motion to “(1) Include a copy of the proposed amendment or amended pleading, which must be serially numbered to differentiate it from previous pleadings or amendments; (2) State what allegations in the previous pleading are proposed to be deleted, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the deleted allegations are located; and (3) State what allegations are proposed to be added to the previous pleading, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the additional allegations are located.”

 

Additionally, Rule 3.1324(b) requires that the declaration in support of a motion for leave to file an amended complaint must state: “(1) the effect of the amendment; (2) why the amendment is necessary and proper; (3) when the facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered; and (4) the reasons why the request for amendment was not made earlier.”

 

Plaintiff seeks to add a premises liability cause of action against new defendant DrinkPAK, LLC.  Plaintiff presents the declaration of attorney Scott E. Braybrooke, a copy of the original complaint, and a copy of the proposed First Amended Complaint and Summons.  (Declaration of Scott E. Braybrooke, Exhs. A, B.)

 

 

1.  The motion fails to comply with California Rules of Court, rule 3.1324(a).

 

The court has reviewed the notice of motion, supporting exhibits, and the memorandum of points and authorities and finds that Plaintiff has again failed to comply with Rule 3.1324(a).

 

Mr. Braybrooke states the only amendment is a third cause of action for premises liability against new defendant DrinkPak, LLC, states no allegations will be deleted, and includes a copy of the proposed First Amended Complaint.  (Braybrooke Decl. ¶¶ 4-5.)  However, rather than setting forth the proposed allegations in the notice of motion or the memorandum and points of authority (Rule 3.1324(a)(2)), Plaintiff states he learned new facts supporting a premises liability claim against DrinkPak, LLC concerning the alleged incident and directs the court to page six of the proposed amended complaint.  (Notice of Motion at p. 2:4-9.)

 

Mr. Braybrooke’s declaration satisfies the requirements of Rule 3.1324(b).  Mr. Braybrooke states the legal effect of the amendment – asserting a premises liability claim against DrinkPak, LLC.  (Braybrooke Decl. ¶ 5.)  On February 23, 2026, Mr. Braybrooke deposed defendant Cervantes who testified he was unaware that the private driveway he entered shortly before the accident was posted as “Do Not Enter.”  (Id. ¶ 3.)  Cervantes also produced dashcam video from his vehicle which supposedly showed the “Do Not Enter” sign was bent and twisted and not visible for drivers attempting to enter the driveway.  (Id.)  The bent sign supports asserting a premises liability cause of action but does not clarify when Mr. Braybrooke first believed or became informed that DrinkPAK, LLC may own and control the property where the driveway was located on the day of the accident.  (Id. ¶ 5.)  Mr. Braybrooke claims defense counsel, Jan Buddingh, initially suggested bringing DrinkPak, LLC into litigation but does not state when the suggestion occurred.  (Id. ¶ 5.)  Regardless, the court infers Mr. Braybrooke initially learned facts supporting a premises liability claim as early as February 23, 2026.

 

Therefore, Plaintiff fails to comply with California Rules of Court, rule 3.1324(a).

 

                        2.  Defendants will not suffer prejudice from the amendment.

 

Plaintiff claims the amendment will cause no prejudice to defendant Andy Gump, Inc. and Ricardo Cervantes, considering their attorney suggested the prospect of bringing DrinkPak, LLC into this litigation as a cross-defendant.  (Braybrooke Decl. ¶¶ 5-6.)

 

If the party seeking the amendment has been “dilatory,” and the delay has prejudiced the opposing party, the judge has discretion to deny leave to amend.  (Weil & Brown, Cal. Practice Guide: Civil Procedure Before Trial (The Rutter Group 2020) [Weil & Brown], ¶ 6:655, citations omitted.)  “Prejudice exists where the amendment would result in a delay of trial, along with loss of critical evidence, added costs of preparation, increased burden of discovery.”  (Id., at ¶ 6:656, citations omitted.)  However, “the fact that the amendment involves a change in legal theory which would make admissible evidence damaging to the opposing party is not the kind of prejudice the court will consider.”  (Id.)  Absent prejudice, any claimed delay alone is not grounds for denial.  (Id., at ¶ 6:653 [citing Higgins v. Del Faro (1981) 123 Cal.App.3d 558, 564-565].)  “Even if some prejudice is shown, the judge may still permit the amendment but impose conditions, as the Court is authorized to grant leave ‘on such terms as may be proper.’”  (Weil & Brown, supra, at ¶ 6:663, citation omitted.)

 

The court agrees the parties will suffer no prejudice from the amendment because this action is relatively new and defense counsel suggested bringing DrinkPak, LLC into the matter.

 

Because Plaintiff’s motion does not comply with Rule 3.1324(a), the court denies Plaintiff’s motion without prejudice.

 

 

CONCLUSION and ORDER

 

Motion is denied without prejudice.

 

Plaintiff to give notice.
