ANALYSIS:

 

This is the second go at demurrer proceedings in this action.  The litigation’s basic contours are easily stated:  Atlas Audio Video & Communications, Inc., installed, well, audio, video, and communications equipment in a house on Azure Place owned by the Robert W. Harrison and Lissa Jody Harrison Family Trust and occupied by Michelle and Conrad Adamczak.  Atlas alleges it didn’t get paid everything it was owed for that equipment and its labor, and is suing on basically the theories that one would expect under the circumstances.  The prolonged pleading-stage litigation has been over who the appropriate targets of those claims ought to be. 

 

Michelle’s identity as an appropriate defendant hasn’t really been disputed.  She signed the contract with Atlas and her name is on all its invoices.  Once the Family Trust became known to Atlas as the owner of the Azure Place house, identifying it as a defendant against which to enforce a mechanics lien is not (or is no longer) the subject of dispute.  One could also say that the Family Trust and Conrad, along with Michelle, received and retained the benefit of Atlas’s work at Atlas’s expense, subjecting them to liability on a quasi-contract theory.  The parties are now basically fighting only over whether Conrad and the Family Trust can also be liable for a straightforward breach of contract (for which Michelle can already be held liable) and on common counts that the Court will once again call breach-of-contract lite (again, for which Michelle can already be held liable).  Candidly, the Court is puzzled by the continuing litigation of this question—does Atlas believe, for example, that Michelle would thumb her nose at a judgment and the consequences that flow from one?—but it will now resolve the issue. 

 

The Court OVERRULES the demurrer to the second amended complaint.   

 

First, the breach of contract.  Atlas contends that Conrad and the Family Trust are liable for breaching the contract that Michelle entered, because Michelle acted as their agent when she did so and they subsequently ratified the contract.  (See 3 Witkin, Summary of Cal. Law (May 2026 Update) Agency and Employment, § 168 [when an agent doesn’t disclose her principal, “i.e., if an agent acting for (her) principal makes a contract in . . . her own name either orally or in writing, the result will ordinarily be that either the principal or agent may be held liable on it by the third party”]; id., § 170 [a third party may sue both a principal and her agent, but must eventually elect which to have judgment against].)  The question is whether Atlas has pleaded the ultimate facts establishing a principal–agent relationship between Michelle and Conrad and Michelle and the Family Trust.  This is a fairly straightforward analysis:  “[A]n allegation of agency as such is a statement of ultimate fact.  Consequently further allegations explaining how this fact of agency originated become unnecessary.”  (Skopp v. Weaver (1976) 16 Cal.3d 432, 439; e.g., City of Industry v. City of Fillmore (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 191, 212.)

 

The demurring defendants argue that Skopp doesn’t mean precisely what it says, or at least that it also means that if a pleading contains specific allegations that undermine a general allegation of agency, then the Court should disregard the general allegation of agency.  Fair enough, but the demurring defendants haven’t identified anything in the second amended complaint negates the general allegation that Michelle was acting as an agent for Conrad and the Family Trust.

 

Nor does it matter, ultimately, that the alleged agency agreement authorizing Michelle to contract on Conrad’s or the Family Trust’s behalf hasn’t been reduced to writing.  To be sure, the equal dignities rule means that if a contract must be in writing, then an agreement authorizing an agent to enter that contract on a principal’s behalf must also be in writing.  (Civ. Code, §§ 2309, 2310; Estate of Stephens (2002) 28 Cal.4th 665, 673.)  And, the demurring defendants observe, Business and Professions Code section 7159 says that a contract “for the sale, installation, or furnishing of home improvement good or services” must be in writing if it’s for over $500.  But the word “must” is doing different work in the two preceding sentences.  (Cf. County of Los Angeles v. Superior Court (2012) 209 Cal.App.4th 543, 549–550 [the word “shall” doesn’t always mandate the conduct to which it is applied].)  If a contractor fails to reduce a home-improvement contract to a signed writing, the contract isn’t void.  (E.g., Hinerfeld-Ward, Inc. v. Lipian (2010) 188 Cal.App.4th 86, 91–94; compare Civ. Code, § 1624 [“The following contracts are invalid, unless they . . . are in writing.”] with Bus. & Prof. Code, § 7159 [silent on the validity of contracts made in violation of the statutory requirements].)    

 

So it isn’t true, in the relevant sense, that a home-improvement contract “must” be in writing.  It therefore isn’t true that an agreement authorizing an agent to enter a home-improvement contract on a principal’s behalf must likewise be in writing.

 

The net of this is that Atlas has alleged, at least based on an agency theory, that Michelle bound her principals to the contract at issue in this case.

 

That brings the Court to the second cause of action, on common counts, around which there is also some dispute.  It isn’t much of one, though.  The same agency allegations that are sufficient to allege a breach of contract against Conrad and the Family Trust are sufficient to allege this breach-of-contract lite claim against them.  The bigger dispute here is whether Atlas exceeded the scope of its leave to amend by expanding the nature of the common counts.  The first amended complaint sought recovery for goods sold and delivered.  The second amended complaint seeks recovery for that, but also for an account-stated and on an open book account.  The Court’s previous order granted Atlas leave to amend only to address the issue of the parties properly sued on this cause of action.

 

Two things are true here.  One, Atlas played fast and loose with the scope of the leave it was granted.  Two, it makes basically no difference that the Court can discern.   

 

As the Court observed at the beginning, the basic contours of this action are straightforward.  With the overruling of the demurrer, the parties should get on with litigating it.

 

Atlas is to give notice.

ANALYSIS:

 

            California Injury PLC filed this complaint in interpleader against Maria Teresa Rojo-Roman, Jax Orthopedics, Gutierrez Chiropractic, Diagnostic Imaging Network, Abraham Ishaaya MD, Cloud Anesthesia, Manhattan Beach Surgery Center, Bay Rehabilitation, and Weststar Physical Therapy regarding $4,838.00 in net settlement proceeds received by California Injury on behalf of Rojo-Roman.  All defendants defaulted.  California Injury now moves for an order to deposit the funds with the clerk, be discharged from liability, and for attorney fees and costs.

 

            Interpleader is a procedure whereby a person holding money or personal property to which conflicting claims are being made by others, or may be made, can join the adverse claimants and force them to litigate their claims among themselves.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 386, subd. (b).)  Once the stakeholder’s right to interplead is established and he or she deposits the money or personal property in court, he or she may be discharged from liability to any of the claimants, enabling the stakeholder to avoid multiplicity of actions and the risk of inconsistent results if each of the claimants were to sue him or her separately.  (Cantu v. Resolution Trust Corp. (1992) 4 Cal.App.4th 857, 874; City of Morgan Hill v. Brown (1999) 71 Cal.App.4th 1114, 1122.)

 

            Substantively, California Injury contends it cannot determine the validity of conflicting demands that have been made on the settlement proceeds by the defendants and that it does not have an interest in the proceeds.  All the defendants have defaulted.  Thus, California Injury has shown it is entitled to deposit the funds and be discharged from liability on the settlement proceeds.

 

            California Injury asks for the attorney fees and costs it expended in interpleading this action and filing this motion.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 386.6; UAP-Columbus JV 326132 v. Nesbitt (1991) 234 Cal.App.3d 1028, 1036.)  California Injury seeks the entire $4,838.00 in funds, reflecting $2,095.40 in costs and $2,742.60 in attorney fees.  The Court finds the $2,095.40 in costs proper and reasonable.  The Court reduces the attorney fees by $1,224, to $1518.60, because it will not compensate California Injury at an attorney’s billing rate for tasks like “Sent Interpleader Letters via US Mail” or other tasks that do not require an attorney’s skill to perform (including researching addresses for service, though the Court did not deduct even all that time).

 

            So the Court GRANTS California Injury’s motion.  California Injury will be discharged from liability effective upon its compliance with this order as follows:

 

            California Injury is ordered to deposit $1,224.00 with the Court within 30 days.  That reflects the $4,838.00 sum, less $1,518.60 in attorney fees and $2,095.40 in costs.

 

            California Injury is to give notice.

MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER DEPOSITION AND PRODUCTION OF DOCUMENTS

The Court DENIES Maria Lopez’s motion and request for sanctions.

ANALYSIS:

 

            Oscar Julio Martinez Nunez filed a complaint that says (only) “Maria Lopez Ran Me Over On Dec-29-2023 Causing Me An Injury For What I Needed An Ambulance To Get Carried To A Hospital For Medical Treatment.”  Lopez answered, and then attempted to depose Nunez.  Nunez declined to answer certain questions and declined to produce evidence demanded in Lopez’s deposition notice.  Lopez’s counsel adjourned the deposition, “subject to court intervention.”  The reporter certified the transcript of those proceedings on May 11, 2026.  Lopez now moves for an order compelling Nunez to continue his deposition and to produce the documents specified in the deposition notice.  Lopez also requests sanctions.

 

Having reviewed the deposition transcript, Lopez’s motion is patently meritorious.  But it’s also patently untimely.  A party must make a motion under Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.480, the statute on which Lopez relies, “no later than 60 days after the completion of the record of the deposition.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.480, subd. (b).)  The record of the deposition was complete when the reporter certified the transcript on May 11, 2026.  Sixty days elapsed on July 10.  Lopez filed her motion on July 21—11 days too late.  Section 2025.480’s 60-day deadline is mandatory.  (Weinstein v. Blumberg (2018) 25 Cal.App.5th 316, 321.)

 

            The Court therefore DENIES Lopez’s motion.

 

            Lopez is to give notice.

ANALYSIS:

 

            Ferguson Enterprises, LLC sued Panda Rooter Inc. and Roberto Carlos Carpio for breach of contract and of personal guaranty.  Panda Rooter and Carpio filed a joint answer as self-represented litigants.  In May 2026, Ferguson filed and served a motion to strike Panda Rooter’s answer because it is an unrepresented corporation.  The case was then reassigned to this Court and Ferguson refiled the same motion to strike. 

 

Motions to strike in limited jurisdiction courts may only challenge pleadings “on the ground that the damages or relief sought are not supported by the allegations of the complaint.”  (Code Civ. Proc. § 92, subd. (d).)  Ferguson’s request falls outside the bounds of permitted motions to strike in limited civil cases, so the Court DENIES it.

 

            Ferguson is correct, though, that an entity or corporation must be represented by a lawyer in legal proceedings and may not represent itself (either directly or through a non-lawyer agent) in litigation.  (Merco Constr. Engineers, Inc. v. Municipal Court (1978) 21 Cal.3d 724, 727, 729; Caressa Camille, Inc. v. Alcoholic Beverage Control Appeals Bd. (2002) 99 Cal.App.4th 1094, 1103.) 

 

             Panda Rooter is currently a self-represented corporation, has been on notice of this motion since May 2026, and has not filed an opposition or other document indicating it has since acquired counsel.  Thus, the Court, on its own motion, STRIKES Panda Rooter’s portion of the answer filed on May 6, 2026. 

 

            Ferguson is to give notice.


Case Number: 26STLC03600    Hearing Date: September 10, 2026    Dept: 25

CASE NAME:           Omni Therapy, Inc. v. Destiny Home Health Services, Inc., et al.

COMP. FILED:         05-28-26

CASE NUMBER:      26STLC03600

NOTICE:                  OK                                                                 

                                                                                   

 

PROCEEDINGS:      DEMURRER OF DEFENDS TO PLAINTIFF’S COMPLAINT OMNI THERAPY INC.

 

MOVING PARTY:   Non-Party and Purported Novation Assignee of Defendant Destiny Home Health Services, Inc. Robert Umoh and Defendant Aniema Umoh

RESP. PARTY:         Plaintiff Omni Therapy, Inc.

 

DEMURRER TO COMPLAINT

(CCP §§ 430.10, et seq.)

 

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

            The Court OVERRULES the demurrer in its entirety.

 

            Omni Therapy is to give notice.

 

SERVICE: 

 

[X] Proof of Service Timely Filed (CRC, rule 3.1300)                     OK

[X] Correct Address (CCP §§ 1013, 1013a)                                      OK

[X] 16/21 Court Days Lapsed (CCP §§ 12c, 1005(b))                       OK

 

OPPOSITION:          Filed on July 17, 2026                                    [   ] Late                      [   ] None

REPLY:                     Filed on July 22, 2026                                    [   ] Late                      [   ] None

 

ANALYSIS:

 

            Omni Therapy, Inc. sued Destiny Home Health Services, Inc. and Aniema Umoh for breaching a contract.  Robert Umoh—who purports to be the novation assignee of Destiny Home’s contract with Omni—and Aniema Umoh now demur to all of Omni’s causes of action pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (e) for failure to state sufficient facts to constitute a valid cause of action.

 

As an initial matter, Robert is not currently a proper defendant in this case.  He contends that Destiny Home assigned to him its indebtedness, rights, obligations, interests, titles, and benefits, but the “Assignment and Novation Agreement” provided to the Court does not reference the contract between Omni and Destiny Home, and is signed only by Robert as assignee and Aniema as president of Destiny Home.  In this context, a proper novation requires the former debtor to be released of their obligation “by consent of the former debtor as well as the creditor.”  (Wells Fargo Bank v. Bank of America (1995) 32 Cal.App.4th 424, 432, italics added.)  There is no evidence that Omni consented to any novation.

 

Destiny Home, meanwhile, is a corporation that must be represented by a lawyer in legal proceedings and may not represent itself (either directly or through a non-lawyer agent) in litigation.  (Merco Constr. Engineers, Inc. v. Municipal Court (1978) 21 Cal.3d 724, 727, 729; Caressa Camille, Inc. v. Alcoholic Beverage Control Appeals Bd. (2002) 99 Cal.App.4th 1094, 1103.)  The Court continued a hearing on this demurrer from July 30 to today in part to allow Destiny Home to retain counsel.  There is, as of now, no substitution of counsel filed with the Court. 

 

Putting all that aside for the time being, the Court OVERRULES the demurrer in its entirety.

 

A demurrer assumes the truth of the facts in a complaint and asks if they are sufficient to state a cause of action.  (Fix the City, Inc. v. City of Los Angeles (2026) 118 Cal.App.5th 957, 971.)  Arguments that rely on extrinsic evidence to dispute the facts alleged in the complaint aren’t appropriate.  (Saint Francis Memorial Hospital v. State Dept. of Public Health (2021) 59 Cal.App.5th 965, 973–974.) 

 

With that in mind, start with Omni’s breach of written agreement cause of action against Destiny Home and its breach of personal guarantee cause of action against Aniema, both of which are breach of contract causes of action.  The elements of a cause of action for breach of contract are (1) a contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance of the contract, (3) defendant’s breach of the contract, and (4) damages to plaintiff resulting from that breach.  (Coyote Aviation Corp. v. City of Redlands (2025) 111 Cal.App.5th 955, 973.)  Omni’s complaint attaches a contract signed by Aniema—both as president of Destiny Home and individually as guarantor—and Omni’s president.  It alleges that Omni provided services to Destiny Home in accordance with that agreement, that Destiny Home failed to pay for those services (and that Aniema also failed to pay as guarantor), and that Omni suffered damages in the principal sum of $14,448.25 because of the breaches.  Those allegations are sufficient.

 

The three remaining causes of action for services rendered, reasonable value, and account stated are each a form of a common counts cause of action.  Generally, the elements of a cause of action for common counts are (1) a statement of indebtedness in a certain sum, (2) consideration, and (3) nonpayment.  (Allen v. Powell (1967) 248 Cal.App.2d 502, 510.)  The elements of services rendered are (1) work performed at the request of defendant, (2) defendant promised to pay for them, and (3) defendant is indebted to plaintiff in a certain sum.  (Evans v. Zeigler (1949) 91 Cal.App.2d 226, 230; Haggerty v. Warner (1953) 115 Cal.App.2d 468, 475.)  The elements of reasonable value, also known as quantum meruit, are (1) plaintiff’s performance of services, work, or labor, (2) at defendant’s request, and (3) circumstances inferring defendant’s promise to pay a reasonable value.  (Maglica v. Maglica (1998) 66 Cal.App.4th 442, 449-450; Palmer v. Gregg (1967) 65 Cal.2d 657, 660.)  And the elements of account stated are (1) an account statement of indebtedness between the parties, (2) the balance or sum due, (3) the time of the statement, and (4) the debt was found, or the debtor expressly or impliedly promised to pay the amount.  (Truestone, Inc. v. Simi West Industrial Park II (1984) 163 Cal.App.3d 715, 725.) 

 

Omni alleged that there was a contract between it and Destiny Home (with a personal guaranty from Aniema), that Omni performed services under the contract, that the contract obligated Destiny Home and Aniema to pay for those services, that Destiny Home and Aniema failed to pay for those services, and that the principal sum of the debt is $14,448.25.  Again, those allegations are sufficient to state causes of action for services rendered, reasonable value, and account stated.

 

Because the demurrer is overruled, an answer is due within 10 days of notice of this order.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1320(g).)   Omni Therapy is to give notice.
