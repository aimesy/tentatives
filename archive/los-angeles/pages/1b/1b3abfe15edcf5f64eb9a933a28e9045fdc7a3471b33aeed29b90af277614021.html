DEPARTMENT A LAW AND MOTION RULINGS

INSTRUCTIONS: If the parties wish to submit on the tentative ruling and avoid a court appearance on the matter, the moving party must:

1. Contact the opposing party and all other parties who have appeared in the action and confirm that each will submit on the tentative ruling.

2. No later than 4:00 p.m. on the court day before the hearing, call the Courtroom (310-761-4302) advising that all parties will submit on the tentative ruling and waive hearing; and

3. Serve notice of the Court's ruling on all parties entitled to receive service.

If this procedure is followed, when the case is called the Court will enter its ruling on the motion in accordance with its tentative ruling. If any party declines to submit on the tentative ruling, then no telephone call is necessary, and all parties should appear at the hearing. If there is neither a telephone call nor an appearance, then the matter may either be taken off calendar or ruled on.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: DEFENDANT LOS ANGELES UNIFIED SCHOOL DISTRICT’S MOTION TO TAX COSTS

 

Dept. A

DATE: June 29, 2026

TIME: 8:30 A.M.

 

COMPLAINT FILED: 12/09/2022

SAC FILED: 06/22/2023

 

MOVING PARTY:              Defendant Los Angeles Unified School District

RESPONDING PARTY:     Plaintiff T.A.

1.   Background

This is a childhood sexual assault revival action filed pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure § 340.1. Plaintiff T.A. (“Plaintiff”) alleged that in 1979 and 1980, Plaintiff was a student at Alain LeRoy Locke College Preparatory Academy (“Locke”). Plaintiff alleged that an employee at Locke, Jack Jacobson, sexually abused Plaintiff in 1979 and 1980 while Plaintiff was a student at School. Plaintiff alleged that School was part of defendant Los Angeles Unified School District (“District”) and that District breached its duty of care to Plaintiff.

Trial was held in this action between March 9, 2026 and March 18, 2026. The jury entered a verdict on March 18, 2026. Judgment was entered for Plaintiff and against District on March 24, 2026.  On June 22, 2026, the court entered partial judgment notwithstanding the verdict on the second cause of action, leaving the judgment in tact as to the first cause of action.

 

2.   Discussion

On April 13, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Memorandum of Costs, seeking to recover $71,333.40 in costs. District moves to strike or tax Plaintiff’s memorandum of costs. District contends that Plaintiff’s memorandum of costs and the attached worksheet are inconsistent, that some of Plaintiff’s claimed costs are noncompensable, and that Plaintiff failed to provide sufficient information to establish that the costs claimed were incurred in connection with the instant case rather than other, similar cases.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff concedes that his claimed expert fee costs are noncompensable but contends that other costs were reasonably incurred in connection with this action. Plaintiff also contends that additional costs were discovered while working on his opposition.

 

“In ruling upon a motion to tax costs, the trial court’s first determination is whether the statute expressly allows the particular item and whether it appears proper on its face. ‘If so, the burden is on the objecting party to show [the costs] to be unnecessary or unreasonable.’ (Nelson v. Anderson (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 111, 131 (Nelson).) Where costs are not expressly allowed by the statute, the burden is on the party claiming the costs to show that the charges were reasonable and necessary. (Id. at p. 132.) ‘Whether a cost item was reasonably necessary to the ligation presents a question of fact for the trial court and its decision is reviewed by abuse of discretion.’” (Foothill-De Anza Community College Dist. v. Emrich (2007) 158 Cal.App.4th 11, 29-30; citing Ladas v. California State Auto. Ass. (1993) 19 Cal.App.4th 761, 774.) “[I]f the items are properly objected to, they are put in issue and the burden of proof is on the party claiming them as costs.” (Melnyk v. Robleto (1976) 64 Cal.App.3d 618, 624.)  The omission of an item from a cost memorandum may generally be regarded as a waiver of the prevailing party’s claim to that item. (Gorman v. Tassajara Development Corp. (2009) 178 Cal.App.4th 44, 70.)

 

Plaintiff’s memorandum of costs and the summary therein are indeed inconsistent with the attached worksheets.  District contends that the inconsistency between the memorandum of costs summary and worksheet renders Plaintiff’s memorandum of costs improper on its face. Although District cites to Nelson for the proposition that the filing of a motion to tax costs is a proper objection to claimed costs that are improper on their face, District provides no authority stating that the worksheet for a memorandum of costs invalidates the memorandum of costs where it is inconsistent with the summary. Nelson concerns whether particular items are clearly proper or improper, not the technical specifications of the memorandum of costs. (Id. at p. 131.)

 

The summary is declared to be correct by Plaintiff’s counsel under penalty of perjury. The worksheet also constitutes a representation made to the court.  However, there is not a substantial variance between the amounts sought in the summary versus those listed in the worksheet.  The court will therefore tax costs in the amount of the difference between the two documents, awarding costs in the amount of the lower of the figures set forth in the two documents with respect to the costs the court deem recoverable, with the exceptions noted:

1.     Filing fees: The court awards $1,507.81, as reflected on the summary.  Though the total on the worksheet is less, the total of the itemized entries is larger than the amount sought on the summary.

4.                 4. Deposition costs: The court awards $4,512.55, as the amount is identical on both documents.

5.                 5. Service of process costs: The court awards $690.40, as reflected on the worksheet.

 8.  Expert witness fees: The court taxes the entire $46,454.60.

11. Court reporter costs: The court awards $11,808.35, as reflected on the

worksheet.

14. Efiling/service costs: The court taxes $899.688.

15. Other/Mediation: The court awards $4,000.00 as reasonably necessary.

 

District contends that Plaintiff fails to provide adequate supporting information for his claimed costs, including an itemized breakdown, and failed to provide documentation showing that the claimed costs were incurred in connection with this action rather than other, similar actions. However, unless Plaintiff’s claimed costs are improper on their face under Nelson, the burden is on District to show that Plaintiff’s claimed costs were unnecessary or unreasonable. It is therefore District’s burden to show that claimed costs were shared with other actions and were not actually incurred solely in connection with this action, and District has not carried that burden.

 

District contends that Plaintiff’s deposition costs in the amount of $4,512.55 were not reasonably incurred because Plaintiff failed to provide proof that the costs were actually incurred and because some deponents, in particular Dr. John Schwartzberg, did not testify at trial. As previously noted, it is District’s burden to establish that costs were not reasonably and necessarily incurred, including whether they were actually incurred. Deposition costs are allowed by Code of Civil Procedure §1033.5. Additionally, depositions of parties who do not testify at trial may be necessary and reasonable. (Seever v. Copley Press, Inc. (2006) 141 Cal.App.4th 1550, 1557.) Plaintiff argues that Dr. Schwartzberg’s deposition was reasonably taken in good faith in preparation for litigation, and that the strategic decision not to call Dr. Schwartzberg at trial does not negate the good faith of taking his deposition. The court finds that Plaintiff’s deposition costs as claimed in the memorandum of costs were reasonably incurred in the amount of $4,512.55.

 

District contends that Plaintiff’s claimed service of process fees in the amount of $1,183.71 are improper because Plaintiff failed to provide documentation supporting that these costs were actually and reasonably incurred. However, as previously noted, it is District’s burden to establish that these costs were not reasonably incurred if the claimed costs are allowed by statute or otherwise proper on their face. Service fees are permitted by Code of Civil Procedure § 1033.5. District has not carried its burden. However, the court taxes these costs in part due to the variance between the summary and the worksheet and awards $690.40 as reflected on the worksheet.

 

District contends that Plaintiff’s expert witness fees in the amount of $46,454.60 corresponded to an expert who was not ordered by the court and therefore that these costs are noncompensable. Plaintiff concedes that these costs are noncompensable and states that they were included in error. The court will therefore tax these costs in the amount of $46,454.60.

 

District contends that Plaintiff fails to state what statute provides court reporter fees, as Code of Civil Procedure § 1033.5 states that court reporter fees are recoverable “as allowed by statute.” District misreads Code of Civil Procedure § 1033.5. Court reporter fees are recoverable even in the absence of a different statute permitting them to be recovered, as Code of Civil Procedure § 1033.5 allows their recovery. (See Chaaban v. Wet Seal, Inc. (2012) 203 Cal.App.4th 49, 58.) District also contends that Plaintiff fails to provide supporting documentation; as previously noted, it is District’s burden to establish that these costs were not reasonably incurred.

 

District contends that Plaintiff fails to provide adequate supporting documentation for filing and service fees. As previously noted, it is District’s burden to establish that these costs were not reasonably incurred because filing and service fees are compensable under Code of Civil Procedure § 1033.5.  However, the court taxes the $899.68 in e-filing costs reflected on the summary but not reflected on the worksheet.

 

District contends that voluntary, unsuccessful mediation costs were not reasonably incurred and are not expressly allowable under Code of Civil Procedure § 1033.5. Costs for a voluntary, unsuccessful mediation are not necessarily unrecoverable. (Berkeley Cement, Inc. v. Regents of University of California (2019) 30 Cal.App.5th 1133, 1143.) Rather, under such circumstances, whether the mediation was reasonably necessary depends upon the facts of the case. District contends that the mediation was not reasonably necessary because it was merely incurred in an effort to facilitate settlement. This proposition, if credited, would form a blanket rule that voluntary mediation is not reasonably necessary. In Berkeley Cement, Inc. v. Regents of University of California (2019) 30 Cal.App.5th 1133, the court expressly rejected this blanket rule. Therefore, District has not shown that Plaintiff’s mediation costs were not reasonably incurred.  Moreover, the court finds that mediation expenses were reasonably necessary.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff contends that additional costs were located that had not been included in the memorandum of costs. Failure to include these claimed costs in the memorandum of costs waived them, particularly as Plaintiff provides no explanation for their omission from the memorandum of costs.

 

3.   Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, The court DENIES District’s request to strike Plaintiff’s memorandum of costs.  The court GRANTS IN PART and DENIES IN PART District’s motion to tax Plaintiff’s memorandum of costs. The court taxes $46,454.60 in costs pertaining to expert witness fees, and taxes $2,359.69 in additional costs, for a total reduction of $48,814.29.  The court awards Plaintiff costs in the amount of $22,519.11.

District is ordered to give notice.

IT IS SO ORDERED.

 

DATED: June 29, 2026

   

Hon. Elizabeth L. Bradley

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: DEFENDANT JONATHON I. SON’S MOTION FOR SUMMARY ADJUDICATION

 

Dept. A

 

DATE: June 29, 2026

TIME: 8:30 A.M.

 

COMPLAINT FILED: 09/15/2023

TRIAL: 10/28/2026

 

MOVING PARTY:              Defendant Jonathon I. Son dba JC Galaxy Security

RESPONDING PARTY:     Plaintiff Yolanda Elliott

1.   Background

This is an action for battery. Plaintiff Yolanda Elliott alleges that on September 30, 2021, Plaintiff was present at the Del Amo Swapmeet business establishment located at 2787 E. Del Amo Blvd., Rancho Dominguez, CA 90221 (the “Property”). Plaintiff alleges that she was confronted by a security guard while present on the Property, and that, unprovoked, the security guard shoved Plaintiff down a flight of stairs, causing Plaintiff personal injury. Plaintiff alleges that defendant Sent-Pac Corporation employed and/or contracted with defendant Jonathon I Son dba JC Galaxy Security (“JC Galaxy”) to provide security services at the Property.

2.   Evidentiary Objections

Elliott objects to portions of the declarations of Jonathon I. Son and Brian P. Stewart. The court overrules objections 1-14.

3.   Discussion

JC Galaxy moves for summary adjudication of Plaintiff’s claims for punitive damages and negligent hiring, retention, and supervision. JC Galaxy contends that the security guard, Alexander Rhodes, had a long history of working at the Property without issue and therefore JC Galaxy had no reason to believe that Rhodes was anything other than an experienced and professional security guard. In opposition, Plaintiff contends that JC Galaxy ratified Rhodes’ conduct and that it conducted no training of Rhodes or investigation into his history as a security guard when it hired him.

On June 18, 2026, District filed a reply separate statement prohibited under Code of Civil Procedure § 437c, subd. (b)(4). The court will therefore not consider it.

The purpose of a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication “is to provide courts with a mechanism to cut through the parties’ pleadings in order to determine whether, despite their allegations, trial is in fact necessary to resolve their dispute.”  (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843 (Aguilar).)  “Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (c), requires the trial judge to grant summary adjudication if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.)

“On a motion for summary [adjudication], the initial burden is always on the moving party to make a prima facie showing that there are no triable issues of material fact.”  (Scalf v. D.B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1519.)  A defendant or cross-defendant moving for summary adjudication “has met his or her burden of showing that a cause of action has no merit if the party has shown that one or more elements of the cause of action . . . cannot be established, or that there is a complete defense to the cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) 

“Once the defendant or cross-defendant has met that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff or cross-complainant to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).)  “If the plaintiff cannot do so, summary [adjudication] should be granted.”  (Avivi v. Centro Medico Urgente Medical Center (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 463, 467.)  “When deciding whether to grant summary [adjudication], the court must consider all of the evidence set forth in the papers (except evidence to which the court has sustained an objection), as well as all reasonable inferences that may be drawn from that evidence, in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment.”  (Id. at p. 467; Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c).) 

To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence. (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.) A triable issue of material fact exists only if the evidence would allow a reasonable trier of fact to find the underlying fact in favor of the party opposing the motion. (Falcon v. Long Beach Genetics, Inc. (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 1263, 1271.)

 

a. Punitive Damages

JC Galaxy moves for summary adjudication of Plaintiff’s claim for punitive damages. JC Galaxy contends that, as a corporate entity, it may not be held liable for punitive damages because it did not have advance knowledge of Rhodes’ unfitness and, as its sole corporate officer was not present at the time of the incident, it could not have ratified or authorized Rhodes’ conduct. In opposition, Plaintiff contends that JC Galaxy failed to take any corrective or disciplinary action against Rhodes, thereby ratifying his conduct.

 A request for punitive damages requires a showing that the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud or malice. (Civ. Code § 3294, subd. (a).)¿¿¿

“Malice is defined in the statute as conduct intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.” (College Hospital, supra, 8 Cal.4th at p. 725 [examining Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (c)(1)].) “A conscious disregard of the safety of others may constitute malice within the meaning of section 3294 of the Civil Code. In order to justify an award of punitive damages on this basis, the plaintiff must establish that the defendant was aware of the probable dangerous consequences of his conduct, and that he willfully and deliberately failed to avoid those consequences.”¿(Taylor v. Superior Court (1979) 24 Cal.3d 890, 895-896.)¿

“As amended to include [despicable], the statute plainly indicates that absent an intent to injure the plaintiff, ‘malice’ requires more than a ‘willful and conscious’ disregard of the plaintiffs’ interests.¿The additional component of ‘despicable conduct’ must be found.”¿(College Hospital, supra, 8 Cal.4th at p. 725.)¿The statute’s reference to despicable conduct represents a “new substantive limitation on punitive damage awards.” (Ibid.)¿Despicable conduct is “conduct which is so vile, base, contemptible, miserable, wretched or loathsome that it would be looked down upon and despised by ordinary decent people.¿Such conduct has been described as ‘having the character of outrage frequently associated with crime.’ ”¿(Tomaselli v. Transamerica Ins. Co. (1994) 25 Cal.App.4th 1269, 1287 (Tomaselli).) Despicable conduct is most often found in cases involving intentional torts rather than unintentional torts, such as vehicle collisions. (Lackner v. North (2006) 135 Cal.App.4th 1188, 1212.)

JC Galaxy contends that Rhodes’ conduct did not rise the level of malice as required for a showing of punitive damages. However, JC Galaxy did not cite to any evidence in its separate statement in support of this contention. Furthermore, even if JC Galaxy had cited to Rhodes’ incident report stating that he pushed Elliott in response to her attempting to grab and push him (Son Decl., Ex. A), Elliott provided her deposition testimony alongside her opposition, in which he states that Rhodes pushed her down a flight of stairs unprovoked after she asked him not to touch her. (Elliott Depo. 16:3 – 16:13.) A reasonable trier of fact could find that Rhodes engaged in despicable conduct with a willful and conscious disregard for Elliott’s safety, such that there is a triable issue of material fact as to the issue of whether Rhodes’ conduct rose to the level of malice.

“[T]he imposition of punitive damages upon a corporation is based upon its own fault.¿ It is not imposed vicariously by virtue of the fault of others.”¿ (City Products Corp. v. Globe Indemnity Co. (1979) 88 Cal.App.3d 31, 36.)¿ “Corporations are legal entities which do not have minds capable of recklessness, wickedness, or intent to injure or deceive.¿ An award of punitive damages against a corporation therefore must rest on the malice of the corporation’s employees.¿ But the law does not impute every employee’s malice to the corporation.¿ Instead, the punitive damages statute requires proof of malice among corporate leaders:¿ the ‘officer[s], director[s], or managing agent[s].’ ”¿ (Cruz v. Home Base (2000) 83 Cal.App.4th 160, 167 [citation omitted].)

Corporate ratification of malice is generally shown where “the employer demonstrates an intent to adopt or approve oppressive, fraudulent, or malicious behavior by an employee in the performance of his job duties.” (College Hospital Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 726.) This may occur where the corporation’s managing agent knows of the misconduct at issue and fails to investigate, discipline, or discharge the errant employee. (Ibid; C.R. v. Tenet Healthcare Corp. (2009) 169 Cal.App.4th 1094, 1111.) It therefore may occur after the incident in question.

JC Galaxy contends that its CEO, Jonathon I. Son, could not have ratified or authorized Rhodes’ conduct because he was not there at the time. However, a corporation may ratify or authorize an employee’s conduct in advance or retroactively without a managing agent being present at the time of the incident. JC Galaxy provides no evidence that Jonathon I. Son or any other managing agent did not, at some time before or after the incident, authorize or ratify Rhodes’ conduct during the incident. JC Galaxy also provides no evidence tending to show that Plaintiff cannot provide evidence of such. Although Son declares that he had never met Elliott before or after the incident, and that he was not present at the Property on the date of the incident and was unaware of it until a report was presented to him, this does not constitute evidence that he or any other managing agent did not authorize or ratify Rhodes’ conduct at some other time.

As such, JC Galaxy has failed to carry its burden to establish that there are no triable issues of material fact as to the issue of Plaintiff’s claim for punitive damages. The court will deny JC Galaxy’s motion for summary adjudication as to this claim.

b. Negligent hiring, supervision, and retention

“Under California law, an employer may be held directly liable for the behavior of an unfit employee where the employer was negligent in the hiring, training, supervising, or retaining of that employee. [Citation.] Negligence liability will be imposed upon the employer if it knew or should have known that hiring the employee created a particular risk or hazard and that particular harm then materializes.” (Doe v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (N.D. Cal. 2016) 184 F.Supp.3d 774, 788.)

JC Galaxy contends that it had no advance knowledge of Rhodes’ unfitness for the job of security guard and should not have had that knowledge. In support of this contention, JC Galaxy provides the declaration of Jonathon I. Son, who states that during the hiring process, “Thomas Kim, the Manager of Del Amo Plaza recommended that JSGS hire Alexander Rhodes. Mr. Rhodes was already working security at the Del Amo Plaza, and Mr. Kim was satisfied with his work as a security officer.” (Son Decl. ¶ 12.) JC Galaxy also provides evidence that Rhodes was, as of the date of the incident, licensed as a security guard by the Bureau of Security and Investigative Services. (Son Decl., Ex. B.) JC Galaxy provides the declaration of Thomas Kim, the manager of the Property, who states that he has never received a complaint from a customer about a security guard’s use of force at the Property, outside of the incident. (Stewart Decl., Ex. F., 99:1 – 99:6.)

This is sufficient to carry JC Galaxy’s burden to establish that there are no triable issues of material fact as to Plaintiff’s claim for negligent hiring, supervision, and retention, and that it is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. The burden now shifts to Plaintiff.

In opposition, Plaintiff contends that JC Galaxy did not conduct any background check on Rhodes prior to hiring him, and that if it had, it would have discovered that his license to operate as a security guard had at one point been suspended due to reports of domestic abuse. Plaintiff also contends that JC Galaxy did not provide Rhodes with any training, testing, or instruction.

Plaintiff provides the deposition testimony of Jonathon I. Son, who states that he did not conduct any background check of Rhodes before hiring him. (Son Depo. 61:14 – 61:18.) Son also states that JC Galaxy provided no training to Rhodes on the issues of deescalation or use of force. (Son Depo. 65:7 – 65:13.)

Based on the provided evidence, a reasonable trier of fact could find that JC Galaxy failed to conduct a background check on Rhodes, should have conducted a background check on Rhodes, and would have discovered that Rhodes was unfit had it done so. A reasonable trier of fact could therefore find that JC Galaxy negligently hired, supervised, trained, or retained Rhodes based on this and based on its failure to provide Rhodes with any training on deescalation or use of force. Plaintiff has therefore shown the existence of a triable issue of material fact as to her claim for negligent hiring, supervision, and retention.

As such, the court will deny JC Galaxy’s motion for summary adjudication as to this claim.

4.   Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, the court DENIES JC Galaxy’s motion for summary adjudication.

JC Galaxy is ordered to give notice.

IT IS SO ORDERED.
