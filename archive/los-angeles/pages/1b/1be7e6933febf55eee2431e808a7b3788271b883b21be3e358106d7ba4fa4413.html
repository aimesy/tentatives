   II.          Preliminary Procedural Issues

 

Meet and Confer Requirement – Before filing a demurrer or motion to strike, the demurring or moving party is required to meet and confer at least five days before the date the responsive pleading is due, in person, by telephone, or by video conference, with the party who filed the pleading for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer or motion to strike. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 430.41, subd. (a), 435.5, subd. (a).).) The demurring or moving party shall file and serve with the demurrer or motion to strike a declaration stating the means by which the parties met and conferred and that the parties did not reach an agreement to resolve the issues raised, or that the party who filed the pleading demurred or objected to failed to respond or otherwise failed to meet and confer in good faith. (Ibid.)  

 

Here, Defense counsel Jessica Wan declares she sent a meet and confer letter to Plaintiff’s counsel on March 13, 2026 outlining the deficiencies in the TAC, and that counsel met and conferred via telephone on March 23, 2026 regarding the letter and discussing Plaintiff’s position. (Wan Decl., ¶¶ 4-5, Ex. 1.) The parties agreed to continue Defendants’ response deadline to April 14, 2026 to allow Plaintiff additional time to consider Defendants’ meet and confer letter, but following the telephonic meet and confer, Wan has contacted Plaintiff’s counsel Hanassab on two separate occasions without any response from Plaintiff or his counsel. (Wan Decl., ¶¶ 6-8, Ex. 2.)

 

The meet and confer requirement is satisfied.

 III.          Legal Standard

Standard for Demurrer – A party against whom a complaint has been filed may object by demurrer to the pleading on the ground that the pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (e).) A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations reasonably, liberally, and in context. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

As a general matter, in a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) “A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Ibid.) Courts must “treat the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Blank, supra, 39 Cal.3d at 318.) The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action. (Hahn, supra, 147 Cal.App.4th at 747.)

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Id.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 126.) The threshold for meeting this burden is low, and “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245).

 

Standard for Motion to Strike – A motion to strike lies where either (1) there is “irrelevant, false or improper matter inserted in any pleading,” or (2) where a pleading or part thereof is “not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule or order of court.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) The Court has the authority to strike out any part of a pleading not filed in conformity with an order of the court. (See Ibid; Ferraro v. Camarlinghi (2008) 161 Cal.App.4th 509, 528.) 

When the pleadings are defective, “the defect may be raised by demurrer or motion to strike, or by motion for judgment on the pleadings.” (Coyne v. Krempels (1950) 36 Cal.2d 257, 262.) The grounds for a motion to strike shall appear on the face of the challenged pleading or form any matter of which the court is required to take judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437, subd. (a).) A matter to be judicially noticed shall be specified in the notice of motion. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437, subd. (b).) The court then may strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading and strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) When the defect which justifies striking a complaint is capable of cure, the court should allow leave to amend. (Perlman v. Municipal Court (1979) 99 Cal.App.3d 568, 575.)

 IV.          Discussion

 

Application – Defendants demurrer to the TAC as to the second, fifth, seventh, and tenth causes of action pursuant to California Code of Civil Procedure, section 430.10, subdivision (e), on the grounds that each fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action against one or more Defendant. (Demurrer, p. 2:7-17.) Defendants also move to strike punitive damages from the TAC as to Yu and Grand. (Motion, p. 3:24-25.)

 

a.     Second Cause of Action – Age Discrimination in Violation of FEHA (Gov. Code, § 12940(a))

 

Defendants demur to the second cause of action on the grounds that it fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action against Hong because the factual allegations within the TAC are insufficient to establish that age substantially motivated the challenged adverse action. (Demurrer, p. 3:6-8, 7:1-3.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that Defendants are statutorily barred from demurring to the second cause of action pursuant to California Code of Civil Procedure, section 430.41, subdivision (b) because they failed to demur to this cause of action in their previous demurrer to the SAC. (Opp., p. 4:6-12.)

 

On reply, Defendants argue that Section 430.41, subdivision (b) limits a renewed demurrer after the court sustains a demurrer to an entire pleading and the plaintiff amends in response, which did not occur as to either challenged claim as Defendants did not previously demur to the second cause of action. (Reply, p. 2:26-28.) Defendants further argue that Section 430.41, subdivision (f) expressly preserves Defendants’ rights under Section 430.80, including an objection that a pleading fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. (Reply, p. 3:9-12.)

 

California Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, subdivision (b) states: “A party demurring to a pleading that has been amended after a demurrer to an earlier version of the pleading was sustained shall not demur to any portion of the amended complaint, cross-complaint, or answer on grounds that could have been raised by demurrer to the earlier version of the complaint, cross-complaint, or answer.” (Emphasis added.)

 

The Court disagrees with Defendants’ arguments that Section 430.41(b) only applies where a demurrer is brought to a prior version of the complaint as a whole, that it must be sustained in whole rather than in part, or that it must be sustained as to the particular cause of action presently demurred to. Section 430.41(b) contains no such limiting language, as this section only requires that a demurrer to a prior version of the pleading be sustained, not that it be sustained in whole to the entire pleading or sustained to the particular claim on which the defendant files a renewed demurrer. To so read would circumvent the purpose of this statute, which is to prevent piecemeal arguments as to why causes of action should be dismissed.

 

Here, Defendants demurred to certain causes of action in the SAC. That demurrer was sustained in part. Plaintiff subsequently amended the SAC, but did not amend this cause of action or to any of the facts pertaining to this cause of action in his TAC. The second cause of action in the TAC is, word for word, exactly identical to the second cause of action as asserted in the SAC. (SAC, ¶¶ 49-59; TAC ¶¶ 49-59.) Defendants presently argue that the TAC fails to state any facts alleging that Hong replaced Plaintiff with a significantly younger worker, that any decision-maker made an age-related remark in connection with the termination, or state any comparator facts establishing that Hong treated similarly situated but younger employees more favorably. (Demurrer, p. 6:9-13.) However, no new facts are introduced in the TAC on which Defendants are entitled to a renewed demurrer.

 

Defendants had every opportunity to demur to the second cause of action in their demurrer to the SAC and they failed to do so. They may not now demurrer on grounds which existed at the time they demurred to the SAC. Therefore, based on a plain reading of the statute, Defendants are procedurally barred from objecting to the second cause of action on demurrer. (See Apple Inc. v. Superior Court (2017) 18 Cal.App.5th 222, 249, n. 13.)

 

Defendants’ demurrer to the second cause of action is OVERRULED.

 

b.     Fifth Cause of Action – Whistleblower Retaliation (Cal. Lab. Code, § 6310)

 

Defendants demur to the fifth cause of action on the grounds that it fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action against Goldberg, Yu, and Grand.

 

However, Defendants previously demurred to the fifth cause of action as pled in the SAC, which was overruled as to all Defendants. The fifth cause of action in the TAC is, like the second cause of action, entirely unchanged from the SAC, and no factual allegations have been introduced which may give rise to a renewed demurrer on this cause of action. (SAC, ¶¶ 82-91; TAC, ¶¶ 82-91.) The Court’s prior ruling stands.

 

The demurrer to the fifth cause of action is OVERRULED.

 

c.      Seventh Cause of Action – IIED

 

Defendants demurrer to the seventh cause of action on the grounds that it fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action against Yu and Grand.

 

A cause of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress exists when there is ‘(1)

extreme and outrageous conduct by the defendant with the intention of causing, or reckless

disregard of the probability of causing, emotional distress; (2) the plaintiff's suffering

severe or extreme emotional distress; and (3) actual and proximate causation of the

emotional distress by the defendant’s outrageous conduct.’ A defendant’s conduct is

‘outrageous’ when it is so ‘extreme as to exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated in a

civilized community.’ And the defendant’s conduct must be ‘intended to inflict injury or

engaged in with the realization that injury will result.’” (Hughes v. Pair (2009) 46 Cal.4th

1035, 1050-1051.)

 

Whether a defendant’s particular conduct rises to such level sufficient to make a claim for

IIED is a fact-intensive issue. (Alcorn y. Anbro Eng’g, Inc. (1970) 2 Cal. 3d 493, 499

[‘‘Where reasonable men may differ, it is for the jury, subject to the control of the court, to determine whether, in the particular case, the conduct has been sufficiently extreme and

outrageous to result in liability”); Smith v. BP Lubricants USA Inc. (2021) 64 Cal. App. 5th 138, 148, [holding “whether conduct is outrageous is usually a question of fact”’] (internal quotations and citations omitted); Trerice v. Blue Cross of California (1989) 209 Cal. App. 3d 878, 883 [holding “the outrageousness of a defendant’s conduct normally presents an issue of fact to be determined by the trier of fact”’].

 

Defendants demur to the IIED cause of action for failure to state facts as to Yu or Grand’s extreme or outrageous conduct and intent. (Demurrer, p. 9:4-5.) Specifically, Defendants argue that the TAC fails to allege facts showing that Yu or Grand acted within intent or knowledge that the conduct would cause emotional distress, and contend, relying on Janken v. GM Hughes Electronics (1996) 46 Cal.App.4th 55, that personnel management activity in an employment setting does not support an IIED claim, even if the plaintiff alleges management activity was taken upon improper motive. (Demurrer, p. 8:20-23.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff asserts that the Court has already determined that the same course of conduct is sufficient to establish IIED as to Hong and Goldberg, and that he has remedied the defects raised by the Court in its order sustaining the demurrer to the SAC, being that insufficient facts were alleged as to their individual liability. (Opp., p. 6:17-20, 7:12-15.)

 

The TAC alleges the following relevant facts, which remain unchanged from the SAC:

 

1.     Plaintiff was a dedicated and honest employee.

2.     Defendants ruthlessly retaliated against Plaintiff once he reported harassment and dangerous business practices.

3.     Plaintiff was consistently harassed at work, and when he reported the harassment, nothing was done and he was still required to work with his harasser.

4.     Not only was Plaintiff distressed when he noticed other employees and managers engaging in unlawful and dangerous business practices, but he also was regularly pressured to do the same.

5.     Each time Plaintiff reported unlawful and dangerous activities to his supervisors, Defendants ignored Plaintiff and subsequently retaliated against him.

6.     Defendants suddenly required Plaintiff to work all holidays and made other changes to the conditions of Plaintiff's employment that harmed Plaintiff.

7.     Defendants abused Plaintiff until they terminated him for pretextual reasons.

8.     Defendants denied Plaintiff equal terms, conditions, and/or privileges of employment enjoyed by other employees and failed to remedy or prevent harassment and retaliation.

9.     Defendants’ illegal conduct, including the harassment and retaliation against Plaintiff, in whole or in part, on the basis of Plaintiff's complaints of in a harassment, and/or his complaints of unlawful business practices, constitutes “severe and outrageous” misconduct and exceeds all bounds of conduct usually tolerated in a decent and civilized society.

10.  Defendants’ conduct was so extreme so as to exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated decent and civilized society.

11.  Defendants’ conduct was intentional in nature, and/or done with reckless disregard of the probability that Plaintiff would suffer severe emotional distress.

12.  Defendants’ conduct was a substantial factor in causing Plaintiff’s severe emotional distress.

13.  Defendants’ conduct was a substantial, direct, and proximate factor in causing Plaintiff’s harm.

 

(TAC, ¶¶ 102-04, 107-09.)

 

The TAC has been amended to include additional factual allegations as to Grand, now alleging the following facts:

 

1.     Grand, as Plaintiff’s direct manager, had supervisory authority over Plaintiff’s working conditions, schedule, and daily assignments.

2.     Grand received Plaintiff’s September 2022 report of age-based harassment but took no corrective action.

3.     When Plaintiff confronted Grand about why he was still working with his harasser, Grand stated “it was the easier thing to do,” consciously choosing his own convenience over Plaintiff’s safety and wellbeing.

4.     Grand participated in or facilitated the retaliatory schedule changes that required Plaintiff to work every holiday and altered his trailer assignments—changes not imposed on other drivers.

5.     Grand knew these changes were retaliatory and intended to punish Plaintiff for his protected complaints.

6.     Grand’s conduct was extreme and outrageous because he used his supervisory authority to perpetuate a hostile work environment, deliberately choosing to keep Plaintiff working with his harasser out of convenience, and personally implementing retaliatory adverse employment actions against a longtime exemplary employee.

7.     Defendant Grand, as Plaintiff’s direct manager, exercised substantial discretionary authority over Plaintiff’s working conditions, schedule, and daily assignments.

 

(TAC, ¶¶ 105, 112.)

 

The TAC has also been amended to include additional factual allegations as to Yu, now alleging the following facts:

 

1.     Defendant Yu, as the Human Resources representative, had authority over personnel decisions, including the investigation and resolution of harassment complaints and the authority to terminate employees.

2.     Yu received Plaintiff’s September 2022 report of age-based harassment but failed to conduct any meaningful investigation, failed to take corrective action, and failed to implement any measures to protect Plaintiff from ongoing harassment and retaliation.

3.     Yu personally effectuated Plaintiff’s termination on January 23, 2023, informing Plaintiff he was being terminated because he was “not a good fit”—a reason Yu knew to be pretextual given Plaintiff’s exemplary performance record of nearly five years with no reprimands, no write-ups, and multiple raises.

4.     Yu’s conduct was extreme and outrageous because he weaponized the HR function to punish rather than protect Plaintiff, deliberately fabricated a pretextual basis for termination, and personally carried out the wrongful termination of a veteran employee in retaliation for Plaintiff’s protected complaints of harassment and illegal business practices.

5.     Defendant Yu, as Human Resources representative, exercised substantial discretionary authority over personnel decisions, including the investigation of harassment complaints and the termination of employees, and personally effectuated Plaintiff’s pretextual termination.

 

(TAC, ¶¶ 106, 112.)

 

Finally, the TAC alleges the following:

 

1.     Goldberg, Grand, and Yu each made substantial discretionary decisions that determined corporate policy with respect to Plaintiff’s employment, his complaints, and his termination.

2.     These acts were done with malice, fraud, oppression, and in reckless disregard of Plaintiff’s rights, including that Defendants asserted false (pretextual) grounds for terminating Plaintiff’s employment, knowingly pressured Plaintiff to engage in dangerous and illegal practices, deliberately ignored his complaints of harassment and retaliation, and intentionally perpetuated a hostile work environment.

3.     Such acts were despicable in character and warrant the imposition of punitive damages against each Defendant within the meaning of California Civil Code section 3294 and in a sum sufficient to punish and deter Defendants’ future conduct.

 

(TAC, ¶ 112.)

 

The Court finds these additionally pled facts sufficient to state a cause of action at the pleadings stage. These additional facts speaking to Grand and Yu’s individual, affirmative, and intentional wrongdoings remedy the Court’s prior finding that there were insufficient facts alleged to establish their individual liability.

 

Defendants are correct that mere management of personnel “is not outrageous conduct beyond the bounds of human decency, but is conduct essential to the welfare and prosperity of society,” and “pleading of personnel management activity is insufficient to support a claim of intentional infliction of emotional distress, even if improper motivation is alleged.” (Janken v. GM Hughes Electronics (1996) 46 Cal.App.4th 55, 80.) However, a plaintiff “may pursue a claim for intentional infliction of emotional distress in the employment context where the conduct at issue violates FEHA and also satisfies the elements of the claim.” (Light v. Light v. Department of Parks & Recreation (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 75, 101.) While there are limitations as discussed in Jaken, a jury may still find extreme and outrageous conduct where a plaintiff alleges facts beyond mere improperly motivated termination and retaliation within the scope of employment. (Jaken, supra, 46 Cal.App.4th at 80.)

 

Here, Plaintiff alleges not only that the conduct giving rise to the IIED claim violates FEHA, but also that the conduct extended beyond the bounds of mere failure to listen to his complaints or encouragement of retaliation, but that Yu and Grand both intentionally and actively participated in this conduct, including engagement and pressure to participate in illegal business practices, intentionally assigning illegal work to Plaintiff in retaliation, age discrimination, constructing a pretextual basis on which to fire Plaintiff, and terminating his employment.

 

Therefore, given their supervisory positions and the allegations of affirmative, individual conduct, the Court cannot find as a matter of law at this stage that Plaintiff has failed to state a cause of action for IIED against Yu and Grand. Therefore, the demurrer to the seventh cause of action is OVERRULED.

 

d.     Tenth Cause of Action – Intentional Interference with Prospective Economic Advantage

 

Defendants demur to the tenth cause of action on the grounds that it fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action against all Defendants.

 

The elements of a claim for intentional interference with prospective economic advantage include “(1) an economic relationship between the plaintiff and some third party, with the probability of future economic benefit to the plaintiff; (2) the defendant’s knowledge of the relationship; (3) intentional [or negligent] acts on the part of the defendant designed to disrupt the relationship; (4) actual disruption of the relationship; and (5) economic harm to the plaintiff proximately caused by the acts of the defendant.” (Crown Imports, LLC v. Superior Court (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 1395, 1404 (internal citations omitted).) Further, “the alleged interference must have been wrongful by some measure beyond the fact of the interference itself. For an act to be sufficiently independently wrongful, it must be unlawful, that is, it is proscribed by some constitutional, statutory, regulatory, common law, or other determinable legal standard.” (Ibid.)

 

Defendants argue that the TAC fails as to Hong, Yu, and Grand on the grounds that the alleged statements are only attributed to Goldberg, and fails as to Goldberg because the TAC does not allege any facts that the statements made by Goldberg caused Pacific Tank Line (PTL) to rescind the offer or refuse to hire Plaintiff because of the statements or any other fact showing the relationship between Plaintiff and PTL was disrupted or ended. (Demurrer, p. 9:25-26, 10:5-9.) Defendants further contend that the TAC does not adequately plead defamation as a separate wrongful act. (Demurrer, p. 10:12-13.)

 

Plaintiff argues that in previously sustaining the demurrer to the tenth cause of action as pled in the TAC, the Court identified only one issue, which was that the SAC lacked specificity as to what statements were made and in what manner they were untrue, which Plaintiff has now cured in the TAC. (Opp., p. 8:7-9.) Plaintiff also argues that he has sufficiently pled independent wrongful conduct supporting this cause of action because the TAC asserts that (1) statements by a former employer to a prospective employer that impute legal disqualification from employment directly injure the former employee and support defamation and interference claims, and (2) Goldberg’s alleged conduct violates Labor Code section 1050, which makes it a misdemeanor for any person, after an employee has left employment, to make any misrepresentation that prevents or attempts to prevent the former employee from obtaining employment. (Opp., p. 8:20-22, 9:3-6.) Finally, Plaintiff argues that the claim is properly pled against Hong as well, as the TAC alleges agency, ratification, and course-and-scope relationships among all Defendants, and Goldberg’s statements arose from his role as manager and the relationship between the two companies. (Opp., p. 9:24-10:2.)

 

The TAC alleges the following relevant facts:

 

1.     An economic relationship existed between Plaintiff and PTL, a third party. Plaintiff sought employment from PTL and received an offer for employment.  

2.     The economic relationship between Plaintiff and PTL carried a probability of an economic benefit to Plaintiff in that Plaintiff was likely to become employed by PTL following his termination from Defendant. 

3.     Defendants had knowledge of the economic relationship between Plaintiff and PTL. Hong and  PTL operate out of the same or adjacent premises. Plaintiff is informed and believes that Defendants became aware of Plaintiff’s pursuit of employment with PTL due to relationships between Goldberg and PTL personnel.

4.     Defendants engaged in independently wrongful conduct by intentionally spreading false and/or misleading information to PTL so that Plaintiff would not get hired.

5.     Defendants knew that interference with the relationship between Plaintiff and PTL was certain or substantially certain to occur as a result of Defendant’s wrongful conduct.  

6.     After learning of Plaintiff’s job offer, Defendant Goldberg intentionally contacted personnel at PTL and made false statements concerning Plaintiff, including but not limited to statements that: (a) Plaintiff was subject to legal or contractual restrictions prohibiting his employment with Pacific Tank Lines; (b) Plaintiff could not lawfully be hired due to alleged contractual obligations to Conico; and (c) Pacific Tank Lines would face legal consequences if it hired Plaintiff.

7.     Plaintiff was not subject to any enforceable non-compete agreement, non-solicitation restriction, or other legal prohibition preventing his employment with PTL.

8.     Defendants knew these statements were false at the time they were made, or made them with reckless disregard for their truth.

9.     Defendants made these false statements with the intent to prevent Plaintiff from obtaining employment and to blacklist Plaintiff within the industry, constituting defamation and an independently wrongful act proscribed by law.

10.  As a direct and proximate result of Defendants’ interference, Plaintiff has suffered and continues to suffer harm, including lost past and future income and employment benefits, damages to his career, and lost wages, unpaid expenses, and penalties, as well as interest on unpaid wages at the legal rate from and after each payday on which those wages should have been paid, in a sum to be proven at trial.

11.  As a direct and proximate result of Defendant’s interference, Plaintiff has suffered and will suffer non-economic damages for loss of sleep, irritability, humiliation, loss of reputation, and mental and emotional distress, in an amount according to proof at the time of trial.

12.  The above-described actions were perpetrated and/or ratified by managing agents, officers or directors of Defendants and were done with malice, fraud, oppression, and in reckless disregard of Plaintiff’s rights, including that Defendant maliciously prevented Plaintiff from obtaining other employment after wrongfully terminating him.

13.  Such acts were despicable in character and warrant the imposition of punitive damages within the meaning of California Civil Code section 3294 and in a sum sufficient to punish and deter Defendant’s future conduct.

 

(TAC, ¶¶ 126-33.)

 

Plaintiff has sufficiently pled as to all Defendants that there was an economic relationship between himself and PTL, and that Defendants knew of the relationship and the probability of Plaintiff’s future economic benefit arising from a potential employment opportunity. (TAC, ¶¶ 127-30.) However, as to Yu and Grand, Plaintiff has failed to plead sufficient facts as to how they were responsible for or ratified the statements made by Goldberg, that they independently made any similar statements or took any affirmative acts designed to disrupt the potential employment relationship, or the actual disruption of the relationship.

 

The TAC has sufficiently alleged that Goldberg made statements could subject him to liability under the cause of action. The TAC  further alleges that, as a direct and proximate result of this interference, Plaintiff suffered lost past and future income, employment benefits, and damage to his career (TAC ¶ 131). Read liberally, the TAC alleges that Plaintiff did not obtain the PTL position because of Goldberg’s statements. Any inference is drawn in Plaintiff’s favor. (Code Civ. Proc. § 452; Del E. Webb, supra, 123 Cal.App.3d at 604.) As to Hong, the TAC alleges facts that Goldberg was acting within the course and scope of his employment with Hong when he made the statements. TAC ¶¶ 129-130.)

 

Accordingly, Defendants’ demurrer to the tenth cause of action is SUSTAINED as to Yu and Grand, and OVERRULED as to Goldberg and Hong.

 

e.      Motion to Strike

 

Yu and Grand move to strike paragraphs 33-36, 91, 112, 133, and the prayer for relief for exemplary and punitive damages on the grounds that the TAC does not plead ultimate facts sufficient to support punitive damages against Yu or Grand. (Motion, p. 2:1-3:25.)

 

“In order to survive a motion to strike an allegation of punitive damages, the ultimate facts showing an entitlement to such relief must be pled by a plaintiff. [Citations.] In passing on the correctness of a ruling on a motion to strike, judges read allegations of a pleading subject to a motion to strike as a whole, all parts in their context, and assume their truth. [Citations.]” (Clauson v. Superior Court, 67 Cal. App. 4th 1253, 1255.)

 

As discussed above as to Plaintiff’s seventh cause of action for IIED, the Court finds that Plaintiff has pled ultimate facts sufficient to establish that Yu and Grand personally engaged in conduct constituting malice, oppression, or fraud, warranting punitive damages and had supervisory authority over the allegedly wrongful acts. (TAC, ¶¶ 105-06, 112.)

 

Accordingly, Yu and Grand’s motion to strike punitive damages is DENIED.

 

f.      Leave to Amend

 

Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976), 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) While under California law leave to amend is liberally granted, “leave to amend should not be granted where, in probability, amendment would be futile.” (Vaillette v. Fireman's Fund Ins. Co. (1993), all 18 Cal. App. 4th 680, 685). “A trial court does not abuse its discretion when it sustains a demurrer without leave to amend if either (a) the facts and the nature of the claims are clear and no liability exists, or (b) it is probable from the nature of the defects and previous unsuccessful attempts to plead that the plaintiff cannot state a claim.” (Cantu v. Resolution Trust Corp. (1992) 4 Cal.App.4th 857, 889.)

 

The liberal policy favoring amendments is not without limitation. In response to a demurrer and prior to the case being at issue, a complaint “shall not be amended more than three times, absent an offer to the trial court as to such additional facts to be pleaded that there is a reasonable possibility the defect can be cured to state a cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (e)(1).) However, the “three-amendment limit shall not include an amendment made without leave of the court pursuant to Section 472, provided the amendment is made before a demurrer to the original complaint or cross-complaint is filed.” (Ibid.) California Code of Civil Procedure section 472, subdivision (a) states that “[a] party may amend its pleading once without leave of the court at any time before the answer, demurrer, or motion to strike is filed, or after a demurrer or motion to strike is filed but before the demurrer or motion to strike is heard if the amended pleading is filed and served no later than the date for filing an opposition to the demurrer or motion to strike.”

 

Here, Plaintiff has been granted leave to amend twice, and thus has not surpassed the three-amendment limit requiring a showing to the Court. However, the only portion of the demurrer that is sustained is as to Yu and Grand on the Tenth Cause of Action. Plaintiff’s Opposition does not mention Yu or Grand in its argument on the Tenth Cause of Action, and does not represent that there are additional facts that could be alleged as to Yu or Grand. For these reasons, the Court is inclined to deny leave to amend as to the Tenth Cause of Action.

    V.          Conclusion

 

Defendants Hong Holdings, LLC dba Conico Wholesale, LLC, Mike Goldberg, John Yu, and Larry Grand’s Demurrer to the tenth cause of action is SUSTAINED as to Yu and Grand, and OVERRULED as to Hong and Goldberg.

 

Defendants’ Demurrer to the second, fifth, and seventh causes of action is OVERRULED.

 

Defendants Hong Holdings, LLC dba Conico Wholesale, LLC, Mike Goldberg, John Yu, and Larry Grand’s Motion to Strike is DENIED.

 III.          Legal Standard

 

Standard for Recovery of Attorney’s Fees – Pursuant to Lancaster Municipal Code section 8.28.250, a prevailing party in a nuisance abatement action may be entitled to recover reasonable attorney’s fees.

 

The lodestar method for calculating attorneys' fees applies to a statutory attorneys' fee award unless there is a clear legislative intent to the contrary. (Galbiso v. Orosi Pub. Util. Dist. (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 1063, 1089 [citing Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1135-36].) Lancaster Municipal Code section 8.28.250 allows the prevailing party to recover reasonable attorneys' fees, but does not provide additional methods of calculating reasonable attorneys' fees; thus, the lodestar method is the appropriate calculation.

 

The lodestar method determines recoverable fees by multiplying the reasonable hourly rate of the attorneys by the reasonable number of hours billed for the work performed. “A trial court assessing attorney fees begins with a touchstone or lodestar figure, based on the careful compilation of the time spent and reasonable hourly compensation of each attorney involved in the presentation of the case.” (Christian Research Institute v. Alnor (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 1315, 1321 [internal quotations omitted].) “The reasonableness of attorney fees is within the discretion of the trial court, to be determined from a consideration of such factors as the nature of the litigation, the complexity of the issues, the experience and expertise of counsel and the amount of time involved. [Citation.] The court may also consider whether the amount requested is based upon unnecessary or duplicative work.”  (Wilkerson v. Sullivan (2002) 99 Cal.App.4th 443, 448 (citations omitted).) “The basis for the trial court’s calculation must be the actual hours counsel has devoted to the case, less those that result from inefficient or duplicative use of time.” (Horsford v. Board of Trustees Of California State University (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 359, 395.)  “The law is clear, however, that an award of attorney fees may be based on counsel’s declarations, without production of detailed time records.” (Raining Data Corp. v. Barrenechea (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 1363, 1375.)

 

In setting the hourly rate for an attorney fees award, courts are entitled to consider the rate of “fees customarily charged by that attorney and others in the community for similar work.” (Bihun v. AT&T Information Systems, Inc. (1993) 13 Cal. App. 4th 976, 997 [affirming rate of $450 per hour], overruled on other grounds by Lakin v. Watkins Associated Indus. (1993) 6 Cal. 4th 644, 664; see also Heritage Pacific Financial, LLC v. Monroy (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 972, 1009 [“[R]ate determinations in other cases, particularly those setting a rate for the plaintiffs’ attorney, are satisfactory evidence of the prevailing market rate.”].)

 

Standard for Recovery of Costs – California Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5 sets forth the specific categories of costs that a prevailing party may or may not be entitled to recover as a matter of right in civil litigation.

 

California Code of Civil Procedure § 1033.5 provides in relevant part as follows: 

 

“(a) The following items are allowable as costs under Section 1032:

(1) Filing, motion, and jury fees.

…

(4) Service of process by a public officer, registered process server, or other means…If service is by a process server…the recoverable cost is the amount actually incurred in effecting service, including, but not limited to, a stakeout or other means employed in locating the person to be served…

…

(14) Fees for the electronic filing or service of documents through an electronic filing service provider if a court requires or orders electronic filing or service of documents.

…

(b) The following items are not allowable as costs, except when expressly authorized by law:

(1) Fees of experts not ordered by the court.

(2) Investigation expenses in preparing the case for trial.

(3) Postage, telephone, and photocopying charges, except for exhibits.

(4) Costs in investigation of jurors or in preparation for voir dire.

(5) Transcripts of court proceedings not ordered by the court.

(c) An award of costs shall be subject to the following:

(1) Costs are allowable if incurred, whether or not paid.

(2) Allowable costs shall be reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation rather than merely convenient or beneficial to its preparation.

(3) Allowable costs shall be reasonable in amount.

(4) Items not mentioned in this section and items assessed upon application may be allowed or denied in the court's discretion.

…”

 

Generally, the Court has discretion to allow costs not listed as a recoverable category in subdivision (a) and not barred by subdivision (b). (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (c)(4).)

 IV.          Discussion

 

Application – Plaintiff moves for an award of attorney’s fees and costs incurred in the amount of $71,921.93 pursuant to Lancaster Municipal Code section 8.28.250. (Notice of Motion, p. 2:5-9.) This total consists of $67,471 in attorney’s fees and $4,450.93 in costs. (Motion, p. 4:18-20, Litvak Decl., ¶ 26.)

 

a.     Plaintiff’s Entitlement to Attorney’s Fees and Costs

 

Plaintiff moves for attorney’s fees and costs as the prevailing party in the present action pursuant to Lancaster Municipal Code section 8.28.250.

 

Lancaster Municipal Code section 8.28.250 states:

 

A.    A prevailing party in any administrative, civil or equitable judicial action to abate, or cause the abatement of a public nuisance as defined in Part 1 of this chapter, or in any appeal or other judicial action arising therefrom, may recover reasonable attorney's fees in accordance with the following subsections:

1. Attorney's fees are not recoverable by any person as a prevailing party unless the city manager, or a designee thereof, or an attorney for, and on behalf of, the city, elects in writing to seek recovery of the city's attorney's fees at the initiation of that individual action or proceeding. Failure to make such an election precludes any entitlement to, or award of, attorney's fees in favor of any person or the city;

2. The city is the prevailing party when an administrative or judicial determination is made or affirmed by which a person is found to be responsible for one or more conditions or activities that constitute a public nuisance. A person is the prevailing party only when a final administrative or judicial determination completely absolves that person of responsibility for all conditions or activities that were alleged, in that action or proceeding, to constitute a public nuisance. An administrative or judicial determination that results in findings of responsibility and non-responsibility on the part of a person for conditions or activities that were alleged in that action or proceeding to constitute a public nuisance, shall nevertheless result in the city being the prevailing party.

B.   Provided that the city has made an election to seek attorney's fees, an award of attorney's fees to a person shall not exceed the amount of reasonable attorney's fees incurred by the city in that action or proceeding.

 

In opposition, Conner argues that (1) Plaintiff has failed to establish entitlement to fees because it failed to make an election at the commencement of the action for recovery of attorney’s fees in compliance with Municipal Code section 8.28.250, (2) any fee award must be reduced and apportioned to reflect that Rumford and not Conner was responsible for the conduct and litigation that generated the fees, and (3) the Court should consider Conner’s limited, fixed means, disability, and caretaker status. (Opp., p. 2:25-28, 4:1-7, 6:6-15.)

 

Here, Plaintiff the Court finds that Plaintiff may recover its reasonable attorney’s fees and costs in the underlying action. As to Conner’s first argument, Plaintiff’s complaint satisfies the requirement of Municipal Code section 8.28.250(A)(1), as it states multiple times: “The City hereby elects to seek recovery and is entitled to an award of the City's reasonable attorneys' fees incurred in this matter, as authorized pursuant to LMC section 8.28.250, which provides that the City may recover reasonable attorneys' fees in any action to abate or cause the abatement of a public nuisance.” (Compl., ¶¶ 35, 43, p. 14:10.)

 

As to Conner’s second and third arguments, like Plaintiff notes in its reply, the Court has already considered and rejected most of these same defenses to liability. (See Court’s Ruling and Order dated 2/24/26, p. 2-3.) The Court’s March 12, 2026 Order and Final Judgment for Permanent Injunction made the express finding that “as record owner of the Subject Property, Defendant Lynne Conner is legally responsible for the conditions that have existed and persisted on her property.” (Judgment, ¶ 1.2.) The Court found that both “Defendants Lynne Conner and Tony Rumford have hosted, operated, permitted, allowed, leased, and/or maintained loud, chaotic, and disruptive commercial parties to occur at the Subject Property. (Judgment, ¶ 1.6.) The Court reiterated Conner’s liability for the nuisance in finding that “the Subject Property has been and continues to be maintained as a public nuisance and that both Defendants Lynne Conner and Tony Rumford…are responsible for that nuisance.” (Judgment, ¶ 2.1.) Furthermore, while the Court recognizes that Conner may be facing financial difficulties and insolvency, the Court declines to reduce any award of attorney’s fees based on Plaintiff’s financial hardship.

 

However, while the Court found that Conner is legally liable as the owner of the Property who was on notice of the nuisance conditions, the Court made no finding as to whether Conner should be liable for Plaintiff’s attorney’s fees and costs. (Court’s Ruling and Order dated 2/24/26, p.4 [“the Court has not yet resolved whether Conner should be liable for City’s attorneys’ fees”].) Thus, the Court will consider Plaintiff’s arguments that she should not be jointly liable for Plaintiff’s attorney’s fees and costs and that the Court should exercise its discretion and apportion the fees according to relative fault.

 

It is true that the general rule in California, as argued by Plaintiff on reply, is that where there are multiple non-prevailing parties, those parties are typically jointly and severally liable for an attorney’s fee award. (See Walker v. Ticor Tile Co. of California (2012) 204 Cal.App.4th 363, 374.) However, trial courts have discretion to apportion an award for attorney’s fees based upon relative culpability of the defendants, and failure to do so may constitute an abuse of discretion when a party requests equitable apportionment based on varying degrees of liability shared amongst the liable parties and the evidence supports an equitable apportionment. (Gorman v. Tassajara Development Corp. (2009) 178 Cal.App.4th 44, 97-98 [“California law has recognized, in different contexts, that trial courts have discretion not only in setting the amount of an award of attorney fees, but in allocating the award among various defendants based on their relative culpability. [Citations.]”]; See also Feminist Women’s Health Center v. Blythe (1995) 32 Cal.App.4th 1641, 1672, n. 7; El Escorial Owners' Assn. v. DLC Plastering, Inc. (2007) 154 Cal.App.4th 1337, 1345.)

 

Here, the evidence supports an equitable allocation of liability for attorney’s fees and costs which diverts from the general joint and several liability rule. While the Court necessarily found Conner legally liable for the nuisance and named her as a party subject to the permanent injunction, this was due to her legal ownership of the Property and the Court’s finding that she had notice of the nuisance conditions. However, this does not necessarily warrant a finding of joint liability and several liability for the entirety o fPlaintiff’s attorney’s fees. While Conner as owner of the Property shares some responsibility for the continuation of the conditions after she was on notice, Conner was not residing on the Property, did not directly cause the existence of the nuisance conditions, and the record and billing records provided by Plaintiff establish that Conner was not responsible for much of the underlying litigation in an equal capacity as Rumford.

 

The Defendants’ respective litigation activity cannot be said to have equally contributed to Plaintiff’s fees and costs. Conner’s litigation activity in this case included (1) opposing Plaintiff’s ex parte application for a TRO, (2) filing an answer and a subsequent verified answer, (3) agreeing to try the matter via declaration rather than a bench trial and filing one declaration in support, and (4) opposing the present motion for attorney’s fees.

 

Rumford’s litigation activity included: (1) opposing Plaintiff’s ex parte application and filing numerous documents regarding his sovereign citizenship, (2) filing a motion to prove averment of jurisdiction, (3) filing a special motion to strike (anti-SLAPP) against Plaintiff, (4) filing an appeal, and (5) attempting to remove the case to federal court, then subsequently defaulting. Rumford also admits to his predominate, if not sole, liability in causing the nuisance even despite his estrangement from Conner, stating “Lynne Conner is the owner of the property and should be dismissed from this lawsuit because she is not in control of my actions and has NO Knowledge of the activities that occur in this house. Defendant Conner does not reside at the property located at 4207 W. Avenue J-13, Lancaster, CA 93536.” (Rumford Decl. dated 8/30/24, ¶ 4.)

 

Plaintiff filed numerous declarations from members of the community in advance of the hearing of the matter via declarations, in which members of the community attribute ownership of the Property, but not fault, to Conner. For example, the declarations state, in relevant part: (1) “Mr. Rumford held a few large outdoor parties at the Subject Property…Rumford either began holding outdoor parties at the Subject Property or rented all or part of the Subject Property to third persons for them to hold parties…Rumford continued to hold these loud outdoor parties…Rumford continued to hold large parties…Rumford’s conduct at the Subject Property has had a negative impact on our neighborhood…Rumford’s regular, repeated, disruptive parties…” (Samaan Decl., ¶¶ 2-5, 25, 29); (2) “[The Property] is owned by Defendant Lynne Conner and is occupied by Defendant Tony Rumford…For the last three years, Mr. Rumford has held parties at the Subject Property…Mr. Rumford had a party at the Subject Property that was so loud enough that I called the police.” (Fadoul Decl., ¶¶ 2-3); (3) “I am concerned that Mr. Rumford and his activities have had and will continue to have a detrimental impact on the value of our property…my quality of life, and that of my wife and our infant baby will continue to be irreparably disrupted unless the Court issues an Order commanding Mr. Rumford to comply with the law.” (Botros Decl., ¶¶ 2-5, 29); (4) “Rumford held a party that went on until 1-2 am in the morning.” (Hobart Decl., ¶ 4); (5) “Defendant Rumford came to the Lancaster Police Department to give me written notice that he was having a party at the Subject Property …he was expecting 50 people.” (Katz Decl., ¶ 11.)

 

Not a single one of these declarations speaks to any wrongdoing of Defendant Conner. While the Court named Conner as an individual subject to the permanent injunction because of her ownership of the Property, the Court finds that Conner engaged in significantly less wrongdoing that Rumford, that she did not engage in frivolous or aggressive litigation to any degree which unnecessarily increased Plaintiff’s attorney’s fees, and that joint apportionment for attorney’s fees under these circumstances would be inherently inequitable to Conner.

 

The Court notes that it is unable to attribute each individual billing charge to either Conner or Rumford, as many of the billing statements are naturally vague as to what work was done as to which Defendant. There is no exact measure by which the Court can allocate the billings to one Defendant or the other. Thus, the Court assigns liability for the attorney’s fees based on reasonable and appropriate degree of fault. Conner’s responsibility for the attorney’s fees and costs shall be limited to 25% of the total award.  Rumford shall remain jointly and severally liable for the entirety of the award. ,.

 

Accordingly, Plaintiff, as prevailing party, may recover its reasonable attorney’s fees and costs incurred in this action, and liability for said fees will be equitably allocated amongst the Defendants.

 

b.     Reasonableness of Fees and Costs Sought

 

As stated above, the lodestar method is the appropriate way to calculate the reasonable attorney’s fees and costs Plaintiff’s counsel may recover.

 

i.                Reasonableness of Hourly Rates

 

Plaintiff’s counsel Dapeer, Rosenblit & Litvak, LLP (DRL) charge $245 per hour for attorney work, regardless of attorney, and $195 per hour for paralegal work.

 

Conner does not argue that any of these rates are unreasonable or should be reduced. As the issue of rate reasonableness is uncontested, the Court accepts the rates for the purposes of this motion. This ruling should not be construed by any other court that this Court has found, after a disputed adjudication, that Plaintiff’s counsel’s rates are reasonable.

 

Accordingly, the rates requested for the billing attorneys and paralegals are accepted as requested.

 

ii.              Reasonableness of Hours Billed

 

Plaintiff’s counsel argues it reasonably spent 291.3 hours on this case and provides detailed billing records in support of its request. (Motion, p. 3:19; Litvak Decl., Ex. A.)

 

Where a party submits detailed billing records, they "are entitled to credence in the absence of a clear indication the records are erroneous." (Horsford, supra, 132 Cal.App.4th at p. 396.) On presentation of a detailed fee bill, accompanied by a sworn affidavit as to the accuracy of the fee bill, the bill is presumed credible, and the court must use these records as a starting point for its lodestar determination. (Id at 396-97.)

 

Pre-litigation attorney's fees are recoverable where the activity contributed to the success of the litigation. (See Hogar v. Community Development Comm. of City of Escondido (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 1358, 1370 [allowing pre-litigation fees under Civil Code section 1021.5 because the activity directly contributed to the litigation].) 

 

In opposition, Conner argues the fee request contains numerous inconsistencies in the fees requested, the billing statements provided are block-billed, vaguely coded as “City Civil/Appeals”, the matter was overstaffed, and the work billed for includes work unrelated to the nuisance claim at issue. (Opp., p. 7:1-5, 8:7-12, 8:17-21, 9:5-11.)

 

Here, Plaintiff submits detailed billing records detailing the time spent on various tasks throughout the commencement of the present action. (Litvak Decl., Ex. A.) While Conner generally objects as to the coding of these entries or the number of timekeepers on the matter, Conner does not point to a single duplicative entry. The Court also notes that despite the coding of the entries, the individual entries are seemingly appropriately redacted pursuant to attorney-client and work-product privilege, and the Court can still discern with reasonable specificity the general nature of the work to determine whether the entries are reasonable. The Court also acknowledges Conner’s objection to the billing entries related to review of the documents in her dissolution case. However, the Court does not find that this work is facially unreasonable, as property is often divided or sold amidst separation or divorce, which would likely be reflected in the Court’s docket. Furthermore, the Court notes the inconsistencies in the amount of fees and costs requested in the moving documents, but Litvak clarifies that his original declaration in support of the motion contains a clerical error, and confirms the total requested amount is $71,921.93, which is what is requested in the Notice of Motion. (Litvak Reply Decl., ¶ 10.) Accordingly, the Court will start with Plaintiff’s request for $67,471 in its lodestar calculation.

 

Here, absent any evidence to the contrary, the Court finds most of Plaintiff’s billing reasonable.

 

However, the Court notes regular billing pertaining to the federal case, amounting to 9.9 hours of work after the date the case was removed and remanded, totaling $2,227.50. These fees are not recoverable in this action and are stricken in their entirety.

 

Further, while calendaring dates, properly recording and saving client files and Court documents, and coordinating appearances by counsel are important and necessary tasks in litigation, these tasks are secretarial in nature and do not warrant attorney, or even paralegal, billing. As the United States Supreme Court has held: “Of course, purely clerical or secretarial tasks should not be billed at a paralegal rate, regardless of who performs them. What the court in Johnson v. Georgia Highway Express, Inc., 488 F. 2d 714, 717 (1974), said in regard to the work of attorneys is applicable by analogy to paralegals: ‘It is appropriate to distinguish between legal work, in the strict sense, and investigation, clerical work, compilation of facts and statistics and other work which can often be accomplished by non-lawyers but which a lawyer may do because he has no other help available. Such non-legal work may command a lesser rate. Its dollar value is not enhanced just because a lawyer does it.’” (Missouri v. Jenkins (1989) 491 U.S. 274, 288 n.10.) Thus, while the Court finds the time spent on this task to be reasonable absent evidence to the contrary, billing these tasks as attorney or paralegal fees is unwarranted.

 

Therefore, the Court strikes 2.4 hours of work, totaling $376.00, for calendaring deadlines throughout the case.

 

Accordingly, Plaintiff is awarded reasonable attorney’s fees in the amount of $64,867.50 for approximately 279 hours of work.

 

iii.             Reasonableness of Costs

 

Plaintiff states it seeks a total of $4,450.93 in costs, consisting of obtaining documents, filing fees, service of process efforts, WestLaw and LexisNexis access, DataTree access, photocopies, postage, and Pacer data charges. (Litvak Decl., ¶ 26, Ex. 1; Litvak Reply Decl., ¶ 11, Ex. A.) Plaintiff’s Memorandum of Costs seeks only a total of $2,480.84 for filing and motion fees ($293.94) and service of process ($2,186.90).  

 

"A prevailing party who claims costs must serve and file a memorandum of costs within 15 days after the date of service of the notice of entry of judgment or dismissal by the clerk under Code of Civil Procedure section 664.5 or the date of service of written notice of entry of judgment or dismissal, or within 180 days after entry of judgment, whichever is first. The memorandum of costs must be verified by a statement of the party, attorney, or agent that to the best of his or her knowledge the items of cost are correct and were necessarily incurred in the case." (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1700(a)(1).)

 

Here, Plaintiff has provided a verified memorandum of costs detailing the nature of the costs incurred and the total amount requested. (Cost Memo., p. 1.) None of the costs requested appear facially invalid and Conner has provided no evidence contradicting the reasonableness or necessity of the filing and service costs. However, as Conner points out, the additional costs sought as detailed in Plaintiff’s billing records are not included on the Memorandum of Costs and are not recoverable, including postage, investigatory efforts, obtaining documentation, photocopies, and research fees. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (b).)

 

Plaintiff’s request for costs is GRANTED in the reduced amount of $2,480.84 as requested on the Memorandum of Costs.

 

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s request for fees and costs is GRANTED in the total amount of $67,348.34.

   II.          Preliminary Procedural Issues

 

Meet and Confer – A motion for protective order must be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2017.020, subd. (a).) Here, Defendant has satisfied this burden, as Defense counsel Jonquil L. Whitehead asserts meet and confer efforts with Plaintiff’s counsel were made. (Whitehead Decl., ¶ 5, Ex. B.)

 III.          Legal Standard

 

Standard for Protective Orders – Generally, California encourages broad and liberal civil discovery in litigation, permitting discovery of any matter not privileged that is relevant to the subject matter of the action. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2017.010.) Any party may obtain discovery by taking the oral deposition of any person. (Id., § 2025.010.) When the deponent named is not a natural person, the deposition notice shall describe with reasonable particularity the matters on which examination is requested, and the deponent shall designate and produce at the deposition its officers, directors, managing agents, employees, or agents most qualified to testify on its behalf as to those matters to the extent of any information known or reasonably available to the deponent. (Id., § 2025.230.) Generally, the service of a deposition notice is effective to require any deponent who is an officer, director, managing agent, or employee of a party to attend and testify. (Id., § 2025.280, subd. (a).)

Notwithstanding this general preference for broad discovery, before a deposition, any party, deponent, or affected organization may move for a protective order. (Id., § 2025.420, subd. (a).) The court may make an order to protect any party, deponent, or organization from unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, oppression, undue burden, or expense if good cause is shown by the moving party. (Id., § 2025.420, subd. (b).) “The court shall limit the scope of discovery if it determines that the burden, expense, or intrusiveness of that discovery clearly outweighs the likelihood that the information sought will lead to the discovery of admissible evidence. The court may make this determination pursuant to a motion for protective order by a party or other affected person.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2017.020, subd. (a).) “The issuance and formulation of protective orders are to a large extent discretionary.” (Nativi v. Deutsche Bank National Trust Co. (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 261, 316.) “[T]he burden is on the party seeking the protective order to show good cause for whatever order is sought.” (Id. at 318.)

 

-----

 

 IV.          Discussion

 

Application – Defendant moves for a protective order to preclude the deposition of Deborah Cockerham-Slaughter (Slaughter) on the grounds that the information and testimony that Slaughter may provide is privileged, confidential, and the notice of taking her deposition has created unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, and oppression. (Motion, p. 2:4-6.)

 

Slaughter is Defendant’s Director of Risk Management, and Defendant avers that she has no personal knowledge of the incident at issue. (Motion, p. 3:6-9.) Plaintiff has represented that she seeks to take Slaughter’s deposition solely based on her execution of the verification pages of Defendant’s written discovery responses, but Defendants note that the verifications state “matters stated in the foregoing document are not all within my personal knowledge.” (Motion, p. 3:9-13; Whitehead Decl., ¶ 7, Ex. D.) Defendant argues Slaughter’s involvement in this case is limited to her role as Risk Manager in assisting counsel with obtaining information related to this claim, which necessarily implicates information protected by the attorney-client privilege and attorney work-product doctrine per Scripps v. Superior Court (2003) 109 Cal.App.4th 59. (Motion, p. 3:13-16.) Defendant argues that any knowledge known by Slaughter is derived exclusively from privileged material, including communications made in anticipation of litigation and information transmitted for assisting counsel in providing legal advice, and therefore, good causes exists for issuance of a protective order to preclude Slaughter’s deposition altogether. (Motion, p. 5:2-4.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues she does not seek to discover any privileged communications between Defendant and its counsel, nor mental impressions or legal opinions, and only seeks testimony regarding Defendant’s factual investigation into Plaintiff’s claims, including the investigation undertaken in response to Plaintiff’s written discovery. (Opp., p. 2:7-10.) Plaintiff further argues that Defendant has failed to establish good cause for precluding the deposition in its entirety, as it fails to establish that appearance would result in unwarranted annoyance, oppression, or undue burden beyond that which is routinely experienced by witnesses in civil litigation, and Defendant merely assumes that privileged questions may be asked, which is not sufficient to warrant a blanket protective order. (Opp., p. 3:13-21.) Plaintiff further asserts that she has received no response identifying the individual who escorted her back into the hospital the date of the incident, and is entitled to discover who investigated the issue, what records were reviewed, whether surveillance footage was reviewed, whether personnel were interviewed, whether other employees were contacted, when those steps occurred, and why the employee has not been identified. (Opp., p. 4:7-14.)

 

Here, the Court finds good cause to limit the scope of the discovery, but declines to issue a blanket protective order precluding the taking of Slaughter’s deposition altogether.

 

California Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.250, subdivision (a) provides that the party to whom the demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling is directed shall sign the response under oath unless the response contains only objections.

 

Section 2031.250, subdivision (b) establishes that if that party is a public or private corporation or a partnership or association or governmental agency, one of its officers or agents shall sign the response under oath on behalf of that party.

 

While the moving party here is not claiming that Slaughter is an attorney, it is claiming that she obtained all information by virtue of attorney-client communications or at the direction of attorneys, which thus could reveal attorney work product.

 

 Cal. Code Civ. Proc. § 2031.250(b) addresses in part the problem of a verifier whose knowledge of discovery responses may otherwise be protected attorney-client communication or reveal attorney-work product::

 

If that party is a public or private corporation or a partnership or association or governmental agency, one of its officers or agents shall sign the response under oath on behalf of that party. If the officer or agent signing the response on behalf of that party is an attorney acting in that capacity for a party, that party waives any lawyer-client privilege and any protection for work product under Chapter 4 (commencing with Section 2018.010) during any subsequent discovery from that attorney concerning the identity of the sources of the information contained in the response.

 

Thus, to the extent Slaughter’s knowledge rests on communications from attorneys, she still could be deposed, just as an attorney verifier could, regarding the “identity of the sources of the information contained in the response.” “[W]hen an individual verifies discovery responses, further discovery can be directed to that individual to determine the sources for the initial responses.” (Melendrez v. Superior Court (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 1343, 1352; see also Code Civ. Proc., § 2017.010 [scope of discovery]; Lopez v. Watchtower Bible & Tract Society of New York, Inc. (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 566, 590-591 [“relevance” in the context of discovery is broad; for discovery purposes, information is relevant if it might reasonably assist a party in evaluating the case, preparing for trial, or facilitating settlement].)

 

For these reasons, as a verifier, Slaughter can be deposed on the identity of the sources for the discovery responses. There is otherwise no waiver of privilege, and Defendant may appropriately object on privilege grounds in the event that privilege is implicated during the deposition.

 

Accordingly, the Motion for Protective Order is GRANTED in part. Plaintiff may depose Slaughter to the extent that the deposition adheres to statues and procedures governing discovery (i.e., determine the identity of the sources of information for the responses she verified).  Any inquiry into the nature and substance of any attorney-client communications or attorney work product is prohibited, and Defendant may object when appropriate if any answers would compromise attorney-client or work product privilege.

 III.          Legal Standard

 

Standard for Motion for Attorney’s Fees, Costs, and Expenses – Under Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d), the prevailing buyer under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act is entitled to fees that were reasonably incurred: “If the buyer prevails under this section, the buyer shall be allowed by the Court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.” (Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).) The trial court has an obligation to award only those attorneys’ fees that are reasonable. (See PLCM Group v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095-96; see also Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132.) A prevailing party includes the party with a net monetary recovery in settlement of the case. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1032, subd. (a)(4).) 

 

The lodestar method is the primary method for determining a reasonable attorney fee award under section 1794, subdivision (d). (Robertson v. Fleetwood Travel Trailers of California, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 785, 818-19.) “A trial court assessing attorney fees begins with a touchstone or lodestar figure, based on the careful compilation of the time spent and reasonable hourly compensation of each attorney involved in the presentation of the case.” (Christian Research Institute v. Alnor (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 1315, 1321 [internal quotations omitted].)  “The reasonableness of attorney fees is within the discretion of the trial court, to be determined from a consideration of such factors as the nature of the litigation, the complexity of the issues, the experience an expertise of counsel and the amount of time involved. [Citation.] The court may also consider whether the amount requested is based upon unnecessary or duplicative work.” (Wilkerson v. Sullivan (2002) 99 Cal.App.4th 443, 448 (citations omitted).) “The basis for the trial court’s calculation must be the actual hours counsel has devoted to the case, less those that result from inefficient or duplicative use of time.” (Horsford v. Board of Trustees Of California State University (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 359, 395.) “The law is clear, however, that¿an award of attorney fees may be based on counsel’s declarations, without production of detailed time records.” (Raining Data Corp. v. Barrenechea (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 1363, 1375.)

 

In setting the hourly rate for an attorney fees award, courts are entitled to consider the rate of “fees customarily charged by that attorney and others in the community for similar work.” (Bihun v. AT&T Information Systems, Inc. (1993) 13 Cal. App. 4th 976, 997 [affirming rate of $450 per hour], overruled on other grounds by Lakin v. Watkins Associated Indus. (1993) 6 Cal. 4th 644, 664; see also Heritage Pacific Financial, LLC v. Monroy (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 972, 1009 [“[R]ate determinations in other cases, particularly those setting a rate for the plaintiffs’ attorney, are satisfactory evidence of the prevailing market rate.”].)

Here, there is no dispute that Plaintiff is the prevailing party to this litigation, nor that he is entitled to recover reasonable attorney’s fees, costs, and expenses incurred in litigation. (Opp., p. 1:27-28.) Where an accepted settlement offer designates the Plaintiff as the prevailing party and specifically delegated determination of fees to the Court, it thereby entitles the Plaintiff to bring the subject motion. (Engle v. Copenbarger & Copenbarger, LLP (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 165, 168-69.)

 

b.     Reasonableness of Attorney’s Fees Amount

The prevailing party has the burden of showing that the requested attorney fees were "reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation, and were reasonable in amount." (Robertson, supra, 144 Cal.App.4th at 817.) The party seeking attorney fees "'is not necessarily entitled to compensation for the value of attorney services according to [his] own notion or to the full extent claimed by [him].'" (Levy v. Toyota Motor Sales, USA, Inc. (1992) 4 Cal.App.4th 807, 816.) Therefore, if the "time expended or the monetary charge being made for the time expended are not reasonable under all the circumstances, then the court must take this into account and award attorney fees in a lesser amount." (Nightingale v. Hyundai Motor America (1994) 31 Cal.App.4th 99, 104.)

 

The detailed billing records Plaintiff has submitted to validate the billed hours for which Plaintiff requests reimbursement "are entitled to credence in the absence of a clear indication the records are erroneous." (Horsford, supra, 132 Cal.App.4th at p. 396.) On presentation of a detailed fee bill, accompanied by a sworn affidavit as to the accuracy of the fee bill, the bill is presumed credible, and the court must use these records as a starting point for its lodestar determination. (Id at 396-97.)

 

Here, the Court has reviewed the entire file in this matter (which is quite brief) and finds much of Plaintiff’s counsel’s billing to not be reasonable. reasonable. For instance, in the 56-paragraph complaint, there are perhaps only three paragraphs (nos. 7, 20, and 26) that contain any information regarding the vehicle at issue, and even those paragraphs do not more than identify the vehicle, when it was sold, and when problems arose. The Complaint does not even describe what’s wrong with the vehicle. Every allegation in the Complaint is generic.

1.     Pre-litigation tasks – 3.1 hours ($785.00) billed on April 24 and May 5, 2025 – reduce by 2.1 hours ($445) to 1 hour.

2.     Review of 18 pages of client documentation – 1.4 hours ($725) billed by two attorneys on June 16 and July 18, 2025 – reduce by 1 hour ($500) to 0.4 hours.

3.     Preparation of complaint – 4.2 hours ($1,275) billed July 2, 18, and September 23, 30, 2025 – reduce by 3.2 hours ($1,025) to 1.0 hour.

4.     Fee motion– 6.4 hours ($3,200) billed on April 2, 2026 – reduce fee motion fees by 3.4 ($1,700) hours to 3 hours.

 

The Court does not find it appropriate to reduce or strike billing entries for client communication. Attorneys have a legal and ethical obligation to promptly provide their clients with updates and advise them on the progress of their case, and the Court finds entries related to client communication reasonable, necessary, and appropriate. The Court similarly declines to strike or reduce time spent communicating with Defense counsel, as it appears reasonable, necessary, and Defendant has submitted no evidence refuting the time actually spent in said correspondence. The Court also finds 3.5 hours of anticipated time for reviewing Defendant’s opposition, preparing a reply, and preparing for and attending the hearing reasonable.

 

The Court also agrees with Defendant’s characterization of many of the paralegal and case manager charges as secretarial or clerical in nature, and notes that many of these entries do not constitute substantive legal work. While calendaring dates, properly recording and saving client files and Court documents, and coordinating appearances by counsel are important and necessary tasks in litigation, these tasks are secretarial in nature and do not warrant attorney, or even paralegal, billing. As the United States Supreme Court has held: “Of course, purely clerical or secretarial tasks should not be billed at a paralegal rate, regardless of who performs them. What the court in Johnson v. Georgia Highway Express, Inc., 488 F. 2d 714, 717 (1974), said in regard to the work of attorneys is applicable by analogy to paralegals: ‘It is appropriate to distinguish between legal work, in the strict sense, and investigation, clerical work, compilation of facts and statistics and other work which can often be accomplished by non-lawyers but which a lawyer may do because he has no other help available. Such non-legal work may command a lesser rate. Its dollar value is not enhanced just because a lawyer does it.’” (Missouri v. Jenkins (1989) 491 U.S. 274, 288 n.10.) Thus, while the Court finds the time spent on this task to be reasonable absent evidence to the contrary, billing these tasks as attorney or paralegal fees is unwarranted.

 

Furthermore, staffing a routine, simple Song-Beverly action with over ten timekeepers may result in inefficiency, unreasonable billing, duplicative work, and excessive review. This is not always the case, however, the detailed billing records provided by Plaintiff identify excessive time for the tasks completed, including large amounts of time billed for internal correspondence, support staff meetings and communications with the attorneys, and excessive review of the case during internal meetings. Based on the nature of these entries derived from the internal description of the work done, the Court finds that the staffing likely contributed to excessive time billed for correspondence and review of the client’s file.

c.      Fee Multiplier

 

Plaintiff requests a fee multiplier of 1.3 to the lodestar amount given the result obtained, the delay in payment, and the risk associated with contingency-based representation. (Motion, p. 18:13-22.)

 

Once the Court has determined¿an appropriate lodestar¿figure, the court may then determine whether that figure should be adjusted with a positive or negative multiplier. (Graham v. DaimlerChrysler Corp.¿(2005) 34 Cal.4th 553, 582.) Whether a multiplier or negative multiple is appropriate depends on several factors, including (1) the risks presented by the litigation; (2) the novelty and difficulty of the legal and factual issues involved; (3) the results obtained on behalf of the plaintiff; (4) the skill exhibited by counsel; (5) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys; and (6) the contingent nature of the fee award based on the uncertainty of prevailing on the merits and of establishing eligibility for the award. (Consumer Privacy Cases¿(2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 545, 556; Robertson, supra, 144 Cal.App.4th at 819.) “The ‘results obtained’ factor can properly be used to enhance a lodestar calculation where [1] an exceptional effort produced [2] an exceptional benefit.”¿(Graham supra, 34 Cal.4th at 582.)¿ 

 

In opposition, Defendant argues that this case does not warrant a fee multiplier because there was nothing novel, difficult, or unique about the present case or its outcome, as it is a standard Song-Bevely action in which the issues presented are virtually identical case to case, the case spanned only six months and presented no unusual or complex issues, there was no unique skill required to obtain Plaintiff’s outcome, and the outcome was no more extraordinary than other similar Song-Beverly actions. (Opp., p. 7:8-23.)

 

The Court finds that a multiplier is not warranted in this case. While Plaintiff received an excellent result in settlement, and while Plaintiff’s counsel took this case on contingency, this is a standard Song-Beverly action. While the Court acknowledges that there is inherent risk in taking a case on contingency, the Court finds the risk in this action to be minimal considering a prevailing buyer in a Song-Beverly action is legally entitled to recovery of all reasonable and necessary attorney’s fees, costs, and expenses incurred pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1794, subdivision (d). Based on the relatively sparse record, the similarly short time span of the litigation, the lack of any depositions or extraordinary discovery efforts, and the moving and opposition papers filed for the present motion, the Court does not find the novelty or difficulty of the legal and factual issues involved, nor the skill required of Plaintiff’s counsel in this case, rising to the level of warranting a multiplier.

 

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s request for a fee multiplier is DENIED.

 

d.     Costs and Expenses

 

Plaintiff requests costs and expenses totaling $896.28. (Notice of Motion, p. 2:16; Memorandum of Costs.) The costs and expenses requested include filing and motion fees, fees for hosting electronic documents, service of process, and filing and service fees. (Memo. Of Costs.) Plaintiff also attaches invoices from One Legal to substantiate the costs requested, the dates incurred, and the nature of the charge. (See Memo. of Costs Attachments.)

 

"Except as otherwise expressly provided by statute, a prevailing party is entitled as a matter of right to recover costs in any action or proceeding." (Code Civ. Proc., § 1032, subd. (b).) "Allowable costs shall be reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation rather than merely convenient or beneficial to its preparation." (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (c)(2).) "If the items appearing in a cost bill appear to be proper charges, the burden is on the party seeking to tax costs to show that they were not reasonable or necessary." (Ladas v. California State Auto. Assn. (1993) 19 Cal. App. 4th 761, 774.) "On the other hand, if the items are properly objected to, they are put in issue and the burden of proof is on the party claiming them as costs." (Ibid.)

 

"A prevailing party who claims costs must serve and file a memorandum of costs within 15 days after the date of service of the notice of entry of judgment or dismissal by the clerk under Code of Civil Procedure section 664.5 or the date of service of written notice of entry of judgment or dismissal, or within 180 days after entry of judgment, whichever is first. The memorandum of costs must be verified by a statement of the party, attorney, or agent that to the best of his or her knowledge the items of cost are correct and were necessarily incurred in the case." (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1700(a)(1).) “Any notice of motion to strike or tax costs must be served and filed 15 days after service of the cost memorandum.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1700(b)(1).)

 

In opposition to Plaintiff’s request, Defendant requests that the Court reduce the costs in the amount of $226.03 on the grounds that Plaintiff has provided no explanation or evidence for the costs requested to substantiate the request and that certain of the costs, namely One Legal’s convenience fees, e-filing charges, and disbursement administration charges, were incurred solely for the convenience of Plaintiff, which is excluded under Code of Civil Procedure, section 1033.5, subdivision (a). (Opp., p. 9:15-20.)

 

To start, the Court must permit the prevailing buyer to recover such costs and expenses if reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of the action. (Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).) This statutory language has been expressly found by courts to exceed the scope of Section 1033.5, subdivision (a). (Jensen v. BMW of North America, Inc. (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 112, 137-138 (overruled in part on other grounds in Rodriguez v. FCA US LLC (2024) 17 Cal.5th 189) [a prevailing buyer-plaintiff under the Song-Beverly Act may recover fees and expenses where otherwise prohibited under Section 1033.5 subdivision (b), as they are covered under "costs and expenses" in California Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d)].)  The party filing a memorandum of costs is not required to attach any supporting documents. (Jones v. Dumrichob (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 1258, 1267.) “[A]¿verified memorandum of costs is prima facie evidence of the propriety of the items listed on it, and the burden is on the party challenging these costs to¿demonstrate¿that they were not reasonable or necessary.”¿(Bender v. County of Los Angeles (2013) 217 Cal.App.4th 968, 989, internal citations omitted; see also Benach v. County of Los Angeles (2007) 149 Cal.App.4th 836, 855; Nelson v. Anderson (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 111, 131-132 ["trial court erred in requiring additional proof from” the party claiming costs when party opposing costs bore the burden of proving the cost unnecessary or unreasonable]; Santantonio v. Westinghouse Broadcasting Co. (1994) 25 Cal.App.4th 102, 116, 121 [“where a party shows a prima facie entitlement to costs, the burden is on an objector to prove the costs should be disallowed”].) The burden is on the party opposing the memorandum to either (a) show that the request is facially improper or (b) produce some evidence to show that the request is improper. (Bender, supra, 217 Cal.App.4th at 989.)

 

Here, the burden is on Defendant to show that the costs included on Plaintiff’s Memorandums of Costs are unreasonable or unnecessary. Defendant has provided no evidence that any of these fees or costs are unreasonable. Further, while Defendant objects to certain fees, such as convenience and e-filing fees, the Court finds no reason to deny the requested relief, as Section 1794, subdivision (d) is far more expansive than Section 1033.5, attorneys are required to e-file documents, and service providers often include standard charges such as the ones Defendant takes issue with here for processing payments and other administrative expenses.

 

Defendant is also incorrect that further supporting documentation is needed, and Defendant fails to point to a single entry which is neither allowable nor reasonably necessary. In reviewing Plaintiff’s Memorandum of Costs, none of the costs or expenses for which Plaintiff seeks reimbursement are facially improper.

 

In the absence of evidence or argument to the contrary, the Court finds that Plaintiff’s request of $896.28 in costs is reasonable and recoverable.
