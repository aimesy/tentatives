[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: MOOTION TO SEAL EXHIBITS TO DECLARATION OF X. MORROW IN SUPPORT OF PETITION TO VACATE ARBITRATION AWARD

On January 27, 2026, plaintiff Kening Xu (“Xu”) filed a motion to seal certain documents submitted in support of his motion for vacatur, including Exhibits A, B, C, F, H, I, J, K, L, N, O, P, and Q to the Declaration of Xinlin Li Morrow (“Morrow Declaration”). The motion was brought in anticipation of defendant Charles Chunhua Huang’s (“Huang”) contention that the materials are confidential and require sealing.

The Court previously identified two procedural issues with Xu’s motion to seal: (1) Xu failed to lodge a complete and unredacted version of the Morrow Declaration and (2) the redactions were not narrowly tailored. These issues have since been rectified; a complete and unredacted version of the Morrow Declaration was lodged with the Court and the proposed redactions are appropriately limited to information regarding Xu’s PCI shares.

“The court may order that a record be filed under seal only if it expressly finds facts that establish: (1) There exists an overriding interest that overcomes the right of public access to the record; (2) The overriding interest supports sealing the record; (3) A substantial probability exists that the overriding interest will be prejudiced if the record is not sealed; (4) The proposed sealing is narrowly tailored; and (5)¿No less restrictive means exist to achieve the overriding interest. (CRC 2.550(d).) “An order sealing the record must: (A) Specifically state the facts that support the findings; and¿ (B) Direct the sealing of only those documents and pages, or, if reasonably practicable, portions of those documents and pages, that contain the material that needs to be placed under seal. All other portions of each document or page must be included in the public file.” (CRC 2.550(e).)

On March 16, 2026, Huang filed a declaration to fulfill the requirements of CRC 2.550 and 2.551. Huang argues he has a substantial overriding interest in keeping the financial information of Pasaca Capital, Inc. (“PCI”) confidential because PCI is a privately-held company. (Cassidy v. Cal. Bd. of Accountancy (2013) 220 Cal.App.4th 620, 625.) Huang states he wishes to safeguard information in the arbitration records lodged in support of the motion to vacate, specifically, the amount of shares purchased by Xu, the price at which he purchased those shares, and the price at which he later sold them. (Huang Decl., ¶¶ 3-6.)

Therefore, the Court makes the following findings: (1) there exists an overriding interest that overcomes the right of public access to this PCI’s confidential shareholder information in that PCI is a privately-held company whose financial information is confidential and the public interest in this information is low because it was not specifically relied on in reaching the merits of the motion to vacate arbitration.; (2) the overriding interest of PCI’s right of privacy and confidentiality in its financial information supports sealing this portion of the record, (3) the right of privacy and confidentiality will be prejudiced if the documents are not sealed, (4) the sealing order is narrowly tailored, and (5) there are no less restrictive means to protect PCI’s confidential financial information.

Accordingly, the motion to seal page 5, lines 2-3 and 7-8 of the Morrow Declaration, as well as portions of Exhibits C, I, J, K, L, M, N, O, Q and S of the Morrow Declaration is GRANTED.

I.            INTRODUCTION

On June 9, 2026, plaintiff James Matyas (“Plaintiff”) filed this motion for an order imposing evidentiary sanctions on defendants Weaver & Associates, Inc., Ralph Weaver, Matthew Weaver, Denise Weaver, and Dana Dattola (collectively, “Defendants”) by striking their supplemental responses to Plaintiff’s Special Interrogatories (Set One) and Requests for Admission (Set One), deeming Defendants’ original discovery responses binding, precluding inconsistent trial testimony, and awarding Plaintiff attorney and filing fees for this motion.

Defendants filed an opposition brief on June 17, 2026.

Plaintiffs filed a reply brief on June 24, 2026.

On July 1, 2026, the Court continued the hearing to allow the parties to submit supplemental briefs. Supplemental briefs were filed on July 10, 2026.

II.          LEGAL STANDARD

Typically, interrogatories are not preclusive in the way requests for admission are and a party, without leave of court, “may serve an amended interrogatory that contains information subsequently discovered, inadvertently omitted, or mistakenly stated in the initial interrogatory [response].” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.310, subd. (a).) The propounding party of an interrogatory to which an amended answer has been served may move for an order that the initial answer be deemed binding on the responding party for the purpose of the pending action. (Id., subd. (b).) The Court shall grant this motion if all of the following conditions are satisfied:

(1)         The initial failure of the responding party to answer the interrogatory correctly has substantially prejudiced the party who propounded the interrogatory.

 

(2)         The responding party has failed to show substantial justification for the initial answer to that interrogatory.

 

(3)         The prejudice to the propounding party cannot be cured either by a continuance to permit further discovery or by the use of the initial answer under Section 2030.410.

(Id., subd. (c).) The Court shall impose a monetary sanction against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to deem binding an initial answer to an interrogatory, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust. (Id., subd. (d).)

 

III.        DISCUSSION

Plaintiff argues that Defendants should be bound to their initial responses to interrogatories because their amended responses (referred to in briefing as “supplemental” responses) contradicted their initial responses and deposition testimony. Plaintiff also argues that evidentiary sanctions should be imposed because Defendants’ initial responses are also evasive and contradictory, which have impaired Plaintiff’s ability to prepare for trial.

Plaintiff’s motion is DENIED and each individual request is addressed in further detail below. First, the Court notes that Plaintiff failed to meet and confer with Defendants before bringing this motion. This ground is sufficient to deny the motion based on Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.310(b). Second, many of Plaintiff’s complaints about Defendants’ supplemental responses to special interrogatories, upon investigation, do not reveal any purported contradiction in testimony or responses and, even if there were, Plaintiff fails to demonstrate any substantial prejudice or an inability to cure any prejudice through further discovery.

The Court disagrees with Plaintiff’s characterization of Defendants’ actions as obfuscatory or evasive and does not find a “pattern of willful discovery abuse.” The only improper action taken by Defendants is their withdrawal/amendment of admissions without first seeking leave of court. This may warrant monetary sanctions, but not an extreme measure such as evidentiary sanctions such as an order “binding” them to their initial responses. To make such an order would circumvent Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.300, which allows withdrawal or amendment of an admission with leave of court.

A.   Dana Dattola

Response to SROG No. 1 vs. Deposition Testimony: Deny; no substantial prejudice shown. Dattola initially stated that she was employed from 2009 to the present and later testified that she began working for the firm in 2007. Plaintiff does not show how he is substantially prejudiced by this two-year difference or that further discovery would not resolve any questions about any timeline.

Response to SROG No. 5 vs. Supplemental Response to RFA No. 5: Deny. Plaintiff does not demonstrate how this two-year difference resulted in substantial prejudice. Initial response was not an admission, therefore supplemental response is permitted without leave of court.

Response to RFA No. 6 vs. Supplemental Response to RFA No. 6 vs. Deposition Testimony: No showing of substantial prejudice and initial response to RFA No. 6 was not an admission, therefore supplemental response is permitted without leave of court.

Plaintiff claims there is an inconsistency between Dattola’s claim that she did not know of any contingency bonuses attributable to Plaintiff’s accounts, and her interrogatory response identifying specific years in which she knew of contingency bonuses. Defendants argue there is no true contradiction between Dattola was aware of contingency bonuses generally, without knowing whether any were attributable to Plaintiff’s accounts.

IV.        CONCLUSION

Plaintiff’s motion for an order binding Defendants to their initial responses to interrogatories is DENIED. Plaintiff’s motion for evidentiary sanctions is DENIED. The Court strikes Defendants’ supplemental responses to Plaintiff’s RFAs, without prejudice to any future motion seeking leave to withdraw or amend their admissions, as follows:

Denise Weaver: Supplemental Responses to RFA Nos. 4, 10;

Matthew Weaver: Supplemental Response to RFA No. 1;

Ralph Weaver: Supplemental Response to RFA No. 10;

Weaver & Associates, Inc.: Supplemental Responses to RFA Nos. 2, 3, 10, 14.

No sanctions are imposed because some of Defendants’ supplemental responses were improperly served without leave of court and most of Plaintiff’s arguments in support of this motion were unpersuasive. Imposing sanctions on any party in these circumstances would be unjust.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: PLAINTIFF’S MOTION FOR SPOLIATION SANCTIONS

 

Dept. 3

I.            INTRODUCTION

On June 9, 2026, plaintiffs James Matyas and Eugene Robison Company, Inc. dba Oxford Risk Management & Insurance Services (collectively, “Plaintiffs”) filed this motion for an order imposing issue sanctions, monetary sanctions, and an adverse inference jury instruction on defendants Weaver & Associates, Inc., Ralph Weaver, Matthew Weaver, Denise Weaver, and Dana Dattola (collectively, “Defendants”).

Plaintiffs claim that sanctions and an adverse jury instruction are warranted due to “Defendants’ intentional, bad-faith destruction and failure to preserve critical financial, accounting, and database management records that document contingency bonuses paid to Defendants on Plaintiffs’ accounts.” (Notice of Motion, p. 2.) Plaintiffs request the Court: (1) instruct the jury that they may infer the destroyed financial records would have established that Defendants failed to pay Plaintiffs their 50% share of contingency bonuses as mandated by the Management Service Agreement (“MSA”), executed in 1995, (2) order that it has been established that Defendants received contingency bonuses attributable to Plaintiffs’ accounts under the MSA from 1995 to 2021, and (3) grant an award of fees and costs against Defendants and counsel of record for bringing this motion.

Defendants filed an opposition brief on June 17, 2026.

Plaintiffs filed a reply brief on June 24, 2026.

II.          EVIDENTIARY OBJECTIONS

Defendants’ Objection Nos. 1 through 4 to the Declaration of Neva Cirkveni are SUSTAINED as improper opinion and characterization of evidence. The materials objected to also lack foundation and personal knowledge.  

III.        DISCUSSION

Spoilation of evidence in anticipation of, or in response to, a discovery request, is a misuse of discovery, as to which all discovery sanctions are available. (Victor Valley Union High School Dist. v. Superior Court (2023) 91 Cal.App.5th 1121, 1139-1140.)

Plaintiffs argue that Defendants had an obligation to preserve evidence due to the terms of MSA and that Defendants improperly destroyed data and documents in 2007, when Defendants purportedly shredded “years’ worth of underlying physical accounting files, historical financial records, and profit-sharing data.” (Motion, p. 4.) This argument is unpersuasive because any shredding was limited to “routine destruction of client insurance files under the Insurance Code’s ten-year retention rule, and was performed before any dispute [between the parties] existed. (Opp., p. 2.) The deposition testimony attached to Plaintiffs’ counsel’s declaration reflects that contracts such as the MSA, “would not have been in that pile” and that the documents that were shredded were “client-specific documents.” (Cirkveni Decl., Ex. E, 50:18-22.)

Next, Plaintiffs claim that Defendants purposefully destroyed electronic evidence between 2021 and 2023, despite having discovered the MSA in 2017 and knowing that a dispute had arisen over the terms of the MSA by 2019. A party in possession or control of information has a duty to preserve the evidence where the party was objectively aware it would be relevant to anticipated litigation because litigation was reasonably foreseeable and probable, and more than just a potential or distant claim. (Id. at p. 1149.) One remedy for spoliation of evidence is an evidentiary inference that the party destroyed, or rendered unavailable, evidence, because it was unfavorable to that party. (Id. at p. 1139.)

These “data purges” purportedly began on April 15, 2021, when Denise Weaver extracted a “comprehensive backend duplicative of the firm’s historical transactional history”, but the extraction file was subsequently allowed to expire. Plaintiffs also claim that systematic destruction of evidence was underway when Denise Weaver executed: (1) an “Authorized User Reduction Form” on March 17, 2022, which limited access to data management platforms, and (2) a “Nexure Termination Notice Form” on October 24, 2023, which migrated data from Nexsure onto another platform, Applied Epic, without also requesting data extraction.

Last, Plaintiff claims that Defendants are further withholding documents and refusing to “release the active Applied Epic system logs.” (Motion, p. 10.) Plaintiff argues that sanctions should be imposed because Defendants refuse to execute a “standard owner release form required to unlock [the records] for production.” (Motion, p. 10.) However, Defendants argue in their opposition brief that sanctions are unwarranted because defense counsel never received any letter, email, or other communication requesting the execution of a release form for Applied Systems or other custodians of records. Defense counsel declares that Plaintiff only demanded that Defendants stipulate to an adverse-inference instruction, not document production. (Opp., Hayden Decl., ¶¶ 4-7, Ex. 1.)

The Court is unpersuaded that these events warrant something as drastic as an adverse-inference instruction. Plaintiff provides no evidence that the 2021 data extraction file contained any relevant data from 2007 or that the Nexsure platform ever contained historical bonus records from 1995 to 2007. Defendants additionally argue, and the Court agrees, that the 2021 “data extraction”, the 2022 user-license reduction, and the 2023 migration to Applied Epic were ordinary operational decisions about a software platform, not evidence of “willful suppression” of evidence. (Opp., pp. 8-9.) Furthermore, it appears that records are available through Applied Epic but that Plaintiff has not requested Defendants’ cooperation in obtaining it and instead chose to proceed with this motion. Also, Defendants point out that documents predating 2017 have been produced, including materials from 2015-2016, which is inconsistent with the alleged “campaign” of destruction. Sanctions are additionally unwarranted because no court order has been violated. Plaintiff argues in his reply brief that the Court previously ordered Defendants to produce documents, but Plaintiff tellingly does not specify the date on which any purported order was issued. Rather, on January 28, 2026, the Cout ordered Defendants to serve responses to Plaintiffs’ written discovery requests; there is no order for production and thus no court order has been violated.

IV.        CONCLUSION

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s motion for sanctions is DENIED in its entirety.

On June 10, 2026, respondent Mobilitas Insurance Company of Arizona (“Mobilitas”) filed this motion to compel further responses to Form Interrogatories (Set One), Nos. 10.1, 10.3, and 11.1, from claimant/petitioner Naira Ordukhanian (“Ordukhanian”), within 15 days of the date of this hearing.

If a timely motion to compel has been filed, the burden is on the responding party to justify any objection or failure fully to answer the interrogatories. (Coy v. Sup.Ct. (Wolcher) (1962) 58 Cal.2d 210, 220-221; Fairmont Ins. Co. v. Sup.Ct. (Stendell) (2000) 22 Cal.4th 245, 255.)

Ordukhanian did not oppose this timely motion and failed to show why further responses should not be provided. Accordingly, Mobilitas’s motion is GRANTED and Ordukhanian is ordered to serve further responses to Form Interrogatories (Set One) Nos. 10.1, 10.3, and 11.1 within 15 days of the date of this order.
