DEPARTMENT 514 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 20STCP04290    Hearing Date: September 14, 2026    Dept: 514

Seyde v. Skyline Outdoor Media LLC
20STCP04290
Defendant’s Motion to Stay

Tentative Ruling

The Court will call this matter. The Court’s tentative ruling is to deny the motion.

Background

This action was filed in December 2020 and has a very long procedural history, only some of which is summarized here.

In the operative Fifth Amended Complaint (“FAC”), filed on March 16, 2023, Plaintiff David Seyde (“Plaintiff” or “Seyde”) asserts causes of action for: (1) breach of contract; (2) breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing; (3) breach of fiduciary duty; (4) fraud; (5) intentional interference with contractual relations; and (6) aiding and abetting breach of fiduciary duty. The named defendants are Brian Kennedy (“Defendant” or (“Brian”), Drake Kennedy (“Drake”), Regency Outdoor Advertising, Inc. (“Regency”), Skyline Outdoor Media LLC (“Skyline”), and NTGS Acquisition, LLC (“NTGS”). (The first names of Brian and Drake Kennedy are used in this ruling for ease of reference and to avoid confusion; no disrespect or excessive familiarity is intended.)

Seyde alleges (among other things) that he is owed millions of dollars for work that he performed during more than 19 years for Brian, Drake, and entities they owned or controlled. (FAC, ¶¶ 1, 4., 20-29) According to Plaintiff, after years of contentious litigation between Brian, Drake, and various other entities, the parties settled the case and executed a Binding Term Sheet (the “BTS”). (FAC, ¶¶ 3, 30-36, 41, 51, 60 & Exhs. A-D.) Under the terms of the BTS, the assets of various companies were to be sold, and Seyde, a key employee, was to receive between 1 and 1.95 percent of the sales proceeds, plus an additional payment of up to 2 percent for his work to consummate the sale. (FAC, ¶¶ 4, 36, 41, 51, 60 & Exhs. A-D.)

Netflix eventually purchased some of the companies’ assets for $122 million. (FAC, ¶¶ 6, 61-69.) Plaintiff received some of the money due to him, but Brian and Drake worked together to deny Plaintiff other payments. (FAC, ¶ 6, 44, 70-72.) Brian also worked to frustrate further sales. (FAC, ¶¶ 73-77.)

In subsequent litigation between Drake and Brian (Case No. 18STCP02617), a receiver was appointed. (FAC, ¶¶ 78-79.) Drake and Brian bid against each other, but there was no sale to a third party, as required by the BTS; ultimately Brian purchased the assets for less than what a third party would have paid. (FAC, ¶¶ 79-80.) Plaintiff was not paid. (FAC, ¶¶ 80-84.)

Plaintiff asserts the First, Second, and Fourth Causes of Action against Brian, Drake, Regency, and Skyline; the Third Cause of Action against Brian, Drake, and Skyline; and the Fifth and Sixth Causes of Action against NGTS, which is alleged to have financed Brian’s asset purchase.

Brian filed an answer to the FAC on June 5, 2023. Brian generally denied the allegations in the FAC and asserted 47 affirmative defenses.

There is also a cross-complaint in this action. On July 1, 2022, Brian and Regency filed a cross-complaint against Seyde and Porter 24, LLC alleging causes of action for: (1) breach of fiduciary duty; (2) breach of implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing; (3) misappropriation of trade secrets; (4) common law right of publicity; (5) statutory misappropriation of name and likeness; (6) trademark infringement; (7) intentional interference with prospective economic relations; (8) unfair business practices; and (9) unjust enrichment.

On January 22, 2025, the Court granted the motion of Defendant NTGS for summary judgment on Seyde’s FAC. Seyde filed a notice of appeal, but progress in the appeal has been slow (to say the least). Seyde’s opening brief was just recently filed (on or about September 8, 2026.

On August 19, 2026, Regency, Skyline, and Brian moved to stay further litigation of this matter pending resolution of the appeal. Seyde and Porter 24 LLC filed an opposition on August 31, and moving parties filed a reply on September 4.

Trial is set for September 22, 2026.

Legal Standard

Code of Civil Procedure section 128, subdivision (a)(8), provides that the court has the power “[t]o amend and control its process and orders so as to make them conform to law and justice.” This “entitles trial courts to exercise reasonable control over all proceedings connected with pending litigation ... in order to insure the orderly administration of justice.” (Rutherford v. Owens-Illinois, Inc. (1997) 16 Cal.4th 953, 967.)

Request for Judicial Notice

Moving parties ask the Court to take judicial notice of the docket in Court of Appeal Case No. B345136. The request is granted.

Discussion

The Court begins with a procedural matter and an observation.

As a procedural matter, opposing parties have filed a public redacted version of their opposition papers and have lodged an unredacted version. The material redacted includes the medical and health information of Brian and Drake.

Assuming that the redacted information was produced in discovery subject to a confidentiality agreement or protective order, and assuming that proper notice was given to the moving parties, under California Rules of Court, rule 2.551(b)(3), this shifts the obligation to file a motion or application to seal to the party who produced the information. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.551(b)(3)(B).) If no such motion or application is granted, the Court must promptly transfer the lodged records to the public file. (Ibid.)

The Court has not received a motion or application to seal. Should the lodged records now be placed into the public file?

Next, the Court makes an observation: both sides point the finger of delay at the other side, and both sides are correct. Seyde filed a notice of appeal in March 2025 and did not file the opening brief until September 2026, a delay of more than 17 months. Moving parties had notice of the appeal in March 2025 and did not file this motion to stay until August 2026, a delay of more than 16 months. These delays on both sides give rise to skepticism by the Court regarding the motivations of both sides. It is unclear whether Seyde is actually serious about pursuing its appeal, and it is unclear whether this motion is nothing more than last minute tactics by Brian, Regency, and Skyline to delay the trial.

Regardless of the parties’ actual motivations, the Court has considered the motion and the competing interests and concerns. There is some degree of overlap between the matters currently before this Court and the matters on appeal: if the motion for a stay is denied and the appeal is successful, a second trial will be necessary. Because Seyde alleges that NTGS intentionally interfered with contractual relations and aided and abetting a breach of fiduciary duty, the trial against NTGS will necessarily require a second trial on the issues of whether there was any breach of contract or breach of fiduciary duty. Moreover, if there are two trials and Seyde prevails in both, there will also be overlapping issues of causation and damages.

Weighing against a stay, however, are substantial interests. Many of the issues in the matter set for trial next week have nothing to do with NTGS, which became involved only years after many of the allegations in the FAC. Defendants Brian and Drake are each advanced in age and have had health issues; if the trial is delayed for a year or two, there is no way to know whether they will be able to testify. Seyde filed the complaint in this action in 2020, and he has an interest in the prompt resolution of the claims he has raised; there have already been substantial delays, and the stay requested by the moving parties would almost certainly lead to a further substantial delay. And, of course, the Court has an independent interest in the prompt resolution of all matters before it.

The Court has considered all of the evidence and argument presented by the parties (including, but not limited to, the arguments summarized above) and all of the facts and circumstances of this matter. Considering the totality of the circumstances, the Court exercises its discretion to deny the motion to stay.

Conclusion

The Court DENIES the motion to stay filed by Regency Outdoor Advertising, Inc., Skyline Outdoor Media LLC, and Brian Kennedy.

Moving Party is ORDERED to give notice. 


Case Number: 22STCV15847    Hearing Date: September 14, 2026    Dept: 514

Camaya v. Cainglit
22STCV15847
Plaintiff’s Motion to Amend Judgment

Tentative Ruling

The motion is denied.

Background

On May 12, 2022, Rowena Camaya (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Gloria R. Cainglit  (“Defendant”) for breach of promissory note arising from a loan made to Defendant by Nelson Ramos Pulanco, who assigned his rights to Plaintiff.

On September 26, 2022, default was entered against Gloria R. Cainglit.

On May 4, 2023, default judgment was entered against Gloria R. Cainglit.

On March 17, 2026, Plaintiff filed a motion to amend the judgment to add Pete Cainglit (“Cainglit”) as a judgment debtor.  The motion was placed off calendar on May 11, 2026, when no one appeared for the hearing.

On July 23, 2026, Plaintiff filed this motion again seeking to amend the judgment to add Cainglit as a judgment debtor.

On August 21, 2026, Plaintiff filed proof of personal service of this motion on Cainglit.

No opposition has been filed.

Legal Standard

“[T]he community estate is liable for a debt incurred by either spouse before or during marriage, regardless of which spouse has the management and control of the property and regardless of whether one or both spouses are parties to the debt or to a judgment for the debt.” (Fam. Code § 910.)

Discussion

Plaintiff seeks to amend the judgment to add Cainglit as a judgment debtor.  At the time the debt was incurred, Cainglit was married to Defendant.  Under Family Code section 910, the community estate is liable for debt incurred by either spouse during the marriage.  Courts have equitable authority, under certain circumstances, to amend a judgment to add parties who are legally responsible for the obligation.

The motion to amend is denied, however.  In Oyakawa v. Gillett (1992) 8 Cal.App.4th 628, our Court of Appeal stated:

“Although community property is liable for a debt incurred by either spouse during marriage, it does not follow that a wife can be added to a judgment rendered against her husband in an action in which she was not named and had no opportunity to defend.”

(Id. at p. 631.)  Moreover, there has been no showing that the newly added party had control of the litigation and thus was “virtually represented” in the lawsuit.  (Ibid.)  Accordingly, allowing the amendment would not comport with due process.

The motion is denied.

Conclusion

The Court DENIES the motion of Plaintiff Rowena Camaya to amend judgment.

Moving party is ORDERED to give notice.


Case Number: 25STCV09624    Hearing Date: September 14, 2026    Dept: 514

Universal Genetics, LLC v. Kaiser Foundation Health Plan, Inc.
25STCV09624
Defendant’s Demurrer to Second Amended Complaint
Plaintiff’s Motion for Protective Order

Tentative Ruling

The motion for a protective order is denied.

The demurrer is overruled.

Background

On April 1, 2025, Universal Genetics, LLC (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Kaiser Foundation Health Plan, Inc. (“Defendant”) and Does 1 through 50, asserting causes of action for (1) unfair competition and (2) common count.  In the complaint, Plaintiff alleged (among other things) that it was in the business of providing in-home COVID-19 tests, that Defendant was obligated under both federal and state law to reimburse providers for COVID-19 tests, and that Defendant improperly denied approximately 100,000 claims for reimbursements.

On December 4, 2025, the Court sustained Defendant’s demurrer to the second cause of action with leave to amend.

On December 24, 2025, Plaintiff filed the First Amended Complaint (the “FAC”), asserting the same two causes of action against the same defendants. 

On April 27, 2026, the Court sustained Defendant’s demurrer to the second cause of action again, with leave to amend.

On May 18, 2026, Plaintiff filed the Second Amended Complaint (the “SAC”), asserting the same two causes of action against the same defendants. 

Two motions are currently before the Court and set for hearing on September 14.

First, on May 27, 2026, Plaintiff filed a motion for a protective order.  Defendant filed an opposition on July 1, and Plaintiff filed a reply on July 9.  The hearing was initially set for July 16 and was continued to September 14.

Second on June 17, 2026, Defendant filed a demurrer to the SAC.  Plaintiff filed an opposition on July 31, and Defendant filed a reply on August 6.  The hearing was initially set for August 13 and was continued to September 14.

Trial is set for September 13, 2027.

Legal Standard

Motion for Protective Order

Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.420 states in part:

“(a) Before, during, or after a deposition, any party, any deponent, or any other affected natural person or organization may promptly move for a protective order. The motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.

(b) The court, for good cause shown, may make any order that justice requires to protect any party, deponent, or other natural person or organization from unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression, or undue burden and expense.”

Demurrer

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10 provides:

“The party against whom a complaint or cross-complaint has been filed may object, by demurrer or answer as provided in Section 430.30, to the pleading on any one or more of the following grounds:

…

(e) The pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action.  …”

A general demurrer under Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (e), tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Lewis v. Safeway, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 385, 388; Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747; see also 1 Weil & Brown, California Practice Guide: Civil Procedure Before Trial [2025], ¶ 7:40.)

“We treat the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.  We also consider matters which may be judicially noticed.”  (Centinela Freeman Emergency Medical Associates v. Health Net of California (2016) 1 Cal.5th 994, 1010; Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318; accord Yvanova v. New Century Mortgage Corp. (2016) 62 Cal.4th 919, 924; Marina Pacific Hotel and Suites v. Fireman’s Fund Insurance Company (2022) 81 Cal.App.5th 96, 104.)  All reasonable inferences must be drawn in favor of the pleading.  (Schifando v. City of Los Angeles (2003) 31 Cal.4th 1074, 1081; Marina Pacific Hotel and Suites, supra, 81 Cal.App.5th at p. 104.)  Even “improbable” facts alleged in the pleading must be accepted as true.  (Marina Pacific Hotel and Suites, supra, 81 Cal.App.5th at pp. 104-105.)

Courts must “liberally construe the pleading” and “give the complaint a reasonable interpretation, reading it as a whole and its parts in context.”  (Id. at p. 105.)

A complaint must contain a “statement of facts constituting the cause of action, in ordinary and concise language.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.10, subd. (a)(1); see also C.A. v. William S. Hart High School Dist. (2012) 53 Cal.4th 861, 872 [“the complaint need only allege facts sufficient to state a cause of action”].)  Ordinarily, a complaint “is sufficient if it alleges ultimate rather than evidentiary facts.”  (Doe v. City of Los Angeles (2007) 42 Cal.4th 531, 550.)  Ultimate facts are those upon which “the right to recover depends” and are “essential” to the cause of action.  (Estes v. Eaton Corp. (2020) 51 Cal.App.5th 636, 643 fn. 2; see also 1 Weil & Brown, supra, ¶ 6:124.)

A plaintiff is required to plead only “the essential facts of [its] case” that are sufficient “to acquaint a defendant with the nature, source and extent of [the] cause of action.”  (Doe, supra, 42 Cal.4th at p. 550.)  Mere boilerplate or pleading of legal conclusions is not sufficient.  (Id. at p. 551 fn. 5.)  But a plaintiff need not allege “each evidentiary fact that might eventually form part of plaintiff’s proof” at trial.  (C.A., supra, 53 Cal.4th at p. 872.)

A demurrer can be sustained only when it disposes of an entire cause of action. (Poizner v. Fremont General Corp. (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 97, 119; Kong v. City of Hawaiian Gardens Redev. Agency (2003) 108 Cal.App.4th 1028, 1046.)

Leave to amend should be granted when “there is a reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment.”  (Centinela Freeman, supra, 1 Cal.5th at p. 1010; Blank, supra, 39 Cal.3d at p. 318; Shaeffer v. Califia Farms (2020) 44 Cal.App.5th 1125, 1145.)

Request for Judicial Notice

In support of its opposition to the demurrer, Plaintiff asks the Court to take judicial notice of two Superior Court rulings.  The request is granted, but the Court notes that a trial court ruling has no precedential value.

Meet and Confer

The requirement for counsel to meet and confer is met for both the motion for a protective order and the demurrer.  (Newborn Decl., ¶¶ 3-4 & Exh. C; Brooks Decl., ¶ 2.)

Discussion             

Motion for Protective Order

Defendant initially noticed two depositions: (1) the deposition of Plaintiff’s person most qualified on 29 topics; and (2) the deposition of David Ku.  (Newborn Decl., ¶ 2 & Exhs. A-B.)  The depositions were noticed for consecutive days, to be taken in person at the office of Defendant’s counsel in Costa Mesa, California.

Dr. David Ku is the managing member of Plaintiff.  (Ku Decl., ¶ 2.)  Plaintiff has designated Dr. Ku as the person most qualified to testify for Plaintiff on the topics in the deposition notice.  (Ku Decl., ¶ 4.)

Plaintiff now moves for a protective order.  Plaintiff seeks an order that Defendant be limited to one deposition of Dr. Ku (in both his individual and his representative capacity) and that the deposition be taken remotely, rather than in person.  According to Dr. Ku, he works and lives in Florida, he has primary responsibility for the care of a young child, and he rarely visits Plaintiff’s location in Los Angeles.  (Ku Decl., ¶ 2.)

After the motion was filed, Defendant withdrew (at least for now) its deposition notice for Dr. Ku to testify in his individual capacity.

Plaintiff’s request for a protective order to preclude Defendant from deposing Dr. Ku in both his individual capacity and as a person most qualified has no merit and is denied.  A deposition of a person most qualified serves different purposes than an individual deposition.  The taking of one does not preclude the taking of the other.

Of course, Defendant cannot use two depositions (or one deposition) to harass Dr. Ku (or anyone else) with duplicative or unnecessary questioning.  The Court has no reason to expect that any such misconduct will occur, but if this becomes an issue the Court retains ample authority to address it.

Plaintiff’s request for a protective order to preclude Defendant from conducting the deposition of Plaintiff’s person most qualified in person in California is also denied.  The Court expects that issues relating to the location and manner of taking depositions will ordinarily be resolved through discussions among counsel.  Where no agreement is reached, a party may generally insist on an in-person deposition of the opposing party within certain geographical limits.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.250, subd. (b).)  This location may not be convenient for the person whom Plaintiff has designated as its person most qualified, but the evidence of inconvenience is not, on this record, sufficient to support Plaintiff’s request for a protective order.  Plaintiff will of course also be entitled to insist on an in-person deposition of Defendant’s person(s) most qualified and that the deposition be taken within the geographical limits set by statute.

Plaintiff’s motion for a protective order is denied.

Demurrer

Defendant demurs to the Second Cause of Action in the SAC, arguing that it again fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a claim.

The Second Cause of Action is labeled as “Common Count – Account Stated.”

“A common count is not a specific cause of action ...; rather, it is a simplified form of pleading normally used to aver the existence of various forms of monetary indebtedness.”  (Avidor v. Sutter's Place, Inc. (2013) 212 Cal.App.4th 1439, 1454; accord Camden Systems, LLC v. 409 North Camden, LLC (2024) 103 Cal.App.5th 1068, 1082; McBride v. Boughton (2004) 123 Cal.App.4th 379, 394.)  “A common count is proper whenever the plaintiff claims a sum of money due, either as an indebtedness in a sum certain, or for the reasonable value of services, goods, etc., furnished.  It makes no difference in such a case that the proof shows the original transaction to be an express contract, a contract implied in fact, or a quasi contract.”  (Utility Audit Co., Inc. v. City of Los Angeles (2003) 112 Cal.App.4th 950, 958; see also Kawasho International, U.S.A., Inc. v. Lakewood Pipe Service, Inc. (1983) 152 Cal.App.3d 785, 793; 4 B.E. Witkin, California Procedure (6th ed. 2021) Pleadings § 562.)

The basic elements of a cause of action for account stated are: (1) that the defendant owes money to plaintiff from previous financial transactions establishing a debtor-creditor relationship; (2) that the parties, by words or conduct, agreed that the amount that plaintiff claimed to be due from the defendant was the correct amount owed; (3) that the defendant, by words or conduct, promised to pay the stated amount to plaintiff; and (4) that the defendant has not paid some or all of the amount stated. (Leighton v. Forster (2017) 8 Cal.App.5th 467, 491; Truestone Inc. v. Simi West Industrial Park II (1984) 163 Cal.App.3d 715, 725; Gleason v. Klamer (1980) 103 Cal.App.3d 782, 786-787; Zinn v. Fred R. Bright Co. (1969) 271 Cal.App.2d 597, 600; CACI No. 373.)  In certain contexts, if the plaintiff sends the defendant a statement of the account showing the amount, and if defendant fails to object within a reasonable time, the law may imply an agreement by defendant that the amount shown on the statement is correct, due, and owing.  (Trafton v. Youngblood (1968) 69 Cal.2d 17, 25; Maggio, Inc. v. McNeal (1987) 196 Cal.App.3d 745, 752-753; Gleason, supra, 103 Cal.App.3d at p. 787.)

The Court previously sustained demurrers to this cause of action. The Court determined that Plaintiff had failed to show that Defendant agreed to pay a specific amount due. (See Minute Orders dated December 4, 2025, and April 27, 2026.)

Although Plaintiff adds additional background allegations, this defect persists.  There is no allegation that Defendant, by words or deeds, agreed to pay an amount billed to it or otherwise stated by Plaintiff.  (See Zinn v. Fred R. Bright Co. (1969) 271 Cal.App.2d 597, 600 [stating a plaintiff must show “a promise by the debtor, express or implied, to pay the amount due”].)  Plaintiff has again failed to allege facts sufficient to support a cause of action for account stated.

The Court’s analysis, however, does not end there.  The California Supreme Court and California Courts of Appeal have repeatedly stated that a general demurrer may not be sustained unless the allegations in the complaint fail to state a cause of action under any available legal theory.  (E.g., Sheehan v. San Francisco 49ers, Ltd. (2009) 45 Cal.4th 992, 998; Quelimane Co. v. Stewart Title Guaranty Co. (1998) 19 Cal.4th 26, 38-39; Adelman v. Associated International Ins. Co. (2001) 90 Cal.App.4th 352, 359.)

Here, Plaintiff does allege facts that state a cause of action under an available legal theory: the SAC, as pleaded, asserts a cause of action for breach of an implied-in-fact contract.

The elements of a cause of action for breach are contract are: “(1) the existence of the contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance, (3) defendant’s breach, and (4) the resulting damages to the plaintiff.” (Oasis West Realty, LLC v. Goldman (2011) 51 Cal.4th 811, 821; see also, e.g., Richman v. Hartley (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 1182, 1186.)

In an implied-in-fact contract, “the existence and terms … are manifested by conduct.”  (Civil Code, § 1621.)  “An implied-in-fact contract is based on the conduct of the parties.  Like an express contract, an implied-in-fact contract requires an ascertained agreement of the parties.”  (Unilab Corp. v. Angeles-IPA (2016) 244 Cal.App.4th 622, 636.)

In the SAC, Plaintiff now alleges that in May 2023, it submitted an initial set of reimbursement claims to Defendant, billed at $18 per test, and Defendant paid those claims without any objection.  (SAC, ¶ 10.)  Although subsequent claims were rejected, a Kaiser representative by the name of Carlo Rabaza acknowledged that the services provided by Plaintiff were eligible for reimbursement and stated that if the claims were resubmitted, the claims “would be processed and paid just as the previously approved claims were.”  (SAC, ¶ 13.)  Plaintiff then resubmitted the claims, but they were not paid.  (SAC, ¶¶ 14-15.)  Plaintiff also alleges that Defendant was required to pay Plaintiff’s claims for COVID-19 tests by federal and state law “without undue medical management or barriers to reimbursement.”  (SAC, ¶ 19.)

Taking these allegations as true, and drawing all reasonable inferences in favor of the Plaintiff, the Court determines that this is a sufficient allegation to support a cause of action for an implied-in-fact contract in which Plaintiff would provide services to Defendant’s members and Defendant would pay Plaintiff for those services.  Whether these allegations are true (and, if so, the price term of any implied-in-fact contract) will be decided based on the evidence.

The demurrer to the Second Cause of Action is overruled.

Conclusion 

The Court DENIES the motion of Universal Genetics, LLC for a protective order.

The Court OVERRULES demurrer of Defendant Kaiser Foundation Health Plan, Inc..

Defendant Kaiser Foundation Health Plan, Inc. is to file an answer or other responsive pleading to Plaintiff’s Second Amended by no later than September 30, 2026.

Moving party is ordered to give notice.


Case Number: 25STCV15199    Hearing Date: September 14, 2026    Dept: 514

Aron v. Skousen
25STCV15199
Plaintiff’s Motion to Seal

Tentative Ruling

The motion to seal is granted,

Background

This action arises from a dispute over real property located at 10250 West Sunset Boulevard in Los Angeles (the “Property”).

On May 23, 2025, Marc Aron (“Aron”) filed the complaint in this action against Sara Skousen, trustee of the Azria Family Trust dated August 11, 1995, as amended; Lubov Azria, former trustee of the Azria Family Trust dated August 11, 1995, as amended; and Does 1 through 100, asserting causes of action for (1) breach of contract, (2) declaratory relief, and (3) injunctive relief.

On December 16, 2025, Michael Henri Azria and Joyce Judith Trojanowski, successor co-trustees of the Azria Family Trust, dated August 11, 1995, as amended and restated December 28, 2017 (collectively “Trustees”) filed an answer to the complaint. 

Also on December 16, 2025, Trustees filed a cross-complaint against Aron; Reserve Escrow, Inc.; and Roes 1 through 20, asserting causes of action for breach of contract and declaratory relief.

Aron filed an answer to the cross-complaint on January 22, 2026.

Of particular importance to the pending motion, on May 29, 2025, Aron filed a notice of recording of notice of pendency of action (a lis pendens). The notice was amended on June 6, 2025.

On September 23, 2025, Trustees – who had not yet been named as defendants and had not previously appeared – filed a motion as nonparties to expunge the lis pendens.  On November 20, 2025, the Court denied the Trustees’ motion.

On July 15, 2026, Trustees filed a renewed motion to expunge the lis pendens.  The hearing on that motion was initially set for August 25 and was continued to September 28.

Currently before the Court and set for hearing on September 14, 2026, is a motion filed by Aron on July 27, 2026, to seal certain portions of records submitted by Trustees in connection with their motion to expunge the lis pendens.  Trustees filed a response on August 31, and Aron filed a reply on September 4.

Trial is set for January 11, 2027.

Legal Standard

“[T]he First Amendment provides a right of [public] access to ordinary civil trials and proceedings.” (NBC Subsidiary (KNBC-TV), Inc. v. Super. Ct. (1999) 20 Cal.4th 1178, 1212.) “[C]onstitutional standards governing closure of trial proceedings apply in the civil setting.”  (Ibid.)  Those standards are embodied in California Rules of Court, rules 2.550 and 2.551.

“Unless confidentiality is required by law, court records are presumed to be open.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.550(c).  “A record must not be filed under seal without a court order.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.551(a).)  “The court must not permit a record to be filed under seal based solely on the agreement or stipulation of the parties.” (Ibid.)

California Rules of Court, rule 2.550(d) sets forth the basic standard for an order permitting the filing of documents under seal:

“The court may order that a record be filed under seal only if it expressly finds facts that establish:  

(1) There exists an overriding interest that overcomes the right of public access to the record;  

(2) The overriding interest supports sealing the record;  

(3) A substantial probability exists that the overriding interest will be prejudiced if the record is not sealed;  

(4) The proposed sealing is narrowly tailored; and  

(5) No less restrictive means exist to achieve the overriding interest.”   

(Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.550(d).)  

Rule 2.550(e) requires that a sealing order must contain certain findings:

“An order sealing the record must: (A) Specifically state the facts that support the findings; and (B) Direct the sealing of only those documents and pages, or, if reasonably practicable, portions of those documents and pages, that contain the material that needs to be placed under seal.  All other portions of each document or page must be included in the public file.” 

(Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.550(e)(1).

A motion or application to file under seal must be accompanied by a memorandum and a declaration containing facts sufficient to justify the sealing. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.551(b)(1).)  “A request to seal a document must be filed publicly and separately from the object of the request.  It must be supported by a factual declaration or affidavit explaining the particular needs of the case.” (In re Marriage of Lechowick (1998) 65 Cal.App.4th 1406, 1416.) “[T]he party seeking to seal documents, or maintain them under seal, must come forward with a specific enumeration of the facts sought to be withheld and specific reasons for withholding them.”  (H.B. Fuller Co. v. Doe (2007) 151 Cal.App.4th 879, 894.)

Discussion

Pursuant to California Rules of Court, rule 2.551(b)(3), Aron moves for an order keeping under seal the unredacted version of Trustees’ motion to expunge the lis pendens, the supporting memorandum, and supporting evidence.

The documents and information at issue relate to Aron’s confidential financial and personal records, including information regarding Aron’s net worth, assets, liquidity, and a prospective IPO.  (Hami Decl., ¶¶ 16-18.)

Although the Court must make an independent determination of whether the requirements for sealing are met, the Court notes that the Trustees do not oppose the motion to seal.  Trustees “oppose any result that prevents this Court from reviewing the records” (Response, at pp. 2, 4), but no one is suggesting such a result.  If the motion to seal is granted, the Court will review the sealed records but they will not be in the public file.  If the motion to seal is denied, Aron will be given a choice: he can withdraw his opposition and stipulate to the relief requested by Trustees (in which case the information at issue need not be placed in the public file), or he can stand on his opposition to the motion to expunge and accept that the records at issue will be placed in the public file.  Contrary to Trustee’s suggestion, no one is giving Plaintiff “veto” power over evidence.

Trustees also request their own affirmative relief in their response to this motion: they ask the Court to advance the hearing on the motion to expunge.  An opposition (or “response”) to a motion is not the proper place to seek affirmative relief.  If Trustees seek to advance a hearing (or any other affirmative relief), they must file their own motion or application.

Turning now to the merits of Aron’s motion, the Court has reviewed the evidence in the record and finds that Aron has made a sufficient showing for a sealing order.  The Court finds that Aron has demonstrated that there is an overriding interest in protecting his financial privacy (and in protecting the confidentiality of information relating to a prospective IPO) that overrides the right of public access to the records at issue and supports sealing those records.  Absent a sealing order, there is a substantial probability that the overriding interest will be prejudiced.  The proposed sealing order is narrowly tailored, and there are no less restrictive means that would protect the overriding interest.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.550(d).)  

The motion is granted.

Conclusion

The Court GRANTS the motion of Plaintiff Marc Aron to seal certain records.

The Court ORDERS that the unredacted version of Trustees’ motion to expunge the lis pendens, supporting memorandum, and supporting evidence is to placed under seal.  The redacted version is to remain in the public file.
