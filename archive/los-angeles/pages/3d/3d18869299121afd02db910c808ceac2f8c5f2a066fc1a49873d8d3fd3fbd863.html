DEPARTMENT L LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 24NWCV00679    Hearing Date: August 11, 2026    Dept: L

Plaintiff Victor M. Curiel’s Motion for Summary Adjudication is GRANTED as to the first and fourth causes of action and is DENIED as to the second and third causes of action.

 

Plaintiff to give notice.

 

Background

 

This is an action for breach of contract. On March 5, 2024, Plaintiff Victor M. Curiel, Trustee of the Alice S. Gomes Living Trust, (“Plaintiff”) filed this action against Defendant Maribel Cruz (“Defendant”) and Does 1 to 10. The Complaint alleged that Plaintiff loaned $100,000 to Defendant on November 21, 2022, and Defendant has not repaid any part of the loan. The Complaint alleges the following causes of action: 1) Breach of Contract, 2) Fraud, 2) General Negligence, and 4) Common Counts.

 

On September 18, 2024, Defendant filed an Answer.

 

On October 21, 2025, Defendant filed a Substitution of Attorney, wherein Defendant became self-represented.

 

Plaintiff now moves for summary judgment, or in the alternative, summary adjudication as to all causes of action.

 

No opposition has been filed as of August 10, 2026.


Legal Standard

 

The function of a motion for summary judgment or adjudication is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party cannot show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (c) “requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119, internal citations omitted.) “The function of the pleadings in a motion for summary judgment is to delimit the scope of the issues; the function of the affidavits or declarations is to disclose whether there is any triable issue of fact within the issues delimited by the pleadings.” (Juge v. County of Sacramento (1993) 12 Cal.App.4th 59, 67, citing FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 367, 381-382.)

 

In moving for summary judgment, a plaintiff has met its “burden of showing that there is no defense to a cause of action if that party has proved each element of the cause of action entitling the party to judgment on the cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(1).)

 

Once a moving party has satisfied the initial burden of proof, the burden “shifts to the opposing party to show, by responsive separate statement and admissible evidence, that triable issues of fact exist.” (Ostayan v. Serrano Reconveyance Co. (2000) 77 Cal.App.4th 1411, 1418, disapproved on other grounds by Black Sky Cap., LLC v. Cobb (2019) 7 Cal.5th 156, 165.) To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence. (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

 

Discussion

 

          First Cause of Action – Breach of Contract

 

To state a cause of action for breach of contract, Plaintiff must be able to establish “(1) the existence of the contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance, (3) defendant’s breach, and (4) the resulting damages to the plaintiff.” (Oasis West Realty, LLC v. Goldman (2011) 51 Cal.4th 811, 821.) 

 

If a breach of contract claim “is based on alleged breach of a written contract, the terms must be set out verbatim in the body of the complaint or a copy of the written agreement must be attached and incorporated by reference.” (Harris v. Rudin, Richman & Appel (1999) 74 Cal.App.4th 299, 307.) In some circumstances, a plaintiff may also “plead the legal effect of the contract rather than its precise language.” (Construction Protective Services, Inc. v. TIG Specialty Ins. Co. (2002) 29 Cal.4th 189, 198-199.)

 

Plaintiff argues that each element of the breach of cause of action is established, as summarized and supported by the following evidence:

 

-      Existence of a Contract: Defendant asked Plaintiff is she could borrow $100,000.00 via telephone. (Flores Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. A, Cruz Depo., 24:9, 24:17-22.) Plaintiff agreed to loan Defendant $100,000. (Flores Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. A, Cruz Depo., 22:16-17, 25:5 9, 32:2-4.) On November 21, 2022, Plaintiff delivered a cashier's check to Defendant in the amount of $100,000, which stated "Loan for six months" in the memo line. (Flores Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. A, Cruz Depo., 22:24-25, 32:2-6, Exh. 2; Curiel Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. A.) Defendant deposited the check into her account. (Flores Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. A, Cruz Depo., 36:1-3, 36:5-6.)

-      Plaintiff’s Performance: Plaintiff delivered the $100,000.00 check to Defendant on November 21, 2022. 9 Flores Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. A, Cruz Depo., 22:24-25, 23:11-12, Exh. 2.)

-      Defendant’s Breach: Defendant thereafter failed to make any payments on the loan. (Flores Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. A, Cruz Depo., 27:23-24, 28:1, 28:1-7.)

-      Damages: Plaintiff suffered damages in the amount of $100,000, representing the principal amount of the loan that remains unpaid, plus prejudgment interest at the legal rate of 10% per annum from the date the loan became due on May 21, 2023. (Flores Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. A, Cruz Depo., 22:24-25, 32:2-6, Exh. 2; Curiel Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. A.)

 

The Court finds that Plaintiff has met his burden in proving all elements of the breach of contract cause of action. The burden thus shifts to Defendant.

 

As Defendant has filed no opposition, the Court finds that Defendant has failed to meet her burden in establishing a triable issue of fact exists.

 

Accordingly, the Motion for Summary Adjudication is GRANTED as to the first cause of action.

 

          Second Cause of Action – Fraud

 

The Court initially notes that Plaintiff alleges various theories of fraud in the Complaint, including intentional or negligent misrepresentation, fraudulent concealment, and false promise.

 

The elements of fraud are (a) a misrepresentation (false representation, concealment, or nondisclosure); (b) scienter or knowledge of its falsity; (c) intent to induce reliance; (d) justifiable reliance; and (e) resulting damage.” (Hinesley v. Oakshade Town Ctr. (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 289, 294.) Negligent misrepresentation requires the same elements as intentional misrepresentation except for “knowledge of falsity.” (See Apollo Capital Fund, LLC v. Roth Capital Partners, LLC (2007) 158 Cal.App.4th 226, 243.)

 

“One who willfully deceives another with intent to induce him to alter his position to his injury or risk, is liable for any damage which he thereby suffers.” (Civ. Code § 1709.) Deceit may be defined as “[a] promise, made without any intention of performing it.” (Civ. Code, § 1710, subd. (4).) “[I]n a promissory fraud action, to sufficiently allege[] defendant made a misrepresentation, the complaint must

allege (1) the defendant made a representation of intent to perform some future action, i.e., the defendant made a promise, and (2) the defendant did not really have that intent at the time that the promise was made, i.e., the promise was false.” (Beckwith v. Dahl (2012) 205 Cal.App.4th 1039, 1060.)

 

The elements for fraudulent concealment are as follows: 1) defendant concealed or suppressed a material fact, 2) defendant was under a duty to disclose the fact to the plaintiff, 3) defendant intentionally concealed or suppressed the fact with the intent to defraud the plaintiff, 4) plaintiff was unaware of the fact and would not have acted in the same way knowing of the concealed or suppressed fact, 5) causation, and the plaintiff sustained damage. (See Blickman Turkus, LP v. MF Downtown Sunnyvale, LLC (2008) 162 Cal.App.4th 858, 868; see also Lovejoy v. AT&T Corp. (2001) 92 Cal.App.4th 85, 96; Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 748.)

 

Plaintiff asserts that all elements of fraud had been established. Plaintiff asserts that, “[b]y accepting a loan with a six-month repayment term, Defendant impliedly represented she would be able to repay within that timeframe.” (Mot., 8:25-26.) However, Defendant concealed certain material facts suggesting that she had no intention or ability to pay back the loan. For example, Defendant admitted that her salary was insufficient to repay the loan at the time she borrowed it. (Flores Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. A, Cruz Depo., 32:14-22.) Additionally, Defendant testified that he plan to repay Plaintiff was to obtain other personal loans but that she was unable to qualify for such loans after she borrowed the loan from Plaintiff. (Flores Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. A, Cruz Depo., 30:15-20.) Defendant also made an express false representation on July 2, 2023, she told Plaintiff she had “a check for 100 thousand that is waiting to clear.” (Flores Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. A, Cruz Depo., 46:1-4, Exh. 3.) However, Defendant admitted that no such check existed. (Flores Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. A, Cruz Depo., 47:11.) Further, when asked about Defendant’s intent and plan to repay, Defendant gave no response or refused to answer. (Flores Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. A, Cruz Depo., 28:25, 29:1-2, 38:1, 38:3, 40:22, 40:25, 41:1, 47:5, 47:15, 47:19, 48:1-3.) Plaintiff argues that Defendant’s “evasiveness regarding her fraudulent conduct and intent supports the inference of fraud.” (Mot., 10:10.) Plaintiff justifiability relied on Defendant’s concealment/representations, as he had no reason to suspect that Defendant would be unable to pay. Plaintiff suffered $100,000.00, plus interest.

 

The Court finds that Plaintiff has failed to meet his burden in proving all elements required for intentional misrepresentation/deceit or fraudulent concealment.

 

First, as to fraudulent concealment, Plaintiff does not address Defendant’s duty to disclose, a required element for that claim.

 

As to intentional misrepresentation/deceit, the Court is not persuaded by the evidence presented that Defendant’s promise to repay the loan was false at the time she made the promise—i.e., that she had no intent to perform her promise. When asked how Defendant intended to repay the loan, Defendant testified that she intended to get other personal loans. (Flores Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. A, Cruz Depo., 26:12-25.) A close reading of Defendant’s testimony only indicates that she did not qualify for such loans after she received the loan from Plaintiff. (Flores Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. A, Cruz Depo., 27:1-8, 30:3-8.) Thus, there is no indication that Defendant knew she could not qualify for a separate personal loan, to repay Plaintiff’s loan, prior to receiving the loan from Plaintiff. Further, the Court finds that Defendant’s failure to respond or refusal to answer questions regarding her intent to repay the loan is not affirmative evidence or her intent not to repay. Plaintiff’s other evidence—i.e., that Defendant gifted $75,000.00 to her uncle in Mexico and $25,000.00 to her friend) after receiving the loan—is speculative as to the issue of Defendant’s intent. (Flores Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. A, Cruz Depo., 37:7-15.)

 

Further, the Court finds that Plaintiff’s damages for fraud are duplicative of Plaintiff’s damages for breach of contract.

 

“[T]he economic loss rule requires a [contractual party] to recover in contract for purely economic loss due to disappointed expectations, unless [the party] can demonstrate harm above and beyond a broken contractual promise. . .[T]ort recovery for breach of a contract duty is generally barred. . .unless two conditions are satisfied. A plaintiff must first demonstrate the defendant's injury-causing conduct violated a duty that is independent of the duties and rights assumed by the parties when they entered the contract. Second, the defendant's conduct must have caused injury to persons or property that was not reasonably contemplated by the parties when the contract was formed.”

 

(Rattagan v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1, 20–21, internal quotations and citations omitted.)

 

Here, the Court finds that, as alleged, the economic loss rule bars Plaintiff’s fraud claim. In other words, Plaintiff has failed to demonstrate any separate harm arising from the breach of contract as a result of the alleged fraud.

 

Thus, the Court finds that Plaintiff has failed to meet his burden of proof in establishing no triable issue of fact exists as to the second cause of action.

 

Accordingly, the Motion for Summary Adjudication is DENIED as to the second cause of action.

 

          Third Cause of Action – Negligence

 

In order to state a claim for negligence, Plaintiff must allege the elements of (1) “the existence of a legal duty of care,” (2) “breach of that duty,” and (3) “proximate cause resulting in an injury.” (McIntyre v. Colonies-Pacific, LLC (2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 664, 671.)

 

Plaintiff argues that Defendant “negligently caused damages to Plaintiff by failing to exercise reasonable care in her financial dealings.” (Mot., 12:11-12.) Plaintiff essentially cites the same evidence offered in support of the breach of contract and fraud causes of action—i.e., that Defendant failed to repay the loan.

 

The Court finds that Plaintiff has failed to meet his burden in proving all elements required for negligence. Specifically, Plaintiff offers no evidence that Plaintiff suffered non-economic damages based on a breach of duty separate from Defendant’s contractual duties. For reasons stated in the Court’s discussion on the fraud cause of action, the Court finds that this cause of action is barred by the economic loss rule.

 

Accordingly, the Motion for Summary Adjudication is DENIED as to the third cause of action.

 

          Fourth Cause of Action – Common Counts (Money Lent)

 

The required elements of a common count claim are “(1) the statement of indebtedness in a certain sum, (2) the consideration, i.e., goods sold, work done, etc., and (3) nonpayment.” (Farmers Insurance Exchange v. Zerin (1997) 53 Cal.App.4th 445, 460, citation and quotation marks omitted.) 

 

“To state a common count for money lent, the plaintiff need only allege that the defendant is indebted in a certain sum for money loaned by the plaintiff and that the defendant has not repaid the money. [Citations.]” (Etchegaray Farms, LLC v. Lehr Brothers, Inc. (E.D.Cal. 2018) 326 F.Supp.3d 987, 994; see also 55 Cal. Jur. 3d Restitution § 25; Moya v. Northrup (1970) 10 Cal.App.3d 276, 280.)

 

Plaintiff asserts that all elements of the common counts cause of action are met. Defendant is indebted to Plaintiff in the amount of $100,000.00, and the sum remains due and unpaid. Plaintiff cites to evidence offered in support of the breach of contract cause of action.

 

The Court finds that Plaintiff has met his burden in proving all elements of the breach of contract cause of action. The burden thus shifts to Defendant.

 

As Defendant has filed no opposition, the Court finds that Defendant has failed to meet her burden in establishing a triable issue of fact exists.

 

Accordingly, the Motion for Summary Adjudication is GRANTED as to the second cause of action.


Case Number: 25NWCV04114    Hearing Date: August 11, 2026    Dept: L

Defendant General Motors, LLC’s Demurrer to the fifth cause of action in the Complaint is OVERRULED.

 

Defendant’s Motion to Strike is GRANTED without leave to amend.

 

Defendant to give notice.

 

Background

 

This is a lemon law action. On November 18, 2025, Plaintiffs Michael P Magee and Jessica L Magee (“Plaintiffs”) filed this action against Defendant General Motors, LLC (“Defendant”) and Does 1 to 10. The Complaint alleges Plaintiffs entered into a warranty contract with Defendant regarding a 2023 Chevrolet Silverado 1500 (“Subject Vehicle”). The Complaint alleges the Subject Vehicle manifested defects, including but not limited to, “transmission defects, engine defects, infotainment defects, electrical defects, brake defects” within the warranty period. (Compl., ¶ 11.) The Complaint alleges the following causes of action: 1) Violation of Subdivision (D) of Civil Code Section 1793.2, 2) Violation of Subdivision (B) of Civil Code Section 1793.2, 3) Violation of Subdivision (A)(3) of Civil Code Section 1793.2, 4) Breach of Implied Warranty of Merchantability, and 5) Fraudulent Inducement – Concealment.

 

Defendant now demurs to the fifth cause of action. Defendant also moves to strike portions of the Complaint pertaining to civil penalties.


Legal Standard

 

“[A] demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the allegations in a complaint.” (Lewis v. Safeway, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 385, 388.) A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. (See Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (“Donabedian”) (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994 [in ruling on a demurrer, a court may not consider declarations, matters not subject to judicial notice, or documents not accepted for the truth of their contents].) For purposes of ruling on a demurrer, all facts pleaded in a complaint are assumed to be true, but the reviewing court does not assume the truth of conclusions of law. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hosp. Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 967.) 

 

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Id.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.) However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245.) 

 

Meet and Confer

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, subdivision (a) and section 435.5 require that the parties meet and confer “in person,¿by telephone, or by video conference.” 

 

On December 15, 2026, the parties met and conferred telephonically regarding the issues in the Demurrer and Motion to Strike but were unable to come to a resolution. (Goh Decl., ¶¶ 2-3.)

 

The Court finds that the meet and confer requirement has been met.

 

Discussion

 

Defendant makes two primary arguments in support of its Demurrer to the fifth cause of action for fraudulent inducement – concealment: 1) the Complaint fails to allege facts with the requisite specificity to state a cause of action, and 2) the claim is barred by the economic loss rule.

 

The Court will address each argument in turn.

 

          Particularity

 

     i.        Content of Omitted Facts and Justifiable Reliance

 

Defendant first argues that the Complaint fails to allege with particularity the defect Defendant allegedly concealed. Further, the Complaint fails to identify by name the person who made the alleged omissions on behalf of Defendant. Lastly, the Complaint’s allegations as to Plaintiff’s justifiable and actual reliance are conclusory.

 

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that particularity standards for fraud claims based on affirmative misrepresentations do not apply to fraudulent concealment cases. The Complaint sufficiently alleges the elements of a fraudulent inducement by concealment under Dhital v. Nissan North America, Inc. (“Dhital”) (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 828, 843-44. Specifically, the Complaint alleges misrepresentation / concealment, knowledge of falsity, intent to induce reliance, justifiable reliance, and damages.

 

The Court initially finds that the Complaint sufficiently alleges that the Subject Vehicle suffered from a defective 8-Speed transmission, and that Defendant had prior knowledge of such defect but failed to disclose such fact to Plaintiffs. (See Compl., ¶¶ 11, 46-48, 50, 54.) The Court finds such allegations are sufficient to establish that Defendant omitted certain material facts from Plaintiffs.

 

The Court further finds that pleading standards regarding the names of persons who made the allegedly fraudulent representations, their authority to speak, to whom they spoke, what they said or wrote, and when it was said or written are generally relaxed for fraudulent concealment claims. (See Miles v. Deutsche Bank Nat’l Trust Co. (2015) 236 Cal.App.4th 394, 403-404 [finding that plaintiff sufficiently alleged fraud, without alleging the names of the speakers and their authority to speak, where defendants possessed superior knowledge, and it is unrealistic to expect plaintiffs to know such information].) Rather, a fraudulent concealment claim primarily concerns a defendant’s failure to reveal information that they had a duty to reveal to the plaintiff.

 

Lastly, the Court finds that the Complaint sufficiently alleges exclusive and superior knowledge of dangerous defects learned from identified sources (Compl., ¶¶ 49, 51-53, 56), and Plaintiffs’ reliance upon the concealment that led to their unwanted purchase (Compl., ¶¶ 59-60). Such allegations are sufficient at this stage.

 

    ii.        Duty to Disclose

 

“A duty to disclose a material fact can arise if (1) it is imposed by statute; (2) the defendant is acting as plaintiff's fiduciary or is in some other confidential relationship with plaintiff that imposes a disclosure duty under the circumstances; (3) the material facts are known or accessible only to defendant, and defendant knows those facts are not known or reasonably discoverable by plaintiff (i.e., exclusive knowledge); (4) the defendant makes representations but fails to disclose other facts that materially qualify the facts disclosed or render the disclosure misleading (i.e., partial concealment); or (5) defendant actively conceals discovery of material fact from plaintiff (i.e., active concealment). (Civ. Code, § 1710, subd. (3); [Citation.] Circumstances (3), (4), and (5) presuppose a preexisting relationship between the parties, such as ‘between seller and buyer, employer and prospective employee, doctor and patient, or parties entering into any kind of contractual agreement. [Citation.] All of these relationships are created by transactions between parties from which a duty to disclose facts material to the transaction arises under certain circumstances.’ [Citation.] Such a transaction must necessarily arise from direct dealings between the plaintiff and the defendant; it cannot arise between the defendant and the public at large. [Citation.]”

 

(Rattagan v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (“Rattagan”) (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1, 40–41.)

 

Defendant argues that the Complaint fails to allege facts establishing it had any duty to disclose. Firstly, the Complaint does not allege a duty to disclose based on a fiduciary relationship between the parties. Thus, the duty to disclose must arise from a direct transactional relationship. However, the Complaint fails to allege that direct transactional relationship existed between the parties. Further, the Complaint fails to allege active concealment.

 

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that the Complaint alleges a direct transactional relationship. Specifically, the Complaint alleges that Plaintiffs entered into a “warranty contract” with Defendant regarding the vehicle. (Compl., ¶ 6.) Thus, Plaintiffs had entered into a contractual agreement giving rise to a duty to disclose. Such relationship was between Defendant and Plaintiffs, not Defendant and the public at large. Plaintiffs further argue that a “transactional relationship” does not necessarily require privity in the sale.

 

The Court notes that the reply does not specifically address Plaintiff’s argument that the Complaint alleges a contractual relationship via the warranty agreement. Defendant simply does not recognize the warranty as a contract as alleged. Rather, Defendant argues that the fact that the warranty was provided to Plaintiffs in connection with the vehicle sale does not arise to a direct dealing between the parties.

 

The Court finds that the relationship between Plaintiffs and Defendant arises from a specific contractual transaction—the alleged “warranty contract.” (Compl., ¶ 6.) Defendant entered into such a relationship with Plaintiff, and not merely with the¿general public, creating a sufficient “transactional relationship” supporting a duty to disclose. (See Rattagan, supra, 17 Cal.5th at p. 41, citing Bigler-Engler v. Breg, Inc. (2017) 7 Cal.App.5th 276, 312; see also¿Scherer v. FCA US, LLC¿(S.D. Cal. 2021) 565 F.Supp.3d 1184, 1194 [discussing the¿Bigler-Engler¿case and holding that a duty to disclose was properly grounded in the parties’ contractual relationship reflected in the warranty agreement].)¿Further, the Complaint alleges that Defendant has superior and exclusive knowledge of the defect, as discussed previously. (See Compl., ¶¶ 49, 51-53, 56)

 

Thus, the Court finds that the Complaint adequately alleges grounds for Defendant’s duty to disclose.

 

          Economic Loss Rule

 

“A plaintiff may assert a tort claim for fraudulent concealment based on conduct occurring in the course of a contractual relationship, if the elements of the cause of action can be established independently of the parties' contractual rights and obligations and the tortious conduct exposes the plaintiff to a risk of harm beyond the reasonable contemplation of the parties when they entered into the agreement. (Rattagan, supra, 17 Cal.5th 1, 38.)

 

Defendant argues that the fraudulent inducement – concealment claim is barred by the economic loss rule according to Rattagan. Specifically, Defendant argues that the “risk that the transmission in Plaintiff[s’] vehicle might be defective is precisely one of the risks contemplated and allocated to GM when Plaintiff[s] accepted GM’s warranty promises.” (Dem., 15:17-19.) Plaintiffs’ remedies sound in breach of the warranty, not in tort.

 

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that the economic loss rule does not apply to claims for fraudulent inducement. As Plaintiffs have alleged fraud in the inducement, the Rattagan case’s rule applying the economic loss rule to fraud-in-the-performance claims does not apply. Rather, the Dhital case held that the economic loss rule does not apply to fraudulent inducement claims. Even if the Court applies the Rattagan test, the economic loss rule still does not apply because 1) the warranty did not allocate the risks of Defendant fraudulently concealing defects, 2) Defendant has an independent tort duty to refrain from fraudulent practices, and 3) Defendant breach of its duty disclose such defects are separate from its duties to repair the Subject Vehicle under the warranty.

 

The Court finds that Plaintiffs allege fraudulent concealment claim is partially based on omissions that occurred prior to the sale of the Subject Vehicle—i.e., it is based on fraudulent inducement. Thus, the Rattagan test in applying the economic loss rule does not apply. (Compl., ¶ 47.)

 

To the extent the Complaint is based on alleged concealment during the course of the contract (Compl., ¶ 47), the Court finds that a party purchasing a vehicle does not bargain with the expectation that the automotive manufacturer is concealing known material defects from them. The risks of such a concealment are not allocated within the warranty. There are also public policy reasons, exclusive of the contract, for not wanting an automotive manufacturer to withhold information on such defects, including safety. As noted by the Rattagan court, public policy has viewed fraudulent concealment on equal footing with affirmative misrepresentations. (Rattagan, supra, 17 Cal.5th 1, 26.) These public policy reasons are exclusive of the contract, and therefore tort liability would exist in such instances of fraudulent concealment. Plaintiffs’ fifth cause of action is therefore not barred by the economic loss rule.

 

Based on the foregoing, Defendant’s Demurrer to the fifth cause of action in the Complaint is OVERRULED.

 

Motion to Strike

 

Code of Civil Procedure, section 436 permits trial courts to strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in a pleading:

 

“The court may, upon a motion made pursuant to Section 435, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper:

(a)¿Strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading.

(b)¿Strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court.”

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 436.)

 

Defendant moves to strike the following portions of the Complaint relating to civil penalties:

 

-      Paragraphs 29, 30, and 31 in the First Cause of Action

-      Paragraph 36 in the Second Cause of Action

-      Paragraph 39 in the Third cause of Action

-      Subdivision (f) of the Prayer

 

Defendant argues the Complaint fails to allege that Plaintiffs complied with certain statutory requirements for seeking civil penalties, and thus, the allegations must be stricken.

 

Civil Code of Procedure section 871.24, effective July 1, 2025, states the following, in relevant part:

 

“(a) At least 30 days prior to the commencement of an action seeking civil penalties under subdivision (c) of Section 1794 of the Civil Code, the consumer shall do all of the following:

(1) Notify the manufacturer of the consumer's name, the accurate Vehicle Identification Number (“VIN”) of the motor vehicle, and a brief summary of the repair history and problems with the motor vehicle.

(2) Demand that the manufacturer repurchase or replace the motor vehicle.

 

[…]

 

(c) At the time that the notice submitted pursuant to subdivision (a) is sent, the consumer shall have possession of the motor vehicle.

 

[…]

 

(h) An action seeking restitution or replacement under Section 871.20 may be commenced without compliance with subdivision (a). In that event, the consumer shall have possession of the motor vehicle at the time of the filing of the complaint, and shall not seek civil penalties, whether by amendment of the complaint or otherwise.”

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 871.24, subds. (a)(1)-(2), (c), (h), emphasis added.)

 

Defendant argues that Complaint fails to allege that Plaintiffs complied with the 30-day notice requirement pursuant to Civil Code of Procedure section 871.24, subdivisions (a) and (c) prior to filing this action.

 

No opposition has been filed.

 

The Court agrees with Defendant. The Complaint is devoid of allegations that Plaintiffs complied with the pre-filing notice requirement pursuant to Civil Code of Procedure section 871.24, subdivisions (a) and (c). Thus, Plaintiffs are not entitled to civil penalties.

 

Accordingly, Defendant’s Motion to Strike is GRANTED without leave to amend.

 

 


Case Number: 26NWCV01856    Hearing Date: August 11, 2026    Dept: L

     I.        Defendant Mitsubishi Motors North America, Inc.’s Application for Admission Pro Hac Vice of Rachel A. Sternlieb is GRANTED.

    II.        Defendant Mitsubishi Motors North America, Inc.’s Application for Admission Pro Hac Vice of Brandon L. Bigelow is GRANTED.

 

Defendant to give notice.

 

Background

 

This is an action for breach of contract. Defendant Mitsubishi Motors North America, Inc. (“MMNA”) now applies for admission pro hac vice of Rachel A. Sternlieb and Brandon L. Bigelow.

 

No oppositions have been filed as of August 10, 2026.


Legal Standard

 

California Rules of Court Rule 9.40 provides that an attorney in good standing in another jurisdiction may apply to appear pro hac vice in this State by way of written application with proof of service by mail and notice of hearing on the application to all parties who have appeared in the case and on the State Bar of California at its San Francisco office, so long as that attorney is not a resident of California, does not work in California, and does not perform regular or substantial business, professional, or other activities in the State. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(a), (c)(1).) The State Bar of California may set an appropriate application fee to by paid by counsel pro hac vice. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(e).)

 

The written application must provide the following information: (1) applicant attorney’s residence and office addresses; (2) the courts to which the applicant attorney has been admitted and dates of admission; (3) representation that the attorney applicant is a member in good standing in the courts of admission; (4) representation that the attorney is not currently suspended or disbarred in any court; (5) the title of each court and action in which the applicant attorney has appeared pro hac vice in this State in the preceding two years, if any; and (6) the name, address, and telephone number of the active California State Bar member who is counsel of record in the local action. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(d).)
