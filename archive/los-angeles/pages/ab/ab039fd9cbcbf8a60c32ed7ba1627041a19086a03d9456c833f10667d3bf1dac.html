DEPARTMENT 516 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 23STCV25116    Hearing Date: July 15, 2026    Dept: 516

Judge James I. Montgomery

Department 516

Hearing Date:             July 15, 2026 

Case Name:                 Liu v. RPM Property Management, LLC, et al.

Case No.:                    23STCV25116

Matter:                        Demurrer with Motion to Strike   

Moving Party:             Defendants RPM Property Management, LLC, Chen-Wei Liu, also known as Victor Liu, Yiuli Yeh, and Catherine Liu

Responding Party:       Plaintiff Hsiao Yen Liu, also known as Grace Liu

 

Tentative Ruling:      Defendants RPM Property Management, LLC, Chen-Wei Liu, also known as Victor Liu, Yiuli Yeh, and Catherine Liu’s demurrer is overruled in its entirety.

 

                                    Defendants RPM Property Management, LLC, Chen-Wei Liu, also known as Victor Liu, Yiuli Yeh, and Catherine Liu’s motion to strike is denied in its entirety.

 

             Plaintiff Hsiao Yen Liu, also known as Grace Liu (“Plaintiff”), filed this action against

RPM Property Management, LLC (“RPM”), Chen-Wei Liu, also known as Victor Liu , Yiuli

Yeh, and Catherine Liu (collectively “Defendants”) and Does 1 through 10. The First-Amended

Complaint (“FAC”) alleges five causes of action for: (1) accounting; (2) failure to pay

distributions; (3) breach of fiduciary duty; (4) conversion; and (5) money had and received.

 

            Defendants demur to the whole FAC and move to strike portions of the FAC. Defendants

oppose.

 

            Plaintiff dismissed her fourth cause of action against Defendants on June 3, 2026.

           

Legal Standard

 

A demurrer is an objection to a pleading, the grounds for which are apparent from either the face of the complaint or a matter of which the court may take judicial notice.¿(Code Civ. Proc., § 430.30, subd. (a); see also Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)¿The purpose of a demurrer is to challenge the sufficiency of a pleading by raising questions of law.¿(Postley v. Harvey (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 280, 286.)¿“In the construction of a pleading, for the purpose of determining its effect, its allegations must be liberally construed, with a view to substantial justice between the parties.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 452.)¿The court “ ‘ “treat[s] the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law . . . .” ’ ”¿ (Berkley v. Dowds (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 518, 525.)¿In applying these standards, the court liberally construes the complaint to determine whether a cause of action has been stated.¿(Picton v. Anderson Union High School Dist. (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 726, 733.) 

            “The court may, upon a motion made pursuant to Section 435, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper: (a) Strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (b) Strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court.”¿(Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) “Immaterial” or “irrelevant” matters include allegations not essential to the claim, allegations neither pertinent to nor supported by an otherwise sufficient claim or a demand for judgment requesting relief not supported by the allegations of the complaint. (Code Civ. Proc., § 431.10, subds. (b)(1)-(3).) 

Request for Judicial Notice

 

            Defendants request that the Court take judicial notice of two exhibits: (1) an equity agreement; and (2) discovery responses from the consolidated case.

 

            The Court denies the request for judicial notice because there is no basis for the Court to take judicial notice of these documents.

 

Analysis

 

            Meet and Confer

 

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, “a demurring party is required to meet and confer in person, by telephone, or video conference with the party that filed the pleading. . . .” (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41, subd. (a).) A moving party in a motion to strike must meet and confer before filing the motion. (Code Civ. Proc. §435.5, subd. (a).)

 

            Defendants contend that the parties met and conferred over the telephone. (Sugars Decl., ¶ 4.) Thus, the parties fulfilled their meet-and-confer obligations.

 

            Demurrer

 

                        Preliminary Matters

 

            California Rules of Court, rule 3.1320(a) states that “[e]ach ground of demurrer must be in a separate paragraph and must state whether it applies to the entire complaint, cross-complaint, or answer, or to specified causes of action or defenses.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1320(a).)

 

            Here, Defendants’ demurrer does not specify each basis for its demurrer for each cause of action, and instead groups the demurrer as to the entire FAC, which is procedurally improper. The Court will address the demurrer in the same manner that Defendants demur to the FAC.

 

                        Basis for Demurrer

 

            Defendants demur to Plaintiff’s FAC on the basis that all causes of action are derivative causes of action and that her claims are time-barred.

 

                                    Derivative Claims

 

            ““A shareholder's derivative suit seeks to recover for the benefit of the corporation and its whole body of shareholders when injury is caused to the corporation that may not otherwise be redressed because of failure of the corporation to act. Thus, ‘the action is derivative, i.e., in the corporate right, if the gravamen of the complaint is injury to the corporation, or to the whole body of its stock and property without any severance or distribution among individual holders, or it seeks to recover assets for the corporation or to prevent the dissipation of its assets.’ [Citations.] ‘... The stockholder's individual suit, on the other hand, is a suit to enforce a right against the corporation which the stockholder possesses as an individual.’ [Citation.]” (PacLink Communications Intern., Inc. v. Superior Court (2001) 90 Cal.App.4th 958, 964.) “In determining whether an individual action as opposed to a derivative action lies, a court looks at ‘the gravamen of the wrong alleged in the pleadings.’” (Id. at p. 965.)

 

            Defendants argue that Plaintiff has no basis to pursue these claims individually because they argue that Plaintiff sold her interest in RPM. (Mot., 8:5-7.) However, the FAC alleges that Plaintiff “is, and at all times material to this action has been, the owner of an interest in” RPM. (FAC, ¶ 11.) Defendants are requesting that the Court make an evidentiary determination based on the items that they requested the Court to take judicial notice of, which the Court denied. The demurrer as written alleges that Plaintiff is an owner of the RPM, and in the demurer stage the Court must take the allegation as true. Similarly, Defendants argue that Plaintiff has not furnished a security or contributed capital towards the formation of RPM. However, these arguments also exceed the four corners of the FAC.

 

            Thus, Defendants’ demurrer is overruled.

 

                        Statute of Limitations

 

            Defendants also contend that Plaintiff’s FAC is barred by the statute of limitations.

 

            ““The defense of statute of limitations may be asserted by general demurrer if the complaint shows on its face that the statute bars the action.” ([Citations.]) There is an important qualification, however: “[i]n order for the bar of the statute of limitations to be raised by demurrer, the defect must clearly and affirmatively appear on the face of the complaint; it is not enough that the complaint shows merely that the action may be barred.”” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Services (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315–1316 (E-Fab, Inc.) (citations omitted); Committee for Green Foothills v. Santa Clara County Bd. of Supervisors (2010) 48 Cal.4th 32, 42.) “To address these concerns, we have long settled that separate, recurring invasions of the same right can each trigger their own statute of limitations.” (Aryeh v. Canon Business Solutions, Inc. (2013) 55 Cal.4th 1185, 1198.)

 

            As addressed above, Defendants seek for the Court to make evidentiary findings based on the items it requested the Court take judicial notice of. The FAC also does not make an affirmative showing that her claims are time-barred. (Compl., ¶¶ 14-15.)

 

            Thus, the demurrer is overruled.

 

            Motion to Strike

 

            Defendants move to strike all information pertaining to “conduct, failures, refusals, distributions, payments, or breaches occurring prior to the applicable statutes of limitation cutoff dates.” (Mot., 2:20-23.) Defendants specifically identify the following paragraphs: 14, 15, 16, 23-27, 31-34, Ex. , and portions of the Prayer for Relief seeking damages based on time-barred conduct.

 

            Defendant relies on the same arguments as in their demurrer, asserting that the claims are barred by the statute of limitations; however, as addressed in this ruling, the alleged defects must “clearly and affirmatively appear on the face of the complaint.” (E-Fab, Inc, supra, 153 Cal.App.4th at p. 1316.) These alleged defects are not clearly and affirmatively alleged in the FAC. Thus, the Court cannot determine whether these claims are barred or not at this stage of the proceedings. Similarly, Defendants rely on evidence that is outside the four corners of the pleading, which is also procedurally improper.

 

            Thus, Defendants’ motion to strike is denied in its entirety.

 

Conclusion

 

Defendants RPM Property Management, LLC, Chen-Wei Liu, also known as Victor Liu, Yiuli Yeh, and Catherine Liu’s demurrer is overruled in its entirety.

 

            Defendants RPM Property Management, LLC, Chen-Wei Liu, also known as Victor Liu, Yiuli Yeh, and Catherine Liu’s motion to strike is denied in its entirety.

 

 


Case Number: 26STCV01633    Hearing Date: July 15, 2026    Dept: 516

Judge James I. Montgomery

Department 516

Hearing Date:             July 15, 2026 

Case Name:                 Lee v. Ko, et al.

Case No.:                    26STCV01633

Matter:                        Motion for Leave to File an Anti-SLAPP  

Moving Party:             Defendants David Ko, Audrey Ko, Eden Home Care, Inc., and Eden Hospice Care, Inc.

Responding Party:       Plaintiff Young-Jik Lee

 

Tentative Ruling:      Defendants David Ko, Audrey Ko, Eden Home Care, Inc., and Eden Hospice Care, Inc.’s motion is denied.

 

 

            Plaintiff Young-Jik Lee (“Plaintiff”) filed this action against Defendants 1818

Western, LLC, David Ko (“Ko”), Audrey Ko, Eden Home Care, Inc., and Eden

Hospice Care, Inc., and Does 1 to 100. The Complaint alleges 15 causes of action for: (1) breach

of contract; (2) breach of fiduciary duty; (3) fraudulent concealment; (4) violation of California

Corporate Code section 17704.10; (5) declaratory relief; (6) derivative claim for breach of

fiduciary duty; (7) derivative claim for conspiracy to commit breach of fiduciary duty; (8)

derivative claim for aiding and abetting breach of fiduciary duty; (9) derivative claim for

fraudulent concealment; (10) derivative claim for conspiracy to commit fraud; (11) derivative

claim for aiding and abetting fraud; (12) derivative claim for violation of California Penal Code

section 496; (13) derivative claim for violation of the California Business and Professions Code

section 17200; (14) derivative claim for judicial expulsion of member under California

Corporations Code section 17706.02, subdivision (e); and (15) direct and derivative claim for

appointment of a receiver under California Civil Procedure Code section 564.

 

            Defendants Ko, Audrey Ko, Eden Home Care, Inc., and Eden Hospice Care, Inc. (collectively “Defendants”) move for leave to file an Anti-SLAPP motion past the statutory sixty days. Plaintiff opposes.

 

Legal Standard

 

            Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16, subdivision (f), states that “[t]he special motion may be filed within 60 days of the service of the complaint or, in the court’s discretion, at any later time upon terms it deems proper. The motion shall be scheduled by the clerk of the court for a hearing not more than 30 days after the service of the motion unless the docket conditions of the court require a later hearing.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (f).)

 

            “[A] trial court must be wary about freely granting a party the right to file an anti-SLAPP motion past the 60–day deadline.” (Platypus Wear, Inc. v. Goldberg (2008) 166 Cal.App.4th 772, 787.) “The overall purpose of the SLAPP statute is to provide defendants with a procedural remedy “which would allow prompt exposure and dismissal of SLAPP suits.” The 60 day period in which a defendant may file a SLAPP motion as a matter of right appears to be intended to permit the defendant to test the foundation of the plaintiff's action before having to “devote its time, energy and resources to combating” a “meritless” lawsuit.” (Morin v. Rosenthal (2004) 122 Cal.App.4th 673, 681, as modified on denial of reh'g (Oct. 15, 2004) (Morin).)

 

Analysis

 

            Defendants argue that leave should be granted because: (1) the case remains in its earliest stages and no trial date has been set; (2) the challenged claims arise from communications required by federal regulations to government regulatory agencies, constituting protected petitioning activity; (3) Defendants had compelling reasons for not filing within sixty days, including the need to address the existential threat to the Company’s solvency and the recent association of new counsel; and (4) Dr. Lee will not be prejudiced by permitting Movants to file the anti-SLAPP Motion at this time. (Not., 2:12-19.)

 

            “Discretion to permit or deny an untimely motion cannot turn on the final determination of the merits of the motion.” (Olsen, supra, 134 Cal.App.4th at p. 286.) “In determining whether to permit a late motion, the most important consideration is whether the filing advances the anti-SLAPP statute's purpose of examining the merits of covered lawsuits in the early stages of the proceedings. ([Citation.]) Other relevant factors include the length of the delay, the reasons for the late filing, and any undue prejudice to the plaintiff.” (San Diegans for Open Government v. Har Construction, Inc. (2015) 240 Cal.App.4th 611, 624.)

 

            Defendants argue that they are entitled to leave to file an Anti-SLAPP past the 60 days of service from the Complaint. The Court is not persuaded by Defendants’ arguments. While Defendants repeatedly argue that this case is in its early stages, the number of motions and appearances before the Court demonstrates the opposite. Both parties have heavily prosecuted and defended the present action and cross-action.  

 

            Here, Defendants have filed an answer, a demurrer, and several ex parte applications. Defendants chose to “devote their time, energy and resources” to defend their case. (Morin, supra, 122 Cal.App.4th at p. 681.) Ko has filed a motion for a preliminary injunction, which the Court denied. Defendants also filed a Cross-Complaint and a First Amended Cross-Complaint. Moreover, Defendants have served responses to propounded discovery. (Clements Decl., ¶ 6.) The Court also notes that there is an Informal Discovery Conference and six motions to compel further discovery responses scheduled for later this month, and a motion for a protective order scheduled for September. Thus, granting leave beyond the 60-day period would not advance the purpose of Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16. The Court finds that Defendants made a strategic choice not to exercise their right to file an anti-SLAPP within the first 60 days.

 

            Defendants also argue that they needed to hire new counsel, as a reason for their delay. Defendants were served with the Complaint on January 20, 2026, and  new counsel was brought into this case on May 5, 2026. The Courts have determined that “[a] claim of excuse from untimeliness based on late discovery after obtaining new counsel is generally unavailing.” (Olsen, supra, 134 Cal.App.4th at p. 285; Platypus Wear, Inc. v. Goldberg (2008) 166 Cal.App.4th 772, 786.) Defendants have been represented throughout this action, and their former counsel had an opportunity to file their motion before the 60 days elapsed. Thus, the Court finds that there is no basis for the Court to exercise its discretion to grant Defendants’ motion.

 

            Therefore, Defendants’ motion for leave to file an anti-SLAPP is denied.

 

            Conclusion

 

            Defendants David Ko, Audrey Ko, Eden Home Care, Inc., and Eden Hospice Care, Inc.’s motion is denied.


Case Number: 26STCV09081    Hearing Date: July 15, 2026    Dept: 516

Judge James I. Montgomery

Department 516

Hearing Date:             July 15, 2026             

Case Name:               26STCV09081

Case No.:                   Mirakhor, et al. v. Galen, et al.

Matter:                        Demurrer with Motion to Strike  

Moving Party:             Defendants Steven Galen doing business as The Colonial Square, Suzanne Galen, individually and as Trustee of The Eugene H. Galen and Suzanne E. Galen Family Trust, and Sead Nurkovic

Responding Party:       Plaintiff Ebrahim Mirakhor and Treza Mirakhor

 

Tentative Ruling:      Defendants Steven Galen, doing business as The Colonial Square, Suzanne Galen, individually and as Trustee of The Eugene H. Galen and Suzanne E. Galen Family Trust, and Sead Nurkovic’s demurrer is overruled in part and sustained in part.

 

                                    Defendants’ motion to strike was not filed with the Court.

 

 

            Plaintiff Ebrahim Mirakhor and Treza Mirakhor (“Plaintiffs”) filed this action against Defendants Steven Galen doing business as The Colonial Square, Suzanne Galen, individually and as Trustee of The Eugene H. Galen and Suzanne E. Galen Family Trust, and Sead Nurkovic (collectively “Defendants”). The Complaint alleges eight causes of action for: (1) negligence; (2) constructive eviction; (3) violation of the Los Angeles Tenant Anti-Harassment Ordinance; (4) breach of contract; (5) nuisance; (6) trespass; (7) intentional infliction of emotional distress; and (8) unfair business practices.

 

            Defendants demur to Plaintiffs’ third, sixth, and seventh causes of action. Plaintiff opposes.

 

            The Court notes that Defendants reserved this hearing as a motion to strike, and that an opposition and reply were filed; however, the motion to strike was not filed with the Court.

 

Legal Standard

 

A demurrer is an objection to a pleading, the grounds for which are apparent from either the face of the complaint or a matter of which the court may take judicial notice.¿(Code Civ. Proc., § 430.30, subd. (a); see also Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)¿The purpose of a demurrer is to challenge the sufficiency of a pleading by raising questions of law.¿(Postley v. Harvey (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 280, 286.)¿“In the construction of a pleading, for the purpose of determining its effect, its allegations must be liberally construed, with a view to substantial justice between the parties.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 452.)¿The court “ ‘ “treat[s] the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law . . . .” ’ ”¿ (Berkley v. Dowds (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 518, 525.)¿In applying these standards, the court liberally construes the complaint to determine whether a cause of action has been stated.¿(Picton v. Anderson Union High School Dist. (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 726, 733.) 

            “The court may, upon a motion made pursuant to Section 435, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper: (a) Strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (b) Strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court.”¿(Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) “Immaterial” or “irrelevant” matters include allegations not essential to the claim, allegations neither pertinent to nor supported by an otherwise sufficient claim or a demand for judgment requesting relief not supported by the allegations of the complaint. (Code Civ. Proc., § 431.10, subds. (b)(1)-(3).) 

 

Analysis

 

            Meet and Confer

 

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, “a demurring party is required to meet and confer in person, by telephone, or video conference with the party that filed the pleading. . . .” (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41, subd. (a).) A moving party in a motion to strike must meet and confer before filing the motion. (Code Civ. Proc. §435.5, subd. (a).)

 

            Defendants state that the parties met and conferred on April 20, 2026. (Messih Decl., ¶ 3.) However, the parties did not fulfill their obligation to meet and confer in accordance with Code of Civil Procedure, section 430.41, subdivision (a). In the interest of judicial efficiency, the Court will address the merits of Defendants’ demurrer and motion to strike.

 

            Demurrer

 

            Defendant demurs to Plaintiffs’ third, sixth, and seventh causes of action on the basis of uncertainty and for failure to allege sufficient facts.

 

                        Uncertainty

 

Per Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (f), a demurrer must be sustained if a pleading is uncertain. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (f).) The term uncertain means “ambiguous and unintelligible.” (Ibid.) However, demurrers for uncertainty will not be sustained unless “‘the pleading is so incomprehensi[ve] that a defendant could not reasonably respond.’” (A.J. Fistes Corp. v. GDL Best Contractors, Inc. (2019) 38 Cal.App.5th 677, 695 , quoting Mahan v. Charles W. Chan Ins. Agency, Inc. (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 841, 848, fn.3 (A.J. Fistes Corp.).)

 

            In looking at the Complaint as a whole, it is not incomprehensible to sustain the demurrer on this basis. The Complaint alleges sufficient facts for Defendants to respond.

 

            Thus, the Court overrules the demurrer.

 

                        Failure to Allege Sufficient Facts

 

                                    Third Cause of Action—Violation of the Los Angeles Tenant Anti-

                                    Harassment

 

            Los Angeles Municipal Code section 45.33, also known as the Tenant Anti-Harassment Ordinance (“TAHO”), prohibits landlords from engaging in a wide range of harassing conduct directed at tenants with the intent to influence them to vacate their rental unit or waive rights under law. Prohibited conduct includes reducing or eliminating housing services required by lease or law, failing to make necessary repairs, engaging in repeated acts or omissions that disturb a tenant’s comfort or peace, and other actions or threats that interfere with a tenant’s lawful occupancy. (LAMC § 45.33.) Tenant Harassment is defined as a landlord’s bad-faith conduct directed at a specific tenant or tenants that causes the latter detriment or harm. (Ibid.)  “‘Bad faith’ refers to willful, reckless, or grossly negligent conduct.” (Ibid.)  

           

            Defendants contend that Plaintiffs did not allege any facts to support that Defendants acted in “bad faith.” Defendants argue that a violation of TAHO requires more than “an existence of ongoing maintenance issues.” (Mot., 6:13-15.)

           

            The Complaint alleges that Defendants did not respond to rodent infestation over a month. (Compl., ¶¶ 13-15.) Plaintiffs allege that issue was ongoing and that from April 2024 through June 2025, Plaintiffs would not use their air conditioning and heater out of “fear[] it would blow debris or feces into the unit or worsen the foul odor from the vents. Plaintiffs endured falling debris in the bathroom, constant scratching and squeaking inside the walls, the stench of rodent urine and feces, and a mite infestation.” (Compl., ¶ 19.) Moreover, the Complaint also alleges that Defendant Suzanne Galen told Plaintiffs that they may prefer to live elsewhere, questioned why Plaintiffs did not move, and recommended that Plaintiffs move. (Compl., ¶¶ 21-25.)

 

            While Defendants contend that the allegations do not support bad faith conduct, the Court finds this argument unpersuasive. At this stage of the proceedings, the Court finds that sufficient allegations have been made in the Complaint to allege a cause of action.

 

            Thus, the Court overrules the demurrer as to the third cause of action.

 

                                    Sixth Cause of Action—Trespass

 

            “Generally, landowners and tenants have a right to exclude persons from trespassing on private property; the right to exclude persons is a fundamental aspect of private property ownership.” (Ralphs Grocery Co. v. Victory Consultants, Inc. (2017) 17 Cal.App.5th 245, 258, as modified (Nov. 6, 2017).) “[I]n order to state a cause of action for trespass a plaintiff must allege an unauthorized and tangible entry on the land of another, which interfered with the plaintiff's exclusive possessory rights.” (McBride v. Smith (2018) 18 Cal.App.5th 1160, 1174.)

 

            Plaintiffs allege that Defendants trespassed in Plaintiffs’ unit by allowing mice and rodents to enter their unit without their consent. (Compl., ¶¶ 74-75.) Plaintiff argues that vermin, mice, and rats may be the basis for trespass according to Roberts. (Opp., 6:20-21.) However, the Court is not persuaded. It is unclear to the Court how Roberts supports the proposition that vermin, mice, and rodents constitute a trespass. In the absence of any legal authority to support its argument, the Court finds that Plaintiffs have not alleged sufficient facts to support the cause of action.

 

            Thus, the Court sustains the demurrer as to the sixth cause of action.

 

            Seventh Cause of Action—Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress

 

            “The elements of the tort for intentional infliction of mental distress are: (1) outrageous conduct by the defendant, (2) intention to cause or reckless disregard of the probability of causing emotional distress, (3) severe emotional suffering and (4) actual and proximate causation of emotional distress.” (Stoiber v. Honeychuck (1980) 101 Cal.App.3d 903, 921.)  “A defendant's conduct is “outrageous” when it is so “‘“extreme as to exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated in a civilized community.” ” [Citation.] And the defendant's conduct must be “‘“intended to inflict injury or engaged in with the realization that injury will result.”’”” (Hughes v. Pair (2009) 46 Cal.4th 1035, 1050–1051.) ““The requirement that the defendant's conduct be directed primarily at the plaintiff is a factor which distinguishes intentional infliction of emotional distress from the negligent infliction of such injury.”” (Potter v. Firestone Tire & Rubber Co. (1993) 6 Cal.4th 965, 1002.)

 

            Defendants argue that the Complaint is devoid of allegation that establish that Defendants intended to cause intentional infliction of emotional distress, or that Defendants suffered severe emotional distress. The Court agrees, The Complaint alleges conclusory statements to support its cause of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress. (See Compl., ¶¶ 26-27; 80-89.)

 

            Thus, the Court sustains the demurrer as to the seventh cause of action.

           

            Motion to Strike

 

            As addressed above, it appears that Defendants served a motion to strike but did not timely file it with the Court. The Court is unable to rule on Defendants’ motion. 

 

Leave to Amend

 

Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (See Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349 [court shall not “sustain a demurrer without leave to amend if there is any reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment”]; Kong v. City of Hawaiian Gardens Redevelopment Agency (2002) 108 Cal.App.4th 1028, 1037 [“A demurrer should not be sustained without leave to amend if the complaint, liberally construed, can state a cause of action under any theory or if there is a reasonable possibility the defect can be cured by amendment.”]; Vaccaro v. Kaiman (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 761, 768 [“When the defect which justifies striking a complaint is capable of cure, the court should allow leave to amend.”].) The burden is on the complainant to show the Court that the pleading can be successfully amended. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

 

Plaintiffs will be given the opportunity to amend as to the sixth and seventh causes of action.

 

Conclusion

 

            Defendants Steven Galen doing business as The Colonial Square, Suzanne Galen, Individually and as Trustee of The Eugene H. Galen and Suzanne E. Galen Family Trust, and Sead Nurkovic’s demurrer to the third cause of action is overruled.

 

            Defendants Steven Galen doing business as The Colonial Square, Suzanne Galen, Individually and as Trustee of The Eugene H. Galen and Suzanne E. Galen Family Trust, and Sead Nurkovic ‘s demurrer as to the sixth and seventh cause of action is sustained.

 

            Plaintiffs shall have leave to amend and must file their amended pleading by August 14, 2026.

 

 


Case Number: 26STCV10334    Hearing Date: July 15, 2026    Dept: 516

Judge James I. Montgomery

Department 516

Hearing Date:             July 15, 2026 

Case Name:                 Daghlian v. Cliffside Malibu, et al.

Case No.:                    26STCV10334       Matter:                        Motion to Compel Arbitration and Stay Proceedings

Moving Party:             Defendant Discovery Practice Management Inc.

Responding Party:       Plaintiff Sarin Daghlian

 

Tentative Ruling:      Defendant Discovery Practice Management Inc.’s motion is granted.

 

 

            Plaintiff Sarin Daghlian (“Plaintiff”) filed this action against Defendants Cliffside Malibu, Discovery Practice Management (“DPM”), Behavioral Health, and Does 1 through 20. The First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) seven causes of action for: (1) discrimination in violation of Government Code section 12940 et seq.; (2) retaliation in violation of Government Code section 12940 et seq.; (3) failure to prevent discrimination and retaliation in violation of Government Code section 12940(k); (4) failure to provide reasonable accommodation in violation of Government Code section 12940 et seq.; (5) failure to engage in good faith interactive process in violation of Government Code section 12940 et seq.; (6) for declaratory relief; and (7) wrongful termination in violation of public policy.

 

            DPM moves to compel arbitration of Plaintiff’s claims pursuant to the arbitration agreement that Plaintiff signed when she completed her onboarding paperwork. Plaintiff opposes.

 

Legal Standard

 

            Under both the Federal Arbitration Act and California law, arbitration agreements are valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, except on such grounds that exist at law or equity for voiding a contract. (Winter v. Window Fashions Professions, Inc. (2008) 166 Cal.App.4th 943, 947.) Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.2 authorizes the court to order arbitration of a case if it finds the parties agreed to arbitrate that dispute, “and that a party to the agreement refuses to arbitrate that controversy.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2, subd. (a).) Arbitration agreements should be liberally interpreted and ordered unless the agreement clearly does not apply to the dispute in question.  (Weeks v. Crow (1980) 113 Cal.App.3d 350, 353; Segal v. Silberstein (2007) 156 Cal.App.4th 627, 633.)   

 

            The party moving to compel arbitration has the initial burden to (1) affirmatively admit and allege the existence of a written arbitration agreement, and (2) prove the existence of that agreement by a preponderance of the evidence.  (Rosenthal v. Great W. Fin. Sec. Corp, 14 Cal. 4th 394, 413 (Rosenthal).)  Once this is met, the burden shifts to the responding party to prove that the agreement is unenforceable by a preponderance of the evidence. (Ibid.)   

 

            “‘Doubts as to whether an arbitration clause applies to a particular dispute are to be resolved in favor of sending the parties to arbitration. The court should order them to arbitrate unless it is clear that the arbitration clause cannot be interpreted to cover the dispute.’” (California Correctional Peace Officers Assn. v. State¿(2006) 142 Cal.App.4th 198, 205.) 

 

Evidentiary Objections

 

            Plaintiff makes the five evidentiary objections to Beth Randall’s declaration. The Court overrules all five of Plaintiff's evidentiary objections to Beth Randall’s declaration.

           

            Plaintiff makes the nine evidentiary objections to Jesse Rio’s declaration. The Court overrules all nine evidentiary objections to Jesse Rio’s declaration.

 

Analysis

 

            DPM  moves to compel arbitration of Plaintiff’s claims against it and stay the matter pending arbitration.

 

            Federal Arbitration Agreement

 

            Defendant argues the Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”) governs the Arbitration Agreement (“Agreement”). The Court agrees. The Agreement expressly states:

 

This Agreement shall be enforced under the provisions of the Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”) (9 USC §§ 1, et seq.) and preempts state arbitration statutes, including but not limited to the California Arbitration Act, to the fullest extent permitted by law. Employer and Employee agree that Employer and its relationship with Employee involve and relate to interstate commerce and therefore the FAA applies to and governs this Agreement.

(Randall Decl., Ex. A, ¶ 2.)

 

Plaintiff does not dispute the applicability of the FAA.

 

            Thus, the FAA applies.

 

            Existence of a Valid Agreement

 

            Defendant seeks to compel arbitration based on a 2025 Agreement that Plaintiff executed when she began her employment and completed her onboarding. In support of the existence of the Agreement, Defendant submits as evidence the executed Agreement signed by Plaintiff on October 27, 2025, at 9:19:24 a.m. (Randall Decl., Ex. A.) The Agreement provides that “any and all claims, controversies or disputes (“Disputes”) relating to any aspect of Employee’s employment with Employer” shall be resolved through binding arbitration. (Randall Decl., Ex. A, ¶ 1.) Specifically, the Agreement states that:

 

1. Final and Binding Arbitration: Employer and Employee agree that any and all claims, controversies or disputes (“Disputes”) relating to any aspect of Employee’s employment with Employer (pre-hire through post-termination), which Employer may have against Employee, or which Employee may have against Employer or any related entity, owner, partner, officer, director, shareholder, employee, contractor, representative or agent, shall be resolved through final and binding arbitration, except as otherwise provided by law or this Agreement. Employer and Employee acknowledge that they are relinquishing the right to a court trial or jury trial of any Dispute, except as otherwise provided by law or this Agreement. Employee’s continued employment with Employer will constitute assent to this Agreement.

(Ibid.)  

 

            Plaintiff does not dispute that she signed the agreement, nor did she submit any evidence to challenge the position that she did not sign the agreement. Plaintiff disputes the admissibility of the evidence regarding Defendant’s burden and that the Agreement does not apply to Plaintiff’s claims because the Ending Forced Arbitration of Sexual Assault and Sexual Harassment Act (“EFFA”) bars arbitration.  

 

            At this point in the analysis to establish the existence of an agreement to arbitrate, it is sufficient for the defendants to provide a copy of the arbitration agreement or state the paragraph verbatim.¿(See Rosenthal, supra, 14 Cal. 4th at p. 413; Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1330.) The court need not make an evidentiary determination of its validity at this stage. (Ruiz v. Moss Bros. Auto Group, Inc. (2014) 232 Cal.App.4th 836, 846 (Ruiz); Condee v. Longwood Management Corp. (2001) 88 Cal.App.4th 215, 218–219 (Condee).) Here, Defendant has met its burden.

 

            Thus, an Agreement exists between the parties. 

 

            Applicability of the Ending Forced Arbitration of Sexual Assault and Sexual Harassment Act

 

            Plaintiff argues that the Agreement does not apply to Plaintiff’s claims because her claims are barred from arbitration pursuant to the EFAA.

 

            Under the EFAA, a plaintiff “alleging conduct constituting sexual harassment or a sexual assault dispute” may elect to render invalid and unenforceable an arbitration provision applicable to their case. (9 U.S.C. §402(a).) The EFAA specifically states “at the election of the person alleging conduct constituting a sexual harassment dispute or sexual assault dispute, or the named representative of a class or in a collective action alleging such conduct, no predispute

arbitration agreement or predispute joint-action waiver shall be valid or enforceable with respect to a case which is filed under Federal, Tribal, or State law and relates to the sexual assault dispute or the sexual harassment dispute.” (9 U.S.C. §402(a), emphasis added.) A sexual harassment dispute is defined as “relating to conduct that is alleged to constitute sexual harassment under applicable Federal, Tribal, or State law.” (9 U.S.C.A. § 401(4).)

 

            Plaintiff argues that “pregnancy discrimination and pregnancy related harassment are forms of sex discrimination and sex-based harassment under California law.” (Opp., 2:13-14.) Plaintiff further contends that “[b]ecause Plaintiff's claims arise directly from Defendant's treatment of her after learning she was pregnant, this action falls squarely within the protections of the” EFAA. (Id. at p. 2:14-16.)

 

            Plaintiff cites four cases to support her position, Akers, Johnson, Roby, and Polluck. However, the Court finds these cases unpersuasive to support her arguments. It is unclear to the Court what Plaintiff’s reliance is on Akers and Johnson. In Akers, the Court addressed an adverse employment action, but the question before the Court is whether the EFFA bars Plaintiff’s claims from being compelled to arbitration. (Akers v. County of San Diego (2002) 95 Cal.App.4th 1441, 1454.) Likewise, in Johnson, the Court reversed and remanded a motion for summary judgment on the ground that a triable issue of material fact existed as to whether a pregnant employee was discriminated against. (Johnson v. United Cerebral Palsy/Spastic Children's Foundation of Los Angeles and Ventura Counties (2009) 173 Cal.App.4th 740, 766.)

 

            “[D]iscrimination refers to bias in the exercise of official actions on behalf of the employer, and harassment refers to bias that is expressed or communicated through interpersonal relations in the workplace.” (Roby v. McKesson Corp. (2009) 47 Cal.4th 686, 707, as modified (Feb. 10, 2010).) ““‘[H]arassment consists of conduct outside the scope of necessary job performance, conduct presumably engaged in for personal gratification, because of meanness or bigotry, or for other personal motives.... [¶] ... [¶] ... [C]ommonly necessary personnel management actions ... do not come within the meaning of harassment.... These actions may retrospectively be found discriminatory if based on improper motives, but in that event the remedies provided by the FEHA are those for discrimination, not harassment.... This significant distinction underlies the differential treatment of harassment and discrimination in the FEHA.’””
(Ibid.) “‘[A]lthough discrimination and harassment are separate wrongs, they are sometimes closely interrelated, and even overlapping, particularly with regard to proof.’” (Pollock v. Tri-Modal Distribution Services, Inc. (2021) 11 Cal.5th 918, 932.)

 

            While Plaintiff is correct that in some situations there is an overlap between harassment and discrimination, there is no evidence before the Court that supports that Plaintiff’s discrimination claims are interrelated with sexual harassment claims. Plaintiff is requesting that the Court expand the applicability of EFAA without any basis to do so.

 

            In reply, DPM argues that Doe and Casey do not support Plaintiff’s arguments. (Reply, 3:3-17.) The Court notes that Plaintiff did not cite to these cases. So, it is unclear why DPM disputes these cases.

 

            Moreover, in looking at Plaintiff’s FAC, she does not allege sexual harassment claims or any facts to support her claims that the EFFA is applicable. The Court notes that Plaintiff filed an FAC to include the word "gender” in her allegations. The Complaint alleges:

 

65. Plaintiff contends that her termination was a pretext for discrimination and retaliation based on perceived and/or actual disability, medical condition. Plaintiff had consistently provided updated doctor’s notes regarding her disability and ongoing medical care and was terminated right after sending in a doctor’s note.

(Compl., ¶ 65 (emphasis added).)

 

While the FAC alleges,

 

63. Plaintiff contends that her termination was a pretext for discrimination and retaliation based on perceived and/or actual disability, medical condition, pregnancy, gender, and/or future need for medical leave and childbirth. Plaintiff had consistently provided updated doctor’s notes regarding her disability and ongoing medical care and was terminated right after sending in a doctor’s note.

(FAC, ¶ 63 (emphasis added).)

 

            Throughout the FAC, the allegations made are about the “discrimination” and “retaliation” Plaintiff endured due to her medical condition—the complications she experienced with her pregnancy. (FAC, ¶¶ 64-66, 71, 78-79, 82, 92, 101, 109, 130, 141.) Plaintiff does not make any allegations regarding sexual abuse or harassment.

 

            Thus, the EFFA does not bar arbitration of Plaintiff’s claims.

 

            Authenticity of Plaintiff’s Signature

 

            “Once the petitioners had alleged that the agreement exists, the burden shifted to respondents to prove the falsity of the purported agreement.” (Condee, supra, 88 Cal.App.4th at p. 219.) “[T]he burden shifts to the party opposing arbitration to identify a factual dispute as to the agreement's existence—in this instance, by disputing the authenticity of their signatures. To bear this burden, the arbitration opponent must offer admissible evidence creating a factual dispute as to the authenticity of their signatures. The opponent need not prove that his or her purported signature is not authentic, but must submit sufficient evidence to create a factual dispute and shift the burden back to the arbitration proponent, who retains the ultimate burden of proving, by a preponderance of the evidence, the authenticity of the signature.” (Iyere v. Wise Auto Group (2023) 87 Cal.App.5th 747, 755 (Iyere).)

 

            Plaintiff’s contention is that she was unaware of any arbitration agreement and does not remember signing the Agreement. (Daghlian Decl., ¶¶ 3-4.) Plaintiff does not deny signing the Agreement, only that she does not recall reviewing it. (Id. at ¶¶ 4-8.) This is not enough to contend that she did not sign the Agreement. The facts of this case are analogous to Iyere. In Iyere, an employee did not state that he “had not signed the agreement, or that his physical signature was forged or inauthentic.” (Iyere, supra, 87 Cal.App.5th at p. 756.) Each employee also claimed, “‘I do not recall ever reading or signing any document entitled Binding Arbitration Agreement .... I do not know how my signature was placed on [the document].’ Each plaintiff stated further that if he had understood that the agreement waived his right to sue [employer], he would not have signed it.” (Ibid.) The court held that the employees did not create a factual dispute. (Ibid.) Here, Plaintiff makes the same claims in her declaration; thus, she does not meet her burden.

 

             Even assuming the burden is shifted back to DPM, it fulfills its burden to prove the existence of the agreement and authenticate Plaintiff’s signature. Plaintiff argues that the declarations do not authenticate her signature on the Agreement. The Court finds that the case law opposes her claim.  

 

            “Civil Code section 1633.9 addresses how a proponent of an electronic signature may authenticate the signature—that is, show the signature is, in fact, the signature of the person the proponent claims it is. The statute states: ‘(a) An electronic record or electronic signature is attributable to a person if it was the act of the person. The act of the person may be shown in any manner, including a showing of the efficacy of any security procedure applied to determine the person to which the electronic record or electronic signature was attributable.’” (Espejo, supra, 246 Cal.App.4th at p. 1061.)

 

            In Ruiz, the Court held that the employer did not authenticate the employee’s signature on the following grounds:

 

[The business manager] did not explain that an electronic signature in the name of “Ernesto Zamora Ruiz” could only have been placed on the 2011 agreement (i.e., on the employee acknowledgement form) by a person using Ruiz's “unique login ID and password”; that the date and time printed next to the electronic signature indicated the date and time the electronic signature was made; that all . . . employees were required to use their unique login ID and password when they logged into the HR system and signed electronic forms and agreements; and the electronic signature on the 2011 agreement was, therefore, apparently made by Ruiz on September 21, 2011, at 11:47 a.m. Rather than offer this or any other explanation of how she inferred the electronic signature on the 2011 agreement was the act of Ruiz, [the business manager] only offered her unsupported assertion that Ruiz was the person who electronically signed the 2011 agreement. In the face of Ruiz's failure to recall electronically signing the 2011 agreement, the fact the 2011 agreement had an electronic signature on it in the name of Ruiz, and a date and time stamp for the signature, was insufficient to support a finding that the electronic signature was, in fact, “the act of” Ruiz. (Civ. Code, § 1633.9, subd. (a).) For the same reason, the evidence was insufficient to support a finding that the electronic signature was what [employer] claimed it was: the electronic signature of Ruiz. (Evid. Code, § 1400, item (a).)

(Ruiz, supra, 232 Cal.App.4th at p. 844.)

 

            While in Espejo, the employer’s systems consultant detailed the employer’s security precautions regarding the transmission and use of an applicant’s unique username and password and the steps an applicant takes to sign the document, and then, based on those procedures, she concluded that the employee signed the arbitration agreement. (Espejo, supra, 246 Cal.App.4th at p. 1062.)

 

            Espejo is on point with the facts of this case. Beth Randall (“Randall”) is the “Director of Employee Experience & Engagement at DPM for the last eleven years, including throughout Plaintiff’s employment with DPM; one of my responsibilities is to implement and issue the Arbitration Agreement that Plaintiff signed.” (Randall Decl., ¶ 3.) Randall explained the procedures DPM executes when onboarding new employees. (Id. at p. ¶ 4.) While the declaration of Jesse Rios (“Rios”), who is the Director of Human Resources Operations for Defendant Discovery Practice Management, Inc., provides insight into the steps the employee takes to sign the arbitration agreement. (Rios Decl., ¶¶ 3-6.) Rios further declares that Plaintiff signed the Agreement “given this process for signing documents and protecting the privacy of the information with unique employee emails, passwords, and two-factor authentication, the electronic signature and name on Plaintiff’s Arbitration Agreement could have only been placed on the signature page of the Arbitration Agreement by someone using Plaintiff’s unique employee email and password, and with access to Plaintiff’s cellular phone, and the date and time placed on the Arbitration Agreement is the date and time the signature was made by Plaintiff.” (Rios Decl., ¶ 8.) The exhibit attached to Rios’s declaration  shows that Plaintiff viewed the Agreement at 9:18:32 a.m. on October 27, 2025, and signed it that same day at 9:19:24 a.m. through DocuSign. (Rios Decl., Ex. 6.)       

 

            Thus, DPM properly authenticated Plaintiff’s signature.

 

                        Delegation Clause

 

            “Parties to an arbitration agreement may agree to delegate to the arbitrator, instead of a court, questions regarding the enforceability of the agreement. ([Citation.]) They “can agree to arbitrate almost any dispute—even a dispute over whether the underlying dispute is subject to arbitration.”” (Tiri v. Lucky Chances, Inc. (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 231, 241(citations omitted).) There are two prerequisites for a delegation clause to be effective. First, the language of the clause must be clear and unmistakable. ([Citation.]) Second, the delegation must not be revocable under state contract defenses such as fraud, duress, or unconscionability. (Id. at p. 242.)

 

            Here, the Agreement is clear and unmistakable that the parties agreed to delegate arbitrability to the arbitrator. The Agreement states:

 

10. Enforceability of this Agreement: Unless otherwise provided by law, in the event of a dispute related to the validity, enforceability, formation or interpretation of this Agreement, in whole or in part, the arbitrator shall have the sole and exclusive authority to consider and resolve the dispute. This includes, but is not limited to, a claim that all or any portion of this Agreement is void, voidable or otherwise unenforceable based on unconscionability or any other basis. If any provision of this Agreement is determined to be void, voidable or unenforceable, in whole or in part, that portion of the provision may be severed, and the remainder of the provision and this Agreement shall remain in full force and effect

(Randall Decl., Ex. A, ¶ 10 (emphasis added).)

 

Plaintiff contends that the delegation provision is unconscionable because “such a clause presumes that the parties are bound by the arbitration clause, although that is the very issue that has yet to be decided.” (Opp., 16:17-20.) Plaintiff cites to Bruni, but provides no argument as to how the delegation clause in the Agreement is unconscionable. Plaintiff makes no argument that it is. Moreover, in Bruni, the court determined that the court needs to look at the nature of the parties claim whether the party opposing arbitration claims forgery, fraud, illegality, or fraud in the inducement. (See Bruni v. Didion (2008), 160 Cal. App. 4th 1272, 1287.) Here, Plaintiff did not make such argument.

 

            Thus, the delegation clause is enforceable, and an arbitrator, not the Court, is to decide “the validity, enforceability, formation or interpretation of this Agreement.” (Randall Decl., Ex. A, ¶ 10 .) The Court will not address Plaintiff’s remaining arguments.

           

            Stay of Proceedings

 

            Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.4 provides that if the court has ordered the arbitration of a controversy, it “shall, upon motion of a party to such action or proceeding, stay the action or proceeding until an arbitration is had in accordance with the order to arbitrate or until such earlier time as the court specifies.”¿ (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.4.)

¿ 

            Thus, the Court stays this action pending the conclusion of the arbitration proceedings.¿¿ 

 

Conclusion

 

            Defendant Discovery Practice Management Inc.’s motion to compel arbitration is granted. The Court stays this action pending the conclusion of the arbitration process.
