I.                BACKGROUND

On or about October 20, 2019, Plaintiffs David Pacheco Mata, Consuelo Tolentino, Marisela Mata, a minor, by and through her guardian ad litem David Pacheco Mata, and David Mata, a minor by and through his guardian ad litem David Pacheco Mata (“Plaintiffs”) were at Defendants Eurostar, Tanzey Properties II, LLC (“Tanzey”), Alon Realty, LLC and WSS’s property, a shoe warehouse (the “Property”). (First Amended Complaint [“FAC”] ¶ 17.) Defendants Hazel Pacheoco Gonzalez and Ana Cabreranoriega were involved in a car accident, which resulted in a fire hydrant breaking that caused a water geyser. (Complaint ¶ 18.) The Property flooded and the roof collapsed. (FAC ¶ 24.) Plaintiffs sustained injuries. (FAC ¶ 24.)

On January 14, 2021, Plaintiffs filed a complaint against Defendants Eurostar, Tanzey, Alon Realty, LLC, WSS, City of Los Angeles, Hazel Pacheoco Gonzalez, Ana Cabreranoriega, and Does 1 to 50, alleging causes of action for: (1) negligence; (2) negligent infliction of emotional distress; (3) negligence, premise liability; and (4) negligent hiring, training, supervision and retention.

On April 8, 2022, Plaintiffs filed a first amended complaint against Defendants Eurostar, Tanzey, Alon Realty, LLC, WSS, City of Los Angeles, Hazel Pacheoco Gonzalez, Ana Cabreranoriega, and Does 1 to 50, alleging causes of action for: (1) negligence; (2) negligent infliction of emotional distress; (3) negligence, premise liability; and (4) negligent hiring, training, supervision and retention.

On December 15, 2025, Plaintiffs filed a notice of settlement of entire case.

On July 20, 2026, Petitioner David Pacheco Mata (“Petitioner”) filed a petition for approval of compromise of claim or action or disposition of proceeds of judgment for minor or person with a disability (the “Petition”). As of August 7, 2026, no opposition has been filed.

II.             LEGAL STANDARD

 

“An agreement to settle or compromise a claim made by a minor ‘is valid only after it has been approved, upon the filing of a petition, by the superior court . . .’ in the county where the minor resides or the action could have been brought.” (Pearson v. Superior Court (2012) 202 Cal.App.4th 1333, 1337.) 

 

“[T]he protective role the court generally assumes in cases involving minors, [is] a role to assure that whatever is done is in the minor’s best interests. . . .[I]ts primary concern is whether the compromise is sufficient to provide for the minor’s injuries, care and treatment.” (Goldberg v. Superior Court (1994) 23 Cal.App.4th 1378, 1382.) 

 

“A petition for court approval of a compromise or covenant not to sue under Code of Civil Procedure section 372 must comply with rules 7.950 or 7.950.5, 7.951, and 7.952.” (Cal. Rules of Court 3.1384(a).) The petition “must be verified by the petitioner and must contain a full disclosure of all information that has any bearing on the reasonableness of the compromise, covenant, settlement, or disposition.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.950.) “The person petitioning for approval of the compromise of the claim on behalf of the minor or person with a disability and the minor or person with a disability must attend the hearing on the petition unless the court for good cause dispenses with their personal appearance.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.952(a).) 

 

“An order for the deposit of funds of a minor or a person with a disability, and a petition for the withdrawal of such funds, must comply with rules 7.953 and 7.954.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1384(b).)

 

California Rules of Court, rule 7.955(a) requires the court to use “a reasonable fee standard when approving and allowing the amount of attorney's fees payable from money or property paid or to be paid for the benefit of a minor or a person with a disability” and requires that the court “give consideration to the terms of any representation agreement made between the attorney and the representative of the minor . . . and must evaluate the agreement based on the facts and circumstances existing at the time the agreement was made, except where the attorney and the representative of the minor or person with a disability contemplated that the attorney's fee would be affected by later events.”   

 

California Rules of Court, rule 7.955(b) sets forth fourteen nonexclusive factors the court may consider in determining a reasonable attorney’s fee. Rule 7.955(b)’s factors include,

(1)  The fact that a minor or person with a disability is involved and the circumstances of that minor or person with a disability.

(2)  The amount of the fee in proportion to the value of the services performed.

(3)  The novelty and difficulty of the questions involved and the skill required to perform the legal services properly.

(4)  The amount involved and the results obtained.

(5)  The time limitations or constraints imposed by the representative of the minor or person with a disability or by the circumstances.

(6)  The nature and length of the professional relationship between the attorney and the representative of the minor or person with a disability.

(7)  The experience, reputation, and ability of the attorney or attorneys performing the legal services.

(8)  The time and labor required.

(9)  The informed consent of the representative of the minor or person with a disability to the fee.

(10)  The relative sophistication of the attorney and the representative of the minor or person with a disability.

(11)  The likelihood, if apparent to the representative of the minor or person with a disability when the representation agreement was made, that the attorney's acceptance of the particular employment would preclude other employment.

(12)  Whether the fee is fixed, hourly, or contingent.

(13)  If the fee is contingent:

(A)  The risk of loss borne by the attorney;

(B)  The amount of costs advanced by the attorney; and

(C)  The delay in payment of fees and reimbursement of costs paid by the attorney.

(14)  Statutory requirements for representation agreements applicable to particular cases or claims.

(Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.955(b).)

“A petition requesting court approval and allowance of an attorney's fee under (a) must include a declaration from the attorney that addresses the factors listed in (b) that are applicable to the matter before the court.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.955(c).)

 

III.           DISCUSSION

 

The Petition is properly filed on Form MC-350 and is verified by Petitioner. (Petition pdf p. 10.) Marisela Mata (“Minor Claimant”) settled with WSS (Eurostar)/Tanzey Properties, Hazel Tatiana Pacheoco-Gonzalez, Ana Laura Cabrera Noriega, and Nationwide MedPay/Tanzey MedPay for $322,142.85. (Petition ¶ 10 pdf p. 3.) On October 20, 2019, a car collided with a fire hydrant, causing water to spray upwards and the roof of Warehouse Shoe Sale to collapse on Minor Claimant. (Petition ¶¶ 4-5, pdf p. 2.) Minor Claimant sustained a broken right leg and emotional trauma. (Petition ¶ 6 pdf p. 2.) Minor Claimant received care and treatment for the injuries. (Petition ¶ 7 pdf p. 2.) Minor Claimant has recovered completely and there are no permanent injuries. (Minor Claimant ¶ 8 pdf p. 2.)

 

The total amount offered by all defendants to others is $4,837,664.48. (Petition ¶ 11 pdf p. 3.) Plaintiff Consuelo Tolentino is to receive $2,359,643.85, Petitioner is to receive $2,359,642.85, and Plaintiff David Mata is to receive $118,378.78. (Petition ¶ 11 pdf p. 3.) The reasons for the apportionment are described in Attachment 11b(6). (Petition ¶ 11 pdf p. 3.) Attachment 11b(6) states:

 

Petitioner is the minor's father. Petitioner, the minor, the minor's mother, and the minor's brother were all plaintiffs in this action arising out of the same incident. After evaluating the injuries, damages, and available insurance coverage, the parties agreed to separate settlement amounts for each claimant. The minor's settlement was independently negotiated based on the minor's injuries and damages and is fair, reasonable, and in the minor's best interests. The settlement amounts allocated to the other claimants do not reduce, impair, or otherwise adversely affect the minor's recovery.

 

(Petition Attachment 11b(6) pdf p. 12.)

 

The total amount of medical expenses before any reductions is $64,895.84. (Petition ¶ 12 pdf p. 4.) After reductions, the total amount of medical expenses to be paid or reimbursed from the proceeds is $34,762.88. (Petition ¶ 12 pdf p. 4.)

 

Minor Claimant’s counsel requests $80,535.71 in attorneys’ fees and $448.00 in costs. (Petition ¶ 13 pdf p. 5.)

 

The balance of the proceeds of settlement or judgment available for Minor Claimant is $206,396.26. (Petition ¶ 16 pdf p. 6.) This sum is to be invested in a single-premium deferred annuity, subject to withdrawal only on authorization of the court. (Petition ¶ 18b pdf p. 8.) The terms and conditions of the annuity are specified in Attachment 18b(3). (Petition ¶ 18b pdf p. 8; Attachment 18b(3) pdf pp. 34-36.)

 

The Petition contains multiple deficiencies.

 

Paragraph 7 lists the medical providers that treated Minor Claimant but does not describe Minor Claimant’s care and treatment. (Petition ¶ 7 pdf p. 2.) Paragraph 7 must summarize the treatment Minor Claimant received for the injuries described in paragraph 6.

 

The sums listed in paragraph 11b(5) do not add up to $4,837,664.48. (Petition ¶ 11 pdf p. 3.)

 

Attachment 12b(5) states Anthony Bledin - Granada Hills Radiology charged $200 and the negotiated reduction was $100. (Petition Attachment 12b(5) pdf p. 22.) Attachment 12b(5) further states the amount to be paid from the proceeds is $200. (Petition Attachment 12b(5) pdf p. 22.) If the amount charged was $200 and the negotiated reduction was $100, then the amount to be paid would be $100, not $200.

 

The medical expenses listed as the amounts to be paid from the proceeds of settlement or judgment in paragraph 12 and Attachment 12b(5) do not total $19,496.00. (Petition ¶ 12 pdf pp. 4-5, Attachment 12b(5) pdf p. 22.)

The Petition states Minor Claimant’s counsel has not received attorney's fees or other compensation in addition to that requested in this petition for services provided in connection with the claim giving rise to this petition. (Petition ¶ 17(c) pdf p. 7.) Similarly, the Petition states Minor Claimant’s counsel is not representing or employed by any other party or any insurance carrier involved in the matter. (Petition ¶ 17(e) pdf p. 7.) Finally, the Petition states Minor Claimant’s counsel does not expect to receive attorney's fees or other compensation in addition to that requested in this petition for services provided in connection with the claim giving rise to this petition. (Petition ¶ 17(f) pdf p. 7.) Minor Claimant’s counsel represents the other Plaintiffs in this matter. Minor Claimant’s counsel should confirm they answered the questions in paragraph 17 correctly.

The Petition does not state how many pages are attached. (Petition ¶ 23 pdf p. 10.)

Minor Claimant’s counsel’s declaration does not adequately address all of the relevant factors set forth in California Rules of Court, rule 7.955(b).  (Petition Attachment 13a pdf pp. 24-25.) The court requires more information as to all of the relevant factors but also specifically requires more information on the time and labor required in this action. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.955(b).) If Minor Claimant’s counsel does not track hours for matters taken on a contingency basis, the court will accept an estimate.

The attached Retainer for Legal Services and Power of Attorney is signed by Petitioner but does not show it was signed by Petitioner on Minor Claimant’s behalf. (Petition Attachment 17a pdf p. 32.) Petitioner has not submitted the retainer agreement between Minor Claimant’s counsel and Minor Claimant.  A copy of the written fee agreement between Minor Claimant and Minor Claimant’s counsel must be submitted as Attachment 17a. (Petition ¶ 13a pdf p. 5.)

There are medical records attached to the Petition but they are not labeled as Attachment 8. (Petition pdf pp. 37-127.)  The records must be labeled as Attachment 8. (Petition ¶ 8 pdf p. 2.)

The Petition states Minor Claimant has recovered completely and has no permanent injuries. (Petition ¶ 8a pdf p. 2.) The submitted medical records indicate Minor Claimant continued to experience stiffness as of January 22, 2024. (Petition pdf p. 117.) A report dated January 30, 2022 also indicates Minor Claimant continued to experience psychological symptoms. (Petition pdf pp. 80-83.) These records do not support the statement Minor Claimant has recovered completely from the effects of the injuries. (Petition ¶ 8 pdf p. 2.) The records also do not show Minor Claimant’s current condition.  The court requires a medical report showing Minor Claimant’s current condition that supports the statement Minor Claimant has recovered completely from the effect of the injuries and there are no permanent injuries.

IV.           CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Petition is denied.

Grant

 

I.                BACKGROUND

 

On or about September 24, 2023, Plaintiff Kelly Ramirez (“Plaintiff”) leased a 2023 Mercedes Light Tru (the “Vehicle”). (Complaint ¶ 6.) The Vehicle was defective. (Complaint ¶ 12.)

 

On February 27, 2026, Plaintiff filed a complaint against Defendants Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC and Does 1 to 100, alleging causes of action for: (1) breach of implied warranty; (2) breach of express warranty; and (3) violation of the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act.

 

On June 29, 2026, Defendant Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC (“Defendant”) filed a motion to compel arbitration (the “Motion”). On July 30, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition. On August 5, 2026, Defendant filed a reply.

 

II.             LEGAL STANDARD¿

 

The court is empowered to compel parties to arbitrate disputes pursuant to an agreement to do so. (Code Civ. Proc., §¿1281.2.)

 

Under 9 United States Code section 2:

 

A written provision in any maritime transaction or a contract evidencing a transaction involving commerce to settle by arbitration a controversy thereafter arising out of such contract or transaction, or the refusal to perform the whole or any part thereof, or an agreement in writing to submit to arbitration an existing controversy arising out of such a contract, transaction, or refusal, shall be valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, save upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract or as otherwise provided in chapter 4.

 

“California law strongly favors arbitration ‘as a speedy and relatively inexpensive means of dispute resolution.’” (Villareal v. LAD-T, LLC (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 446, 455.) “[T]here is a presumption in favor of arbitrability.” (Id. at p. 456.) “However, notwithstanding the policy favoring arbitration, because arbitration is a matter of consent, ‘a party cannot be required to submit to arbitration any dispute which he has not agreed so to submit.’” (Id.) Agreements to arbitrate are “valid, enforceable and irrevocable, save upon such grounds as exist for the revocation of any contract.” (Id.)

 

“Because the existence of the arbitration agreement is a statutory prerequisite to granting the petition, the petitioner bears the burden of proving its existence by a preponderance of the evidence. If the party opposing the petition raises a defense to enforcement . . . that party bears the burden of producing evidence of, and proving by a preponderance of the evidence, any fact necessary to the defense.” (Villareal v. LAD-T, LLC, supra, 84 Cal.App.5th at p. 456, brackets removed.)¿

 

III.           DISCUSSION

 

  1. The Arbitration Provision

 

The petitioner bears the burden of proving the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by a preponderance of the evidence. (Villareal v. LAD-T, LLC, supra, 84 Cal.App.5th at p. 456.) 

 

Defendant argues the court should compel arbitration because the Lease Agreement (“Lease”) contains a binding arbitration provision. (Memorandum pp. 6-8.)

 

Defendant submits the Lease as an exhibit. (Gruzman Declaration ¶ 3; Ex. B.) The Lease contains an arbitration provision, which states, in relevant part:

 

Any claim or dispute, whether in contract, tort, or otherwise (including any dispute over the interpretation, scope, or validity of this lease, arbitration section or the arbitrability of any issue), between you and us or any of our employees, agents, successors, assigns, or the vehicle distributor, including Mercedes-Benz USA LLC (each a “Third Party Beneficiary”), which arises out of or relates to a credit application, this lease, or any resulting transaction or relationship arising out of this lease (including any such relationship with third parties who do not sign this contract) shall, at the election of either you, us, or a Third Party Beneficiary, be resolved by a neutral, binding arbitration and not by a court action. Any claim or dispute is to be arbitrated on an individual basis and not as a class action. The arbitration shall be administered by the American Arbitration Association, or by any other organization that you may choose, subject to our or a Third Party Beneficiary’s approval. You may get a copy of the rules of the American Arbitration Association by visiting its website at www.adr.org.

 

(Gruzman Declaration Ex. B pdf p. 16.) The arbitration provision expressly states it is governed by the Federal Arbitration Act. (Gruzman Declaration Ex. B pdf p. 16.) The Lease is signed by Plaintiff. (Gruzman Declaration ¶ 3; Ex. B pdf p. 17.) Plaintiff does not challenge the authenticity of the signature.  Thus, Defendant meets its burden of showing a valid arbitration agreement exists.

 

  1. Third-Party Beneficiary

 

Defendant argues it may compel arbitration as an express third-party beneficiary under the Lease. (Memorandum pp. 13-14.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues the Lease’s identifying Defendant is insufficient and “[t]he Court must still determine the scope of the benefit the contracting parties intended to confer.” (Memorandum p. 5.) Plaintiff further argues that the Lease does not show the parties intended to require arbitration of statutory warranty claims because the Lease is a financing document and it does not address a manufacturer’s statutory warranty obligations under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act. (Memorandum p. 5.) Plaintiff also argues the complaint does not seek to enforce a provision in the Lease. (Memorandum p. 5.)

 

In reply, Defendant argues that when Plaintiff entered into the Lease, they gained manufacturer warranties and those warranties required plaintiff to take the Vehicle to an authorized repair facility for repairs. (Reply p. 6.) Defendant also argues that “Plaintiff specifically contracted to lease a warranted vehicle, and she would not have paid the money she did for the Subject Vehicle if it did not come with manufacturer warranties” and “the Lease is the source of the warranties at the heart of this case, and Plaintiff’s claims for breaches of those exact same warranties are necessarily and inextricably intertwined with the Lease.” (Reply p. 6.)

 

Plaintiff cites “Olson v. FCA US LLC (9th Cir. 2026) 187 F.4th 1094” for the proposition “where a nonsignatory manufacturer's authority to invoke an arbitration agreement is disputed, that issue must be resolved by the Court before any delegation provision becomes operative.” (Opposition p. 4.)

 

The court cannot locate a case with the cite “Olson v. FCA US LLC (9th Cir. 2026) 187 F.4th 1094.” The court believes Plaintiff intended to cite Olson v. FCA US, LLC (“Olson”) (9th Cir. 2026) 176 F.4th 612.  In Olson, supra, 176 F.4th at p. 618, the defendant FCA US, LLC argued that “if a court concludes that a party has entered into any valid arbitration agreement that contains a delegation clause, the court must send all questions about the scope of that agreement to an arbitrator, regardless of the identity of the party invoking the delegation clause.”  The Court rejected that argument, noting that it “suggests that FCA could locate an arbitration agreement with a delegation clause that Olson entered with his cell phone provider, use that agreement to move to compel arbitration of the present automobile dispute, and thereby force a court to send the case to an arbitrator to let the arbitrator decide whether the cell phone arbitration agreement covered the automobile dispute.” (Id. at pp. 619-620.)

 

Defendant argues that the Lease contains a delegation clause so disputes over whether the Lease applies to Plaintiff’s claims should be decided by the arbitrator. (Memorandum pp. 14-15.)  However, the court does not understand Defendant’s argument to be that the parties delegated the question of whether Defendant may enforce the Lease’s arbitration provision, but rather whether the Lease applies to Plaintiff’s claims is a matter that has been delegated to the arbitrator. (See Memorandum p. 15.)

.

“The general rule is that one must be a party to an arbitration agreement either to be bound by or to invoke it.”  (Ford Motor Warranty Cases (2025)  17 Cal.5th 1122, 1128.)  Under the third party beneficiary doctrine, the court considers:  “(1) whether the third party would in fact benefit from the contract, but also (2) whether a motivating purpose of the contracting parties was to provide a benefit to the third party, and (3) whether permitting a third party to bring its own breach of contract action against a contracting party is consistent with the objectives of the contract and the reasonable expectations of the contracting parties.” (Goonewardene v. ADP, LLC (“Goonewardene”) (2019) 6 Cal.5th 817, 830.) “All three elements must be satisfied to permit the third party action to go forward.”  (Id.)

In Ford Motor Warranty Cases, supra, 17 Cal.5th at pp. 1125-1126, 1128, 1138, the Supreme Court of California held that the non-signatory manufacturer, Ford Motor Company, could not compel arbitration by relying on arbitration clauses in sales contracts between the buyers and seller dealerships. The Court noted that the sales contracts were between the plaintiffs and the respective dealers and “[t]here is no question here that Ford is not a party to those contracts, and plaintiffs did not expressly agree to arbitrate any dispute with Ford.” (Id. at p. 1128.) 

The arbitration provision here is distinguishable from the arbitration provision at issue in Ford Motor Warranty Cases because it expressly states that Defendant is a “Third Party Beneficiary” and that Third Party Beneficiaries may compel arbitration. (Gruzman Declaration Ex. B pdf p. 16.)

The court understands Plaintiff’s argument that the Lease concerns financing. But the Lease’s arbitration provision states:

Any claim or dispute, whether in contract, tort, or otherwise (including any dispute over the interpretation, scope, or validity of this lease, arbitration section or the arbitrability of any issue), between you and us or any of our employees, agents, successors, assigns, or the vehicle distributor, including Mercedes-Benz USA LLC (each a “Third Party Beneficiary”), which arises out of or relates to . . . any resulting transaction or relationship arising out of this lease (including any such relationship with third parties who do not sign this contract) shall, at the election of either you, us, or a Third Party Beneficiary, be resolved by a neutral, binding arbitration and not by a court action.

(Gruzman Declaration Ex. B pdf p. 16, italics added.) Plaintiff alleges they leased the Vehicle and Defendant “provided an express written warranty relating to the VEHICLE.” (Complaint ¶ 6.) Plaintiff’s relationship with Defendant arose out of the Lease because the Lease was the impetus for Plaintiff’s relationship with Defendant.

These provisions show: (1) Defendant would benefit from the Lease; (2) a motivating purpose of the contracting parties was to benefit Defendant; and (3) permitting Defendant to compel arbitration would be consistent with the reasonable expectations of the contracting parties, because they expressly agreed Defendant could compel arbitration. Thus, the Lease’s arbitration provision meets the elements identified in Goonewardene.

The court determines Defendant may compel arbitration as a third party beneficiary. Thus, the court need not consider the parties’ arguments relating to equitable estoppel. (Memorandum pp. 11-13; Opposition pp. 8-9.)

  1. The Arbitration Provision’s Scope

 

Defendant argues disputes over the scope of the arbitration clause must be decided by the arbitrator because the arbitration provision contains a delegation clause. (Memorandum pp. 14-15.)

 

“[C]hallenges to the validity of [an] arbitration clause itself are generally resolved by the court in the first instance. [Citations.] An exception to this rule applies when the parties have clearly and unmistakably agreed to delegate questions regarding the validity of the arbitration clause to the arbitrator.” (Nielsen Contracting, Inc. v. Applied Underwriters, Inc. (2018) 22 Cal.App.5th 1096, 1108.)

 

The Lease states:

 

Any claim or dispute, whether in contract, tort, or otherwise (including any dispute over the interpretation, scope, or validity of this lease, arbitration section or the arbitrability of any issue), between you and us or any of our employees, agents, successors, assigns, or the vehicle distributor, including Mercedes-Benz USA LLC (each a “Third Party Beneficiary”), which arises out of or relates to a credit application, this lease, or any resulting transaction or relationship arising out of this lease (including any such relationship with third parties who do not sign this contract) shall, at the election of either you, us, or a Third Party Beneficiary, be resolved by a neutral, binding arbitration and not by a court action.

 

(Gruzman Declaration Ex. B pdf p. 16.)

 

Thus, the arbitration provision delegates disputes over the scope of the arbitration provision to the arbitrator.

 

IV.           STAY PROCEEDINGS 

 

Defendant requests the court stay the proceedings until arbitration is complete.  (Memorandum pp. 15-16.)

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.4 states, in relevant part:

 

If a court of competent jurisdiction, whether in this State or not, has ordered arbitration of a controversy which is an issue involved in an action or proceeding pending before a court of this State, the court in which such action or proceeding is pending shall, upon motion of a party to such action or proceeding, stay the action or proceeding until an arbitration is had in accordance with the order to arbitrate or until such earlier time as the court specifies.

 

Accordingly, the court stays the action.

 

V.              CONCLUSION 

¿ 

For the foregoing reasons, the Motion is granted. The court stays the action pending the outcome

of arbitration.
