Tentative Ruling

Zargaryan v. Sargsyan, Case no. 24VECV05351

Hearing date July 7, 2026

Defendant Sargsyan’s Motion for Sanctions - UNOPPOSED

Plaintiff Zargaryan sues defendant Sargsyan for defamation. Defendant moves for sanctions pursuant to Code Civ. Proc. §128.5, arguing she is not a proper defendant. Plaintiff did not file opposition.

Per Code Civ. Proc. §128.5 a court may order a party to pay reasonable expenses, including attorneys’ fees, incurred as a result of bad faith or frivolous action or tactics, including filing of a complaint. “Frivolous” means “totally and completely without merit or for the sole purpose of harassing” a party. Civ. Proc. Code §128.5(b). Per section 128.7, “by presenting to the court… a pleading… an attorney… is certifying that to the best of the person’s knowledge, information, and belief, formed after” a reasonable inquiry, the pleading is not being presented for an improper purpose, such as to harass or delay and allegations and other factual contentions have evidentiary support or are likely to have evidentiary support. Civ. Proc. Code §128.7(b).

Plaintiff alleges defendant owned and operated an Instagram social media page that defamed plaintiff and her husband. Defendant argues: (1) defendant did not own and/or operate the Instagram handle, “lilitsfemalecorner;” (2) a screenshot in the complaint suggests defendant’s actual Instagram handle is “lilitvoguer;” and (3) plaintiff and counsel failed to provide any reliable source of information or evidence that the claims have any merit. See Compl., para. 14; see also Decl. Sargsyan, paras. 3-4; Decl. Agazaryan, paras. 3-7, exhs. A-B.

Defendant argues the complaint, which alleges defendant used the Instagram name “lilitsfemalecorner” to make defamatory statements, contains photographic evidence of defendant’s actual Instagram name “lilitvoguer.” See Compl., para. 14. Defendant argues plaintiff failed to produce evidence in support of her claims, despite meet-and-confer efforts by defense counsel. Decl. Agazaryan, paras. 3-7, exhs. A-B.

To determine whether an action is frivolous, the following objective standard applies: “any reasonable attorney would agree it is totally and completely without merit.” In re Marriage of Sahafzadeh-Taeb &Taeb (2019) 39 Cal.App.5th 124, 135. Such requirement is met when the action is brought despite knowledge of the facts and law that clearly indicate the defendant has no liability or in the face of a complete lack of evidence to substantiate plaintiff’s charges. Frank Annino & Sons Constr. v. McArthur Rests. (1989) 215 Cal.App.3d 353, 359.

Defendant’s affidavit under penalty of perjury declares she neither owns nor operates the Instagram handles at issue. Defendant’s counsel’s affidavit states plaintiff failed to produce competent evidence, either in litigation or meet-and-confer efforts, showing any basis for bringing this lawsuit. Further, following defendant’s request for plaintiff to produce supporting evidence, plaintiff and counsel ceased communication. See Decl. Agazaryan, paras. 5-6, exh. A.

Defendant offers arguments regarding alleged bad faith tactics by plaintiff’s counsel Hasic, including alleged falsification of proofs of service. The court declines to address these arguments, as well as references to the Bay Area Legal Aid v. Achievable Solutions, Inc., et al. (Case No. 22CV008464). This motion is unopposed; the court must construe this as an admission that the motion is meritorious.

Monetary sanctions against plaintiff and counsel and terminating sanctions are warranted. Defendant requests $15,073.10 in attorney’s fees and $595.52 in costs. See Decl. Agazaryan, paras. 10-13, exhs. E-F. Defendant requests additional, unspecified fees and costs in relation to this motion, but the court cannot grant a fee request when no details or amount was presented. The court considers only the request for $15,073.10 in attorney’s fees and $595.52 in costs.

Defendant requests $4,453.10 in fees and $546.90 in costs for December 2025; $6,570 in fees and $43.12 in costs for January 2026 and $4,050 in fees and $5.50 in costs for February 2026. Decl. Agazaryan, paras. 10-12, exh. E-F. These fees were billed at $450/hour, which is reasonable. Id. at exhs. E-F. These requests are substantiated by billing records and are awarded. The court awards $15,073.10 in attorney’s fees and $595.52 in costs against plaintiff and counsel Hasic, joint and several. GRANTED; the complaint is dismissed with prejudice. Sanctions as above, payable within 30 days.


Case Number: 26VECV01225    Hearing Date: July 7, 2026    Dept: B

Tentative Ruling

Amiran v. Tesla, Case no. 26VECV01225

Hearing date July 7, 2026

Defendant Tesla’s Motion to Compel Arbitration

Plaintiffs Amirian and Nazarian sue defendant Tesla, Inc., alleging violations of the Song-Beverly Act. Defendant moves to compel arbitration. Plaintiffs oppose.

Defendant requests judicial notice of plaintiffs’ complaint. GRANTED. Ev. Code §452(d).

California public policy strongly favors arbitration. Code Civ. Proc. §1280 et seq., Madden v. Kaiser Found. Hosps. (1976) 17 Cal.3d 699, 706. Under the California Arbitration Act, where there is a valid written agreement to arbitrate, arbitration is mandatory. Code Civ. Proc. §1281.2. The opposing party bears the burden of proving any defense to arbitrability, including unconscionability. See Pinnacle Museum Tower Ass’n. v. Pinnacle Mkt. Dev. (US), LLC (2012) 55 Cal.4th 223, 236. “[T]he doctrine of unconscionability has both a procedural and substantive element, the former focusing on oppression or surprise due to unequal bargaining power, the latter on overly harsh or one-sided results.” See Sanchez v. Valencia Holding Co., LLC (2015) 61 Cal. 4th 899, 910. 

The Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”) codifies strong federal policy favoring arbitration, making an arbitration agreement in the Terms of Service “valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, save upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract…” 9 U.S.C. §2. “Courts must ‘rigorously enforce’ arbitration agreements according to their terms…” Am. Exp. Co. v. Italian Colors Rest. (2013) 570 U.S. 228, 233. 

Plaintiffs leased a 2023 Tesla Model Y, VIN 7SAYGDEE4PF965856, on 12/26/23. Decl. Kim, para. 3. The lease included a binding arbitration clause pursuant to 9 U.S.C. §2 and the American Arbitration Association (“AAA”).

Plaintiffs argue procedural and substantive unconscionability. Procedural unconscionability concerns the manner in which the contract was negotiated and respective circumstances of the parties, focusing on any oppression or surprise involved in the agreement. See Kinney v. United Healthcare Services, Inc. (1999) 70 Cal.App.4th 1322, 1329. Substantive unconscionability exists when the contract terms are unduly harsh, oppressive, or one-sided and outside the expectations of non-drafting party. See Sanchez v. Valencia Holdings, LLC (2015) 61 Cal.4th 899. For a contract to be invalidated based on unconscionability, opposing party must show procedural and substantive unconscionability are present. Armendariz v. Fdtn. Health Psychcare Services, Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 83, 114. 

Plaintiffs argue the agreement was pre-drafted and did not give plaintiffs an opportunity to negotiate, and Tesla failed to provide a copy of the relevant arbitration rules. Plaintiffs assert this constitutes procedural unconscionability.  

Case law has held failure to attach AAA rules to arbitration agreement does not render the agreement procedurally unconscionable because the rules are easily accessible on the internet, and plaintiff did not lack means or capacity to retrieve them. Lane v. Francis Capital Mgmt. LLC (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 676, 691-692. Further, “[i]n general, nothing prevents purchasers of ... vehicles from bargaining with dealers, even though dealers use form contracts, and nothing in the record shows that plaintiff could not bargain in this case.” Sanchez, supra, at 931. Plaintiffs’ decision to lease the vehicle was volitional; they had the opportunity to opt-out of the arbitration clause but did not. See Decl. Kim, para. 8., exh. 1. Plaintiffs fail to show procedural unconscionability. 

Plaintiffs assert substantive unconscionability, as the arbitration provisions give defendant sole discretion in selecting the arbitrator and unfairly apportion cost. This argument is unavailing; the AAA rules set guidelines for selection of neutral arbiters. See Chavarria v. Ralphs Grocery Co. (9th Cir. 2013) 733 F.3d 916, 923. Further, while plaintiffs assert substantive unfairness as to costs, they offers no specific facts or arguments regarding financial hardship. Plaintiffs fail to show substantive unconscionability. 

A binding arbitration agreement exists, and plaintiffs failed to exercise the opt-out provision. California favors arbitration. GRANTED; the parties are ordered to binding arbitration pursuant to the terms of the lease agreement.

Ford Motor Credit Company v. Tumasyan, Case no. 26VECV01312

Hearing date July 7, 2026

Specially Appearing Defendant Tumasyan’s Motion to Compel Arbitration

Plaintiff Ford Motor Credit Company sues defendant Tumasyan for the alleged breach of motor vehicle installment contract. Defendant specially appears and moves to compel arbitration. Plaintiff does not oppose but requests that the court order the parties to commence arbitration within 30 days, as the vehicle remains in defendant’s possession.

California public policy favors arbitration. Code Civ. Proc. §1280 et seq., Madden v. Kaiser Found. Hosps. (1976) 17 Cal.3d 699, 706. Under California’s Arbitration Act, where a valid written agreement to arbitrate exists, arbitration is mandatory. Code Civ. Proc. §1281.2. The opposing party bears the burden of proving any defense to arbitrability. See Pinnacle Museum Tower Ass’n. v. Pinnacle Mkt. Dev. (US), LLC (2012) 55 Cal.4th 223, 236. “[T]he doctrine of unconscionability has both a procedural and substantive element, the former focusing on oppression or surprise due to unequal bargaining power, the latter on overly harsh or one-sided results.” See Sanchez v. Valencia Holding Co., LLC (2015) 61 Cal. 4th 899, 910.

The Federal Arbitration Act codifies federal policy favoring arbitration, making an arbitration agreement in the Terms of Service “valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, save upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract…” 9 U.S.C. §2. “Courts must ‘rigorously enforce’ arbitration agreements according to their terms…” Am. Exp. Co. v. Italian Colors Rest. (2013) 570 U.S. 228, 233.

The parties concur that a valid arbitration agreement exists. See Mtn. 4:2-6; see also Decl. Ahearn, exh. A. Defendant requests that the court compel the matter to arbitration. Plaintiff does not oppose and requests that the matter be ordered to arbitration within 30 days. As this is a collections matter with the vehicle remaining in defendant’s possession, this request is reasonable. GRANTED; the parties are ordered to binding arbitration per the terms of the installment contract within 30 days. The court will set an OSC re: compliance for 60 days.
