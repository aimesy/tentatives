NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: Hearing on Defendant’s Motion to Reclassify Case from Unlimited Civil to Limited Civil Jurisdiction

Defendant Cultureedit, LLC’s Motion to Reclassify is denied.

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Tom of Finland Foundation, Inc. (Plaintiff), a public benefit corporation, together with seven wholesale suppliers, filed this action against Defendant Cultureedit, LLC (Defendant). Plaintiff alleges that it licensed Defendant to operate an online store using Plaintiff’s trademarks and intellectual property, that Defendant failed to pay royalties owed under the license agreement, and that Defendant retained the store’s proceeds and misappropriated Plaintiff’s intellectual property and internet domain.

The operative Second Amended Complaint (SAC) alleges six causes of action: (1) breach of contract; (2) breach of the duty of loyalty; (3) violation of the Unfair Competition Law; (4) conversion; (5) fraud; and (6) an accounting. Plaintiff seeks compensatory damages, an accounting, disgorgement, declaratory relief, injunctive relief, and punitive damages.

The Court previously sustained Defendant’s demurrer to the third and fifth causes of action with leave to amend and overruled the demurrer on the ground of misjoinder. The seven wholesale suppliers were later dismissed or removed from the action, leaving Plaintiff as the only remaining plaintiff.

Defendant filed a Motion to Reclassify. Plaintiff filed a Notice of Non-Opposition.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure section 403.040 governs a motion to reclassify a civil action. A defendant may move for reclassification within the time allowed to respond to the initial pleading, and the court shall grant the motion if the case has been classified in an incorrect jurisdictional classification. (Code Civ. Proc., § 403.040, subd. (a).) When a party moves for reclassification after that time, the court shall grant the motion only if both the case is incorrectly classified and the moving party shows good cause for not seeking reclassification earlier. (Code Civ. Proc., § 403.040, subd. (b).)

An action is a limited civil case only if all applicable conditions are satisfied, including that the amount in controversy, exclusive of attorney’s fees, interest, and costs, does not exceed thirty-five thousand dollars ($35,000), and that the relief sought is of a type that may be granted in a limited civil case. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 85, 86, subd. (a)(1).)

“[A] matter may be reclassified as a limited civil action ‘when (i) the absence of jurisdiction is apparent before trial from the complaint, petition, or related documents, or (ii) during the course of pretrial litigation, it becomes clear that the matter will “necessarily” result in a verdict below the superior court’s jurisdictional amount ….’” (Ytuarte v. Superior Court (2005) 129 Cal.App.4th 266, 276-277 (Ytuarte), quoting Walker v. Superior Court (1991) 53 Cal.3d 257, 262 (Walker).)

“The court may believe it highly unlikely that plaintiff will recover the amount demanded, but this is not enough to defeat jurisdiction, unless it appears to a legal certainty that plaintiff cannot recover the amount which he has demanded.” (Walker, supra, 53 Cal.3d at p. 270.) The standard evaluates the amount fairly in controversy and is not an adjudication of the merits of the claim. (Ytuarte, supra, 129 Cal.App.4th at p. 277.) It “is not met when it appears a verdict within the unlimited court’s jurisdiction is ‘possible.’” (Ibid.)

The high standard reflects the circumscribed procedures and recovery available in the limited civil courts. (Ytuarte, supra, 129 Cal.App.4th at p. 278.) A plaintiff in a limited civil action may not obtain a permanent injunction and has fewer rights to declaratory relief than a litigant in an unlimited case. (Id. at p. 275; see Code Civ. Proc., § 86, subds. (a)(7), (a)(8).)

 

ANALYSIS

Because Defendant moved to reclassify after the time to respond to the initial pleading, Defendant must show both that the case is incorrectly classified and good cause for the delay. (Code Civ. Proc., § 403.040, subd. (b).) Plaintiff’s non-opposition does not establish that the case is incorrectly classified.

I. The Amount in Controversy

Defendant argues that, with the wholesale suppliers no longer parties, Plaintiff’s remaining claims cannot yield a recovery exceeding $35,000 to a legal certainty.

Defendant’s showing addresses the contract claim. Under the license agreement, Plaintiff is entitled to royalties only on the net proceeds of Custom Products sold through the online store, and Defendant’s evidence is that total net proceeds for those products since 2017 were $25,739.82, yielding a maximum royalty of approximately $2,573.98, further reduced by a $15,000 advance. (Andreasson Decl. ¶¶ 6-9.) Taken alone, the contract claim falls below the jurisdictional threshold.

Plaintiff’s recovery, however, is not confined to contract royalties. The SAC pleads conversion of Plaintiff’s intellectual property and internet domain, breach of the duty of loyalty, fraud, and disgorgement of the store proceeds Defendant allegedly retained, and it seeks punitive damages on the conversion and fraud causes of action. The SAC fixes no amount for these claims, and the amount in controversy includes the recovery sought on them, including punitive damages. (Code Civ. Proc., § 85, subd. (a).)

Defendant responds that these claims cannot produce a meaningful recovery because the agreement forecloses the loyalty claim, the Unfair Competition Law affords no damages, the conversion claim is preempted, and the fraud claim states no coherent basis for recovery. Those are contentions about the merits. On a motion to reclassify, the Court evaluates the amount fairly in controversy and does not adjudicate the merits of the claims. (Ytuarte, supra, 129 Cal.App.4th at p. 277.) The test is whether a jurisdictionally sufficient verdict is possible, not whether it is probable. (Maldonado v. Superior Court (1996) 45 Cal.App.4th 397, 402.)

Because Plaintiff’s conversion, fraud, disgorgement, and punitive damages claims seek unliquidated amounts that the SAC does not cap, and because the Court may not resolve the merits of those claims on this motion, it does not appear to a legal certainty that Plaintiff’s recovery will necessarily be $35,000 or less. A verdict within the unlimited court’s jurisdiction remains possible. (Ytuarte, supra, 129 Cal.App.4th at p. 277.)

II. The Relief Sought

Independent of the amount in controversy, the relief Plaintiff seeks is not of a type available in a limited civil case. An action qualifies as a limited civil case only if the relief sought is of a type that may be granted in such a case. (Code Civ. Proc., § 85.)

The SAC seeks permanent injunctive relief: an order compelling Defendant to return the internet domain, to cease using Plaintiff’s intellectual property, and to refrain from representing any association with Plaintiff. A plaintiff in a limited civil action may not obtain a permanent injunction. (Ytuarte, supra, 129 Cal.App.4th at p. 275.) Section 86, subdivision (a)(8) authorizes a limited civil court to issue only a temporary restraining order or preliminary injunction necessary to preserve the property or rights of a party to a case that is otherwise a limited civil case. It does not authorize the permanent injunctive relief Plaintiff seeks.

Defendant contends that the requested relief is authorized under section 86, subdivision (a)(8). That subdivision applies to provisional relief ancillary to a case that already qualifies as limited, not to a permanent injunction sought as ultimate relief. Plaintiff also seeks declaratory relief, which a limited civil court may grant only by cross-complaint or after nonbinding attorney-client fee arbitration. (Code Civ. Proc., § 86, subd. (a)(7).) Neither circumstance is present.

Because the SAC seeks relief that a limited civil court cannot grant, the action is not correctly classified as limited on this ground as well. For the reasons stated above, the case is not incorrectly classified, and the Court does not reach the question of whether Defendant showed good cause for failing to seek reclassification earlier.

Accordingly, the Motion is denied.

 

CONCLUSION

Defendant Cultureedit, LLC’s Motion to Reclassify is denied.


Case Number: 24STCV11590    Hearing Date: July 17, 2026    Dept: 515

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: Hearing on Petitions of Petitioner Daniel Onofre for Approval of Compromise of Claim of Minors Angie Delilah Onofre, Daniel Onofre Jr., and Zachary Isaiah Marquez

 

Petitioner’s Petitions for Minor’s Compromise are granted.

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiffs Daniel Onofre, Maria Mazariegos Marquez, Daniel Onofre Jr., Angie Delilah Onofre, and Zachary Isaiah Marquez (collectively, “Plaintiffs”), tenants of an apartment building, filed this action against Salvador Munoz and Ana M. Hernandez, as trustee of the Ana Maria Hernandez Trust (collectively, “Defendants”).

The causes of action are: (1) breach of the implied warranty of habitability; (2) breach of the statutory warranty of habitability; (3) breach of the covenant of quiet enjoyment; (4) negligence; (5) violation of Civil Code section 1942.4; (6) private nuisance; (7) violation of the City of Los Angeles Tenant Anti-Harassment Ordinance; and (8) violation of the Unfair Competition Law.

Three of the Plaintiffs are minors: Angie Delilah Onofre, Daniel Onofre Jr., and Zachary Isaiah Marquez (collectively, “Minors”). Each Minor appears through a guardian ad litem, Daniel Onofre (Petitioner). The parties settled the action for a global sum of $375,000, allocated among the five Plaintiffs. As guardian ad litem, Petitioner filed three substantively identical petitions requesting the Court’s approval of the compromise of each Minor’s claim.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

Courts must decide whether to approve a compromise by determining whether petitioners, such as guardians, are acting in the best interests of minors. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 372, subd. (a); Scruton v. Korean Air Lines Co. (1995) 39 Cal.App.4th 1596, 1603-1607.) “[T]he protective role the court generally assumes in cases involving minors, [is] a role to assure that whatever is done is in the minor’s best interests…. [I]ts primary concern is whether the compromise is sufficient to provide for the minor’s injuries, care and treatment.” (Goldberg v. Superior Court (1994) 23 Cal.App.4th 1378, 1382.) Judicial discretion in approving petitions for minors’ compromises, and in ordering distributions, must be exercised in the best interests of minors, on a case-by-case basis tailored to the circumstances. (Christensen v. Superior Court (1987) 193 Cal.App.3d 139, 142-144.)

A petition for court approval of a compromise under Code of Civil Procedure section 372 must comply with California Rules of Court, rules 7.950, 7.951, and 7.952. (See Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1384(a).) Provisions of the Probate Code and the Code of Civil Procedure apply to compromises of minors’ claims. (See Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.950.)

Payment of attorney’s fees on a minor’s behalf must be approved as reasonable by the court. (Niles v. City of San Rafael (1974) 42 Cal.App.3d 230, 244.) “The trial court itself must develop and resolve any counterarguments on behalf of the minor, lest the attorney receive an excessive award of fees.” (Gonzalez v. Chen (2011) 197 Cal.App.4th 881, 887.) Unless the court has approved the fee agreement in advance, the court must use a reasonable fee standard in approving attorney’s fees payable from money paid or to be paid for the benefit of a minor. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.955(a)(1).) The court must consider the terms of the agreement between the attorney and the minor’s representative and evaluate the agreement based on the facts and circumstances existing when the agreement was made. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.955(a)(2).) Rule 7.955(b) sets out nonexclusive factors bearing on the reasonableness of the fee, and the petition must include a declaration from the attorney addressing the applicable factors. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.955(c).)

 

ANALYSIS

I. Settlement

In seeking approval of the settlement, Petitioner represents that he made a careful and diligent inquiry and investigation into the facts and circumstances of the incident, the responsibility for it, and the nature and extent of each Minor’s injuries. (Petitions ¶ 9.) The Minors resided with their family in one unit of an apartment building that suffered from uninhabitable conditions, including rodent, cockroach, and bedbug infestations. Each Minor’s claim is for bodily injury alone, consisting of insect bites and allergy symptoms treated with over-the-counter medication, from which each Minor has fully recovered without treatment by a physician. (Petitions ¶¶ 5-8 & Attach. 13a.)

Each Minor is to receive a gross recovery of $20,000. The Court finds that this amount is fair, reasonable, and in each Minor’s best interest, and is consistent with amounts this Court has approved in comparable cases involving minors who lived in uninhabitable conditions, fully recovered, and required no medical treatment. Accordingly, the Court approves the compromise of each Minor’s claim.

II. Disposition of Funds

The net balance available to each Minor after costs and attorney fees is $14,722.73. Probate Code section 3611, subdivision (b), authorizes the Court to order that the remaining balance of a minor’s settlement be deposited in an insured account in a financial institution in this state, subject to withdrawal only upon the authorization of the court. (See also Cal. Rules of Court, rule 7.953.) Petitioner requests that each Minor’s net recovery be deposited in a blocked account at JP Morgan Chase Bank, subject to withdrawal only on order of the Court, until the Minor reaches the age of majority. The Court finds this disposition to be in each Minor’s best interest and approves it.

III. Attorney’s Fees

Counsel requests attorney’s fees of $4,907.57 for each Minor, representing twenty-five percent of the Minor’s recovery after deduction of costs, under a written contingency fee agreement. (Attach. 17a.) The petitions include the declaration of counsel required by California Rules of Court, rule 7.955(c). (Attach. 13a.) The declaration addresses the applicable factors under rule 7.955(b): the nature of each Minor’s injuries; the time and labor involved, estimated at twenty-five hours per Minor; counsel’s experience and reputation in habitability litigation; the contingent nature of the representation and the risk of no recovery; counsel’s advancement of costs; and the informed consent of the guardian ad litem to the fee. The requested fee equals approximately $196 per hour. The Court finds the requested fee reasonable. Accordingly, the Court approves attorney’s fees of $4,907.57 and costs of $369.70 as to each Minor.

 

CONCLUSION

The Petitions are granted. The Court approves the compromise of each Minor’s claim in the gross amount of $20,000, approves attorney’s fees of $4,907.57 and costs of $369.70 as to each Minor, and orders that the net balance of $14,722.73 for each Minor be deposited in a blocked account subject to withdrawal only upon further order of the Court.
