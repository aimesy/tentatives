SUMMARY JUDGMENT/ADJUDICATION

 

MOVING PARTY: Plaintiff, the People of the State of California

RESPONDING PARTY: Defendants, 2551 Beverly, LLC, Sophia Woo, Sarah Kim, and Best Wellness Center LLC

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Motion for Summary Judgment, or, in the Alternative, Summary Adjudication

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

On November 21, 2024, Plaintiff the People of the State of California filed the instant action against Defendants 2551 Beverly, LLC, Sophia Woo, Sarah Kim, and Best Wellness Center LLC to enjoin and abate an alleged prostitution nuisance at a massage parlor. It alleges the parlor operates from a commercial building at 2551 Beverly Boulevard in Los Angeles, California 90057. It claims that 2551 Beverly owns the property and Woo manages it and Best Wellness, and its principal, Kim, own, manage, and/or control the massage business. Plaintiff asserts claims for violation of the Red Light Abatement Law (RLAL) and the Unfair Competition Law (UCL).      

 

RULING: Granted.

Request for Judicial Notice: Granted in part.

The Court only takes judicial notice of the fact that the City of Los Angeles has a population in excess of 750,000. (Evid. Code, § 452, subd. (g).) Judicial notice is not taken of the remaining documents because they are immaterial to the Court’s ruling on the motion.  

 

Evidentiary Objections: Overruled.

Defendants’ evidentiary objections to Plaintiff’s evidence are overruled. The objections fail to comply with California Rules of Court, rule 3.1354, because they fail to identify the specific documents containing the objectionable material and the particular statements objected to. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1354(b).) This alone is grounds to overrule the objections. (Hodjat v. State Farm Mutual Auto. Ins. Co. (2012) 211 Cal.App.4th 1, 8-9; see Reid v. Google Inc. (2010) 50 Cal.4th 512, 532-33 [evidentiary objections must be in “proper form”].) In any event, the Court notes that police reports reflecting the direct observations of an officer are admissible under the official-records exception to the hearsay rule. (Evid. Code, § 1280; People v. Baeske (1976) 58 Cal.App.3d 775, 780-81.) A sex worker’s solicitation of sex contained within the report is admissible as a “verbal act” of independent legal significance (i.e., crime of prostitution). (People v. Dell (1991) 232 Cal.App.3d 248, 258-60.) And the “general reputation” of a place is admissible in RLAL actions. (Pen. Code, § 11228.)

 

The Court does not rule on Plaintiff’s objections to Defendants’ evidence because, as explained below, Defendants’ asserted facts do not raise a triable issue of material fact. The objections are therefore immaterial to disposition of the motion. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (q).)

 

Plaintiff moves for summary judgment arguing that no triable issue of material fact exists as to the existence of a nuisance on the property via the prostitution activity. This constitutes a violation of the RLAL and accordingly a violation of the UCL. They alternatively move for summary adjudication on both causes of action. Defendants oppose the motion, disputing their knowledge and participation in the prostitution activities and claiming credibility issues regarding the reporting officers. Because Defendants’ asserted facts and credibility challenges fail to raise triable issues of material fact, Plaintiff’s motion is granted.      

   

The pleadings frame the issues for motions, “since it is those allegations to which the motion must respond. (Citation.)” (Scolinos v. Kolts (1995) 37 Cal. App. 4th 635, 640-641; FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 367, 382-83; Jordan-Lyon Prods., LTD. v. Cineplex Odeon Corp. (1994) 29 Cal.App.4th 1459, 1472.) The purpose of a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication “is to provide courts with a mechanism to cut through the parties’ pleadings in order to determine whether, despite their allegations, trial is in fact necessary to resolve their dispute.” (Aguilar v. Atl. Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) “Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (c), requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.”  (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.) 

 

“On a motion for summary judgment, the initial burden is always on the moving party to make a prima facie showing that there are no triable issues of material fact.” (Scalf v. D.B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1519.) A plaintiff moving for summary judgment “has met that party’s burden of showing that there is no defense to a cause of action if that party has proved each element of the cause of action entitling the party to judgment on the cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(1).) “Once the plaintiff . . . has met that burden, the burden shifts to the defendant or cross-defendant to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.” (Ibid.)

 

“When deciding whether to grant summary judgment, the court must consider all of the evidence set forth in the papers (except evidence to which the court has sustained an objection), as well as all reasonable inferences that may be drawn from that evidence, in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment.” (Avivi v. Centro Medico Urgente Medical Center (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 463, 467; see also Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c).) “An issue of fact can only be created by a conflict in the evidence. It is not created by speculation, conjecture, imagination or guesswork.” (Lyons v. Security Pacific National Bank (1995) 40 Cal.App.4th 1001, 1041 (citation omitted).)

 

Plaintiff argues that no triable issues of material fact exist because the prostitution activity on the property constitutes a nuisance under the RLAL. The statute provides that “[e]very building or place used for the purpose of . . . lewdness, assignation, or prostitution, and every building or place in or upon which acts of . . . lewdness, assignation, or prostitution, are held or occur, is a nuisance which shall be enjoined, abated, and prevented, and for which damages may be recovered, whether it is a public or private nuisance.” (Pen. Code, § 11225, subd. (a)(1).) The city attorney may “maintain an action in equity to abate and prevent the nuisance and to perpetually enjoin the person conducting or maintaining it, and the owner, lessee, or agent of the building or place, in or upon which the nuisance exists, from directly or indirectly maintaining or permitting it.” (Id. § 11226.)

 

Plaintiff has satisfied its initial burden. 2551 Beverly is the owner of the property and Woo is the property’s manager and designated agent. [Appendix of Exhibits, Ex. 5: Grant Deed, Ex. 27: Power of Attorney.] The property was leased to Kim and Best Wellness owned and operated the massage business. [Id. Exs. 20-21: Kim’s Response to Interrogatory No. 50, Ex. 27: Commercial Lease Agreement, pp. 321-27, Ex. 38: Secretary of State Filing, pp. 706-09, Ex. 36: Permit Documents, pp. 629-31, 633, 681-85, 689-91.] Between May 2023 and November 2024, undercover police officers made 4 arrests at the massage parlor for prostitution after masseuses offered to perform sex acts in exchange for money. [Id. Exs. 13-16: Arrest Reports, Exs. 7-10: Officer Declarations.] The prostitution activities are sufficient to meet Plaintiff’s initial burden of establishing a violation of the RLAL. (People ex rel. Trutanich v. Joseph (2012) 204 Cal.App.4th 1512, 1521 [unlawful sale, storage, or giving away of marijuana at premises sufficient to meet burden of establishing violation of Narcotics Abatement Law].) Plaintiff likewise meets its burden as to its UCL claim because prostitution in violation of the RLAL is an unlawful business practice under the UCL. (Id. at p. 1525 [violation of Narcotics Abatement Law constituted unlawful business practice under the UCL].) The burden therefore shifts to Defendants to raise triable issues of material fact.

 

Defendants fail to meet their burden. Defendants do not dispute that prostitution occurred at the property but instead try to raise triable issues by denying their knowledge of and participation in the prostitution activity as well as asserting credibility challenges regarding the reporting officers. This is insufficient to satisfy their burden. A violation of the RLAL does not require Defendants to have knowledge of or participate in the unlawful activity; the existence of the nuisance is sufficient. (People ex rel. Hicks v. Sarong Gals (1974) 42 Cal.App.3d 556, 561 [“[A] showing of personal knowledge on the part of the owner or operator of the premises is not a prerequisite to relief under the Red Light Abatement Law”]; Lew v. Superior Court (1993) 20 Cal.App.4th 866, 871 [Narcotics Abatement Law “does not require that the unlawful activity which makes the building a nuisance be conducted by the owner of the building, a tenant of the building, or a person entering with permission”].) And Defendants’ bare credibility challenges, without evidence controverting the information provided by the reporting officers, do not raise a triable issue of material fact. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (e) [“[S]ummary judgment shall not be denied on grounds of credibility or for want of cross-examination of witnesses furnishing affidavits or declarations in support of the summary judgment . . .”]; Trujillo v. First American Registry, Inc. (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 628, 636.) Defendants therefore fail to raise a triable issue of material fact and Plaintiff is entitled to summary judgment.

 

Plaintiff’s motion is therefore granted.

 

Plaintiff may separately move for an injunction, civil penalties, and costs and fees. (People ex rel. Feuer v. Superior Court (2015) 234 Cal.App.4th 1360, 1374-76.)

 

Plaintiff to give notice.


Case Number: 25STCV21728    Hearing Date: August 11, 2026    Dept: 617

Dept. 617

Date: 8-11-26

Case #: 25STCV21728

Trial Date: None Set

 

DEMURRER

 

MOVING PARTY: Defendants, Aetna Health of California, Inc., Aetna Life Insurance Company, Aetna Better Health of California, Inc., and Aetna Health and Life Insurance Company

RESPONDING PARTY: Plaintiff, Pasteur, Inc. d/b/a Sinai Labs

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Demurrer to Each Cause of Action in Complaint

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

Plaintiff Pasteur, Inc. d/b/a Sinai Labs filed the instant action against Defendants Aetna Health of California, Inc., Aetna Life Insurance Company, Aetna Better Health of California, Inc., and Aetna Health and Life Insurance Company, alleging that Defendants have not fully paid for COVID-19 testing and services Plaintiff allegedly provided to Defendants’ insured or members during the pandemic. Defendants’ reimbursement obligation is predicated on section 1342.2 of the Health and Safety Code and section 10110.7 of the Insurance Code. Under both provisions, health care plans are required to cover the costs for COVID-19 testing “regardless of whether the services are provided by an in-network or out-of-network provider.” (Health & Saf. Code, § 1342.2, subd. (a); Ins. Code, § 10110.7, subd. (b).) Plans must reimburse out-of-network providers for testing and services “in an amount that is reasonable, as determined in comparison to prevailing market rates for testing items or services in the geographic region where the item or service is rendered.” (Health & Saf. Code, § 1342.2, subd. (a)(4); Ins. Code, § 10110.7, subd. (b)(4).) “Beginning six months after the federal public health emergency expires [i.e., the COVID-19 pandemic], a health care service plan shall no longer be required to cover the cost sharing for COVID-19 diagnostic and screening testing and health care services related to testing when delivered by an out-of-network provider, except as otherwise required by law.” (Health & Saf. Code, § 1342.2, subd. (a)(5); Ins. Code, § 10110.7, subd. (b)(5).)

  

Plaintiff asserts claims for: (1) Violation of California Business & Professions Code § 17200 et seq.; (2) Negligence; (3) Goods and Services Rendered; (4) Money Had and Received; (5) Open Book Account; (6) Account Stated; (7) Unjust Enrichment; (8) Breach of Implied-in-Law Contract; (9) Breach of Implied-in-Fact Contract; (10) Quantum Meruit; (11) Violation of California Health & Safety Code § 1342.2/California Insurance Code § 10110.7; and (12) Declaratory Relief.

 

RULING: Sustained in part.

Defendants demur to each cause of action in Plaintiff’s complaint. They argue that Plaintiff fails to sufficiently state its claims and the complaint is uncertain. Plaintiff opposes the demurrer, denying the complaint is uncertain and maintaining the sufficiency of the allegations in support of its claims. Defendants’ demurrer is sustained without leave to amend as to the negligence, violation of Health & Safety Code/Insurance Code, money had and received, unjust enrichment, and declaratory relief claims. The demurrer is sustained with leave to amend as to the implied-in-fact contract, open book account, and account stated claims. The demurrer is overruled as to the UCL, quantum meruit, goods and services rendered, and implied-in-law contract claims.

 

A demurrer is an objection to a pleading, the grounds for which are apparent from either the face of the complaint or a matter of which the court may take judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.30, subd. (a); see also Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) The purpose of a demurrer is to challenge the sufficiency of a pleading “by raising questions of law.” (Postley v. Harvey (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 280, 286.) “In the construction of a pleading, for the purpose of determining its effect, its allegations must be liberally construed, with a view to substantial justice between the parties.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 452.) The court “treat[s] the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law . . . .” (Berkley v. Dowds (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 518, 525.) In applying these standards, the court liberally construes the complaint to determine whether a cause of action has been stated. (Picton v. Anderson Union High School Dist. (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 726, 733.) “If the complaint states a cause of action under any theory, regardless of the title under which the factual basis for relief is stated, that aspect of the complaint is good against a demurrer.” (Quelimane Co. v. Stewart Title Guaranty Co. (1998) 19 Cal.4th 26, 38.)

 

“A demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Khoury v. Maly’s of Cal., Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616; Williams v. Beechnut Nutrition Corp. (1986) 185 Cal.App.3d 135, 139, fn. 2 [“[U]nder our liberal pleading rules, where the complaint contains substantive factual allegations sufficiently apprising defendant of the issues it is being asked to meet, a demurrer for uncertainty should be overruled or plaintiff given leave to amend”].)

 

The Court first considers and rejects Defendants’ uncertainty arguments. Defendants argue the complaint fails for uncertainty because Plaintiff does not provide the dates services were rendered or when Defendants issued remittances and does not identify the plans and policies of Defendants’ enrollees. The law does not require Plaintiff to provide such details. The complaint sufficiently apprises Defendants of the issues they are being asked to meet. The demurrer for uncertainty is therefore overruled.  

 

First Cause of Action: Violation of California Business & Professions Code § 17200 et seq.

The demurrer to the UCL claim is overruled. Defendants argue that Plaintiff’s UCL claim fails because it does not seek appropriate equitable relief and does not plead actionable violations. But Plaintiff may seek restitution under the UCL for Defendants’ alleged violation of its duty under the Health and Safety Code and Insurance Code to reimburse Plaintiff for the reasonable amount of its testing services. (Long Beach Memorial Medical Center v. Kaiser Foundation Health Plan, Inc. (2021) 71 Cal.App.5th 323, 343.) Those statutory violations form the basis for the UCL claim. Defendants’ demurrer to this claim is thus overruled.

 

Second Cause of Action: Negligence

The demurrer to the negligence claim is sustained. Defendants contend that Plaintiff’s negligence claim fails because Defendants did not owe it a duty of care. Plaintiff’s position that a duty was owed via Defendants’ statutory reimbursement obligation was rejected by the Court of Appeal in Long Beach Memorial Medical Center. There, the Court held that a health plan does not have a legal duty “not to negligently or intentionally underreimburse a hospital or other medical provider” for emergency medical services provided to its enrollee. (Long Beach Memorial Medical Center, supra, 71 Cal.App.5th at p. 341.) The same applies to Defendants’ alleged failure to reimburse Plaintiff for COVID-19 testing services. Plaintiff’s attempt to distinguish Long Beach Memorial Medical Center on the basis that the court’s determination was due to “indeterminacy in rate-setting” is unavailing because those same concerns are present here where Defendants are statutorily required to reimburse Plaintiff “in an amount that is reasonable.” (Health & Saf. Code, § 1342.2, subd. (a)(4); see Long Beach Memorial Medical Center, supra, 71 Cal.App.5th at pp. 336-41.) The demurrer to this claim is therefore sustained without leave to amend.   

 

Eleventh Cause of Action: Violation of California Health & Safety Code § 1342.2/California Insurance Code § 10110.7

Defendants argue that Plaintiff’s claim under the reimbursement provisions of the Health and Safety Code and Insurance Code fails because those provisions do not provide it with a private right of action. It further contends that Plaintiff’s claims for unjust enrichment, breach of implied-in-fact contract, quantum meruit, and implied-in-law contract fall with it.  

 

The demurrer to the eleventh cause of action is sustained because there is no private right of action under either Health and Safety Code section 1342.2 or Insurance Code section 10110.7. “[A] private right of action exists only if the language of the statute or its legislative history clearly indicates the Legislature intended to create such a right to sue for damages.” (Vikco Ins. Services, Inc. v. Ohio Indemnification Co. (1999) 70 Cal.App.4th 55, 62.) No legislative intent to create a private right of action for providers exists here. Neither provision explicitly provides for such a right of action. That the Health and Safety Code and the Insurance Code vest enforcement in the Department of Managed Health Care and Insurance Commissioner, respectively, also cuts against a finding of a private right of action. (Health & Saf. Code, § 1387, subd. (a)(1); Ins. Code, §§ 12926, 12928.6 subd. (a); see Thornburg v. El Centro Regional Medical Center (2006) 143 Cal.App.4th 198, 205.)

 

The Court also finds persuasive the Ninth Circuit’s analysis in Saloojas, Inc. v. Aetna Health of California Inc. of a similar reimbursement provision in the federal Coronavirus Aid, Relief, and Economic Security Act (CARES Act). Like here, the statutory scheme required insurers to cover COVID-19 testing and to reimburse out-of-network providers at a publicly listed “cash price.” (Saloojas, Inc. v. Aetna Health of Cal., Inc. (9th Cir. 2023) 80 F.4th 1011, 1013.) The court found that the CARES Act did not create a private right of action for providers because, in addition to granting enforcement power in the Secretary of Health and Human Services, the focus of the provision was on health plans and insurers as regulated parties. (Id. at pp. 1015-16.) Providers were only the “object” of the reimbursement obligation. (Id. at p. 1015.) Similarly, here, the focus of section 1342.2 is requiring health care plans to cover testing costs and to reimburse providers for such costs and providers are only the object of that payment obligation. Thus, neither section 1342.2 of the Health and Safety Code nor section 10110.7 of the Insurance Code create a private right of action for providers like Plaintiff. Defendants’ demurrer to this claim is therefore sustained without leave to amend.

 

The absence of a private right of action does not, however, preclude recovery for unpaid or underpaid reimbursements under common law theories such as quantum meruit. Courts have consistently reaffirmed a provider’s ability to recoup reimbursement under such theories. (Santa Clara v. Superior Court (2023) 14 Cal.5th 1034, 1044 & fn. 4; Long Beach Memorial Medical Center, supra, 71 Cal.App.5th at p. 335; Bell v. Blue Cross of Cal. (2005) 131 Cal.App.4th 211, 216-18.) Plaintiff’s implied contract and quantum meruit claims therefore do not fail due to the lack of a private right of action under the statutory provisions. Because Defendants’ demurrer to Plaintiff’s claim for implied-in-law contract is based solely on the absence of a private right of action, the demurrer to that claim is overruled.

 

Ninth Cause of Action: Implied-in-Fact Contract

The demurrer to the implied-in-fact contract claim is sustained. As Defendants argue, Plaintiff fails to allege that the parties agreed on the rate to be paid for the testing services, an essential term of the alleged implied contract. (Allied Anesthesia Medical Group, Inc. v. Inland Empire Health Plan (2022) 80 Cal.App.5th 794, 809.) While Plaintiff identifies its publicly posted cash price of $475 for a PCR test, it fails to allege that it communicated this price to Defendants before performing services or that Defendants agreed to the posted price. (See id. at pp. 809-10 [noting absence of allegations regarding plaintiffs’ communication of its “usual and customary rates” before providing services].) Because there is a reasonable possibility that Plaintiff can amend its complaint to allege an agreement at such a price, it is granted leave to amend.

 

Tenth Cause of Action: Quantum Meruit

The demurrer to the quantum meruit claim is overruled. Defendants argue that Plaintiff’s claim fails because it does not allege Defendants’ express or implied request for testing services and that the services were intended to and did benefit Defendants. (Pacific Bay Recovery, Inc. v. Cal. Physicians’ Services, Inc. (2017) 12 Cal.App.5th 200, 214-15.) However, as mentioned before, California law recognizes a provider’s ability to recoup reimbursements for services rendered under a quantum meruit theory. Contrary to Defendants’ claim, the Court does not find that this theory of recovery is limited to the emergency services context where the provider has a statutory duty to provide such services. Rather, the quantum meruit claim arises from the provider’s provision of services and the health plan’s statutory duty to provide reimbursement. (See Bell, supra, 131 Cal.App.4th at pp. 217-19.) Here, Plaintiff alleges its provision of testing services to Defendants’ enrollees, Defendants’ statutory duty to provide reimbursements at a reasonable amount, and Defendants’ failure to provide said reimbursements. [Compl. ¶¶ 32-35, 137-38, 141-42.] This is sufficient to plead its quantum meruit claim. (See Bell, supra, 131 Cal.App.4th at p. 219.) The demurrer to this claim is thus overruled.

 

Third Cause of Action: Goods and Services Rendered

The demurrer to the goods and services rendered common count is overruled. Defendants argue that goods and services rendered is not a standalone claim but merely a measure of recovery under quantum meruit and thus fails for the same reasons as Plaintiff’s quantum meruit claim. The lone authority Plaintiff cites does not support a lack of a claim for goods and services rendered (see Advanced Choices, Inc. v. State Dept. of Health Services (2010) 182 Cal.App.4th 1661, 1673) and, as explained above, Plaintiff’s quantum meruit claim is not subject to demurrer. Defendants’ demurrer to this claim is thus overruled.

 

Fourth Cause of Action: Money Had and Received

The demurrer to the common count for money had and received is sustained. Defendants argue this claim fails because Plaintiff does not allege Defendants received money belonging to Plaintiff. “A cause of action is stated for money had and received if the defendant is indebted to the plaintiff in a certain sum ‘for money had and received by the defendant for the use of the plaintiff.’” (Schultz v. Harney (1994) 27 Cal.App.4th 1611, 1623.) It is viable “wherever one person has received money which belongs to another, and which in equity and good conscience should be paid over to the latter.” (Avidor v. Sutter’s Place, Inc. (2013) 212 Cal.App.4th 1439, 1454.) Plaintiff claims an indirect retainment of money received from Defendants’ enrollees in the form of premiums which, “in part,” are intended to cover the costs of the testing services. [Compl. ¶ 105.] But this connection between premiums paid and reimbursements is too attenuated to support this claim, especially considering enrollees pay premiums to obtain coverage for themselves and not to specifically benefit Plaintiff. The demurrer to this claim is therefore sustained without leave to amend.  

 

Fifth Cause of Action: Open Book Account

The demurrer to the open book account common count is sustained. Defendants argue this claim fails because Plaintiff does not allege a contractual relationship between the parties or an agreement to maintain an open book account. An open book account is a “detailed statement, kept in a book, in the nature of debit and credit, arising out of contract or some fiduciary relation.” (Joslin v. Gertz (1957) 155 Cal.App.2d 62, 65-66.) There must be some agreement, express or implied, that an account would be created. (Maggio, Inc. v. Neal (1987) 196 Cal.App.3d 745, 752.) Here, Plaintiff does not allege an agreement with Defendants to create an open book account concerning the transactions for testing services. Because there is a reasonable possibility that Plaintiff can cure this defect, it is granted leave to amend. Defendants cite no authority to support or elaborate on their claim that the nature of health insurance precludes the creation of an open book account here.

 

Sixth Cause of Action: Account Stated

The demurrer to the account stated common count is sustained. Defendants contend Plaintiff fails to plead this claim because it does not allege an agreement on the final balance due. An account stated requires “an agreement between the parties, express or implied, on the amount due.” (Zinn v. Fred R. Bright Co. (1969) 271 Cal.App.2d 597, 600.) Plaintiff does not allege any agreement on the amount due between the parties. Because there is a reasonable possibility that Plaintiff can cure this defect, it is granted leave to amend.

 

Seventh Cause of Action: Unjust Enrichment

The demurrer to the unjust enrichment claim is sustained without leave to amend. Defendants are correct that unjust enrichment “is not a cause of action.” (Rutherford Holdings, LLC v. Plaza Del Rey (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 221, 231.)

 

Twelfth Cause of Action: Declaratory Relief

The demurrer to the declaratory relief claim is sustained. Defendants contend that declaratory relief is unavailable because Plaintiff seeks relief for prior unpaid claims involving cost-covering and reimbursement obligations that are no longer in effect. Declaratory relief is inappropriate where “the rights of the complaining party have crystallized into a cause of action for past wrongs,” “a money judgment will fully resolve the dispute,” and “no continuing relationship exists to justify a declaration of future rights.” (Cardellini v. Casey (1986) 181 Cal.App.3d 389, 396.) Here, Plaintiff alleges past wrongs involving unpaid and underpaid reimbursements, which can be relieved through a money judgment. No continuing relationship exists between the parties as to the testing reimbursements because Defendants are no longer required to cover the costs for testing performed by out-of-network providers like Plaintiff. (Health & Saf. Code, § 1342.2, subd. (a)(5); Ins. Code, § 10110.7, subd. (b)(5).) The demurrer to this claim is thus sustained without leave to amend.

 

Plaintiff is granted 30 days’ leave to amend. Plaintiff may only add facts in support of the successfully challenged claims. (Harris v. Wachovia Mortgage, FSB (2010) 185 Cal.App.4th 1018, 1023.) If Plaintiff declines or fails to file an amended complaint, Defendants shall answer the operative complaint within 10 days of the lapsed deadline. Any new causes of action outside the scope of the order may be addressed via a motion to strike.

 

Defendants to give notice.

TRANSFER/CHANGE VENUE

 

MOVING PARTY: Defendants, Finfare Financial Inc., Finfare Financial Delaware Inc., Wayne Lin, and Finfare Money Inc.

RESPONDING PARTY: Plaintiff, Charles Shaw

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Motion to Change Venue/Transfer the Action to Orange County Superior Court

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

In September 2025, Plaintiff Charles Shaw was recruited by Defendants Finfare Financial Inc. and Finfare Financial Delaware Inc. (collectively, “Finfare”) for a senior executive position of “Director of Engineering, AI” for an internal Finfare initiative branded “Snowpeak AI.” Multiple interviews were conducted via Zoom and Finfare CEO Defendant Wayne Lin offered Plaintiff the position. Plaintiff relocated from his Sherman Oaks residence to work full-time for Finfare in Irvine. However, within a month of his employment, Finfare eliminated Snowpeak AI and terminated Plaintiff. Following an acquisition, Finfare’s operations were consolidated with another company, Adro, and rebranded and operated as Defendant Finfare Money Inc.

 

Plaintiff alleges that at the time the offer letter was issued, Lin and Finfare were actively engaged in advanced acquisition planning involving Adro that contemplated discontinuing Snowpeak AI and the executive position but failed to disclose this to Plaintiff. On January 26, 2026, Plaintiff filed a complaint against Defendants for: (1) Fraudulent Inducement to Relocate; (2) Fraud; and (3) Negligent Misrepresentation.   

 

RULING: Granted.

Defendants move to transfer the action to Orange County Superior Court. They argue that venue is improper in Los Angeles County because their liability did not arise there. Instead, their liability arose in Orange County because that is where the alleged misrepresentations occurred and Plaintiff’s employment was to be performed. Plaintiff opposes the motion, arguing that the liability arose in Los Angeles County because that is where he received, relied, and acted upon the alleged misrepresentations. Defendants’ motion is granted because here Lin has the right to change venue to the county of his residence in Orange County.[1]

 

“Except as otherwise provided in Section 396a, if an action or proceeding is commenced in a court having jurisdiction of the subject matter thereof, other than the court designated as the proper court for the trial thereof, under this title, the action may, notwithstanding, be tried in the court where commenced, unless the defendant, at the time he or she answers, demurs, or moves to strike, or, at his or her option, without answering, demurring, or moving to strike and within the time otherwise allowed to respond to the complaint, files with the clerk, a notice of motion for an order transferring the action or proceeding to the proper court, together with proof of service, upon the adverse party, of a copy of those papers. Upon the hearing of the motion the court shall, if it appears that the action or proceeding was not commenced in the proper court, order the action or proceeding transferred to the proper court.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 396b, subd. (a).)

 

“Except as otherwise provided by law and subject to the power of the court to transfer actions or proceedings as provided in this title, the superior court in the county where the defendants or some of them reside at the commencement of the action is the proper court for the trial of the action. If the action is for injury to person or personal property or for death from wrongful act or negligence, the superior court in either the county where the injury occurs or the injury causing death occurs or the county where the defendants, or some of them reside at the commencement of the action, is a proper court for the trial of the action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 395, subd. (a).)

 

“A corporation or association may be sued in the county where the contract is made or is to be performed, or where the obligation or liability arises, or the breach occurs; or in the county where the principal place of business of such corporation is situated, subject to the power of the court to change the place of trial as in other cases.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 395.5.)

 

Venue is proper in Orange County. “[W]hen a plaintiff brings an action against several defendants, both individual and corporate, in a county in which none of the defendants reside, an individual defendant has the right to change venue to the county of his or her residence. This is true even though the action was initially brought in a county where the corporate defendants may be sued under Code of Civil Procedure section 395.5.” (Brown v. Superior Court (1984) 37 Cal.3d 477, 482, fn. 6.) Here, none of the defendants reside in Los Angeles County: Finfare has its principal place of business in Irvine, Lin resides in Irvine, and Finfare Money has its principal place of business in Dover, Delaware. [Compl. ¶¶ 2-3, 5-6.] Thus, even if venue is proper as to corporate defendants Finfare and Finfare Money under section 395.5, Lin has the right to change venue to his county of residence in Orange County. Defendants’ motion is therefore granted.

 

The action is ordered transferred to Orange County Superior Court. All scheduled items on calendar are vacated.

 

Defendants to give notice.


[1] The Court acknowledges that Defendants raised Lin’s right to transfer for the first time in reply. Generally, new arguments in reply are not permitted. (Maleti v. Wickers (2022) 82 Cal.App.5th 181, 227-28.) However, the Court finds no prejudice to Plaintiff in considering this basis for transfer given its clear application here and Lin’s entitlement “to have an action tried in the county of his [] residence unless the action falls within some exception to the general venue rule.” (Brown v. Superior Court (1984) 37 Cal.3d 477, 483.)

Plaintiffs ROKiT Drinks Limited and ROKiT Stars Limited filed the instant action seeking equitable relief from a judgment Defendant Jeff Moses obtained against it in 2023. They allege the judgment was procured by extrinsic fraud in that Defendant failed to disclose the purported invalidity of a trademark during the proceedings while having a fiduciary duty to do so via his contractual obligation under a Trademark Assignment and as a compensated agent for Plaintiffs. In addition to their equitable relief claim, Plaintiffs assert causes of action for declaratory relief and restitution and accounting.

 

RULING: Sustained.

Request for Judicial Notice: Denied.

The Court does not take judicial notice of the court records because they are immaterial to the ruling on the demurrer.

 

Defendant demurs to Plaintiffs’ complaint arguing that Plaintiffs’ equitable relief claim fails because it is based on intrinsic rather than extrinsic fraud and is barred by laches, Plaintiffs’ lack of diligence, and the statute of limitations. Plaintiffs do not oppose the demurrer, instead filing a “response” effectively conceding the demurrer and requesting leave to amend given counsel’s recent substitution into the case. Given the demurrer challenges Plaintiffs’ original complaint, the liberal policy in permitting amendment, and the reasonable possibility that Plaintiffs can cure any defects in the complaint (e.g., by alleging facts showing how the purported fraud is intrinsic and/or falls within an exception (see Hudson v.
