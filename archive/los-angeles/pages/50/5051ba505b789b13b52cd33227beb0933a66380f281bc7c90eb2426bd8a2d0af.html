Case Number: 24NNCV01970    Hearing Date: September 18, 2026    Dept: T

Sarkis Gezalyan vs Azat Gezalyan, et al.

Motion for Summary Judgment, or in the alternative, Summary       Adjudication of Complaint and Cross-Complaint

Moving Party:                         Plaintiff/Cross-Defendant Sarkis Gezalyan

Responding Party:                  Defendant/Cross-Complainant Azat Gezalyan

Tentative Ruling:                    Deny

 

BACKGROUND

 

On May 31, 2024, Plaintiff Sarkis Gezalyan (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Defendants Azat Gezalyan (“Defendant”), Shapiro Ventures LLC, and Does 1 through 20, alleging the following causes of action: (1) breach of fiduciary duty; (2) intentional interference with prospective business advantage; (3) temporary restraining order (TRO) permanent injunction and equitable relief; (4) interlocutory decree ordering winding up and dissolution of the corporation; and (5) demand for repayment of shareholder loans.

 

On July 12, 2024, Defendant filed a cross-complaint against Plaintiff, Susanna Muradian, and Roes 1 through 10, alleging the following causes of action: (1) breach of contract – express; (2) breach of contract – implied; (3) breach of fiduciary duty; (4) conversion; (5) penal code 496; (6) unjust enrichment; (7) accounting; and (8) injunctive relief.

 

On December 18, 2024, Defendant filed a first amended cross-complaint (“FACC”), alleging the same cause of action against these cross-defendants.

 

On March 14, 2025, the Court sustained Cross-Defendant Susanna Muradian’s demurrer to the FACC without leave to amend.  The Court also sustained Plaintiff’s demurrer to first and second causes of action in the FACC. 

 

On August 19, 2025, Plaintiff filed a first amended complaint (“FAC”), which included a newly alleged sixth cause of action for breach of contract against Defendant.

 

In a combined motion, Plaintiff now moves for summary judgment, or in the alternative, summary adjudication of the remaining breach of fiduciary duty, intentional interference with prospective economic advantage, and breach of contract causes of action in the FAC, as well as the remaining causes of action for breach of fiduciary duty, conversion, violation of California Penal Code section 496, and unjust enrichment in the FACC.

 

Defendant opposes, and Plaintiff replies.

 

MOVING PARTY POSITION

 

            As to the FAC, Plaintiff argues that there are no triable issues of fact related to his cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty as Defendant breached the duty he owed to Plaintiff as partners in Shapiro Ventures LLC in several ways.  Plaintiff also argues that there are no triable issues of fact on his intentional interference with prospective business advantage cause of action as it is indisputable that he and Defendant had an economic relationship with each other and that Defendant engaged in conduct that interfered with that relationship.  There are also no triable issues of fact as to the breach of contract cause of action based on Defendant’s breach of the promises made under the operating agreement for Shapiro Ventures LLC.

 

            As to the FACC, Plaintiff argues that Defendant cannot establish that Plaintiff breached any fiduciary duty owed to him or that Defendant was damaged by any actions taken by Plaintiff.  Defendant also cannot establish that Plaintiff wrongfully took the real property from Defendant in support of his conversion claim.  Defendant further cannot establish that Plaintiff violated Penal Code section 496 by taking the real property from Defendant.  Finally, Defendant cannot establish the elements of his unjust enrichment cause of action as there is no claim made by Defendant that Plaintiff received a benefit at his expense.

 

OPPOSITION

 

            Defendant contends that a material dispute of fact exists as to the cause of the foreclosure and that Plaintiff’s exclusive authority over the refinancing and sale process preclude his attempt to shift responsibility to Defendant.  Defendant also contends that there is substantial documentary evidence to establish the existence of triable issues concerning the legitimacy of the refinancing transactions and that his evidence directly contradicts the contention that Defendant obstructed the sale of the real property.  Defendant further contends that Plaintiff excluded him from the auction process while simultaneously claiming that Defendant interfered with that process.  In addition, Defendant contends that numerous disputed issues still required resolution by the trier of fact and warrant denial of Plaintiff’s request for summary judgment.

REPLY

Plaintiff responds to deficiencies in Defendant’s opposition and reiterates his arguments.

ANALYSIS

I.                   Legal Standard         

“[T]he party moving for summary judgment bears the burden of persuasion that there is no triable issue of material fact and that he is entitled to judgment as a matter of law[.] There is a triable issue of material fact if, and only if, the evidence would allow a reasonable trier of fact to find the underlying fact in favor of the party opposing the motion in accordance with the applicable standard of proof.” (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850.)  “[T]he party moving for summary judgment bears an initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing of the nonexistence of any triable issue of material fact; if he carries his burden of production, he causes a shift, and the opposing party is then subjected to a burden of production of his own to make a prima facie showing of the existence of a triable issue of material fact.” (Ibid.; Smith v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 1463, 1474 [summary judgment standards held by Aguilar apply to summary adjudication motions].)  Further, in line with Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co., “[o]n a motion for summary adjudication, the trial court has no discretion to exercise.  If a triable issue of material fact exists as to the challenged causes of action, the motion must be denied. If there is no triable issue of fact, the motion must be granted.”  (Fisherman’s Wharf Bay Cruise Corp. v. Superior Court (2003) 114 Cal.App.4th 309, 320.) 

“On a summary judgment motion, the court must therefore consider what inferences favoring the opposing party a factfinder could reasonably draw from the evidence. While viewing the evidence in this manner, the court must bear in mind that its primary function is to identify issues rather than to determine issues.  Only when the inferences are indisputable may the court decide the issues as a matter of law. If the evidence is in conflict, the factual issues must be resolved by trial.”  (Binder v. Aetna Life Ins. Co. (1999) 75 Cal.App.4th 832, 839 [cleaned up].)  Further, “the trial court may not weigh the evidence in the manner of a factfinder to determine whose version is more likely true.  Nor may the trial court grant summary judgment based on the court's evaluation of credibility.”  (Id. at p. 840 [cleaned up]; see, also, Weiss v. People ex rel. Department of Transportation (2020) 9 Cal.5th 840, 864 [“Courts deciding motions for summary judgment or summary adjudication may not weigh the evidence but must instead view it in the light most favorable to the opposing party and draw all reasonable inferences in favor of that party”].)  

II.        Evidentiary Objections

            The Court declines to rule on Plaintiff’s evidentiary objections, as they have no effect on the ruling herein.

III.       Request for Judicial Notice

            Pursuant to the request of each party, the Court takes judicial notice of the exhibited documents.  (Evid. Code § 452, subd. (d).)

IV.       Discussion

Initially, to the extent that Plaintiff submits evidence in support of its reply brief, the Court notes that Code of Civil Procedure section 437c(h)(4) provides that “[t]he reply shall not include any new evidentiary matter, additional material facts, or separate statement submitted with the reply and not presented in the moving papers or opposing papers.” 

Here, Plaintiff seeks summary judgment, or summary adjudication, of the FAC’s remaining breach of fiduciary duty, intentional interference with prospective business advantage, or breach of contract causes of action.  Plaintiff also seeks summary judgment, or summary adjudication, of the FACC’s remaining breach of fiduciary duty, conversion, violation of California Penal Code section 496, and unjust enrichment causes of action.

In strictly construing Plaintiff’s evidence and drawing all reasonable inferences therefrom, the Court finds that Plaintiff has failed to carry his initial burden of production in making a prima facie showing that no triable issue exists as to any of his causes of action.  “And where the plaintiff has also moved for summary judgment—or, as in this case, summary adjudication—that party has the burden of showing there is no defense to a cause of action. [Citation.] That burden can be met if the plaintiff ‘has proved each element of the cause of action entitling the party to judgment on that cause of action.’ [Citation].”  (S.B.C.C., Inc. v. St. Paul Fire & Marine Ins. Co. (2010) 186 Cal.App.4th 383, 388.)  Nor has Plaintiff otherwise shown that Defendant has no defense to his breach of fiduciary duty, intentional interference with prospective business advantage, or breach of contract causes of action.

Likewise, the Court finds Plaintiff fails to make a prima facie showing that Defendant cannot produce evidence establishing any of the elements of his cross-claims against Plaintiff.  If a defendant seeks summary judgment by showing the absence of evidence on a critical element of the plaintiff’s claim, “[t]he defendant must show that the plaintiff does not possess needed evidence, because otherwise the plaintiff might be able to establish the elements of the cause of action; the defendant must also show that the plaintiff cannot reasonably obtain needed evidence, because the plaintiff must be allowed a reasonable opportunity to oppose the motion.”  (Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at p. 854, italics in original.)  To make this showing, a defendant must “present evidence, and not simply point out that the plaintiff does not possess, and cannot reasonably obtain, needed evidence” to establish any elements of the underlying causes of action.  (Ibid.)  Moreover, under this approach, “a defendant’s initial evidentiary showing may ‘consist of the deposition testimony of the plaintiff's witnesses, the plaintiff’s factually devoid discovery responses, or admissions by the plaintiff in deposition or in response to requests for admission that he or she has not discovered anything that supports an essential element of the cause of action.’ [Citation.]” (Leyva v. Garcia (2018) 20 Cal.App.5th 1095, 1102.)  Nor has Plaintiff affirmatively shown that any of the elements of these causes of action cannot be established. 

Furthermore, even if the Court had determined that Plaintiff made a prima facie showing as to either the FAC or FACC, Defendant’s admissible evidence was sufficient to carry any shifted burden of proof in establishing triable issues on all of the causes of action at-issue in these pleadings.  

Therefore, the motion is denied in its entirety.  

RULING

            Plaintiff/Cross-Defendant Sarkis Gezalyan’s Motion for Summary Judgment, or in the alternative, Summary Adjudication is DENIED.

Case Number: 24NNCV04790    Hearing Date: September 18, 2026    Dept: T

Jose Juan Morales Gomez, et al. vs AHMC Healthcare, Inc., et al. 

Motion for Summary Judgment

Moving Party:             Defendants AHMC Healthcare, Inc., and AHMC Monterey Park Hospital, LP dba Monterey Park Hospital             

Responding Party:                  None

Tentative Ruling:                    Deny

 

BACKGROUND

 

On October 3, 2024, Plaintiffs Jose Juan Morales Gomez (“Gomez”) and Margarita Morales (“Morales”) (collectively “Plaintiffs”) filed a complaint against Defendants AHMC Healthcare, Inc. (“AHMC”), AHMC Monterey Park Hospital, LP dba Monterey Park Hospital (“Monterey Park Hospital”), David Duarte, M.D. (“Dr. Duarte”), and Does 1 to 100, and alleging general negligence based on medical malpractice. 

 

On February 25, 2025, default was entered against Dr. Duarte.

 

Defendants AHMC and Monterey Park Hospital (collectively “Defendants”) now move for summary judgment.  No opposition was filed.

 

MOVING PARTY POSITION

 

            Defendants argue that they are entitled to summary judgment because the undisputed facts, supported by medical expert testimony, demonstrate that Plaintiffs cannot establish the elements of their medical malpractice against Defendants.  There is no evidence to support the allegations that Defendants were negligent in the nursing care they provided, and the undisputed evidence shows that Defendants complied with applicable standard of care in doing so and did not cause or contribute to Plaintiffs’ alleged injuries.  This is also supported by the declaration of Defendants’ expert Howard Kaufman, M.D.

 

OPPOSITION

 

            No opposition was filed.

 

REPLY


            No reply was filed. 

ANALYSIS

I.                   Legal Standard

“[T]he party moving for summary judgment bears the burden of persuasion that there is no triable issue of material fact and that he is entitled to judgment as a matter of law[.] There is a triable issue of material fact if, and only if, the evidence would allow a reasonable trier of fact to find the underlying fact in favor of the party opposing the motion in accordance with the applicable standard of proof.” (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850.)  “[T]he party moving for summary judgment bears an initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing of the nonexistence of any triable issue of material fact; if he carries his burden of production, he causes a shift, and the opposing party is then subjected to a burden of production of his own to make a prima facie showing of the existence of a triable issue of material fact.” (Ibid.)

“On a summary judgment motion, the court must therefore consider what inferences favoring the opposing party a factfinder could reasonably draw from the evidence. While viewing the evidence in this manner, the court must bear in mind that its primary function is to identify issues rather than to determine issues.  Only when the inferences are indisputable may the court decide the issues as a matter of law. If the evidence is in conflict, the factual issues must be resolved by trial.”  (Binder v. Aetna Life Ins. Co. (1999) 75 Cal.App.4th 832, 839 [cleaned up].)  Further, “the trial court may not weigh the evidence in the manner of a factfinder to determine whose version is more likely true.  Nor may the trial court grant summary judgment based on the court's evaluation of credibility.”  (Id. at p. 840 [cleaned up]; see, also, Weiss v. People ex rel. Department of Transportation (2020) 9 Cal.5th 840, 864 [“Courts deciding motions for summary judgment or summary adjudication may not weigh the evidence but must instead view it in the light most favorable to the opposing party and draw all reasonable inferences in favor of that party.”].)  

II.        Discussion

            To start, “[t]he pleadings play a key role in a summary judgment motion” because “the burden of a defendant moving for summary judgment only requires that he or she negate plaintiff's theories of liability as alleged in the complaint; that is, a moving party need not refute liability on some theoretical possibility not included in the pleadings.” (Hutton v. Fidelity National Title Co. (2013) 213 Cal.App.4th 486, 493, emphasis in original.)

            In their complaint, Plaintiffs allege that Gomez was a patient of Defendants and Dr. Duarte from approximately April 1, 2024, through July 30, 2024.  (Compl., at p. 4.)  Plaintiffs allege that, during this period of time, Defendants breached their duty of care by negligently treating Gomez’s medical, including surgeries performed in April and May of 2024.  (Ibid.)  Plaintiffs allege that Gomez was injured as a result of this negligent treatment and that his spouse Morales also suffered a loss of consortium.  (Id. at p. 5.) 

            Defendants move for summary judgment based on the nonexistence of triable issues of fact as to their breach of the applicable standard of care and as to causation.

            “‘[I]n any medical malpractice action, the plaintiff must establish: ‘(1) the duty of the professional to use such skill, prudence, and diligence as other members of his profession commonly possess and exercise; (2) a breach of that duty; (3) a proximate causal connection between the negligent conduct and the resulting injury; and (4) actual loss or damage resulting from the professional's negligence.’ [Citation.]”  (Hanson v. Grode (1999) 76 Cal.App.4th 601, 606, as modified (Nov. 29, 1999) [quoting Gami v. Mullikin Medical Center (1993) 18 Cal.App.4th 870, 877].) 

            “Where, as here, the material issues framed by the complaint are whether the conduct of a medical professional fell below the standard of care and proximately caused the plaintiff's injury, expert testimony is required to establish the reasonable degree of skill, knowledge, and care ordinarily possessed and exercised by members of the medical profession under similar circumstances [citation], as well as whether the defendant’s conduct proximately caused the plaintiff’s injuries within a reasonable medical probability [citation].”  (Zaragoza v. Adam (2025) 109 Cal.App.5th 113, 118–119, review denied (June 11, 2025).)  “‘[T]he opinion of any expert is only as good as the facts and reasons on which it is based’ [citation], and the trial court ‘properly act[s] as a gatekeeper to exclude speculative expert testimony’ [citation].”  (Id. at p. 119, alterations in original.)  

Here, the following material facts are undisputed:  At all times herein, Dr. Duarte was a physician providing medical and surgical services at Defendants between April and July 2024. Dr. Duarte was not an agent or employee of Defendants at any time.  On April 24, 2024, Plaintiff Jose Juan Morales Gomez was admitted to Monterey Park Hospital for acute appendicitis. Dr. Duarte performed the laparoscopic appendectomy under general anesthesia on April 25, 2024.  Plaintiff remained at Monterey Park Hospital for his post-surgical care by Defendants’ staff until he was ordered discharged by Dr. David Duarte on April 27, 2024.  (Defendant’s Separate Statement of Undisputed Material Facts (“SSUMF”) ¶¶ 1–4.) 

The following day, on April 28, 2024, Plaintiff returned to the emergency department of Monterey Park Hospital with complaints of an ongoing fever and right quadrant abdominal pain. A CT performed in the emergency department revealed residual appendix stump, wall thickening, and inflammation of the ascending colon and terminal ileum. Plaintiff was admitted to Monterey Park Hospital that same day on April 28, 2024. Subsequently, Dr. Duarte performed an exploratory laparotomy where the physician found an intraabdominal hematoma, and ischemic necrosis of the right colon and terminal ileum which the physician removed. Plaintiff remained at Monterey Park Hospital for treatment of sepsis and necrotic bowel through discharge on July 16, 2024.  (SSUMF ¶¶ 5–7.) 

Plaintiff returned to the emergency department of Monterey Park Hospital eight days later on July 24, 2024, with reports of abdominal pain. He was admitted to Monterey Park Hospital for observation and treatment. During his six-day hospital admission through July 30, 2024, Plaintiff was treated for an intra-abdominal abscess. He was followed by Dr. Duarte and infectious disease. Plaintiff continued to improve and stabilize through his discharge on July 30, 2024, when he was instructed to follow up as an outpatient with Dr. Duarte. Following his discharge, Defendants’ staff had no further involvement in the Plaintiff’s care.  (SSUMF ¶¶ 8–10.) 

Defendants seek to establish the nonexistence of triable issues of material fact regarding their breach of the applicable standard of care by relying on the opinions of their expert, Howard Kaufman, M.D. 

Here, Dr. Kaufman opines as follows:

Based upon my review of the above-referenced materials, as well as my education, training, and experience, it is my opinion that, to a reasonable degree of medical probability, Defendants and their nursing and hospital staff met the standard of care at all times during their care and treatment of the plaintiff at Monterey Park Hospital between April 24, 2024, and July 30, 2024. It is further my opinion that, to a reasonable degree of medical probability, no act or omission by Defendants, or their nursing and other staff was the cause, or substantial factor in causing, plaintiff’s injuries.

(Kaufman Decl. ¶ 5.) 

            Dr. Kaufman next offers the following opinion on the cause of Plaintiff’s alleged injuries: “When Dr. David Duarte performed the laparoscopic appendectomy under general anesthesia on April 25, 2024, the entire appendix was not removed. To a reasonable degree of medical probability, this is the only reason the plaintiff suffered injuries and damages including infection and prolonged hospitalization.”  (Kaufman Decl. ¶ 6.) 

            Dr. Kaufman finally opines that, “[t]o a reasonable degree of medical probability, plaintiff’s sepsis, necrotic bowel, and prolonged hospital course, were due solely to the infection which developed from the remaining appendix, a condition known as stump appendicitis, following incomplete removal of the appendix on April 25, 2024.”  (Kaufman Decl. ¶ 7.) 

            “A moving defendant’s ‘burden to show the absence of any genuine issue of material fact cannot be satisfied by an expert declaration consisting of ultimate facts and conclusions that are unsupported by factual detail and reasoned explanation, even if it is admitted and unopposed.’”
(Zaragoza, supra, 109 Cal.App.5th at p. 119 [quoting Doe v. Good Samaritan Hospital (2018) 23 Cal.App.5th 653, 657].)  Although Dr. Kaufman provides a list of the supporting facts underlying his opinions, Dr. Kaufman critically fails to provide any reasoned explanation for these opinions.  (See Kaufman Decl. ¶¶ 4–7.)  In particular, Dr. Kaufman does not describe the applicable standard of care and does not elaborate or explain why Defendants’ treatment and care of Plaintiff was within this standard of care.  Nor does Dr. Kaufman explain whether the failure to remove Plaintiff’s entire appendix during the laparoscopic appendectomy was a type of complication that can occur even when the procedure is performed by the surgeon with due care, or why the infection that developed from the “stump appendectomy” could not have been caused by Defendants’ negligence in their treatment and care of Plaintiff following the surgery.  Nor does he offer sufficient factual detail to support any of his conclusory opinions regarding the cause of Plaintiff’s injuries and the standard of care.  (Cf. Bushling v. Fremont Medical Center (2004) 117 Cal.App.4th 493, 509 [“The expert has given an explanation for that expert's conclusion that defendants are not guilty of medical malpractice: Based on the expert's experience and the patient's medical records, there is no evidence to support a claim of negligence as a cause of injury. The reason for the opinion is the absence of evidence of medical malpractice. That opinion is significantly different than one that concludes that the standard of care has not been met resulting in an injury, but fails to give a reasoned explanation, based on facts and not on speculation, of why the expert has come to those conclusions.”].)  

            Thus, as Dr. Kaufman’s standard of care and causation opinions are unsupported by factual detail and reasoned explanation, the Court finds that Defendants failed to carry their initial burden.

            Therefore, the motion for summary judgment is denied.

RULING

            Defendants AHMC Healthcare, Inc., and AHMC Monterey Park Hospital, LP dba Monterey Park Hospital’s Motion for Summary Judgment is DENIED.

 

 


Case Number: 24NNCV05579    Hearing Date: September 18, 2026    Dept: T

Mansoor Shams, an individual vs. Vons, an unknown entity, et al.

Motion to Compel Deposition of Defendant The Vons Companies, Inc.’s PMK Witness

Moving Party:                         Plaintiff Mansoor Shams

Responding Party:                   Defendant The Vons Companies, Inc.

Tentative Ruling:                    Deny; Request for Sanctions Granted

 

BACKGROUND

 

            This is a premises liability and negligence action arising from an alleged slip and fall incident on November 20, 2022. This action is brought by Plaintiff Mansoor Shams (“Plaintiff”) against Defendants The Vons Companies, Inc., erroneously sued as Vons, an unknown entity, (“Vons” or “Defendant”) and Ventura Flores (“Flores”).

 

Plaintiff now moves to compel the deposition of Defendant’s Person Most Knowledgeable (“PMK”) for the following subject matter category: “The disposition of any video depicting the INCIDENT.” 

 

Defendant opposes, and Plaintiff replies.

 

MOVING PARTY POSITION

 

            Plaintiff asks for an order compelling Vons to provide a PMK witness for this category because Vons had an obligation to preserve the relevant video footage in anticipation of pending litigation and failed to do so.

 

            Plaintiff also seeks $5,460.00 in monetary sanctions against Defendant’s counsel.

 

OPPOSITION

           

            Defendant contends that it has already produced a PMK witness who testified as to the disposition of the video footage of the subject incident and that there is not a PMK witness with greater or different knowledge to offer on this issue.  Defendant contends further PMK testimony on this topic would be cumulative, disproportionate, and unduly burdensome.

           

            Defendant contends that Plaintiff’s sanctions request should be denied and that sanctions should instead be imposed on Plaintiff’s counsel in the amount of $1,400.00.

 

REPLY

            Plaintiff responds that Defendant’s opposition seeks to mislead the Court because PMK witness Flores did not provide any testimony as to the disposition of this video footage.  Plaintiff also reiterates his request for sanctions.   

ANALYSIS

I.                   Legal Standard

            A.        Motion to Compel Deposition

            “If, after service of a deposition notice, a party to the action … , without having served a valid objection under Section 2025.410, fails to appear for examination, or to proceed with it, or to produce for inspection any document … described in the deposition notice, the party giving the notice may move for an order compelling the deponent’s attendance and testimony, and the production for inspection of any document … described in the deposition notice.”  (Code Civ. Proc. § 2025.450, subd. (a).)

“A motion under subdivision (a) [above] shall comply with both of the following:

1. The motion shall set forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the production for inspection of any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing described in the deposition notice.

2. The motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040, or, when the deponent fails to attend the deposition and produce the documents, electronically stored information, or things described in the deposition notice, by a declaration stating that the petitioner has contacted the deponent to inquire about the nonappearance.”

(Code Civ. Proc. § 2025.450, subd. (b).)

II.                Discussion

            A.        Meet and Confer

            Based on the declaration of Plaintiff’s counsel, the Court finds that the parties sufficiently met and conferred prior to the filing of the instant motion.  (Khorshidi Decl. ¶ 11.)

            B.        Compel Deposition

Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.010 provides that, subject to specific limits and restrictions in the Civil Discovery Act, any party may obtain discovery “by taking in California the oral deposition of any person, including any party to the action.” “The person deposed may be a natural person, an organization such as a public or private corporation, a partnership, an association, or a governmental agency.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2025.010.) “If the deponent named is not a natural person, the deposition notice shall describe with reasonable particularity the matters on which examination is requested. In that event, the deponent shall designate and produce at the deposition those of its officers, directors, managing agents, employees, or agents who are most qualified to testify on its behalf as to those matters to the extent of any information known or reasonably available to the deponent.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2025.230.)

Here, Plaintiff seeks to compel the deposition of Defendant’s PMK witness for the following subject matter category: “The disposition of any video depicting the INCIDENT.”  (Khorshidi Decl. ¶ 8, Exh. F, at p. 3.)

In objecting to the underlying deposition notice, Defendant stated as follows:  “The Notice fails to comply with Code of Civil Procedure §2025.230. The category description is vague, ambiguous, overly broad and unintelligible and fails to describe with particularity the matters upon which examination is requested. Without waiving said objections, answering defendant does not have any security surveillance of the INCIDENT, and thus is unable to produce any employee to respond to this category.”  (Khorshidi Decl. ¶ 9, Exh. G, at p. 4.) 

Based on the available record, and the nature of the underlying action, the production of a PMK witness for this subject matter category is unduly burdensome on Defendant, as this category is not reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence and places more burden on Defendant than the value of the information sought therein.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2019.030; cf. Calcor Space Facility, Inc. v. Superior Court (1997) 53 Cal.App.4th 216, 218 [“Generalized demands, insupportable by evidence showing at least the potential evidentiary value of the information sought, are not permitted.”].) 

Therefore, the motion is denied.

            C.        Sanctions

Initially, the Court notes that it may consider this sanctions request, as there is no statutory requirement that a separate motion be brought to seek monetary sanctions for a misuse of discovery when sought in opposition to a discovery motion.  (Dalessandro v. Mitchell (2019) 43 Cal.App.5th 1088, 1092 [“[A] separate motion is not required, nor is a separate hearing on discovery sanctions.”]; see, e.g., ibid. [finding that the trial court properly awarded monetary sanctions that were sought as part of the opposition to the motion to compel].) 

 

Here, the Court finds that Plaintiff’s motion does represent a misuse of the discovery process.  Defendant has repeatedly and consistently maintained that the subject video was not preserved because there was no apparent slip and fall and there were no apparent injuries.  As much as Plaintiff is dissatisfied with this response, it is quite clear there is nothing left to discover and any further discovery efforts on this issue, including this motion, represent an abuse of the discovery process.  Defendant’s resaonable request for sanctions against Plaintiff’s counsel is granted in the amount of $1,400. 

RULING

Plaintiff Mansoor Shams’ Motion to Compel Deposition of Defendant The Vons Companies, Inc.’s PMK Witness is DENIED. Defendant’s request for sanctions is GRANTED in the amount of $1,400.00 against Plaintiff’s attorney of record only.  Sanctions to be paid within 30 days of this order. 

 


Case Number: 25NNCV07572    Hearing Date: September 18, 2026    Dept: T

Nektarios C. Jacovides vs. People’s Care, et al.

(1) Demurrer to Plaintiff’s First Amended Complaint; (2) Motion to Strike Portions of Plaintiff’s First Amended Complaint

Moving Party:                         Defendant Los Angeles County Developmental Services Foundation Inc. dba Frank D. Lanterman Regional Center

Responding Party:                   Plaintiff Nektarios C. Jacovides

Tentative Ruling:                    (1) Sustain; (2) Deny

 

BACKGROUND

 

            On August 15, 2025, Plaintiff Nektarios C. Jacovides, in his individual capacity, (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Defendants People’s Care, People’s Care LLC, People’s Care Los Angeles, LLC, People’s Care Healthcare Systems, LLC, People’s Care Holdings, LLC,  People’s Care Developmental Services, LLC, People’s Care Bliss, LLC, People’s Care Autism Services, LLC, People’s Care Holdings, Inc, Bartlett Holding’s, LLC, Los Angeles County Development Services Foundation Inc. dba Frank D. Lanterman Regional Center (“Defendant”), and Does 1 to 50, alleging the following causes of action: (1) financial dependent adult abuse; (2) dependent adult abuse – neglect; (3) conversion; (4) claim of ownership under Probate Code §§ 850, 859; and (5) negligence/willful misconduct.

 

            On April 3, 2026, the Court sustained Defendant’s demurrer to the complaint with leave to amend based solely on Plaintiff’s failure to allege sufficient facts establishing his standing to bring the instant action as a person who lacks legal capacity under Code of Civil Procedure section 372(a)(4).  In so ruling, the Court noted that it declined to address all other grounds for demurrer.  In addition, the Court denied the concurrent motion to strike as moot.  (Min. Order, 4/3/26.) 

 

            On May 18, 2026, following the appointment of Selma Blunk as the Plaintiff’s limited conservator, Plaintiff filed the operative first amended complaint (“FAC”).  

Defendant now concurrently demurs to all causes of action and moves to strike Plaintiff’s request for punitive damages. 

Plaintiff opposes, and Defendant replies.

 

MOVING PARTY POSITION

 

            A.        Demurrer

 

            Defendant argues that all of Plaintiff’s causes of action are subject to demurrer because Plaintiff failed to exhaust his administrative remedies prior to filing the instant action and plead facts sufficient to show that he exhausted or attempted to exhaust these remedies. 

            Defendant also argues that Plaintiff failed to state facts sufficient to constitute his first, second, third, and fifth  causes of action against it and that the fourth cause of action improperly seeks probate relief.  

 

OPPOSITION

 

            A.        Demurrer

 

            Plaintiff contends that the exhaustion doctrine does not convert financial exploitation, concealment, and dependent-adult abuse into an administrative service dispute and that California recognizes exception to exhaustion where no administrative remedy exists for the relief sought, the remedy is inadequate or unavailable, or the controversy lies outside the agency's jurisdiction.

 

            Plaintiff also contends that his causes of action are sufficiently pleaded and that the fourth cause of action is properly brought before the Court as decisional law authorizes his probate claim against Defendant in the instant action. 

 

            Plaintiff requests leave to amend should the Court sustain any portion of the demurrer.

 

REPLY

           

            A.        Demurrer

 

            Defendant responds that Plaintiff was required to exhaust his administrative remedies, as Plaintiff’s allegation concerns the provision of services by Defendant and are thereby subject to the exhaustion doctrine.  Defendant also reiterates its arguments as to the independent grounds for demurrer of each cause of action brought against it.

 

ANALYSIS

I.          Legal Standard

A.        Demurrer

A demurrer is a pleading used to test the legal sufficiency of other pleadings.  It raises issues of law, not fact, regarding the form or content of the opposing party's pleading (complaint, answer or cross-complaint).  (Code Civ. Proc. §§ 422.10, 589; see, also, Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)  It is not the function of the demurrer to challenge the truthfulness of the complaint; and for purposes of the ruling on the demurrer, all facts pleaded in the complaint are assumed to be true.  (Donabedian, supra, 116 Cal.App.4th at p. 994.) 

The general rule is that the plaintiff need only allege ultimate facts, not evidentiary facts. (Doe v. City of Los Angeles (2007) 42 Cal.4th 531, 550.)  “All that is required of a plaintiff, as a matter of pleading, even as against a special demurrer, is that his complaint set forth the essential facts of the case with reasonable precision and with sufficient particularity to acquaint the defendant with the nature, source and extent of his cause of action.”  (Rannard v. Lockheed Aircraft Corp. (1945) 26 Cal.2d 149, 156-157.)¿ 

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment.  (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.)  The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully.  (Id.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.)  However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.”  (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245.)

B.        Motion to Strike

California law authorizes a party’s motion to strike matter from an opposing party’s pleading if it is irrelevant, false, or improper.¿ (Code Civ. Proc. §§ 435, 436(a).)¿ Motions may also target pleadings or parts of pleadings which are not filed or drawn in conformity with applicable laws, rules or orders.¿ (Code Civ. Proc. § 436(b).)¿ A motion to strike is used to address defects that appear on the face of a pleading or from judicially noticed matter but that are not grounds for a demurrer.¿ (Pierson v Sharp Memorial Hospital (1989) 216 Cal.App.3d 340, 342; see, also, City & County of San Francisco v Strahlendorf (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1911, 1913 [motion may not be based on a party's declaration or factual representations made by counsel in the motion papers].)¿  In particular, a motion to strike can be used to attack the entire pleading or any part thereof—in other words, a motion to strike may target single words or phrases, unlike demurrers.  (Warren v. Atchison, Topeka & Santa Fe Railway Co. (1971) 19 Cal.App.3d 24, 40.)¿

The Code of Civil Procedure also authorizes the Court to act on its own initiative to strike matters, empowering the Court to enter orders striking matter “at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper.”  (Code Civ. Proc. § 436.)¿ 

II.        Meet and Confer

            Based on the declarations of defense counsel Molly M. Loy, it does not appear that counsel for the parties e met and conferred by telephone, video conference, or in person prior to the filing of the instant demurrer and motion to strike.  Nevertheless, “[a] determination by the court that the meet and confer process was insufficient shall not be grounds to overrule or sustain a demurrer.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41, subd. (a)(4).)  Furthermore, “[a] determination by the court that the meet and confer process was insufficient shall not be grounds to grant or deny the motion to strike.”  (Code Civ. Proc. § 435.5, subd. (a)(4).)

III.       Discussion

A.                Demurrer

“It is well established that where a statute provides an administrative remedy, that remedy must be exhausted as a jurisdictional prerequisite to filing suit.”  (Romero v. County of Kern (2025) 116 Cal.App.5th 1189, 1200.)  “Under this rule, an administrative remedy is exhausted only upon ‘termination of all available, nonduplicative administrative review procedures.’ [Citation.]”  (Coachella Valley Mosquito & Vector Control Dist. v. California Public Employment Relations Bd. (2005) 35 Cal.4th 1072, 1080.)  “The doctrine of exhaustion of administrative remedies requires a party to exhaust all available administrative remedies and obtain a final administrative decision as a condition precedent for judicial. [Citation.]  Thus, when an applicable ordinance, regulation or statute provides an adequate administrative remedy, a party must exhaust that remedy before seeking judicial relief. [Citation.]”  (Muskan Food & Fuel, Inc. v. City of Fresno (2021) 69 Cal.App.5th 372, 383.)  “In the context of administrative proceedings, a controversy is not ripe for adjudication until the administrative process is completed and the agency makes a final decision that results in a direct and immediate impact on the parties.  [Citation].”  (Tejon Real Estate, LLC v. City of Los Angeles (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 149, 156, internal quotes omitted.) 

Here, Plaintiff alleges that he is “a developmentally disabled adult who was subjected to neglect and financial abuse while receiving caregiving services from” People’s Care and that Defendant “contributed to the unsafe conditions by failing to appropriately vendor, oversee, investigate, and provide information about the facility to its disabled clients, despite having a duty to do so.”  (FAC ¶¶ 1, 5.)  Plaintiff alleges that Defendant is a regional center “tasked with, among other things, working with developmentally disabled persons who reside in a certain area and the facilities that serve them.”  (Id. ¶ 9.)  Plaintiff also alleges that Defendant is “is mandated by law to, among other things: identify the services and supports needed by each individual disabled client; develop and maintain individualized program plans (“IPP plans”) to assure that the needed services or supports are being provided; locate service providers that can provide the needed services or supports; monitor and supervise service providers to ensure that the disabled person is in a safe environment and provided services that meet their IPP plan goals; monitor and supervise service providers for violations of statute, duty, or internal policies and procedures; remedy and prevent violations of statute, duty, or internal policies and procedures; and advocate on behalf of disabled persons.”  (Ibid.) 

After Plaintiff’s mother died in 2013, and from 2018 onward, Defendant allegedly “was supposed to be providing him with certain money management service providers or vendors contracted with the Department of Developmental Services to provide such FMS services to disabled persons.”  (FAC ¶¶ 16–17.)  In addition, after his residence burned down in 2019, Plaintiff alleges that Defendant moved him to another rental location and failed to ensure his rent was paid in 2020 and 2021, although Defendant was in receipt of Plaintiff’s money.  (Id. ¶ 18.)  Despite obligations to handle Plaintiff’s money management, no spending plan was ever attached to Plaintiff’s IPP.  (Id. ¶ 19.)  Defendant also allegedly failed to delegate these services to Department of Developmental Services vendors and knew that these entities were misusing Plaintiff’s funds.  (Id. ¶ 20.)  No full accounting of these funds, including Plaintiff’s stimulus checks, was ever made.  (Id. ¶¶ 30, 40.)  

Under California law, the State Department of Developmental Services (“DDS”) has “jurisdiction over the laws relating to the care, custody, and treatment of developmentally disabled person.”  (Harbor Regional Center v. Office of Administrative Hearings (2012) 210 Cal.App.4th 293, 306 [citing Welf. & Inst. Code § 4416].)  As the DDS has jurisdiction over such matters, the Lanterman Developmental Disabilities Services Act (the “Lanterman Act”) specifies that the DDS “shall promulgate regulations to implement this chapter, which shall be binding on every regional center or state-operated facility.”  (Welf. & Inst. Code § 4705.)  Subject to these regulations, the Lanterman Act further requires that “[e]very regional center or state-operated facility shall, as a condition of continued receipt of state funds, have an appeals procedure for resolving conflicts between the regional center or state-operated facility and recipients of, or applicants for, service.”  (Ibid.)  The Act also sets forth the following requirements regarding the procedure for fair hearings:   

(a)(1) Any public or private agency receiving state funds for the purpose of serving persons with developmental disabilities not otherwise subject to the provisions of this chapter shall, as a condition of continued receipt of state funds, adopt and periodically review a written internal grievance procedure.

(b) An agency that employs an appeals procedure mandated by any other statute shall be considered to have an approved procedure for purposes of this chapter.

(c)(1) The regional center’s and the state-operated facility’s appeals procedures for informal meetings, mediations, and fair hearings shall be stated in writing, in English and any other language that may be appropriate to the preferred language of the applicants and recipients served by the regional center or the state-operated facility.

(2) All recipients and applicants, and persons having legal responsibility for recipients or applicants, shall be informed verbally of, and shall be notified in writing in their preferred language of, the regional center’s or the state-operated facility's appeals procedures for informal meetings, mediations, and fair hearings when they apply for service, when they are denied service, when notice of service modification is given pursuant to Section 4710, and upon request.

(d) If, in the opinion of any person, the rights or interests of a claimant who has not personally authorized a representative will not be properly protected or advocated, the State Council on Developmental Disabilities and the clients’ right advocate assigned to the regional center or state-operated facility shall be notified, and the State Council on Developmental Disabilities may appoint a person or agency as representative, pursuant to subdivision (a) of Section 4541, to assist the claimant in the mediation and fair hearing procedure. The appointment shall be in writing to the authorized representative and a copy of the appointment shall be immediately mailed to the regional center director or the state-operated facility director.

(Id., subds. (a)–(d).) 

            Moreover, the Lanterman Act expressly states that, with the exception of certain Medicaid home and community-based services, “all issues concerning the rights of persons with developmental disabilities to receive services under this division shall be decided under this chapter, including those issues related to fair hearings.”  (Welf. & Inst. Code § 4706.)  Accordingly, as set forth under the Act’s appeal procedure chapter, “[a]ny applicant for or recipient of services, or authorized representative of the applicant or recipient, who is dissatisfied with a decision or action of the regional center or state-operated facility under this division shall, upon filing a request within 60 days after notification of that decision or action, be afforded an opportunity for an informal meeting, a mediation, and a fair hearing.”  (Welf. & Inst. Code § 4710.5.)  “Furthermore, the Act's fair hearing procedures are a claimant's exclusive remedy ‘for issues relating to the provision of services,’ which must first be exhausted before seeking judicial relief in the superior court. [Citation.]”  (Harbor Regional Center, supra, 210 Cal.App.4th at p. 312.) 

Although Plaintiff sets forth various facts in the FAC regarding Defendant's alleged misconduct, these allegations reasonably appear to concern Defendant's provision of services to Plaintiff under the Lanterman Act, including the implementation and development of his IPP plan.   As a regional center, Defendant was specifically assigned responsibility for the provision of this service to Plaintiff, as evident from the Legislature's stated intent in codifying the regional center system's role in relation to IPP plans:  

It is the intent of the Legislature that the individual program plan and provision of services and supports by the regional center system is centered on the individual and the family of the individual with developmental disabilities and takes into account the needs and preferences of the individual and the family, if appropriate. It is further the intent of the Legislature that the individual program plan be developed using a person-centered approach that reflects the needs and preferences of the consumer, and, as appropriate, their family. The services and supports provided by the regional center should assist each consumer in achieving their personal outcomes and life goals and promote inclusion in their community. It is the further intent of the Legislature to ensure that the provision of services to consumers and their families be effective in meeting the goals stated in the individual program plan, reflect the preferences and choices of the consumer, and reflect the cost-effective use of public resources. 

(Welf. & Inst. Code § 4646.)   

Because the underlying factual allegations concern issues relating to Defendant's provision of this service and others stated in the FAC, it is evident from the face of the complaint that the Act encompasses these alleged issues. 

            Given this statutory framework and the FAC’s factual allegations concerning Defendant’s provision of services to Plaintiff, the Court finds that the Lanterman Act provides an administrative remedy for “all issues concerning” Plaintiff’s right to receive the alleged services, a right which extended to any of Defendant’s alleged decisions or actions in providing said services that Plaintiff or his guardian ad litem believed were not in his best interest.  (Welf. & Inst. Code § 4710.5; see, e.g., Harbor Regional Center, supra, 210 Cal.App.4th at p. 311 [“These are three broadly worded provisions which, as part of a remedial act, we must construe liberally to carry out the purposes underlying the Act.”].) 

The Court further notes that Plaintiff specifically alleges that he “engaged in administrative remedies, both directly and by the probate Guardian ad litem, to try and remedy some of the budget and supervisory violations” but offers no factual allegations establishing his exhaustion of these remedies prior to commencing this action.  (FAC ¶ 6.)  Instead, Plaintiff only offers a legal contention that the alleged statutory violations and direct conduct of Defendant “was not subject to administrative remedies and as such, the conduct complained of does not mandate exhaustion of administrative remedies.”  (Ibid.)  However, as Plaintiff’s factual allegations concern the provision of services, including development and implementation of Plaintiff’s IPP plan, the Court finds that the Lanterman Act provides fair hearings as the exclusive remedy for any of the alleged issues concerning Defendant’s provision of these services.  (See, also, Conservatorship of Whitley (2007) 155 Cal.App.4th 1447, 1463 [“This determination of exclusivity is not only supported by the comprehensive statutory scheme but by language found in the Lanterman Act itself, expressly making fair hearings the exclusive remedy for issues relating to the provision of services.”].)  Nor does Plaintiff set forth any factual allegations establishing a recognized exception to the exhaustion doctrine. 

Thus, as Plaintiff failed to alleged facts establishing the exhaustion of his remedies under the Act, the Court further finds that the FAC does not set forth a controversy that is ripe for adjudication. 

        Therefore, as Plaintiff has not shown that the defect is curable through further amendment, the demurrer is sustained without leave to amend.  Accordingly, the Court declines to address the other grounds upon which Defendant demurs to each of Plaintiff’s causes of action.  Moreover, as the Court sustained the demurrer in its entirety, the concurrent motion to strike is denied as moot.

RULING

Defendant Los Angeles County Developmental Services Foundation Inc. dba Frank D. Lanterman Regional Center’s Demurrer to Plaintiff’s First Amended Complaint is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.

Defendant’s Motion to Strike Portions of Plaintiff’s First Amended Complaint is DENIED.

 


Case Number: 26NNCV00655    Hearing Date: September 18, 2026    Dept: T

Arlene Aranes Zapanta DMD, Inc., a California Corporation vs. JPMorgan Chase Bank National Association, a National Banking Association, et al.

Motion to Compel Arbitration and Stay Proceedings

Moving Party:                         Defendant JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A.

Responding Party:                   Plaintiff Arlene Aranes Zapanta DMC, Inc.

Tentative Ruling:                    Grant

 

BACKGROUND

 

On January 1, 2026, Plaintiff Arlene Aranes Zapanta DMC, Inc (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Defendants JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A. (“Defendant” or “Chase”), Sarah Charousapha (“Charousapha”), alleging the following causes of action: (1) violation of California Commercial Code section 4401 – payment of item not properly payable; (2) negligence in cashing altered check; (3) breach of contract; (4) violation of California Business and Professions Code section 17200 et seq.; (5) Violation of California Penal Code section 496  -receipt and withholding of stolen property; and (6) conversion.

 

Defendant now moves to compel Plaintiff to arbitrate only the claims made against it under the FAA, pursuant to the arbitration provision found in the Deposit Account Agreement, effective November 16, 2025 (the “2025 DAA”).  

 

Plaintiff opposes, and Defendant replies.

 

MOVING PARTY POSITION

 

            Defendant argues that a valid, written agreement to arbitrate exists between the parties and that Plaintiff’s claims fall within the scope of the 2025 DAA’s arbitration provision.  Defendant argues that the arbitration agreement is subject to the FAA’s substantive and procedural rules and that the agreement is enforceable against Plaintiff.

 

            Defendant also seeks a stay of the proceedings pending the outcome of the arbitration.

 

OPPOSITION

 

            Plaintiff contends that Defendant has not carried its burden of proof as to the issue of whether the 2025 DAA can be enforced to compel arbitration of Plaintiff’s already-accrued claims against Defendant.  Plaintiff contends that Defendant’s own evidence shows that its motion cannot rely on the 2025 DAA to enforce arbitration or a stay in connection with Plaintiff’s claims against Charousapha.  Plaintiff contends that Defendant has not shown that the 2025 DAA’s third-party arbitration language applies retroactively to these claims and that the 2025 opt-out language does not cure Defendant’s failure to prove retroactive application of the expanded third-party provision.  Plaintiff contends that Defendant has also not carried its burden to establish that Plaintiff’s claims against Charousapha are subject to arbitration and that the factual intertwine between the claims against Defendant and Charousapha does not permit enforcement of arbitration. 

 

REPLY


Defendant responds that it has established the existence of an enforceable agreement to arbitrate that covers the accrued claims based on the language of 2025 DAA and that Plaintiff failed to meet its shifted burden of proof in challenging the validity of this agreement.  Defendant also responds that because Plaintiff’s claims against it are subject to arbitration and the FAA’s rules, the requested stay is mandated pending completion of arbitration.  

ANALYSIS

I.          Legal Standard

Under the Federal Arbitration Act (the “FAA”), a “written provision in any ... contract evidencing a transaction involving commerce to settle by arbitration a controversy ... shall be valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, save upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of the contract.”  (9 U.S.C. § 2.)   

 

Given the liberal federal policy favoring arbitration, “the FAA limits courts’ involvement to ‘determining (1) whether a valid agreement to arbitrate exists and, if it does, (2) whether the agreement encompasses the dispute.’”  (Cox v. Ocean View Hotel Corp. (9th Cir. 2008) 533 F.3d 1114, 1119 [quoting Chiron Corp. v. Ortho Diagnostic Sys., Inc. (9th Cir. 2000) 207 F.3d 1126, 1130; see, also, 9 U.S.C. § 2.) 

 

If an arbitration agreement is determined to be enforceable, a court “shall on application of one of the parties stay the trial of the action until such arbitration has been had in accordance with the terms of the agreement, providing the applicant for the stay is not in default in proceeding with such arbitration.”  (9 U.S.C. § 3.)   

II.        Discussion

A.        Governing Law

At the outset, the Court notes that Defendant clarified in its reply brief that it does not seek to compel arbitration of Plaintiff’s claims against Charousapha.  Accordingly, in solely moving to compel arbitration of Plaintiff’s claims against it, Defendant argues that the FAA’s substantive and procedural rules apply under the express terms of the 2025 DAA’s arbitration provision.

“[A]rbitration-related proceedings in California courts are governed by the CAA's procedural rules unless their application is preempted or the parties have expressly agreed that the FAA’s procedural rules apply.”  (Quach v. California Commerce Club, Inc. (2024) 16 Cal.5th 562, 576; see Cronus Investments, Inc. v. Concierge Services (2005) 35 Cal.4th 376, 394 [clarifying that the parties may expressly designate that arbitration must proceed under the FAA’s procedural rules rather than state procedural law].)  “The party asserting the FAA applies to an arbitration agreement bears the burden to ‘demonstrate FAA coverage by declarations and other evidence.’ [Citation].”  (Evenskaas v. California Transit, Inc. (2022) 81 Cal.App.5th 285, 292.) 

In reviewing the 2025 DAA, the Court finds that Defendant has demonstrated that the FAA covers the subject arbitration agreement, as the 2025 DAA’s arbitration provision expressly states the following: “This arbitration agreement is entered into pursuant to the Federal Arbitration Act, 9 U.S.C. §§ 1-16 (“FAA”) with respect to both substance and procedure.”  (Williams Decl. ¶ 8, Exh. C (“2025 DAA”), at p. 26 of 28.)  By expressly stating that the arbitrated agreement is entered into “pursuant to” the FAA “with respect to both substance and procedure,” this language requires that the parties arbitrate claims in conformance with the FAA’s substantive and procedural rules.  (Ibid.)  Moreover, there is no language in this provision to suggest that the parties intended that California arbitration law apply to any arbitration proceedings between them.  (Ibid.)  Given this plainly broad and unconditional adoption of the FAA’s substantive and procedural rules, the Court finds that these rules govern the subject arbitration agreement between the parties.  (Rodriguez v. American Technologies, Inc. (2006) 136 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1122 [“Thus, there is no ambiguity regarding the parties' intent. They adopted the FAA—all of it—to govern their arbitration.”]; compare id. at p. 1222 [explaining that the underlying contract’s use of the phrase “pursuant to the FAA” supported a finding that the FAA governed the parties’ arbitration] with Wright v. WellQuest Elk Grove, LLC (2026) 119 Cal.App.5th 267, 280 [distinguishing the phrase “in accordance with” the FAA from “pursuant to the FAA” as the former did not specifically incorporate the FAA’s procedural rules].)

Thus, as Defendant has shown that the FAA governs the subject arbitration agreement, the Court shall proceed with determining whether a valid agreement to arbitrate exists and whether the agreement covers the underlying dispute between the parties.

B.        Existence of the Agreement 

Under the FAA, arbitration agreements are valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, except on such grounds that exist at law or equity for voiding a contract.  (9 U.S.C. § 2.)  “Even when an agreement provides that it is governed by the FAA, courts must first apply state law principles in determining whether the parties entered into an agreement to arbitrate.” (Garcia v. Stoneledge Furniture LLC (2024) 102 Cal.App.5th 41, 51; City of Vista v. Sutro & Co. (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 401 [“Whether the parties formed a valid agreement to arbitrate is determined under general California contract law.”].)  “Although the burden of persuasion is always on the moving party to prove the existence of a valid agreement to arbitrate the controversy, the burden of production may shift in a three-step process.”  (Brockman v. Kaiser Foundation Hospitals (2025) 114 Cal.App.5th 569, 586.)  At the first step, “a movant need not ‘follow the normal procedures of document authentication and need only ‘allege the existence of an agreement and support the allegation as provided in rule [3.1330].’ [Citation.]”  (Iyere v. Wise Auto Group (2023) 87 Cal.App.5th 747, 755; see Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1330.) 

Here, Defendant submitted a copy of the signed acknowledgment form for the Business Signature Card (the “Signature Card”), which was signed on December 1, 2025, by Arlene Aranes Zapanta and Ramulfo Fernandez Zapanta Jr. for Plaintiff’s Chase Business Complete Checking Account (the “Account”).  (Williams Decl. ¶ 7, Exh.             Based on the prima facie evidence provided, the Court finds that Defendant has met its initial burden in proving the existence of a written agreement between the parties, thus shifting the burden of production to Plaintiff.

“If the moving party meets its initial prima facie burden and the opposing party disputes the agreement, then in the second step, the opposing party bears the burden of producing evidence to challenge the authenticity of the agreement. [Citation.] The opposing party can do this in several ways. For example, the opposing party may testify under oath or declare under penalty of perjury that the party never saw or does not remember seeing the agreement, or that the party never signed or does not remember signing the agreement.” (Gamboa v. Northeast Community Clinic (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 158, 165.)  “If the opposing party meets its burden of producing evidence, then in the third step, the moving party must establish with admissible evidence a valid arbitration agreement between the parties. Although Plaintiff contends that Defendant’s “own evidence does not clearly establish which arbitration provision governed Plaintiff’s claims when they accrued, how the later 2025 terms were delivered or accepted before the alleged losses occurred, or how a post-loss signature card retroactively applied to claims that had already matured,” (Opp’n at p. 6), Plaintiff failed to offer any evidence controverting the validity of the 2025 DAA, including any evidence showing that Plaintiff’s secretaries were uninformed of the 2025 DAA’s existence, do not recall or never saw the 2025 DAA, or were unable to access a copy of it prior to executing the Signature Card on Plaintiff’s behalf, (see Mirkhan Decl. ¶¶ 2–8).  Given the absence of any such evidence, the Court finds that Plaintiff has failed to carry its shifted burden of proof.

            Thus, based on the record evidence, the Court finds a valid arbitration agreement exists.

B.        Scope of the Agreement

In determining whether the parties actually agreed to arbitrate the underlying dispute, the FAA “establishes that, as a matter of federal law, any doubts concerning the scope of arbitrable issues should be resolved in favor of arbitration.”  (Chiron Corp., supra, 207 F.3d at p. 1130 [citing Moses H. Cone Memorial Hosp. v. Mercury Const. (1983) 460 U.S. 1, 24].) 

Here, the 2025 DAA’s arbitration provision states as follows regarding its scope:

What claims or disputes are subject to arbitration?

Claims or disputed factual or legal issues between you and us that arise out of or relate in any way to any aspect of our relationship, interactions, or dealings with each other, including but not limited to your deposit account, transactions involving your deposit account, whether actual, potential, canceled, or other transactions, any product, service, or agreement with us, or interactions of any kind with Chase employees or agents are subject to arbitration. Additionally, any claims or disputed factual or legal issue arising from or relating in any way to this agreement, any prior account agreement between us, the advertising of our products, or the application for, or the denial, approval or establishment of your account are included. Claims or disputed factual or legal issues that fall within the scope of this arbitration provision are subject to arbitration, regardless of what legal claim or theory they are based on or whether they seek legal or equitable remedies. Arbitration applies to any and all such claims or disputed factual or legal issues, whether they arose in the past, may currently exist, or may arise in the future. All such claims or disputed factual or legal issues are referred to in this section as “Claims.”

The only exception to arbitration of Claims is that both you and we have the right to pursue a Claim in a small claims court instead of arbitration, if the Claim is in that court’s jurisdiction and proceeds on an individual basis.

Any disputes regarding whether any Claim is subject to arbitration and/or the scope of this arbitration provision shall be decided by a court, not the arbitrator

(2025 DAA, at p. 26 of 28.) 

As the scope of arbitrable issues is expressly defined to include any and all “claims or disputed factual or legal issues, whether they arose in the past, may currently exist, or may arise in the future,” (2025 DAA, at p. 26 of 28), the arbitration provision encompasses Plaintiff’s claims against Defendant arising out of the alleged unauthorized transactions that occurred between June 4, 2024 and November 25, 2025, (FAC ¶¶ 15–19), even if those transactions predated the 2025 DAA and Signature Card.  Furthermore, “the ‘contention that an agreement to arbitrate a dispute must pre-date the actions giving rise to the dispute is misplaced. Such a suggestion runs contrary to contract principles which govern arbitration agreements.’ [Citation.]”  (Salgado v. Carrows Restaurants, Inc. (2019) 33 Cal.App.5th 356, 361.)  Thus, the Court finds that Plaintiff’s claims against Defendant in the instant action lie within the scope of the arbitration provision.

Accordingly, as the Court determined that a valid arbitration agreement exists between the parties and that this agreement encompasses the underlying dispute between them, and as no other defenses to agreement’s enforcement have been provided by Plaintiff, the Court finds that the 2025 DAA’s arbitration provision is enforceable against Plaintiff as to only its claims against Defendant. 

As to the effect of Charousapha’s claims on the enforceability of this arbitration agreement between Plaintiff and Defendant, Plaintiff is unable to rely on the presence of this third-party claim as a basis for denying the motion because the express language of the 2025 DAA makes clear that Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.2(c) is preempted by the FAA’s procedural rules. (2025 DAA, at p. 26 of 28; see, e.g., Rodriguez, supra, 136 Cal.App.4th at p. 1115 [holding that Section 1281.2(c) was preempted by the parties’ express contractual language and that the trial court erred in denying the motion to compel on this basis].)  However, as the parties do not appear to dispute that the claims against Charousapha are not subject to arbitration, Charousapha is not bound by the FAA’s rules, and the Court may properly exercise its discretion under Section 1281.2(c) to stay the action as to the claims made against her pending the outcome of arbitration.  (See, e.g., id. at pp. 1122–1123.) 

Therefore, the motion to compel arbitration and stay proceedings is granted. 

RULING

Defendant JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A.’s Motion to Compel Arbitration and Stay Proceedings is GRANTED.  The Court sets a Post-Arbitration Status Conference for September 16, 2027, at 8:30 a.m.

 


Case Number: 26NNCV05109    Hearing Date: September 18, 2026    Dept: T

Rosemarie Ramos vs. Terrace View Homeowners’ Association, Inc., et al.

Motion to Strike Post-Default Answer and Cross-Complaint

Moving Party:                         Plaintiff Rosemarie Ramos

Responding Party:                  Defendants Terrace View Homeowners’ Association, Inc. and Lordon Enterprises, Inc.

Tentative Ruling:                      Deny

 

BACKGROUND

 

            On June 29, 2026, Plaintiff Rosemaries Ramos (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Defendants Terrace View Homeowners’ Association, Inc (“Terrace View”), Lordon Enterprises, Inc. (“Lordon”), and Does 1 through 20, alleging premises liability.

 

            On July 6, 2026, Plaintiff personally served the complaint on Defendants Terrace View and Lordon (collectively “Defendants”). 

 

            On August 6, 2026, after Defendants failed to timely respond to the complaint, Plaintiff filed requests for entry of default, which were entered against them that same day.  A few hours after these defaults were entered, Defendants filed an answer to the complaint and Terrace View filed a cross-complaint. 

 

On August 20, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion to strike the cross-complaint and answer as improper based on the defaulted status of Defendants.

 

Defendants oppose, and Plaintiff replies. 

                       

ANALYSIS

I.          Legal Standard

A.        Motion to Strike

            California law authorizes a party’s motion to strike matter from an opposing party’s pleading if it is irrelevant, false, or improper.¿ (Code Civ. Proc. §§ 435, 436(a).)¿ Motions may also target pleadings or parts of pleadings which are not filed or drawn in conformity with applicable laws, rules or orders.  (Code Civ. Proc. § 436(b).)  A motion to strike is used to address defects that appear on the face of a pleading or from judicially noticed matter but that are not grounds for a demurrer.  (Pierson v Sharp Memorial Hospital (1989) 216 Cal.App.3d 340, 342; see, also, City & County of San Francisco v Strahlendorf (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1911, 1913 [motion may not be based on a party's declaration or factual representations made by counsel in the motion papers].)  In particular, a motion to strike can be used to attack the entire pleading or any part thereof – in other words, a motion to strike may target single words or phrases, unlike demurrers.  (Warren v. Atchison, Topeka & Santa Fe Railway Co. (1971) 19 Cal.App.3d 24, 40.)¿

            The Code of Civil Procedure also authorizes the Court to act on its own initiative to strike matters, empowering the Court to enter orders striking matter “at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper.”  (Code Civ. Proc. § 436.)¿ 

II.        Meet and Confer

            Based on the declaration of Plaintiff’s counsel Joseph A. Govea, it does not appear that the parties met and conferred by telephone, video, or in person.  (Govea Decl. ¶¶ 8–11.)  Nevertheless, “[a] determination by the court that the meet and confer process was insufficient shall not be grounds to grant or deny the motion to strike.”  (Code Civ. Proc. § 435.5, subd. (a)(4).)

III.       Discussion

A.                Motion to Strike

In her motion to strike the answer and cross-complaint, Plaintiff argues that these pleadings must be stricken as improper because Defendants filed these pleadings after default was already entered against them on August 6, 2026. 

“The entry of a default terminates a defendant’s rights to take any further affirmative steps in the litigation until either its default is set aside or a default judgment is entered.”  (Devlin v. Kearny Mesa AMC/Jeep/Renault, Inc. (1984) 155 Cal.App.3d 381, 385.)  “A defendant against whom a default has been entered is out of court and is not entitled to take any further steps in the cause affecting plaintiff's right of action; he cannot thereafter, until such default is set aside in a proper proceeding, file pleadings or move for a new trial or demand notice of subsequent proceedings.”  (Id. [quoting Brooks v. Nelson (1928) 95 Cal.App. 144, 147–148.)

At the outset, the Court notes that Plaintiff concedes that she failed to timely bring the instant motion under Code of Civil Procedure section 435(b)(1).  Although this procedural defect warrants denial of the motion, this defect does not alter the undisputed chronology establishing the improper filing of these pleadings by Defendants after default was entered against them. 

Therefore, the motion to strike is denied as untimely.  However, as the post-default filing of these pleadings by Defendants was indisputably not made in conformity with California law, the Court shall exercise its discretion under Code of Civil Procedure section 436 and strike the answer and cross-complaint in their entirety.  Furthermore, the Court notes that its ruling does not preclude Defendants from seeking relief under Code of Civil Procedure section 473 by way of a noticed motion.

RULING

Plaintiff Rosemarie Ramos’s Motion to Strike Post-Default Answer and Cross-Complaint is DENIED. 

The Court on its own motion orders that the Answer and Cross-Complaint filed on August 6, 2026, be stricken.
