DEPARTMENT H LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 24PSCV02402    Hearing Date: September 23, 2026    Dept: H

Pasamonte v. Rosales, et al., Case No. 24PSCV02402

 

ORDER ON MOTION FOR RECONSIDERATION

Plaintiffs’ Motion for Reconsideration is DENIED.

 

Background 

Plaintiffs Lourdes I. Pasamonte (“Lourdes”) and Angelito F. Pasamonte (“Angelito”), individuals and Trustees of the Lourdes I. Pasamonte & Angelito F. Pasamonte Revocable Living Trust dated August 4, 2022 (together, “Plaintiffs”) brought this lawsuit based on their allegations that they are the sole owners of the property located at 2609 E. Mary Court in West Covina (“subject property”). They alleged that while their grandchildren Marcus Timothy P. Rosales (“Marcus”) and Arnold Paolo I. Rosales (“Arnold”) were placed on title to the subject property to facilitate their parents’ (i.e., Angelo Rosales [“Angelo”] and Maria Rosales [“Maria”]) use and occupation of the subject property as tenants with regard to utilities and maintenance, Plaintiffs never intended to grant any ownership interest in the subject property to Marcus and/or Arnold. 

On July 14, 2026, the matter proceeded to bench trial. On July 23, 2026, the Court took the matter under submission. On July 27, 2026, the Court issued its decision following the bench trial. In doing so, the Court stated that “[t]his order constitutes an interlocutory determination and not the final judgment on accounting or distribution.” The Court ruled, in relevant part, as follows:

“The Court finds that Plaintiffs have not met their burden of proof as to the causes of action in the Operative Complaint and thus will order judgment in Defendants’ favor on the Operative Complaint. As to the Cross-Complaint, at least for the majority of the causes of action, the Court similarly concludes that the Cross-Complainants have not met their burden of proof (i.e., as to the causes of action for Conversion, Replevin, Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress, and Civil Harassment). However, as to the key property dispute, the Court affirms that Plaintiffs hold a 1/3 interest and Defendants Marcus and Arnold collectively hold a 2/3 interest in the Subject Property. The Court thus will order partition by sale of the Subject Property, as further detailed herein. The parties are directed to meet and confer within 20 days of the notice of ruling regarding the procedures for partition by sale and as to the selection of a referee and are ordered to file a Joint Status Report within 30 days of the notice of ruling. Cross-Complainants are ordered to file a Proposed Interlocutory Judgment consistent with this order within 30 days of the notice of ruling. Finally, the parties are directed to meet and confer within 20 days of the notice of ruling regarding arranging a time for Plaintiffs to retrieve the undisputed items of personal property left by Plaintiffs at the Subject Property.” 

A Status Conference Re: Joint Status Report is set for September 23, 2026.

 

Legal Standard 

Code of Civil Procedure section 1008, subdivision (a) provides as follows: “When an application for an order has been made to a judge, or to a court, and refused in whole or in part, or granted, or granted conditionally, or on terms, any party affected by the order may, within 10 days after service upon the party of written notice of entry of the order and based upon new or different facts, circumstances, or law, make application to the same judge or court that made the order, to reconsider the matter and modify, amend, or revoke the prior order. The party making the application shall state by affidavit what application was made before, when and to what judge, what order or decisions were made, and what new or different facts, circumstances, or law are claimed to be shown.”  

The legislative intent was to restrict motions for reconsideration to circumstances where a party offers the court some fact or circumstance not previously considered, and some valid reason for not offering it earlier. (Gilberd v. AC Transit (1995) 32 Cal.App.4th 1494, 1500; Mink v. Superior Court (1992) 2 Cal.App.4th 1338, 1342 [“[T]he party seeking reconsideration must provide not only new evidence but also a satisfactory explanation for the failure to produce that evidence at an earlier time.”]; New York Times Co. v. Superior Court (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 206, 212-213 [“The burden under section 1008 is comparable to that of a party seeking a new trial on the ground of newly discovered evidence: the information must be such that the moving party could not, with reasonable diligence, have discovered or produced it at the trial.”].) 

Reconsideration cannot be granted based on claims the court misinterpreted the law in its initial ruling; that is not a “new” or “different” matter. (Gilberd v. AC Transit, supra, 32 Cal.App.4th at p. 1500.) Moreover, counsel’s mistake based on ignorance of the law is not a proper basis for reconsideration. (Pazderka v. Caballeros Dimas Alang, Inc. (1998) 62 Cal.App.4th 658, 670.) 


Discussion

Plaintiffs move for an order reconsidering the Court’s Ruling on Submitted Matter entered on July 27, 2026, that followed the bench trial conducted from July 14-July 23, 2026. [FN] 

Plaintiffs, however, failed to file an affidavit, or even a declaration, with the moving papers as required under Code of Civil Procedure section 1008, subdivision (a). Plaintiffs acknowledge said failure but argue that the “omission is cured” because Defendants were on notice of every issue and counsel filed a declaration with the reply. (Reply at p. 2.)

Plaintiffs argue that “Section 1008(a) itself contains no provision barring a later-filed declaration from supplying the required showing.” (Id.) Plaintiffs’ contention is unpersuasive and misstated. The very words of the statute require an affidavit be filed with the application. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1008, subd. (a) [“The party making the application shall state by affidavit what application was made before, when and to what judge, what order or decisions were made, and what new or different facts, circumstances, or law are claimed to be shown (emphasis added).”].) 

A court does not have jurisdiction to hear a motion for reconsideration if the motion is not compliant with section 1008 (i.e., lacking an affidavit). Section 1008, subdivision (e) clearly states: “This section specifies the court’s jurisdiction with regard to applications for reconsideration of its orders and renewals of previous motions, and applies to all applications to reconsider any order of a judge or court, or for the renewal of a previous motion, whether the order deciding the previous matter or motion is interim or final. No application to reconsider any order or for the renewal of a previous motion may be considered by any judge or court unless made according to this section.” 

An application without an affidavit is invalid. (See Rowan v. Kirkpatrick (2020) 54 Cal.App.5th 289, 296-297 [“Kirkpatrick’s June 2, 2020, motion for reconsideration did not extend the time to appeal because it was procedurally defective and therefore not a ‘valid’ motion under Code of Civil Procedure section 1008, subdivision (a). . . . Under Code of Civil Procedure section 1008, subdivision (a), ‘any party affected’ by an order of the trial court ‘may, within 10 days after service upon the party of written notice of entry of the order and based upon new or different facts, circumstances, or law, make application to the same judge or court that made the order, to reconsider the matter and modify, amend, or revoke the prior order.’ . . . Both a motion for reconsideration under Code of Civil Procedure, subdivision (a), and a so-called ‘renewed’ motion under Code of Civil Procedure section 1008, subdivision (b), must be accompanied by a supporting affidavit. (internal citation omitted). . . . Kirkpatrick’s renewed motion was not ‘valid’ because it was not supported by an affidavit setting forth new or different facts, circumstances, or law.]”.) 

Furthermore, in Branner v. Regents of Univ. of California (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 1043, the Court of Appeal addressed the issue of a later-filed affidavit and concluded that the trial court’s allowance of a later-filed affidavit was improper. (Branner, supra, 175 Cal.App.4th at pp. 1048-1049.) There, the court held that Branner’s motion for reconsideration could not “be transformed from invalid to valid when the trial court later allowed him to file the affidavit” for two principal reasons: (1) “the language of rule 8.108(e) does not appear to countenance a piecemeal filing of a motion” and (2) “the requirement that a motion to reconsider be valid at the time it is served and filed serves the purpose of precluding the improper and uncertain extension of the time to appeal,” thus “promot[ing] the finality of judgments by forcing the losing party to take an appeal expeditiously or not at all.” (Id.) 

Plaintiffs point to In re Marriage of Barthold (2008) 158 Cal.App.4th 1301, in support of their argument that while the motion technically violates Code of Civil Procedure section 1008, the Court still has the right to consider the motion on its merits. Plaintiffs, however, acknowledge that the holdings in In re Marriage of Barthold and the relied upon case Le Francois v. Goel (2005) 35 Cal.4th 1094, state that a court can move “on [its] own motion” to reconsider its earlier motion even if the filed motion is procedurally barred. (In re Marriage of Barthold, supra, 158 Cal.App.4th at 1308 [“We conclude that Le Francois simply requires that the trial court reconsider a prior ruling based on its own realization that the ruling was erroneous, and not based upon a determination that the motion to reconsider should itself be granted on its merits.”].) The Court is not persuaded that its July 27, 2026 ruling was erroneous and thus declines to act sua sponte. 

Based on the foregoing, Plaintiffs’ motion for reconsideration is DENIED.

  

[FN] As a preliminary matter, the Court notes that Code of Civil Procedure section 1008 applies to motions to reconsider interlocutory orders. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1008, subds. (e), (h) [“(e) This section specifies the court’s jurisdiction with regard to applications for reconsideration of its orders and renewals of previous motions, and applies to all applications to reconsider any order of a judge or court, or for the renewal of a previous motion, whether the order deciding the previous matter or motion is interim or final. …(h) This section applies to all applications for interim orders.”].)

Case Number: 25PSCV03077    Hearing Date: September 23, 2026    Dept: H

Perrera v. Claremont Gardens Apartments, LLC, Case No. 25PSCV03077 

 

ORDER ON DEFENDANT’S MOTION FOR PROTECTIVE ORDER

Defendant’s Motion for Protective Order is CONTINUED to November 13, 2026, at 9:00 a.m. so the parties can conduct a reasonable and good faith meet-and-confer session and provide supplemental briefing to the Court.

 

Background 

Plaintiff Sue B. Perrera (“Plaintiff”) alleges as follows:

On September 29, 2023, Plaintiff was walking towards a delivery person at the front door of her apartment, located at 856 W. Bonita Ave., Apt. I, Claremont, CA 91711 (“Apartment”), when her foot caught on a crack in the kitchen’s vinyl linoleum floor. She fell onto her right shoulder, arm, and head, sustaining a right shoulder fracture. The kitchen floor is old and has developed large cracks and gaps. 

On August 25, 2025, Plaintiff filed a Personal Injury Complaint, alleging causes of action against Defendant Claremont Gardens Apartments, LLC (“Defendant”) and Does 1-50, inclusive, for: (1) Premises Liability and (2) Negligence.  

A Post-Mediation Status Conference/Case Management Conference is set for January 14, 2027. 

 

Legal Standard 

The court must restrict the frequency or extent of use of discovery if (a) “the discovery sought is unreasonably cumulative or duplicative, or is obtainable from some other source that is more convenient, less burdensome, or less expensive;” or (b) “the selected method of discovery is unduly burdensome or expensive.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2019.030, subd. (a).)  Such an order may be made on a motion for a protective order by a party or affected person, which must be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration.  (Code Civ. Proc. § 2019.030, subd. (b).) 

“When interrogatories have been propounded, the responding party, and any other party or affected natural person or organization may promptly move for a protective order.  This motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.090, subd. (a).) 

“When an inspection, copying, testing, or sampling of documents, tangible things, places, or electronically stored information has been demanded, the party to whom the demand has been directed, and any other party or affected person, may promptly move for a protective order.  This motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.060, subd. (a).)   

“The Court, for good cause shown, may make any order that justice requires to protect any party…from unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression, or undue burden and expense.”  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.090, subd. (b), 2031.060, subd. (b).)  Sections 2030.090, subdivision (b) and 2031.060, subdivision (b) provide a nonexclusive list of directions that may be included in a protective order, including orders directing that the interrogatories need not be answered, that the items not be produced or made available, and/or that the time to respond be extended, etc.  (Id.) 

 

Discussion

Defendant is moving for a protective order generally limiting the breadth of Plaintiff’s First Set of Special Interrogatories and Requests for Production. As to the Form Interrogatories, Defendant states it “does not take issue with plaintiff’s Form Interrogatories and will gladly respond to them once the Court rules on this motion.” (Mot., at p. 2.) 

A.       Meet and Confer 

Defendant’s counsel represents as follows:

On June 5, 2026, counsel emailed opposing counsel to initiate a meet and confer conference to discuss the issues outlined in the operative motion. (Gutierrez Decl., ¶ 6, Ex. D.) On June 12, 2026, Plaintiff’s counsel responded: 

“We are going to stand on our discovery requests as presently drafted. You propounded a significant number of discovery to our office and we handled it and responded timely. Part of our discovery propounded is based on a line of inquiry that you made including Section 8 inquiries and others. We will oppose any motion that is propounded and request sanctions for having to do so. 

If you have some specific objectionable items of discovery, then the Code requires that you identify them in a meet and confer letter and we will respond accordingly.”

(Id., ¶ 7, Ex. E.)

Plaintiff’s counsel notes that “Defendant did not thereafter identify any specific interrogatory or request for production that it contended was improper. Defendant did not provide a request-by-request analysis. Defendant did not propose any targeted limitation. Defendant did not call Plaintiff’s counsel to discuss any specific discovery request prior to filing its Motion for Protective Order.” (Paris Decl., ¶ 8.) 

In Defendant’s counsel’s initial email, however, counsel stated multiple times that he was hoping to set up a phone call to discuss the discovery issues and that the parties “need to have that discussion . . . no later than Friday, June 12, 2026” with Defendant’s counsel noting, “My calendar is clear and I will keep it clear for such a call.” (Gutierrez Decl., Ex. D.)

Plaintiff’s counsel misstates the Code requirements. The moving party must show “a reasonable and good faith attempt, either in person, by telephone, or by videoconference, to informally resolve each issue presented by the motion.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2016.040, subd. (a).) A letter is not required or the only means of meeting and conferring; meet and confer efforts by letter are usually found deficient in California courts.

Based on the foregoing, while the Court finds the meet and confer efforts to be insufficient, Defendant’s counsel did attempt to meet and confer and to schedule a telephonic conference that was not acknowledged in opposing counsel’s reply email. Accordingly, the Court elects to address the merits of the motion. 

B.       Protective Order 

Defendant argues that the 150 Special Interrogatories (“SROGs”) and the 100 Requests for Production (“RPDs”) are overbroad and seek unnecessary and irrelevant information. (Mot., at p. 7.) Defendant also takes issue with the discovery requests seeking “all facts,” “all persons,” and “all documents” and labels said breadth of requests as “shotgun” style. (Mot., at p. 8.) Defendant further argues that the requests are cumulative at times and seek information that is readily and equally available to Plaintiff, such as Plaintiff’s prior reports on the kitchen floor. (Mot., at pp. 8-9.) Defendant notes that it “does not take the position that plaintiff’s entire set of attorney drafted interrogatories or document production requests should be stricken,” and instead is seeking an order limiting the scope. (Mot., at p. 9.) 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that Defendant has not carried its burden because “Defendant does not identify a single interrogatory or request that should be barred, narrowed, deferred, or limited[,] . . . does not estimate the time or expense required to respond[,] . . . does not identify any custodian, file, record category, or search burden[, and] . . . does not explain why any specific request is duplicative, cumulative, oppressive, or improper.” (Opp., at p. 4.) In addition, Plaintiff argues that her discovery “seeks the corresponding information” that Defendant had requested in its own discovery requests. (Opp., at p. 5.) 

Plaintiff seeks to justify the number of special interrogatories by pointing to, among other things, the number of disputed factual issues and the fact that interrogatories are a more efficient and less expensive means compared to depositions. (Opp., at p. 7.) 

Based on the foregoing, the Court finds the number of SROGs, far exceeding the 35 allowed by the Code, and RPDs to be excessive and that Plaintiff fails to provide sufficient justification for the bloated amount. This is a personal injury complaint with two causes of action. The action arises from a “trip and fall” in Plaintiff’s apartment. The issues here are not complex and do not warrant over 250 SROGs and RPDs. In addition, the latter two “justification” arguments speak more towards the benefits of interrogatories as a discovery method rather than as support for the excess number propounded here. 

Plaintiff unpersuasively attempts to point the finger at Defendant in a quasi “tit-for-tat” argument. First, the contention is attenuated by the difference in quantity of Defendant’s SROGs and RPDs: Defendant puts forth 44 SROGs and 49 RPDs. (Paris Decl., Ex. E.) Second, by putting forth such an argument, the implications cut across Plaintiff’s prior contention that the number of SROGs and RPDs are not meant to harass or oppress Defendant. 

The Court also finds that even at a cursory glance, the discovery requests seem redundant. For example, SROGs Nos. 2 and 3 ask Defendant to state whether there was any procedure for reporting and then to again identify and to describe that procedure. (Gutierrez Decl., Ex. B.) This issue extends to other interrogatories as well, and the Court determines that many interrogatories can be combined and/or stricken as duplicative. 

The Court, however, agrees with Plaintiff that Defendant’s own motion suffers from the same issue identified in its motion: it “paints with a broad brush.” (Mot., at p. 9.) Defendant has not identified or spoken clearly as to which SROGs and/or RPDs are at issue here and instead relies on the Court to carry its burden. Defendant does not address said argument in its reply. 

Based on the foregoing, because it is unclear which specific discovery requests are at issue, and Defendant is not seeking a protective order covering all requests, the Court continues the hearing on the motion to allow for supplemental briefing identifying the discovery requests at issue. Moreover, because the hearing is being continued, the Court also will require a reasonable and good faith meet-and-confer session to take place either by telephone or videoconference or in person. The Court expects the parties to work together in good faith to resolve or narrow the disputes at issue.

Accordingly, the hearing on Defendant’s motion for a protective order is CONTINUED to November 13, 2026, at 9:00 a.m. The parties are directed to conduct a reasonable and good faith meet-and-confer session in person or by telephone or videoconference by October 7, 2026 and are to file by October 8, 2026 a joint declaration summarizing the results of the meet-and-confer session and indicating which specific issues remain in dispute. If disputes remain, Defendant’s supplemental brief to the motion for protective order¿shall be filed and served on or before October 20, 2026, and Plaintiff’s supplemental opposition shall be filed and served on or before November 3, 2026. [FN]

 

  

[FN] As to the issue of sanctions, because the Court finds that the number of discovery requests are overbroad and impermissibly excessive, Plaintiff’s request is denied. Defendant does not move for sanctions. (Gutierrez Decl., ¶ 9.)
