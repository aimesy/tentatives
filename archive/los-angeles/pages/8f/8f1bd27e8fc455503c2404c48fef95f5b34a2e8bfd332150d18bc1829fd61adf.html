Case Number: 24NWCV01744    Hearing Date: September 15, 2026    Dept: L

JOSE ANTONIO SUCILLA CARDENAS vs CHEDRAUI USA, INC.

CASE NO.: 24NWCV01744

HEARING: September 15, 2026 @ 9:30 a.m.

 

#17

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Defendant Chedraui USA, Inc.’s Motion for Summary Judgment is GRANTED.

 

Defendant to give notice.

 

Background

 

This is a premises liability action regarding property located at 9320 Slauson Ave Pico Rivera, CA 90660 (“Premises”). On June 4, 2024, Plaintiff Jose Antonio Sucilla Cardenas (“Plaintiff”) filed this action against Defendant Chedraui USA, Inc., doing business as El Super, (“Defendant”) and Does 1 to 50. The gravamen of the Complaint is as follows: “Plaintiff was assigned to security for the Defendant. The Plaintiff was asked by a store associate to assist him in moving broken down carts roughly 6 feet off the floor. In the process, the Plaintiff fell while carrying the carts and ultimately was injured at the [Premises].” (Compl., p. 4.) The Complaint alleges two causes of action for 1) General Negligence, and 2) Premises Liability.

 

On August 24, 2024, Defendant filed its Answer.

 

Defendant now moves for summary judgment, or in the alternative, summary adjudication on all causes of action.

 

On August 27, 2026, the Court continued the hearing on this Motion to today’s date on its own motion. (8/27/26 Minute Order.)


Legal Standard

 

The function of a motion for summary judgment or adjudication is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party cannot show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (c) “requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119, internal citations omitted.) “The function of the pleadings in a motion for summary judgment is to delimit the scope of the issues; the function of the affidavits or declarations is to disclose whether there is any triable issue of fact within the issues delimited by the pleadings.” (Juge v. County of Sacramento (1993) 12 Cal.App.4th 59, 67, citing FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 367, 381-382.)

 

In moving for summary judgment, a defendant has met its “burden of showing that a cause of action has no merit if the party has shown that one or more elements of the cause of action, even if not separately pleaded, cannot be established, or that there is a complete defense to the cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).)

 

Once a moving party has satisfied the initial burden of proof, the burden “shifts to the opposing party to show, by responsive separate statement and admissible evidence, that triable issues of fact exist.” (Ostayan v. Serrano Reconveyance Co. (2000) 77 Cal.App.4th 1411, 1418, disapproved on other grounds by Black Sky Cap., LLC v. Cobb (2019) 7 Cal.5th 156, 165.) To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence. (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

 

Discussion

 

The parties primarily dispute two issues: 1) whether the Privette doctrine and its exceptions apply to bar Plaintiff’s claims, and 2) if the Privette doctrine does not apply, whether Plaintiff can establish a triable issue of fact as to his claims based on a dangerous condition.

 

The Court will discuss each issue in turn.

 

          Application of the Privette Doctrine and its Exceptions

 

Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s causes of action are barred by the Privette doctrine, established by the seminal case Privette v. Superior Court (“Privette”) (1993) 5 Cal.4th 689. The Privette doctrine stands for the proposition that, when employees of independent contractors are injured at a worksite, they generally cannot sue the party that hired the independent contractor. Workers compensation provided for by the independent contractor employer is the employees’ exclusive remedy. Here, Plaintiff was an employee of Defender Private Security which contracted with Defendant for security guard services. (Taylor Decl., ¶¶ 3-4, Exhs. B-C; Cardenas Depo., 14:19-25, 15:1-14, 18:23-19:12.) Plaintiff is pursuing a workers’ compensation claim for his injuries that are the subject of this case. (Taylor Decl., ¶ 9, Ex. H.) Thus, Plaintiff’s claim is barred by the Privette doctrine.

 

Defendant preemptively argues that the “negligent exercise of retained control” exception to the Privette doctrine does not apply here. Such exception is established in the case of Hooker v. Department of Transportation (“Hooker”) (2002) 27 Cal.App.4th 198. The Hooker case stated that the hirer can be liable to an independent contractor’s employee if the hirer retains control over safety conditions on the job site and exercises that control in a manner that “affirmatively contributes” to the employee’s injury. (Id. at p. 214.) However, if the hirer passively permits an unsafe condition to exist on the job site, such conduct is not an “affirmative contribution” to the employee’s injury, and the hirer cannot be liable. (Id. at pp. 214-215.) Here, Plaintiff admitted that he fell due to his foot failing when he attempted to reach the carts on the shelf. (Taylor Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. C; Cardenas Depo., 64:20-65:7, 66:7-66:18.) Plaintiff admitted that he is not aware of any defects or dangerous conditions at the premises. (Taylor Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. C; Cardenas Depo., 68:20-69:7.) At most, Plaintiff voluntarily responded to a request for assistance by a coworker. (Taylor Decl., ¶¶ 3-5, Exhs. B-D; Cardenas Depo., 60:22 61:15, RFA No. 45.) Thus, Plaintiff’s injury was not caused by any dangerous condition or “affirmative contribution” by Defendant.

 

Further, Defendant argues that the “concealed hazard exception” to the Privette doctrine does not apply to this case. That exception states that a hirer/landowner may be held liable for an injury of an independent contractor employee if the landowner knew of a concealed preexisting hazardous condition on its property, the contractor did not know and could not have reasonably discovered this hazardous condition, and the landowner failed to warn the contractor about the condition. As admitted by Plaintiff and as established by the video footage, no dangerous condition existed. (Taylor Decl., ¶¶ 4, 8, Exhs. C, G; Cardenas Depo., 68:20-69:7.) Even if a dangerous condition existed, Plaintiff should have or reasonably could have realized potential risks of reaching for carts on top of a tall shelf. Thus, the “concealed hazard exception” does not apply.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that the Privette doctrine does not apply because Plaintiff was performing work unrelated to his contracted security services when he was injured. Further, the policies behind the Privette doctrine would not be furthered by application of the doctrine to this case. Specifically, independent contractors are in the best position to provide workers’ compensation insurance for injuries that occur while the worker is performing contracted work. The hirer indirectly pays for such insurance in the negotiated contract price. Here, Plaintiff was injured while performing non-contracted work—i.e., grocery store-related work. Plaintiff’s employer, Defender Private Security, did not contract with Defendant with the expectation that Plaintiff would be performing grocery store-related tasks. Thus, Defendant, rather than Plaintiff’s employer, is in a better position to compensate Plaintiff.

 

Alternatively, assuming that the Privette doctrine applies, Plaintiff argues that the “retained control” exception applies. Specifically, Defendant retained control of the manner in which Plaintiff could perform his security duties. As a result, Plaintiff treated “requests” from the store employees as a part of his job duties. (Alberts Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. 1; Cardenas Depo., 30:1-25, 34:14-35:4.) Plaintiff performed such store-related tasks in the middle of performing security services, two to three times each week. (Alberts Decl., ¶ 3, Exh. 1; Cardenas Depo., 32:9-13.) Further, in directing Plaintiff to reach the shopping carts, Defendant (through its employee), “affirmatively contributed” to Plaintiff’s injury.

 

In reply, Defendant notes that the opposition inconsistently argues that 1) Plaintiff was acting outside of the scope of his contracted duties at the time he was injured, and 2) Plaintiff treated the cashier’s request as a duty, which led to his injury. Defendant argues that it did not affirmatively contribute to Plaintiff’s injury. Plaintiff himself testified that the cashier asked him for help in retrieving the carts from the shelf. (Alberts Decl., ¶ 3 Exh. 1, 60:19-25, 63:2-5.) A hirer is not liable under the “retained control” exception unless there is evidence that the hirer retained control over the contractor’s assigned work. Here, “[n]othing about the cashier’s request for help gave El Super control over the manner in which DPS or its employees performed contracted security services.” (Reply, 3:9-11.)

 

“The Privette doctrine holds that a hirer generally delegates to an independent contractor all responsibility for workplace safety and is not liable for injuries sustained by the contractor or its workers while on the job.” (Gonzalez v. Mathis (“Gonzalez”) (2018) 12 Cal.5th 29, 40.)¿

 

The “retained control” exception was recognized in Hooker, supra, 27 Cal.App.4th 198, 202. The exception applies if: (1) the hirer retains control over the manner in which the contractor performs the work; (2) the hirer actually exercises its retained control by involving itself in the work such that the contractor is not entirely free to do the work in its own manner; and (3) the hirer’s exercise of retained control affirmatively contributes to the worker's injury. (Sandoval v. Qualcomm Incorporated (“Sandoval”) (2021) 12 Cal.5th 256, 276-277.) Under this exception, the hirer’s delegation of tort duties to the independent contractor can be seen as “incomplete” or “only partial[ ]” because it retains control over some aspect of the work and actually exercises that retained control. (Id. at pp. 271, 283.)¿ 

¿

The “concealed hazard” exception was recognized in Kinsman v. Unocal Corp. (2005) 37 Cal.4th 659, 664, as modified (Mar. 1, 2006). This exception applies if the hirer is also an owner or possessor of land, and if “the landowner knew, or should have known, of a latent or concealed preexisting hazardous condition on its property, the contractor did not know and could not have reasonably discovered this hazardous condition, and the landowner failed to warn the contractor about this condition.” (Id. at p. 664.) Under this exception, the hirer’s delegation of tort duties can be seen as “ineffective” because the independent contractor cannot protect its workers against a hazard it does not know about and could not reasonably discover. (Sandoval, supra, 12 Cal.5th at p. 271).

 

“[T]he Privette doctrine gives rise to a rebuttable presumption that affects the burden of producing evidence. If the moving party establishes that it hired an independent contractor to perform certain work, and that the plaintiff is an employee of the contractor who was injured in the course of the work, the burden shifts to the opposing party to come forward with evidence raising a triable issue of fact as to whether an exception to the Privette doctrine applies.” (Degala v. John Stewart Co. (“Degala”) (2023) 88 Cal.App.5th 158, 166-167.)

 

Here, the Court is not persuaded that Defendant has met its burden in proving that the Privette doctrine applies. Both parties generally agree that the cashier’s request and Plaintiff’s injury occurred outside of Plaintiff’s contracted duties as a security guard. The Court relies on the following evidence:

 

-      At the time of Plaintiff’s injury, he acted as a security guard with duties including protecting the store and its customers/employees. (See Taylor Decl., ¶¶ 3-4, Exhs. B-C; Cardenas Depo., 29:1, 31:20, 35:24-36:5.)

-      The agreement between the parties shows that Plaintiff was contracted to provide security services only, not grocery store-related tasks. (Ibid.)

-      Plaintiff testified that the cashier merely asked for help in reaching for the carts on the shelf. (See Alberts Decl., ¶ 3 Exh. 1, 60:19-25, 63:2-5.)

-      Plaintiff admitted that he “voluntarily decided to help the employee at the SUBJECT LOCATION to move the ROLLING BASKET down form the top of the shelf.” (Taylor Decl., ¶ 5, Exh. D., RFA No. 45.)

-      Plaintiff also admitted that 1) his “duties at the time of the incident did not include moving shopping carts,” and 2) his “job duties did not include taking orders from other employees at the [store],” and 3) he was “not required in [his] role as a security guard at the time of the Incident to move any shopping carts from the top of a shelf.” (Taylor Decl., ¶ 5, Exh. D., RFA Nos. 37-38, 42.)

 

There is otherwise no evidence that the cashier’s request was a part of Plaintiff’s contracted duties. Plaintiff’s subjective belief that the cashier’s request was a duty, not a favor, is irrelevant. Overall, the Court finds that Defendant has failed to demonstrate that Plaintiff “was injured in the course of the work” he was contracted for. (See Degala, supra, 88 Cal.App.5th at p. 167.) Thus, the Privette doctrine does not apply here.

 

Relatedly, authority on the “retained control” exception further sheds light why the Privette doctrine does not apply. “‘[R]etained control’ refers specifically to a hirer’s authority over work entrusted to the contractor, i.e., work the contractor has agreed to perform. . . A hirer's authority over noncontract work — although potentially giving rise to other tort duties — thus does not give rise to a retained control duty unless it has the effect of creating authority over the contracted work. [Citation.] (Sandoval, supra, 12 Cal.5th at pp. 274–275.) In other words, the retained control exception only applies if the hirer retains control of the manner in which contracted work is performed. Here, the Court finds that the cashier’s request 1) was unrelated to the contracted work (security services), and 2) did not constitute control over the manner in which his security duties were performed. For example, the cashier did not tell Plaintiff to use certain equipment or methods in protecting the store.

 

Accordingly, the Court finds that Defendant has failed to meet its burden of proof in establishing that the Privette doctrine bars Plaintiff’s claims.

 

          Negligence and Premises Liability

 

“The elements of a cause of action for premises liability are the same as those for negligence: duty, breach, causation, and damages.” (Castellon v. U.S. Bancorp (2013) 220 Cal.App.4th 994, 998.)

 

Defendant separately argues that Plaintiff cannot establish all elements of his negligence and premises liability claims. Firstly, Plaintiff cannot establish the element of duty because Defendant was not Plaintiff’s employer and had no control over his work. Second, Plaintiff cannot establish the elements of breach and causation. As to breach/existence of a dangerous condition, Plaintiff admitted that 1) he is not aware of any defect or dangerous condition at the Premises and 2) the shelf did not move when he was bringing down the carts. (Taylor Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. C; Cardenas Depo., 66:7-66:18, 68:20-69:7.) Plaintiff further admitted that he fell because his foot failed him. (Taylor Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. C; Cardenas Depo., 64:20-65:7, 66:7-66:18.) Thus, Plaintiff contributed to his own damages by agreeing to help someone who was not his manager (Taylor Decl., ¶ 5, Exh. D, RFA No. 45) and by not using any stool or ladder to help him reach the shopping carts (Taylor Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. C; Cardenas Depo., 64:20 65:7, 66:7-66:18). Lastly, Defendant argues that Plaintiff voluntarily assumed the risk of injury—he was aware of the height of the shelf and could plainly see the shopping carts. (Taylor Decl., ¶ 6, Exh. E.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that Defendant, as owner of the Premises, owed a non-delegable duty to make the Premises safe. Plaintiff asserts that a triable issue of fact exists as to the elements of breach and causation. Firstly, it is a question of fact as to whether the shopping carts stacked on top of a 6-foot shelf, without warning to obtain a stool or ladder, constituted an “unsafe” or “dangerous” condition that led to the Plaintiff’s injuries. Further, the assumption of risk doctrine does not apply. Rather, any negligence by Plaintiff should be assessed in the context of assigning each party’s proportion of fault.

 

In reply, Defendant argues that Plaintiff “attempts to manufacture a triable issue of fact by focusing on whether the stacked carts on a shelf could be characterized as an ‘unsafe’ or ‘dangerous’ condition.” (Repl., 6:12-14.) However, such issue is immaterial where Plaintiff’s own testimony establishes that there is no causation between the alleged unsafe condition (i.e., the stacked shopping carts) and the injury.

 

The Court finds that, where the Privette doctrine does not apply, Defendant owed a regular duty of care to maintain its premises as landowner. It is irrelevant that Defendant was not Plaintiff’s employer. “It is well established in California that although a store owner is not an insurer of the safety of its patrons, the owner does owe them a duty to exercise reasonable care in keeping the premises reasonably safe.” (Ortega v. Kmart Corp. (2001) 26 Cal.4th 1200, 1205.)

 

The Court further finds that Defendant has not met its burden in disproving the element of breach/existence of a dangerous condition. Defendant only cites Plaintiff’s testimony that no dangerous condition existed and that the shelf did not move when he was reaching for and bringing down the baskets. (Taylor Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. C; Cardenas Depo., 66:7-66:18, 68:20-69:7.) Defendant’s evidence is insufficient. For example, Defendant provides no expert evidence that the shopping carts were stacked according to industry standards or that the shelf was stable.

 

However, the Court finds that Defendant has met its burden as to the issue of proximate causation, and Plaintiff fails to establish a triable issue of fact as to this element. Plaintiff admitted that he fell because his foot failed under him. (Taylor Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. C; Cardenas Depo., 64:20-65:7, 66:7-66:18.) Plaintiff does not necessarily dispute this fact. Plaintiff only argues that Plaintiff fell because he was “ordered” by a cashier to bring a cart down, he had never gotten a cart down before, he is 5-feet, 9-inches tall, the shelf was 1 foot taller than him, there were six to nine carts on the shelf, each cart had 4 or 5 carts folded inside of each cart, he didn’t know there were more carts inside the cart, and the baskets weighed about 10 to 15 pounds each. (See Plaintiff’s Response to Sep. Statement, UMF No. 11.) Plaintiff also notes that he was not trained to bring down the shopping carts. (See Plaintiff’s Response to Sep. Statement, UMF No. 10.) Plaintiff only cites to his own deposition testimony. The Court finds that Plaintiff’s evidence bears on whether a dangerous condition existed, not whether the condition caused Plaintiff’s injury.

 

Upon closer inspection of Plaintiff’s testimony, when asked whether he could see the condition of the stacked shopping carts on the shelf, Plaintiff stated the following: “I saw them, but -- I thought that I could handle them, but I couldn't. My -- my foot ended up folding, my toes broke, and at that time, they fell on top of me.” (Taylor Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. C; Cardenas Depo., 64:20-24.) Such testimony refutes any theory by Plaintiff that 1) he could not fully appreciate the risk of reaching for the shopping carts and 2) the shopping carts caused the fall.

 

Further, the Court notes that, the fact that Plaintiff had never performed this task and was not trained to perform such task prior to the incident is further evidence that the task was not within his contracted work. As discussed previously, the Court is not persuaded that Plaintiff was acting within his contracted duties when the incident occurred.

 

Therefore, the Court grants summary judgment on grounds that Plaintiff has failed to establish a triable issue of fact as to causation, a necessary element of general negligence and premises liability claims.

 

Conclusion

 

Based on the foregoing, Defendant’s Motion for Summary Judgment is granted.


Case Number: 25NWCV01601    Hearing Date: September 15, 2026    Dept: L

MAXINE CARRASCO vs GABRIEL DEL ANGEL DOMINGUEZ, et al.

CASE NO.: 25NWCV01601

HEARING: September 15, 2026 @ 9:30 a.m.

 

#18

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Plaintiff Maxine Carrasco’s Motion to Compel Further Responses and Document Production from Defendant Gabriel Del Angel Dominguez is GRANTED.

 

Plaintiff to give notice.

 

Background

 

This is a motor vehicle negligence action involving an accident that occurred on June 14, 2023. On May 5, 2025, Plaintiff Maxine Carrasco (“Plaintiff”) filed this action against Defendants Gabriel Del Angel Dominguez, Del Angel Trucking, and Does 1 to 100. The Complaint alleges two causes of action for 1) Negligence and 2) Negligence (California Vehicle Code § 21703).

 

On June 26, 2025, Plaintiff served Defendant Gabriel Del Angel Dominguez (hereinafter, “Dominguez”) with Requests for Production, Set Two (“RFPs”). (Ortiz Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. A.)

 

On September 11, 2025, Dominguez served responses to the RFPs. (Ortiz Decl., ¶ 5, Exh. B.) On November 13, 2025, Dominguez served unverified supplemental responses to the RFPs. (Ortiz Decl., ¶ 7, Exh. D.)

 

Plaintiff now moves to compel Dominguez’s further responses to RFP Nos. 5, 6, and 16, involving surveillance/sub rosa information.


Legal Standard

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.210 states the following:

 

“(a) The party to whom a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling has been directed shall respond separately to each item or category of item by any of the following:

(1) A statement that the party will comply with the particular demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling by the date set for the inspection, copying, testing, or sampling pursuant to paragraph (2) of subdivision (c) of Section 2031.030 and any related activities.

(2) A representation that the party lacks the ability to comply with the demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling of a particular item or category of item.

(3) An objection to the particular demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling.”

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.210, subd. (a).)

 

On receipt of responses to a demand for production, the demanding party may move for an order compelling a further response if that party finds 1) a statement of compliance with the demand is incomplete, 2) the response is inadequate, incomplete, or evasive, or 3) an objection in the response is without merit or too general. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (a).)

 

Meet and Confer

 

A motion to compel further responses to requests for production shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(2).)

 

On September 26, 2025, Plaintiff served a meet and confer letter to Dominguez regarding his initial responses. (Ortiz Decl., ¶ 6, Exh. C.) After Dominguez served his supplemental responses, Plaintiff served another meet and confer letter, particularly discussing the sub rosa materials requests. (Ortiz Decl., ¶ 8, Exh. E.) The parties thereafter agreed to extend Dominguez’s response deadline and Plaintiff’s motion to compel deadline. (Ortiz Decl., ¶ 9, Exh. F.) Although the parties thereafter attempted to meet and confer, they were unable to come to a resolution. (Ortiz Decl., ¶ 10.) Plaintiff thus requested an Informal Discovery Conference, which took place on April 27, 2026. (Ortiz Decl., ¶¶ 11-12, 14.) The parties were unable to come to an agreement. (Ibid.)

 

The Court finds that the meet and confer requirement has been met.

 

Discussion

 

Plaintiff moves to compel Dominguez’s further responses to the following RFPs:

-      RFP No. 5: All color photographs or videos taken of PLAINTIFF from the date of the INCIDENT through present.

-      RFP No. 6: A copy of all sub rosa films taken of PLAINTIFF as a result of the INCIDENT.

-      RFP No. 16: All Accident/Investigation reports in connection with this INCIDENT.

 

Dominguez primarily objected to these RFPs on grounds that they seek information that is protected by attorney-client privilege and the work product doctrine. Dominguez argues that photographs or films reflecting counsel’s strategies in preparation for trial—e.g., video or surveillance film impeaching plaintiff’s claim of injury—should be treated as “qualified” work product. Plaintiff must show that that she would be unduly prejudiced without such discovery. Dominguez further responded that he “should not be required to disclose whether such a video exists until after [P]laintiff has been deposed. This would allow [Dominguez] to impeach plaintiff at deposition, while still allowing plaintiff the opportunity to obtain said video for trial.” (See Sep. Statement.)

 

Plaintiff firstly argues that the attorney-client privilege does not apply to surveillance evidence, citing Suezaki v. Superior Court of Santa Clara County (“Suezaki”) (1962) 58 Cal. 2d 166. Specifically, the Suezaki court found that sub rosa materials are not a “communication” for purposes of the attorney-client privilege and that a photograph does not become privileged by the mere fact that it was taken for the purpose of litigation and eventually transmitted to counsel.

 

Next, Plaintiff argues that the work product doctrine does not bar discovery of the sub rosa materials. The materials do not constitute a “writing that reflects an attorney’s impressions, conclusions, opinions, or legal research or theories is not discoverable under any circumstances” under Code of Civil Procedure section 2018.030, subdivision (a). In other words, the materials are not entitled to absolute protection. Thus, assuming the work product doctrine applies, the sub rosa materials would only be subject to qualified protection under Code of Civil Procedure section 2018.030, subdivision (b). Under Suezaki, whether work product is entitled to qualified protection should be determined on a case-by-case basis, considering all factors. Plaintiff argues that “[she needs] the surveillance evidence obtained by [Dominguez] to protect against surprise and to prepare to examine the person who took the video/photographs” and that such evidence will “allow Plaintiff[] to review the materials for authenticity and otherwise prepare effectively for trial, and it may also even encourage settlement of the suit, a legitimate function of pre-trial discovery.” (Mot., 8:4-10.)

 

Plaintiff additionally argues that Dominguez’s intention to use the surveillance evidence to impeach Plaintiff at her deposition is not a proper objection. Plaintiff cites Atchison, Topeka & S. F. Ry. Co. v. Superior Court (“Atchinson”) (1962) 208 Cal. App. 2d 73, 77.

 

In opposition, Dominguez initially clarifies that, he does not oppose producing the sub rosa materials all together. Rather, during meet and confer discussions, Dominguez offered to produce the materials 48 hours after the deposition of Plaintiff. (Arshansky Decl., ¶ 3.) Dominguez argues that, at the Informal Discovery Conference, the Court discouraged Plaintiff from filing a motion to compel and suggested that the parties meet and confer on the issue. (Arshansky Decl., ¶ 5.) Dominguez cites to federal cases in arguing that “allowing discovery of surveillance materials after the deposition of the plaintiff, but before trial, best meets the ends of justice and the spirit of the discovery rules to avoid surprise at trial.” (Opp., 4:1-3.)

 

Dominguez emphasizes that the Suezaki court found that sub rosa materials should be treated as qualified work product, advising that trial courts must weigh all circumstances in determining whether the discovery would serve the interests of justice. Dominguez also argues that the Atchinson case did not analyze how the work product doctrine applied to production of sub rosa materials. At the time Suezaki and Atchinson were decided, the qualified work product doctrine was not yet codified—the prior standard of “good cause” has evolved into “unfair prejudice” under the qualified protection provision. Thus, the burden is on Plaintiff to overcome qualified work product protection by demonstrating unfair prejudice if the sub rosa materials are not produced. Plaintiff has failed to do so—Plaintiff’s own actions cannot cause her own surprise. Dominguez further cites to various California superior court decisions to support his contention that the Court should order that Defendant need only produce the sub rosa materials after Plaintiff’s deposition to preserve their impeachment value.

 

In reply, Plaintiff reiterates that surveillance evidence is not absolutely privileged. To the extent that the sub rosa materials have qualified protection as work product, Plaintiff argues that she will be unfairly prejudiced in preparing her case. Plaintiff argues that she “should be allowed to review the material to properly prepare Plaintiff for her deposition and prevent any unfair surprise.” (Reply, 3:17-18.)

 

The Court finds that the Suezaki case is instructive here. That case involved the issue of whether surveillance footage of a plaintiff in a personal injury action, taken by an independent investigator engaged by defense counsel, was protected by the attorney-client privilege and/or the work product doctrine. The Supreme Court held that the attorney-client privilege did not apply to the sub rosa footage, finding that 1) the footage did not become privileged by the mere fact that it was taken for the purpose of litigation by a party and transmitted to that party’s attorney and 2) the footage is not attorney-client “communication.” (Suezaki, supra, 58 Cal. 2d at pp. 176-177.)

Similarly, here, the Court finds that the sub rosa materials are not protected by the attorney-client privilege on grounds that such materials are not a “communication.”

 

As to the issue of whether the work product doctrine applied, the Supreme Court held that “the fact that the material sought to be discovered is the ‘work product’ of the attorney is one factor to be used by the trial court in the exercise of its discretion in determining whether or not discovery should be granted. It does not mean that, simply because the material involved is the ‘work product’ of the attorney, it can or should deny discovery. Something more must exist. The trial court must consider all the relevant factors involved and then determine whether, under all the circumstances, discovery would or would not be fair and equitable.” (Suezaki, supra, 58 Cal. 2d at p. 178.) The Supreme Court ultimately remanded the case to the trial court to decide whether the surveillance footage was discoverable under this standard. (Id. at p. 179 [“While it would appear that inspection of the films should be permitted, the discretion granted by the statute is the discretion of the trial court, not of this court.”].) 

 

The Court notes that, although Suezaki was decided before the principle of qualified protection based on the work product doctrine was codified, its principles still apply. Work product statutes are “intended to be a restatement of existing law relating to protection of work product” and are “not intended to expand or reduce the extent to which work product is discoverable.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2018.040.) 

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2018.030 states the following:

 

“(a) A writing that reflects an attorney's impressions, conclusions, opinions, or legal research or theories is not discoverable under any circumstances.

 

(b) The work product of an attorney, other than a writing described in subdivision (a), is not discoverable unless the court determines that denial of discovery will unfairly prejudice the party seeking discovery in preparing that party's claim or defense or will result in an injustice.”

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 2018.030, emphasis added.)

Dominguez asserts that Plaintiff has failed to establish “unfair prejudice” under Code of Civil Procedure section 2018.030, subdivision (b) because 1) he is willing to produce the materials after Plaintiff’s deposition, and 2) Plaintiff is not subject to any surprise because she is aware of her own actions that might be revealed through the sub rosa materials. Plaintiff argues that she will be unfairly prejudiced if the sub rosa materials are not produced before her deposition because she will not be able to examine the authenticity of the footage and prepare for the deposition.

 

The Court finds that the parties do not necessarily dispute that the sub rosa materials are entitled to qualified protection under the work product doctrine. The Court finds that the factors weigh in favor of production. The work product doctrine exists to “[p]reserve the rights of attorneys to prepare cases for trial with that degree of privacy necessary to encourage them to prepare their cases thoroughly and to investigate not only the favorable but the unfavorable aspects of those cases.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2018.020.) This right is minimally invaded by the sub rosa materials at issue. Dominguez’s strategy embodied in the requested footage is readily apparent even before the footage is produced—the footage exists to impeach and otherwise cast doubt on the nature and extent of Plaintiff’s claimed injuries. Dominguez essentially admits to this use by requesting that the materials be produced after Plaintiff’s deposition. Dominguez has not otherwise demonstrated how the materials will reveal any detailed opinions, research, theories, or trial strategies of counsel. Plaintiff states that she would be unfairly prejudiced because she will be unable to challenge the sub rosa material’s authenticity and prepare for trial. Lastly, as noted, Dominguez does not object to producing the materials all together.

 

The Court notes that the parties primarily dispute the timing of production. Dominguez argues that he should not have to produce the sub rosa materials until after Plaintiff’s deposition in order to preserve their impeachment value. The Court does not opine on the timing of the production—a motion to compel further only deals with whether a further response is warranted. Dominguez has not moved for a protective order, which may affect the timing of production.

 

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel Further Responses and Document Production from Defendant Gabriel Del Angel Dominguez is granted.

 

The parties are encouraged to meet and confer further regarding the timing issue.

 

Sanctions

 

“[T]he court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel further response to a demand, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (h).)

 

Plaintiff requests sanctions in the amount of $3,500.00, plus $123.00 filing fees.

 

In opposition, Dominguez requests sanctions in the amount of $2,137.50.

 

Under the circumstances, the Court declines to issue monetary sanctions.

“If the answer to an interrogatory would necessitate the preparation or the making of a compilation, abstract, audit, or summary of or from the documents of the party to whom the interrogatory is directed, and if the burden or expense of preparing or making it would be substantially the same for the party propounding the interrogatory as for the responding party, it is a sufficient answer to that interrogatory to refer to this section and to specify the writings from which the answer may be derived or ascertained. Case Number: 25NWCV02063    Hearing Date: September 15, 2026    Dept: L

JENNIFER CARBINE vs NORMS RESTAURANTS, LLC

CASE NO.: 25NWCV02063

HEARING: September 15, 2026 @ 9:30 a.m.

 

#20

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Defendant Norms Restaurants, LLC’s Demurrer to the Complaint is OVERRULED.

 

Defendant is ordered to file an answer within 10 days of this order.

 

Defendant to give notice.

 

Background

 

This is a disability rights action. On June 9, 2025, Plaintiff Jennifer Carbine (“Plaintiff”) filed this action against Defendant Norms Restaurants, LLC (“Defendant”) and Does 1 to 10.

 

On December 22, 2025, Plaintiff filed the First Amended Complaint (FAC). The FAC alleges that Plaintiff is a legally blind person who requires screen-reading software to read website content. Plaintiff asserts that Defendant's website, www.norms.com, (hereinafter, “Website”) which provides consumers to an array of services and information related to Defendant’s brick-and-mortar locations, is not fully-accessible to blind and visually-impaired consumers. On multiple occasions in January 2025, Plaintiff encountered multiple accessibility barriers. Due to such barriers, “Plaintiff was unable to fully and independently access and use the Website’s online ordering system when visiting for the purpose of placing an order for pick up at the brick-and-mortar location in Bellflower, California, including being unable to reliably select the Bellflower restaurant as the pickup location, navigate to and select desired menu items, customize those items, and activate the final control needed to place her order.” (FAC, ¶ 52.) In September 2025, “even though she intended to place an order in advance while she would be in the area running errands so that her food would be ready when she arrived. . .Plaintiff was forced to go to Defendant’s brick-and-mortar restaurant in person to place and customize her order in a noisy environment. . . which caused her frustration and inconvenience.” (FAC, ¶ 55.) The FAC alleges one cause of action for Violations of the Unruh Civil Rights Act (Cal. Civ. Code § 51, et seq.).

 

Defendant now demurs to the FAC on grounds that the Unruh Civil Rights Act (UCRA) cause of action fails to state a claim and is uncertain.


Legal Standard

 

“[A] demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the allegations in a complaint.” (Lewis v. Safeway, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 385, 388.) A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. (See Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (“Donabedian”) (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994 [in ruling on a demurrer, a court may not consider declarations, matters not subject to judicial notice, or documents not accepted for the truth of their contents].) For purposes of ruling on a demurrer, all facts pleaded in a complaint are assumed to be true, but the reviewing court does not assume the truth of conclusions of law. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hosp. Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 967.) 

 

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Id.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.) However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245.) 

 

Meet and Confer

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41 requires that the parties meet and confer “in person, by telephone, or by video conference.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a).)

 

The parties met and conferred via email and telephone regarding the issues in this Demurrer but were unable to reach a resolution. (Arnold Decl., ¶¶ 3-4.) The Court finds that the meet and confer requirement has been met.

 

Discussion

 

Pursuant to UCRA, “All persons within the jurisdiction of this state . . . no matter what their . . . disability . . . are entitled to the full and equal accommodations, advantages, facilities, privileges, or services in all business establishments of every kind whatsoever.” (Civil Code, § 51, subd. (b).) “A plaintiff can recover under the [UCRA] on two alternate theories: (1) a violation of the ADA [Civil Code section 51, subd. (f)]; or (2) denial of access to a business establishment based on intentional discrimination.” (Martinez v. San Diego County Credit Union (“SDCCU”) (2020) 50 Cal.App.5th 1048, 1059.)

 

To establish a violation of the ADA, “a plaintiff must show: (1) a covered disability; (2) ‘the defendant is a private entity that owns, leases, or operates a place of public accommodation; and (3) the plaintiff was¿denied¿public accommodations¿by the defendant because of [the] disability.’” (Martinez, supra, 50 Cal.App.5th p. 1060, quoting Molski v. M.J. Cable, Inc. (9th Cir. 2007) 481 F.3d 724, 730.) Websites alone are not considered places of public accommodation under the ADA. (Martinez v. Cot’n Wash, Inc. (“Cot’n Wash”) (2022) 81 Cal.App.5th 1026, 1043-1044, 1052-1053.)

 

However, under the “nexus” test, courts may “consider whether the alleged website deficiencies impinge on the plaintiff's ability to have equal access to, and enjoyment of, the products and services offered at the physical location.” (SDCCU, supra, 50 Cal.App.5th at p. 1067.) Thus, even if the website has accessibility issues, “a plaintiff who fails to allege any connection between the website barriers and a physical location does not have an ADA claim.” (Gomez v. Trinitas Cellars, LLC (N.D. Cal., Mar. 9, 2022, No. 3:21-CV-09006-WHO) 2022 WL 704857, at *3, quoting Langer v. Pep Boys Manny Moe & Jack of California (N.D. Cal., Jan. 15, 2021, No. 20-CV-06015-DMR) 2021 WL 148237, at *5.)

 

          Nexus

 

Defendant argues that Plaintiff fails to establish a sufficient nexus between the Website’s alleged accessibility barriers and her ability to patronize the restaurant. The FAC merely alleges that the Website barriers deterred her from visiting Defendant’s brick-and-mortar locations. However, the FAC simultaneously admits that, in September 2025, Plaintiff successfully located and traveled to the Bellflower restaurant, placed and customized her order, and obtained her order. (FAC, ¶ 55.) Such admission defeats any arguments that Plaintiff was denied access to Defendant’s goods and services. Defendant argues that the FAC’s allegations “amount to nothing more than arguable technical accessibility issues, which courts have held are insufficient to establish a barrier under the ADA.” (Dem., 8:27-28, 9:1.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that the FAC allege a sufficient nexus to state a claim under the ADA and UCRA. Plaintiff notes that Defendant’s cited authorities are distinguishable or merely persuasive federal cases.

 

Here, the Court finds that Plaintiff has alleged a sufficient nexus. The FAC alleges that, due to various inaccessible website features, Plaintiff “was unable to fully and independently access and use the Website’s online ordering system when visiting for the purpose of placing an order for pick up at the brick-and-mortar location in Bellflower, California, including being unable to reliably select the Bellflower restaurant as the pickup location, navigate to and select desired menu items, customize those items, and activate the final control needed to place her order.” (FAC, ¶ 52.) The inability place an order at a physical location via the Website is a recognized basis for establishing a nexus. For example, the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals found such a nexus between defendant Domino’s website and physical locations where customers could locate nearby restaurants and place orders via the website for delivery or pickup. (Robles v. Domino’s Pizza, LLC (9th Cir. 2019) 913 F.3d 898, 905 [involving a visually-impaired plaintiff that was unable to place an order for delivery or pick-up on defendant’s website].) In another case, the court found a nexus where a restaurant’s website included a menu and reservation system that were inaccessible to visually impaired customers. (Thurston v. Midvale Corp. (2019) 39 Cal.App.5th 634, 638, 646 [noting that “the website connects customers to the services of the restaurant”].)

 

The Court addresses Defendant’s argument that Plaintiff was ultimately able to travel to the restaurant and place an order in a later section.

 

          Particularity

 

Defendant further argues that the FAC fails to identify the specific barriers Plaintiff encountered on the Website that impeded her access to the restaurant. Specifically, Defendant argues that the FAC “offers only generalized categories of alleged coding issues, without identifying any specific webpage, element, or function that was inaccessible, and without explaining how any alleged barrier prevented her from accessing the Bellflower Norms.” (Dem., 9:22-25.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that Defendants cited case, Whitaker v. Tesla Motors (2021) 985 F.3d 1173, is inapplicable, as it involved construction-based access barriers (i.e., whether a service counter at a car dealership was inaccessible). Plaintiff argues that the FAC adequately explains that, due to certain inaccessible features of the Website, Plaintiff was unable to review Defendant’s menu before visiting and was unable to place a pick-up order. (FAC, ¶¶ 52-53.) Plaintiff describes these features with sufficient particularity.

 

The Complaint alleges the following:

 

“a. The home page and menu pages contained numerous graphics, links, and buttons associated with the online ordering pathway (including “Order Now,” “Start Order,” and location selection controls) that were not labeled or were incorrectly labeled, or lacked alternative text (“Alt text”), which prevented Plaintiff’s screen reader from announcing the existence and function of those elements. Web accessibility requires that Alt-text be coded with each picture so that screen-reading software can speak the Alt-text where a sighted user sees pictures. Alt-text does not change the visual presentation, but instead a text box shows when the cursor moves over the picture. The lack of Alt-text on these graphics prevents screen-readers from accurately vocalizing a description of the graphics.

 

b. Plaintiff encountered multiple unlabeled or mislabeled buttons and links within the online ordering interface, including controls needed to select a restaurant location, select menu items, customize items, and proceed to the checkout page, such that Plaintiff had no way to discern the purpose or function of those buttons or links using her screen reader;

 

c. Plaintiff encountered multiple pages containing insufficient and disorganized navigational headings and landmarks, requiring Plaintiff to expend substantial additional time and effort to try to locate basic information such as store locations, menu categories, and ordering options, and causing her screen reader focus to jump unpredictably when she attempted to navigate by heading or link; and,

 

d. Plaintiff was ultimately unable to complete the process of placing an order for pick up because essential portions of the ordering system, including location selection and the “checkout” or equivalent confirmation controls, were inaccessible to her screen-reading software, leaving Plaintiff unable to independently review her order, confirm her selections, or submit payment, thereby denying her full and equal access to the Website and the goods and services offered in connection with Defendant’s brick-and-mortar locations.”

 

(FAC, ¶ 51(a)-(d).)

 

The Court finds that the FAC adequately identifies specific accessibility barriers on the Website and links those barriers to Plaintiff’s inability to review the menu and place an advance pick-up order as other non-visually impaired patrons are able to do. (See FAC, ¶¶ 52-53.) The Court is not persuaded that more detail is needed—such issues can be resolved through discovery.

 

          Deterrence and Injury

 

Defendant asserts that the FAC fails to allege that Plaintiff was deterred from accessing the physical Bellflower restaurant location. Defendant reiterates that Plaintiff was able to locate and travel to the restaurant and place an order. (See FAC, ¶ 55.) Thus, Plaintiff was not actually prevented from accessing the restaurant. Rather, the FAC alleges that Plaintiff was “deterred, on a regular basis, from accessing the Website,” and that the Website has “deterred Plaintiff from visiting Defendant’s brick-and-mortar locations.” (FAC, ¶ 57.) Mere deterrence from accessing a website is insufficient. Relatedly, Defendant argues that the FAC fails to allege any actionable injury.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff asserts that her ultimate access to Defendant’s restaurant does not foreclose her ADA claim because the ADA requires “full and equal” access. The FAC alleges that, due to the Website’s inaccessibility features, “Plaintiff was forced to go to Defendant’s brick-and-mortar restaurant in person to place and customize her order in a noisy environment, where background noise made it difficult for the cashier to hear and understand her.” (FAC, ¶ 55.) In other words, the physical visit occurred because Plaintiff was unable to place an advance pick-up order on the Website. The ADA focuses on equality of access, not ultimate access.

 

The Court is not persuaded by Defendant’s arguments. The FAC alleges that Plaintiff attempted to use the website on multiple occasions in January 2025, during which she could not review the menu or place an order. (FAC, ¶¶ 51, 52-53, 56, 57.) Plaintiff did not visit the physical location until September 2025, in which Plaintiff was required to place an order in person, although placing an online order would have been more convenient. (FAC, ¶ 55.) The Court finds that Plaintiff has alleged sufficient deterrence. This case is distinct from cases where the plaintiff alleges only deterrence from use of a website without any connection to accessing the physical store. (C.f. Langer v. Cooke City Raceway, Inc. (E.D. Cal., July 27, 2022, No. 121CV01488JLTBAK) 2022 WL 2966172, at *9 [finding complaint only alleged that the plaintiff was deterred from use of the website and failed to allege “what information he was not able to obtain that impeded his ability to access the physical goods and services at the public accommodation”], emphasis in the original.)

 

          Intentional Discrimination under UCRA

 

The parties dispute whether the FAC adequately alleges that Defendant intentionally discriminated against Plaintiff for purposes of UCRA.

 

Pursuant to UCRA, if a claim is not an ADA violation, the plaintiff must prove the discrimination was “intentional” or constituted “willful, affirmative misconduct.” (Cot'n Wash, supra, 81 Cal.App.5th at p. 1036.)

 

Here, the Court finds that the FAC adequately alleges a claim under the ADA, which may act as a predicate for the UCRA claim. Thus, the Court need not analyze whether the alleged Website barriers constituted intentional discrimination.

 

Conclusion

 

Based on the foregoing, Defendant’s Demurrer to the Complaint is overruled.
