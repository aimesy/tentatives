Case Number: 24STCV08657    Hearing Date: August 10, 2026    Dept: 510

No. 2 – Wilshire Law Firm, PLC v. Ahmmed Ilyas Akbari and Kennedy Wilshire PC

Plaintiff Wilshire Law Firm, PLC’s Request for Default Judgment

Plaintiff Wilshire Law Firm, PLC requests court judgment by default against Defendants Ahmmed Ilyas Akbari and Kennedy Wilshire PC.

As an initial matter, the Court notes that Defendants filed two briefs in opposition to the default judgment packet—one on June 4, 2026, and the other on August 3, 2026. Because Defendants are in default, they are not entitled to file these briefs. “The entry of a default terminates a defendant’s rights to take any further affirmative steps in the litigation until either its default is set aside or a default judgment is entered.” (Devlin v. Kearny Mesa AMC/Jeep/Renault, Inc. (1984) 155 Cal.App.3d 381, 385.) As such, their contents will not be considered by the Court and the two briefs are stricken.

Sufficiency of Evidence

The Court acknowledges that Plaintiff requests a large damage award: $7,861,365.60 in compensatory damages, $1,823,261.55 in prejudgment interest, and $21,092.41 in costs. “Even when the allegations of a complaint do support the judgment a plaintiff seeks, he is not automatically entitled to entry of that judgment by the court, simply because the defendant defaulted. Instead, it is incumbent upon the plaintiff to prove up his damages, with actual evidence.” (Kim v. Westmoore Partners, Inc. (2011) 201 Cal.App.4th 267, 272.) “When determining if the evidence at the [default] prove-up hearing supports the requested damages, the court’s role is serious, substantive, and often complicated.” (LCPFV, LLC v. Somatdary Inc. (2024) 106 Cal.App.5th 743, 754.)

Here, the Court finds Plaintiff presents sufficient evidence showing it is entitled to the fee award for the five settled cases in the amount of $5,201,618.33. In support of this award, Plaintiff submits evidence of costs incurred on each case, the clients’ respective retainer agreements with Plaintiff, and copies of the release and/or distribution agreements. This evidence is sufficient to support the requested damage amounts for the settled cases.

However, Plaintiff presents insufficient evidence showing it is entitled to the fee award for the nine unsettled cases in the amount of $2,659,747.27. Here, Plaintiff relies on an “estimated case value,” which is “the expected settlement [Plaintiff] would have received had the case remained at the firm with [them]. For expected settlement value, [Plaintiff uses] Mr. Akbari’s own valuations from when he was a contractor at the firm.” (Mims Decl., ¶  37.)

Here, this amount was not ascertained with reasonable certainty as required. “[W]here the operation of an established business is prevented or interrupted, as by a ... breach of contract ..., damages for the loss of prospective profits that otherwise might have been made from its operation are generally recoverable for the reason that their occurrence and extent may be ascertained with reasonable certainty from the past volume of business and other provable data relevant to the probable future sales.” (Sargon Enterprises, Inc. v. University of Southern California (2012) 55 Cal.4th 747, 774 [quoting Grupe v. Glick (1945) 26 Cal.2d 680, 692].)

Plaintiff’s lost profit projection is speculative, as it only relies on Akbari’s personal valuations of what he believed the case’s value was at an unspecified point in time based on unspecified information. Plaintiff submits no supporting evidence showing that these case valuations are based on an accurate assessment of past business or other relevant considerations. Indeed, in the unverified Second Amended Complaint, Plaintiff takes issue with Defendant’s competence and veracity. (See, e.g., SAC ¶ 2 (noting “underperformance” by Defendant, and Defendant “falsely agreed, and made false statements …”), ¶ 16 (Defendant violated policies relating to “satisfactory completion of litigation services”), ¶ 17 (Defendant’s “performance was consistently below what Plaintiff expected”).) The Court cannot rely on these speculative valuations, especially when Plaintiff, as the party proffering them, raises serious questions about the source of those valuations.

To recover the amount of damages requested for unsettled cases, Plaintiff must present admissible and credible evidence at a prove-up hearing. In the alternative, Plaintiff can revise its default judgment fee award request to seek only the $5,201,618.33 amount for settled cases plus costs and interest, and submit a new proposed judgment with revised prejudgment interest calculations and cost award.

Disposition

Plaintiff Wilshire Law Firm, PLC’s request for default judgment is denied without prejudice.


Case Number: 24STCV18520    Hearing Date: August 10, 2026    Dept: 510

No. 7 – Euler Hermes North America Ins. Co. v. One Vision Enterprises, Inc.

Cross-Defendant One Vision Enterprises, Inc.’s Demurrer to Farmer’s Business Network, Inc. and Generic Corp Science, LLC’s First Amended Cross-Complaint

Cross-Defendant One Vision Enterprises, Inc. (“OVE”) demurs to the fifth cause of action for unjust enrichment in Farmer’s Business Network, Inc. and Generic Corp Science, LLC’s (collectively “Cross-Complainants”) first amended cross-complaint.

OVE argues Cross-Complainants do not state a claim for unjust enrichment because there is no claim for unjust enrichment under California law. The Court agrees. “California does not recognize a cause of action for unjust enrichment.” (Hooked Media Group, Inc. v. Apple Inc. (2020) 55 Cal.App.5th 323, 336.) “Unjust enrichment is ‘ “a general principle, underlying various legal doctrines and remedies,” ’ rather than a remedy itself.” (Melchior v. New Line Productions, Inc. (2003) 106 Cal.App.4th 779, 793.) “It is synonymous with restitution.” (Ibid.) 

However, “[i]t has long been established that in ruling on a demurrer, the trial court is obligated to look past the form of a pleading to its substance. Erroneous or confusing labels attached by the inept pleader are to be ignored if the complaint pleads facts which would entitle the plaintiff to relief.” (Saunders v. Cariss (1990) 224 Cal.App.3d 905, 908.)

In opposition, Cross-Complainants do not identify a specific alternate cause of action that it could be asserting. Cross-Complaintants assert: “As alleged in the First Amended Cross-Complaint, OVE knowingly accepted payments for the same amounts from Alterna and OVE, unjustly enriching OVE beyond what it could have been owed.” (Opp., p. 4.) Even when considering the substance of the allegations in the cross-complaint, it is not clear to the Court that those allegations support a specific legal theory that is not already asserted as fraud, negligent misrepresentation or promissory estoppel, for which restitution (i.e., unjust enrichment) may be a remedy. Cross-Complainants cite to cases that suggest the claim is in fact for restitution rather than unjust enrichment. (Opp., p. 4, citing O’Grady v. Merchant Exchange Prods. (2019) 41 Cal.App.5th 771, 791-792.)

From the cases cited by Cross-Complainants, three possible causes of action are quasi-contract, quantum meruit and money had and received. Yet the facts currently alleged do not seem to support any of these causes of action. Regardless, it is not up to the Court – or cross-defendant – to guess the legal theory on which the remedy is premised. That is the pleading parties’ obligation. The Court will allow Cross-Complainants an opportunity to specify the cause of action for which it seeks recovery and the facts supporting that cause of action.

Disposition

Cross-Defendant One Vision Enterprises, Inc.’s demurrer to the fifth cause of action in the first amended cross-complaint is sustained with 21 days’ leave to amend.

No. 6 – Hany A Z Mansour v. American Honda Motor Co., Inc.

Defendant American Honda Motor Co. Inc.’s Motions to Compel Plaintiff’s Responses to Form Interrogatories, Special Interrogatories, and Requests for Production

Defendant American Honda Motor Co., Inc moves for orders compelling Plaintiff Hany A Z Mansour to respond to form interrogatories (sets one and two), special interrogatories (sets one and two), and requests for production (sets one and two).

A party must respond to written discovery “[w]ithin 30 days after service.” (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.260(a) [interrogatories], 2031.260(a) [requests for production].) When the responding party fails to serve a timely response to interrogatories or requests for production, the requesting party may move for an order compelling responses. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.290(b) [interrogatories], 2031.300(b) [requests for production].) Failing to serve a timely response results in waiving any objections. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.290(a) [interrogatories], 2031.300(a) [requests for production].)

Plaintiff failed to serve timely responses to Defendant’s discovery requests. Defendant served its discovery requests on December 12, 2025. (Kenny Decl., ¶ 2.) The extended deadline to respond was January 27, 2026. (Id., ¶ 5.) As of June 1, 2026, the date these motions were filed, Plaintiff had still not responded. (Id., ¶ 7.) However, Plaintiff later served responses on June 12, 2026, and accompanying verifications on June 17, 2026. (Abdo Decl., Exs. 1-2.) Therefore, the motions are moot with respect to seeking an order compelling response.

Sanctions

Defendant requests monetary sanctions for $5,280 against Plaintiff. Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.300(c) provides, “[T]he court shall impose a monetary sanction … against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel a response to a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (See also Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290(c) [interrogatories].) Plaintiff failed to provide any responses by the deadline. There is no evidence that Plaintiff acted with substantial justification. At the same time, Plaintiff did not reasonably spend four hours per motion. The Court will exercise its discretion and award sanctions of $675 per motion (3 hours @ $205/hour plus $60 filing fee), for a total of $4,050.

The Court exercises its discretion to award the requested sanctions in full.

Disposition

Defendant American Honda Motor Co., Inc.’s motions to compel responses to form interrogatories, special interrogatories, and request for production are granted in part as to sanctions only. Plaintiff Hany A Z Mansour and counsel of record are ordered, jointly and severally, to pay Defendant $4,050 in sanctions within 21 days.


Case Number: 26STCV14761    Hearing Date: August 10, 2026    Dept: 510

No. 8 - Marlene Y. Martin Estrada v. Angel Estrada, et al.

Defendants Angel Estrada and Dynasty Employment Solutions, Inc.’s Demurrer and Motion to Strike Portions of Plaintiffs’ Complaint

Defendants Angel Estrada and Dynasty Employment Solutions, Inc. demur to Plaintiff Marlene Y. Martin Estrada’s complaint. Defendants also move to strike portions of Plaintiff’s complaint. Plaintiff did not file an opposition.

The Court finds there was no sufficient meet and confer between the parties.

California Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41(a) states that, before filing a demurrer, “the demurring party shall meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer.” (CCP § 430.41(a).) A declaration setting forth such meet and confer efforts must accompany the demurrer. (CCP § 430.41(a)(3).) A motion to strike has identical meet and confer requirements. (See CCP § 435.5(a).)

The Court notes that there is no declaration filed in support of the demurrer or motion to strike detailing meet and confer efforts. There is also no description of meet and confer efforts in the memorandum of points and authorities.

The hearing on the motion will be continued on the demurrer and motion to strike to allow parties time to meet and confer. Defendants should submit the required meet and confer declaration at least 16 court days before the next hearing as required under the Code.

Disposition

Defendants Angel Estrada and Dynasty Employment Solutions, Inc.’s demurrer and motion to strike as to Marlene Y. Martin Estrada’s complaint are continued to September 11, 2026, at 8:30 am.
