Case Number: 24STCV22455    Hearing Date: August 7, 2026    Dept: 510

No. 11 - Maria Del Carmen Serrano v. Palisades Restaurant Group Inc., et al.

Defendant Palisades Restaurant Group Inc.’s Motion for Summary Judgment

Defendant Palisades Restaurant Group Inc. moves for summary judgment of this action brought by Plaintiff Maria Del Carmen Serrano. In the alternative, Defendant moves for summary adjudication of each cause of action.[1] Both Plaintiff and Co-Defendant Jacqueline Montalva (“Montalva”) filed an opposition to the motion. Plaintiff also filed a joinder to Montalva’s opposition.

Evidentiary Objections

          Plaintiff makes 8 objections to Defendant’s evidence. Objections nos. 6-8 are sustained. The remaining objections are overruled.

Montalva makes 5 objections to Defendant’s evidence. Objections nos. 1-2 are sustained. The remaining objections are overruled.

Request for Continuance

As an initial matter, the Court notes that Plaintiff requests in her opposition a 60-day continuance under Code of Civil Procedure section 437c(h) to obtain deposition testimony from witness Yolanda Garcia Pinzon. The applicable statute provides: “If it appears from the affidavits submitted in opposition to a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication, or both, that facts essential to justify opposition may exist but cannot, for reasons stated, be presented, the court shall deny the motion, order a continuance to permit affidavits to be obtained or discovery to be had, or make any other order as may be just.” (CCP § 437c(h).)

The Court declines to grant a continuance. The parties already stipulated to a prior 60-day continuance on May 11, 2026, for this same purpose. Though Plaintiff expresses that she has had difficulty obtaining the desired deposition testimony—Pinzon failed to appear for her deposition—Plaintiff has not moved to compel this deposition. In addition, it is not apparent to the Court that this deposition testimony is essential for Plaintiff’s opposition. The request for a continuance is denied.

Legal Standard for Summary Judgment

          Summary judgment should be granted where no triable issues of fact exist and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. (CCP § 437c(c); Villa v.  McFerren (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 733, 741.) Courts use a three-step analysis: “(1) identify the issues framed by the pleadings; (2) determine whether the moving party has negated the opponent’s claims; and (3) determine whether the opposition has demonstrated the existence of a triable, material factual issue.” (Hinesley v. Oakshade Town Center (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 289, 294.)

Factual Background

          This case concerns a verbal and physical altercation that occurred between a customer (Plaintiff) and an employee (Montalva) at a Carl’s Jr. restaurant. In September 2022, Plaintiff and her husband, David Martinez, accessed the drive-thru window at the restaurant. Montalva, the shift leader at the time, took their order. During this interaction, Martinez tried to use a coupon for the order, and a verbal confrontation arose. Martinez subsequently exited the drive-thru and parked their car. Plaintiff entered the restaurant and insulted Montalva. In response, Montalva followed Plaintiff out of the store and grabbed Plaintiff’s hair. A physical altercation ensued outside of the restaurant. Later, the police were contacted, and Montalva and Plaintiff were each cited for battery. Montalva’s employment was terminated that same day.

Propriety of the Court Ruling on the Motion

Defendant noticed this motion seeking summary judgment or adjudication “on Plaintiff’s Complaint.” (Def. Notice, at p. 1.) The relief sought includes adjudication of a cause of action for assault and battery and Plaintiff’s lack of entitlement to punitive damages. While those items were included in Plaintiff’s complaint, filed on September 3, 2024, Plaintiff filed an amended complaint on March 3, 2025 that did not include either a cause of action for assault and battery or a request for punitive damages. The amended complaint includes only two causes of action, for negligence and negligent hiring and retention.

Defendant thus filed its motion seeking adjudication on a pleading that has been superseded and has no effect.

“ ‘It is well established that an amendatory pleading supersedes the original one, which ceases to perform any function as a pleading.’ ” [Citations.] Thus, an amended complaint supersedes all prior complaints. [Citations.] The amended complaint furnishes the sole basis for the cause of action, and the original complaint ceases to have any effect either as a pleading or as a basis for judgment. [Citation.]

Because there is but one complaint in a civil action [citation], the filing of an amended complaint moots a motion directed to a prior complaint. [Citation.] Thus, once an amended complaint is filed, it is error to grant summary adjudication on a cause of action contained in a previous complaint. [Citation.]

(State Compensation Ins. Fund v. Superior Court (2010) 184 Cal.App.4th 1124, 1130-1131; see also S.C. v. Doe 1 (2025) 115 Cal.App.5th 365, 384 (“ ‘It is well settled that the pleadings set the boundaries of the issues to be resolved at summary judgment.’ [Citation.] In addition, ‘[i]t is well established that an amendatory pleading supersedes the original one, which ceases to perform any function as a pleading. [Citations.]’ [Citation.] Because there is but one complaint in a civil action [citation], the filing of an amended complaint moots a motion directed to a prior complaint. [Citation.]”)

There is, though, a recent decision that concluded that ruling on a summary judgment motion on a superseded complaint where the same causes of action are asserted in both versions could be considered harmless error. In Harding v. Lifetime Financial, Inc. (2025) 109 Cal.App.5th 753, the plaintiff filed a motion for leave to amend his complaint just before the trial court heard defendant’s motion for summary judgment. The trial court granted leave to amend, and then granted the motion for summary judgment, which had been noticed with respect to the original complaint. The court was thus faced with a situation similar to that presented here, where the defendant sought summary judgment on a complaint that was no longer at issue.

On appeal, the appellate court noted that “the factual narrative and legal theories embodied in the first amended complaint were extensively briefed in conjunction with Defendants’ summary judgment motion on the original complaint.” (109 Cal.App.5th at 761.) The same is true here, where the facts supporting the two causes of action in the amended complaint are nearly identical to those asserted in the original complaint. All parties had ample opportunity to brief those issues in connection with the pending motion. In Harding, the court of appeal found that “the amended pleading does not materially change the issues at play, and the theory alleged in it was thoroughly briefed in the summary judgment proceedings. [¶] Given how this case unfolded, we cannot see how the outcome would have been any different had the trial court denied the summary judgment motion and forced Defendants to file a new motion challenging the amended complaint. Thus, any procedural error was not prejudicial ….” (Ibid. at 762.)

In the interests of judicial efficiency, and finding neither party that opposed the motion asserted any prejudice, the Court will consider the motion for summary adjudication on the causes of action for negligence and negligent hiring as applying to the two causes of action in the first amended complaint.

Negligence

To state a claim for negligence, Plaintiff must allege (1) “the existence of a legal duty of care,” (2) “breach of that duty,” and (3) “proximate cause resulting in an injury.” (McIntyre v. Colonies-Pacific, LLC (2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 664, 671.)

Defendant argues that it cannot be held vicariously liable for negligence because Montalva’s improper conduct fell outside the scope of her employment. “The rule of respondeat superior is familiar and simply stated: an employer is vicariously liable for the torts of its employees committed within the scope of the employment.” (Lisa M. v. Henry Mayo Newhall Memorial Hospital (1995) 12 Cal.4th 291, 296.) The key issue is whether the employee’s conduct was “within the scope” of the employment or agency. “[A]n intentional tort gives rise to respondeat superior liability only if it was engendered by the employment.” (Id., at p. 298.)

Defendant tries to meet its initial burden in showing that it is not vicariously liable for Montalva’s conduct because there was no required “causal nexus” between her conduct and her work-related duties. Defendant points to Montalva’s discovery responses, in which she attests that Plaintiff cursed at her multiple times in Spanish in the restaurant, and told her she “wasn’t worth anything” and that her job “wasn’t even a real job.” (Def. Compendium, Ex. 5, Resp. to SPROG No. 1, at p. 4.) Plaintiff also told her to “get a husband” and called her a failure and a racist. (Ibid.) Montalva further attests that during the dispute she “felt so humiliated and overwhelmed, that [she] lost control and followed [Plaintiff] outside, where [she] pulled [Plaintiff] by the hair. [Plaintiff] began hitting [her] in the face.” (Def. Compendium, Ex. 7, Resp. to SPROG No. 1, at p. 5.) This evidence suggests that the verbal and physical dispute that ensued was of a personal nature, and was not engendered by Montalva’s employment at Carl’s Jr.

However, Plaintiff and Montalva raise triable issues of material fact as to whether there is a causal nexus between Montalva’s conduct and her employment-related duties.

For example, the assault began over a dispute about the validity of a store coupon. Montalva provides evidence that she was one of two shift managers on duty at the time of the incident, and no store manager was present. (Karayan Decl., Ex. A, Montalva Depo 32:21-33:1.) As a shift lead, Montalva reports that, after Plaintiff entered the restaurant, she followed her outside for the primary purpose of kicking her out of the premises and locking the doors so she wouldn’t return. (Id., at 58:12-59:18.) Montalva testified that during the physical altercation she did not want to harm Plaintiff, but wanted her to “shut up” about the coupons and what she thought about her job performance. (Id., at 105:12-20.)

Plaintiff also relies on Flores v. AutoZone West, Inc. (2008) 161 Cal.App.4th 373, 377. In Flores, a customer went to an AutoZone location to purchase motor oil. While shopping in the store, the customer made a noise to get the employee’s attention; the employee then rebuked the customer for whistling at him instead of saying “excuse me,” and asked the customer if he was too stupid to read the prices on the shelf. (Id., at pp. 377-378.) The customer then told the employee that he shouldn’t have come into work if he did not actually want to work. (Id., at p. 378.) After this comment, the employee struck the customer in the head with a metal pipe. (Ibid.) Reviewing the evidence on summary judgment, the trial court found that the employee’s conduct was outside the scope of his employment as a matter of law and, therefore, the employer could not be held vicariously liable. (Ibid.) On appeal from the trial court’s entry of summary judgment in the employer’s favor, the employer argued that the employee’s spark of anger was only ignited after he had completed his work-related task of showing the customer where the price of oil was displayed, and thus, the tortious conduct was not engendered by his employment. (Id., at p. 381.)

The appellate court disagreed and reversed, reasoning in part:

In our view, the anger generated during the interaction between Flores and Gomez cannot be so tidily compartmentalized—at least not as a matter of law. While it may be true that Gomez did not develop his malicious ‘spark’ until the moment Flores made his last comment, a jury could certainly infer from his prior comments that his anger had been ‘sparked’ at an earlier point. ‘Ordinarily, the determination whether an employee has acted within the scope of employment presents a question of fact; it becomes a question of law, however, when “the facts are undisputed and no conflicting inferences are possible.” ’ (Mary M. v. City of Los Angeles (1991) 54 Cal.3d 202, 213, 285 Cal.Rptr. 99, 814 P.2d 1341, italics added.) Here, even if the ‘facts’ could be characterized as undisputed, the possible inferences to be drawn from those facts cannot.

(Ibid.)

Here, the Court finds that Plaintiff has sufficiently raised a triable issue of material fact as to whether the dispute arose from Montalva’s work-related duties. Plaintiff provides evidence that the verbal altercation grew out of a dispute about the use of a store coupon, that Plaintiff insulted Montalva’s job performance while Montalva was at work, and that Montalva followed Plaintiff outside the store for the primary purpose of making sure she left the premises in her role as shift lead. Given the evidence, the Court cannot determine, as a matter of law, that Montalva’s tortious conduct fell outside the scope of her employment. Triable issues of material fact preclude summary adjudication of this cause of action.

Negligent Hiring, Retention, Training, and Supervision

“Negligent hiring/retention is a theory of direct liability—not vicarious liability. In a negligent hiring/retention cause of action, the neglect alleged is not that of the employee. The neglect pleaded is that of the employer itself.” (J.W. v. Watchtower Bible and Tract Society of New York, Inc. (2018) 29 Cal.App.5th 1142, 1163.) “Negligence liability will be imposed on an employer if it ‘knew or should have known that hiring the employee created a particular risk or hazard and that particular harm materializes.’ ” (Phillips v. TLC Plumbing, Inc. (2009) 172 Cal.App.4th 1133, 1139.)

Defendant provides evidence showing that it did not know, and could not have known, that Montalva would engage in violent behavior toward customers. In support, Defendant submits a declaration from Patricia Ramirez, a district manager who oversaw “employee training, operations, and compliance with company policies” at Montalva’s store location. (Ramirez Decl., ¶ 3.) Ramirez testifies that all employees, including Montalva, undergo a comprehensive training program when they are hired that emphasizes courteous customer service and de-escalation techniques. (Id., ¶¶ 4-6.) She also attests that Montalva worked without incident through her employment and was not subject to any disciplinary actions. (Id., ¶¶ 9-10.) Further, there were no prior complaints that Montalva engaged in aggressive behavior or physical altercations with customers. (Id., ¶ 11.) Therefore, Defendant had no reason to know that Montalva’s behavior “created a particular risk or hazard” that later materialized. (See Phillips, supra, 172 Cal.App.4th at p. 1139.) This is sufficient to meet Defendant’s burden.

Plaintiff and Montalva fail to raise a triable issue of material fact. While Plaintiff provides evidence of four disciplinary write-ups in June and July 2022, the write-ups themselves only report two separate attendance issues, one report for Montalva’s failing to punch in for a meal break, and one report for Montalva erroneously discounting an order for a customer. (Jackson Decl., Ex. 3.) These reports are not only unauthenticated hearsay, but they do not show prior events of poor customer service, aggression, or verbal/physical disputes with others. Instead, they recount minor attendance and policy issues. Therefore, even if this evidence had been admissible, it would not create a triable issue of material fact as to whether Defendant should have known Montalva would engage in aggressive conduct.

Plaintiff also states that Yolanda Pinzon, Montalva’s fellow co-worker, reported Montalva’s “history of being disrespectful” to the police on the scene after the incident occurred, and further requested anonymity from police because she feared retaliation. (Pl. Opp., at p. 6.) However, Plaintiff does not identify any admissible evidence to support these arguments. Instead, Plaintiff cites counsel’s declaration, in which attorney Howlett S. Jackson Jr. attests that he was present at Deputy Santacruz’s deposition. (Jackson Jr. Decl., ¶ 5.) He states, “Deputy Santacruz further testified that Ms. Pinzon originally requested anonymity from law enforcement specifically because she was in fear of retaliation from Defendant Montalva.” (Ibid.) Santacruz’s deposition testimony itself was not submitted with the opposition brief. Jackson’s account thus constitutes double or triple hearsay and cannot be considered by the Court.

Lastly, Montalva argues that she could not remember receiving training regarding de-escalation policies, and alleges she was undertrained. (See Karayan Decl., Ex. A, Montalva Depo at 38:14-39:15; 113:12-115:12.) However, Montalva’s lack of recollection of receiving certain training and her subjective beliefs about the quality of her training fail to create a triable issue of material fact as to whether Defendant should have known Montalva would engage in violent conduct. Nor does her lack of recollection impeach or undermine Ramirez’s testimony that such training was provided to Montalva. Defendant is thus entitled to summary adjudication of this cause of action.

Disposition

          Defendant Palisades Restaurant Group Inc.’s motion for summary judgment is denied. The Court grants summary adjudication for Defendant on Plaintiff’s second cause of action for negligent hiring, retention, training, and supervision, and denies summary adjudication for Defendant on Plaintiff’s first cause of action for negligence.


[1] Defendant’s motion specifies that it seeks summary adjudication of the second cause of action for assault and battery, and on the issue of punitive damages, even though neither is included in the operative first amended complaint. Therefore, the Court will disregard Defendant’s arguments regarding the assault claim and punitive damages.


Case Number: 24STCV24206    Hearing Date: August 7, 2026    Dept: 510

No. 1 – MF Downtown Properties LLC v. Make Spaces LLC

Plaintiff MF Downtown Properties LLC’s Request for Default Judgment

Plaintiff MF Downtown Properties LLC requests court judgment by default against Defendants Diamond Enterprise LLC and Robert Johnson. Plaintiff’s application for default judgment still suffers multiple defects.

Insufficient Factual Allegations Against Defendant Diamond Enterprise LLC

First, Plaintiff has not shown sufficient grounds for court judgment against Defendant Diamond Enterprise LLC. The complaint concerns a contract entered between Plaintiff and Defendant Make Spaces, LLC—a party that has since been dismissed. Defendant Robert Johnson signed a guaranty and is therefore personally liable under the contract. However, Defendant Diamond Enterprise LLC did not sign the contract.

“Under the ‘well pleaded’ complaint rule, it is error to enter a default judgment on a complaint that fails to state a cause of action against the defaulting defendant.” (Ferraro v. Camarlinghi (2008) 161 Cal.App.4th 509, 539.)

In the complaint, Plaintiff alleges:

Diamond Enterprises LLC’s income, financial records, bank statements, proof of business contacts etc. was provided to PLAINTIFF as evidence of Make Spaces ability to pay the monthly rent. The Lease was entered into by MAKE SPACES, guarantor JOHNSON with DIAMOND ENTERPRISES LLC providing their financial records, bank statements, etc. as support and proof that MAKE SPACES will be able to pay the rent. Robert Johnson is not only involved in all entities, but is the owner/managing member of these entities and personal guarantor of the Lease. Among other factors, there is clearly a unity of ownership and interest, co-mingling, and the same owner.

(Compl., ¶ 9.)

These allegations of alter ego liability are insufficient to prove liability. To obtain judgment against Defendant Diamond Enterprise LLC, Plaintiff should provide additional evidence showing that this corporate entity is indeed an alter ego of the other parties to the contract and is in some manner responsible for the amounts sought.

Excessive Damages

Plaintiff requests default judgment for $112,400 in damages. “A default judgment greater than the amount specifically demanded in the complaint is void as beyond the court’s jurisdiction.” (Airs Aromatics, LLC v. CBL Data Recovery Technologies, Inc. (2018) 23 Cal.App.5th 1013, 1018 (Airs Aromatics).) The complaint’s prayer for relief seeks $100,000. (See Compl., prayer, ¶ 1.)

To recover the damages Plaintiff requests, it must amend the complaint to specifically demand that amount. Doing so would vacate Defendant’s default. After a plaintiff “file[s] an amended complaint praying for a different amount of damages and/or other appropriate relief… she must serve her amended complaint on defendants, who will be entitled to file a new answer; all issues will then be at large, including liability.” (Greenup v. Rodman (1986) 42 Cal.3d 822, 830; accord Airs Aromatics, supra, 23 Cal.App.5th at p. 1025 [filing amended complaint vacates default].) Any amended complaint must be served in the manner of a summons. (Engebretson & Co. v. Harrison (1981) 125 Cal.App.3d 436, 444.) Plaintiff may also modify its request for default judgment to seek only those damages specified in the complaint.

Disposition

Plaintiff MF Downtown Properties LLC’s request for default judgment is denied without prejudice.


Case Number: 25STCV02207    Hearing Date: August 7, 2026    Dept: 510

No. 9 - Enrique Alejandro Luevano Ibarra, et al. v. Rudolph & Sletten Inc., et al.

Defendants Rudolph & Sletten Inc. and Minegar Contracting Inc.’s Motion for Leave to Conduct Neuropsychological Exam of Plaintiff Enrique Alejandro Luevano Ibarra

          Defendants Rudolph & Sletten Inc. and Minegar Contracting Inc. (collectively “Defendants”) move for leave to conduct a neuropsychological examination of Plaintiff Enrique Alejandro Luevano Ibarra.

Good Cause for Mental Examination

A defendant who seeks to conduct a mental examination of plaintiff must file a motion and “obtain leave of court.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2032.310(a).) Such a motion must “specify the time, place, manner, conditions, scope, and nature of the examination, as well as the identity and the specialty, if any, of the person or persons who will perform the examination.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2032.310(b).) The court may grant such a motion “only for good cause shown.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2032.320(a).)

Here, Plaintiff agrees that there is good cause for a mental examination. Plaintiff opposes the motion only based on the requested conditions of the exam. 

Disclosure of Raw Data  

In their motion, Defendants request that the Court allow the raw testing data and testing materials used to evaluate Plaintiff to be distributed between the parties’ experts only, and not counsel.

“If a party submits to a . . .  mental examination . . . that party has the option of making a written demand that the party at whose instance the examination was made deliver . . .  to the demanding party . . . [a] copy of a detailed written report setting out the history, examinations, findings, including the results of all tests made, diagnoses, prognoses, and conclusions of the examiner.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2032.610(a)(1).) “If the option under [Code Civ. Proc. § 2032.610(a)] is exercised, a copy of the requested reports shall be delivered within 30 days after service of the demand, or within 15 days of trial, whichever is earlier.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2032.610(b).)  

“There is no statutory authority . . . precluding a trial court from ordering the disclosure of test materials or test data when ordering a mental examination.” (Randy’s Trucking Inc. v. Super. Ct. (2023) 91 Cal.App.5th 818, 834.) A plaintiff has a “right to take discovery and cross-examine defendants’ expert witnesses, which includes being able to examine the expert on the matter upon which the expert’s opinion is based and the reasons for that opinion.” (Id. at p. 838.) Without access to raw data, a plaintiff “cannot effectively scrutinize the way the data was collected, determine if there are discrepancies, and cross-examine the neuropsychologist on the basis and reasons for the neuropsychologist’s opinion.” (Ibid.)  

The Court finds that a discussion of Randy’s Trucking, supra, 91 Cal.App.5th 818 is appropriate. In Randy’s Trucking, plaintiff sustained injuries in a vehicle accident and during discovery claimed that she suffered a severe traumatic brain injury. The defendants moved for an order compelling plaintiff to undergo a mental examination by their neuropsychologist. The defendants moved for such an order after plaintiff refused to proceed with the examination unless their attorney received all raw data and test information from the mental examination. The trial court granted defendants’ motion to compel; however, the trial court denied defendants’ request to limit the transmission of raw data and test materials to a licensed psychologist or neuropsychologist. Instead, the trial court ordered defendants’ neuropsychologist to transfer the raw data and an audio recording of the examination to plaintiff’s attorney pursuant to a protective order.  

Thereafter, defendants sought a petition for writ of mandate to direct the trial court to modify its order to require the transmission of the raw data and audio recording to plaintiff’s retained psychologist or neuropsychologist as opposed to plaintiff’s attorney. The defendants asserted that: (1) the trial court erred in ordering transmission of raw data and audio recordings to plaintiff’s counsel because those items are not among the materials set forth in Code Civ. Proc. § 2032.610(a); and (2) psychologist confidentiality prohibited disclosure of test materials. In rejecting defendants’ contentions, the Randy’s Trucking court held that there is no statutory authority prohibiting a trial court from ordering the disclosure of test materials or test data when ordering a mental examination. (Randy’s Trucking, Inc., supra, 91 Cal.App.5th 818, 834.) Moreover, the court opined that given the trial court’s broad discretion in discovery matters, the trial court retained jurisdiction to order the production of materials and, in doing so, the trial court did not abuse its discretion. (Id. at p. 837.) 

Here, Defendants argue that the disclosure of raw data to Plaintiff’s counsel would violate ethical obligations and potentially endanger test security. Dr. Roger Light, Defendants’ retained neuropsychologist, provides a declaration in support of the Motion. Dr. Light asserts that he has concerns about sharing raw test data with attorneys. (See Light Decl., ¶ 17.) Dr. Light states that the California Board of Psychology has imposed restrictions on the distribution of test materials. (Id., ¶ 22.) He contends that the imposition of a protective order would not suffice to guard the sensitive information. (Id., ¶ 34.) Dr. Light states he is willing to provide the raw testing data directly to Plaintiff’s retained expert, Dr. Delis. (Id., ¶ 15.)

Here, exercising its discretion, the Court finds that it is appropriate to allow disclosure of the raw test data between the parties’ experts. The data would in the ordinary course of the case be produced to counsel at some point, so both sides’ experts can perform their analysis and the attorneys can cross-examine the experts. The facts in this action are akin to those in Randy’s Trucking. Dr. Light has requested that raw test data and materials not be disclosed to Plaintiff’s counsel due to ethical concerns and to protect the integrity of the testing process, arguments rejected in Randy's Trucking. Without access to raw test data and materials, the Court finds that Plaintiff would not be able to scrutinize the data collected by Dr. Light and prepare for cross examination if this case goes to trial. While Defendants argue that Plaintiff’s counsel is not equipped to interpret the raw data, such argument was rejected in Randy’s Trucking, where the court stated that such interpretation is “not necessarily . . . required . . . to use the materials for purposes of cross-examination, since disclosure of [such] materials may help to protect against abuse and disputes over what transpired during the examination.” (Randy’s Trucking, supra, 91 Cal.App.5th 818, 838.)

While the Court finds that the raw test data from Plaintiff’s neuropsychological examination should be exchanged between the experts, the Court presumes that the data will also be disclosed to each side’s counsel, so the Court finds that such disclosure is warranted pursuant to a protective order. (Randy’s Trucking, supra, 91 Cal.App.5th 818, 828.)

Because the parties have not requested or adopted a protective order for this case, the Court orders the following protective order—identical to that which was issued in Randy’s Trucking, supra, 91 Cal.App.5th 818, 828—be entered in this matter: 

Plaintiff’s counsel, defense counsel and all experts, consultants and employees of the respective firms shall maintain the security of all raw data, test materials, and other medically private information obtained during the examination. However, such raw data, test materials and other medically private information may be disclosed to plaintiff’s counsel, defense counsel and all experts, consultants and employees of the respective firms for use in this case. Such materials and data may also be shown to the trier of fact at the time of trial, or such other time as may be necessary for the adjudication of the above-captioned matter. These materials may be used for no other purpose, may not be disseminated to any other party and the parties shall take reasonable steps to maintain the confidentiality of the above-identified materials. [The parties are also ordered to] destroy the above-identified materials at the cessation of this case, in accordance with the California Rules of Professional Conduct.

Counsel may seek to add or modify the above terms by stipulation or motion; however, pending a protective order from the parties concerning disclosure, the above protective order shall apply.  

Audio Recording

Defendants also contend that Dr. Light’s ethical obligations preclude him from providing the audio recording of the exam to Plaintiff’s counsel.

Where a mental examination occurs, “[t]he examiner and examinee shall have the right to record a mental examination on audio tape.” (Golfland Entertainment Centers, Inc. v. Superior Court (2003) 108 Cal.App.4th 739, 750.) The main purposes of audiotaping a mental examination “are to ensure that the examiner does not overstep the bounds set by the court for the mental examination, that the context of the responses can be judged for purposes of trial, that the examinee’s interests are protected (especially since the examinee’s counsel ordinarily will not be present), and that any evidence of abuse can be presented to the court.” (Ibid.) Code Civ. Proc. § 2032.530(a) grants the examinee the right to record a mental examination by audio technology and implies that the examinee can retain a copy of the recording. (Randy’s Trucking, Inc, supra, 91 Cal.App.5th at p. 837.) A court has discretion to order the production of an audio recording from a mental examination. (Ibid.)  

The Court finds that if an audio recording of the examination is made, disclosure of the audio recording to Plaintiff’s expert is appropriate. Disclosure of the audio recording of the examination can ensure that Dr. Light does not overstep the bounds of the examination and can protect Plaintiff’s interests. The Court presumes that both parties’ experts will share the audio recording, like the raw data, with the retaining attorneys. As such, the recording would be covered by the same protective order.

Disposition

Defendants Rudolph & Sletten Inc. and Minegar Contracting Inc.’s motion for leave to conduct a neuropsychological examination of Plaintiff Enrique Alejandro Luevano Ibarra is granted in part, with the above discussed conditions.

Case Number: 25STCV30184    Hearing Date: August 7, 2026    Dept: 510

No. 10 - Reginald Snowden v. Veolia Water Contract Operation USA Inc., et al.

Defendant Veolia Water Contract Operation USA Inc.’s Demurrer to Plaintiff’s Complaint

Defendant Veolia Water Contract Operation USA Inc. demurs to Reginald Snowden’s complaint. The complaint alleges causes of action for violation of Plaintiff’s right of publicity (Civil Code § 3344); common law misappropriation of likeness; and unfair competition.

Evidentiary Objections

          Plaintiff makes three evidentiary objections to Defendant’s evidence submitted in support of its demurrer. All three objections are sustained.

Release Agreement

Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s causes of action fail because he cannot adequately plead the element of “lack of consent.” For support, Defendant points to a Release Agreement Plaintiff allegedly signed that shows Plaintiff provided written consent and authorizes Defendant to use his image. Even though the Release Agreement is not mentioned in the complaint, nor attached as an exhibit to it, Defendant urges the Court to consider its contents. Specifically, Defendant argues that the Court may consider it because Plaintiff incorporated the release agreement by reference when he discussed “consent” in his complaint.

The Court is not convinced. The Court is limited to the contents of the complaint and any judicially noticeable documents when ruling on a demurrer. General demurrers can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable; evidence or extrinsic matters are not considered. (Code of Civ. Proc. §§ 430.30, 430.70; Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) 

In addition, the incorporation by reference doctrine has no application here. In the complaint, Plaintiff does not reference the Release Agreement at all. Plaintiff specifically alleges that there was no consent. Plaintiff alleges “VEOLIA and DOES did not obtain Plaintiff's consent before using Plaintiff's picture/likeness in the above, or any, marketing and advertising materials. . . . At no time did Plaintiff provide consent, written or otherwise, for VEOLIA and DOES to use Plaintiff's photograph or likeness in any marketing or advertising materials.” (Compl., ¶¶ 16, 19.) The Court does not find that Plaintiff’s mere use of the word “consent” in his complaint meaningfully guides the reader to any document.

Therefore, the face of the complaint does not show that Plaintiff’s claims are barred by a release agreement.

Disposition

Defendant Veolia Water Contract Operation USA Inc.’s demurrer to Plaintiff’s complaint is overruled. The Court hereby orders Defendant to file its answer within 21 days.
