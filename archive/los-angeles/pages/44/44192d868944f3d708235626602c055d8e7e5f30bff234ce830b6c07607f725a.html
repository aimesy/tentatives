Case Name: Albertoni v. LAUSD, et al.

Case No.: 22STCP00098

Matter: Motion to Compel Mental Exam

Moving Party: Defendant LAUSD

Responding Party: Plaintiff Diane Albertoni

Notice: OK

Ruling: The Motion is denied without prejudice.


Moving party to give notice.


The Court encourages all parties to appear remotely via LA

CourtConnect.  If submitting on the Court's tentative ruling, please follow the instructions provided above.


On June 20, 2024, Plaintiff Diane Albertoni filed the operative Third Amended Complaint (“TAC”), which alleges what appears to be twelve causes of action for FEHA failure to provide reasonable accommodations and failure to engage in the interactive process against Defendant LAUSD.

Defendant now seeks to compel Plaintiff’s mental exam.

This Court has the authority to compel a mental examination of a party.  Code of Civil Procedure section 2032.310(a) provides that any party seeking to obtain discovery by a mental examination “shall obtain leave of court.”  A motion for a mental examination “shall specify the time, place, manner, conditions, scope, and nature of the examination, as well as the identity and the specialty, if any, of the person or persons who will perform the examination.  The motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.” (Id. § 2032.310(b).)  Any grant of a motion for a mental examination must be based on “good cause.” (Id. § 2032.320(a).)  Good cause requires “that the party produce specific facts justifying discovery and that the inquiry be relevant to the subject matter of the action or reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence.”  (Vinson v. Superior Court (1987) 43 Cal.3d 833, 840.)

The Court finds that Plaintiff's mental condition is squarely "in controversy" within the meaning of Code of Civil Procedure section 2032.020(a). While Plaintiff correctly cites Vinson v. Superior Court (1987) 43 Cal.3d 833 for the proposition that a simple claim for emotional distress or an oppressive work environment does not automatically put a plaintiff's mental state in controversy, Plaintiff here does not merely allege transient "humiliation and embarrassment." In her verified discovery responses, Plaintiff explicitly claimed specific, ongoing clinical mental health injuries, including "Major depression, panic attacks, anxiety, sadness, [and] withdrawal," and noted that the conditions vary daily. Because Plaintiff alleges a specific, severe, and continuing mental injury directly caused by Defendant’s conduct, Defendant has demonstrated good cause to test those claims via an independent evaluation. 

Despite demonstrating good cause, LAUSD’s motion suffers from a fatal procedural defect. Plaintiff rightly objects that Defendant’s moving papers fail to specify the precise diagnostic tests and procedures to be employed, running afoul of long-standing California appellate authority. In Carpenter v. Superior Court (2006) 141 Cal.App.4th 249, the Court of Appeal held that the trial court must identify the permissible diagnostic tests and procedures by name in its order. This ensures the court has properly weighed the risks of an unwarranted intrusion upon the plaintiff's privacy against the defendant's need for discovery. Critically, Carpenter clarified that "the defendant, aware that the court must name the diagnostic tests and procedures in the order granting a mental examination, will identify the potential tests and procedures in its moving papers." (Id. at p. 267). If the moving papers omit this information, the trial court cannot fulfill its legislative mandate, and its duty is to deny the motion (Id. at p. 276, fn. 5).

Here, Defendant’s Notice of Motion and Memorandum of Points and Authorities merely seek a generic order compelling Plaintiff "to attend a mental examination." The body of the motion fails to list any specific diagnostic tests or clinical instruments. While Defendant did attach a proposed unexecuted stipulation as Exhibit 3, which lists 27 distinct clinical tests (such as the MMPI, CVLT, and WAIS-IV), attaching a proposed stipulation as an exhibit does not satisfy the requirement that the moving party explicitly demand and justify those tests within its primary moving papers.

By failing to integrate the specified tests into the motion itself, Defendant failed to give proper due process notice to Plaintiff within the moving framework, depriving Plaintiff of a clear opportunity to object to individual tests as overly intrusive or cumulative.

Given that the trial date is set for October 12, 2026, the parties are ordered to engage in a meaningful, verbal dialogue regarding the logistics and parameters of this examination before seeking further court intervention.

The Motion is denied without prejudice.

Moving party to give notice.

Matter: Motion to Dismiss

Moving Party: Defendants Janus Development, LLC, Habitat Partners Inc., and Joshua

Krom

Responding Party: Plaintiff Yu Wen

Ruling: The Motion is granted.


Moving party to give notice.


The Court encourages all parties to appear remotely via LA CourtConnect.  If submitting on the Court's tentative ruling, please follow the instructions provided above.


This is a landlord-tenant and roommate dispute filed by a pro per litigant on July 27, 2022.

Defendants Janus Development, LLC, Habitat Partners Inc., and Joshua Krom now seek to dismiss this action because they were not served within 3 years.

“The summons and complaint shall be served upon a defendant within three years after the action is commenced against the defendant” (CCP § 583.210(a)), and “If service is not made in an action within the time prescribed in this article: . . . (2) The action shall be dismissed by the court on its own motion or on motion of any person interested in the action, whether named as a party or not, after notice to the parties. (b) The requirements of this article are mandatory and are not subject to extension, excuse, or exception except as expressly provided by statute.”  (CCP § 583.250.)

“In computing the time within which service must be made pursuant to this article, there shall be excluded the time during which any of the following conditions existed: . . .  (d) Service, for any other reason, was impossible, impracticable, or futile due to causes beyond the plaintiff’s control. Failure to discover relevant facts or evidence is not a cause beyond the plaintiff’s control for the purpose of this subdivision.”  (CCP § 583.240.)

Plaintiff filed an opposition on July 10, 2026, arguing that Defendants' timeline is a narrow, technical chronology, highlighting heavy ongoing discovery and litigation activity by Defendants, and alternatively requesting a continuance to gather further evidence of diligence. Defendants filed their reply on July 14, 2026.

The complaint was filed on July 27, 2022. Accordingly, the three-year statutory deadline to serve Defendants expired on July 27, 2025. Even factoring in a potential court-ordered extension referenced by the parties through July 30, 2025, Plaintiff's service remains untimely as a matter of law. Plaintiff's proofs of service establish that substituted service was attempted on July 26, 2025, by leaving copies at a commercial mail receiving agency and mailing copies on that same date. Under CCP § 415.20(b), substituted service is explicitly "deemed complete on the 10th day after the mailing." Consequently, Plaintiff's service was not legally complete until August 5, 2025—which falls outside both the statutory three-year window and the purported July 30, 2025 extension. Under California law, the physical act of attempting service or mailing within the three-year window does not suffice; service must be legally complete before the statutory timeline expires (Highland Stucco & Lime, Inc. v. Superior Court (1981) 119 Cal.App.3d 99, 103–104).

Plaintiff's opposition argues that the Court should address difficulties, service obstacles, and individual diligence under Code of Civil Procedure section 583.240, which excludes periods where service was impossible, impracticable, or futile due to causes beyond a plaintiff's control.

However, Plaintiff bears the evidentiary burden to establish these exceptions (Dill v. Berquist Construction Co. (1994) 24 Cal.App.4th 1426, 1439–1440). Plaintiff failed to submit any declarations, affidavits, or competent documentary evidence alongside the opposition to substantiate a specific period of tolling. Speculative or generalized assertions in a memorandum of points and authorities do not constitute competent evidence to satisfy this burden.

Plaintiff objects to dismissal on the ground that Defendants have actively litigated the case, propounded extensive discovery, and filed discovery-enforcement motions—including a motion to deem matters admitted scheduled just one day prior to this hearing. Plaintiff argues that this post-motion litigation pressure is prejudicial and inconsistent with a request for dismissal.

Long-standing California authority establishes that a defendant's general appearance or participation in discovery after the statutory three-year period has run does not waive or cure a plaintiff's failure to complete timely service (Busching v. Superior Court (1974) 12 Cal.3d 44, 52–53; Weatherby v. Van Diest (1991) 233 Cal.App.3d 506, 511). Because the service defect occurred and became permanent in August 2025, subsequent litigation behavior required to preserve defense positions under looming trial deadlines cannot retroactively validate the untimely service. Under section 583.250, prejudice to the defendant is not an element of the statutory mandate, and the Court lacks equitable discretion to balance hardships once the deadline has passed.

Plaintiff alternatively requests a continuance of the hearing to organize and submit supplementary service logs and evidence of diligence.

This request is denied. The critical dates concerning the filing of the case and the physical execution of substituted service have been known to Plaintiff since the proofs of service were executed nearly a year ago. A continuance cannot change the legally undisputed reality that substituted service under section 415.20 was completed in August 2025, past the boundary of the statutory window. Given the mandatory nature of section 583.250 and the absence of a threshold showing of a viable statutory exclusion, delaying resolution is unwarranted.

For all these reasons, the Motion to Dismiss is granted.

Moving party to give notice.

Case Name: Rebollar v. Emanuel Motors, LLC, et al.

Case No.: 25STCV02024

Matter: Motion for Sanctions

Ruling: The Motion is granted in part.


Moving party to give notice.


The Court encourages all parties to appear remotely via LA CourtConnect.  If submitting on the Court's tentative ruling, please follow the instructions provided above.


On June 11, 2026, the Court denied Defendant Emanuel Motors, LLC’s motion to compel arbitration, finding: “By failing to fund the initial JAMS consumer arbitration, resulting in the termination of that case, and taking no action to preserve or cure its default for nearly 14 months, Defendant materially breached the arbitration agreement and waived its right to compel arbitration under CCP § 1281.97.”

Plaintiff Rebollar now seeks $5,509.25 in sanctions for Defendant’s failure to timely pay arbitration fees.

CCP § 1281.97(d) states, “If the employee or consumer proceeds with an action in a court of appropriate jurisdiction, the court shall impose sanctions on the drafting party in accordance with Section 1281.99.”

CCP § 1281.99(a) states, “(a) The court shall impose a monetary sanction against a drafting party that materially breaches an arbitration agreement pursuant to subdivision (a) of Section 1281.97 or subdivision (a) of Section 1281.98, by ordering the drafting party to pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney's fees and costs, incurred by the employee or consumer as a result of the material breach.”

The Court has reviewed the billing records and will only deduct $690, consisting of $630 in pre-arbitration and CLRA work and what seems to be $60 associated with a billing error for Laura Turner.

The remaining fees and costs will be awarded. The Motion is granted in part as set forth herein.

Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel nonparty Los Angeles County District Attorney’s ("LADA") compliance with a deposition subpoena for business records is granted in part.

Under the binding authority of Cooley v. Superior Court (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 1039, the LADA cannot be compelled to produce investigative or police reports generated by an outside agency (here, the Hawthorne Police Department), as the prosecutor is not the legal custodian of those outside files and cannot provide the necessary statutory affidavit under Evidence Code section 1561. Furthermore, production of the remaining criminal history data, probation files, and internal records is barred by absolute attorney work product protections (Code Civ. Proc. § 2018.030) and strict statutory confidentiality provisions (Pen. Code §§ 1203.05, 13300). Accordingly, the motion is denied as to all records generated by third-party law enforcement agencies, and is granted solely to the extent that the LADA possesses its own natively generated, non-privileged administrative documents, subject to the mandatory redaction of all confidential victim and witness identifying information pursuant to Penal Code section 293.
