I.                   INTRODUCTION

This is a FEHA action.

On November 25, 2024, Plaintiff Jennifer L. Freeman (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint

against Defendant Cedars-Sinai Medical Center (“Defendant”), alleging causes of action for (1) Discrimination, including Discriminatory Termination, Based on Plaintiff’s Actual or Perceived Disabilities, (2) Failure to Engage, (3) Failure to Accommodate, (4) Violation of the California Family Rights Act, (5) Discrimination, including Discriminatory Termination, Based on Plaintiff’s Age (6) Retaliation, including Retaliatory Termination Based on Plaintiff’s Actual or Perceived Participation in Protected Activities, (7) Failure to Prevent, and (8) Unfair Business Practices.

            On July 27, 2026, the Court granted Plaintiff’s motion quash deposition subpoena for production of business records issued by Defendant to the EDD. The Court also granted Plaintiff’s motion to compel the attendance and testimony of Defendant’s personal most qualified.

            On August 10, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion to compel Defendant to comply with the Court’s July 27, 2026 Order. On September 2, 2026, Defendant filed an opposition. On September 9, 2026, Plaintiff filed a reply.

            Also on August 10, 2026, Defendant filed the instant motion for a protective order prohibiting or limiting the deposition of Bryan Hickey in his individual capacity. On September 2, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition. On September 9, 2026, Defendant filed a reply.

            On August 19, 2026, Defendant filed the instant motion to compel Plaintiff to obtain and produce her EDD records. On September 2, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition. On September 9, 2026, Defendant filed a reply.

II.                LEGAL STANDARD

1.      Compel PMQ

Any party may obtain discovery, subject to restrictions, by taking the oral deposition of any person, including any party to the action.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.010.)  A properly served deposition notice is effective to require a party or party-affiliated deponent to attend and to testify, as well as to produce documents for inspection and copying.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.280, subd. (a).) 

California Code of Civil Procedure section¿2025.450, subdivision (a) provides:¿“If, after service of a deposition notice, a party to the action . . . , without having served a valid objection under Section 2025.410, fails to appear for examination, or to proceed with it, or to produce for¿inspection any document . . . described in the deposition notice, the party giving the notice may move for an order compelling the deponent’s attendance and testimony, and the production for inspection of any document . . . described in the deposition notice.”

California Code of Civil Procedure section¿2025.450, subdivision (b) provides:¿“A motion under subdivision (a) shall comply with both of the following:¿ 

  1. The motion shall set forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the production for inspection of any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing described in the deposition notice.¿ 

 

  1. The motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040, or, when the deponent fails to attend the deposition and produce the documents, electronically stored information, or things described in the deposition notice, by a declaration stating that the petitioner has contacted the deponent to inquire about the nonappearance.”¿

 

2.      Protective Order

“The court, for good cause shown, may make any order that justice requires to protect any party or other natural person or organization from unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression, or undue burden and expense.” (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.090, subd. (b) [interrogatories], 2031.060, subd. (b) [production of documents], 2033.080, subd. (b) [requests for admission].) Such orders may include excusing the party from answering the discovery requests at issue, limiting the number of discovery requests, or extending the time to respond to discovery requests. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.090, subd. (b), 2031.060, subd. (b), 2033.080, subd. (b).) The court has considerable discretion in granting and crafting protective orders. (Raymond Handling Concepts Corp. v. Superior Court (1995) 39 Cal.App.4th 584, 588.) 

The burden of proof is generally on the party seeking the protective order to show good cause¿for whatever order is sought.¿¿(Fairmont Ins. Co. v. Superior Court¿(2000) 22 Cal.4th 245, 255.)¿¿The concept of good cause requires a showing of specific facts demonstrating undue burden, etc., and justifying the relief sought.¿¿(See¿Goodman v. Citizens Life & Casualty Ins. Co.¿(1967) 253 Cal.App.2d 807, 819.)¿¿The facts are normally established in a declaration by counsel for the party seeking the protective order.¿¿The declaration must contain competent evidence—i.e., first-hand knowledge of the facts.¿¿Hearsay allegations¿on information and belief¿and conclusory statements that particular relief is¿necessary¿are not enough.¿(Ibid.)¿

3.      Compel EDD Records

Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.220 states: “A statement that the party to whom a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling has been directed will comply with the particular demand shall state that the production, inspection, copying, testing, or sampling, and related activity demanded, will be allowed either in whole or in part, and that all documents or things in the demanded category that are in the possession, custody, or control of that party and to which no objection is being made will be included in the production.”   

            Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.320(a) states: “If a party filing a response to a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling under Sections 2031.210, 2031.220, 2031.230, 2031.240, and 2031.280 thereafter fails to permit the inspection, copying, testing, or sampling in accordance with that party's statement of compliance, the demanding party may move for an order compelling compliance.”

            The demanding party may move for an order compelling further responses to the demand if the demanding party deems that (1) a statement of compliance with the demand is incomplete, (2) a representation of inability to comply is inadequate, incomplete, or evasive, or (3) an objection in the response is without merit or too general.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310(a).)  

Motions to compel further responses to Requests for Production must set forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought by the request.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310(b).)  To establish good cause, a discovery proponent must identify a disputed fact that is of consequence in the action and explain how the discovery sought will tend in reason to prove or disprove that fact or lead to other evidence that will tend to prove or disprove the fact.  (Digital Music News LLC v. Superior Court (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 216, 224, disapproved on other grounds by Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531; see also Kirkland v. Superior Court (2002) 95 Cal.App.4th 92, 98 [characterizing good cause as “a fact-specific showing of relevance”]; Tbg Ins. Servs. Corp. v. Superior Court (2002) 96 Cal.App.4th 443, 448 [the burden to show good cause for production “is met simply by a factspecific showing of relevance.”].) If good cause is shown by the moving party, the burden shifts to the responding party to justify any objections made to disclosure of the documents.  (Kirkland, supra, 95 Cal.App.4th at 98.) 

Code Complaint Response

A code-compliant response to a request for production consists of any of the following: (1) a statement that the party will comply, (2) a representation that the party lacks the ability to comply, or (3) an objection.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.210.)  A statement that the party will comply must state that the requests for production “will be allowed either in whole or in part, and that all documents or things in the demanded category that are in the possession, custody, or control of that party and to which no objection is being made will be included in the production.”  (Id. § 2031.220.)  “If only part of an item or category of item in a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling is objectionable, the response shall contain a statement of compliance, or a representation of inability to comply with respect to the remainder of that item or category.”  (Id. § 2031.240(a).)  If an objection is made, the responding party must “[i]dentify with particularity any document, tangible thing, land, or electronically stored information falling within any category of item in the demand to which an objection is being made.”  (Id. § 2031.240(b)(1).)

Failing to respond to an authorized method of discovery is a misuse of the discovery process. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.010(d).) 

III.             DISCUSSION

A.    Compel Compliance & Protective Order

Plaintiff moves for an order compelling Defendant to comply with this Court’s July 27,

2026 Order by producing its designated Person Most Qualified and/or Knowledgeable, Bryan Hickey, for Volume III of his deposition. Conversely, Defendant moves for a protective order Plaintiff from deposing Bryan Hickey in his individual capacity. Given the overlap in issues, the Court will address both motions in one ruling.

            Plaintiff seeks for the Court to compel Volume III of the deposition of Bryan Hickey on the grounds that on July 27, 2026, Court granted Plaintiff’s motion to compel the attendance and testimony of Defendant’s designated Person Most Qualified and/or Knowledgeable, which at the time the Court made its ruling, was Bryan Hickey. (See July 27, 2026 Order.) The Court in its ruling dismissed Defendant’s contention that producing its PMQ, Mr. Hickey, for a third day of deposition would be unnecessary as Defendant could not unilaterally decide to abstain from producing the witness after a proper notice. (Ibid.) The Court noted that the proper procedural avenue for Defendant would have been to move for a protective order. (Ibid.) The Court then granted “Plaintiffs motion to compel the attendance and testimony of Defendant’s person most qualified (“PMQ”)” and ordered the deposition to occur within thirty (30) days. (Ibid.)

            Plaintiff contends that Defendant still has not produced Mr. Hickey, but instead has designated Ms. Allison Rotter, a human resources employee, as the PMQ for Plaintiff’s remaining categories. (Kirk Decl., ¶ 10, Ex. 5.)

            Defendant thus is also moving for a protective order prohibiting or limiting the deposition of Mr. Hickey in both his capacity as Cedars-Sinai’s person most qualified (“PMQ”) and in his individual capacity. As for Mr. Hickey’s deposition in its individual capacity, Defendant asserts that the close of Volume I, Plaintiff’s counsel stipulated on the record that Hickey’s personal capacity examination was being folded into the PMQ examination, stating: “We’re taking both his PMK and personal capacity at the same time. So you don’t have to produce him again as an individual once we’re done.” (Andrews Decl., ¶ 5, Ex. 2 [Hickey Depo. Vol. I at 335.1-5.) As for Mr. Hickey in in his capacity as Defendant’s PMQ, the Court granted Plaintiff’s motion to compel the deposition of Defendant’s PMQ, which was Mr. Hickey at the time, in its July 27, 2026 Order. T

As an initial matter, the Court notes that its July 27, 2026 expressly does not concern compelling the deposition of Mr. Hickey in its individual capacity. (Luri v. Greenwald (2003) 107 Cal.App.4th 1119, 1125 [“As a general rule, the trial court may consider only the grounds stated in the notice of motion.”].)  Thus, the Court rejects any request by Plaintiff to seek the deposition of Mr. Hickey in an individual capacity. (Opp. at p. 15.)

            The Court therefore agrees with Defendant that the Court’s July 27, 2026 Order provides the relief of compelling the deposition of Defendant’s PMQ, who at the time was Mr. Hickey, rather than providing the relief of compelling the deposition of specifically Mr. Hickey, who is now no longer Defendant’s PMQ.

            Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.230 provides that “[i]f the deponent named is not a natural person, the deposition notice shall describe with reasonable particularity the matters on which examination is requested. In that event, the deponent shall designate and produce at the deposition those of its officers, directors, managing agents, employees, or agents who are most qualified to testify on its behalf as to those matters to the extent of any information known or reasonably available to the deponent.” Consistent with section 2025.230’s designation discretion, Defendant informed Plaintiff that it designates Allison Rotter, Director of Human Resources, as its PMQ for the remaining categories to which Mr. Hickey did not testify. (Andrews Decl., ¶ 11.) Defendant asserts that Ms. Rotter’s testimony as PMQ will provide Defendant’s position on the remaining categories. Any information Mr. Hickey personally possesses that is relevant to those categories is “known or reasonably available” to Defendant and can be conveyed through a properly prepared PMQ. (Maldonado v. Superior Court (2002) 94 Cal.App.4th 1390, 1396-1397; [If the subject matter of the questioning is clearly stated, the burden is on the entity, not the examiner, to produce the right witnesses. And, if the particular officer or employee designated lacks personal knowledge of all the information sought, he or she is supposed to find out from those who do”] (emphasis added).)

            It is within Defendant’s discretion to designate its own PMQ as to Plaintiff’s remaining noticed categories. The July 27, 2026 Order solely compelled Defendant’s PMQ and explicitly stated that the burden is on Defendant “to produce the correct witness for the noticed categories, such that definitive, unequivocal answers could be obtained.” (July 27, 2026 Order at p. 14.) Defendant did that, designated Ms. Rotter as the correct witness, and attempted to schedule her deposition within the Court’s delineated time-frame, specifically on August 21, 24, 25, and 27, 2026. (Andrews Decl., ¶ 11, Ex. 7.) Plaintiff refused. (Ibid.) So long as Defendant designates a witness most qualified to testify on the noticed topics, which is no longer Mr. Hickey, Plaintiff does not have the right to refuse that designation simply because she prefers Mr. Hickey. (See Maldonado, supra, 94 Cal.App.4th at p. 1398 [holding party was not required to produce a former employee even if “the former employees are far more knowledgeable about the litigation than anyone currently employed by the company. [Holding party’s] duty is limited, as we have said, to producing the most knowledgeable person currently in its employ and making sure that that person has access to information and documents reasonably available within the corporation.”].)

            Defendant’s motion is granted. Plaintiff’s motion is denied. As such, Plaintiff’s accompanying request for monetary sanctions is also denied.

B.     Compel EDD Records

Defendant moves for an order requiring Plaintiff to execute and submit Form DE 5600

(Authorization for Release of Records) to the California Employment Development Department (“EDD”); an order requiring Plaintiff to produce all responsive EDD records to Defendant within two (2) business days of Plaintiff’s receipt of those records to complete compliance with her prior agreement to produce all documents responsive to Request for Production No. 40; and an order requiring Plaintiff to serve a supplemental response to Request for Production No. 74, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.310.

            Plaintiff moved to quash the subpoena issued by Defendant upon the EDD on June May 21, 2026, seeking information regarding Plaintiff’s disability benefits. The Court heard and granted the motion on July 27, 2026. The Court found that given that the subpoena was issued upon the EDD and the EDD concurrently was asserting the official information privilege, which the Court found applicable, EDD cannot be compelled to produce the requested the documents. (July 27, 2026 Order at p. 7.)

            While the Court found that Defendant was seeking independent relief, which was not properly noticed, Defendant alternatively requested the Court to compel Plaintiff to execute the Authorization for Release of Records (Form DE 5600) necessary to obtain the responsive records from the EDD and to produce all responsive records she receives.

            The Court urged the parties to meet and confer regarding a number of questions to avoid unnecessary motion practice. (See Chavez v. Sw. Key Program, Inc. (S.D. Cal. Aug. 2, 2012) No. 11CV1878- CAB (NLS), 2012 WL 12868258, at *2 (applying California law) [“Applied to the facts of this case, the gravamen of the disability discrimination suit is inconsistent with the continued assertion of the protections of the California Unemployment Insurance code for documents relating to disability applications and benefits. Thus, Plaintiff has waived the protection of EDD documents by maintenance of an action inconsistent with the assertion of the privilege.”]; ibid. [“Plaintiff must show she can, with or without reasonable accommodation, perform all the essential functions of the job. As Defendant argues, Plaintiff cannot put her ability to perform the essential job functions at issue and, at the same time, assert a privilege governing statements she herself made as to her ability to perform those job functions.”];  Gomez Cabrales v. Aerotek, Inc. (C.D. Cal. May 8, 2018) 2018 WL 2121829, at *4 (applying California law) [finding “to the extent [ ] EDD documents are protected under California law, such protection has been waived because ‘the gravamen of the disability discrimination suit is inconsistent with the continued assertion of the protections of the California Unemployment 99 Insurance [C]ode for documents relating to disability applications and benefits.’ ”]; Romano v. AT&T Mobility Servs., LLC (S.D. Cal. Nov. 17, 2020) No. 320CV00698JLSKSC, 2020 WL 6741677, at *3 (applying California law) [“The gravamen of plaintiff's age discrimination suit is inconsistent with the continued assertion of the protections of the California Unemployment Insurance Code for documents pertaining to unemployment benefits, particularly because plaintiff alleges that he was terminated because of his age. [] The Court finds Plaintiff has waived the protection of the EDD and UIAB documents ‘by maintenance of an action inconsistent with the assertion of the privilege.’”] Colosimo v. Alere San Diego, Inc. (S.D. Cal. June 15, 2026) No. 26-CV-809-LLJAC, 2026 WL 1725376, at *5 (applying California law) [granting a motion to compel Plaintiff to execute “an authorization to the California EDD to release records” as Plaintiff waived the privilege by filing an employment discrimination action]; Wesley v. Gates (N.D. Cal. July 2, 2009) No. C 08-2719 SI, 2009 WL 1955997, at *1 [granting a motion to require Plaintiff’s authorization of release of his EDD records because Plaintiff’s records at the EDD “are relevant to plaintiffs disability discrimination claims”].)

            Defendant provides that following the Court’s directive, it requested that Plaintiff execute and submit Form DE 5600 (Song Decl., Ex. 10), obtain and produce her EDD records, and resolve the dispute without motion practice. (Id., ¶ 4, Ex. 2.) Defendant further identified Requests for Production (“RFPDs”) Nos. 40 and 74 as the requests to which the records respond. (Ibid.)

RFPD No. 40 seeks “[a]ll COMMUNICATIONS between [Plaintiff] and any governmental agency that evidence, reflect, support, refute, or refer to any of the conduct underlying the claims asserted in [Plaintiff’s] COMPLAINT.” RFP No. 74 sought “[a]ll DOCUMENTS reflecting any statement by [Plaintiff] or any representative of [Plaintiff] regarding [Plaintiff’s] employment status, inability to work, disability, medical condition, or work restrictions submitted to the EDD from June 1, 2019 to October 1, 2022.” While Plaintiff agreed “to produce responsive documents in her possession, custody, or control” in response to RFPDs No. 40, Plaintiff produced EDD records for only four months—June 2019, August 2019, January 2020, and May 2020—despite receiving SDI benefits for approximately 39 months. (Id., ¶¶ 10, 12, Ex. 7 [Pl.’s Resp. to RFPDs No. 40], Ex. 9 [documents Bates-stamped J.Freeman 000722–29, 742–43, and 747–54].)

Plaintiff produced no documents in response to RFPDs No. 74. Instead, she asserted she was “unable to comply in full” because the responsive records were “in the exclusive possession” of the EDD. (Id., ¶ 11, Ex. 8 [Pl.’s Resp. to RFPDs No. 74].)

The EDD records are squarely relevant to this action, as provided for in the authority this Court cites above. Plaintiff explicitly alleges that she was able to perform the essential functions of her position with the accommodation of remote work, as is needed to support her disability claims pursuant to FEHA. The EDD records may show whether Plaintiff was unavailable, her job-search efforts, work sought or obtained, and representations about availability, which is relevant to Plaintiff’s mitigation efforts. Furthermore, “an employer is not liable for backpay during periods that an improperly discharged employee is unavailable for work due to a disability.” (Davis v. Los Angeles Unified School Dist. Personnel Com. (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 1122, 1133–1134.)

Plaintiff waived any personal confidentiality protection through conduct “so inconsistent with an intent to enforce the right as to induce a reasonable belief that such right has been relinquished.” (Crest Catering Co. v. Superior Court (1965) 62 Cal.2d 274, 278.) She alleges she was “fully capable of performing the essential functions of her job remotely,” seeks $317,750 in lost wages on that theory, and received SDI benefits for approximately 39 months based on repeated certifications that she could not perform her “regular or customary work.” Having placed her work capacity and wage loss at issue, she cannot invoke confidentiality to shield documents pertaining those same subjects. (Chavez v. Sw. Key Program, Inc. (S.D. Cal. Aug. 2, 2012) No. 11CV1878- CAB (NLS), 2012 WL 12868258, at *2 [“One example of an inconsistent act is the bringing of a lawsuit, the gravamen of which is ‘so inconsistent with the continued assertion of the ... privilege as to compel the conclusion that the privilege has in fact been waived.’ ”].)

There is persuasive authority to support the relief sought – specifically for an order requiring Plaintiff to execute and submit Form DE 5600 (Authorization for Release of Records) to the EDD. In Colosimo v. Alere San Diego, Inc. (S.D. Cal. 2026) 2026 WL 1725376, at *5, applying California law, the Court applied Crest Catering and compelled the plaintiff, who was pursuing an employment discrimination action, to execute an authorization releasing EDD records; and it held that privacy concerns could be addressed through protective measures. (See also Wesley v. Gates (N.D. Cal. July 2, 2009) No. C 08-2719 SI, 2009 WL 1955997, at *1 [“Furthermore, the Court may order plaintiff to sign the required authorization releasing the SSA and EDD records”]; see also Miranda v. 21st Century Ins. Co. (2004) 117 Cal.App.4th 913, 929 [affirming the trial court’s order granting a motion requesting the terminating sanction of dismissal after the trial court ordered the plaintiff to sign authorizations for release of her medical records from Kaiser and plaintiff refused to sign the authorizations].)

Should Plaintiff have any such privacy concerns, the Court finds that Defendant’s counsel’s representation to provide Plaintiff’s counsel’s first review, redactions limited to unrelated personal or private information, and a privilege log identifying each redaction is sufficient to assuage Plaintiff’s legitimate concerns. (See Motion at p. 6.)

Defendant’s motion is granted in part. At least some of the documents are with Plaintiff’s custody or control, given that they must be provided upon her request.  The exact scope of what she can cause to be produced remains to be seen.  To that end, the Court orders Plaintiff to execute and submit Form DE 5600 (Authorization for Release of Records) to the California Employment Development Department (“EDD”) within ten (10) days of this order. Should any documents be provided by the EDD, the Court orders Plaintiff to produce them to Defendant, pursuant to the stated protective measures. As to addition documents responsive to the RFPDs at issue, Plaintiff need merely provide Code-compliant responses as well as the relevant documents.   If that response turns out to be incomplete, or if there are documents that should have been preserved but were not, the Court can fashion appropriate remedies, including but not limited to, excluding the evidence at trial or asking the jury to draw an adverse inference.

IV.             CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, Defendant’s motion for a protective order is GRANTED.

Plaintiff’s motion to compel compliance is DENIED. Plaintiff’s request for sanctions is DENIED.

Defendant’s motion to compel EDD records is GRANTED in part. Defendant is ordered to prepare a Proposed Order consistent with this opinion within five (5) days. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 5.125(b).) Any potential objection to the Proposed Order must be made pursuant to Rules of Court, rule 5.125(c).

I.                   INTRODUCTION

On September 17, 2025, Plaintiff David A. Romero (“Romero”) Dos Potrillos LLC

(“Potrillos”) and David A. Romero, as trustee of the Laguna Seca Living Trust (“the Trust”) (collectively, “Plaintiffs”) filed a complaint against Defendants RJS Financial Inc. (“RJSI”), RJS Financial, a California Corporation (“RJS”), Matthew Garrett Gross (“Gross”), Bret L. McClure, Esq. (“McClure”), Frandzel Robins Bloom & Csato, L.C. (“FRBC”), Paul J. Carter, Esq. (“Carter”), and Bergkvist Bergkvist & Carter, LLP (“BBC”) (collectively, “Defendants”).

            On January 5, 2026, Plaintiff added Michael Gross as DOE 1.

The Complaint alleges the following causes of action: (1) Fraud and Deceit (2) Violation of Usury Laws (3) Conspiracy to Defraud (4) Breach of Contract (5) Breach of the Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing (6) Financial Elder Abuse (7) Professional Negligence (8) Breach of Fiduciary Duties (9) Quiet Title Action (10) Wrongful foreclosure (11) Abuse of Process and (12) Intentional Interference with Prospective Economic Advantage.

Plaintiffs allege the following. Plaintiff, David A. Romero, is a 70-year-old retired immigrant. Romero owned and had a right to possession to three properties (1) an apartment building, located at 329 West Magnolia Street, Compton, CA 90220, APN 6157-017-036 (“Magnolia Property”), (2) an apartment building located at 534-536 E. 19th St., Long Beach, CA 90806, APN 7209-005-010 (“Long Beach Property”). (3) a vacant lot located at 902 Nicholson Avenue, Wilmington, CA 90744 CA 90220; APN: 7429-007-009, 7429-007-010, 7429-007-017, 7429-007-020, 7429-007-021. (“Nicholson Property”). (Compl., ¶ 11.) 

On June 19, 2019, Romero, as a member of Potrillos, executed a promissory note for the sum of $2.1 million in favor of RJSI, claiming to be a California Corporation.” The interest’s rate was set at 11.99%. The default rate was set at 16.99% (“Loan”). (Id. ¶ 13, Ex. A.) The Loan was secured by the Magnolia and Long Beach Properties. Later, RJSI claimed that the Loan was also secured by the Nicholson Property. (Id. ¶ 14.) Plaintiffs contend that the Loan was usurious.

The Nicholson Property is a vacant lot that Romero leased to a trucking company for $12,000 a month. This was Romero sole income, except for his Social Security checks. From the onset, Gross knew that, based on Romero monthly income of $12,000, he will not be able to make the monthly interest payments of $20,982.50, as required by the promissory note. (Id. ¶ 16.)

While the Loan was for $2.1 million, Plaintiffs received only $1.511 million. The deductions of $589,000, which included a payment of $63,000.00 fees to RJSI, the Lender, raised the interest rate to about 40% per annum. (Id. ¶ 17, Ex. B [Escrow Disbursement Sheet].)

Plaintiffs then used the $1.5 million to purchase the Magnolia Property, a “fixer upper.” Out of the $2.1 million loan, the amount of $298,000 was allocated for the rehab of the Magnolia Property. To complete the rehab, Romero invested another $150,000 of his own money, for a total of about $450,000. (Id. ¶ 18.)

After the completion of the rehab, the offices of the Los Angeles County were closed due to Covid-19. As a result, Romero was unable to obtain the City Permit of Occupancy. After exhausting the six-month interest reserve of $125,895.00, and Romero making about $100,000 payments on interest, RJSI declared a default. (Id. ¶ 19.)

On November 16, 2020, RJSI filed a Complaint against Plaintiffs for (1) Quiet Title; and (2) Declaratory Relief, Case No. 20STCV43490 (“Quiet Title Action”). On July 23, 2020, RJSI’s trustee, PLM Loan Management Services, Inc. (“PLM”), recorded three Notices of Default and Election to Sell (1) the Magnolia, (2) Long Beach, and the Nicholson Properties, seeking $81,327.95 in interest as of July 21, 2020.

On June 17, 2021, RJSI foreclosed on the Magnolia Property, bidding $1.5 million. (Id. ¶ 23, Ex. E [trustee’s deed upon sale of the Magnolia Property].) Prior to the foreclosure sale of the Magnolia property, Romero invested $450,000 to rehab the Property, obtained a Certificate of Occupation and few apartments were rented out. At that time, the property was worth about $3 million, but RJSI bid only $1.5 million, the same price Romeo paid when the building was a “fixer wrapper” and before Romero Invested $450,000 to rehab the Building. (Id. ¶ 24.)

At the same auction, of June 17, 2021, RJSI also foreclosed on the Long Beach Property, bidding $635,000. (Id. ¶ 25, Ex. F [trustee’s deed upon sale of the Long Beach Property].) RJSI sold the Long Beach Property for $873,000. (Id. ¶ 27, Ex. G [Grant Deed and a profile of the sale of the Long Beach Property].)

II.                LEGAL STANDARD

“The contempt power is a court’s ultimate weapon. [Citation.] In the first case by the California Supreme Court to consider the contempt power, the Court cautioned that the power to adjudicate direct contempt is necessarily arbitrary and should be used with great prudence, the judge bearing in mind that the exercise of that power is not so much to vindicate the judge’s character as to promote the respect due the administration of the laws. [Citation.] The enforcement of an order of contempt in this state is not for the vindication of a private right but is for the maintenance of the dignity and authority of the court, and to preserve the peace and dignity of the people of the State of California. [Citation.]”  (McCann v. Municipal Court (1990) 221 Cal.App.3d 527, 536-537 (McCann), internal citations and quotation marks omitted.)  “Contempt is generally a summary procedure designed to protect the dignity of the court in the exercise of its jurisdiction.”  (In re Nolan W. (2009) 45 Cal.4th 1217, 1231.) 

“When a contempt is committed in the immediate view and presence of the court, or of the judge at chambers, it may be punished summarily; for which an order must be made, reciting the facts as occurring in such immediate view and presence, adjudging that the person proceeded against is thereby guilty of a contempt, and that he or she be punished as therein prescribed.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1211, subd. (a).)  To hold a person in contempt for actions “committed in the immediate view and presence of the court, “[a]ll that is required is that an order be made reciting the facts, adjudging the person guilty, and prescribing the punishment. [Citations.] Despite the power to treat direct contempt summarily, however, it is common practice to defer adjudication of contempt for courtroom misconduct to the conclusion of a pending trial unless prompt punishment is imperative. In such case, due process considerations require notice and a hearing with the opportunity to be heard.”  (McCann, supra, (1990) 221 Cal.App.3d at p. 538.)

“When the contempt is not committed in the immediate view and presence of the court, or of the judge at chambers, [i.e., “indirect contempt”] an affidavit shall be presented to the court or judge of the facts constituting the contempt, or a statement of the facts by the referees or arbitrators, or other judicial officers.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1211, subd. (a).)  “The facts supporting indirect contempt arise outside the judg’'s presence, requiring a more elaborate procedure to notify the person charged and to afford an opportunity to be heard. … A common example is a party’s disobedience of a judge’s order.”  (In re M.R. (2013) 220 Cal.App.4th 49, 57, internal citations and quotation marks omitted; italics in original.)  “The affidavit requirement can be satisfied with a declaration under penalty of perjury.  [Citation.] The filing of the affidavit (or declaration) is a jurisdictional prerequisite to a contempt proceeding. [Citations.] It is, in effect, a complaint, which frames the issues before the court. [Citations.]. Without an initiating affidavit, a contempt order is void. [Citations.] The facts supporting indirect contempt require a more elaborate procedure to notify the person charged and to afford an opportunity to be heard.”  (Cal. Judges Benchbook Civ. Proc. Trial § 17.34, internal citations omitted.)   

“If the judge is satisfied with the sufficiency of the affidavit, the next step is for the judge to issue an order to show cause (OSC) re contempt that is directed to the accused, in which the date and time for a hearing are specified. See CCP § 1212; [citation]. As an alternative to an OSC, a warrant of attachment may be issued to bring the person charged to answer, although this is rare. See CCP § 1212. The OSC must be served in the same manner as a summons.”  (Cal. Judges Benchbook Civ. Proc. Trial § 17.34, some internal citations omitted.)  Importantly, “[d]ue process considerations apply to an indirect contempt proceeding, which is criminal in nature.”  (Moore v. Superior Court (2020) 57 Cal.App.5th 441, 456 (Moore).)  “The issuance of the order to show cause commences a separate action on the contempt charges. [Citation.] The accused is entitled to a full and fair hearing that satisfies due process.  (In re M.R. (2013) 220 Cal.App.4th 49, 58, internal citations omitted.)

“Due process requires notice of the specific charges at issue in the contempt proceeding. [Citation.] The OSC must give the accused notice if there are multiple distinct counts of contempt. [Citation.] The OSC is not required, however, to cite the specific subsection of CCP § 1209(a) that has been violated to pass constitutional muster. [Moore, at p. 457] (individual charged with contempt admitted at contempt trial his understanding of conduct for which he was being held to account and conducted his defense in conformity with that awareness; court rejected his contention that he would have conducted his defense differently if charging documents had been more artfully drawn).”  (Cal. Judges Benchbook Civ. Proc. Trial § 17.34, certain internal citations omitted.) ¿¿¿

“Taking judicial notice of a document is not the same as accepting the truth of its contents or accepting a particular interpretation of its meaning.” (Joslin v. H.A.S. Ins. Brokerage (1986) 184 Cal.App.3d 369, 374.) “When judicial notice is taken of a document, ... the truthfulness and proper interpretation of the document are disputable.” (StorMedia, Inc. v. Superior Court (1999) 20 Cal.4th 449, 457, fn. 9.) Additionally, “A court may take judicial notice of something that cannot reasonably be controverted, even if it negates an express allegation of the pleading.” (Poseidon Dev., Inc. v. Woodland Lane Ests., LLC (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 1106, 1117 [judicial notice is proper when the parties dispute only the legal effect of a document, not its existence]; Herrera v. Deutsche Bank Nat'l Tr. Co. (2011) 196 Cal.App.4th 1366, 1375 [“While courts take judicial notice of public records, they do not take notice of the truth of matters stated therein. (Citation.)”].)

The Court grants judicial notice as to the existence of such documents, but not to the truth of the matter asserted in them.  

IV.             DISCUSSION

Carter Defendants move for an order (1) Enforcing the Petition Court’s July 11, 2025 order disqualifying attorney Nick A. Alden and the Law Offices of Nick A. Alden as Plaintiffs’ counsel in this action, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure § 1209(a)(5), as the Complaint filed in this action is a direct continuation of the prior Petition action; (2) To Show Cause why Nick A. Alden and the Law Offices of Nick A. Alden should not be held in contempt of court under Code of Civil Procedure § 1209(a)(4) and (5) for his willful disobedience of the prior lawful court orders disqualifying him as counsel for the Romero Plaintiffs; (3) To Show Cause why monetary sanctions should not be imposed against Nick A. Alden and the Law Offices of Nick A. Alden, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure § 1218 and § 177.5 for Alden’s ongoing and intentional violation of three lawful court orders, without good cause or substantial justification, including payment of the reasonable attorney’s fees and costs incurred in bringing and arguing this motion and statutory fees in accordance with section 177.5; and (4) That Alden be reported to the State Bar for disciplinary action for his willful and contemptuous disobedience of the prior lawful court orders disqualifying him as counsel of record for the Romeros related to the subject matter at issue in this case, without good cause or substantial justification.

Carter Defendants move for this relief on the grounds that Alden has been previously disqualified as counsel for Plaintiffs in the prior Petition Action and the instant action is a mere continuation of that proceeding and arises from the same conduct related to the same underlying dispute at issue herein and involves the same parties (and their attorneys). Additionally, Carter Defendants provide that Alden was previously disqualified as counsel for the Romero Plaintiffs by the underlying Quiet Title court, which was affirmed by the Appellate Court, Second District.

i.                    Quiet Title Action

On November 12, 2020, RJSI filed a quiet title action against Potrillos, Romero and The Trust (LASC Case No. 20STCV43490) seeking to establish the enforceability of a mortgage and for quiet title of The Nicholson Property (“Quiet Title Action”). (Millea Decl., 5, RJN, Ex. 1.)

On August 3, 2021, RJSI’s in-house counsel Richard D. Marks (“Marks”) inadvertently forwarded to Alden a privileged email authored by Defendant McClure. Within two hours, Marks sent a follow-up email identifying the inadvertent disclosure, explaining the email was privileged, and demanding Alden delete it and refrain from reading or using it. (Millea Decl., ¶ 6.) But Alden refused. He not only read the privileged email and retained it but, thereafter, he repeatedly used, disseminated and relied on its contents by attaching and quoting from it in multiple court filings and even went so far as to read from it in open court during a September 30, 2021 hearing. (Id. ¶ 7.)

On November 5, 2021, the Quiet Title court granted RJSI’ motion disqualifying Alden. (Id. ¶ 9, RJN, Ex. 2.) The Quiet Title court stated:

The email stated that Mr. McClure had reviewed the docket in this case and was not impressed with Mr. Marks's work, since a series of filings had been rejected over a time span of six months. Mr. McClure conveyed his impression of the legal risks to which he feared this had exposed Plaintiff, and expressed his displeasure with those risks. The email closes with a direct client instruction ("as of now our instructions to you, on behalf of your client and the insured") to negotiate a settlement of the case.

2 hours and 19 minutes after this email was forwarded to Mr. Alden, counsel for Plaintiff realized their mistake and sent a follow-up note,identifying the email by precise date and time and explaining that it contained privileged material. Counsel for Plaintiff asked Mr. Alden to delete the email and disregard the contents. Instead, Mr. Alden chose to submit the email as evidence in multiple court filings and to quote from it in open court during a subsequent hearing.

The email Mr. Alden received is clearly and on its face a privileged communication. It contains the legal impressions and analysis of in-house counsel, followed by a direct client instruction to outside counsel. Even a cursory review of the email would lead any reasonable counsel to believe that their ethical obligations under Rule 4.4 had been triggered, with or without any subsequent warning or request from opposing counsel. But in this case, a warning and a request for deletion was provided almost immediately. There could be no doubt that this was a privileged communication, which Mr. Alden was obligated to delete and disregard. Yet he did not.

(RJN, Ex. 2 at p. 3.)

            Alden continued representing the defendants in the Quiet Title Action (Plaintiffs, here) and even filed a first amended cross-complaint. Alden appealed the disqualification order and the Appellate Court affirmed. (Millea Decl., ¶ 12, RJN, Ex. 4.) The Appellate Court concluded:

 . . . that the trial court properly granted RJS’s motion to disqualify the Alden firm. The forwarded e-mail obviously is protected by the attorney-client privilege and/or contains protected attorney work product. It is a communication between Marks and McClure, two attorneys representing RJS. It sets forth McClure’s criticism of Marks’s representation of RJS in this matter. And, McClure gives instructions to Marks to settle the case immediately. Upon receipt, Alden should have immediately notified RJS’s attorneys that he received a confidential communication not intended for him or his clients.

(RJN, Ex. 4 at p. 12.)

            The Appellate Court referred the matter to the State Bar. (Id. at p. 15.)

            Despite being disqualified by the trial court, which was affirmed by the Court of Appeal, Alden continued to file applications, declarations, and motions in the quiet title action as counsel for the Romeros who, at that time, were represented by other counsel, including the Carter Defendants. (Millea Decl., 13, Exs. 3, 8, 9.)

ii.                  Petition Action

On November 13, 2024, Alden filed a petition, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure

section 1714.10, seeking the court’s permission to file the Complaint in the instant action against the Defendant attorneys and RJSI for civil conspiracy. (Mille Decl., ¶ 24, RJN, Ex. 13.)

            On August 12, 2025, the Petition court granted the petition. (Mille Decl., ¶ 25, RJN, Ex. 14.) The Petition court found that Plaintiffs established both that the requirements of Civil Code section 1714.10 do not apply to the allegations at hand and that Defendants’ conduct falls within one of the exceptions delineated in section 1714.10. (Ibid.)

            Prior to granting the petition based on the inapplicability of section 1714.10, the court granted RJS’ motion again disqualifying Alden as attorney for the Romeros. (Mille Decl., ¶ 26.) In its order disqualifying Alden, the court held that “[b]ased on Alden’s egregious conduct and the factual overlap between this action and the Underlying [quiet title] Action, justice would not be served by now allowing Alden to yet again oppose RJS[I]. The fact that these cases were not deemed related is essentially irrelevant.” (RJN, Ex. 15 at p. 3.)

            Despite the court’s disqualification order, Alden continued to represent the Romeros in the Petition action by recording notices of lis pendens on their behalf. (Mille Decl., ¶ 27.)

iii.                Instant Action

Carter Defendants now contend that Alden, who has been disqualified as counsel for Plaintiffs in three underlying judicial proceedings, is continuing to represent Plaintiffs in the instant action, which arise from the same loan and property dispute involving the same parties (and their attorneys). The Court agrees.

The Court acknowledges that the Court granted FRBC, McClure, RJS, RJSI and Gross’ anti-SLAPP. (See Aug. 27, 2026 Order.) In that Order the Court noted that FRBC, McClure, RJS, RJSI and Gross in the Petition action also filed an anti-SLAPP motion, which was denied by the Petition court. (Ibid.) But this Court did not find that FRBC, McClure, RJS, RJSI and Gross filing an anti-SLAPP motion in the instant action to be a “relitigation” of the anti-SLAPP motion in the Petition action. (Ibid.) The Petition Court solely addressed whether Plaintiffs were permitted to file the instant complaint, which alleges a cause of action for conspiracy, while not even addressing the substantive allegations pertaining to the complaint filed in this action. (Ibid.)

However, the Court’s anti-SLAPP holding does not bar this Court from concluding that the underlying disqualification orders, from the Quiet Title Action, the Appellate Court, and the Petition Court, should be enforced. The fact that the Petition Court’s anti-SLAPP holding did not address the substantive allegations in the instant complaint, instead merely holding that that the requirements of Civil Code section 1714.10 do not apply so Plaintiffs were free to file the instant complaint, does not mean that the Petition Action and the instant are not interrelated.

In Militello v. VFARM 1509 (2023) 89 Cal.App.5th 602, 619 the Court of Appeal upheld disqualification in a subsequent action based on counsel's improper use of privileged communications in a separate proceeding, reasoning that “given their past improper use of confidential information allowing them to continue to represent [client] in this case would negatively affect the public's trust in both the scrupulous administration of justice and the integrity of the bar.” (Citation omitted). Here too, the Court finds that Alden’s continued representation of Plaintiffs in the instant action after he “has been provided with, and then used, improperly obtained confidential information would undermine the public's trust in the fair administration of justice and the integrity of the bar.” (Id. at p. 622.)

The facts are documented and undisputed. In the three separate proceedings wherein Alden was disqualified, in the Quiet Title Action, the Appellate Court, and the Petition Court, the parties remained the same, the underlying factual allegations remain the same, specifically concerning Romero’s default on a 2019 loan that was secured by the real properties at issue in this action, and the same attorney was disqualified from representing the same parties (Plaintiffs here).

The Quiet Title court in disqualifying Alden and the Appellate Court in the subsequent appeal of that order both unequivocally found Alden to be in violation of fundamental ethical duties regarding the handling of inadvertent disclosures of obvious attorney- client communications. The Appellate Court, for example, stated “[p]ursuant to well-settled legal authority and the California Rules of Professional Conduct, upon receipt of the e-mail, Alden should have immediately notified Marks, Alden should have deleted the e-mail, and Alden certainly should not have used it against RJS[I]. His ethical violations let the cat out of the bag, so to speak.” (RJN, Ex. 4 at p. 14.) The Appellate Court even referred Alden to the State Bar considering this misconduct. The Petition court then disqualified Alden, “[b]ased on Alden’s egregious conduct and the factual overlap between this action” and the prior two orders in the Quiet Title Action and its subsequent appeal. (Id., Ex. 15 at p. 3.)

The Court agrees and thus disqualifies Alden from representing Plaintiffs in the instant action. (See Great Lakes Construction, Inc. v. Burman (2010) 186 Cal.App.4th 1347, 1355 [“’The important right to counsel of one's choice must yield to ethical considerations that affect the fundamental principles of our judicial process.’ (Citation.)”].)

Even if the Court were to consider Plaintiffs’ opposition, Plaintiffs primarily dispute the validity of the three prior orders, such as arguing that the “the circumstances surrounding the email explain Alden’s good-faith judgment.” (Opp. at p. 6.) The underlying facts that supported each of the three disqualification orders are simply irrelevant. What is before the Court is whether Alden shall be disqualified from continuing to represent Plaintiffs in the instant action after three prior court rulings ordering him to be disqualified from representing Plaintiffs in actions premised on the same facts.

Furthermore, Plaintiffs also contend that the language of the three underlying orders do not reflect an outright prohibition of Aldren representing Plaintiffs in any dispute involving RJSI. (Id. at p. 9.) While Plaintiffs can be correct to an extent, that is simply not what is happening. As explained above, the Quiet Title Action and Petition Action concern the same underlying factual allegations present here.

As for Carter Defendants’ requests for an Order to Show Cause regarding whether Alden should be held in contempt for disobeying prior court orders; for an Order to Show Cause why monetary sanctions should not be imposed against Alden; and for a referral to the State Bar, the Court finds such requests to be premature at this stage.

V.                CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, Carter Defendants’ motion is GRANTED in part. The Court hereby disqualifies attorney Nick A. Alden and the Law Offices of Nick A. Alden as Plaintiffs’ counsel in this action. The Court otherwise DENIES Carter Defendants’ request for an OSC re: Contempt.
