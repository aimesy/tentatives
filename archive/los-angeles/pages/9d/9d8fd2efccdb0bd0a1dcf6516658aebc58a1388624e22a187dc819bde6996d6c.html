MOTION FOR DETERMINATION OF GOOD FAITH SETTLEMENT

 

Motion filed on May 8, 2026.

MOVING PARTY: Defendant/Cross-Complainant/Cross-Defendant Angelina Sanchez

RESPONDING PARTY: Defendants/Cross-Complainants Warner Media, LLC, Cooler Waters Productions, LLC, and Scott Jay Gochis

NOTICE: OK

RELIEF REQUESTED: Determination that the $75,000 settlement between Plaintiff Walter Aviles Linares and Defendant Angelina Sanchez was made in good faith under Code of Civil Procedure sections 877 and 877.6, dismissal of pending pleadings against Sanchez with prejudice, and bar of equitable comparative contribution and partial or comparative indemnity claims against Sanchez.

RULING: Granted.

 

BACKGROUND

On July 31, 2023, Walter Aviles Linares (Plaintiff) filed this action against Scott Jay Gochis (Defendant Gochis), Warner Media, LLC, Cooler Waters Productions, LLC, Angelina Sanchez (Defendant Sanchez), and others, asserting one cause of action for negligence.

 

On August 2, 2024, Defendant Gochis, Warner Media, LLC, and Cooler Waters Productions, LLC (collectively, Warner Defendants) filed a cross-complaint against Defendant Sanchez asserting causes of action for (1) equitable indemnity; (2) contribution; (3) declaratory relief; and (4) apportionment of fault.

 

On August 29, 2025, Defendant Sanchez filed a cross-complaint against Warner Defendants asserting causes of action for (1) indemnification; (2) apportionment of fault; and (3) declaratory relief.

 

On March 31, 2026, Plaintiff executed a Release of All Claims in favor of Defendant Sanchez for $75,000, with payment contingent on a good faith settlement determination. (Zinn-McBride Decl. ¶ 7.)

 

Defendant Sanchez filed the instant motion on May 8, 2026, supported by the declaration of Emily T. Zinn-McBride. Warner Defendants opposed on June 12, 2026, supported by the declarations of Defendant Gochis and Carol Girgis. Defendant Sanchez replied on June 23, 2026.

 

Plaintiff alleges that Defendants, including Defendant Gochis and Defendant Sanchez, owned and operated a vehicle, that Plaintiff was driving a Ford, and that Defendants negligently entrusted, managed, maintained, drove, and operated a vehicle on SR 2 so as to collide with Plaintiff’s vehicle. (Complaint ¶¶ 6-9.) Defendant Sanchez alleges the accident was caused in whole or in part by the negligence of Warner Defendants. (Id. at 3.) Warner Defendants allege Defendant Sanchez was legally responsible in some manner for the events alleged in Plaintiff’s complaint and was responsible in whole or in part for Plaintiff’s alleged injuries. (Id. ¶¶ 7, 11, 13, 17-18.)

 

LEGAL STANDARD

 

A determination by the court that the settlement was made in good faith shall bar any other joint tortfeasor or co-obligor from any further claims against the settling tortfeasor or co-obligor for equitable comparative contribution, or partial or comparative indemnity, based on comparative negligence or comparative fault.”  (C.C.P. §877.6(c).)

 

There is no precise yardstick for measuring “good faith” of a settlement with one of several tortfeasors. But it must harmonize the public policy favoring settlements with the competing public policy favoring equitable sharing of costs among tortfeasors. To accomplish this, the settlement must be within the “reasonable range” (within the “ballpark”) of the settling tortfeasor’s share of liability for the plaintiff’s injuries—taking into consideration the facts and circumstances of the particular case.  (Tech-Bilt, Inc. v. Woodward-Clyde & Associates (1985) 38 Cal. 3d 488, 499.)

 

Whether the settlement was within the “good faith ballpark” is to be evaluated on the basis of information available at the time of settlement, including: (1) a rough approximation of plaintiffs’ total recovery and the settlor’s proportionate liability; (2) the amount paid in settlement; (3) a recognition that a settlor should pay less in settlement than if found liable after a trial; (4) the allocation of the settlement proceeds among plaintiffs; (5) the settlor’s financial condition and insurance policy limits, if any; and (6) evidence of any collusion, fraud, or tortious conduct between the settlor and the plaintiffs aimed at making the non-settling parties pay more than their fair share.  (Id.)

 

“The party asserting lack of good faith (in a settlement) shall have the burden of proof on that issue.”  (C.C.P. §877.6(d).)

 

DISCUSSION

The Court finds the settlement between Plaintiff and Defendant Sanchez was made in good faith.

 

Defendant Sanchez contends she was not physically involved in the collision, made no contact with any party, and stopped after the accident to render assistance and provide contact information as a witness. (Zinn-McBride Decl. ¶ 3.) Warner Defendants rely on Defendant Gochis’s declaration, the police report, and Warner Defendants’ internal incident report to show Defendant Sanchez cut off Defendant Gochis and caused him to swerve into Plaintiff. (Gochis Decl. ¶ 2; Girgis Decl. ¶¶ 2, 5, Exs. A, D.) However, Plaintiff testified that he did not see Defendant Sanchez’s gray vehicle before the accident, first saw it after the accident, and knew why it pulled over only from what Defendant Gochis told him. (Zinn-McBride Decl. ¶ 10, Ex. A at 31:17-32:24.) The police report likewise notes that Plaintiff could not confirm Defendant Gochis’s account, saw only Defendant Gochis’s vehicle enter his lane, and could not positively identify Defendant Sanchez’s vehicle as cutting off Defendant Gochis. (Girgis Decl., Ex. A at 6.) The officer also noted no physical evidence, investigative leads, or independent witnesses to support further follow-up. (Id. at 5.)

 

Plaintiff’s discovery responses identify past medical bills of approximately $528,221, future medical care in an unspecified amount, and general damages including emotional distress, pain and suffering, disfigurement, disability, impaired enjoyment of life, susceptibility to future harm or injury, and shortened life expectancy. (Girgis Decl. ¶¶ 3-4, Exs. B-C.)

 

In light of the disputed liability record, and in recognition that Defendant Sanchez should pay less in settlement than if found liable after trial, the Court finds the $75,000 settlement offered falls within the reasonable range of Defendant Sanchez’s share of liability for Plaintiff’s injuries. Although Defendant Sanchez denies liability, she agreed to settle Plaintiff’s claims against her for $75,000. (Zinn-McBride Decl. ¶ 7.) Defendant Sanchez represents that the settlement is funded by two applicable automobile liability policies, one with limits of $50,000 and one with limits of $25,000. (Mot. at 9.) Warner Defendants acknowledge that Defendant Sanchez offered her maximum insurance policy coverage. (Opp. at 6.) “When the insurance company for a settling defendant pays its total available policy limits, that is very strong evidence of a ‘good faith’ settlement, absent evidence of collusion or grossly inappropriate allocation or apportionment of the settlement proceeds to injure the nonsettling alleged tortfeasors.” (Fisher v. Superior Court (1980) 103 Cal.App.3d 434, 445.) Warner Defendants identify no evidence of collusion, fraud, or tortious conduct aimed at injuring their interests.

 

Warner Defendants separately argue the settlement is deficient because Defendant Sanchez did not allocate the settlement among issues, injuries, and parties. This is a single-plaintiff personal injury action with multiple defendants arising from one motor vehicle collision. The settlement is an all-cash $75,000 settlement resolving Plaintiff’s claims against Defendant Sanchez arising from that collision. “In the typical one-plaintiff, multiple-defendants, personal injury action each tortfeasor is potentially liable for the same injury to the plaintiff. Therefore the full settlement by one defendant will offset a judgment against other tortfeasors; no allocation of the settlement is required.” (Alcal Roofing & Insulation v. Superior Court (1992) 8 Cal.App.4th 1121, 1124.)

 

The Court finds Warner Defendants have not met their burden to show that the settlement between Plaintiff and Defendant Sanchez is so far out of the ballpark as to defeat good faith. The motion is granted.

 

CONCLUSION

Defendant Sanchez’s Motion for Determination of Good Faith Settlement is GRANTED.

DEFENDANT'S MOTION TO COMPEL PLAINTIFF'S FURTHER RESPONSES TO REQUESTS FOR PRODUCTION OF DOCUMENTS, SET TWO, AND FOR MONETARY SANCTIONS

 

Motion filed on June 2, 2026.

MOVING PARTY: Defendant Joel Mosquera

RESPONDING PARTY: Plaintiff Deivi Ramirez Aguila

NOTICE: OK

RELIEF REQUESTED: Order compelling further verified responses and production as to Requests for Production, Set Two, Nos. 1-15, and monetary sanctions of $167.45.

RULING: Continued.

 

BACKGROUND

Defendant filed the instant motion to compel further on June 2, 2026. Plaintiff opposed on June 12, 2026. Defendant replied on June 15, 2026.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

Where respondents object or respond inadequately to discovery requests, a motion lies to compel further responses, as to which respondent has the burden to justify the objections. (Fairmont Ins. Co. v. Superior Court (2000) 22 Cal.4th 245, 255; Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (a) [motion to compel further responses lies “[o]n receipt of a response to an inspection demand….”].) A propounding party's remedy when it deems objections in discovery responses to be without merit is to move for an order compelling further responses. (Catalina Island Yacht Club v. Superior Court (2015) 242 Cal.App.4th 1116, 1127.)

 

A different motion-- to compel compliance as agreed with a request for production of documents-- is based upon a showing that the respondents failed “to permit an inspection ‘in accordance with that party's statement of compliance.’” (Standon Co. v. Superior Court (1990) 225 Cal.App.3d 898, 903. See also Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.320, subd. (a).) “If the party fails to make a production in accordance with its statement of compliance, the opposing party may file another motion to compel seeking compliance.” (Bd. of Registered Nursing v. Superior Court (2021) 59 Cal.App.5th 1011, 1033 [citing Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.320].) A separate motion to compel production of documents as agreed in responses cannot be substituted for a motion to compel further responses. (Standon Co. v. Superior Court (1990) 225 Cal.App.3d 898, 903.)

 

A document response must consist of: 1) An agreement to comply, stating whether the productions or inspection will be allowed “in whole or in part,” and that all documents or things in the possession, custody or control of the respondent, as to which no objection is made, will be included, by the date set for inspection (unless informally extended in writing, or the designated timing is subject to objection); 2) a representation of inability to comply, with a specification of any person believed or known to have possession of documents; or, 3) objections and specification of withheld documents. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2031.210, subd. (a), 2031.220, 2031.270, 2031.280.)

 

Compliance includes all documents or things in the demanded category that are in the “‘possession, custody, or control’” of the responding party, such as from other corporations. (Roche v. Hyde (2020) 51 Cal.App.5th 757, 813 [quoting Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.220].)

 

With regard to document requests, a response expressing an inability to comply shall state that a diligent search and reasonable inquiry was made to locate the items, and the reason for an inability to comply, including that the item never existed, was lost or stolen, was destroyed, or is not in respondent’s possession, along with the identity and address of anyone believed to have the document. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.230.)

 

“Any documents or category of documents produced in response to a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling shall be identified with the specific request number to which the documents respond.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.280, subd. (a).)

 

However, when a party objects to discovery requests but also provides complete responses to the requests, then nothing is left to address in a motion to compel. (American Federation of State, County & Municipal Employees v. Metropolitan Water Dist. (2005) 126 Cal.App.4th 247, 269.)

 

DISCUSSION


The Court has reviewed the motion, opposition, and reply, and will not decide the motion on the merits at this time.

 

Defendant’s moving papers asserted that Plaintiff produced no documents in response to Defendant’s Requests for Production, Set Two, served March 25, 2026, and that each response was either an objection or an unfulfilled promise to comply. (Mot. At 2; Mosquera Decl. ¶ 10-11.)

 

Plaintiff’s counsel declares that Plaintiff served verified responses with documents on or about April 25, 2026, before the instant motion was filed, including documents labeled as responsive to RFP Nos. 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 11, and 12. (Irwin Decl. ¶¶ 5-9.)

 

In reply, Defendant acknowledges Plaintiff’s production and corrects the prior statement, but contends that Plaintiff transmitted approximately 106 unpaginated, un-Bates-stamped pages, that no documents were transmitted for RFP Nos. 1, 2, 5, 7, 10, 13, 14, and 15, and that the production for the remaining requests is still deficient. (Reply at 1-3; Suppl. Mosquera Decl. ¶¶ 3-12.)

 

Plaintiff states that, to the extent clarification is necessary, he is willing to serve supplemental verified responses clarifying whether responsive nonprivileged documents have been produced, whether additional responsive nonprivileged documents exist, whether no responsive documents exist after diligent search and reasonable inquiry, and whether any responsive documents are being withheld on attorney-client privilege or work-product grounds. (Irwin Decl. ¶ 18.)

 

Given this record, the Court finds the better course is to require a focused meet and confer, and submission of a joint statement as to any outstanding requests, before reaching the merits.

 

CONCLUSION

 

The motion is CONTINUED to August 26, 2026,  at 8:30 a.m. in Department F-46.

 

Prior to the continued hearing, the parties are ordered to meet and confer directly, not by email or letter. The meet and confer shall address each request still in dispute, whether Plaintiff will serve supplemental verified responses or further production prior to the continued hearing, any proposed limitations or redactions, and any remaining asserted defects under Code of Civil Procedure sections 2031.220, 2031.230, 2031.240, and 2031.280.

 

After any agreed supplementation, production, narrowing, or withdrawal of disputed issues, the parties shall file a joint statement of remaining issues no later than five court days before the continued hearing. As to each request still in dispute, the joint statement shall identify the request, the operative response, any production made or withheld, Defendant’s position, and Plaintiff’s position.

 

The Court reserves ruling on the merits and on Defendant’s request for monetary sanctions.

DEFENDANTS’ MOTION TO STAY PROCEEDINGS

 

Motion filed on March 17, 2026.

MOVING PARTY: Robert John Fauls III, Flatiron Networks, Inc., and Rob Fauls Holdings, LLC (collectively, Defendants)

RESPONDING PARTY: IT Creations, Inc. (ITC or Plaintiff)

NOTICE: OK

RELIEF REQUESTED: Defendants seek a discretionary stay of this action pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 410.30, 128, subdivision (a)(8), and 187 pending resolution of a first-filed action in Colorado state court.

RULING: Denied.

 

BACKGROUND

 

On September 29, 2025, ITC filed the operative Complaint against Robert John Fauls III (Fauls), Flatiron Networks, Inc. (Flatiron), and Rob Fauls Holdings, LLC (Holdings) asserting causes of action for breach of contract, breach of fiduciary duty, misappropriation of trade secrets, conversion, constructive trust, and unjust enrichment.

 

On November 20, 2025, Defendants filed a motion to compel arbitration and stay proceedings. ITC filed an opposition. On February 26, 2026, the Court denied the motion to compel arbitration on waiver grounds.

 

On March 17, 2026, Defendants filed the instant motion to stay proceedings, supported by a memorandum of points and authorities, the declarations of Saundra K. Wootton and Robert John Fauls III, and a request for judicial notice attaching the Colorado Complaint (Ex. 1), Flatiron’s Verified Motion for Preliminary Injunction in the Colorado Action (Ex. 2), and ITC’s California Complaint (Ex. 3).

 

On May 5, 2026, ITC filed its opposition, supported by the declaration of Michael S. Adler with exhibits and a request for judicial notice attaching the declaration of Robert John Fauls III filed in support of the prior motion to compel arbitration, including the Employee Confidentiality and Ownership Agreement as Exhibit A.

 

On May 11, 2026, Defendants filed a reply.

 

On May 18, 2026, after hearing, the Court continued the motion to June 30, 2026 for limited supplemental briefing. The Court directed Defendants to file the Colorado answer and cross-complaint referenced at oral argument and any other Colorado court record necessary to establish the current procedural posture of that action.

 

On June 15, 2026, Defendants filed a supplemental brief and request for judicial notice attaching Colorado court records. On June 23, 2026, Defendants filed a notice of errata with a corrected supplemental brief. On June 23, 2026, ITC filed a supplemental opposition and supplemental Adler declaration.

 

DISCUSSION

Request for Judicial Notice

 

The parties filed three requests for judicial notice. Defendants’ March 17, 2026 request seeks judicial notice of: Exhibit 1, Flatiron’s Colorado complaint; Exhibit 2, Flatiron’s verified motion for preliminary injunction in the Colorado action; and Exhibit 3, ITC’s complaint in this action. ITC’s May 5, 2026 request seeks judicial notice of Exhibit 1, the declaration of Robert J. Fauls III filed in support of Defendants’ prior motion to compel arbitration, including the Employee Confidentiality and Ownership Agreement. Defendants’ June 15, 2026 supplemental request seeks judicial notice of: Exhibit 4, ITC’s Colorado answer and counterclaims; Exhibit 5, ITC’s amended Colorado answer and counterclaims; Exhibit 6, Flatiron’s answer to ITC’s amended Colorado counterclaims; Exhibit 7, the Colorado case schedule; and Exhibit 8, the Colorado register of actions and party information.

 

The requests are granted under Evidence Code section 452, subdivisions (d) and (h), as to the existence, filing, and contents of the referenced court records and records capable of accurate determination. The Court does not take judicial notice of the truth of disputed factual assertions within those records.

 

Discretionary Stay Based on Colorado Action

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 410.30, subdivision (a) provides that “[w]hen a court upon motion of a party or its own motion finds that in the interest of substantial justice an action should be heard in a forum outside this state, the court shall stay or dismiss the action in whole or in part on any conditions that may be just.” The Court also has inherent power to stay proceedings in the interest of justice and to promote judicial efficiency. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 128, subd. (a)(8), 187.)

 

 “When an action is brought in a court of this state involving the same parties and the same subject matter as an action already pending in a court of another jurisdiction, a stay of the California proceedings is not a matter of right, but within the sound discretion of the trial court.” (Farmland Irrigation Co. v. Dopplmaier (1957) 48 Cal.2d 208, 215.) Defendants bear the burden. (Stangvik v. Shiley Inc. (1991) 54 Cal.3d 744, 751.)

 

The analysis is affected by the parties’ forum-selection language. A section 410.30 motion may be used to enforce a contractual forum-selection clause. (Grove v. Juul Labs, Inc. (2022) 77 Cal.App.5th 1081, 1090.) A mandatory forum-selection clause is generally given effect unless enforcement would be unfair or unreasonable. (Richtek USA, Inc. v. uPI Semiconductor Corp. (2015) 242 Cal.App.4th 651, 661; Berg v. MTC Electronics Technologies (1998) 61 Cal.App.4th 349, 358-359; Cal-State Business Products & Services, Inc. v. Ricoh (1993) 12 Cal.App.4th 1666, 1679.) Such a clause is not rendered unreasonable merely because litigating in the chosen forum may be inconvenient or more expensive, particularly where any such inconvenience was contemplated when the parties contracted. (Smith, Valentino & Smith, Inc. v. Superior Court (1976) 17 Cal.3d 491, 496; CQL Original Products, Inc. v. National Hockey League Players' Assn. (1995) 39 Cal.App.4th 1347, 1354; Intershop Communications v. Superior Court (2002) 104 Cal.App.4th 191, 199-200.)

 

Here, the Employee Confidentiality and Ownership Agreement provides: “This Agreement shall be governed by the laws of the State of California, without regard to California’s conflicts of law rules. To the extent that any lawsuit is permitted under this Agreement, the Parties hereby expressly consent to the personal and exclusive jurisdiction and venue of the state and federal courts located in California.” (Pl.’s RJN, Ex. 1, Ex. A, § 14.A.)

 

The clause is mandatory. In Berg, upon which Defendants rely, the clause stated only that the company submitted to California jurisdiction and did not expressly make California the exclusive forum. (Berg, supra, 61 Cal.App.4th at pp. 357-360.) Here, the Agreement uses express exclusive-jurisdiction and venue language.

 

The phrase “[t]o the extent that any lawsuit is permitted under this Agreement” does not make California optional and does not authorize litigation in Colorado. Read as a whole, the Agreement makes arbitration the ordinary remedy for claims subject to arbitration, permits court petitions for injunctive relief involving intellectual property, confidential information, or noninterference, and separately contemplates court actions at law or equity to enforce or interpret the Agreement. (Pl.’s RJN, Ex. 1, Ex. A, §§ 13.C, 13.D, 14.H.) The Court previously denied Defendants’ request to compel arbitration. Defendants seek a discretionary stay of this California action in favor of the Colorado action. The Agreement’s California forum language weighs against that relief.

 

Defendants characterize the Colorado claim as falling outside the clause because it asserts an independent tortious-interference claim arising from preexisting Flatiron relationships rather than the claims at issue here. The Court is not persuaded. Torts related to the parties' contractual relationship fall within the scope of a forum-selection clause. (See Smith, Valentino & Smith, Inc. v. Superior Court (1976) 17 Cal.3d 491, 497.) As the Court found in denying Defendants' motion to compel arbitration, the Colorado and California actions arise from the same post-separation dispute over alleged misuse of confidential and trade secret information, which the Agreement governs, including through its noninterference provision and the corresponding carve-out permitting court petitions for noninterference relief. (Pl.'s RJN, Ex. 1, Ex. A, §§ 11, 13.D.) Whether the Colorado claim relates to the Agreement, and so falls within section 14.A, is distinct from whether the Colorado action is identical to this one or would fully resolve it — a separate question addressed below. The Colorado tortious-interference claim is sufficiently related to the Agreement to fall within section 14.A.

 

Defendants argue the forum-selection clause does not control because Flatiron and Holdings are not signatories, Flatiron disputes whether it is bound by the Agreement, and Defendants seek a comity-based stay rather than dismissal or transfer. But a forum-selection clause may apply to nonparties who are closely related to the contractual relationship. (Bugna v. Fike (2000) 80 Cal.App.4th 229, 233-235; Net2Phone, Inc. v. Superior Court (2003) 109 Cal.App.4th 583, 588-589.) In Bugna, the Court explained: “The key to the closely related test is whether the nonsignatories were close to the contractual relationship, not whether they were close to the third party signator.” (Bugna, supra, 80 Cal.App.4th at p. 235.)

 

Here, ITC alleges Flatiron became a party to the relevant agreements by agreeing to employ Fauls on behalf of ITC, with Fauls to provide the same services to ITC on the same basis as before. (Complaint ¶ 27.) ITC also alleges Fauls operated Flatiron and Holdings without appropriate separateness, and that Fauls is the alter ego of each entity. (Complaint ¶ 8.) Defendants themselves seek a stay based on the asserted overlap among Fauls, Flatiron, the Colorado action, and the claims in this action. Flatiron’s and Holdings’s non-signatory status does not defeat the California forum-selection language or show that this action should yield to Colorado.

 

Defendants have not shown that enforcing the California forum-selection language would be unfair or unreasonable. Their principal arguments are that the Colorado action was filed first, the Colorado action is set for trial earlier, and ITC has now asserted overlapping counterclaims in Colorado. Those circumstances do not establish that California is an unfair or unreasonable forum.

 

Nor does the timing show that ITC chose parallel litigation. ITC filed this action on September 29, 2025 without knowing Flatiron had filed the Colorado action and before ITC had been served. (Adler Decl. ¶ 4.) The Colorado complaint was filed four days after the September 15, 2025 legal-hold communications that form the basis for Flatiron’s tortious-interference claim. (Adler Decl. ¶ 3, Ex. 2; Defs.’ RJN, Ex. 1, ¶ 22.)

 

By contrast, ITC is a California corporation with its principal place of business in Los Angeles County. (Complaint ¶ 3.) The Agreement contains both California choice-of-law and California forum-selection language. The claims in this action arise from Fauls’s alleged employment and consulting relationship with ITC, alleged misuse of ITC’s confidential and trade secret information, alleged diversion of ITC business, and alleged retention of ITC property. (Complaint ¶¶ 10-25.)

 

Nor do ITC’s Colorado counterclaims change the result. ITC states that it filed the Colorado counterclaims defensively and compulsorily to preserve its rights against Flatiron under Colorado Rule of Civil Procedure 13(a) while this Court was considering the present motion. (Adler Supp. Decl. ¶ 2.) ITC’s responsive filing in Colorado does not establish that ITC selected Colorado as the preferred forum or that California should yield despite the Agreement’s California forum-selection language.

 

The parties also are not identical. The Colorado action currently has Flatiron and ITC as parties. This action has ITC, Fauls, Flatiron, and Holdings. Although ITC’s Colorado counterclaims allege conduct by Fauls and Flatiron, Fauls and Holdings are not currently named counter-defendants in the Colorado action. Defendants have not shown that any Colorado judgment would fully determine ITC’s claims in this action against all Defendants or afford complete relief against Fauls and Holdings. (See Farmland, supra, 48 Cal.2d at pp. 215-216.)

 

Defendants’ reliance on Simmons v. Superior Court (1950) 96 Cal.App.2d 119 is misplaced. In Simmons, the Texas action was “determinative of all issues included in the California action,” both parties were Texas residents, and the Texas court had appointed a receiver giving the action in rem characteristics. (Id. at pp. 127-130.) Those circumstances are not present here.

 

Defendants have not shown that convenience independently supports a stay. The motion asserts that most third-party witnesses reside in Colorado, but the supporting declarations do not identify those witnesses, their locations, their expected testimony, or whether compulsory process would be unavailable in California. (Mot. at p. 2; Fauls Decl. ¶¶ 7-14; Wootton Decl. ¶¶ 5-12.) Fauls’s Colorado residence and Defendants’ general reference to Colorado witnesses are insufficient.

 

Considering the California forum-selection language, Defendants’ failure to show that enforcing the California forum would be unfair or unreasonable, the defensive posture of ITC’s Colorado counterclaims, the non-identical parties, and Defendants’ limited convenience showing, Defendants have not shown that this action should be stayed in favor of the Colorado action.

 

CONCLUSION

 

Defendants’ motion to stay proceedings is denied.
