DEPARTMENT 12 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS



Case Number: 23STCV31192    Hearing Date: July 13, 2026    Dept: 12

SUPERIOR COURT OF THE STATE OF CALIFORNIA

FOR THE COUNTY OF LOS ANGELES

 

Sibrel v. The Misfit Restaurant, LLC, et al.

23STCV31192

 

Dept. 12 SSC

Hon. Carolyn B. Kuhl

Date of Hearing: July 13, 2026

 

Plaintiff Jacquelyn N. Sibrel’s Motion for Class Certification

 

Tentative Ruling:  The unopposed Motion is granted.  The court certifies the following class with respect to claims based on (1) Defendant’s alleged failure to provide compliant meal periods, (2) Defendant’s alleged failure to provide compliant rest breaks, and (3) Defendant’s alleged failure to pay for off-the-clock work:

All persons who worked for The Misfit Restaurant, LLC as hourly paid and/or non-exempt employees in the State of California at any time from December 20, 2019 through the date of this order granting class certification.

 

 

Plaintiff filed this putative class action on December 20, 2023, alleging the following ten causes of action: (1) Failure to Pay Overtime Wages; (2) Failure to Pay Minimum Wages; (3) Failure to Provide Compliant Meal Periods/Premiums; (4) Failure to Provide Compliant Rest Periods/Premiums; (5) Failure to Pay Wages During Employment; (6) Failure to Timely Pay Wages Upon Termination; (7) Failure to Provide Compliant Wage Statements; (8) Failure to Maintain Requisite Payroll Records; (9) Failure to Reimburse Necessary Business Expenses; and (10) UCL.  On June 26, 2025, Plaintiff filed the First Amended Complaint, which added a PAGA cause of action.

 

        On August 22, 2025, Plaintiff filed the instant Motion.  Plaintiff moves to certify a class of “all persons who worked for The Misfit Restaurant, LLC (‘Defendant’) as hourly paid and/or non-exempt employees in the State of California at any time from December 20, 2019 through the date of the order granting class certification … .”  (Pl’s Not. Mot., at p. 2.)  Plaintiff seeks to certify this class in order to litigate (1) meal break claims, (2) rest break claims, (3) claims for unpaid wages for off-the-clock work, and (4) claims that are derivative of the first three types of claims.  Because Defendant operates a single restaurant location in Santa Monica, California, the class is limited to employees working that that restaurant.  (See Pl’s Ex. 12, at p. 6.) 

 

        On March 23, 2026, the court held a further status conference regarding Plaintiff’s Motion.  Defense counsel appeared at the status conference, but Plaintiff’s counsel did not appear.  The court set the Motion for hearing on July 13, 2026.  The court’s Minute Order states: “The Motion will be unopposed.”  (Minute Order, Mar. 23, 2026.) 

 

Defendant has not filed any opposition to the Motion.

 

Discussion

 

Plaintiff has adequately demonstrated that class certification is appropriate. 

 

The class, as defined, is sufficiently numerous and ascertainable.  The class is defined in terms of objective criteria, especially because the class covers employees of just a single restaurant.  And Plaintiff has already determined from Defendant’s discovery that there are well over 200 putative class members.  (Pl’s Ex. 12, at p. 3.)  Furthermore, Defendant has records of the names of its current and former employees. 

 

Plaintiff has also presented sufficient evidence to demonstrate that common questions of law and fact predominate, and that Plaintiff’s own claims are typical of those of the class.  (See Brinker Restaurant Corp. v. Superior Court (2012) 53 Cal.4th 1004, 1021.)  Plaintiff contends that the alleged Labor Code violations occurred with respect to all class members as a result of Defendant’s uniform policies and practices at the restaurant in Santa Monica.  Plaintiff herself attests that her own experiences as an employee were the same as those of the putative class members.  (See Pl’s Ex. 14, ¶¶ 3-20.)

 

With respect to meal period violations, Plaintiffs contends that “Defendant’s time and pay records demonstrate Defendant systematically failed to provide employees compliant meal breaks.”  (Pl’s Mot., at p. 8.)  Plaintiff will attempt to prove liability by relying on her expert’s analysis of Defendant’s records, which suggests a high rate of violations.  (See Pl’s Mot., at p. 8, citing Plaintiff’s expert report.)  Moreover, despite the high rate of violations, Defendant has admitted that it only paid approximately 15 meal period premiums per year during the class period.  (Pl’s Ex. 12, at p. 10.)  It is Plaintiff’s position that Defendant’s records thus raise a classwide presumption that meal breaks were not provided.  (See Donohue v. AMN Services, LLC (2021) 11 Cal.5th 58, 61 [“time records showing noncompliant meal periods raise a rebuttable presumption of meal period violations, including at the summary judgment stage”].)  By failing to oppose the Motion, Defendant has not showed that it would seek to overcome the presumption with individualized evidence.

 

The rest period claims can likewise be certified.  It is Plaintiff’s contention that Defendant had a common practice of failing to provide employees lawful rest breaks.  Plaintiff’s theory of liability is based on (1) the fact that the sole written rest break policy is only found in Defendant’s “Employee Discount Policy,” which explains that employees are entitled to a discount on food prepared at the restaurant.  (Pl’s Ex. 8.)  Plaintiff thus contends that class members were not made aware of their right to take compliant rest breaks.  Plaintiff also argues that the written rest break policy found in the “Employee Discount Policy” is improper on a classwide basis because it “place[s] the onus on employees to affirmatively request rest breaks.”  (Pl’s Mot., at p. 12, citing Pl’s Ex. 8 [“It is the employee’s responsibility to communicate their need for a rest break”].)  Plaintiff attests that she and the other putative class members “were almost always too busy to take rest breaks due to the volume of work. It was part of the workplace culture not to take rest breaks, and there was pressure to continue working rather than step away.”  (Pl’s Ex. 14, ¶ 18.)  Other employees have offered the same testimony regarding the classwide failure to provide compliant rest breaks.  (Pl’s Ex. 15, ¶ 5.)  Plaintiff could thus show that Defendant’s restaurant-wide practices led to the failure to provide compliant rest breaks.  Furthermore, Defendant has admitted that it did not pay putative class members rest break premiums.  (Pl’s Ex. 12, at p. 10.)  Lastly, by failing to oppose the Motion, Defendant has not identified how any individualized issues might arise concerning the alleged failure to provide rest breaks. 

 

Plaintiff has also shown that the theory of liability based on off-the-clock work can be adjudicated on a classwide basis.  Plaintiff, relying on her expert’s analysis of Defendant’s records, contends that Defendant had a common practice of paying only for “scheduled hours,” and not for actual hours worked.  (Pl’s Mot., at p. 13; Pl’s Ex. 2, ¶¶ 22-25.)  Plaintiff’s expert concludes from the records that “payments were based on shift start and end times as opposed to the employees’ actual time punches,” and that this resulted in a failure to pay for all time worked.  (Pl’s Ex. 2, ¶ 22.)  Plaintiff also relies on declarations by putative class members that could be used to show that Defendant operated the restaurant in a way that routinely caused putative class members to perform off-the-clock work.  (See, e.g., Pl’s Ex. 14, ¶¶ 3-7; Ex. 19, ¶¶ 3-4.)

 

Finally, Plaintiff has shown that she and her counsel can adequately represent the putative class.  Plaintiff’s counsel has substantial experience litigating class actions.  (Pl’s Ex. 1, ¶¶ 22-33.)  And Plaintiff has shown that she is willing and able to litigate on behalf of other non-exempt employees.  (Pl’s Ex. 1, ¶ 36.)


Case Number: 25STCV31025    Hearing Date: July 13, 2026    Dept: 12

SUPERIOR COURT OF THE STATE OF CALIFORNIA

FOR THE COUNTY OF LOS ANGELES

 

Orozco v. Sysco Los Angeles, Inc.

25STCV31025

 

Dept. 12 SSC

Hon. Carolyn B. Kuhl

Date of Hearing: July 13, 2026

 

Defendant’s Motion to Stay Proceedings Pending Related Ramirez Action

 

Tentative Ruling:  The Motion is granted.  The court stays the class claims in this action.  However, Plaintiff may proceed with his individual claims if he so desires.

 

 

        This wage-and-hour putative class action was filed on October 22, 2025.  Plaintiff alleges claims for overtime, meal and rest break premiums, wages, and unpaid sick leave, was well as derivative causes of action.  Plaintiff seeks to represent “[a]ll current and former hourly-paid or non-exempt employees who worked for any of the Defendants within the State of California at any time during the period from four years preceding the filing of this Complaint to final judgment.”  (Compl., ¶ 13.)

 

This action is related to two other wage-and-hour actions brought against Defendant.  One of the related cases is Ramirez v. Sysco Los Angeles, Inc., Case No. 23STCV06356 (Ramirez action).  The Ramirez action was filed by Sergio Ramirez (Ramirez) on March 21, 2023.  On January 17, 2024, Ramirez and Gabriel Arteaga (Arteaga) filed the Second Amended Complaint, alleging the following nine causes of action: (1) Failure to Pay Minimum and Regular Rate Wages; (2) Failure to Pay Overtime Compensation; (3) Failure to Provide Meal Periods; (4) Failure to Provide Rest Periods;(5) Failure to Indemnify Necessary Expenditures; (6) Failure to Provide Accurate Itemized Wage Statements; (7) Failure to Pay Wages at Discharge or Quitting; (8) UCL; and (9) PAGA.  Ramirez and Arteaga sought to represent a class of all non-exempt employees of Defendant who were employed any time after March 2019.

 

In the Ramirez action, this court did not grant the plaintiffs’ certification motion in its entirety.  The plaintiffs in the Ramirez action originally sought certification of multiple claims (meal and rest breaks, minimum and overtime wages, compliant wage statements, reimbursement of expenses, and failure to timely pay wages) as to a class of all of Defendant’s non-exempt employees.  (Ruling, Dec. 30, 2025 in Ramirez action, at p. 2.)  On December 30, 2025, this court certified the following class “with respect to Plaintiffs’ theory of liability that Defendant failed to properly compensate employees at the Walnut, California location for time spent after their shifts going through the ‘bag check’ or ‘security screening’:

 

All persons who worked for Defendant at its Walnut, California location as non-exempt employees at any time between March 2020 and mid-2022 when Defendant was imposing its “bag check” or “security screening.”

 

(Ruling, Dec. 30, 2025 in Ramirez action, at p. 1.)  The court also certified a “reimbursement class” solely for the purposes of seeking relief under the fifth cause of action:

 

All persons who worked for Defendant in California as nonexempt employees in the position of “driver” at any time during the period beginning on August 20, 2023, and ending on the date of this Order.

 

(Ruling, Dec. 30, 2025 in Ramirez action, at p. 1.)  The certified reimbursement claim for drivers is limited to costs for drivers’ uniforms. 

 

        This action and the Ramirez action are also related to Salas v. Sysco Los Angeles, Inc., Case No. 25STCV11307 (Salas action).  The plaintiff in the Salas action asserts the following six causes of action on behalf of all “truck drivers” employed by Defendant from May 2022 to the present: (1) Meal Period Violations; (2) Rest Period Violations; (3) Failure to Reimburse Expenses; (4) Wage Statement Violations; (5) UCL; and (6) Waiting Time Penalties.

 

On February 17, 2026, Defendant moved to stay the Salas action pending the Ramirez action.  The motion to stay the Salas action was made on the grounds that compelling judicial and party efficiencies would be realized by a stay order because the Ramirez action (1) alleges virtually identical wage and hour violations on behalf of the same group of allegedly aggrieved employees against the same defendant, (2) arises from the same facts and theories of liability, and (3) covers overlapping time periods, with the Salas action’s period being subsumed entirely within the Ramirez action period.

 

On June 2, 2026, this court granted Defendant’s motion to stay the Salas action (Salas Stay Ruling).  (Minute Order, June 2, 2026.)  The court noted that the plaintiff in the Salas action did “not suggest that there [were] any facts on which he could base a request for certification of [new claims for unpaid overtime] that [were] different from those presented by Ramirez in an unsuccessful attempt to certify a class to seek recovery for unpaid meal and rest breaks.”  (Salas Stay Ruling, at p. 4.)  The court reasoned that it would be improper to force Defendant to defend against repeated attempts to certify a class of its employees after prior attempts failed, at the same time that Defendant continued to litigate certified class claims in the Ramirez action.  (Salas Stay Ruling, at pp. 4-5.)  The court stated:

 

The class claims [the plaintiff in Salas] seeks to litigate in his Complaint overlap completely with, or are a subset of, claims for which class certification was unsuccessfully sought in [the Ramirez action]. Plaintiff in this case does not suggest factual theories for certifying a class of truck drivers that were not presented to the court in [the Ramirez action]. Allowing duplicative class discovery and duplicative briefing to move forward in [the Salas action] is inconsistent with this court’s responsibilities for reducing litigation costs and moving cases toward resolution in complex cases. (See California Rules of Court, Rule 3.400.) The stay extends only to Plaintiff Salas’s class claims. He may pursue his individual claims.

 

(Salas Stay Ruling, at p. 5.)

 

        Defendant now moves to stay this action pending the Ramirez action.  The Motion is made on the grounds that compelling judicial and party efficiencies will be realized by entry of a stay because the Ramirez action: (1) alleges virtually identical wage and hour violations on behalf of the same group of allegedly aggrieved employees against the same Defendant; (2) arises from the same factual allegations and theories of liability; and (3) covers overlapping time periods, with the time period alleged in this action wholly subsumed within the period at issue in the Ramirez Action.  If the court is not inclined to stay this action, Defendant requests, in the alternative, that this action and the Ramirez action be coordinated “to avoid duplicative discovery, conserve judicial and party resources, and prevent inconsistent rulings.”  (Def’s Not. Mot., at p. 2.)

 

Discussion

 

“[A] court ordinarily has inherent power, in its discretion, to stay proceedings when such a stay will accommodate the ends of justice.”  (People v. Bell (1984) 159 Cal.App.3d 323, 329.)  “[T]he power to stay proceedings is incidental to the power inherent in every court to control the disposition of the causes on its docket with economy of time and effort for itself, for counsel, and for litigants.”  (OTO, L.L.C. v. Kho (2019) 8 Cal. 5th 111, 141.) 

 

        As it did in the Salas Stay Ruling, this court once again exercises its discretion to stay the action pending the Ramirez action.  As Defendant demonstrates, both the time period and all claims except for one (Plaintiff’s claim for failure to provide paid sick leave) are fully encompassed by the complaint and motion for class certification in the Ramirez action.  The plaintiffs in the Ramirez action took extensive class discovery and presented a motion for class certification of essentially the same claims alleged in this action.   

 

Plaintiff argues that it would be improper to stay this action “before Plaintiff has had any opportunity to seek certification based on different evidence, different subclasses, different theories of common proof, and a different litigation record than Ramirez.”  (Pl’s Opp., at p. 1.)  However, Plaintiff has failed to identify any such different evidence and theories.  Once again, this court sees no reason why Defendant should be required to oppose numerous class certification motions seeking to certify essentially identical claims while it attempts to litigate the merits of a certified class’s claims. 

 

This is not to say, of course, that this court’s certification ruling in the Ramirez action has preclusive effect and “binds” Plaintiff in this action.  (See Pl’s Opp., at p. 6.)  By staying this action pending the Ramirez action’s proceedings, this court is not dismissing Plaintiff’s class claims.  The United States Supreme Court, in an opinion relied upon by Plaintiff here, has explained that, in order to avoid a situation where a defendant is subjected to “serial relitigation of class certification,” courts can rely “on principles of stare decisis and comity among courts to mitigate the sometimes substantial costs of similar litigation brought by different plaintiffs.”  (Smith v. Bayer Corp. (2011) 564 U.S. 299, 316-317, italics in original.)  Here, allowing duplicative class discovery and duplicative briefing to move forward in this action while the class claims in the Ramirez action are litigated is inconsistent with this court’s responsibilities for reducing litigation costs and moving cases toward resolution in complex cases.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.400.)
