Tentative Ruling

Ryden L. Loong v. Edmund G. Sarraf, M.D., et al., Case No. 24SMCV05452

Unilab Corporation’s Motion for Leave to Conduct Two additional physical medical examinations.

Hearing Date: July 14, 2026

Background

Plaintiff Ryden L. Loong, a minor, by and through his guardian ad litem, Sepideh Loong, sues Defendants Edmund G. Sarraf, M.D.; Unilab Corporation d/b/a Quest Diagnostics Clinical Laboratories, Inc. (“Quest”); The Southern California Integrative Wellness Center; The Regents of the University of California (“The Regents”); and UCLA Medical Center for medical malpractice. Loong alleges he developed bilateral sensorineural hearing loss and mobility impairments as a result of Auditory Neuropathy Spectrum Disorder (ANSD), caused by hyperbilirubinemia due to Quest’s errors processing his blood tests and the other Defendants’ delays in providing appropriate treatment. (Complaint, ¶

On November 7, 2024, Loong filed his complaint.

On February 6, 2025 Quest, Sarraf, and Southern California Integrative Wellness Center filed their answers. On February 24, 2025, The Regents answered.

On February 24, 2025, Loong dismissed UCLA Medical Center on the grounds that it is an entity subsumed by The Regents.

On January 8, 2026, the parties filed a stipulated protective order.

On May 20, 2026, The Regents filed a motion for summary judgment.

On May 21, 2026, the parties stipulated to continue trial and all corresponding deadlines to September 14, 2026.

On June 4, 2026, Quest filed this motion for leave to conduct two additional physical examinations: a genetics examination and a neurotology exam with an auditory brainstem response test. On June 5, 2026, The Regents joined Quest’s motion. On June 30, 2026, Plaintiffs filed opposition. On July 7, 2026, Quest replied.

Arguments

Quest moves for a court order authorizing two independent medical examinations in addition to the neurological independent medical examination the parties stipulated to. Quest argues that good cause exists for a genetic examination because Loong’s medical records show that he was born with microcephaly and a G6PD deficiency, a hereditary enzymatic defect that may have contributed to or caused Loong’s hearing loss. (Id. at p. 9.) Quest argues that a genetic examination is necessary to determine if Loong’s hearing loss could have a genetic factor. (Id. at p. 10.) Quest then argues that good cause also exists for an auditory brainstem response examination to confirm Loong’s diagnosis and evaluate his damages. (Id. at p. 11.)

In opposition, Loong argues that Quest does not demonstrate good cause in light of Plaintiff’s statutory and constitutional privacy rights. (Opposition, p. 4.) Loong argues that Quest’s experts have not properly laid the foundation for their opinions. (Id. at p. 6.) Loong also argues that the “possibility” of an underlying genetic condition is insufficient to justify an additional medical exam. (Ibid.) Loong argues that Quest’s request for a genetic test is too vague and opens the door for the defense to collect protected genetic material for unspecified testing. (Id. at p. 7.) Loong argues that the identified indicators for genetic testing do not necessarily lead to the conclusion that there is a genetic issue—Loong’s microcephaly appeared to resolve after birth, and his G6PD deficiency is very common and does not always cause hearing loss. (Id. at p. 8). Loong also argues that a G6PD deficiency is incredibly common, and most effected individuals have not been diagnosed with ANSD. (Id. at p. 10.) Finally, Loong argues that all of the tests that Quest requests have already been conducted by others fairly recently. (Id. at p. 11.)

In reply, Quest argues that Loong’s arguments confirm that these issues are factual and should be resolved through cross-examination at trial. (Reply, p. 3.) Quest argues that hyperbilirubinemia is not the only recognized cause of hearing loss, and that other conditions, many genetic, account for 40% of cases. (Id. at p. 4.) Quest argues that it will preserve Loong’s privacy and that Loong has placed his genetic status in controversy because his G6PD deficiency is undisputedly hereditary. (Id. at p. 5.) Quest argues that the eggshell plaintiff doctrine is irrelevant and that the neurotologic examination is not duplicative. (Id. at pp. 7-8.)

Evidentiary Objections

Loong objects to the Declaration of Elina Kari, M.D. in its entirety, and specifically to ¶¶ 3-4 on the grounds that Dr. Kari does not properly lay the foundation for her medical opinion. The Court disagrees. Dr. Kari testified that she reviewed Loong’s medical history and the record in this case and based her opinion on that record. (Kari Decl., ¶ 3.) Loong’s objections are overruled.

Loong also objects to the Declaration of Robin Clark, M.D., in its entirety and ¶¶ 3-6 specifically, on the same grounds. Loong’s objections are overruled for the same reason Loong’s objections to the Kari Declaration are overruled. (See Clark Decl., ¶ 3.)

Motion to Conduct Medical Examination

“If any party desires to obtain discovery by a physical examination other than that described in Article 2 (commencing with Section 2032.210), or by a mental examination, the party shall obtain leave of court.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2032.310, subd. (a).) “A motion for an examination under subdivision (a) shall specify the time, place, manner, conditions, scope, and nature of the examination, as well as the identity and the specialty, if any, of the person or persons who will perform the examination. The motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.” (Id. subd. (b).)  

“The court shall grant a motion for a physical or mental examination under Section 2032.310 only for good cause shown.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2032.320, subd. (a).) Section 2032.320 subdivision (b) requires a motion for an IME to specific “the time, place, manner, conditions, scope, and nature of the examination . . . .” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2032.310 subd. (b).) Moreover, An order granting a physical or mental examination shall specify the person or persons who may perform the examination, as well as the time, place, manner, diagnostic tests and procedures, conditions, scope, and nature of the examination.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2032.320, subd. (d).) 

The California Supreme Court has held that a prior, but substantially similar, statute allowing for IMEs “defines a showing of ‘good cause’ as requiring that the party produce specific facts justifying discovery and that the inquiry be relevant to the subject matter of the action or reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence.” (Vinson v. Superior Court (1987) 43 Cal.3d 833, 840.) 

Discussion

The Clark and Keri Declarations specify the time, place, manner, conditions, and scope of the proposed examinations. Dr. Keri proposes to conduct a neurotologic history and physical examination; audiologic evaluation, including behavioral audiometry; ABR testing; and any indicated, non-invasive, audiologic or electrophysiologic measures with Dr. Zettner and Dr. Gibson for 2-4 hours at the University of California San Diego. (Keri Decl., ¶ 6.) Dr. Clark proposes a standard clinical genetics consultation by a board-certified geneticist: a 90 minute to 2 hour medical history, physical and dysmorphology examination, buccal swab for genetic testing, a review of growth records, and any other warranted testing. (Clark Decl., ¶ 6.) The proposed examination would take place at a time and place convenient to Loong.

a.      Good Cause for the neurotology examination

Quest argues that there is good cause for the neurotology examination for two reasons. First, to confirm Loong’s ANSD diagnosis, and second, to evaluate the nature and extent of his damages. (Motion for leave to conduct two physical examinations, pp. 7-8.) Quest’s expert declaration explains that ANSD “is a type of hearing impairment characterized by preserved outer hair cell function (often normal otoacoustic emissions) but abnormal or absent auditory brainstem response (ABR) and poor speech discrimination out of proportion to pure-tone thresholds.” (Kari Decl., ¶ 4.) “A comprehensive neurotologic evaluation, including a detailed history, physical examination of the ears and cranial nerves, and targeted audiologic testing, is the accepted standard of care to confirm or rule out ANSD, quantify the degree and nature of any hearing loss, assess functional impact, and determine whether the condition is stable, progressive, or attributable to the neonatal hyperbilirubinemia alleged in this case.” (Ibid.)

In opposition, Loong only argues that these tests would be duplicative of testing and ABR assessments Loong has undergone at UCLA Medical Center, USC Caruso Family Center for Childhood Communication, Project TALK, and Cedars Sinai Medical Group ENT. (Opposition, p. 4.)

Here, Loong does not dispute that the proposed tests are the standard of care for diagnosing and determining the nature and extent of hearing loss in patients like Loong. In fact, Loong admits that he has undergone extensive evaluation of this type for precisely this purpose. Loong’s complaint is based on his ANSD diagnosis and resulting long-term hearing loss, placing those conditions directly at issue. This type of testing was necessary to make Loong’s initial diagnosis, which is the subject of this litigation. Quest has good cause to evaluate those claims with their own expert examinations.

b.      Good Cause for the Genetic Examination

Quest contends that there is good cause for the genetic examination because Loong was born with microcephaly and a G6PD deficiency, an “X-linked genetic disorder.” Quest’s expert evidence cites that G6PD deficiency can lead to hyperbilirubinemia and Loong’s hearing loss when left untreated. (Clark Decl., ¶ 4.) Specifically, “the presence of microcephaly and SGA at birth raises the distinct possibility of an underlying genetic syndrome, chromosomal microdeletion/duplication, or other constitutional genetic condition that may independently contribute to, or interact with, the hyperbilirubinemia and the claimed long-term neurological injures.” (Ibid.)

Loong correctly points out that he has a constitutional right to genetic privacy that requires a proposed genetic exam to be directly relevant to a claim or defense and essential to a claim’s fair resolution. (Vinson v. Superior Court (1987) 43 Cal.3d 833, 842.) Loong also argues convincingly that his microcephaly resolved soon after birth and that the G6PD deficiency is incredibly common.[1] The Court is particularly concerned about the extent to which the proposed genetic test produces private information that is not relevant to this case in addition to information that is relevant. Though Quest describes the genetic test as minimally stressful, it is not clear if the genetic test they intend to run is a type of test that would reveal substantially more about Loong’s genetic background than is relevant to his hearing loss.[2]

Dr. Clark’s supplemental declaration, however, explains in great detail why this genetic testing is indicated: Loong’s MRI showed abnormalities in brain regions that are not typical sites of injury from hyperbilirubinemia. (Clark Decl., ¶ 17.) Dr. Clark also explains that these tests are the standard of care to evaluate potential genetic conditions in a child with Loong’s symptoms and are “directly responsive” to Loong’s microcephaly, SGA, G6PD, ANSD, and seizures. (Id. at p. 19.) Though this still does not provide additional information about the scope of the testing, it provides very strong evidence that there is good cause for the proposed genetic tests in this case. As such, the Court finds that Quest has shown good cause for genetic testing, but would like to discuss with the parties the scope of the proposed testing to ensure that the testing is limited to conditions which may be relevant to the injuries alleged.  

Conclusion

Quests Motion for Leave to Conduct Two Independent Medical Examinations is GRANTED.  


[1] Loong does not submit expert declarations to support this argument, however.

[2] For example, some types of genetic tests show things like BRCA1 or BRCA2 gene mutations, which are not relevant to this case at all.


Case Number: 25SMCV01427    Hearing Date: July 14, 2026    Dept: P

Tentative Ruling

Phoebe Eskovitz, et al. v. Backroads, Case no. 25SMCV01427

Backroads’ Motion for Stay of Execution

Hearing Date: July 14, 2026

Motion for Stay of Execution

Plaintiffs Phoebe and Katherine Eskovitz sue Defendant Backroads for negligence, alleging that Backroads’ misrepresentations in its marketing materials and understaffing caused Phoebe’s injuries when she fell from her bicycle on a major roadway while on a Backroads bike tour in Mallorca, Spain. (Complaint, ¶ 58.)

On March 20, 2025, Plaintiffs filed the complaint. This case was initially assigned to Judge Jay Ford III in Department O.

On July 1, 2025, Backroads moved to compel arbitration.

On September 11, 2025, Backroads moved for a protective order staying discovery until the motion to compel arbitration was decided. Backroads also separately moved for a discovery stay.

On December 2, 2025, the Court denied Backroads’ motion to compel arbitration, mooting its motion for a protective order and motion to stay.

On December 22, 2025, Backroads appealed the Court’s ruling on its Motion to Compel Arbitration.

On December 31, 2025, Backroads moved to stay this case pending the interlocutory appeal, or for an order staying discovery pending the interlocutory appeal. On April 30, 2026, Plaintiffs filed opposition. On May 6, 2026, Backroads replied.

On January 1, 2026, as part of an inventory transfer, this case was transferred to Judge Bradley S. Phillips in Department O.

On February 3, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a motion to compel further responses to Plaintiff’s form interrogatories, special interrogatories, requests for production of documents and requests for admission.

On May 12, 2026, Judge Bradley S. Phillips recused himself from this case pursuant to Code Civ. Proc. 170.1 and this case was re-assigned to Judge David W. Swift in Department P.

Backroads moves to stay this case pending its interlocutory appeal of the Court’s ruling denying Backroads’ Motion to Compel Arbitration under Code Civ. Proc. §§ 916(a), and 1281.4. Backroads argues that though a stay pending interlocutory appeal is not as of right under California law, the Court should nonetheless stay this matter pending its interlocutory appeal because prior California procedure and federal law stayed actions pending interlocutory appeal. Backroads argues that California’s statute allowing cases to proceed while an interlocutory appeal is pending is preempted by the Federal Arbitration Act because it discriminates against arbitration. (Id. at p. 9-10.)  Backroads argues that a stay will not prejudice plaintiffs. (Id. at p. 11.)

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that a stay would cause them prejudice by further delaying this action and requests sanctions under Code Civ. Proc. § 2025.420(h).

In reply, Backroads argues that its interlocutory appeal is likely to be successful on the merits and that the delays are the result of Plaintiffs’ refusal to arbitrate this dispute.

Statutory Stay

Code Civ. Proc. § 916(a) stays trial court proceedings on matters affected by the appeal. Code Civ. Proc. § 1281.4 stays actions after the Court has ordered arbitration. Backroads acknowledges that there is no stay pending interlocutory appeal as of right but nonetheless requests a stay pursuant to the Court’s power under the Discovery Act and its inherent powers to control the proceedings before it. (Motion to Stay, pp. 5-6.) Until and unless a law is invalidated, it is binding on this Court. Under California law, cases are not stayed pending interlocutory appeal. (Code Civ. Proc. § 1294(a).)

Discretionary Stay

When statutes do not call for an automatic stay pending appeal, the trial court has the power to issue a discretionary stay. (Code civ. Proc. §§ 918, 923.) “The factors regulating the issuance of a stay are generally the same: (1) whether the stay applicant has made a strong showing that he is likely to succeed on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will be irreparably injured absent a stay; (3) whether issuance of the stay will substantially injure the other parties interested in the proceeding; and (4) where the public interest lies.” (Daly v. San Bernardino County Bd. Of Supervisors (2021) 11 Cal.5th 1030, fn. 13, citing Hilton v. Braunskill, (1987) 481 U.S. 770, 776.)

Backroads has not made a showing that it is likely to succeed on appeal. On Backroads’ motion to compel arbitration, Judge Ford found both that Backroads had not provided admissible evidence showing that Katherine Eskovitz signed the arbitration agreement and that the Plaintiffs met their burden to show that the agreement was unconscionable. (Minute Order (12/02/2025), p. 17.) Backroads has also not met its burden to show that it would be harmed if discovery were allowed to progress—Plaintiffs can seek discovery responses both before the Court and before an arbitrator. In addition, Backroads has not shown that a stay would not harm plaintiffs or that a stay is in the public interest. Fifteen months have already passed without meaningful discovery in this matter. As time passes, less evidence is available. Simply put, there is no reason to delay discovery any further in this matter.

Sanctions

Plaintiffs request sanctions under Code Civ. Proc. § 2025.420(h), the mandatory sanctions provision for a party who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion for a protective order, unless the Court finds that the party acted with substantial justification or some other circumstance would make the sanction unjust. Backroads invoked the Discovery Act when moving for a stay. (Notice of Motion, p. 2.; Motion to Stay, p. 6.) Indeed, this motion could easily be interpreted as a motion for a protective order staying discovery pending appeal.

Considering the totality of the circumstances, sanctions are appropriate. Backroads moved to stay this action despite clear California law to the contrary. Further, Backroads argued primarily that this case should be stayed because California law is preempted by the FAA, even after Judge Ford found that there was no enforceable arbitration agreement invoking the FAA. (Minute Order (12/02/2025), p. 15.) As such, the Court awards sanctions in an amount to be determined by supplemental declaration. The Court sets an OSC re discovery sanctions for July 31, 2026, at 9:00 a.m. Plaintiffs’ fee declaration is due by July 17, 2026, Defendants’ opposition is due by July 24, 2026 and any reply papers are due July 29, 2026.

Conclusion

Backroads’ Motion to Stay is DENIED and the Court sets as OSC re discovery sanctions for July 31, 2026 at 9:00 a.m.


Case Number: 25SMCV04299    Hearing Date: July 14, 2026    Dept: P

Tentative Ruling

Security National Insurance Company v. Cox Automotive, Case No. 25SMCV04299

Security National Ins. Co.’s Motion for Leave to Amend to File a Second Amended Complaint

Hearing: July 14, 2026

Background

Plaintiff Security National Insurance Company, as subrogee of its insured Detention Equipment Specialties, Inc. (“Detention Equipment”), sued Cox Automotive and Does 1-25 for negligence and breach of contract (only Doe defendants) arising from the theft of a car. Plaintiff alleges that its insured entered into a contract with defendant to transport a 2024 Cadillac Escalade from Beverly Hills, CA to Carson City, NV. (Complaint, ¶ 8.) Plaintiff alleges that Defendant operates an online platform that connects brokers and car dealers with licensed auto transport carriers. (Complaint, ¶ 6.) Plaintiff claims that its insured contracted with Rodz and Bodz LLC (“R&B”) to transport the vehicle, who posted the job on Defendant’s platform. (Complaint, ¶ 8.) Plaintiff claims that Cruzin’ Auto Carriers (“Cruzin’”) bid and accepted the transportation job. (Complaint, ¶ 9.) Instead of Cruzin,’ Plaintiff alleges that Foundation Transportation picked up the car and transported it to an unknown party. (Complaint, ¶ 10.)

On October 30, 2025, the Court sustained Cox Automotive’s demurrer with leave to amend.

On November 7, 2025, Plaintiff filed the First Amended Complaint (“FAC”).

On April 28, 2026, the Court sustained Cox Automotive’s second demurrer without leave to amend, finding that Plaintiff could not state a claim against Cox Automotive as owner of the online platform because Cox Automotive did not owe it a duty of care.

On June 17, 2026, Plaintiff filed this motion for leave to amend to file a second amended complaint naming Silvertip Automotive Group, Inc. as a defendant. As of July 13, 2026, no opposition has been filed. National Ins. Co. argues that Silvertip had custody and control of the vehicle and released it to the unauthorized carrier. (Motion for Leave to Amend, ¶ 2.)

Motion for Leave to Amend

The trial court has discretion to allow amendments to pleadings in the furtherance of justice. (Code Civ. Proc. § 473, subd. (a)(1).) “Any judge, at any time before or after commencement of trial, in the furtherance of justice, and upon such terms as may be proper, may allow the amendment of any pleading.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 576.) “There is a policy of great liberality in permitting amendments to the pleadings at any stage of the proceeding.” (Berman v. Bromberg (1997) 56 Cal.App.4th 936, 945 [citations omitted].) “An application to amend a pleading is addressed to the trial judge’s sound discretion.”  (Ibid. [citations omitted].) A judge may deny a motion for leave to amend where a moving party has been dilatory in seeking leave to amend and such delay has prejudiced the opposing party. (Hirsa v. Superior Court (1981) 118 Cal.App.3d 486, 490 (“Hirsa”).) “It is difficult to understand how [a named defendant] can be prejudiced by amendment to add an additional theory of liability against it.” (Ibid.) 

“[I]t is an abuse of discretion to deny leave to amend where the opposing party [is] not misled or prejudiced by the amendment.” (Kittredge Sports Co. v. Superior Court (1989) 213 Cal.App.3d 1045, 1048 (“Kittredge”).) Prejudice exists where the amendment would require delaying the trial, resulting in loss of critical evidence, or added costs of preparation. (Solit v. Tokai Bank, Ltd. New York Branch (1999) 68 Cal.App.4th 1435, 1448 (“Solit”).)  

Following a review of the Kirsch Declaration and the attached proposed second amended complaint, the Court finds that Plaintiff has met the procedural and substantive requirements on a Motion for Leave to Amend. This case is not yet at issue; and this motion was brought without significant delay. Absent any opposition showing prejudice to defendants, the Court finds that leave to amend is appropriate.

Conclusion

Plaintiff’s Motion for Leave to Amend is GRANTED.  


Case Number: 25SMCV06019    Hearing Date: July 14, 2026    Dept: P

Tentative Ruling

Mitra Mehdizadeh Nazarian v. Raymond F. Jerozal, Jr., et al., Case No. 25SMCV06019

Motions:

(1)   Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel Responses to Form Interrogatories and Request for Sanctions against Defendant Raymond F. Jerozal, Jr.

(2)   Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel Responses to Special Interrogatories and Request for Sanctions against Defendant Raymond F. Jerozal, Jr.

(3)   Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel Responses to Request for Production of Documents and Request for Sanctions against Defendant Raymond F. Jerozal, Jr.

(4)   Plaintiff’s Motion to Deem Requests for Admissions Admitted against Defendant Raymond F. Jerozal, Jr.

(5)   Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel Responses to Form Interrogatories and Request for Sanctions against Defendant Gilbert Francisco Rodriguez

(6)   Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel Responses to Special Interrogatories and Request for Sanctions against Defendant Gilbert Francisco Rodriguez

(7)   Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel Responses to Request for Production of Documents and Request for Sanctions against Defendant Gilbert Francisco Rodriguez

(8)   Plaintiff’s Motion to Deem Requests for Admissions Admitted against Defendant Gilbert Francisco Rodriguez

Hearing Date: July 14, 2026

Background

Plaintiff Mitra Nazarian (“Nazarian”) sues Defendants Raymond F. Jerozal, Jr., d/b/a Rayco Security Loss Prevention Systems and Gilbert Francisco Rodriguez (“Defendants”) for personal injury arising from a car accident on August 21, 2024, on or near 3rd St. and Oakhurst Dr. in Beverly Hills, CA.

On November 18, 2025, Nazarian filed the complaint.

On December 24, 2025, Defendants answered.

On March 9, 2026, Plaintiff filed these eight motions to compel Defendants to respond to form interrogatories, special interrogatories, requests for production of documents, and to deem RFAs admitted. On June 29, 2026, Defendants filed opposition to these four motions. On July 6, 2027, Plaintiff replied.

Governing Law

If a responding party has not produced any responses to form interrogatories, special interrogatories, requests for production of documents, or requests for admission, they may move to compel the responding party to produce answers, and objections are waived. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.290(a) [interrogatories]; Code Civ. Proc. § 2031.300 [requests for production of documents]; Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280(a) [requests for admission.].) However, when the responding party serves substantially compliant responses before the hearing, the court can still grant the motion (if it finds the responses fail to resolve the issues raised by the motion) and grant sanctions.  (Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pacific Healthcare Consultants¿(2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, 409.) 

California Rules of Court, rule 3.1348(a) provides, “The court may award sanctions under the Discovery Act in favor of a party who files a motion to compel discovery, even though no opposition to the motion was filed, or opposition to the motion was withdrawn, or the requested discovery was provided to the moving party after the motion was filed.”

Analysis

Defendants contend that they responded to the form interrogatories, special interrogatories, requests for production of documents, and requests for admission on March 11, 2026, two days after Nazarian filed these motions, and that the delay was due to their attorney’s family emergency. (Starinieri Decl., ¶ 2.)

In reply, Plaintiff does not contest that Defendants’ responses were substantially compliant but argues that sanctions are still mandatory. (reply, p. 2.)  

The Court has reviewed Plaintiff’s discovery responses and finds them to be substantially compliant. The responses are verified and substantively respond to the questions asked. Plaintiff’s eight motions to compel are MOOT.

Sanctions are mandatory against an unsuccessful moving party or opposing party unless the party’s motion or opposition is substantially justified or the failure to respond was the result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect. (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.290 [interrogatories]; Code Civ. Proc. § 2031.300 [requests for production]; Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280(c) [requests for admission].)

Defendants argue that the delay was due to their attorneys’ family emergency. However, Defendants’ family emergency only arose after their discovery responses were due on February 13. Nazarian repeatedly followed up on the discovery requests. Defendants’ paralegal’s failure to communicate Defendants’ attorney’s family emergency is not excusable neglect.

Nazarian requests $1,860 in sanctions for each of his eight motions: attorneys’ fees for two hours of work preparing each motion, two hours reviewing and replying to each motion, and two hours for travelling to and appearing at the hearing at $300 per hour. (Domingo Decl., ¶ 9.) Plaintiff’s counsel’s rate is reasonable for attorneys in the Los Angeles Area. Two hours of work preparing each motion is reasonable, but two hours to review each opposition and file a reply is excessive given that the oppositions were essentially identical and only a single brief consolidated reply was filed. Accordingly, the Court awards Nazarian sanctions for two hours of work preparing each motion, 2 hours total for reviewing the oppositions and preparing a consolidated reply, plus one hour appearing for the hearing – for a total sanction amount of $6,180 (8 motions at two hours each, plus two hours for consolidated reply; plus 1 hour for hearing, times $300 per hour, plus $60 of filing fees for each motion).

Conclusion

Nazarian’s motions to compel responses are DENIED as moot. Plaintiff is GRANTED $6,180 in sanctions.
