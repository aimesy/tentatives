CASE:                    Surinderpal S. Arora v. Saljeet Singh and Gurpreet Bindra

 

CASE NO.:             18STCV10316

 

MOTION FOR ATTORNEYS’ FEES IN ENFORCING SETTLEMENT AGREEMENT

 

MOVING PARTY:          Defendants Daljeet Singh and Gurpreet Singh

 

RESPONDING PARTY(S): Plaintiff Surinderpal S. Arora

 

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

          Defendants move for attorneys’ fees.  They do so based on an attorneys’ fee provision in an oral settlement agreement that was reached on the record.  The motion is DENIED.

 

          The settlement agreement contains the following relevant terms: “In any dispute between the parties as to the terms of the settlement or enforcement of this settlement agreement, either this oral recitation or a written settlement agreement, the prevailing party will be awarded reasonable attorneys’ fees and costs.  This provision does not apply to the accounting dispute resolution process provided by this agreement.”

 

          Here, the Defendants obtained a mixed verdict on initial cross-motions to enforce the settlement agreement in the trial court.  The trial court declined to award attorneys’ fees in connection with its own ruling.  Plaintiff appealed the denial.  But Plaintiff withdrew the appeal, which was ultimately dismissed by the Court of Appeal, after the Defendants pointed out by motion that the appeal was untimely.  Defendants now seek their fees incurred in connection with the unsuccessful appeal.

 

          The Court denies the motion.  Defendants, as the moving party, have not met their burden of showing that the appeal was covered by the contractual fees provision, and thus that they are entitled to fees under Civil Code section 1717. 

 

Under the parties’ agreement, fees incurred in connection with the “accounting dispute resolution process,” are explicitly not recoverable.  A review of the record reveals that the matters at issue in the July 2025 order that was the subject of the appeal, as well as the Court’s denial of reconsideration of that order (both orders were entered by a different judicial officer) heavily involved the accounting procedure negotiated in the parties’ settlement agreement. 

 

In the instant motion, Plaintiffs try to make a distinction between the ”accounting dispute resolution process” and litigation over the scopre of that accounting process.  Defendants suggest that the phrase “accounting dispute resolution process” meant only “specific challenges to the accountants’ report” (in Defendants’ words) and nothing more.  Thus, according to Defendants, issues involving the scope of the accounting are not part of the “accounting dispute resolution process.”  The oral settlement agreement, however, contains no such limitation.  “[A]ccounting dispute resolution process” is not a defined term.  Neither party has introduced extrinsic evidence as to its meaning.  Accordingly, in interpreting the phrase, the Court applies its plain meaning.  “[A]ccounting dispute resolution process” is, in the Court’s view, clearly a broad enough phrase to encompass disputes over the scope of the accounting.  Litigation over an accounting “process” would ordinarily include litigation over the scope of the accounting—indeed, that would be one of the primary anticipated areas of litigation.  Thus, under the plain terms of the parties’ settlement, attorneys fees are not recoverable for litigation over the scope of the accounting in the parties’ agreement.

 

While the underlying motion to enforce the settlement agreement, and thus the appeal of that motion, may have involved some issues beyond the accounting dispute resolution process, Defendants have made no effort whatsoever to delineate any such matters.  Accordingly, the Court cannot grant the motion for attorneys’ fees even in part.  Defendants have simply not met their burden.

 

CONCLUSION:

 

          The motion for attorneys’ fees is DENIED.

CASE:                    Willis Oliver v. BMW of North America LLC, et al.

 

CASE NO.:             25STCV09520 

 

MOTION FOR JUDGMENT ON THE PLEADINGS

 

MOVING PARTY:          Defendants BMW of North America, LLC and Century West BMW

 

RESPONDING PARTY(S): Plaintiff Willis Oliver.

 

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

Defendants’ Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings is GRANTED as to the fifth cause of action and otherwise DENIED.

 

Oliver shall have thirty (30) days leave to amend.

 

Moving Parties to give notice.

 

BACKGROUND

         

          This is a lemon law action.

 

DISCUSSION:

 

          Defendants move for judgment on the pleadings. The Court grants the motion as to the fifth cause of action for negligent repair only.

 

Legal Standard

 

A motion for judgment on the pleadings is the functional equivalent to a general demurrer.  (Lance Camper Mfg. Corp. v. Republic Indemnity Co. of Am. (1996) 44 Cal.App.4th 194, 198).  Like demurrers, motions for judgment on the pleadings challenge the legal sufficiency of the allegations, not their veracity.  (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994). Any defects must either appear on the face of the pleading, or else be taken by judicial notice.  (Bezirdjian v. O'Reilly (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 316, 321-22).  The parties’ ability to prove their respective claims is of no concern.  (Cloud v. Northrop Grumman Corp. (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 995, 99.)  Though the Court must accept the allegations of the complaint and answer as true (Gerawan Farming, Inc. v. Lyons (2000) 24 Cal.4th 468, 515), it will not do so for “conclusions of law or fact, opinions, speculation, or allegations contrary to law or [judicially noticed] facts…” (Stevenson Real Estate Servs., Inc. v. CB Richard Ellis Real Estate Servs., Inc. (2006) 138 Cal.App.4th 1215, 1219-20).

 

Sufficiency of Pleadings (Warranty Claims)

 

          Defendants contend that the warranty claims are not adequately pled. The Court disagrees. A cursory review of the pleadings demonstrates that ample facts are alleged to support these causes of action.

 

Negligent Repair

 

          Defendants also attack the negligent repair cause of action as barred by the economic loss rule. The Court agrees. While all reasonable inferences must be drawn in Plaintiffs’ favor, the only inference available from the allegation that Plaintiffs delivered the vehicle for repair is that Plaintiffs and CW BMW had some contractual relationship of which the repairs were part. Thus, on its face, this allegation appears to fall within the economic loss rule. (See Robinson Helicopter Co., Inc. v. Dana Corp. (2004) 34 Cal.4th 979, 988.)

 

Sufficiency of Pleadings (Fraud)

 

          Defendants argue that the fraud cause of action is inadequately pled. The Court rejects this argument.

 

1.     Duty to Disclose

 

There are four circumstances in which nondisclosure or concealment may constitute actionable fraud: (1) when the defendant is in a fiduciary relationship with the plaintiff; (2) when the defendant had exclusive knowledge of material facts not known to the plaintiff; (3) when the defendant actively conceals a material fact from the plaintiff; or (4) when the defendant makes partial representations but also suppresses some material fact.” (LiMandri v. Judkins (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 326, 336.) A duty to disclose may also arise when a defendant possesses or exerts control over material facts not readily available to the plaintiff. (Jones v. ConocoPhillips Co. (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 1198, 1199.)  

 

          Defendants argue that the pleadings fail to establish a duty to disclose any concealed facts.  Ordinarily, a duty to disclose absent a fiduciary relationship “presupposes the existence of [a] relationship between the plaintiff and defendant in which a duty to disclose can arise.” (Bigler-Engler v. Breg, Inc. (2017) 7 Cal.App.5th 276, 311.) Defendants’ rely on the general rule to argue that there was not a “direct dealing” between the parties that gives rise to a duty to disclose. (See Bigler-Engler, supra, at 311.) Defendants’ reliance on this general rule as stated in Bigler-Engler is misplaced, because it ignores that California courts have developed a distinct doctrine that applies when, as here, an original seller manufactures or sells a product to whom it reasonably expects the product to be resold.

 

          A vendor has a duty to disclose material facts “not only to immediate purchasers, but also to subsequent purchasers when the vendor has reason to expect that the item will be resold.” (OCM Principle Opportunities Fund v. CIBC World Markets Corp. (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 835, 859-60.)  The duty to disclose to subsequent purchasers is limited: the plaintiff must show that the seller had special “reason to expect” that the fraud will be passed onto subsequent purchasers. (Geernaert v. Mitchell (2007) 31 Cal.App.4th 601, 607-08.) Mere foreseeability is insufficient. (Id. [citing Restatement (Second) of Torts § 305 cmt. d (1977)].)  In determining whether the seller had “reason to expect” that the fraud will be passed onto subsequent purchasers, courts generally consider two factors: “(1) the extent of the seller's knowledge of resale to a particular person or class of persons and (2) the likelihood that the particular misrepresentation (or concealment) would be passed on to them.” (Id. at 608.)

 

Here, the essential allegation is that the plaintiff is a subsequent purchaser of a product manufactured by BMW. Moreover, as alleged, Defendants clearly had reason to expect that the fraud could be passed on. Defendants entered into a warranty agreement with them regarding the subject vehicle. Thus, the pleadings have sufficiently alleged a duty of disclosure on Defendants’ part.

 

2.      Specificity

 

Defendant argues that the pleadings do not allege fraudulent concealment with adequate specificity. Defendants rely on the general rule for pleading fraud against a corporation, which requires a plaintiff to allege the identity of the speaker, their authority, to whom they spoke, what they said, and when it was said. (See, e.g., Tarmann v. State Farm Mut. Auto Ins. Co. (1991) 2 Cal.App.4th 153, 157.) However, this rule applies differently when the claim of fraud is based upon an assertion of fraudulent concealment. (Alfaro v. Community Housing Improvement System & Planning Association, Inc. (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1356, 1384 [“How does one show ‘how’ and ‘by what means’ something didn’t happen, or ‘when’ it never happened, or ‘where’ it never happened?”].) Instead, such details, in the context of a fraudulent concealment claim, “are properly the subject of discovery, not demurrer.” (Id. at 1384-85.)

 

Moreover, the specificity requirement is “relaxed when it is apparent from the allegations that the defendant necessarily possesses knowledge of the facts.” (Quelimane Co. v. Steward Title Guaranty Co. (1998) 19 Cal.4th 26, 27.) That is the case here.

 

3.     Economic Loss Rule

 

Defendants argue that the fraud claim is barred by the economic loss rule. It is not.

 

Under the economic loss rule, “[w]here a purchaser’s expectations in a sale are frustrated because the product he bought is not working properly, his remedy is in contract alone, for he has suffered only ‘economic losses.’” (Robinson Helicopter Co., Inc. v. Dana Corp. (2004) 34 Cal.4th 979, 988.) The economic loss rule “hinges on a distinction drawn between transactions involving the sale of goods for commercial purposes where economic expectations are protected by commercial and contract law, and those involving the sale of defective products to individual consumers who are injured in a manner which has traditionally been remedied by resort to the law of torts.” (Id.) Simply stated, the economic loss rule “prevents the law of contract and the law of tort from dissolving one into the other.” (Id.)  

 

The restrictions on contract remedies serve purposes not found in tort law—they protect the parties’ freedom to bargain over special risks, and they promote contract formation by limiting liability to the value of the promise. (Harris v. Atlantic Richfield (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 70, 77.) This encourages efficient breaches, resulting in increased production of goods and services at a lower cost to society. (Id.) Because of these overriding policy considerations, the California Supreme Court has proceeded with caution in carving out exceptions to the traditional contract remedy restrictions. (Id.)  

 

Nevertheless, there is a core distinction between a tort committed during an ongoing contractual relationship (which may, or may not, be barred by the economic loss rule) and a tort committed in the formation of a contract. When one party commits fraud during contract formation, the injured party may recover in both tort (for the fraudulent inducement) and in contract (by affirming the contract. (Id.)  Allowing recovery in tort for fraudulent inducement to enter a contract does not interfere with parties’ freedom to bargain over economic risk, because the fundamental nature of a fraudulent inducement claim is that the parties were not able to properly bargain over economic risk because an initial transaction was induced by fraud.

 

Defendants argue that this case is governed by the California Supreme Court’s recent holding in Rattagan v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1, clarifying the application of the economic loss rule to fraudulent concealment in the performance of a contract. The Court disagrees. This action is more properly governed by Dhital v. Nissan North America, Inc. (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 828. The Rattagan case was brought by a foreign national against Uber Technologies, Inc. for fraudulent concealment in Uber’s performance of a contract. Here, however, the fraudulent concealment claim is grounded in Defendants’ alleged fraud in the inducement of the contract. Indeed, our Supreme Court expressly addressed this point, stating “Rattagan's tort claims are, of course, based on alleged conduct committed during the contractual relationship but purportedly outside the parties' chosen rights and obligations. (Rattagan, supra, 17 Cal.5th at p. 41, fn. 12 [italics in original].) The Court in Rattagan noted that it had granted review of  Dhital “which involve[d] claims of fraudulent inducement by concealment” that the Court specifically declined to address. (Id.)

 

In Dhital, the Court of Appeal addressed the application of the economic loss rule to claims against vehicle manufacturers for fraudulent inducement by concealment. The Dhital Court expressly held that the economic loss rule does not bar such claims. (Dhital, supra, 84 Cal.App.5th at 833, 843.) The Court of Appeal reasoned that “the plaintiffs’ fraudulent inducement claim alleges presale conduct by Nissan (concealment) that is distinct from Nissan's alleged subsequent conduct in breaching its warranty obligations.” (Id. at 841.) Review of Dhital was dismissed on December 18, 2024 following the issuance of Rattagan. (Dhital v. Nissan North America (2024) 327 Cal.Rptr.3d 898 (Mem).) Dhital is a published opinion addressing an identical issue to the one presented in this case. Rattagan, in expressly declining to address the issue presented in Dhital, does not supersede that opinion.  Cases are not authority for propositions not considered. (People v. Alvarez (2002) 27 Cal.4th 1161, 1176.) Inversely, cases are binding authority for propositions expressly addressed. Under Dhital, the claim for fraudulent inducement by concealment is not barred by the economic loss rule.

 

Leave to Amend

 

When a demurrer is sustained, the Court determines whether there is a reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318).  When a plaintiff “has pleaded the general set of facts upon which his cause of action is based,” the court should give the plaintiff an opportunity to amend his complaint, since plaintiff should not “be deprived of his right to maintain his action on the ground that his pleadings were defective for lack of particulars.” (Reed v. Norman (1957) 152 Cal.App.2d 892, 900.) Accordingly, California law imposes the burden on the plaintiffs to demonstrate the manner in which they can amend their pleadings to state their claims against a defendant.  (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349.) “Denial of leave to amend constitutes an abuse of discretion unless the complaint shows on its face it is incapable of amendment.  [Citation.]  Liberality in permitting amendment is the rule, if a fair opportunity to correct any defect has not been given." (Angie M. v. Superior Court (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 1217, 1227.)

 

In deference to the strong precedent in favor of amendment where a fair opportunity has not been given, the Court will grant leave to amend.

 

Meet and Confer

 

Before bringing a motion for judgment on the pleadings, the moving party shall meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference with the party who has filed the pleading subject to the motion and file a declaration detailing their meet and confer efforts.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 439(a).) However, an insufficient meet and confer process is not grounds to overrule or sustain a demurrer.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 439(a)(4).)

 

The declaration of Samantha Sheehan states that counsel for Defendant attempted to meet and confer with opposing counsel via an unspecified method, but was unsuccessful in resolving this dispute. This is not sufficient. Nevertheless, the Court addresses the merits of the demurrer.

 

Request for Judicial Notice

 

          BMW NA requests that the Court take judicial notice of its Statement of Information filed with the Secretary of State. The request is DENIED. BMW NA seeks to have the Court take judicial notice of the truth of the factual representations within it. This is improper.

 

CONCLUSION:

 

Accordingly, Defendants’ Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings is GRANTED as to the fifth cause of action and otherwise DENIED.

 

Oliver shall have thirty (30) days leave to amend.

 

Moving Parties to give notice.

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

CASE:                    Golden Boy Promotions, LLC v. Rick Mirigian

 

CASE NO.:             26STCV03099

 

MOTION TO COMPEL ARBITRATION

 

MOVING PARTY:          Defendant Rick Mirigian

 

RESPONDING PARTY(S): Plaintiff Golden Boy Productions, LLC (“GBP”)

 

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

Mirigian’s Motion to Compel Arbitration is GRANTED.

 

The Court sets a Status Conference Re: Arbitration for Monday, June 21, 2027 at 8:30 A.M.

 

Moving Party to give notice.

 

BACKGROUND

         

          This is an action for interference with contract. GBP alleges that Mirigian, as manager for professional boxer Vergil Ortiz, Jr., sabotaged GBP’s promotional agreement with Ortiz to benefit himself.  

 

DISCUSSION:

 

          Mirigian moves to compel arbitration based on an arbitration clause in the Promotional Rights Agreement between GBP and Ortiz. The Court grants the motion under an equitable estoppel theory. Although Mirigian is not a party to the Promotional Agreement, GBP’s claims against Mirigian are premised on specific rights granted to it, which entitles Mirigian to arbitration as a matter of equity.

 

Existence of Arbitration Agreement

 

          Mirigian contends that he is a party to a valid arbitration agreement between himself and GBP. He is not.

 

Under California law, arbitration agreements are valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, save upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract. (Blake v. Ecker (2001) 93 Cal.App.4th 728, 741 (overruled on other grounds by Le Francois v. Goel (2005) 35 Cal.4th 1094).) A party petitioning to compel arbitration has the burden of establishing the existence of a valid agreement to arbitrate, and the party opposing the petition has the burden of proving, by a preponderance of the evidence, any fact necessary to its defense. (Banner Entertainment, Inc. v. Superior Court (1998) 62 Cal.App.4th 348, 356-57.)

 

As to the burden of production, rather than persuasion, courts have articulated a three-step burden shifting process:

 

First, the moving party bears the burden of producing “prima facie evidence of a written agreement to arbitrate the controversy.” [citation] The moving party “can meet its initial burden by attaching to the [motion or] petition a copy of the arbitration agreement purporting to bear the [opposing party’s] signature.” [citation] Alternatively, the moving party can meet its burden by setting forth the agreement’s provisions in the motion. [citations] For this step, “it is not necessary to follow the normal procedures of document authentication.” [citation] If the moving party meets its initial prima facie burden and the opposing party does not dispute the existence of the arbitration agreement, then nothing more is required for the moving party to meet its burden of persuasion.

 

If the moving party meets its initial prima facie burden and the opposing party disputes the agreement, then in the second step, the opposing party bears the burden of producing evidence to challenge the authenticity of the agreement. [citation] The opposing party can do this in several ways. For example, the opposing party may testify under oath or declare under penalty of perjury that the party never saw or does not remember seeing the agreement, or that the party never signed or does not remember signing the agreement. [citations]

 

If the opposing party meets its burden of producing evidence, then in the third step, the moving party must establish with admissible evidence a valid arbitration agreement between the parties. The burden of proving the agreement by a preponderance of the evidence remains with the moving party. [citation].

 

(Gamboa v. Northeast Community Clinic (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 158, 165-66.)

 

          Mirigian has furnished a copy of the Promotional Rights Agreement between GPB and Ortiz. (Declaration of Rick Mirigian ISO Mot. Exh. A.) Section 12 of the Agreement contains an arbitration clause which states:

 

Other than issues related to each Bout, which will be controlled by the rules of the applicable Commission, this Agreement will be governed and construed in accordance with the laws of the State of Nevada applicable to agreements entered into and who11y perfom1ed therein. Any dispute, controversy or claim arising out of or relating to this Agreement, including the formation, interpretation, breach, or termination thereof: including whether the claims asserted are arbitrable, will be referred to and finally determined by arbitration· in accordance with the JAMS Commercial Arbitration Rules before a single arbitrator who shall be selected by the parties to the arbitration and who shall be a retired judge or justice. If the parties are unable to select an arbitrator, the arbitrator will be selected in accordance with the JAMS Rules. The place of arbitration will be Las Vegas, Nevada. Judgment upon the award rendered by the arbitrator(s) may be entered in any court having jurisdiction thereof: The prevailing Party in arbitration shall be entitled to its reasonable costs, including the costs of arbitration, and attorneys' fees.

 

Although the Agreement bears Mirigian’s signature and names him as a “Manager,” there is no other specific reference to Mirigian either by name or title in the Agreement. (See Id.) In contrast, the Agreement specifies that only GBP and Ortiz, as “Promoter” and “Boxer” respectively, “are each a “Party” to the agreement and together are the “Parties.” (Id. at p.1.) Mirigian nonetheless asserts that he is a party to the Promotional Rights Agreement between GBP and Ortiz based on his identification in the preamble, his signature, and various references to “Boxer’s representatives and affiliates.” (See, e.g., Exh. A. p. 12 at § 13.)   This argument is specious. The plain language of the contract identifies GBP and Ortiz alone as the parties to the agreement. Generalized references to Ortiz’s “representatives” with respect to Ortiz’s rights and obligations are binding on Ortiz, and do not confer the status of party onto Mirigian.

 

          Mirigian is not a party to the Promotional Rights Agreement. He is therefore not entitled to compel arbitration on that basis.

 

Equitable Estoppel

 

          Mirigian argues in the alternative that he is entitled to compel arbitration under an equitable estoppel theory. This contention is much more persuasive. GBP’s Complaint against Mirigian is facially premised on enumerated rights and obligations in the Promotional Rights Agreement. Well-settled precedent entitles Mirigian to arbitration in equity.

 

The doctrine of equitable estoppel applies if (1) the plaintiff relies upon the contract’s terms in asserting claims against the non-signatory defendant, or those claims are “intimately founded in or intertwined with” the contract itself, or (2) if the plaintiff alleges “substantially interdependent and concerted misconduct” by the defendant, where such allegations of misconduct are “founded in or intimately connected with” the obligations of the contract. (Goldman v. KPMG LLP (2009) 173 Cal.App.4th 209, 221.)  The rule allowing a nonsignatory to enforce an arbitration agreement on equitable estoppel grounds is based on the principle that a party should be precluded “‘from asserting rights “he otherwise would have had against another” when his own conduct renders assertion of those rights contrary to equity.’” (Metalclad Corp. v. Ventana Environmental Organizational Partnership (2003) 109 Cal.App.4th 1705, 1713 [Citations omitted].)

 

Here, the Complaint plainly relies on the terms of the Promotional Agreement in asserting claims against Mirigian. Paragraph 11 of the Complaint alleges that the Promotional Agreement granted GBP exclusive promotional rights for Ortiz’s boxing and the exclusive right to negotiate all of Ortiz’s matches. (Complaint ¶ 11.) Ortiz’s representatives were required to forward any inquiries to GBP for negotiation and could only participate in negotiations with GBP’s express permission. (Id.) GBP alleges that Mirigian ignored these terms to negotiate promotional deals for Ortiz with third parties to advance Mirigian’s own standing and reputation. (Complaint ¶¶ 16-17, 27.) GBP’s assertion that the claims are “based on Mirigian’s conduct as an outsider to the Promotional Agreement” (Opposition p.9:5-6) miss the forest for the trees. Contrary to GBP’s claims, this is not a scenario where the existence of a contract is a background fact, as was the case in Goldman. (See 173 Cal.App.4th at 231.) Rather, GBP’s claims rely upon the Promotional Agreement’s terms to impose liability. Mirigian is therefore entitled to compel arbitration on equitable estoppel grounds.

 

Requests for Judicial Notice

 

          Mirigian requests that the Court take judicial notice of certain filings in the United States Disctrict Court District of Nevada in an action between Ortiz and GBP. These filings are not material to the Court’s ruling, and so the request is DENIED. (Gbur v. Cohen (1979) 93 Cal.App.3d 296, 301 (“[J]udicial notice . . . is always confined to those matters which are relevant to the issue at hand.”].) 

 

CONCLUSION:

 

Accordingly, Mirigian’s Motion to Compel Arbitration is GRANTED.

 

The Court sets a Status Conference Re: Arbitration for Monday, June 21, 2027 at 8:30 A.M.

 

Moving Party to give notice.

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

CASE:                    IRP Grace Apartments LLC v. Williams

 

CASE NO.:             26STCV16215

 

DEMURRER TO COMPLAINT

 

MOVING PARTY:          Defendant Saadiq Williams

 

RESPONDING PARTY(S): Plaintiff IRP Grace Apartments LLC

 

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

Williams’s Demurrer to the Complaint is OVERRULED.

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

BACKGROUND

         

          This is an unlawful detainer action for nonpayment of rent.

 

DISCUSSION:

 

          Williams demurs to the Complaint for failure to state a cause of action for unlawful detainer.  Based on the allegations in the complaint, and accepting those allegations as true, the Court overrules the demurrer. 

 

Legal Standard

 

A demurrer tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (Taylor v. City of Los Angeles Dept. of Water and Power (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 1216, 1228.) In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)

 

Analysis

 

          Williams raises two arguments.  First, he claims that the 30-day notice alleged in the Complaint overstates the amount of rent due.  This is not a proper issue for demurrer.  The Complaint alleges that Williams owed a certain amount of rent at the time the notice was served.  The Court, at this procedural posture, must accept that allegation as true.  Williams points to nothing in the pleadings or in the judicially-noticeable record that demonstrates that the rent amount alleged in the Complaint is incorrect.

 

Second, Williams argues that the three-day notice alleged in the Complaint was served before the rent amount on the notice became due.  But that defect is not apparent from the Complaint.  The three-day notice seeks rent due “from 11/1/2025 through 4/30/26.”  (Compl, Ex. A.)  The three-day notice was served on April 10, 2026.  (Id.)  The Complaint explains that “[t]he current monthly rent is $4,715.00, due by the first of each month.”  (Complaint, ¶ 3, emphasis added.)  Thus, per the Complaint, April rent for 2026 was due in full on April 1, 2026.  The notice seeks rent through April 30, 2026, and was served on April 10, or after April 1, 2026 when that rent became due.  Thus, the pleadings do not indicate that the three-day notice was served before the amount on it became due.

 

          Accordingly, neither of Williams’ two bases for demurrer succeeds.  The Court must overrule the demurrer.

 

CONCLUSION:

 

          The demurrer to the Complaint is OVERRULED.

Judge Nicholas F. Daum, Department 507

 

 

HEARING DATE:  July 8, 2026                    TRIAL DATE: August 11, 2026

                                                

CASE:                    Ryan Stanton v. Frances Fontane Marques, et al.

 

CASE NO.:             BC572198

 

(1) MOTION FOR SUMMARY ADJUDICATION

(2) RENEWED MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT

 

MOVING PARTY:          (1) Plaintiff Ryan Stanton; (2) Defendants Frances Marques, Pablynie Marques Jamison, Modern Medical Management, LLC, and Marques Management, LLC

 

RESPONDING PARTY(S): (1) Defendants Frances Marques, Pablynie Marques Jamison, Modern Medical Management, LLC, and Marques Management, LLC (2) Plaintiff Ryan Stanton

 

TENTATIVE RULING

 

          Stanton’s Motion for Summary Adjudication is DENIED.

 

Defendants’ Renewed Motion for Summary Judgment is DENIED.

 

          Moving parties to give notice.

 

DISCUSSION:

 

          This case is (on both sides) an object lesson in the importance of preparing proper separate statements in connection with motions for summary adjudication and summary judgment.

 

Motion for Summary Adjudication (Stanton)

 

          Stanton moves for summary adjudication of the third cause of action for breach of operating agreement, fourth cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty, sixth cause of action for constructive fraud, ninth cause of action for violation of Penal Code section 496, and tenth cause of action for fraudulent transfer.

 

The Court denies the motion.  The separate statement is so deficient that the Court cannot (or, at a minimun, in the Court’s view should not) rule on the merits of the motion.

 

In bringing a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication, the supporting papers must include “a separate statement setting forth plainly and concisely all material facts that the moving party contends are undisputed.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 437c(b)(1).) Each of the material facts stated “shall be followed by a reference to the supporting evidence.” (Id.) “The requirement of a separate statement from the moving party and a responding statement from the party opposing summary judgment serves two functions: to give the parties notice of the material facts at issue in the motion and to permit the trial court to focus on whether those facts are truly undisputed.” (Parkview Villas Assn., Inc. v. State Farm Fire & Casualty Co. (2005) 133 Cal.App.4th 1197, 1210.) This separate statement must be presented in the two column format specified in the Rules of Court and must “separately” identify “[e]ach supporting material fact claimed to be without dispute with respect to the cause of action” –that is, each cause of action – “that is the subject of the motion.” (Cal Rules of Court Rule 3.1350(d)(1)(B); subd. (h).)

 

Stanton has not followed this rule. Rather than present each material fact with direct citations to the evidence tending to prove those facts, Stanton has simply offered a set of bare legal conclusions and sweeping references with bulk citations to this Court’s prior rulings. Those rulings are not themselves evidence foreclosing the existence of a triable issue of fact. Nor are they dispositive of any of these issues, because the Court’s findings on a lis pendens motion or a motion for preliminary injunction are not subject to the same standards as a motion for summary judgment.  By failing to present the underlying evidence, Stanton’s Separate Statement does not serve either essential function. This defective Separate Statement cannot be fairly said to put Defendants on notice of the material facts at issue. Moreover, the Separate Statement certainly does not permit this Court to focus on whether the facts are truly undisputed, as Stanton instead asks the Court to sort through the assembled discovery material to determine what facts are indeed material. Although the body of the moving papers repeatedly cite underlying evidence, that is not a substitute for the failure to present all material facts and supporting evidence in the Separate Statement itself. The Golden Rule of Summary Adjudication is “if it is not set forth in the separate statement, it does not exist.” (United Community Church v. Garcin (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 327, 337 [italics in original].) The failure to provide a separate statement is, in the Court’s discretion, grounds for denial of the motion. (Code Civ. Proc. § 437c(b)(1).) Appellate precedent has consistently encouraged trial courts to require strict compliance with the requirements for separate statements. (United Community Church, supra,  231 Cal.App.3d at 335; North Coast Business Park v. Nielsen Construction Co. (1993) 17 Cal.App.4th 22, 30-32.) As the Court of Appeal for the Fourth District has bluntly stated, “[t]rial courts should not hesitate to deny summary judgment motions when the moving party fails to draft a compliant separate statement.” (Beltran v. Hard Rock Hotel Licensing, Inc. (2023) 97 Cal.App.5th 865, 876.) Because Stanton has failed to draft a compliant Separate Statement, that discretion is exercised here to deny the motion.

 

Accordingly, Stanton’s Motion for Summary Adjudication is DENIED.

 

Renewed Motion for Summary Judgment (Defendants)

 

Defendants move for summary judgment on all causes of action. For the third time, Defendants argue that Stanton lacks standing to pursue his claims. This is an improper renewed motion under Code of Civil Procedure section 437c(f)(2). For that reason alone, the Court can and does deny the motion. Separately, Defendants also attempt to argue that Stanton cannot present evidence to support his claims on their merits. However, Defendants have (as they did in their prior motions) failed to draft a compliant separate statement that separately identifies each material fact with respect to each cause of action asserted against them. (Cal Rules of Court Rule 3.1350(d)(1)(B); subd. (h).) The Court therefore exercises its discretion to deny the motion for a non-compliant separate statement. (Beltran v. Hard Rock Hotel Licensing, Inc. (2023) 97 Cal.App.5th 865, 876.) 

 

Accordingly, Defendants’ Renewed Motion for Summary Judgment is DENIED.

 

CONCLUSION:

 

Stanton’s Motion for Summary Adjudication is DENIED.

 

Defendants’ Renewed Motion for Summary Judgment is DENIED.
