#5 - HAI J. WONG vs GARABED OUSTAYAN, et al.

MOTION TO INTERVENE AND TO EXPUNGE LIS PENDENS

A.  PRD LLC is entitled to intervene for the limited purpose of moving to expunge the lis pendens.

PRD LLC has shown a direct interest in the property affected by the lis pendens. Lai declares that PRD LLC acquired the property at foreclosure and that the lis pendens remains recorded against it. Because the lis pendens burdens the property, disposition of the matter without PRD LLC’s participation could impair PRD LLC’s ability to protect its claimed interest.

PRD LLC states it discovered the lis pendens shortly after obtaining possession in March 2026, attempted informal resolution by the end of March 2026, and filed its motion on May 20, 2026. Wong identifies no prejudice from intervention and instead expressly does not oppose it. 

Accordingly, the motion to intervene is GRANTED, limited to the lis pendens issue presented in the motion.

B.  The lis pendens should be expunged.

Whatever the operative complaint may originally have alleged, Wong now expressly states that she does not oppose expungement and is not seeking recovery of title to, ownership of, or possession of the Arcadia property. She further states that her remaining claims concern alleged wrongful conduct and financial damages. (Response, ¶ 5.)

Wong has not carried her burden to establish the probable validity of any presently asserted real property claim supporting continued maintenance of the lis pendens. Her current position disclaims the very title- or possession-based relief that would justify the notice. To the extent her remaining claims are damages-based, a lis pendens is not a proper device to secure those claims.

The motion to expunge is therefore GRANTED.

The notice of pendency of action recorded as Los Angeles Recorder’s No. 20250273748 against the real property commonly known as 2223 S. 2nd Avenue, Arcadia, California 91006, APN 5789-018-040, is EXPUNGED.

This order resolves only the intervention and lis pendens issues. It does not adjudicate the merits of Wong’s remaining claims against any existing defendant.

Moving Party is to give notice.

#8 - KEVA DILORENZO PAGE vs 713 E. ALTADENA DRIVE

Defendants Ahmed Hassan, Gail Cargill, and Raina Hassan’s motion to quash service of Plaintiff’s First Amended Complaint is GRANTED.  Service of the First Amended Complaint on those moving Defendants is quashed.

Much of Plaintiff’s opposition addresses the merits of the underlying dispute and the parties’ competing factual accusations. Those issues are not before the Court on this motion.  This motion is confined to personal jurisdiction and service, not the merits of Plaintiff’s claims. 

The Court finds its May 26, 2026 ruling did not decide service of the First Amended Complaint.  The opposition asserts the motion is “moot” because it was already resolved by the Court’s prior May 26, 2026 order.  (Opp., p. 2.)  The May 26, 2026 order says otherwise, as it expressly states that the earlier motion concerned the original complaint, that the earlier motion was moot because that complaint was no longer operative, and that the Court was not deciding whether service of the First Amended Complaint was effective.  The minute order also specifically identifies the later motion to quash service of the First Amended Complaint as a separate motion not decided at that hearing.  Accordingly, the instant motion is properly before the Court.

As for the service theory advanced in the opposition—that the First Amended Complaint was mailed to defense counsel and received by counsel’s office—the theory fails. (Opp., pp. 2-3.)  Plaintiff relies on that mailing and on counsel’s prior participation in the case.  However, Defendants expressly state that counsel never agreed to accept service, that Defendants have appeared only specially, and that no general appearance has been made. (Reply, p. 3.)

The Court finds Plaintiff’s showing is insufficient to establish effective service of the First Amended Complaint on these moving Defendants. Receipt of mailed papers by counsel, coupled with a special appearance to challenge jurisdiction, does not establish authorized acceptance of service here.  The motion is, therefore, granted.

DEMURRERS AND MOTIONS TO STRIKE

The Complaint alleges that on November 12, 2020, Plaintiff entered into a home-improvement contract with Premier Improvement A Partnership and Gustavo Arias for work at Plaintiff’s property, 405 Myrtle Street, Glendale, California; that over approximately two years the contractor defendants extracted more than $400,000, issued fraudulent change orders, and abandoned the project in late 2022, and left Plaintiff with additional completion costs and lost rental income.  (Compl., ¶¶ 8, 12-15, 19-31.)  The Complaint also alleges that the CSLB issued Citation No. 2-2023-1384 against Premier for violations of Business and Professions Code sections 7117(a), 7159, and 7159.5. (Compl., ¶¶ 33-34.)

The Complaint further alleges that Plaintiff made a claim on SureTec’s contractor bond, that SureTec denied the claim, and that SureTec provided incorrect information regarding the legal requirements for recovery.  (Compl., ¶¶ 37-45.)

Defendants Premier Improvement A Partnership and Gustavo Arias’s filed a Demurrer to the Complaint and a motion to strike portions of the Complaint.  Defendants demur to all ten causes of action and move to strike specified damages allegations, remedies, and argumentative phrases.

Separately, Defendant SureTec demurs to the second through tenth causes of action, and moves to strike allegations and prayer language.

Premier Improvement’s Demurrer

First Cause of Action — Breach of Contract

The Complaint alleges a valid construction contract, the basic subject matter of the agreement, the completion timeline, plaintiff’s performance, specific categories of breach, and resulting damages.  Liberally construed, those allegations give fair notice of a contract claim.  The Demurrer to the first cause of action is overruled.

Second Cause of Action — Financial Elder Abuse

The Complaint alleges plaintiff was an individual over 65, that defendants wrongfully took her property with intent to defraud, that they used false urgency and phantom change orders, and that the financial abuse caused substantial monetary loss.  (Compl., ¶¶ 1, 21, 23, 61, 67.)  At the pleading stage, those allegations are sufficient.  The Demurrer to the second cause of action is overruled.

Third Cause of Action — Breach of Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing

The Complaint alleges an underlying contract and alleges that Defendants deprived Plaintiff of the benefits of that agreement by abandoning the project while retaining payments, demanding additional payments, and failing to communicate honestly about project status and completion. (Compl., ¶ 72.)  The Court finds these allegations sufficient.  The Demurrer to the third cause of action is overruled.

Fourth Cause of Action — Violations of Business and Professions Code Sections 7159, 7159.5, and 7117

The Complaint alleges violations of sections 7159, 7159.5, and 7117 and alleges that those violations entitle Plaintiff to damages, restitution, and attorney’s fees under “applicable statutory provisions.”  The opposition argues that Business and Professions Code section 7160 supplies a private damages and fee remedy and states that, if necessary, she can amend to invoke that statute expressly.  Based on the latter, Plaintiff appears to concede that the fourth cause of action is not clearly pleaded as a private statutory claim.  Thus, the Demurrer to the fourth cause of action is sustained with leave to amend.

Fifth Cause of Action — Unfair Competition Law

The Complaint alleges that Premier violated Business and Professions Code sections 7159, 7159.5, and 7117; demanded and accepted down payments far exceeding the statutory limit; conducted business under the name “Premier Improvement” although the license was issued to “Premier Improvement A Partnership”; and that the CSLB issued Citation No. 2-2023-1384 imposing civil penalties for those violations.  Those allegations are sufficient at the pleading stage to support the UCL’s unlawful prong.

The Complaint also alleges that Premier targeted an elderly and vulnerable consumer, demanded excessive payments, created artificial urgency to obtain payment decisions, extracted maximum payments, and then abandoned the project.  These allegations are adequately tethered to the contractor-protection statutes plaintiff invokes and are sufficient to plead the unfair prong.

The Complaint further alleges that defendants made false representations about their ability to complete the work within stated timeframes, created a phantom change order, misrepresented the necessity and reasonableness of payment demands, and concealed an intent to abandon the project while continuing to extract payments.  For purposes of the UCL, those allegations identify the challenged practices with reasonable particularity.  As to standing, the Complaint alleges that Plaintiff paid Defendants more than $400,000, incurred $150,000 to hire other contractors to complete the work, lost rental income of more than $105,000, and seeks restitution and injunctive relief under the UCL.  Those allegations sufficiently plead economic injury and causation.  The Demurrer to the fifth cause of action is overruled.

Sixth Cause of Action — Fraud and Misrepresentation

The Complaint alleges broad categories of misrepresentations, including representations about timing, payment necessity, work progress, and a fraudulent change order.  In opposition, Plaintiff states she can add further specificity as to particular fraudulent statements by Arias on identified dates.  That concession shows the current fraud allegations are not yet pleaded with the needed particularity.  The Demurrer to the sixth cause of action is sustained with leave to amend.

Seventh Cause of Action — Negligence

The Complaint alleges Defendants owed a duty to perform construction work in accordance with building codes, industry standards, and a good and workmanlike manner, and alleges resulting remediation and completion damages.  (Compl., ¶¶ 98-100.)

The opposition asserts additional facts concerning independent statutory duties and damage to property other than the new construction and states Plaintiff can further clarify the claim. (Opp. (0809), p. 9.)  As currently pleaded, the negligence claim is not clearly distinct from the contract-based claims.  Thus, the Demurrer to the seventh cause of action is sustained with leave to amend.

Eighth Cause of Action — Money Had and Received

“A cause of action for money had and received is stated if it is alleged the defendant ‘is indebted to the plaintiff in a certain sum “for money had and received by the defendant for the use of the plaintiff.”’  [Citation.]”  (Farmers Ins. Exchange v. Zerin (1997) 53 Cal.App.4th 445, 460.)  A cause of action for money had and received “is viable wherever one person has received money which belongs to another, and which in equity and good conscience should be paid over to the latter.”  (Avidor v. Sutter’s Place, Inc. (2013) 212 Cal.App.4th 1439, 1454 [internal quotations omitted].)  “[T]he plaintiff must prove that the defendant received money intended to be used for the benefit of [the plaintiff], that the money was not used for the plaintiff’s benefit, and that the defendant has not given the money to the plaintiff.”  (Ibid. [internal quotations omitted].)

The Complaint alleges Defendants received substantial money from Plaintiff under circumstances making retention inequitable and seeks restoration of those funds.  (Compl., ¶¶ 102-104.)  A common count is proper notwithstanding that it relates to an original transaction involving an express or implied contract.  (Utility Audit Co. v. City of L.A. (2003) 112 Cal.App.4th 950, 958.)  At the pleading stage, common count may be asserted in the alternative.  The Demurrer to the eighth cause of action is overruled.

Ninth Cause of Action — Quantum Meruit / Unjust Enrichment

The ninth claim is not well-labeled to the extent it invokes “quantum meruit,” because quantum meruit traditionally concerns compensation for services rendered.  Quantum meruit refers to the well-established principle that the law implies a promise to pay for services performed under circumstances disclosing that they were not gratuitously rendered.  (Miller v. Campbell, Warburton, Fitzsimmons, Smith, Mendel & Pastore (2008) 162 Cal.App.4th 1331, 1344.)  Here, Plaintiff is not alleging that she rendered services to Premier; she alleges that Premier received her money and unjustly retained it after failing to provide corresponding value.

On the other hand, the elements for a claim of unjust enrichment are: (1) receipt of a benefit; and (2) unjust retention of the benefit at the expense of another. (Elder v. Pacific Bell Telephone Co. (2012) 205 Cal.App.4th 841, 857.)  Courts have construed unjust enrichment claims as quasi-contract claims seeking restitution.  (Rutherford Holdings LLC v. Plaza Del Rey (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 221, 231.)  The Complaint alleges that Plaintiff paid Defendants more than $400,000, that Defendants failed to complete the contracted work, that Defendants abandoned the project in late 2022, and that Plaintiff was required to spend approximately $150,000 to complete the work.  Those allegations adequately plead that Premier received a benefit and that its retention of the benefit may be unjust.

At this stage, the Court will permit the alternative pleading.  The Demurrer to the ninth cause of action is overruled to the extent the claim is construed as one for restitution/unjust enrichment pleaded in the alternative.  The Court does not construe the claim as a true quantum meruit claim for services rendered by Plaintiff. Plaintiff may not obtain duplicative recovery for both breach of contract and restitution, but she may plead alternative theories at this stage.

Tenth Cause of Action — Bad Faith Claims Handling

The tenth cause of action is expressly directed to  SureTec and alleges only SureTec’s conduct in handling a contractor’s bond claim. As pleaded, that cause of action is not asserted against Premier or Arias.  The Demurrer by Premier and Arias to the tenth cause of action is overruled as moot.

Premier Improvement’s Motion to Strike

The motion to strike is GRANTED in part.

Paragraph 68 of the Complaint demands “treble damages” under Welfare and Institutions Code section 15657.5.  California’s Elder Abuse laws authorize civil actions for elder abuse, including specifically for financial abuse, and permit enhanced remedies.  (Wel. & Inst. Code § 15657.5 [permitting recovery of compensatory damages, attorneys’ fees and costs, and punitive damages based on a finding of recklessness, oppression, fraud or malice].  These enhanced remedies incentivize private enforcement of the Elder Abuse statutes and demonstrate the importance the California Legislature placed on protecting this vulnerable population.  As pleaded, the Complaint seeks treble damages under Welfare and Institutions Code section 15657.5, which is not identified as a treble-damages statute in the pleading.

Plaintiff’s opposition clarifies that the intended statutory basis is Civil Code section 3345.  Civil Code section 3345 may apply in actions brought for the benefit of senior citizens to redress unfair or deceptive acts or practices where the trier of fact is otherwise authorized by statute to impose a discretionary punitive or deterrent remedy.  However, the statute does not itself create an independent entitlement to treble compensatory damages or treble UCL restitution.  The treble-damages references as presently pleaded are unsupported.  The motion to strike the phrase “including treble damages” from paragraph 68 and prayer paragraph 2 is granted with leave to amend.

Premier’s motion also identifies paragraph 57 as seeking emotional-distress damages on the contract claim.  Erlich v. Menezes (1999) 21 Cal.4th 543, 557, as referenced in Defendants’ motion to strike, held that emotional distress damages are generally not recoverable for an ordinary home-construction contract claim involving economic and property loss without physical injury or breach of an independent tort duty.  Here, however, the Complaint also alleges intentional fraud, financial elder abuse, and related intentional misconduct.  The California Supreme Court later clarified that Erlich does not apply to bar recovery of tort damages in connection with intentional fraud and misrepresentation, even if the intentional acts of fraud were committed in the context of a contractual relationship.  (Robinson Helicopter v. Dana Corp (2004) 34 Cal.4th 979, 989, 992-993.)  “In each of these cases, the duty that gives rise to tort liability is either completely independent of the contract or arises from conduct which is both intentional and intended to harm.”  (Id., at pp. 989-991.)  Premier’s request to strike the phrase wholesale is therefore overbroad.  This ruling does not determine whether Plaintiff can ultimately recover emotional-distress damages, or on which causes of action; it holds only that the phrase should not be stricken at the pleading stage.  The motion to strike the phrase “emotional distress” from paragraph 57 is denied.

Paragraph 66 alleges oppression, fraud, and malice, and the prayer seeks punitive damages on that basis.  The Complaint alleges a nearly two-year pattern of excessive payment demands, a phantom change order, deliberate exploitation of an elderly plaintiff, and abandonment after receipt of substantial funds.  At the pleading stage, those factual allegations are sufficient to allow the punitive-damages request to remain.  The motion to strike punitive-damages allegations in paragraph 66 and prayer paragraph 3 is denied.

Paragraph 108 and prayer paragraph 5 seek disgorgement of “ill-gotten gains” under the UCL.  Plaintiff’s claim for disgorgement of profits is improper.  A UCL plaintiff can only obtain disgorgement of profits to the extent he gave the defendant the profits or has an ownership interest in them.  (Korea Supply Co. v. Lockheed Martin Corp. (2003) 29 Cal.4th 1134, 1149 [“The object of restitution is to restore the status quo by returning to the plaintiff funds in which he or she has an ownership interest.”].)  A private UCL plaintiff may seek injunctive relief and restitution but not non-restitutionary disgorgement.  (Id. at 1144-1145.)  Because the present language is broader than necessary, the pleading should be narrowed to restitutionary relief if that is what Plaintiff intends.  The motion to strike the phrase “disgorgement of ill-gotten gains” from paragraph 108 and the phrase “disgorgement of all ill-gotten gains” from prayer paragraph 5 is granted with leave to amend.

Paragraph 82 further seeks damages, restitution, and attorney’s fees under Business and Professions Code sections 7159, 7159.5, and 7117.  Paragraph 82 alleges that the pleaded contractor-statute violations entitle plaintiff to damages, restitution, and attorney’s fees.  The opposition relies on Business and Professions Code section 7160, the UCL, and the Elder Abuse Act as the sources of those remedies and requests leave to amend for greater specificity if needed.  Because the current allegation does not clearly identify the statutory basis for the remedies sought, it should be clarified by amendment.  The motion to strike paragraph 82 is granted with leave to amend.

SureTec’s Demurrer

SureTec argues that the claims asserted against it are barred by Business and Professions Code section 7071.11(c).

The Complaint expressly alleges that SureTec issued Contractor’s Bond No. 462248 for Premier Improvement A Partnership effective January 1, 2023, and that the bond was cancelled on July 7, 2023.  (Compl., ¶ 4.)  The Complaint also alleges that Premier’s contractor license expired on September 30, 2023. (Compl., ¶ 2.) The specific contractor misconduct identified in the Complaint includes a fraudulent change order in October 2022, excessive payment demands through December 2022, and abandonment of the project in late 2022.

Business and Professions Code section 7071.11(c)(1) provides that an action against a contractor’s bond must be brought “[w]ithin two years after the expiration of the license period during which the act or omission occurred.”  On the face of the Complaint, any act or omission occurring during the license period ending September 30, 2023 had to be sued upon no later than September 30, 2025.  This action was filed on November 17, 2025.

The pleaded bond dates create an additional defect.  As pleaded, SureTec’s bond was not effective until January 1, 2023, but the Complaint’s specific allegations of the contractor’s fraudulent change order, excessive payment demands, and abandonment all occurred in 2022, before the bond took effect.  Plaintiff’s opposition emphasizes the relevant license period, but the Complaint presently identifies no discrete act or omission by Premier within SureTec’s pleaded bond period that would support bond liability, as opposed to SureTec’s later denial of the bond claim.

Business and Professions Code section 7071.5 makes the contractor’s bond available for specified violations or fraud by the licensee.  The Complaint’s allegations that SureTec later denied the claim and gave incorrect legal information do not supply a timely covered act by the licensee within the bond period.  The Demurrer is sustained in its entirety, and leave to amend as to Defendant SureTec is denied.

SureTec’s Motion to Strike

Considering the Court’s ruling on the Demurrer, Defendant SureTec’s motion to strike is MOOT.

Tentative Ruling

SureTec’s Demurrer is sustained without leave to amend.  SureTec’s motion to strike is denied as MOOT.

Premier Improvement’s Demurrer is overruled as to the first, second, third, fifth, eighth, ninth, and tenth causes of action.  The Demurrer is sustained with leave to amend as to the fourth, sixth, and seventh cause of action.

Premier Improvement’s Motion to Strike is denied as to Paragraphs 57 and 66, and prayer paragraph 3.  The motion is granted with leave to amend as to paragraphs 68, 82, and 108, as well as prayer paragraphs 2 and 5.

Plaintiffs shall file and serve any amended complaint within 20 days of service of notice of this order.

MOTION TO STRIKE

As to moving defendant David S. Kim DDS Corp., the First Amended Complaint ("FAC") alleges that it owned, leased, controlled, managed, maintained, supervised, and exercised authority over the premises, and had the ability to prohibit dangerous uses, require corrective action and code compliance, terminate occupancy, and otherwise prevent continuation of the operations.  (FAC, ¶ 92.)  The FAC further alleges that this defendant deliberately continued permitting the hazardous operations despite notice of the risks and continued permitting operations even after the January 10, 2026 fire.  (FAC, ¶ 96.)  Plaintiffs also allege they personally observed ongoing operations during an inspection on March 24, 2026.  (FAC, ¶ 136.)

The Court finds that the FAC does not rely only on conclusory adjectives.  The pleading sets out a specific factual theory: a hazardous flammable operation; alleged years of notice of odors and physical symptoms; alleged lack of basic fire-safety systems; an alleged fire caused by methyl methacrylate vapors igniting at an open flame; and alleged continuation of operations afterward. Those allegations go beyond just recitation of Civil Code section 3294.  At the pleading stage, they are sufficient to support an inference of willful and conscious disregard rather than mere inadvertence.

Further, the allegations against David S. Kim DDS Corp. are sufficient at the pleading stage.  The FAC alleges that David S. Kim DDS Corp. had authority over the premises and the ability to require corrective action, require code compliance, prohibit dangerous uses, or terminate occupancy.  It also alleges that the corporation deliberately chose to continue permitting and ratifying the hazardous operations despite notice of the substantial probability of serious harm, including after the fire.

The FAC also alleges that the challenged acts and omissions were authorized, ratified, approved, directed, or carried out by officers, directors, managing agents, owners, and persons exercising substantial discretionary authority on behalf of David S. Kim DDS Corp.  Ultimately, Plaintiffs will have to prove they complied with Civil Code section 3294(b).  But at this stage, the Court is not persuaded that the punitive allegations fail as a matter of pleading.

The Court therefore declines to strike the punitive damages allegations.  The request to strike the corresponding punitive damages prayer is also declined.  Defendant is to answer the complaint within 10 days, or by July 20, 2026.

#11 - ALEJANDRO RIOS vs FORD MOTOR COMPANY

DEMURRER

Plaintiff Alejandro Rios filed his complaint on April 15, 2026, and it pleads four causes of action: violation of the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act, breach of express warranty, breach of implied warranty of merchantability, and violation of Business and Professions Code section 17200 et seq.

Defendant Ford Motor Company’s demurrer asserts that the action is barred by prior settlements, dismissals with prejudice, and a written release arising from two prior lawsuits between these parties concerning the same 2022 Ford Escape, VIN 1FMCU0G63NUB51969.

Request for Judicial Notice

Ford requests judicial notice of six documents from two prior Los Angeles Superior Court actions involving these parties, including the complaint, a Code of Civil Procedure section 998 offer, and dismissal in case no. 24NNCV06861, and the complaint, settlement and release agreement, and dismissal request in case no. 25NNCV07708.

Judicial notice may be taken of records of any court in this state.  (Evid. Code, § 452, subd. (d)(1).)  Because the above documents are all court records in this state, the Court may take judicial notice of them.  (Ibid.)  However, “while courts are free to take judicial notice of the existence of each document in a court file, including the truth of results reached, they may not take judicial notice of the truth of hearsay statements in decisions and court files.  Courts may not take judicial notice of allegations in affidavits, declarations and probation reports in court records because such matters are reasonably subject to dispute and therefore require formal proof.”  (Lockley v. Law Office of Cantrell, Green, Pekich, Cruz & McCort (2001) 91 Cal.App.4th 875, 882 [cleaned up].)

The request is GRANTED as to the existence, filing, and operative legal effect of the prior court records and the February 10, 2026 settlement and release agreement.  The request is DENIED to the extent it seeks judicial notice of the truth of factual allegations contained in prior pleadings.

Tentative Ruling

The Demurrer to the operative Complaint is sustained without leave to amend.

Legal Authority and Analysis

A general demurrer may be taken to a complaint where “[t]he pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10(e).)  A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action.  (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)  In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or by proper judicial notice.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.30(a).)  A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters.  (SKF Farms v. Superior Court (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 902, 905.)¿

The complaint alleges that Plaintiff purchased the subject 2022 Ford Escape on or about August 15, 2023, paid $2,955.00 for a Transmission Control Assembly repair completed on April 17, 2025, and later received an April 7, 2026 Ford email acknowledging a reimbursement request and requesting a receipt showing the VIN, amount, repair description, date of repair, part numbers used, and mileage at the time of repair.  (Compl., ¶¶ 16, 21, 25.)  The complaint further alleges that the repair invoice contained those categories of information and that Ford failed to reimburse Plaintiff.  (Compl., ¶ 1.)

The first prior action, LASC Case No. 24NNCV06861, was filed on December 31, 2024 (RJN, Exh. A), resolved by a Code of Civil Procedure section 998 offer dated April 7, 2025 (RJN, Exh. B), and dismissed with prejudice on August 21, 2025 (RJN, Exh. C).

The second prior action, LASC Case No. 25NNCV07708, was filed on October 30, 2025 (RJN, Exh. D), and was resolved by a written settlement and release dated February 10, 2026 (RJN, Exh. E).  That release states that Plaintiff released Ford from “all claims relating to the purchase, lease, condition, use, or repair of the Subject Vehicle that arose during Plaintiff’s purchase or possession of the Subject Vehicle,” and the release specifically included claims under Civil Code sections 1793.2, 1793.22, and 1794.  (RJN, Exh. E, ¶ 3.)  Plaintiff filed a request for dismissal with prejudice in case no. 25NNCV07708 on April 28, 2026.  (RJN, Exh. F.)

The Court notes Ford’s motion is captioned as a demurrer to a “First Amended Complaint” and include arguments directed to causes of action not pleaded in the operative complaint, while the operative complaint on file pleads only the four causes identified above.  The Court therefore construes the motion as directed only to the operative complaint on file.

The release bars the claims as pleaded.

Each cause of action in the operative complaint seeks recovery based on the same alleged loss: Plaintiff’s payment for the April 17, 2025 repair and Ford’s failure to reimburse that repair cost and related damages or equitable relief.  The repair indisputably occurred before the February 10, 2026 settlement and while plaintiff owned and possessed the vehicle.

The release language is broad and unambiguous.  It covers all claims relating to the purchase, lease, condition, use, or repair of the subject vehicle arising during plaintiff’s purchase or possession, and it expressly encompasses statutory claims under Civil Code sections 1793.2, 1793.22, and 1794.

As pleaded, the present Song-Beverly, express warranty, implied warranty, and UCL claims all arise from a repair-related expense incurred during plaintiff’s ownership of the vehicle and therefore fall within the scope of that release.

The complaint alleges that Ford later acknowledged receipt of a reimbursement request and requested invoice details, but it does not allege that Ford entered a new agreement, expressly promised reimbursement notwithstanding the release, or expressly modified the February 10, 2026 settlement.  Those allegations do not plead a new post-release contractual obligation independent of the released repair claim.

The Court finds the release is dispositive.

Leave to amend is denied.

In opposition and in the bench brief, Plaintiff argues that he can amend to add the exact reimbursement emails, approval or refund language, a payment timeline, reliance, waiver, equitable estoppel, promissory estoppel, an independent reimbursement agreement, UCL allegations directed to reimbursement administration, and declaratory relief regarding the scope of the release.  Plaintiff’s argument does not identify facts establishing a concrete post-release promise or contractual term that would alter the effect of the written release already before the Court.  Given the breadth of the release and the pre-release timing of the repair expense at issue, the Court finds no reasonable possibility that amendment could cure the defect.  Leave to amend is therefore denied.
