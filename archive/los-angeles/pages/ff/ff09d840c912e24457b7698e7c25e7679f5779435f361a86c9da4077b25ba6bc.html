Case Number: 22STV10441    Hearing Date: June 30, 2026    Dept: 414

22STV10441 John R. Villanueva, Jr. v. Volkswagen Group of America

Tuesday, June 30, 2026

 

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING MOTION FOR PLAINTIFF’S ATTORNEY FEES

 

I.       BACKGROUND

      This action arose from alleged violations of the Song Beverly Consumer Warranty Act. Plaintiff moves for an award of attorney’s fees in the total amount of $58,211.88, consisting of $43,369.50 in attorneys’ fees; a 1.25 multiplier enhancement on the attorney fees (or $10,842.38); and an additional $4,000.00 for Plaintiff’s counsel to review Defendant’s opposition, prepare the reply; and attend the hearing on this motion.

      Defendant argues that the motion is untimely made, the requested fees are inflated and unreasonable, and Plaintiff should be awarded no more than $5000, which Defendant has paid. Defendant argues the hourly fee should be fixed at between $400/$300.

      The Court has considered the motion, opposition, reply, and all papers submitted in support.

II.     LEGAL STANDARDS

      A party prevailing on a Song-Beverly claim shall be allowed to recover fees, costs, and expenses as part of the judgment. (Civ. Code, § 1794 (d).) The court applies the lodestar method to determine a reasonable fee award, which requires a calculation of the number of hours reasonably spent multiplied by a reasonable hourly rate. The latter is based on the prevailing community rate for similar work. (Graciano v. Robinson Ford Sales, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 140.)

      The court may rely on his or her own experience and is given broad discretion in calculating reasonable attorney’s fees. (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132.) Ultimately, the trial court “is the best judge of the value of professional services rendered in its court” and its determination will not be disturbed unless it is “clearly wrong.” (Akins v. Enterprise Rent-A-Car Co. (2000) 79 Cal.App.4th 1127, 1134.)

III.    DISCUSSION

A.     The Code of Civil Procedure Section 998 offer and acceptance

      Plaintiff accepted the § 998 offer on January 30, 2024. (B. Mckinley decl., Ex. A. The salient terms are:

“No later than 45 days after Plaintiff accepts Defendant’s Offer to Compromise, Defendant will pay Plaintiff’s costs and expenses – including Plaintiff’s attorney’s fees – incurred in this action in the amount of $5,000.00; or, at Plaintiff’s option, pursuant to Cal. Civil Code §1794(d), Defendant will pay a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses – including Plaintiff’s attorney's fees based on actual time expended – reasonably incurred by Plaintiff in connection with the commencement and prosecution of this action as determined and ordered by the Court pursuant to a noticed motion filed by Plaintiff; …” (Ex. A,  ¶ 2.)

 

“A judgment will not be entered in this action; rather, Plaintiff’s claims against

Defendant shall be dismissed with prejudice after completion of the terms set forth herein.” (Id. ¶ 7.)

 

       

      Plaintiff filed a notice of settlement on February 25, 2024. Settlement was conditional upon completion of the terms of settlement, and a dismissal would be filed April 21, 2024. (NOS 2.25.24). On February 10, 2025, the court dismissed the action without prejudice.

      The parties' accepted Code of Civil Procedure section 998 offer expressly provided that Defendant would pay Plaintiff's attorney's fees either in the amount of $5,000 or, at Plaintiff's election, in an amount "determined and ordered by the Court pursuant to a noticed motion filed by Plaintiff." The offer further provided that "[a] judgment will not be entered in this action; rather, Plaintiff's claims against Defendant shall be dismissed with prejudice after completion of the terms set forth herein." Thus, unlike the ordinary case in which dismissal follows immediately upon settlement, the parties expressly contemplated a future noticed motion regarding attorney's fees and conditioned dismissal with prejudice upon completion of all settlement terms.

      Defendant contends Hatlevig v. General Motors LLC (2026) 118 Cal.App.5th 644 controls. Plaintiff should have filed the motion within 180 days of dismissal. In Hatlevig, the clerk served notice stating the case would be dismissed on August 15, 2024, unless good cause why dismissal should not be entered was shown. As no one showed good cause, the case was dismissed on August 15, 2024. The Hatlevig court held that August 15, 2024, was the date that started the clock on Plaintiff’s motion for attorney’s fees. Plaintiff had 180 days to serve and file the motion from that date. (Hatlevig at 650.)

      Therefore, Defendant argues that Plaintiff had 180 days from February 25, 2025, to file and serve this motion. The eCourt file states that this matter was dismissed on February 25, 2025, but no such minute order appears. The 180th day elapsed (from February 10, 2025) on August 9, 2025 (Saturday). The next business day to file and serve a fee motion was Monday, August 11, 2025. This motion was filed April 23, 2026.

      As Plaintiff argues, this case is distinguishable from Hatlevig because the parties’ settlement agreement here stated that dismissal with prejudice would be entered after completion of the terms of dismissal. Plaintiff’s reply brief cites  Madrigal v. Hyundai Motor America (2023) 90 Cal.App.5th 385. In that case the parties entered into a stipulated settlement which provided as follows:

 "(1) Hyundai would pay plaintiffs $39,000; (2) there would be no surrender of the vehicle because plaintiffs no longer owned it; (3) plaintiffs would release Hyundai and the selling dealership of any claims that arise out of or [relate] to the facts and circumstances described in the complaint or relating to the sale of the vehicle or relating to the service and repair history of the subject vehicle; (4) the settlement would be subject to section 664.6; and (5) plaintiffs could seek fees by motion, to be filed within six months of the date of settlement. Hyundai's counsel added that plaintiffs’ counsel wanted to make sure that the payment be made and then ... dismissal with prejudice would only happen after payment of the restitution to the Plaintiffs and the fees which will be [by] motion [for] fees and costs." (Madrigal at 392-93.)

 

      The Madrigal court construed the term ‘judgment” for purposes of section 998 “to include a dismissal with prejudice, like the one agreed to by the parties in this case.") While Madrigal  determined a different issue, in reaching that conclusion, the court had to construe what “judgment” meant according to the section 998 offer, and since the case had not been dismissed with prejudice, the court should have considered the plaintiff’s fee motion and request for costs. (Id.at 397.) Accordingly, this case is more analogous to Madrigal v. Hyundai Motor America than to Hatlevig v. Board of Trustees of California State University. Under the parties' agreement, a future fee motion was not merely possible; it was expressly contemplated by the settlement itself.
      A final dismissal with prejudice has not been entered in this case. The offer that Plaintiff accepted shows the parties’ mutual intent that a dismissal with prejudice would not be entered until Plaintiff’s entitlement to fees and costs was determined by the court.

B.      Standards for determining a reasonable fee.

      The trial court makes an initial determination of the actual time spent and whether under all the circumstances of the case the amount of time spent and the charge being made for that time is reasonable. (Tidrick v. FCA US LLC (2025) 112 Cal.App.5th 1147, 1158.) If the court determines the time spent and the charge made for that time are not reasonable under all the circumstances, then the court “must take this into account and award attorney fees in a lesser amount." (Id. at 1158.)

      The prevailing party is entitled to “compensation for all the hours reasonably spent in litigating the action to a successful conclusion. (Ibid., italics in original.) ‘Reasonably spent’ means that time spent ‘in the form of inefficient or duplicative efforts is not subject to compensation.”(Horsford v. Board of Trustees of California State University (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 359, 394 [italics in original].)

      The court has discretion to apply a reduction in hours for duplicative and/or excessive billing. Proper factors to consider in applying a negative reduction are the lack of complexity, that the matter did not go to trial, that name partners were doing work that could have been done by lower-billing attorneys, and that all the attorneys were doing work that could have been done by paralegals. (Morris v. Hyundai Motor America (2019) 41 Cal.App.5th 24, 41.)

In setting an reasonable hourly rate, "the court may rely on its own knowledge and familiarity with the legal market, as well as the experience, skill, and reputation of the attorney requesting fees [citation], the difficulty or complexity of the litigation to which that skill was applied [citations], and affidavits from other attorneys regarding prevailing fees in the community and rate determinations in other cases." (Morris at 41.)

      The court can reduce the rates based on excessive time spent on a “not so complex case.” (Morris at 39.) Reasons for reduction could include that the matter did not go to trial, "that the name partners were doing work that could have been done by lower-billing attorneys; and that all the attorneys were doing work that could have been done by paralegals.” (Morris at 41.)

      The procedural history remains highly relevant to the reasonableness of the fees requested. On February 10, 2025, Plaintiff's counsel represented to the Court that the settlement agreement provided for retention of jurisdiction pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6. Based upon that representation, the Court dismissed the action without prejudice and ordered Plaintiff to file a written request for dismissal. Plaintiff never complied with that order. Instead, on February 25, 2025, the parties filed a stipulation pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6 requesting that the Court retain jurisdiction to enforce the settlement. The stipulation and order are silent regarding dismissal and did not comply with the Court's directive that Plaintiff file a written request for dismissal.
      Moreover, settlement funds had been paid by May 2024, yet Plaintiff did not file the present motion until April 23, 2026—nearly two years after payment of the settlement proceeds and approximately fourteen months after the Court dismissed the action without prejudice. Plaintiff offers no satisfactory explanation for this extraordinary delay. Although the Court will reach the merits of the motion under the circumstances presented by the parties' settlement agreement, Plaintiff's significant delay and failure to comply with the Court's prior orders are properly considered in determining the reasonableness of the fees sought.

D.     The time billed is not reasonable.

      The case was filed on March 25, 2022, Plaintiff filed notice of settlement on January 22, 2024. The case lingered from that time wherein hearings on orders to cause were scheduled and held until February 6, 2025, when the parties filed a stipulation and order for settlement pursuant to Civil Procedure § 664.6.

      Defendant takes issue with the time spent on discovery, a total of 25.4 hours preparing and analyzing discovery that in defense counsel’s and experience are routine. The record reflects that this was a routine Song-Beverly action resolved before depositions, expert discovery, dispositive motions, or trial. The written discovery propounded and responded to in this matter consisted primarily of standard, form discovery commonly utilized in Song-Beverly litigation. The Court finds the time expended excessive and duplicative for a matter of this nature and reduces the discovery-related time by 15.0 hours.

      After Plaintiff accepted the section 998 offer, Plaintiff billed 18.7 hours for repeatedly preparing for hearings on Orders to Show Cause for over one year. The repeated OSC proceedings were necessitated in substantial part by Plaintiff's own delay in concluding the case and Plaintiff's failure to comply with the Court's February 10, 2025, order directing Plaintiff to file a written request for dismissal. The Court will not require Defendant to bear the cost of attorney time attributable to Plaintiff's lack of diligence in completing the settlement process and bringing a timely fee application. The OSC re: Dismissal proceedings were routine, non-complex calendar matters requiring minimal preparation. The Court finds that no more than two total hours of attorney time were reasonably necessary for all work associated with the OSC re: Dismissal proceedings. Accordingly, the Court reduces the 18.7 hours billed for post-settlement and OSC-related work by 16.7 hours.
      After deducting 31.7 hours billed, the court finds that 82.4 hours billed (including the fee motion) is reasonable for a total fee award of $34,525.60 at the blended rate (82.4 x 419.)

C.  The case does not warrant a multiplier.

      The court may consider the following factors to augment or diminish the lodestar: (1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, (4) the contingent nature of the fee award. (Id. [“the court determines, retrospectively, whether the litigation involved a contingent risk or required extraordinary legal skill justifying augmentation of the unadorned lodestar in order to approximate the fair market rate for such services.”].)

      Plaintiffs have not shown that case was so complex to warrant a multiplier.

IV.   CONCLUSION

      Accordingly, Plaintiff’s motion is GRANTED. The Court awards attorney's fees of $34,525.60.

Case Number: 24STCV29478    Hearing Date: June 30, 2026    Dept: 414

24STCV29478 Willie Winston v. Allstate Insurance Company

Tuesday, June 30, 2026

 

[TENTATIVE] ORDER OVERRULING DEMURRER TO SECOND AMENDED COMPLAINT

[TENTATIVE] ORDER DENYING MOTION TO STRIKE PORTIONS OF PLAINTIFF’S SECOND AMENDED COMPLAINT

 

I.         BACKGROUND

      The second amended complaint (“SAC”) alleges that Plaintiff suffered property damage and made a claim with Defendant, Plaintiff’s insurer. Allstate Insurance Company allegedly promised to pay for the replacement and repair cost for the damage. Plaintiff alleges Defendant intentionally undervalued and underpaid the claim. The second amended complaint alleges claims for (1) breach of contract, (2) fraud, and (3) breach of the implied duty of good faith and fair dealing (“bad faith.”). Plaintiff seeks recovery of punitive damages.

      Defendant demurs to the entire SAC on grounds it is barred by the statute of limitations. Defendant demurs to the second and third causes of action for failure to state facts to support the elements of each claim. Defendant separately moves to strike the claim for punitive damages.

      The Court has considered the motions, oppositions, and replies to both motions and the requests for judicial notice.

II.        LEGAL STANDARDS

      A demurrer tests the sufficiency of a complaint as a matter of law and raises only questions of law. (Schmidt v. Foundation Health (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 1702, 1706.) In testing the complaint’s sufficiency, the court must assume the truth of the properly pleaded factual allegations as well as facts that can be reasonably inferred from those expressly pleaded facts. The court may also consider matters properly subject to judicial notice. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

      The court may not consider contentions, deductions, or conclusions of fact or law. (Moore v. Conliffe (1994) 7 Cal.4th 634, 638.) Plaintiff is required to allege facts sufficient to establish every element of each cause of action. (Rakestraw v. California Physicians Service (2000) 81 Cal.App.4th 39, 43.) Where the complaint fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action, courts should sustain the demurrer. Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10(e); (Zelig v. County of Los Angeles (2002) 27 Cal.4th 1112, 1126.)

      Sufficient facts are the essential facts of the case “with reasonable precision and with particularity that is sufficiently specific to acquaint the defendant with the nature, source, and extent of his cause of action.” (Gressley v. Williams (1961) 193 Cal.App.2d 636, 643-644.)  Whether the Plaintiff will be able to prove the pleaded facts is irrelevant. (Stevens v. Superior Court (1986) 180 Cal.App.3d 605, 609–610.)

      A demurrer may also be sustained if a complaint is “uncertain.” Uncertainty exists where a complaint’s factual allegations are so confusing, they do not sufficiently apprise a defendant of the issues it is being asked to meet. (Williams v. Beechnut Nutrition Corp. (1986) 185 Cal.App.3d 135, 139, fn. 2; Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10(f).)

      A pleading is required to assert general allegations of ultimate fact. Evidentiary facts are not required. (Quelimane Co. v. Stewart Title Guaranty Co. (1998) 19 Cal. 4th 26, 47; Lim v. The.TV Corp. Internat. (2002) 99 Cal. App. 4th 684, 690.)

III.       DISCUSSION

A.     Demurrer to the fraud claim is OVERRULED.

      This claim is based on affirmative misrepresentations. A claim for fraud requires facts to support the following elements: (1) a misrepresentation, (2) made with knowledge of its falsity, (3) Defendant intended to defraud Plaintiff, i.e., induce Plaintiff’s reliance, (4) Plaintiff justifiably relied on the misrepresentation, (5) causing damage. (Nagy v. Nagy (1989) 210 Cal.App.3d 1262, 1268.)

      Fraud claims are subject to strict requirements of particularity in pleading. (Id.) The particularity requirements necessitate pleading facts showing “how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were tendered." (Stansfield v. Starkey (1990) 220 Cal.App.3d 59, 73.) The requirement “applies not only to the alleged misrepresentation, but also to the elements of causation and damage." (Moncada v. West Coast Quartz Corp. (2013) 221 Cal.App.4th 768, 776.)

      Where fraud is alleged against a corporation, the pleading standards are even greater. Plaintiff must allege “the names of the persons who made the allegedly fraudulent representations, their authority to speak, to whom they spoke, what they said or wrote, and when it was said or written." (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal. 4th 631, 645.)

      This claim is adequately alleged with specificity. The alleged misrepresentations were made between February 17, 2022, and February 23, 2022, and consist of the written estimates, replacement costs, and other items given. Plaintiff attaches all the written estimates. Plaintiff describes precisely how the representations were false namely that the actual losses exceeded the estimates. (SAC Att. FR-2b.) Plaintiff relied on the misrepresentations and paid premiums, costs, and repairs. (Id. Att. FR-5) Plaintiff suffered damages. (Id. Att. FR-6.)

B.     Demurrer to the third cause of action for breach of the implied duty of good faith and fair dealing (bad faith) is OVERRULED.

       The prerequisite for any action for breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing is the existence of a contractual relationship between the parties, since the covenant is an implied term in the contract." (Smith v. City and County of San Francisco (1990) 225 Cal.App.3d 38, 49.) Plaintiff can recover in tort on a contract claim in limited circumstances: “where a breach of duty directly causes physical injury [citation]; for breach of the covenant of good faith and fair dealing in insurance contracts [citation]; for wrongful discharge in violation of fundamental public policy [citation]; or where the contract was fraudulently induced.” (Robinson Helicopter Co., Inc. v. Dana Corp. (2004) 34 Cal.4th 979, 989–990.)

      Plaintiff has adequately alleged the existence of a contract as well as that the benefits due under the policy were wrongfully withheld, consisting of low estimates in relation to the loss suffered. (Love v. Fire Ins. Exchange (1990) 221 Cal.App.3d 1136, 1151.)

      Defendant argues that Plaintiff incorrectly alleges that the third-party public adjusters were Defendant’s agents which were not as shown by the attachments. This is not fatal to the claim, which depends on the factual elements as discussed.

      Defendant argues that Plaintiff has not alleged an independent duty on which the tort claim can be based. There is an exception for insurance contracts. (Chu v. Old Republic Home Protection Co., Inc. (2021) 60 Cal.App.5th 346, 354 ["Because the covenant of good faith and fair dealing essentially is a contract term that aims to effectuate the contractual intentions of the parties, ‘compensation for its breach has almost always been limited to contract rather than tort remedies.’ [Citations.]  This court recognizes only one exception to that general rule: tort remedies are available for a breach of the covenant in cases involving insurance policies. [Citations.] In the insurance policy setting, an insured may recover damages not otherwise available in a contract action, such as emotional distress damages resulting from the insurer's bad faith conduct [citation] and punitive damages if there has been oppression, fraud, or malice by the insurer [citation].”].)

      As noted, the fraud claim is adequately alleged.

C.     Defendant has not shown that the claims are barred by the statute of limitations.

      Defendant argues the action should have commenced 12 months after the inception of the loss. (Ins. Code, § 2071 ["No suit or action on this policy for the recovery of any claim shall be sustainable in any court of law or equity unless all the requirements of this policy shall have been complied with, and unless commenced within 12 months next after inception of the loss."].)

      The purpose of the shortened limitations period under Ins. Code § 2071 is to "relieve insurance companies of the burden imposed by defending old, stale claims.” (Aliberti v. Allstate Ins. Co. (1999) 74 Cal.App.4th 138, 145.) Plaintiff is not suing for action on the policy; Plaintiff is alleging separate claims arising from breach of contract, fraud, and breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing.

      The statute of limitations for a fraud claim is three years. (Code Civ. Proc., § 338.). (Code Civ. Proc., § 337.) While Plaintiff alleges the dates when the misrepresentations were made, the statute of limitations on fraud claims "is not deemed to have accrued until the discovery, by the aggrieved party, of the facts constituting the fraud or mistake.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 338 (d).) The dates of the estimates on which Plaintiff relied are not the dates of accrual.

      In order for the court to sustain demurrer to the complaint based on a statute of limitations defect, “the defect must clearly and affirmatively appear on the face of the complaint. A demurrer will not lie where the action may be but is not necessarily barred.” (Citizens for a Responsible Caltrans Decision v. Department of Transportation (2020) 46 Cal.App.5th 1103, 1117; Marshall v. Gibson, Dunn & Crutcher (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 1397, 1398.)

D.     Defendant’s motion to strike punitive damage allegations

      A plaintiff may recover on a claim for exemplary damages where the defendant is guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice. (Civ. Code, § 3294 subd. (a).) The predicate acts to support the claim must be intended to cause injury or must constitute “malicious” or “oppressive” conduct as defined by statute. “Malice” is defined as “conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.” (Civ. Code, § 3294 subd. (c)(1); College Hospital Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 725 ["malice involves awareness of dangerous consequences and a willful and deliberate failure to avoid them"].) "Oppression" is defined as “despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person's rights.” (Civ. Code, § 3294 subd. (a) subd. (c)(2).)

      Absent an intent to injure the plaintiff, ‘malice’ requires more than a ‘willful and conscious’ disregard of the plaintiffs' interests. The conduct must be “despicable” which is a "powerful term that refers to circumstances that are ‘base,’ ‘vile,’ or ‘contemptible.’" (College Hospital Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 725.)

      The fraud claim adequately provides predicate facts on which punitive damages can be recovered.

IV.       CONCLUSION

      Accordingly, the demurrer is OVERRULED. The motion to strike is DENIED. Defendant is ordered to file an answer within 20 days.

 


Case Number: 25STCV19487    Hearing Date: June 30, 2026    Dept: 414

25STCV19487 Eric Chairez v. FCA US LLC, et al.

Tuesday, June 30, 2026

 

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING PLAINTIFF’S MOTION TO COMPEL INITIAL DISCLOSURES AND FOR SANCTIONS OF $2,500

 

      This action alleges violations of the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act. Plaintiff moves to compel Defendant to comply with statute and serve documents.

      The statute at issue requires both parties to disclose documents 60 days after the answer or responsive pleading. (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26(b).). Defendant did not comply. (Breana Rucker  decl., ¶4.)

      The motion was timely served on Defendant.  Defendant's opposition was untimely filed. Nevertheless, a trial court retains discretion to consider late-filed papers in furtherance of resolving matters on their merits. (Carlton v. Quint (2000) 77 Cal.App.4th 690, 697; Bozzi v. Nordstrom, Inc. (2010) 186 Cal.App.4th 755, 765.) Trial courts possess broad authority in the management of their calendars and in determining whether consideration of untimely filings will assist in the just resolution of a dispute. (Hobson v. Raychem Corp. (1999) 73 Cal.App.4th 614, 625.)

      Exercising that discretion, the Court has considered Defendant's opposition both on the merits of the motion and in determining whether monetary sanctions should be imposed.

Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26(b) provides that "the parties" shall provide initial disclosures and documents pursuant to subdivisions (f), (g), and (h). Read as a whole, the statute imposes different disclosure obligations depending upon the party. Plaintiffs are required to provide the disclosures identified in subdivisions (f) and (g), while manufacturer defendants are required to provide the categories of documents identified in subdivision (h). Defendant's argument that it had no disclosure obligations under section 871.26 is inconsistent with the plain language of the statute because it would effectively read subdivision (h) out of the statutory scheme.

      Plaintiff's motion specifically seeks documents falling within subdivisions (h)(6), (h)(7), (h)(8), (h)(9), (h)(10), (h)(12), and (h)(13). The Court agrees that the complete warranty transaction history, including AutoPay and Trouble Not Found claims if they form part of that history, together with TSBs, ISBs, recalls, warranty policies and procedures, and service manuals, are categories expressly encompassed by section 871.26(h).

      The Court further concludes, however, that section 871.26(h) does not require production of every internal database, report, or electronic system that may contain information touching upon those categories. Accordingly, the Court declines to order production of a complete, unfiltered DealerCONNECT report or other internal databases except to the extent such materials themselves constitute or contain documents expressly required by section 871.26(h).

Defendant argues that Plaintiff seeks documents beyond those expressly enumerated in section 871.26(h), such as internal databases and DealerCONNECT reports. The Court agrees that there may be legitimate disputes concerning whether certain internal reports and databases are themselves subject to mandatory disclosure under section 871.26(h). However, that dispute does not excuse Defendant's failure to timely provide documents expressly required by the statute, including warranty transaction histories, TSBs, ISBs, recall information, warranty policies and procedures, and service manuals. A dispute regarding the outer boundaries of section 871.26(h) does not constitute good cause for noncompliance with categories that plainly fall within the statute.

      The Court therefore grants the motion to compel to the extent Plaintiff seeks documents expressly required by Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26(h)(6), (7), (8), (9), (10), (12), and (13), and otherwise denies the motion to the extent Plaintiff seeks production of internal databases, reports, or information not expressly encompassed by those subdivisions.

As to sanctions, the Court finds Defendant failed to demonstrate good cause for its failure to timely disclose categories of documents expressly required by section 871.26(h). Although there may be a legitimate dispute concerning whether certain internal databases and reports are subject to mandatory disclosure, Defendant's position as to those categories cannot be used to excuse its failure to comply with the statute's plainly enumerated disclosure obligations. Accordingly, monetary sanctions pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26(j) are warranted.

      Plaintiff's motion to compel compliance with Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26 is GRANTED IN PART and DENIED IN PART.  Within 15 days, Defendant shall produce any documents required by Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26(h)(6), (7), (8), (9), (10), (12), and (13) that have not already been produced. The motion is denied to the extent it seeks production of internal databases, reports, or materials not expressly required by section 871.26(h). The court imposes sanctions of $2,500 against Defendant’s counsel, payable to Plaintiff within 15 days.

 

 


Case Number: 26STCV03838    Hearing Date: June 30, 2026    Dept: 414

26STCV03838 Oxford International, Inc. v. Imanoel Davodpour, et al.

Tuesday, June 30, 2026

 

[TENTATIVE] ORDER TAKING OFF CALENDAR DEMURRER TO COMPLAINT (5241)

 

       Plaintiff timely filed a first amended complaint before opposition was due. The ninth day for filing an opposition was June 16, 2026. (Code Civ. Proc., § 472.)

      Accordingly, the demurrer is taken off calendar.
