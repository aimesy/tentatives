DEPARTMENT A15 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS

SUBMITTING ON A TENTATIVE RULING

A tentative ruling is not the final ruling of the Court. The Court may adopt, modify, or withdraw the tentative ruling at the scheduled hearing. Tentative rulings are not invitations or opportunities to file further documents related to the hearing. No such document will be considered by the Court.

If all parties wish to submit the matter without oral argument:

1. The parties must confer and confirm that every party agrees to submit.

2. One party—not each party separately—must send a single joint email to ATPdeptA15@lacourt.ca.gov no later than 8:00 a.m. on the date scheduled for the hearing.

3. The email must copy all other counsel and self-represented parties and use this subject line:

SUBMISSION — [CASE NUMBER] — [CASE NAME] — [HEARING DATE]

4. The email must state: “All parties have conferred and agree to submit the matter on the Court’s tentative ruling without oral argument. No party intends to appear. This email copies all counsel and self-represented parties.”

The email must identify the sender’s name, the party represented, and must include a telephone number for the sender.

Do not telephone the courtroom to submit on a tentative ruling. Do not send separate submission emails. Court staff will not acknowledge or confirm receipt.

If every party does not agree to submit, do not send a submission email. If all parties have not agreed to submit, any party wishing to be heard may appear at the scheduled hearing without giving advance notice to the Court.

A submission does not signify agreement with the tentative ruling. It means only that the parties do not request oral argument.

No party may submit another party’s position without that party’s express agreement.

If all parties submit and the tentative does not expressly require an appearance, the parties need not appear. The Court’s final ruling will be reflected in the minute order.

Emails are not part of the official court record. Do not use the Court's email address for any purpose other than submitting on a tentative ruling or as ordered by the Court. Do not copy the Court on emails among the attorneys, parties, or others. Do not use the Court's email address to ask questions regarding a case. 

The Court will not respond to emails submitted through the department's email address.

Inappropriate use or misuse of the Court's email address or violation of these or other rules may result in sanctions, including blocking receipt of emails by the violating sender, after the first misuse or violation. 

Case Number: 23AVCV01026    Hearing Date: September 17, 2026    Dept: A15

BEAD v. HAWATMEH et al.

Defendant Hayel Hawatmeh’s Motion for Summary Adjudication

Defendant Hayel Hawatmeh, doing business as Harry’s Mobil Mart (Harry’s), moves for summary adjudication of plaintiff Kemon Bead’s claim for punitive damages. The motion is scheduled for hearing on September 17, 2026, at 8:30 a.m. in Department A15. The Court has reviewed the moving, opposition, and reply papers, including the separate statements, declarations and exhibits, requests for judicial notice, and evidentiary objections and responses. The Court tentatively denies the motion.

DISPOSITION

1. Defendant Hayel Hawatmeh’s motion for summary adjudication of plaintiff’s claim for punitive damages is DENIED.

2. Defendant’s request for judicial notice of exhibits 1 through 6 is GRANTED as to the existence, filing, and contents of the court records, including the allegations made. Judicial notice does not establish the truth of disputed factual assertions in those records.

3. Plaintiff’s request for judicial notice of the federal agency materials attached as exhibits 4 through 7 and 9 to the declaration of Alex Farzan filed August 28, 2026, is GRANTED as to their existence and publication. The Court does not judicially notice the truth of disputed factual assertions, defendant’s receipt or knowledge of the publications, or the ingredients of the pill plaintiff allegedly ingested.

4. The Court does not rule on plaintiff’s evidentiary objections Nos. 1 through 4 or defendant’s evidentiary objections Nos. 1 through 7 because they are not material to the disposition. Those objections are preserved under Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (q).

REASONS

A. Background and governing standard

Plaintiff alleges that he purchased a Special Rhino Gold 18000 male-enhancement pill at Harry’s, ingested it, and suffered a prolonged erection and other complications requiring surgery. He seeks compensatory and punitive damages. (Second Amended Complaint, ¶¶ 1, 4-6, 15.) The present motion addresses only punitive damages against Hayel Hawatmeh. It does not request adjudication of the underlying causes of action or of the claims against New Rainbow LLC.

A claim for punitive damages may be resolved by summary adjudication if the motion completely disposes of that claim. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (f)(1).) Defendant bears the initial burden of showing that a necessary element cannot be established or that a complete defense applies. Only then must plaintiff demonstrate a triable issue. (Id., subd. (p)(2).) A defendant relying on an asserted absence of evidence must support that position with evidence; the defendant cannot shift the burden merely by arguing that plaintiff has not proved the claim. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 854-855.)

Civil Code section 3294, subdivision (a), requires clear and convincing evidence of oppression, fraud, or malice. The Court considers that heightened burden in deciding whether the evidence permits a reasonable trier of fact to find for plaintiff, without weighing credibility or resolving conflicting evidence. (Basich v. Allstate Ins. Co. (2001) 87 Cal.App.4th 1112, 1121; Butte Fire Cases (2018) 24 Cal.App.5th 1150, 1158-1159.) The heightened standard does not dispense with defendant’s initial showing.

Malice includes despicable conduct carried on with a willful and conscious disregard of another’s rights or safety. (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (c)(1).) Ordinary negligence, even serious negligence, does not suffice. The conduct must meet the statutory requirements of willfulness and despicability. (College Hospital Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 725.) Liability of an employer for an employee’s conduct also requires the showing specified in section 3294, subdivision (b), discussed below.

B. Whether Harry’s sold the pill remains disputed

Defendant’s declaration denies that Harry’s ever sold Special Rhino Gold 18000 and states that the store has no records of ordering or selling it. (Hawatmeh Decl., ¶ 9.) Shadi Hawatmeh likewise denied that the store sold the product. (Defendant’s Compendium of Evidence, Ex. B, Shadi Hawatmeh Dep. 22:4-15.) Plaintiff, however, testified that he purchased the pill at Harry’s and identified the product in photographs. He explained that he paid cash and did not receive a receipt. (Id., Ex. D, Bead Dep. 27:19-24, 33:14-34:4, 34:18-35:1, 35:11-18.)

That conflict cannot be resolved on this motion. Plaintiff’s firsthand account is evidence of the sale. The absence of a receipt and defendant’s contrary testimony and inventory evidence may bear on its weight, but do not conclusively disprove it. Even considering all of defendant’s challenged evidence, the Court would have to choose between competing accounts to find that Harry’s did not sell the pill. Summary adjudication cannot rest on that credibility determination. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c).)

C. The testimony about safety does not establish an absence of malice

Defendant emphasizes that Shadi had received no information that the pills contained sildenafil, had received no prior injury complaint, and had received no FDA notice concerning their harmful effects. That evidence supports defendant’s position, but it does not exhaust the record. Shadi testified that he managed the store, alone decided which products to purchase for resale, and had made no effort to learn the ingredients of the male-enhancement products. (Farzan Decl., filed Aug. 28, 2026, Ex. 1, Shadi Hawatmeh Dep. 14:15-24, 27:17-28:8, 29:20-30:2, 31:13-24.)

More significantly, Shadi testified that he did not believe the male-enhancement pills were safe for human consumption, but continued to sell them. Asked why, he answered, “People want them. So I buy them.” He also testified that he would continue to sell the pills while customers wanted them. (Farzan Decl., Ex. 1, Shadi Hawatmeh Dep. 30:11-31:12, 32:3-6.) A failure to investigate ingredients, standing alone, would not establish malice. A deliberate decision to sell products actually believed unsafe presents a different question. The testimony permits an inference of a conscious choice to expose customers to a perceived danger in order to continue sales.

There are limits to that inference. Shadi gave the testimony in June 2024, and did not expressly say that he held the same belief when plaintiff allegedly purchased the pill in May 2022. He also denied selling Special Rhino Gold 18000. His testimony therefore is not a direct admission that he knowingly sold this particular pill with a dangerous ingredient in May 2022. Shadi also testified that Harry’s sold male-enhancement pills at the time of the alleged May 2022 incident and continued to sell them. (Farzan Decl., Ex. 1, Shadi Hawatmeh Dep. 22:1-6, 28:22-25.) His description of those practices, considered with plaintiff’s testimony identifying the purchase, permits competing inferences concerning his awareness and conduct. Defendant’s evidence concerning ingredient knowledge, complaints, and FDA warnings does not establish that plaintiff cannot prove the asserted safety concern existed when the alleged sale occurred.

In G. D. Searle & Co. v. Superior Court (1975) 49 Cal.App.3d 22, 32-33, the court distinguished conscious disregard from allegations that a manufacturer knew unspecified products of the same type might cause injury. That distinction matters here. The Court does not infer malice merely because a retailer should have investigated or knew that products of this type might pose some risk. The pertinent evidence is Shadi’s professed belief that the products he chose to sell were unsafe for human consumption, coupled with his explanation for continuing those sales. If credited and found applicable to the alleged transaction, that evidence could support a finding of willful and despicable disregard under the clear-and-convincing standard. Lack of knowledge of the precise ingredient or absence of an earlier complaint does not conclusively negate that inference.

D. The moving showing does not resolve Hayel Hawatmeh’s liability as an individual employer

Shadi’s state of mind is not automatically Hayel’s. Civil Code section 3294, subdivision (b), limits an employer’s punitive liability for an employee’s conduct to circumstances in which the employer had advance knowledge of the employee’s unfitness and employed the employee with conscious disregard of others’ rights or safety, authorized or ratified the wrongful conduct, or was personally guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice. The additional officer, director, or managing-agent requirement in the subdivision’s second sentence concerns corporate employers. Hayel operates Harry’s as a sole proprietorship. (Hawatmeh Decl., ¶¶ 1-3.) Shadi’s management role therefore does not, by itself, establish Hayel’s punitive liability.

Plaintiff relies on Hayel’s delegation of purchasing authority to Shadi. But authorization to purchase inventory generally is not equivalent to knowing authorization or ratification of wrongful conduct. Ratification requires approval or adoption with knowledge of the conduct’s wrongful nature. (College Hospital Inc., supra, 8 Cal.4th at p. 726.) The Court does not find that delegation alone satisfies Civil Code section 3294, subdivision (b), or that plaintiff has established Hayel’s personal culpability.

The difficulty with defendant’s motion is the antecedent burden. Hayel’s declaration describes shared supervision and management, Shadi’s responsibility for selecting and purchasing products, and inventory decisions made by Hayel and others he hired, including Shadi. (Hawatmeh Decl., ¶¶ 5, 8, 10.) It does not deny advance knowledge of unfitness, knowing authorization or ratification of wrongful conduct, or personal malice. Its material factual denial is that the store ever sold the particular product. (Id., ¶ 9.) That denial is disputed for the reasons already explained.

Nor does defendant identify discovery establishing that plaintiff lacks and cannot reasonably obtain evidence of the employer connection required by Civil Code section 3294, subdivision (b). The moving evidence does not establish the absence of that connection merely by describing the division of ordinary business responsibilities. The reply correctly distinguishes an individual employer from a corporation, but a correct statement of the governing law does not substitute for the initial evidentiary showing. (Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at pp. 854-855.) Accordingly, shortcomings in plaintiff’s present proof of Hayel’s personal involvement do not independently warrant adjudication on this record. Plaintiff retains the burden of proving the statutory employer connection at trial.

E. The remaining arguments and evidentiary requests do not change the result

Defendant also relies on plaintiff’s earlier experience with a different male-enhancement product and prior litigation to contest plaintiff’s claimed ignorance of the risks and reliance on packaging representations. Even assuming that evidence is admissible and crediting defendant’s characterization for purposes of this motion, it does not eliminate the separately pleaded theory of malicious disregard for consumer safety. (Second Amended Complaint, ¶¶ 9-12.) The Court therefore need not decide whether plaintiff can prove fraud to determine that defendant has not established entitlement to adjudication of the entire punitive damages claim.

Plaintiff’s reliance on the earlier order permitting amendment likewise does not determine this motion. Permission to plead punitive damages does not establish evidentiary sufficiency under the summary adjudication standard. The present result rests on the parties’ evidentiary showing and the allocation of burdens under Code of Civil Procedure section 437c.

The court records and federal agency publications are judicially noticed only within the limits stated in the disposition. (Evid. Code, § 452, subds. (c), (d).) Their existence does not establish disputed facts, prove defendant saw an agency warning, or establish the contents of plaintiff’s pill. The denial does not depend on the truth of those publications or on the challenged packaging evidence. It also remains the same if all evidence challenged by plaintiff is considered. The identified evidentiary objections therefore are not material to the disposition, and the Court declines to rule on them under Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (q).

The material disputes concern whether Harry’s sold the pill and whether the sales conduct reflected the conscious disregard described above. In addition, defendant has not carried the initial burden necessary to shift to plaintiff the obligation to establish a triable issue concerning Hayel’s individual-employer liability. The motion is denied. This ruling does not determine that punitive damages will be recoverable at trial.

Case Number: 26AVCV00447    Hearing Date: September 17, 2026    Dept: A15

LOPEZ v. GENERAL MOTORS LLC

Plaintiff’s Demurrer to Answer

Plaintiff Breanna Lynn Lopez’s demurrer to the answer of defendant General Motors LLC (GM) is scheduled for hearing on September 17, 2026. The Court has considered the complaint, answer, notice of demurrer, supporting memorandum, and declaration of Astrid Souto with exhibits. Through September 16, 2026, the file contains no opposition or reply. The Court tentatively rules as follows.

DISPOSITION

1. The demurrer is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND as to the third, fourth, sixth, eighth, ninth, tenth, eleventh, thirteenth, fourteenth, fifteenth, seventeenth, and twenty-first affirmative defenses.

2. The demurrer is OVERRULED as to the second, fifth, seventh, twelfth, sixteenth, eighteenth, nineteenth, twentieth, twenty-second, twenty-third, and twenty-fourth affirmative defenses, subject to the scope explained below. This ruling determines pleading sufficiency, not the truth or ultimate merits of those defenses.

3. If this tentative ruling is adopted, GM may file and serve an amended answer within 20 days after service of notice of the Court’s ruling. The first affirmative defense is not challenged. GM’s general denial remains unaffected.

4. Plaintiff shall give notice of the ruling after it is adopted.

REASONS

A. Allegations and issues presented

The complaint alleges that plaintiff acquired a new 2025 Chevrolet Traverse on May 24, 2025, with express and implied warranties. The vehicle allegedly had electrical defects, including failure to start and failure to recognize the key fob. (Complaint, ¶¶ 6-9, 28.) Plaintiff alleges that she repeatedly presented the vehicle for repairs, but GM failed to conform it to the warranties after a reasonable number of opportunities. She also alleges that GM failed to provide restitution after a December 3, 2025, demand. (Complaint, ¶¶ 16-18, 23, 29-35.) She seeks restitution and damages, including incidental and consequential damages, and civil penalties for allegedly willful noncompliance with the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act. (Complaint, ¶¶ 35-37, 46-48, 63.)

GM’s answer contains a general denial and 24 separately labeled affirmative defenses. Plaintiff challenges defenses two through twenty-four, principally for failure to plead supporting facts or uncertainty. She separately challenges contributory negligence, the statutory specificity of the limitations defense, and the legal availability of the qualified dispute-resolution defense. (Answer, pp. 1-7; Demurrer, p. 2; Notice of Demurrer, p. 2.)

B. Governing pleading standard

A plaintiff may demur to an answer that fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a defense or is uncertain. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.20, subds. (a), (b).) An answer may contain denials and new matter constituting a defense. (Code Civ. Proc., § 431.30, subd. (b).) New matter must be supported by ultimate facts; a defense consisting only of legal conclusions is insufficient. (FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 367, 384 (Nakashima).) Ultimate facts are sufficient without evidentiary detail, but the distinction does not permit bare conclusions. (Doe v. City of Los Angeles (2007) 42 Cal.4th 531, 550-551 & fn. 5.)

The fact-pleading requirement remains applicable. The Court of Appeal has held that conclusory affirmative defenses, including waiver and laches, cannot withstand a demurrer or judgment on the pleadings. (Westly v. California Public Employees’ Retirement System Bd. of Administration (2003) 105 Cal.App.4th 1095, 1118.) Discovery may develop a defense’s evidentiary support, but it does not dispense with the requirement to plead its ultimate facts.

An allegation controverting an element of plaintiff’s claim, however, is a denial rather than new matter. (Nakashima, supra, 231 Cal.App.3d at p. 383.) Because the complaint is unverified, GM may deny its allegations generally. (Code Civ. Proc., § 431.30, subd. (d).) Labeling a denial an affirmative defense does not create a requirement to plead an independent factual avoidance. The Court applies these distinctions to each challenged defense rather than treating all 23 defenses alike.

C. Defenses requiring amendment

Third, sixth, fourteenth, and fifteenth defenses. These defenses invoke mitigation, laches, estoppel, and waiver without identifying the conduct supporting them. The third does not identify a reasonable measure plaintiff failed to take or the resulting avoidable loss. The sixth asserts unreasonable delay and prejudice without describing either. The fourteenth identifies no conduct supporting estoppel, and the fifteenth identifies no relinquishment of a right. (Answer, pp. 2-3, 5.) These are conclusions requiring supporting ultimate facts.

Fourth and seventeenth defenses. The fourth combines negligence, unclean hands, fault, and assumption of risk without identifying the conduct supporting any asserted avoidance. The seventeenth likewise identifies neither the particular risk nor the conduct by which plaintiff allegedly assumed it. (Answer, pp. 2-3, 6.) Li v. Yellow Cab Co. (1975) 13 Cal.3d 804, 829, replaced contributory negligence as a complete bar in negligence actions with comparative negligence; it does not establish that every concept mentioned in the fourth defense is categorically unavailable. The pleading nevertheless lacks supporting facts. Leave to amend permits GM to state any factually and legally supportable defense.

Eighth, ninth, tenth, and thirteenth defenses. The eighth asserts intervening third party fault and proportional reduction without identifying the conduct or actors involved. The ninth alleges misuse without describing the misuse. The tenth refers to changes or alterations without identifying them. The thirteenth alleges an unintended use without stating what that use was. (Answer, pp. 3-5.) As affirmative matters offered to bar or reduce recovery, these allegations do not give a factual account of the asserted avoidance. Amendment is required. GM remains free under its general denial to dispute that a covered defect or its own conduct caused plaintiff’s claimed loss.

Eleventh defense. GM alleges that it disclaimed all express and implied warranties but identifies no disclaimer, its terms, or the circumstances making it applicable. (Answer, p. 4.) The Court sustains the factual pleading challenge with leave to amend; it does not decide the enforceability of a disclaimer that has not been identified.

Twenty-first defense. This defense states only that the implied-warranty claims are barred by Civil Code section 1791.1, subdivision (c). (Answer, p. 7.) That provision addresses the duration of the implied warranty; it is not itself a deadline for filing suit. The answer alleges no facts explaining how the warranty’s duration defeats the pleaded claim. Merely naming the statute supplies no intelligible factual defense. Leave to amend is appropriate.

D. Defenses that survive the demurrer

Fifth defense. Code of Civil Procedure section 458 permits a limitations defense to be pleaded by identifying the applicable statute and subdivision without alleging the supporting facts. (Brown v. World Church (1969) 272 Cal.App.2d 684, 691.) Contrary to plaintiff’s blanket objection, GM identifies subdivisions for several listed Code of Civil Procedure provisions. (Answer, p. 3; Memorandum in Support of Demurrer, p. 11.) Its additional reference to California Uniform Commercial Code section 2725 omits a subdivision, and the catchall reference identifies no statute. Those deficiencies do not, by themselves, establish that the entire separately stated defense fails. The Court overrules the pleading challenge without determining which limitations provision ultimately applies or whether any claim is time barred.

Second, seventh, twelfth, and twenty-second defenses. These allegations dispute, respectively, whether plaintiff allowed a reasonable opportunity to cure, performed required conditions, suffered damage caused by GM, and received proper repairs. (Answer, pp. 2-4, 7.) They controvert allegations in the complaint and may stand as denials. (Complaint, ¶¶ 15-18, 30-37.) The Court does not construe the reference to consent in the twenty-second defense as an independent waiver of statutory rights.

Sixteenth defense. Unlike the bare labels discussed above, this defense describes alleged conduct: continued operation despite a known need for repairs, failure to obtain needed repairs, and improper service or maintenance that allegedly caused or aggravated the claimed damage. (Answer, pp. 5-6.) Those allegations provide sufficient ultimate facts to meet the challenge presented. The ruling permits GM to dispute causation and the extent of compensable loss. It does not hold that ordinary continued use alone defeats a warranty claim or that tort comparative fault percentages necessarily govern statutory restitution.

Eighteenth defense. GM alleges that it maintains a qualified dispute resolution process, gave plaintiff timely written notice of its availability, and that plaintiff did not use it. (Answer, p. 6.) A qualifying program has legal significance for penalties under Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (e)(2), and resort to the process may condition use of the repair presumption under Civil Code section 1793.22, subdivision (c). The defense therefore is not categorically unavailable. The operative presumption is in subdivision (b), subject to subdivision (c), rather than subdivision (e)(1), as the answer states. These provisions do not establish a general bar to this action or to penalties under Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (c).

Nineteenth and twenty-fourth defenses. These defenses dispute plaintiff’s entitlement to civil penalties, including her allegation of willfulness. (Answer, pp. 6-7; Complaint, ¶¶ 35, 63.) A reasonable, good-faith belief that the facts requiring replacement or restitution were absent may negate willfulness. (Kwan v. Mercedes-Benz of North America, Inc. (1994) 23 Cal.App.4th 174, 185-186.) The defenses may stand as denials of the penalty claim; a bare belief that conduct was lawful does not establish immunity from liability. The twenty-fourth defense’s conditional reference to a repurchase offer is not a factual allegation that GM made one, and the Court makes no such finding.

Twentieth defense. This defense identifies the statutory use reduction under Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (d)(2)(C). (Answer, p. 7.) It disputes the amount of recoverable restitution under a specified statutory formula and sufficiently identifies the adjustment sought. The amount and factual basis of any reduction remain to be established.

Twenty-third defense. GM alleges that plaintiff did not provide the notice required by Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (e)(3). (Answer, p. 7.) That is an identifiable factual contention, although plaintiff alleges a prelitigation demand. (Complaint, ¶ 23.) The conflict cannot be resolved on demurrer. The statutory consequence concerns subdivision (e) penalties. Failure to give that particular notice does not itself eliminate the repair presumption or bar a willfulness penalty under subdivision (c).

The surviving defenses are sufficiently intelligible to identify the matters disputed. Their evidentiary particulars may be developed in discovery. The Court therefore also overrules the uncertainty objections to those defenses. The defects in the other defenses concern omitted facts that GM may be able to supply, warranting leave to amend rather than a determination that those defenses can never apply.
