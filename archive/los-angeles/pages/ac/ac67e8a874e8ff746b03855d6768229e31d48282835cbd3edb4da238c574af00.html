DEPARTMENT 515 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 26STCV13940    Hearing Date: September 22, 2026    Dept: 515

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: Hearing on Motion to Compel Arbitration

The Motion to Compel Arbitration is granted, and the action is stayed pending arbitration.

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Stashhh Loot, LLC (Plaintiff) filed this action against Whatnot, Inc. (Whatnot) and John Walters (Walters), alleging that Whatnot withheld cash compensation and equity promised in exchange for promotional, consulting, tradeshow, and warehouse-management services, and afterward exploited a confidential concept Plaintiff had developed on its own.

The causes of action are: (1) Breach of Contract; (2) Breach of the Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing; (3) Fraud (Intentional Misrepresentation); (4) Breach of Confidence; and (5) Specific Performance.

Whatnot filed a Motion to Compel Arbitration. Plaintiff filed an Opposition.

 

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

Plaintiff requests judicial notice of the Notice of Errata filed in this action on May 21, 2026 (Errata), which attaches two agreements the Complaint identified as exhibits and omitted. The request is granted. (Evid. Code, §§ 452, subd. (d), 453.) Both sides treat the attachments as the agreements the parties executed, and both rely on them.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.2 provides:

“On petition of a party to an arbitration agreement alleging the existence of a written agreement to arbitrate a controversy and that a party to the agreement refuses to arbitrate that controversy, the court shall order the petitioner and the respondent to arbitrate the controversy if it determines that an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists, unless it determines that: [¶] (a) The right to compel arbitration has been waived by the petitioner; or [¶] (b) Grounds exist for rescission of the agreement ....”

The moving party bears the burden of proving the existence of the agreement by a preponderance of the evidence, and the policy favoring enforcement “is insufficient to warrant imposing a higher burden on a party opposing arbitration.” (Rosenthal v. Great Western Fin. Securities Corp. (1996) 14 Cal.4th 394, 414.) Once existence is shown, the burden falls upon “the party opposing arbitration to demonstrate that an arbitration clause cannot be interpreted to require arbitration of the dispute.” (Coast Plaza Doctors Hospital v. Blue Cross of California (2000) 83 Cal.App.4th 677, 686-687.) Arbitration is upheld “unless it can be said with assurance that an arbitration clause is not susceptible to an interpretation covering the asserted dispute.” (Id. at p. 686, quoting Bos Material Handling, Inc. v. Crown Controls Corp. (1982) 137 Cal.App.3d 99, 105.)

That policy does not override ordinary contract interpretation, and “the terms of the specific arbitration clause under consideration must reasonably cover the dispute as to which arbitration is requested.” (Ahern v. Asset Management Consultants, Inc. (2022) 74 Cal.App.5th 675, 688 (Ahern).) A contract is interpreted to give effect to the mutual intention of the parties as it existed at the time of contracting (Civ. Code, § 1636), the whole of a contract is taken together so that each clause helps interpret the other (Civ. Code, § 1641), and however broad the terms of a contract, “it extends only to those things concerning which it appears that the parties intended to contract” (Civ. Code, § 1648).

 

ANALYSIS

I. The Agreements

Several contracts bear on this motion, and three of them address where a dispute between these parties will be resolved.

Plaintiff and Whatnot first documented their services bargain in a signed schedule of work and fees with a starting date of February 1, 2023, which set out $620,000 in cash and $50,000 in equity and contemplated that a binding contract would follow. (Errata, Ex. A.) They then executed a Master Services Agreement (MSA) effective February 1, 2023, retaining Whatnot as the customer and Plaintiff as the service provider, together with a Statement of Work (SOW) effective the same day and running through March 31, 2024. (Errata, Ex. B, MSA, preamble; SOW, preamble & § 3.) The Statement of Work repeats the same services and consideration, and it provides that Whatnot will recommend to its board an option to purchase 308 shares of Class A Common Stock vesting in 12 monthly installments. (Errata, Ex. B, SOW, §§ 1, 2 & 4.) It also requires Plaintiff to host a minimum of three livestreams per week on Whatnot’s platform, to keep a minimum of 100 different products available in the Whatnot marketplace, and to refrain from selling on competing platforms. (Errata, Ex. B, SOW, § 1.) Neither the Master Services Agreement nor the Statement of Work contains an arbitration provision. Section 20 of the MSA provides that “[a]ny action arising from or relating to this Agreement will be filed only in a federal or state court located in Los Angeles, California,” that the parties irrevocably consent and submit to the personal jurisdiction of those courts, and that neither will claim that a court in that forum is an improper or inconvenient venue. (Errata, Ex. B, MSA, § 20.)

Six months later the parties executed the Purchase of Future Revenue Agreement (Revenue Agreement), dated as of July 15, 2023 and signed on August 4, 2023 by Plaintiff’s President. (Hirsch Decl., ¶ 17 & Ex. D, Order Form.) Whatnot bought a set amount of Plaintiff’s future sales revenue for $450,000, recouped through an additional 3% commission charged against Plaintiff’s merchant account on every item Plaintiff sold on the Whatnot platform until Whatnot recovered the price or four years passed. (Hirsch Decl., Ex. D, Order Form.) The Order Form also provides that Plaintiff will not sell during the term on platforms Whatnot deems competitive. (Ibid.) Section 20 provides that the parties “agree to arbitrate any and all claims and disputes relating in any way to this Agreement or the parties’ dealings with one another (‘Claims’), except for Claims concerning the validity, scope or enforceability of this Arbitration Agreement, through BINDING INDIVIDUAL ARBITRATION,” designates the American Arbitration Association, and gave Plaintiff 60 days after signing to reject the arbitration agreement in writing. (Hirsch Decl., Ex. D, § 20.) Plaintiff did not do so. (Hirsch Decl., ¶ 18.) Section 22 provides that the Revenue Agreement, its exhibits and addenda, and Whatnot’s service terms of use and privacy policy “represents the entire understanding between the parties with respect to the subject matter hereof,” and that no provision “may be amended or waived except by a writing signed by the parties hereto.” (Hirsch Decl., Ex. D, § 22.)

Plaintiff opened an account on the Whatnot platform on or about May 25, 2020 and last used the account on June 25, 2026. (Hirsch Decl., ¶¶ 9, 14.) Each updated version of Whatnot’s Terms of Service has contained a mandatory arbitration provision, and the version in effect when Plaintiff last used the account was updated March 13, 2026. (Hirsch Decl., ¶¶ 13-14 & Ex. B.) Plaintiff did not opt out of section 21 of that version within the 30 days it allowed, and section 21 requires arbitration of “any dispute of any kind between you and Whatnot arising under or related to these Terms.” (Hirsch Decl., ¶ 16 & Ex. B, § 21 & subd. (a).)

Whatnot established two written agreements to arbitrate, and Plaintiff refuses to arbitrate. Plaintiff does not dispute that it assented to each provision, and it does not contend that either is unconscionable or otherwise unenforceable. (Reply, at p. 4.) Rather Plaintiff aruges that the scope of those provisions do not reach this action. Plaintiff adds that the MSA’s forum provision requires these claims to be filed in court.

II. Revenue Agreement

Whatnot contends that every cause of action falls within section 20 of the Revenue Agreement, which covers claims relating in any way to that agreement or to the parties’ dealings with one another.

Section 20 states two categories of covered claims in the alternative: those relating in any way to the Revenue Agreement, and those relating in any way to the parties’ dealings with one another. Plaintiff reads the second category as though it said, “the parties’ dealings with one another under this Agreement.” However, no such limitation appears.

The question is what “dealings with one another” meant to these parties in August 2023. By then Plaintiff had held a seller account on the Whatnot platform for more than three years, had contracted to provide Whatnot with tradeshow, warehouse, consulting, and livestream services, had agreed not to sell on competing platforms, and had bound itself to host three livestreams a week and keep 100 products in the Whatnot marketplace. (Hirsch Decl., ¶ 9; Errata, Ex. B, SOW, §§ 1-2.) The Revenue Agreement then financed the inventory Plaintiff sold on that platform, recouped its price through a commission on those sales, imposed a platform-exclusivity covenant of the same kind, and integrated Whatnot’s service terms of use. (Hirsch Decl., Ex. D, Order Form & § 22.) Against that background, “the parties’ dealings with one another” denotes, at least, the commercial course of dealing in which these two parties were already engaged.

Plaintiff essentially argues that it occupied two positions, serving Whatnot as a vendor under the Master Services Agreement and selling to the public as a merchant on Whatnot’s platform, so that the services relationship and the financing relationship were separate enterprises. The record does not separate this framing. The Statement of Work required Plaintiff to host three livestreams each week on Whatnot’s platform and to keep 100 different products available in the Whatnot marketplace, and those are acts Plaintiff performed as a merchant on the platform and owed Whatnot as the service it had sold. (Errata, Ex. B, SOW, § 1.) The Revenue Agreement financed the inventory those same sales moved and recouped its price from them. (Hirsch Decl., Ex. D, Order Form.) Section 20’s second category asks what these parties did with one another, not in which capacity either acted, and the two capacities here ran through the same conduct.

Here, each cause of action arises from that course of dealing. First, the contract, implied covenant, and specific performance counts seek the cash compensation, the $50,000 equity, and the stock option Whatnot promised in exchange for services Plaintiff performed for Whatnot. (Compl., ¶¶ 82, 84, 97, 128.) Second, Plaintiff alleges that Walters made representations on Whatnot’s behalf to induce Plaintiff to continue and expand its performance, among them that Whatnot would finance at least $7,500 of Plaintiff’s $8,000 monthly warehouse lease, honor the equity and the stock option, and collaborate on Plaintiff’s concepts. (Compl., ¶¶ 101-102.) Third, Plaintiff alleges that it disclosed its concept to Whatnot in confidence for the limited purpose of exploring a collaboration. (Compl., ¶¶ 114-115.) Each of these disputes concerns what Whatnot did in the course of that relationship.

That the fraud and breach of confidence counts sound in tort changes nothing. Broad arbitration clauses “are consistently interpreted as applying to extra-contractual disputes between the contracting parties.” (Khalatian v. Prime Time Shuttle, Inc. (2015) 237 Cal.App.4th 651, 660.)

Plaintiff further argues that its references to Whatnot’s $31,000 commission demand describe conduct and do not make the Revenue Agreement the source of any right it asserts, and it rests the point on the statement that “the evidence Marsch may choose to employ in proving his claims will not, by itself, demonstrate the existence of such an agreement.” (Compl., ¶¶ 61, 70-71, 96; Marsch v. Williams (1994) 23 Cal.App.4th 250, 257 (Marsch II).)

That would matter under the “claims and disputes relating in any way to this Agreement” prong of but fails as to the “parties’ dealings with one another” prong. In Marsch, a case distinguishable from this one, the plaintiff sued on the agreements of a La Jolla real estate partnership that contained no arbitration clause, and the defendant invoked the arbitration provision of a separate Horizon partnership formed thirteen years later among a different group. (Marsch II, supra, 23 Cal.App.4th at pp. 252-253.) A companion appeal decided the same day, from the judgment confirming the Horizon arbitration award, sets out the Horizon provision, which covered“[a]ny dispute, controversy or claim arising out of, or relating to, this Agreement or any alleged breach thereof.” (Marsch v. Williams (1994) 23 Cal.App.4th 238, 243, fn. 3 (Marsch I).)

Plaintiff’s reliance on Moritz v. Universal City Studios LLC (2020) 54 Cal.App.5th 238 is also misplaced. A producer there sued on an alleged oral agreement to make a film that the earlier written agreements did not concern, and those agreements were mentioned in the complaint only as historical background. (Id. at p. 246.) The clauses covered disputes arising out of or relating to the agreements themselves. Therefore, the court asked whether the dispute had “its real source in the contract.” (Ibid.) Section 20’s second category locates the source in the parties’ dealings, and this dispute has its source there. The Revenue Agreement also bears on it, financing the inventory Plaintiff sold while performing the services bargain and binding Plaintiff to the same platform exclusivity that bargain imposed. (Hirsch Decl., Ex. D, Order Form.)

Plaintiff invokes Ahern to the same end. Investors there sued over misrepresentations inducing their purchase of property interests, and the defendants invoked an arbitration clause in a separate cotenancy agreement governing management of the same property. The court held that the claims were not sourced in the cotenancy relationship but in the distinct purchase-and-sale relationship. (Ahern, supra, 74 Cal.App.5th at pp. 688-689, 692-693.) The clause that produced that result was, in the court’s own words, “particularly narrow, both omitting any general reference to disputes ‘related to’ the agreement and specifically providing for arbitration only of those disputes arising in connection with ‘the interpretation and enforcement of the provisions of the agreement.’” (Id. at p. 690.) Section 20 is not built that way, and its second category directs the inquiry to these parties’ dealings rather than to a single instrument.

Nor does Chan v. Drexel Burnham Lambert, Inc. (1986) 178 Cal.App.3d 632, 643, change the result. The canon stated there, that doubt about contract interpretation should be resolved in favor of preserving the jury trial right, governed whether an arbitration provision had been incorporated into the parties’ form at all. Plaintiff does not dispute that its President signed section 20 and that it let the 60-day rejection period pass. (Hirsch Decl., ¶ 18 & Ex. D, Order Form.)

Accordingly, section 20 of the Revenue Agreement covers each cause of action Plaintiff pleads against Whatnot, and those claims are ordered to arbitration before the American Arbitration Association as section 20 provides.

III. Forum Provision

Plaintiff argues that section 20 of the MSA requires any action arising from or relating to that agreement to be filed in a Los Angeles court, and that reading the Revenue Agreement to cover these claims would render the MSA’s provision superfluous, void, or inexplicable.

Section 20 of the MSA says where an action will be filed. It records the parties’ consent to personal jurisdiction in the courts it names, and it waives any objection to venue there. (Errata, Ex. B, MSA, § 20.) Nothing in it mentions arbitration, and nothing in it promises that a dispute will be adjudicated by the court in which the action is filed.

Section 20 of the Revenue Agreement excepts from arbitration “Claims concerning the validity, scope or enforceability of this Arbitration Agreement,” and a claim in that excepted class is filed in court. (Hirsch Decl., Ex. D, § 20.) This motion raises a claim in that class, and Whatnot brought it in the court the MSA designates. A petition to confirm, correct, or vacate an award goes to the same place, as does any claim the arbitration agreement does not cover.

Whatnot contends in the alternative that section 22 of the Revenue Agreement superseded the MSA’s forum provision. Section 22 provides that the Revenue Agreement, together with its exhibits and addenda and Whatnot’s service terms of use and privacy policy, “represents the entire understanding between the parties with respect to the subject matter hereof and supersedes all prior and contemporaneous understandings and agreements with respect to such subject matter.” (Hirsch Decl., Ex. D, § 22.)

How these parties would resolve disputes about their dealings with one another is part of the Revenue Agreement’s subject matter. Section 20 is a term of that Agreement, it fixes the forum for every claim it covers, and section 22 integrates Whatnot’s service terms of use into the Agreement alongside its own exhibits and addenda. An instrument that specifies the forum for disputes about the parties’ dealings, and whose integrated whole includes the terms governing Plaintiff’s use of the platform, has made dispute resolution one of the things it is about. Section 22 therefore supersedes the prior understanding on that subject, and section 20 of the MSA is that understanding.

Accordingly, the MSA’s forum provision does not defeat the parties’ agreement to arbitrate.

IV. Stay

Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.4 provides that where a court has ordered arbitration of a controversy at issue in a pending action, “the court in which such action or proceeding is pending shall, upon motion of a party to such action or proceeding, stay the action or proceeding until an arbitration is had in accordance with the order to arbitrate or until such earlier time as the court specifies.”

Accordinly, the action is ordered stayed pending arbitration.

CONCLUSION

The Motion to Compel Arbitration is granted, and the action is stayed pending arbitration.


Case Number: 26STCV22182    Hearing Date: September 22, 2026    Dept: 515

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: Hearing on Demurrer

The Demurrer is sustained in part and overruled in part, with 20 days leave to amend.

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Christopher Nelson Berndt (Plaintiff) filed this action against his sister, Cherilin Lee Berndt (Defendant), alleging that Defendant has held herself out as trustee of the Berndt Family Trust dated October 25, 2001 (the Trust) while their mother, Mary L. Berndt (Mary), remains its trustee and is still living.

The causes of action are: (1) Quiet Title; (2) Declaratory Relief; (3) Accounting; (4) Fraud; (5) Intentional Misrepresentation; and (6) Elder Abuse.

Defendant filed a Demurrer. Plaintiff filed an Opposition.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (Wilson v. Transit Authority of City of Sacramento (1962) 199 Cal.App.2d 716, 720-21.) In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) “‘A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or the facts alleged.’ [Citation.]” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Services (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, courts assume the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Ibid.) However, they do not accept as true deductions, contentions, or conclusions of law or fact. (Stonehouse Homes LLC v. City of Sierra Madre (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 531, 538.)

“The court may, upon a motion made pursuant to Section 435, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper: [¶] (a) [s]trike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading….” (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) “The grounds for a motion to strike shall appear on the face of the challenged pleading or from any matter of which the court is required to take judicial notice.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437, subd. (a).)

 

ANALYSIS

I. Subject Matter Jurisdiction

Defendant contends that the whole Complaint must be dismissed because it seeks adjudication of the internal affairs of the Trust, which Probate Code section 17000 commits to the exclusive jurisdiction of the probate court.

Plaintiff argues that this Court has subject matter jurisdiction over every cause of action pleaded, and that an internal affairs concern calls for transfer or coordination instead of dismissal.

A demurrer lies where “[t]he court has no jurisdiction of the subject of the cause of action alleged in the pleading.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (a).) Probate Code section 17000, subdivision (a), provides that “[t]he superior court having jurisdiction over the trust pursuant to this part has exclusive jurisdiction of proceedings concerning the internal affairs of trusts.” That jurisdiction over the trust arises from the trustee’s personal submission on accepting the trusteeship and from Code of Civil Procedure section 410.10. (Prob. Code, §§ 17003, 17004.) The court that holds it is a superior court of a county, identified by where the trust is administered and by the trustee’s residence. (David v. Hermann (2005) 129 Cal.App.4th 672, 683 [Marin County Superior Court had jurisdiction over a living trust whose trustee resided in that county].) Subdivision (b) gives that same court concurrent jurisdiction over actions to determine the existence of trusts, actions by or against creditors or debtors of trusts, and other actions involving trustees and third persons. “The superior court is divided into departments, including the probate department, as a matter of convenience; but the subject matter jurisdiction of the superior court is vested as a whole.” (Estate of Bowles (2008) 169 Cal.App.4th 684, 695.)

Defendant correctly identifies this Complaint as one that concerns the Trust’s internal affairs. Internal affairs “‘include modification of the terms of the trust, changes in a designated successor trustee, other deviation from trust provisions, authority over the trustee’s acts, or the administration of the trust’s financial arrangements.’” (Harnedy v. Whitty (2003) 110 Cal.App.4th 1333, 1345.) Plaintiff seeks a declaration concerning Defendant’s position as trustee and her duties, and an order compelling an accounting of the Trust. (Compl., ¶¶ 28, 34.) Both requests fall within that definition. The identification takes Defendant no further. Section 17000 places exclusive jurisdiction of proceedings concerning internal affairs in the superior court having jurisdiction over the trust, and that court is this Court.

Defendant asks in the alternative that the Court order the Complaint transferred to the probate department.

Distribution of this Court’s business among its departments rests with the Presiding Judge. (Gov. Code, § 69508, subd. (a); Cal. Rules of Court, rule 10.603(c)(1)(A).) Assignment among the divisions of this Court is governed by this Court’s own rule, which assigns to the Probate Division “[a]ny matter arising under the Probate Code, and any other action, proceeding, or procedure which by statute is to be heard by a judge hearing probate matters.” (Super. Ct. L.A. County, Local Rules, rule 2.13(a).) A demurrer is not the means of moving this action between divisions. Defendant filed no application or motion seeking reassignment, and an order sustaining or overruling a demurrer would neither assign this action to the Probate Division nor reassign it. The Court therefore does not decide whether this Complaint presents a matter arising under the Probate Code within the meaning of rule 2.13(a).

Accordingly, the Demurrer on the ground of lack of subject matter jurisdiction is overruled.

II. Another Action Pending

Defendant contends that an earlier action between the same parties, Los Angeles Superior Court case No. 25STCV30056, was filed first, remains pending, and involves the same Trust, trustee authority, properties, and primary rights. Defendant asks that this action be abated pending resolution of that one.

A demurrer lies where “[t]here is another action pending between the same parties on the same cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (c).) “[A] demurrer may be sustained on the ground that another cause of action is pending only when the parties in the two cases are identical and the cause of action and issues in the two actions are substantially the same.” (Childs v. Eltinge (1973) 29 Cal.App.3d 843, 848.) Where a demurrer on that ground is sustained and no other special defense is, “an interlocutory judgment shall be entered in favor of the defendant pleading the same to the effect that no trial of other issues shall be had until the final determination of that other action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 597.)

Here, Plaintiff’s allegations do not show that the two actions state the same causes of action. First, Plaintiff alleges that the earlier Los Angeles action is pending, that Defendant answered it claiming to be the trustee, and that the actions Plaintiff identifies concern the same Trust properties and the same claimed trustee authority. (Compl., ¶¶ 16(A), 17, 21(A), 22.) Second, the allegations that name causes of action describe this action and not the earlier one. (Compl., ¶¶ 17, 22.) Finally, Plaintiff does plead what another action seeks. He describes an Orange County action for quiet title, declaratory relief, fraud, intentional misrepresentation, and elder abuse as to the Trust properties, and that action names four defendants who are not parties here. (Compl., ¶¶ 16(C), 21(C).) The parties in those two cases are therefore not identical.

Grounds 1 and 2 are the only grounds Defendant directs at the declaratory relief and accounting causes of action. (Dem., at pp. 2-3.) Accordingly, the Demurrer on the ground of another action pending is overruled, and this action is not abated. The Demurrer to the Second and Third Causes of Action is overruled.

III. Quiet Title

Defendant contends that the First Cause of Action fails because Plaintiff pleads no legal description, identifies no instrument vesting present title in him, and claims an interest the Trust contradicts.

Plaintiff argues that the missing legal description is a technical defect and that he can plead the correct property and the correct basis of his interest.

A quiet title complaint “shall be verified” and must include a description of the property, which “[i]n the case of real property” must include “both its legal description and its street address or common designation, if any.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 761.020, subd. (a).) The complaint must also include “[t]he title of the plaintiff as to which a determination under this chapter is sought and the basis of the title.” (Id., subd. (b).) Where an exhibit to a pleading contradicts the pleading’s allegations, “the facts in the exhibits take precedence.” (Holland v. Morse Diesel Internat., Inc. (2001) 86 Cal.App.4th 1443, 1447.)

Here, Plaintiff insufficiently alleges a claim to quiet title. First, Plaintiff describes the property at 8617 Verdosa Drive in Whittier (the Whittier Property) by street address and gives no legal description. (Compl., ¶ 20.) Second, the basis of title Plaintiff pleads is that he “is permitted to reside at a home listed in the Trust” and is “permitted to remain at the Subject Property during his lifetime.” (Compl., ¶¶ 9, 23.) Third, the Trust attached to the Complaint gives Plaintiff a rent-free right to occupy 1331 Orangewood Street in La Habra, not the Whittier Property, and it does so only after the death of the survivor of the two Grantors. (Compl., Ex. A, § 8.01, at p. 8-1.) Plaintiff acknowledges the omission and the terms of that provision. (Opp., at p. 11:7-15.) Finally, the Trust directs the trustee to sell the Whittier Property as soon as practicable after the death of Larry L. Berndt, “even if [Mary] is still living,” and Plaintiff alleges that Larry L. Berndt has died. (Compl., Ex. A, § 8.03, at p. 8-4 [captioned “Section 6.03”]; Compl., ¶ 9.) Thus, the facts in the Trust take precedence over the contrary allegation about the Whittier Property.

Accordingly, the Court sustains the Demurrer to the First Cause of Action with leave to amend.

IV. Fraud

Defendant contends that the Fourth and Fifth Causes of Action plead no representation with the required specificity and no reliance by Plaintiff.

Plaintiff argues that the defect is one of specificity and is curable by amendment.

The elements of fraud are: (1) “misrepresentation (false representation, concealment, or nondisclosure)”; (2) “knowledge of falsity (or ‘scienter’)”; (3) “intent to defraud, i.e., to induce reliance”; (4) “justifiable reliance”; and (5) “resulting damage.” (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 638.) Each element must be pleaded with particularity. That requirement “necessitates pleading facts which ‘show how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were tendered.’” (Id. at p. 645.)

Here, Plaintiff insufficiently alleges fraud. First, Plaintiff alleges that Defendant represented to “Plaintiff, various courts and individuals” that she owned the Trust Property and other Trust properties, and that she “made many representations that were false” in writing and orally. (Compl., ¶ 36.) Second, those allegations identify no particular statement, no date, no place, and no recipient beyond a category. Third, Plaintiff alleges in the same paragraph that Defendant “concealed many facts,” and he identifies no fact withheld from him and no occasion on which Defendant withheld it. Finally, Plaintiff pleads no act he took or forbore in reliance on any representation or omission, and no facts showing that reliance was justifiable. Plaintiff alleges that the fraud produced an indebtedness on the Trust Property and damage to the property and to his well-being, without tying either to anything he relied upon. (Compl., ¶ 37.) Plaintiff acknowledges that the representations are pleaded in general terms. (Opp., at p. 12:15-19.)

The Fifth Cause of Action repeats the Fourth almost word for word and supplies no further facts. (Compl., ¶ 39.) Plaintiff’s arguments here are the same as above. For the same reasons articulated above, the Court sustains the Demurrer to the Fourth and Fifth Causes of Action with leave to amend.

V. Elder Abuse

Defendant contends that Plaintiff lacks standing on the Sixth Cause of Action because the alleged elder is Mary, who is alive, and because Plaintiff pleads no authority to sue on her behalf.

Plaintiff argues that the defect is a missing allegation of representative capacity that he can supply.

“‘Elder’ means any person residing in this state, 65 years of age or older.” (Welf. & Inst. Code, § 15610.27.) Financial abuse of an elder occurs when a person “[t]akes, secretes, appropriates, obtains, or retains real or personal property of an elder or dependent adult for a wrongful use or with intent to defraud, or both,” or does so “by undue influence.” (Id., § 15610.30, subd. (a)(1), (3).) “Every action must be prosecuted in the name of the real party in interest, except as otherwise provided by statute.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 367.)

Here, Plaintiff insufficiently alleges standing to prosecute an elder abuse claim. First, Plaintiff identifies Mary as the person abused, alleging that she is more than 65 years old, that Defendant isolated her, and that Defendant has been taking her property and the Trust’s. (Compl., ¶¶ 43, 47 [Sixth Cause of Action].) Second, Plaintiff alleges that Mary is alive, so the injury the Sixth Cause of Action pleads is hers. (Compl., ¶ 10.) Both parties invoke the provision passing an elder abuse action to specified successors after an elder’s death, which by its terms says nothing about a living elder’s claim. (Welf. & Inst. Code, § 15657.3, subd. (d).) Third, Plaintiff alleges no conservatorship, guardianship ad litem, power of attorney, or other authority to sue in Mary’s name. Plaintiff acknowledges that the Complaint does not allege representative capacity. (Opp., at p. 13:8-14.)

Defendant separately contends that the cause of action identifies no particular property, transaction, or taking. Plaintiff alleges that Defendant has been “borrowing money” repeatedly without repaying it, denying services or medical care to conserve funds, and giving away or selling Mary’s possessions without permission. (Compl., ¶ 47 [Sixth Cause of Action].) Those allegations describe categories of conduct and name no item of property, no transaction, and no date. Thus, Plaintiff alleges no act of financial abuse within section 15610.30.

Accordingly, the Court sustains the Demurrer to the Sixth Cause of Action with leave to amend.

VI. Punitive Damages

Defendant’s Demurrer is also directed at the Complaint’s claim for punitive damages. (Dem., at pp. 1-2.) “Since a demurrer does not lie to a part of a cause of action,” allegations of punitive damages are not subject to demurrer, and their adequacy is tested by a motion to strike. (Grieves v. Superior Court (1984) 157 Cal.App.3d 159, 163-164.) Defendant reserved and filed a demurrer without a motion to strike. The Demurrer to the claim for punitive damages is therefore overruled.

 

CONCLUSION

The Demurrer is sustained in part and overruled in part, with 20 days leave to amend.

The Court also notes that Plaintiff has filed a Motion to Consolidate, or in the Alternative to Relate Cases, Coordinate Pretrial Proceedings and Stay Related Unlawful Detainer Action Pending Resolution of Overlapping Issues, set for 9/23/2026. The Court advances that motion and takes it off-calendar, for the following reason.

Plaintiff’s motion seeks to consolidate this case with 26NWUD00654, an unlawful detainer case filed by Cherilin Berndt as Trustee of the Berndt Family Trust against Plaintiff. Pursuant to Local Rule 3.3(g), cases may not be consolidated unless they are in the same department. Plaintiff never filed a Notice of Related Case in this case and in 26NWUD00654, and thus no decision on relating the cases has occurred. The cases therefore are not related and remain in their respective departments, and thus they cannot be consolidated.
