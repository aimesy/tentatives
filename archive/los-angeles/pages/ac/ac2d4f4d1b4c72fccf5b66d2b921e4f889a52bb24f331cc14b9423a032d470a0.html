LEGAL STANDARD:

 

A motion to compel further interrogatory responses may be brought based on: (1) answers that are evasive or incomplete, (2)¿an unwarranted or insufficiently specific exercise of an option to produce documents in lieu of a substantive response, or (3) unmerited or overly generalized objections.  (C.C.P. §¿2030.300(a).)

(C.C.P. § 2031.310(b)(1).)  “Good cause” is shown by “a disputed fact that is of consequence in the action and the discovery sought will tend in reason to prove or disprove that fact or lead to other evidence that will tend to prove or disprove the fact.”  (Digital Music News LLC v. Superior Court (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 216, 224.)

 

A motion to compel further RFA responses may be brought based on: (1) an evasive or incomplete answer; or (2) unmerited or overly generalized objections.)  (C.C.P. § 2033.310(a).) 

 

A motion to compel further must be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration.  (C.C.P. §§ 2030.300(b)(1), 2031.310(b)(1), 2033.290(b)(1).)  Additionally, the motion must be made within 45 days of service of the verified response or supplemental verified response, or on/before any specific late date to which the parties have agreed in writing.  (C.C.P. §§ 2030.300(c), 2031.310(c), 2033.310(c).)

ALLEGATIONS: 

 

A.    Operative Complaint

(“JHC”) in exchange for $300,000.  (SAC, ¶¶1, 7-8.)  Plaintiffs allege that the parties operated under the SPA, such that Keuroghlian issued business cards identifying Dginguerian as Senior Vice President of JHC, Dginguerian received Schedule K-1 forms through 2019 identifying him as a 23% owner, and he was on the payroll from February 2, 2014 to January 9, 2015 until the parties agreed to cease taking a salary and instead use the funds to grow JHC.  (Id., ¶¶9-10.)  On April 12, 2024, Dginguerian began questioning the lack of distributions from JHC and demanded access to JHC’s books and records pursuant to the SPA. The Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”), filed January 16, 2026, alleges: (1) Breach of Contract against all Defendants; (2) Breach of Contract by Dginguerian against all Defendants; (3) Breach of Fiduciary Duty by Dginguerian against Keuroghlian; (4) Fraud by Dginguerian against Keuroghlian and JHC; (5) Negligence by Dginguerian against Keuroghlian; (6) Accounting against Keuroghlian and JHC; (7) Declaratory Relief by Dginguerian against Keuroghlian and JHC; (8) Statutory Claim for Improper Disposition of Property – Violation of Penal Code, § 496 by Dginguerian against Keuroghlian and JHC; and (9) Conversion by Dginguerian against Keuroghlian and JHC.

Cross-Complainants allege that Keuroghlian is JHC’s CEO and sole shareholder.  (XC, ¶4.)  On January 24, 2014, Keuroghlian and Dginguerian entered into the SPA, wherein Dginguerian acquired 23 shares.  (Id., ¶5.)  In early 2020, Dginguerian voluntarily agreed to transfer and relinquish his shares in JHC back to Keuroghlian in exchange for being released from any obligation to personally guarantee JHC’s business loans.  (Id., ¶6.)  They allege that following the transfers, Dginguerian lacked any ownership interest in JHC and from 2020 onward, JHC issued Schedule K-1 tax forms solely to Keuroghlian, reflecting his ownership of 100 shares and 100% ownership.  (Id., ¶7.)  In May and June 2024, Dginguerian began making false statements to third parties claiming he remained an owner of JHC and that Keuroghlian improperly denied him ownership interest.  (Id., ¶8.)  Cross-Complainants allege that written statements were published on Facebook on June 3, 5, and 10, 2024, misrepresenting that he was a current owner of JHC and that Keuroghlian wrongfully excluded him.  (Id., ¶9(a)-(d).)  They allege the statements falsely represent that Dginguerian remained an owner of JHC and/or falsely implied that Keuroghlian engaged in fraudulent or criminal conduct.  (Id., ¶10.)  In August 2025, Dginguerian also published additional written statements on Facebook that he was suing his partner for fraud, that (nearly) everyone at JHC would be deposed, and that the truth would be revealed.  (Id., ¶11.)  In addition to written posts, Dginguerian allegedly made oral statements to third parties, including business associates, clients, and/or potential clients, repeating false claims that he remained an owner and implying misconduct by Keuroghlian.  (Id., ¶12.)

LEGAL STANDARD:

 

A.    Demurrer

 

The grounds for a demurrer must appear on the face of the pleading or from judicially noticeable matters. (C.C.P. § 430.30(a); Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal. 3d 311, 318.) A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) The only issue involved in a demurrer hearing is whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Id.)

 

A demurrer assumes the truth of all factual, material allegations properly pled in the challenged pleading. (Blank, supra, 39 Cal. 3d at p. 318.) No matter how unlikely or improbable, the plaintiff’s allegations must be accepted as true for the purpose of ruling on the demurrer. (Del E. Webb Corp. v. Structural Materials Co. (1981) 123 Cal.  App. 3d 593, 604.) But this does not include contentions; deductions; conclusions of fact or law alleged in the complaint; facts impossible in law; or allegations contrary to facts of which a court may take judicial notice.  (Blank, supra, 39 Cal. 3d at 318.)

 

Pursuant to C.C.P. §§ 430.10(e) and (f), the party against whom a complaint has been filed may demur to the pleading on the grounds that the pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action, or that the pleading is uncertain, ambiguous and/or unintelligible. It is an abuse of discretion to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend if there is a reasonable probability that the defect can be cured by amendment. (Schifando v. City of Los Angeles (2003) 31 Cal. 4th 1074, 1082.)

 

B.    Motion to Strike

 

Motions to strike are used to reach defects or objections to pleadings that are not challengeable by demurrer, such as words, phrases, and prayers for damages. (See C.C.P. §§ 435, 436, and 437.) The proper procedure to attack false allegations in a pleading is a motion to strike. (C.C.P. § 436(a).) In granting a motion to strike made under C.C.P. § 435, “[t]he court may, upon a motion made pursuant to Section 435 [notice of motion to strike whole or part of complaint], or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper: (a) Strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading.” (C.C.P. § 436(a).) Irrelevant matters include immaterial allegations that are not essential to the claim or those not pertinent to or supported by an otherwise sufficient claim. (C.C.P. § 431.10.)

 

The court may also “[s]trike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court.” (C.C.P. § 436(b).)

 

MEET AND CONFER:

 

C.C.P. §§ 430.41(a) and 435.5(a) require that the moving party meet and confer with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to the demurrer and/or motion to strike. Upon review, the Court finds the meet and confer requirements were met. (See Fisher Decl., ¶¶2-5.)

 

DEMURRER ANALYSIS: 

 

Dginguerian demurs to the First and Second Causes of Action in the Cross-Complaint on the grounds that they fail to state sufficient facts and are time-barred.   

 

A.    Statute of Limitations – Overruled

 

“Defamation constitutes an injury to reputation; the injury may occur by means of libel or slander. (Civ.Code, § 44.)”  (Shively v. Bozanich (2003) 31 Cal.4th 1230, 1242.)  The elements of a defamation claim are: (1) publication that is (2) false, (3) defamatory, (4) unprivileged, and (5) has a natural tendency to injure or cause special damage.  (Wong v. Tai Jing (2010) 189 Cal.App.4th 1354, 1369.)  The elements of a libel claim are: (1) a written communication that (2) is false, (3) is unprivileged, and (4) exposes a person to contempt or ridicule or certain other reputational injuries.  (Shively, supra, 31 Cal.4th at 1242 [citing Civ. Code, §45].)  The elements of a slander claim are: (1) an oral communication that is (2) false, (3) unprivileged, and attributes to a person specific misdeeds or certain unfavorable characteristics or qualities, or uttering certain other derogatory statements regarding a person.  (Id. [citing Civ. Code, §46].)  False statements charging the commission of crime, or tending directly to injure a plaintiff in respect to his or her profession by imputing dishonesty or questionable professional conduct are defamatory per se.  (Burrill v. Nair (2013) 217 Cal.App.4th 357, 383.)  In the case of slander or libel per se, the defamed party is not required to prove that she suffered any damages.  (Mann, supra, 120 Cal.App.4th at 107 [slander per se]; Barnes-Hind, Inc. v. Superior Court (1986) 181 Cal.App.3d 377, 382 [libel per se].) 

 

C.C.P. § 340(c) provides for a one-year statute of limitations for an action for libel and slander. 

 

In the First Cause of Action, Keuroghlian alleges that on June 10, 2024 and thereafter, Dginguerian made oral statements to third parties, including JHC’s business associates, agents, and/or clients, accusing Keuroghlian of having “robbed” him and having taken advantage of him behind his back.   (XC, ¶19.)  Keuroghlian alleges that the statement that he “robbed” Dginguerian accused him of criminal conduct and dishonesty in the operation of his business, was false and unprivileged, and was made with knowledge of their falsity or with reckless disregard for the truth.  (Id., ¶¶20-23.)  Keuroghlian contends the statements injured him in his profession as JHC’s CEO as they imputed criminal conduct and tended to injury him in his business/profession, thereby constituting slander per se under Civil Code, §46.  (Id., ¶¶24-25.) 

 

In the Second Cause of Action, Keuroghlian alleges that Dginguerian made false, written statements about his business conduct, personality, and professional behavior to JHC’s current and former employees, and the employees reasonably believed the statements to be true.  (XC, ¶¶27-28.)  He contends Dginguerian’s statements have been damaging and injurious to his career and professions and harmed his health due to stress.  (Id., ¶29.)  He asserts that because the statements imputed criminal conduct and tended directly to injury his business and profession, they constituted libel per se under Civil Code, § 45a.  (Id., ¶30.) 

 

Dginguerian argues that the claims are time-barred because the Cross-Complaint alleges that written statements were made on Facebook on June 3, 5, and 10, 2024, and that oral statements were made on June 10, 2024.  (See XC, ¶¶9, 19.)  The Cross-Complaint was filed on May 7, 2026, which is over a year from the alleged statements.

 

However, Cross-Complainants rely on the relation back doctrine, arguing their claims are not barred by the statute of limitations.  They rely on ZF Micro Devices, Inc. v. TAT Capital Partners, Ltd. (2016) 5 Cal.App.5th 69, arguing that the cross-complaint relates back to the time of the filing of Plaintiffs’ initial Complaint on June 27, 2024. 

 

In ZF Micro, there were a series of three cases: (1) the NSC lawsuit filed on April 25, 2002 by ZF Solutions against NSC; (2) the TAT lawsuit filed on February 14, 2005 by ZF Devices investors (including TAT) against ZF Solutions, where the court granted ZF’s motion for leave to file a cross-complaint against TAT (filed March 18, 2009), but later the court had granted TAT’s motion to sever ZF’s cross-complaint and consolidated with the third lawsuit; and (3) the ZF Lawsuit filed on February 17, 2009 by the ZF Entities against Mark Putney (TAT’s representative), which included a separately filed cross-complaint by ZF (originally filed in the TAT Lawsuit and consolidated with the ZF Lawsuit).  On appeal, the Court of Appeal determined whether the tolling doctrine applied to ZF’s cross-complaint.  The appellate court stated that if tolling applied, then the cross-complaint would be deemed filed when TAT filed its complaint on February 14, 2005 (2nd lawsuit), and the cross-complaint would have timely been asserted less than four years after the claim arose on February 28, 2002 (when harm to ZF Devices occurred).  Relying on the Supreme Court, the appellate court found: “the Supreme Court characterized the tolling doctrine as embracing all cross-claims by a defendant against the plaintiff, regardless of their relatedness to the claims asserted in the complaint”—irrespective of whether it is related to the matters asserted the complaint.  (ZF Micro Devices, Inc., supra, 5 Cal.App.5th 91, 92.) 

 

As stated by the appellate court in Sidney v. Superior Court (1988) 198 Cal.App.3d 710:

 

The Trindade court pointed out: “It has consistently been held that the commencement of an action tolls the statute of limitations as to a defendant's then unbarred cause of action against the plaintiff” related to the accident or occurrence upon which the action is brought. [Citation.]

 

We are satisfied that the same “relation back” standard applies for an amended cross-complaint as for an initial cross-complaint. …

 

…  “ ‘The statute is a bar to the defendant's affirmative claim only if the period has already run when the complaint is filed. The filing of the complaint suspends the statute during the pendency of the action, and the defendant may set up his cross claim by appropriate pleading at any time.’ [Citation].” (Trindade v. Superior Court, supra, 29 Cal.App.3d at p. 860, 106 Cal.Rptr. 28; see also 3 Witkin, Cal.Proc.3d (1985) § 322 p. 353.) Early California cases show that the principle is well established that as long as a defendant's claim existed at the commencement of the action, the statute remains suspended throughout the action. (See e.g. McDougald v. Hulet (1901) 132 Cal. 154, 160–161, 64 P. 278.)

 

(Sidney v. Superior Court (1988) 198 Cal.App.3d 710, 715.) 

 

Applying the relation-back doctrine, the Court finds that the Slander and Libel claims relate back to the filing of Plaintiffs’ initial complaint on June 27, 2024.  The written statements were made in June 2024 and August 2025, while oral statements were made on June 10, 2024.  (Compl., ¶¶9, 11, 19.)  The statute of limitation period would not have expired on the June 2024 and August 2025 statements by the time of the filing of the initial complaint, such that these cross-claims are timely pursuant to the relation back doctrine’s tolling.  (The Court also notes that the Libel Cause of Action would potentially be timely regardless of whether the relation back doctrine applied as Cross-Complainants did not isolate the Libel claim solely to the June 2024 written statements, but also alleged written statements in August 2025. The August 2025 statement would be timely based on a May 7, 2026 Cross-Complaint filing date.) 

 

Lastly, Dginguerian argues that the relation back doctrine does not addressed the relation-back factors by showing that the amendment: “(1) rests on the same general set of facts; (2) involves the same injury; and (3) refers to the same instrumentality.”  (Estrada v. Royalty Carpet Mills, Inc. (2022) 76 Cal.App.5th 685, 714–715.)  However, Estrada is distinguishable as that dealt with the second and third amended complaints, and did not discuss cross-complaints.  Rather, the ZF Micro Court stated that the “tolling doctrine applies to both permissive and compulsory cross-complaints.”  (ZF Micro, supra, 5 Cal.App.5th at 73.) 

 

Thus, the demurrer to the First and Second Causes of Action based on the statute of limitations is overruled. 

 

A.    First Cause of Action for Slander – Overruled

 

Dginguerian argues that the Slander claim fails because the August 2025 statement in paragraph 11 was truthful and protected by the litigation privilege. 

 

Paragraph 11 alleges in relevant part: “In or around August 2025, Cross-Defendant published additional written statements on Facebook stating, among other things, that he was ‘suing his partner for fraud,’ that depositions would soon be conducted, that ‘everyone or almost everyone at JOHNHART would be deposed,’ and that ‘the truth will be revealed.’”  (XC, ¶11 [emphasis added].)  However, paragraph 11 refers to written statements and not oral statements. 

 

Furthermore, the Slander Cause of Action alleges that the oral statement at issue occurred on June 10, 2024 regarding accusations that Keuroghlian “robbed” Dginguerian and took advantage of him.  (See XC, ¶19.) 

 

As Dginguerian did not otherwise directly address the oral statement, the demurrer to the First Cause of Action is overruled.  

 

B.    Second Cause of Action for Libel – Overruled

 

The litigation privilege (Civil Code, § 47(b)) provides that a “publication or broadcast” made as part of a “judicial proceeding” is privileged.  “This privilege is absolute in nature, applying ‘to all publications, irrespective of their maliciousness.’ [Citation.] ‘The usual formulation is that the privilege applies to any communication (1) made in judicial or quasi-judicial proceedings; (2) by litigants or other participants authorized by law; (3) to achieve the objects of the litigation; and (4) that [has] some connection or logical relation to the action.’ [Citation.] The privilege ‘is not limited to statements made during a trial or other proceedings, but may extend to steps taken prior thereto, or afterwards.’”  (Action Apartment Assn., Inc. v. City of Santa Monica (2007) 41 Cal.4th 1232, 1241.) 

If applying Dginguerian’s arguments above to the Libel Cause of Action, the Court finds that paragraph 11 involves litigation matters—or matters in anticipation of litigation.  The statements themselves do not make any false statements against Kueroghlian that accuse him of criminal conduct or dishonesty in his business.  As alleged, Dginguerian’s statement that he intended to sue Kueroghlian for fraud lacks any specificity as to his profession or business.  Dginguerian’s statements that depositions will occur (or that they would occur for JHC employees) and that the truth will be revealed are mere general statements that do not constitute libel per se. 

 

Thus, there would potentially be a ground to sustain the demurrer on the Second Cause of Action if it relied solely on paragraph 11.  However, the Cross-Complaint also alleges in paragraph 9 that four other written statements were made.  As the statements in paragraph 9 were not addressed (with respect to the litigation privilege or otherwise), the demurrer is overruled.  A demurrer cannot rightfully be sustained to part of a cause of action or to a particular type of damage or remedy.  (Kong v. City of Hawaiian Gardens Redevelopment Agency (2002) 108 Cal. App. 4th 1028, 1047.)  The Court notes that Dginguerian concurrently filed a motion to strike to address paragraphs 9 and 11.  (See Dem. at p.4, fn.2.) 

 

The demurrer to the Second Cause of Action is overruled.   

 

MOTION TO STRIKE ANALYSIS:

 

A.    Litigation Privilege Regarding Paragraph 11 – Granted with Leave to Amend

 

Paragraph 11 alleges: “In or around August 2025, Cross-Defendant published additional written statements on Facebook stating, among other things, that he was ‘suing his partner for fraud,’ that depositions would soon be conducted, that ‘everyone or almost everyone at JOHNHART would be deposed,’ and that ‘the truth will be revealed.’ ”

 

Dginguerian moves to strike the allegations in paragraph 11, arguing they are barred by the litigation privilege. As discussed above, the Court finds merit to this argument that paragraph 11 includes statements barred by the litigation privilege. 

 

In opposition, Cross-Complainants argue that a motion to strike is improper to determine whether the statements are barred by the litigation privilege, constitute opinions, or are truthful/false, since the Court must accept the Complaint’s allegations that Dginguerian’s statements were false (XC, ¶¶20, 27) as true. 

 

However, as currently alleged, the statements do not allege any false statement, but either constitute statements made in anticipation of litigation or opinions that depositions will occur and that the “truth” (whether in favor of Keuroghlian or Dginguerian) will be revealed.

 

The motion to strike paragraph 11 is granted with leave to amend.

 

B.    Statute of Limitations Regarding Paragraphs 9, 11, and 19 – Denied

 

Dginguerian argues that paragraphs 9, 11, and 19 are time-barred.  For the reasons discussed above, the motion to strike on this ground is denied. 

 

C.    Truthful Statements in Paragraphs 9, 19, and 20 – Denied in Part and Granted in Part

 

Dginguerian argues that paragraphs 9 and 19 are not actionable because they were not false statements.  Paragraphs 9 and 19 allege:

 

·       Paragraph 9(a): “On June 3, 2024, Cross-Defendant posted: ‘Who wants to meet at one of our JohnHart offices this week and talk real estate?’ ”

 

·       Paragraph 9(b): “On June 5, 2024, Cross-Defendant posted: ‘Anyone want to make a day trip to Palm Springs this week? I still haven't seen our JohnHart Real Estate Palm Springs office.’”

 

·       Paragraph 9(c): “On June 5, 2024, Cross-Defendant posted: ‘Who can guess which JohnHart office this was back in 2016, before I remodeled it?’ ”

 

·       Paragraph 9(d): “On June 10, 2024, Cross-Defendant posted statements accusing KEUROGHLIAN of having ‘rob[bed]’ him and ‘tak[en] advantage’ of him behind his back, further stating that ’10 years of text logs’ and ‘10 years of emails’ would expose the truth and that the ‘clock is ticking.’ ”

 

·       Paragraph 19: “On or about June 10, 2024, and on other occasions thereafter, Cross-Defendant made oral statements to third parties, including business associates, agents, and/or clients of JOHNHART, accusing KEUROGHLIAN of having ‘robbed’ him and having taken advantage of him behind his back.”

 

The Court notes in the Notice of Motion, Dginguerian also moves to strike paragraph 20, but did not specifically address it in the memorandum of points and authorities.  Paragraph 20 alleges: “The statement that KEUROGHLIAN ‘robbed’ Cross-Defendant was false.”  The Court will consider this in connection with paragraphs 9(d) and19.

 

Although Cross-Complainants allege that paragraphs 9(a)-(c)’s statements are defamatory per se, they have not shown how they impute dishonesty or questionable professional conduct against Keuroghlian.  Thus, these allegations will be stricken. 

 

In contrast, statements that Keuroghlian “robbed” Dginguerian impute either criminal conduct, unfavorable characteristics, and/or injure Keuroghlian by imputing dishonesty or questionable professional conduct.  As such, the motion is denied as to paragraphs 9(d), 19, and 20. 

 

D.    Punitive Damages

 

Dginguerian moves to strike the Prayer for Relief at ¶4, which requests punitive damages. 

 

A complaint including a request for punitive damages must include allegations showing that the plaintiff is entitled to an award of punitive damages.  (Clauson v. Superior Court (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1253, 1255.)  A claim for punitive damages cannot be pleaded generally and allegations that a defendant acted "with oppression, fraud and malice" toward plaintiff are insufficient legal conclusions to show that the plaintiff is entitled to an award of punitive damages.  (Brousseau v. Jarrett (1977) 73 Cal.App.3d 864, 872.)  Specific factual allegations are required to support a claim for punitive damages.  (Id.)

 

Civil Code § 3294 authorizes a plaintiff to obtain an award of punitive damages when there is clear and convincing evidence that the defendant engaged in malice, oppression, or fraud.  Section 3294(c) defines the terms in the following manner:

 

(1) "Malice" means conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.

 

(2) "Oppression" means despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person's rights.

 

(3) "Fraud" means an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known to the defendant with the intention on the part of the defendant of thereby depriving a person of property or legal rights or otherwise causing injury. 

 

(Civ. Code, § 3294(c).)

 

The Court has reviewed the Cross-Complaint’s allegations and finds that it fails to allege specific facts that Dginguerian acted with malice, oppression, and/or fraud.  Other than the request for punitive damages in the prayer, there is no other reference to punitive damages in the Cross-Complaint.  Thus, the motion to strike punitive damages is granted with leave to amend.

LEGAL STANDARD

 

The function of a motion for summary judgment or adjudication is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party cannot show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) C.C.P. § 437c(c) “requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.”  (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.)

 

As to each claim as framed by the complaint, the defendant moving for summary judgment must satisfy the initial burden of proof by presenting facts to negate an essential element, or to establish a defense. (CCP § 437c(p)(2); Scalf v. D. B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1520.)  Courts “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.”  (Dore v. Arnold Worldwide, Inc. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 384, 389.) Once the defendant has met that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to that cause of action or a defense thereto.  To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence. (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

 

EVIDENTIARY OBJECTIONS:

 

Plaintiff submitted objections to Colton Carr Declaration (Lyft’s Safety Specialist) filed in support of Lyft’s motion.  The objections are overruled as to Objection Nos. 1-13 to paragraphs 1, 3, 12, 13, 15-16, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 28, and 29. 

 

ANALYSIS: 

 

Lyft moves for summary judgment on Plaintiff’s Complaint or, alternatively, seeks summary adjudication on the following issues:

 

·       Issue No. 1: As asserted by Lyft in its Answer, Lyft cannot be held vicariously liable for Gevorkyan’s alleged negligence because Gevorkyan was an app-based, independent contractor at the time of the subject accident, not an employee or agent. Pursuant to Business & Professions Code § 7451, Lyft is not vicariously liable for the actions of independent contractors as a matter of law.

 

·       Issue No. 2: Lyft cannot be held directly liable for negligence as a matter of law because Lyft did not own or control the vehicle driven by Gevorkyan at the time of the subject accident.

 

A.    Lyft’s Material Facts

 

Lyft provides the following material facts in support of Issue No. 1.  Lyft provides a digital platform (the “Lyft Platform”) that connects app-based drivers (“drivers”) using their personal vehicles with passengers seeking rides to certain destinations. The Lyft Platform operates over the internet to connect drivers and riders, arrange rideshare requests between those two groups, and transmit payments from riders to drivers. Lyft users in need of a ride can open the Lyft mobile application on their mobile devices (the “Lyft App”) to request rideshare services. The Lyft Platform attempts to match a driver with a rider. At their discretion, drivers may accept ride requests, pick up the riders, and transport them to their requested destinations. After riders complete the ride, the Lyft Platform transmits payment from the rider to the driver via the internet.  (Lyft Fact 1.) 

 

Gevorkyan applied to utilize the Lyft Platform as an app-based independent contractor driver on April 12, 2022.  (Lyft Fact 2.)  When Gevorkyan applied to use the Lyft App as an app-based independent contractor driver, he submitted proof of a valid California driver’s license, vehicle inspection, and automobile insurance. (Id. at 3.)  After applying, Lyft procured an independent criminal background check and driving record check on Gevorkyan, which revealed no prior disqualifying felony criminal convictions or disqualifying driving incidents.  (Id. at 4-5.)

 

Lyft approved Gevorkyan to drive on the Lyft App as an app-based independent contractor driver on April 13, 2022. (Id. at 7.)  Gevorkyan and Lyft entered into a written contract—the December 12, 2022 Terms of Service (“TOS”)—on January 16, 2023.  (Id. at 6.)  The December 12, 2022, TOS was the operative contract that governed the relationship between Gevorkyan and Lyft at the time of the subject accident.  (Id. at 8.)  Pursuant to the TOS’s terms, Gevorkyan and Lyft agreed that Gevorkyan was an app-based independent contractor driver, not an employee or agent; Lyft had no right to “direct or control” Gevorkyan; Gevorkyan “retained the sole right to determine when, where, and for how long [he] will utilize the Lyft platform”; and Gevorkyan had “complete discretion to provide Rideshare Services or otherwise engage in any other business or employment activities, including but not limited to providing services similar to the Rideshare Services to other companies, and that Lyft does not, and shall not be deemed to, restrict you from engaging in any such activity.”  (Id. at 9.) 

 

After Gevorkyan was approved to use the Lyft App as an app-based independent contractor driver, and before the subject incident, Lyft provided Gevorkyan with required safety training as required under Prop. 22, including completion of: (1) Defensive Driving training on April 12, 2022, which included identification of collision-causing elements such as excessive speed, DUI, and distracted driving; (2) Responding to Sexual Misconduct training on April 12, 2022; and (3) Safe Food Delivery training.  (Id. at 10.)  After Gevorkyan was approved to use the Lyft App, Lyft initiated regular driving record and criminal background checks to ensure Gevorkyan continued to meet the requirements to use the Lyft App. Lyft also ensured that Gevorkyan’s submitted documents, such as his driver’s license, remained current.  (Id. at 11.) During the time Gevorkyan used the Lyft Platform, Lyft did not prescribe specific hours during which he was required to be logged onto the Lyft Platform—Gevorkyan was equally free not to drive on the Lyft Platform at all.  (Id. at 12.)  When Gevorkyan logged onto the Lyft Platform, he had absolute discretion to accept, decline, or ignore any ride request he received, and Lyft did not require Gevorkyan to accept any specific rideshare or delivery service request as a condition to maintaining access to the Lyft Platform.  (Id. at 13.)  Gevorkyan was free to use any other digital network or other network company of his choice and was equally free to work in any other occupation or business of his choice too.  (Id. at 14.)  While using the Lyft Platform, Gevorkyan was able to concurrently perform rideshare services or delivery services through other transportation network companies, including Lyft’s direct competitors.  (Id. at 15.) 

 

The subject incident occurred on March 14, 2023, at California Street and S. 3rd Ave, Arcadia, California.  (Id. at 16.)  At that time, Gevorkyan was using the Lyft Platform as an app-based independent contractor driver pursuant to Lyft’s TOS.  (Id. at 17.) 

 

Plaintiff filed the Complaint against Gevorkyan and Lyft on September 16, 2024, alleging that Gevorkyan operated a motor vehicle in the course of his employment with Lyft, that he was an agent and employee of Lyft, and that he was acting within the scope of his agency with Lyft at the time of the Accident.  (Id. at 18-19.)  Lyft filed an Answer, asserting that Gevorkyan was an app-based independent contractor driver consistent with Prop. 22, and it denied any direct negligence as it pertains to the Accident.  (Id. at 20.)

 

In support of Issue No. 2, Lyft provides Facts 21-22.  Plaintiff also alleges that Lyft owned, drove, operated, controlled, entrusted and maintained the vehicle operated by Gevorkyan.  (Lyft Fact 21.)  At the time of the Accident, Lyft did not own, operate, control, or entrust the vehicle operated by Gevorkyan.  (Id. at 22.) 

 

B.    Lyft’s Vicarious Liability for Gevorkyan’s Alleged Negligence (Issue 1)

 

Proposition 22, the Protect App-Based Drivers and Services Act (“Prop. 22”) was codified in Business & Professions Code, §§ 7448-7467.  (Castellanos v. State of California (2023) 89 Cal.App.5th 131, 142.) 

 

Business & Professions Code, § 7450 states that the purposes of the chapter are:

 

(a) To protect the basic legal right of Californians to choose to work as independent contractors with rideshare and delivery network companies throughout the state.

 

(b) To protect the individual right of every app-based rideshare and delivery driver to have the flexibility to set their own hours for when, where, and how they work.

 

(c) To require rideshare and delivery network companies to offer new protections and benefits for app-based rideshare and delivery drivers, including minimum compensation levels, insurance to cover on-the-job injuries, automobile accident insurance, health care subsidies for qualifying drivers, protection against harassment and discrimination, and mandatory contractual rights and appeal processes.

 

(d) To improve public safety by requiring criminal background checks, driver safety training, and other safety provisions to help ensure app-based rideshare and delivery drivers do not pose a threat to customers or the public.

 

(Bus. & Prof. Code, § 7450.)

 

Section 7451 states:

 

Notwithstanding any other provision of law, including, but not limited to, the Labor Code, the Unemployment Insurance Code, and any orders, regulations, or opinions of the Department of Industrial Relations or any board, division, or commission within the Department of Industrial Relations, an app-based driver is an independent contractor and not an employee or agent with respect to the app-based driver's relationship with a network company if the following conditions are met:

 

(a) The network company does not unilaterally prescribe specific dates, times of day, or a minimum number of hours during which the app-based driver must be logged into the network company's online-enabled application or platform.

 

(b) The network company does not require the app-based driver to accept any specific rideshare service or delivery service request as a condition of maintaining access to the network company's online-enabled application or platform.

 

(c) The network company does not restrict the app-based driver from performing rideshare services or delivery services through other network companies except during engaged time.

 

(d) The network company does not restrict the app-based driver from working in any other lawful occupation or business.

 

(Bus. & Prof. Code, § 7451.) 

 

Section 7463 defines the following terms:

 

(a) “App-based driver” means an individual who is a DNC courier, TNC driver, or TCP driver or permit holder; and for whom the conditions set forth in subdivisions (a) to (d), inclusive, of Section 7451 are satisfied.

…

(l) “Network company” means a business entity that is a DNC or a TNC.
….

(p) “Transportation network company” (TNC) has the same meaning as the definition contained in subdivision (c) of Section 5431 of the Public Utilities Code.

 

(Bus. & Prof. Code, § 7463.)  Public Utilities Code, § 5431(c) defines TNC as “an organization, including, but not limited to, a corporation, limited liability company, partnership, sole proprietor, or any other entity, operating in California that provides prearranged transportation services for compensation using an online-enabled application or platform to connect passengers with drivers using a personal vehicle.”

 

Lyft argues that Plaintiff’s vicarious liability theory fails because Prop. 22 renders Gevorkyan an independent contractor.  (City and County of San Francisco v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (2019) 36 Cal.App.5th 66, 71 [stating “(Lyft, another TNC)”].)

 

To show that it satisfies Business & Professions Code, § 7451’s factors, Lyft relies on the TOS.  The TOS which Gevorkyan agreed to shows that Gevorkyan agreed he was an independent contractor and not Lyft’s employee or agent.  Under the TOS Gevorkyan retained the sole right to decide when, where, and for how long he used the Lyft platform and to engage in other business/employment activities.  (Lyft Fact 9; Mot., Ex. D [TOS at § 19].)  Therefore, Lyft has shown it considered and treated Gevorkyan as an independent contractor pursuant to the terms of TOS section 7451(a)-(d). 

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that Lyft has not addressed Public Utilities Code, § 5354, which imposes liability on Lyft regardless of worker classification. He argues that Prop. 22 does not disturb section 5354 and that the Legislature confirmed the opposite in section 5353(f). 

 

Public Utilities Code, § 5354 states:

 

In construing and enforcing the provisions of this chapter relating to the prescribed privileges and obligations of the holder of a permit or certificate issued hereunder, the act, omission, or failure of any officer, agent, or employee, or person offering to afford the authorized service with the approval or consent of the permit or certificate holder, is the act, omission, or failure of the permit or certificate holder.

 

(Pub. Util. Code, § 5354.)  Section 5353(f) states: “This chapter does not apply to any of the following: … (f) Passenger vehicles carrying passengers on a noncommercial enterprise basis.”

 

Plaintiff argues that section 5354 is not limited to officers, agents, or employees, but to any “person offering to afford the authorized service with the approval or consent of the permit or certificate holder,” which includes persons like Gevorkyan who was carrying a passenger for compensation on a ride Lyft matched him to and recorded.  (Opp. at p.13.)  However, sections 5353 and 5354 are limited to “this chapter”—Public Utilities Code, Chapter 8, which governs the Passenger Charter-Party Carriers’ Act.  (Pub. Util. Code, § 5351.) 

 

Section 5352(a) states: “The use of the public highways for the transportation of passengers for compensation is a business affected with a public interest. It is the purpose of this chapter to preserve for the public full benefit and use of public highways consistent with the needs of commerce without unnecessary congestion or wear and tear upon the highways; to secure to the people adequate and dependable transportation by carriers operating upon the highways; to secure full and unrestricted flow of traffic by motor carriers over the highways which will adequately meet reasonable public demands by providing for the regulation of all transportation agencies with respect to accident indemnity so that adequate and dependable service by all necessary transportation agencies shall be maintained and the full use of the highways preserved to the public; and to promote carrier and public safety through its safety enforcement regulations.” 

 

As plainly stated, section 5354’s applicability is limited to regulatory enforcement of permit-holder obligations under the Public Utilities Code, Chapter 8.  Other than citing this section, Plaintiff has not shown through case law, legislative history, or by any other means whether the Legislature intended to impose vicarious liability on companies like Lyft through section 5354, or how this regulatory chapter extends to or imposes liability for common-law negligence claims.  Finally, even if applying section 5354, Plaintiff has not shown how Lyft violated the regulatory scheme, as his claims against Lyft are based on Gevorkyan’s alleged negligent driving.  Thus, the Court finds that section 5354 has no application here. 

 

Next, Plaintiff contends that Lyft has not met the Business & Professions Code, § 7451 conditions. 

 

First, Plaintiff argues that under subsection (c), he is not contending that Lyft forbade outside work, but that Lyft contends in its separate statement that while using the Lyft Platform, Gevorkyan was able to “concurrently” perform rideshare/delivery services through outer TNCs.  (Lyft Fact 15.)  Plaintiff contends that Gevorkyan testified that it was impossible to work for Uber and Lyft at the same time, as one either does rides through Uber or for Lyft.  (Gevorkyan Depo. at pp.40-41.)  Thus, Plaintiff argues that sequential switching between the apps is not “concurrent” such that there is a triable issue of material fact. 

 

More fully, Gevorkian testified:

 

            Q. Did you ever work for Uber and Lyft at the same time?

A. It’s impossible to work for Uber and Lyft at the same time. You either do rides through Uber or for Lyft.

Q. And I’m not speaking at the very same moment, but were there days or weeks or months where you would switch between driving through Lyft and driving for Uber.

A. Yes, that happened, that I switched.

Q. And for approximately how long did you provide services for both Uber and Lyft and switch between the two?

A. I worked like this for about approximately two and a half years.

 

(Gevorkian Depo. at pp.40-41.) 

 

Section 7451(c) does not require that an app-based driver perform “concurrent” rideshare service, but only that Lyft not restrict app-based drivers from performing rideshare services through other network companies.  Plaintiff construes Lyft’s Fact 15 too narrowly.  As the Court reads Lyft’s Fact 15, as supported by Gevorkian’s deposition testimony, Gevorkyan was able to use both Uber and Lyft’s platforms.  As pointed out by Lyft in reply, subsection (c) does not require simultaneous platform operation, as it carves out an exception “during engaged time.”  The Court does not find that Plaintiff has raised a triable issue of material fact regarding subsection (c)’s condition.

 

Second, Plaintiff argues that there are triable issues of material fact regarding Lyft’s Fact 12 and section 7451(a).  Plaintiff cites to Gevorkyan’s deposition:

 

Q. … [D]id Lyft require you to work any minimum number of hours, either daily, weekly or monthly?

A. No. There’s no such requirement, but that’s something that you’re rating. Depends on the more you work and the better reviews you get from your riders, the higher your rating. But as to how many specific hours you’re supposed to work, there is no such requirement.

 

(Gevorkian Depo. at pp.39-40.) 

 

While Plaintiff does not dispute that Lyft imposes no minimum-hours requirement, he argues that Lyft scores its drivers by hours worked and that this is a factual question.  However, Plaintiff has not raised a triable issue of material fact on whether Lyft imposed hours or set times for work; rather, he concedes this point.  While Plaintiff contends there is a rating system, Plaintiff has not shown how this amounts to Lyft prescribing specific dates, times of day, or a minimum number of hours during which Gevorkian must have been logged into Lyft’s app. 

 

Third, Plaintiff argues that there are triable issues of material fact as to section 7451(b) and Lyft’s Fact 13, regarding whether Gevorkian had discretion to accept, decline, or ignore ride requests.  Gevorkian testified:

 

Q. Again, talking around the time of the accident, did Lyft have a requirement that you were required to accept specific rides when they came up on that app?

A. Accepting or not accepting a ride, it depends on a driver. And Lyft does not require that I accept a ride against my will.

Q. You have the ability to decline or ignore a ride request; is that correct?

A. Right. I don't have to accept a ride that I don't like for one reason or another.

 

(Gevorkian Depo. at p.40.)  Gevorkian further testified that there were no rules to canceling rides, as a driver could cancel a ride for any reason, but that one’s ranking—given by Lyft as opposed to passenger reviews—could decrease.  (Id. at p.37.) 

 

Plaintiff argues Gevorkian was not asked about deactivation rates, suspension, or consequences of declining rides.  He argues that Lyft failed to address consequences of declining rides, such that it has not established that acceptance was never a condition of access.  (Opp. at p.19.)  However, Gevorkian’s testimony confirms that he could decline rides for any reason.  While this may decrease his score while using the app, Plaintiff has not shown that this prevented Gevorkyan or other Lyft drivers from accessing the app. 

 

Fourth, Plaintiff argues that Lyft has not established that Gevorkyan was an approved app-based driver on March 14, 2023, the day of the subject incident.  Plaintiff cites to Lyft’s memorandum of points and authorities at page 13, lines 12-13, wherein Lyft states it approved Gevorkyan to drive on the Lyft app as an independent contractor on June 16, 2023, citing to Fact 7.  This appears to be a typographical error in the memorandum of points and authorities, as Lyft’s Fact 7 actually refers to April 13, 2022, which is supported by Colton Carr’s declaration at paragraph 18.  A typographical error in the memorandum of points and authorities is a not a triable issue of material fact. 

 

Thus, the Court does not find that Plaintiff has raised a triable issue of material fact regarding the section 7451 factors.  (Plaintiff did not specifically address subsection (d).)

 

Finally, Plaintiff argues that Lyft’s Fact 17 relies solely on the Complaint’s allegations, which is improper to support the fact that Gevorkyan was using the Lyft platform as an independent contractor pursuant to Lyft’s TOS.  However, even if this fact were disregarded, Lyft’s Facts 6 (Gevorkian and Lyft entered into the December 12, 2022 version of the TOS on January 16, 2023), 7 (Lyft approved Gevorkian to drive on the Lyft app as an independent contractor), and 8 (the December 12, 2022 TOS was the operative contract governing Gevorkyan and Lyft’s relationship at the time of the subject incident) taken together already reiterate in parts what Fact 17 states, as well as cite to Mr. Carr’s declaration and the December 12, 2022 TOS terms.  This will not be a basis to deny the motion. 

 

Based on the above, the Court finds that Plaintiff has not raised a triable issue of material fact as to Issue No. 1 – regarding Gevorkyan’s status as an independent contractor and whether Lyft is vicariously liable for Gevorkyan’s alleged negligence.  As such, the motion is granted as to Issue No. 1. 

 

C.    Lyft’s Direct Liability for Negligence (Issue 2)

 

Lyft argues that it cannot be directly liable for negligence because it did not (1) operate/drive/control, (2) own/maintain the vehicle, or (3) entrust the vehicle to Gevorkyan, at the time of the subject incident. 

 

First, it argues that it did not drive the vehicle.  Vehicle Code, § 305 defines “driver” as “a person who drives or is in actual physical control of a vehicle.”  Gevorkyan testified he drove a 2022 Toyota Sienna.  (Gevorkian Depo. at p.12.)  Colton Carr, Lyft’s Safety Specialist, states that: “Based upon information and belief, I understand that Gevorkyan was driving a 2022 Toyota Sienna at the time of the Accident. Lyft does not, in the ordinary course, own, possess, or operate the vehicles that app-based drivers utilize while providing transportation services via the Lyft Platform. Lyft has no record of owning, maintaining, or repairing the 2022 Toyota Sienna that Gevorkyan was driving at the time of the accident.”  (Carr Decl., ¶29.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that the Court cannot rely on Mr. Carr’s statements made upon information and belief.  However, Mr. Carr only stated upon information and belief that Gevorkyan drove a 2022 Toyota Sienna.  Mr. Carr then states (not upon information and belief) that Lyft does not own, possess, or operate the vehicles that its app-based drivers utilize.  The logical conclusion from these statements is that because Lyft did not own any vehicles driven by its app-based drivers, Lyft did not own the vehicle driven by Gevorkyan at the time of the subject incident.  Plaintiff has not raised a triable issue of material fact on whether Lyft owned the vehicle driven by Gevorkyan. 

 

Second, Lyft argues it did not negligently entrust the vehicle to Gevorkyan because it did not own the vehicle in the first place.  (Veh. Code, § 17150 [“Every owner of a motor vehicle is liable and responsible for death or injury to person or property resulting from a negligent or wrongful act or omission in the operation of the motor vehicle, in the business of the owner or otherwise, by any person using or operating the same with the permission, express or implied, of the owner.”] [emphasis added].)  For the same reasons discussed above, Lyft has established its burden on negligent entrustment theories and Plaintiff has not raised a triable issue of material fact on this claim. 

 

Third, Lyft argues that any other theories of direct negligence are unavailing as it did not maintain the vehicle or control Gevorkian.  (As discussed above, Gevorkyan had the freedom to choose when to accept rides, etc.) 

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that Lyft undertook screening of Gevorkyan, required him to take training classes, and ran driving record and criminal background checks to ensure he met necessary regularly requirements.  Plaintiff contends Prop. 22 does not abolish Lyft’s direct negligence in “performing undertakings it elected… to perform.”  (Opp. at p.24.)  Plaintiff argues: “Plaintiff’s general-negligence cause of action alleges that Defendants ‘negligently operated, owned, controlled, maintained, and entrusted their vehicle in such a way that it collided with Plaintiff’; Lyft’s own screening, training, and monitoring of the driver it authorized to carry the public fall within that allegation. If the Court concludes otherwise, Plaintiff requests leave to amend rather than judgment.”  (Id.)

 

However, Plaintiff did not assert a claim for negligent hiring, supervising, or retaining Gevorkyan against Lyft in the Complaint.  “A party may not oppose a summary judgment motion based on a claim, theory, or defense that is not alleged in the pleadings.”  (California Bank & Trust v. Lawlor (2013) 222 Cal.App.4th 625, 637, fn.3; Schmidt v. Citibank, N.A. (2018) 28 Cal.App.5th 1109, 1125 [“Again, we may not consider a new theory raised by a plaintiff in opposition to summary judgment, or on appeal from the granting of summary judgment, where that theory is not supported by the pleadings.”].)  This is not a situation where Plaintiff’s Complaint allegations were deficient such that the Court would be inclined to consider transmuting this motion for summary judgment/adjudication into a motion for judgment on the pleadings.  Rather, Plaintiff seeks leave to amend the complaint to assert a new theory of liability against Lyft.  Such a request is improper at this time.  Plaintiff has not filed a motion for leave to amend the complaint to add a new cause of action, nor has he sought a continuance based on C.C.P. § 437c(h).  The request for leave to amend the complaint by way of his opposition request is denied. 

 

Additionally, even if briefly considered, Plaintiff has not raised triable issues of material fact that any such screening/training of Gevorkyan was improperly or negligently conducted, that Gevorkyan had a criminal, DUI, or any other records relevant to his driving history, etc.  He has not raised triable issues of material fact showing that Lyft’s regulatory screenings of Gevorkyan or vehicle inspection were negligent in any manner.  

 

As such, Plaintiff has not raised a triable issue of material fact against Lyft regarding direct liability.  As such, the motion is granted as to Issue No. 2.

The grounds for a demurrer must appear on the face of the pleading or from judicially noticeable matters. (C.C.P. § 430.30(a); Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal. 3d 311, 318.) (1981) 123 Cal.  App. 3d 593, 604.) But this does not include contentions; deductions; conclusions of fact or law alleged in the complaint; facts impossible in law; or allegations contrary to facts of which a court may take judicial notice.  (Blank, supra, 39 Cal. 3d at 318.)

It is an abuse of discretion to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend if there is a reasonable probability that the defect can be cured by amendment. (Schifando v. ANALYSIS:

 

City demurs to the Second Cause of Action for Premises Liability as to Count 3 – Dangerous Condition of Public Property on the grounds that it fails to allege sufficient facts because: (1) the SAC fails to allege any City-owned property; and (2) City is statutorily immune under Government Code, § 818.2 for any injury caused by its alleged failure to enforce parking laws and obstruction ordinances. 

 

The Second Cause of Action’s Premises Liability Cause of Action at Count 3 is directed against City.  Plaintiff alleges that City, who owned public property on which a dangerous condition existed, had actual and constructive notice of the existence of a dangerous condition in sufficient time prior to the injury to have corrected it.  (SAC, Second Cause of Action at PLD-PI-001(4) at Prem. L-4, Count 3(a).)  Plaintiff alleges the condition was created by City’s employees.  (Id. at Prem. L-4, Count 3(b).)  In the attachment page, Plaintiff alleges in relevant part:  

 

Defendants negligently maintained and controlled the sidewalk area at or near 11829 and 11835 Victory Blvd., Los Angeles, CA 91606. In that area, wooden beams or similar objects were protruding from a parked vehicle and extended into the pedestrian pathway, creating a hazardous obstruction.

 

Defendants allowed this condition to exist by permitting the vehicle with protruding beams to remain in a manner that encroached upon the sidewalk and posed a danger to those using the area with ordinary care. Defendants failed to take reasonable steps to remove the obstruction, correct the condition, or provide any warning of the hazard.

 

Defendants owned, controlled, and/or maintained the public property where this incident occurred and knew or should have known that the vehicle and its protruding beams created a dangerous condition. Despite such knowledge, Defendants failed to remedy the condition within a reasonable time.

 

On or about February 16, 2024, Plaintiff was riding an electric scooter along the sidewalk in this area when he encountered the protruding beams extending from the parked vehicle. Due to the placement and nature of the obstruction, Plaintiff was unable to avoid it, collided with the beams, fell, and sustained serious injuries.

 

The presence of the vehicle with protruding beams obstructing the sidewalk constituted a dangerous condition that posed a substantial risk of harm to persons using the area with due care, and this condition was a direct and proximate cause of Plaintiff’s injuries.

 

This claim is brought pursuant to Government Code Section 835 and 815.2.

 

Defendants, CITY OF LOS ANGELES; and DOES 2 through 25, were served with a claim for damages pursuant to Government Code Section 911.2 on or about August 16, 2024 and was rejected on September 16, 2024.

 

…

 

(SAC, MC-025, Attachment to Premises Liability Cause of Action.) 

 

A.    City-Owned Property – Sustained Without Leave to Amend

 

“The sole statutory basis for imposing liability on public entities as property owners is Government Code section 835.”  (Cerna v. City of Oakland (2008) 161 Cal.App.4th 1340, 1347.)  Section 835 state:

 

Except as provided by statute, a public entity is liable for injury caused by a dangerous condition of its property if the plaintiff establishes that the property was in a dangerous condition at the time of the injury, that the injury was proximately caused by the dangerous condition, that the dangerous condition created a reasonably foreseeable risk of the kind of injury which was incurred, and that either:

 

(a) A negligent or wrongful act or omission of an employee of the public entity within the scope of his employment created the dangerous condition; or

 

(b) The public entity had actual or constructive notice of the dangerous condition under Section 835.2 a sufficient time prior to the injury to have taken measures to protect against the dangerous condition.

 

(Gov. Code, § 835.) 

 

“A plaintiff's allegations, and ultimately the evidence, must establish a physical deficiency in the property itself. [Citations.] A dangerous condition exists when public property ‘is physically damaged, deteriorated, or defective in such a way as to foreseeably endanger those using the property itself,’ or possesses physical characteristics in its design, location, features or relationship to its surroundings that endanger users. [Citation.]”  (Cerna v. City of Oakland (2008) 161 Cal.App.4th 1340, 1347–1348.)  The Cerna court also addressed third-party conduct as follows:

 

A public entity may be liable for a dangerous condition of public property even where the immediate cause of plaintiff's injury is a third party's negligent or illegal act … if some physical characteristic of the property exposes its users to increased danger from third party negligence or criminality. [Citation.] But it is insufficient to show only harmful third party conduct, like the conduct of a motorist. “ ‘[T]hird party conduct, by itself, unrelated to the condition of the property, does not constitute a “dangerous condition” for which a public entity may be held liable.’ ” [Citation.] There must be a defect in the physical condition of the property and that defect must have some causal relationship to the third party conduct that injures the plaintiff. [Citation.] “[P]ublic liability lies under [Government Code] section 835 only when a feature of the public property has ‘increased or intensified’ the danger to users from third party conduct.” [Citation.]

 

(Cerna, supra, 161 Cal.App.4th at 1348 [citations omitted].)

 

City argues that Plaintiff has not alleged a dangerous condition on City-owned property and that that third-party conduct does not constitute a dangerous condition. 

 

A review of the SAC shows that Plaintiff’s allegations rely on the placement of wooden beams or similar objects protruding from a parked vehicle, which extended into the pedestrian pathway.  However, Plaintiff has not alleged facts regarding any physical condition of the sidewalk itself (or any other City-owned property).  A parked vehicle carrying beams/objects is not a deficient physical condition of the public property. 

 

Plaintiff argues that the FAC alleges that the condition was created by City’s employees.  Plaintiff argues also that City may be liable for a dangerous condition if Plaintiff’s injury is the result of a third party’s negligent/illegal act if some physical condition of the property exposed its users to increased danger from a third-party’s negligent conduct.  Plaintiff contends the FAC’s allegations “clearly assert that the dangerous condition was the condition of the sidewalk which included a physical impediment to pedestrians and cyclists.”  (Opp. at p.6.)  However, based on the bare allegations of the FAC, it is unclear what defective/dangerous physical conditions were created by City’s employees or what the physical impediments entailed, as no physical condition of the sidewalk was specifically alleged. 

 

Moreover, the fact that there was a parked vehicle in the parking lot or had beams protruding onto the sidewalk (it is unclear if the City-owned property included the parking lot and/or the sidewalk) does not amount to a dangerous condition without further allegations that a defect in the physical condition of the property had a causal relationship to the third-party conduct of parking the vehicle and having protruding beams.  Thus, the Second Cause of Action fails to state sufficient facts to constitute a Premises Liability – Dangerous Condition of Public Property claim against City.

 

For these reasons, the demurrer to the Second Cause of Action is sustained.  At this point, the Court finds that leave to amend would be futile.  As previously discussed in the Court’s February 23, 2026 ruling on City’s demurrer to the FAC, Plaintiff’s Claim for Damages to Person or Property (“government claim”) alleged facts that Plaintiff was riding his scooter on the sidewalk in a poorly lit and/or illuminated area, when he collided with improperly placed wooden beams/similar objects, causing him to fall and sustain injuries.  (See City RJN, Ex. A.)  The Court sustained the demurrer to the FAC with respect to inadequate lighting allegations—which the Court observes is no longer alleged in the SAC.  His government claim does not assert any physical defects in City-owned property.  Thus, the only remaining allegations are with respect to the parked vehicle with protruding beams, which is not a defect to the physical condition of the property/sidewalk.  Thus, the demurrer is sustained without leave to amend. 

 

 

B.    Government Code, § 818.2 – Sustain Without Leave to Amend  

 

Government Code, § 818.2 states: “A public entity is not liable for an injury caused by adopting or failing to adopt an enactment or by failing to enforce any law.” 

 

“Where a city provides streets or sidewalks, or both, it does so with the expectation that motorists and pedestrians will make a lawful and not an unlawful use of them. The fact that the city may reasonably foresee that some motorists and pedestrians will use them in a negligent manner to the injury of others does not make it a joint tortfeasor with every motorist or pedestrian who uses them to the injury of another. While a city may by ordinance prohibit a misuse or negligent use of its streets and sidewalks, its failure to enforce such an ordinance imposes no liability upon it, in the absence of statute. This is so regardless of whether its failure is occasioned by want of barricades or signs or by want of necessary police officers.”  (Campbell v. City of Santa Monica (1942) 51 Cal.App.2d 626, 629.) 

City argues that it is immune from liability under section 818.2 for failure to enforce any parking and obstruction laws. 

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that his allegations do not assert claims against City for failure to tow a vehicle parked next to a sidewalk or to enforce parking/obstruction laws.  (Opp. at p.6.) 

 

Although Plaintiff does not explicitly allege failure to enforce parking/obstruction laws, the SAC alleges that “Defendants allowed this condition to exist by permitting the vehicle with protruding beams to remain in a manner that encroached upon the sidewalk and posed a danger to those using the area with ordinary care. Defendants failed to take reasonable steps to remove the obstruction, correct the condition, or provide any warning of the hazard.”  (SAC, MC-025, Attachment to Premises Liability Cause of Action [emphasis added].)  Plaintiff also alleges that Defendants “failed to remedy the condition” and that “[d]ue to the placement and nature of the obstruction,” Plaintiff was unable to avoid it, collided with the beams, and was injured.  (Id.) 

 

To the extent that Plaintiff alleges that City allowed the condition to exist and failed to remove or correct the condition, City is immune from adopting or failing to adopt an enactment or by failing to enforce the law, including parking and/or obstruction violations.  The mere fact that the vehicle and beams existed over the sidewalk or potentially City-owned property, is not enough without a specific statute imposing liability against City. 

 

This is another ground to sustain the demurrer to the Second Cause of Action.

ANALYSIS:

 

Davis and Cedar demur to the FAC’s Second to Fifth Causes of Action on the grounds that they fail to state sufficient facts and are uncertain. 

 

A.    Second Cause of Action for Promissory Fraud – Sustained with Leave to Amend

 

Promissory fraud is a subspecies of a fraud and deceit claim.  (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 638; Civ. Code, § 1710(4).)  “A promise to do something necessarily implies the intention to perform; hence, where a promise is made without such intention, there is an implied misrepresentation of fact that may be actionable fraud.”  (Lazar, supra, 12 Cal.4th at 638.)  An action for promissory fraud may lie where a defendant fraudulently induces the plaintiff to enter into a contract.  (Id.) “[T]o sufficiently allege[] defendant made a misrepresentation, the complaint must allege (1) the defendant made a representation of intent to perform some future action, i.e., the defendant made a promise, and (2) the defendant did not really have that intent at the time that the promise was made, i.e., the promise was false.”  (Beckwith v. Dahl (2012) 205 Cal.App.4th 1039, 1060.)  “Each element must be alleged with particularity.”  (Id.)  To plead the first element that the defendant made a promise, the complaint must state facts showing how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were tendered.  (Id.)  The second element of the falsity of the promise is sufficiently pled with a general allegation the promise was made without an intention of performance.  (Id.) 

 

In the Second Cause of Action, Plaintiff alleges that Davis represented he would perform booking services, coordinate with Plaintiff, and utilize Plaintiff’s systems, but the representations were false and made without intent to perform at the time they were made.  (FAC, ¶¶47-48.)  Plaintiff contends it relied on the representations and paid Davis and that Davis’s subsequent conduct demonstrates fraudulent intent.  (Id., ¶¶49-50.)  Plaintiff also alleges that on December 20, 2025, Davis knowingly and falsely misrepresented to the performer that the show had been moved, intending the performer’s reliance, such that Plaintiff was damaged.  (Id., ¶¶51-53.)

 

The allegations do not rise to the level of specificity required for a fraud claim.  Plaintiff generally alleges that Davis represented he would perform booking services orally on November 12, 2025, but the allegations are not specific as to where, to whom (as Plaintiff is a corporation), and by what means the representations were tendered.  Moreover, Plaintiff has not alleged specific facts regarding Davis’s representations regarding coordinating with Plaintiff and utilizing Plaintiff’s systems. 

 

Next, Plaintiff alleges that Davis made misrepresentations to the performer, to which the performer relied, thereby damaging Plaintiff.  However, Plaintiff has not alleged facts that Davis made misrepresentations to Plaintiff regarding the December 20, 2025 performance.  Rather, the Complaint alleges that the performer (not Davis) confirmed on December 13, 2025 that she would be available on December 20, 2025.  (FAC, ¶21.)  Thus, Plaintiff has not alleged a valid basis for Fraud against Davis for representations (if any) he made to Plaintiff regarding the December 20, 2025 performance.

 

The demurrer to the Second Cause of Action is sustained with leave to amend.

 

B.    Third Cause of Action for Intentional Interference with Contractual Relations – Sustained with Leave to Amend

 

“The elements which a plaintiff must plead to state the cause of action for intentional interference with contractual relations are (1) a valid contract between plaintiff and a third party; (2) defendant's knowledge of this contract; (3) defendant's intentional acts designed to induce a breach or disruption of the contractual relationship; (4) actual breach or disruption of the contractual relationship; and (5) resulting damage.”  (Pac Gas & Electric Co. v. Bear Stearns & Co. (1990) 50 Cal.3d 1118, 1126.)

 

In the Third Cause of Action, Plaintiff alleges that it had a valid contract with the performer, Defendants had actual knowledge of that contract through the December 17, 2025 cease-and-desist letter, and they intentionally interfered by inducing the performer not to perform at Plaintiff’s venue through false representations.  (FAC, ¶¶56-58.)  Plaintiff alleges the performer relied, such that the contract was disrupted.  (Id., ¶59.)  Plaintiff contends Defendants’ conduct was independently wrongful, including fraud and misrepresentation.  (Id., ¶60.)  

 

Plaintiff alleges in a conclusory manner that it had a valid contract with the performer, but no further details are provided regarding this alleged contract.  (See FAC, ¶56.)  At most, Plaintiff alleges that on December 13, 2025, a performer appeared at Plaintiff’s venue and confirmed her availability for a subsequent performance scheduled for December 20, 2025.  (Id., ¶21.)  However, these two allegations together do not rise to the level of establishing a valid contract between Plaintiff and the performer. 

 

Next, Plaintiff alleges that all Defendants were on notice due to the December 17, 2025 cease-and-desist letter, but the allegations only mention that Plaintiff sent the letter to the Yamashiro parties and that this notice was transmitted to Davis, such that “all Defendants” had actual notice.  (FAC, ¶¶25-26.)  Davis and Cedar argue there are no allegations regarding Cedar’s knowledge.  Plaintiff alleges that Cedar managed, operated, and/or controlled the Yamashiro venue.  (Id., ¶5.)  However, it is unclear if Cedar was included in Plaintiff’s undefined “Yamashiro parties” term.  The FAC does not otherwise allege that Cedar had actual notice or whether it engaged in intentional acts of interference with the alleged contract. 

 

The demurrer to the Third Cause of Action is sustained with leave to amend.

 

C.    Fourth Cause of Action for Interference with Prospective Economic Relations – Sustained with Leave to Amend

 

The elements of the tort of intentional interference with prospective economic advantage are: “(1) an economic relationship between the plaintiff and some third party, with the probability of future economic benefit to the plaintiff; (2) the defendant's knowledge of the relationship; (3) intentional acts on the part of the defendant designed to disrupt the relationship; (4) actual disruption of the relationship; and (5) economic harm to the plaintiff proximately caused by the acts of the defendant.”  (Korea Supply Co. v. Lockheed Martin Corp. (2003) 29 Cal.4th 1134, 1153.)  “In addition, the plaintiff must allege that the defendant's conduct was wrongful by some measure beyond the fact of the interference itself. [Citation.] In this context, an act is independently wrongful if it is unlawful, that is, if it is proscribed by some constitutional, statutory, regulatory, common law, or other determinable legal standard. [Citation.] The conduct must be independently actionable. The act of interference with prospective economic advantage is not tortious in and of itself, but requires pleading that a defendant has engaged in an act that was independently wrongful in order to distinguish lawful competitive behavior from tortious interference.”  (Stevenson Real Estate Services, Inc. v. CB Richard Ellis Real Estate Services, Inc. (2006) 138 Cal.App.4th 1215, 1220 [internal quotation marks and citations omitted].) 

 

In the Fourth Cause of Action, Plaintiff allege it had ongoing and prospective economic relationships with performers, customers, and patrons; Defendants knew of the relationships; Defendants engaged in wrongful conduct, including fraud, concealment, and diversion to disrupt he relationships; and Defendants knew harm was substantially certain to occur.  (FAC, ¶¶64-67.) 

 

Plaintiff’s allegations are conclusory as Plaintiff has not identified the third parties or what future economic benefit they would have provided to Plaintiff.  Furthermore, Plaintiff has not alleged an independently wrongful act by Davis and/or Cedar.  To the extent Plaintiff relies on fraudulent conduct by Davis, that claim is not sufficiently alleged as discussed above.  With respect to Cedar, Plaintiff has not alleged what independently wrongful act Cedar engaged in to disrupt the economic relationship.

 

The demurrer to the Third Cause of Action is sustained with leave to amend.

 

D.    Fifth Cause of Action for Unjust Enrichment/Restitution – Sustained with Leave to Amend

 

Under California law, unjust enrichment is not a cause of action.  (Melchior v. New Line Productions, Inc. (2003) 106 Cal. App. 4th 779, 794.)  Instead, it is the failure to make restitution under circumstances where it is equitable to do so.  (Id.)  Unjust enrichment is a general principle, underlying various legal doctrines and remedies, rather than a remedy itself.  (Id.)  It is synonymous with restitution.  (Id.)  This is the basis for the general rule in California, which finds that unjust enrichment is not a cause of action, but a claim for restitution.  (Hill v. Roll Int'l Corp. (2011) 195 Cal. App. 4th 1295, 1307.)  “[R]estitution is a remedy and not a freestanding cause of action.”  (Reid v. City of San Diego (2018) 24 Cal.App.5th 343, 362.)

 

Therefore, the demurrer to the Fifth Cause of Action is sustained as there is no cause of action for Unjust Enrichment or Restitution.  In opposition, Plaintiff seeks leave to amend to allege a different quasi-contract type claim in the alternative if the benefits sought lie outside the booking contract.  The Court will allow leave to amend.

Steven Gringer (“Plaintiff”) alleges that on December 30, 2025, he leased a 2026 Volkswagen Tiguan, which was warranted by Defendant Volkswagen Group of America Inc. (“VW”).  Plaintiff alleges that the vehicle was delivered with serious defects and nonconformities to the warranty.  He presented the vehicle for repair, but VW was unable to conform the vehicle to applicable warranties.  Plaintiff also contends that Defendant New Century Volkswagen (“New Century”) failed to properly store, prepare, and repair the subject vehicle. 

 

The Complaint, filed June 16, 2026, alleges: (1) Violation of Song-Beverly Act – Breach of Express Warranty against VW; (2) Violation of Song-Beverly Act – Breach of Implied Warranty against VW; and (3) Negligent Repair against New Century only.

LEGAL STANDARD:

 

The grounds for a demurrer must appear on the face of the pleading or from judicially noticeable matters. (C.C.P. § 430.30(a); Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal. 3d 311, 318.) A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) The only issue involved in a demurrer hearing is whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Id.)

 

A demurrer assumes the truth of all factual, material allegations properly pled in the challenged pleading. (Blank, supra, 39 Cal. 3d at p. 318.) No matter how unlikely or improbable, the plaintiff’s allegations must be accepted as true for the purpose of ruling on the demurrer. (Del E. Webb Corp. v. Structural Materials Co. (1981) 123 Cal.  App. 3d 593, 604.) But this does not include contentions; deductions; conclusions of fact or law alleged in the complaint; facts impossible in law; or allegations contrary to facts of which a court may take judicial notice.  (Blank, supra, 39 Cal. 3d at 318.)

 

Pursuant to C.C.P. §§ 430.10(e) and (f), the party against whom a complaint has been filed may demur to the pleading on the grounds that the pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action, or that the pleading is uncertain, ambiguous and/or unintelligible. It is an abuse of discretion to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend if there is a reasonable probability that the defect can be cured by amendment. (Schifando v. City of Los Angeles (2003) 31 Cal. 4th 1074, 1082.)

 

MEET AND CONFER:

 

C.C.P. §§ 430.41(a) and 435.5(a) require that the moving party meet and confer with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to the demurrer and/or motion to strike. Upon review, the Court finds the meet and confer requirements were met. (See Phan Decl., ¶¶3-6.)  

 

ANALYSIS:

 

New Century demurs to the Third Cause of Action in the Complaint.

 

In the Third Cause of Action, Plaintiff alleges that he delivered the subject vehicle to New Century for repair during the express warranty period for a warranted defect in the subject vehicle.  (Compl., ¶41.)  He contends New Century owed him a duty to use ordinary care and skill in storage, preparation and repair of the subject vehicle in accordance with industry standards, and breached that duty.  (Id., ¶¶42-43.)  He alleges New Century’s negligent breach of its duties proximately caused Plaintiff’s damages.  (Id., ¶44.)   

 

The elements of negligence are “duty, breach of duty, proximate cause, and damages.”  (Carlsen v. Koivumaki (2014) 227 Cal.App.4th 879, 892.)

 

The allegations merely allege the elements of Negligence but fail to allege what conduct New Century engaged in that constituted a breach of its duties or how Plaintiff was injured.  For example, Plaintiff alleges that New Century owed him a duty to store, prepare, and repair the subject vehicle and that New Century’s failure to do so constituted breach, thereby injuring Plaintiff.  However, it is unclear what the nature of this breach and resulting damages involved.  Did New Century negligently store the vehicle such that it got damaged and Plaintiff is claiming property damages? How were New Century’s attempted repairs negligent?  Plaintiff should provide additional facts to support this claim.  The demurrer is sustained with leave to amend on this basis.

 

New Century also argues that the Negligence claim is barred by the economic loss rule.  However, at this time, the Court cannot ascertain whether the economic loss rule applies because it is not clear on the face of the Complaint whether this cause of action is based on the warranty or for separate conduct that caused damage to the vehicle (such as improper storage that caused the subject vehicle to get scratched, etc.).  

 

The demurrer to the Third Cause of Action is sustained with leave to amend.

On August 26, 2026, Defendants filed a Demurrer to the FAC.  On August 28, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition. 

 

LEGAL STANDARD:

MEET AND CONFER:

 

C.C.P. §§ 430.41(a) and 435.5(a) require that the moving party meet and confer with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to the demurrer and/or motion to strike.  However, this requirement does not apply to unlawful detainer actions. (C.C.P. § 430.41(d)(2).)  As this is an unlawful detainer action, there is no meet and confer requirement for this motion.

 

ANALYSIS:

 

First, Defendants argue that the Notice is defective because it was not properly served. 

 

The Lease at section 23.1 states in relevant part:

 

Notice Requirements. All notices required or permitted by this Lease or applicable law shall be in writing and may be delivered in person (by hand or by courier) or may be sent by regular, certified or registered mail or U.S. Postal Service Express Mail, with postage prepaid or by facsimile transmission, or by email, and shall be deemed sufficiently given if served in a manner specified in this Paragraph 23. The addresses noted adjacent to a Party’s signature on this Lease shall be that party’s address for delivery or mailing of notices. Either Party may by written notice to the other specify a different address for notice, except that upon Lessee’s taking possession of the Premises, the Premises shall constitute Leesee’s address for notice. …

 

(Compl. at Standard Multi-Tenant Shopping Center Lease – Net at p.14, § 23.1.)  On page 19, under the Lessee Gevorg Khalatyan and Ani Kababchian’s signature portion, Plaintiffs state their addresses as follows:

 

12717 Vanowen St., Apt 202, North Hollywood, CA 91605

7020 Lennox Ave., Apt 5, Van Nuys, CA 91405

 

(Lease at p.19.) 

 

Defendants argue that the Lease’s Notice Requirements provision requires that Defendants send notices to Defendants’ Venowen and Lennox addresses, relying on section 23.1’s sentence: “The addresses noted adjacent to a Party’s signature on this Lease shall be that party’s address for delivery or mailing of notices.”  Defendants argue that The Three-Day Notice to Pay Rent or Quit was not served at either of these addresses. 

 

However, section 23.1 further states: “Either Party may by written notice to the other specify a different address for notice, except that upon Lessee’s taking possession of the Premises, the Premises shall constitute Lessee’s address for notice.” 

 

The Three-Day Notice’s proofs of service show that it was personally served on Gevorg Khalatyan, and that Ani Kababchian was served by leaving a copy with Gevorg Khalatyan and mailing at the same address.   The Court finds service of the Three-Day Notice was proper at the Lankershim address as the Lease stated that upon Defendants’ taking possession of the Premises, the Premises would constitute their address for notice purposes.  The demurrer on this basis is overruled.

 

Second, Defendants argue that the initial Complaint and the FAC are inconsistent because Plaintiff alleged in the Complaint, ¶4 that its interest in the premises was as “PROPERTY MANAGEMENT.”  In the FAC, Plaintiff now alleges that its interest in the premises is “as owner.”  (FAC, ¶4.)  Defendants argue that Plaintiff cannot allege that it is both the owner and property manager.

 

The Court does not find that allegations inconsistent.  As argued by Plaintiff, the LLC owns the property and thereby manages its own property.  The demurrer on this basis is overruled.

 

Third, Defendants argue that the FAC inconsistently alleges that under paragraph 6.a that the parties entered into the Lease on July 1, 2024 for $4,120.80 monthly rent, but it did not mark paragraph 6.d for any Lease amendments.  Defendants argue that the Lease states monthly rent was $4,000, but no other addendums or amendments were attached to explain the rental increase. 

 

The Lease at section 1.9 states the base rent was $4,000 for the period of November 1, 2024.  (Lease at § 1.9 at p.1.)  Paragraph 51 or the Rent Adjustment(s) (Original Term) Standard Lease Addendum (dated June 27, 2024 and signed July 1, 2024) states that the monthly base rent shall be increased on July 1, 2025 and every 12 months thereafter during the Original Term based on the calculation stated in the document.  Thus, the Lease included rental increases.  The demurrer on this basis is overruled.

 

Lastly, Defendants argue that Plaintiff did not properly identify its exhibits as ordered by the Court in its ruling on the prior demurrer to the initial Complaint.

 

Previously, the Court observed there were issues with Plaintiff’s lack of labeling exhibits and advised Plaintiff to properly label the Complaint’s attachments.  (Aug. 19, 2026 Minute Order at p.5.)  However, the Court did not sustain the demurrer to the initial Complaint for Plaintiff’s failure to label exhibits.  Similarly, the Court declines to sustain the demurrer to the FAC based on this reason but cautions Plaintiff to ensure that exhibits are properly labeled for future filings.

 

The demurrer is overruled.
