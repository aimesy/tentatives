DEPARTMENT M LAW AND MOTION RULINGS



Case Number: 25SMCV00569    Hearing Date: September 18, 2026    Dept: M

CASE NAME:             Klein, v. Bavandi, et al. 

CASE NO.:                25SMCV00569

MOTION:                  Petition/Motion to Compel Arbitration 

HEARING DATE:   9/18/2026

 

 

LEGAL STANDARD 

 

Under California and federal law, public policy favors arbitration as an efficient and less expensive means of resolving private disputes. (Moncharsh v. Heily & Blase (1992) 3 Cal.4th 1, 8-9; AT&T Mobility LLC v. Concepcion (2011) 563 U.S. 333, 339.) Accordingly, whether an agreement is governed by the California Arbitration Act (“CAA”) or the Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”), courts resolve doubts about an arbitration agreement’s scope in favor of arbitration.  (Moncharsh, supra, 3 Cal.4th at 9; Comedy Club, Inc. v. Improv West Assocs. (9th Cir. 2009) 553 F.3d 1277, 1284; see also Engalla v. Permanente Med. Grp., Inc. (1997) 15 Cal.4th 951, 971-972 [“California law incorporates many of the basic policy objectives contained in the Federal Arbitration Act, including a presumption in favor of arbitrability [citation] and a requirement that an arbitration agreement must be enforced on the basis of state law standards that apply to contracts in general”].) “[U]nder both the FAA and California law, ‘arbitration agreements are valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, save upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract.’ ” (Higgins v. Superior Crout (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 1238, 1247.) 

 

“Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.2 requires a trial court to grant a petition to compel arbitration if the court determines that an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists.” (Avery v. Integrated Healthcare Holdings, Inc. (2013) 218 Cal.App.4th 50, 59, quotations omitted.) Accordingly, “when presented with a petition to compel arbitration, the court’s first task is to determine whether the parties have in fact agreed to arbitrate the dispute.”  (Ibid.) A petition to compel arbitration is in essence a suit in equity to compel specific performance of a contract. (Id. at 71.) As with any other specific performance claim, “a party seeking to enforce an arbitration agreement must show the agreement’s terms are sufficiently definite to enable the court to know what it is to enforce.” (Ibid. [internal citations omitted].) “Only the valid and binding agreement of the parties, including all material terms well-defined and clearly expressed, may be ordered specifically performed.” (Ibid.) An arbitration agreement “must be so interpreted as to give effect to the mutual intention of the parties as it existed at the time of contracting, so far as the same is ascertainable and lawful.” (Civ. Code, § 1636.) The language of the contract governs its interpretation if it is clear and explicit. (Civ. Code, § 1368.) If uncertainty exists, “the language of a contract should be interpreted most strongly against the party who caused the uncertainty to exist.” (Civ. Code, § 1654.)  

 

The party seeking to compel arbitration bears the burden of proving the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by the preponderance of the evidence. (Engalla v. Permanente Medical Group, Inc. (1997) 15 Cal.4th 951, 972.) It would then be plaintiff’s burden, in opposing the motion, to prove by a preponderance of the evidence any fact necessary to her opposition. (See Ibid.) “In these summary proceedings, the trial court sits as a trier of fact, weighing all the affidavits, declarations, and other documentary evidence, as well as oral testimony received at the court’s discretion, to reach a final determination.” (Ibid.) 

 

As with any contract, mutual assent or consent is necessary for the formation of a valid arbitration agreement. (Civ. Code, §§ 1550, 1565.) “Consent is not mutual, unless the parties all agree upon the same thing in the same sense.” (Civ. Code, § 1580.) The moving party bears the initial burden of showing the existence of an agreement to arbitrate by a preponderance of the evidence. (Mitri v. Arnel Mgmt. Co. (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 1164, 1169 [“Because the existence of the agreement is a statutory prerequisite to granting the petition, the petitioner bears the burden of proving its existence by a preponderance of the evidence.”].)   

 

ANALYSIS 

 

Defendants Uber Technologies Inc., Rasier-CA, LLC, and Rasier, LLC, move to compel Plaintiff Kevin Klein’s complaint to arbitration. Defendants assert that Plaintiff’s instant claims are required to go to arbitration, even though he is not a signatory to any arbitration agreement, because he is an intended third-party beneficiary of an arbitration agreement between Defendants and Lauren Hope Goldberg, and Plaintiff’s claims arise from said agreement, such that Plaintiff is equitably estopped from denying the existence of the arbitration clause’s application here.

 

            As an initial matter, Defendants demonstrate that Defendants and Goldberg electronically entered into an arbitration agreement. Under the Uniform Electronic Transactions Act, an electronic signature “is attributable to a person if it was the act of the person. The act of the person may be shown in any manner, including a showing of the efficacy of any security procedure applied to determine the person to which the electronic record or electronic signature was attributable.” (Civ. Code, § 1633.9(a).) The effect of such electronic signature “is determined from the context and surrounding circumstances at the time of its creation, execution, or adoption, including the parties’ agreement.” (Civ. Code., § 1633.9(b).)  

 

On May 31, 2022, and March 19, 2023, Goldberg was presented with an in-app blocking pop-up screen with the header “We’ve updated our terms.” It also stated in large type, “We

encourage you to read our Updated Terms in full” and under that message had the phrases “Terms of Use” and “Privacy Notice,” which were displayed underlined and in bright blue text, all of which set the text apart from other text on the screen and indicated a hyperlink. When a user clicked the hyperlinks, the Terms of Use or Privacy Notice, that were published on Uber’s website respectively, were displayed. (Kosinski Decl. ¶ 13, Ex. F.) The in-app blocking pop-up screen precluded the use of the Uber app unless or until a user clicked the checkbox on the screen and clicked the large “Confirm” button at the bottom of the screen. (Id. ¶ 14) The in-app blocking pop-up screen expressly stated that: “By checking the box, I have reviewed and agree to the Terms of Use and acknowledge the Privacy Notice.” It also states that: “I am at least 18 years of age.” (Id. ¶ 15.) When a user clicks the checkbox and clicks the “Confirm” button a record of

this consent is simultaneously and electronically captured, recorded, maintained, safeguarded, and stored in the ordinary course of Uber’s business at the time of the events being recorded. (Id. ¶ 16.) This record is linked to the user’s unique identifier associated with the user’s email address and/or mobile telephone number used to access the Uber platform. (Id.) These records show Goldenberg expressly consented to the April 4, 2022, and January 17, 2023, Terms on

February May 31, 2022, and March 19, 2023, by clicking the checkbox and tapping “Confirm.”

(Kosinski Decl., ¶¶ 17-18, Ex. A.) The terms contain a clear and conspicuous Arbitration

Agreement. (Kosinski Decl., ¶ 17, Ex. G.) For instance, under a large header entitled “2. Arbitration Agreement,” the 2023 Agreement states in relevant part:

 

By agreeing to these Terms, you agree that you are required to resolve any claim that you may

have against Uber on an individual basis in arbitration as set forth in this Arbitration Agreement…

 

(a) Agreement to Binding Arbitration Between You and Uber.

 

(1) Covered Disputes: Except as expressly provided below in Section 2(b), you and Uber agree that any dispute, claim, or controversy in any way arising out of or relating to (i) these Terms and prior versions of these Terms, or the existence, breach, termination, enforcement, interpretation, scope, waiver, or validity thereof; (ii) your access to or use of the Services at any time; (iii) incidents or accidents resulting in personal injury to you or anyone else that you allege occurred in connection with your use of the Services (including, but not limited to, your use of the Uber Marketplace Platform or the driver version of the Uber App), regardless whether the dispute, claim, or controversy occurred or accrued before or after the date you agreed to these Terms, and regardless whether you allege that the personal injury was experienced by you or anyone else; and (iv) your relationship with Uber, will be settled by binding individual arbitration between you and Uber, and not in a court of law. This Arbitration Agreement survives after your relationship with Uber ends.

 

[…]

 

(5) Application to Third Parties:

 

This Arbitration Agreement shall be binding upon, and shall include any claims brought by or against any third parties, including but not limited to your spouses, heirs, third-party beneficiaries and assigns, where their underlying claims arise out of or relate to your use of the Services. To the extent that any third-party beneficiary to this agreement brings claims against the Parties, those claims shall also be subject to this Arbitration Agreement.”

 

(Kosinski Decl., ¶ 17, Ex. G, § 2(a)(1), (5).) The Goldberg agreements also have unmistakable delegation clauses. (Id., §2(a)(5); see Rent-A-Center, West, Inc. v. Jackson (2010) 561 U.S. 63, 68-69 [parties may agree to arbitrate gateway questions of arbitrability such as the enforceability of an arbitration agreement and whether claims are covered by the arbitration agreement]; First Options of Chicago, Inc. v. Kaplan (1995) 514 U.S. 938, 944 [to establish this exception, it must be shown by clear and unmistakable evidence that the parties intended to delegate the issue to the arbitrator].)

 

Defendants assert that Plaintiff may be bound as a non-signatory on the basis of equitable estoppel and as a third-party beneficiary of the agreement. (See Suh v. Superior Court (2010) 181 Cal.App.4th 1504, 1513 [stating the various ground recognized for enforcement of an agreement against a non-signatory].)  A defendant may enforce an arbitration clause on grounds¿of¿equitable estoppel when the claims are “dependent upon, or founded in and inextricably intertwined with,” the obligations imposed by the agreement containing the arbitration clause. (Goldman v. KPMG LLP¿(2009) 173 Cal.App.4th 209, 217-218.) To determine whether the plaintiff’s claim is founded on or intimately connected with the contract, courts must examine the facts of the operative complaint. (Id. at 229-230.) A nonsignatory is also estopped from refusing to comply with an arbitration clause when it receives a “direct benefit” from a contract containing an arbitration clause. (Boucher v. Alliance Title Co., Inc. (2005) 127 Cal.App.4th 262, 269.) 

 

A party may enforce an arbitration agreement under a third-party beneficiary theory when the parties to the agreement agreed, upon the formation of their agreement, to confer the benefits to the nonsignatory. (Ronay Family Limited Partnership v. Tweed (2013) 216 Cal.App.4th 830, 838.) A “third-party beneficiary” may enforce a contract where the contract was intended to benefit it. (Ford Motor Warranty Cases, (2023) 89 Cal. App. 5th 1324, 1337.) To show the contracting parties intended to benefit a third party, the record must show that under the express terms of the contract at issue and any other relevant circumstances under which the contract was made: (1) “the third party would in fact benefit from the contract”; (2) “a motivating purpose of the contracting parties was to provide a benefit to the third party”; and (3) enforcement of the contract “is consistent with the objectives of the contract and the reasonable expectations of the contracting parties.” (Goonewardene v. ADP, LLC (2019) 6 Cal.5th 817, 830.) 

 

Plaintiff argues that Defendants have not presented evidence that Goldenberg requested the subject ride, that Klein was the passenger on a ride requested through her account, or that the subject December 16, 2023, trip was requested via Goldenberg’s account. Plaintiff notes that the Complaint does not supply any missing link, as it only alleges that Klein, “utilizing the UBER app, ordered an UBER ride.” (Compl., ¶ 40.)

 

Plaintiff is correct that the Kosinski declaration does not expressly tie the subject Uber ride with Goldberg’s agreement.  However, the Court is still inclined to find an enforceable arbitration agreement exists between Plaintiff and Defendants on the current record. Under the presented, undisputed record, there are only two possibilities, each of which requires the Court to compel arbitration. Critically, Defendants’ proffered declaration establishes that to use Uber’s platforms, a user (including Plaintiff) must register for an account and agree to the Terms of Use. (Kosinski Decl., ¶ 6.) A user could not proceed to order a ride without agreeing to the terms of use. (Id., ¶¶ 14-16.) At the time of the alleged Uber ride, the Terms of Use contained the above arbitration/delegation clauses. (Id., ¶¶ 13, 18.) The Complaint concedes that Plaintiff used the Uber app to order the subject ride. (Compl., ¶ 40.) In combination, these facts necessarily establish either: (1) Plaintiff agreed to the Terms of Use directly; or (2) he used someone else’s Uber app (Goldberg or otherwise) to order the ride. If Plaintiff used someone else’s Uber app to directly hire the ride, then the doctrines of third-party beneficiary and/or equitable estoppel would apply to these facts.

 

Plaintiff’s claims arise from personal injuries sustained during a traffic collision during an Uber ride. Plaintiff alleges that on December 16, 2023, he ordered an Uber ride as a passenger using the Uber app. (Compl., ¶ 40.) Defendant Bavandi was selected by Uber as Plaintiff’s driver. (Id., ¶ 41.) Bavandi pulled up to the curb where Plaintiff was waiting, Plaintiff entered defendant Bavandi’s vehicle to begin the trip, but before Plaintiff had the opportunity to take his seat and to secure his seatbelt, Bavandi suddenly and without warning accelerated forward and began traveling approximately 30 mph eastbound in lane number 1 on Sunset Blvd. (Id., ¶ 43.) Defendant Kenneth Phelp Rice, a pedestrian walking on Sunset Blvd., suddenly and failing to use due and reasonable care, stepped off the sidewalk and into the roadway of Sunset Blvd. directly in the path of Bavandi’s vehicle. (Id., ¶ 44.) Bavandi, suddenly and without warning Plaintiff, slammed on his brakes as he collided with Rice. (Id., ¶ 45.) As a result, Plaintiff was violently jolted and thrown forward and backward due to the sudden forceful collision and abrupt stop. (Id., ¶ 46.) Plaintiff sustained personal injuries due to this collision. (Id., ¶¶ 51-54.) Based upon these allegations, Plaintiff’s claim arises out of, or relates to, the use of the Uber’s services subject to the Terms of Service, and Plaintiff received a direct benefit from the Terms of Service agreement. Unlike the plaintiff in Pillar, Plaintiff received a direct benefit from any agreement between Goldberg (or another individual) and Defendants, since without that agreement, Plaintiff would not have directly hired an Uber ride for his transportation needs. (See Project AG v. Payward Ventures, Inc. (2021) 64 Cal.App.5th 671, 680 [where the plaintiff contracted with a third party to convert money in digital currency (e.g., Bitcoin), and that third party used a digital currency exchange subject to certain terms and conditions which included an arbitration clause, and the defendant exchange used inadequate security protocols which allowed digital currency held in the third party’s account to be stolen, no direct benefit existed because of the intervening contract meant that any benefits Plaintiff received from the earlier agreement between Defendant and Epiphyte were remote].)

 

Accordingly, the motion is GRANTED. The entire action is STAYED pending the completion of the arbitration. (CCP § 1281.4.) The Court sets a status conference re arbitration for July 13, 2027, at 8:30 a.m.


Case Number: 26SMCV00302    Hearing Date: September 18, 2026    Dept: M

CASE NAME:           Chan v. Naami, et al. 

CASE NO.:                26SMCV00302

MOTION:                  Demurrer and Motion to Strike to the Complaint  

HEARING DATE:   9/18/2026

 

LEGAL STANDARD 

 

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context. In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed. (CCP §§ 430.30, 430.70.) At the pleading stage, a plaintiff need only allege ultimate facts sufficient to apprise the defendant of the factual basis for the claim against him. (Semole v. Sansoucie (1972) 28 Cal. App. 3d 714, 721.) A “demurrer does not, however, admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law alleged in the pleading, or the construction of instruments pleaded, or facts impossible in law.” (S. Shore Land Co. v. Petersen (1964) 226 Cal.App.2d 725, 732, internal citations omitted.) 

 

A special demurrer for uncertainty is disfavored and will only be sustained where the pleading is so bad that defendant cannot reasonably respond—i.e., cannot reasonably determine what issues must be admitted or denied, or what counts or claims are directed against him/her. (CCP § 430.10(f); Khoury v. Maly’s of Calif., Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.) Moreover, even if the pleading is somewhat vague, “ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Ibid.)  

 

Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading may serve and file a notice of motion to strike the whole or any part thereof. (CCP § 435(b)(1); Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1322(b).) The court may, upon a motion or at any time in its discretion and upon terms it deems proper: (1) strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or (2) strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of California, a court rule, or an order of the court. (CCP §§ 436(a)-(b); Stafford v. Shultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782 [“Matter in a pleading which is not essential to the claim is surplusage; probative facts are surplusage and may be stricken out or disregarded”].) 

 

“Liberality in permitting amendment is the rule, if a fair opportunity to correct any defect has not been given.” (Angie M. v. Superior Court (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 1217, 1227.) It is an abuse of discretion for the court to deny leave to amend where there is any reasonable possibility that plaintiff can state a good cause of action. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349.) The burden is on plaintiff to show¿in what manner¿plaintiff can amend the complaint, and¿how¿that amendment will change the legal effect of the pleading.¿(Id.) 

 

 

 

ANALYSIS 

 

Defendants Moussa Naami and Fariba Naami demur to Plaintiff Joellen Chan’s Complaint, including the third, seventh and ninth cause of action, and move to strike the request for punitive damages, mental distress, and disgorgement.

 

Third Cause of Action for Unlawful Business Practices

 

            Business and Professions Code section 17200 prohibits “any unlawful, unfair or fraudulent business act or practice.” (Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200.) “An unlawful business practice or act is an act or practice, committed pursuant to business activity, that is at the same time forbidden by law.” (Klein v. Earth Elements, Inc. (1997) 59 Cal.App.4th 965, 969.) “A business practice is unfair within the meaning of the UCL if it violates established public policy or if it is immoral, unethical, oppressive or unscrupulous and causes injury to consumers which outweighs its benefits.” (McKell v. Washington Mutual, Inc. (2006) 142 Cal.App.4th 1457, 1473.)¿To establish a fraudulent practice under the UCL, the plaintiff must show that members of the public are likely to be deceived. (West v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A. (2013) 214 Cal.App.4th 780, 806; see Morgan v. AT&T Wireless Services, Inc. (2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 1235, 1256 [fraud prong of the UCL is less rigorous than common law fraud as common law fraud requires allegations of actual falsity and reasonable reliance while fraud under UCL does not].) 

 

The Complaint alleges Defendants engaged in unlawful, unfair, and fraudulent business practices by repeatedly violating California landlord-tenant laws, local housing ordinances, and duties imposed by common law. (Compl., ¶¶ 65, 67-69.) The Complaint specifically cites violations including: failing to maintain habitable premises; refusing to accept or process lawful rent payments; manufacturing alleged nonpayment of rent; serving defective and inflated notices to pay or quit; engaging in retaliation; reducing or eliminating required housing services; performing unlawful entries; and using the eviction process as a harassment. (Id., ¶ 65.) The Complaint alleges detailed facts concerning the alleged habitability issues (including pest infestations, mold, defective electrical outlets posing fire hazards) (Id., ¶¶15-23.) The Complaint also alleges detailed facts Defendants maintenance which exposed Plaintiff to gas, carbon monoxide, and other toxic fumes. (Id., ¶¶ 24-27.) The Complaint alleges additional facts concerning Defendants harassment and stalking behavior, including that Defendants repeatedly entered Plaintiff’s premises and storage unit without notice, loitered around the unit, peered into the windows, and surveilled Plaintiff by taking photos. (Id., ¶¶ 28-35, 45-47.) The Complaint alleges that Defendants reduced housing services in retaliation against Plaintiff’s complaints of the habitability issues. (Id., ¶¶ 36-39.) Defendants also fraudulently refused rent payment(s) and used this refusal as a pretext to file a retaliatory unlawful detainer action. (Ibid.) Such conduct may be fairly described as unlawful or fraudulent.

 

            Defendants attack Plaintiff’s standing under the UCL. “To satisfy the narrower standing requirements imposed by Proposition 64, a party must now (1) establish a loss or deprivation of money or property sufficient to qualify as injury in fact, i.e., economic injury, and (2) show that that economic injury was the result of, i.e., caused by, the unfair business practice or false advertising that is the gravamen of the claim.”¿ (Kwikset Corp. v. Superior Court (2011) 51 Cal.4th 310, 322.)¿ “There are innumerable ways in which economic injury from unfair competition may be shown. A plaintiff may (1) surrender in a transaction more, or acquire in a transaction less, than he or she otherwise would have; (2) have a present or future property interest diminished; (3) be deprived of money or property to which he or she has a cognizable claim; or (4) be required to enter into a transaction, costing money or property, that would otherwise have been unnecessary.”¿ (Id. at 323; see Law Offices of Mathew Higbee v. Expungement Assistance Services (2013) 214 Cal.App.4th 544, 555-56 [“an identifiable trifle [of injury] is enough for standing”].) Plaintiff alleges a basis for standing under the UCL. As a result of Defendants’ unlawful, unfair, and fraudulent business practices, Plaintiff suffered an injury-in-fact, including the loss of rental value due to diminished habitability, emotional distress, and the expenses and burdens associated with defending against an improperly initiated eviction proceeding. (Compl., ¶ 70.) Plaintiff paid rent while Defendants engaged in the above unlawful conduct. (Id., ¶¶ 5-6.) The unlawful conduct required Plaintiff to surrender more money in rent than she would have reasonably done under the circumstances of diminished habitability and housing services. (Id. ¶¶ 15-27, 36-39.) The conduct also deprived Plaintiff of cognizable property rights. (Id.) This conduct also required Plaintiff to enter into transactions that would have otherwise been unnecessary, such as for medical care. (Id., ¶ 18.)

 

Accordingly, the demurrer is OVERRULED based upon a lack of standing.

 

Seventh Cause of Action for IIED

 

A cause of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress (“IIED”) exists when there is: (1) extreme and outrageous conduct by the defendant with the intention of causing, or reckless disregard of the probability of causing, emotional distress; (2) the plaintiff's suffering severe or extreme emotional distress; and (3) actual and proximate causation of the emotional distress by the defendant's outrageous conduct. (Hughes v. Pair (2009) 46 Cal.4th 1035, 1050-1051.) A defendant’s conduct is “outrageous” when it is so extreme as to exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated in a civilized community. (Ibid.) The defendant must either intend his or her conduct to inflict injury or engaged in it with the realization that injury will result. (Ibid.) Liability for intentional infliction of emotional distress does not extend to mere insults, indignities, threats, annoyances, petty oppressions, or other trivialities. (Ibid.)¿Generally, the question of whether the conduct is in fact outrageous is a question of fact to be determined beyond the pleading stage. (Spinks v. Equity Residential Briarwood Apartments (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1004.) Courts have held that a tenant's emotional distress caused by landlord's alleged “knowing, intentional, and willful” failure to correct defective conditions of the premises can form the basis of an emotional distress claim. (Stoiber v. Honeychuck (1980) 101 Cal.App.3d 903, 921.) “With respect to the requirement that a plaintiff show severe emotional distress, this court has set a high bar. ‘Severe emotional distress means “‘“emotional distress of such substantial quality or enduring quality that no reasonable [person] in civilized society should be expected to endure it.” ’ ”’ [Citation.]” (Hughes, supra, at 1051.) 

 

The Complaint alleges facts supporting the elements of IIED. First, Defendants allegedly engaged in intentional, extreme and outrageous conduct. (Compl., ¶ 97.) Such conduct includes refusing to accept or process Plaintiff’s lawful rent payments; manufacturing a false claim of nonpayment; initiating an eviction proceeding based on their own refusal to accept rent; failing to remedy hazardous and unsanitary conditions (including a refusal to repair hazardous openings caused by rats); venting dangerous gas into Plaintiff’s property; ignoring mold, dampness, and water intrusion; allowing rodent and pest infestations; performing unauthorized entries into Plaintiff’s residence; repeatedly invading Plaintiff’s privacy, including via alleged stalking and surveillance; and withholding housing services. (Id., ¶¶ 15-47, 97.) Whether such behavior, in fact, rises to the level of extreme and outrageous conduct would be a question of fact to be determined outside the pleading stage.

 

Defendants engaged in such conduct in order to pressure Plaintiff into vacating her home through fear, intimidation, and intolerable living conditions. (Compl., ¶ 97.) Defendants intended to cause Plaintiff emotional harm or with reckless disregard for the probability of such harm. (Id., ¶98.) Defendants knew, or reasonably should have known, that their actions would cause Plaintiff to experience severe emotional distress, anxiety, and fear. (Id.)

 

Defendants’ conduct allegedly caused Plaintiff’s emotional distress. (Compl., ¶¶ 28, 31, 34, 43, 47, 99.) Plaintiff suffered severe emotional distress, including anxiety, fear, distress related to the habitability conditions, worry about her housing stability, and disruption of her daily life. (Ibid.)

 

Accordingly, the demurrer is OVERRULED.

 

Ninth Cause of Action for Negligence Per Se

 

The doctrine of negligence per se “is not a separate cause of action, but creates an evidentiary presumption that affects the standard of care in a cause of action for negligence.” (Johnson v. Honeywell Int’l Inc., (2009) 179 Cal. App. 4th 549, 555-56, emphasis added, internal quotations omitted.) As no such independent cause of action exists, the demurrer is SUSTAINED without leave to amend. Plaintiff may rely on the evidentiary doctrine of negligence per se as part of the sixth cause of action for general negligence. 

 

Motion to Strike – “Standalone” Cause of Action for Punitive Damages

 

Defendants assert that Plaintiff improperly pleads a separate cause of action captioned “Punitive Damages." The Court agrees that there is no cause of action for punitive damages, since punitive or exemplary damages “are remedies available to a party who can plead and prove the facts and circumstances [set forth in Civil Code §3294]." (Grieves, supra, 157 Cal.App.3d at 164.)  However, the Complaint does not allege such an independent cause of action. Instead, the Complaint alleges several causes of action claiming punitive damages. (Compl., ¶¶ 8, 56, 80, 100, 122.) The section entitled punitive damages does not claim to be an independent, standalone cause of action. (Id., ¶¶ 123-127.) Defendants cite no rule of pleading which would disallow stating additional allegations in support of the remedy of punitive damages in its own “section” of a complaint. Therefore, the Court denies the motion to strike the targeted paragraphs for this reason.

 

Motion to Strike - Punitive Damages

 

Defendants assert that the Complaint fails to state ultimate facts in support of punitive damages.

 

In order to state a prima facie claim for punitive damages, a complaint must set forth the elements as stated in the general punitive damage statute, Civil Code Section 3294. These statutory elements include allegations that the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud or malice. (Civ. Code § 3294 (a).) Civil Code section 3294(c) defines malice as conduct “intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff,” or “despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.” “‘Oppression’ means despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person’s rights.” (Civ. Code § 3294(c)(2).) Despicable is a powerful term used to describe circumstances that are “base,” “vile,” or “contemptible.” (Coll. Hosp. v. Superior Ct. (1994) 9 Cal.4th 704, 726.) The statute “plainly indicates that absent an intent to injure the plaintiff, “malice” requires more than a “willful and conscious” disregard of the plaintiffs' interests. The additional component of “despicable conduct” must be found.” (Id.)  

 

“In order to survive a motion to strike an allegation of punitive damages, the ultimate facts showing an entitlement to such relief must be pled by a plaintiff. [Citations.] In passing on the correctness of a ruling on a motion to strike, judges read allegations of a pleading subject to a motion to strike as a whole, all parts in their context, and assume their truth. [Citations.] In ruling on a motion to strike, courts do not read allegations in isolation. [Citation.]” (Clauson v. Superior Court (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1253, 1255.) “The mere allegation an intentional tort was committed is not sufficient to warrant an award of punitive damages. [Citation.] Not only must there be circumstances of oppression, fraud or malice, but facts must be alleged in the pleading to support such a claim. [Citation.]” (Grieves v. Superior Ct. (1984) 157 Cal.App.3d 159, 166, fn. omitted.) 

 

Generally, punitive damages are available for breach of implied warranty of habitability where a landlord defendant willfully and consciously disregards a known unhabitable condition. (Stoiber, supra, 101 Cal.App.3d at 925-926.) For example, in Stoiber, the court found that a plaintiff could recover punitive damages for numerous persistent uninhabitable conditions of which she repeatedly notified the landlord defendants. (Id. at 921.) The plaintiff alleged specific uninhabitable conditions, including heavy cockroach infestation, broken interior walls, broken deteriorated flooring on front porch, falling ceiling, deteriorated, overfused electrical wiring, lack of proper plumbing connection to sewage system in bathroom, sewage under bathroom floor, leaking roof, broken windows, and fire hazard. (Ibid.) Despite notice, the defendants knowingly, intentionally and willfully failed to correct the defective conditions by not even attempting to repair the defects, except on one occasion where they sent a plumber in response to a complaint regarding the toilet. (Id. at 913.)

 

            The Complaint alleges facts in support of the allegations of malice. As discussed, the Complaint pleads specific facts supporting the allegations that Defendants maintained unhabitable conditions, knowingly and with a conscious disregard for Plaintiff’s safety. For example, Defendants installed an illegal heater that vented toxic fumes into Plaintiff's home (¶¶ 24-25) and left carbon monoxide detectors and fire alarms nonfunctional despite notice from officials (¶ 27). The Complaint also supports the claim for punitive damages in connection with Defendants alleged intentional harassment and retaliation. (Compl., ¶¶ 28-35.) Defendants repeatedly entered Plaintiff's home and private areas at night without notice, photographed her home without consent. (Id.) Defendants manufactured a false claim of nonpayment after years of collecting rent by check under the mat and filed a retaliatory unlawful detainer based on their own refusal of rent (Id., ¶¶ 39, 51-53.) Such acts of harassment and neglect were intended to make living conditions unbearable so that Plaintiff would vacate the Premises. (Id., ¶¶ 44, 47, 54, 60, 77, 97, 102-103, 124.) Such intentional torts may be fairly considered “intended to harm” within the meaning of malice as defined by section 3294.

 

However, the Court agrees that paragraph 80 should be stricken. Paragraph 80 requests punitive damages in connection with the breach of covenant of good faith and fair dealing, which sounds in contract. (Civ. Code § 3294(a) [excluding breach of contract claims]; see Hicks v. E.T. Legg & Associates (2001) 89 Cal.App.4th 496, 508 [every contract imposes upon each party a duty of good faith and fair dealing in its performance and its enforcement].) “In the absence of an independent tort, punitive damages may not be awarded for breach of contract ‘even where the defendant’s conduct in breaching the contract was wilful [sic], fraudulent, or malicious.’”  (Applied Equipment Corp. v. Litton Saudi Arabia Ltd. (1994) 7 Cal.4th 503, 516.) Stoiber only permits punitive damages in the landlord-tenant context in connection with tortious breaches of the warranty of habitability and for nuisance, which are duties independent of the contract. (Stoiber, supra, 101 Cal.App.3d at 919-920.)

 

Accordingly, the motion is GRANTED in part. Paragraph 80 is stricken. The remainder of the motion is DENIED.

 

Motion to Strike – Disgorgement

 

            Defendants move to strike the request for damages and disgorgement under the UCL, arguing that these remedies are not available. The remedies for UCL claims brought by private individuals are limited to injunctive relief and restitution. (Prakashpalan v. Engstrom, Lipscomb and Lack (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 1105, 1133.)¿“The word ‘restitution’ means the return of money or other property obtained through an improper means to the person from whom the property was taken.”¿ (Clark v. Superior Court (2010) 50 Cal.4th 605, 614.)¿“‘The object of restitution is to restore the status quo by returning to the plaintiff funds in which he or she has an ownership interest.’”¿(Id.)¿Disgorgement of profits is not an available remedy under a UCL claim brought by a private individual. (Korea Supply Co. v. Lockheed Martin Corp. (2003) 29 Cal.4th 1134, 1150-52.)  

 

            The Complaint alleges that Plaintiff suffered “economic and non-economic damages” as a result of Defendants’ unlawful business conduct. (Compl., ¶ 70.) Damages are not an available remedy under the UCL. Therefore, the allegation of resulting damages from the unlawful business conduct is irrelevant to that extent. However, the allegations of resulting damage also supports the standing requirement of the UCL claim. Thus, the allegation of resulting damages is not entirely irrelevant, false, or improperly drawn.

 

As to disgorgement, the Complaint requests “disgorgement of any money obtained or retained by Defendants through their unlawful practices.” (Compl., ¶ 71.) Paragraph 71 does not seek disgorgement of “profits.” It only alleges a claim of restitutionary disgorgement, which is permitted by the UCL. The Prayer, however, alleges entitlement to disgorgement “of ill-gotten gains.” The term “gains” implies that Plaintiff seeks disgorgement of profits.

 

Accordingly, the motion GRANTED as to the allegation “of ill-gotten gains” in Prayer ¶ F, and the remainder of the motion is DENIED.
