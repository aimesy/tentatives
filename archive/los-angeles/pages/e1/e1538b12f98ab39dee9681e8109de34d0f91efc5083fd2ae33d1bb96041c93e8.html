DEPARTMENT 515 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 24STCV10882    Hearing Date: September 1, 2026    Dept: 515

Plaintiff Julianna Bardon’s motion for terminating sanction or in the alternative, issue and evidence sanctions against Defendants Tarek Aly and 3025-3027 Vermont Plaza, LLC and for monetary sanctions is granted in part and denied in part.

 

Plaintiff seeks an order for terminating sanctions or alternatively issue and evidence sanctions against Defendants, as well as monetary sanctions against Defendants and counsel, based on Defendants’ failure to comply with the Court’s prior discovery orders.  Plaintiff seeks monetary sanctions of $4,600 against Defendants and counsel, jointly and severally. Defendants did not file an opposition.

Background

On June 12, 2026, the Court took Plaintiff’s motions to have RFAs deemed admitted against Defendants off calendar as moot because Defendants served substantially compliant verified written responses prior to the hearing. The Court did, however, award monetary sanctions of $1,270 against Defendants and counsel, jointly and severally, to be paid within 30 days of the order.

On July 1, 2026, the Court granted motions to compel responses to form interrogatories, special interrogatories, document requests against both Defendants, plus a total of $7,260 in monetary sanctions against Defendants and counsel, jointly and severally. The Court ordered Defendants to serve verified Code-compliant written responses, with no objections, plus responsive documents, by July 15, 2026. The Court ordered the monetary sanctions paid within 30 days of the order.  

Analysis

Ultimate discovery sanctions are justified where there is a willful discovery-order violation, a history of abuse, and evidence showing that less severe sanctions would not produce compliance with discovery rules. Van Sickle v. Gilbert (2011) 196 Cal.App.4th 1495, 1516.

Plaintiff argues that terminating sanctions, or alternatively, issue or evidence sanctions, are warranted because Defendants have not complied with the Court’s June 12 and July 1 orders.

June 12 Order: Defendants have not paid the $1,270 monetary sanctions order from June 12 as of the filing date of the motion, and they filed no opposition. The Court concludes their non-payment constitutes willful disobedience with the Court’s order. Plaintiff also contends that Defendant Aly did not serve substantially compliant responses to the RFAs prior to the June 12 order, noting that the responses attached to Defendant Aly’s opposition to Plaintiff’s RFA motion were from a different case. Plaintiff has never sought relief from the Court regarding its order taking the RFA motion off calendar, and in fact its counsel did not raise any objection to the Court’s conclusion that Defendant Aly had served substantially compliant responses. On this record, the Court cannot conclude that Defendant Aly has willfully withhold a court order on the RFAs when the Court’s order deemed the issue moot.

July 1 Order:  As with the June 12 order, Defendants have failed to pay the monetary sanctions in the July order. This is further evidence of their willful disobedience with the Court’s order. The willfulness is compounded by the fact that Defendants have also failed to serve verified Code-compliant written responses, with no objections, plus responsive documents, by July 15, 2026. To date, Defendants have not provided any of the ordered discovery responses.

Defendants had served (and filed) inadequate responses to the interrogatories and document requests prior to the July 1 hearing, which contained objections and did not include a document production. The Court therefore granted the motions to compel. Defendants have not opposed the terminating sanctions motions and thus have provided no explanation for their utter disregard of the Court’s July 1 order. The Court is left with the conclusion that Defendants have no intention of living up to their discovery obligations. The fact that they served some prior responses, albeit inadequate and not Code-compliant, militates against terminating sanctions at this time. However, the Court agrees with Plaintiff that increased sanctions are necessary given Defendants’’ intransigence regarding discovery.

The discovery Defendants have failed to answer relates to the issues Plaintiff needs to develop in this habitability case. The issue and evidence sanctions provided below are tailored to address the various discovery requests that Plaintiff sought, and which Defendants have steadfastly refused to provide. The Court issues the following order:

(1)    It is established that the premises at 1303 W. 30th Place, Los Angeles, CA were substandard and untenantable within the meaning of Civil Code Section 1941.1 throughout Plaintiff’s tenancy;

(2)    It is established that Defendants had notice of the substandard and untenantable conditions and failed to repair them within a reasonable time;

(3)    It is established that Defendants owned, managed, and operated the premises and were Plaintiff’s landlords at all relevant times;

(4)    Defendants are prohibited from supporting their affirmative defenses with any evidence; and

(5)    Defendants are prohibited from introducing at trial any document they failed to produce, and any witness they failed to identify, in response to the discovery requests covered by the July 1 order.

The Court declines to impose further monetary sanctions. Defendants have, to date, failed to pay any of the outstanding monetary sanctions. Their failure to pay the money sanctions, coupled with their failure to answer the discovery, forms the basis for why the Court has now exercised its discretion to increase the sanctions to the issue and evidence sanctions listed above.

Conclusion

The motion is denied with regards to  terminating sanctions and monetary sanctions, and granted as to the request for issue and evidence sanctions.


Case Number: 25STCV00598    Hearing Date: September 1, 2026    Dept: 515

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS

Hearing on Demurrer.

The Demurrer is sustained without leave to amend as to the derivative breach of contract claim, and is otherwise overruled.

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Antonio Ponce (Plaintiff), individually and derivatively on behalf of nominal defendant Caliente Landscape, Inc. (Landscape), filed this action against Julio Cesar Ponce (Cesar) and Pierre Charbat (Charbat), collectively “Defendants.” Plaintiff alleges that he built a landscaping and maintenance business beginning in 1992, and that in 2013 he and Cesar, who is his son, formed Landscape as its operating entity, with Plaintiff holding seventy-five percent of the shares and Cesar twenty-five. He alleges that Defendants induced him to give up that interest in stages, that they formed a second company, Caliente Maintenance, Inc. (Maintenance), and divided the business between the two entities, and that Cesar then kept Landscape’s customers and pushed him out of the business he built.

The causes of action in the Fourth Amended Complaint (4AC) are: (1) Breach of Oral Contract; (2) Fraud; (3) Conversion (Direct); (4) Conversion (Derivative); (5) Breach of Fiduciary Duty; (6) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress; and (7) Inspection of Books and Records.

Defendants filed a Demurrer. Plaintiff filed an Opposition.

Defendants previously filed demurrers to the First Amended Complaint and to the Third Amended Complaint (TAC). The Court sustained the demurrer to the TAC in part, with leave to amend, and overruled it in part.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

A demurrer tests whether the pleading states facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (e).) The ground must appear on the face of the pleading or from matter subject to judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.30, subd. (a).) The Court “‘treat[s] the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.’” (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318 (Blank).) It gives the pleading “a reasonable interpretation, reading it as a whole and its parts in their context.” (Ibid.) “If the complaint states a cause of action under any theory, regardless of the title under which the factual basis for relief is stated, that aspect of the complaint is good against a demurrer.” (Quelimane Co. v. Stewart Title Guaranty Co (1998) 19 Cal.4th 26, 38.)

 

ANALYSIS

I. Oral Contract- Derivative Claim

Defendants argue that the First Cause of Action fails because the 4AC pleads no facts showing Landscape was a party to or a beneficiary of the oral agreement.

The First Cause of Action is pleaded both as Plaintiff’s claim in his own right and derivatively on Landscape’s behalf. (4AC, at p. 6.) The Demurrer is directed to the derivative claim. (Dem., at p. 3:5-6.) “[T]he elements of a cause of action for breach of contract are (1) the existence of the contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance, (3) defendant’s breach, and (4) the resulting damages to the plaintiff.” (Oasis West Realty, LLC v. Goldman (2011) 51 Cal.4th 811, 821.)

Here, the 4AC alleges that in May 2022 Cesar and Charbat formed Maintenance and named Plaintiff its sole officer and director. (4AC, ¶ 24.) Cesar and Plaintiff agreed to divide Landscape from Maintenance for tax purposes, with Plaintiff running Maintenance and Cesar running Landscape. (4AC, ¶ 24.) The division was to be one on paper alone. The two entities would operate as a single business, and Landscape would continue to bill the same customers. (4AC, ¶¶ 25-26, 41.)

The 4AC alleges three promises by Cesar in return: $49,000 per month for the work performed and for Maintenance’s payroll; a continuing fifty percent ownership interest and fifty percent of the profits of both entities, whichever of them the income was paid to; and the amounts earned above $4,000 per day on the projects Plaintiff procured. (4AC, ¶¶ 26, 40.) Plaintiff’s consideration was the transfer of his remaining interest in Landscape together with his agreement to the changes in structure Cesar proposed. (4AC, ¶ 43.) Plaintiff performed by continuing to generate business and to operate it, the promised payments diminished and then stopped in 2024, and Plaintiff was damaged in an amount subject to proof. (4AC, ¶¶ 48-52.)

Landscape is not alleged to be a party to the oral agreement. The 4AC alleges an agreement “between the father and son,” under which Cesar promised to pay Plaintiff and Plaintiff transferred his ownership interest as consideration. (4AC, ¶¶ 41, 43.) The derivative claim depends instead on the allegation that “Landscape was to be the beneficiary of the oral agreement therefore a derivative claim is warranted.” (4AC, ¶ 45.)

A person who is not a party to a contract may enforce it only as a third-party beneficiary. “A contract, made expressly for the benefit of a third person, may be enforced by him at any time before the parties thereto rescind it.” (Civ. Code, § 1559.) The section “excludes enforcement of a contract by persons who are only incidentally or remotely benefited by it.” (Martinez v. Socoma Companies, Inc. (1974) 11 Cal.3d 394, 400.) A court examines “the express provisions of the contract at issue, as well as all of the relevant circumstances under which the contract was agreed to, in order to determine not only (1) whether the third party would in fact benefit from the contract, but also (2) whether a motivating purpose of the contracting parties was to provide a benefit to the third party, and (3) whether permitting a third party to bring its own breach of contract action against a contracting party is consistent with the objectives of the contract and the reasonable expectations of the contracting parties. All three elements must be satisfied to permit the third party action to go forward.” (Goonewardene v. ADP, LLC (2019) 6 Cal.5th 817, 830.) The second element asks for “a motivating purpose to benefit the third party, and not simply knowledge that a benefit to the third party may follow from the contract.” (Ibid.)

Here, the statements the 4AC attributes to Defendants do describe a corporate purpose. Cesar represented that the split “was for tax purposes” and that Landscape would continue to run as one. (4AC, ¶ 40.) Defendants told Plaintiff that the split and the change in ownership “would be to the company’s benefit, make it run more smoothly and provide tax benefits.” (4AC, ¶ 55.) Charbat represented that the earlier transfer of a twenty-five percent interest “would be beneficial to the business.” (4AC, ¶ 18.)

The pleading alleges that Defendants knew none of it was true. They “knew it was not for legitimate purposes in the best interests of the company, but instead to strip [Plaintiff] of his ownership interest in the company he built.” (4AC, ¶ 56.) There was “no legitimate business purpose” for granting Cesar a controlling interest, and “[t]he only purpose was to allow CESAR to gain unfettered control over Landscape.” (4AC, ¶ 59.)

The 4AC alleges that Plaintiff himself believed the corporate purpose. He understood the changes “would be helpful to the business,” he “had no reason to disbelieve his own son and business partner,” and he “justifiably relied on Defendants’ assertions that the changes they were making were good for the company.” (4AC, ¶¶ 41, 57, 61.) Those allegations describe what Plaintiff believed the restructuring would do for the company, and Defendants induced that belief. The second element asks instead for a motivating purpose of the contracting parties in making the agreement. This derivative claim depends on Cesar’s promise to pay Plaintiff, and no allegation attributes to either party a purpose of benefiting the corporation by that promise.

The agreement’s benefits ran to Plaintiff, who was to receive half the profits of Landscape and Maintenance, the amounts above $4,000 per day, and his continued fifty percent ownership. (4AC, ¶¶ 26, 40.) The damages alleged for the breach are damages to Plaintiff. (4AC, ¶ 52.) The only role the pleading gives Landscape in the agreement is as the source of the payments Cesar promised to make. (4AC, ¶¶ 49-50.) That role is an obligation on the corporation, not a benefit to it. The allegation that Cesar’s conduct caused Landscape’s foreman and two designers to quit describes an injury to Landscape, not a benefit the contracting parties meant to give it. (4AC, ¶ 44.)

The beneficiary allegation states a legal characterization and no facts. (4AC, ¶ 45.) A demurrer does not admit it. (Blank, supra, 39 Cal.3d at p. 318.) The Court concludes that Landscape was neither a party to nor an intended beneficiary of the oral agreement, and that it has no claim for the agreement’s breach.

The Demurrer to the derivative breach of contract claim is sustained without leave to amend.

II. Direct Conversion

Defendants argue that the Third Cause of Action pleads no wrongful act because the 4AC alleges Plaintiff was induced to transfer his interest voluntarily, leaving fraud and rescission as his remedies. (Dem., at p. 9:22-28.)

“Conversion is the wrongful exercise of dominion over the property of another. The elements of a conversion claim are: (1) the plaintiff’s ownership or right to possession of the property; (2) the defendant’s conversion by a wrongful act or disposition of property rights; and (3) damages.” (Burlesci v. Petersen (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 1062, 1066.) The first element is the plaintiff’s “‘ownership or right to possession of the property at the time of the conversion.’” (Oakdale Village Group v. Fong (1996) 43 Cal.App.4th 539, 543-544.) Shares of stock are subject to an action for conversion. (Payne v. Elliot (1880) 54 Cal. 339, 342.) “It is the general rule that in actions for the conversion of personal property where the property has been procured by fraud, it is not necessary to allege the fraud, but it is sufficient to declare generally, that the property was wrongfully converted.” (Wendling Lumber Co. v. Glenwood Lumber Co (1908) 153 Cal. 411, 414 (Wendling).)

Here, the 4AC alleges that Plaintiff held seventy-five percent of Landscape’s shares, that Defendants took that interest by inducing him to give it up “through false representations about their motives,” and that he is entitled to the return of the shares. (4AC, ¶¶ 67, 69-70.) That is the general averment Wendling holds sufficient. The pleading goes further. The Third Cause of Action is pleaded against Cesar, and Cesar received both transfers the pleading describes. (4AC, at p. 9; 4AC, ¶¶ 19, 56.) Charbat advised Plaintiff to give Cesar an additional twenty-five percent interest because Cesar held a contractor license. Charbat represented that the transfer “would be beneficial to the business,” and Charbat and Cesar both represented that the operations would not change and that Plaintiff would still run the company. (4AC, ¶ 18.) Plaintiff transferred the interest to Cesar in reliance. (4AC, ¶ 19.) Defendants later told Plaintiff that dividing the company and changing its ownership to fifty percent each “would be to the company’s benefit, make it run more smoothly and provide tax benefits.” They knew at the time that the purpose was to strip him of his interest. (4AC, ¶¶ 55-56.)

Consent obtained through fraud “is not absolutely void, but may be rescinded by the parties, in the manner prescribed by the Chapter on Rescission.” (Civ. Code, § 1566.) A party whose consent was obtained through fraud may rescind the contract. (Civ. Code, § 1689, subd. (b)(1).) To effect a rescission the rescinding party must give notice and restore or offer to restore what he received. (Civ. Code, § 1691.) “When notice of rescission has not otherwise been given or an offer to restore the benefits received under the contract has not otherwise been made, the service of a pleading in an action or proceeding that seeks relief based on rescission shall be deemed to be such notice or offer or both.” (Ibid.) The rescinding party may then seek “any other relief to which he may be entitled under the circumstances,” and “[a] claim for damages is not inconsistent with a claim for relief based upon rescission.” (Civ. Code, § 1692.)

Plaintiff alleges that the transfer of his ownership interest was procured through fraud, seeks rescission of it, and prays for an order returning the stock to him. (4AC, ¶ 63; 4AC, Prayer, ¶¶ 1, 3.) Service of that pleading is the notice of rescission and the offer to restore, so the 4AC need not allege a rescission accomplished before the action was filed. (Civ. Code, § 1691.) On the allegations Plaintiff has given notice of rescission and seeks to avoid the transfers. The 4AC therefore pleads a right to possession of shares that Cesar holds and has not returned. (4AC, ¶¶ 47, 70.)

Defendants’ position is that Plaintiff consented, and that a transfer induced by fraud gives him an action for fraud and rescission and nothing else. A conversion claim does not rest on the fraud that procured the property. It rests on the plaintiff’s ownership and the conversion. (Wendling, supra, 153 Cal. at p. 414.) Consent obtained by fraud is consent the defrauded party may avoid, and Plaintiff has avoided it by the pleading he served. Section 1692 permits him to seek damages alongside relief based on the rescission.

The Court concludes that the 4AC pleads each element of conversion, and the Demurrer to the Third Cause of Action is overruled.

III. Derivative Conversion

Defendants challenge Plaintiff’s standing to sue in Landscape’s right, because the 4AC alleges he transferred his entire interest to Cesar in 2022, before the conduct underlying this cause of action, and has held no shares since. (Dem., at pp. 10:12 - 11:6.)

A shareholder suing in a corporation’s right must allege “that plaintiff was a shareholder, of record or beneficially, ... at the time of the transaction or any part thereof of which plaintiff complains.” (Corp. Code, § 800, subd. (b)(1).) California law also “generally requires a plaintiff in a shareholder’s derivative suit to maintain continuous stock ownership throughout the pendency of the litigation.” (Grosset v. Wenaas (2008) 42 Cal.4th 1100, 1119 (Grosset).)

Here, the 4AC alleges that Plaintiff transferred a twenty-five percent interest to Cesar around the end of 2020, leaving each of them a fifty percent owner. (4AC, ¶¶ 18-19.) It alleges that after that transfer Cesar took control of the company’s finances, moved over $200,000 out of the Landscape account to a different account for his benefit, and closed the company’s bank account without Plaintiff’s knowledge. (4AC, ¶¶ 21, 76.) The next dated event in the pleading is November 2021, and the transfer of Plaintiff’s remaining interest is alleged in connection with the agreement made in May 2022. (4AC, ¶¶ 23-24, 43.) Read in the sequence the pleading sets out, the diversion of Landscape’s funds occurred while Plaintiff held half of Landscape’s shares. That satisfies the contemporaneous ownership requirement on the face of the 4AC.

The premise that the 4AC pleads away all of Plaintiff’s ownership in 2022 also overstates the pleading. Plaintiff agreed to the change in structure “with the understanding that the company would continue to run as one and that ANTONIO would still be a fifty percent owner of the entire company,” and the 4AC alleges that he “was still owner of the business that he built.” (4AC, ¶¶ 40-41.)

Grosset reserved the question whether equitable considerations warrant an exception where a merger is used to deprive a plaintiff of standing. (Grosset, supra, 42 Cal.4th at p. 1119.) Haro took up that question outside the merger setting. Equitable considerations bear on shareholder standing, and a shareholder fraudulently induced to transfer his stock “‘remains the equitable owner of it, and may maintain a stockholder’s suit.’” (Haro v. Ibarra (2009) 180 Cal.App.4th 823, 837 (Haro).) On that reasoning Haro held that plaintiffs whose shares the controlling directors had declared forfeited “alleged equitable considerations that warrant an exception to the continuous ownership requirement,” and reversed a judgment of dismissal entered after demurrers were sustained. (Id. at pp. 837-838.) Defendants further argue that the 4AC never alleges Plaintiff has obtained rescission restoring his shareholder status. (Dem., at pp. 10:27 - 11:6.) Haro applied the exception at the pleading stage, before anything had undone the forfeiture. (Id. at p. 838.) The continuous ownership requirement does not defeat the claim at this stage.

Defendants argue that the 4AC pleads no predicate for the exception, because it alleges neither that Plaintiff has obtained or is entitled to a constructive trust over Landscape’s shares, nor that title has reverted to him, nor that he has obtained rescission of the 2022 transfer. (Dem., at pp. 10:27 - 11:6; Reply, at p. 4:3-8.) Defendants further argue that a transfer procured by fraud is voidable rather than void so legal title stays with the transferee until the transfer is rescinded and set aside. (Reply, at p. 4:6-10.)

The voidable-title argument is directed at a claim of present legal title, which Plaintiff does not make. The exception Haro describes turns on equitable ownership, which is what one fraudulently induced to transfer his stock retains before any rescission is adjudicated. (Haro, supra, 180 Cal.App.4th at p. 837.) Conditioning the exception on a completed rescission would deny it to every plaintiff in Plaintiff’s position, because a defendant who takes shares by fraud can always answer that the plaintiff no longer holds them. Haro reversed at the pleading stage, before anything had undone the forfeiture. (Id. at p. 838.) Whether Plaintiff obtains rescission is a merits question and not a pleading question.

The 4AC alleges that Plaintiff parted with his interest because Defendants misrepresented their intentions, and that he seeks rescission of the transfer. (4AC, ¶¶ 56-57, 63.) His loss of shareholder status is the wrong he sues on. Taking those allegations as true, the 4AC does not show on its face that Plaintiff lost standing to sue derivatively.

The Demurrer to the Fourth Cause of Action is overruled.

IV. Fiduciary Duty

Defendants argue that the Fifth Cause of Action fails for the same standing reason. (Dem., at pp. 10:3 - 11:6.) They contend independently that the duty the 4AC describes runs from Cesar to Plaintiff personally rather than to Landscape, and that no corporate harm is pleaded apart from harm to Plaintiff. (Dem., at p. 11:12-25.) The standing argument fails for the reasons discussed above.

An action is derivative “‘if the gravamen of the complaint is injury to the corporation, or to the whole body of its stock and property without any severance or distribution among individual holders, or it seeks to recover assets for the corporation or to prevent the dissipation of its assets.’” (Jones v. H. F. Ahmanson & Co. (1969) 1 Cal.3d 93, 106, quoting Gagnon Co., Inc. v. Nevada Desert Inn, Inc. (1955) 45 Cal.2d 448, 453.) A director “shall perform the duties of a director ... in good faith, in a manner such director believes to be in the best interests of the corporation and its shareholders and with such care, including reasonable inquiry, as an ordinarily prudent person in a like position would use under similar circumstances.” (Corp. Code, § 309, subd. (a).) “‘Corporate officers and directors are not permitted to use their position of trust and confidence to further their private interests.’” (Bancroft-Whitney Co. v. Glen (1966) 64 Cal.2d 327, 345, quoting Guth v. Loft, Inc. (1939) 23 Del.Ch. 255 [5 A.2d 503, 510].)

Here, the Fifth Cause of Action alleges that Cesar diverted funds from Landscape for his personal use. (4AC, ¶ 83.) It incorporates the allegations that Cesar exercised complete dominion over Landscape’s bank accounts, removed $100,000 from the Landscape account and moved over $200,000 to another account for his benefit. (4AC, ¶¶ 74-76, 80.) A director who takes corporate funds for his personal use takes an asset of the corporation, and the claim to recover that asset is itself an asset of the corporation. The sums taken and the accounts they came from give that injury factual content beyond the allegation that Plaintiff and Landscape “have been damaged.” (4AC, ¶¶ 75-76, 84.)

The breach allegation also describes a duty running to Plaintiff, the duty to give him value for his agreement to the change in ownership structure and to pay him his share of the profits. (4AC, ¶ 83.) That describes an individual injury, but it does not defeat the derivative claim the allegation pleads alongside it. Accordingly, the Demurrer to the Fifth Cause of Action is overruled.

V. Inspection Rights

Defendants contend that Plaintiff is not a current shareholder entitled to inspect Landscape’s books and records under Corporations Code section 1601, and that the 4AC alleges no written demand on the corporation. (Dem., at pp. 11:26 - 12:18.) Plaintiff responds that both grounds could have been raised by demurrer to the TAC and are barred now. (Opp., at pp. 4:25 - 5:1.)

Here, the Seventh Cause of Action is pleaded against Landscape alone. (4AC, at p. 13.) Landscape did not join the Demurrer, which Cesar and Charbat bring as to the causes of action “pleaded against them.” (Dem., at pp. 2:5-6, 3:2-3.) A demurrer is available to “[t]he party against whom a complaint or cross-complaint has been filed.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10.) A cause of action asserted only against a co-defendant is outside that grant, so the objection is not one these Defendants may make.

In any event, both grounds were available on the demurrer to the TAC. “A party demurring to a pleading that has been amended after a demurrer to an earlier version of the pleading was sustained shall not demur to any portion of the amended complaint, cross-complaint, or answer on grounds that could have been raised by demurrer to the earlier version of the complaint, cross-complaint, or answer.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (b).) The Seventh Cause of Action in the 4AC repeats the Seventh Cause of Action in the TAC in materially identical terms. Both allege that Plaintiff made requests to Landscape for its books and records, “including the service of a subpoena,” and that Landscape neither confirmed it would produce them nor produced them. (4AC, ¶ 95; TAC, ¶ 92.) Both allege that Plaintiff seeks the records for purposes reasonably related to his interests as a shareholder. (4AC, ¶ 96; TAC, ¶ 93.) The allegations of the 2022 transfer that Defendants rely on appear in the TAC in the same words. (4AC, ¶¶ 43, 47; TAC, ¶¶ 43, 45.) Defendants state that Plaintiff has pleaded them consistently across the First Amended Complaint, the TAC, and the 4AC. (Dem., at p. 13:2-5.) Section 430.41, subdivision (b), forecloses both grounds now.

The Demurrer to the Seventh Cause of Action is overruled.

 

CONCLUSION

The Demurrer is sustained without leave to amend as to the derivative breach of contract claim and is otherwise overruled. Defendants shall file and serve an Answer within 20 days.


Case Number: 25STCV05429    Hearing Date: September 1, 2026    Dept: 515

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: Hearing on Motion to Seal

The Motion to Seal is denied.

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Cordeny Kelly (Plaintiff) filed this action against Sprouts Farmers Market, LLC and Christian Mccormick, alleging that he slipped on a store rack and was injured. SF Markets, LLC dba Sprouts Farmers Market (Sprouts), sued as Sprouts Farmers Market, LLC, and Christian Mccormick (collectively, “Defendants”) are the moving parties.

The causes of action are: (1) General Negligence; and (2) Premises Liability.

Defendants filed a Motion to Seal. Plaintiff filed a Statement of Non-Opposition.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

“Unless confidentiality is required by law, court records are presumed to be open.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.550(c).) “[I]n general, the First Amendment provides a right of access to ordinary civil trials and proceedings.” (NBC Subsidiary (KNBC-TV), Inc. v. Superior Court (1999) 20 Cal.4th 1178, 1212.)

A court may order a record filed under seal “only if it expressly finds facts that establish: [¶] (1) There exists an overriding interest that overcomes the right of public access to the record; [¶] (2) The overriding interest supports sealing the record; [¶] (3) A substantial probability exists that the overriding interest will be prejudiced if the record is not sealed; [¶] (4) The proposed sealing is narrowly tailored; and [¶] (5) No less restrictive means exist to achieve the overriding interest.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.550(d).) An order sealing a record must “[s]pecifically state the facts that support the findings,” and must direct the sealing of “only those documents and pages, or, if reasonably practicable, portions of those documents and pages, that contain the material that needs to be placed under seal.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.550(e)(1)(A), (B).)

“A record must not be filed under seal without a court order. The court must not permit a record to be filed under seal based solely on the agreement or stipulation of the parties.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.551(a).) The moving party files “a memorandum and a declaration containing facts sufficient to justify the sealing.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.551(b)(1).) That party “must come forward with a specific enumeration of the facts sought to be withheld and specific reasons for withholding them.” (H.B. Fuller Co. v. Doe (2007) 151 Cal.App.4th 879, 894.)

 

ANALYSIS

I. Late Filing

Plaintiff acknowledges that the Statement of Non-Opposition was filed after the deadline Code of Civil Procedure section 1005, subdivision (b), sets for opposition papers, and asks the Court to consider it. (Non-Opp., at p. 1:20-23.) “No paper may be rejected for filing on the ground that it was untimely submitted for filing,” and a court that declines to consider a late filed paper must say so in its minutes or order. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1300(d).) The statement opposes no relief and addresses the findings rule 2.550(d) requires the Court to make whether or not any party appears. The Court exercises its discretion to consider it.

II. Confidentiality Stipulation

First, Defendants argue that a Confidentiality Agreement and Stipulation the parties executed makes the CCTV recordings confidential and supports sealing them. (Mot., at pp. 2:1-5, 3:8-11.) The Court “must not permit a record to be filed under seal based solely on the agreement or stipulation of the parties.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.551(a).) Defendants offer the stipulation as evidence that the parties recognize the material as sensitive and have consistently treated it as confidential. (Kawakami Decl., ¶ 1.) A stipulation is evidence of how the parties have handled the material between themselves. It is not a finding, and rule 2.551(a) forbids the Court to treat it as one.

The Court signed the stipulation as an order on July 25, 2025. That order approved an agreement governing how the parties would handle Sprouts’s confidential material among themselves. It made no finding under rule 2.550(d). Rule 2.551(a) requires those findings before any record is filed under seal.

Plaintiff states that he does not dispute that Defendants have identified potential privacy and confidentiality concerns, and that the asserted interests may support some protection. (Non-Opp., at p. 3:6-9.) A concession that an interest may exist is not evidence of the facts that would establish one, and rule 2.550(d) conditions sealing on facts the Court expressly finds.

III. Overriding Interest

Next, Defendants identify two interests they say overcome the right of public access. The first is the privacy of the customers the cameras recorded, none of whom has a connection to this action. The second is the confidentiality of the camera coverage, which is part of the store’s loss prevention. (Mot., at p. 4:16-27.)

A. Customer Privacy

Defendants cite article I, section 1 of the California Constitution for the first interest, which lists privacy as an inalienable right. (Mot., at p. 4:7-14.) “A ‘reasonable’ expectation of privacy is an objective entitlement founded on broadly based and widely accepted community norms.” (Hill v. National Collegiate Athletic Assn. (1994) 7 Cal.4th 1, 37.)

Defendants provide the declaration of their counsel, Kristie Kawakami, who describes the recordings as an overhead view of the produce department at one of Defendants’ stores, including the produce area, the refrigerated area where alcohol is kept, and the bakery. (Kawakami Decl., ¶ 2.) It states that the recordings depict a significant number of third-party individuals not involved in this suit, and that they will disclose the conduct of many customers unrelated to it. (Kawakami Decl., ¶ 6.) Nothing in the record identifies a community norm bearing on the recordings, and nothing attributes to any recorded customer conduct beyond presence and ordinary shopping. Customers shopping in a market’s public aisles conduct themselves where anyone present can see them, and “there can be no privacy in that which is already public.” (Gill v. Hearst Publishing Co. (1953) 40 Cal.2d 224, 230.)

B. Camera Placement

Defendants support the second interest with the same declaration. Kawakami states that the CCTV video system “is one tool businesses, like Defendants, use for loss prevention.” (Kawakami Decl., ¶ 2.) She states that the footage lets a viewer determine what the camera’s view reaches, and that disclosure would reveal “the extent to which certain areas of the store are monitored and how to avoid them.” (Ibid.)

Defendants lodged the recordings with the Court. (Mot., at p. 2:2-3.) The recording shows what one camera captured from one angle on one occasion. It does not show how many cameras the store operates, which areas go unmonitored, whether the coverage is already apparent to anyone walking the aisle, or what disclosure would cost the store’s loss prevention. Those are the facts that would make camera coverage an interest overcoming public access. Rule 2.551(b)(1) required the moving papers to state them.

IV. Probability of Prejudice

Next, Defendants argue that the interests they identify would be prejudiced without sealing. They say the information the recordings contain “could be used for shoplifting, vandalism, or other harmful purposes,” that public access would violate the privacy of third-party individuals not involved in the lawsuit, and that the information could be republished. (Mot., at p. 5:10-13.)

Rule 2.550(d)(3) asks whether “[a] substantial probability exists that the overriding interest will be prejudiced if the record is not sealed.” A statement that harm could follow describes a possibility. Universal City Studios accepted the contractual interest and denied sealing anyway, because the party made no showing of prejudice to its business interests. (Universal City Studios, supra, 110 Cal.App.4th 1273.) The declaration here states that public disclosure would cause Defendants harm “including, but not limited to, competitive disadvantage, misuse of proprietary information, and potential economic injury.” (Kawakami Decl., ¶ 8.) It identifies no fact by which the Court could measure the likelihood of any of them.

Defendants fail to show that their interest in the camera’s placement is an overriding one, or that it is likely that they will suffer prejudice if it is disclosed. Accordingly, the Motion to Seal is denied.

The recordings remain lodged conditionally under seal. Defendants may notify the Court within 10 days of this order that the lodged record is to be filed unsealed, and on that notification the clerk unseals and files it. If Defendants do not notify the Court within 10 days, the clerk will permanently delete the video. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.551(b)(6).)

 

CONCLUSION

The Motion to Seal is denied.
