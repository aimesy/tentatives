DEPARTMENT S27 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS



Case Number: 24LBCV02761    Hearing Date: August 13, 2026    Dept: S27

 

1.     Background Facts

Plaintiff, Tyler Chambers (“Plaintiff”), filed this action against Defendants, FCA US, LLC and Cerritos Dodge, Inc. dba Cerritos Dodge Chrysler Jeep Ram (collectively, “Defendants”), for violations of the Song-Beverly Act, breach of implied warranty, negligent repair, and fraudulent inducement – concealment. 

On 6/26/26, the Court (Hon. Mark C. Kim) held a Mandatory Settlement Conference (MSC) and a Trial Setting Conference. Plaintiff’s counsel appeared, but Plaintiff did not appear. Therefore, the Court continued the MSC to August 13, 2026, and set: “Hearing on Motion to Dismiss and sanctions for Plaintiff’s failure to appear and Plaintiff’s counsel’s failure to appear to timely request a continuance … for 08/13/26 ….” (6/26/26 Minute Order, p. 1.)

On 7/2/26, Defendants filed the instant motion for sanctions against Plaintiff’s counsel of record (Strategic Legal Practices, APC) in the amount of $8,335 and/or dismissal for Plaintiff’s failure to appear and timely request a continuance of the MSC.

On 7/30/26, the Court conducted a Non-Appearance Case Review and vacated the MSC date because it is not holding Settlement Conferences at this time.

On 8/6/26, Plaintiff’s counsel, Breana Rucker of Strategic Legal Practices, filed a declaration explaining Plaintiff’s reasons for failing to appear at the MSC. No reply has been filed.  

           

2.     Motion for Sanctions and/or Dismissal for Failing to Appear at MSC or Request Continuance of MSC

Under the Court’s local rules: “The court may hold a mandatory settlement conference in each case before trial. The settlement conference shall be scheduled a sufficient time after the exchange of appraisals to allow the parties to conduct expert depositions and engage in settlement discussions. All counsel and all persons with settlement authority must attend the settlement conference in person, unless prior arrangements have been made with the court for that person to appear by telephone.” (Super. Ct. L.A. County, Local Rules, rule 3.245.)

The rules further state: “The court may impose appropriate sanctions for the failure or refusal to comply with the rules in [Chapter Three (Civil Division Rules) of the Local Rules], including the time standards and/or deadlines, and any court order made pursuant to the rules. Counsel are directed to Code of Civil Procedure sections 128, 128.7, 177.5, 575.2, 583.150, 583.430, 2016.010-2036.050, Government Code section 68608, and California Rules of Court, rule 2.30. The sanctions may be imposed on a party and, if appropriate, on counsel for that party.” (Super. Ct. L.A. County, Local Rules, rule 3.10 [underlining added].)

Defendants move for sanctions against Plaintiff’s counsel (instead of Plaintiff).

However, Plaintiff’s counsel appeared at the MSC and testifies that his client’s failure to appear was due to his client’s alleged preexisting travel arrangements. Plaintiff’s counsel testifies: “Plaintiff and his counsel respectfully apologize to the Court for Plaintiff’s absence from the June 26, 2026 MSC and for counsel’s failure to timely notify the Court in advance regarding Plaintiff’s unavailability.” (Rucker Decl., ¶ 3.) “Prior to the June 26, 2026 MSC, Plaintiff advised counsel that he would be out of state from June 20 through June 27, 2026, and therefore would be unavailable to personally attend the MSC on June 26, 2026.” (Id. ¶ 4.) “Plaintiff’s absence was not intended to disregard the Court’s order or the importance of the Mandatory Settlement Conference. Plaintiff had a preexisting travel conflict that prevented his personal attendance on that date.” (Id. ¶ 5.) “Counsel appeared on Plaintiff’s behalf at the June 26, 2026 hearing. The Court thereafter continued the [MSC] and Trial Setting Conference and set a hearing concerning Plaintiff’s nonappearance.” (Id. ¶ 6.) “Counsel recognizes that Plaintiff’s unavailability should have been brought to the Court’s attention in advance and sincerely apologizes to the Court for failing to do so and for any inconvenienced caused to the Court.” (Id. ¶ 7.) “Plaintiff remains actively engaged in this litigation and ready, willing, and available to proceed with this matter. Plaintiff intends to prosecute his claims and will make himself available for all future proceedings at which his personal attendance is required.” (Id. ¶ 8.) Defendants have not presented evidence that would justify sanctions against Plaintiff’s counsel or dismissal.

            For those reasons, the motion is DENIED. However, Plaintiff is admonished for the failure to appear and is warned that future failure to personally appear when required may result in monetary sanctions.

Plaintiff is ordered to give notice.

Case Number: 25LBCV02173    Hearing Date: August 13, 2026    Dept: S27

 

  1. Background Facts

On August 1, 2025, Plaintiffs, Brian Gonzalez and Angelica Gutierrez (collectively, “Plaintiffs”) filed this action against defendants, American Honda Motor Co., Inc. (“Defendant”) and DOES 1 through 10, inclusive, arising from the purchase of an allegedly defective 2022 Honda CR-V (the “Subject Vehicle”). (Compl. ¶ 6.) Plaintiffs allege, inter alia, that “[o]n or about March 5, 2022, Plaintiff[s] entered into a warranty contract with Defendant . . . .” (Id.) “Defects and nonconformities to warranty manifested themselves within the applicable express warranty period, including but not limited to transmission defects, engine defects, electrical defects; among other defects and non-conformities.” (Id. ¶ 11.)

On September 29, 2025, Plaintiffs filed the operative First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) against Defendants alleging the following causes of action: (1) Violation of Subdivision (D) of Civil Code Section 1793.2; (2) Violation of Subdivision (B) of Civil Code Section 1793.2; (3) Violation of Subdivision (A)(3) of Civil Code Section 1793.2; (4) Breach of the Implied Warranty of Merchantability; and (5) Fraudulent Inducement – Concealment.

On November 7, 2025, Defendant filed an answer to the FAC.

On May 18, 2026, Defendant filed and served a motion for judgment on the pleadings. The notice of motion provides that Defendant “move[s] for an order granting judgment on the pleading in favor of American Honda and dismissing the Complaint with prejudice.” (Not. of Mot. at p. 1:23-24.) Upon review of the memorandum of points and authorities, the Court notes that no reference is made to the operative FAC. (Memo. of Ps and As at pp. 3-11.) Also, it appears that Defendant only challenges the fifth cause of action for fraudulent concealment in the complaint. (Id.)

On May 28, 2026, the Court granted the ex parte application of Defendant and advanced the hearing on the motion from December 17, 2026, to August 13, 2026. (05/28/26 Minute Order at p. 1.)

On July 31, 2026, Plaintiffs filed and served a notice of non-opposition to the motion for judgment on the pleadings.

  1. Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings

Although Plaintiffs filed a notice of non-opposition to the motion, the Court cannot grant the motion. “An amended complaint supersedes all prior complaints . . . and the original complaint ceases to have any effect either as a pleading or as a basis for judgment.” (Malear v. State of California (2023) 89 Cal.App.5th 213, 221 [citations omitted, internal quotations omitted].)

The Court finds that Defendant moved for judgment on the pleadings as to a complaint which is no longer operative. The notice of motion is directed at the complaint and makes no reference to the operative FAC. (Not. of Mot. at p. 1:22-24.) Also, the memorandum of points and authorities addresses the fraudulent concealment cause of action in the complaint. Critically, the complaint is no longer an operative pleading. Defendant should have sought judgment on the pleadings as to the FAC.

Thus, pursuant to Malear v. State of California, supra, 89 Cal.App.5th 213, 221, the instant motion cannot be granted irrespective of the lack of opposition. 

  1. Conclusion

Defendant American Honda Motor Co., Inc.’s Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings is DENIED WITHOUT PREJUDICE.

 

Defendant American Honda Motor Co., Inc. is ordered to give notice.

Case Number: 25LBCV02306    Hearing Date: August 13, 2026    Dept: S27

 

1.     Complaint

On 8/20/25, Plaintiff, Roots, Inc. filed a complaint against Defendant, Roots Addiction and Psychiatry Medical Group, P.C., Kourosh Parsapour, M.D., Danina Rodriguez, and Andrei Dokukin, M.D. for injunctive relief and attorneys’ fees.  The complaint alleges Plaintiff has an independent contractor agreement with Defendants, and Plaintiff terminated the contract because Defendants were engaged in unauthorized activity in breach of the parties’ agreement and otherwise in breach of the agreement. 

 

On 11/21/25, Plaintiff filed a First Amended Complaint.  The FAC adds two new defendants – Open Text, Inc. and Summitt Reprographics, Inc.  These are the entities alleged to be storing the information taken by the other defendants from Plaintiff.  The FAC includes causes of action for breach of independent contractor agreements, violation of the California Confidentiality of Medical Information Act, misappropriation of trade secrets, money lent (alternatively unjust enrichment), and conversion.

 

2.     Pertinent Allegations of the Operative FAC

The Court finds it necessary to set forth the pertinent allegations of the FAC.  Plaintiff Roots, Inc.

alleges, inter alia, that it “is a licensed healthcare provider that offers substance abuse treatment and related medical services.  In the course of its operations, [Plaintiff] maintains and manages sensitive Protected Health Information (PHI) and Substance Use Disorder (SUD) records that are subject to strict confidentiality protections under federal and state law, including the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 (‘HIPAA’), 42 C.F.R. Part 2, the California Confidentiality of Medical Information Act (‘CMIA’), and related regulations.”  (FAC ¶ 1.)

 

            Defendants Andrei Dokukin, M.D. (“Dokukin”) and Kourosh Parsapour, M.D. (“Parsapour”) are alleged to have been engaged by Plaintiff as independent contractors pursuant to a separate Independent Contractor Agreement.  (FAC ¶¶ 2-3.)  Defendants Dokukin and Parsapour were both “granted authorized access to [Plaintiff’s] Google Workspace systems.  These systems contain PHI, SUD records, and proprietary business records, including but not limited to patient medical data, financial records, operational documents, and internal communications.”  (Id.)

 

            Defendant Danina Rodriguez (“Rodriguez”) was engaged by Plaintiff and, via that engagement and in connection with her work, defendant Rodriguez “was granted authorized access to [Plaintiff’s] secure Google Workspace systems.  These systems contain PHI, SUD records, and proprietary business records . . . .”  (FAC ¶ 4.)  Defendant Roots Addiction and Psychiatry Medical Group, P.C. (“RAP”) was engaged by Plaintiff under a Professional Services Agreement to provide professional medical services. (Id. ¶ 5.)  Defendants Dokukin and Parsapour are principals, owners, or agents of defendant RAP.  (Id.)  Defendant Open Text Inc. “operates under the name ‘Hightail by opentext.’ (‘Hightail’)[.]”  (Id. ¶ 6.)  Hightail operates a file-sharing service and “received, stored, and facilitated access to [Plaintiff’s] unauthorizedly exported data . . . acting as a recipient and custodian of such data.” (Id.)  Defendant Summitt Reprographics, Inc. (“Summitt Reprographics”) “specializes in document scanning, photocopying, electronic data conversion (EDD), [and] archiving services . . . .”  (Id. ¶ 7.)  Defendant Summitt Reprographics is alleged to have “received, stored, scanned, and digitized [Plaintiff’s] unauthorizedly removed physical documents . . . acting as a scanning and archiving vendor.”  (Id.)

 

            On or about February 5, 2025, defendants Dokukin and Parsapour entered into a Professional Services Agreement with Plaintiff to provide medical services.  (FAC ¶ 10.)  Plaintiff alleges that “[o]n or about August 5-6, 2025, [d]efendants Dokukin, Parsapour, and Rodriguez, acting through RAP and under their Independent Contractor Agreements, initiated unauthorized ‘Google Takeout’ exports of [Plaintiff’s] electronic data.”  (Id. at ¶ 11.)  “These exports transferred a total of approximately 8.12 GB of data from [Plaintiff’s] secure Google Workspace to external storage.  Specifically, [d]efendant Dokukin exported approximately 2.93 GB, [d]efendant Parsapour exported approximately 2.33 GB, and [d]efendant Rodriguez exported approximately 2.86 GB.” (Id.)

 

            Plaintiff alleges the following:

            “The exported data included a large volume of sensitive PHI and SUD records, such as patient

            names, medical diagnoses, treatment histories, urinalysis results, Social Security numbers,

            addresses, insurance information, and session notes.  It also included confidential and

proprietary business records belonging to [Plaintiff], such as operational manuals, billing information, and internal communications.  These unauthorized exports were documented through [Plaintiff’s] Google Workspace audit logs.”

(FAC ¶ 12.)

 

On or about August 7, 2025, Plaintiff terminated the Independent Contractor Agreements with

defendants Dokukin and Parsapour.  (FAC ¶ 13.)  Due to her affiliation with said defendants, defendant Rodriguez was also terminated.  (Id.)  “After termination, Defendants retained the downloaded data without [Plaintiff’s] authorization.”  (Id. ¶ 14.)  Plaintiff alleges as follows: “Defendants’ actions violated the confidentiality provisions of their Independent Contractor Agreements, the [Professional Services Agreement], and multiple federal and state laws governing the use and disclosure of PHI and SUD records.”  (Id. ¶ 15.)

 

            Plaintiff transmitted written demand letters to Defendants “requiring that they immediately return or destroy all downloaded data, certify compliance under penalty of perjury, and disclose third-party disclosures by August 20, 2025.”  (FAC ¶ 16.)  “On August 19, 2025, Defendants Dokukin, Parsapour, Rodriguez, and RAP responded in writing and admitted that they had retained the data on a third-party website . . . and planned to transfer the data to law enforcement agencies . . . without obtaining a court order.”  (Id.)  “Subsequently, Defendants disclosed that the six boxes of physical patient medical records unauthorizedly removed on June 6, 2025, are being held at Defendant Summitt Reprographics in Century City.”  (Id. ¶ 17.)  “On or about October 2025, [Plaintiff] retrieved the physical records from Defendant Summitt Reprographics.”  (Id. ¶ 18.)  However, defendant Summitt Reprographics is alleged to have

“scanned and digitized the records, as evidenced by the provision of scanned versions via Hightail.” (Id.)

 

            Plaintiff alleges that Defendants’ justifications are unfounded.  (FAC ¶ 19.)  The alleged “retention of this data poses significant and ongoing risks to [Plaintiff] and its patients.  These risks include patient privacy violations, stigmatization and discrimination resulting from unauthorized disclosure of SUD treatment records, [and] regulatory penalties . . . .”  (FAC ¶ 20.)  Plaintiff “has incurred and will continue to incur economic damages as a result of Defendants’ conduct.”  (Id. ¶ 21.)

c.     Applicable Law

The purpose of a preliminary injunction is to preserve the status quo pending a decision on the merits.  (MaJor v. Miraverde Homeowners Ass’n. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 618, 623.)  In deciding whether or not to grant a preliminary injunction, the court looks to two factors, including “(1) the likelihood that the plaintiff will prevail on the merits, and (2) the relative balance of harms that is likely to result from the granting or denial of interim injunctive relief.”  (White v. Davis (2003) 30 Cal.4th 528, 553.)  The factors are interrelated, with a greater showing on one permitting a lesser showing on the other.  (Dodge, Warren & Peters Ins. Services, Inc. v. Riley (2003) 105 Cal.App.4th 1414, 1420.)  However, the party seeking an injunction must demonstrate at least a reasonable probability of success on the merits.  (IT Corp. v. County of Imperial (1983) 35 Cal.3d 63, 73-74.)  The party seeking the injunction bears the burden of demonstrating both a likelihood of success on the merits and the occurrence of irreparable harm.  (Savage v. Trammell Crow Co. (1990) 223 Cal.App.3d 1562, 1571.)  Irreparable harm may exist if the plaintiff can show an inadequate remedy at law.  (Code Civ. Proc. § 526(a).)  

 

“The granting or denying of a preliminary injunction does not constitute an adjudication of the ultimate rights in controversy.”  (Cohen v. Board of Supervisors (1985) 40 Cal.3d 277, 286; see also Yee v. American National Ins. Co. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 453, 457-458 [“a preliminary injunction is not a determination on the merits….”].)

f.      Analysis

Based on the Joint Statement, Plaintiff contends that the sole issue is “whether the remaining unauthorized copies of the patient records must be securely destroyed . . . .”  (See Joint Statement at p. 3:16-17.)  The Court references its 1/20/26 Minute Order and incorporates such recitation herein.  The Court stated therein:

 

“To the extent Plaintiff is seeking to have Defendants return or destroy the records and/or provide a sworn declaration, this would be a mandatory injunction. 

 

Pursuant to Brown v. Pacific Found., Inc., (2019) 34 Cal.App.5th 915, 925, mandatory injunctions are rarely granted, and should only be granted in extreme cases where the right thereto is firmly established.  The Court finds, at most, it would be inclined to issue a prohibitory injunction (precluding Defendants from further using or disseminating the subject data), as Plaintiff has not made any showing that a mandatory injunction meets this very high standard.  Notably, the Court provided this analysis in its prior tentative ruling, and Plaintiff’s new moving papers do not address the requirements for obtaining a mandatory injunction.”

 

(01/20/26 Minute Order at p. 3.)

 

The Court finds that the same issue exists that was identified in the Court’s minute order issued on 1/20/26.  Neither the supplemental memorandum of Plaintiff nor the supplemental declaration of Plaintiff’s counsel addresses the requirements for obtaining a mandatory injunction.  According to the Joint Statement, Plaintiff seeks to have Defendants destroy the records at issue.  Such request is a mandatory injunction as it would require Defendants “to take affirmative action . . . .”  (Shaw v. Los Angeles Unified School Dist. (2023) 95 Cal.App.5th 740, 768.) 

 

Further, although not raised in the opposition, the Court finds that Plaintiff has not argued (or provided admissible evidence) that there is no adequate remedy at law.  Plaintiff has not argued or provided evidence via a declaration that it “has no adequate alternative remedy . . . .”  (Davenport v. Blue Cross of California (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 435, 450.)  “A party seeking injunctive relief must show the absence of an adequate remedy at law.”  (Department of Fish & Game v. Anderson-Cottonwood Irrigation Dist. (1992) 8 Cal.App.4th 1554, 1564 [emphasis added].)  Thus, Plaintiff has not met its burden to warrant a preliminary injunction.

 

6.     Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, the Court DENIES the motion for preliminary injunction.

 

Plaintiff is ordered to give notice.

Case Number: 25PSCV02072    Hearing Date: August 13, 2026    Dept: S27

On May 20, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the instant motion for summary judgment of their Complaint, arguing that there are no triable issues of material fact for the following reasons. It is undisputed that they remain in possession of five original, un-surrendered and non-transferrable Certificates of Deposit (CDs), each of which state that payment is contingent upon “presentation and surrender of this certificate properly endorsed” to Defendant. They contend that since they still retain the CDs, their retention constitutes conclusive evidence that the condition precedent to payment (i.e., the surrender and cancellation of the CDs) was never met and, therefore, Defendant has breached its contractual obligations by failing to pay the deposit.

On July 24, 2026, Defendant filed its opposition, arguing (among other things) that the absence of business records concerning Plaintiffs’ alleged CDs raises a triable issue of material fact regarding its alleged breach of nonpayment of the CDs.

Non-jury trial is set for February 1, 2027.

On August 4, 2026, Plaintiffs filed their reply, arguing that Defendant’s arguments are speculative, conjectural, and not supported by actual knowledge. In addition, Defendant is repeating the same or similar arguments it made in its motion for summary judgment, which the Court denied.

1.     Background Facts

Plaintiffs Jack and Barbara Greenberg filed their Complaint against Defendant on June 5, 2025, asserting one cause of action for breach of contract. The Complaint alleges that Plaintiffs purchased CDs that Defendant refused to pay when the certificates matured on March 1, 2024. Plaintiffs allege that the principal and interest Defendant owes on the five CDs is $510,817.09.

2.     Motion for Summary Judgment

a.     Burdens on Summary Judgment

Summary judgment is proper “if all the papers submitted show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.”  (Code Civ. Proc. §437c(c).)  The moving party bears the initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing that there are no triable issues of material fact.  (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850.)

“A plaintiff … has met that party’s burden of showing that there is no defense to a cause of action if that party has proved each element of the cause of action entitling the party to judgment on the cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(1).) “Once the plaintiff or cross-complainant has met that burden, the burden shifts to the defendant or cross-defendant to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto. The defendant or cross-defendant shall not rely upon the allegations or denials of its pleadings to show that a triable issue of material fact exists but, instead, shall set forth the specific facts showing that a triable issue of material fact exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(1).)

  1. Evidentiary Objections

Defendant has filed objections to the declaration of Plaintiff Barbara Greenberg. (Objections filed July 24, 2026, p. i-iii [objecting to Paragraphs 4, 12, and 13 of the declaration].)

Objections to Paragraph 4 of the declaration are OVERRULED.

Objections to Paragraphs 12 and 13 are SUSTAINED as to the plaintiff’s testimony regarding her husband (i.e., Plaintiff Jack Greenberg never signed any renewal instructions or renewal documentation, never endorsed any of the CDs for payment, renewal, or transfer, and never received payment) for lack of personal knowledge and foundation. The objections are otherwise OVERRULED.

The Court notes that Plaintiffs did not file evidentiary objections.

  1. Discussion

Because Plaintiffs are moving for summary judgment, they bear the burden of proving each element of their sole cause of action for breach of contract. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(1).) “[T]he elements of a cause of action for breach of contract are (1) the existence of the contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance, (3) defendant’s breach, and (4) the resulting damages to the plaintiff.”  (Thrifty Payless, Inc. v. The Americana at Brand, LLC (2013) 218 Cal.App.4th 1230, 1244.) 

To prove their breach of contract claim, Plaintiffs present the following evidence and law. In 1986, Plaintiffs “purchased a total of five Certificates of Deposit [hereafter, “Certificates” or “CDs”] from Queen City Bank, each at or exceeding $100,000 in principal.” (Defendant’s separate statement in response to Plaintiffs’ separate statement of undisputed material facts (“UMF”) No. 1.) “The Certificates were issued on March 14, May 15, June 16, July 16, and August 15, 1986.” (UMF No. 2.) “Each Certificate contains repayment language requiring presentation and surrender of the original Certificate before payment. Each Certificate also states that it is ‘NON-TRANSFERABLE,’ ‘NON-NEGOTIABLE,’ and that there is ‘NO INTEREST AFTER MATURITY.’” (UMF No. 3.) “Each CD constitutes a written obligation by Queen City Bank to repay the deposited funds according to the terms stated on the Certificates.” (UMF No. 5.) “Plaintiffs retained uninterrupted possession of all five original Certificates from the time they were issued through the present. Although the Certificates were misplaced among long-stored records for many years, they were located in 2024 in the same condition in which they were original issued.” (UMF No. 6.) Under the California Commercial Code: “An action to enforce the obligation of a party to a certificate of deposit to pay the instrument shall be commenced within six years after demand for payment is made to the maker ….” (Com. Code, § 3118, subd. (e).) “On March 1, 2024, Plaintiffs made a formal written demand for payment and attempted to present all five original Certificates at First Bank’s Rowland Heights branch, the successor office of issue following First Bank’s merger with Queen City Bank. Plaintiffs had not previously made formal demand for payment on the Certificates.” (UMF No. 8.) Plaintiffs argue that because they filed this lawsuit on June 5, 2025, within six years of making the demand to Defendant on March 1, 2024, their action is timely. Plaintiff Barbara Greenberg testifies that, after the demand, Defendant declined to take custody of the Certificates and refused to accept them for any form of processing, redemption, or payment. (Motion, Greenberg Decl., ¶ 9.) “At no time did the bank assert that the Certificates had ever been surrendered, redeemed, paid, or cancelled, and stated only that it had ‘no records.’ The five Certificates collectively reflect principal deposits totaling $508,169.57. The Certificates further reflect accrued contractual interest totaling $2,780.85 through maturity. [Therefore,] [t]he total amount due and owing under the Certificates as of maturity is $510,950.42 [as alleged in the Complaint], exclusive of any additional prejudgment interest recoverable by law.” (Id. ¶ 9.)

The Court finds that Plaintiffs have met their initial burden of proving each element of their breach of contract claim through the evidence above. Therefore, the burden shifts to Defendant to show that one or more triable issues of material fact exist as to that claim or defense.

In its opposition, Defendant first argues that “recent fabrication can be inferred when it is shown that a witness did not speak about an important matter at a time when it would have been natural for him [ or her] to do so." (People v. Riccardi (2012) 54 Cal.4th 758, 803, citing People v. Manson (1976) 61 Cal.App.3d 102, 143.) Here, Plaintiffs claim that they did not request repayment non-interest bearing deposits totaling over $500,000 between 1986 and 2024. It would have been natural for a customer of Queen City Bank to request repayment of matured CDs in 1986, even if he or she lost the certificates; and even more so for Plaintiffs, who were shareholders and friends of the bank president. Therefore, based solely on the passage of time, a trier of fact could reasonably infer that Barbara Greenberg's statement that Plaintiffs never received repayment is a fabrication intended to advance Plaintiffs' interest in this dispute.” (Opposition, pp. 6:24-7:7.)

The Court finds Defendant’s fabrication argument unpersuasive because “‘[s]peculation … is not evidence’ that can be utilized in opposing a motion for summary judgment. [Citations.]” (Knapp v. Doherty (2004) 123 Cal.App.4th 76, 99.)

Defendant also argues that Plaintiffs fail to submit competent evidence of breach and Plaintiff Jack Greenberg, who has not submitted any declaration or affidavit in support of this motion, “could have withdrawn the funds or rolled over the CDs without Barbara Greenberg’s knowledge or consent.” (Opposition, p. 8:25-27.)

The Court also finds the above argument is unpersuasive. Although the Court has sustained Defendant’s objections to Barbara Greenberg’s testimony regarding her spouse, the Court has still found that Plaintiffs have established breach particularly through the undisputed facts that: (1) Plaintiffs obtained the CDs from First Bank’s predecessor Queen City Bank, (2) each CD constitutes a written obligation by Queen City Bank to repay the deposited funds according to the terms stated on the CDs, (3) each CD contains repayment language requiring presentation and surrender of the original CD before payment, (4) each CD states that it is “NON-TRANSFERABLE,” (5) Plaintiffs retained uninterrupted possession of all five original CDs from the time they were issued through the present, (6) Plaintiffs made the first demand for payment in March 2024, and (7) Defendant refused to pay. Those undisputed facts have established each element of the breach of contract claim, including breach. Plaintiffs have also established damages through the testimony of Barbara Greenberg that [t]he total amount due and owing under the Certificates as of maturity is $510,950.42 [as alleged in the Complaint], exclusive of any additional prejudgment interest recoverable by law.” (Motion, Greenberg Decl., ¶ 9.)

Notwithstanding the above, the Court finds that Defendant has met its burden through the declaration of expert witness Glen C. Terry (“Terry”) and Director of Operations, Mandi Willis (“Willis”).

Terry testifies that he is a Partner and Consulting CFO at an advisory group in California, holds a Master of Business Administration with a concentration in Finance and Management, attended the Pacific Coast School of Banking, and has more than 40 years of experience in the banking industry, including serving as President, Chief Executive Officer, and/or Director at multiple banks. (Terry Decl., ¶¶ 1-3.)

Terry determined from the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation’s website that Queen City Bank (hereafter, “QCB”) was established on September 26, 1983, and merged into First Bank & Trust (Santa Ana, California) on April 15, 1996. (Terry Decl., ¶ 9.) At the time of the merger, QCB had total assets of approximately $50.8 million and total deposits of approximately $44.27 million. (Ibid.)

Based on his review of the five CDs, Terry opines that the CDs represent a single initial deposit of $100,000 that was successively rolled over into new short term CDs with accrued interest added to principal at each rollover; the CDs do not represent an aggregate of $500,000 in deposits. (Terry Decl., ¶ 12.) This is because “each subsequent CD’s principal precisely equal[ed] the prior CD’s principal plus its accrued interest … [which] is, in [his] experience, the unmistakable signature of a CD rollover. It is not consistent with five separate, independent deposits. If the Plaintiffs had made five separate deposits, one would expect round-number principal amounts, or amounts unrelated to the compounding of interest from a prior instrument. Instead, the odd-cent principal ($101,236.66, $101,791.38, $101,314.28, and $102,827.25) can only be explained as the product of successive interest compounding on a single original deposit of $100,000. [Terry is] aware of no innocent way for five wholly independent deposits to produce principal balances that dovetail to the penny in this manner.” (id. ¶ 14.)

Willis in turn attests to the following facts. “As the Director of Operations, [she] manage[s] the bank’s daily operations, including the entry of deposits, CDs, withdrawals, checks, automatic clearing house operations, and wire transfers.” (Willis Decl., ¶ 3.)

“First Bank’s deposits (including certificates of deposit) and withdrawals are recorded on a core data system through a central processing system that tracks transactions in real-time. When a deposit or a withdrawal is made, the core system updates the customer’s account balance, posts the transaction to the general ledger, and makes the updated information available across all banking channels, such as the only system used by bank tellers and ATMs. This system is the foundation for First Bank’s internal records, including customer data and balances, and enables the processing of daily banking transactions. It is First Bank’s policy and practice to maintain customer account records for seven years.” (Willis Decl., ¶ 4.) “First Bank acquired [QCB], National Association, including its customer relationships, in or around April 1996.” (Id. ¶ 5.)

“When First Bank acquires another financial institution, the records of deposits and certificates of deposit for the institution that was acquired are merged into First Bank’s core data.” (Willis Decl., ¶ 6.) Willis is informed and believes “that the records of [QCB’s] deposits were merged into First Bank’s core data in or around 1996, because First Bank became liable to [QCB’s] customers for the deposits that they made at [QCB] that remained due and owing as of the date of the acquisition, according to records of the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation.” (Id. ¶ 6.)

Willis “conducted searches of First Bank’s core data to determine if either Jack Greenberg or Barbara individually, or Jack Greenberg and Barbara Greenberg jointly, have an account at First Bank that is still open or a certificate of deposit that has not been repaid …,” and “determined that no such account is open and no certificate of deposit that has not been repaid by First Bank or [QCB] exists. [She is] familiar with the methods used to search First Bank’s core data. If a deposit account or an unpaid certificate of deposit owned by Jack Greenberg and/or Barbara Greenberg existed, it would have been discovered by [her] through the searches that [she] performed.” (Willis Decl., ¶ 7.)

The Court finds that through the testimonies of Terry and Willis, Defendant has met its burden of showing that there is a triable issue of material fact regarding the damages element of the breach of contract claim, and whether there was any breach in the first place (i.e., whether Defendant refused to pay Plaintiffs for CDs that were still outstanding). In their reply, Plaintiffs insist that the possession of the CDs is conclusive evidence supporting their breach of contract claim given that it is undisputed that each CD requires presentation and surrender of the original CD before payment. However, in their moving papers, Plaintiffs did not submit evidence showing that (1) Defendant’s roll-over theory is impossible, and/or (2) QCB could not have both allowed payment of the CDs while allowing Plaintiffs to retain possession. The answers to those questions have not been answered. Therefore, the Court is unable to conclude as a matter of law that Plaintiffs’ possession of the CDs is conclusive evidence of their claim. Even if there are no answers to those questions, the Court cannot determine through this motion whether Plaintiffs’ version of the story is more believable than Defendant’s. Credibility determinations are reserved for trial.

For those reasons, the Court denies the motion.

3.     Conclusion

Plaintiffs’ motion for summary judgment is DENIED.

Plaintiffs are ordered to give notice.

a.     Legal Standard

“The court may, in furtherance of justice, and on any terms as may be proper, allow a party to amend any pleading or proceeding by adding or striking out the name of any party, or by correcting a mistake in the name of a party, or a mistake in any other respect; and may, upon like terms, enlarge the time for answer or demurrer. “Any judge, at any time before or after commencement of trial, in the furtherance of justice, and upon such terms as may be proper, may allow the amendment of any pleading or pretrial conference order.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 576.)¿

3.     Conclusion

Case Number: 26LBCV00152    Hearing Date: August 13, 2026    Dept: S27

 

1.     Background Facts

On January 20, 2026, Plaintiff Bernice Hernandez filed this action against Defendants Golden Horn Transportation Inc., Amazon.com Services, LLC., Amazon Logistics, Inc., Velocity Trust LTD., and Ghatih Ahmed Aldhamdani aka Alex Kayak for negligence arising from a motor vehicle collision.

On June 23, 2026, Proposed Intervenor DB Insurance Company LTD. filed this motion for leave to intervene.

On July 28, 2026, Amazon.com Services, LLC and Amazon Logistics, Inc (“Amazon”), jointly filed Opposition to this motion.

On August 6, 2026, Proposed Intervenor DB Insurance Company LTD. filed Reply to Opposition.

 

 

 

2.     Leave to Intervene

Code of Civil Procedure Section 387 states in part: 

(c) A nonparty shall petition the court for leave to intervene by noticed motion or ex parte application. The petition shall include a copy of the proposed complaint in intervention or answer in intervention and set forth the grounds upon which intervention rests. 

(d) (1) The court shall, upon timely application, permit a nonparty to intervene in the action or proceeding if either of the following conditions is satisfied:  

(A) A provision of law confers an unconditional right to intervene. 

(B) The person seeking intervention claims an interest relating to the property or transaction that is the subject of the action and that person is so situated that the disposition of the action may impair or impede that person’s ability to protect that interest, unless that person’s interest is adequately represented by one or more of the existing parties. 

(2) The court may, upon timely application, permit a nonparty to intervene in the action or proceeding if the person has an interest in the matter in litigation, or in the success of either of the parties, or an interest against both. 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 387(c)-(d).)  

“The trial court has discretion to permit a nonparty to intervene where: (1) the proper procedures have been followed, (2) the nonparty has a direct and immediate interest in the action, (3) the intervention will not enlarge the issues in the litigation, and (4) the reasons for the intervention outweigh any opposition by the parties presently in the action….” (Chavez v. Netflix (2008) 162 Cal.App.4th 43, 51.) 

“Because the decision whether to allow intervention is best determined based on the particular facts in each case, it is generally left to the sound discretion of the trial court.” (City and County of San Francisco v. State of Cal. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1030, 1036. Accord, Hinton v. Beck (2009) 176 Cal.App.4th 1378, 1382 [“Intervention pursuant to section 387, subdivision (a) is not a matter of right, but is discretionary with the trial court.”].) 

Proposed Intervenor DB Insurance seeks to intervene on the following basis. DB Insurance is the insurer of Defendants Golden Horn Transportation Inc., and Ghaith Ahmed Alhamadani aka Alex Kayak (“Defendants”) (Orlando Decl. ¶ 7.) DB Insurance states it has a direct and immediate interest in this litigation because Defendants may be liable for judgment entered in this action, and DB Insurance’s interests may be affected by the proceeding and outcome. (Id)

Amazon opposes this motion for several reasons. First, Amazon argues that DB Insurance lacks sufficient interest in this litigation because it completely denied coverage (see Declaration of Darrion Jackson-Laws, Exhibit A). Second, Amazon asserts that DB Insurance’s intervention would improperly enlarge the issues. Finally, Amazon contends that DB Insurance cannot disclaim its obligations to its insureds while simultaneously claiming a protectable interest (Opposition p. 5).

On reply, DB Insurance argues that intervention is mandatory because it may be obligated to satisfy a judgment under the MCS-90 endorsement. Second, DB Insurance asserts that it meets all elements required for mandatory intervention.

When an action asserting a claim for bodily injury or property damage is brought against an insured, and the insured tenders the defense to the insurer, there are three possible positions the insurer can take towards the request for coverage and a defense. An insurer can (1) admit coverage and provide a defense; (2) deny coverage and refuse to provide a defense; or (3) provide a defense under a reservation of rights to contest coverage. (Gray v. Begley (2010) 182 Cal.App.4th 1509)

If the insurer denies coverage and refuses to provide a defense, the insurer “does not have a direct interest in the litigation between the plaintiff and the insured to warrant intervention. The rationale behind this rule is that by its denial, the insurer has lost its right to control the litigation.” (Hinton v. Beck (2009) 176 Cal.App.4th 1378)

Here, DB Insurance denied coverage and refused to provide a defense. (Opposition, Jackson-Laws Decl., ¶ 4, Exhibit B – 001.) As a result, DB Insurance has lost its right to control the litigation.

.

3.     Conclusion

Based on the forgoing, the motion for leave to intervene is denied.
