BACKGROUND

Factual Background

On June 25, 2025, plaintiff Cresencio Cruz De La Cruz (“Plaintiff”) filed the Complaint against defendants Ford Motor Company (“FMC”), Fox Hills Auto, Inc. dba Airport Marina Ford, and DOES 1 through 10.  The Complaint includes the following causes of action: (1) Violation of the Song-Beverly Act – Breach of Express Warranty; (2) Violation of the Song-Beverly Act – Breach of Implied Warranty; and (3) Negligent Repair.

On December 3, 2025, FMC produced a Person Most Qualified (“PMQ”) for deposition.  (Declaration of Sydney O. Sloas (“Sloas Decl.”), ¶ 3.)

On February 10, 2026, Plaintiff served a Notice of Deposition of FMC’s PMQ and Custodian(s) of Records on FMC through email, scheduling the deposition for March 11, 2026.  (Declaration of Colby Meagle (“Meagle Decl.”), ¶¶ 3, 5, Exh. A.)  The deposition notice identified twenty-seven matters for examination and seventeen categories of documents.  (Meagle Decl., Exh. A.)  Furthermore, the email requested three alternative dates by February 1, 2026, should FMC or its counsel be unavailable.  (Meagle Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. B, p. 3.)

On February 24, 2026, Plaintiff’s counsel followed up to determine whether the scheduled deposition date was acceptable and requested alternative dates if it was not.  (Meagle Decl., ¶ 6, Exh. B, p. 3.)  This inquiry was made with the understanding that FMC’s objections were not yet due.  (Meagle Decl., ¶ 6, Exh. B, p. 3.)

On March 5, 2026, FMC served its responses and objections to the deposition notice.  (Meagle Decl., ¶ 7, Exh. C; Sloas Decl., ¶ 5, Exh. 2.)  FMC contended that Plaintiff had unilaterally scheduled the deposition, asserted that it had already produced a PMQ witness for Matters Nos. 1 through 5, and declined to provide a witness for Matters Nos. 6 through 27.  (Meagle Decl., ¶ 7, Exh. C.)

On March 11, 2026, Plaintiff’s counsel emailed FMC’s counsel to request alternative dates for the deposition.  (Meagle Decl., ¶ 10, Exh. B, p. 2.)  They stated, “In the event that your office does not provide alternative dates on or before the noticed time, to protect our clients’ rights we will proceed by appearing on the Microsoft Teams link below to memorialize your failure to appear.”  (Meagle Decl., ¶ 10, Exh. B, p. 2.)

Later that same day, Plaintiff’s counsel reiterated that they had already attempted to meet and confer with FMC regarding the rescheduling of the depositions, but FMC had failed to do so.  (Meagle Decl., ¶ 11, Exh. In response, FMC’s counsel stated that FMC had “already provided a witness for several of the matters sought in Plaintiff’s Notice and objected to all other matters.  As such, no additional dates will be provided.  Should you wish to meet and confer further, please provide your availability for a call.”  (Meagle Decl., ¶ 12, Exh. LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.450, subdivision (a) provides: “If, after service of a deposition notice, a party to the action or an officer, director, managing agent, or employee of a party, or a person designated by an organization that is a party under Section 2025.230, without having served a valid objection under Section 2025.410, fails to appear for examination, or to proceed with it, or to produce for inspection any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing described in the deposition notice, the party giving the notice may move for an order compelling the deponent’s attendance and testimony, and the production for inspection of any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing described in the deposition notice.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subd. (a).)

“Unless otherwise limited by order of the court in accordance with this title, any party may obtain discovery regarding any matter, not privileged, that is relevant to the subject matter involved in the pending action or to the determination of any motion made in that action, if the matter either is itself admissible in evidence or appears reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence.  Discovery may relate to the claim or defense of the party seeking discovery or of any other party to the action.  Discovery may be obtained of the identity and location of persons having knowledge of any discoverable matter, as well as of the existence, description, nature, custody, condition, and location of any document, electronically stored information, tangible thing, or land or other property.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2017.010.)

The motion must “be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040, or, when the deponent fails to attend the deposition and produce the documents, electronically stored information, or things described in the deposition notice, by a declaration stating that the petitioner has contacted the deponent to inquire about the nonappearance.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subd. (b)(2).)

“If a motion under subdivision (a) is granted, the court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) in favor of the party who noticed the deposition and against the deponent or the party with whom the deponent is affiliated, unless the court finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subd. (g)(1).)

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.030, subdivision (a), “The court may impose a monetary sanction ordering that one engaging in the misuse of the discovery process, or any attorney advising that conduct, or both pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney’s fees, incurred by anyone as a result of that conduct.  The court may also impose this sanction on one unsuccessfully asserting that another has engaged in the misuse of the discovery process, or on any attorney who advised that assertion, or on both.  If a monetary sanction is authorized by any provision of this title, the court shall impose that sanction unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030, subd. (a).)  “Misuses of the discovery process include … (h) Making or opposing, unsuccessfully and without substantial justification, a motion to compel or to limit discovery.  ¶  (i) Failing to confer or to attempt to confer, in person, by telephone, or by videoconference with an opposing party or attorney in a reasonable and good faith attempt to resolve informally any dispute concerning discovery, if the section governing a particular discovery motion requires the filing of a declaration stating facts showing that an attempt at informal resolution has been made..”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.010, subds. (h)-(i).)

“The court may award sanctions under the Discovery Act in favor of a party who files a motion to compel discovery, even though no opposition to the motion was filed, or opposition to the motion was withdrawn, or the requested discovery was provided to the moving party after the motion was filed.”  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1348(a).)

MEET AND CONFER REQUIREMENT

Plaintiff must meet and confer with FMC before bringing this motion, either by contacting the deponents to inquire about the nonappearance or by following Code of Civil Procedure section 2016.040.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subd. (b)(2).)  Code of Civil Procedure section 2016.040 states that a meet and confer declaration “in support of a motion [to compel] shall state facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt at an informal resolution of each issue presented by the motion.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2016.040.)  “‘The parties must present to each other the merits of their respective positions with the same candor, specificity and support during informal negotiations as during the briefing of discovery motions.  Only after all the cards have been laid on the table, and a party has meaningfully assessed the relative strengths and weaknesses of its position in light of all available information, can there be a ‘sincere’ effort to resolve the matter.’”  (In re Marriage of Moore (2024) 102 Cal.App.5th 1275, 1293-1294, quoting Townsend v. Superior Court (1998) 61 Cal.App.4th 1431, 1435.)

DISCUSSION

Plaintiff seeks (i) an order compelling FMC to produce its PMQ for deposition on twenty-seven matters for examination, along with the requested documents spanning seventeen distinct categories, and (ii) an award of monetary sanctions against FMC and its counsel.  (Motion, p. 13:2-5.)  However, the Court has identified two significant issues concerning these requests.

First, the Court notes that Plaintiff has not provided a legal analysis demonstrating the relevance of each of the twenty-seven matters proposed for examination.  In the absence of these analyses, the Court cannot ascertain the appropriateness and relevance of the matters for deposition.  Although the Court acknowledges that Plaintiff is not required to demonstrate good cause by “identify[ing] a disputed fact of consequence and explain how the discovery sought will tend to prove or disprove that fact or lead to other evidence that will tend to prove or disprove the fact[,]” Plaintiff must still establish the relevance of each matter.  (Opp., p. 7:24-26; see Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2017.010, 2025.450, subd. (a).)  A generalized argument that fails to meet this burden transfers the responsibility of establishing relevance from Plaintiff to the Court.

Here, Plaintiff presents a generalized argument that they apply to all matters: “Broadly speaking, Plaintiff’s categories for examination seek testimony on categories including Plaintiff’s request for repurchase, repairs, warranty claims, and warranty coverage, and [FMC’s] policy and procedures regarding repurchases and customer complaints.  Matters related to the purchase of the vehicle, repairs performed on the vehicle, warranty claims regarding the vehicle, phone call handling of complaints and requests for repurchase of the vehicle, and [FMC’s] decision not to issue a buy back are relevant to proving Plaintiff’s claims under the Song-Beverly Act.  

Additionally, [FMC’s] policies and procedures regarding the manner in which it deals with similar complaints are relevant to determining whether Plaintiff is entitled to a civil penalty.  More specifically, these matters are relevant to an analysis of the criteria that [FMC] uses to determine whether a vehicle is a ‘lemon’ in compliance with the Song Beverly

Consumer Warranty Act and subject to a buyback or replacement.  …  ¶  The requested information is also relevant to determine whether [FMC], in dealing with Plaintiff, acted in good faith and in a manner that comports with the lemon law or, instead, sought to avoid repurchasing or replacing Plaintiff’s vehicle.  This determination is important because, although Plaintiff is entitled to a civil penalty if [FMC’s] violation of the Song-Beverly Act was willful, [FMC’s] violation is not willful if its failure to replace or refund was the result of a good faith and reasonable belief that the facts imposing the statutory obligation were not present.  [FMC’s] policies and procedures are, therefore, relevant to show whether [FMC’s] conduct lacked good faith.”  (Motion, pp. 7:8-8:3; Plaintiff’s Separate Statement, pp. 3:16-4:11, 7:23-8:18, 11:16-12:11, 15:11-16:6, 19:25-20:20, 23:21-24:16, 27:18-28:13, 31:28-32:23, 36:10-37:5, 40:19-41:14, 45:4-27, 49:22-40:17, 54:12-55:7, 59:1-24, 63:19-64:14, 68:9-69:4, 72:26-73:21, 77:15-78:10, 81:23-82:18, 86:11-87:6, 91:6-92:1, 95:19-96:14, 100:4-27, 104:20-105:15, 109:8-110:3, 113:23-114:18, 118:6-119:1.)

While Plaintiff argues “broadly” why the matters are relevant, they do not provide an argument as to why each specific matter is relevant.  Therefore, Plaintiff’s motion to compel FMC to produce a PMQ regarding these matters is DENIED.

Finally, although Plaintiff requests the production of documents referenced in the deposition notice, they have not provided any legal analysis to explain why there is good cause to compel the production of each individual category of document.  (Motion, p. 13:2-4.)  This argument is notably absent from Plaintiff’s motion, separate statement, and reply brief.  As Plaintiff articulates, “where a party [] seeks production of documents at the deposition [] that party [must] set forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the production of those documents[,]” and “[g]ood cause is established by a showing that the request is made in good faith and the documents sought are relevant to the subject matter of the action and material to the issues in the litigation.”  (Motion, p. 6:17-21.)  The Plaintiff has failed to carry its burden. 

As such, Plaintiff’s motion in its entirety, including the associated request for monetary sanctions, is DENIED.

LEGAL STANDARD

A motion to compel further responses to a demand for inspection or production of documents may be brought based on: (1) incomplete statements of compliance; (2) inadequate, evasive, or incomplete claims of inability to comply; or (3) unmeritorious or overly generalized objections.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (a).)  Moreover, “[t]he motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.”  (Code Civ. Proc., §¿2031.310, subd. (b)(2).)

A motion to compel further production must set forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought by the inspection demand.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(1).)  “To establish good cause, a discovery proponent must identify a disputed fact that is of consequence in the action and explain how the discovery sought will tend in reason to prove or disprove that fact or lead to other evidence that will tend to prove or disprove the fact.”  (Digital Music News LLC v Superior Court (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 216, 224 (“Digital Music News LLC”).)  If the moving party has shown good cause for the requests for production, the burden is on the objecting party to justify the objections.  (Kirkland v. Superior Court (2002) 95 Cal. App.4th 92, 98.)

“[T]he court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel further response to a demand, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (h).)

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.030, subdivision (a), “The court may impose a monetary sanction ordering that one engaging in the misuse of the discovery process, or any attorney advising that conduct, or both pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney’s fees, incurred by anyone as a result of that conduct.  The court may also impose this sanction on one unsuccessfully asserting that another has engaged in the misuse of the discovery process, or on any attorney who advised that assertion, or on both.  If a monetary sanction is authorized by any provision of this title, the court shall impose that sanction unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030, subd. (a).)  “Misuses of the discovery process include … (d) Failing to respond or to submit to an authorized method of discovery.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.010, subd. (d).)  “The court may award sanctions under the Discovery Act in favor of a party who files a motion to compel discovery, even though no opposition to the motion was filed, or opposition to the motion was withdrawn, or the requested discovery was provided to the moving party after the motion was filed.”  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1348(a).)

In this case, Plaintiffs issued the discovery on December 17, 2025, via email, with responses due on January 20, 2026, due to January 18, 2026, falling on a Sunday and January 19, 2026, being a holiday.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 1010.6, subd. (a)(3)(B).)  Dennis P. Block acknowledges that they served their responses on January 21, 2026.  (Opp., p. 3:16-17.)  Consequently, Dennis P. LEGAL STANDARD

A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable.  (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)  “To survive a demurrer, the complaint need only allege facts sufficient to state a cause of action; each evidentiary fact that might eventually form part of the plaintiff’s proof need not be alleged.”  (C.A. v. William S. Hart Union High School Dist. (2012) 53 Cal.4th 861, 872.)  For the purpose of testing the sufficiency of the cause of action, the demurrer admits the truth of all material facts properly pleaded.  (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 966-967.)  A demurrer “does not admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.”  (Daar v. Yellow Cab Co. (1967) 67 Cal.2d 695, 713.)

A pleading is uncertain if it is ambiguous or unintelligible.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (f).)  A demurrer for uncertainty may lie if the failure to label the parties and claims renders the complaint so confusing that the defendant cannot tell what he or she is supposed to respond to.  (Williams v. Beechnut Nutrition Corp. (1986) 185 Cal.App.3d 135, 139, fn. 2.)  However, “[a] demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.”  (Khoury v. Maly’s of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.)

MEET AND CONFER REQUIREMENT

Before filing a demurrer, the moving party is required to meet and confer in person, by telephone, or via video conference with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to the motion, to determine whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer or motion to strike.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a).)  The parties are to meet and confer at least five days before the date the responsive pleading is due.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(2).)  Thereafter, the moving party shall file and serve a declaration detailing their meet and confer efforts.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(3).)  A determination by the court that the meet and confer process was inadequate shall not be grounds to overrule or sustain a demurrer.  (Code Civil Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(4).)

On May 14, 2026, Save’s counsel met and conferred with Plaintiff’s counsel over the phone.  (Webb Decl., ¶¶ 3-4.)  Subsequently, on May 22, 2026, Save’s counsel sent a meet-and-confer letter via email to address the alleged deficiencies identified in the demurrer.  (Webb Decl., ¶¶ 3-4.)  The parties were unable to resolve the issues through informal discussion, necessitating the filing of this demurrer.  (Webb Decl., ¶ 5.)

Consequently, the Court finds that Save sufficiently met and conferred with Plaintiff prior to filing this demurrer.

DISCUSSION

Second Cause of Action – Fraud – Concealment

Save demurs to the second cause of action for fraud – concealment on the grounds that (1) Plaintiff fails to plead with the required specificity; and (2) “Plaintiff fails to adequately allege justifiable reliance and independent fraud damages.”  (Dem., pp. 6:9-10, 7:3-4.) 

“The elements of fraud, which give rise to the tort action for deceit, are (a) misrepresentation (false representation, concealment, or nondisclosure); (b) knowledge of falsity (or ‘scienter’); (c) intent to defraud, i.e., to induce reliance; (d) justifiable reliance; and (e) resulting damage.”  (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 638 (Lazar), quoting 5 Witkin, Summary of Cal. Law (9th ed. 1988) Torts, § 676, p. 778.)

The facts constituting the alleged fraud must be alleged factually and specifically as to every element of fraud, as the policy of “liberal construction” of the pleadings will not ordinarily be invoked.  (Lazar, supra, 12 Cal.4th at p. 645.)  In alleging a cause of action for fraud, “[t]he specificity requirement means a plaintiff must allege facts showing how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were made, and, in the case of a corporate defendant, the plaintiff must allege the names of the persons who made the representations, their authority to speak on behalf of the corporation, to whom they spoke, what they said or wrote, and when the representation was made.”  (West v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A. (2013) 214 Cal.App.4th 780, 793.)  However, “to prove reliance on an omission[, the plaintiff] need only prove that, had the omitted information been disclosed, [the plaintiff] would have been aware of it and behaved differently.”  (Mirkin v. Wasserman (1993) 5 Cal.4th 1082, 1093.)

Here, Plaintiff contends that Choi “is the chief financial officer, agent, and director of [Save].  [Lee] is the chief executive officer and director of [Save].  [Choi and Lee] are nurse practitioners providing medical services at [Save,] located in Torrance, California.”  (FAC, ¶ 3.)  “[O]n or about May 7, 2025, before and continuing thereafter, [P]laintiff presented to [Save] for cosmetic procedures, including body fat dissolving injections in her thighs.  Among other negligent acts and omissions, defendants, and each of them, negligently performed the injections into plaintiff’s thighs using improper equipment and technique, causing the injection needle to break off and became lodged in [P]laintiff’s left thigh”  (FAC, ¶ 9.)  Choi and Lee “intentionally failed to disclose to [P]laintiff the fact that the injection needle had broken off and was lodged in her left thigh during the injections performed that same day.”  (FAC, ¶ 17.)  “Plaintiff was positioned on her stomach during the procedure and could not observe the injections being performed.  Plaintiff did not see the needle that was used either during or after the procedure and had no way to know that it had broken.”  (FAC, ¶ 17.)  “On May 9, 2025, defendants [Choi and Lee] asked [P]laintiff to return to [Save] without disclosing this material fact.  Upon [P]laintiff’s arrival on May 9, 2025, [Choi and Lee] asked [P]laintiff to sign a consent form and to backdate it to May 7, 2025.  Defendants then performed what they represented to be additional fat-dissolving injections in Plaintiff’s left thigh.  Only after completing this procedure did defendants inform [P]laintiff that the needle had broken off in her left thigh during the May 7, 2025 injections.”  (FAC, ¶ 18.)

Plaintiff further claims: “Had [P]laintiff known this material fact, she would have behaved differently.  Specifically, [P]laintiff would have sought immediate medical evaluation and treatment on May 7, 2025, to have the needle promptly located and removed by competent, qualified medical providers, before any complications such as migration, infection, or other injuries developed.  Plaintiff would not have simply gone home and continued her normal activities for two days without seeking medical attention.  Further, [P]laintiff would not have returned to [Save] on May 9, 2025, nor consented to any additional injections or procedures performed by [Choi and Lee.]”  (FAC, ¶ 20.)

Moreover, Plaintiff contends that “[t]he injection needle migrated within [P]laintiff’s body and became significantly more difficult to locate and remove.  As it migrated, the needle became very painful, uncomfortable and disturbing to [P]laintiff, causing her severe pain, suffering, emotional distress, anxiety, stress, despair, mental anguish, inconvenience, and other injuries to her body, nervous system, and overall health.  As a further direct and proximate result of defendants’ intentional fraudulent conduct, [P]laintiff was required to undergo multiple surgical procedures to locate and remove the migrated needle, suffering infection, prolonged recovery, multiple incisions and their corresponding scars, and other related sequalae.”  (FAC, ¶ 21.)  Plaintiff’s alleged injuries include “shock and injury to [P]laintiff’s nervous system and person, all of which has caused and continues to cause [P]laintiff great mental, emotional, physical and nervous pain, suffering, anguish, fear, anxiety, and distress.”  (FAC, ¶ 22.)

Save first contends that Plaintiff’s claim is deficient because she “repeatedly lumps all Defendants together and alleges that ‘defendants’ collectively and concealed information or performed procedures.”  (Dem., p. 7:1-2.)  This assertion is only partially accurate.  Although Plaintiff does not specify who administered the fat-dissolvent, this detail is not pertinent to this claim.  The primary issue is whether Plaintiff has identified which party concealed the alleged broken needle.  Plaintiff explicitly names Choi and Lee in this context.  (FAC, ¶ 17.)  Consequently, Save’s argument lacks merit.

Furthermore, Save contends that “While Plaintiff identifies Choi and Lee as corporate officers, the FAC does not allege specific facts demonstrating that Save[], as a corporate entity, authorized, directed, or ratified any alleged concealment regarding the retained needle.  Nor does the FAC allege any corporate policy, directive, or entity-level decision concerning nondisclosure, or facts demonstrating that Save [] independently possessed knowledge of and approved the alleged concealment.  Such respondeat superior allegations are insufficient to satisfy the heightened pleading standard applicable to fraud claims against a corporate defendant.”  (Dem., p. 7:2-8.)  However, the Court observes that Save does not cite any supporting case law or statute to substantiate its assertion regarding the necessity for such detailed allegations.  Save references Archuleta v. Grand Lodge of Intern. Ass’n of Machinists and Aerospace Workers, AFL-CIO (1968) 262 Cal.App.2d 202, 208-209, and Tarmann v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. (1991) 2 Cal.App.4th 153, 157-158, but neither case supports this argument.  Neither case mandates that Plaintiff must provide “facts demonstrating that [the defendant], as a corporate entity, authorized, directed, or ratified any alleged concealment” nor do they stipulate that Plaintiff must “allege any corporate policy, directive, or entity-level decision concerning nondisclosure, or facts demonstrating that [the corporate defendant] independently possessed knowledge of and approved the alleged concealment.”  Moreover, neither case addresses the principle of respondeat superior.

The focal question is whether Plaintiff alleges that Lee and Choi had the authority to speak or conceal on behalf of Save.  Plaintiff meets this requirement by asserting that Choi serves as the CEO, agent, and director of Save, while Lee also holds the position of CEO and director of Save.  (FAC, ¶ 3.)

Save further contends that “Plaintiff does not allege with specificity the particular statements or omissions attributable to the corporate entity, the manner in which such statements were communicated, or facts showing that Save [] itself engaged in intentional fraudulent conduct separate and apart from the acts allegedly undertaken by the individual providers[.]”  (Dem, p. 7:11-15.)  Again, Save fails to provide any supporting case law or statutes that prove Plaintiff must include this information.  Save argues that “the complaint must allege facts showing why the corporation itself is liable for those acts” under Committee On Children’s Television, Inc. v. General Foods Corp. (1983) 35 Cal.3d 197, 216.  (Reply, p. 3:16-18.)  However, this citation does not discuss corporations.  Additionally, as Plaintiff points out, “A corporation, as an artificial entity, cannot of itself perform or refrain from performing an act; only its officers and agents can.”  (Opp., p. 3:19-20, quoting Signal Oil & Gas Co. v. Ashland Oil & Refining Co. (1958) 49 Cal.2d 764, 779.)

Save contends that “even assuming Plaintiff sufficiently alleges reliance as to the individual providers, the FAC still fails to allege facts showing that Plaintiff relied on any specific concealment attributable to [Save] itself.  The FAC does not identify any particular statement, omission, or concealment made by Save [] as a corporate entity upon which Plaintiff allegedly relied, nor does it allege facts showing that Save [] itself communicated any information to Plaintiff through an authorized corporate representative.”  (Dem., p. 8:11-16.)  Similarly, Save asserts that “the FAC does not allege facts demonstrating that Plaintiff’s alleged reliance arose from any corporate policy, directive, or entity-level decision by Save” and “contains no factual allegations establishing that Save [] authorized, directed, or ratified any alleged concealment that purportedly caused Plaintiff to return for further treatment on May 9, 2025.”  (Dem., p. 8:19-23.)    Again, Save provides no case law that indicates Plaintiff must allege that Save itself made any specific statement, omission, or concealment.  Plaintiff must demonstrate that she relied upon a statement or concealment made by an individual authorized to speak on behalf of Save, and that such reliance resulted in damages.  Plaintiff has fulfilled this requirement.

Plaintiff contends that Lee and Choi, executives and directors of Save, concealed the broken needle that became lodged in her thigh.  (FAC, ¶¶ 3, 17-18.)  Plaintiff further alleges that the needle migrated, “causing her severe pain, suffering, emotional distress, anxiety, stress, despair, mental anguish, inconvenience, and other injuries to her body, nervous system, and overall health.”  (FAC, ¶ 21.)  Consequently, she had to “undergo multiple surgical procedures to locate and remove the migrated needle, suffering infection, prolonged recovery, multiple incisions and their corresponding scars, and other related sequalae.”  (FAC, ¶ 21.)

Furthermore, Plaintiff contends that she would have pursued “immediate medical evaluation and treatment on May 7, 2025, to have the needle promptly located and removed by competent, qualified medical providers, before any complications such as migration, infection, or other injuries developed.  Plaintiff would not have simply gone home and continued her normal activities for 2 days without seeking medical attention.”  (FAC, ¶ 20.)

Accordingly, Save’s demurrer with respect to the second cause of action for fraud – concealment (and scienter) is OVERRULED.

Third Cause of Action – Medical Battery

Save demurs to the third cause of action for medical battery on the grounds that (1) “Plaintiff fails to allege sufficient facts to state a claim for medical battery[.]”  (Dem., p. 9:3.) 

“A battery is a violation of an individual’s interest in freedom from intentional unlawful, harmful or offensive unconsented contacts with his or her person.  [Citation.]  The element of lack of consent to the particular contact is an essential element of battery.  [Citation.]”  (Rains v. Superior Court (1984) 150 Cal.App.3d 933, 938, internal citations omitted.)

“Where a doctor obtains consent of the patient to perform one type of treatment and subsequently performs a substantially different treatment for which consent was not obtained, there is a clear case of battery.”  (Cobbs v. Grant (1972) 8 Cal.3d 229, 239.)  “The battery theory should be reserved for those circumstances when a doctor performs an operation to which the patient has not consented.  When the patient gives permission to perform one type of treatment and the doctor performs another, the requisite element of deliberate intent to deviate from the consent given is present.  However, when the patient consents to certain treatment and the doctor performs that treatment but an undisclosed inherent complication with a low probability occurs, no intentional deviation from the consent given appears; rather, the doctor in obtaining consent may have failed to meet his due care duty to disclose pertinent information.  In that situation the action should be pleaded in negligence.”  (Id. at pp. 240-241.)

Here, Plaintiff contends that “On May 9, 2025, [P]laintiff was told by defendants [Choi and Lee] that they were going to perform additional fat-dissolving injections in her thighs.  Unaware of the broken needle lodged in her left thigh since May 7, 2025, [P]laintiff consented to the additional injections.  On information and belief, the purported additional injections were a pretext for defendants [Choi and Lee] to go re-enter [P]laintiff’s left thigh and search for the broken needle without having to first inform [P]laintiff of its presence, and to subsequently document the incident in the medical records as though it occurred on May 9, 2025, rather than 2 days earlier on May 7, 2025.  Accordingly, defendants [Choi and Lee] performed a substantially different medical procedure than that which plaintiff consented to – namely, an exploratory attempt to locate a retained foreign object rather than the administration of additional fat-dissolving injections – by deceiving [P]laintiff regarding the true reason for and nature of the medical procedure performed on her left thigh on May 9, 2025.”  (FAC, ¶ 26.)

Save contends that the claim is deficient because “[t]he pleading [] contains no specific factual allegations showing that Save [] itself engaged in any intentional unauthorized touching, directed such conduct, or otherwise committed acts sufficient to impose liability for an intentional tort.”  (Dem., p. 9:7-11.)  This argument lacks persuasive force, as a corporate entity such as Save cannot itself commit battery but may nonetheless be held accountable for intentional torts perpetrated by its executives and agents, including Choi and Lee.  (FAC, ¶ 3.)

Save further asserts that “the FAC pleads no specific factual allegations” indicating that “the May 9, 2025, procedure was not actually additional fat-dissolving injections, but rather a concealed exploratory attempt to locate the retained needle fragment.”  (Dem., p. 9:14-16.)   

However, as indicated, Plaintiff contends that although she consented to additional injections, she was subjected to an entirely different procedure, an exploratory surgery or procedure aimed at removing the broken needle, instead of receiving the fat-dissolving injections.  (FAC, ¶ 26.)  The Court find these facts sufficient to survive the demurrer.

Finally, Save contends that this constitutes a negligence claim rather than a medical battery because “Plaintiff expressly alleges that she consented to additional injections in her thighs on May 9, 2025[,]” and “[t]he alleged conduct involved the same anatomical site, the same general type of needle- or incision-based intervention, and occurred during the very procedure for which Plaintiff admittedly provided consent.”  (Dem., pp. 10:15, 18-20.) 

As previously noted, Plaintiff contends that she did not receive the injections but rather underwent an exploratory procedure.  (FAC, ¶ 26.)  Based on these allegations, the exploratory procedure did not fall within the scope of the procedure to which Plaintiff consented; it constituted a distinct procedure to which she did not provide consent.  The similarities in the anatomical site or the nature of the needle- or incision-based intervention are not pertinent to this matter.  Plaintiff did not consent to this specific procedure.

Accordingly, Save’s demurrer with respect to the third cause of action for medical battery is OVERRULED.
