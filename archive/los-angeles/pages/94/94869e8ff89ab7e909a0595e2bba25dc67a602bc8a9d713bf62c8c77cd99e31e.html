VACATE JUDGMENT

 

MOVING PARTY: Plaintiff, Hossein Shahali

RESPONDING PARTY: Defendants, Universal Protection Service, LP and Luisa Rodriguez

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Motion to Vacate Judgment

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

Plaintiff Hossein Shahali was formerly employed by Allied Universal and Universal Protection Service, LP. On February 1, 2019, Plaintiff filed a complaint against Allied Universal, Universal Protection Service, Luisa Rodriguez, and Alexis Castillo asserting multiple causes of action under the Fair Employment and Housing Act, among other claims. On June 5, 2019, the Court granted Universal Protection Service’s motion to compel arbitration. An award was issued in favor of Universal Protection Service and Rodriguez and was confirmed on January 11, 2022. Judgment was subsequently entered on March 8, 2022.

 

RULING: Denied.

Plaintiff moves to vacate the March 8, 2022 judgment on grounds of extrinsic fraud, citing purportedly fabricated documents and technical irregularities in the arbitration. Defendants Universal Protection Service and Rodriguez oppose the motion, arguing that the motion is not based on extrinsic fraud, is not supported by competent evidence, and attempts to improperly relitigate previously decided issues. Plaintiff’s motion is denied because he relies on purely alleged intrinsic rather than extrinsic fraud.

 

“A motion to vacate a judgment for extrinsic fraud is not governed by any statutory time limit, but rather is addressed to the court’s ‘inherent equity power’ to grant relief from a judgment procured by extrinsic fraud.” (Dept. of Industrial Relations v. Davis Moreno Constr., Inc. (2011) 193 Cal.App.4th 560, 570.) “Extrinsic fraud occurs when a party is deprived of the opportunity to present his claim or defense to the court; where he was kept ignorant or, other than from his own negligence, fraudulently prevented from fully participating in the proceeding. [Citation.] Examples of extrinsic fraud are: ... failure to give notice of the action to the other party, and convincing the other party not to obtain counsel because the matter will not proceed (and then it does proceed). [Citation.] The essence of extrinsic fraud is one party’s preventing the other from having his day in court.” (Id. at p. 570.)

 

Plaintiff solely relies on alleged intrinsic fraud to vacate the judgment. Plaintiff points to purportedly fabricated court documents and irregularities in the arbitration proceeding such as pausing of recording of the hearing, omissions in the hearing transcript, and the arbitrator’s untimely issuance of the award. [See Plaintiff’s Exs. 1-22.] Plaintiff presents no evidence that he was prevented from participating in the hearings before the Court or the arbitrator. To the contrary, the evidence shows that Plaintiff participated in both proceedings and presented similar arguments and concerns he now brings. [See, e.g., Green Decl. ¶¶ 9-12, 14-16, Exs. C-E, G-I: final award, arbitrator’s denial of Plaintiff’s motion for reconsideration, Plaintiff’s filings in this Court, and confirmation of award.] There is thus no basis to vacate the Court’s judgment on grounds of extrinsic fraud and Plaintiff’s motion is denied.

 

Plaintiff to give notice.

RELIEVED AS COUNSEL

 

MOVING ATTORNEY: Eric A. Jimenez  

RESPONDING PARTY: Unopposed/Defendant, El Zarape Cocina On Melrose Inc.

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Motion to be Relieved as Counsel of Record for Defendant

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

Plaintiff Angelica Montano sued Defendants El Zarape Melrose Corp., El Zarape Fountain Inc., El Zarape Vine Inc., El Zarape Redondo Beach, El Zarape Cocina on Melrose Inc., and El Zarape Beverly Kitchen Inc. for multiple alleged violations of the Labor Code. All Defendants are in default.

 

RULING: Continued.

Counsel for Defendant El Zarape Cocina on Melrose moves to be relieved as counsel of record due to a “fundamental and unresolvable breakdown in communication.” Counsel and Defendant have not communicated in over 10 months despite counsel’s efforts. Counsel further states that Defendant is currently a suspended corporation. The motion does not comply with the form requirements in two respects: (1) a proposed order was not served and filed and (2) the notice of motion and motion and the declaration were not served on all parties who have appeared in the case. “The notice of motion and motion, the declaration, and the proposed order must be served on the client and on all other parties who have appeared in the case.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362(d).) “The proposed order relieving counsel must be prepared on the Order Granting Attorney’s Motion to Be Relieved as Counsel-Civil (form MC-053) and must be lodged with the court with the moving papers.” (Id. rule 3.1362(e).) The motion is therefore continued to July 8, 2026.

COMPEL ARBITRATION

 

MOVING PARTY: Defendant, Los Angeles Truck Service, LLC dba Velocity Vehicle Group

RESPONDING PARTY: Plaintiff, Joshua Gonzalez

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Motion to Compel Arbitration and Stay Action

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

Plaintiff Joshua Gonzalez, on behalf of himself and current and former aggrieved employees, filed the instant PAGA action against Defendant Los Angeles Truck Service, LLC alleging wage and hour violations.

 

RULING: Granted.

Evidentiary Objections: Overruled.

 

Defendant moves to compel arbitration of Plaintiff’s individual PAGA claim under a prior court order in Plaintiff’s class action suit and the Dispute Resolution Agreement (Agreement) between the parties. Defendant also seeks sanctions against Plaintiff and his counsel of record for fees and costs incurred in bringing the motion. Plaintiff opposes the motion, arguing that the class action order did not compel arbitration of PAGA claims, the Agreement is unconscionable, and sanctions are unwarranted. As an initial matter, the Court declines to compel arbitration based on the order in the class action suit because that order does not require Plaintiff to arbitrate his PAGA claim. The analysis is therefore focused on challenges to the Agreement. Although the Agreement contains certain unconscionable provisions, these provisions are severable and Defendant’s motion is granted.

 

“A written agreement to submit to arbitration an existing controversy or a controversy thereafter arising is valid, enforceable and irrevocable, save upon such grounds as exist for the revocation of any contract.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.) “On petition of a party to an arbitration agreement alleging the existence of a written agreement to arbitrate a controversy and that a party thereto refuses to arbitrate such controversy, the court shall order the petitioner and the respondent to arbitrate the controversy if it determines that an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists, unless it determines that: (a) The right to compel arbitration has been waived by the petitioner; or (b) Grounds exist for the revocation of the agreement.” (Id. § 1281.2.)

 

Defendant moves to compel arbitration under the Federal Arbitration Act (FAA). The Agreement explicitly states that it is governed by the FAA. [Montano Decl. ¶ 8, Ex. A, p. 2.] This is sufficient to invoke application of the FAA to the arbitration agreement. (Victrola 89, LLC v. Jaman Properties 8 LLC (2020) 46 Cal.App.5th 337, 355.) While the FAA governs the rules for conducting arbitration, barring citation to a case precluding California law, motions to compel arbitration are still governed by California law. (Adolph v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (2023) 14 Cal.5th 1104, 1119; Viking River Cruises, Inc. v. Moriana (2022) 596 U.S. 639, 653; Volt Information Sciences, Inc. v. Board of Trustees of Leland Stanford Junior University (1989) 489 U.S. 468, 477-79; Victrola 89, supra, 46 Cal.App.5th at p. 346.)

 

The law creates a general presumption in favor of arbitration. In a motion to compel arbitration, the moving party must prove by a preponderance of evidence the existence of the arbitration agreement and that the dispute is covered by the agreement. The burden then shifts to the resisting party to prove by a preponderance of evidence a ground for denial (e.g., fraud, unconscionability, etc.). (Rosenthal v. Great Western Fin’l Securities Corp. (1996) 14 Cal.4th 394, 413-14; Hotels Nevada v. L.A. Pacific Ctr., Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 754, 758.) Any challenges to the formation of the arbitration agreement should be considered before any order sending the parties to arbitration. The trier of fact weighs all evidence, including affidavits, declarations, documents, and, if applicable, oral testimony to determine whether the action goes to arbitration. (Hotels Nevada, supra, 144 Cal.App.4th at p. 758.)

 

The moving party bears the initial burden of producing “prima facie evidence of a written agreement to arbitrate the controversy.” (Gamboa v. Northeast Community Clinic (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 158, 165.) This burden may be met “by attaching to the [motion or] petition a copy of the arbitration agreement purporting to bear the [opposing party’s] signature” or “setting forth the agreement’s provisions in the motion.” (Id. at p. 165.) If the opposing party disputes the agreement, then the burden shifts to the opposing party to produce “evidence to challenge the authenticity of the agreement.” (Ibid.) The opposing party can do this by testifying under oath or declaring under penalty of perjury “that the party never saw or does not remember seeing the agreement, or that the party never signed or does not remember signing the agreement.” (Ibid.) If the opposing party meets its burden, the burden then shifts to the moving party to “establish with admissible evidence a valid arbitration agreement between the parties.” (Ibid.) “The burden of proving the agreement by a preponderance of the evidence remains with the moving party.” (Id. at p. 166).

 

Defendant has proven the existence of an agreement to arbitrate Plaintiff’s PAGA claim. It attaches to the motion a copy of the Agreement containing Plaintiff’s electronic signature. [Montano Decl. ¶ 8, Ex. A, p. 3.] The Agreement covers Plaintiff’s claim because it provides that individual claims under PAGA are subject to arbitration. [Id. at p. 1.] Plaintiff presents no challenge to the authenticity of the Agreement. The attention thus turns to Plaintiff’s defenses to enforcement of the Agreement.

 

Plaintiff challenges the arbitration agreement as unconscionable. He argues the Agreement is procedurally unconscionable because it is a contract of adhesion, he could only access it via his company email address while at work, he signed it within a minute, he did not understand many of its terms and no one explained them to him, and the Agreement misleadingly suggests that an attorney is not necessary for arbitration. Plaintiff contends the Agreement is substantively unconscionable because, when read together with other onboarding documents, it lacks mutuality, violates the Labor Code, unlawfully restrains competition and chills future employment, and provides for an unlawful attorneys’ fees award.

 

Unconscionability claims have both a procedural and substantive element. (Stirlen v. Supercuts, Inc. (1997) 51 Cal.App.4th 1519, 1531.) “Procedural unconscionability” concerns the manner in which the contract was negotiated and the circumstances of the parties at that time. (Kinney v. United HealthCare Services, Inc. (1999) 70 Cal.App.4th 1322, 1329.) “‘The procedural element focuses on two factors: “oppression” and “surprise.”  “Oppression” arises from an inequality of bargaining power which results in no real negotiation and an absence of meaningful choice. “Surprise” involves the extent to which the supposedly agreed-upon terms of the bargain are hidden in the prolix printed form drafted by the party seeking to enforce the disputed terms.’” (Stirlen, supra, 51 Cal.App.4th at p. 1532.) “Substantive unconscionability” involves contracts leading to “overly harsh” or “one-sided” results. (Ibid.) “[U]nconscionability turns . . . on an absence of justification for it” and therefore “must be evaluated as of the time the contract was made.” (Ibid.)

 

There is minimal procedural unconscionability. A “low level” of procedural unconscionability exists given the adhesive nature of the contract. (Alvarez v. Altamed Health Services Corp. (2021) 60 Cal.App.5th 572, 591.) The circumstances surrounding Plaintiff’s opportunity to review the Agreement confirm the low degree of procedural unconscionability. Plaintiff had 11 days to review and sign the Agreement. [Montano Decl. ¶ 13, Ex. B.] This provided Plaintiff ample opportunity to review the Agreement, print it and consult with an attorney, and present questions to Human Resources. [Id. ¶ 12.] Plaintiff’s signing of the Agreement within one minute of viewing it is reflective of his own decision to not read its terms rather than sharp practices by Defendant. [Id. ¶ 13, Ex. B] And contrary to Plaintiff’s claim, the Agreement does not state that an attorney is unnecessary for arbitration. Instead, it states that he is not required to have an attorney to participate in arbitration. [Id. ¶ 8, Ex. A, p. 2.] This is a legally correct statement. (Cf. Velarde v. Monroe Operations, LLC (2025) 111 Cal.App.5th 1009, 1015-16 [HR manager misinformed employee that arbitration agreement gave employer power to resolve disputes without parties having to pay for lawyers].) Thus, a low degree of procedural unconscionability exists.

 

In assessing substantive unconscionability, the Court reads the Agreement together with the Offer Letter and Employee Confidentiality and Non-Disclosure Agreement (NDA). Under Civil Code section 1642, “[s]everal contracts relating to the same matters, between the same parties, and made as parts of substantially one transaction, are to be taken together.” The writings need not be contracts or executed on the same day. (Cadigan v. American Trust Co. (1955) 131 Cal.App.2d 780, 786-87.) The Offer Letter, NDA, and Agreement are parts of a single transaction because they govern the employment relationship between Plaintiff and Defendant. (See Alberto v. Cambrian Homecare (2023) 91 Cal.App.5th 482, 490-91 [arbitration and confidentiality agreements read together because they both governed how to resolve disputes arising from employment].)

 

Reading these writings together, the Agreement is substantively unconscionable. While Defendant is required to arbitrate breaches of the NDA under both the Agreement and NDA [Montano Decl. ¶ 8, Ex. A, p. 1; Gonzalez Decl. ¶ 4, Ex. 2, p. 8 [NDA is “[s]ubject to any applicable arbitration agreement”]], the NDA lacks mutuality because it waives Defendant’s need to show irreparable harm and precludes Plaintiff from raising an adequate remedy at law as a defense. [Gonzalez Decl. ¶ 4, Ex. 2, p. 7.] Plaintiff is not provided a similar benefit when he is seeking injunctive relief. These provisions thus “exceed the legitimate ‘margin of safety’ for the employer and are not mutual.” (Alberto, supra, 91 Cal.App.5th at p. 492.)

 

Substantive unconscionability also exists because the NDA violates Labor Code section 232. “A facially illegal provision, in direct contravention of the Labor Code, is unconscionable.” (Alberto, supra, 91 Cal.App.5th at p. 493.) Under section 232, an employer may not require “as a condition of employment, that an employee refrain from disclosing the amount of his or her wages” or that the employee “sign a waiver or other document that purports to deny the employee the right to disclose the amount of his or her wages.” (Lab. Code, § 232, subds. (a)-(b).) Here, the NDA violates this provision because it designates as “Confidential Information” information related to current and former employees’ “compensation.” [Gonzalez Decl. ¶ 4, Ex. 2, pp. 2-3.] Though a carve-out exists for wage discussions with “other employees,” there is no similar exception for communications with former employees or counsel. [Id. at p. 5.] The NDA thus impedes Plaintiff’s ability to investigate facts for use in a PAGA action. (Alberto, supra, 91 Cal.App.5th at p. 493.)

 

Finally, substantive unconscionability is further present due to the Offer Letter’s fees provision. The Offer Letter incorporated by reference the initial Dispute Resolution Agreement and under Civil Code section 1642 can likewise be read to incorporate the later Agreement at issue. [Gonzalez Decl. ¶ 3, Ex. 1.] The provision does not account for situations where an employee successfully presents arguments warranting severance of unconscionable provisions, but arbitration is otherwise compelled. Faced with the threat of fees even for such partial success, an employee might refrain from exercising the right to challenge the enforceability of the Agreement. (Ramirez v. Charter Communications, Inc. (2024) 16 Cal.5th 478, 508-09.)  

 

There is no substantive unconscionability, however, due to any violation of Labor Code section 232.5 or Business and Professions Code section 16600. Labor Code section 232.5 prohibits an employer from requiring an employee to refrain from disclosing information about the employer’s working conditions. (Lab. Code, § 232.5, subds. (a)-(b).) This encompasses physical working conditions like safety hazards in the workplace. (See, e.g., Zirpel v. Alki David Productions, Inc. (2023) 93 Cal.App.5th 563, 567-68, 577 [employee fired for reporting plumbing and electrical violations at theater].) There is no provision in the NDA forbidding Plaintiff from disclosing such information. There is no violation of the Business and Professions Code because although Plaintiff cannot use Confidential Information to solicit business from Defendant’s clients after termination, this does not restrain him from engaging in his chosen profession. (See AMN Healthcare, Inc. v. Aya Healthcare Services, Inc. (2018) 28 Cal.App.5th 923, 939 [nonsolicitation of employee provision void because it restrained recruiters from recruiting].)    

 

Because of the presence of some degree of both procedural and substantive unconscionability, the next inquiry is whether the unconscionable provisions may be severed. “If a contractual clause is found unconscionable, the court may, in its discretion, choose to do one of the following: (1) refuse to enforce the contract; (2) sever any unconscionable clause; or (3) limit the application of any clause to avoid unconscionable results.” (Ramirez, supra, 16 Cal.5th at p. 513; Civ. Code, § 1670.5, subd. (a).) “At the outset, a court should ask whether ‘the central purpose of the contract is tainted with illegality.’” (Ramirez, supra, 16 Cal.5th at p. 516.) “If so, the contract cannot be cured, and the court should refuse to enforce it. If that is not the case, the court should go on to ask first, whether the contract’s unconscionability can be cured purely through severance or restriction of its terms, or whether reformation by augmentation is necessary.” (Ibid.) “If no ‘reformation is required,’ the offending provision can be severed or limited, and ‘the rest of the arbitration agreement left intact,’ then severance or restriction is the preferred course for provisions that are collateral to the agreement’s main purpose.” (Ibid.) If augmentation is required to cure the unconscionability, then the contract is unenforceable as courts cannot “rewrite agreements and impose terms to which neither party has agreed.” (Ibid.)

 

“Even if a contract can be cured, the court should also ask whether the unconscionability should be cured through severance or restriction because the interests of justice would be furthered by such actions.” (Ramirez, supra, 16 Cal.5th at p. 516.) This focuses on “whether mere severance of the unconscionable terms would function to condone an illegal scheme and whether the defects in the agreement indicate that the stronger party engaged in a systematic effort to impose arbitration on the weaker party not simply as an alternative to litigation, but to secure a forum that works to the stronger party’s advantage.” (Id. at pp. 516-17.) “If the answer to either question is yes, the court should refuse to enforce the agreement.” (Id. at p. 517.)

 

Severance of the unconscionable provisions is appropriate. The central purpose of the Agreement is not tainted with illegality because the unconscionable provisions are limited to certain terms providing Defendant with an unequal advantage in obtaining injunctive relief, prohibiting Plaintiff from discussing wages with individuals other than current employees, and awarding Defendant fees for enforcing the Agreement. Unconscionability can be cured by severing these provisions from the Agreement. There is no indication that severance would condone an illegal scheme of Defendant or that Defendant included the offending provisions to create an inferior forum for the resolution of Plaintiff’s claims. The Court therefore severs the unconscionable provisions from the Agreement.

 

Defendant’s motion is therefore granted. Plaintiff’s individual PAGA claim is to be arbitrated.

 

“If a court of competent jurisdiction, whether in this State or not, has ordered arbitration of a controversy which is an issue involved in an action or proceeding pending before a court of this State, the court in which such action or proceeding is pending shall, upon motion of a party to such action or proceeding, stay the action or proceeding until an arbitration is had in accordance with the order to arbitrate or until such earlier time as the court specifies.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.4.) The action is stayed.

 

The Court vacates the July 1, 2026 Case Management Conference. The Court will set an OSC re: Status of Arbitration and Stay at the time of the hearing.

 

The Court declines to award sanctions.

 

Defendant to give notice.

DEMURRER

 

MOVING PARTY: Plaintiff, Sidetrack Agency, LLC

RESPONDING PARTY: Defendant, Live Nation Worldwide, Inc.

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Demurrer to Amended Answer

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

Defendant Live Nation Worldwide, Inc. hired Plaintiff Sidetrack Agency, LLC to build, install, dismantle, store, and transport a “brand activation” for music festivals throughout the country. The structure, called the “Hacienda,” promoted the products of Bacardí Patrón. Plaintiff alleges that despite the successful debut of the Hacienda, Defendant terminated Plaintiff’s services, retained the Hacienda materials for future festivals, and refused to pay. On August 5, 2025, Plaintiff filed a complaint for: (1) Breach of Contract; (2) Unjust Enrichment; (3) Quantum Meruit; (4) and Violation of Business and Professions Code section 17200 et seq.  

 

RULING: Sustained in part.

Plaintiff demurs to the first, fifth through seventh, tenth through eleventh, and thirteenth through fourteenth affirmative defenses in Defendant’s amended answer.[1] It argues these defenses are insufficiently pled, fail to identify the causes of action they apply to, and are uncertain. Defendant opposes the demurrer, maintaining the sufficiency of its defenses. Plaintiff concedes that affirmative defenses 10, 11, and 14 are not affirmative defenses, but denials. They are thus not at issue. Plaintiff’s demurrer is sustained with leave to amend as to affirmative defense 7 and sustained without leave to amend as to affirmative defense 13. The demurrer is overruled as to affirmative defenses 1 and 5-6.

 

A demurrer is an objection to a pleading, the grounds for which are apparent from either the face of the complaint or a matter of which the court may take judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.30, subd. (a); see also Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) The purpose of a demurrer is to challenge the sufficiency of a pleading “by raising questions of law.” (Postley v. Harvey (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 280, 286.) “In the construction of a pleading, for the purpose of determining its effect, its allegations must be liberally construed, with a view to substantial justice between the parties.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 452.) The court “treat[s] the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law . . . .” (Berkley v. Dowds (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 518, 525.) In applying these standards, the court liberally construes the complaint to determine whether a cause of action has been stated. (Picton v. Anderson Union High School Dist. (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 726, 733.) “If the complaint states a cause of action under any theory, regardless of the title under which the factual basis for relief is stated, that aspect of the complaint is good against a demurrer.” (Quelimane Co. v. Stewart Title Guaranty Co. (1998) 19 Cal.4th 26, 38.)

 

“A demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Khoury v. Maly’s of Cal., Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616; Williams v. Beechnut Nutrition Corp. (1986) 185 Cal.App.3d 135, 139, fn. 2 [“[U]nder our liberal pleading rules, where the complaint contains substantive factual allegations sufficiently apprising defendant of the issues it is being asked to meet, a demurrer for uncertainty should be overruled or plaintiff given leave to amend”].)

 

An “answer to a complaint must include ‘[a] statement of any new matter constituting a defense.’ The phrase ‘new matter’ refers to something relied on by a defendant which is not put in issue by the plaintiff. [Citation.] Thus, where matters are not responsive to essential allegations of the complaint, they must be raised in the answer as ‘new matter.’ [Citation.] Where, however, the answer sets forth facts showing some essential allegation of the complaint is not true, such facts are not ‘new matter,’ but only a traverse.” (Walsh v. West Valley Mission Community College Dist. (1998) 66 Cal.App.4th 1532, 1546.) Affirmative defenses must aver facts “as carefully and with as much detail as the facts which constitute the cause of action and are alleged in the complaint.” (FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 367, 384.)

The demurrer is overruled as to affirmative defenses 1 and 5-6. The statement in affirmative defense 1 that the “Complaint, and each purported cause of action therein, fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action against Defendant” is sufficient to raise this defense. (See Baker v. Sudo (1987) 194 Cal.App.3d 936, 942, fn. 7.) Plaintiff challenges affirmative defense 5 only as to its UCL claim; however, the defense is raised as to all of Plaintiff’s causes of action and stands as to those claims. The Court declines to consider insufficiencies in the facts supporting the defense because Plaintiff asserts this point for the first time in reply. (Maleti v. Wickers (2022) 82 Cal.App.5th 181, 227-28.) Affirmative defense 6 sufficiently identifies that the offset is based on repairs Defendant made to remediate alleged unsafe conditions in the delivered Hacienda. [Amended Answer at p. 5:15-20.]

The demurrer is sustained with leave to amend as to affirmative defense 7 (equitable estoppel) and sustained without leave to amend as to affirmative defense 13 (comparative fault). “Equitable estoppel requires that: (1) the party to be estopped was aware of the operative facts and either intended that its act or omission be acted upon, or acted in such a way that the party asserting estoppel rightfully believed it was intended; and (2) the party asserting estoppel was unaware of the facts and relied on the other party’s conduct to its detriment.” (Gaines v. Fidelity Nat. Title Ins. Co. (2016) 62 Cal.4th 1081, 1097.) Defendant does not allege that Plaintiff had knowledge that the Hacienda would be defective. Because there is a reasonable possibility that Defendant can cure this defect, it is granted leave to amend. The demurrer to affirmative defense 13 is sustained without leave to amend because “contractual breaches are generally excluded from comparative fault allocations.” (Kransco v. American Empire Surplus Lines Ins. Co. (2000) 23 Cal.4th 390, 402.) 

Defendant is granted 30 days’ leave to amend. Affirmative defense 7 and defenses conceded by Plaintiff may be amended.

 

Plaintiff to give notice.
