[TENTATIVE] RULING ON PETITION FOR WRIT OF MANDATE

 

Petitioner JE Real Estate Holding Company, LLC (“Petitioner”) petitions for writs of traditional and administrative mandate against respondent City of Los Angeles (“Respondent”), directing it to set aside the decision to revoke Petitioner’s permits to build a truck and trailer storage yard (“Project”) at 832 N. Dominguez Ave, Wilmington, CA (“Property”) and to allow heavy truck access to the Property.

The petition for writ of mandate is granted.

I.                Factual Background

Petitioner owns the Property, a 41,786 square foot parcel on Dominguez Avenue and Sanford Avenue within the Wilmington-Harbor City Community Plan (“Plan”) area and zoned [Q]MR2-1VL-O-CUGU. (AR 39.) Zoning code MR2 indicates light industrial land uses. (AR 39.) The prefix [Q] indicates a qualifying condition, here:

“No cargo containers may be kept or stored on the site. However, this condition shall not prohibit the loading and/or parking of trucks and truck trailers, including containers on wheels, provided that no trailers or containers may be ‘stacked’ vertically at any time, and that any truck loading or parking facility shall maintain a landscaped buffer at least 10 feet wide that includes trees and/or shrubs, designed to visually screen the use, facing any residential zone that is adjacent, or directly across a street or alley.” (AR 40-41.)

The Property is also within Respondent’s Clean Up Green Up (“CUGU”) Supplemental Use District which requires clearance from Respondent’s Department of Building and Safety (“LADBS”) affirming compliance with objective standards. (AR 40-41; see Los Angeles Municipal Code [“LAMC”] § 13.18.)

On September 30, 2021, LADBS issued a building permit and certificate of occupancy for the Property (“Permit”) specifying the use “OPEN STORAGE TRUCK AND TRAILERS AND 6-CAR PARKING”. (AR 230.)

On April 29, 2024, Citizens for a Better Wilmington (“Appellant”) filed a Request for Modification of Building Ordinances with LADBS seeking revocation of the Permit. (AR 227.) On June 25, 2024, LADBS issued its written decision determining that it had not erred or abused its discretion in issuing the Permit and other permits on the Property for a fence, grading, and a trash enclosure. (AR 218, 224.)

On July 9, 2024, Appellant appealed this decision. (AR 524-26.) On September 23, 2024, a Zoning Administrator (“ZA”) heard the appeal. (AR 60.) On March 5, 2025, the ZA determined that LADBS had not erred or abused its discretion in issuing the Permit and other permits on the Property for a fence, grading, and a trash enclosure. (AR 38, 40, 58, 63.)

On April 18, 2025, Appellant filed an appeal of the ZA’s decision to the Harbor Area Planning Commission (“HAPC”). (AR 183-92.) On June 3, 2025, HAPC publicly heard the appeal. (AR 71.) At the hearing, Commissioner Sheikh moved to grant the appeal and overturn the ZA’s determination, and HAPC passed the motion (with clarifying amendments) unanimously. (AR 129-35.)

On October 21, 2025, Respondent mailed a Letter of Determination. (AR 755-82.)

II.             Procedural History

On August 4, 2025, Petitioner filed its petition for administrative mandamus. A proof of service filed August 7, 2025 shows Petitioner served Respondent by personal service on August 6, 2025.

On September 4, 2025, Respondent filed its answer to the Petition.

On March 19, 2026, the Court granted Petitioner’s motion to compel deposition of Respondent’s person most qualified (“PMQ”) concerning Respondent’s Department of Transportation’s installation, interpretation, and enforcement of truck-weight restriction signage on Dominguez Avenue and Sanford Avenue.

On July 2, 2026, Petitioner filed its opening brief. On August 5, 2026, Respondent filed its opposition. On August 28, 2026, Petitioner filed its reply.

III.           Standard of Review

1.               Traditional Mandamus

A petition for traditional mandamus, governed by Code of Civil Procedure (“CCP”) section 1085, is appropriate in all actions “to compel the performance of an act which the law specially enjoins as a duty resulting from an office, trust, or station . . . .” (CCP § 1085; see Rodriguez v. Solis (1991) 1 Cal.App.4th 495, 501-02.) Mandamus will lie when (1) there is no plain, speedy, and adequate alternative remedy, (2) the respondent has a duty to perform, and (3) the petitioner has a clear and beneficial right to performance. (Pomona Police Officers’ Assn. v. City of Pomona (1997) 58 Cal.App.4th 578, 583-84 [internal citations omitted].)

“Generally, mandamus is available to compel a public agency’s performance or to correct an agency’s abuse of discretion when the action being compelled or corrected is ministerial…” (AIDS Healthcare Foundation v. Los Angeles County Dept. of Public Health (2011) 197 Cal.App.4th 693, 700.) Whether a statute imposes a ministerial duty for which mandamus is available, or a mere obligation to perform a discretionary function, is a question of statutory interpretation. (Id. at 701.)

In the absence of a ministerial duty, traditional mandamus relief is unavailable unless the petitioner can demonstrate an abuse of that discretion. (See CV Amalgamated LLC v. City of Chula Vista (2022) 82 Cal.App.5th 265, 278-79.) An agency decision is an abuse of discretion only if it is “arbitrary, capricious, entirely lacking in evidentiary support, unlawful, or procedurally unfair.” (Kahn v. Los Angeles City Employees’ Retirement System (2010) 187 Cal.App.4th 98, 106.) In applying this deferential test, a court “must ensure that an agency has adequately considered all relevant factors, and has demonstrated a rational connection between those factors, the choice made, and the purposes of the enabling statute.” (Western States Petroleum Assn v. Superior Court (1995) 9 Cal.4th 559, 577.) In making this determination, the court may not substitute its judgment for that of the agency, whose decision must be upheld if reasonable minds may disagree as to its wisdom. (Manjares v. Newton (1966) 64 Cal.2d 365, 371.) In other words, traditional mandamus is only available when an agency’s discretion can be exercised only in one way. (Hurtado v. Superior Court (1974) 11 Cal.3d 574, 579.)

In a petition for writ of mandate brought under CCP section 1085, “the petitioner bears the burden of pleading and proving the facts on which the claim for relief is based.” (California Correctional Peace Officers Assn. v. State Personnel Bd. (1995) 10 Cal.4th 1133, 1153.)

2.               Administrative Mandamus

CCP section 1094.5 is the administrative mandamus provision that structures the procedure for judicial review of adjudicatory decisions rendered by administrative agencies. (Topanga Ass’n for a Scenic Community v. County of Los Angeles (1974) 11 Cal.3d 506, 514-15 [“Topanga”]).) CCP section 1094.5 does not specify which cases are subject to independent review, leaving that issue to the courts. (Fukuda v. City of Angels (1999) 20 Cal.4th 805, 811 [“Fukuda”]). In cases reviewing decisions that affect a vested, fundamental right, the trial court exercises independent judgment on the evidence. (Bixby v. Pierno (1971) 4 Cal.3d 130, 143 [“Bixby”]; see CCP § 1094.5(c).)

“Substantial evidence” is relevant evidence that a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion (California Youth Authority v. State Personnel Board (2002) 104 Cal.App.4th 575, 585 [“California Youth Authority”]) or evidence of ponderable legal significance, which is reasonable in nature, credible and of solid value (Mohilef v. Janovici (1996) 51 Cal.App.4th 267, 305, n. 28). Substantial evidence can be the opinion of a single expert (Coastal Southwest Dev. Corp. v. Coastal Zone Conservation Comm’n (1976) 55 Cal.App.3d 525, 532), or opinions in a staff report (Griffin Dev. Co. v. City of Oxnard (1985) 39 Cal.3d 256, 261).

The court considers all evidence in the administrative record, including evidence that detracts from evidence supporting the agency’s decision. (California Youth Authority, supra, 104 Cal.App.4th at p. 585.) The court must uphold the decision unless it concludes, based on the evidence before the agency, a reasonable person could not reach the conclusion reached by the administrative agency.¿(Harris v. City of Costa Mesa¿(1994) 25 Cal.App.4th 963, 969.) Where “reasonable persons may differ,” the courts will not disturb the judgment of the administrative agency. (Breakzone Billiards v. City of Torrance (2000) 81 Cal.App.4th 1205, 1246.) The court does not weigh evidence or decide who has the better argument and must resolve reasonable doubts in favor of the findings and decision. (Topanga, supra, 11 Cal.3d at p. 514.)

An agency is presumed to have regularly performed its official duties (Evid. Code § 664), and the petitioner therefore has the burden of proof on mandamus. (Steele v. Los Angeles County Civil Service Commission (1958) 166 Cal.App.2d 129, 137.) “[T]he burden of proof falls upon the party attacking the administrative decision to demonstrate wherein the proceedings were unfair, in excess of jurisdiction or showed prejudicial abuse of discretion.” (Alford v. Pierno (1972) 27 Cal.App.3d 682, 691 [“Alford”].)

IV.           Analysis

1.               Traditional Mandamus and Declaratory Relief

Petitioner seeks a writ of traditional mandamus preventing Respondent from enforcing weight restrictions on Dominguez Avenue to the extent such restrictions would, as applied, bar lawful truck access to the Property; a declaration that the posted restriction signs do not prohibit truck access to the Property; or a declaration that the posted restriction signs are illegal and unenforceable. Respondent contends that traditional mandamus and declaratory relief are not available because administrative mandamus is the exclusive remedy for review of the HAPC decision. In reply, Petitioner argues its traditional mandamus claims do not seek review of the HAPC decision but instead challenge the separate, antecedent City action of the City’s Department of Transportation (“LADOT”) posting and enforcement of truck weight restriction signage.

As the Court explained in its March 19, 2026 decision on Petitioner’s motion to compel deposition, the traditional mandamus claim is an independent legal claim challenging Respondent’s compliance with LAMC section 80.36.1(b). (March 19, 2026 Final Decision at p. 7, incorporated by reference into March 19, 2026 Order at p. 1.) Specifically, Respondent posted restriction signage, which, in conjunction with Vehicle Code section 21461, subsection (a) and LAMC section 80.36.1, operates as a de facto prohibition on truck access to the Property, because trucks are prohibited on all possible access routes. (See AR 51 [Zoning Administrator: “thus there are no alternate routes available”].) These claims therefore challenge LADOT’s installation and enforcement of the signage rather than HAPC’s appellate determination.

The traditional mandamus and declaratory relief claims are also ripe. Respondent frames the issue as requiring the Court either to conclude that Respondent has made no decision regarding truck access to the Property, or to find that any such determination must be attributed to the HAPC, thereby rendering the matter one of administrative mandamus. However, Respondent’s position does not account for the effect of its own installation and maintenance of the posted signs. Those signs, when read together with Vehicle Code section 21461, subdivision (a), and LAMC section 80.36.1, operate to restrict truck access to the Property because trucks are prohibited on all possible access routes. (See AR 51 [Zoning Administrator: “thus there are no alternate routes available”].) This constitutes sufficient governmental action to create a present and concrete controversy, and the claims are therefore ripe. (See California Water & Tel. Co. v. Los Angeles County (1967) 253 Cal.App.3d 16, 26 [declaratory relief was ripe because utilities needed to know whether an ordinance was valid in order to plan facilities and equipment].)

Accordingly, the Court finds that the traditional mandamus and declaratory relief claims are independent of the administrative mandamus claim and ripe for adjudication.

Arguably, the traditional mandamus and declaratory relief claims are moot, as Respondent claims, “LADOT has since clarified that the weight restriction signs on Dominguez Avenue near the Property do not prohibit truck and trailer access to the Property if access via Dominguez Avenue is the sole access to the Property.” (Op. at 13, citing Hinks Decl., Ex. A at p. 48-49.) However, Respondent does not affirmatively concede this position in its filings, and the cited PMQ testimony does not constitute an official act, policy, or written determination by Respondent. The Court is not informed of any official determination rescinding or modifying the signage. The Court therefore cannot conclude that no remaining controversy exists.

For these reasons, traditional mandamus and declaratory relief are available.

While Respondent does not affirmatively concede the question, Respondent fails to raise any arguments about the traditional mandamus and declaratory relief claims except those stated above as to availability of the remedy. Having rejected Respondent’s exclusivity and ripeness arguments, the Court briefly addresses the merits.

LAMC section 80.36.1 provides that Respondent may restrict vehicles over 6,000 pounds from traveling on certain streets or portions of streets. (LAMC § 80.36.1(a), (b).) Respondent may impose such restrictions only on streets listed in subsection (d) (LAMC § 80.36.1(a)) or where Respondent makes certain factual determination regarding traffic on the restricted street (LAMC § 80.36.1(b)). In the latter case, alternate routes must be available. (LAMC § 80.36.1(b).) Here, Dominguez Avenue is not a street listed in subsection (d) (LAMC § 80.36.1(d)), and it is undisputed that no alternate routes are available. (E.g. Hinks Decl., Ex. A at p. 48-49; AR 51.) Because the statutory precondition for posting weight restriction signage under subsection (b) is absent, the posted signs on Dominguez Avenue are unenforceable as to truck access to the Property.

Petitioner seeks various forms of relief based on its position that LAMC section 80.36.1(b) does not prohibit trucks from accessing the Property via Dominguez Avenue. These requests include a declaration and a writ finding the signage unlawful and unenforceable, as well as an order directing Respondent to remove the signs. Although Respondent did not address the merits of these requests, the factual record presented by both parties focuses solely on the Property and the question of access to it. On this record, the Court cannot determine whether the posted signs are categorically unauthorized or must be removed in all circumstances. Petitioner bears the burden to establish entitlement to traditional mandamus relief. (California Correctional Peace Officers Assn. v. State Personnel Bd., supra, 10 Cal.4th at 1153.) Petitioner likewise bears the burden to demonstrate an actual controversy warranting declaratory relief, and the record shows only a dispute regarding whether trucks may lawfully access the Property using Dominguez Avenue. (Fox Paine & Company, LLC v. Twin City Fire Insurance Company (2026) 20 Cal.5th 188, 832.) The Court therefore denies Petitioner’s broader request for removal of the signage.

Accordingly, Petitioner is entitled to declaratory relief stating that trucks may access the Property by Dominguez Avenue unless and until alternate routes become available, and a permanent injunction preventing enforcement of truck restrictions on Dominguez Avenue against trucks accessing the Property.

2.               Administrative Mandamus

Petitioner contends that HAPC abused its discretion in granting Appellant’s appeal and revoking the Permit. Specifically, Petitioner argues HAPC erred by relying on LAMC § 80.36.1, on CUGU, and on the [Q] condition. Petitioner further alleges that the Permit was ministerial and thus HAPC abused its discretion by relying on facts outside the ministerial grounds. Finally, Petitioner alleges the findings do not support the conclusion because HAPC failed to bridge the analytic gap.

In opposition, Respondent argues HAPC’s decision was supported under CUGU, based on traffic conditions at the Property and the residential nature of Dominguez Avenue, and under the [Q] condition. Respondent, however, does not address Petitioner’s central contention that issuance of the Permit was ministerial and therefore subject only to objective criteria based in the Code.

LAMC section 91.106.4.1 provides that “[w]hen the department determines that the information on the application and plans is in conformance with this Code and other relevant codes and ordinances, the department shall issue a permit upon receipt of the total fees.” (LAMC § 91.106.4.1.) This section establishes a ministerial duty once LADBS determines that the application conforms to applicable standards. The administrative appeal at issue challenges LADBS’ underlying conformance determination. (See LAMC § 13B.10.2.) Even so, the plain text of LAMC section 91.106.4.1 makes clear that LADBS may deny a permit only based on nonconformance with relevant codes or ordinances.

Accordingly, while HAPC had discretion to resolve factual disputes in evaluating the appeal, it did not have discretion to uphold the appeal and revoke the Permit on grounds unrelated to whether the application and plans conformed to the governing codes and ordinances.

Respondent identifies various considerations allegedly supporting HAPC’s decision, for example, that uses listed in LAMC section 13.18E.2, subdivision (d) “could cause cumulative health effects” (Opp. at 11); that open-ended permits “would be contrary to intent of CUGU” (Opp. at 11); that “Wilmington is a disadvantaged community and is worthy of protection” (Opp. at 11); that the community is “overburdened with open storage facilities” (Opp. at 13); and that trucks “will more than likely be idling on the roads” (Opp. at 13). Neither HAPC’s Letter of Determination nor Respondent’s briefing, however, explains how any of these considerations demonstrate actual nonconformance in the application or plans. With limited exceptions discussed below, [1] Respondent’s arguments therefore do not establish a permissible basis for revoking the Permit.

While the Court reviews administrative agency determinations for abuse of discretion, it addresses questions of law de novo. (Sieg v. Fogt (2020) 55 Cal.App.5th 77, 88 [“the court . . . reviews questions of law de novo, giving respectful consideration to an agency’s decision on legal questions to the extent its reasoning is persuasive.”].)

Respondent frames certain legal conclusions as factual findings, presumably to avoid this less deferential de novo standard of review. For example, Respondent contends that “[t]he HAPC was presented with evidence that there were three signs on the right, northbound side of Dominguez Avenue that prohibited vehicles over 6,000 lbs. from accessing Dominguez Avenue.” (Opp. at 12.) Based on this, Respondent asserts that HAPC found a “conflict of policies” with LAMC section 80.36.1. (Opp. at 12; see AR 65.) This is more properly understood as a legal conclusion that the Permit conflicts with LAMC section 80.36.1, a conclusion reviewed de novo. As discussed above, LAMC section 80.36.1 does not prohibit trucks from accessing the Property using Dominguez Avenue.

Similarly, Respondent argues, “HAPC was presented with evidence that there was conflicting language in the [Q] condition applicable to the Project site.” (Opp. at 14.) Again, this presents a legal question. HAPC concluded that the [Q] condition “in essence allow[s] the storage of cargo containers while intending otherwise.” (Opp. at 14, citing AR at 116-19, 132-35.) This argument is unpersuasive.

The [Q] condition provides, in part, that “[n]o cargo containers may be kept or stored on the site.” It then expressly states:

However, this condition shall not prohibit the loading and/or parking of trucks and truck trailers, including containers on wheels, provided that no trailers or containers may be ‘stacked’ vertically at any time, and that any truck loading or parking facility shall maintain a landscaped buffer at least 10 feet wide that includes trees and/or shrubs, designed to visually screen the use, facing any residential zone that is adjacent, or directly across a street or alley.

(AR 40-41.)

It is fundamental that “each word and phrase in the statute should be interpreted to give meaning to every word and phrase in the statute to accomplish a result consistent with the legislative purpose.” (Segura v. Superior Court (2025) 113 Cal.App.5th 1242, 1252 [internal quotation marks omitted, emphasis in original].) “When the statutory language is clear and unambiguous, there usually is no need for further construction and courts adopt the plain (i.e., literal) meaning of that language.” (Cisneros v. Department of Motor Vehicles (2024) 103 Cal.App.5th 381, 407.)

Here, the condition is not ambiguous. By attempting “to harmonize” the [Q] condition with CUGU, HAPC effectively disregarded the express exception permitting trucks, trailers, and containers on wheels. The statute does not permit excising this exception. While HAPC questioned the motivation behind the exception (AR 120), this provides no basis for discounting its plain text.

To summarize: HAPC could consider evidence and make factual findings, but it could uphold the appeal and revoke the Permit only if it identified nonconformance with applicable codes or ordinances. (LAMC § 91.106.4.1.) HAPC’s generalized policy findings do not constitute findings of nonconformance. The limited exceptions are discussed as follows. (See AR 129-36.) First, HAPC found a conflict with LAMC section 80.36.1. (AR 131.) This conclusion was legally incorrect, and Respondent does not defend it. Additionally, HAPC has no authority to enforce traffic ordinances. Second, HAPC found the Permit conflicted with the [Q] condition, but this finding relied on disregarding the explicit exception rather than identifying nonconformance. (AR 116-19, 129-36.)

Because HAPC identified no actual nonconformance with relevant codes or ordinances, the findings do not support Respondent’s decision.

Accordingly, Petitioner has met its burden to show that Respondent abused its discretion.

V.              Conclusion

HAPC’s findings do not support its conclusion. The petition for traditional and administrative mandamus is GRANTED. A writ shall issue directing Respondent to vacate HAPC’s decision and reinstate Petitioner’s Permit and associated approvals and further declare that trucks may lawfully access the Property via Dominguez Avenue unless and until alternate routes become available. Respondent is enjoined from enforcing weight restriction signage on Dominguez Avenue to prevent such access to the Property.

Pursuant to Local Rule 3.231(n), petitioner JE Real Estate Holding Company, LLC shall prepare, serve, and ultimately file a proposed judgment.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: MOTION FOR ATTORNEY FEES

 

Real party Marius Denisiu (“RPI”) moves for attorney fees and costs[1] in the amount of $16,885.

The motion is granted only as to costs.

I.                Background

On December 4, 2025, petitioners Patricia Stephenson (“Stephenson”) and Executive Network Enterprises, Inc. (“ENE”) (collectively, “Petitioners”) filed their petition against respondent California Department of Industrial Relations, Division of Labor Standards Enforcement (“DLSE” or “Respondent”), naming RPI as a real party. A proof of service filed December 18, 2025 shows Petitioners served the petition on Respondent by electronic service on December 15, 2025. On January 16, 2026, RPI filed his Answer to the petition.

The petition challenges the DLSE’s 2018 Order, Decision, and Award (“ODA”), alleging Petitioners were never properly served, that DLSE mailed all notices to an incorrect address, misidentified the employer, and failed to perform mandatory duties under Labor Code section 98. Petitioners assert these defects deprived DLSE of jurisdiction and violated their due process rights, rendering the ODA void. The petition also seeks to invalidate the later 2019 superior court judgment enforcing the ODA, alleging it too is void due to lack of service, misidentification, and extrinsic fraud. Petitioners seek an order directing DLSE to vacate the ODA and conduct a properly noticed hearing, or alternatively relief under Code of Civil Procedure section 473(d) declaring the ODA and resulting judgment void.

On March 26, 2026, the Court denied Stephenson’s [2] ex parte application for a temporary restraining order and order to show cause re: preliminary injunction.

On April 30, 2026, the Court heard Respondent’s demurrer, joined by RPI, to Petitioners’ petition. The Court continued the demurrer for supplemental briefing on a single issue only: whether an alter ego finding means that Stephenson is bound by the merits determination against ENE and was obligated to appeal. The Court ordered five-page cross-briefs, due May 29, 2026.

On May 28, 2026, RPI filed his supplemental brief. On May 29, 2026, Stephenson and Respondent filed their supplemental briefs. On June 2, 2026, Stephenson filed an unauthorized 12-page response brief and request for judicial notice.

On June 9, 2026, the Court heard the demurrer and took the matter under submission. Later the same day, the Court sustained the demurrer without leave to amend.

On June 24, 2026, the Court entered Judgment of Dismissal.

On July 22, 2026, RPI filed the instant motion for attorney fees.

On August 25, 2026, the Court denied Stephenson’s motion for reconsideration, clarification, stay of entry of judgment, and leave to amend.

On September 1, 2026, Stephenson filed her opposition to the instant motion. On September 8, 2026, RPI filed his reply.

II.             Discussion

RPI seeks an award of $16,885, composed of $16,450 in attorney fees and $435 in costs. (Mot. at 5.) RPI claims entitlement of attorney fees under Labor Code section 98.2, subdivision (k) and Civil Code section 685.070, subdivision (a).

RPI fails to show a statutory basis for a fee award.

Labor Code section 98.2, subdivision (k) authorizes attorney fees only in a specific type of proceeding: a judgment creditor’s efforts to enforce a judgment issued in a superior court action brought under section 98.2. Section 98.2 establishes the exclusive mechanism by which a party may seek superior court review of a Labor Commissioner’s order, decision, or award; namely, by filing an appeal within ten days, triggering a de novo hearing in the superior court. (Lab. Code § 98.2(a).) Subdivision (k) then provides that, after that type of proceeding has resulted in a judgment, the judgment creditor may recover attorney fees incurred in enforcing that judgment.

This case is not an appeal under Labor Code section 98.2. Petitioners did not appeal the DLSE decision within the statutory period, and no section 98.2 de novo proceeding ever occurred in this action. Rather, Petitioners filed a separate writ petition years later, seeking to collaterally attack the administrative order and subsequent judgment entered in the 2019 enforcement case. Because this action is not a section 98.2 appeal and does not involve enforcement of a judgment issued in such an appeal, section 98.2, subdivision (k) does not apply.

Nor does Civil Code section 685.070 assist RPI. Section 685.070 authorizes a judgment creditor to recover enumerated costs incurred in enforcing a judgment. This writ proceeding was not initiated to enforce any judgment; it is a collateral challenge to administrative and judicial determinations already made in the earlier enforcement action. RPI cites no authority, and the Court is aware of none, extending section 685.070 to allow recovery of attorney fees for defending against a collateral attack on a judgment.

Because neither statute reaches this type of proceeding, RPI has identified no basis for an attorney fee award.

The memorandum of costs, however, supports an award of $435 in costs. Petitioners did not file a motion to strike or tax costs, and the time to do so has passed.

III.           Conclusion

RPI has not shown any statutory basis for an attorney fee award. The motion for fees is therefore DENIED. However, the Court awards costs to the moving party in the amount of $435.
