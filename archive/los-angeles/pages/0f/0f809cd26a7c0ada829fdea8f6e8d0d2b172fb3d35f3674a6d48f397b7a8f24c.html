BRYANNA COSTACHE, a minor, by and through her Guardian ad Litem, ANDREEA N. OFTEZ v. CHA HOLLYWOOD MEDICAL CENTER, L.P., et al. [25STCV18482]

 

MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT by DEFENDANT CHILDREN’S HOSPITAL LOS ANGELES (DOE 1)

 

TIMELINE: Complaint alleges medical malpractice

 

4/18/2023: Plaintiff Bryanna Costache (“Plaintiff”), a minor, is born via crash Cesarean section (“C-section”) at Defendant CHA Hollywood Medical Center, L.P., dba CHA Hollywood Presbyterian Medical Center (“CHA”). (Suttner Decl., ¶ 4(a).) Plaintiff’s biological mother, Andreea N. Oftez (“Oftez”), is Plaintiff’s guardian ad litem in the instant lawsuit. (Ibid.)

 

Plaintiff alleges that the medical staff at CHA negligently provided obstetric triage to Oftez, including by failing to offer and perform a timely C-section delivery despite ongoing fetal intolerance to labor, thereby causing injuries to Plaintiff, including brain injury and cerebral palsy. (Compl., ¶ 14.) Shortly after her delivery, Plaintiff was transferred to the neonatal intensive care unit (“NICU”) team at CHA, where she presented with heart and respiratory issues and was diagnosed with severe metabolic acidosis and anemia. (Suttner Decl., ¶¶ 4(c)-(d).)

 

Due to her critical condition, Plaintiff required an additional transfer from CHA to Defendant Children’s Hospital Los Angeles (“CHLA”) for further evaluation and treatment, including therapeutic hypothermia. (Id. ¶ 4(f).) Plaintiff’s father gave written consent for the transfer. (Id. ¶ 4(g).) The CHLA transport team included Dr. Jared Schiff (an employee of Defendant Children’s Hospital Los Angeles Medical Group) and non-physician CHLA employees, Michele Pisarsksy, R.N., Syd Nessary, R.T., and Luis Romero, R.T. (Id. ¶ 4(j).) The CHLA transport team was first contacted at 2:50am. (Id. ¶ 4(h).) Plaintiff was accepted by the CHLA transport team at 3:09am. (Id. ¶ 4(i).) The CHLA transport team left CHLA via ambulance at 3:25am and arrived at Plaintiff’s bedside at 3:35am. (Id. ¶¶ 4(j)-(k).)

 

Dr. Schiff examined Plaintiff at 3:35am, Plaintiff’s vital signs were measured at 3:43am and 3:50am, and the CHLA transport team left with Plaintiff for CHLA at 3:55am. (Suttner Decl., ¶¶ 4(l), (m), (n), (o).) The CHLA transport team arrived at CHLA’s ambulance bay at 4:02am. (Id. ¶ 4(q).) At 4:06am, CHLA’s NICU team assumed care of Plaintiff. (Id. ¶ 4(r).) At CHLA, Plaintiff was diagnosed with hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy, respiratory failure, suspected sepsis and anemia, and active cooling was initiated at 4:15am. (Id. ¶ 4(s).) An emergency blood transfusion was ordered at 4:17am and administered at 4:36am. (Id. ¶ 4(t).) Plaintiff was then followed by a number of CHLA medical specialists where she remained until her discharge on 6/29/2023. (Id. ¶ 4(u).)

 

6/25/2025: Plaintiff files the Complaint, alleging a single cause of action for negligence (medical malpractice).

 

7/16/2025: Plaintiff files an Amendment to the Complaint, identifying CHLA as Doe 1. As to CHLA, Plaintiff only contends that the CHLA transport team who transferred Plaintiff from CHA to CHLA was negligent.

 

5/4/2026: CHLA files this Motion for Summary Judgment. On 7/14/2026, Plaintiff files a Notice of Non-Opposition to CHLA’s Motion.

 

TENTATIVE RULING: MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT OF DEFENDANT CHILDREN’S HOSPITAL LOS ANGELES is GRANTED.

 

MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT

 

Defendant CHLA moves for summary judgment on Plaintiff’s medical malpractice claim on the grounds that CHLA’s care and treatment of Plaintiff complied with the standard of care at all times, and thus, CHLA cannot be liable to Plaintiff as a matter of law. (Mot., at p. 5.) Plaintiff does not oppose the motion. (Opp., at p. 1.)

 

The Court finds that CHLA has overcome its initial moving burden, and based on Plaintiff’s non-opposition, CHLA is entitled to summary judgment. It is well-established that the elements of a claim for professional negligence, including medical malpractice, are the same basic elements as a claim for general negligence: duty, breach, causation, and damages. (Banerian v. O'Malley (1974) 42 Cal.App.3d 604, 610–612; Flowers v. Torrance Memorial Hospital Medical Center (1994) 8 Cal.4th 992, 997–998.) “The first element, standard of care, is the key issue in a malpractice action and can only be proved by expert testimony, unless the circumstances are such that the required conduct is within the layperson's common knowledge.” (Lattimore v. Dickey (2015) 239 Cal.App.4th 959, 968 (Lattimore); accord Jambazian v. Borden (1994) 25 Cal.App.4th 836, 843 (Jambazian).) In those cases where a medical specialist is alleged to have acted negligently, the “specialist must possess and use the learning, care and skill normally possessed and exercised by practitioners of that specialty under the same or similar circumstances.” (Lattimore, supra, 239 Cal.App.4th at p. 968.)

 

Here, there is no question that the CHLA-employed members of the CHLA transport team (Michele Pisarsksy, R.N., Syd Nessary, R.T. and Luis Romero, R.T.)[1] had a duty to use the skill, prudence, and diligence commonly possessed and exercised by R.N.s and R.T.s in the neonatal field. “[A] nurse's conduct must not be measured by the standard of care required of a physician or surgeon, but by that of other nurses in the same or similar locality and under similar circumstances.” (Lattimore, supra, 239 Cal.App.4th at p. 969.) “Just as with physicians and surgeons, however, ‘expert opinion testimony is required to prove that a defendant nurse did not meet the standard of care and therefore was negligent, “except in cases where the negligence is obvious to [laypersons].’’” (Ibid.)

 

The practice of neonatal medicine is not within the common knowledge of laypersons, and thus, expert testimony is necessary here to prove whether the CHLA transport team breached the applicable standard of care. (Willard v. Hagemeister (1981) 121 Cal.App.3d 406, 411 [“Expert evidence in a malpractice suit is conclusive as to the proof of the prevailing standard of skill and learning…”].) CHLA submits the expert testimony of Dr. Denise Suttner, a physician board-certified in neonatology, who opines on the standard of care required of physicians and nursing personnel treating newborns like Plaintiff. Dr. Suttner declares that the care and treatment provided to Plaintiff by the nursing and non-physician members of the CHLA transport team was appropriate and complied with the standard of care in all respects. (Suttner Decl., ¶ 5.) Dr. Suttner further explains that the CHLA transport team timely responded to CHA after a request for transfer and for the entire duration that Plaintiff was in the CHLA transport team’s care, the nursing and non-physician personnel carried out their duties and responsibilities in a manner consistent with the standard of care, including patient monitoring, nursing, and respiratory care. (Id. ¶ 6.)

 

Based on the testimony of Dr. Suttner, the Court finds that CHLA has overcome its moving burden of demonstrating that Plaintiff cannot establish the prima facie elements of her professional negligence. As Plaintiff does not oppose this motion, she fails to overcome her responsive burden of raising a triable issue of material fact. Therefore, CHLA’s motion for summary judgment is GRANTED.

TENTATIVE RULING: MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT OF DEFENDANT FCA US, LLC is GRANTED.

 

I.        MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT

 

A.           1st Cause of Action: Violation of Civ. Code § 1793.2, subd. (d) — GRANTED

 

Plaintiff fails to raise a triable dispute of material fact with respect to her first cause of action. The California Supreme Court decision in¿Rodriguez v. FCA US LLC¿(2024) 17 Cal.5th 189¿(Rodriguez) is controlling here.¿In¿Rodriguez, the plaintiffs¿purchased¿a vehicle from a used car dealer.¿(Id. at p. 196.)¿The used vehicle came with¿an unexpired five-year/100,000-mile powertrain warranty from the manufacturer, FCA, that remained in effect at the time of the purchase.¿(Ibid.) While the warranty was in effect, the plaintiffs experienced problems with the vehicle’s engine. (Ibid.) The plaintiffs filed a lawsuit against FCA for violating the¿SBA’s refund-or-replace provision. (Ibid.) The trial court granted summary judgment¿in FCA’s favor on the grounds that the¿SBA’s refund-or-replace remedy only applies to a “new motor vehicle” within the meaning of the¿SBA. (Ibid.)¿The higher courts affirmed.¿ 

 

As the California Supreme Court explained, Civil Code § 1793.22, subd. (e)(2) “defines¿‘new motor vehicle’¿to include a new vehicle¿‘bought or used primarily for personal’¿purposes as well as¿‘a dealer-owned vehicle and a ‘demonstrator’ or other motor vehicle¿sold with a manufacturer's new car warranty.’” (Rodriguez, supra, 17 Cal.5th at p. 195, italics added.)¿“This text makes clear that certain used cars —¿‘a dealer-owned vehicle and a ‘demonstrator’ or other motor vehicle sold with a manufacturer's new car warranty’¿— qualify as¿‘new motor vehicles’¿for purposes of the statute.”¿(Id. at p. 198.)¿“As the Court of Appeal explained,¿‘What makes these vehicles unique is that even though they aren't technically new, manufacturers (or their dealer-representatives) treat them as such upon sale¿by providing the same type of manufacturer's warranty that accompany new cars.’” (Id., italics added.) “In other words, they are vehicles for¿which a new car warranty¿‘was¿issued with¿the sale.’” (Ibid.)¿Thus, the¿Court confirmed that “a motor vehicle purchased with an unexpired manufacturer's new car warranty does not qualify as a¿‘motor vehicle sold with a manufacturer's new car warranty’¿under section 1793.22, subdivision (e)(2)’s definition of¿‘new motor vehicle’¿unless the new car warranty was issued with the sale.” (Id. at p. 196.)

 

Here, FCA submits a copy of the Sale Contract governing the transaction between Plaintiff and BBA. (Tremonti Decl., ¶ 5, Exh. 2.) Plaintiff does not dispute that this contract is a true and correct copy of the agreement governing the sale of her Vehicle. The Sale Contract expressly indicates that the Vehicle sold to Plaintiff was “used.” (Ibid.) It is further undisputed that the Vehicle was originally purchased from another dealership in June 2018 and was used as an independent rental vehicle before Plaintiff ultimately purchased the Vehicle. (Sheridan Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. 3.) Plaintiff’s contention that the Vehicle was given a Certified Pre-Owned (“CPO”) status does not alter the fact that the Vehicle was used. (Williams Decl., ¶¶ 3-4.)

 

Further, the Rodriguez decision demonstrates that the critical distinguishing fact for determining whether Plaintiff’s claims fall under the scope of Civ. Code § 1793.2 is whether the warranty issued by FCA in connection with her Vehicle purchase was a “manufacturer’s new vehicle warranty” first issued at the time of the sale as opposed to a preexisting, unexpired vehicle warranty issued during a prior sale. (Rodriguez, supra, 17 Cal.5th at p. 196, italics added.) The decision in Kiluk v. Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC (2019) 43 Cal.App.5th 334 (Kiluk) is consistent with the Rodriguez reasoning. Kiluk involved the sale of a CPO vehicle which was accompanied by an additional used vehicle warranty issued by the defendant manufacturer in connection with the sale. (Id. at p. 336.) A jury found the manufacturer was liable under the SBA for breach of express and implied warranties. (Ibid.) In other words, Kiluk demonstrates that a vehicle’s CPO status does not preclude the application of the SBA where a new or additional vehicle warranty is issued by the manufacturer in connection with the CPO vehicle’s purchase. Thus, although Plaintiff cannot show that she purchased the Vehicle new, the central issue here is whether FCA first issued an additional “new vehicle warranty” in connection with her purchase from BBA.

 

In this case, Plaintiff alleges that she “entered into a warranty contract” with FCA on July 6, 2019. (FAC, ¶ 7.) A copy of the written warranty is attached as Exhibit A to the FAC. (Id., Exh. A.) The written warranty provides a Basic Limited Warranty to any purchaser of the Vehicle, which “lasts for 36 months from the date it begins or for 36,000 miles on the odometer, whichever occurs first” (subject to certain exceptions, not applicable here). (Id. at pp. 5-6.) The written warranty also includes a Powertrain Limited Warranty to any purchaser of the Vehicle, which “lasts for up to five years or 60,000 miles on the odometer, whichever occurs first.” (Id. at p. 9.) Critically, there is no indication on the face of the warranty that it was a new CPO warranty first issued in connection with Plaintiff’s purchase of the Vehicle, or that the warranty was exclusively intended to provide coverage for a CPO vehicle (as opposed to a new vehicle). (Ibid.) Indeed, the warranty itself characterizes the Powertrain Limited Warranty as a “new vehicle limited warranty” and references the maintenance and operation of “your new vehicle.” (Id. at pp. 10, 28.) Further, Plaintiff does not allege that the warranty was newly issued to her in connection with the sale, as opposed to merely receiving the pre-existing warranty contract from the previous owner. (See FAC, generally.) Based on Plaintiff’s allegations concerning the warranty and the specific language of the written warranty, FCA infers that Plaintiff only received the remaining portions of the Vehicle’s original manufacturer’s warranties, which were issued on the date of the original new vehicle purchase in June 2018. (Tremonti Decl., ¶ 4, Exh. 1 [FAC], Exh. A; see Sheridan Decl., ¶ 6, Exh. 3 [reflecting the original purchase on June 13, 2018].) The Court finds that this evidence is sufficient to overcome FCA’s moving burden. Thus, the burden shifts to Plaintiff to raise a triable issue of material fact pertaining to whether FCA issued an additional “manufacturer’s new vehicle warranty” in connection with Plaintiff’s purchase.

 

Plaintiff fails to overcome this burden, as she has not produced sufficient evidence that a newly issued CPO warranty exists. First, Plaintiff declares that she “understood the Vehicle to be backed by manufacturer warranty protection specific to its [CPO] status” and that “the Vehicle’s [CPO] warranty coverage was an important part of [her] decision to purchase it.” (Williams Decl., ¶ 4.) However, Plaintiff fails to produce any written warranty document reflecting that she received an updated or additional warranty first issued in connection with Plaintiff’s purchase. Plaintiff’s mere speculation or belief that she received a warranty specific to the Vehicle’s CPO status is insufficient to raise a triable factual dispute where she offers no documentary evidence to demonstrate the terms of the unidentified additional warranty contract. Second, Plaintiff submits a copy of FCA’s New Customer Assistance Inquiry Record (CAIR), which contains the following service note in the “case comments” dated 10/17/2024: “Certified pre-owned limited warranty 7 years/100,000 miles.” (Lister Decl. ¶ 4, Exh. 2, at p. 1058.) Plaintiff characterizes this service note at admission that the Vehicle was sold as a CPO vehicle but fails to conclusively demonstrate how this isolated service comment confirms the existence of an additional “new vehicle warranty” first issued in connection with the sale, let alone its essential terms. (Lister Decl. ¶ 4.) Tellingly, Plaintiff neither offers any explanation as to why she has not produced a copy of her purported CPO-specific warranty, nor does Plaintiff request additional time for discovery of such a document. At minimum, Plaintiff’s evidence only further confirms that the written warranty attached to the face of the FAC is not a “new vehicle warranty” first issued at the time of the sale of the Vehicle to Plaintiff.

 

Even assuming that a hypothetical CPO warranty (which is not in evidence) did exist, Plaintiff fails to offer any legal authority for her position that the mere existence of a CPO warranty (devoid of any other factual context) is sufficient to bring a used Vehicle within the scope of the relevant SBA provisions. By contrast, FCA relies on various federal authorities (applying California law) which suggest that a CPO vehicle warranty does not constitute a “new vehicle warranty” for purposes of the SBA. (Rep., at p. 3.) While these authorities are not binding, they are persuasive in that they provide a framework for interpreting Rodriguez and the application of California’s SBA. (See e.g. Dubrosky v. American Honda Motor Co., Inc. (C.D. Cal., Aug. 13, 2025, No. 2:24-CV-09153-HDV-AS) 2025 WL 2451031, at *9 [“[the plaintiff] does not allege that Honda issued him a new car warranty with the sale of his certified pre-owned vehicle. Plaintiffs fail to adduce any authority indicating that a certified pre-owned warranty constitutes “a new car warranty[.]”].)

 

Accordingly, the Court concludes that Plaintiff fails to overcome her responsive burden of raising a triable factual dispute as to whether the Vehicle constitutes a “new motor vehicle” under the meaning of the SBA.

 

B.            2nd and 3rd Causes of Action: Violation of Civ. Code § 1793.2, subds. (b) and (a)(3) and Prayer for Civil Penalties — GRANTED

 

Plaintiff also fails to raise a triable dispute of material fact with respect to her second and third causes of action. FCA argues that the above analysis under the Rodriguez precedent also extends to Plaintiff’s claim under Civ. Code § 1793.2, subd. (b). (Mot., at p. 9.) The same logic can be extended to Plaintiff’s claim under Civ. Code § 1793.2, subd. (a)(3). Specifically, while only Civ. Code, § 1793.2, subd. (d)(2) uses the specific term “new motor vehicle,” the entire section broadly applies to “consumer goods” and “buyer[s]” as defined in Civ. Code § 1791. “Consumer goods” is defined in relevant part as “any new product or part thereof that is used, bought, or leased for use primarily for personal, family, or household purposes, except for clothing and consumables.” (Civ. Code, § 1791, subd. (a), italics added.) “Buyer” is defined in relevant part as “any individual who buys consumer goods from a person engaged in the business of manufacturing, distributing, or selling consumer goods at retail.” (Civ. Code, § 1791, subd. (b), italics added.) Based on these definitions, the Legislature plainly intended for the remedies provided under Civ. Code § 1793.2 to apply to buyers of new products, not used products. Because the “good” at issue in Plaintiff’s SBA claims here is a motor vehicle, Plaintiff necessarily must demonstrate that the Vehicle was a “new motor vehicle” to qualify for the requested protections under the SBA. Common sense requires that the definition of “new motor vehicle” discussed in Rodriguez should be consistently applied across the entire statutory section where motor vehicles are involved.

 

Further, even if the California Supreme Court’s reasoning in Rodriguez does not specifically extend to Plaintiff’s second and third causes of action, Plaintiff still cannot demonstrate that her Vehicle was a “new” vehicle, given the undisputed factual record discussed supra. (See supra, Section I.A; Rodriguez, supra, 17 Cal.5th at p. 202 [“The Legislature has shown it knows how to legislate on “used” products (§ 1795.5), and it has employed clear language when it wants to make a “used” product subject to the warranty protections that apply to the product when new…”].)

Alternatively, Plaintiff argues that Civ. Code § 1795.5 extends the scope of potential liability under Civ. Code § 1793.2 because Plaintiff purchased her Vehicle “in a sale in which an express warranty is given…” (Civ. Code § 1795.5.) Plaintiff argues that FCA concedes that an original/unexpired warranty, which is attached to the FAC, was given to Plaintiff at re-sale. (Opp., at p. 11.) Even so, the exception for “used” goods under Civ. Code § 1795.5 does not apply to all manufacturers, only to a “distributor or retail seller of used consumer goods.” (Civ. Code § 1795.5.) For example, in Kiluk, the court held that a vehicle manufacturer could be liable under Civ. Code § 1793.2 in connection with a pre-owned vehicle because it had partnered with a dealership to sell the vehicle and was thus subject to the obligations of a retailer under Civ. Code § 1795.5. (Kiluk, supra, 43 Cal.App.5th at p. 340.) In other words, in order for Plaintiff’s SBA claims to fall within the scope of Civ. Code § 1795.5, Plaintiff must be able to demonstrate that FCA was acting as a seller when it issued the express warranty in connection with her Vehicle.

 

Here, however, Plaintiff fails to submit sufficient, non-speculative evidence demonstrating that FCA partnered with the BBA to sell the Vehicle. FCA is not identified as a party to the Sale Contract. (Tremonti Decl., ¶ 5, Exh. 2.) Plaintiff also concedes that she did not pay any money directly to FCA in connection with the sale of the Vehicle. (Ibid.) Meanwhile, Plaintiff offers no legal analysis on the existence of an agency or “partnership” relationship between FCA and BBA to suggest that FCA was involved in the sale transaction at issue. At most, Plaintiff infers that BBA was an agent of FCA for purposes of the sale transaction simply because BBA participated in FCA’s CPO certification program. (Lister Decl., Exh. 1 [confirming Plaintiff’s Vehicle had CPO status].)  However, Plaintiff fails to identify any legal authority to support her inference that such a program gives rise to an agency relationship with a dealership such that a vehicle manufacturer can be deemed a seller as a matter of law.

 

Accordingly, the Court finds that Plaintiff has failed to raise a triable factual dispute regarding the application of Civ. Code § 1795.5 to her two remaining SBA claims. The second and third causes of action must fail because the Vehicle at issue is not a new product. As no remaining SBA claim survives, Plaintiff’s request for civil penalties must also fail.

 

Therefore, FCA’s Motion for Summary Judgment is GRANTED in its entirety.
