DEPARTMENT 515 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 23STCV31247    Hearing Date: August 4, 2026    Dept: 515

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: Hearing on Motion for Summary Judgment or, in the Alternative, Summary Adjudication

The hearing on the Motion for Summary Judgment or, in the Alternative, Summary Adjudication, is continued to permit supplemental briefing on the timeliness of the second, third, fourth, fifth, and sixth causes of action.

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Joseph Palladino (Plaintiff) filed this action against RTX Corporation (RTX), Raytheon Company (Raytheon), and Pablo Gibu (Gibu) (collectively, Defendants), alleging that Defendants discriminated against him because of his age and a shoulder condition, harassed him, and retaliated against him during his final years as an opto-mechanical technician at Raytheon's El Segundo facility.

The causes of action are: (1) Age Discrimination; (2) Age Harassment; (3) Disability Discrimination; (4) Disability Harassment; (5) Failure to Provide Reasonable Accommodation; (6) Failure to Engage in the Interactive Process; (7) Failure to Prevent Discrimination, Harassment, and Retaliation; (8) Retaliation in Violation of the Fair Employment and Housing Act; (9) Whistleblower Retaliation; (10) Retaliation for Reporting Unsafe Conditions; (11) Wrongful Termination in Violation of Public Policy; and (12) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress. The Complaint also prays for punitive damages.

Defendants filed a Motion for Summary Judgment or, in the Alternative, Summary Adjudcation. Plaintiff filed an Opposition.

LEGAL STANDARD

A defendant moving for summary judgment or summary adjudication bears the burden of persuasion that one or more elements of the cause of action cannot be established, or that a complete defense to it exists. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850.) The moving defendant bears an initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing that no triable issue of material fact exists, and once the defendant carries that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to make a prima facie showing that a triable issue exists. (Id. at pp. 850-851.) The Court considers all of the evidence and all reasonable inferences from it in the light most favorable to the opposing party, and may not grant the motion on an inference contradicted by other inferences or evidence that raise a triable issue of material fact. (Id. at p. 843; Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c).) A motion for summary adjudication shall be granted only if it completely disposes of a cause of action, an affirmative defense, a claim for punitive damages, or an issue of duty. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (f)(1).)

ANALYSIS

I. Grounds Raised

Defendants argue that Plaintiff's age harassment and disability-related claims are time-barred. A complaint alleging an unlawful practice under the Fair Employment and Housing Act “shall not be filed after the expiration of three years from the date upon which the unlawful practice or refusal to cooperate occurred.” (Gov. Code, § 12960, subd. (e)(5).) Plaintiff filed his charge with the Civil Rights Department on December 12, 2023, which places the cutoff at December 12, 2020. Plaintiff does not dispute that date, and he does not dispute that he submitted no further medical records and requested no further accommodation after he returned to full duty on June 23, 2020.

Limitations is not among the twenty-three issues stated in the notice of motion, and no issue in the separate statement identifies it as a ground for adjudicating a cause of action. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1350(b).) Defendants raise it in three other places. A footnote in the moving memorandum states that the conduct on which the second cause of action rests, which Plaintiff traces to 2018, is time-barred to the extent it predates December 12, 2020. (Mot. at p. 10, fn. 13.) The reply states that the third, fourth, fifth, and sixth causes of action are barred because the disability-related conduct occurred on or before June 23, 2020. (Reply at p. 8:17-18.) Between the two, Defendants assert the statute of limitations as a ground of objection to nearly all of the evidence Plaintiff offers, including McAlpine's account of Gibu's statement about the lab's older technicians and of the relocation of Plaintiff's work station, Garcia's account of the same statement, and the Littleton Declaration's account of the overtime records, the October 2021 human resources investigation, and the 2022 grievance proceedings. (Defs.' Objs. to McAlpine Decl., objs. 2-12; Defs.' Objs. to Garcia Decl., objs. 1, 2, 4; Defs.' Objs. to Littleton Decl., objs. 2-4, 16-18, 33, 34.)

II. Opportunity to Respond

Where the court identifies a ground of law that is dispositive when applied to an undisputed material fact appearing in the parties' separate statements, the court may in its discretion reach that ground even though the moving party did not explicitly tender it. When it does so, due process requires that the party opposing the motion “must be provided an opportunity to respond to the ground of law identified by the court and must be given a chance to show there is a triable issue of fact material to said ground of law.” (Juge v. County of Sacramento (1993) 12 Cal. App.4th 59, 70.)

A reply “shall not include any new evidentiary matter, additional material facts, or separate statement submitted with the reply and not presented in the moving papers or opposition.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (b)(4).) Defendants shall therefore argue the limitations period on the evidence already before the Court. Plaintiff, as the party opposing the motion, may submit evidence directed to the timeliness of the second, third, fourth, fifth, and sixth causes of action.

Deciding the period now would resolve five causes of action against a party who has never briefed the answer. The Court therefore continues the hearing and orders supplemental briefing.

Plaintiff shall file and serve a supplemental opposition, not to exceed ten pages, together with any evidence and any separate statement of additional material facts directed to the timeliness of those causes of action, not less than twenty days before the continued hearing date. Defendants shall file and serve a supplemental reply, not to exceed five pages, not less than eleven days before that date. The supplemental reply shall not include any new evidentiary matter, additional material facts, or separate statement.

 

CONCLUSION

The hearing on the Motion for Summary Judgment is continued to permit supplemental briefing on the timeliness of the second, third, fourth, fifth, and sixth causes of action.


Case Number: 25STCV28326    Hearing Date: August 4, 2026    Dept: 515

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: Hearing on Demurrer

The Demurrer filed by Defendant Ka Wai Kwan is overruled.

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff 3 Alameda Plaza, LLC (Plaintiff) filed this action against Alameda Restaurant, LLC (Tenant), Ka Wai Kwan also known as Michael Kwan (Kwan), Lauro Calonzo, and Hera Ramos (collectively, “Defendants”), alleging that Tenant failed to pay amounts owed under a commercial lease, that Kwan guaranteed Tenant’s payment obligations, and that the individual Defendants are Tenant’s alter egos.

The causes of action are: (1) Breach of Lease; and (2) Breach of Personal Guaranty.

Kwan filed a Demurrer. Plaintiff filed an Opposition.

LEGAL STANDARD

When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (Wilson v. Transit Authority of City of Sacramento (1962) 199 Cal.App.2d 716, 720-721.) In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) “‘A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or the facts alleged.’ [Citation.]” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Services (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) Courts therefore assume the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Ibid.) They do not accept as true deductions, contentions, or conclusions of law or fact. (Stonehouse Homes LLC v. City of Sierra Madre (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 531, 538.)

ANALYSIS

I. Alter Ego

Kwan argues that he did not sign the Lease and cannot be liable for breaching an agreement to which he is not a party. (Demurrer, at p. 5:2-4, p. 8:10-19.) The Lease names Tenant as the only tenant, and the Complaint does not allege that Kwan signed it. (Compl., Ex. 1 [Summary of Basic Lease Information and Definitions, § 1.2].) Plaintiff pleads instead that Kwan is liable for Tenant’s obligations as its alter ego. (Compl., ¶¶ 7-10.)

A member of a limited liability company is subject to alter ego liability at common law to the same extent, and under the same or similar circumstances, as a shareholder of a corporation. (Corp. Code, § 17703.04, subd. (b).) Two requirements govern. Unity of interest and ownership must have left the separate personalities of the entity and the individual no longer existing in fact, and treating the acts as the entity’s alone must produce an inequitable result. (Mesler v. Bragg Management Co. (1985) 39 Cal.3d 290, 300.) Where a plaintiff makes that showing, the court disregards the corporate entity and holds the individual liable for what the entity did. (Ibid.)

A plaintiff pleading alter ego is held to ultimate rather than evidentiary facts, and less particularity is required where the defendant may be assumed to know the facts at least as well as the plaintiff does. (Rutherford Holdings, LLC v. Plaza Del Rey (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 221, 236.) Allegations that an individual dominated and controlled an entity, that a unity of interest and ownership existed between them, and that the entity served as a mere shell for the individual’s affairs survive a demurrer. (Id. at p. 235.)

Here, the Complaint alleges each of these elements. Kwan, Calonzo, and Ramos are alleged to be the only persons in control of Tenant and to have dominated it in bad faith, so that recognizing its separate existence would promote injustice. (Compl., ¶ 7.) Plaintiff further alleges that the three used Tenant as a mere shell for their personal financial advantage, acquired its entire ownership interest for an inadequate capital contribution, and left it without assets to meet its debts. (Compl., ¶ 8.) According to the Complaint, they caused Tenant to divert its assets to their personal expenses and paid Tenant’s obligations with personal funds. (Compl., ¶ 9.) Tenant is alleged to have disregarded corporate formalities, including keeping corporate funds separate from personal funds. (Compl., ¶ 10.) Therefore, the Court concludes that Plaintiff has pleaded alter ego liability sufficiently at this stage.

II. Privity

Kwan next argues that the Complaint pleads no contract between Plaintiff and himself, no breach by him, and no damages caused by him. (Demurrer, at p. 7:18-21, p. 8:1-3; Reply, p. 3:20-26.) The argument assumes that Plaintiff must plead a separate agreement between Plaintiff and Kwan. Alter ego liability does not operate that way. Where the doctrine applies, Tenant’s obligations under the Lease become Kwan’s, so the elements pleaded as to Tenant are pleaded as to him. Plaintiff attaches the Lease and alleges its terms, Plaintiff’s performance, the failure to pay, and the resulting damages. (Compl., ¶¶ 13-15, 22-25, Ex. 1.)

Kwan also invokes the rule that an exhibit controls over an allegation it contradicts. (Demurrer, at p. 7:10-12; Reply, p. 3:8-11.) He points to the allegations that Defendants defaulted under the Lease and that Defendants agreed in the November 2024 amendment that they had failed to pay, when the instruments name Tenant alone. (Demurrer, at p. 5:19-6:4.) Read as a whole, the Complaint does not allege that the individual Defendants signed those instruments. It alleges that they are liable for Tenant’s obligations as its alter egos. So read, the Complaint and its exhibits are consistent. Accordingly, the Demurrer to the First Cause of Action on the ground that Kwan is not a party to the Lease is overruled.

II. Uncertainty

Kwan argues that the Complaint does not allege when the amounts composing the $725,331.30 were incurred, and that the November 2024 amendment neither allocates nor itemizes the balance it recites. (Demurrer, at p. 8:20-9:7.) That omission, he argues, leaves him to guess at the claim and prevents him from showing on the face of the pleading that amounts more than four years old fall outside the limitations period for an action on a written contract. (Code Civ. Proc., § 337, subd. (a).)

A demurrer for uncertainty tests whether the pleading alerts the defendant to the issues he must meet, and it fails where the complaint’s substantive factual allegations do that. (Williams v. Beechnut Nutrition Corp. (1986) 185 Cal.App.3d 135, 139, fn. 2.) Here, the Complaint identifies the Lease, the Personal Guaranty, and the amendments, and attaches each. (Compl., ¶¶ 13, 16, 19, Exs. 1-4.) It alleges the month Tenant’s default began, the balance the parties acknowledged in the November 2024 amendment, and the sum Plaintiff now claims. (Compl., ¶¶ 18-20, Ex. 4.) The Lease itself names Kwan as the guarantor who was to execute a separate personal guaranty. (Compl., Ex. 1 Those allegations identify the instruments sued on, the period of nonpayment, and the amount demanded. The detail Kwan wants, an accrual date for each component of the balance, is a subject for discovery, and a demurrer for uncertainty is “strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Khoury v. Maly’s of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.) Accordingly, the Demurrer to the First Cause of Action for uncertainty is overruled.

III. Statute of Limitations

A demurrer resting on an affirmative defense succeeds only where the face of the complaint discloses that the action is necessarily barred by that defense. (Casterson v. Superior Court (2002) 101 Cal.App.4th 177, 182-183.) The Complaint alleges a failure to pay beginning in March 2020 and continuing thereafter, and a failure to make the installments set by the November 2024 amendment. (Compl., ¶¶ 18-20, Ex. 4.) Breaches falling within four years of the September 29, 2025 filing of this action appear on the face of the pleading. A demurrer does not lie to a portion of a cause of action in any event, so a time-barred component of the claimed balance would leave the cause of action intact. (PH II, Inc. v. Superior Court (1995) 33 Cal.App.4th 1680, 1682.)

IV. Personal Guaranty

Kwan raises three grounds against the Second Cause of Action. First, he argues that Plaintiff failed to allege facts supporting each element of a claim on a guaranty, and in particular failed to allege that it notified him of Tenant’s default. Next, he argues that the Personal Guaranty expired by its terms five years after the Lease Commencement Date. Finally, he argues that the cause of action carries the uncertainty and limitations defects he attributes to the First Cause of Action. (Demurrer, at p. 9:8-10:16.)

A. Notice

The elements of a cause of action for breach of a personal guaranty are as follows: (1) the guarantor guaranteed payment of a third party’s indebtedness to the lender; (2) the third party defaulted on its loans; (3) the lender notified the guarantor of the default and demanded payment; and (4) the guarantor did not make payment to the lender. (Walsh v. West Valley Mission Community College Dist. (1998) 66 Cal. App.4th 1532, 1546-1547.)

The Complaint pleads each of these elements. It alleges that Kwan executed the Personal Guaranty and unconditionally guaranteed payment under the Lease; that Tenant failed to pay from March 2020 onward; and that the balance remains unpaid. (Compl., ¶¶ 16, 18, 20, 28-29, Ex. 2.) As to notice, Plaintiff alleges that it provided notice to Defendants that they were in default under the terms of the Lease. (Compl., ¶ 19.) Kwan is one of the Defendants, so the allegation is an allegation of notice to him. When Plaintiff gave that notice, and whether it satisfied the Lease and the Personal Guaranty, are questions of proof. The allegation suffices at the pleading stage whether or not notice to the guarantor is an element of a claim on a guaranty.

Kwan responds that Exhibit 4 contradicts paragraph 19, because the amendment names only Plaintiff and Tenant and attaches no notice document. (Demurrer, at p. 6:1-4.) An exhibit displaces an allegation it contradicts, not one it omits. Exhibit 4 recites a delinquent balance and sets an installment schedule, and it says nothing about whether notice of default was given, to whom, or when. The allegation of notice therefore survives the exhibit.

B. Guaranty Expiration

The expiration argument rests on a conditional clause. The Personal Guaranty provides that it “shall expire and be null and void at the end of five (5) years from the Lease Commencement Date (as specified in the Lease itself) (the ‘End Date’), provided that Tenant is not in default of its obligations under the Lease beyond any applicable notice and cure periods thereunder (an ‘Event of Default’) as of the End Date, and provided that an Event of Default has not occurred three (3) or more times during the Lease term prior to the End Date.” (Compl., Ex. 2.) Two further provisions address the failure of those conditions. Where an Event of Default exists at the End Date, and fewer than three have occurred before it, the Personal Guaranty expires only when Tenant cures that default and any others outstanding on the date of the cure. Where an Event of Default occurred three or more times before the End Date, the Personal Guaranty continues for the remainder of the Lease term. (Ibid.)

The Demurrer succeeds on this ground only if the face of the Complaint establishes that both conditions to expiration were satisfied. (Casterson, supra, 101 Cal.App.4th at pp. 182-183.) The Lease Commencement Date is August 1, 2015, which places the End Date at August 1, 2020. (Compl., Ex. 1 [Summary, § 1.7].) The notice and cure periods that the definition of “Event of Default” incorporates appear in the Lease. A monetary default arises when Tenant fails to pay “within three (3) days after written notice that the same is due,” and once Landlord has delivered a notice of default, a further monetary default within the following twelve months “shall be deemed an event of default without any notice or grace period.” (Compl., Ex. 1 [Lease, § 23.1(b)].)

Read against those provisions, the Complaint permits the inferences that defeat expiration. Plaintiff alleges that Defendants failed to make payments when due beginning in March 2020 and continuing thereafter, and that Plaintiff gave notice of default under the Lease. (Compl., ¶¶ 18-19.) The Complaint does not date that notice, and on demurrer the Court draws the inference in Plaintiff’s favor. So drawn, the allegations describe a notice of default followed by continued nonpayment, which yields an Event of Default at the End Date and, under the twelve-month provision, three or more Events of Default before it. Either condition alone defeats expiration on August 1, 2020.

Kwan’s third ground restates the uncertainty and limitations arguments directed at the First Cause of Action, and it fails for the reasons stated above. (Demurrer, at p. 10:12-16.)

Accordingly, the Demurrer to the Second Cause of Action for Breach of Personal Guaranty is overruled.

CONCLUSION

The Demurrer filed by Defendant Ka Wai Kwan is overruled. Kwan shall file and serve an answer within 10 days.


Case Number: 26STCV08967    Hearing Date: August 4, 2026    Dept: 515

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: Hearing on Motion to Quash Service of Summons

Defendant Ashley Liu’s Motion to Quash Service of Summons is granted.

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Weishi Yang (Plaintiff) filed this action against Ashley Liu (Liu) and Carol Xu aka Jiayin Xu, alleging that he holds written assignments from five people who sent Chinese currency to accounts the defendants designated and never received the dollars they were promised.

The causes of action are: (1) Breach of Written Agreement; (2) Money Had and Received; (3) Fraudulent Misrepresentation; (4) Negligent Misrepresentation; (5) Promissory Fraud; (6) Conversion; and (7) Unjust Enrichment.

Liu filed a Motion to Quash Service of Summons. Plaintiff filed an Opposition.

EVIDENTIARY OBJECTIONS

Plaintiff lodges five objections to the Declaration of Barry L. Greenhalgh filed with the Motion and to two exhibits.

1.      Sustained. Hearsay. (Evid. Code, § 1200.)

2.      Sustained. Improper opinion. (Evid. Code, § 800.)

3.      Sustained. Improper opinion. (Evid. Code, § 800.)

4.      The Court declines to rule. Exhibit B is secondary commentary offered as argument, and the Court does not rely on it.

5.      Sustained. Lacks foundation. (Evid. Code, § 702.) Counsel establishes that he printed the search results, not that the searches he ran would have located a registration that exists.

LEGAL STANDARD

A defendant may move, on or before the last day to plead, “[t]o quash service of summons on the ground of lack of jurisdiction of the court over him or her.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 418.10, subd. (a)(1).) “[C]ompliance with the statutory procedures for service of process is essential to establish personal jurisdiction.” (Dill v. Berquist Construction Co. (1994) 24 Cal.App.4th 1426, 1444 (Dill).) A defendant is under no duty to act upon a defectively served summons. (Kappel v. Bartlett (1988) 200 Cal.App.3d 1457, 1466.) When a defendant challenges jurisdiction by bringing a motion to quash, “the burden is on the plaintiff to prove the existence of jurisdiction by proving, inter alia, the facts requisite to an effective service.” (Dill, supra, 24 Cal.App.4th at pp. 1439-1440.)

ANALYSIS

The service at issue is undisputed. On April 24, 2026, Michael Rodgers, a registered California process server, left the summons and complaint with Lupe Lopez, the manager of The UPS Store at 122 East Foothill Boulevard, Unit 126, Arcadia. (Mot., Ex. A.) Liu had rented Box 126 at that store, and her rental agreement ended in January 2025. (Liu Decl. at p. 2:1-2.) On the return, Mr. Rodgers checked box 5b(3), the box for service at a usual mailing address where the physical address is unknown, described Ms. Lopez as the manager of a mail receiving agency where the recipient holds a box, left box 5b(5) unchecked, and wrote at item 5d that he served a mail receiving agency under Code of Civil Procedure section 415.20 and placed the documents for mailing. (Mot., Ex. A, items 5b, 5b(3), 5b(5), 5d.) No one attempted to serve Liu anywhere else.

I. Substituted Service

A. Reasonable Diligence

Liu argues that no one attempted personal service before the process server went to The UPS Store, and that no declaration of diligence accompanies the return. (Mot. at p. 2:23-3:3; Reply 4:14-17.)

Code of Civil Procedure section 415.20, subdivision (b), permits substituted service at a dwelling house, usual place of abode, usual place of business, or usual mailing address only where the summons and complaint “cannot with reasonable diligence be personally delivered to the person to be served.” Ordinarily, “two or three attempts at personal service at a proper place and with correct pleadings should fully satisfy the requirement of reasonable diligence and allow substituted service to be made.” (Kremerman v. White (2021) 71 Cal.App.5th 358, 373 (Kremerman).) Section 415.20, subdivision (c), governs private mailboxes and states a different condition: “Notwithstanding subdivision (b), if the only address reasonably known for the person to be served is a private mailbox obtained through a commercial mail receiving agency [CMRA], service of process may be effected on the first delivery attempt by leaving a copy of the summons and complaint with the [CMRA] in the manner described in subdivision (d) of Section 17538.5 of the Business and Professions Code.”

Plaintiff rests the Opposition on subdivision (c) and on the absence of any diligence requirement within it, and the entries on the return invoke that subdivision. (Opp. 5:7-24; Mot., Ex. A, items 5b(3), 5d.) Where subdivision (c) applies, one delivery attempt suffices, and neither the unchecked box 5b(5) nor the missing declaration of diligence would defeat the service. Liu’s argument, so far as it assumes that a plaintiff must always attempt personal delivery before leaving process with a CMRA, reads subdivision (b)’s condition into a subdivision that operates notwithstanding it.

Kremerman holds nothing to the contrary. The court considered both routes to substituted service and found the service defective under each. Measured against Code of Civil Procedure section 415.20, subdivision (b), the plaintiff’s attempts showed no reasonable diligence, because the first nonservice report described attempts to serve unlawful detainer pleadings rather than the summons and complaint in the contract action, and the second described a stakeout at a property the defendant had vacated six months earlier. (Kremerman, supra, 71 Cal.App.5th at pp. 372-373.) The proof of service compounded the problem by reciting that the papers were left with a competent member of the household at the defendant’s dwelling house or usual place of abode, when the recipient was a Postal Annex employee, and the Postal Annex was neither. (Id. at p. 373.) Section 415.20, subdivision (c), was independently unavailable, because the plaintiff knew the defendant’s home address, which appeared on exhibits to his complaint and in the third nonservice report. (Ibid.) The diligence discussion in Kremerman therefore measures attempts under subdivision (b), and subdivision (c) imposes no requirement of prior attempts.

B. Reasonable Knowledge

Liu argues that no one made any effort to locate her, and that a mailbox cannot be the only address reasonably known to Plaintiff where no effort was made to find another. (Mot. at p. 5:3-7; Reply 6:4-7.)

Subdivision (c) opens with a condition: the private mailbox must be “the only address reasonably known for the person to be served.” A plaintiff whose jurisdiction is challenged must prove the facts requisite to effective service, and the facts that bring the service within the subdivision he invokes are among them. (Dill, supra, 24 Cal.App.4th at pp. 1439-1440.)

Subdivision (c) does not ask what the plaintiff knew. It asks what was “reasonably known,” and the qualifier would be surplus if the subdivision turned on the plaintiff’s actual knowledge alone. Elsewhere in the same section, the Legislature conditioned service at a mailing address on whether a physical address “is known,” without the qualifier. (Code Civ. Proc., § 415.20, subd. (a).) A plaintiff who has looked for nothing else cannot say that a mailbox was the only address reasonably known to him, because he does not know what looking would have shown.

Substituted service under subdivision (b) already reaches a private mailbox at a CMRA, because a usual mailing address “other than a United States Postal Service post office box” includes a commercial box. (Ellard v. Conway (2001) 94 Cal.App.4th 540, 545-546 (Ellard).) A plaintiff taking that route must first attempt personal delivery with reasonable diligence, ordinarily two or three times. Subdivision (c) excuses those attempts for the plaintiff whose knowledge of the defendant’s whereabouts is confined to the mailbox itself. The excuse and the condition go together. What justifies serving a mail drop on the first try is that the search for the defendant has already been made and has turned up nothing else. Reading subdivision (c) to excuse the search as well would give the plaintiff who learns the least the shortest route to service.

Here, the evidence of what Plaintiff knew consists of one sentence in his counsel’s declaration stating that Plaintiff does not know Liu’s address and that counsel’s firm hired Mr. Rodgers to obtain her mailing address through a skip trace. (Khojayan Decl., ¶ 2.) Counsel does not state how he knows what his client knows, and a witness may testify only to matters within his personal knowledge. (Evid. Code, § 702, subd. (a).) The declaration is equally silent about the search. Counsel engaged the process server to locate a mailing address, and the process server produced one. Nothing describes an effort to locate Liu’s residence, her place of business, or any location where she could be served with the papers, and the return describes no attempt to serve her anywhere other than the UPS Store counter. (Mot., Ex. A, items 5b, 5d.) The standard is not exacting. Ellard did not require the plaintiffs there to “exhaust all avenues of obtaining a current address.” (Ellard, supra, 94 Cal.App.4th at p. 545.) Far from exhausting all avenues, Plaintiff fails to show that any effort was made to find a physical address.

Plaintiff asks the Court to treat Liu’s silence as proof of the condition. That Liu has not identified a home address, and has not asserted that Plaintiff knew one, is not evidence that a reasonable search would have found nothing, and the burden of proving the facts requisite to effective service rests with Plaintiff. (Dill, supra, 24 Cal.App.4th at pp. 1439-1440.) The plaintiff in Kremerman knew the defendant’s home address, as discussed above, and subdivision (c) was therefore unavailable to him. That actual knowledge of another address defeats the condition does not mean that ignorance establishes it.

The Court concludes that Plaintiff has not proved that Liu’s box at The UPS Store was the only address reasonably known to him, and that Code of Civil Procedure section 415.20, subdivision (c), did not authorize this service. Subdivision (b) governed instead, and the single delivery to Ms. Lopez, preceded by no attempt at personal delivery anywhere, did not satisfy it.

II. Private Mailbox Service

Even assuming that the mailbox was the only address reasonably known to Plaintiff, the service was never perfected. Liu argues that the return cannot show what The UPS Store did with the papers after the process server left, that no certificate of mailing exists, and that Plaintiff never inquired. (Mot. at p. 5:14-6:6; Reply 3:12-4:5.)

Code of Civil Procedure section 415.20, subdivision (c), permits service at a CMRA only “in the manner described in subdivision (d) of Section 17538.5 of the Business and Professions Code.” Business and Professions Code section 17538.5, subdivision (d)(1), directs the CMRA, upon receipt of process for a mailbox customer, to place a copy of the documents or a notice in the customer’s mailbox within 48 hours unless the service was previously terminated, and, “within five days after receipt, send all documents by first-class mail, to the last known home or personal address of the mail receiving service customer.” The CMRA “shall obtain a certificate of mailing in connection with the mailing of the documents,” and “[s]ervice of process upon the mail receiving service customer shall then be deemed perfected 10 days after the date of mailing.” (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 17538.5, subd. (d)(1).) Delivery to the CMRA opens the sequence. The mailing perfects the service and fixes the date from which the ten days run.

Here, Liu’s rental agreement ended in January 2025, and the process server delivered the papers in April 2026, more than a year later. (Liu Decl. at p. 2:1-2.) The statutes anticipate that sequence. A CMRA must accept service for its customers “and for two years after termination of any mail receiving service customer agreement,” and the customer’s written authorization runs “until two years after my mail receiving service has been terminated.” (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 17538.5, subds. (d)(1), (f).) A box closed within the preceding two years therefore remains a private mailbox obtained through a CMRA within the meaning of Code of Civil Procedure section 415.20, subdivision (c), and remains a proper place to leave a service of summons. Business and Professions Code section 17538.5, subdivision (d)(1), excuses the CMRA from placing documents in the box where “the mail receiving service for the customer was previously terminated.” However, it does not remove the obligation to mail them within five days. Service of process is then perfected 10 days after the mailing date. (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 17538.5, subds. (d)(1).)

Kremerman confirms this reading. Listing the defects in the service before it, the court identified the CMRA’s 48-hour placement and five-day mailing as what “California law requires,” citing Code of Civil Procedure section 415.20, subdivision (c), together with Business and Professions Code section 17538.5, subdivision (d)(1), and then wrote: “There is no evidence in the judgment roll that indicates whether the referenced statutory requirements were met.” (Kremerman, supra, 71 Cal.App.5th at p. 373.) Turning to the record the plaintiff had made in the trial court, the court found that The UPS Store employee placed the documents in the customer’s box the day they arrived, within the 48 hours the statute allows, but mailed them twenty days after receipt, and held that the trial court had erred in concluding that the service was proper. (Id. at p. 374.) The mailing is therefore a requirement of the service and not the CMRA’s internal housekeeping, and it is the act that perfects the service even where the papers reach the box on time.

Here, the record says nothing about that mailing. The return describes one delivery and three mailings, all by Mr. Rodgers, and each mailing went to The UPS Store’s address. (Mot., Ex. A, items 5b(4), 5c, 5d.) None is the mailing to the customer’s last known home or personal address that the statute requires. The return is silent on whether The UPS Store mailed the papers to Liu and whether it obtained a certificate of mailing, and Evidence Code section 647 presumes the facts a registered server states in his return, not the later acts of a third party the server neither witnessed nor described. Plaintiff filed no declaration from The UPS Store and no certificate of mailing.

III. Actual Notice

Plaintiff argues that Liu learned of the case, retained counsel, and filed this motion, and that courts construe the service statutes liberally where the defendant knows of the suit. (Opp. 3:16-19, 4:5-7, 7:8-9.) Code of Civil Procedure section 418.10, subdivision (d), gives a defendant this motion so that she may contest jurisdiction without submitting to it. It follows that the filing of the motion establishes nothing about the service that preceded it. Our Supreme Court held that the service statutes should be “liberally construed to effectuate service and uphold the jurisdiction of the court if actual notice has been received by the defendant.” (Pasadena Medi-Center Associates v. Superior Court (1973) 9 Cal.3d 773, 778.) That principle has a limit. A defendant’s knowledge of the lawsuit is not a substitute for proper service and does not confer jurisdiction, and no California appellate court has upheld service based on actual notice alone where the plaintiff failed to comply with the service statutes. (American Express Centurion Bank v. Zara (2011) 199 Cal.App.4th 383, 392 (American Express).)

To the extent that Plaintiff suggests that the fact that Liu filed the instant motion proves that The UPS Store complied with the 5-day mailing requirement, that assumption is inconsistent with the evidence. Liu declares that no one other than her attorney has contacted or visited her regarding this lawsuit, and that she knows of no attempt to serve her other than what her attorney showed her. (Liu Decl. at p. 2:6-10.) Her counsel declares that, after the dismissal of a prior case involving the same parties, Plaintiff’s counsel advised him that a new lawsuit would be filed, that he found the new filing on the court’s website, had the pleadings copied, and telephoned his client. (Greenhalgh Decl. at p. 3:12-15.)

Because Plaintiff fails to show that Liu’s mailbox at The UPS Store was the only reasonably known address, and alternatively because he fails to show that The UPS Store complied with the five-day mailing requirement, the Motion is granted.

CONCLUSION

Ashley Liu’s Motion to Quash Service of Summons is granted.
