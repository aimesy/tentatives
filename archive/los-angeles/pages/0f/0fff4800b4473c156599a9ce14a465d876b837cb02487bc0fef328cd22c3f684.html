DEPARTMENT 316 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 25STCV26095    Hearing Date: September 23, 2026    Dept: 316

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Marilyn Jefferson alleges that she was wrongfully terminated from her role as an Electrical Mechanic Trainee because of undue discrimination and pressure from her superiors.

On March 20, 2026, Plaintiff filed a first amended complaint (“FAC”) against Defendants City of Los Angeles, Los Angeles Department of Water and Power, Jesus Gonzales, Matt Wilson, Daniel Rogers, and John Bietsch. The complaint alleges eight causes of action stemming from the facts alleged above.

On June 10, 2026, Plaintiff filed three Motions to Quash Defendant’s Deposition Subpoenas.

On September 10, 2026, Defendant City of Los Angeles filed three corresponding oppositions.

On September 16, 2026, Plaintiff filed three corresponding replies.

TENTATIVE RULING

Plaintiff’s three Motions to Quash Defendant’s Deposition Subpoenas are DENIED but the scope of the subpoenas is limited as described herein.

Dr. Rodney D. Collins, NP Yvette Holman, Dr. John W. O’Donoghue, and Dr. Rivkah Cunin must produce documents responsive to the subpoenas as limited herein within thirty (30) days. No sanctions are awarded.

LEGAL STANDARD

Any party may obtain discovery by taking the oral deposition of any person, including any party to the action. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.010.)¿A party¿desiring¿to take an oral deposition shall give a notice in writing which¿states¿the specification of¿reasonably particularly¿of any materials to be produced by the deponent. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.220, subd. (a)(4).) A properly served deposition notice is effective to require a party to attend and to testify, as well as to produce documents for inspection and copying. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.280, subd. (a).)

“If a subpoena requires the attendance of a witness or the production of books, documents, electronically stored information, or other things before a court, or at the trial of an issue therein, or at the taking of a deposition, the court, upon motion reasonably made by any person described in subdivision (b) . . . may make an order quashing the subpoena entirely, modifying it, or directing compliance with it upon those terms or conditions as the court shall declare, including protective orders. In addition, the court may make any other order as may be appropriate to protect the person from unreasonable or oppressive demands, including unreasonable violations of the right of privacy of the person.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.1, subd. (a).) 

Furthermore, the Court may “award the amount of the reasonable expenses incurred in making or opposing the motion, including reasonable attorney’s fees, if the court finds the motion was made or opposed in bad faith or without substantial justification or that one or more of the requirements of the subpoena was oppressive.” (See Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.2, subd. (a).) 

DISCUSSION

“[E]ven¿when discovery of private information is found directly relevant to the issues of ongoing litigation, it will not be automatically allowed; there must then be a careful balancing of the compelling public need for discovery against the fundamental right of privacy.” (Lantz v. Superior Court¿(1994) 28 Cal.App.4th 1839, 1854-1855.) “[I]f an intrusion on the right of privacy is deemed necessary under the circumstances of a particular case, any such intrusion should be the minimum intrusion necessary to achieve its objective.”¿(Ibid.) 

A “[p]plaintiffs¿is not compelled, as a condition to entering the courtroom, to discard entirely¿[his or] her mantle of¿privacy.” (Vinson¿v. Superior Court¿(1987)¿43 Cal.3d 833, 841.) A¿plaintiff who has filed a lawsuit has not completely waived his or her right to¿privacy¿by¿bringing¿suit; rather, the implicit waiver of a party’s constitutional right to¿privacy¿“encompasses only¿discovery directly relevant to the plaintiff’s claim and essential to the fair resolution of the lawsuit.”¿(Id.¿at p. 842;¿see also Hale v. Superior Court¿(1994) 28 Cal.App.4th 1421, 1424 [“even if part of¿Hale’s medical condition is in issue, it does not follow that Hale waived the¿privilege as to otherwise¿protected aspects of her medical history during her lifetime, or some condition she may have¿suffered from at the time of her death clearly unrelated to the accident”].)¿ 

The burden is on “the party asserting a¿privacy¿interest to establish its extent and the seriousness of the prospective invasion,” and then the court must “weigh¿the countervailing interests the opposing party identifies.” (Williams v. Superior Court¿(2017)¿3 Cal.5th¿531,¿557.)¿ 

Here, Plaintiff moves to quash Defendant’s subpoenas to Dr. Rodney D. Collins, NP Yvette Holman, Dr. John W. O’Donoghue, and Dr. Rivkah Cunin. Plaintiff seeks to quash the subpoenas on the ground that they are overly broad and will lead to unnecessary disclosure of private information that is not relevant to this specific matter.

Given that the arguments regarding the three deposition subpoenas are not substantially different, the Court will evaluate them together

I.                The Deposition Subpoenas

The Court finds that the requested documents are very likely relevant to this matter. Plaintiff has placed her psychological condition at issue in the lawsuit and it is a critical issue to both liability and damages. (See FAC, ¶ 14(e), 15(b), 15(d), 17.) Further, each of the providers to whom the subpoenas are directed were identified by Plaintiff in discovery responses as health care providers from whom she received treatment for the injuries she attributes to Defendants in this case. (See Shea Decl., Ex. A.) Therefore, the Court finds it reasonable for Defendant to request documents relating to Plaintiff’s mental condition from these providers. The documents will help illustrate the extent of Plaintiff’s condition, whether that condition stems from Defendant’s alleged conduct, whether Plaintiff’s requests for accommodations were reasonable, and whether Plaintiff could return to work. Accordingly, the relevance of the requested documents is clear.  

Plaintiff contends there are privacy concerns that justify quashing or at least limiting the scope of the subpoena. More specifically, Plaintiff contends that the request is overly broad and will lead to the unnecessary disclosure of other irrelevant medical information.

The Court agrees with Plaintiff that Plaintiff’s decision to place a medical condition at issue does not open her entire medical history to unrestricted scrutiny and that she maintains a strong privacy interest in keeping her medical records and information free from unnecessary disclosure. However, after balancing the interests, the Court finds the breadth and scope of the subpoenas reasonably limits any intrusion of Plaintiff’s privacy rights. First, the deposition subpoenas are directed at Plaintiff’s mental health providers as identified by Plaintiff in this case. Therefore, the documents to be produced should relate only to Plaintiff’s mental health. Second, the subpoenas are limited to documents from 2023 forward, a relatively narrow timeframe that will exclude any prior condition not manifesting itself during the relevant timeframe.

The Court believes the subpoenas should be further limited, however, to be explicitly limited to only documents related to mental health. The Court also believes that a protective order would be appropriate to maintain confidentiality of any produced documents, and potentially an attorneys’ eyes only designation, but leaves that to the parties to negotiate and submit for approval.

With these limitations, the Court finds the subpoenas reasonably balance Defendants’ right to relevant information to defend themselves against Plaintiff’s claims with Plaintiff’s privacy interest in her medical records. For these reasons, the Court will deny the motion but limit the subpoena as stated above.

II.             Monetary Sanctions  

Defendant requests monetary sanctions. However, the Court does not believe that imposing sanctions is just and reasonable in this instance.

Plaintiff brought these motions to ensure her medical privacy was protected. As discussed above, Plaintiff has a strong interest in ensuring this interest is not improperly disclosed and the Court finds that Plaintiff had reasonable justification to file the motions. Further, the Court has further narrowed the subpoenas in this ruling.

The Court finds that Plaintiff’s motions were justified and made in good faith and will not order monetary sanctions. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.2, subd. (a).) Accordingly, the Court will not order

CONCLUSION

Plaintiff’s three Motions to Quash Defendant’s Deposition Subpoenas are DENIED but the scope of the subpoenas is limited as described herein.

Dr. Rodney D. Collins, NP Yvette Holman, Dr. John W. O’Donoghue, and Dr. Rivkah Cunin must produce documents responsive to the subpoenas as limited herein within thirty (30) days. No sanctions are awarded to Defendant.

Case Number: 26STCV16977    Hearing Date: September 23, 2026    Dept: 316

BACKGROUND

This case is a personal injury dispute. Plaintiff Efren Herrera Lopez alleges that he was injured in a motor vehicle accident caused by Defendant Bernardino Cruz Martinez while driving for Defendant Earthscapes Landscape, Inc.

On May 27, 2026, Plaintiff filed a complaint against Defendant. The complaint alleges three causes of action from the facts alleged above.

On July 7, 2026, Defendants filed a Motion to Strike Portions of Plaintiff’s Complaint.

On September 14, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition.

Defendants have not filed a reply.

TENTATIVE RULING

Defendants’ Motion to Strike is GRANTED with leave to amend.

LEGAL STANDARD

“Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading may serve and file a notice of motion to strike the whole or any part thereof, but this time limitation shall not apply to motions specified in subdivision (e).” (Code Civ. Proc., § 435, subd. (b)(2).) “The court may, upon a motion made pursuant to Section 435, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper: (a) Strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (b) Strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.)

DISCUSSION

I.                Meet and Confer

Before filing a motion to strike, the demurring party shall meet and confer with the opposing party in person, by telephone, or by video conference. (Code Civ. Proc., § 435.5, subd. (a).) The demurring party must file a declaration detailing their meet and confer efforts. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 435.5, subd. (a)(3).)

“A determination by the court that the meet and confer process was insufficient shall not be grounds to grant or deny the motion to strike.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 435.5, subd. (a)(4).)    

Here, Defendants attempted to meet and confer with Plaintiff, but were unsuccessful. (See Alderman Decl., ¶¶ 2-4.) Therefore, the meet and confer requirement has not been satisfied. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 435.5, subd. (a).) Despite this, the Court will consider the merits of the motion. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 435.5, subd. (a)(4).) 

II.             Motion to Strike

The Court will strike Plaintiff’s request for punitive damages.

To succeed on a motion to strike punitive damages, it must be said as a matter of law that the alleged behavior was so vile, base, or contemptible that it would be looked down upon and despised by ordinary decent people. (See Angie M. v. Superior Court (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 1217, 1228-1229.) Civil Code section 3294 provides that punitive damages are available in non-contract actions where defendant is guilty of malice, oppression, or fraud, defined as follows:    

1. “Malice” means conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.  

2. “Oppression” means despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person’s rights.  

3. “Fraud” means an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known to the defendant with the intention on the part of the defendant of thereby depriving a person of property or legal rights or otherwise causing injury.      

(Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (c).)      

Defendants seek to strike Plaintiff’s claim for punitive damages. Defendants contend that Plaintiff has failed to allege intentional and malicious conduct with requisite specificity so as to support an award of such damages.

The Court agrees. In his complaint, Plaintiff alleges that Defendant Martinez “while driving fatigued and tired, traveling at a high rate of speed and while using a cell phone caused a collision by crashing DEFENDANT’S VEHICLE into PLAINTIFF’S VEHICLE. . . .” (Compl., ¶ 15.) While these allegations infer negligence on Defendants’ part, they do not establish that Defendants intentionally caused the accident or acted with such conscious disregard of likely causing said accident. (See Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (c)(1); see also McDonell v. American Trust Co. (1955) 130 Cal.App.2d 296, 299-300 [“‘The actor’s conduct is in reckless disregard of the safety of another if he intentionally does an act or fails to do an act which it is his duty to the other to do, knowing or having reason to know of facts which would lead a reasonable man to realize that the actor’s conduct not only creates an unreasonable risk of bodily harm to the other but also involves a high degree of probability that substantial harm will result to him.’”].) Therefore, Defendants’ alleged conduct does not constitute intentional or malicious conduct. Accordingly, there is no basis for which to request punitive damages.

Plaintiff also argues that allegations inferring Defendant Earthscapes’ prior knowledge of Defendant Hernandez’s poor, negligent driving also are sufficient to support an award of punitive damages. (See Compl., ¶ 52-67.) The Court finds the allegations are simply too broad and conclusory to support an inference that Defendant Earthscapes had such prior knowledge. Accordingly, these allegations also do not provide a basis for which to request punitive damages.

The allegations set forth, accepted as true, are not sufficient to support an award of punitive damages. The motion is granted.

Because there is a reasonable possibility that Plaintiff could successfully amend the complaint, the motion will be granted with leave to amend.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Defendants’ Motion to Strike is GRANTED with leave to amend. Plaintiff has twenty days to file and serve an amended complaint. If Plaintiff is not amending the complaint, Defendants are to file and serve an answer to the complaint within thirty days.
