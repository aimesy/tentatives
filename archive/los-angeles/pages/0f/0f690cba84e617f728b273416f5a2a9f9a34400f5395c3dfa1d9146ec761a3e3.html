Attorney James Howard and the Law Offices of James Howard, counsel of record for defendant Caruso’s Cucina Italiana, seeks to be relieved as counsel for that party.  Counsel has filed and served the required forms MC-051, MC-052, and MC-053 on the defendant.

Case Number: 25CHCV04521    Hearing Date: August 10, 2026    Dept: F43

Dept. F43

Hearing Date: 08-10-26

Case # 25CHCV04521, Avery v. Choice Respicare, Inc. et al.

Trial Date: None set.

 

DEMURRER AND MOTION TO STRIKE

 

MOVING PARTIES:  Defendant Choice Respicare, Inc. and Prashant Sudhir Katte

RESPONDING PARTY:  Plaintiff Crystal Avery, by and through her successor-in-interest, Nykirah Thompson

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Order sustaining defendants’ demurrer to the Second Amended Complaint’s Third and Fourth Causes of Action.  Order striking alter ego allegations and the Fifth Cause of Action from the Second Amended Complaint.

 

RULING:  Demurrer is sustained with leave to amend as to the Third Cause of Action and without leave to amend as to the Fifth Cause of Action.  Demurrer is overruled as to the Fourth Cause of Action.  Motion to strike is denied as to the alter ego allegations and is moot concerning the Fifth Cause of Action.

 

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

 

Plaintiff Crystal Avery, by and through her successor-in-interest, Nykirah Thompson filed this dependent adult neglect action against defendants Choice Respicare, Inc. and Prashant Sudhir Katte on December 19, 2025.

 

On January 30, 2026, Ms. Thompson filed a First Amended Complaint (FAC), asserting (1) dependent adult abuse Welf. & Inst. Code, §§ 15600, et seq.;  (2) negligence;  (3) violation of Bus. & Prof. Code, §§ 17200, et seq.;  (4) violation of Bus. & Prof. Code, §§ 17500, et seq.;  and (5) violation of the Consumer Legal Remedies Act.  The court sustained a demurer to the FAC’s Third and Fourth Causes of Action with leave to amend and to the Fifth Cause of Action without leave to amend.  The court also granted in part Defendants’ motion to strike alter ego allegations with leave to amend.

 

On June 1, 2026, Ms. Thompson filed a Second Amended Complaint, asserting the same causes of action.  Thompson newly alleges defendant Katte along with other management defendants siphon scarce funds and assets away from residents at Choice Respicare’s facility through various accounts under the guise of inflated payments for supplies, rent, and other illusory management and administrative services.  (SAC ¶ 23.)  Because these funds should have gone to the payment of labor costs to ensure staffing needs of the residents were met, failing to hold Defendants liable under an alter ego theory would lead to an inequitable result.  (Id. ¶ 24.)  Furthermore, defendant Katte owned a 100% equity interest in defendant Choice Respicare, is the sole director of Choice Respicare, and solely exercises all corporate powers of the Choice Respicare’s facility as the sole director of licensee Choice Respicare.  (Id. ¶¶ 25-26.)  As such, defendant Katte owed several mandatory, nondelegable duties, such as providing nursing care to meet the needs of his residents and protecting the residents from health and safety hazards.  (Id. ¶ 27.)

 

Moreover, Ms. Thompson also includes new economic injury allegations to support her third and fourth causes of action.  According to Ms. Thompson, Defendants misrepresented that the nursing facility was adequately staffed with skilling nursing care while concealing that the facility was actually understaffed.  (SAC ¶ 98.)  Plaintiff relied on the representations and concealment and chose to be admitted as a resident at Defendants’ facility.  However, the understaffing resulted in Plaintiff receiving substandard care and suffering pain and injuries over an extended period, including developing stage II+ pressure sores.  Furthermore, Ms. Thompson claims Plaintiff’s property interest in her Medi-Cal benefits were diminished because she would have received better skilled-nursing treatment at another facility.  (Id. ¶ 99.)  According to Plaintiff, both issues constitute loss of property and an economic injury under Business and Professions Code sections 17200, et seq.

 

On July 6, 2026,  defendants Choice Respicare, Inc. and Prashant Sudhir Katte (Defendants) filed the instant demurrer to the Second Amended Complaint’s Third and Fourth Causes of Action.  On July 7, 2026, Defendants filed a motion to strike alter ego allegations and the Fifth Cause of Action.  Ms. Thompson filed oppositions, and Defendants replied.

 

 

MEET AND CONFER

 

Demurrers and motions to strike must be accompanied by a “meet and confer” declaration stating that the parties met and conferred “in person, by telephone, or by video conference” and stating that (1) the parties failed to reach an agreement resolving the issues raised in the demurrer and motion to strike;  or (2) that the party who filed the pleading subject to the demurrer or motion to strike failed to respond to the meet and confer request or failed to meet and confer in good faith.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 430.41, subd. (a)(3), 435.5, subd. (a)(3).)  In Department F43, meet and confer means in person or via phone, not by letter or email.  (Department F43 Courtroom Information, p. 2.)

 

Counsel telephonically met and conferred on June 9, 2026 but did not come to an agreement.  (Declaration of Michael W. Feenberg ¶ 4.)

 

 

ANALYSIS

 

A.  Demurrer

 

A party may respond to a pleading against it by demurrer based on one or more of eight enumerated grounds, including that “the pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action” and is uncertain, meaning “ambiguous and unintelligible.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subds. (e), (f).)  The grounds for demurring must be apparent from either the face of the complaint or a matter of which the court may take judicial notice.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.30, subd. (a); see also Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

 

The purpose of a demurrer is to challenge the sufficiency of a pleading “by raising questions of law.”  (Postley v. Harvey (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 280, 286.)  “In the construction of a pleading, for the purpose of determining its effect, its allegations must be liberally construed, with a view to substantial justice between the parties.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 452.)  The court “treat[s] the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law[.]”  (Berkley v. Dowds (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 518, 525.)  In applying these standards, the court liberally construes the complaint to determine whether a cause of action has been stated.  (Picton v. Anderson Union High School Dist. (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 726, 733.)

 

1.  Third Cause of Action:  Violation of Bus. & Prof. Code, §§ 17200, et seq.

 

Defendants contend Ms. Thompson has not and cannot allege an economic injury to support her unfair business practices claim.  California law has not recognized Medi-Cal benefits paid on behalf of beneficiaries as protected property interests.  To the extent that Plaintiff’s Medi-Cal benefits are a property interest, Plaintiff still lacks standing to support her UCL claim because the property interest was not injured—the amount Medi-Cal would pay Plaintiff remained unchanged and undiminished.  Ms. Thompson does not allege Plaintiff actually paid for her care or allege that she seeks injunctive relief or restitution.

 

In opposition, Ms. Thompson claims she has adequately alleged loss of money or property based on Defendants’ concealment and misrepresentations.  As a result of Defendants’ conduct Plaintiff “acquired in the transaction of being admitted to and becoming a resident of the facility less than she otherwise would have had she become a resident of another facility.”  Moreover, the SAC specifically states Plaintiff’s property interest in her Medi Cal benefits was diminished due to Defendants’ misconduct.

 

California’s Unfair Competition Law (UCL) prohibits unlawful, unfair or fraudulent business acts or practices.  (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 17200, et seq.)  “The UCL’s purpose is to protect both consumers and competitors by promoting fair competition in commercial markets for goods and services.”  (Kasky v. Nike, Inc. (2002) 27 Cal.4th 939, 949.)  “Its coverage is sweeping, embracing anything that can properly be called a business practice and that at the same time is forbidden by law.”  (Cel-Tech Commc’ns, Inc. v. L.A. Cellular Tel. Co. (1999) 20 Cal.4th 163, 180.)  A plaintiff may plead a UCL violation under the “unlawfulness” prong by pleading that a business practice violated a predicate federal, state, or local law.  (See Cel-Tech Commc’ns, Inc. v. Los Angeles Cellular Telephone. Co. (1999) 20 Cal.4th 163, 180.)  A business practice is unlawful if it violates laws other than the UCL.  (Farmers Ins. Exchange v. Superior Court (1993) 2 Cal.4th 337, 383.)  The complaint must allege facts sufficient to show violation of the predicate law and resulting harm.  (People v. McKale (1979) 25 Cal.3d 626, 635 [“Without supporting facts demonstrating the illegality of a rule or regulation, an allegation that it is in violation of a specific statute is purely conclusionary and insufficient to withstand demurrer.”].) 

 

To satisfy the standing requirements under the UCL, the complete must plead (1) loss or deprivation of money or property sufficient to qualify as an injury-in-fact and (2) show that the injury was caused by an unfair business practice.  (Kwikset Corp. v. Superior Court (2011) 51 Cal.4th 310, 323-324;  Demeter v. Taxi Computer Services, Inc. (2018) 21 Cal.App.5th 903, 915.)  “At the pleading stage, general factual allegations of injury resulting from the defendant’s conduct may suffice.”  (Kwikset Corp., supra, 51 Cal.4th at p. 327.)  The plaintiff fails to satisfy the causation prong if they would have suffered “the same harm whether or not a defendant complied with the law.”  (Daro v. Superior Court (2007) 151 Cal.App.4th 1079, 1099.)

 

Kwikset identified four non-exclusive ways a plaintiff may show economic injury: “(1) surrender in a transaction more, or acquire in a transaction less, than he or she otherwise would have; (2) have a present or future property interest diminished; (3) be deprived of money or property to which he or she has a cognizable claim; or (4) be required to enter into a transaction, costing money or property, that would otherwise have been unnecessary.”  (Kwikset Corp., supra, 51 Cal.4th at p. 323.)

 

Here, Ms. Thompson’s standing argument rests on two separate theories: (1) that Plaintiff acquired less in a transaction than she otherwise would have because the facility misrepresented its staffing adequacy, and (2) that Plaintiff holds a cognizable property interest in her Medi-Cal benefits.

 

In Moore v. Centrelake Medical Group, Inc. (2022), several patients alleged they relied on a medical facility’s false representations about data security in entering contracts for medical services, and that they accepted pricing terms they would not have accepted had they known the truth.  (83 Cal.App.5th 515, 527-530.)  The Court of Appeal held the patients adequately pleaded UCL standing, emphasizing the materiality of the misrepresentation to the plaintiff’s decision to enter the transaction is the key inquiry.  (Id. at pp. 528-529.)  However, the Moore patients paid money out of pocket rather than relied on Medi-Cal benefits.  But the payment in this instance relates more to the damages amount rather than the actual loss of property.

 

Under the “benefit of bargain” theory, Ms. Thompson adequately alleges Plaintiff acquired less in a transaction than she would have elsewhere.  Plaintiff became a resident of Defendants’ facility in reliance on representations about adequate and skilled nursing staf and reliance on Defendants’ concealment that the facility was not adequately staffed.  (SAC ¶  98.)  After entering the transaction and being admitted to the facility, Plaintiff received materially inferior care than what was promised with Medi-Cal paying for her treatments through benefit payments.  (Ibid.)  According to Ms. Thompson, the substandard care Plaintiff received was less than what she was promised and less than the care she would have received at another skilled facility that was adequately staffed.  (Ibid.)  As a result, Plaintiff developed various wounds that progressed into more serious health issues.  This demonstrates an economic injury.

 

The main issue raised by both Thompson and Defendant is whether benefits that Medi-Cal, rather than plaintiff, directly paid to Defendants for the services is lost money.  Beneficiaries of Med-Cal benefits hold a protected property interest in those benefits.  “[W]hen a person has a legally enforceable right to receive a government benefit provided certain facts exist, this right constitutes a property interest protected by due process.”  (American Federation of Labor v. Employment Dev. Dep’t (1979) 88 Cal.App.3d 811, 819 [quoting Skelly v. State Personnel Board (1975) 15 Cal.3d 194, 207].)  No California court has directly held that a Medi-Cal recipient possesses a property interest in Medi-Cal benefits for purposes of UCL standing under section 17204.  Thus, Plaintiff’s diminished value theory on its own does not establish an economic injury.

 

But the California Supreme Court has made it clear that economic injury is not limited to “out-of-pocket” expenditures.  (California Med. Ass’n v. Aetna Health of California, Inc. (2023) 14 Cal.5th 1075, 1089.)  In Clayworth v. Pfizer, Inc. (2010), the California Supreme Court held that retail pharmacies had standing to assert UCL claims against drug manufacturers for price-fixing even though the pharmacies had passed on the overcharges to their customers and thus suffered no net financial loss.  (49 Cal.4th 758, 789 [suffering economic injury even though passed charges on to customers to mitigate loss]; see also Campbell v. FPI Mgmt., Inc. (2024) 98 Cal.App.5th 1151, 1169 [parties were conflating the issues of standing and remedies]; Sarun v. Dignity Health (2014) 232 Cal.App.4th 1159, 1169-1170.)  Similarly here, even though Medi-Cal paid Defendants for Plaintiff’s care, this does not mean Plaintiff suffered no injury in the transaction.  Plaintiff received inadequate and substandard care to that promised, which is the economic harm.

 

Moreover, the transaction here is Plaintiff’s residency at Defendants’ facility, choosing Defendants’ facility over others in reliance on Defendants’ representations.  (SAC ¶¶ 96-99.)  Medi-Cal funding the transaction does not eliminate Plaintiff’s interest in receiving the quality and type of service she was promised. No California appellate court has held that a Medi-Cal beneficiary categorically lacks UCL standing because Medi-Cal paid for services or products which caused an injury.

 

This is distinct from the allegations at issue in Williamson v. Genentech, Inc. (2023) 94 Cal.App.5th 410.  There the plaintiff alleged a UCL claim against a pharmaceutical company because his insurer paid the additional costs beyond a deductible paid by the plaintiff.  (Id. at p. 415.)  Because the plaintiff would have paid the same deductible regardless of the size of the medicine vial, the court found that plaintiff did not suffer an injury.  Nor could plaintiff borrow an injury from his health insurer who paid the difference.  (Id. at pp. 415-416 [attempting to apply the collateral source rule].)

 

In contrast here, even though Medi-Cal paid all Plaintiff’s benefits, Plaintiff alleges she would have received more (standard and qualify service) for those same benefits from a different facility. In other words, but for Defendants’ misrepresentations, Plaintiff would have chosen a different facility to receive proper care.

 

Thus, the court finds that Thompson adequately alleges an economic injury to support her Third Cause of Action.

 

However, Thompson again fails to allege whether she seeks restitution or injunctive relief for her supposed economic injury.  (See Cortez v. Purolator Air Filtration Products Co. (2000) 23 Cal.4th 163, 173-178 [prevailing plaintiffs are generally limited to injunctive relief or restitution of funds gained through unfair practices].)  Although Plaintiff may have a some sort of property interest in the Medi-Cal benefits, Plaintiff cites no case law where the plaintiff may recover restitution for such benefits.

 

Therefore, the court sustains Defendants’ demurrer to the Third Cause of Action.  The court will allow Ms. Thompson one final opportunity to amend the complaint to allege her Third Cause of Action.

 

2.  Fourth Cause of Action:  Violation of Bus. & Prof. Code, §§ 17500, et seq.

 

Defendant contends the Fourth Cause of Action fails because Thompson does not adequately allege Plaintiff suffered a loss or deprivation of money or property.

 

Because the court finds the Plaintiff has adequately alleged an economic injury through receiving less in her residency transaction with Defendants than promised, the court finds Defendant’s argument lacks merit.

 

Therefore, the court overrules Defendants’ demurrer to the Fourth Cause of Action.

 

                        3.  Fifth Cause of Action:  Violation of Consumer Legal Remedies Act

 

The court previously sustained Defendants’ demurrer to Thompson’s Consumer Legal Remedies Act claim without leave to amend, and Thompson concedes that the Fifth Cause of Action was included in the Second Amended Complaint by mistake.

 

Therefore, the court again sustains the demurrer to the Fifth Cause of Action without leave to amend.

 

 

            B.  Motion to Strike

 

“Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading may serve and file a notice of motion to strike the whole or any part thereof.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 435.)  A court may, upon motion or at any time, strike from the complaint “any irrelevant, false, or improper matter” or “all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court.”  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 436, subds. (a)-(b), 187; Greshko v. County of Los Angeles (1987) 194 Cal.App.3d 822, 830 [noting the court’s inherent power to limit the focus of the proceedings to the issues framed by the remaining viable pleadings].)  An “irrelevant matter” is an allegation that “is not essential to the statement of a claim or defense,” “is neither pertinent to nor supported by an otherwise sufficient claim or defense,” or “a demand for judgment requesting relief not supported by the allegations of the complaint or cross-complaint.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 431.10, subds. (b)-(c).)  The court must read the factual allegations in the complaint as a whole and assume their truth.  (Clauson v. Superior Court (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1253, 1255.) 

 

Defendants move to strike the following from the Second Amended Complaint:

 

  • Paragraphs 19 through 30 (Alter Ego allegations); and
  • Paragraphs 106 through 115 (Fifth Cause of Action).

 

                        1.  Alter Ego Allegations

 

Defendants contend the court should strike Thompson’s alter ego allegations because she alleges no facts to suggest any defendants are using defendant Choice Respicare as a shell entity, or that an inequitable result would follow unless the corporate form was disregarded.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff contends her new allegations specifically allege the defendant Katte owns all equity in defendant Choice Respicare, that Defendants siphon scarce funds and assets under false pretenses, and that defendant Katte is the sole director of Choice Respicare.

 

A party may sue under the alter ego doctrine, “where a corporation is used by an individual or individuals, or by another corporation, to perpetrate fraud, circumvent a statute, or accomplish some other wrongful or inequitable purpose[.]”  (See Capon v. Monopoly Game LLC (2011) 193 Cal.App.4th 344, 357.)  “[A] court may disregard the corporate entity and treat the corporation’s acts as if they were done by the persons actually controlling the corporation.”  (Id.)  A defendant cannot claim surprise of a plaintiff’s reliance on the alter ego theory where much discovery on the issue has occurred and plaintiff bases its claims on the relationship between several defendants.  (Mesler v. Bragg Management (2010) 39 Cal.3d 290, 297.)

 

To adequately allege an alter ego theory of liability, the plaintiff must allege facts showing (1) a unity of interest and ownership so that the separate personalities of the corporation and the individual no longer exist and (2) an unjust or inequitable result will follow if the corporation is treated as the sole actor.  (Leek v. Cooper (2011) 194 Cal.App.4th 399, 415.)  “An allegation that a person owns all of the corporate stock and makes all of the management decisions is insufficient to cause the court to disregard the corporate entity.”  (Id.)  The complaint must contain facts showing improper domination of the corporation as a basis for imposing liability on the individuals.

 

For the unity of interest element, courts examine various factors including commingling of funds and assets, failure to segregate corporate funds, unauthorized diversion of corporate assets for personal use, treatment of corporate assets as the individual’s own, failure to maintain corporate formalities, identical equitable ownership, use of the same offices and employees, and whether the corporation is adequately capitalized.  (Sonora Diamond Corp. v. Superior Court (2000) 83 Cal.App.4th 523, 538-539.)  The plaintiff must allege “specific” facts  demonstrating these factors rather than making conclusory allegations.  Common ownership alone is insufficient.  (Baize v. Eastridge Companies, LLC (2006) 142 Cal.App.4th 293, 303.)

 

For the “inequitable result” element, the plaintiff must allege facts showing that adherence to the fiction of separate corporate existence would sanction a fraud or promote injustice.  (F. Hoffman-La Roche, Ltd. v. Superior Court (2005) 130 Cal.App.4th 782, 797-798;  see also, e.g., JPV I, L.P. v. Koetting (2023) 88 Cal.App.5th 172, 195, 200 [alleging the corporation was used as a mere shell or instrumentality for the individual’s purposes, the corporate assets were diverted or depleted leaving creditors unable to collect, or the corporate form was used to evade existing obligations].)

 

Ms. Thompson again alleges defendant Choice Respicare, Inc. and management defendants Katte (Choice Respicare’s Administrator, Director, Chief Executive Officer, Secretary and Chief Financial Officer) and Does 51 through 100 are alter egos of one another because they operated in such a way as to make their individual identities indistinguishable.  (SAC ¶ 19.)  Choice Respicare, Katte, and the other management defendants committed tortious acts and omissions in concert with one another (underfunding and understaffing the facility) to further their common design and agreement to maximize profits.  (Id. ¶ 20.)  For example, Defendants through Katte, Elliot Salynsky, and others would retain as many residents as possible for the purpose of generating revenue from the providing “skilled” nursing care and other services where were offered but were non-existent or subpar.  (Id. ¶ 17.)  As part of this scheme, Defendants were funded by $672,023 in Medi-Cal funds.  (Id.)  According to Ms. Thompson, Defendants aided and abetted each other in accomplishing the wrongful acts.

 

Ms. Thompson newly alleges that the management defendants siphoned scare funds and assets away from residents of the facility through “administration” accounts under the guise of artificially inflated payments for supplies, rent, and other non-clinical, illusory management and/or administrative services.  (SAC ¶ 23.)  These funds were intended to cover labor costs to sufficiently meet staffing needs for the residents.  (Id. ¶ 24.)  Allowing the management defendants to pocket these funds while providing inadequate services to residents will lead to an inequitable result.

 

Moreover, Ms. Thompson newly alleges defendant Katte owns a 100% equity interest in defendant Choice Respicare based on filings with the California Department of Public Health.  (SAC ¶ 25.)  As the sole director and officer of Choice Respicare, Katte solely manages all business and affairs and directs and exercises all corporate powers of the facility.  (Id. ¶¶ 3, 25-28.)  Katte’s control and ownership of Choice Respicare and his alleged participation in the “siphoning of funds” scheme demonstrate a unity of interest.  Based on these allegations, the court infers that the inequitable result would be that only Choice Respicare would be held liable for the wrongful conduct even though Katte and the Doe management defendants used Choice Respicare as a shell entity to perform their siphoning scheme.

 

The court finds these facts constitute alter ego allegations.

 

Therefore, the court denies Defendants’ motion to strike alter ego allegations from the Second Amended Complaint.

 

 

CONCLUSION and ORDER

 

Demurrer is sustained with leave to amend as to the Third Cause of Action and without leave to amend as to the Fifth Cause of Action.  Demurrer is overruled as to the Fourth Cause of Action.

 

Motion to strike is denied as to the alter ego allegations and is moot concerning the Fifth Cause of Action.

 

Ms. Thompson may file and served an amended complaint within 20 days of this order.

 

Defendants to give notice.


Case Number: 26CHCV01046    Hearing Date: August 10, 2026    Dept: F43

Dept. F43

Hearing Date: 08-10-26

Case # 26CHCV01046, Oldenburg v. Extra Space Storage, Inc.

Trial Date: None set.

 

MOTION TO STRIKE

 

MOVING PARTY:  Defendant Extra Space Storage, Inc.

RESPONDING PARTY:  Plaintiff Carissa Oldenburg, in pro per

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Order striking punitive damages from the First Amended Complaint (paragraphs 62, 75, 77, 87, and Prayer for Relief Item number 5).

 

RULING:  Motion is granted in part with leave to amend.

 

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

 

On March 13, 2026, plaintiff Carissa Oldenburg (Plaintiff) filed this action against defendant Extra Space Storage, Inc. (Defendant), alleging damages from Defendant’s violation of California’s Self-Service Storage Facility Act.  Plaintiff filed a First Amended Complaint on May 7, 2026, asserting seven causes of action:  (1) violation of the Self-Service Storage Act (Bus. & Prof. Code, §§ 21700, et seq.); (2) conversion; (3) Unfair Business Practices; (4) negligence; (5) failed accounting; (6) claim and delivery (Code Civ. Proc., § 512.010, et seq.); and (7) declaratory relief.  Plaintiff is self-represented.

 

On June 8, 2026, Defendant filed the instant motion to strike punitive damages from the First Amended Complaint.  On July 22, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition, and Plaintiff replied on July 30, 2026. 

 

 

MEET AND CONFER

 

Before filing a motion to strike, the parties must meet and confer “in person, by telephone, or by video conference,” and the moving party must file a “meet and confer” declaration discussing the outcome of the meeting.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 435.5, subd. (a)(3);  see also Department F43 Courtroom Information, p. 2 [“meet and confer” does not mean emails and letters].)

 

The “meet and confer” requirement is not met because defense counsel and Plaintiff only exchanged emails instead of meeting directly.  (Declaration of Dylan F. Derakhshanian ¶¶ 2-5, Exhs. A-D.)  However, the court still considers the merits of the moving papers.

 

 

 

 

THE FIRST AMENDED COMPLAINT’S ALLEGATIONS

 

Beginning in March of 2025, Plaintiff and Defendant entered a lease agreement for a self-service storage unit at Defendant’s facility at 26676 Valley Center Drive, Santa Clarita, CA 91351.  (FAC ¶ 1.)  Plaintiff temporarily fell delinquent on payments in May of 2025, and Defendant issued lien notices for auctions to occur in September and October of 2025.  (Id. ¶ 2.)  Plaintiff cured the delinquency by paying the outstanding amount in cash, and the auctions never occurred.  At the time of payment, Defendant’s facility manager, Ms. Brandeis, instructed Plaintiff that all future payments must be made in person and in cash only.  (Id. ¶¶ 3-4.)

 

At some later time, Plaintiff again became delinquent on her payments, but she never received a compliant Preliminary Notice of Lien, Notice of Lien and Foreclosure, or Declaration of Opposition to Lien Sale from Defendant.  (FAC ¶ 5.)  On December 1, 2025, facility manager Breanna Brandeis left a voicemail with Plaintiff, stating her unit was scheduled for an auction on December 3, 2025.  (Id. ¶ 6.)  Plaintiff did not discover the voicemail until January 8, 2026.  The storage unit was sold without Plaintiff’s knowledge or statutory notice on December 3, 2025.  (Id. ¶ 10.)  Also on January 8, 2026, Plaintiff called the facility and texted Ms. Brandeis asking about the proceeds of the sale.  (Id. ¶¶ 7-8.)  Ms. Brandeis responded that the information could not be provided because Plaintiff no longer rented a unit at the facility.  (Id. ¶ 8.)  After Plaintiff invoked her statutory rights (citing Civ. Code, § 21707(b)), a partial receipt was emailed to Plaintiff.  (Id. ¶ 9.)  However, the receipt did not provide detailed accounting.

 

On January 9, 2026, Plaintiff personally delivered a written statutory demand letter to facility manager Ms. Brandeis at the Santa Clarita facility.  (FAC ¶ 11, Exh. A.)  Ms. Bradeis stated she could not accept the demand, and Plaintiff set the letter in front of Ms. Brandeis on the counter and left.  (Id. ¶¶ 12-13.)  The letter include a statutory demand for a complete accounting and a demand for the immediate preservation of all evidence arising from the auction and disposition of Plaintiff’s stored property.  (Id. ¶ 14.)  Defendant did not respond to the demand letter.

 

On January 13, 2026, Plaintiff personally served Defendant’s registered agent for service of process, CT Corporation System, with a written “preservation of evidence” notice and statutory demand concerning Defendant’s unlawful lien sale and wrongful conversion of Plaintiff’s stored property.  (FAC ¶ 16, Exh. B.)  Defendant did not respond.  (Id. ¶ 17.)

 

On January 18, 2026, Plaintiff submitted a complaint to the Better Business Bureau.  (FAC ¶ 18.)  Defendant responded but failed to provide proof that mandatory disclosure and statutory advertising requirements were satisfied.  (Id. ¶¶ 19, 22, Attachment Nos. 1, 2, 3, 4.)  Instead, Defendant’s response incorrectly asserted that return of personal items is “up to the discretion of the auction buyer” and improperly relied on phone calls for putting Plaintiff on notice of the lien sale.  (Id. ¶¶ 20-22.)

 

In response to Defendant’s Better Business Bureau letter and attachments, Plaintiff sent a formal written demand to Defendant’s legal department and corporate headquarters by U.S. certified mail.  (FAC ¶ 48.)  The demand detailed Defendant’s failure to provide a compliant lien notices, lawful sale advertising, purchaser identification, surplus accounting, and return of statutorily protected personal property.  (Id. ¶ 50.)  Despite the many written demands, Defendant knowingly refused provide the requested information.  (Id. ¶ 51.)

 

Defendant’s statutory violations render the lien sale void and the transfer of title to Plaintiff’s property is ineffective.  (FAC ¶ 56.)  The fair market value of Plaintiff’s wrongfully converted personal property is conservatively estimated at a minimum of $30,000.00, and Plaintiff seeks punitive damages for Defendant’s malicious conduct and ratification of the improper conduct.  (Id. ¶¶ 62, 76.)

 

 

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENTS

 

Defendant contends Plaintiff alleges conclusory statements without facts showing its principals or managing agents authorized or approved any fraudulent, malicious, or oppressive conduct.  According to Defendant, Plaintiff alleges violations of the Storage Act which are at most show negligence.  Furthermore, Defendant also contends punitive damages are disfavored and should be struck without leave to amend.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff contends she alleges ultimate facts to support punitive damages.  According to Plaintiff, her allegations demonstrate Defendant’s intentional sale, transfer, concealment of sale information, and refusal to return Plaintiff’s personal property demonstrate Defendant’s “conscious disregard” for her statutory and property rights.  The allegations concerning Defendant’s post-lien sale conduct, lack of response to Plaintiff’s demands, and inadequate response to Plaintiff’s demands, go beyond accidental paperwork error or ordinary negligence.  Next, Paragraph 76 adequately alleges corporate knowledge and ratification of the wrongful conduct through service to Defendant’s registered agent for service and letters mailed to Defendant’s legal department and corporate headquarters followed by a refusal to investigate, correct, disclose, or remediate the disputed lien.  Plaintiff also alleges she will be able to identify the specific corporate officers through discovery.  Finally, paragraph 62, which addresses the fair market value of Plaintiff’s converted property and is relevant to Plaintiff’s tort and statutory causes of action, rather than the punitive damages claim alone.

 

 

ANALYSIS

 

A.  Motion to Strike

 

“Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading may serve and file a notice of motion to strike the whole or any part thereof.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 435.)  A court may, upon motion or at any time, strike from the complaint “any irrelevant, false, or improper matter” or “all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court.”  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 436, subds. (a)-(b), 187; Greshko v. County of Los Angeles (1987) 194 Cal.App.3d 822, 830 [noting the court’s inherent power to limit the focus of the proceedings to the issues framed by the remaining viable pleadings].)  An “irrelevant matter” is an allegation that “is not essential to the statement of a claim or defense,” “is neither pertinent to nor supported by an otherwise sufficient claim or defense,” or “a demand for judgment requesting relief not supported by the allegations of the complaint or cross-complaint.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 431.10, subds. (b)-(c).)  The court must read the factual allegations in the complaint as a whole and assume their truth.  (Clauson v. Superior Court (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1253, 1255.) 

 

Defendant moves to strike punitive damages from the First Amended Complaint:

 

·         Paragraphs 62, 75, 77, and 87; and

·         Prayer for Relief, Page 29, item number 5.

 

            1.  Punitive Damages

 

A motion to strike punitive damages may lie where the alleged facts do not rise to the level of “malice, fraud, or oppression” required to support punitive damages.  (Turman v. Turning Point of Central California, Inc. (2010) 191 Cal.App.4th 53, 63.)

 

Although Plaintiff adequately alleges ratification by Defendant’s corporate officers (refusing to investigate, correct, disclose, account, or remediate the supposed sale) (FAC ¶ 76), the court finds Plaintiff’s allegations concerning Ms. Breanna Brandeis and the defective notices do not demonstrate malicious or oppressive conduct to support punitive damages.

 

To state a prima facie claim for punitive damages, a complaint must set forth specific facts demonstrating the elements stated in Civil Code section 3294.  (College Hospital, Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 721; see also Brousseau v. Jarrett (1977) 73 Cal.App.3d 864, 872.)  Malice is “conduct intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.”  (Id. at p. 725; Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (c)(1).)  Oppression is “despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person’s rights.”  (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (c)(2).)  Fraud is “an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known to the defendant with the intention on the part of the defendant of thereby depriving a person of property or legal rights or otherwise causing injury.”  (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (c)(3).)  “Malice and oppression may be inferred from the circumstances of a defendant’s conduct.”  (J. R. Norton Co. v. General Teamsters, Warehousemen & Helpers Union, Local 890 (1989) 208 Cal.App.3d 430, 444.)

 

Conclusory statements characterizing a defendant’s conduct as intentional, willful, and fraudulent are insufficient to plead fraud or malice.  (Brousseau v. Jarrett (1977) 73 Cal.App.3d 864, 872.)  The complaint must state “specific facts showing that defendant’s conduct was oppressive, fraudulent or malicious (e.g., that defendant acted with the intent to inflict great bodily harm on plaintiff or to destroy plaintiff’s property or reputation).”  (Croskey, et al., Cal. Prac. Guide: Insurance Litigation Ch. 13-C (Thomson Reuters, 2016) ¶ 13:197.2; see Anschutz Entertainment Group, Inc. v. Snepp (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 598, 643 [allegations that defendant’s conduct was “intentional, and done willfully, maliciously, with ill will towards Plaintiffs, and with conscious disregard for Plaintiff’s rights” did not satisfy specific pleading requirements].)  A complaint must plead ultimate facts to support punitive damages.  (Clauson v. Superior Court (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1253, 1255; Antelope Valley Groundwater Cases (2020) 59 Cal.App.5th 241, 265 [“[T]he term ‘ultimate fact’ generally refers to a core fact, such as an essential element of a claim.”].)

 

Plaintiff seeks punitive damages based on her “conversion” cause of action.  The elements of conversion are that (1) plaintiff owns or has a right to possession of personal property at the time of conversion, (2) defendant unlawfully exercised dominion over the property, and (3) plaintiff suffered financial harm or loss due to defendant’s actions.  (Regent Alliance Ltd. v. Rabizadeh (2014) 231 Cal.App.4th 1177, 1181.)  Because conversion is a strict liability tort, “the tort consists in the breach of an absolute duty,” rather than the defendant’s good faith, lack of knowledge, and motive.  (Ibid.)  However, Plaintiff must still allege ultimate facts that demonstrate fraud, malice, or oppression to support punitive damages.

 

Plaintiff alleges Defendant intentionally and substantially interfered with her ownership, possession, and right to possession of the storage unit by preventing her from acing the unit, conducting and causing a lien sale without strict compliance with California’s Self-Storage Facility Act, transferring and disposing of Plaintiff’s property, refusing to disclose the identity of the purchaser, refusing to provide sale records and a complete accounting, and refusing to return protected personal property after Plaintiff demanded its return.  (FAC ¶ 82.)  According to Plaintiff, because Defendant did not strictly comply with statutory requirements governing non-judicial self-storage lien sales, Defendant lacked lawful authority to sell, transfer, or dispose of Plaintiff’s property, making the sale void and ineffective.  (Id. ¶ 85.)

 

California’s Self-Storage Facility Act grants a self-storage facility owner a lien on all personal property stored at the facility for unpaid rent, late fees, and other charges incurred under the rental agreement.  (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 21702.) 

 

Based on the exhibits attached to the First Amended Complaint and the attachments within the complaint itself, Defendant did not have a right to exercise dominion over Plaintiff’s property and unit because the required lien notices did not comply with Business and Professions Code sections 21703 and 21705.

 

Plaintiff made her last rent payment on August 26, 2025, but failed to make the next payment on September 25, 2025.  (FAC, Exh. C at p. 6.)  Plaintiff does not oppose this information.

 

                        The preliminary lien notice was defective.

 

Section 21703 governs the preliminary lien notice.  When the renter or occupant’s charges remain unpaid for 14 consecutive days, the owner may terminate the occupant’s right to use the storage space by sending a notice that includes (1) an itemized statement of amounts owed, (2) a statement that the occupant’s right to use the storage space will terminate on a specified date (not less than 14 days after mailing the notice) unless all sums due are paid before a specified date, (3) a notice that the occupant may be denied access to the storage space after the termination date if sums are not paid and that an owner’s lien may be imposed thereafter, and (4) the name, street address, and telephone number of the owner or their designated agent whom the occupant may contact to respond to the notice.  (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 21703, subds. (a)-(d).)

 

On October 10, 2025, Defendant mailed Plaintiff a “Preliminary Lien Notice” informing her that (1) she was 14 days late on rent and other charges listed, (2) providing the name of the owner (Defendant’s name) and Defendant’s contact information for Plaintiff’s questions about her account, (3) stating if not paid within 14 days, her right to use the storage space would be terminated, she would be denied access to the storage unit, and an owner’s lien would be imposed on her storage property.  (FAC, Attachment No. 1.)  The notice also stated that if Plaintiff did not pay the current amount due by her “monthly anniversary date,” she would owe an additional month’s rent, late fees, and tax and insurance.  Defendant listed a total due of $76.20:  $55 unpaid rent, $10 unpaid fees, and $11.20 in unpaid insurance.  However, the notice did not specify the payment due date or the date on which Plaintiff’s right to use would terminate.  Section 21703 expressly and unambiguously requires the facility owner to state these dates.  Because the preliminary lien notice did not strictly comply with Section 21703(b)-(c), the Preliminary Lien Notice is deficient in violation of the Self-Storage Act.

 

The notice of lien was defective.

 

Section 21705 provides that if the preliminary lien notice has been sent and the total sum due has not been paid by the termination date specified in the preliminary lien notice, the lien attaches and the owner may deny access to the space, enter it, and remove property to safekeeping.  (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 21705, subd. (a).)  Upon the taking of such actions, the facility owner must mail to the occupant’s last known address a notice of lien sale which states:  (A) the occupant’s right to use the storage space has terminated and that the occupant no longer has access to the stored property; (B) the stored property is subject to a lien, the current amount of the lien, and the that the lien will continue to increase if rent is not paid; (C) the property will be sold to satisfy the lien after a specified date that is not less than 14 days from the date of mailing the notice, unless the occupant executes and returns by certified mail a declaration in opposition to lien sale; (D) the occupant may regain full use of the space by paying the full lien amount before the date specified in subparagraph C; and (E) “any excess proceeds of the sale over the lien amount and costs of sale will be retained by the owner and may be reclaimed by the occupant or claimed by another person at any time for a period of one year from the sale and that thereafter the proceeds will escheat to the county in which the sale is to take place.”  (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 21705, subd. (b)(1).)  The owner must also mail the occupant a “blank declaration in opposition to lien sale” that complies with the statutory code.  (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 21705, subd. (b)(2).)

 

On November 3, 2025, Defendant mailed a “Notice of Lien and Foreclosure” along with a blank “Declaration in Opposition to Lien Sale.”  (FAC, Attachment No. 2.)  However, the notice is dated November 1, 2025.  The notice stated Plaintiff’s right to use her unit was terminated and access to the unit and her stored property would continue to be denied; the stored property was subject to a $240 lien that would continue to increase if rent was not paid; Plaintiff’s property would be sold at an auction on December 3, 2025, to satisfy the lien if the lien amount was not paid within 15 days of the due date November 1, 2025 unless Plaintiff executed and returned the declaration of opposition; and includes the statements required by sub-subdivisions (D) and (E).  However, the notice is defective because the specified due date and the notice date itself are two days before Defendant mailed the notice.  Fifteen days from November 1, 2025 is November 16, 2025, but fifteen days from November 3, 2025 is November 18.  Either way, the notice is ambiguous as to when Plaintiff must pay the lien or return the declaration to stop the auction. 

 

The based on the attachments in the First Amended Complaint, the lien sale advertisements complied with Section 21707(a)(3).

 

After expiration of the time given to in the notice of lien sale, the owner must publish an advertisement of the sale “[o]nce in a newspaper of general circulation published in the public notice district where the sale is to be held or in the county where the self-service storage facility is located and once on any publicly accessible internet website that customarily conducts or advertises online auctions or sales.  The online advertisement shall remain online for seven days before the sale.”  (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 21707, subd. (a)(1)(B).)  “The advertisement shall include the name of the person on whose account the goods are being stored and the name and location of the storage facility.”  (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 21707, subd. (a)(3).)

 

Here, a screenshot taken on November 25, 2025, of the website Auctions-Storage.com, lists Defendant’s name, facility address, the date of the auction, the time of the auction, the method of payment (cash at the time of sale), the unit number, Plaintiff’s name, a general description of Plaintiff’s property (Home items garage items), and the website where bidding occurs, “www.storagetreasures.com.”  (FAC, Attachment No. 3.)  The newspaper advertisement was published once on November 21, 2025, in the Daily Journal but failed to describe the property to be sold.  However, the advertisement did include the location of the property, the name of the property owners, the website for bidding, and the type of payment allowed.

 

Based on the Plaintiff’s allegations and the images included in the First Amended Complaint, the preliminary lien and lien notices were noncompliant.  As a result, Plaintiff had insufficient notice concerning the specific dates by when she was cure the delinquency and payoff the lien.  Nor is it clear when and whether the notices were mailed to Plaintiff according to the statute.  Thus, Defendant lacked authority to exercise dominion over Plaintiff’s personal property and to dispossess Plaintiff of her personal property by lien sale.

 

Although this establishes conversion and violations of the Self-Storage Act, the defective notices and Ms. Brandeis waiting until after Plaintiff invoked her rights to send the receipt, do not rise to level of intentional or reckless conduct done with a disregard for Plaintiff’s rights.  As alleged, Defendant’s pre-sale conduct amounts to nothing more than negligent conduct because Plaintiff did not raise objections to the sale until over a month after it occurred.  (Krusi v. Bear, Stearns & Co. (1983) 144 Cal.App.3d 664, 679 [negligence, gross negligence, and reckless are insufficient to support punitive damages].)  Plaintiff alleges no intentional or reckless pre-sale conduct by Defendant which demonstrates Defendant or Ms. Brandeis intentionally or consciously acted with disregard to Plaintiff’s rights to her property.  Defendant’s responses to Plaintiff’s complaint to the Better Business Bureau (FAC, Exh. C.), demonstrates that Defendant believed it had complied with the Self-Storage Act, even providing copies of the defective notices to prove its compliance.  Furthermore, Plaintiff cites no case law or statutory authority which requires Defendant to provide her with a full accounting of the auction or sale.  Even still, after Plaintiff invoked her statutory rights, Defendant, through Ms. Brandeis provided Plaintiff with a receipt from the sale.

 

Finally, the court finds that Plaintiff adequately alleges ratification.  Under a ratification theory, the complaint must allege facts showing that after the alleged incident, the employer’s officer, director, or managing agent became aware of the wrongful conduct and approved, adopted, or failed to investigate and discipline the employee under circumstances showing a conscious disregard.  “[R]atification may be inferred from the fact that the employer, after being informed of the employee’s actions, does not fully investigate and fails to repudiate the employee’s conduct by redressing the harm done and punishing or discharging the employee.”  (Fisher v. San Pedro Peninsula Hospital (1989) 214 Cal.App.3d 590, 621, citation omitted.)  The complaint must allege that a corporate officer or managing agent ratified the wrongful conduct and allege the specific conduct beyond merely failing to terminate the employee.  (Delfino v. Agilent Technologies, Inc. (2006) 145 Cal.App.4th 790, 811-812.)

 

Here, Plaintiff alleges she personally served Defendant with demand letters on January 9, 13 and February 26, 2026, and filed a BBB complaint on January 18, 2026, along with several responses.  (FAC ¶¶ 11, 16, 18, 48, Exhs. A, B, C.)  Although Defendant responded to the complaint with documentation and claiming it complied with its statutory duties, Plaintiff alleges that after Defendant had actual notice of the defective notices, its corporate officers ratified the notices and wrongful conversion of her property by failing and refusing to investigate, correct, disclose, account, preserve, or remediate the sale.  (Id. ¶¶ 76-77, Exh. C.)  Defendant’s failure to act after receiving Plaintiff’s demand letters and complaint shows a conscious disregard for Plaintiff’s right to possession of her property despite having notice that its notices were defective.  However, because the underlying conduct (the defective pre-sale notices) is negligent as alleged, Plaintiff fails to establish a valid basis for punitive damages.

 

Therefore, the court grants in part Defendant’s motion to strike punitive damages from the First Amended Complaint with leave to amend.  The court strikes the following from the First Amended Complaint: Paragraphs 75, 77, and 87, and Prayer for Relief item number 5.  The court does not strike paragraph 62 in full but does strike Page 20, line 8 “punitive damages,”.

 

The court notes Plaintiff’s opposition lacks sufficient legal authority to support her arguments.  Absent a good faith basis for modifying or extending an existing law, litigants are generally prohibited from asserting a position in litigation without authority.  (See, e.g., In re Estate of Randall (1924) 194 Cal. 725, 728-729 [“Contentions supported neither by argument nor by citation of authority are deemed to be without foundation, and to have been abandoned.”];  see also Lafferty v. Wells Fargo Bank (2013) 213 Cal. App. 4th 545, 571-572 [“When a point is asserted without argument and authority for the proposition, ‘it is deemed to be without foundation and requires no discussion by the reviewing court.’”];  Atchley v. City of Fresno (1984) 151 Cal. App. 3d 635, 647 [“Where a point is merely asserted by appellant's counsel without any argument of or authority for the proposition, it is deemed to be without foundation and requires no discussion by the reviewing court.”].)

 

Although Plaintiff is self-represented, Plaintiff must properly support her contentions to the same standard as an attorney.  (Nuño v. California State University, Bakersfield (2020) 47 Cal.App.5th 799, 811, citation omitted [“[A] self-represented litigant who is not indigent ‘must expect and receive the same treatment as if represented by an attorney—no different, no better, no worse.’”].)

 

CONCLUSION and ORDER

 

Motion to strike is granted in part with leave to amend.

MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT

 

MOVING PARTY:  Defendant Raymond Mateyko, Trustee of the Raymond Mateyko Living Trust, in pro per

RESPONDING PARTY:  Plaintiff Laurie Mateyko, in pro per

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Order granting summary judgment for defendant on plaintiff’s complaint.

 

RULING:  Motion is denied without prejudice.

 

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

 

On June 12, 2026, plaintiff Laurie Mateyko (Plaintiff) filed this action seeking to prohibit defendant Raymond Mateyko, Trustee of the Raymond Mateyko Living Trust from evicting her from property located at 21115 Chase Street in Canoga Park, CA (the Property).  Plaintiff asserts a violation of Civ. Code, § 789.3 (unlawful lockout), wrongful eviction, conversion, and trespass to chattels.  No answer was filed.

 

On June 16, 2026, the court granted Plaintiff a temporary restraining order, directing Defendant to immediately restore Plaintiff’s possession of and access to the Property, to refrain from interfering with, obstructing, or denying Plaintiff access to the Property, to refrain from selling, transferring, or giving any of Plaintiff’s property at the Property, and to refrain from re-locking, re-securing, or otherwise excluding Plaintiff from the Property.  (6/6/2026 Minute Order.)

 

On June 30, 2026, Plaintiff and Defendant appeared at a hearing where the court scheduled the Case Management Conference for August 14, 2026.  (06/30/2026 Minute Order.)  Both parties are self-represented.

 

On July 13, 2026, Defendant filed the instant motion for summary judgment on the complaint.  Plaintiff filed an objection to the motion on July 20, 2026.  No reply was filed.

 

On July 28, 2026, Plaintiff filed a request for entry of default as to Defendant, which the Clerk of Court granted that same day.  (07/28/2026 Minute Order.)  A copy of the request was mailed to Defendant that same day.  (Id. at p. 2.)

 

 

 

 

 

SUMMARY OF ARGUMENTS

 

Defendant contends the undisputed evidence demonstrates Plaintiff cannot establish a landlord-tenant relationship exists because the Plaintiff never entered a written or oral lease agreement.  Without establishing this relationship, Plaintiff cannot prove her claims.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff contends the motion is premature because it was filed 31 days after Plaintiff’s general appearance and the motion is procedurally improper because Plaintiff was never served.  Furthermore, the supporting declarations were served in advance as templates, the undisputed material facts misrepresent the supporting declarations, and the Defendant is currently in default.

 

 

ANALYSIS

 

A.  Motion for Summary Judgment

 

The purpose of a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication “is to provide courts with a mechanism to cut through the parties’ pleadings in order to determine whether, despite their allegations, trial is in fact necessary to resolve their dispute.”  (Aguilar v. Atl. Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.)  “Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (c), requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.”  (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.) 

 

1.  Defendant’s motion is invalid because the motion was filed before the 60-day cutoff, the noticed hearing is scheduled to occur before the 60-day cutoff.

 

A party cannot file a motion for summary judgment until at least “60 days have elapsed since the general appearance” of the party against whom the motion is directed, unless the court allows an earlier motion based on a showing of “good cause.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (a)(1).)  A Plaintiff makes a general appearance in an action when the plaintiff files a complaint.  (See State v. Lyons (1885) 67 Cal. 380, 384 [“The plaintiff appears when he files his complaint, subscribed by himself or his attorney.”]; Mansour v. Superior Court (1995) 38 Cal.App.4th 1750, 1756 [“A general appearance occurs where a party, either directly or through counsel, participates in an action in some manner which recognizes the authority of the court to proceed.”].)

 

Plaintiff generally appeared in this action when she filed her complaint on June 12, 2026.  Because 60 days from June 12, 2026, is August 11, 2026, Defendant’s motion is early.  A trial court may still consider a motion for summary judgment that filed less than 60 days after the plaintiff’s general appearance if the hearing at which the motion is made occurs after the 60-day period.  (See Sadlier v. Superior Court (1986) 184 Cal.App.3d 1050, 1054-1055 [“[W]hen as in this case under section 437c the motion must be ‘made’ after rather than before a certain date, we do not believe that [Code of Civil Procedure] section 1005.5 was intended to deprive a party of a right to a hearing on that motion if either the filing or the hearing at which the motion is made occurs after the 60-day period.”].)  If a motion is heard after the 60-day period, it satisfies section 437c regardless of when it was filed, and no exercise of discretion or good cause finding is required.

 

Here, the motion was filed before the 60-day period and the hearing is scheduled before the 60-day period.  Furthermore, Defendant’s motion fails to make a showing or argument for “good cause.”  Thus, the motion is invalid due to being premature.

 

2.  Defendant’s motion is procedurally deficient because Defendant failed to timely file and serve the motion.

 

“Notice of the motion and supporting papers shall be served on all other parties to the action at least 81 days before the time appointed for hearing.  If the notice is served by mail, the required 81-day period of notice shall be increased by 5 days if the place of address is within the State of California, 10 days if the place of address is outside the State of California but within the United States, and 20 days if the place of address is outside the United States.  If the notice is served by facsimile transmission, express mail, or another method of delivery providing for overnight delivery, the required 81-day period of notice shall be increased by two court days.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (a)(2).)  “An opposition to the motion shall be served and filed not less than 20 days preceding the noticed or continued date of hearing, unless the court for good cause orders otherwise.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (b)(2).)  “Proof of service of the moving papers must be filed no later than five court days before the time appointed of the hearing.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1300(c).)

 

Here, Defendant filed his motion 28 days before the noticed hearing date (August 10, 2026).  And the moving papers do not demonstrate Defendant ever served the motion and supporting papers on Plaintiff, potentially depriving Plaintiff of an opportunity to review and meaningfully respond to Defendant’s arguments by the opposition deadline (July 21, 2026).

 

Plaintiff claims she was never served with the moving papers.  However, Plaintiff was able to file and serve her opposition one day before the opposition deadline and to address both the procedural and substantive defects.  Plaintiff does not state how she learned about the motion.  By addressing both procedural and substantive issues, Plaintiff may have waived the procedural defects and precluded the court from denying or continuing Defendant’s motion.

 

A party waives any claim of inadequate service or notice when the party files an opposition to a motion for summary judgment, appears and argues at the motion hearing, never requests a continuance, and never claims prejudice by reason of the defective service or inadequate notice.  (Carlton v. Quint (2000) 77 Cal.App.4th 690, 696-698.)

 

Here, Plaintiff attacks the procedural issues across all Defendant’s filings, Defendant’s evidence and statement of undisputed material facts, and asks the court to strike the motion, order the motion off-calendar, or deny the motion without prejudice as premature and unserved.  As such, the court finds that Plaintiff has not waived the procedural defects related to Defendant’s untimely motion.

 

Regardless, the court finds that the motion is procedurally defective because Defendant did not timely file or provide proof that the moving papers were served on Plaintiff.

 

 

3.  The court denies Defendant’s motion because default has been entered against Defendant for failing to file and serve any of the enumerated pleadings in Code of Civil Procedure section 585, subdivision (b).

 

The clerk must enter a defendant’s default upon written application of the plaintiff if the defendant has been served and has not filed an answer, demurrer, notice of motion to strike, notice of motion to transfer, notice of motion to quash, a motion to dismiss, or other designated motion within the time specified in the summons.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 585, subd. (b).) 

 

“A motion for summary judgment is not a pleading under Code of Civil Procedure sections [422.10] and 422.  Nor is it mentioned among the motions which preclude entry of default judgment under Code of Civil Procedure section 585.”  (Barragan v. Banco BCH (1986) 188 Cal.App.3d 283, 299.)  Although “one who moves for summary judgment before filing an answer” has made a general appearance, the general appearance does not prevent entry of default.  (See Roy v. Superior Court (2005) 127 Cal.App.4th 337, 341.)  Nor does filing a summary judgment motion “extend the time within which a party must otherwise file a responsive pleading.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (a)(3).)

 

Here, Defendant filed his summary judgment motion without first filing an answer or any of the other enumerated pleadings in Section 585(b).  Thus, default was properly entered against Defendant on July 28, 2026.  The entry of default terminates a Defendant’s rights to take any further affirmative steps in this matter, and Defendant cannot move forward in this matter without first remedying the issue of the default.  (Harbour Vista, LLC v. HSBC Mortgage Services, Inc. (2011) 201 Cal.App.4th 1496, 1502-1506; Mackie v. Mackie (1960) 186 Cal.App.2d 825, 830-832.)

 

 

Because the court finds Defendant’s summary judgment motion is plagued by a myriad of procedural issues and Defendant is currently in default, the court denies Defendant’s motion for summary judgment without prejudice. 

 

The court also cautions Defendant to comply with the statutory requirements for filing, notice, and service of the moving papers going forward.

 

 

CONCLUSION and ORDER

 

Motion for summary judgment is denied without prejudice.
