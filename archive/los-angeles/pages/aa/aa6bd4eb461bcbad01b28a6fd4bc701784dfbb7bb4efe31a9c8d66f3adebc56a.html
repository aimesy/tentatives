BACKGROUND

 

On July 1, 2024, Plaintiff Creditors Adjustment Bureau, Inc. (“Plaintiff”) brought suit against Defendants Knock Out Creative, Inc. (“Knock Out”) and Kristi Kilday (“Kilday”) (together, “Defendants”) for Action on Judgment. 

 

Kilday answered the Complaint on October 3, 2024 and default was entered against Knock Out on October 25, 2024.

 

On August 5, 2025, the Court granted Plaintiff’s motion for summary judgment against Kilday.

 

Plaintiff now requests the Court to enter judgment against Kilday in the amount of $1,206,829.35, comprised of damages in the amount of $1,082,026.84; prejudgment interest in the amount of $123,615.48, plus costs in the amount of $1,187.03.  No objections were filed.

 

Subsequent to the first hearing, Plaintiff revised the request for judgment in the amount of $1,183,493.78, comprised of the original judgment of $557,109.38; $670,656.22 in post-judgment and pre-judgment interest accrued on the original judgment amount; $1,068.27 in “post judgment costs”; $1,187.03 in costs incurred in connection with this matter, less $46,539.22 in payments received.

 

Plaintiff now seeks the revised amount of $1,205,987.62, representing the original judgment of $557,109.38 and postjudgment interest on the original judgment balance less payments made thereon in the total amount of $648,878.24.

(Landa Decl. ¶¶ 2-9.)

 

In 1982, the Legislature enacted the Enforcement of Judgments Law (EJL). (§ 680.010 et seq.) Prior to enactment of the EJL, the means for renewal of a judgment in California was by filing an independent action on the judgment within the 10–year statute of limitations.

 

In the [EJL], the Legislature adopted an alternative summary procedure for renewal [of judgments]. Under this procedure, a money judgment is enforceable for 10 years from the date it is entered. (§ 683.020.) To obtain a renewal of the judgment, the judgment creditor must file an application for renewal with the clerk of the court that entered the judgment before the expiration of the 10–year period of enforceability. Upon the filing of the application, the court clerk shall enter the renewal of the judgment in the court records.

 

But nothing in the EJL limits a judgment creditor's right to pursue an independent action on the judgment. (§ 683.050.)

 

An independent action based on a judgment is an action based on contract. The original judgment becomes a debt which the judgment debtor is obligated to pay and the law implies a contract on his part to pay it.  Thus, when successful, an action on a judgment results in the entry of a new judgment.

 

(Cadle Co. II, Inc. v. Fiscus (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 1232, 1237-1238 [cleaned up].)

 

Here, the purported additional interest was neither part of Plaintiff’s request on summary judgment, nor part of the Court’s order granting summary judgment.  Rather, the arguments Plaintiff submitted to the Court, and as the Court indicated in its ruling on the motion for summary judgment, judgment was originally entered in the amount of $557,109.38 on March 21, 2014, the judgment became final on September 21, 2014, and this action was filed within the 10-year statute of limitations.  Thus, Plaintiff has demonstrated that summary judgment is appropriate as to the original $557,109.38 judgment.

 

There was no argument, explanation, request, or calculation made in connection with the motion for summary judgment concerning the interest that has accrued on the judgment in the interim.  Instead, Plaintiff has submitted the conclusory and unsupported statement in the January 22, 2025 Declaration of Brian Mitteldorf that “At the time of filing the instant action, there was due and owing the sum of $1,082,026.84.”  As this statement was unsupported and irrelevant to the relief requested, the Court did not consider it or incorporate it into the ruling on the motion for summary judgment.

ANALYSIS

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5, which outlines recoverable costs to a prevailing party under Code of Civil Procedure section 1032, permits the recovery of attorneys’ fees when authorized by contract, statute, or law.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (a)(10).) 

 

Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d) provides: “If the buyer prevails in an action under this section, the buyer shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.” 

 

Defendant does not contest that Plaintiff is entitled to recover reasonable attorneys’ fees, costs, and expenses under the Song-Beverly Act.  Instead, Defendant requests a reduction to fees and costs requested on the grounds that they were excessive and not reasonably incurred and the hourly rates are too high. 

 

I.                ATTORNEYS’ FEES

 

a.     Hourly Rates

 

Plaintiffs seek hourly rates of $595-$615 for partner JK, $375 for junior attorney and paralegal/legal assistant rates ranging from $175-250 per hour (though most time is billed at $175). 

 

In support of the requested rates, Plaintiffs advance the attorney Declaration of Joseph Kaufman, which lists fourteen (14) cases in which his 2026 rate of $615 has been approved as reasonable, as well as thirty (30) cases in which his 2023-2025 rate of $595 was approved as reasonable.  (Kaufman Decl. ¶¶ 6-7.) 

 

Plaintiffs also advance the attorney Declaration of Scott A. Sanchez, which lists three (3) cases in which his 2026 hourly rate of $395 was deemed reasonable and seven (7) cases in which his 2023-2025 hourly rate of $375 was deemed reasonable.  (Sanchez Decl. ¶¶ 18-19.)  Sanchez further points out that this court accepted his $395 hourly rate in awarding sanctions in this case.  (Sanchez Decl. ¶ 19.)

 

Sanchez’s declaration also references the Laffey Matrix, indicating first year associates in high cost of living areas bill an average of $473 per hour, in contrast to Mr. Sanchez’s billing rate of $375, which went up to $395 in 2026, now that he has three years of experience.  (Sanchez Decl. ¶ 17; Kaufman Decl. ¶ 13.)

 

In opposition, Honda urges the Court to disregard Plaintiffs’ counsels’ statements about what other consumer protection attorneys charge and their own self-serving declarations and to use a more objective metric of prevailing market rates instead.

 

But Plaintiffs have provided numerous examples of Courts in California upholding their requested rates, as well as the Laffey Matrix for objective comparison.  By contrast, Honda has not supplied the Court with any such objective metric data to support the rate reductions it requests. 

 

Therefore, the Court does not implement any across-the-board hourly rate reductions.

 

b.     Reasonableness of Time Entries

 

Mr. Sanchez’s Time Spent on Administrative Tasks

Defendant first challenges a total of 8.3 hours Mr. Sanchez purportedly billed to assemble exhibits, format pages, bates label documents, and complete binders, which are tasks better suited to a law clerk or paralegal. 

 

Defendant references Exhibit B to the Michel declaration at entries 176, 193, 201, 299, 370, 424, 427, 431, and 434.  Upon review, the Court agrees that 7.8 hours of the identified entries were administrative tasks, better billed by a paralegal.  As such, the Court reduces the 0.4 hours billed prior to 2026 from $375 to the then-paralegal rate of $175, for a reduction of $80, and the 7.4 hours billed in 2026 from $395 to the current paralegal rate of $195, for a reduction of $1,480. 

 

However, the Court finds entry 299, which involved drafting and serving objections to a notice of deposition is appropriately a junior associate task.  As such, the Court does not adjust the 0.5 hours of time billed to these tasks.

 

In total, the Court reduces Mr. Sanchez fees by $1,560 for time billed for administrative tasks.

 

Duplicate Entries

 

Defendant next challenges $12,357 billed to duplicative entries, however, the Court cannot easily discern which entries are being challenged as duplicative.  The opposition references Exhibit B to the Michel declaration at entries 5, 8, 12, 19, 27, 29, 44, etc.  But there are no entries numbered 5, 12, 27, or 29 on that spreadsheet.  It starts at line # 8 and skips over various lines, including 12, 27, and 29. 

 

Further, upon review of the line items that are represented in the spreadsheet, the Court does not find Honda’s characterization of the time to be accurate.  For example, for Line 8, Mr. Sanchez billed a total of 3 hours to reviewing/analyzing repair orders, vehicle evaluation records, client payment history per the sales contract, identifying repeat defects across multiple repair visits, calculating the cumulative days the vehicle was out of service, prelitigation communications with Honda, legal research, and litigation strategy.  This entry does not involve a review of work done by other attorneys.

 

Similarly, line 19 involves a review of Honda’s answer to the complaint and taking notes on arguments challenging Honda’s various affirmative defenses.  And line 44 involves a review of Honda’s objections to Plaintiff’s PMQ deposition notice.  Again, these entries do not involve a duplicative review of work done by other attorneys at Plaintiff’s firm.

 

            Therefore, Defendant has not adequately substantiated any reduction due to duplicative entries.

 

            Motion for Attorneys’ Fees

 

            Defendant next challenges $1,777.50, representing four (4) hours of Mr. Sanchez’s time requested in anticipation of replying to Honda’s opposition and attending the hearing and requests that the time be reduced by two (2) hours.

 

            In reply, Mr. Sanchez indicates he actually spent more than nine (9) hours reviewing Honda’s opposition and reply.

 

            While the Court would ordinarily find four and a half (4.5) hours excessive to review an opposition and draft a reply in an ordinary Song-Beverly motion for attorneys’ fees, the Court agrees that Honda has vigorously opposed the instant motion, requiring Plaintiffs’ counsel to expend longer than what would normally be required.

 

            As such, the Court does not find any reductions warranted for the estimated time requested to review the opposition, draft the reply, and attend the hearing on the fee motion.

 

            Fees Incurred in Connection with Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel

 

            Defendant next argues that the 9.2 hours billed between February 9, 2026 and April 13, 2026 to draft, oppose, reply, and appear for/attend Plaintiffs’ successful Motion to Compel the deposition of Kentaro Ogawa should be subtracted from the fee award here, because attorney time incurred in connection with that motion was already awarded as monetary sanctions.

 

            The Court agrees, and Plaintiffs have not raised any arguments to the contrary in reply.  Therefore, the Court reduces the fee request by $3,634.

 

            In grand total, the Court reduces the fee request by $5,194.00.

 

c.      Multiplier

 

Factors to be considered in applying a multiplier include “the quality of the representation, the novelty and complexity of the issues, the results obtained, and the contingent risk presented.”  (Consumer Privacy Cases (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 545, 556.) 

 

Plaintiffs request a lodestar multiplier of 1.2 to account for the “exceptional” settlement in Plaintiff’s favor and the inherent contingent risk in taking on this case.

 

Defendants argue that no multiplier is warranted in a garden variety lemon law case like this.  Although the litigation tactics may have been more contentious in this case than in an ordinary lemon law case, that additional time is adequately reflected in the fee award.  The Court does not find that an additional multiplier is warranted here.

 

II.             COSTS

 

Plaintiffs seek $16,249.44 in costs, comprised of $1,746.22 in filing and motion fees, $2,391.15 in deposition costs, $143.50 for service of process, and $11,968.57 in “other” fees, consisting primarily of fees incurred for transcripts, delivery of courtesy copies to the court, attorney appearance services, and deposition costs. 

 

Defendant has not challenged Plaintiff’s claimed costs, therefore the Court awards Plaintiff’s requested costs in full.
