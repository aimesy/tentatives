[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: PLAINTIFF MICHAEL BRIAN SMITH’S MOTION TO SET ASIDE/VACATE JUDGMENT [CRS #8855] Dept. 731 8:30 a.m. July 21, 2026

Moving Party: Plaintiff, Michael Brian Smith

Opposing Party: Defendant, Michael Kors Inc.

Notice: OK

Plaintiff Michael B. Smith brought this suit against his former employer, Defendant Michael Kors Inc., alleging race discrimination in his employment and a subsequent unlawful layoff.

On March 26, 2026, the Court granted a motion for judgment on the pleadings filed by Defendant, which disposed of the then-operative March 19, 2024, Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”). Plaintiff filed a motion for reconsideration on March 30, 2026, which was opposed by Defendant, and was ultimately denied by the Court on May 20, 2026. The Court entered judgment in Defendant’s favor that same day.

Now before the Court is Plaintiff’s motion to set aside/vacate the May 20, 2026, judgment on the grounds that another action affecting the outcome of the proceedings before this Court is purportedly presently pending before the Compton, California branch of the Los Angeles Superior Court as 22CMCV00753, Smith v. Michael Kors Inc. (the “Santa Monica action”). (See Motion, p. 2, ¶ 4.)

The motion is DENIED, with prejudice.

Plaintiff’s motion is noticed as being premised on Code of Civil Procedure sections 473, subdivision (d), and 1008, subdivisions (a), (b), and (e). (Motion, pp. 1-2.) Both premises fail.

As argued in Defendant’s opposition, there is no applicable provision of Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (d), here. (See Opposition pp. 11-12.) Plaintiff is asking that the Court vacate a final judgment based on the purported pendency of the Santa Monica action. This is not a valid ground for relief under section 473, subdivision (d), which is limited to clerical errors and void judgments and orders, including those voided by extrinsic fraud or mistake, neither of which Plaintiff’s paper show. (See Kramer v. Traditional Escrow, Inc. (2020) 56 Cal.App.5th 13, 29 [discussing § 473, subd. (d)].)

Similarly, none of the grounds for party-requested reconsideration—new or different facts, circumstances, or law (Code Civ. Proc., § 1008, subd. (a))—appear here, as the requested relief is based on the purported pendency of the Santa Monica action rather than some fact, circumstance, or new law relating to this action.

Last, the Court takes judicial notice of Exhibit A attached to Defendant’s opposition, which shows a June 18, 2026, minute order for the Santa Monica action, in which the court in that case denied a similar motion by Plaintiff in this case to vacate or set aside the judgment in that case, which was entered on November 6, 2025. (Opposition, Request for Judicial Notice, Ex. A, pp. 6-7.)

Plaintiff’s motion is accordingly DENIED. No further motions seeking identical relief shall be entertained absent a proper statutory basis.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: PLAINTIFF’S MOTION FOR ATTORNEYS’ FEES

 

Dept. 731

September 15, 2026

8:30 a.m.

Matter No. 14

 

 

Moving Party:               Defendant, Volkswagen Group of America, Inc.

Opposing Party:           Plaintiff, Luiz Zamayoa

Notice:                         OK

 

  1. Background Facts

Plaintiff Luis Zamayoa brought this action suing Defendants Volkswagen Group of America, Inc. and Does 1 through 10 pursuant to a May 24, 2024, Complaint alleging statutory claims arising from the Song-Beverly Warranty Consumer Act (“SBA”).

On December 11, 2025, Plaintiff filed a notice of conditional settlement in this action, which provided an estimated dismissal date of March 11, 202[6].

Now before the Court is Plaintiff’s opposed motion for attorneys’ fees in the amount of $42,173.75 as the prevailing party in an SBA action.

 

  1. Motion for Attorneys’ Fees and Costs and Expenses

a.     Legal Standard

A prevailing party is entitled to recover costs as a matter of right. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1032, subds. (a)(4), (b).) Attorney’s fees are also recoverable as costs when authorized by contract, statute, or law. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (a)(10).) Separately, a prevailing buyer in a Song-Beverly Act action is entitled to recover their attorney’s fees and costs under the Act’s express terms. (Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d); see Goglin v. BMW of North America, LLC (2016) 4 Cal.App.5th 462, 464, 471.)

The Court begins this inquiry “with the ‘lodestar,’ i.e., the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate.” (PLCM Group v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095 (PLCM Group).) From there, the “lodestar figure may then be adjusted [according to a multiplier enhancement] based on consideration of factors specific to the case, in order to fix the fee at the fair market value for the legal services provided.” (Ibid.) Relevant multiplier factors include “(1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, [and] (4) the contingent nature of the fee award.” (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132.)

    1. Discussion

As a prevailing buyer in an SBA action, Plaintiff seeks an award of $42,173.75, consisting of (1) $30,539.00 in attorneys’ fees for Plaintiff’s counsel, (2) a 1.25 multiplier enhancement on the attorneys’ fees for $7,634.75, and (3) an additional $4,000.00 for Plaintiff’s counsel to review Defendant’s opposition, draft a reply, and attend the hearing on this motion. (See, e.g., Motion, ii:7-10.) Plaintiff’s motion does not address costs.

When a voluminous fee application is made, the court may make across-the-board percentage cuts either in the number of hours claimed or in the final lodestar figure. (Morris v. Hyundai Motor America (2019) 41 Cal.App.5th 24, 40, citing Warren v. Kia Motors America, Inc. (2018) 30 Cal.App.5th 24, 41.) A trial court is not required to state each charge it finds reasonable or unreasonable. A reduced award might be fully justified by a general observation that an attorney overlitigated a case. (Karton v. Ari Design & Construction, Inc. (2021) 61 Cal.App.5th 734, 744.) But the court must clearly explain its reasons for choosing the particular negative multiplier that it chose; otherwise, the reviewing court is unable to determine that the court had valid, specific reasons for its across-the-board percentage reduction. (Warren v. Kia Motors America, Inc. (2018) 30 Cal.App.5th 24, 41.)

Here, the Court agrees that many of the hours presented by Plaintiff are either not recoverable or inflated. (See Opposition, pp. 7-9.) Simply, the Court determines that the hours spent on this action do not reflect the hours that a reasonable practitioner should have, at the requested rates, expended in litigating this action to settlement, particularly where, as highlighted in the opposition, Plaintiff’s counsel billed about 17.9 additional hours after receiving the 998 offer that concluded this litigation. (See Opposition, pp. 8-9; see also Motion, Jacobson Decl., ¶ 101, Ex. 40 [verified time records].)

However, the Court does not agree that a negative multiplier to reduce the requested fees is appropriate, as advocated by Volkswagen. (Opposition, p. 12.) Instead, the Court makes a 33% reduction to the total requested fees of $30,539.00, which more appropriately reflects reasonable recovery for the efforts expended on this action, yielding $20,461.13. (See Motion, Jacobson Decl., ¶ 101, Ex. 40 [verified time records].)

The Court adds an additional $2,500.00 to be reasonably spent on the opposition, reply, and hearing work for this motion by Plaintiff’s counsel.

The total award of fees is thus $20,461.13 plus $2,500.00, yielding a final lodestar award of $22,961.13.

The Court does not otherwise rule on reasonable rates as the above determination concludes the Court’s lodestar discussion.

Separately, the Court finds insufficient grounds for a multiplier here. While the Court can accept that Plaintiff’s counsel works on contingency, the questions involved are customary in SBA actions and the skills displayed here are likewise of customary quality.

c.     Conclusion

Plaintiff Luiz Zamayoa’s motion for attorneys’ fees is GRANTED, in part, in the amount of $22,961.13.

 

Plaintiff Luiz Zamayoa is ordered to give notice.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: PLAINTIFF/CROSS-DEFENDANT JESUS RODRIGUEZ’S NOTICE OF MOTION AND MOTION TO STRIKE FIRST AMENDED CROSS-COMPLAINT

  1. Background Facts

This action arises from a dispute in real property.

a.     First Amended Complaint

Plaintiff/Cross-Defendant Jesus Rodriguez (“Rodriguez”) sues Defendant/Cross-Complainant Maria Guadalupe Diaz (“Diaz”), both as an individual and as Trustee of The Diaz Family Revocable Living Trust, Dated September 7th, 2024, pursuant to a March 12, 2025, First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) alleging claims of (1) quiet title (verified), (2) cancellation of instrument, (3) accounting, (4) unfair business practices, (5) injunctive relief, and (6) declaratory relief.

The FAC’s claims chiefly arise from the following allegations, among others. Rodriguez purchased Diaz’s interest in 1451 E. Vernon Avenue, Los Angeles, CA 90011 (the “subject property”), a multi-unit residential property, in February 2007 pursuant to a sale agreement giving Diaz certain rights, with the parties’ agreement including the right to tenancy for several months and right to collect rents and pay mortgage on the subject property. Later, Diaz let the property fall into bankruptcy by collecting rent but failing to pay the mortgage, which Rodriguez had to remedy. Later still, after Rodriguez had acquired a full interest in the subject property, Diaz recorded a forged deed conveying the subject property to Diaz, as an individual, then transferring the property to herself as Trustee of the Diaz Family Revocable Living Trust, thus damaging Rodriguez.

b.     First Amended Cross-Complaint

In turn, Diaz, as an individual and as Trustee, sues Rodriguez, as well as Rene Gonzalez (“Gonzalez”), Julia Molina (“Julia”), and Roes 1 through 10 pursuant to an April 24, 2026, First Amended Cross-Complaint alleging claims of (1) quiet title, (2) slander of title, (3) fraud, (4) removal of lien, (5) breach of contract, (6) tortious interference with contractual relationship, (7) violation of covenant of good faith and fair dealing, (8) intentional infliction of emotional distress, and (9) negligent infliction of distress.

The claims chiefly arise from the following allegations, among others. Diaz first acquired full interest in the subject property in 2003, specifically through deed from Diaz and her then-husband, Roberto Diaz, to Diaz as her own separate property. Sometime in late 2006, Angie Villareal (“Villareal”), a real estate agent, introduced Diaz to Israel Luna (“Luna”), who offered to lend Diaz $200,000 as secured by the subject property. Diaz went forward with the transaction. However, Diaz, a person with a third-grade education who does not read English, was presented with a deed conveying title of the property to Luna and to Rodriguez, a person Diaz had never met, as tenants in common, which, without knowledge or any reason to believe that she was conveying the subject property, and for no consideration, she executed. The deed was then recorded. Luna moreover executed a Deed of Trust with Assignment of Rents, designating himself as trustor and Diaz as lender. In turn, Rodriguez executed a deed of trust to secure nearly $200,000.00 in his benefit, which Rodriguez continues to assert as a lien on the subject property. Later, Diaz took an extended trip to Mexico and assigned Villareal and Luna to collect rents, hired Rodriguez as counsel in evicting a certain tenant at the subject property, and in October 2012 rented a unit to Gonzalez, with Molina living in the unit as an unnamed tenant. Then, in October 2024, Diaz attempted to evict Gonzalez and Molina, serving a three-day notice to quit and, after Gonzalez and Molina did not comply, instituted unlawful detainer proceedings. In that litigation, Gonzalez and Molina alleged that Diaz was not the property’s real owner, producing a backdated 2012 rental agreement listing Rodriguez as owner, with rents being paid to Rodriguez. Rodriguez then visited Diaz stating he did not want problems and turning over a deed conveying Rodriguez’s interest in the property to Diaz, which was subsequently recorded. Rodriguez nevertheless went back to Gonzalez and Molina and executed a new agreement to pay rent monies to Rodriguez as owner.

c.     Motion Before the Court

Before the Court is Rodriguez’s opposed motion to strike the FACC as a whole based on Diaz having filed the Cross-Complaint on a date after service of Diaz’s Answer in the action, contravening Code of Civil Procedure section 428.50, with the FACC suffering from this defect downstream. 

 

  1. Motion to Strike
    1. Meet and Confer

The Court determines that while the meet and confer efforts that preceded Rodriguez’s motion were insufficient (Motion, Franco Decl., ¶¶ 4-5 [meet and confer limited to email, failing to reflect any phone call, videoconference, or in person conversation took place]; see Code Civ. Proc., § 435.5, subd. (a)), such a finding is not a basis to deny Rodriguez’s motion. (Code Civ. Proc., § 435.5, subd. (a)(4) [“A determination by the court that the meet and confer process was insufficient shall not be grounds to grant or deny the motion to strike”].)

b.     Legal Standard

The court may, upon a motion or at any time in its discretion and upon terms it deems proper: (a) strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or (b) strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of California, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Code Civ. Proc. § 436, subds. (a), (b); Stafford v. Shultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782 [“Matter in a pleading which is not essential to the claim is surplusage; probative facts are surplusage and may be stricken out or disregarded”].)

    1. Discussion

After review, the Court finds sufficient grounds for relief.

The defendant can file the cross-complaint as a matter of right anytime before or at the time the answer is due, if the cross-complaint is brought against the plaintiff (i.e., a party who files either a complaint or a cross-complaint). (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 426.30, subd. (a), 428.50, subd. (a); Nels E. Nelson, Inc. v. Tarman (1958) 163 Cal.App.2d 714, 730.) The defendant must obtain leave to file a cross-complaint anytime after that date. (Code Civ. Proc., § 428.50, subd. (c); see Code Civ. Proc., § 426.50.)

Here, as reflected in the record, Diaz filed an Answer to Rodriguez’s First Amended Complaint on June 11, 2025, and five days later, on June 16, 2025, Diaz filed a Cross-Complaint against Rodriguez and others. On the face of those facts, prior to filing a Cross-Complaint, Diaz should have filed a motion seeking leave of court to file a cross pleading. Here, no such request appears in the record as Diaz never made such a request, instead filing the Cross-Complaint as a matter of course, i.e., in contravention of Code of Civil Procedure section 428.50, and thus not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of California, rendering the FACC that followed the Cross-Complaint subject to motion to strike.

Critically, as noted in Rodriguez’s reply, Diaz’s opposition fails to address section 428.50, focusing instead on whether service of summons took place and whether a general appearance was made, and only briefly without any elaboration arguing that “[a]ny minor [sic] procedural issues regarding the periods of time between filing the Answer and filing the Amended Cross-Complaint [sic] (3 court days) have been waived by the making [of] his General Appearance,” all without differentiating why a general appearance (affecting jurisdiction over a defendant) waives statutory requirements under the Code of Civil Procedure related to the timing of filing initial pleadings in actions (§ 428.50).

Under these circumstances, relief is warranted.

d.     Conclusion

Plaintiff/Cross-Defendant Jesus Rodriguez’s motion to strike is accordingly GRANTED.

The June 16, 2025, Cross-Complaint and the April 24, 2026, First Amended Cross-Complaint are ORDERED STRICKEN.

 

Plaintiff/Cross-Defendant Jesus Rodriguez is ordered to give notice.

  1. Court Order

Now before the Court is Defendant Arlington II LLC’s opposed motion for terminating sanctions based on Plaintiff’s purported failure to provide responses to Set One discovery served on Plaintiff on May 27, 2025, with the Court ordering initial responses to that discovery on April 7, 2026, and with Plaintiff failing to do so prior to Arlington filing its motion on August 18, 2026.

After review, for at least two reasons, the Court finds insufficient grounds for relief.

First, in imposing sanctions, California courts should consider both the conduct being sanctioned and its effect on the party seeking discovery and, in choosing a sanction, should attempt to tailor the sanction to the harm caused by the withheld discovery. (Padron v. Watchtower Bible & Tract Society of New York, Inc. (2017) 16 Cal.App.5th 1246, 1259-1260.) Critically, the trial court cannot impose sanctions for misuse of the discovery process as a punishment, where the discovery statutes evince an incremental approach to discovery sanctions, starting with monetary sanctions and ending with the ultimate sanction of termination. (Ibid.) If a lesser sanction fails to curb misuse, a greater sanction is warranted, with continuing misuses of the discovery process warranting incrementally harsher sanctions until the sanction is reached that will curb the abuse. (Ibid.)

Here, even assuming that a continued discovery abuse was before the Court (further discussed below), the Court has not imposed evidence or issue sanctions in relation to Set One discovery served on Plaintiff, for which reason imposition of terminating sanctions would fail to adhere to the incremental approach set in Padron.

Second, Plaintiff has filed an opposition to Arlington’s motion, which argues that on August 24, 2026—15 months late—Plaintiff served responses to Set One discovery (Opposition, Bral Decl., ¶ 4, Ex. 1), which is not disputed in a reply by Arlington, and which mitigates but in no way eliminates the discovery abuse here.

Defendant Arlington II LLC’s motion for termination sanctions is accordingly DENIED without prejudice as to Arlington’s ability to seek intermediary sanctions such as evidence and issue sanctions and without prejudice to Arlington’s ability to seek terminating sanctions in the future based on additional discovery abuses.

Notice:                         OK

 

  1. Background Facts

Plaintiff Gabriel C. Ordaz sues Defendants General Motors LLC and Does 1 through 10 pursuant to a June 30, 2025, Complaint, alleging Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act (“SBA”) claims arising from allegations related to Plaintiff’s February 26, 2021, purchase and/or lease of a 2021 Chevrolet Silverado that was manufactured and/or distributed by General Motors.

Now before the Court is General Motors’ unopposed motion to change venue for this action, which was properly served on Plaintiff’s counsel via email at various individual counsel email addresses and to the service email address of SLPCaseDocs+1GCPWBEK2MZ241434Ordaz@slpattorney.com.

 

  1. Motion to Change Venue

a.     Legal Standard

In California, parties can bring a motion to change the venue for the action to a different county. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 397, subds. (b)-(e), 397.5; Passeano v. Superior Court (1988) 204 Cal.App.3d 17, 20-21.) In civil actions generally, a party can file a motion to change venue on the grounds that (1) an impartial trial cannot be had in the county of suit, (2) the convenience of witnesses and the ends of justice would be promoted by the change, or (3) there is no qualified judge in the county of suit. (Code Civ. Proc., § 397, subds. (b)-(d).)

To prevail on the grounds of no impartial trial, the movant must show that it is subject to actual, widespread prejudice in the county. (Ohio Cas. Ins. v. Superior Court (1994) 30 Cal.App.4th 444, 452; People v. Ocean Shore R.R. (1938) 24 Cal.App.2d 420, 427-428.) Relevant factors are pervasiveness of prejudice, length and intensity of prejudice, publicity of the action, and inability to impanel a jury. (Ocean Shore, supra, at pp. 426-428 [prejudice factors]; J.I. Case Threshing Co. v. Copren Bros. (1917) 35 Cal.App. 70, 79 [jury impaneling].)

To prevail on the ground of convenience of witnesses and ends of justice, the movant must (1) provide the names of the witnesses, (2) describe the nature of their testimony, (3) explain how the county of suit would be inconvenient for them, and (4) show how the ends of justice would be promoted by a change in venue. (Peiser v. Mettler (1958) 50 Cal.2d 594, 607; cf. Rycz v. Superior Court (2022) 81 Cal.App.5th 824, 836 & fn. 8 [no express requirement for this information in statute].)

The ground of no qualified judge is essentially obsolete insofar as the chief justice of a court will simply assign an outside judge to hear the matter instead of transferring the entire case. (Code Civ. Proc., § 170.8; People v. Spring Valley Co. (1952) 109 Cal.App.2d 656, 672.)

    1. Discussion

After review, the Court finds sufficient grounds for relief.

General Motors presents unopposed evidence that Plaintiff is a resident of San Bernardino County, that the subject vehicle was purchased from a San Bernardino located dealership, and that the repairs to the subject vehicle also took place in San Bernardino County. (Motion, p. 2, citing Motion, Allison Decl., ¶¶ 2-6, Exs. A [sales contract], B [Plaintiff’s address in disclosures], C [repair work documents], [repair work location in disclosures]; see Complaint, ¶ 1 [failing to specify Plaintiff’s county of residence].) Under these circumstances, evidence and witnesses are almost certainly located in San Bernardino County, a position not disputed by Plaintiff for lack of an opposition.

c.     Conclusion

Defendant General Motors LLC’s motion to change venue is accordingly GRANTED.

  1. Background Facts
    1. Pleadings

This action involves cross allegations of fraud, broken funding promises, and contract breaches after an $8¿million film-financing deal collapsed, leaving multiple sides claiming others lied about project status, financial ability, and compliance the loan agreements.

                                          i.    Plaintiffs’ Second Amended Complaint

Golden Rights LLC and Takashi Cheng sue Incite Inc. and its CEO, Jenner Furst, pursuant to a July 2, 2026, Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”) asserting claims for fraud and deceit, negligent misrepresentation, breach of written contract, unfair business practices under Business & Professions Code section 17200, accounting, and declaratory relief. Plaintiffs allege Incite induced them to provide financing of $8 million by misrepresenting the status, completion, profitability, and distribution readiness of several media projects—including: Thank You, Dr. Fauci; Queen of the Hill; a Red Hot Chili Peppers documentary; and a Bruce Willis project. Plaintiffs allege Incite breached multiple obligations under various written instruments, including failing to meet conditions, failing to reimburse fees, failing to set up a required segregated account, and failing to provide accurate project information. Plaintiffs seek damages exceeding several million dollars, restitution, disgorgement, attorneys’ fees, and declarations regarding the parties’ rights under the parties’ loan agreements.

                                         ii.    Plaintiff’s First Amended Cross-Complaint

In turn, Incite Inc., as Cross-Complainant, sues Ahead Group, Takashi “Bobby” Cheng, and Golden Rights LLC pursuant to a May 4, 2026, First Amended Cross-Complaint (“FACC”), alleging causes of action for fraud and deceit, negligent misrepresentation, breach of written contract, and unfair business practices under Business & Professions Code section 17200. Incite claims Cross-Defendants falsely represented that they possessed sufficient funds to complete the loan, failed to fund all required amounts, and engaged in deceptive practices during negotiations and closing. Incite seeks damages exceeding $20 million, punitive damages, attorneys’ fees, costs, and other relief.

    1. Motions Before the Court

Now before the Court are Golden Rights and Takashia Cheng’s opposed (1) demurrer to the first, second, and fourth causes of action, and (2) motion to strike the prayer for punitive damages from the FACC.

Also before the Court are Ahead Group’s identical and similarly opposed (1) demurrer to the first, second, and fourth causes of action, and (2) motion to strike the prayer for punitive damages from the FACC.

 

  1. Demurrer, Golden Rights and Takishi Cheng
    1. Meet and Confer

The moving papers establish a sufficient meet and confer process preceded the filing of Golden Rights and Takashi Cheng’s demurrer. (Demurrer, Fierro Decl., ¶¶ 5-8; see Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(3)(A).)

b.     Legal Standard

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747; see Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (e).)

To sufficiently allege a cause of action, a complaint must allege all the ultimate facts—that is, the facts needed to establish each element of the cause of action pleaded. (Committee on Children’s Television, Inc. v. General Foods Corp. (1983) 35 Cal.3d 197, 212, superseded by statute as stated in Branick v. Downey Savings & Loan Assn. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 235, 242.) “[E]ach evidentiary fact that might eventually form part of the plaintiff’s proof need not be alleged.” (C.A. v. William S. Hart Union High School Dist. (2012) 53 Cal.4th 861, 872.)

In testing the sufficiency of the cause of action, the demurrer admits the truth of all material facts properly pleaded. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 966-67.) Courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (Taylor v. City of Los Angeles Dept. of Water and Power (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 1216, 1228, disapproved on other grounds, Jones v. Lodge at Torrey Pines Partnership (2008) 42 Cal.4th 1158, 1162.) A demurrer, however, “does not admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Daar v. Yellow Cab Co. (1967) 67 Cal.2d 695, 713.)

A demurrer may only be used to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318 (Blank).) The face of the complaint includes exhibits attached to the complaint. (Frantz v. Blackwell (1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 91, 94.) If facts appearing in the exhibits contradict those alleged, the facts in the exhibits take precedence. (Holland v. Morse Diesel Intern., Inc. (2001) 86 Cal.App.4th 1443, 1447, superseded by statute on other grounds as stated in White v. Cridlebaugh (2009) 178 Cal.App.4th 506, 521.)

    1. Discussion

                                          i.    Demurrer, First and Second Causes of Action, Fraud Claims

1.     Cross-Defendant Golden Rights LLC

After review, the Court finds in favor of Golden Rights.

A general demurrer for sufficiency may be sustained against duplicative claims. (Palm Springs Villas II Homeowners Assn., Inc. v. Parth (2016) 248 Cal.App.4th 268, 290 (“Palm Springs”) [“a cause of action for breach of governing documents [that] appear[ed] to be duplicative of [a] cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty” is “recognized … as a basis for sustaining a demurrer”]; Rodrigues v. Campbell Industries (1978) 87 Cal.App.3d 494, 501 [finding demurrer was properly sustained without leave to amend as to cause of action that contained allegations of other causes and “thus add[ed] nothing to the complaint by way of fact or theory of recovery”]; see also Award Metals, Inc. v. Superior Court (1991) 228 Cal.App.3d 1128, 1135, opn. mod. Apr. 10, 1991 [demurrer should have been sustained as to duplicative causes of action].)

In Palm Springs, the court of appeal found that “the cause of action [therein pleaded] for breach of governing documents appear[ed] to be duplicative of the cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty,” supporting a finding that “the trial court properly sustained [a] demurrer” to the breach of governing documents claim, particularly where “[t]he [the pleading party] d[id] not address [the demurring party’s] argument or explain how its document claim differ[ed] from the fiduciary breach claim.” (Palm Springs, supra, 248 Cal.App.4th at p. 290.) The specific factual allegations at issue in Palm Springs were a “cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty [that] alleged that [the demurring party] had breached her duties to comply with the governing documents and to avoid causing harm to the [pleading party] by, among other things, refusing to submit bids or contracts to the Board, ‘unilaterally terminating’ PPM, and signing the contract with Desert Protection,” while, in turn, the “[t]he breach of governing documents cause of action identified CC&R and bylaw provisions and identified actions taken by [the demurring party] in breach of these provisions, including the termination of PPM and entering into the Desert Protection contract.” (Id. at p. 277.)

Here, with language even more generalized than the language in Palm Springs—there sufficient to find duplicativeness in pleading—the FACC’s fraud claims and its breach of contract claim almost identically relate to Golden Rights’ alleged obligations and failure to fund Incite’s projects, resulting in damages in an amount not less than $20 million, reflecting lost profits. (Compare FACC, ¶¶ 83-85, 88 & 90-92, 96, with ibid. at ¶¶ 98-101.)

Consistent with Palm Springs, the Court thus determines that the Complaint’s first and second causes of action are duplicative of the Complaint’s third cause of action, for which reason the Court SUSTAINS the demurrer to the FACC’s fraud claims, with leave to amend, as relates to Golden Rights LLC. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (e)(1).)

2.     Cross-Defendant Takashi Cheng

In contrast, as argued in Incite’s opposition (Opposition, p. 10), the duplicativeness argument fails as relates to Takashi Cheng, who is not a Defendant subject to the third cause of action for breach of contract (see FACC, ¶¶ 98-101).

Moreover, the Court determines that the FACC sufficiently alleges fraud claims against Takashi Cheng. Specifically, the FACC alleges that, in essence, Cheng promised funding as an investor with a successful background and relevant experience in the target field (FACC, ¶¶ 8-10, 13-15), who expressed interest in investing in Incite for 25% interest, thereafter signing a binding term sheet committing to $8 million in financing (FACC, ¶¶ 17-19, 21, 23), where later conduct by Cheng and his companies showed Cross-Defendants did not in fact have availability to such funding, such as a changing from lender from Ahead Group to Golden Rights, initial funding coming from a third company owned by Cheng, Achievable Solutions, Golden Rights thereafter stalling further funding, and Cheng ultimately admitting he could only obtain more funding if film projects started to do well (see FACC, ¶¶ 26-27, 29-36, 49-50, 54, 57-63, 70-72), with Cheng striking first by issuing default notices against Incite (FACC, ¶ 65), all of which harmed Incite when Incite lost multiple major projects because Golden Rights could not fund its commitments and Incite was forced to terminated staff and faced potential Chapter 11 restructuring (FACC, ¶¶ 76-81). (See Service by Medallion, Inc. v. Clorox Co. (1996) 44 Cal.App.4th 1807, 1816 [Intentional misrepresentation involves “(1) a knowingly false representation by the defendant; (2) an intent to deceive or induce reliance; (3) justifiable reliance by the plaintiff; and (4) resulting damages”]; B.L.M. v. Sabo & Deitsch (1997) 55 Cal.App.4th 823, 834 [Negligent misrepresentation involves (1) an assertion, as a fact, of that which is not true, (2) by one who has no reasonable ground for believing it to be true, (3) made with intent to induce the recipient to alter his position to his injury or his risk, (4) with justifiable reliance on the representation, and (5) resulting damage].)

Last, the Court determines that the economic loss rule does not bar this claim for pleading purposes as against Cross-Defendant Cheng. The economic loss rule does not bar claims for fraudulently inducing entry into a contract. (Dhital v. Nissan North America, Inc. (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 828, 841; cf. Rattagan v. Uber Techs. Inc. (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1, 42 [discussing economic loss rule in context of applicability during performance of a contract].)

                                         ii.    Demurrer, Fourth Cause of Action, Business and Professions Claim

Last, and for reasons largely not briefed by the parties, the Court OVERRULES the demurrer as to the FACC’s fourth cause of action.

To state a cause of action for unfair business practices, a plaintiff must establish defendant engaged in an “unlawful, unfair or fraudulent business act or practice and unfair, deceptive, untrue or misleading advertising.” (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 17200.) A breach of contract in turn may form the predicate for a UCL claim, “‘provided it also constitutes conduct that is “unlawful, or unfair, or fraudulent.”’ [Citations.]” (Puentes v. Wells Fargo Home Mortgage, Inc. (2008) 160 Cal.App.4th 638, 645.) Unfair practices with the meaning of this claim “mean[] conduct that threatens an incipient violation of [a] law[] or violates the policy or spirit of one of those laws because its effects are comparable to or the same as a violation of the law, or otherwise significantly threatens or harms competition.” (Cel-Tech Communications, Inc. v. Los Angeles Cellular Telephone Co. (1999) 20 Cal.4th 163, 187.)

Here, as relates to Golden Rights, though the parties’ papers chiefly focus on whether the FACC sufficiently alleges unlawful practices via fraud, the Court is satisfied that the FACC’s third cause of action provides a sufficient basis to support a derivative UCL claim, as reflected in the fourth cause of action, where a threat to competition is alleged in the FACC. (See, e.g., FACC, ¶¶ 16 [Incite in good financial condition before entering agreement with Cheng], 18 [Incite needed agreement to move quickly as other conversations for funding were taking place and funding was needed in 30 days], 43 [Incite becomes distressed asset as result of Cross-Defendants’ conduct].)

As relates to Takashi Cheng, the fraud claims remain viable and thus support a UCL Claim.

Cross-Defendant Golden Rights LLC and Takashi Cheng’s demurrer [CRS NO. 357473767619] is accordingly:

(1) SUSTAINED, with leave to amend, as to the FACC’s first and second causes of action, as alleged against Golden Rights LLC;

(2) OVERRULED as to the FACC’s second cause of action, as alleged against Takashi Cheng; and

(3) OVERRULED as to the FACC’s fourth cause of action, as alleged against both Golden Rights LLC and Takashi Cheng.

    1. Meet and Confer

b.     Legal Standard

The court may, upon a motion or at any time in its discretion and upon terms it deems proper: (a) strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or (b) strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of California, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Code Civ. Proc. § 436, subds. (a), (b); Stafford v. Shultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782 [“Matter in a pleading which is not essential to the claim is surplusage; probative facts are surplusage and may be stricken out or disregarded”].)

For the purposes of a motion to strike pursuant to Sections 435 to 437 of the Code of Civil Procedure, the term “pleading” generally means a demurrer, answer, complaint, or cross-complaint, (Code Civ. Proc., § 435, subd. (a)), and an immaterial allegation or irrelevant matter in a pleading entails (1) an allegation that is not essential to the statement of a claim or defense, (2) an allegation that is neither pertinent to nor supported by an otherwise sufficient claim or defense, or (3) a demand for judgment requesting relief not supported by the allegations of the complaint or cross-complaint (Code Civ. Proc., § 431.10, subds. (b)(1)-(3), (c)).

    1. Discussion

After review, the Court finds in favor of Golden Rights LLC but against Takashi Cheng.

Tort liability is a necessary predicate for punitive damages, where punitive damages may not be awarded as relief in a breach of contract claim. (Civ.Code, § 3294, subd. (a); Applied Equipment Corp. v. Litton Saudi Arabia Ltd. (1994) 7 Cal.4th 503, 516; Cyrus v. Haveson (1976) 65 Cal.App.3d 306, 316.)

Here, having sustained Golden Rights’ demurrer to the FACC’s fraud claims, the only remaining viable claims alleged in the FACC against Golden Rights are the breach of contract claim and its derivative UCL claim, which cannot support punitive damages per the above authority.

However, because the fraud claims remain viable against Takashi Cheng, punitive damages remain generally available, and here, the Court adopts its above discussion relating to the fraud claims and Takashi Cheng to determine that sufficient grounds exist in the FACC to pray for punitive damages against Takashi Cheng based on, at the very least, fraudulent misrepresentations.

d.     Conclusion

Cross-Defendants Golden Rights LLC and Takashi Cheng’s motion to strike [CRS NO. 357473767619] is accordingly:

(1) GRANTED, with leave to amend, as to Cross-Defendant Golden Rights LLC; and

(2) DENIED as to Cross-Defendant Takashi Cheng.

    1. Meet and Confer

  1. Background Facts
    1. Pleadings

Plaintiff Michael Valles sues Defendants Farnaz Pourzanjani and Does 1 through 25 pursuant to a February 18, 2026, Complaint alleging claims of (1) Civil Code section 3346, (2) Conversion, and (3) Trespass.

Plaintiff’s claims arise from allegations that on or around December 8, 2025, Plaintiff’s security footage captured Defendant, Plaintiff’s neighbor, approaching Plaintiff’s property presumably to inquire about removing or cutting the bamboo vegetation for Plaintiff’s own benefit, but that Defendant instead intentionally entered onto, or reached into, Plaintiff’s Property and cut, removed, and destroyed mature bamboo vegetation for the purpose of improving her own view, sunlight and aesthetic preferences.

b.     Motion Before the Court

On May 5, 2026, Plaintiff filed this motion for leave to serve by publication, which the Court denied on June 16, 2026, for lack of a valid declaration supporting the Complaint’s causes of action.

Now before the Court is Plaintiff Valles’s renewed motion for service by publication.

 

  1. Motion for Order Permitting Service by Alternative Means

a.     Preliminary Note

Plaintiff’s notice of motion notices a motion seeking “an order permitting service of the summons and complaint on the following Defendant Farnaz Pourzanjani, by alternative means pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 415.30 and 415.50 and Government Code section 6064.” (Motion, p. 2.)

Service by means provided in section 415.30 contemplates service by prepaid first-class mail or airmail with a return envelope and notice of acknowledgment of receipt by the party to be served to complete service. Unlike section 415.50, no court authorization is required for service by mail under section 415.30.

b.     Legal Standard

Unlike other service methods, a plaintiff must obtain a court order prior to serving a defendant via publication. Strict compliance with the statutory requirements is required. (County of Riverside v. Sup. Court (1997) 54 Cal.App.4th 443, 450; see Code Civ. Proc., § 415.50.)

To obtain an order for publication, the plaintiff must show reasonable diligence in serving the defendant by some other manner. (Code Civ. Proc., § 415.50, subd. (a).) The plaintiff must establish reasonable diligence via the testimony of an individual with personal knowledge of the efforts to serve the defendant. (Olvera v. Olvera (1991) 232 Cal.App.3d 32, 42.) “The term ‘reasonable diligence’ denotes a thorough, systematic investigation and inquiry conducted in good faith by the party or his agent or attorney. A number of honest attempts to learn defendant's whereabouts or his address by inquiry of relatives, and by investigation of appropriate city and telephone directories, [voter registries, and assessor's office property indices situated near the defendant's last known location], generally are sufficient. These are the likely sources of information, and consequently must be searched before resorting to service by publication. Before allowing a plaintiff to resort to service by publication, the courts necessarily require him to show exhaustive attempts to locate the defendant, for it is generally recognized that service by publication rarely results in actual notice.” (Watts v. Crawford (1995) 10 Cal.4th 743, 749, fn. 5.)

Likewise, the plaintiff must establish that a cause of action exists against the defendant via the testimony of an individual with personal knowledge of the underlying facts. (Harris v. Cavasso (1977) 68 Cal.App.3d 723, 726.) A verified complaint is “no substitute for the required affidavit, and the existence of a cause of action was a jurisdictional prerequisite to the issuance of the order for publication.” (Olvera, supra, 232 Cal.App.3d at p. 42, fn. 9.) 

    1. Discussion

After review, the Court finds sufficient grounds for relief.

First, as with his last motion, Plaintiff provides sufficient evidence of reasonable diligence by registered process servers to serve summons on Defendant Farnaz Pourzanjani, with various outright refusals to accept service over intercom. (Motion, Comp. of Evid., Exhibits 1 [Affidavit of Due Diligence from Mohammad Rafiquzzaman for three attempts at service between March 2, 2026, and March 4, 2026, at 8782 Appian Way, West Hollywood, CA 90069, with one rejection of documents over intercom], 2 [Affidavit of Due Diligence from Javier Sanchez for six attempts at 8782 Appian Way, West Hollywood, CA 90069 between March 17, 2026, and March 25, 2026, with two rejections of documents over intercom].)

Second, Plaintiff supports his claims by sworn declaration establishing factual support for the allegations in the Complaint. (Compare Complaint, ¶¶ 5-15, with Motion, Valles Decl., ¶¶ 3-17.)

d.     Conclusion

Plaintiff Michael Valles’s motion for service by alternative means is accordingly GRANTED.

The Court will sign the proposed order filed with Plaintiff’s motion, which provides for publication in the Los Angeles times for the statutorily required time. The Court will interlineate paragraph 7 to update dates for a hearing on the status of service by publication.

  1. Background Facts
    1. Pleadings

This action arises from a dispute concerning the characterization of a real-property transaction and the parties’ respective rights in connection with a residential construction and refinancing project.

Plaintiff Mikhail Siretskiy (“Plaintiff”) sues Defendants Alla Kutz and Igor Kutz (“Defendants”) pursuant to an April 9, 2026, Verified Complaint alleging claims for Breach of Written Contract, Breach of the Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing, Intentional Interference with Prospective Economic Advantage, Declaratory Relief, Reformation, Rescission, Cancellation of Instrument, and Fraud.

The Complaint’s claims chiefly arise from the following allegations, among others. Plaintiff alleges he purchased 5325 Cangas Drive as his future residence and later received approximately $349,000 from Defendants under what he understood to be a financing arrangement repayable upon refinance or sale of the property. Defendants, who were financially sophisticated and represented by attorneys, structured the transaction documents to appear as though Defendants had acquired a 36% ownership interest. Plaintiff asserts he reasonably believed the documents reflected repayment terms rather than a conveyance of ownership. He claims Defendants contributed no mortgage payments or construction costs and later used the documents to assert ownership rights.

Plaintiff further alleges Defendants initiated litigation in April 2022 claiming ownership, which clouded title and prevented him from refinancing. He contends the inability to refinance led to foreclosure, causing him to lose the property, equity, and invested construction funds. Plaintiff also alleges Defendants previously breached a listing agreement involving another property, depriving him of earned commission income needed to fund the Cangas project. He asserts Defendants’ misrepresentations, concealment, and interference were intentional and designed to convert a financing arrangement into a multi-million-dollar ownership claim.

b.     Motion Before the Court

Now before the Court is Defendants’ opposed anti-SLAPP motion challenging the causes of action alleged in the Complaint.

 

  1. Anti-SLAPP Motion

a.     Legal Standard

Anti-SLAPP analysis under Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16 proceeds in two familiar steps. In the first step, the defendant or moving party must make “a threshold showing that the challenged cause of action is one ‘arising from’ protected activity.” (Barry v. State Bar of California (2017) 2 Cal.5th 318, 321, quotations omitted (Barry).) In this context, the term “protected activity” refers to speech or petitioning activities. (Barry, 2 Cal.5th at p. 321.) A claim arises from protected activity when that activity underlies or forms the basis for the claim; otherwise stated, “the defendant’s act underlying the plaintiff’s cause of action [must] itself [be] … an act in furtherance of the right of petition or free speech.” (Park v. Board of Trustees of California State University (2017) 2 Cal.5th 1057, 1063, quotations omitted (Park).) “[T]he focus is on determining what ‘the defendant’s activity [is] that gives rise to his or her asserted liability—and whether that activity constitutes protected speech or petitioning.’” (Ibid.) In teasing out whether protected conduct exists, courts should consider the elements of the challenged claim and what actions by the defendant supply those elements and consequently form the basis for liability. (Ibid.)

If the court finds the defendant or moving party succeeds at the first step, then the burden shifts to the plaintiff to “demonstrate[] a probability of prevailing on the claim.” (Ibid. [quotations omitted].) At the second step, courts “evaluate the defendants’ evidence only to determine if it defeats that submitted by the plaintiff as a matter of law.’ [Citation.] ‘[I]n order to establish the requisite probability of prevailing [citation], the plaintiff need only have “‘stated and substantiated a legally sufficient claim.’” [Citation.] “Put another way, the plaintiff ‘must demonstrate that the complaint is both legally sufficient and supported by a sufficient prima facie showing of facts to sustain a favorable judgment if the evidence submitted by the plaintiff is credited.’”‘ [Citation.] … That burden [is] not particularly high.” (Area 51 Productions, Inc. v. City of Alameda (2018) 20 Cal.App.5th 581, 602 (Area 51 Productions, Inc.).) “Claims with the requisite minimal merit may proceed.” (Navellier v. Sletten (2002) 29 Cal.4th 82, 94 (Navallier).) If the cause of action satisfies both prongs of the anti-SLAPP statute, then it is subject to being struck. (Barry, supra, 2 Cal.5th at p. 321.)

As stated by our State’s highest Court: “This is a ‘summary-judgment-like procedure at an early stage of the litigation.’” (Varian, supra, 35 Cal.4th at p. 192.)

    1. Discussion

                                          i.    First Prong – Protected Activity

Defendants’ motion challenges various sets of acts as protected petitioning activity that should be stricken from the Complaint pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16, which the Court summarizes as follows:

(1)¿Kutz’s filing of the civil lawsuit Kutz v. Siretskiy, LASC Case No. 22STCV12323, including the April 12, 2022, Complaint and the subsequent First Amended Complaint, which Plaintiff expressly alleges caused a cloud on title, interfered with refinancing, and gave rise to all asserted damages (Complaint, ¶¶¿21, 26, 37, 44);

(2)¿Kutz’s prosecution and maintenance of the 22STCV12323 litigation, including continued assertions of ownership in the judicial proceeding, which Plaintiff alleges constituted wrongful conduct forming the basis of his breach, interference, declaratory-relief, reformation, rescission, cancellation, and fraud claims (Complaint, ¶¶¿31, 95, 107, 118, 129, 140, 152, 165, 171, 179, 196); and

(3)¿Kutz’s litigation-related communications made in connection with the 22STCV12323 litigation, including statements and written filings asserting ownership rights, which Plaintiff contends clouded title, blocked refinancing, and prevented performance under the parties’ agreement (Complaint, ¶¶¿21, 25, 28, 32, 42, 75-77, 179-180).

After review, and on grounds not raised by the parties, the Court determines Defendants fail to carry their burden to show petitioning activity.

Specifically, the Court determines the alleged conduct does not arise from petitioning activity and is otherwise mere background for the allegations in the Complaint. Instead, the Court determines that the actionable conduct supporting the Complaint’s claims serves as mere evidence of wrongful conduct, as reasoned in Department of Fair Employment & Housing v. 1105 Alta Loma Road Apartments, LLC (2007) 154 Cal.App.4th 1273 (DFEH). Moreover, briefly, the requested relief is not altogether clear as to the exact language challenged in the Complaint, as the notice of motion does not clearly state which claims are at issue, a deficiency shared by the points and authorities, thus failing to comply with Bonni v. St. Joseph Health System (2021) 11 Cal.5th 995, 1010 (Bonni) insofar as challenged claims within pleaded counts must be identified to permit the Court to proceed with an anti-SLAPP analysis.

Turning to DFEH, there, the complaint consisted primarily of the details of numerous communications between a tenant or the tenant’s reppraying fortives and the landlord regarding assertions of disability by the tenant, with the concluding paragraph of factual background alleging that because the landlord did not receive documentation it requested regarding the tenant’s disability, it instituted three unlawful detainer actions, resulting in the tenant’s eviction, with the tenant thereafter filing suit for disability discrimination and praying money damages and equitable relief related thereto. (Id. at p. 1284.) The landlord filed an anti-SLAPP motion, which was denied under pre-Bonni gravamen framing on the basis that the causes of action alleged in the pleadings did not arise from the landlord’s protected activity, but rather, arose from disability discrimination. (Id. at p. 1281.) The court of appeal affirmed, reasoning that “the communications and the actual eviction itself were not the acts attacked in DFEH's complaint” and that “[i]nstead, the allegations of wrongdoing in DFEH's complaint arose from [the landlord’s alleged acts of failing to accommodate [the tenant’s] disability[,]” where “[t]he letters, email[,] and filing of unlawful detainer actions constituted DFEH's evidence of Alta Loma's alleged disability discrimination.” (Id. at pp. 1284-1285.)

Similarly, in Copenbarger v. Morris Cerullo World Evangelism (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 1237, 1248-1249 (Copenbarger), which cited DFEH, the court of appeal held that while allegations involving the service of a three-day notice to quit generally arise from protected activity, the challenged allegations there instead involved allegations of the wrongful inducement of a three-day notice to quit, which lay outside of anti-SLAPP protections.

Here, the Court determines that as with DFEH and similar to Copenbarger, and as detailed in the Factual Background above, the allegations of protected activity in the Complaint form a pleaded chain of allegedly unlawful and fraudulent conduct by Defendants to defraud Plaintiff of his interest in the subject property, starting when the parties consummated the agreement transferring 36% of the property to Defendants without Plaintiff’s knowledge, with Defendants benefitting from that fraud by asserting ownership rights over property without just cause and impeding Plaintiff’s ability to refinance the property, subsequently leading to foreclosure, which lies closer to the wrongful inducement and evidence of unlawful conduct supporting the stated claims discussed in DFEH and Copenbarger than a suit purely based on protected activity without the context of fraud.

Accordingly, Defendants fail to meet their burden on anti-SLAPP.

                                         ii.    Second Prong – Probability of Prevailing on the Merits

Defendants having failed to meet their burden on the first prong, the Court does not proceed with further discussion of the second prong.

c.     Conclusion

Defendants Alla Kutz and Igor Kutz’s anti-SLAPP motion is DENIED.
