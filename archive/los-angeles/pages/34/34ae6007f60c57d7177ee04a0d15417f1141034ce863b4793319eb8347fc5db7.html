DEPARTMENT 732 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 22STCV36611    Hearing Date: August 5, 2026    Dept: 732


John Doe v. Roe Youth Organization 1, et al.

 

Wednesday, August 5, 2026

 

 

 

CASE NUMBER: 22STCV36611

 

OPPOSED

___________________________________________________________________

 

Plaintiff John doe’s Motion for In Camera Review and Order to Name Roe Defendant ___________________________________________________________________

 

Facts: This is an action based on liability for child sexual abuse. The Third Amended Complaint (TAC) alleges as follows. Plaintiff John Doe was a minor of 13 years old in 1991 when he was sexually abused by Defendant Roe Mentor. (TAC ¶ 23.) Roe Mentor was an agent, advisor, counselor, and/or volunteer for Defendant Roe Youth Organization 2, which was supervised by Roe Youth Organization 1. (TAC ¶ 23.) Defendants maintained Roe Mentor in his position despite having actual or constructive knowledge of Roe Mentor’s dangerous propensities. (TAC ¶ 28.)

Procedural History: Plaintiff filed the original Complaint on November 21, 2022. Plaintiff filed a First Amended Complaint on December 14, 2022, and a Second Amended Complaint on October 6, 2023. Plaintiff filed the TAC on January 4, 2024, alleging five causes of action:

1.      Negligence

2.      Negligent Supervision/Training/Hiring/Retention

3.      Sexual Battery

4.      Assault

5.      Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress

On April 1, 2025, this court heard Plaintiff’s motion to amend complaint to reveal Defendant’s name, and set a status conference regarding the court’s ruling based on sealed materials for April 25, 2025.

On April 25, 2025, through the judicial assistant, the court found insufficient corroborating evidence to grant Plaintiff’s application.

On May 5, 2025, the court held an OSC re: the April 25, 2025, order.

Plaintiff filed the present motion for in camera review and order to name Roe defendant on July 9, 2026.

Defendant filed an opposition on July 23, 2026.

Plaintiff filed a reply on July 29, 206.

Analysis

 

I.                   MOTION FOR IN CAMERA REVIEW AND ORDER TO NAME DEFENDANT

Code of Civil Procedure § 340.1 prescribes special procedures for the prosecution of claims based on the sexual assault of minors. That statute states that for such claims, “[e]very plaintiff 40 years of age or older at the time the action is filed shall file certificates of merit as specified in subdivision (f).” (Code Civ. Proc. § 340.1, subd. (e).) In actions brought by such plaintiffs, “a defendant shall be named by “Doe” designation in any pleadings or papers filed in the action until there has been a showing of corroborative fact as to the charging allegations against that defendant.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 340.1, subd. (k).)

 

Here, Plaintiff John Doe has accordingly sued Defendants Roe Mentor, Roe youth Organization 1, and Roe Youth Organization 2 through fictitious pseudonyms.

 

However, the statute does not require permanent anonymity for such defendants:

 

(l) At any time after the action is filed, the plaintiff may apply to the court for permission to amend the complaint to substitute the name of the defendant or defendants for the fictitious designation, as follows:

 

(1) The application shall be accompanied by a certificate of corroborative fact executed by the attorney for the plaintiff. The certificate shall declare that the attorney has discovered one or more facts corroborative of one or more of the charging allegations against a defendant or defendants, and shall set forth in clear and concise terms the nature and substance of the corroborative fact. If the corroborative fact is evidenced by the statement of a witness or the contents of a document, the certificate shall declare that the attorney has personal knowledge of the statement of the witness or of the contents of the document, and the identity and location of the witness or document shall be included in the certificate. For purposes of this section, a fact is corroborative of an allegation if it confirms or supports the allegation. The opinion of any mental health practitioner concerning the plaintiff shall not constitute a corroborative fact for purposes of this section.

 

. . .

 

(3) If the application to name a defendant is made after that defendant's appearance in the action, the application shall be served on all parties and proof of service provided to the court, but the certificate of corroborative fact by the attorney shall not be served on any party or their counsel of record.

 

(Code Civ. Proc. § 4340.1, subd. (l)(1)–(3).)

 

The court must “review the application and the certificate of corroborative fact in camera” and its decision must be “based solely on the certificate and any reasonable inferences to be drawn from the certificate.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 340.1, subd. (m).) The certificates of corroborative fact must be kept under seal and remain “confidential from the public and all parties to the litigation other than the plaintiff.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 340.1, subd (n).) Plaintiff has lodged a certificate of corroborative fact with the court under seal.

 

This is Plaintiff’s second application for such relief. The prior motion was heard on April 1, 2025, whereupon the court took into consideration Plaintiff’s documents lodged under seal and set a status conference on the motion for April 25, 2025. At this subsequent hearing, the judge was not present, but an order prepared by the court was issued through the judicial assistant stating as follows:

 

The Court has reviewed the confidential certificate of corroborative facts in camera and find, based solely on the certificate, and any reasonable inferences to be drawn from the certificate, that an insufficient corroborative fact of one or more of the charging allegations against the defendant has been shown.

 

(4/25/2025 Order.)

 

Defendant notes the prior denial of Plaintiff’s prior motion in opposition to the present one. But Plaintiff in reply argues that there was no such denial, as the order was merely read by the judicial assistant, not the judge. (Reply at p. 6.) But the judicial assistant did not render the order; they merely relayed the order of the court. And said order was subsequently confirmed by the judge and parties on May 5, 2025, That order states as follows:

 

All parties consent to the Court's Order of 4/25/25 Re certificate and accompanying lodgment of documents.

 

The Court agrees to an In-Camera hearing to be held telephonically today with Plaintiff's counsel: Sahar Malek.

 

(5/5/2025 Order.)

 

The order reflects no change to the court’s conclusion of April 25, 2025.

The present motion is thus properly construed as a successive application for relief under Code of Civil Procedure § 1008, subd. (b):

(b) A party who originally made an application for an order which was refused in whole or part, or granted conditionally or on terms, may make a subsequent application for the same order upon new or different facts, circumstances, or law, in which case it shall be shown by affidavit what application was made before, when and to what judge, what order or decisions were made, and what new or different facts, circumstances, or law are claimed to be shown. For a failure to comply with this subdivision, any order made on a subsequent application may be revoked or set aside on ex parte motion.

 

(Code Civ. Proc. § 1008, subd. (b).)

 

Plaintiff does not here make the requisite showing.

 

Accordingly, the motion is DENIED.

Case Number: 25STCV30056    Hearing Date: August 5, 2026    Dept: 732


Chrstopher Nelson Berndt v. Mary L. Berndt., et al.

 

Wednesday, August 5, 2026

 

 

 

CASE NUMBER: 25STCV30056

 

UNOPPOSED

___________________________________________________________________

 

Plaintiff Christopher Nelson Berndt’s Motion for Leave to File First Amended Complaint

___________________________________________________________________

 

Facts: This is an action for breach of fiduciary duty. The Complaint alleges as follows. Plaintiff Christopher Nelson Berndt is the beneficiary of a trust and entitled to reside in trust property. (Complaint  9.) Defendant Mary L. Berndt is the trustee of the trust and has failed to adequately account for trust assets or to maintain the property for Plaintiff’s use. (Complaint ¶¶ 10–11.)

Procedural History:  Plaintiff filed the Complaint on October 14, 2025, alleging four causes of action:

1.      Breach of Fiduciary Duty

2.      Breach of Trust

3.      Declaratory Relief

4.      Accounting

This court on April 9, 2026, denied nonparty Cherilin Lee Berndt’s motion for judgment on the pleadings.

On May 19, 2026, this court denied Plaintiff’s motion to consolidate.

Plaintiff filed the present motion for leave to amend on May 19, 2026. No opposition has been filed.

Analysis:

I.                    MOTION FOR LEAVE TO AMEND

Code Civ. Proc. section 473 subd. (a)(1) states that:

 

The court may, in furtherance of justice, and on any terms as may be proper, allow a party to amend any pleading or proceeding by adding or striking out the name of any party, or by correcting a mistake in the name of a party, or a mistake in any other respect; and may, upon like terms, enlarge the time for answer or demurrer. The court may likewise, in its discretion, after notice to the adverse party, allow, upon any terms as may be just, an amendment to any pleading or proceeding in other particulars; and may upon like terms allow an answer to be made after the time limited by this code.

 

“The trial court has discretion to permit or deny the amendment of the complaint, but instances justifying the court's denial of leave to amend are rare.” (Armenta ex rel. City of Burbank v. Mueller Co. (2006) 142 Cal.App.4th 636, 642.)

 

“Although courts are bound to apply a policy of great liberality in permitting amendments to the complaint at any stage of the proceedings, up to and including trial [Citations], this policy should be applied only ‘[w]here no prejudice is shown to the adverse party . . .’ [Citation.] A different result is indicated ‘[w]here inexcusable delay and probable prejudice to the opposing party’ is shown. [Citation.]” (Magpali v. Farmers Group, Inc. (1996) 48 Cal.App.4th 471, 487.)

 

Pursuant to California Rule of Court Rule 3.1324, “[a] motion to amend a pleading before trial must: (1)Include a copy of the proposed amendment or amended pleading, which must be serially numbered to differentiate it from previous pleadings or amendments; (2) State what allegations in the previous pleading are proposed to be deleted, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the deleted allegations are located; and (3)State what allegations are proposed to be added to the previous pleading, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the additional allegations are located.”

Plaintiff Christopher Nelson Berndt (“Plaintiff”) seeks leave to file a first amended complaint (“FAC”) replacing Defendant Mary L. Berndt with Cherilin Berndt as the nominal trustee of the Berndt Family Trust dated October 25, 2001, and adding new causes of action for quiet title, fraud, and elder abuse, seeking to establish Plaintiff’s interest in certain real property and to establish that Defendant Cherilin Berndt is not trustee of the relevant trust. (Motion Exh. B.)

 

Leave is proper here. No defendant has yet appeared in this action, and Plaintiff may amend the complaint once as of right under Code of Civil Procedure § 472, subd. (a). Although Cherilin Berndt filed an answer to the original complaint on February 17, 2026, said complaint did not name Cherilin Berndt as defendant, and as the court noted in its order denying her motion for judgment on the pleadings on April 9, 2026, she is not a party to this action, and will not be made one until the FAC is filed. Thus amendment may be had as of right under section 472, as no functional “answer, demurrer, or motion to strike” has been filed. (Code Civ. Proc. § 472, subd. (a).)

 

The motion is therefore GRANTED. Plaintiff shall file the first amended complaint within 5 days of this order.

Case Number: 25STCV38279    Hearing Date: August 5, 2026    Dept: 732


Tze On Fung v. AIIC Group 13 Lender, LP, et al.

 

Wednesday, August 5, 2026

 

 

 

CASE NUMBER: 25STCV38279

 

OPPOSED

___________________________________________________________________

 

Plaintiff Tze On Fung’s Motion for Leave to File Second Amended Complaint

___________________________________________________________________

 

Facts: This is an action for fraud and breach of fiduciary duty. The First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) alleges as follows. Plaintiff Tze On Fung (“Plaintiff”) invested $500,000 with Defendants, plus a $50,000 administrative fee, based on the assurance of Defendants AIIC Group 13 Lender and AIIC Group 13 Manager, LLC’s (“Defendants”) promise that his $500,000 would be returned within seven years, and on the promise of obtaining a United States EB5 visa. (FAC ¶¶ 19–20.) Defendants have not returned the money as promised. (FAC  ¶¶ 20–24.)

 

Procedural History: Plaintiff filed the Complaint on December 30, 2025, alleging nine causes of action:

1.      Breach of Contract

2.      Breach of Fiduciary Duty

3.      Fraud – Intentional Misrepresentation

4.      Fraudulent Concealment

5.      Promise without Intent to Perform

6.      Accounting

7.      Negligence

8.      Declaratory Relief

 

Plaintiff filed the FAC on April 14, 2026, alleging 12 causes of action and adding as defendants Greenwich Street Holdco Trust, LLC, Tribeca Hotel Equity, LP, Kevin Wright, James R. Parks, Echo Liang, Richard Zhao, and Can-Achieve:

 

1.      Breach of fiduciary Duties

2.      Fraud – Intentional Misrepresentation

3.      Fraud – Negligent Misrepresentation

4.      Fraudulent Concealment

5.      Promise without Intention to Perform

6.      Accounting

7.      Negligence

8.      Declaratory Relief

9.      Business & Professions Code § 17200

10.  Business & Professions Code § 17500

11.  Securities Violations

12.  Aiding and Abetting

 

This court on April 29, 2026, granted Defendants AIIC Group 13 Lender and AIIC Group 13 Manager, LLC motion to compel arbitration.

 

Plaintiff filed the present motion for leave to file second amended complaint on May 5, 2025.

 

Defendants AIIC Group 13 Lender and AIIC Group 13 Manager, LLC filed an opposition on July 23, 2026.

 

Plaintiff filed a reply on July 27, 2026.

 

Analysis:

I.                    MOTION FOR LEAVE TO AMEND

Code Civ. Proc. section 473 subd. (a)(1) states that:

 

The court may, in furtherance of justice, and on any terms as may be proper, allow a party to amend any pleading or proceeding by adding or striking out the name of any party, or by correcting a mistake in the name of a party, or a mistake in any other respect; and may, upon like terms, enlarge the time for answer or demurrer. The court may likewise, in its discretion, after notice to the adverse party, allow, upon any terms as may be just, an amendment to any pleading or proceeding in other particulars; and may upon like terms allow an answer to be made after the time limited by this code.

 

“The trial court has discretion to permit or deny the amendment of the complaint, but instances justifying the court's denial of leave to amend are rare.” (Armenta ex rel. City of Burbank v. Mueller Co. (2006) 142 Cal.App.4th 636, 642.)

 

“Although courts are bound to apply a policy of great liberality in permitting amendments to the complaint at any stage of the proceedings, up to and including trial [Citations], this policy should be applied only ‘[w]here no prejudice is shown to the adverse party . . .’ [Citation.] A different result is indicated ‘[w]here inexcusable delay and probable prejudice to the opposing party’ is shown. [Citation.]” (Magpali v. Farmers Group, Inc. (1996) 48 Cal.App.4th 471, 487.)

 

Pursuant to California Rule of Court Rule 3.1324, “[a] motion to amend a pleading before trial must: (1)Include a copy of the proposed amendment or amended pleading, which must be serially numbered to differentiate it from previous pleadings or amendments; (2) State what allegations in the previous pleading are proposed to be deleted, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the deleted allegations are located; and (3)State what allegations are proposed to be added to the previous pleading, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the additional allegations are located.”

Plaintiff Tze On Fung (“Plaintiff”) seeks leave to file a second amended complaint adding no new causes of action or defendants, but merely to add allegations concerning the relationship of defendants added to this case in the First Amended Complaint while Defendants AIIC Group 13 Lender and AIIC Group 13 Manager, LLC (collectively “AIIC Defenedants”) motion to compel arbitration, now granted was pending.

When Plaintiff opposed AIIC Defendants’ motion to compel arbitration, arguing that he had filed an FAC including new defendants who had not signed the arbitration agreement and seeking discretionary relief under Code of Civil Procedure § 1281.2, subd. (c), the court ruled as follows:

 

Plaintiff’s belated inclusion of new defendants does not warrant discretionary denial of the present motion. Plaintiffs introduce the new defendants expressly to defeat arbitration (FAC ¶¶ 12–13; Opposition at p. 2 [“In response to the motion to compel arbitration, plaintiff has contemplated and evaluated the issues at hand and amended the complaint, adding other defendants who do not have any arbitration agreement with the plaintiff.”].) The FAC’s allegations against these defendants do not articulate any theory by which they partake of the liability alleged against the moving defendants, and the allegations are so threadbare as to justify a conclusion that Plaintiff avoids pleading facts that would warrant these parties’ enforcing the arbitration agreement for themselves. (See, e.g., Ronay Family Limited Partnership v. Tweed (2013) 216 Cal.App.4th 830, 838 [“[A]n agent may enforce an arbitration agreement to which its principal is a party.”]; Molecular Analytical Systems v. Ciphergen Biosystems, Inc. (2010) 186 Cal.App.4th 696, 709 [“When a nonsignatory has the right to enforce an arbitration agreement based on application of the equitable estoppel doctrine, section 1281.2(c) does not come into play.”].) Plaintiff ought not to benefit from the discretionary relief of Code of Civil Procedure § 1281.2, subd. (c), permitting denial of arbitration when interrelated claims are being litigated against non-signatories in court, when Plaintiff’s nonsignatory claims are pleaded so evasively that the court can determine neither whether the claims are interrelated, nor whether the claims against the nonsignatories are themselves arbitrable.

 

 

(4/29/2026 Order.) In its order, the court stated that “[a]rbitration applies only to moving parties,” i.e. AIIC Defendants. (Ibid.)

 

Plaintiff seeks to add allegations that Defendants Kevin Wright and James R. Parks are officers of AIIC defendants, that Defendants Echo Liang and Richard Zhao are agents of Defendant Can-Achieve. (Ho Decl. ¶ 2.)

 

AIIC Defendants argue that the proposed amendment is an attempt to delay arbitration as ordered by the court, as Plaintiff has not sought to commence arbitration in the intervening time and has not made any attempt to serve the defendants added in the FAC. (Opposition at pp. 2–3.)

 

Leave to amend is properly granted here. The amendments pertain not to any party for which arbitration has been ordered, but only to those Defendants added in the FAC who are expressly not bound by the April 29, 2026 order. The proposed amendments merely add allegations respecting the parties’ relationships to one another. Nor is there any reason to believe that Plaintiff by this belated amendment seeks reconsideration of the court’s arbitration order, as Plaintiff in reply states that he “respect[s] and accept[s] the court’s ruling.” (Reply at p.

ruling.” (Reply at p. 2.) The proposed amendments appear to bear only on whether the remaining defendants, as yet not served, may enforce the arbitration agreement for themselves. This matter has not been adjudicated.

 

Accordingly, the motion is GRANTED. The second amended complaint shall be filed within 5 days of this order.

Case Number: 26STCV06534    Hearing Date: August 5, 2026    Dept: 732


Hartbeat, LLC v. Eric Eddings, et al..

 

Wednesday, August 5, 2026

 

 

 

CASE NUMBER: 26STCV06534

 

OPPOSED

___________________________________________________________________

 

Plaintiff Hartbeat, LLC’s Motion to Compel Arbitration ___________________________________________________________________

 

Facts: This is an action for breach of contract and appropriation of trade secrets. The Complaint alleges as follows. Plaintiff Hartbeat, LLC (“Plaintiff”) employed Defendants Eric Eddings and Lesley Gwam (“Defendants”), pursuant to which employment Defendants executed confidentiality agreements concerning their work product and  Plaintiff’s trade secrets. (Complaint ¶¶ 9–24.) Defendants used their access to Plaintiff’s proprietary business and marketing strategies to launch their own competing business, which they started while working for Plaintiff. (Complaint ¶¶ 30–40.)

 

Procedural History: Plaintiff filed the Complaint on February 27, 2026, alleging five causes of action:

 

1.      Breach of Contract

2.      Unfair Business Practices

3.      Misappropriation of Trade Secrets

4.      Breach of Fiduciary Duty

5.      Unjust Enrichment

 

On March 9, 2026, the court in Department 86 granted Plaintiff’s ex parte application for a temporary restraining order.

 

On March 26, 2026, the court in Department 86 denied Plaintiff’s motion for preliminary injunction.

 

Plaintiff filed the present motion to compel arbitration on May 1, 2026.

 

Defendants filed an opposition on July 23, 2026.

 

Plaintiff filed a reply on July 29, 2026.

 

Analysis:

I.                   MOTION TO COMPEL ARBITRATION

On petition of a party to an arbitration agreement to arbitrate a controversy, a court must order the petitioner and respondent to arbitrate the controversy if it determines the arbitration agreement exists, unless (1) the petitioner has waived its right to arbitrate; (2) grounds exist for the revocation of the agreement; or (3) “[a] party to the arbitration agreement is also a party to a pending court action or special proceeding with a third party, arising out of the same transaction or series of related transactions and there is a possibility of conflicting rulings on a common issue of law or fact.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2.)

 

“[T]he party moving to compel arbitration bears the burden of establishing the existence of a preponderance of the evidence any fact necessary to its defense. The role of the trial court is to sit as a trier of fact, weighing any affidavits, declarations, and other documentary evidence, together with oral testimony received at the court's discretion, to reach a determination on the issue of arbitrability.” (Hotels Nevada v. L.A. Pacific Center, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 754, 758.)

 

Plaintiff Hartbeat LLC (“Plaintiff”) moves to compel arbitration of its claims for breach of contract, misappropriation of trade secrets, and others against its former employees, Defendants Eric Eddings and Lesley Gwam (“Defendants”), based on arbitration agreements executed by Defendants as they were onboarded into Plaintiff’s company. (Clanagan Decl. ¶ 2, Exhs. A, B.) These arbitration agreements state:

 

To the maximum extent allowed by law, the Company and I mutually consent to the resolution by binding arbitration of all claims or causes of action, except as provided in Paragraph 2 below, that the Company may have against me or that I may have against the Company or the Company’s current and former owners, partners, members, officers, directors, employees, representatives and agents, all parent companies, subsidiaries and affiliated entities, all benefit plans and benefit plan sponsors, fiduciaries, administrators, and all successors and assigns of any of them.

 

(Clanagan Decl. Exhs. A, B.) The agreements specifically identify various statutory claims as being subject to arbitration, and exclude from arbitration claims for worker’s compensation or petitions for provisional remedies under Code of Civil Procedure § 1281.8. (Clanagan Decl. Exhs. A, B, ¶¶ 1, 2.)

 

The agreements also contain a delegation provision, stating, “The arbitrator shall have exclusive authority to resolve any dispute relating to the interpretation, applicability, enforceability or formation of this Agreement including, but not limited to, any claim that all or any part of this Agreement is void or voidable.” (Clanagan Decl. Exhs. A, B, ¶ 7(f).)

 

Defendants in opposition argue that the arbitration agreements are not applicable to Plaintiff’s claims here, because provisions in the Confidentiality Agreements upon which Plaintiff brings suit establish exclusive jurisdiction of the courts to hear claims relating to those agreements (Opposition at pp. 4–5.) Defendants argue that the arbitration agreements are unconscionable, because they are adhesive contracts imposed upon employees as a condition of employment, and reserves to Plaintiff the right to go to court for expansive equitable remedies, which employees are expressly made to waive. (Opposition at pp. 5–7.) Defendants finally argue that Plaintiff has waived the right to arbitrate by filing suit in court. (Opposition at pp. 7–8.)

 

Plaintiff in reply argues that the questions raised by Defendants are properly resolved by the arbitrator pursuant to the delegation clause. (Reply at pp. 5–6.) However, it is proper to address them here. Where a party opposing arbitration argues that a separate agreement militates against arbitration and the delegation of power to the arbitrator to decide threshold issues of arbitrability, the resolution of that question “must be answered by a court.” (Coinbase, Inc. v. Suski (2024) 602 U.S. 143, 150.)

 

 “The parties may agree to delegate authority to the arbitrator to decide arbitrability, but given the contrary presumption, evidence that the parties intended such a delegation must be ‘clear and unmistakable’ before a court will enforce a delegation provision.” (Mondragon v. Sunrun Inc. (2024) 101 Cal.App.5th 592, 603.)

 

No clear and unmistakable intent to delegate is evident here. The delegation provision, considered on its, own, is clear enough: the arbitrator’s authority to decide questions of “the interpretation, applicability, enforceability or formation of this Agreement” is “exclusive.” (Clanagan Decl. Exhs. A, B, ¶ 7(f).) But the confidentiality agreements that Plaintiff seeks to enforce here, which were executed alongside the arbitration agreements (Complaint ¶ 25), and which are attached to its Complaint, cloud this understanding, as they vest certain courts with exclusive jurisdiction to try matters relating to the confidentiality agreement. Section 5.4 of those agreements states:

 

This Agreement shall be governed in all respects by the laws of the State of California, without regard to the conflict of laws provisions thereof. Each of the Parties irrevocably consents to the exclusive personal jurisdiction and venue of the federal and state courts located in Los Angeles, California, as applicable, for any matter arising out of or relating to this Agreement. Additionally, notwithstanding anything in the foregoing to the contrary, a claim for equitable relief arising out of or related to this Agreement may be brought in any court of competent jurisdiction.

 

(Complaint Exhs. E, F, § 5.4.) This provision raises doubt as to whether suits based on the confidentiality agreements are in any respect to be heard by the arbitrator, rather than the courts in which they vest “exclusive personal jurisdiction and venue.” (Ibid.)  While the delegation clause would perhaps apply in a suit not arising from the confidentiality agreements, its application to the present suit, which expressly seeks to enforce these confidentiality agreements, is not clear and unmistakable. Thus the delegation clause is not applicable here.

 

Proceeding directly to the question of whether Plaintiff’s claims, which arise from and relate to the confidentiality agreements,  are arbitrable, the language in the confidentiality agreements weighs heavily against this conclusion. The above provision vests the state and federal courts in Los Angeles, California with “the exclusive personal jurisdiction and venue . . . for any matter arising out of or relating to this Agreement.” (Complaint Exhs. E, F, § 5.4.) Although Plaintiff characterizes this as a “consent-to-jurisdiction” clause merely describing the proper venue in which to seek provisional remedies while arbitration is pending, the clause is unlike the consent-to jurisdiction clause in the authority that it offers for support, which expressly acknowledged and provided for arbitration in the venue identified. (Scott v. Yoho (2016) 248 Cal.App.4th 392, 398–399 [where the provision stated, “I agree that any lawsuit or arbitration that is brought against Dr. Yoho, his corporation, associates, or organization, will be tried in Pasadena, California.”].)

 

Indeed, rather than providing for provisional remedies, the confidentiality agreements expressly state that “a claim for equitable relief arising out of or related to this Agreement may be brought in any court of competent jurisdiction,” without limiting equitable relief to that of a provisional character. (Complaint Exhs. E, F, § 5.4.) This express allowance for the bringing of claims in court is inconsistent with an interpretation that such claims are subject to the arbitration agreement.

 

Additionally, the agreement contains a severability provision for when “a court of law holds any provision of this Agreement to be illegal, invalid or unenforceable” (Id. at § 5.2, italics added), and an attorney fees provision for when a party “institutes any legal action to enforce its rights under the Agreement or to recover damages for breach of the Agreement. (Id. at § 5.6, italics added.) This language appears to assume that claims under the agreements will be tried in court, not arbitration.

 

The confidentiality agreements vest exclusive jurisdiction in courts,  specifically authorize actions in court for equitable relief, and refer to courts and legal actions repeatedly. But they do not mention arbitration, and do not appear to contemplate it. The arbitration agreements, conversely, contain no reference to the confidentiality agreements, and contain but one reference to the arbitrability of claims for the “breach of any contract or covenant,” a general provision that does not control over the specific provisions of the confidentiality agreements.  (Clanagan Decl. Exhs. A, B, ¶ 1.)

 

Defendants arguments concerning the non-arbitrable nature of Plaintiff’s claims is therefore persuasive. Far less definitive language has been held to constitute an arbitration carve-out for confidentiality-based claims. (See Fuentes v. Empire Nissan, Inc. (2026) 19 Cal.5th 93, 108–109 [“Most significantly, the confidentiality *109 agreements make no reference to arbitration. The arbitration agreement, by contrast, consistently uses the words “arbitrate” and “arbitration” when that is what it means and uses “court” and “action” to refer to judicial proceedings. The absence of any reference to arbitration in the confidentiality agreements suggests that, at the time of their signing, the parties did not intend for claims under them to be subject to mandatory arbitration.”].)

 

Additionally, Defendants make a persuasive showing that the arbitration agreements are unconscionable. “Unconscionability requires a showing of both procedural unconscionability and substantive unconscionability.” (Ajamian v. CantorCO2e, L.P. (2012) 203 Cal.App.4th 771, 795.) The oppression that creates procedural unconscionability arises from an inequality of bargaining power that results in no real negotiation and an absence of meaningful choice.” (Grand Prospect Partners, L.P. v. Ross Dress for Less, Inc. (2015) 232 Cal.App.4th 1332, 1347–1348.) “Substantive unconscionability is not susceptible of precise definition. It appears the various descriptions—unduly oppressive, overly harsh, so one-sided as to shock the conscience, and unreasonably favorable to the more powerful party—all reflect the same standard. Substantive unconscionability is not concerned with a simple old-fashioned bad bargain.” (Id. at p. 1349, citations omitted.)

Here, Plaintiff presents a basis for finding some degree of procedural unconscionability, because arbitration contracts presented to employees on a take-it-or-leave-it basis are at least minimally procedurally unconscionable. (See Armendariz v. Foundation Health Psychcare Services, Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 83, 113.) The agreements here are form contracts expressly offered to employees as a condition of their employment. (Clanagan Decl. Exhs. A, B.)

As to substantive unconscionability, the confidentiality agreements contain at least three substantively unconscionable provisions that would render enforcement of the arbitration agreements unconscionable. Contra Plaintiff’s argument in reply (Reply at p. 8), it is proper to consider the arbitration agreements in connection with the confidentiality agreements, as “[a]everal contracts relating to the same matters, between the same parties, and made as parts of substantially one transaction, are to be taken together.” (Civ. Code § 1642.)

Here, the confidentiality agreements create a unilateral carve-out for the employer’s claims under the arbitration agreement, which has been held to create a lack of mutuality rendering arbitration agreements unenforceable. (See Davis v. Kozak (2020) 53 Cal.App.5th 897, 917, disapproved on other grounds by Ramirez v. Charter Communications, Inc. (2024) 16 Cal.5th 478.) Plaintiff articulates no business justification for such a carve-out.

Additionally, the confidentiality agreements contain a provision creating a presumption in Plaintiff’s favor that violation of the agreement creates irreparable harm, thus absolving it of the necessity of proving damages or posting bond in the pursuit of injunctive relief. (Complaint Exhs. E, F, § 5.5.) Such a provision is one-sided in Plaintiff’s favor and unconscionable.  (See Alberto v. Cambrian Homecare (2023) 91 Cal.App.5th 482, 492 [“[A]dditional provisions that waive the employer's need to obtain a bond before seeking an injunction, waive the employer's need to show irreparable harm, and require an employee to consent to an immediate injunction are unconscionable. They exceed the legitimate ‘margin of safety’ for the employer and are not mutual.”].)

And while Plaintiff arrogates to itself these advantages by its contract of adhesion, it purports to waive the employee’s right to any equitable remedy whatever, including “the right to seek injunctive relief.” (Complaint Exhs. E, F, § 5.5.)

Notwithstanding the severability clauses in the confidentiality and arbitration agreements, these  provisions permeate the arbitration agreement with unconscionability, rendering it unenforceable. (See Carmona v. Lincoln Millennium Car Wash, Inc. (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 74, 90.)

The motion is therefore DENIED.

___________________________________________________________________

 

Defendants Paragon Implant Company, LLC, Paragon Implant MFG, LLC, Gerald Niznick and John Konheim’s Demurrer and Motion to Strike Portions of the Complaint ___________________________________________________________________

 

Facts: This is an action for employment discrimination, harassment, and retaliation. The Complaint alleges as follows. Plaintiff Natalie Guvlekjian (“Plaintiff”) worked for Defendant Paragon Implant Company, LLC and Paragon Implant MFG, LLC as a human resources manager. (Complaint ¶ 11.) While at work, the president of the company made a sexual advance toward her, which when she complained about it to the COO, she was laughed at. (Complaint ¶ 13a.) That COO to whom she complained later sexually propositioned Plaintiff himself. (Complaint ¶ 13b.) When Plaintiff revealed to her manager that she suffered from ADD, she was demoted to an administrator position which required additional accommodation for her ADD. (Complaint ¶¶ 13c–13d.) That manager refused to give Plaintiff time off to visit her doctor, and while she agreed to permit Plaintiff to take time off to care for her sick mother, her manager subjected her to increased scrutiny, criticism, and micromanagement upon her return. (Complaint ¶ 13e.) Plaintiff went on medical leave for herself in August 2024, which was extended for several months, during which time her manager placed her on a performance improvement plan and ultimately terminated her employment. (Complaint ¶¶ 13f–13i, 14.)

Procedural History: Plaintiff filed the Complaint on March 9, 2026, alleging 12 causes of action:

1.      FEHA Discrimination

2.      FEHA Hostile Work Environment

3.      FEHA Retaliation

4.      FEHA Sexual Harassment

5.      FEHA Failure to Accommodate

6.       FEHA Interactive Process Violation

7.      Failure to Prevent FEHA Violations

8.      Negligent Hiring and Supervision

9.      Wrongful Termination

10.  Labor Code § 1102.5 Retaliation

11.  Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress

12.  California Family Rights Act

Defendants filed the present demurrer and motion to strike on May 1, 2026.

Plaintiff filed oppositions on July 23, 2026.

Defendants filed replies on July 29, 2026.

Analysis

I.                   DEMURRER

A demurrer should be sustained only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed. (Code Civ. Pro., §§ 430.30, et seq.) In particular, as is relevant here, a court should sustain a demurrer if a complaint does not allege facts that are legally sufficient to constitute a cause of action. (See id. § 430.10, subd. (e).) As the Supreme Court held in Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311: “We treat the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law. . . . Further, we give the complaint a reasonable interpretation, reading it as a whole and its parts in their context.” (Id. at p. 318; see also Hahn. v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747 [“A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed. [Citation.]”)

 

“In determining whether the complaint is sufficient as against the demurrer … if on consideration of all the facts stated it appears the plaintiff is entitled to any relief at the hands of the court against the defendants the complaint will be held good although the facts may not be clearly stated.”  (Gressley v. Williams (1961) 193 Cal.App.2d 636, 639.)

 

“A demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Khoury v. Maly’s of Cal., Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.) Such demurrers “are disfavored, and are granted only if the pleading is so incomprehensible that a defendant cannot reasonably respond.” (Mahan v. Charles W. Chan Insurance Agency, Inc. (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 841, 848.)

 

A demurrer should not be sustained without leave to amend if the complaint, liberally construed, can state a cause of action under any theory or if there is a reasonable possibility the defect can be cured by amendment. (Schifando v. City of Los Angeles, supra, 31 Cal.4th at p. 1081.) The demurrer also may be sustained without leave to amend where the nature of the defects and previous unsuccessful attempts to plead render it probable plaintiff cannot state a cause of action. (Krawitz v. Rusch (1989) 209 Cal.App.3d 957, 967.)

 

Defendants Paragon Implant Company, LLC, Paragon Implant MFG, LLC, Gerald Niznick and John Konheim (“Defendants”) demurrer to several causes of action alleged by Plaintiff Natalie Guvlekjian (“Plaintiff”). Defendants argue that Plaintiff’s first, third, fifth, sixth, and seventh causes of action are impermissibly pleaded against individual defendants. (Demurrer at pp. 8–9.) Defendants argue that he second and fourth causes of action for FEHA harassment are ill-pleaded and duplicative of one another. (Demurrer at pp. 9–11.) Defendants argue that the eighth cause of action for negligent supervision fails to plead facts establishing negligence. (Demurrer at pp. 11–12.) Defendants contend that the tenth cause of action for whistleblower retaliation fails to allege protected activity. (Demurrer at pp. 12–13.) Defendants finally argue that the eleventh cause of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress is preempted by the worker’s compensation bargain and fails to plead facts establishing outrageous conduct. (Demurrer at pp. 13–14.)

 

1.      Individual Defendants

 

Defendants’ argument with respect to the first, third, fifth, sixth, and seventh causes of action is unpersuasive. Although they argue that these claims are impermissibly pleaded against individual defendants, the causes of action clearly state that they are directed against “Entity Defendants,” which are defined to exclude the individual defendants. (Complaint at pp. 1, 8, 10,  13–15.) Thus this argument furnishes no basis to sustain the demurrer.

 

2.      FHEA Harassment

 

Defendants argue that the facts alleged do not support a claim for FEHA harassment because the conduct alleged is insufficiently severe or pervasive. The elements of a claim for FEHA harassment are (1) plaintiff belongs to a protected group; (2) plaintiff was subject to unwelcome harassment; (3) the harassment complained of was based on plaintiff’s membership in the protected group; (4) the harassment complained of was sufficiently pervasive so as to alter the conditions of employment and create an abusive working environment; and (5) respondeat superior.” (See Guthrey v. State of California (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 1108, 1122.)

 

Courts have held that “the exercise of personnel management authority properly delegated by an employer to a supervisory employee might result in discrimination, but not in harassment.” (Janken v. GM Hughes Electronics (1996) 46 Cal.App.4th 55, 64.) Harassment is said to “consist[] of conduct outside the scope of necessary job performance, conduct presumably engaged in for personal gratification, because of meanness or bigotry, or for other personal motives.” (Roby v. McKesson Corp. (2009) 47, Cal.4th 686, 707.)

 

But the two types of claim are not hermetically sealed off from each other. “[T]he FEHA treats discrimination and harassment as distinct categories, but nothing in the FEHA requires that the evidence in a case be dedicated to one or the other claim but never to both.” (Roby, supra, 47 Cal.4th at p. 710.) “[I]n analyzing the sufficiency of evidence in support of a harassment claim, there is no basis for excluding evidence of biased personnel management actions so long as that evidence is relevant to prove the communication of a hostile message.” (Id. at p. 708.)

 

Defendant’s arguments concerning the severity of the alleged harassment are unpersuasive, and rely upon cases decided prior to the enactment of Government Code § 12923 in 2019. Per that statute, “A single incident of harassing conduct is sufficient to create a triable issue regarding the existence of a hostile work environment if the harassing conduct has unreasonably interfered with the plaintiff's work performance or created an intimidating, hostile, or offensive working environment.” (Gov. Code § 12923, subd. (b).) The legislature has admonished that harassment claims are not generally susceptible to summary disposition and “involve issues ‘not determinable on paper.’” (Gov. Code § 12923, subd. (e).) The cases that Defendants rely upon predate this statute and “are no longer good law when it comes to determining what conduct creates a hostile work environment in the context of a motion for summary judgment or adjudication.” (Beltran v. Hard Rock Hotel Licensing, Inc. (2023) 97 Cal.App.5th 865, 880.) Courts have described the severe and pervasive standard funder Government Code § 12923 as a “low bar.” (Wawrzenski v. United Airlines, Inc. (2024) 106 Cal.App.5th 663, 672.)

 

Plaintiff’s claims for sexual harassment here meet that threshold. She alleges that Defendant Gerald Niznick the president of the company, leaned over her desk, and remarking upon the Hershey’s Kisses candy placed there, told her they were “not the types of kisses he wanted,” which Plaintiff interpreted as a sexual advance. (Complaint ¶ 13a.) Plaintiff then states that she complained to Defendant John Konheim, the company COO, whereupon she was laughed at. (Ibid.) At a later date, Konheim made his own sexual advance toward Plaintiff. (Complaint ¶ 13b.) Given that these defendants were “related” and Plaintiff was an HR manager, Plaintiff alleges “was no one for Plaintiff to report this inappropriate conduct to.” (Complaint ¶ 13b.) Plaintiff alleges this type of conduct was “common place.” (Complaint ¶ 13b.) Plaintiff thus alleges facts sufficient to state a claim for sexual harassment.

 

However, Defendants’ argument concerning Plaintiff’s claim for disability harassment is persuasive. Plaintiff alleges no conduct by Niznick or Konheim respecting her alleged disability, and attributes the actions taken against her on that front to HR Director Robin Ireland. (Complaint ¶¶ 13c–13i.) Plaintiff alleges a demotion, a failure to accommodate her ADD, a failure to permit her to take medical leave, retaliatory scrutiny following a return from leave to care for her sick mother, and finally performance critiques and ultimately termination while Plaintiff was on medical leave. (Complaint ¶¶ 13c–13i, 14.) Plaintiff thus alleges no harassing conduct distinct from personnel management activity in connection with her disability. (See Janken, supra, 46 Cal.App.4th at p. 55.) Although such conduct might form part of a hostile work environment when  done in connection with interpersonal harassing conduct, Plaintiff alleges no such harassing conduct save sexual advances which have no alleged basis in her disability, and personnel management actions that are insufficient on their own to constitute harassment.

 

Defendant notes that Plaintiff’s second and fourth causes of action are both for FEHA harassment, and that the fourth, which alleges only sexual harassment, is duplicative of the second, which alleges both sexual harassment and disability harassment. (Demurrer at p. 11; Complaint ¶¶ 28, 43.) Defendant is correct that the fourth cause of action merely repeats the sexual harassment allegations of the second. Because Plaintiff pleads a viable claim for sexual harassment, but presently pleads no such claim for disability harassment, it is proper to SUSTAIN the demurrer to the second cause of action, with leave to amend, and OVERRULE the demurrer to the fourth cause of action for sexual harassment.

 

3.      Negligent Supervision

 

“[I]n California, an employer can be held liable for negligent hiring if he knows the employee is unfit, or has reason to believe the employee is unfit or fails to use reasonable care to discover the employee's unfitness before hiring him.”  (Evan F. v. Hughson United Methodist Church (1992) 8 Cal.App.4th 828, 843.) “Liability results ... not because of the relation of the parties but because the employer antecedently had reason to believe that an undue risk of harm would exist because of the employment”” (J.W. v. Watchtower Bible and Tract Society of New York, Inc. (2018) 29 Cal.App.5th 1142, 1164 [upholding allegations of negligent supervision “[b]ecause J.W. has alleged that Watchtower had knowledge of the threat posed by Simental, she has sufficiently pled facts from which Watchtower could be held legally responsible for the molestation.”].)

 

Defendant persuasively argues that Plaintiff has not alleged negligent hiring or supervision here. Plaintiff alleges only that Entity Defendants had a duty to hire and supervise their managers in such a way that they would not harass their employees, and breached this duty. (Complaint ¶¶ 77–78.) Plaintiff does not plead even the ultimate fact that Defendants had prior knowledge of Niznick or Konheim’s harassing tendencies before Plaintiff was harassed.

 

Accordingly, the demurrer is SUSTAINED with leave to amend as to the eighth cause of action for negligent hiring and supervision.

 

4.      Labor Code § 1102.5

 

Defendants argue that the ninth cause of action for whistleblower retaliation under Labor Code § 1102.5 fails because Plaintiff does not allege that she engaged in protected activity. (Demurrer at pp. 12–13.)

 

An employer, or any person acting on behalf of the employer, shall not retaliate against an employee for disclosing information, or because the employer believes that the employee disclosed or may disclose information, to a government or law enforcement agency, to a person with authority over the employee or another employee who has the authority to investigate, discover, or correct the violation or noncompliance, or for providing information to, or testifying before, any public body conducting an investigation, hearing, or inquiry, if the employee has reasonable cause to believe that the information discloses a violation of state or federal statute, or a violation of or noncompliance with a local, state, or federal rule or regulation, regardless of whether disclosing the information is part of the employee's job duties.

 

(Lab. Code, § 1102.5, subd. (b).)

 

To establish a prima facie case under this statute, ”the plaintiff must show (1) the plaintiff engaged in protected activity, (2) the defendant subjected the plaintiff to an adverse employment action, and (3) there is a causal link between the two.” (Ross v. County of Riverside (2019) 36 Cal.App.5th 580, 592.)

 

The Complaint here alleges ultimate facts constituting protected activity. Plaintiff alleges both that she complained to Konheim concerning Niznick’s sexual advance, and that she complained about her demotion, which was allegedly motivated by her disclosure of her disclosure of her ADD. (Complaint ¶¶ 13b, 13c.) Although Defendant argues that these  allegations do not disclose to whom she complained or what violation she complained of, the Complaint alleges that the acts complained of and reported constituted FEHA violations. (Complaint ¶¶ 13b, 13c, 80.) Although the Complaint does not allege Plaintiff’s complaints verbatim, an employee is “not required to use legal terms or buzzwords when opposing discrimination.” (Yanowitz v. L'Oreal USA, Inc. (2005) 36 Cal.4th 1028, 1047.)

 

Accordingly, the demurrer is OVERRULED as to the ninth cause of action for whistleblower retaliation.

 

5.      Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress (IIED)

 

“The elements of a cause of action for IIED are as follows: (1) defendant engaged in extreme and outrageous conduct (conduct so extreme as to exceed all bounds of decency in a civilized community) with the intent to cause, or with reckless disregard to the probability of causing, emotional distress; and (2) as a result, plaintiff suffered extreme or severe emotional distress.” (Berry v. Frazier (2023) 90 Cal.App.5th 1258, 1273.) “Whether a defendant’s conduct can reasonably be found to be outrageous is a question of law that must initially be determined by the court; if reasonable persons may differ, it is for the jury to determine whether the conduct was, in fact, outrageous.” (Berkley v. Dowds (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 518, 534.)

Defendants argue, unpersuasively, that the IIED claim is barred by the worker’s compensation bargain. (Demurrer at p. 13.) Authority holds otherwise. (Light v. Department of Parks & Recreation (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 75, 101 [”we are unwilling to abandon the long-standing view that unlawful discrimination and retaliation in violation of FEHA falls outside the compensation bargain and therefore claims of intentional infliction of emotional distress based on such discrimination and retaliation are not subject to workers' compensation exclusivity.”].)

 

However, Defendants persuasively argue that the Complaint does not allege outrageous conduct within the meaning of the IIED tort. While Government Code § 12923 prescribes a “low bar” for actionable harassment in under FEHA, at least with respect to dispositive motions such as this (Wawrzenski, supra, 106 Cal.App.5th at p. 672), that statute prescribes no alteration to the ordinary rule that the conduct supporting an IIED claim must “exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated in a civilized community.”  (Hughes v. Pair (2009) 46 Cal.4th 1035, 1051.) Here, Plaintiff alleges that she suffered, over an uncertain span of time,  one sexual advance by a senior company officer, was laughed at when she complained about it, and then subjected to another sexual advance by the senior officer to whom she complained. This is akin to conduct elsewhere held to be insufficient to constitute outrageous conduct. (Hughes, supra, 46 Cal.4th at pp. 1050–1051 [holding insufficient two comments directly propositioning the plaintiff for sex, and threatening adverse consequences if she did not acquiesce].)  Plaintiff’s other allegations of adverse employment actions are not alleged with respect to the same decisionmakers, are alleged with respect to alleged bias against her disability, and constitute personnel management actions that are not actionable in isolation. (See Janken v. GM Hughes Electronics (1996) 46 Cal.App.4th 55, 80 [“Managing personnel is not outrageous conduct beyond the bounds of human decency, but rather conduct essential to the welfare and prosperity of society.”].)

 

Accordingly, the demurrer is SUSTAINED with leave to amend as to the eleventh cause of action for IIED.

 

In summary, the demurrer is SUSTAINED with leave to amend as to the second, eighth, and eleventh causes of action, with leave to amend. Any amended pleading shall be filed within 20 days of this order.

 

I.                   MOTION TO STRIKE

Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading, may serve and file a notice of motion to strike the whole or any part thereof. (Code Civ. Proc., § 435(b)(1)). The notice of motion to strike a portion of a pleading shall quote in full the portions sought to be stricken except where the motion is to strike an entire paragraph, cause of action, count or defense. (California Rules of Court Rule 3.1322.)

The grounds for a motion to strike shall appear on the face of the challenged pleading or form any matter of which the court is required to take judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437(a)). The court then may strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading and strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) When the defect which justifies striking a complaint is capable of cure, the court should allow leave to amend. (Perlman v. Municipal Court (1979) 99 Cal.App.3d 568, 575.)

Defendants move to strike Plaintiff’s prayer for punitive damages as being supported by insufficient facts, and to Plaintiff’s prayer for attorney fees in connection with the ninth cause of action for wrongful discharge. (Motion at pp. 5–8.)

Punitive damages are allowed in non-contract cases when a defendant is guilty of “oppression, fraud, or malice . . . .” (Civ. Code § 3294, subd. (a).) The terms are defined as:

1.      “Malice” means conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.

2.      “Oppression” means despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person's rights.

3.      “Fraud” means an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known to the defendant with the intention on the part of the defendant of thereby depriving a person of property or legal rights or otherwise causing injury.

(Civ. Code § 3294, subd. (c).)

Something more than the mere commission of a tort is always required for punitive damages. (Taylor v. Superior Court (1979) 24 Cal.3d 890, 894.) Proof of negligence, gross negligence, or recklessness is insufficient to warrant an award of punitive damages. (Dawes v. Sup.Ct. (Mardian) (1980) 111 Cal.App.3d 82, 88–89.) Punitive damages may be recovered in an action for negligence or other nonintentional torts if the plaintiff pleads and proves that the defendant acted with the state of mind described as “conscious disregard” of the potential dangers to others. (Pfeifer v. John Crane, Inc. (2013) 220 Cal.App.4th 1270, 1299.) When malice is based on a defendant’s conscious disregard of Plaintiff’s rights, the conduct must be both despicable and willful. (College Hospital v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 794, 713 (“College Hospital”).)

Plaintiff’s Complaint adequately alleges malicious and oppressive conduct. Plaintiff alleges harassing conduct and a series of adverse employment actions, including demotion and termination, that she straightforwardly alleges were intentional, retaliatory, and discriminatory. Such allegations, in connection with the alleged conduct, support a prayer for punitive damages. (See Perkins v. Superior Court (1981) 117 Cal.App.3d 1, 6 [“Taken in context, the words “wrongfully and intentionally” in paragraph eight describe a knowing and deliberate state of mind from which a conscious, disregard of petitioner's rights might be inferred a state of mind which would sustain an award of punitive damages.”].)

 

Defendants’ arguments as to the prayer for attorney fees, meanwhile, properly await adjudication in a motion for attorney fees at the conclusion of the action. Plaintiff alleges entitlement to fees in her claim for wrongful discharge via Code of Civil Procedure § 1021.5. (Complaint ¶ 84.) Fees are allowable under Code of Civil Procedure § 1021.5 as follows:

 

Upon motion, a court may award attorneys' fees to a successful party against one or more opposing parties in any action which has resulted in the enforcement of an important right affecting the public interest if: (a) a significant benefit, whether pecuniary or nonpecuniary, has been conferred on the general public or a large class of persons, (b) the necessity and financial burden of private enforcement, or of enforcement by one public entity against another public entity, are such as to make the award appropriate, and (c) such fees should not in the interest of justice be paid out of the recovery, if any.

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 1021.5.)

 

Per the above statute, such fees may be sought “[u]pon motion,” and “[t]here is no requirement that the intent to seek attorney fees under section 1021.5 must be pleaded in the underlying action..” (Washburn v. City of Berkeley (1987) 195 Cal.App.3d 578, 583.) As such striking the fees here would have no necessary bearing on their availability in a subsequent motion, based on facts adduced during the case.

 

The motion to strike is therefore DENIED.
