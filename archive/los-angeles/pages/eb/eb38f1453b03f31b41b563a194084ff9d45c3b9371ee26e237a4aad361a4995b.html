Case Number: 21STCV22133    Hearing Date: September 21, 2026    Dept: 732


Elvis Tran v. Victor L. George, et al.

 

Monday, September 21, 2026

 

 

 

CASE NUMBER: 21STCV22133

 

OPPOSED

___________________________________________________________________

 

Plaintiff Elvis Tran’s Motion to Strike or Tax Costs. ___________________________________________________________________

 

Facts: This is an action for declaratory relief between two attorneys over a common settlement fund. The Complaint alleges as follows. Defendant Victor L. Goerge, and then Plaintiff Elvis Tran, were attorneys in succession for one Briana Scott. (Complaint ¶ 3.) These representations culminated in a settlement payment to Scott, of which $500,000 is earmarked for attorney fees. (Complaint ¶ 3.) Plaintiff seeks declaratory relief concerning the parties’ respective entitlement to fees. (Complaint ¶¶ 12–13.)

Procedural History: Plaintiff filed the Complaint on June 14, 2021, alleging one cause of action for declaratory relief.

This matter came on for non-jury trial on May 18, 2026, and the court on June 5, 2026, ordered that the $500,000 fund was to be apportioned 80% for Defendant ($400,000), and 20% for Plaintiff ($100,000).

The judgment entered on July 2, 2026 states that each party is to bear their own costs.

 Defendant filed a memorandum of costs on July 17, 2026, seeking $116,271.52 in costs.

Plaintiff filed the present motion to tax costs on July 22, 2026.

Defendant filed an opposition on September 8, 2026.

Plaintiff filed a reply on September 11, 2026.

Discussion:

I.                    MOTION TO TAX COSTS

“Any notice of motion to strike or to tax costs must be served and filed 15 days after service of the cost memorandum. If the cost memorandum was served by mail, the period is extended as provided in Code of Civil Procedure section 1013. If the cost memorandum was served electronically, the period is extended as provided in Code of Civil Procedure section 1010.6(a)(4).” (California Rules of Court (“CRC”) Rule 3.1700, subd. (b)(1).)

 

“Code of Civil Procedure section 1032, subdivision (b) [], guarantees prevailing parties in civil litigation awards of the costs expended in the litigation: ‘Except as otherwise expressly provided by statute, a prevailing party is entitled as a matter of right to recover costs in any action or proceeding.’” (Williams v. Chino Valley Independent Fire Dist. (“Williams”) (2015) 61 Cal.4th 97, 100.).

 

“If the items on a verified cost bill appear proper charges, they are prima facie evidence that the costs, expenses and services therein listed were necessarily incurred.” (Rappenecker v. Sea-Land Service, Inc. (1979) 93 Cal.App.3d 256, 266.) Although individual cost items are ordinarily challenged by a motion to tax costs, no cost-item is effectively put in issue by “mere statements” claiming them to be unreasonable. (Ibid.) However, where “it cannot be determined from the face of the cost bill whether the items are proper,” “the mere filing of a motion to tax costs may be a ‘proper objection’ to an item.” (Nelson v. Anderson (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 111, 131, 132.)

 

Plaintiff Elvis Tran (“Plaintiff”) moves to strike or tax the memorandum of costs filed by Defendant Victor L. George (“Defendant”) seeking $116,271.52 in costs following judgment on Plaintiff’s declaratory relief claim awarding $400,000 to Defendant and $100,000 to Plaintiff out of a $500,000 settlement fund. Plaintiff argues that the judgment states expressly that each party is to bear their own costs, and further that the bulk of the costs sought in Defendant’s memorandum or non-compensable expert witness fees. (Motion at pp. 8–12.)

 

Plaintiff is correct that Defendant is not the prevailing party entitled to costs under Code of Civil Procedure § 1032. That statute entitles prevailing parties to their costs (Code Civ. Proc. § 1032, subd. (b), and defines prevailing party as follows:

 

“Prevailing party” includes the party with a net monetary recovery, a defendant in whose favor a dismissal is entered, a defendant where neither plaintiff nor defendant obtains any relief, and a defendant as against those plaintiffs who do not recover any relief against that defendant. If any party recovers other than monetary relief and in situations other than as specified, the “prevailing party” shall be as determined by the court, and under those circumstances, the court, in its discretion, may allow costs or not and, if allowed, may apportion costs between the parties on the same or adverse sides pursuant to rules adopted under Section 1034.

 

(Code Civ. Proc. § 1032, subd. (a)(4).)

 

Despite obtaining a larger share of the settlement fund, Defendant has not obtained a “net monetary recovery” within the meaning of the statute, because the recovery directed is against the fund to which the parties had competing claims, not against Plaintiff. No party in this action sought damages against the other; Plaintiff’s complaint sought a declaration of the parties’ interest in the fund, which declaration he obtained.  This is thus a case in which the  prevailing party “shall be as determined by the court” (Code Civ. Proc. § 1032, subd. (a)(4)), which determination, as indicated in the judgment, is that neither party may be said to be the prevailing party under this statute, and thus each party is to bear their own costs.

 

Additionally, Plaintiff is correct that the costs memorandum is dramatically inflated with impermissible costs. $102,004.81 of the costs sought by Defendant consists of the fees paid to expert consultant Erin Joyce, which fees are expressly prohibited under Code of Civil Procedure § 1033.5, subd. (b)(1). (See Opposition at p. 3.) Defendant offers no basis in law to seek the bulk of the costs claimed in the memorandum. 

 

The motion is therefore GRANTED, and the memorandum of costs is stricken.

Case Number: 25STCV19581    Hearing Date: September 21, 2026    Dept: 732


Patricia Cornejo v. Ford Motor Company, et al.

 

Monday, September 21, 2026

 

 

 

CASE NUMBER: 25STCV19581

 

OPPOSED

___________________________________________________________________

 

Defendant Ford Motor Company’s Demurrer to the First Amended Complaint ___________________________________________________________________

 

Facts: This is a lemon law action. The First Amended Complaint (“FAC”)alleges as follows. Plaintiff Patricia Cornejo (“Plaintiff”) purchased a 2019 Ford F-150 on December 26, 2018, with an express written warranty. (FAC ¶ 7.) During the warranty period, the vehicle developed defects which the manufacturer has been unable to remedy. (FAC ¶¶ 11–21.)

Procedural History: Plaintiffs filed the Complaint on June 30, 2025, and filed the FAC on November 18, 2025, alleging six causes of action:

1.      Civ. Code § 1793.2, subd. (d)

2.      Civ. Code § 1793.2, subd. (b)

3.      Civ. Code § 1793.2, subd. (a)(3)

4.      Breach of Implied Warranty of Merchantability

5.      Negligent repair

6.      Fraudulent Concealment

Defendant filed a demurrer on January 14, 2026.

Plaintiff filed an opposition on September 8, 2026..

Defendant filed a reply on September 14, 2026.

Analysis

I.                   DEMURRER

A demurrer should be sustained only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed. (Code Civ. Pro., §§ 430.30, et seq.) In particular, as is relevant here, a court should sustain a demurrer if a complaint does not allege facts that are legally sufficient to constitute a cause of action. (See id. § 430.10, subd. (e).) As the Supreme Court held in Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311: “We treat the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law. . . . Further, we give the complaint a reasonable interpretation, reading it as a whole and its parts in their context.” (Id. at p. 318; see also Hahn. v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747 [“A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed. [Citation.]”)

 

“In determining whether the complaint is sufficient as against the demurrer … if on consideration of all the facts stated it appears the plaintiff is entitled to any relief at the hands of the court against the defendants the complaint will be held good although the facts may not be clearly stated.”  (Gressley v. Williams (1961) 193 Cal.App.2d 636, 639.)

 

“A demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Khoury v. Maly’s of Cal., Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.) Such demurrers “are disfavored, and are granted only if the pleading is so incomprehensible that a defendant cannot reasonably respond.” (Mahan v. Charles W. Chan Insurance Agency, Inc. (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 841, 848.)

 

A demurrer should not be sustained without leave to amend if the complaint, liberally construed, can state a cause of action under any theory or if there is a reasonable possibility the defect can be cured by amendment. (Schifando v. City of Los Angeles, supra, 31 Cal.4th at p. 1081.) The demurrer also may be sustained without leave to amend where the nature of the defects and previous unsuccessful attempts to plead render it probable plaintiff cannot state a cause of action. (Krawitz v. Rusch (1989) 209 Cal.App.3d 957, 967.)

 

A.    STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS

 

Defendant Ford Motor Company (“Defendant”) argues that the first through third causes of action under the Song Beverly Act are time-barred under the statute of repose set forth in Code of Civil Procedure § 871.21, that the fourth cause of action for breach of the implied warranty of merchantability is barred by its own limitations period, and that the sixth cause of action for fraud is barred by the three-year statute of limitations applicable to such claims. (Demurrer at pp. 9–15.)

 

Code of Civil Procedure § 871.21 states as follows: “n action covered by Section 871.20 shall not be brought later than six years after the date of original delivery of the motor vehicle.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 871.21, subd. (b).) Section 871.20 covers actions brought under certain lemon law code sections against manufacturers who have opted into certain litigation procedures, as Defendant has done here. (Code Civ. Proc. § 871.20, subd. (a). 

 

This six-year period constitutes a statute of repose rather than a statute of limitations. “A statute of repose has nothing to do with the date of injury, but bars all suits after the expiration of a specified time from the manufacture or delivery of a product or a transaction.” (Inco Development Corp. v. Superior Court (2005) 131 Cal.App.4th 1014, 1020.) This describes the six-year bar set in Code of Civil Procedure § 871.21, and Plaintiff does not dispute this characterization of the statute.

 

A statute of repose differs from a statute of limitations in that statutes of limitation bar suits filed more than a specified period of time after an injury occurs or is discovered, whereas a statute of repose is a bar on all suits brought more than a specified period after the date of manufacture of a product and delivery to the purchaser. Because the date of injury is not a factor used in computing the running of the time period and because statutes of repose typically do not have tolling provisions, they “acquire a substantive nature, barring rights of action even before injury has occurred if the injury occurs subsequent to the prescribed time period.

 

(Burroughs v. Precision Airmotive Corp. (2000) 78 Cal.App.4th 681, 689–690.)

 

“Although the defendant must plead a statute of limitations defense to avoid forfeiture, it is the plaintiff who must plead facts showing their substantive right has not been extinguished by a statute of repose.  And unlike a procedural statute of limitations, substantive statutes of repose are generally not subject to statutory or equitable tolling.” (PGA West Residential Assn., Inc. v. Hulven Internat., Inc. (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 156, 178, internal citations omitted.)

 

Plaintiff Patricia Cornejo (“Plaintiff”) here purchased the subject vehicle on December 26, 2018, meaning under the above statute of repose his Song Beverly claims were time-barred as of December 26, 2024. Plaintiff filed the Complaint on June 30, 2025, after the statute of repose expired.

 

Plaintiff argues that the statute of repose ought not to apply retroactively. (Opposition at pp. 5–6.)

 

The doctrine of retrospectivity of limitations statutes is one of constitutional dimension. In California, statutes of limitations, being procedural, are normally retroactively applied to accrued causes of action; but the court must inquire whether, in a given case, that retrospective application may violate due process by in effect eliminating the plaintiff's right. If the time left to file suit is reasonable, no such constitutional violation occurs, and the statute is applied as enacted. If no time is left, or only an unreasonably short time remains, then the statute cannot be applied at all. This analysis is set forth clearly in Rosefield Packing Co., supra, pointing out the question is one of constitutionality; the statute may not operate immediately to cut off the accrued remedy “or within so short a time as to give the party no reasonable opportunity to exercise his remedy;” if the statute does so operate it is unconstitutional. (Rosefield Packing Co., supra, 4 Cal.2d at p. 122, 47 P.2d 716.) Further, the issue is one of law for appellate resolution. In Rosefield, retrospective application left the plaintiff “practically an entire year” to bring suit after enactment of the amendment (id. at p. 123, 47 P.2d 716); “[t]here can be no doubt but that this was a reasonable time....” (Ibid.)

 

(Aronson v. Superior Court (1987) 191 Cal.App.3d 294, 297.)

 

Under the above authority, direct application of the statute of repose in Code of Civil Procedure § 871.21, subd. (a) would cut off Plaintiff’s claims immediately upon the effective date of the statute, without giving a reasonable time in which to sue. Thus the statute of repose cannot be applied to Plaintiff’s first through third causes of action consistent with due process.

However, the fourth cause of action for breach of the implied warranty of merchantability is time-barred. The statute of repose articulated in Code of Civil Procedure § 87121 applies to claims subject to Code of Civil Procedure § 871.20, which applies “where the request for restitution or replacement is based on noncompliance with the applicable express warranty. (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.20, subd. (a).) The claim for breach of implied warranty is thus not governed by the bounds of the statute of repose.

 

The statute of limitations for breach of warranties under Commercial Code § 2725 is four years after accrual. (Com. Code § 2725, subd. (a).) There is a delayed discovery exception,  but the language of the discovery exception is that a cause accrues upon delivery of the good, “except that where a warranty explicitly extends to future performance of the goods.” (Com. Code § 2725, subd. (2), italics added.) An implied warranty by definition cannot “explicitly extend[] to future performance of the goods,” because an implied warranty contains no explicit terms. Courts have accordingly held that “the exception must be narrowly construed, and that it applies only when the seller has expressly agreed to warrant its product for a specific and defined period of time.” (Cardinal Health 301, Inc. v. Tyco Electronics Corp. (2008) 169 Cal.App.4th 116, 130.) “Because an implied warranty is one that arises by operation of law rather than by an express agreement of the parties, courts have consistently held it is not a warranty that ‘explicitly extends to future performance of the goods.’” (Id. at p. 134.)

 

Thus the fourth cause of action was time-barred within four years of delivery of the vehicle in December 2018, and was time-barred before the original Complaint was filed in June 2025. The demurrer is therefore SUSTAINED without leave to amend to the fourth cause of action for breach of implied warranty.

 

The sixth cause of action for fraud is also time-barred as pleaded. There is a three-year statute of limitations for “[a]n action for relief on the ground of fraud or mistake. The cause of action in that case is not deemed to have accrued until the discovery, by the aggrieved party, of the facts constituting the fraud or mistake.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 338, subd. (d.) Plaintiff’s fraud claim would be barred, absent application of the discovery rule or some other equitable tolling doctrine, by December 26, 2021.

“Where a claim is time-barred on its face, the plaintiff must specifically plead facts that would support equitable tolling.” (Long v. Forty Niners Football Co., LLC (2019) 33 Cal.App.5th 550, 555.) The “discovery rule. . . postpones accrual of a cause of action until the plaintiff discovers, or has reason to discover, the cause of action.” (Fox v. Ethicon Endo-Surgery, Inc. (2005) 35 Cal.4th 797, 807.) “A plaintiff has reason to discover a cause of action when he or she ‘has reason at least to suspect a factual basis for its elements.’” (Ibid.) Courts “look to whether the plaintiffs have reason to at least suspect that a type of wrongdoing has injured them.” (Ibid.) The accrual of a cause of action in relation to the discovery rule is normally a question of fact. (Id. at p. 810.)

On a demurrer, the court must take as true statements such as that as plaintiff “did not discover, nor suspect, nor was there any means through which her reasonable diligence would have revealed, or through which she would have suspected,” the cause of an injury was the defendant in question. (Fox, supra, 35 Cal.4th at p. 811.) However: “A plaintiff whose complaint shows on its face that his claim would be barred without the benefit of the discovery rule must specifically plead facts to show (1) the time and manner of discovery and (2) the inability to have made earlier discovery despite reasonable diligence.” (Id. at p. 808.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show diligence, and conclusory allegations will not withstand demurrer. (Ibid.)

Here, Plaintiff fails to plead facts supporting application of the delayed discovery rule or any other equitable tolling doctrine to the fraud claim. Plaintiff alleges in conclusory terms that repair technicians on unspecified dates represented that the vehicle had been repaired (FAC ¶ 12), and that “Plaintiff discovered Defendants' wrongful conduct alleged herein shortly before the filing of the complaint.” (FAC ¶ 45.) These allegations are insufficient to invoke the delayed discovery rule.

The demurrer is accordingly SUSTAINED without leave to amend as to the fourth cause of action, SUSTAINED with leave to amend as to the sixth cause of action for fraud, and is otherwise OVERRULED.
