Case Number: 23SMCV02976    Hearing Date: July 7, 2026    Dept: I

This is a fee motion brought by the City of West Hollywood following a successful summary judgment motion.  The issue in the case is an allegedly dangerous condition on public property.  On summary judgment, the court concluded that defendant had established that there was no triable issue of fact as to notice and therefore granted the motion over plaintiff’s opposition.  Judgment was entered in April and that ruling is now on appeal.  West Hollywood, however, is seeking recovery of its attorneys’ fees, limited to the issue of causation.  Its theory is that it asked plaintiff to admit that there was no notice to the City through RFAs, but plaintiff denied the assertion.  West Hollywood says that plaintiff did that after the City had produced discovery showing that there was in fact no notice, but that it was nonetheless put to the burden and expense of proving up the matter in order to prevail.  Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.420, states that where a party fails to admit a fact in an RFA and the other party later proves it, the court must award payment of reasonable expenses, including fees, unless it finds that the party failing to make the admission had a reasonable ground to believe that it would prevail on the matter or there is some other good reason for the failure to admit.  Normally, courts do not allow this sort of motion where it is based on RFAs issued early on going to the fundamental issues in the case, for doing so would essentially convert the American rule into a loser pays rule.  (Universal Home Improvement, Inc. v. Robertson (2020) 51 Cal.App.5th 116.)  But West Hollywood argues that such is not the case here.  Rather, defendant claims, it waited until after it had provided discovery to plaintiff.  Therefore, it concludes, plaintiff knew the salient underlying facts establishing that the admission should be admitted, and accordingly there is no reason why the sanction ought not be awarded.

 

Unlike typical sanctions, this provision is not designed as a penalty.  Instead, it is designed to reimburse a party for having to prove that which it should not have had to work to prove.  (City of Glendale v. Marcus Cable Associates (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 344.)  That is what West Hollywood claims is being sought here.  West Hollywood also notes that it seeks no fees for other aspects of the summary judgment motion or its work on the case, or for any fees incurred before the request was denied.  The court notes that the burden of persuasion on such a motion lies with the party opposing the motion, not the party making it.  (Spahn v. Richards (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 1007.) 

 

Here, plaintiff argues that it did have a reasonable belief that it would prevail on the notice question.  A reasonable ground for the belief means a good faith objective belief, not just a hope or a roll of the dice.  (Association for Los Angeles Deputy Sheriffs v. Macias (2021) 63 Cal.App.5th 1007.)  Thus, the court agrees that this has two components.  First, the plaintiff must actually believe that there is a ground to deny the request, and second that there must be an objective basis to so deny.  An unreasonable but honest belief will not do it.  (Grace v. Mansourian (2015) 240 Cal.App.4th 523.)  On the other hand, a good faith and reasonable belief will be enough even if ultimately mistaken.  (Miller v. American Greeting Corp. (2008) 161 Cal.App.4th 1055; Orange County Water Dist. v. The Arnold Engineering Co. (2018) 31 Cal.App.5th 96.)  And the court emphasizes, the question is not whether there is a good faith belief that there is something out there somewhere; it must be a reasonable good faith belief that the party refusing to admit the request will in fact prevail on that question.

 

In this case, plaintiff retained an expert, Avrit.  The court does not know when plaintiff obtained the expert, but the expert did supply a declaration in opposition to the motion and the court will at least assume that Avrit had been retained or consulted before the RFA response was sent.  In Avrit’s declaration, the expert testified that, in his opinion, the defendant knew or should have known of the dangerous condition; that is, that there was notice.  Avrit’s view was that the as built plans were not fully consistent with the actual project because the top group of panels were installed but not in the plans, and that is the surface upon which plaintiff slipped.  Avrit also contended that the actual slope exceeded the project plans such that the City should have known that there was a variance between the plans and the actual area.  Avrit went on to declare that the Facilities and Landscape Maintenance Superintended for the Department of Public Works had testified to power washing the surface and that in Avrit’s view, it was more likely than not that the surface upon which plaintiff slipped had deteriorated and become smooth over time due to that and also wear and use, something he claimed the City should have known or did know.  He went on to say that the City routinely had employees in the area, but the condition had not been corrected even though the defendant should have known that the surface was worn and needed repairs. 

 

Where a request for admission involves a sophisticated analysis of technical issues, it is harder to obtain a 2033.420 penalty than in other instances because it is more reasonable that a party reasonably relied on the expert’s view.  Where that is the case, the motion for fees will only prevail where the reliance is unreasonable or where the evidence was fatally undermined by other facts of which the party knew or where the testimony would be inadmissible.  Inherent in that is whether the expert has reasonable qualifications and experience to support the opinion, whether the methodology is reasonable, and whether the opinion is grounded in logic.  That is not to say that any expert is enough.  A sham expert or opinion, or a plainly unqualified expert is akin to no expert at all.  But where there is a reasonable, good faith belief that the expert’s testimony can carry the day, that will defeat the motion.  The Orange County case cited above provides a good benchmark.  There, the trial court awarded sanctions under 2033.420 after the plaintiff failed to admit an RFA during discovery.  The Court of Appeal reversed, finding an abuse of discretion as to certain RFAs (although not as to others).  The court notes that in that case, the defense prevailed at a bench trial in that case, not summary judgment.  But even so, the court there noted that requests that require expert opinion are treated differently than requests that do not, and that a denial is not improper just because the expert is not ultimately believed.  So, while the court declined to find a per se rule that an expert’s opinion will always shield a party from 2033.420 sanctions, it did so because it concluded that there are “limited circumstances” where even with an expert, the belief is unreasonable.  The Orange County court concluded that reliance on the expert was not so unreasonable as to warrant an award, even though the trial court (sitting as a trier of fact) found the expert’s opinion to be insufficient.  And one can understand the logic.  Compare a request for admission stating “Admit that you were driving the car at the time of the accident.”  Presumably, that is a fact and a fact alone.  It requires no deep analysis and no specialized knowledge.  If the request is denied so that the other side has to depose the witnesses to the event and get the traffic camera footage, section 2033.420 is pretty easy to apply.  Compare that to “Admit that Dr. Jones acted within the standard of care during the surgery.”  That will require the plaintiff to retain an expert and review many documents to determine what the standard of care was and whether Dr. Jones met it during the surgery.  If that is denied, it is harder to claim that plaintiff should be forced to pay Dr. Jones’s fees for failing to admit it, at least where plaintiff has an expert who says that Dr. Jones did not act within the standard of care.

 

The court recognizes that here, the defense is in a stronger position than the defense in Orange County because here Avrit’s position was not rejected on a weighing of the evidence basis.  But even so, the court needs to look back at the purpose of the statute.  Requests for Admission were likely originally intended to get admissions of things that no one really disputed, like “Admit you signed the contract” or (like the above example) “Admit you were the driver of the car.”  They are facts that ought not to be in dispute and it is a waste of everyone’s time and money to force a party to prove it up.  However, in modern practice they are also used for a different purpose.  Instead of admitting discrete facts, they are being used to force a denial of ultimate facts or conclusions.  “Admit you were reckless” or “Admit that you suffered no damage.”  Or, as in this case, “Admit that the CITY did not have actual notice of a DANGERIOUS CONDITION at the SUBJECT SIDEWALK on the date of the SUBJECT INCIDENT” and “Admit that the CITY did not have constructive notice of a DANGEROUS CONDITION at the SUBJECT SIDEWALK on the date of the SUBJECT INCIDENT.”  The responses here included an objection that the request called for a legal conclusion and expert opinion (and certainly the latter is true), and that plaintiff lacked sufficient information to admit or deny.  While these sorts of RFAs are not improper, neither do they easily flow into the notion that failing to admit that your case is meritless leads to a fee award should you not prevail. 

 

The court believes that actual or constructive notice is the sort of thing upon which a party might well feel expertise is needed, and the court cannot say that in light of Avril’s opinion there was not a reasonable good faith belief that plaintiff could prevail.  The question of notice is not like a normal fact question; it is an ultimate legal issue and it involves many considerations.  (That would be different than, for example “Admit that the CITY received no prior complaints concerning the SUBJECT PROPERTY.”  If the City had already turned over the relevant database, or a declaration regarding such a lack, it might well be that the denial would not be in subjective good faith, and that is one of the building blocks toward an ultimate conclusion.  But that is not the issue here.)  The court’s point is that the sanction is going to be a stronger tool when used to establish the basic facts rather than when used to establish an ultimate fact.  There is a difference between “Admit that you were driving at 50 miles per hour when you hit defendant’s car” and “Admit that you were negligent when you hit defendant’s car”; or “Admit that defendant was stopped at the light when you hit defendant’s car” and “Admit that you were negligent when you hit defendant’s car.”  In each example, the first is a straightforward fact requiring no analysis or expertise.  The defendant’s car was stopped or it was not; the responding party was going 50 mph or not.  Whether the various facts amounted to legal negligence is something to which the request can be made, but it is harder to fault the responding party for not conceding the case.

 

On balance, even though the City prevailed on summary judgment, the court’s concern is that turning 2033.420 into a weapon that essentially requires a party to admit that the case has no merit or risk paying the other side attorneys’ fees is too great.  To be sure, there will be outlier cases where the opinion is a sham or from an obviously unqualified person.  There are “experts” like that out there, and practitioners (and many courts) know who they are.  Courts, though, out to be slow to require a plaintiff to concede the case even after discovery or risk paying attorneys’ fees.  At least this court will be slow to impose such a sanction.  The court notes that the Legislature could have, but did not, direct that where the defendant prevails on a contested motion for summary judgment, it should get fees, or at least where a party unsuccessfully declares a fact disputed.  After all, the statement of undisputed facts are, essentially, the equivalent of an RFA; they are assertions fact to which the other side must either admit (by agreeing the fact is undisputed) or dispute with evidence.  But the Legislature did not do so.  The court is aware that the analogy is not perfect and the fact that there are no fees for unsuccessfully opposing an MSJ does not mean that fees cannot be recovered under 2033.420—a different and independent statute.  The court only notes that it is an interesting point and illustrates a general view on fees.

 

In any event, the court finds that plaintiff had a reasonable good faith belief that it would prevail on this issue.  Therefore, the motion is DENIED.

Case Number: 24SMCV03028    Hearing Date: July 7, 2026    Dept: I

This is a fee motion in a Song-Beverly case.  The motion was originally filed in March.  The court continued the matter to see if the parties would have a real conversation about the rates and hours.  The court had assumed that lead counsel would participate, but instead, lead counsel was very busy and appointed others.  As of the subsequent hearing, there had been no discussion.  As just stated, lead defense counsel was too busy.  And the appointed defense counsel included some with no involvement in the case at all.  Plaintiff’s counsel stated a concern that although the counsel on the other side would have the technical authority to cut a deal, there would be no practical authority really to examine the time entries and have a meaningful give and take about the fees, and therefore refused to meet.  The court then told the parties to try again—this time with lead counsel.  The parties apparently did have a voice to voice discussion, but the court understands that no agreement was reached, and so we are back.

 

The court notes that plaintiff seeks $18,000 in lodestar fees plus $4000 for the original motion, hardly a king’s ransom.  Defendant opposed the motion stating that the award was way too big and that the motion was untimely because it was brought long after the settlement was inked.  The active litigation lasted about 9 months according to the defense, and 44 hours over a 9 month period is not reasonable according to the defense.  Further, the defense notes that the court dismissed the merits of the case on June 27, 2025, but without prejudice to a fee motion.  By waiting 9 more months to file the motion, the defense says plaintiff is being unreasonable, and that the fee motion had to be filed within 60 days of the dismissal and therefore untimely.  Further, the defense notes that there were no depositions, experts, or motions, although there was standard written discovery.  Finally, relying on the Real Rate Report for 2023, the defense also concluded that the $500 hourly rate was too high.  The bottom line seemed to be that the actual lodestar number is $1462.30.  In reply, plaintiff argues that the settlement was only conditional, and as such, the time periods to file an appeal (which is where the 60 days from the defense comes from) had not even started.  Plaintiff also contends that the 2023 report is outdated, and that at a minimum, the defense should use the 2025 report assuming that the Real Rate Report is useful at all.  A note on that.  The court has no idea how the numbers in the Real Rate Report were derived.  All defendant says is that it should be used and that some other trial courts in unpublished cases have used it.  That is not evidence.

 

The court has gone through the time entries.  The court agrees that in some respects, some of the time did not seem to be efficient, but the court also notes that the defense made no effort to identify any particular entry that it felt was improper.  Nor can the court say, based on its experience, that $500/hour is unreasonably high.  On the other side of the coin, plaintiff’s only evidence as to the reasonableness of the rate is that it has been awarded in the past.  However, plaintiff has not provided any information of a more useful kind.  For example, plaintiff has not given the court a list of contested awards issued by courts over the last two years in Song-Beverly cases.  The court has some concern about cherry-picking.  But that said, plaintiff’s rates are consistent with the Laffey Matrix, which has been used by a number of courts.  Finally, the court believes that the notion that plaintiff should have spent not more than $1462.30 on the case, which is what the defense suggests, is not reasonable.  It lends credence to what the court has been hearing from the plaintiff’s bar: that the opposition to these motions is brought in bad faith in an effort to put plaintiff’s legitimate fees at risk.  Even at the rates defendant suggests, this is about 7 hours or less of time.  The court is aware that some of the time was while there was a debate on forum.  The court agrees with the defense that fees incurred to defend the forum are not recoverable, that does not mean that none of the work is recoverable.  Much of it directly translates.

 

In short, the court disagrees with the defense that the motion is time barred.  And the court does not believe that the defense has demonstrated that the rates are unreasonably high or that the time, generally speaking, is unreasonable.  What the court does find is that the defendant’s suggestion that the case should have taken no more than a couple of hours is unreasonable.  The court has gone through the billing statements.  The court’s views are as follows: 8/21/23—approved; 8/28—approved; 8/29—approved as to the first two entries, but not the third; 9/7/23—approved; 9/14—approved; 9/29—approved; 10/2—disapproved; 10/11—approved for .5 hours for all entries combined in part because a more junior person could have done this; 12/8—approved; 12/27—disapproved for the first entry, approved for the second entry; 1/3/24—approved; 1/8—approved for .5 hours for all entries; 1/15—approved; 4/4—approved at .1 for the first entry and approved in full as to the second entry; 4/22—approved; 5/7—approved for .4 hours combined; 5/24—approved; 6/6—approved; 6/7—approved; 9/3—approved; 9/4—approved; 9/5—approved; 10/4—approved; 10/29—approved; 10/30—approved; 11/8—approved at .6; 11/14—approved; 11/15—approved at 1.6 hours; 11/20—approved at .3 hours for both entries; 12/19—approved; 12/27—approved; 1/29/25—approved at .1 hours; 1/30—approved; 1/31—approved; 2/20-approved; 2/26—disapproved; 3/24—approved; 3/31—approved; 6/30—disapproved; 9/25—approved; 10/6 disapproved; 12/5—approved; 12/12 approved at .2 hours for the entries; 3/10—approved.  The court notes that while some of these numbers could go one way or the other, the court has tried to find what it believes are reasonable. 

 

The court will GRANT the motion in the lodestar amount as reflected above.  However, because the opposition seeks a number that is patently unreasonable, the court finds that it placed plaintiff’s legitimate fee at undue risk and in was made in bad faith.  The court believes that this added risk justifies—on that basis alone—an enhancement to the fee award.  The court notes that it generally does not enhance fees in a Song-Beverly case because the likelihood of success is so great.  But where the defense, through actions such as this, puts a legitimate fee at risk, an enhancement is appropriate.  Therefore, the court will award a 25% enhancement to account for the additional risk that plaintiff’s counsel undertook in recovering its fee.  Plaintiff will prepare the order.

 

 


Case Number: 25SMCV06443    Hearing Date: July 7, 2026    Dept: I

These are similar special motions to strike brought in two similar cases.  Plaintiff in these cases is a tenant who sues the former landlord and property manager.  There is also a UD case in which the landlord is attempting to evict plaintiff.  In the instant case, plaintiff claims a number of torts.  The underlying alleged facts are that on November 4, 2025, defendant wrote and delivered a letter asking to talk to plaintiff concerning various complaints that defendant had received.  This was a first step toward investigating issues that could lead to a Measure RR letter and potential eviction.  The eventual Measure RR letter was written on official letterhead and was addressed to “Semen.”  Measure RR is a part of the Santa Monica rent control structure.  It extends protection against eviction in a number of ways.  It requires landlords to give tenants written notice to correct a lease violation before starting eviction proceedings, and also limits the landlord’s ability to evict tenants who are terminally ill or who have lived in the unit for at least five years or who are disabled or who are at least 62.  The notice here accused plaintiff of abusing, harassing, and threatening others, which would be cause for eviction if true.  It also accused plaintiff of being a nuisance.  On December 5, defendants sent plaintiff a cure or quit notice.  The notice was allegedly verified by management.  Plaintiff also claims that defendant disseminated the letter to the Housing Authority and to two individuals: Dominique Thurman and Jonathan Campbell.  Plaintiff alleges that the foregoing conduct constituted defamation by discussing the alleged behaviors without plaintiff’s permission and communicating the accusations to others.  Plaintiff also claims that defendants did not respond to her requests for accommodation made by herself and her representative Jonathan Campbell to have meetings through Zoom or a similar method or to use Zoom to inspect the premises rather than conduct a physical inspection.  She also alleges that the foregoing conduct constituted intentional infliction of emotional distress.  The third cause of action is for negligence, and again, it is based on the same set of facts.  The fourth cause of action is for the public disclosure of private facts.  It is based on the same allegations as set forth above—there is no additional detail.  The fifth cause of action is for intrusion into private affairs, again by discussing the issues raised in the Measure RR notice and failing to accommodate plaintiff’s requests.  The sixth and final cause of action is for false light, and is again based on the same set of facts.  The other case is similar, but it alleges sexual harassment.  However, the supporting facts are the same.  The court notes that at the last hearing, the court had mistakenly believed that the UD case had been adjudicated in defendants’ favor.  The court was in error on that, as plaintiff properly pointed out.  Accordingly, any arguments relating to collateral estoppel, res judicata, or law of the case that might otherwise be leveled against plaintiff would not apply.  But that was not the thrust of the court’s prior tentative, and it does not change the outcome.  Indeed, as explained below, the outcome of this motion would be the same even were the UD case finally adjudicated in plaintiff’s favor (that is, plaintiff’s favor here, not plaintiff’s favor in the UD case). 

 

A Special Motion to Strike (SMS) is a California response to what the Legislature concluded were a series of lawsuits designed to chill speech.  The underlying problem was that a person would engage in free speech—such as picketing or the like—and would be met with a lawsuit claiming that the speech was tortious in some way.  True, the defendant would ultimately prevail if the speech was in fact protected speech, but only after litigating the issue—potentially through summary judgment or trial.  In the meantime, the defendant would be forced to run the litigation gauntlet and retain counsel.  The effect, the Legislature concluded, was that people were afraid to engage in protected speech or activity due to this improper and heavy-handed mis-use of the legal process.  The response was Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16—the Special Motion to Strike.  (It is often referred to as an anti-SLAPP motion.  The court prefers SMS.)  This is a very powerful tool that is given to defendants who believe that they are being sued for engaging in this kind of constitutionally-protected conduct.  It allows an early resolution of the case on the merits.  The motion is a speaking motion, meaning that the court goes beyond the allegations in the complaint, and once brought, the motion freezes discovery.  A plaintiff is generally not allowed to amend if the motion is successful, and the motion—win or lose—is appealable.  A successful defendant is entitled to recover attorneys’ fees as a matter of right as well.  The same is not true of a party successfully defeating the motion unless the motion was brought in a way that (essentially) violates CCP section 128.5. 

 

The SMS sets forth a two step process to balance the desire to get a resolution early against the need to be sure that due process is maintained.  The first step is to determine whether the suit really alleges a tort based on the exercise of a constitutionally protected right.  After all, most torts have nothing to do with the Constitution.  The burden on establishing this prong lies with the defense, and the court looks primarily to the complaint to answer the question.  The court can look beyond the complaint, but generally, plaintiff is the master of the complaint and a plaintiff is entitled to plead around constitutionally protected conduct.  (For example, in a case similar in some ways to this one, plaintiff mentioned a UD case in the complaint, but was careful only to sue the defendant for other things, like turning off the utilities, and expressly disclaimed any effort to impose liability on the defense for what the plaintiff claimed was a bad faith UD action.)  Further, an allegation of constitutionally protected activity will not trigger the statute if it is only background.  It has to be a part of the charging allegations—meaning it needs to form a part of the actual basis for the cause of action.  If the defendant can meet the first prong, then the burden shifts to the plaintiff to defeat the motion under the second prong.  The statute states that it is up to the plaintiff to show a reasonable probability of success.  But those words are deceiving.  Recall that this is the start of the case.  The plaintiff has not had the benefit of discovery, and the point of the SMS is not to do away with the fact-finder’s role.  Rather, this prong has been interpreted as a test to see whether the case has “minimal merit,” which is a standard akin to that needed to oppose successfully a motion for summary judgment.  If the plaintiff can present evidence—not just allegations—that (if believed) would lead to a plaintiff’s verdict, that is sufficient.  The court can consider the evidence, but it is not to weigh the evidence.  If there is a triable issue of fact, the motion fails. 

 

The court turns to the first prong.  The allegations there are that Leguizamon, who was employed by Community Corporation as a Property Supervisor for the relevant property, requested a call with plaintiff to discuss certain complaints that had been received.  He provided his contact information.  He mistakenly began the letter to her with “Dear Semen.”  The defense has put in evidence that this was a mistake.  He took the heading from a recent letter he had written to a tenant whose first name was “Semen” (pronounced Simon).  Plaintiff did not call, so a formal Measure RR letter was sent to plaintiff.  Under that measure, such a letter is required before eviction proceedings can begin.  That ultimately led to an inspection of plaintiff’s residence in December 2025, which ultimately led to a new Measure RR letter and then eviction proceedings.  Therefore, the November letter was a predicate to the start of potential eviction procedures, which is constitutionally protected conduct.  The same is true of the December cure or quit notice.

 

All of that conduct is protected.  Measure RR is the predicate that leads to an unlawful detainer action.  That is an action in court, and as such, it involves the right to petition government.  Suing someone for taking a first step toward bringing a legal action is a suit that does involve a constitutionally protected right.  While plaintiff does not allege that the UD action itself is the wrong, the things about which she does sue are necessary predicates to that action.  (That is true even if the ultimate grounds for the UD action were different from those in the original Measure RR notice.  The fact is that the original notice was a predicate to suit; the fact that the landlord ultimately brought the UD on different grounds after learning more information does not change the character of the claim.)  That is sufficient, and the burden shifts.  The court notes that while it is plaintiff’s burden to present evidence, the defense here has raised the litigation privilege codified in Civil Code section 47(b).  That statute makes conduct in litigation absolutely privileged as against tort claims (other than malicious prosecution).  It is often called the “absolute” privilege, although pre-litigation conduct is not really absolutely privileged.  Even so, it is very potent and does extend to core conduct necessary to pursue an eventual claim in court.  Here, plaintiff has not presented evidence in opposition to the motion, so there is no evidence to support her claims at all.  (She does ask the court to take judicial notice of various state and local laws, which the court will do.)  But even if there were, pretty much everything done was done in furtherance of the eventual or contemplated UD claim.  The defamation assumes a false statement, but there is no evidence that any statement was false.  (Further, to the extent that the statement was an allegation of complaints by others, if the defendant really received the complaints, the statements are true.)  The other causes of action are all derivative of the fact that defendant was starting to take steps that eventually led to plaintiff’s eviction.  Further, plaintiff has failed to show minimal merit or how her claims will survive the section 47 privilege.  A famous example is the decision is Malin v. Singer (2013) 217 Cal.App.4th 1283.   There, attorney Singer sent a settlement demand letter to a potential defendant threatening that if defendant did not settle, Singer (on behalf of his client) would bring a lawsuit and seek a full accounting of defendant’s books and records (suggesting that he would uncover misconduct that would become public in the litigation) and that the defendant had arranged for sexual liaisons between certain victims and older men.  The target of the litigation sued, claiming that Singer’s letter constituted an attempted extortion—seeking to disclose embarrassing facts unless money was paid.  Singer brought an SMS.  The trial court denied the motion, finding that the letter could be so interpreted and thus was not protected conduct, or at least whether it was protected was a question of fact.  The Court of Appeal reversed.  It noted that because the letter dealt only with the threatened litigation, it was the start of the litigation process.  Unlike a threat simply to disclose embarrassing facts unless money is paid (which could be extortion), this was a threat to file a lawsuit, and the facts in question were relevant to the lawsuit and would become public, therefore, should a suit be filed.  Accordingly, even though the letter was not itself a court document, it was the first step toward a lawsuit, and thus was protected under the litigation privilege.  The court ordered that the suit be dismissed.  So here.  The conduct of which plaintiff complains all involves actions that appear to be things necessary in order to bring an unlawful detainer case.  And that is protected.  Even if the UD case ultimately fails, it is the right to bring it that counts.  (If the UD action fails, plaintiff here might have a malicious prosecution claim against the landlord, but that is not a question before this court.)

 

Plaintiff also contends that sending the letter to third parties is defamation and thus is not protected.  But this was not widely disseminated.  Rather, the letter was allegedly sent to the Housing Department and two individuals apparently not unrelated to the matter.  Because the dissemination was in furtherance of the Measure RR process, there remains a privilege.  And plaintiff has still not demonstrated that there was anything in the letter that was false or that put plaintiff in a false light.

 

The court has considered the various statutes to which plaintiff cites.  But the bottom line remains that the defense is privileged to begin the process that might lead to eviction.  That begins with an attempt to contact plaintiff, but it continues with reasonable investigation of the complaints, a Measure RR letter, a 3 day notice, and eventually a lawsuit.   All of that is protected activity.  Because there is no evidence from the defense that any of the statements of fact are even false, let alone defamatory, the motion has merit.

 

Because defendant has demonstrated that the lawsuits involve constitutionally protected conduct, and because plaintiff has presented no evidence suggesting that there is even a triable issue of fact on the merits, the motions succeed.  Therefore, the Special Motions to Strike are GRANTED.  The complaint is STRICKEN.  Defendants will prepare a written order for the court’s signature.

 

Finally, the court notes that plaintiff filed papers yesterday.  They appeared for the first time today.  They are unauthorized and they are STRICKEN.  This is not the first time plaintiff has done this sort of thing.  Basic fairness to the other side means that the papers have to be filed and served in a way that allows the other side to digest them before the hearing.  And where the other side is entitled to a reply, then the papers must be filed in a way to allow a reply.  And basic fairness to the court requires that papers be filed in a way that allows the court adequate time to review them and consider them.  This sort of thing is not proper.  If plaintiff needed more time, she should have said so at the last hearing or sought a continuance to allow the filings.  That said, to the extent that it is a request that the court take judicial notice of the law, the court will do so.

The sixth and final cause of action is for false light, and is again based on the same set of facts.  The other case is similar, but it alleges sexual harassment.  However, the supporting facts are the same.  The court notes that at the last hearing, the court had mistakenly believed that the UD case had been adjudicated in defendants’ favor.  The court was in error on that, as plaintiff properly pointed out.  Accordingly, any arguments relating to collateral estoppel, res judicata, or law of the case that might otherwise be leveled against plaintiff would not apply.  But that was not the thrust of the court’s prior tentative, and it does not change the outcome.  Indeed, as explained below, the outcome of this motion would be the same even were the UD case finally adjudicated in plaintiff’s favor (that is, plaintiff’s favor here, not plaintiff’s favor in the UD case). 

 

A Special Motion to Strike (SMS) is a California response to what the Legislature concluded were a series of lawsuits designed to chill speech.  The underlying problem was that a person would engage in free speech—such as picketing or the like—and would be met with a lawsuit claiming that the speech was tortious in some way.  True, the defendant would ultimately prevail if the speech was in fact protected speech, but only after litigating the issue—potentially through summary judgment or trial.  In the meantime, the defendant would be forced to run the litigation gauntlet and retain counsel.  The effect, the Legislature concluded, was that people were afraid to engage in protected speech or activity due to this improper and heavy-handed mis-use of the legal process.  The response was Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16—the Special Motion to Strike.  (It is often referred to as an anti-SLAPP motion.  The court prefers SMS.)  This is a very powerful tool that is given to defendants who believe that they are being sued for engaging in this kind of constitutionally-protected conduct.  It allows an early resolution of the case on the merits.  The motion is a speaking motion, meaning that the court goes beyond the allegations in the complaint, and once brought, the motion freezes discovery.  A plaintiff is generally not allowed to amend if the motion is successful, and the motion—win or lose—is appealable.  A successful defendant is entitled to recover attorneys’ fees as a matter of right as well.  The same is not true of a party successfully defeating the motion unless the motion was brought in a way that (essentially) violates CCP section 128.5. 

 

The SMS sets forth a two step process to balance the desire to get a resolution early against the need to be sure that due process is maintained.  The first step is to determine whether the suit really alleges a tort based on the exercise of a constitutionally protected right.  After all, most torts have nothing to do with the Constitution.  The burden on establishing this prong lies with the defense, and the court looks primarily to the complaint to answer the question.  The court can look beyond the complaint, but generally, plaintiff is the master of the complaint and a plaintiff is entitled to plead around constitutionally protected conduct.  (For example, in a case similar in some ways to this one, plaintiff mentioned a UD case in the complaint, but was careful only to sue the defendant for other things, like turning off the utilities, and expressly disclaimed any effort to impose liability on the defense for what the plaintiff claimed was a bad faith UD action.)  Further, an allegation of constitutionally protected activity will not trigger the statute if it is only background.  It has to be a part of the charging allegations—meaning it needs to form a part of the actual basis for the cause of action.  If the defendant can meet the first prong, then the burden shifts to the plaintiff to defeat the motion under the second prong.  The statute states that it is up to the plaintiff to show a reasonable probability of success.  But those words are deceiving.  Recall that this is the start of the case.  The plaintiff has not had the benefit of discovery, and the point of the SMS is not to do away with the fact-finder’s role.  Rather, this prong has been interpreted as a test to see whether the case has “minimal merit,” which is a standard akin to that needed to oppose successfully a motion for summary judgment.  If the plaintiff can present evidence—not just allegations—that (if believed) would lead to a plaintiff’s verdict, that is sufficient.  The court can consider the evidence, but it is not to weigh the evidence.  If there is a triable issue of fact, the motion fails. 

 

The court turns to the first prong.  The allegations there are that Leguizamon, who was employed by Community Corporation as a Property Supervisor for the relevant property, requested a call with plaintiff to discuss certain complaints that had been received.  He provided his contact information.  He mistakenly began the letter to her with “Dear Semen.”  The defense has put in evidence that this was a mistake.  He took the heading from a recent letter he had written to a tenant whose first name was “Semen” (pronounced Simon).  Plaintiff did not call, so a formal Measure RR letter was sent to plaintiff.  Under that measure, such a letter is required before eviction proceedings can begin.  That ultimately led to an inspection of plaintiff’s residence in December 2025, which ultimately led to a new Measure RR letter and then eviction proceedings.  Therefore, the November letter was a predicate to the start of potential eviction procedures, which is constitutionally protected conduct.  The same is true of the December cure or quit notice.

 

All of that conduct is protected.  Measure RR is the predicate that leads to an unlawful detainer action.  That is an action in court, and as such, it involves the right to petition government.  Suing someone for taking a first step toward bringing a legal action is a suit that does involve a constitutionally protected right.  While plaintiff does not allege that the UD action itself is the wrong, the things about which she does sue are necessary predicates to that action.  (That is true even if the ultimate grounds for the UD action were different from those in the original Measure RR notice.  The fact is that the original notice was a predicate to suit; the fact that the landlord ultimately brought the UD on different grounds after learning more information does not change the character of the claim.)  That is sufficient, and the burden shifts.  The court notes that while it is plaintiff’s burden to present evidence, the defense here has raised the litigation privilege codified in Civil Code section 47(b).  That statute makes conduct in litigation absolutely privileged as against tort claims (other than malicious prosecution).  It is often called the “absolute” privilege, although pre-litigation conduct is not really absolutely privileged.  Even so, it is very potent and does extend to core conduct necessary to pursue an eventual claim in court.  Here, plaintiff has not presented evidence in opposition to the motion, so there is no evidence to support her claims at all.  (She does ask the court to take judicial notice of various state and local laws, which the court will do.)  But even if there were, pretty much everything done was done in furtherance of the eventual or contemplated UD claim.  The defamation assumes a false statement, but there is no evidence that any statement was false.  (Further, to the extent that the statement was an allegation of complaints by others, if the defendant really received the complaints, the statements are true.)  The other causes of action are all derivative of the fact that defendant was starting to take steps that eventually led to plaintiff’s eviction.  Further, plaintiff has failed to show minimal merit or how her claims will survive the section 47 privilege.  A famous example is the decision is Malin v. Singer (2013) 217 Cal.App.4th 1283.   There, attorney Singer sent a settlement demand letter to a potential defendant threatening that if defendant did not settle, Singer (on behalf of his client) would bring a lawsuit and seek a full accounting of defendant’s books and records (suggesting that he would uncover misconduct that would become public in the litigation) and that the defendant had arranged for sexual liaisons between certain victims and older men.  The target of the litigation sued, claiming that Singer’s letter constituted an attempted extortion—seeking to disclose embarrassing facts unless money was paid.  Singer brought an SMS.  The trial court denied the motion, finding that the letter could be so interpreted and thus was not protected conduct, or at least whether it was protected was a question of fact.  The Court of Appeal reversed.  It noted that because the letter dealt only with the threatened litigation, it was the start of the litigation process.  Unlike a threat simply to disclose embarrassing facts unless money is paid (which could be extortion), this was a threat to file a lawsuit, and the facts in question were relevant to the lawsuit and would become public, therefore, should a suit be filed.  Accordingly, even though the letter was not itself a court document, it was the first step toward a lawsuit, and thus was protected under the litigation privilege.  The court ordered that the suit be dismissed.  So here.  The conduct of which plaintiff complains all involves actions that appear to be things necessary in order to bring an unlawful detainer case.  And that is protected.  Even if the UD case ultimately fails, it is the right to bring it that counts.  (If the UD action fails, plaintiff here might have a malicious prosecution claim against the landlord, but that is not a question before this court.)

 

Plaintiff also contends that sending the letter to third parties is defamation and thus is not protected.  But this was not widely disseminated.  Rather, the letter was allegedly sent to the Housing Department and two individuals apparently not unrelated to the matter.  Because the dissemination was in furtherance of the Measure RR process, there remains a privilege.  And plaintiff has still not demonstrated that there was anything in the letter that was false or that put plaintiff in a false light.

 

The court has considered the various statutes to which plaintiff cites.  But the bottom line remains that the defense is privileged to begin the process that might lead to eviction.  That begins with an attempt to contact plaintiff, but it continues with reasonable investigation of the complaints, a Measure RR letter, a 3 day notice, and eventually a lawsuit.   All of that is protected activity.  Because there is no evidence from the defense that any of the statements of fact are even false, let alone defamatory, the motion has merit.

 

Because defendant has demonstrated that the lawsuits involve constitutionally protected conduct, and because plaintiff has presented no evidence suggesting that there is even a triable issue of fact on the merits, the motions succeed.  Therefore, the Special Motions to Strike are GRANTED.  The complaint is STRICKEN.  Defendants will prepare a written order for the court’s signature.

 

Finally, the court notes that plaintiff filed papers yesterday.  They appeared for the first time today.  They are unauthorized and they are STRICKEN.  This is not the first time plaintiff has done this sort of thing.  Basic fairness to the other side means that the papers have to be filed and served in a way that allows the other side to digest them before the hearing.  And where the other side is entitled to a reply, then the papers must be filed in a way to allow a reply.  And basic fairness to the court requires that papers be filed in a way that allows the court adequate time to review them and consider them.  This sort of thing is not proper.  If plaintiff needed more time, she should have said so at the last hearing or sought a continuance to allow the filings.  That said, to the extent that it is a request that the court take judicial notice of the law, the court will do so.
