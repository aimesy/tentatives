DEPARTMENT 406 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 23STCV11457    Hearing Date: July 1, 2026    Dept: 406

 

KENNAN TILLMAN,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

CHA HOLLYWOOD MEDICAL CENTER,

                        Defendant.

 

  Case No.:  23STCV11457

  Hearing Date:  July 1, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

defendant’s motion for summary judgment or adjudication

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On May 22, 2023, Plaintiff Kennan Tillman filed this action against CHA Hollywood Medical Center. Plaintiff filed the operative First Amended Complaint on July 31, 2023, alleging (1) wrongful termination in violation of public policy based on Labor Code section 1102.5, (2) blacklisting in violation of Labor Code section 1050, and (3) intentional infliction of emotional distress.

            On April 9, 2026, Defendant filed the instant motion for summary judgment or adjudication. Plaintiff filed an opposition on June 11, 2026. Defendant filed a reply on June 18, 2026.

LEGAL STANDARD

The function of a motion for summary judgment or adjudication is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party cannot show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) Code of Civil Procedure section 437c(c) “requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.)

“[T]he party moving for summary judgment bears an initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing of the nonexistence of any triable issue of material fact; if he carries his burden of production, he causes a shift, and the opposing party is then subjected to a burden of production of his own to make a prima facie showing of the existence of a triable issue of material fact.” (Leyva v. Garcia (2018) 20 Cal.App.5th 1095, 1101.) To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce “substantial responsive evidence.” (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.) Courts “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.” (Dore v. Arnold Worldwide, Inc. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 384, 389.)

EVIDENTIARY OBJECTIONS

            Defendant’s objections are overruled.

DISCUSSION

I. Whistleblower Retaliation

            a. Protected Activity

Labor Code section 1102.5(b) makes it unlawful for an employer to retaliate against an employee for reporting a violation of a local, state, or federal statute or regulation. “[T]he protections of section 1102.5(b) apply only where the disclosing employee ‘has reasonable cause to believe that the information discloses a [legal] violation.’” (People ex rel. Garcia-Brower v. Kolla's, Inc. (2023) 14 Cal.5th 719, 734.) Section 1102.5 “does not protect employees who do not believe or who unreasonably believe that the information they are disclosing shows a violation of the law.” (Contreras v. Green Thumb Produce, Inc. (2025) 116 Cal.App.5th 1251, 1260.) An employee does not engage in protected activity where “the disclosures indisputably encompassed only the context of internal personnel matters involving a supervisor and her employee, rather than the disclosure of a legal violation.” (Patten v. Grant Joint Union High School Dist. (2005) 134 Cal.App.4th 1378, 1384-85.)

            Defendant argues that the only complaint Plaintiff personally filed concerned his subordinate, Cynthia Plummer, but Plaintiff admits that he does not believe Plummer did anything illegal. (Def.’s Stmt. of Undisputed Facts (UF) 1-2.) Citing Patten, Defendant argues that Plaintiff did not engage in whistleblowing activity by merely forwarding others’ complaints, which reflects internal personnel matters, not legal violations.

            However, Patten did not create a categorical rule that internal complaints cannot constitute protected activity. To the contrary, section 1102.5 specifies that reporting “to a person with authority over the employee or another employee who has the authority to investigate, discover, or correct the violation or noncompliance” constitutes protected activity. (Lab. Code, § 1102.5(b).) This is true “regardless of whether disclosing the information is part of the employee’s job duties.” (Ibid.) The court in Patten found that the reporting in that particular case consisted of purely internal personnel matters which did not implicate legal violations. But that does not mean internal personnel complaints can never implicate legal violations falling within the purview of section 1102.5. Thus, the fact that Plaintiff escalated complaints by others in line with his job duties and company policy does not, by itself, exclude the activity from the protection of section 1102.5.

Rather, the question is whether “the employee has reasonable cause to believe that the information discloses a violation of state or federal statute, or a violation of or noncompliance with a local, state, or federal rule or regulation.” (See Lab. Code, § 1102.5(b).) Plaintiff testified, “the issues that I reported were issues that were specifically coming from patient complaints that are documented, um, family -- I mean, family -- family complaints and the patient family complaints from employee complaints within our department from external department complaints.” (Def.’s Ex. 1 at 27:12-18.) Plaintiff also recalled discussing with HR and Director Toni Bernardo that Plummer was mistreating or making negative comments about Koreans. (Id. at 99:11-24.) Defendant has not precluded the possibility that these complaints involved legal violations or at least reflected Plaintiff’s reasonable belief that a legal violation had occurred.  

Plaintiff’s testimony that Plummer was not “doing anything illegal” is not dispositive. (See Def.’s Ex. 1 at 43:1-8.) This is a vague, conclusory assertion, not a specific admission that Plaintiff did not engage in protected activity. Moreover, Plaintiff testified that he expressed concern about Defendant retaining Plummer despite the issues with her conduct, telling Defendant “I believe we have a huge liability.” (Id. at 244:13-16.) Plaintiff testified that he was concerned because “you have somebody who is harassing, um, outside staff members, someone who's harassing patients, family members, an entire protected group then, um, they are putting this hospital in risk and creating a liability for them.” (Id. at 244:18-22.) This latter testimony suggests that Plaintiff did in fact believe Plummer’s actions violated the law. To the extent the testimony is contradictory, that is a matter of weight and credibility for the trier of fact to resolve. Ultimately, the record contains sufficient evidence to raise a triable issue.

            b. Causation

            To establish a claim under section 1102.5, the plaintiff must show a causal link between his protected activity and the adverse employment action. (St. Myers v. Dignity Health (2019) 44 Cal.App.5th 301, 314.)

            Defendant argues that even if Plaintiff engaged in protected activity, he cannot establish a causal link because he admittedly does not know why he was terminated. (UF 3-4.) However, Plaintiff’s testimony that he could not understand why he was terminated reflects his belief that the termination was unjustified, especially because witnesses testified that Plummer was the aggressor during the subject altercation, not Plaintiff. (See Def.’s Ex. 1 at 253:5-255:22.) Plaintiff did not admit that there is no evidence of prohibited animus. At the very least, the testimony is reasonably open to interpretation. The fact that Plaintiff was suspended and terminated shortly after making complaints about Plummer sufficiently raises an inference of retaliation. Thus, triable issues remain over causation.  

II. Blacklisting

            “Any person, or agent or officer thereof, who, after having discharged an employee from the service of such person or after an employee has voluntarily left such service, by any misrepresentation prevents or attempts to prevent the former employee from obtaining employment, is guilty of a misdemeanor.” (Lab. Code, § 1050.) “Labor Code section 1050 applies only to misrepresentations made to prospective employers other than the defendant. It does not apply to misrepresentations made by employees of the defendant to other of the defendant's employees.” (Kelly v. General Telephone Co. (1982) 136 Cal.App.3d 278, 288.)

            In his deposition, Plaintiff denied that the blacklisting claim was based on “HPMC contact[ing] potential employers to make sure that [he] didn't get a job.” (Def.’s Ex. 1 at 257:21-25.) Instead, Plaintiff testified that he was blacklisted in the following manner: “after I've applied for the job and you put down your references and you get interviewed and they check your references or when you are applying for a job, you're ineligible to be rehired -- my termination letter said I was terminated and ineligible to be rehired. That's a really large hurtical (sic) -- hurtle to overcome.” (Id. at 257:25-258:6.) However, Plaintiff also confirmed that “[n]o one sent [the letter] to potential employers.” (Id. at 258:7-9.)

            Defendant argues that this claim fails because the only supporting evidence is a termination letter sent to Plaintiff himself, not to any prospective employer. (UF 6, 8.) According to Defendant, the fact that Plaintiff has had to tell prospective employers he is ineligible to be rehired by Defendant does not constitute blacklisting, because the statement is true, and Defendant is not the one making the statement as required under section 1050. The Court agrees that this precludes the blacklisting claim as a matter of law. Thus, Defendant has satisfied its initial burden on the motion.  

            Plaintiff argues that the termination letter itself contains misrepresentations about Plaintiff. However, this does not rebut the fact that the letter was not transmitted to anyone other than Plaintiff. If the letter is the source of the alleged misrepresentations, and the letter was indisputably not communicated to any third party, then Defendant did not make a misrepresentation within the meaning of section 1050. (See Kelly, supra, 136 Cal.App.3d at p. 288.) Plaintiff has no evidence that Defendant otherwise transmitted false information to a third party.

            Plaintiff argues that “[a] jury could find that HPMC's disputed accusations and ineligible-for-rehire designation were intended to impede future employment, including with HPMC affiliates and employers checking prior employment eligibility.” (Opp. 8:14-17.) However, section 1050 does not create freestanding liability for any act that impedes future employment. The statute specifically requires “misrepresentations made to prospective employers other than the defendant.” (Kelly, supra, 136 Cal.App.3d at p. 288.) Plaintiff testified that Defendant did not contact prospective employers to prevent them from hiring Plaintiff. Plaintiff testified that the termination letter was not transmitted to any prospective employer. Plaintiff identifies no other misrepresentation by Defendant to a prospective employer. Thus, as a matter of law, there was no misrepresentation within the meaning of section 1050.

            Lastly, Plaintiff argues that Defendant has not affirmatively proven that it did not send any false information to a prospective employer. However, as discussed above, Defendant satisfied its initial burden by presenting Plaintiff’s testimony that Defendant did not contact any prospective employer to prevent him from being hired and did not transmit the termination letter containing allegedly false accusations to any prospective employer. Besides the letter, Plaintiff was unable to identify any action that Defendant took against him after his termination. (Def.’s Ex. 1 at 260:8-14.) The burden thus shifted to Plaintiff to present “substantial responsive evidence” that Defendant communicated false information to a third party. (See Sangster, supra, 68 Cal.App.4th at p. 166.) Plaintiff has not met this burden.   

            Therefore, the blacklisting claim fails as a matter of law.

III. Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress

To establish a cause of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress, a plaintiff must prove: (1) outrageous conduct by the defendant; (2) the defendant’s intention of causing, or reckless disregard of the probability of causing, emotional distress; (3) the plaintiff’s suffering severe or extreme emotional distress; and (4) actual and proximate causation of the emotional distress by the defendant’s outrageous conduct. (Vasquez v. Franklin Management Real Estate Fund, Inc. (2013) 222 Cal.App.4th 819, 832.) “For conduct to be outrageous, it must be so extreme as to exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated by a civilized community.” (Faunce v. Cate (2013) 222 Cal.App.4th 166, 172.) “Severe emotional distress [is] emotional distress of such substantial quantity or enduring quality that no reasonable man in a civilized society should be expected to endure it.” (Fletcher v. Western Life Insurance Co. (1970) 10 Cal.App.3d 376, 397.)

The potential whistleblower retaliation is not outrageous as a matter of law. While Defendant may have violated the Labor Code by terminating Plaintiff, there are no facts demonstrating aggravating circumstances to warrant recovery for IIED. Even assuming Defendant unlawfully blacklisted Plaintiff, those alleged actions were also not egregious as a matter of law. Therefore, the IIED claim fails as a matter of law.

 

 

 

CONCLUSION

            Defendant’s motion for summary judgment is DENIED. Defendant’s motion for summary adjudication is GRANTED as to the second and third causes of action and DENIED as to the first cause of action.

 


Case Number: 25STCV01640    Hearing Date: July 1, 2026    Dept: 406

 

JAIME VILLAREAL,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

MARVIN ENGINEERING CO., INC., et al.,

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  25STCV01640

  Hearing Date:  July 1, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

defendant marvin engineering co., inc.’s motion for summary adjudication  

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On January 21, 2025, Plaintiff Jaime Villareal filed this action against Defendants Marvin Engineering Co., Inc. and Robert Huizar. The complaint asserts claims for (1) harassment, (2) retaliation, (3) failure to prevent, (4) violation of Labor Code section 1102.5, (5) violation of Labor Code section 6310, (6) violation of Labor Code section 232.5, and (7) wrongful termination.

            On March 16, 2026, Defendant Marvin Engineering filed the instant motion for summary adjudication of the second, fourth, fifth, and sixth causes of action. Plaintiff has not filed an opposition.

LEGAL STANDARD

The function of a motion for summary judgment or adjudication is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party cannot show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) Code of Civil Procedure section 437c(c) “requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.)

“[T]he party moving for summary judgment bears an initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing of the nonexistence of any triable issue of material fact; if he carries his burden of production, he causes a shift, and the opposing party is then subjected to a burden of production of his own to make a prima facie showing of the existence of a triable issue of material fact.” (Leyva v. Garcia (2018) 20 Cal.App.5th 1095, 1101.) To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce “substantial responsive evidence.” (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.) Courts “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.” (Dore v. Arnold Worldwide, Inc. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 384, 389.)

DISCUSSION

I. FEHA Retaliation

a. Legal Standard

To establish a prima facie case of retaliation under the FEHA, a plaintiff must show: (1) she engaged in a protected activity; (2) the employer subjected the employee to an adverse employment action; and (3) a causal link existed between the protected activity and the employer’s action. (Yanowitz v. L’Oreal USA, Inc. (2007) 36 Cal.4th 1028, 1042.)

Under the McDonnell-Douglas framework, (1) the plaintiff must initially establish a prima facie case of discrimination, (2) the defendant must then articulate a legitimate nonretaliatory explanation for its acts, and (3) in response, the plaintiff must show that this explanation is pretextual. (Patten v. Grant Joint Union High School Dist. (2005) 134 Cal.App.4th 1378, 1384.) Therefore, on summary judgment, the defendant employer must either (1) undermine an element of the plaintiff’s prima facie case or (2) provide a legitimate nonretaliatory reason for the adverse employment action. (Cornell v. Berkeley Tennis Club (2017) 18 Cal.App.5th 908, 926.)

In the face of a nondiscriminatory reason, the plaintiff must offer “substantial evidence” allowing a reasonable conclusion that “the employer engaged in intentional discrimination.” (Foroudi v. The Aerospace Corp. (2020) 57 Cal.App.5th 992, 1007.) “The employee’s evidence must relate to the motivation of the decision makers and prove, by nonspeculative evidence, ‘an actual causal link between prohibited motivation and termination.’” (Id. at p. 1008, citing Featherstone v. Southern California Permanente Medical Group (2017) 10 Cal.App.5th 1150, 1159.)

b. Application

Here, Defendant terminated Plaintiff because he engaged in a physical altercation with a coworker which was captured on surveillance video. (Def.’s Stmt. of Undisputed Facts (UF) 2-4.) Defendant has a written policy against workplace violence, which establishes that violence is grounds for termination. (UF 5-6.) Defendant’s Vice President of Materials & Facilities, Chuck Minter, investigated the incident and determined that Plaintiff and the coworker violated the workplace violence policy. (UF 7-15.) Therefore, Mr. Minter terminated both Plaintiff and the coworker. (Ibid.) At the time of this decision, Mr. Minter was unaware that Plaintiff had complained about discrimination, harassment, unsafe conditions, or other violations of law. (UF 16.)

These undisputed facts establish as a matter of law that Defendant had a legitimate, non-retaliatory reason for terminating Plaintiff. In the face of this evidence, Plaintiff has failed to present “substantial evidence” that the termination was actually motivated by prohibited animus. (See Foroudi, supra, 57 Cal.App.5th at pp. 1007-08.) Therefore, no reasonable trier of fact would find that Defendant engaged in retaliation in violation of FEHA. Accordingly, summary adjudication of the second cause of action is warranted.

II. Labor Code Retaliation

            Labor Code section 6310 prohibits retaliation against an employee who has “[m]ade any oral complaint to . . . their employer” or “[r]eported a work-related fatality, injury, or illness.” (Lab. Code, § 6310(a).) Labor Code section 232.5 makes it unlawful for an employer to prevent employees from disclosing information about the employer’s working conditions or to retaliate against an employee who discloses such information.  

            Absent authority providing otherwise, the McDonnell-Douglas test applies to retaliation claims under Labor Code sections 6310 and 232.5. (See Scheer v. Regents of University of California (2022) 76 Cal.App.5th 904, 913-17.) For the reasons discussed above, Defendant had a legitimate, non-retaliatory reason for terminating Plaintiff. Therefore, the fifth and sixth causes of action fail as a matter of law.

III. Whistleblower Retaliation

            Labor Code section 1102.5(b) makes it unlawful for an employer to retaliate against an employee for reporting a perceived violation of a local, state, or federal statute or regulation. “[O]nce it has been demonstrated by a preponderance of the evidence that an activity proscribed by Section 1102.5 was a contributing factor in the alleged prohibited action against the employee, the employer shall have the burden of proof to demonstrate by clear and convincing evidence that the alleged action would have occurred for legitimate, independent reasons even if the employee had not engaged in activities protected by Section 1102.5.” (Id., § 1102.6.) The traditional McDonnell-Douglas framework does not apply, and the plaintiff is not required to show that the employer’s proffered justification is pretextual. (Lawson v. PPG Architectural Finishes, Inc. (2022) 12 Cal.5th 703, 712.)

            For the reasons articulated above, Defendant has shown that Plaintiff’s protected activity was not a contributing factor in his termination. Defendant has established by clear and convincing evidence that it would have terminated Plaintiff for legitimate, independent reasons even if Plaintiff had not engaged in protected activity. Plaintiff has failed to rebut this showing and presents no evidence that Defendant “had at least one retaliatory reason that was a contributing factor in the action.” (See Lawson, supra, 12 Cal.5th at p. 716.) Therefore, the fourth cause of action fails as a matter of law.

CONCLUSION

            Defendant Marvin Engineering Co., Inc.’s motion for summary adjudication of the second, fourth, fifth, and sixth causes of action is GRANTED.

 


Case Number: 25STCV11454    Hearing Date: July 1, 2026    Dept: 406

 

FRANCISCO ADAN LOPEZ,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

NISSAN NORTH AMERICA, INC.,

                        Defendant.

 

  Case No.:  25STCV11454

  Hearing Date:  July 1, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

plaintiff’s motion to compel initial disclosures

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On April 18, 2025, Plaintiff Francisco Adan Lopez filed this action against Defendant Nissan North America, Inc. for violations of the Song-Beverly Act, breach of warranty, and fraud.

            On April 8, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion to compel initial disclosures. Defendant filed an opposition on June 17, 2026.

LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure sections 871.20 to 871.30 set forth specific rules applying to actions for restitution or replacement of motor vehicles under the Song-Beverly Act. With regards to discovery, the Code provides that “[w]ithin 60 days after the filing of the answer or other responsive pleading, all parties shall, without awaiting a discovery request, provide to all other parties an initial disclosure and documents pursuant to subdivisions (f), (g), and (h).” (Code Civ. Proc., § 871.26(b).) Subdivisions (f) and (g) specify the documents and information that the plaintiff must provide, while subdivision (h) specifies the documents that the defendant must provide. Section 871.26 sets forth a schedule of sanctions which “shall” be imposed “[u]nless the party failing to comply with this section shows good cause.” (Id., § 871.26(j).) 

DISCUSSION

            Defendant filed its answer on August 12, 2025. Defendant did not complete its initial disclosures within 60 days of that date. However, the parties agreed to toll initial disclosures pending informal resolution. (Sweeney Decl. ¶ 6.) After Plaintiff filed this motion, Defendant served its initial disclosures on June 12, 2026. (Id., ¶ 10.) Defendant avers that it has served all required disclosures under the code. (Id., ¶ 13.)

            Because Defendant has satisfied the initial disclosure requirements, the motion is moot. Sanctions are not warranted because Defendant has demonstrated good cause. Defendant reasonably believed that initial disclosures were tolled during informal negotiations.

CONCLUSION

            Plaintiff’s motion to compel initial disclosures is DENIED. Sanctions are denied.

 

 


Case Number: 25STLC08481    Hearing Date: July 1, 2026    Dept: 406

 

CONEJO VALLEY HEATING & AIR CONDITIONING, INC.,

                       

                         Plaintiff,

            v.

 

JERRY KOWAL,

                        Defendant.

 

  Case No.:  25STLC08481

  Hearing Date:  July 1, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

cross-defendants’ demurrer to first amended cross-complaint

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On November 10, 2025, Plaintiff Conejo Valley Heating & Air Conditioning, Inc. (Conejo) filed this action against Defendant Jerry Kowal (Kowal). Conejo filed the operative First Amended Complaint on December 9, 2025, asserting claims for (1) breach of contract, (2) common counts, and (3) quantum valebant. The FAC alleges that Kowal failed to pay the amount owed to Conejo under a home improvement contract.

            On December 18, 2025, Kowal filed a cross-complaint against Conejo, Branden Price Dickey (Dickey), and Merchants Bonding Company (MBC). Kowal filed the operative First Amended Cross-Complaint on February 19, 2026, adding CV 2025 Holdings, Inc. (CV) as a cross-defendant. Kowal later dismissed MBC. The FACC asserts claims for (1) violation of CLRA, (2) violation of the Business and Professions Code section 7150 et seq., (3) negligence, (4) breach of contract, (5) fraudulent misrepresentation, (6) negligent misrepresentation, (7) contractor’s license bond, and (8) violation of the UCL.

The FACC alleges that Conejo failed to perform adequate repairs on Kowal’s property and overcharged Kowal for unnecessary services. The FACC alleges that Dickey and CV are members of Conejo. Dickey is allegedly the sole shareholder and director of CV.

On May 22, 2026, Conejo, CV, and Dickey (collectively, Cross-Defendants) filed the instant demurrer to the FACC. Kowal filed an opposition on June 17, 2026. Cross-Defendants filed a reply on June 24, 2026.

LEGAL STANDARD

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (Taylor v. City of Los Angeles Dept. of Water and Power (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 1216, 1228.) In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or by proper judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.30(a).) A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. (SKF Farms v. Superior Court (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 902, 905.) “California law emphasizes ultimate fact pleading (with some exceptions, notably for fraud and related torts) ‘in ordinary and concise language,’ and the test for adequacy is not absolute but ‘whether the pleading as a whole apprises the adversary of the factual basis of the claim.’” (Lim v. The.TV Corp. Internat. (2002) 99 Cal.App.4th 684, 690.)

MEET AND CONFER

Before filing a demurrer or a motion to strike, the demurring or moving party is required to meet and confer with the party who filed the pleading demurred to or the pleading that is subject to the motion to strike for the purposes of determining whether an agreement can be reached through a filing of an amended pleading that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 430.41, 435.5.) The Court finds that Cross-Defendants have satisfied the meet and confer requirement. (See Rodgers Decl.)

DISCUSSION

I. Alter Ego

            The debts, obligations, or liabilities of an LLC “do not become the debts, obligations, or other liabilities of a member or manager solely by reason of the member acting as a member or manager acting as a manager for the limited liability company.” (Corp. Code, § 17703.04(a).) However, a member “shall be subject to liability under the common law governing alter ego liability.” (Id., § 17703.04(b).)

“The alter ego doctrine arises when a plaintiff comes into court claiming that an opposing party is using the corporate form unjustly and in derogation of the plaintiff's interests . . . In certain circumstances the court will disregard the corporate entity and will hold the individual shareholders liable for the actions of the corporation.” (Greenspan v. LADT LLC (2010) 191 Cal.App.4th 486, 510.) Establishing alter ego “generally requires the proponent to demonstrate two elements: (1) a unity of interest and ownership such that the separate personalities of the corporation and the individual do not exist; and (2) an inequitable result if the corporate identity is not disregarded.” (JPV I L.P. v. Koetting (2023) 88 Cal.App.5th 172, 189.)

In Rutherford Holdings, LLC v. Plaza Del Rey (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 221, 235-36, the court of appeal held that the following alter ego allegations were sufficient to survive a demurrer: that the individual defendant dominated and controlled the entity defendant; that a unity of interest and ownership existed between the individual defendant and entity defendant; that the entity defendant was a mere shell and conduit for the individual defendant’s affairs; that the entity defendant was inadequately capitalized; that the entity defendant failed to abide by corporate formalities; that the individual defendant used the entity defendant’s assets as her own; and that recognizing the separate existence of the entity defendant would promote injustice.

            Cross-Defendants argue that the FACC fails to establish CV’s or Dickey’s liability for the acts of Conejo because there are no facts showing CV’s or Dickey’s personal participation in the alleged wrongful acts. However, the FACC alleges ultimate facts similar to those found sufficient in Rutherford Holdings, establishing an alter ego relationship between Dickey and Conejo. (See FACC ¶¶ 5-6.) This sufficiently states a claim against Dickey for pleading purposes. Cross-Defendants cite no authority for imposing an additional requirement that the alter ego allegations must specifically relate to the alleged wrongful conduct.

            Cross-Defendants insist that the personal participation requirement still applies to Dickey, citing Reynolds v. Bement (2005) 36 Cal.4th 1075 and United States Liability Ins. Co. v. Haidinger-Hayes, Inc. (1970) 1 Cal.3d 586. However, both cases predate the enactment of section 17703.04, which expressly states that “[a] member of a limited liability company shall be subject to liability under the common law governing alter ego liability.” (Corp. Code, § 17703.04(b).) Cross-Defendants fail to establish that individual members are exempted from this provision. Section 17703.04(c) separately preserves a member’s liability for personal participation, showing that alter ego and personal participation are separate and distinct bases for liability. Therefore, the FACC adequately alleges a basis for liability against Dickey.

            However, the FACC does not sufficiently plead liability against CV. The FACC only alleges that Dickey is the alter ego of Conejo and CV, and alleges that Dickey should be held personally liable for the obligations of Conejo and CV. (FACC ¶¶ 5-6.) The FACC does not allege facts showing that CV is the alter ego of Conejo or that CV and Conejo operated as a single enterprise. Plaintiff cites no authority suggesting that an LLC and corporation are alter egos of one another just because the same individual is the alter ego of both. Therefore, the demurrer is sustained as to CV.

II. Business and Professions Code Section 7159

            “Pursuant to section 7159, home improvement contracts for work in excess of $500 and any changes in such contracts, between a licensed contractor and an owner or tenant, must be evidenced by a writing and signed by all the parties. A violation of this provision by the licensee is a misdemeanor.” (Davenport & Co. v. Spieker (1988) 197 Cal.App.3d 566, 569; see also Phillippe v. Shapell Industries (1987) 43 Cal.3d 1247, 1266, fn. 12 [“Violation of Business and Professions Code section 7159 is a misdemeanor punishable by fine and/or imprisonment”].)

            Section 7159 provides no private right of action, and Plaintiff cites no authority suggesting that it does. Plaintiff argues that the legislative purpose behind section 7159 favors a private right of action. However, the Supreme Court has recognized that the criminal penalties “should be sufficient to serve the underlying legislative policy of that statute.” (Phillippe, supra, 43 Cal.3d at p. 1266, fn. 12.) Thus, the Court agrees with Cross-Defendants that there is no actionable claim under section 7159.

            However, the second cause of action is not solely based on section 7159. Rather, it asserts “violation of Business and Professions Code § 7150 et seq.” (FAC at 10:19-22.) The FACC alleges that “Business and Professions Code §7160 also creates a private right of action.” (FAC ¶ 40.) Section 7160 provides as follows: “Any person who is induced to contract for a work of improvement, including but not limited to a home improvement, in reliance on false or fraudulent representations or false statements knowingly made, may sue and recover from such contractor or solicitor a penalty of five hundred dollars ($500), plus reasonable attorney’s fees, in addition to any damages sustained by him by reason of such statements or representations made by the contractor or solicitor.” (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 7160.)

            Cross-Defendants do not argue that the FACC fails to state a claim under section 7160. Thus, the demurrer to the second cause of action fails to dispose of the entire cause of action and must be overruled. (See Poizner v. Fremont General Corp. (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 97, 119 [“A demurrer must dispose of an entire cause of action to be sustained”].)

CONCLUSION

            Cross-Defendants’ demurrer is SUSTAINED with leave to amend to establish alter ego liability against CV, and OVERRULED in all other respects.
