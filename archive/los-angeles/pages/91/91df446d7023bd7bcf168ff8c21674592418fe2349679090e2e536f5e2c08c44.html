CASE:                          3800 West Sixth Street, LLC, et al. v. Steven C. Kim., et al.

 

CASE NO.:                   22STCV21799

           

MOTION FOR ATTORNEYS’ FEES

 

 

MOVING PARTY/PARTIES: Defendants Susan Ahn and Serrano & Sixth LLC

 

RESPONDING PARTY/PARTIES: Plaintiffs 3800 West Sixth Street, LLC and Leo Lee

 

PROOF OF SERVICE: Plaintiffs oppose motion on the merits, and thereby waived any procedural objections.  (See In re Marriage of Falcone (2008) 164 Cal.App.4th 814, 826.)  

.

 

CASE HISTORY:

·        July 6, 2022: Complaint filed.

·        January 5, 2023: First amended complaint filed.

·        November 14, 2024: Defendants’ motions for summary judgment denied.

·        September 4, 2025: Court trial commenced.

·        October 1, 2025: Court trial concluded.

·        February 13. 2026: Court issued statement of decision after trial in favor of Defendants.

 

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

            Defendants Susan Ahn and Serrano & Sixth LLC (“Defendants”) prevailed at trial against Plaintiffs 3800 West Sixth Street, LLC and Leo Lee (“Plaintiffs”).  Defendants move to recover their attorneys’ fees in this case.  Plaintiffs oppose the motion.

 

TENTATIVE RULING:

Defendants’ motion for attorneys’ fees is DENIED.

Moving party to give notice. 

 

DISCUSSION:

The prevailing party must seek an award of attorneys’ fees via a noticed motion, and bears the burden of proof to justify the amount sought.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (c)(5)(A).) 

Contractual provision

Defendants contend they are entitled to recover attorneys’ fees under the Agreement for Purchase and Sale of Real Property and Joint Escrow Instructions.  In an action to enforce a contract that authorizes an award of attorneys’ fees, the party “prevailing on the contract” is entitled to recover attorneys’ fees.  (Civ. Code, § 1717.)  Here, in the operative first amended complaint, Plaintiffs did not sue Defendants for breach of contract.  Plaintiffs sued Defendants for misappropriation of trade secrets, unfair competition, and interference with prospective economic advantage.  Plaintiffs did not seek attorneys’ fees.  (See First Amended Complaint.)  As such, Plaintiffs did not sue Defendants on the Agreement for Purchase and Sale of Real Property and Joint Escrow Instructions, and Defendants did not prevail on a breach of contract claim.  Defendants are not entitled to recover attorneys’ fees under the Agreement for Purchase and Sale of Real Property and Joint Escrow Instructions.

In support of the argument that Defendants are entitled to recover fees under the Agreement for Purchase and Sale of Real Property and Joint Escrow Instructions, Defendants mischaracterize the holding of Lerner v. Ward (1993) 13 Cal.App.4th 155.  In that case, the Court of Appeal held that a party cannot recover attorneys’ fees based on a contract where the claims on which the party prevailed were tort claims, rather than contract claims.  (Lerner v. Ward (1993) 13 Cal.App.4th 155, 159.)  As such, Lerner v. Ward (1993) 13 Cal.App.4th 155 does not support Defendants’ argument that Defendants are entitled to recover fees.

 

Civil Code section 3426.4

Defendants seek attorneys’ fees and costs under Civil Code section 3426.4.  Civil Code section 3426.4 allows the Court to award reasonable attorney's fees and costs to the prevailing party where the other party has made a claim of misappropriation of trade secrets in bad faith.  (Civ. Code, § 3426.4.)  Under Civil Code section 3426.4, for a claim to have been made in bad faith, the claim must have been objectively specious, and must have been maintained in subjective bad faith.  (Gemini Aluminum Corp. v. California Custom Shapes, Inc. (2002 95 Cal.App.4th 1249, 1261-1262.)  As set forth above, Plaintiffs sued Defendants for misappropriation of trade secrets.  Defendants prevailed on that claim.  As such, Defendants are entitled to recover fees for defending the claim for misappropriation of trade secrets if the claim was frivolous and Plaintiffs acted in bad faith.  As Defendants point out, the Court granted summary adjudication of Plaintiffs’ claim for misappropriation of trade secrets.  (November 14, 2024 Minute Order.)  However, Plaintiffs’ claim for misappropriation of trade secrets is not frivolous simply because Defendants prevailed on the claim.  Further, Defendants do not address the issue of Plaintiffs’ bad faith.  The Court does not infer that Plaintiffs acted in bad faith because their claim for misappropriation of trade secrets failed.  By Defendants’ reasoning, any failed claim is frivolous and in bad faith.  Accordingly, Defendants fail to show they are entitled to recover fees under Civil Code section 3426.4.

Accordingly, Defendants’ motion for attorneys’ fees is DENIED. 

Moving party to give notice.

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

CASE:                          3800 West Sixth Street, LLC, et al. v. Steven C. Kim., et al.

 

CASE NO.:                   22STCV21799

           

MOTION FOR ATTORNEYS’ FEES

 

 

MOVING PARTY/PARTIES: Defendant Steven C. Kim

 

RESPONDING PARTY/PARTIES: Plaintiffs 3800 West Sixth Street, LLC and Leo Lee

 

PROOF OF SERVICE: Plaintiffs oppose motion on the merits, and thereby waived any procedural objections.  (See In re Marriage of Falcone (2008) 164 Cal.App.4th 814, 826.)  

.

 

CASE HISTORY:

·        July 6, 2022: Complaint filed.

·        January 5, 2023: First amended complaint filed.

·        November 14, 2024: Defendants’ motions for summary judgment denied.

·        September 4, 2025: Court trial commenced.

·        October 1, 2025: Court trial concluded.

·        February 13. 2026: Court issued statement of decision after trial in favor of Defendant.

 

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

            Defendant Steven C. Kim (“Defendant”) prevailed at trial against Plaintiffs 3800 West Sixth Street, LLC and Leo Lee (“Plaintiffs”).  Defendant moves to recover his attorneys’ fees in this case.  Plaintiffs oppose the motion.

 

TENTATIVE RULING:

Defendant’s motion for attorneys’ fees is DENIED.

Moving party to give notice. 

 

DISCUSSION:

The prevailing party must seek an award of attorneys’ fees via a noticed motion, and bears the burden of proof to justify the amount sought.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (c)(5)(A).) 

Contractual provision

Defendant contends he is entitled to recover attorneys’ fees under the Agreement for Purchase and Sale of Real Property and Joint Escrow Instructions.  Defendant is not a party to the contract.  As a non-signatory to the Agreement for Purchase and Sale of Real Property and Joint Escrow Instructions, Defendant is entitled to recover fees if Plaintiffs sued Defendant on the Agreement for Purchase and Sale of Real Property and Joint Escrow Instructions, Defendant prevailed on the breach of contract claim, and Plaintiffs would have had a right to recover fees had Plaintiffs prevailed.  (Reynolds Metals Co. v. Alperson (1979) 25 Cal.3d 124, 128-129.)  Here, in the operative first amended complaint, Plaintiffs did not sue Defendant for breach of contract.  Plaintiffs sued Defendant for misappropriation of trade secrets, breach of fiduciary duty, unfair competition, and interference with prospective economic advantage.  Plaintiffs did not seek attorneys’ fees.  (See First Amended Complaint.)  As such, Plaintiffs did not sue Defendant on the Agreement for Purchase and Sale of Real Property and Joint Escrow Instructions, and Defendant did not prevail on a breach of contract claim.  Defendant is not entitled to recover attorneys’ fees under the Agreement for Purchase and Sale of Real Property and Joint Escrow Instructions.

In support of his argument that Defendant is entitled to recover fees under the Agreement for Purchase and Sale of Real Property and Joint Escrow Instructions, Defendant mischaracterizes the holding of Lerner v. Ward (1993) 13 Cal.App.4th 155.  In that case, the Court of Appeal held that a party cannot recover attorneys’ fees based on a contract where the claims on which the party prevailed were tort claims, rather than contract claims.  (Lerner v. Ward (1993) 13 Cal.App.4th 155, 159.)  As such, Lerner v. Ward (1993) 13 Cal.App.4th 155 does not support Defendant’s argument that Defendant is entitled to recover fees.

Likewise, Defendant’s reliance on Kangarlou v. Progressive Title Co. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1174 is misplaced.  In that case, the Court of Appeal held that a claim for breach of fiduciary duty may be a contract claim or a tort claim and entitlement to attorneys’ fees on a claim for breach of fiduciary duty depends on whether the claim is based on contractual duties.  (Kangarlou v. Progressive Title Co. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1174, 1177.)  Plaintiffs’ claim for breach of fiduciary duty against Defendant is based on Defendant’s fiduciary duty as an attorney.  (See First Amended Complaint, ¶ 33,)  As such, it is a tort claim, not a contract claim.

Defendant also cites Wong v. Davidian (1988) 206 Cal.App.3d 264.  In that case, the Court of Appeal held that attorneys’ fees are recoverable in an action for reformation of contract.  (Wong v. Davidian (1988) 206 Cal.App.3d 264, 271.)  No such claim is at issue here.

Defendant argues he is entitled to attorneys’ fees as a third-party beneficiary of the Agreement for Purchase and Sale of Real Property and Joint Escrow Instructions.  That is immaterial, as Plaintiffs did not sue Defendant on the Agreement for Purchase and Sale of Real Property and Joint Escrow Instructions.  Furthermore, Defendant makes no showing that he is a third-party beneficiary.  A third-party beneficiary to a contract may enforce a contract if the third party shows that the third party is likely to benefit from the contract, a motivating purpose of the contracting parties was to provide the third party with a benefit, and permitting the third party to enforce the contract is consistent with the contract's objectives and the contracting parties' reasonable expectations. (Goonewardene v. ADP, LLC (2019) 6 Cal.5th 817, 821.)  Defendant fails to address these factors.  (See Motion for Attorneys’ Fees, pp. 11-12.) 

 

Civil Code section 3426.4

Defendant also seeks attorneys’ fees and costs under Civil Code section 3426.4.  Civil Code section 3426.4 allows the Court to award reasonable attorney's fees and costs to the prevailing party where the other party has made a claim of misappropriation of trade secrets in bad faith.  (Civ. Code, § 3426.4.)  Under Civil Code section 3426.4, for a claim to have been made in bad faith, the claim must have been objectively specious, and must have been maintained in subjective bad faith.  (Gemini Aluminum Corp. v. California Custom Shapes, Inc. (2002 95 Cal.App.4th 1249, 1261-1262.)  As set forth above, Plaintiffs sued Defendant for misappropriation of trade secrets.  Defendant prevailed on that claim.  As such, Defendant is entitled to recover fees for defending the claim for misappropriation of trade secrets if the claim was frivolous and Plaintiffs acted in bad faith.  As Defendant points out, the Court granted summary adjudication of Plaintiffs’ claim for misappropriation of trade secrets.  (November 14, 2024 Minute Order.)  However, Plaintiffs’ claim for misappropriation of trade secrets is not frivolous simply because Defendant prevailed on the claim.  Further, the Court does not infer that Plaintiffs acted in bad faith because their claim for misappropriation of trade secrets failed.  By Defendant’s reasoning, any failed claim is frivolous and in bad faith.  Accordingly, Defendant fails to show he is entitled to recover fees under Civil Code section 3426.4.

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.420

“Under Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.420, a party who unreasonably denies a request for admission may be required to pay the requesting party its reasonable expenses (including reasonable attorney's fees) incurred in proving the truth of the matter at trial.”  (In re Estate of Manuel (2010) 187 Cal.App.4th 400, 402.)  Defendant argues that Plaintiffs unreasonably denied certain requests for admissions that Defendant propounded on Plaintiffs.  Defendant contends he proved the matters set forth in the requests for admissions at trial.  Defendant does not, however, address the issue of whether Plaintiffs’ denial of the requests for admissions was unreasonable.  (See Motion for Attorneys’ Fees, pp. 16-17.)  Accordingly, Defendant fails to show he is entitled to recover fees under Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.420.

Accordingly, Defendant’s motion for attorneys’ fees is DENIED. 

Moving party to give notice.

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR
PROCEEDINGS:

            Defendants
Susan Ahn and Serrano & Sixth LLC (“Defendants”) prevailed at trial against
Plaintiff Leo Lee (“Plaintiff”). 
Plaintiff moves to tax the costs on the memorandum of costs Defendants
served.  Defendants oppose the motion.

After Plaintiff filed this motion, Defendants filed
a supplemental memorandum of costs, in which Defendants seek to recover
additional costs.  The Court’s clerk gave
notice of entry of judgment electronically on February 13, 2026.  As such, Defendants had until March 3, 2026
to file their memorandum of costs.  (Cal.
Rules of Court, rule 3.1700, subd. (a)(1).) 
Accordingly, the Court disregards the untimely supplemental memorandum
of costs.

 

TENTATIVE RULING:

Plaintiff’s
motion to tax costs in GRANTED in part.

Moving
party to give notice. 

 

DISCUSSION:

 

Motion to tax costs

Code
of Civil Procedure section 1033.5, subdivision (a) sets forth items that are
allowable as costs.  Allowable costs
under Section 1033.5 must be “reasonably necessary to the conduct of this
litigation rather than merely convenient or beneficial to its preparation” and
“reasonable in amount.”  (Code Civ.
Proc., § 1033.5, subds. (c)(2), (3).) 
“Items not mentioned in [Section 1033.5] and items assessed upon
application may be allowed or denied in the court’s discretion.” (Id.,
subd. (c)(4).)

On
a motion to tax costs, “[i]f the items appearing in a cost bill appear to be
proper charges, the burden is on the party seeking to tax costs to show that
they were not reasonable or necessary. On the other hand, if the items are
properly objected to, they are put in issue and the burden of proof is on the
party claiming them as costs. Whether a cost item was reasonably necessary to
the litigation presents a question of fact for the trial court and its decision
is reviewed for abuse of discretion. However, because the right to costs is
governed strictly by statute a court has no discretion to award costs not
statutorily authorized.”  (Ladas v.
California State Auto. Assn. (1993) 19 Cal.App.4th 761, 774, internal
citations omitted.)  “The court’s first
determination, therefore, is whether the statute expressly allows the item, and
whether it appears proper on its face. If so, the burden is on the objecting
party to show them to be unnecessary or unreasonable.”  (Nelson v. Anderson (1999) 72
Cal.App.4th 111, 131, internal citations omitted.)  The objecting party does not meet this burden
by arguing that the costs were not necessary or reasonable, but must present
evidence and prove that the costs are not recoverable. (Litt v Med. Ctr.
(2015) 237 Cal.App.4th 1217, 1224; Seever v. Copley Press, Inc. (2006)
141 Cal.App.4th 1550, 1557.)

 

Court
reporter fees

Defendants
seek $17,147.49 in court reporter fees. 
As Plaintiff points out, this figure includes transcript costs, which
are not recoverable as a matter of law. 
The Court did not order any transcripts in this case.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd.
(a)(9).)  As such, the costs for
transcripts are not recoverable.  Defendants
may recover fees Defendants incurred for the presence of a court reporter.  Court reporter fees are recoverable as
otherwise provided by law.  (Code Civ.
Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (a)(11).)  Government Code section 68086 permits a
prevailing party to recover costs “for the presence of a certified shorthand
reporter[.]”  (Gov. Code, § 68086, subd.
(d)(2).)  Defendants’ evidence in
opposition shows that Defendants incurred $650 per appearance for 18
appearances by the court reporter. 
(Declaration of Paul A. de Lorimier, Exhibit F.)  As such, Defendants may recover $11,700 in
court reporter fees.  The Court grants
the motion to tax the remaining $5,447.49 in costs associated with the court
reporter.

 

Copies

Defendants
seek to recover $1,058 for copies. 
“Models and enlargements of exhibits and photocopies of exhibits may be
allowed if they were reasonably helpful to aid the trier of fact.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd.
(a)(13).)  Defendants cannot recover for
copies other than for models or exhibits at trial.  Defendants advance evidence that the costs
they seek to recover were for the preparation of trial binders.  (Declaration of Paul A. de Lorimier, ¶ 4,
Exhibit D.)  Accordingly, these costs are
recoverable. 

 

Jury
fees

Defendant
seeks to recover jury fees in the amount of $150.  The parties waived jury, and tried this case
to the Court.  As such, Defendants bear
the burden to show these costs are recoverable.  In opposition, Defendants argue that
Defendants posted the jury fees, as the parties initially sought jury
trial.  However, as the case did not
proceed to trial by jury, Defendants should have sought a refund of the jury
fees.  (See Code Civ Proc., §
631.3,)  The motion to tax these costs is
granted.

 

Deposition
costs

Defendants
seek to recover $4,431.70 in deposition costs. 
Defendants may recover the costs of deposition transcripts.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd.
(a)(3).)  However, Plaintiff advances
evidence that Defendants did not order a copy of every deposition transcript in
this case, but rather shared transcripts with co-Defendants.  (Declaration of Michael C. Lieb, ¶ 2.)  As such, Plaintiff meets his burden to show
that the costs Defendants seek do not reflect the costs Defendants incurred for
transcripts.  In opposition, Defendants
advance evidence that Defendants seek to recover only for copies Defendants
obtained.  (Declaration of Paul A. de
Lorimier, Exhibit C.)  Defendants’
evidence therefore shows that these costs are recoverable.  The motion to tax these costs is denied.

 

Accordingly,
Plaintiff’s motion to tax costs is GRANTED in part.

party to give notice.

IS SO ORDERED.

Case Number: 23STCV26607    Hearing Date: September 17, 2026    Dept: 415

Judge Rolf M. Treu, Department 415

 

 

HEARING DATE:        September 17, 2026                             TRIAL DATE:  Not set.

                                                          

CASE:                          Kendrick v. Moses

 

CASE NO.:                   23STCV26607

 

           

 

DEMURRER TO CROSS-COMPLAINT

MOTION TO STRIKE PORTIONS OF CROSS-COMPLAINT

 

 

MOVING PARTY/PARTIES: Plaintiff and Cross-Defendant Jonathan Kendrick

 

RESPONDING PARTY/PARTIES:   Defendant and Cross-Complainant Jeff Moses

 

PROOF OF SERVICE:

·        Defendant opposes the motions on the merits, and thereby waived any procedural objections.  (See In re Marriage of Falcone (2008) 164 Cal.App.4th 814, 826.)

 

CASE HISTORY:

·        October 30, 2023: Complaint filed.

·        November 19, 2024: Defendant’s default entered.

·        April 16, 2026: Defendant’s motion to set aside default granted.

·        May 8, 2026: Defendant’s answer and cross-complaint filed.

 

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

            Plaintiff and Cross-Defendant Jonathan Kendrick (“Plaintiff”) sued Defendant and Cross-Complainant Jeff Moses (“Defendant”) for defamation.  Defendant cross-complained against Plaintiff because Defendant contends Defendant has an unsatisfied judgment against Plaintiff from a prior action and Plaintiff has engaged in fraudulent transfers to avoid satisfaction thereof.  Plaintiff demurs to the cross-complaint.  Defendant opposes the motions.

            The Court notes that the caption of the motions list Plaintiff as the moving party, and counsel identify themselves as counsel for Plaintiff only.  Accordingly, the Court construes the motion as by Plaintiff, notwithstanding references to other Cross-Defendants. (See Code Civ. Proc, § 128.7.)

 

TENTATIVE RULING:

Plaintiff’s demurrer to the fourth and sixth causes of action in the cross-complaint is SUSTAINED with leave to amend.  Plaintiff’s demurrer to the fifth cause of action is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.

Plaintiff’s motion to strike punitive damages allegations from Plaintiff’s complaint is GRANTED with leave to amend.  The motion to strike is otherwise DENIED.

Moving party to give notice. 

 

DISCUSSION:

 

Demurrer to cross-complaint

“It is black letter law that a demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the allegations in a complaint.”  (Lewis v. Safeway, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 385, 388.)  In ruling on a demurrer, the court must “liberally construe[]” the allegations of the complaint.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 452.)  “This rule of liberal construction means that the reviewing court draws inferences favorable to the plaintiff, not the defendant.”  (Perez v. Golden Empire Transit Dist. (2012) 209 Cal.App.4th 1228, 1238.)

Request for judicial notice

            Plaintiff requests judicial notice of court filings.  Per Evidence Code section 452, subdivision (d), the Court takes judicial notice of the existence of these court filings.  (Evid. Code, § 452, subd. (d).)  The Court cannot take judicial notice of the truth of the contents thereof, however.

Abatement

Plaintiff contends the Court should abate the cross-complaint pending resolution of Rokit Drinks Limited, et al. v. Moses (Super. Ct. L.A. County, 2026, No. 26STCV15172).  “‘Res judicata’ describes the preclusive effect of a final judgment on the merits. Res judicata, or claim preclusion, prevents relitigation of the same cause of action in a second suit between the same parties or parties in privity with them. Collateral estoppel, or issue preclusion, ‘precludes relitigation of issues argued and decided in prior proceedings.’ [Citation] Under the doctrine of res judicata, if a plaintiff prevails in an action, the cause is merged into the judgment and may not be asserted in a subsequent lawsuit; a judgment for the defendant serves as a bar to further litigation of the same cause of action.”  (Mycogen Corp. v. Monsanto Co. (2002) 28 Cal.4th 888, 896-897.)  “Res judicata precludes piecemeal litigation by splitting a single cause of action or relitigation of the same cause of action on a different legal theory or for different relief. . . . California's res judicata doctrine is based upon the primary right theory. The primary right theory is a theory of code pleading that has long been followed in California. It provides that a cause of action is comprised of a primary right of the plaintiff, a corresponding primary duty of the defendant, and a wrongful act by the defendant constituting a breach of that duty. The most salient characteristic of a primary right is that it is indivisible: the violation of a single primary right gives rise to but a single cause of action. As far as its content is concerned, the primary right is simply the plaintiff's right to be free from the particular injury suffered. . . . The primary right theory has a fairly narrow field of application. It is invoked most often when a plaintiff attempts to divide a primary right and enforce it in two suits. The theory prevents this result by either of two means: (1) if the first suit is still pending when the second is filed, the defendant in the second suit may plead that fact in abatement or (2) if the first suit has terminated in a judgment on the merits adverse to the plaintiff, the defendant in the second suit may set up that judgment as a bar under the principles of res judicata.” (Lincoln Property Co., N.C., Inc. v. Travelers Indem. Co. (2006) 137 Cal.App.4th 905, 912, internal citations and quotations omitted.)

Here, Rokit Drinks Limited, et al. v. Moses (Super. Ct. L.A. County, 2026, No. 26STCV15172) is pending and was filed after the cross-complaint in this action.  As such, Plaintiff must raise the request for abatement in Rokit Drinks Limited, et al. v. Moses (Super. Ct. L.A. County, 2026, No. 26STCV15172), not this case.  Furthermore, if Plaintiff contends that this case and Rokit Drinks Limited, et al. v. Moses (Super. Ct. L.A. County, 2026, No. 26STCV15172) are based on the same cause of action, Plaintiff must file notices of related cases in this case and in Rokit Drinks Limited, et al. v. Moses (Super. Ct. L.A. County, 2026, No. 26STCV15172), which Plaintiff has not done.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.300, subds. (b), (f).) 

 Fourth and sixth causes of action: civil theft and conversion

            Defendant’s fourth cause of action is for civil theft and Defendant’s sixth cause of action is for conversion.  The elements of a cause of action for civil theft or conversion are: (1) Defendant’s ownership of or right to possession of tangible personal property at the time of conversion; (2) Plaintiff’s conversion, which consists of wrongful taking or disposition; and (3) damages.  (Taylor v. S & M Lamp Co. (1961) 190 Cal.App.2d 700, 705.).    Here, Defendant alleges that Plaintiff “has received, concealed, or withheld property of, or proceeds traceable to, [Defendant] that was ‘obtained in any manner constituting theft’ within the meaning of Penal Code sections 484 and 496, knowing such property to have been so obtained.”  (Cross-Complaint, ¶ 89.)  While Defendant’s allegations are not particularly clear, it seems Defendant alleges that Plaintiff benefitted from fraudulent transfers of funds that should have satisfied Defendant’s judgment.  (See, e.g., Cross-Complaint, ¶ 90.)  Defendant does not have ownership of funds it hopes to use to satisfy a judgment.  “[A] certain probability that an outcome . . . may materialize” is not a property interest that could support a claim for civil theft.  (Heller Ehrman LLP v. Davis Wright Tremaine LLP (2018) 4 Cal.5th 467, 478.)  Accordingly, the Court sustains the demurrer to the fourth and sixth causes of action with leave to amend.

Fifth cause of action: conspiracy to commit fraudulent transfer and civil theft

Defendant’s fifth cause of action is for conspiracy to commit fraudulent transfer and civil theft.  This is not a recognized cause of action.  (Favila v. Katten Muchin Rosenman LLP (2010) 188 Cal.App.4th 189, 206; see also Patrick v. Alacer (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 995, 1016, fn.11.)  As such, the Court sustains the demurrer to the fifth cause of action without leave to amend.

Motion to strike portions of cross-complaint

Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading, may serve and file a motion to strike the whole pleading or any part thereof.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 435, subd. (b)(1); Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1322, subd. (b).)  On a motion to strike, the Court may: (1) strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or (2) strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of California, a court rule, or an order of the Court.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a)-(b); Stafford v. Shultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782.)

Plaintiff moves to strike the punitive damages allegations in the cross-complaint.  In ruling on a motion to strike punitive damages, “judges read allegations of a pleading subject to a motion to strike as a whole, all parts in their context, and assume their truth.” (Clauson v. Superior Court (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1253, 1255.)  To state a prima facie claim for punitive damages, a plaintiff must allege the elements set forth in the punitive damages statute, Civil Code section 3294.  (Coll. Hosp., Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 721.)  Per Civil Code section 3294, a plaintiff must allege that the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud or malice.  (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (a).)  “Malice is defined in the statute as conduct intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.”  (Coll. Hosp., Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 725.)  “The mere allegation an intentional tort was committed is not sufficient to warrant an award of punitive damages. Not only must there be circumstances of oppression, fraud or malice, but facts must be alleged in the pleading to support such a claim.”  (Grieves v. Superior Ct. (1984) 157 Cal.App.3d 159, 166, internal citations and footnotes omitted.)  In support of the claim for punitive damages, Defendant alleges that Plaintiff’s “conduct as alleged herein was intentional, oppressive, fraudulent, and malicious within the meaning of Civil Code section 3294(c).”  (See, e.g., Cross-Complaint, ¶ 73.)  These bare conclusions are not allegations of specific facts that could support an award of punitive damages.  Accordingly, the motion to strike the punitive damages allegations is granted with leave to amend.

Plaintiff also moves to strike allegations Plaintiff contends are settlement communications and improper legal conclusions.  A motion to strike cannot be used as a “‘line item veto’ for the civil defendant . . . .” (PH II, Inc. v. Superior Court (1995) 33 Cal.App.4th 1680, 1683.)  Plaintiff is not entitled to remove any allegation Plaintiff contends is superfluous.  The motion to strike these allegations is without merit.

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s demurrer to the fourth and sixth causes of action in the cross-complaint is SUSTAINED with leave to amend.  Plaintiff’s demurrer to the fifth cause of action is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.

Plaintiff’s motion to strike punitive damages allegations from the crosss complaint is GRANTED with leave to amend.  The motion to strike is otherwise DENIED.

Defendant is to file an amended cross-complaint within 10 days.

Moving party to give notice.

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

            Plaintiffs Zoe Walsh and Isabel Osgood-Roach (“Plaintiffs”) move for entry of a proposed case management order in this matter. 

 

TENTATIVE RULING:

Plaintiffs’ motion for entry of case management order is DENIED.

Moving party to give notice. 

 

DISCUSSION:

As authority for their motion, Plaintiffs cite section 19 of the Standards of Judicial Administration Appendix to the California Rules of Court.  Section 19 no longer exists, and is now Standard 3.10, which states, in pertinent part, “In complex litigation, judicial management should begin early and be applied continuously and actively, based on knowledge of the circumstances of each case.”  (Cal. Rules of Court, Standards of Judicial Administration, Standard 3.10, subd. (a).)  This case was deemed non-complex.  Accordingly, Standard 3.10 is inapplicable.  As Plaintiffs fail to cite authority in their notice of motion under which the Court could grant the relief Plaintiffs seek, the Court cannot grant the motion.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 1010; Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1110, subd. (a); People v. American Sur. Ins. Co. (1999) 75 Cal.App.4th 719, 726.)

The Court notes that, at the April 29, 2026 hearing in this matter, all counsel present stipulated to the proposed case management order.  (April 29, 2026 Minute Order.)  Should all parties stipulate to the proposed case management order, the Court will enter the order as stipulated.

Accordingly, Plaintiffs’ motion for entry of case management order is DENIED.
