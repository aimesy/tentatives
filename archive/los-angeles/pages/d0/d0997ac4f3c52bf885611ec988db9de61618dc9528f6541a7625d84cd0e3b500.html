DEPARTMENT 529 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 25STCV03772    Hearing Date: September 18, 2026    Dept: 529

VALINDA BROWN VS JESSE HOWARD

 

DEFENDANTS’ MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT

 

Date of Hearing:        September 18, 2026                          Trial Date:       February 1, 2027

Department:              529                                                      Case No.:        25STCV03772

 

Moving Party:            Defendants Jesse Howard and Jesse L Howard as Trustee of the Howard Jesse Living Trust aka Jesse Howard Living Trust  

Responding Party:     Plaintiff Valinda Brown

 

BACKGROUND

 

On February 11, 2025, Plaintiff Valinda Brown filed a complaint against Defendant Jesse Howard for premises liability. Plaintiff alleges Defendant negligently maintained their property, causing Plaintiff to slip and fall while walking down the front entry stairs, leading to Plaintiff’s injuries. Plaintiff amended the complaint to add Defendant Jesse L. Howard, as Trustee of the Howard Jesse Living Trust aka Jesse Howard Living Trust.

 

[TENTATIVE] RULING

 

Defendants Jesse Howard and Jesse L Howard as Trustee of the Howard Jesse Living Trust aka Jesse Howard Living Trust’s Motion for Summary Judgment or, in the alternative, Summary Adjudication is GRANTED IN PART.

 

EVIDENTIARY OBJECTIONS

 

Plaintiff Valinda Brown requests this court disregard and strike the Supplemental Declaration of Arthur E. Pop, P.E., and the Supplemental Declaration of Jesse Howard submitted in Reply as both present new evidentiary matter and additional material facts that were not presented in Defendants' moving papers or Plaintiffs opposition papers.

 

The court will disregard any new arguments made in reply.

 

DISCUSSION

 

Defendants Jesse Howard and Jesse L Howard as Trustee of the Howard Jesse Living Trust aka Jesse Howard Living Trust move this court for an order granting summary judgment in their favor and against Plaintiff Valinda Brown, or in the alternative, summary adjudication. Defendants make the motion on the grounds that there are no triable issues of material fact with respect to Plaintiff’s sole cause of action for premises liability, including Count One for negligence and Count Two for willful failure to warn under Civil Code section 846.

 

“[T]he party moving for summary judgment bears the burden of persuasion that there is no triable issue of material fact and that he is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850, fn. omitted.) “Once the [movant] has met that burden, the burden shifts to the [other party] to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to that cause of action . . . .” (CCP § 437c(p)(2); see Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at 850.) The party opposing summary judgment “may not rely upon the mere allegations or denials of its pleadings,” but rather “shall set forth the specific facts showing that a triable issue of material fact exists . . . .” (CCP § 437c(p)(2).) A triable issue of material fact exists where “the evidence would allow a reasonable trier of fact to find the underlying fact in favor of the party opposing the motion in accordance with the applicable standard of proof.” (Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at 850.)¿

 

Defendant argues Plaintiff’s claim for premises liability fails for several reasons including (i) no dangerous condition existed; (ii) even if one had existed, Defendants had no notice of it; (iii) Plaintiff cannot show that any condition of the Property caused her fall; and (iv) any count for willful failure to warn under Civil Code § 846 is unsupported. In a claim for premises liability, the elements are the same as those for negligence. (Castellon v. U.S. Bancorp¿(2013) 220 Cal.App.4th 994, citing¿Ortega v. Kmart Corp.¿(2001) 26 Cal.4th 1200, 1205.) Accordingly, the plaintiff must prove, “‘a legal duty to use due care, a breach of such legal duty, and the breach as the proximate or legal cause of the resulting injury.’ ” (Beacon Residential Community Assn. v. Skidmore, Owings & Merrill LLP¿(2014) 59 Cal.4th 568, 573, quoting¿United States Liab. Ins. Co. v. Haidinger-Hayes, Inc.¿(1970) 1 Cal.3d 586, 594.) 

 

Dangerous Condition

 

Defendants argue Plaintiff cannot come forward with substantial evidence – or any evidence – that the front steps posed a dangerous condition. Defendants present the testimony of engineer, Arthur E. Pop, P.E., to support their conclusion that the stairway complied with applicable code requirements for an existing residential stairway. Mr. Pop attests that the front stairway consists of two concrete risers, with tread depths of approximately 12 inches and the riser heights ranged from approximately 7 7/16 inches to 7 5/8 inches, with variation within applicable code tolerances. (Pop Decl. ¶7, Exh. I.) Because the stairway had only two risers, no handrail was required under the California Residential Code, which requires handrails only where a stairway has four or more risers. (Pop Decl. ¶9, Exh. I.) Defendants further argue the condition was also open, obvious, and thoroughly familiar to Plaintiff as she had visited the property many times over the course of a decade and knew that the steps had no handrail. (UMF 6-9.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues Defendants’ premise that the staircase consists of only two uniform risers is squarely in dispute. Plaintiff has also retained an expert, Eris J. Barillas, a forensic expert in investigating premises liability. Barillas attests she found three risers – not two – upon inspection and found that the second step is approximately 24 inches wider than the first, creating exposed left and right areas that operate as a substantially different and unguarded elevation transition with a 15-inch drop. (Barillas Decl. ¶¶6, 8.) Barillas also states that as a result of this condition, the steps were non-uniform and created an unexpected walking surface and the camouflaged stair configuration created a dangerous condition. (Barillas Decl. ¶7, 9.) This evidence directly contradicts Defendants’ position.

 

The court finds a triable issue of material fact exists as to whether a dangerous condition existed. Both parties agree that a handrail may not have been required based solely on the number of risers – and the court will grant motion for summary adjudication as to Issue 2 – but there is a triable issue whether the nonuniform side configuration, visual camouflage, or lateral drop off created a substantial risk of injury. The court notes Plaintiff’s testimony that she did not know what caused her fall. (UMF 12, 15.) However, the court finds Plaintiff's deposition testimony that she did not know what caused her fall is an expression of uncertainty, not an affirmative denial that the drop-off was the cause. (See Taylor v. Financial Casualty & Surety, Inc. (2021) 67 Cal.App.5th 966, 984, holding a deponent's testimony that he did not know whether a company held a valid California bail agent license did not constitute a clear and unequivocal admission contradicting his later declaration that the company was in the business of posting bail bonds.)

 

Notice

 

Defendants next argue even if a dangerous condition existed, Defendants had no notice. Liability for a dangerous condition on the premises requires proof that the defendant had either actual notice (express information of a fact) or constructive notice (knowledge imputed by law). (See Civ. Code §18.)

 

Defendants maintain throughout Defendant Howard's ownership of the Property, he never received a single complaint about the front steps — not from the tenant, not from Plaintiff, and not from anyone else. (Howard Decl. ¶¶6-7.) Defendants also submit the declaration of tenant Haynes to support this contention. (Haynes Decl. ¶¶5-6.) Moreover, Defendants maintain because the property was subject to annual governmental housing-quality inspections – and passed the most recent inspection – Plaintiff cannot charge Defendants with constructive notice of a stair hazard that a trained government inspector, evaluating that very category, twice certified as passing. (See Foreman Decl. ¶9, Exh. F.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiffs submit their own declaration by tenant Haynes who testifies she did complain to Defendant Howard about the steps before Plaintiff’s accident. She also testifies the declaration submitted by Defendant was not submitted by her. The court notes Defendant does not address this contention head on. Rather, Defendant argues the new Haynes declaration does not establish causation or notice. The court cannot resolve credibility disputes on summary judgment under Code of Civil Procedure section 437c(e). As for constructive notice, Plaintiff argues the permanent nature of the condition independently supports constructive notice and the ‘Pass’ finding by HUD/LACDA does not demonstrate the inspection did not detect the hazardous nonuniform side configuration. 

 

The court finds a triable issue of material fact. Tenant Haynes declaration that she also fell on the same steps is sufficient to show “substantially same circumstances.” (See Elsworth v. Beech Aircraft Corp. (1984) 37 Cal.3d 540, 555.)

 

Causation

 

Defendants argue they did not cause Plaintiff’s injuries. California applies the “substantial factor” test for determining factual causation in premises liability cases. (See Mayes v. Bryan (2006) 139 Cal.App.4th 1075, 1092.) Defendants maintain Plaintiff has testified she observed no substance on the step, no liquid or oil on the artificial turf, and no bunching or looseness in the artificial turf; the weather was nice/sunny with no rain; she had no idea what caused her to fall. (UMF 11-15.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues Kaney v. Custance (2022) 74 Cal.App.5th 201, forecloses Defendants’ causation argument. In Kaney, the court held a “slip-and-fall plaintiff need not remember her fall to recover damages provided the evidence gives rise to a reasonable and probable inference that the defendant's negligence was a substantial contributing factor.” (Kaney, supra, 74 Cal.App.5th at p. 217.) In the instant matter, Plaintiff contends she has provided that she reached her foot down from the first step, lost balance, fell leftward, and landed on the adjacent lower concrete area. (Brown Decl. ¶4.) Because her declaration supports a reasonable inference that the nonuniform side drop-off caused her call, the court finds a triable issue of material fact. (See Exh. I, SROG 7.)

 

Civil Code §846

 

Defendants argue to the extent Plaintiff's premises liability cause of action includes a count for willful failure to warn under Civil Code section 846, that theory has no application to these facts.

 

California Civil Code section 846 grants landowners broad immunity from liability for injuries to persons using their property for recreational purposes, with specific exceptions. Defendants contend Plaintiff was a social guest visiting the tenant at a private residence — she was not engaged in any recreational use of the land within the meaning of the statute. (UMF 53.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff contends they are not relying upon Civil Code section 846 to establish liability.

 

Accordingly, the motion for summary adjudication as to Issue 4 is GRANTED.


Case Number: 26STCV06468    Hearing Date: September 18, 2026    Dept: 529

MICHAEL BIRKENHEIR VS WHYTE AND MACKAY AMERICAS LTD, ET AL.

 

DEFENDANTS’ DEMURRER TO THE COMPLAINT

 

Date of Hearing:        September 18, 2026                         Trial Date:       None set.

Department:              529                                                     Case No.:        26STCV06468

 

Moving Party:            Defendants Whyte and Mackay Americas LTD

Responding Party:     No opposition

Meet and Confer:      Yes. (Fields Decl.)

 

BACKGROUND

 

On February 27, 2026, Plaintiff Michael Birkenheir filed a complaint against Defendants Whyte and Mackay Americas LTD, Andrew Kulhem Whyte, and Craig Bridger for (1) Whistleblower Retaliation in Violation of Cal. Lab. Code § 1102.5; (2) Wrongful Retaliation in Violation of Public Policy; (3) Retaliation in Violation of Cal. Lab. Code §98.6; (4) Failure to Pay all Wages Due in Violation of Cal. Lab. Code §204 and §218; and (5) Waiting Time Penalties in Violation of Cal. Lab. Code §203.

 

[Tentative] Ruling

 

Defendants Whyte and Mackay Americas LTD’s Demurrer to the Complaint is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

DISCUSSION

 

Defendants Whyte and Mackay Americas LTD demur to the complaint of Plaintiff Michael Birkenheir on the grounds Plaintiff’s fourth cause of action for failure to pay all wages due to violation of Labor Code §§204 and 218 fail to allege whether the alleged bonus was written, oral or implied by contract.

 

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context. In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) “A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed. (CCP §§ 430.30, 430.70.) At the pleading stage, a plaintiff need only allege ultimate facts sufficient to apprise the defendant of the factual basis for the claim against him. (Semole v. Sansoucie (1972) 28 Cal. App. 3d 714, 721.) A “demurrer does not, however, admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law alleged in the pleading, or the construction of instruments pleaded, or facts impossible in law.” (S. Shore Land Co. v. Petersen (1964) 226 Cal.App.2d 725, 732 (internal citations omitted).) 

 

Defendant argues the fourth cause of action alleges unpaid wages in the form of a bonus. However, it cannot be ascertained from the complaint whether the contract for Plaintiff’s alleged unpaid bonus is written, oral, or implied by conduct.

 

The court finds the cause of action is unclear as to whether the bonus claim rests on express promises, contractual establishment, or implied contract based on representations. (See Compl. ¶54.) While a statutory claim for unpaid wages does not typically require Plaintiff to allege whether the underlying agreement was oral, written, or implied by conduct, the court finds ambiguity in the allegations.

 

Based on the foregoing, the demurrer is SUSTAINED WITH 20 DAYS LEAVE TO AMEND.
