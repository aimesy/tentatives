DEPARTMENT 529 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 24STCV01236    Hearing Date: September 16, 2026    Dept: 529

KIDS EMPIRE ONTARIO, LLC VS DILLWAY ASSOCIATES, L.P., ET AL.

 

PLAINTIFF’S MOTION FOR AN ORDER QUASHING OR MODIFYING THIRD-PARTY SUBPOENA ISSUED TO KIDDER MATHEWS OF CALIFORNIA, INC. AND REQUEST FOR SANCTIONS

 

Date of Hearing:        September 16, 2026                          Trial Date:       January 25, 2027

Department:              529                                                      Case No.:        24STCV01236

 

Moving Party:            Plaintiff Kids Empire Ontario, LLC

Responding Party:     Defendants Dillway Associates, L.P. and Reliable Properties

 

BACKGROUND

 

This action arises from alleged fraud concerning a lease agreement which was entered into on February 23, 2018. On January 17, 2024, Plaintiff Kids Empire Ontario, LLC (“Plaintiff”) filed a Complaint against Defendants Dillway Associates, L.P. (“Dillway”), Reliable Properties (“Reliable”) (collectively “Defendants”) and DOES 1 through 10, inclusive, alleging causes of action for: (1) Fraud; (2) Rescission (Mistake); and (3) Declaratory Relief.

 

Plaintiff alleges that David Gold (“Gold”) of Defendant Reliable conducted the lease negotiations on behalf of Defendants. (Complaint, ¶¶ 7-8; Exh. 1.) Pursuant to the lease, Plaintiff is required to pay rent and its share of common area maintenance (“CAM”) expenses. (Complaint, ¶ 9.) In the negotiations that preceded the execution of the lease, Gold represented that Plaintiff’s estimated monthly CAM expense obligation would be $5,040, and Defendants restated and confirmed this estimate in the lease. (Complaint, ¶ 9.) Defendants used this CAM estimate to induce Plaintiff to enter the lease and Plaintiff later discovered it was false. (Complaint, ¶ 10.) Plaintiff further alleges that it commenced its occupancy and operations in April 2021 and, thereafter, Defendants informed Plaintiff the monthly CAM charge was $8,367.37, substantially higher than Defendants’ estimate of $5,040. (Complaint, ¶ 11.) Plaintiffs sought an explanation from Defendants. (Complaint, ¶ 12.) Plaintiff alleges that Defendants’ representation as to the amount of CAM expenses was false. (Complaint, ¶ 16.)

 

On November 18, 2024, Dillway Associates filed a First Amended Cross-Complaint against Kids Empire Ontario, LLC, Haim Abraham Elbaz and Blandine Marie Clair for breach of contract, accounting, breach of guaranty and declaratory relief.

 

On May 17, 2024, Plaintiff filed and served a Notice of Related Case indicating that the instant action is related to Kids Empire Pomona, LLC v. Royal Oak, L.P., et al., LASC Case No. 24STCV01254 (the “2d Action”), which was filed on January 17, 2024 at 2:07 PM and is pending in Department 40 of this Court. The 2d Action was filed after the instant action.  Judge Recana denied the request to relate the two actions.

 

[TENTATIVE] RULING

 

Plaintiff’s Motion for an Order Quashing or Modifying Third-Party Subpoena Issued to Kidder Mathews of California, Inc. and Request for Sanctions is GRANTED IN PART.

 

DISCUSSION

 

Plaintiff Kids Empire Ontario, LLC move, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 19871, for an order quashing the subpoena issued by Defendants to third-party Kidder Mathews of California, Inc., or directing compliance with the subpoena in a manner that will ensure documents protected by the attorney-client privilege or work product doctrine are not produced to Defendants.

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 1987.1 states, “When a subpoena requires the attendance of a witness or the production of [documents], the court, upon motion reasonably made by the party, the witness or any consumer described in Section 1985.3…may make an order quashing the subpoena entirely, modifying it, or directing compliance with it upon such terms or conditions as the court shall declare, including protective orders.” “The court may make any other order as may be appropriate to protect the parties, the witness, or the consumer from unreasonable or oppressive demands including unreasonable violations of a witness’s or consumer’s right of privacy.” (Ibid.)¿¿ 

 

Plaintiff argues many of the 22 document requests encompass attorney-client privilege and/or attorney work product. As a result, Kids Empire initially proposed to Defense Counsel that: (1) Kidder Mathews produce all the responsive documents it locates to him so that he could screen them for privilege before they were produced to Defendants’ counsel; and (2) if he identified any documents that he contends are privileged or protected by the work product doctrine, he would identify those documents on a privilege log. (Hayes Decl., ¶¶ 3-5, Exhs. 2-4.) However, Defense Counsel rejected the offer. As a result, Plaintiff brings the motion for an order quashing the Subpoena or modifying it such that its counsel may screen Kidder Mathews’ document production for documents covered by the attorney-client privilege or attorney work product doctrine before they are produced to Defendants.

 

In opposition, Defendants Dillway Associates, L.P and Reliable Properties argue the motion to quash should either be denied entirely for its lack of merit, or as moot, as counsel for Plaintiff has already unilaterally conducted a “first look” review of all of the documents. Defendants contend they even offered to have a deposition officer isolate any emails that included Plaintiffs’ counsel and then have Plaintiff’s counsel have a first look at those emails but Plaintiff never agreed to that compromise. Moreover, Defendants argue the “first look” is an overreach and request that for each document withheld on the basis of privilege, a detailed privilege log must be provided which states: (1) The Bates number(s); (2) the date; (3) the author/sender; (4) all recipients (both primary and copied); (5) the privilege claimed; and (6) a concise summary explaining the nature and context of the document sufficient to prove that the privilege actually applies.

 

The court modifies the subpoena. Plaintiff may have a privilege claim in these documents and the court agrees a First Look would help determine what, if any, document contains privileged information. Plaintiff must identify each document being withheld in a privilege log. Defendants may move to compel if warranted.

 

Based on the foregoing, Plaintiff’s Motion for an Order Quashing or Modifying Third-Party Subpoena Issued to Kidder Mathews of California, Inc. is GRANTED in part.

 

Sanctions

 

Plaintiff seeks sanctions in the amount of $1,375.00 against Defendants on the grounds this motion was avoidable. Plaintiff contends Defendants forced Plaintiff to litigate this issue rather than accept the First Look proposal.

 

In opposition, Defendants argue they could not blindly agree to allow Plaintiff’s counsel to hijack Kidder Mtahews’ entire production and unilaterally decide which documents would be produced.

 

The court denies the request for sanctions. The court finds Defendants acted with substantial justification in opposing the motion.


Case Number: 26STCV15225    Hearing Date: September 16, 2026    Dept: 529

JANE DOE 1 VS DOC JOHNSON ENTERPRISES, ET AL.

 

DEFENDANTSs DEMURRER TO THE COMPLAINT

 

Date of Hearing:        September 16, 2026                         Trial Date:       None set.

Department:              529                                                     Case No.:        26STCV15225

 

Moving Party:            Defendants Doc Johnson Enterprises, Health Devices Corporation, Scott Watkins, Chad Braverman and Ronald Braverman

Responding Party:     Plaintiff Jane Doe

Meet and Confer:      Yes.  

 

BACKGROUND

 

On May 12, 2026, Plaintiff Jane Doe 1 filed a complaint against Doc Johnson Enterprises, Health Devices Corporation, Scott Watkins, Chad Braverman and Ronald Braverman for (1) Breach of Contract; (2) Breach of Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing; (3) Intentional Misrepresentation; (4) Negligent Misrepresentation; (5) Violation of Civil Code §3344; (6) Equitable Accounting; (7) Negligence based on Failure to Protect Plaintiff from Sexual Harassment and Invasive Misconduct; (8) Negligent Hiring, Supervision, or Retention; (9) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress; (10) Violation of Civil Code §51.9; (11) Violation of Business & Professions Code §17200 et seq.

 

[Tentative] Ruling

 

Defendants Doc Johnson Enterprises, Health Devices Corporation, Scott Watkins, Chad Braverman and Ronald Braverman’s Demurrer to the Complaint is SUSTAINED IN PART; Defendants Doc Johnson Enterprises, Health Devices Corporation, Scott Watkins, Chad Braverman and Ronald Braverman’s Motion to Strike is DENIED.

 

DISCUSSION

 

Defendants Doc Johnson Enterprises, Health Devices Corporation, Scott Watkins, Chad Braverman and Ronald Braverman demur to the complaint on the grounds that the challenged causes of action fail to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action and are uncertain.

 

First Cause of Action

 

Defendants demur the first cause of action for breach of contract against Defendants Doc Johnson and Health Devices on the grounds that the alleged breaches are contradicted by the terms of the written agreement attached to the complaint as Exhibit A and impermissibly seek to impose obligations that are not contained therein.

 

Defendants argue the Agreement contains no first-quarter-2023 launch date, affiliate and direct-to-consumer “infrastructure” and promotional support. Rather, the asserted promise appears only as an alleged oral statement at a pre-contract pitch meeting. (Compl., ¶¶ 22–23.) Because the parole evidence rule bars prior or contemporaneous extrinsic promises, the alleged breach is unenforceable. Moreover, the affiliate, direct-to-consumer, and promotional-support theories are contradicted by the Agreement’s express terms. The Agreement provides that the company “may, but is not required to[,] promote or advertise other than on its existing and ordinary marketing channels,” and reserves to the company the right to sell Plaintiff’s product “exclusively . . . online on Doc Johnson owned websites.” (Compl., Ex. A, Agreement’s Ex. A, ¶¶ 13, 19.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues Defendants were obliged to comply with their marketing duties, at least through the ordinary marketing channels and Plaintiff alleges that timing of such performance was due in the first quarter of 2023. Moreover, the complaint alleges far more than that, including the failure to pay royalties, failure to provide required royalty statements and sales reporting, continued sales following the alleged contractual reversion of Plaintiffs intellectual-property rights, specific pre-contract representations made during the solicitation process, and circumstances allegedly demonstrating that Defendants never intended to provide the economic benefits used to induce Plaintiff into the transaction. Even assuming solely for purposes of argument that Plaintiff cannot enforce the alleged Ql 2023 launch representation as an express contractual term, the First Cause of Action independently alleges Defendants' failure to provide required royalty statements and sales information and failure to pay royalties due.

 

The court finds the allegations sufficient for the purposes of a demurrer.  A demurrer tests only whether the properly pleaded facts state a cause of action under any legal theory. So while the court may disregard the alleged Q1 launch representations and building of affiliate and direct-to-consumer infrastructure as an express contractual term, the cause of action still alleges Defendants failed to timely pay royalties as well as provide promised promotional support and similar theories which is expressly contracted for in the Agreement. (See Compl., Exh. A p. 3, 14.) At this stage, the court must take the allegations as true and whether that such promotion was required only where “the cost for acquisition remains profitable and scalable” is not the test for a demurrer.

 

Based on the foregoing, the demurrer to the first cause of action is OVERRULED.

 

Third and Fourth Causes of Action

 

Defendants demur to the third cause of action for intentional misrepresentation and fourth cause of action for negligent misrepresentation on the grounds the alleged misrepresentations are not pleaded with the specificity required of a fraud claim and it rests on alleged promises of future conduct, which are not actionable as negligent misrepresentation.

 

The elements of fraud are: “(a) misrepresentation (false representation, concealment, or nondisclosure); (b) knowledge of falsity (or ‘scienter’); (c) intent to defraud, i.e., to induce reliance; (d) justifiable reliance; and (e) resulting damage.” (Charnay v. Cobert (2006) 145 Cal.App.4th 170, 184.) Negligent misrepresentation requires the defendant to make false statements believing them to be true, but without reasonable ground for such belief. (See Bily v. Arthur Young & Co. (1992) 3 Cal.4th 370, 407.) “To be actionable, a negligent misrepresentation must ordinarily be as to past or existing material facts.” (Tarmann v. State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co. (1991) 2 Cal.App.4th 153, 158.) Statements as to future action are do not constitute actionable fraud. (Id.)  

 

Defendants argue the complaint does not meet the heightened standard of pleading for fraud claims. Specifically, no specific statement, made at a specified time and place, is attributed to Watkins, Chad Braverman, or Ronald Braverman individually. Moreover, the intentional-misrepresentation claim independently fails because it is, in substance, a promissory-fraud claim that does not plead the requisite fraudulent intent. Lastly, the negligent-misrepresentation claims on the independent reason that it rests entirely on promises of future conduct. Because every representation underlying this claim is a promise of future performance, it fails as a matter of law.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues the complaint is alleged with the requisite specificity. Moreover, Plaintiff does not merely allege that Defendants promised performance and subsequently failed to perform. Rather, she alleges that the representations were false when made and were made without a present intention of fully performing. (Compl. ¶¶34, 44.)

 

The court agrees both fraud claims are not alleged with the requisite specificity. As noted by Defendants, the only speaker identified with any particularity remains Remington Franklin (a non-party consultant) at a precontract meeting. However, the complaint does not allege his authority to speak for Doc Johnson Enterprises. The complaint does not allege that Health Devices separately participated in the solicitation or pitch meeting, does not identify any Health Devices speaker, and does not provide a basis for Franklin's authority to speak for Health Devices. As for the individuals, the complaint attributes misrepresentations collectively to Watkins, Braverman, Ronald Braverman, and their agents. This is insufficient for a fraud cause of action. Similarly, the complaint does not allege the requisite intent with specificity. Plaintiff merely alleges the Defendants made the representations without any intention of fully performing. (Compl. ¶¶44.) Lastly, in regards to future performance, the court finds Plaintiff has not sufficiently alleged claim for negligent misrepresentation as there is no recognized cause of action for a negligent misrepresentation based on a false promise. (See Hooked Media Group, Inc. v. Apple Inc. (2020) 55 Cal.App.5th 323, 331.)

 

Based on the foregoing, the demurrer to the third and fourth causes of action are SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

Fifth Cause of Action

 

Defendants demur to the fifth cause of action for violation of Civil Code section 3344 on the grounds Plaintiff’s own agreement establishes consent and an express waiver of Section 3344.

 

Civil Code section 3344(a) prohibits use of a person's name, likeness, or image for commercial purposes without prior consent. (Civ. Code § 3344.) Lack of consent is an essential element. (Maxwell v. Dolezal (2014) 231 Cal. App. 4th 93, 97.)

 

Defendants argue the Agreement establishes consent on its face. Plaintiff granted the company “the perpetual, worldwide right to use” her name, likeness, image, and photograph, and she “explicitly waive[d] any rights that [she] may have under such statutes or other applicable laws to prevent [the company] from using the Molds or selling the Products,” expressly identifying Civil Code section 3344. (Compl., Exh. A, § 1.7.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues the complaint sufficiently alleges on November 15, 2024, Plaintiff sent formal notice of termination pursuant to the Agreement and Defendants failed to cure within the time permitted by the Agreement. (See Exh. A §3.6.) Accordingly, Defendants continued unauthorized use of the Subject Images was without consent.

 

The court finds the allegations insufficient. The complaint does not clearly distinguish pre-termination from post-termination uses. The pleading should identify that the challenged Section 3344 uses are the post-February 13, 2025 uses and should specify which categories of post-termination use are being challenged.

 

Based on the foregoing, the demurrer to the fifth cause of action is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

Seventh – Tenth Causes of Action

 

Defendant demurs to the seventh through tenth causes of action on the grounds each claim is governed by the two-year limitations period of CCP § 335.1. The conduct on which these claims rest is alleged to have occurred at the September 2, 2022 molding session and at the November 7, 2023 photoshoot. However, Plaintiff did not file suit until May 12, 2026.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues a continued failure to maintain professional safeguards after complaints were made on May 7, 2024, and November 15, 2024, and systematic exploitation causing emotional distress. Moreover, Defendants kept manufacturing and selling products cast from Plaintiff's body after the Agreement was terminated, after Plaintiff requested that Defendants stop, and after Plaintiff's intellectual-property rights reverted under section 3.6, with Amazon listings live through February 2026, and still live today.

 

The court finds the allegations are barred on the face of the complaint. Plaintiff does not allege any continuing violation in the complaint and the complaint alleges nothing after 2023.

 

Based on the foregoing, the demurrer to the seventh, eighth, ninth, and tenth causes of action is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND. 

 

Motion to Strike

 

Defendants move to strike the improper use of Plaintiff’s “Jane Doe” pseudonym on the grounds Plaintiff filed the complaint under the “Jane Doe” pseudonym without a proper basis and without leave of court.

 

Defendants contend pursuant to California law, except in circumstances not applicable here, all plaintiffs must file a lawsuit under their real names. (CCP § 422.40.) In order to proceed under a pseudonym in a civil action, the plaintiff must affirmatively move for an order granting permission to proceed anonymously. (See Dep’t of Fair Emp. & Hous. v. Superior Ct. of Santa Clara Cnty. (2022) 82 Cal.App.5th 105, 112.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues her use of the pseudonym Jane Doe 1 is necessary to preserve privacy in a matter of sensitive and highly personal nature. Here, the dispute arises from an agreement under which Defendants obtained molds taken directly from Plaintiffs genital anatomy so that Defendants could manufacture and sell adult pleasure products reproducing Plaintiffs anatomy. Moreover, Defendants know precisely who Plaintiff is.

 

The court finds Plaintiff has adequately established a basis for proceeding as a Doe plaintiff. (See Department of Fair Employment and Housing v. Superior Court of Santa Clara County (2022) 82 Cal.App.5th 105, fn. 1.)  Although the public has a constitutionally protected right of access to court documents and proceedings, such interest here is outweighed by plaintiff’s privacy rights given the intimate nature of this dispute (use of plaintiff’s genital reproduction).   Further, plaintiff has properly proceeded by submitting the request to file as a “Doe” plaintiff pursuant to Civil Code Section 1708.85 (distribution of sexually explicit material).  That section expressly allows for the use of pseudonymous names where plaintiff alleges that depictions of her sexual parts have been distributed without her consent.   Because defendants know who plaintiff is, there is no prejudice to them in allowing plaintiff to proceed as a Doe. 

 

Based on the foregoing, the motion to strike is DENIED.

    

Case Number: 26STUD02028    Hearing Date: September 16, 2026    Dept: 529

ISLAND PROPERTY, L.L.C. VS MALIK BOOKS LLC

 

PLAINTIFF’S MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT OR, IN THE ALTERNATIVE, SUMMARY ADJUDICATION

 

Date of Hearing:        September 16, 2026             Trial Date:       None set  

Department:              529                                         Case No.:        26STUD02028

 

Moving Party:            Plaintiff Island Property, LLC

Responding Party:     Defendant Malik Books LLC

 

BACKGROUND

 

On February 25, 2026, Plaintiff Island Property, L.L.C filed the instant Unlawful Detainer Action against Malik Books LLC. Plaintiff alleges Defendant failed to comply with a 30-Day Notice to Perform Covenant or Quit. The Notice had identified multiple lease violations by Defendant including performing alterations/construction without providing Plaintiff copies of permits, plan specifications, and proof of city inspector approval and installing signage without permits and Plaintiff’s prior approval.

 

[TENTATIVE] RULING

 

Plaintiff’s Motion for Summary Judgment or, in the alternative, Summary Adjudication is GRANTED IN PART.

 

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

 

Defendant Malik Books, LLC this court take judicial notice of several court documents. (See RJN, Exh. 1 – 9.) The court GRANTS the request for judicial notice as to the existence of theses documents.

 

EVIDENTIARY OBJECTIONS

 

Defendant Malik Books, LLC objections to Evidence  

 

The court sustains the objections to No. 13.

 

Plaintiff Island Properties Objections to Evidence

 

The court SUSTAINS the objections to Nos. 1, 2, 3, and 6. 

 

 

 

DISCUSSION

 

Plaintiff Island Property, LLC moves this court, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 437(c) and Section 1170.7, for summary judgment of the Unlawful Detainer Complaint in favor of Plaintiff and against Defendant Malik Books, LLC. Plaintiff also seeks summary adjudication of Defendant’s affirmative defenses.

 

A “motion for summary judgment shall be granted if all the papers submitted show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to a judgment as a matter of law.” (CCP § 437c(c).) Once the moving party has met its burden of demonstrating that there is no triable issue as to any material fact, the opposing party cannot rest upon the mere allegations of the pleadings but must present admissible evidence showing that there is a genuine issue for trial. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Company (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 844.) “In ruling on the motion, the court must consider all of the evidence and all of the inferences reasonably drawn therefrom… and must view such evidence… in the light most favorable to the opposing party.” (Id. at 844-845; CCP § 437c(p)(2).) “A motion for summary adjudication shall be granted only if it completely disposes of a cause of action, an affirmative defense, a claim for damages, or an issue of duty.” (CCP § 437c(f)(1).) 

 

Plaintiff filed this action for unlawful detainer due to Defendant’s failure to comply with a 30-day Notice to Perform Covenant or Quit. Plaintiff maintains the undisputed facts establish that there are no triable issues of fact and no applicable affirmative defenses as to this UD Action.  To establish a cause of action for unlawful detainer for breach of the lease agreement under Code of Civil Procedure Section 1161, a plaintiff must show the following:   

 

1.      That plaintiff owns or leases the property; 

2.      That plaintiff rented or subleased the property to defendant; 

3.      That under the rental agreement, defendant agreed to perform certain conditions; 

4.      That defendant failed to perform those requirement(s); 

5.      That plaintiff properly gave defendant three days’ written notice to correct failure to perform or vacate the property;  

6.      That defendant did not correct failure to perform; 

7.      That plaintiff properly gave defendant three days’ written notice to vacate the property; and 

8.      That defendant is still occupying the property.  

 

Plaintiff presents evidence of the following: Plaintiff owns the Premises (Frazier Decl. ¶1, Truong Exhs. 8-9.) As the owner and landlord of the Premises, Plaintiff entered into the Lease with Defendant on or about July 29, 2024. (Frazier Decl. ¶3, Exh. 2.) The Lease contains express covenants by which Defendant must abide including Plaintiff’s prior approval before installing any signage at the Premises. (Frazier, ¶4, Exh. 2; Truong, Exhs. 8-9.) As early as July 2025, Plaintiff notified Defendant that they were in violation of Lease by serving Notice 1. (Truong Decl., Exh. 1.) Defendant completely failed to take action to cure Notice 1, which resulted in Plaintiff serving Notice 2 on September 15, 2025 as stated in said notice. (Truong Decl., Exh. 2.) Between July 30, 2025 and December 4, 2025, Plaintiff’s attorney communicated with Defendant’s attorney repeatedly in an attempt to bring Defendant into compliance with the signage issues under the Lease. (Truong, ¶6, Exh. 3.) On January 6, 2026, Plaintiff received a reminder notice from the development management about signage requirements for all tenants in the development. (Frazier, ¶8, Exh. 3.) Plaintiff maintains the signage installed by Defendant at the Premises did not comply with these rules and finally, Notice 3 was served on Defendant on January 9, 2026. (Truong Decl. ¶7, Exh. 4.) Moreover, Notice 3 was served to Defendant in multiple manners. First, it was served via Fedex pursuant to Paragraph 23 of the Lease to the address where Defendant’s registered agent for service is located and the address listed next to Defendant’s signature block on the Lease. (Truong, ¶8, Exhs. 5-7.) Second, Notice 3 was served by posting and mailing a copy to the Premises. (Tilcock Decl. ¶2-4, Exhs. A – C.) And Notice 3 was served via email to Defendant’s attorney of record. (Truong Decl. ¶9.) Plaintiff’s further present evidence Defendant insists that they did not need to comply and Defendant still occupies the Subject Property. (Frazier Decl. ¶¶9-10.)

 

In opposition, Defendant argues whether any covenant was breached is genuinely disputed, the credibility of Plaintiff’s notice-service evidence is itself a triable issue, and Plaintiff waived strict compliance with the lease covenant by accepting the rent. Specifically, Defendant contends reading Paragraph 61 in full, it affirmatively grants Defendant the right to "place the main sign over the Entry Door and two additional smaller signs," reserves to Plaintiff approval of "[s]ize and exact location" only, and makes the tenant "responsible for design." That is irreconcilable with Paragraph 34's grant of "sole discretion" over "size, shape, color, design, and location". Moreover, the issues with the service could weigh against Plaintiff and whether Plaintiff's conduct here waived strict compliance, or estops it from forfeiting the leasehold on the demands in Notice 3, is at minimum a jury question.

 

The court finds there is no conflict between Paragraphs 34 and 61 of the Lease. As noted by Plaintiff, the fact that Paragraph 61 allows installation of specified signs does not relieve Defendant of the obligation to obtain Plaintiff’s prior written consent for any such signs as required by Paragraph 34. In other words, the landlord retains approval authority over the basic design framework under Paragraph 34, while the tenant handles the technical design work, permitting, and installation logistics under Paragraph 61.  Further, Paragraph 61 provides the Landlord must allow one sign over the entry and one side on the front and one on the back of the building, but does not abrogate its right to determine the size, style and content of such signage.  In the instant matter, Notice 3 provides Defendant breached the Lease by installing signage without first submitting plans as required by the Lease, failing to obtain permits for the signage (which Tenant was responsible for doing) and failing to remove such signage upon the Landlord’s demand.

 

However, the court finds a triable issue of material fact as to service. Under California Code of Civil Procedure section 437c(e), the repeated errors, timing inconsistencies, and acknowledged problems with the same service apparatus create material questions about the declarant's truthfulness and intent that cannot be resolved without cross-examination.

 

As for waiver, the court finds there is a triable issue of material fact as well. Even with a no waiver provision, a tenant can still show waiver if the landlord lets the first notice lapse through conduct that demonstrates acceptance of benefits. (See Runnymede Holdings, LLC v. Foster (2023) 96 Cal.App.5th Supp. 1 holding “[e]ven if a lease agreement contains an antiwaiver clause, a party's conduct may, nonetheless, constitute waiver.”)

 

Based on the foregoing, the motion for summary judgment is DENIED.

 

Affirmative Defenses

 

In the alternative, Plaintiff moves for summary adjudication on Defendant’s affirmative defenses.

 

First, Plaintiff argues Defendant relies on erroneous interpretation of the Lease to argue against Plaintiff’s right to control the signage at the premises. As noted above, the court finds the lease interpretation defense not subject to dispute. The court grants summary adjudication as to the second, third, fifth and tenth affirmative defenses.

 

Next, Plaintiff argues the sixth, seventh, eighth and fifteenth affirmative defenses are all based upon Defendant’s arguments that because he filed the civil action entitled Malik Books LLC v. Island Property LLC, et al. (Case No. 25STCV25012) (“Civil Action”) on August 26, 2025, this case cannot proceed forward. The court denies the motion for summary adjudication as to these affirmative defenses. The court finds Plaintiff has not met their burden.

 

Next, the court finds Plaintiff has not met their burden establishing there is no waiver or estoppel defense available to Defendant as noted above.

 

As for the tenth affirmative defense and the Scrivner’s error, the court denies the motion for summary adjudication. Whether a claimed scrivener error is truly a good faith mistake or intentional wrongdoing presents a factual question that cannot be resolved on summary adjudication.

 

As for the eleventh affirmative defense based on SB 1103, the court agrees a defendant generally cannot invoke the Act's protections if it first identified itself as a qualified commercial tenant only after the three-day notice to pay or quit had already been served. For fixed-term leases or periodic tenancies longer than one month, the tenant must have provided the written notice and self-attestation before or upon execution of the lease, and annually thereafter.

 

As for the fourteenth affirmative defense, the court agrees Defendant cannot have a defense under Civil Code section 1942.5 because said code applies only to residential tenancies, while the instant action involves commercial space. The court denies the motion for summary adjudication as to the twelfth and thirteenth causes of action.

 

Based on the foregoing, the motion for summary adjudication is granted as to the second, third, fifth, tenth, eleventh and fourteenth affirmative defenses.
