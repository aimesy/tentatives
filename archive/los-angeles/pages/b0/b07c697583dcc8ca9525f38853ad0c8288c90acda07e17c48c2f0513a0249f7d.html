Case Number: 24STCV20166    Hearing Date: September 16, 2026    Dept: 224

Weingart Tower, LP et al., v. Axis/GFA

TENTATIVE RULING

The Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings is DENIED.

Introduction

On August 9, 2024, Plaintiffs Weingart Tower, LP and Weingart Tower II, LP filed a Complaint in this action.

On February 5, 2025, Plaintiffs filed the operative First Amended Complaint, adding Emmerson Construction, Inc. as a plaintiff and asserting five causes of action against Defendant AXIS/GFA Architecture & Design, Inc. and Does 1 through 100.

On April 14, 2025, AXIS filed a Cross-Complaint naming Cross-Defendant Robison Engineering, Inc. AXIS filed an Amended Cross-Complaint on June 24, 2025.

On July 28, 2025, Robison Engineering, Inc. filed a Special Answer pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 428.70.

On January 12, 2026, Cross-Defendant Robison Engineering, Inc. filed the instant Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings, seeking judgment as to Plaintiffs' entire First Amended Complaint.

No opposition has been filed.

Meet and Confer

Before filing a demurrer or a motion to strike, the demurring or moving party is required to meet and confer with the party who filed the pleading demurred to or the pleading that is subject to the motion to strike for the purposes of determining whether an agreement can be reached through a filing of an amended pleading that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer. (CCP §§ 430.41)

The Declaration of the declaration of David Davidson, executed on January 12, 2026, sets forth the meet and confer efforts of counsel for the Defendants, and counsel’s attempts to resolve the disputes informally by telephone. The Court finds that the declaration satisfies the requirement of Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41.

Legal Standard

A defendant may move for judgment on the pleadings where¿the court has no jurisdiction of the subject of the cause of action alleged in the complaint or¿the complaint does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action against that defendant.¿ (Code Civ. Proc., § 438 subd. (c)(1)(B).)¿ A non-statutory motion for judgment on the pleadings may be made any time before or during trial.¿ (Stoops v.¿Abbassi¿(2002) 100 Cal.App.4th 644, 650.)¿ “Such motion may be made on the same ground as those supporting a general demurrer,¿i.e., that the pleading at issue fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a legally cognizable claim or defense.”¿

(Ibid.)¿¿¿

The standard for ruling on a motion for judgment on the pleadings is essentially the same as that applicable to a general demurrer, that is, under the state of the pleadings, together with matters that may be judicially noticed, it appears that a party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. (Bezirdjian v. O'Reilly (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 316, 321-322, citing Schabarum v. California Legislature (1998) 60 Cal.App.4th 1205, 1216.)

Like demurrers, motions for judgment on the pleadings challenge the legal sufficiency of the allegations, not their veracity. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) The Court “must accept as true all material facts properly pleaded, but does not consider conclusions of law or fact, opinions, speculation, or allegations contrary to law or facts that are judicially noticed.” (Stevenson Real Estate Services, Inc. v. CB Richard Ellis Real Estate Services, Inc. (2006) 138 Cal.App.4th 1215, 1219-1220.)

Standing

Section 438 does not permit any party to move against any pleading. Subdivision (b)(1) provides that "[a] party may move for judgment on the pleadings," but subdivision (c)(1) then specifies the permissible grounds by reference to the movant's status, and it recognizes only two categories of movant: a plaintiff moving against an answer (§ 438, subd. (c)(1)(A)), and a defendant moving against a complaint (§ 438, subd. (c)(1)(B)). The Legislature supplied no third category.

The ground REI invokes is available where "[t]he complaint does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action against that defendant." (§ 438, subd. (c)(1)(B)(ii), italics added.) The qualifying phrase confines the motion to the movant's own exposure under the challenged pleading. REI has no exposure under the FAC. Plaintiffs did not sue REI, and REI could not suffer a judgment on any cause of action the FAC pleads. What REI actually seeks is judgment in favor of AXIS on claims asserted against AXIS – relief AXIS has not requested and has not joined.

REI's asserted authority is section 428.70, which provides that a third-party defendant "may, at the time he files his answer to the cross-complaint, file as a separate document a special answer alleging against the person who asserted the cause of action against the third-party plaintiff any defenses which the third-party plaintiff has to such cause of action." (§ 428.70, subd. (b).)

The statute is specific about the vehicle it creates. It authorizes a pleading — a special answer, filed at a fixed time, served on both the plaintiff and the third-party plaintiff. Having identified that mechanism with precision, the Legislature did not go on to authorize the third-party defendant to file dispositive motions against the plaintiff's complaint, and the Court will not supply that authority by implication. Administrative Management Services, Inc. v. Fidelity & Deposit Co. (1982) 129 Cal.App.3d 484, 488, on which REI relies, is consistent with this reading. That decision describes the purpose of section 428.70 as protecting a third-party defendant against the third-party plaintiff's "failure or neglect to assert a proper defense." The protection it describes is the ability to assert those defenses, not to litigate the plaintiff's claims against the defendant on the defendant's behalf.

REI alternatively invokes the common law motion for judgment on the pleadings, on the premise that section 438's limitations do not bind it. (Stoops v. Abbassi (2002) 100 Cal.App.4th 644, 650; Smiley v. Citibank (South Dakota) N.A. (1995) 11 Cal.4th 138, 145-146; Saltarelli & Steponovich v. Douglas (1995) 40 Cal.App.4th 1, 5.)

The common law motion escapes section 438's procedural prerequisites, not its substantive ground. Stoops and Saltarelli address when the motion may be brought. They say nothing about who may bring it. The traditional common law grounds are lack of subject matter jurisdiction, and that the complaint does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action against the defendant. (Korchemny v. Piterman (2021) 68 Cal.App.5th 1032.) That second ground is identical in substance to section 438, subdivision (c)(1)(B)(ii). The motion operates as a “general demurrer” to the complaint when made by a defendant (Sofias v. Bank of America (1985) 172 Cal.App.3d 583), and "a defendant must show a complaint fails to state a cause of action" (Korchemny, supra, 68 Cal.App.5th 1032).

REI's theory assumes the common law motion to be broader than the statutory motion in the class of movants it admits and the relief it affords. No authority so holds. Because no statute prescribes the grounds or procedure for the common law motion, courts look to demurrer procedure as the closest analogue. (People v. $20,000 U.S. Currency (1991) 235 Cal.App.3d 682.) A demurrer may be interposed only by "[t]he party against whom a complaint or cross-complaint has been filed." (§ 430.10.) Here, the First Amended Complaint has not been filed against REI.

REI lacks standing to move for judgment on the pleadings against the First Amended Complaint. Neither the statutory motion nor its common law counterpart is available to a party against whom the challenged pleading states no claim, and section 428.70 does not enlarge the class of parties who may bring one. Because standing is a threshold requirement, the Court does not reach any of the substantive arguments raised.

Conclusion

The Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings is DENIED.

Berry et al., v. AIDS Healthcare Foundation, et al.,

TENTATIVE RULING

Defendant AIDS Healthcare Foundation's demurrer to the First Amended Complaint is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND as to each of the first through sixth causes of action, as to Plaintiff Deshon Crooms.

ANALYSIS

Introduction

On February 3, 2025, Plaintiffs William Berry and Deshon Crooms filed the Complaint in this action.

On June 22, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the operative First Amended Complaint.

On July 21, 2026, Defendant AIDS Healthcare Foundation filed the Demurrer to Plaintiffs' First Amended Complaint, and the concurrent Motion to Strike Portions of Plaintiffs' First Amended Complain.

On August 26, 2026, Plaintiff William Berry was dismissed from this action.

On September 2, 2026, Plaintiff Deshon Crooms filed the Oppositions.

Request for Judicial Notice

Defendant requests the Court to take judicial notice of (1) the First Amended Complaint, notices of settlement, and Request for Dismissal filed in Grace Antisdel, et al. v. AIDS Healthcare Foundation, et al., Los Angeles Superior Court case no. 21STCV04049, together with the trial court's docket in that action; (2) the Second Amended Complaint, the April 12, 2022 minute order granting class certification, the Motion for Preliminary Approval of Class Action Settlement, the December 23, 2024 minute order, the Class Action Settlement Agreement and Class Notice filed January 9, 2025, the Motion for Final Approval of Class Action Settlement, the Declaration of Anne-Marie Marra, the May 8, 2025 minute order, the Order on Final Approval of Class Action Settlement, the Final Judgment, and the trial court's docket, all in Tammy Davis v. AIDS Healthcare Foundation, Los Angeles Superior Court case no. 20STCV08627; and (3) deeds recorded in the Official Records of the Los Angeles County Registrar-Recorder/County Clerk concerning the real property located at 423 E. 7th Street, Los Angeles, California.

The request is denied as to the Antisdel materials, which are offered solely in support of Defendant's preclusion argument against Plaintiff Berry and are immaterial in light of his August 26, 2026 dismissal.

The remainder of the requests is GRANTED.

Meet and Confer

Before filing a demurrer or a motion to strike, the demurring or moving party is required to meet and confer with the party who filed the pleading demurred to or the pleading that is subject to the motion to strike for the purposes of determining whether an agreement can be reached through a filing of an amended pleading that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer. (CCP §§ 430.41)

The Declaration of Heather Stern, executed on July 21, 2026, sets forth the meet and confer efforts of counsel for the Defendants, and counsel’s attempts to resolve the dispute informally. However, the declaration does not satisfy the requirement of Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, as it does not specify whether the meet and confer took place in person, by telephone, or by video conference, as Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41 requires. The Court will nonetheless exercise its discretion to consider the merits.

Legal Standard

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action.¿ (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)¿When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context.¿ (Wilson v. Transit Authority of City of Sacramento (1962) 199 Cal.App.2d 716, 720-21.)¿In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice.¿(Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)¿“A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not on the evidence or facts alleged.” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Id.) The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action. (Hahn, supra, 147 Cal.App.4th at 747.)

Mootness as to Plaintiff Berry

Berry's dismissal moots both motions as to him. A demurrer tests a pleading asserted by a party; where the plaintiff has dismissed, there is no live controversy and no relief the Court could grant.

The Operative Release Period

The Court first corrects a premise of the moving papers. AHF asserts a release period running through January 9, 2025, described as the date the executed settlement agreement was filed. But section 7.3 of the settlement agreement ties the release to the "Preliminary Approval Date," which the agreement defines by reference to the Preliminary Approval Order. That order was entered December 27, 2024. The January 9, 2025 filing was a declaration of compliance, not the order. The Motion for Preliminary Approval likewise states that the release period ends on the date of preliminary approval.

The release period therefore ran from September 27, 2017 through December 27, 2024. AHF's contrary date is rejected. The thirteen-day difference is not material to the disposition, but it governs any amended pleading and any later motion.

Scope of the Davis Release

Defendant contends Crooms's claims were released in Tammy Davis v. AIDS Healthcare Foundation, LASC No. 20STCV08627, because section 7.3 releases "all common area claims" – enumerating plumbing, electrical, elevator outages, unsanitary common areas, mailboxes, deadbolts, resident caretaker, and public nuisance – and the FAC alleges those same conditions. A written release extinguishes obligations covered by its terms and is enforced as written. (Skrbina v. Fleming Cos. (1996) 45 Cal.App.4th 1353, 1366.)

In opposition, Crooms correctly observes that section 7.3 reserves "all claims not released," expressly excludes "any claims arising from conditions inside Class Member units," and that no class member gave a Civil Code section 1542 waiver. A class release cannot reach claims outside the scope of the settled complaint. (Amaro v. Anaheim Arena Management, LLC (2021) 69 Cal.App.5th 521, 537-539.) Each proposition is sound, but the FAC pleads no facts within the carve-out.

Paragraph 45 alleges "code violations in their individual units throughout their tenancies" without identifying any condition. (FAC ¶ 45.) Paragraph 68 describes an October 4, 2024 citation for defective floors "in a unit," not alleged to be Unit 211, and reasons outward to "building-wide issues related to infestations." (FAC ¶ 68.) Counsel's assertion in opposition that Crooms "still has claims as to his individual unit" appears only in the brief. As pleaded, the FAC alleges the released categories and nothing else.

Further, as to res judicata, claim preclusion requires the same cause of action between the same parties after a final judgment on the merits. (DKN Holdings LLC v. Faerber (2015) 61 Cal.4th 813, 824.) Even assuming identity of primary right as to conduct within the class period, a judgment does not preclude claims arising from conduct occurring after it. Defendant’s alleged continuing failures to maintain habitable premises after December 27, 2024 give rise to claims that could not have been litigated in Davis.

Demurrer on this ground is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

Alter Ego Allegations

Defendant contends the FAC's alter ego allegations are conclusory and that it cannot be liable for conditions after it transferred title on December 30, 2024.

Alter ego requires facts showing both a unity of interest and ownership and that an inequitable result would follow from respecting the corporate form. (Automotriz etc. de California v. Resnick (1957) 47 Cal.2d 792, 796.) To allege alter ego, plaintiffs must plead a unity of interest and ownership such that the separate personalities of the corporations and individuals do not exist, and that an inequity will result if the corporate entity is treated as the sole actor. (Tomaselli v. Transamerica Ins. Co (1994) 25 Cal. App. 4th 1269, 1285; Vasey v. Cal. Dance Co. (1977) 70 Cal. App. 3d 742, 749.) Factors to consider in applying the doctrine include the commingling of funds and other assets, the holding out by one entity that it is liable for the debts of the other, identical equitable ownership in the two entities, inadequate capitalization, and disregard of corporate formalities. (Sonora Diamond Corp. v Superior Court (2000) 83 Cal.App.4th 523, 538-39.)

The FAC alleges a shared business address, that Defendant's president formed H4H in 1995 to manage Defendant's housing projects, a generalized corporate practice of acquiring buildings, transferring title, and continuing to operate them, and that Madison designates H4H as its manager (FAC ¶¶ 7, 13). These allegations speak, at most and in general terms, to common ownership and management. The FAC pleads no commingling of funds or assets, no holding out by Defendant that it is answerable for the debts of H4H or Madison, no inadequate capitalization of either transferee, and no disregard of corporate formalities. As to the second element, the FAC alleges only that Defendants are alter egos and that treating them separately would be inequitable, which states the conclusion rather than facts supporting it.

Paragraph 40 further alleges that H4H and then Madison each assumed Crooms's lease and formed a landlord-tenant relationship with him, and the FAC alleges no facts describing Defendant's own conduct at the property after December 30, 2024.

Because the pre-December 27, 2024 allegations are released as pleaded, and the post-transfer allegations neither connect Defendant to the property directly nor satisfy the Tomaselli and Sonora Diamond pleading requirements, no cause of action states facts sufficient to constitute a claim against this Defendant in either period.

Demurrer on this ground is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

Sham Pleading

Defendant contends the FAC is a sham because it alleges Defendant continued to own and operate the property after December 30, 2024, contradicting paragraph 2 of the original complaint and the recorded deeds.

The sham pleading doctrine applies only where a plaintiff attempts to avoid a prior harmful admission by omitting or contradicting it without explanation. (Larson v. UHS of Rancho Springs, Inc. (2014) 230 Cal.App.4th 336, 344; Deveny v. Entropin, Inc. (2006) 139 Cal.App.4th 408, 425–426.)

The FAC neither omits nor contradicts. It pleads the December 30, 2024 transfer to H4H and the March 21, 2025 transfer to Madison, and separately alleges that Defendant continued to operate the premises through those entities (FAC ¶¶ 11–13). Continued operation is not inconsistent with divested title, and the judicially noticed deeds establish only that legal title transferred.

Demurrer on this ground is OVERRULED.

Motion to Strike

Defendant moves in the alternative to strike the FAC's post-transfer ownership and alter ego allegations and all allegations predating January 9, 2025. Because the demurrer is sustained as to every cause of action, the FAC will be superseded and no operative matter remains to strike. The motion to strike is OFF CALENDAR AS MOOT.

Conclusion

Defendant AIDS Healthcare Foundation's demurrer to the First Amended Complaint is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND as to each of the first through sixth causes of action, as to Plaintiff Deshon Crooms.

The Demurrer is placed OFF CALENDAR AS MOOT as to Plaintiff William Berry.

Case Number: 26STCV05023    Hearing Date: September 16, 2026    Dept: 224

Pineda v. Omnilife USA, Inc.

TENTATIVE RULING

There is a valid, enforceable arbitration agreement. The dispute is covered by the agreement. The FAA governs the agreement. The Agreement bears at most a modest degree of procedural unconscionability, and Plaintiff has not established any substantive unconscionability.

The motion is GRANTED. Plaintiff and Defendants are ordered to arbitration. The Action is stayed.

The Case Management Conference is vacated and a Post Arbitration Status Conference is set for September 30, 2027 at 8:30 a.m. A joint status report is due five court days before the hearing.

ANALYSIS

Introduction

On February 13, 2026, Plaintiff Arnoldo Pineda filed his Complaint against Defendants Omnilife USA, Inc. and Jorge Tisnado, alleging eleven causes of action: (1) retaliation in violation of Labor Code section 98.6; (2) retaliation in violation of Labor Code section 1102.5; (3) wrongful termination in violation of public policy; (4) failure to pay overtime compensation; (5) failure to pay minimum wages; (6) failure to provide meal periods; (7) failure to provide rest breaks; (8) failure to pay wages due upon termination and waiting time penalties; (9) failure to issue accurate and itemized wage statements; (10) failure to indemnify; and (11) unlawful business practices in violation of Business and Professions Code section 17200. The first through third causes of action are alleged against Omnilife only; the fourth through eleventh are alleged against both Defendants.

On March 23, 2026, Defendants filed their Answer.

On April 22, 2026, Defendants filed the instant Motion to Compel Arbitration and to Stay the action.

On May 22, 2026, Plaintiff filed his Opposition.

On May 29, 2026, Defendants filed their Reply.

Motion to Compel Arbitration

Defendants Omnilife USA, Inc. ("Omnilife") and Jorge Tisnado (collectively, "Defendants") move the Court for an order: (i) compelling Plaintiff Arnoldo Pineda to submit all causes of action set forth in his Complaint to binding arbitration in lieu of court proceedings; and (ii) staying the instant lawsuit pending completion of such arbitration.

Legal Standard

In a motion to compel arbitration, the moving party must prove by a preponderance of evidence the existence of the arbitration agreement and that the dispute is covered by the agreement. The burden then shifts to the resisting party to prove by a preponderance of evidence a ground for denial (e.g., fraud, unconscionability, etc.). (Rosenthal v. Great Western Fin. Securities Corp. (1996) 14 Cal.4th 394, 413-414; Hotels Nevada v. L.A. Pacific Center, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 754, 758.)

Generally, on a petition to compel arbitration, the court must grant the petition unless it finds either (1) no written agreement to arbitrate exists; (2) the right to compel arbitration has been waived; (3) grounds exist for revocation of the agreement; or (4) litigation is pending that may render the arbitration unnecessary or create conflicting rulings on common issues. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2; Condee v. Longwood Management Corp. (2001) 88 Cal.App.4th 215, 218-219.)

“California has a strong public policy in favor of arbitration and any doubts regarding the arbitrability of a dispute are resolved in favor of arbitration.” (Coast Plaza Doctors Hospital v. Blue Cross of California (2000) 83 Cal.App.4th 677, 686.) “This strong policy has resulted in the general rule that arbitration should be upheld unless it can be said with assurance that an arbitration clause is not susceptible to an interpretation covering the asserted dispute.” (Ibid. [internal quotations omitted].) This is in accord with the liberal federal policy favoring arbitration agreements under the Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”), which governs all agreements to arbitrate in contracts “involving interstate commerce.” (9 U.S.C. § 2, et seq.; Higgins v. Superior Court (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 1238, 1247.)

The Existence of an Agreement

A party to an arbitration agreement may seek a court order compelling the parties to arbitrate a dispute covered by the agreement. (CCP § 1281.2) The party seeking arbitration has the initial “burden of proving the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by a preponderance of the evidence, while a party opposing the petition bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence any fact necessary to its defense.” (Ruiz v. Moss Bros. Auto Group, Inc. (2014) 232 Cal.4th 836, 842.) A moving party meets its initial burden by showing the agreement at issue exists, and need only establish its validity on challenge by opponent. (HM DG, Inc. v. Amini (2013) 219 Cal.App.4th 1100, 1114; Espejo v. Southern Calif. Permanente Med. Group (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 1047, 1060.)

Defendants submit the Arbitration Agreement in question through the Declaration of Samara Irma Lopez Reyes, Omnilife's Human Resources International Manager, and the Declarations of Monica Vargas, Omnilife's In-House Legal Counsel. The Agreement is a standalone five-page document entitled "Mutual Agreement to Binding Arbitration of Claims," bearing Plaintiff's handwritten signature and dated March 17, 2025 (Lopez Reyes Decl. ¶ 5, Exh. A). Defendants also submit an earlier agreement in substantially the same form, executed by Plaintiff on November 12, 2021. (Vargas Reply Decl. ¶ 6, Exh. 1).

The Agreement provides that "Employee and the Company agree to arbitrate any claim, dispute, and/or controversy that either Employee may have against the Company (or its owners, directors, officers, managers, employees, agents . . .) or the Company may have against Employee, arising from, related to, or having any relationship or connection whatsoever with Employee's seeking employment, Employee's employment, or Employee's other association with the Company," and that included within its scope "are all disputes, whether based on tort, contract, statute (including, but not limited to, any claims of discrimination and harassment . . . or any other state or federal law or regulation), equitable law, or otherwise." (Lopez Reyes Decl., Exh. A, p. 1.) Above the signature line, the Agreement states that it is a voluntary agreement, that "Employee understands that by signing this Arbitration Agreement, Employee is giving up the right to a jury trial," and that Employee "has been given the opportunity to discuss the Arbitration Agreement with private legal counsel." (Id., pp. 1, 5.)

“With respect to the moving party’s burden to provide evidence of the existence of an agreement to arbitrate, it is generally sufficient for that party to present a copy of the contract to the court.” (Baker v. Italian Maple Holdings, LLC, 13 Cal. App. 5th 1152, 1160 (2017).)

Plaintiff declares that he "do[es] not recall signing" either the 2021 or the 2025 Agreement, that he recalls receiving the 2025 Agreement, and that "[i]n the past" he had an issue with Omnilife's HR "regarding someone else signing my direct deposits and cashing out two of my checks." (Pineda Decl. ¶¶ 2, 4-6.) He does not state that the signature on either Agreement is not his.

That showing is insufficient. "[T]he fact that a person does not recall signing the agreement neither creates a factual dispute as to the signature's authenticity nor affords an independent basis to find that a contract was not formed." (Iyere v. Wise Auto Group (2023) 87 Cal.App.5th 747, 758.) In Ramirez v. Golden Queen Mining Co., LLC (2024) 102 Cal.App.5th 821, 836, the plaintiff's declaration that he did not recall being presented with or signing the agreement, and that no one explained it to him, did not create a factual dispute where he never asserted the signature was not his. Plaintiff's declaration is materially indistinguishable.

Plaintiff's Opposition asserts that "the signature on Defendants' Arbitration Agreement . . . is not his actual signature." (Opp. at 1:15-17.) Argument of counsel is not evidence, and the declaration on which the Opposition rests contains no such statement. The reference to an undated payroll irregularity, unaccompanied by documents, dates, or any connection to the Agreement, is speculative. By contrast, two declarants with access to Plaintiff's personnel file attest that the signatures on the Agreements match Plaintiff's signature on his other personnel documents, exemplars of which are attached. (Lopez Reyes Decl. ¶ 5; Vargas Decl. ¶ 8; Vargas Reply Decl. ¶ 8, Exh. 2.)

Plaintiff's related assertion that there was no meeting of the minds because he did not know what arbitration was and no one explained the document to him does not avoid enforcement. Failure to read or understand an arbitration clause is no defense. (Metters v. Ralphs Grocery Co. (2008) 161 Cal.App.4th 696, 701; Brookwood v. Bank of America (1996) 45 Cal.App.4th 1667, 1673; Madden v. Kaiser Found. Hosp. (1976) 17 Cal.3d 699, 710.)

Plaintiff further argues the 2025 Agreement lacks consideration and is illusory because he was terminated five days after execution, leaving no continued employment to serve as consideration.

A contract is illusory where one party assumes no obligation at all. (Harris v. TAP Worldwide, LLC (2016) 248 Cal.App.4th 373, 385.) The Agreement here binds both sides: it reaches claims "that either Employee may have against the Company . . . or the Company may have against Employee." (Lopez Reyes Decl., Exh. A, p. 1, emphasis added.) Mutual promises to arbitrate are themselves sufficient consideration. (Serafin v. Balco Properties Ltd., LLC (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 165, 182.) The Agreement further provides that it "can only be revoked or modified by a writing signed by all Parties which specifically states the intent to revoke or modify the Arbitration Agreement," which forecloses any claim of unilateral modification. (Id., p. 4; cf. Peleg v. Neiman Marcus Group, Inc. (2012) 204 Cal.App.4th 1425, 1465.)

The brevity of the post-execution employment period does not alter the analysis. Courts do not weigh the adequacy of consideration. (Whelan v. Swain (1901) 132 Cal. 389, 391.) Plaintiff's employment continued after he signed, the Agreement guaranteed no term of employment, and Plaintiff cites no authority holding that consideration is extinguished retroactively when at-will employment ends soon after execution. Plaintiff's suggestion of "gamesmanship" is unsupported. The Reply establishes that the March 17, 2025 agreement was distributed as part of a company-wide update to the California form. (Vargas Reply Decl. ¶ 9, Exh. 3.)

Defendant Tisnado did not sign the Agreement, but its terms expressly extend to claims against the Company's "owners, directors, officers, managers, employees, [and] agents." Tisnado is sued as an officer for conduct within the scope of his employment and may enforce the Agreement as an agent and intended third-party beneficiary. (Thomas v. Westlake (2012) 204 Cal.App.4th 605, 614-615; Garcia v. Pexco, LLC (2017) 11 Cal.App.5th 782, 786-788.)

There is an existing and enforceable Agreement.

Further, the dispute is covered by the Agreement. All eleven causes of action arise out of Plaintiff's employment with Omnilife and his termination.

An agreement to arbitrate exists. The dispute is covered by the Agreement.

The Federal Arbitration Act

Federal law applies to arbitration provisions in contracts involving interstate commerce. (Higgins v. Superior Court (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 1238, 1247.) Where the FAA applies, state procedural rules nonetheless govern the determination of a motion to compel arbitration. (Vivid Video, Inc. v. Playboy Ent. Group, Inc. (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 434, 440.) "Even when the Federal Arbitration Act applies, state law governs such matters as who is bound by and who may enforce an arbitration agreement." (Thomas v. Westlake (2012) 204 Cal.App.4th 605, 614, fn. 7.)

The Agreement expressly provides that "[e]xcept as provided in this Arbitration Agreement, the Federal Arbitration Act shall govern the interpretation, enforcement, and all proceedings pursuant to this Arbitration Agreement." (Lopez Reyes Decl., Exh. A, p. 2.) Defendants have further shown that Omnilife's operations involve interstate and international commerce, including distribution centers in Mexico and the importation of products to and from locations in California, Texas, Brazil, and Spain. (Vargas Decl. ¶ 4.)

The FAA applies.

Unconscionability

Plaintiff further argues that the Arbitration Agreement is unenforceable as it is unconscionable.

For an arbitration agreement to be unenforceable as unconscionable, both procedural and substantive unconscionability must be present. (Armendariz v. Foundation Health Psychcare Services, Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 83, 114.) Procedural unconscionability includes oppression arising from unequal bargaining power causing an absence of meaningful choice and real negotiating, and surprise due to hidden terms drafted by the party seeking to enforce the provisions. (Gatton v. T-

Mobile USA, Inc. (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 571, 581.) An arbitration provision is substantively unconscionable where it applies to one side, but not the other. (Higgins v. Sup. Ct. (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 1238, 1254.)

Procedural Unconscionability

Plaintiff asserts the Agreement is procedurally unconscionable because it was a contract of adhesion presented on a take-it-or-leave-it basis, because he was afforded no opportunity to negotiate its terms or consult an attorney, and because the applicable arbitration rules were neither included nor attached.

“Adhesion contracts are form contracts a party with superior bargaining power offers on a take-it-or-leave-it basis.” (Torrecillas v. Fitness International, LLC (2020) 52 Cal.App.5th 485.) Arbitration provisions imposed on a take-it-or-leave-it basis indicate procedural unconscionability. (Thompson v. Toll Dublin, LLC (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 1360, 1372.) But "a compulsory predispute arbitration agreement is not rendered unenforceable just because it is required as a condition … or offered on a 'take it or leave it' basis." (Lagatree v. Luce, Forward, Hamilton & Scripps (1999) 74 Cal. App. 4th 1105, 1127, Giuliano v. Inland Empire Personnel, Inc. (2007) 149 Cal.App.4th 1276, 1292.) That the Agreement was presented as a condition of employment therefore establishes, at most, a modest or minimal degree of oppression.

In the absence of “surprise or other sharp practices”, Courts do not recognize that “adhesive” arbitration agreements establish a high degree of procedural unconscionability. (Baltazar v. Forever 21 Inc. (2016) 62 Cal.4th 1237, 1246.)

Plaintiff has not established that he could not negotiate the terms of the agreement or opt out of the agreement, as he did not ask any questions or indicate that he sought further explanation of the agreement. He has not established any surprise or sharp practices that establish a high degree of procedural unconscionability.

Regardless, the fact that the arbitration agreement may have been presented on a take it or leave it basis or that there is unequal bargaining power only establishes at most a modest degree of procedural unconscionability. (Torrecillas, supra, 52 Cal.App.5th at p.493; Baltazar v. Forever 21 Inc., supra, 62 Cal.4th at p.1246.)

Plaintiff's contention that the failure to attach the arbitration rules aggravates procedural unconscionability is not persuasive. The Agreement identifies the applicable rules by name, provides the internet address at which they may be obtained, and advises that a copy may be requested from the Company. (Lopez Reyes Decl., Exh. A, pp. 2-3.)

Procedural unconscionability includes oppression arising from unequal bargaining power causing an absence of meaningful choice and real negotiating, and surprise due to hidden terms drafted by the party seeking to enforce the provisions. (Gatton v. T-Mobile USA, Inc. (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 571, 581.) The absence of "authentic informed choice," due to the lack of straightforward, unbiased explanations of an agreement, may constitute procedural unconscionability. (Gentry v. Sup. Ct. (2007) 42 Cal.4th 443, 470.) Surprise caused by provisions buried in a long contract, language not fully explained, or misleading captions, indicates procedural unconscionability. (Thompson v. Toll Dublin, LLC (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 1360, 1373.)

However, it is well settled law that failure to read or understand an arbitration clause is no defense to enforcement or grounds to avoid arbitration. (See Metters v. Ralphs Grocery Co. (2008) 161 Cal.App.4th 696, 701; Brookwood v. Bank of America (1996) 45 Cal.App.4th 1667, 1673; Madden v. Kaiser Found. Hosp. (1976) 17 Cal.3d 699, 710.)

As such, though not dispositive, there exists at most a minimal degree of procedural unconscionability in the Agreement.

Substantive Unconscionability

Plaintiff further argues that the Agreement is substantively unconscionable because it is presumptively so under Ingle v. Circuit City Stores, Inc. (9th Cir. 2003) 328 F.3d 1165, and because it contains no express provision for discovery and does not attach the applicable discovery rules.

“A provision is substantively unconscionable if it ‘involves contract terms that are so one-sided as to “shock the conscience,” or that impose harsh or oppressive terms.’ [Citation.] The phrases ‘harsh,’ ‘oppressive,’ and ‘shock the conscience’ are not synonymous with ‘unreasonable.’ Basing an unconscionability determination on the reasonableness of a contract provision would inject an inappropriate level of judicial subjectivity into the analysis. ¿‘With a concept as nebulous as “unconscionability” it is important that courts not be thrust in the paternalistic role of intervening to change contractual terms that the parties have agreed to merely because the court believes the terms are unreasonable. The terms must shock the conscience.’ [Citations.]”¿¿

(Walnut Producers of California v. Diamond Foods, Inc. supra, 187 Cal.App.4th at 647-48.)

Plaintiff's reliance on Ingle is misplaced. Decisions of the Ninth Circuit on questions of California law are not binding on this Court, and no California decision has adopted Ingle's presumption that employer-drafted arbitration agreements are substantively unconscionable. That presumption cannot be reconciled with Baltazar and Sanchez v. Valencia Holding Co., LLC (2015) 61 Cal.4th 899, 911, which place the burden of proof on the party asserting the defense. In any event, the Agreement is bilateral on its face, extending to claims by the Company against the Employee. (Lopez Reyes Decl., Exh. A, p. 1.) It therefore does not "appl[y] to one side, but not the other." (Higgins, supra, 140 Cal.App.4th at p. 1254.)

Plaintiff's discovery argument rests on a factual premise the record does not support. The Agreement expressly provides that "[t]he Parties shall conduct discovery in accordance with JAMS' Employment Rules and Procedures then in effect." (Lopez Reyes Decl., Exh. A, p. 3.) Incorporation of an established provider's employment rules satisfies the adequate-discovery requirement. (Roman v. Superior Court (2009) 172 Cal.App.4th 1462, 1475-1476; Dotson v. Amgen, Inc. (2010) 181 Cal.App.4th 975, 983-984.) Fitz v. NCR Corp. (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 702, on which Plaintiff relies, involved an agreement that affirmatively limited the parties to two depositions and no expert witnesses. The Agreement here imposes no comparable restriction.

Plaintiff has not established any substantive unconscionability. Because both procedural and substantive unconscionability must be present, the defense fails. (Armendariz, supra, 24 Cal.4th at p. 114.)

Conclusion

There is a valid, enforceable arbitration agreement. The dispute is covered by the agreement. The FAA governs the agreement. The Agreement bears at most a modest degree of procedural unconscionability, and Plaintiff has not established any substantive unconscionability.

The motion is GRANTED. Plaintiff and Defendants are ordered to arbitration.
