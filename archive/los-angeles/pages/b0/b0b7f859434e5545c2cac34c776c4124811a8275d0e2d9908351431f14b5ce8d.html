LEGAL STANDARD

A motion to compel further responses to a demand for inspection or production of documents may be brought based on: (1) incomplete statements of compliance; (2) inadequate, evasive, or incomplete claims of inability to comply; or (3) unmeritorious or overly generalized objections.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (a).)  Moreover, “[t]he motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.”  (Code Civ. Proc., §¿2031.310, subd. (b)(2).)

A motion to compel further production must set forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought by the inspection demand.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(1).)  “To establish good cause, a discovery proponent must identify a disputed fact that is of consequence in the action and explain how the discovery sought will tend in reason to prove or disprove that fact or lead to other evidence that will tend to prove or disprove the fact.”  (Digital Music News LLC v Superior Court (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 216, 224 (“Digital Music News LLC”).)  If the moving party has shown good cause for the requests for production, the burden is on the objecting party to justify the objections.  (Kirkland v. Superior Court (2002) 95 Cal. App.4th 92, 98.)

“[T]he court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel further response to a demand, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (h).)

“The court may award sanctions under the Discovery Act in favor of a party who files a motion to compel discovery, even though no opposition to the motion was filed, or opposition to the motion was withdrawn, or the requested discovery was provided to the moving party after the motion was filed.”  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1348(a).)

 

MEET AND CONFER REQUIREMENT

Plaintiff was required to meet and confer with HPMC before filing this motion.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(2).)  Code of Civil Procedure section 2016.040 states that a meet-and-confer declaration “shall state facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt, either in person, by telephone, or by videoconference, to informally resolve each issue presented by the motion.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2016.040.)  “ ‘The parties must present to each other the merits of their respective positions with the same candor, specificity and support during informal negotiations as during the briefing of discovery motions.  Only after all the cards have been laid on the table, and a party has meaningfully assessed the relative strengths and weaknesses of its position in light of all available information, can there be a ‘sincere’ effort to resolve the matter.’ ”  (In re Marriage of Moore (2024) 102 Cal.App.5th 1275, 1293-1294, quoting Townsend v. Superior Court (1998) 61 Cal.App.4th 1431, 1435.)

Accordingly, the Court finds that Plaintiff sufficiently met and conferred with HPMC before filing this motion.

DISCUSSION

Plaintiff moves to compel further responses to Requests for Production Nos. 5 to 8, 10, 12 to 37, and 39 to 40.  (Plaintiff’s Separate Statement, pp. 5:1-109:17.)  The Court has examined the papers and the separate statements concerning these requests.  The Court finds that Plaintiff does not adequately demonstrate good cause for these requests.

As discussed, “[t]o establish good cause, a discovery proponent must identify a disputed fact that is of consequence in the action and explain how the discovery sought will tend in reason to prove or disprove that fact or lead to other evidence that will tend to prove or disprove the fact.”  (Digital Music News LLC, supra, 226 Cal.App.4th at p. 224.)

Plaintiff presents numerous arguments regarding the relevance of the requested documents in the separate statement.  However, the necessary standard is not merely relevance; rather, it requires a clear demonstration of good cause.  Any explanation concerning relevance fails to specify how each request will tend, in reason, to prove or disprove the fact of consequence or lead to other evidence that will tend to prove or disprove the fact of consequence.  The statute would not specifically obligate the moving party to present “specific facts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought by the demand” if mere relevance sufficed.  As such, Plaintiff’s motion to compel further action and the accompanying request for monetary sanctions are DENIED.

ORDERS

1)      Plaintiff Gloria Gonzalez-Karch’s Motion to Compel Defendant Hollywood Park Management Company, LLC’s Further Responses to Plaintiff’s Request for Production of Documents, Set One, is DENIED.

2)      Plaintiff Gloria Gonzalez-Karch’s Request for Monetary Sanctions is DENIED.

3)      Plaintiff is ordered to give notice of this Court’s ruling.

(1)   DEFENDANT AND PROSPECTIVE CROSS-COMPLAINANT BLUE HORIZON LOGISTICS, INC.’S MOTION TO FILE CROSS-COMPLAINT

 

MOVING PARTY:                Defendant, Blue Horizon Logistics, Inc.

RESPONDING PARTY:       Plaintiff, Fenchem, Inc.

 

(1)   Defendant and Prospective Cross-Complainant Blue Horizon Logistics, Inc.’s Motion to File Cross-Complaint is GRANTED pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 428.50.

The Court considers the moving papers filed on July 21, 2026, the opposition brief filed on August 27, 2026, and the reply brief filed on September 1, 2026.

BACKGROUND

Factual Background

On June 6, 2024, plaintiff Fenchem, Inc. (“Plaintiff”) filed the Complaint against defendants Blue Horizon Logistics, Incorporated (“Defendant”) and DOES 1 through 25.

On April 9, 2025, the clerk of the Court entered default against Defendant.  However, on August 25, 2025, Defendant filed its Answer.

On April 25, 2025, Plaintiff filed the First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) against Defendant and DOES 1 through 25.  The FAC includes the following causes of action: (1) Breach of Bailment; (2) Breach of Contracts; (3) Negligence; and (4) Conversion.

LEGAL STANDARD

A cross-complaint is compulsory if it is transactionally related to the subject matter of the complaint.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 426.10.)  “To be considered a compulsory cross-complaint, a related cause of action must have existed at the time of the service of [the] answer to [the] complaint.”  (Crocker Nat. Bank v. Emerald (1990) 221 Cal.App.3d 852, 864 (Crocker).)  The late filing of a motion for leave to file a compulsory cross-complaint, “absent some evidence of bad faith is insufficient evidence to support denial of the motion.”  (Silver Organizations Ltd. v. Frank (1990) 217 Cal.App.3d 94, 101.)  “Permission to file a permissive cross-complaint is solely within the trial court’s discretion.”  (Crocker, supra, 221 Cal.App.3d at p. 864.)

“A party who fails to plead a cause of action subject to the requirements of this article, whether through oversight, inadvertence, mistake, neglect, or other cause, may apply to the court for leave to amend his pleading, or to file a cross-complaint, to assert such cause at any time during the course of the action.  The court, after notice to the adverse party, shall grant, upon such terms as may be just to the parties, leave to amend the pleading, or to file the cross-complaint, to assert such cause if the party who failed to plead the cause acted in good faith.  This subdivision shall be liberally construed to avoid forfeiture of causes of action.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 426.50.)  The “article” mentioned pertains to “Compulsory Cross-Complaints,” so this section is specifically applicable to compulsory cross-complaints.

Code of Civil Procedure section 428.10 provides, as follows: “A party against whom a cause of action has been asserted in a complaint or cross-complaint may file a cross-complaint setting forth either or both of the following: (a) Any cause of action he has against any of the parties who filed the complaint or cross-complaint against him.  …  ¶  (b) Any cause of action he has against a person alleged to be liable thereon, whether or not such person is already a party to the action, if the cause of action asserted in his cross-complaint (1) arises out of the same transaction, occurrence, or series of transactions or occurrences as the cause brought against him or (2) asserts a claim, right, or interest in the property or controversy which is the subject of the cause brought against him.”

Code of Civil Procedure section 428.50 provides, as follows: “(a) A party shall file a cross-complaint against any of the parties who filed the complaint or cross-complaint against him or her before or at the same time as the answer to the complaint or cross-complaint.  (b) Any other cross-complaint may be filed at any time before the court has set a date for trial.  (c) A party shall obtain leave of court to file any cross-complaint except one filed within the time specified in subdivision (a) or (b).  Leave may be granted in the interest of justice at any time during the course of the action.”

DISCUSSION

Defendant moves to file its proposed cross-complaint pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 426.50 and 428.50.  (Motion, p. 3:15-16.)  The proposed cross-complaint presents two causes of action: (1) Breach of Contract and (2) Open Book Account.  Defendant asserts that “[t]he attached proposed Cross-Complaint alleges a breach of contract and common counts seeking warehousing charges [totaling] $38,870 dating back to January 2022 and continuing to date because the property has remained in the Blue Horizon warehouse.  An additional estimated $10,000 for the removal of the property is also sought.”  (Motion, p. 4:6-9.)

Defendant does not clarify whether its proposed cross-complaint is permissive or compulsory.  However, it quotes a non-published trial case that only discusses permissive cross-complaints.  (Motion, pp. 3:17-4:5.)  Thus, the Court will treat Defendant’s proposed cross-complaint as permissive.  Because the proposed cross-complaint is deemed permissive, Code of Civil Procedure section 426.50 is inapplicable, as it pertains solely to compulsory cross-complaints.  Consequently, the Court will grant leave to file the proposed cross-complaint only if it serves the interests of justice, in accordance with Code of Civil Procedure section 428.50, subdivision (c).  The Court determines that the interests of justice support the filing of the proposed cross-complaint.

Defendant contends, “At the time of the filing of its Answer to the Complaint, Blue Horizon was under the impression that its unpaid charges would operate as a set-off to the Complaint’s assertion of damages.  See California Code of Civil Procedure §431.70.  That impression has since been changed.  Blue Horizon now believes that its warehousing charges must be protected and its opportunity for an affirmative recovery should be preserved.”  (Motion, p. 3:10-14.)  Furthermore, Defendant asserts that it sought a resolution of the alleged unpaid charges and a mutual release during settlement discussions; however, following the unsuccessful mediation held on July 10, it now intends to pursue damages for these alleged unpaid charges through the cross-complaint.  (Motion, p. 3:8-9; Reply, p. 2:14-16.)  The Court finds that denying Defendant the opportunity to present these claims would not serve the interests of justice, particularly given that Plaintiff has not established any prejudice to its claims.

Plaintiff argues that: (1) “[t]he proposed Cross-Complaint is vague and ambiguous as to the time that Plaintiff allegedly refused to remove the materials from Defendant’s warehouse[,]” rendering Defendant’s cross-complaint meritless; and (2) the proposed cross-complaint is being filed to gain an advantage in settlement negotiations, indicating bad faith.  (Opp., pp. 2:20-3:2.)  The Court finds the first argument irrelevant and disagrees with the second.

Regarding the first assertion, whether a claim possesses merit is not pertinent to these proceedings, as such matters are more suitably addressed through a demurrer.  Concerning the second assertion, the Court observes that Plaintiff has provided no supporting evidence for the claim that the intent of the proposed cross-complaint is to ensure that Defendant gains an advantage in settlement negotiations.  The filing, by itself, does not imply that it was employed as a litigation tactic to enhance Defendant’s position.  Rather, following the unsuccessful mediation efforts, Defendant opted to pursue its claim through an alternative avenue.

Accordingly, the Court GRANTS Defendant’s motion to file the proposed cross-complaint.

ORDERS

1)      Defendant and Prospective Cross-Complainant Blue Horizon Logistics, Inc.’s Motion to File Cross-Complaint is GRANTED.

2)      Defendant is ordered to provide notice of this Court’s ruling.

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

OBJECTIONS

GT’s Objection and Request for an Order to Show Cause

GT objects to the “amended opposition” brief submitted by Plaintiff on September 8, 2026, on the basis that it was filed without prior authorization from the Court.  (Obj., p. 2:1-19.)  Furthermore, GT requests that the Court “issue an order to show cause why sanctions should not be imposed based on the time and expense GT Channel has had to undertake in filing this Objection.”  (Obj., p. 2:19-20.)  The Court SUSTAINS this objection and strikes the sur-reply, labeled as an “amended opposition.”  However, the Court DENIES GT’s request to issue an order to show cause.

“Unless otherwise ordered or specifically provided by law, all moving and supporting papers shall be served and filed at least 16 court days before the hearing.  …  All papers opposing a motion so noticed shall be filed with the court and a copy served on each party at least nine court days, and all reply papers at least five court days before the hearing.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1005, subd. (b).)  Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1005, subdivision (b), only moving papers, opposition papers, and reply papers are permitted; this section does not authorize further briefing in response to a reply brief following its submission.  However, the Court agrees that a party may amend their opposition or reply brief if it is filed before the statutory deadlines set forth in Code of Civil Procedure section 1005, subdivision (b).

In this instance, the Court determines that Plaintiff did not merely file an amended opposition brief.  Instead, Plaintiff filed a supplemental brief that not only revised the opposition brief but also included arguments that directly address and reference the reply brief.  (See Amend. Opp., pp. 9:14-20, 14:6-12, 15:14-22.)  This transforms the brief from an amended opposition into a sur-reply, which the Court had not approved.

Consequently, the Court will not consider Plaintiff’s sur-reply filed on September 8, 2026, during its deliberations and strikes the document.

Regarding GT’s request for the Court to issue an order to show cause, the Court notes that GT makes this request under Caldwell v. Samuels Jewelers (1990) 222 Cal.App.3d 970, 978 (“Caldwell”), and California Rules of Court, rule 2.30.  (Obj., p. 7:9-14.)  However, neither the cited case nor the rule provides the Court with the authority to issue an order to show cause under the present circumstances.

Caldwell discusses a trial court’s ability “to ‘make rules for its own government and the government of its officers’ ” under Government Code section 68070.  (Caldwell, supra, 222 Cal.App.3d at p. 978.)  It does not support the assertion that “[t]rial courts possess broad authority to sanction violations of court orders, local rules, and applicable procedural rules.”  (Obj., p. 7:9-10.)  Given that this matter does not involve a government entity, Caldwell is inapplicable.

California Rules of Court, rule 2.30(b) permits the Court to “order a person, after written notice and an opportunity to be heard, to pay reasonable monetary sanctions to the court or an aggrieved person, or both, for failure without good cause to comply with the applicable rules.”  Notably, these specific rules are confined to the California Rules of Court and do not extend to other statutes.  As confirmed by California Rules of Court, rule 2.30(a), “[t]his sanctions rule applies to the rules in the California Rules of Court relating to general civil cases, unlawful detainer cases, probate proceedings, civil proceedings in the appellate division of the superior court, and small claims cases.”

GT contends that Plaintiff violated “Code of Civil Procedure section 1005(b) and this Court’s June 11, 2026, Order,” neither of which constitutes a California Rule of Court.  (Obj., p. 7:16-17.)

Accordingly, the Court DENIES GT’s request for the issuance of an order to show cause.

LEGAL STANDARD

A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable.  (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)  “To survive a demurrer, the complaint need only allege facts sufficient to state a cause of action; each evidentiary fact that might eventually form part of the plaintiff’s proof need not be alleged.”  (C.A. v. William S. Hart Union High School Dist. (2012) 53 Cal.4th 861, 872.)  For the purpose of testing the sufficiency of the cause of action, the demurrer admits the truth of all material facts properly pleaded.  (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 966-967.)  A demurrer “does not admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.”  (Daar v. Yellow Cab Co. (1967) 67 Cal.2d 695, 713.)

A pleading is uncertain if it is ambiguous or unintelligible.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (f).)  A demurrer for uncertainty may lie if the failure to label the parties and claims renders the complaint so confusing that the defendant cannot tell what he or she is supposed to respond to.  (Williams v. Beechnut Nutrition Corp. (1986) 185 Cal.App.3d 135, 139, fn. 2.)  However, “[a] demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.”  (Khoury v. Maly’s of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.)

MEET AND CONFER REQUIREMENT

Before filing a demurrer, the moving party is required to meet and confer in person, by telephone, or via video conference with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to the motion, to determine whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer or motion to strike.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a).)  The parties are to meet and confer at least five days before the date the responsive pleading is due.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(2).)  Thereafter, the moving party shall file and serve a declaration detailing their meet and confer efforts.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(3).)  A determination by the court that the meet and confer process was inadequate shall not be grounds to overrule or sustain a demurrer, or to grant or deny a motion to strike.  (Code Civil Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(4).)

On July 31, 2026, GT’s attorney, Dustin A. Linden, spoke with Plaintiff by phone regarding GT’s intention to file a demurrer.  (Linden Decl., ¶ 2.)  In this conversation, Linden outlined the specific grounds for the anticipated demurrer, to which Plaintiff expressed opposition.  (Linden Decl., ¶ 2.)  The parties were unable to resolve their disagreement.  (Linden Decl., ¶ 2.)

Consequently, the Court finds that GT sufficiently met and conferred in good faith prior to filing this demurrer.

DISCUSSION

GT demurs to Plaintiff’s cause of action for breach of contract on three grounds: (1) “Plaintiff never delivered his Loan Request to GT Channel[;]” (2) “Plaintiff never separately mailed copies of the Loan Request to SEEEN Plc’s London address or designated legal counsel, as the Agreement expressly requires[;]” and (3) Plaintiff’s CPA Letter “identifies no tax year, calculates no gain or loss, and applies no applicable tax rate, among other deficiencies.”  (Dem., pp. 7:14, 18-20, 8:4-5.)  The Court agrees with the first argument, determining that Plaintiff does not provide sufficient facts to demonstrate that the second condition precedent has been satisfied.  As a result, the Court will refrain from addressing the merits of GT’s remaining arguments.

First Cause of Action – Breach of Contract

“[T]he elements of a cause of action for breach of contract are (1) the existence of the contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance, (3) defendant’s breach, and (4) the resulting damages to the plaintiff.”  (Oasis West Realty, LLC v. Goldman (2011) 51 Cal.4th 811, 821, citing Reichert v. General Ins. Co. of America (1968) 68 Cal.2d 822, 830.)  “Ordinarily a plaintiff cannot recover on a contract without alleging and proving performance or waiver of performance of conditions precedent.”  (Sosin v. Richardson (1962) 210 Cal.App.2d 258, 264.)  “[A] condition precedent is either an act of a party that must be performed or an uncertain event that must happen before the contractual right accrues or the contractual duty arises.”  (Platt Pacific, Inc. v. Andelson (1993) 6 Cal.4th 307, 313.)

“It is elementary a plaintiff suing for breach of contract must prove it has performed all conditions on its part or that it was excused from performance.”  (Consolidated World Investments, Inc. v. Lido Preferred Ltd. (1992) 9 Cal.App.4th 373, 380, citing Reichert v. General Ins. Co. of America (1968) 68 Cal.2d 822, 830.)  “This requirement can be satisfied by allegations in general terms.  It is sufficient for a plaintiff to simply allege that he has ‘duly performed all the conditions on his part.’  (Code of Civ. Proc., § 457.)  However, this rule is subject to two important caveats[.]  ¶  First, where the condition is an event, as distinguished from an act to be performed by the plaintiff, a specific allegation of the happening of the condition is a necessary part of pleading the defendant’s breach.  [Citations.]  Second, general pleadings are controlled by specific allegations.  Thus, a general allegation of due performance will not suffice if the plaintiff also sets forth what has actually occurred and such specific facts do not constitute due performance.  [Citation.]”  (Careau & Co. v. Security Pacific Business Credit, Inc. (1990) 222 Cal.App.3d 1371, 1389-1390.)

Plaintiff claims that the GT breached the Employment Agreement “by failing to fund the requested loan within five business days as required by Schedule 2, Section 2.”  (SAC, ¶ 97.)

The Employment Agreement, specifically Schedule 2, Section 1, stipulates the following regarding Loan Requests:

“At any time or from time to time on or before October 31, 2025, Executive may deliver to the Company a request in writing (each, a “Loan Request”) that Executive be advanced an amount of funds specified in such Loan Request to enable Executive to pay (i) any California income tax owing as a result of the receipt of Consideration Shares (as defined in the Merger Agreement) in the GT Merger (as defined in the Merger Agreement) by Executive or any affiliate thereof and (ii) any interest, penalties, charges or fees with respect thereto (clauses (i) and (ii), collectively, “Tax Obligation”), which Loan Request will be accompanied by a letter from Executive’s accountant confirming that such Tax Obligation is due and payable and setting forth the calculation thereof.  In no event shall the aggregate Loan Requests exceed $440,000.00.  Executive will endeavor to give the Company as much advance notice of his intent to deliver a Loan Request as may be reasonably practicable.”  (SAC, Exh. A, Schedule 2, § 1.)

In this context, “Company” refers to GT, while “Executive” refers to Plaintiff.  Consequently, Plaintiff may only receive a loan if the following conditions are met: (1) Plaintiff possesses a Tax Obligation arising from the receipt of the Consideration Shares in the GT Merger, (2) Plaintiff submits a written Loan Request to GT, and (3) the Loan Request includes a letter from Plaintiff’s accountant confirming that the Tax Obligation is due and payable, accompanied by a calculation of that obligation.

Second Condition Precedent

In the SAC, Plaintiff presents the following allegations to establish that the second condition precedent has been satisfied:

“On October 7, 2022, Plaintiff’s Loan Request letter was dated, and on October 8, 2022, Plaintiff transmitted it by email to Adrian Hargrave at Adrian@seeen.com.  The Loan Request was accompanied by the CPA Letter as required by Schedule 2, Section 1.  The Loan Request stated: ‘I am requesting a loan from Seeen plc per schedule 2 of the employment agreement with Scott Schlichter dated September 10th, 2019,’ and further stated, ‘Please see attached letters as specified in the provisions of schedule 2.’  The Loan Request requested $262,500.  Plaintiff’s Loan Request was timely: Schedule 2, Section 1 expressly permitted Plaintiff to deliver a Loan Request ‘[a]t any time or from time to time on or before October 31, 2025,’ and Plaintiff’s request fell well within that contractual period.  A true and correct copy of the October 7, 2022 Loan Request letter is attached hereto as Exhibit E and incorporated by reference.”  (SAC, ¶ 57.)  “Adrian@seeen.com address was the operative communications channel that Defendants themselves had established and consistently used for all four years of dealings with Plaintiff on matters governed by the Employment Agreement, including matters governed by GT’s obligations to Plaintiff.  (SAC, ¶ 58.)

“On October 11, 2022, Plaintiff sent a follow-up communication confirming his loan request, again to Hargrave at his Adrian@seeen.com email address.  Defendants received this communication but did not respond.”  (SAC, ¶ 60.)

“On April 14, 2023, Plaintiff's prior counsel, Erik Gunderson of Gunderson Employment Law, sent a formal demand letter to Hargrave at his Adrian@seeen.com email address and to Hargrave at SEEEN plc’s mailing address, demanding payment of the full $440,000 loan facility, plus $13,279.45 in accrued interest, together constituting a demand for specific performance of the loan facility.  Counsel’s letter further set the rate of continuing daily interest at $120.55 per day beginning April 15, 2023, and gave Defendants until April 28, 2023, to respond.  A true and correct copy of this demand letter is attached hereto as Exhibit F and incorporated by reference.”  (SAC, ¶ 64.)

The Court has thoroughly examined both the formal Loan Request and the demand letter.  It observes that both documents are addressed to Adrian Hargrave in his capacity as Chief Executive Officer of SEEEN plc.  (SAC, Exhs. E-F.)  Notably, neither document references GT nor Adrian Hargrave in his capacity as a member of GT.  Therefore, it can be inferred that these communications, along with any subsequent correspondence, were intended for SEEEN plc rather than for GT.  Consequently, contrary to Plaintiff’s assertions, the Loan Request and all related communications were served solely on Adrian Hargrave in his role as CEO of SEEEN plc.  Accordingly, since Plaintiff did not provide notice to GT, there are insufficient factual allegations to demonstrate that the second condition precedent has been satisfied.

As discussed in the previous ruling, the Court finds that Section 17 of the Employment Agreement requires Plaintiff to serve a copy of his Loan Request and any additional notices to “GT Channel at 1730 E Holly Ave, El Segundo, CA 90245, Attn: President, email: taro@gtchannel.com.”  (SAC, Exh. A, § 17; June 11, 2026, Minute Order, p. 16.)  Furthermore, Plaintiff is required to send a copy to Entertainment AI, plc—which is now known as SEEEN plc—specifically to “Blockchain Worldwide plc, 4th Floor, 43-44 Albemarle Street, London W1S 4JJ, UK, Attention: Rodger Sargent.”  (SAC, Exh. A, § 17; June 11, 2026, Minute Order, p. 16.)

Plaintiff contends that Section 17 does not apply to the Loan Request for the following reasons: (1) Schedule 2 “does not require delivery in accordance with Section 17, does not make delivery by other means ineffective, and does not condition the Company’s funding obligation on the manner of transmittal[;]” (2) “[t]he Agreement incorporates Section 17 six times, and every one is a termination notice[;]” and (3) “GT Channel’s notice-compliance authority presupposes that the notice clause applies.”  (Opp., pp. 8:4, 9:1, 25.)  The Court finds these arguments regarding Section 17 to be disingenuous and barred by the principle of judicial estoppel.

“Judicial estoppel precludes a party from gaining an advantage by taking one position and then seeking a second advantage by taking an incompatible position.  [Citations.]  ...  [Citation.]  The doctrine [most appropriately] applies when: (1) the same party has taken two positions; (2) the positions were taken in judicial or quasi-judicial administrative proceedings; (3) the party was successful in asserting the first position (i.e., the tribunal adopted the position or accepted it as true); (4) the two positions are totally inconsistent; and (5) the first position was not taken as a result of ignorance, fraud, or mistake.  [Citations.]”  (MW Erectors, Inc. v. Niederhauser Ornamental & Metal Works Co., Inc. (2005) 36 Cal.4th 412, 433, internal citations and quotations omitted.)

In his opposition to the demurrer regarding the FAC, the Court noted that “Plaintiff acknowledges that the Employment Agreement required him to serve his Loan Request and any additional notices mandated by the agreement to ‘GT Channel at 1730 E Holly Ave, El Segundo, CA 90245, Attn: President, email: taro@gtchannel.com.’  (Opp., p. 9:26-27; FAC, Exh. A, § 17.)”  (June 11, 2026, Minute Order, p. 16.)  Plaintiff explicitly referenced Section 17 to substantiate his assertion that he fulfilled the requirements of the second condition precedent.  (Opp. to Dem. re: FAC, pp. 9:15-10:5.)  The Court concurred that Section 17 is applicable to the notice requirement of the second condition precedent.  (June 11, 2026, Minute Order, p. 16.)

Nonetheless, in the present opposition brief, Plaintiff adopts a contradictory position.  (Opp., pp. 8:1-10:21.)  There is no clarification in the facts or Plaintiff’s own documents to explain this inconsistency, nor is there any indication that the initial position was taken due to ignorance, fraud, or mistake.

Consequently, the Court finds that judicial estoppel precludes Plaintiff from asserting a position that conflicts with his previous assertion, which the Court accepted in its ruling.  Therefore, Section 17 is still applicable to the second condition precedent.

Furthermore, if the Court deems Section 17 applicable, Plaintiff contends that it was appropriate to serve Adrian Hargrave in his capacity as CEO of SEEEN plc for the following reasons: (1) “Hargrave was the only officer of GT Channel who could receive” the Loan Request; (2) Hargrave “acted as GT Channel’s officer, on GT Channel’s own corporate documents[;]” (3) Hargrave is GT’s agent; (4) “Hargrave responded in writing on May 17, 2023” to Plaintiff’s Loan Request; and (5) “no provision of the Agreement names any individual as the person to receive a Loan Request.”  (Opp., pp. 11:16, 19, 12:1-15, 17, 13:3-4.)  Nonetheless, the Court finds these arguments to be unconvincing.  None of the points presented clarify why Plaintiff did not address the Loan Request to Hargrave in his capacity as a member of GT.

Plaintiff next contends that “[p]erformance was excused by GT Channel’s own conduct.”  (Opp., p. 13:17.)  Specifically, Plaintiff alleges that the actions of “firing the President, deactivating the email, and closing the office—made compliance with Section 17 impossible.”  (Reply, p. 10:12-13.)  Plaintiff argues that GT should have updated the designation of the notice recipient, as he believes that Section 17 mandates such an adjustment.  (Opp., p. 14:11-18.) 

Plaintiff seeks to rely on J&A Mash & Barrell, LLC v. Superior Court of Fresno County (2022) 74 Cal.App.5th 1, 41 (J&A), Section 245 of the Restatement Second of Contracts, Jacobs v. Tenneco West, Inc. (1986) 186 Cal.App.3d 1413, 1417 (Jacobs), and Civil Code section 1511 to substantiate the claim that their performance of the conditions precedent was excused.  (Opp., pp. 13:18-14:3.)  However, none of these precedents support Plaintiff’s position.

In J&A, supra, 74 Cal.App.5th at p. 41, the Court of Appeal stated, “ ‘Each party to a contract has a duty to do everything that the contract presupposes that he will do to accomplish its purpose [citations] and a duty not to prevent or hinder performance by the other party.’  (Bewick v. Mecham (1945) 26 Cal.2d 92, 99, 156 P.2d 757.)  In Bewick, the defendant’s actions ‘prevented the determination of the purchase price by the method contemplated by the contract.’  (Ibid.)  ‘Defendant’s obvious purpose was to make the contract inoperative and to prevent plaintiff from seeking specific performance after the price was determined by the appraisers.  A party who prevents fulfillment of a condition of his own obligation commits a breach of contract [citations] and cannot rely on such condition to defeat his liability.’  (Ibid.)”

Consequently, the Court of Appeal appears to hold that a party cannot fail to perform their own obligations to evade their own liability.  Given that Plaintiff bears responsibility for the condition precedent, this case is not applicable.

In Jacobs, supra, 186 Cal.App.3d at p. 1417, the Court of Appeals stated, “Pursuant to section 245 of the Restatement Second of Contracts, which superseded section 295 of the Restatement of Contracts, it appears that the trial court ruled correctly.  Section 245 states, ‘Where a party’s breach by non-performance contributes materially to the non-occurrence of a condition of one of his duties, the non-occurrence is excused.’  (Rest.2d Contracts, § 245, p. 259.)  Comment b to this section discusses the burden of proof.  This comment states: ‘Although it is implicit in the rule that the condition has not occurred, it is not necessary to show that it would have occurred but for the lack of cooperation.  It is only required that the breach has contributed materially to the non-occurrence.  Nevertheless, if it can be shown that the condition would not have occurred regardless of the lack of cooperation, the failure of performance did not contribute materially to its non-occurrence and the rule does not apply.  The burden of showing this is properly thrown on the party in breach.’ ”

As GT asserts, “The SAC alleges no GT Channel breach related to the second condition precedent; its only alleged breach is refusal to fund the loan under Schedule 2.  Failure to update Section 17’s recipient information is not a breach: Section 17 gives GT Channel the power—not the duty—to redesignate its notice address, and Plaintiff cites no provision stating otherwise.  Without a relevant breach, Section 245 does not apply, and Plaintiff is not excused from satisfying the second condition.”  (Reply, p. 11:23-27.)  As Plaintiff fails to provide a legal analysis demonstrating how GT’s alleged failure to update the notice recipient qualifies as a breach, the Court cannot find so much.

Finally, the Court observes that Civil Code Section 1511 specifically pertains to circumstances involving creditors and debtors.  Plaintiff does not assert that either party qualifies as a creditor or debtor; thus, the Court will not extend the applicability of this statute beyond its intended scope.

Accordingly, GT’s demurrer to the first cause of action for breach of contract is SUSTAINED.
