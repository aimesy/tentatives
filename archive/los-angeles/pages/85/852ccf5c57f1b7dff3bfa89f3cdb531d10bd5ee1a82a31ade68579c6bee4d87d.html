I.                   INTRODUCTION

This case involves the 350 South Los Angeles Partnership (“The Partnership”), a California Limited Liability Partnership that was created to invest in real estate in downtown Los Angeles. Plaintiffs Mohammed Siry (“Mohammed”), Siry Investments L.P. (“Siry Investments”), and Siry Brothers Partnership (“Siry Brothers”) (together, “Plaintiffs”), allege that another limited partner of the Partnership, Saeed Farkhondehpour, individually and as trustee of the 1993 Farkhondehpour Family Trust (“Farkhondehpour”), and his agents, acting through the Partnership’s general partner, have breached the Partnership’s agreement and their fiduciary duties by refusing to turn over management of the Partnership despite a vote to change the management and despite Farkhondehpour’s express agreement to do so; and also by engaging in or facilitating a series of illicit financial transactions in addition to committing fraud and theft.

On February 1, 2021, Plaintiffs initiated this action. On June 17, 2021, Plaintiffs filed a first amended complaint (“FAC”) against Defendants Farkhondehpour, Farkhondehpour Family Trust (“Farkhondehpour Trust”), The Partnership, Downtown Acquisition Group, Inc. (“DAG”), and Investment Consultants LLC (“Investment Consultants”) (together, “Defendants”).

On February 7, 2022, Farkhondehpour, Farkhondehpour Trust, and Investment Consultants filed a cross-complaint against Siry Investments, Siry Brothers, Mohammed, Morad Ben Neman (“Morad”), Simon Neman (“Simon”), Brian Neman (“Brian”) (“the Nemans”), Jessica Kilburn (“Jessica”) and Universal Bank (“Universal Bank”). On March 17, 2022, Cross-Complainants filed a first-amended cross complaint.

On May 4, 2022, the Nemans and Jessica filed a cross-complaint against Farkhondehpour, Investment Consultants, The Partnership and DAG.

On September 2, 2025, the Court reviewed Plaintiff’s first amended complaint and Nemans’ and Jessica’s cross-complaint and found that the claims “asserted are derivative in nature; that is, they allege, in essence, harm to the corporate entity (here an LLC) as the result of Defendants’ and Cross-Defendants’ alleged acts. As such, they cannot survive as direct claims, even after the termination of the LLC. Nor can they be brought at the present time as derivative claims, given the termination of the LLC.” The Court granted leave to amend “for the sole purpose of attempting to state viable direct claims.” (See Sept. 2, 2025 Minute Order.)

On September 22, 2025, Plaintiffs filed a second amended complaint (“SAC”), alleging causes of action for (1) Breach of Written Contract, (2) Breach of Fiduciary Duty, (3) Fraud, (4) Statutory Claim for Improper Disposition of Property, (5) Declaratory Relief, (6) Specific Performance, and (7) Accounting.

On September 23, 2025, the Nemans and Jessica filed a first amended cross-complaint (“FACC”) against Farkhondehpour, Farkhondehpour Trust, The Partnership, DAG, and Investment Consultants, alleging causes of action for (1) Breach of Written Contract, (2) Breach of Fiduciary Duty, (3) Fraud – Concealment, (4) Statutory Claim for Improper Disposition of Property, (5) Declaratory Relief, and (6) Accounting.

On January 26, 2026, the Court heard Defendants/Cross-Defendants Farkhondehpour, Farkhondehpour Trust, The Partnership, DAG, and Investment Consultants’ demurrer to the SAC. The Court sustained the demurrer in its entirety. The Court only granted leave to amend as to the seventh cause of action for Accounting. The demurrer was otherwise sustained without leave to amend.

On January 30, 2026, Plaintiffs filed to dismiss the seventh cause of action for Accounting without prejudice. The Court Clerk entered dismissal on February 3, 2026.

On March 13, 2026, Defendants/Cross-Defendants Farkhondehpour, Farkhondehpour Trust, The Partnership, DAG, and Investment Consultants filed the instant motion to dismiss Plaintiffs’ SAC.

On July 30, 2026, Plaintiffs filed an opposition.

On August 4, 2026, Defendants/Cross-Defendants filed a reply.

II.                LEGAL STANDARD

“An action shall be brought to trial within five years after the action is commenced against the defendant.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 583.310.)  If an action is not brought to trial within the required time period, it is subject to mandatory dismissal.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 583.360.)  The parties may extend the time by written stipulation or by oral agreement made in open court, if entered in the minutes or a transcript is made.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 583.330.)  The rules governing dismissal for delay in prosecution do not abrogate or otherwise affect the principles of waiver and estoppel.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 583.140.)

Also, the court may dismiss a complaint as to a defendant when “after a demurrer to the complaint is sustained with leave to amend, the plaintiff fails to amend it within the time allowed by the court and either party moves for dismissal.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 581(f)(2).)

III.             DISCUSSION

Defendants/Cross-Defendants move to dismiss with prejudice Plaintiffs’ SAC pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 583.310, 583.360, and 581, on the grounds that Plaintiffs failed to bring this case to trial within five years of commencing their action, and that there is no remaining cause of action under the SAC because the Court sustained demurrer without leave to amend as to all causes of action except for the accounting, which Plaintiffs dismissed.

First, Plaintiffs do not oppose the Court dismissing the SAC pursuant to section 583.360, the five-year mandatory dismissal statute. The five-year period “run[s] from the date of the originally filed complaint.” (Davalos v. Cnty. of Los Angeles (1983) 142 Cal.App.3d 57, 63.) The period commenced in this matter on February 1, 2021 when Plaintiffs filed their original complaint. (Anand Decl., ¶ 3, Ex. 1.) Plaintiffs admitted through their attorney on January 16 and 30, 2026, that their deadline to bring this action to trial would run on February 2, 2026. (Id. ¶ 9.) Defendants declined Plaintiffs’ request to extend the deadline. (Id. ¶ 8, Ex. 4.) Plaintiffs thus did not bring this case to trial within five years of filing their original complaint. Dismissal pursuant to section 583.310 is proper. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 581(g) [“The court may dismiss without prejudice the complaint in whole, or as to that defendant, when dismissal is made under the applicable provisions of Chapter 1.5 (commencing with Section 583.110)”] (emphasis added).)

However, Defendants/Cross-Defendants also seek dismissal under section 581(f)(2). Unlike dismissal under section 581.310, this type of dismissal operates with prejudice, and is considered an adjudication on the merits. (Roybal v. Univ. Ford (1989) 207 Cal.App.3d 1080, 1085–86 [“[A] dismissal with prejudice by plaintiff of its action is a bar to a subsequent action on the same cause; otherwise there would be no meaning to the ‘with prejudice’ feature. ‘A dismissal with prejudice terminates the action and the rights of the parties are affected by it. It is a final judgment in favor of defendants....' [Citation.]”].)

 Defendants/Cross-Defendants argue that dismissal is warranted under section 581(f)(2) because the Court sustained demurrer without leave to amend the first six causes of action in the SAC and Plaintiffs dismissed the seventh cause of action for an accounting instead of amending. (Anand Decl., ¶¶ 7, 9-10, Ex. 5.) Defendants/Cross-Defendants contend that since no cause of action in the SAC is pending after Plaintiffs dismissed their one remaining claim, dismissal of the SAC is the next step to enter judgment.

Here, on January 26, 2026, the Court sustained Defendants/Cross-Defendants’ demurrer to the SAC. The Court sustained the demurrer without leave to amend as to all causes of action, except the Accounting cause of action. The Court granted thirty (30) days leave to amend as to the Accounting cause of action. (See Jan. 26, 2026 Order.) Instead of amending this cause of action, Plaintiffs elected to dismiss the claim without prejudice on January 30, 2026, as it was their right to do so. (Code Civ. Proc., § 581(c) [“A plaintiff may dismiss his or her complaint, or any cause of action asserted in it, in its entirety, or as to any defendant or defendants, with or without prejudice prior to the actual commencement of trial.”].)

Defendants/Cross-Defendants’ reliance on Wells v. Marina City Props., Inc. (1981) 29 Cal.3d 781 is misplaced. There, the Court heard a general demurrer on August 4, which was sustained with thirty (30) days leave to amend. (Id. at p. 783.) The time to amend was extended to September 26. (Ibid.) The plaintiff virtually went radio silent. (Ibid.) On October 5, past the time allowed for further amendment, the defendants moved for dismissal with prejudice pursuant to the predecessor of Code of Civil Procedure section 581(f)(2). (Ibid.) The next day, the plaintiff filed a request for dismissal of his case without prejudice. (Ibid.) The Court disapproved of this action and concluded “that once a general demurrer is sustained with leave to amend and plaintiff does not so amend within the time authorized by the court or otherwise extended by stipulation or appropriate order, he can no longer voluntarily dismiss his action pursuant to section 581, subdivision 1, even if the trial court has yet to enter a judgment of dismissal on the sustained demurrer.” (Id. at p. 789.)

The circumstances are different here. Plaintiffs elected to dismiss the Accounting cause of action within the time allowed for amendment, only four days after the Court sustained Defendants/Cross-Defendants’ demurrer to the SAC. (Id. at p. 789 [“we note that such right of voluntary dismissal, which is not barred until expiration of plaintiff's time to amend after the sustaining of the demurrer, would also not be impaired prior to a decision sustaining the demurrer.”] (emphasis added).)

The situation is instead similar to Parsons v. Umansky (1994) 28 Cal.App.4th 867. There, the trial court sustained a general demurrer to the entire complaint, allowing ten (10) days leave to amend. (Id. at p. 869.) The plaintiff elected to dismiss his complaint without prejudice. (Ibid.) Learning of the dismissal order, the defendants moved the court to enter an order dismissing the action with prejudice pursuant to section 581(f)(2). (Ibid.) The trial court granted the defendants' request and later denied plaintiff’s motion to vacate that order. (Ibid.) The Parsons court held this to be erroneous. (Ibid.) The Court held that a “plaintiff may decline to amend a complaint and instead obtain a dismissal without prejudice during the time granted leave to amend following the sustaining of a demurrer.” (Ibid.) 

Because Plaintiffs here elected to dismiss the seventh cause of action for Accounting within the time the Court allowed for amendment, relief under section 581(f)(2) is unavailable and the Court otherwise does not exercise its discretion in affording section 581(f)(2) relief. Cano v. Glover (2006) 143 Cal.App.4th 326, 329 ["The phrase `may dismiss' [in section 581(f)(2)] means discretionary dismissal.”].)

However, given that the Court sustained Defendants/Cross-Defendants’ demurrer without leave to amend as to the SAC’s first through sixth causes of action, these claims are dismissed with prejudice. (Metabyte, Inc. v. Technicolor S.A. (2023) 94 Cal.App.5th 265, 275 [“a dismissal without prejudice is not available if the demurrer is sustained without leave to amend[.]”].)

IV.             CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, Defendants’/Cross-Defendants’ motion to dismiss is GRANTED

in part DENIED in part. The Court orders Defendants/Cross-Defendants to submit a proposed form of judgment consistent with this opinion.

I.                   INTRODUCTION

This case arises from Plaintiff Berta Mirano (“Berta”) getting injured while using a

treadmill at an LA Fitness Club, when the treadmill suddenly accelerated after five minutes of use.

On April 30, 2021, Plaintiffs Berta and Guillermo Munoz Mirano (“Guillermo”) filed a complaint against Defendants Fitness International, LLC (“Fitness ”), LA Fitness (“LA Fitness”) and Pacific Investors Group (“Pacific”) (“Defendants”), alleging causes of action for (1) Negligence, (2) Premises Liability, and (3) Loss of Consortium.

            On September 28, 2023, the Honorable Anne Hwang granted Fitness International’s motion for summary judgment on the grounds that there existed an executed waiver that served as a complete defense.

            On September 11, 2025, the Court of Appeal reversed on the grounds that there exists a triable issue of fact as to the authenticity of Berta’s electronic signature on the waiver. The Court also found that there existed an issue of fact as to whether LA Fitness was reasonably diligent in monitoring and maintaining the treadmill.

            On May 12, 2026, the Court granted Plaintiffs’ request for leave to amend the complaint in light of the Court of Appeal’s reversal.

            On May 18, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the operative first amended complaint (“FAC”), alleging causes of action for (1) Negligence (2) Gross Negligence (3) Premises Liability (4) Products Liability (Strict and/or Negligent) (5) Negligent Hiring, Training, Supervision, and Retention (6) Elder Abuse / Neglect (7) Unfair Competition (Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200 et seq.) and (8) Loss of Consortium.

            On July 1, 2026, Fitness filed the instant Special Motion to Strike pursuant to California’s Anti-SLAPP statute (“anti-SLAPP motion”).

            On July 29, 2026, Plaintiffs filed an opposition.

            On August 4, 2026, Fitness filed a reply.

II.                LEGAL STANDARD

“If a defendant brings a special motion under the anti-SLAPP statute [Code of Civil Procedure Section 425.16] to strike a cause of action, the trial court evaluates that motion using a two-step process: The first examines the nature of the conduct that underlies the plaintiff's allegations to determine whether the conduct is protected by section 425.16; the second assesses the merits of the plaintiff’s claim. [Citation]” (Laker v. Board of Trustees of California State University (2019) 32 Cal.App.5th 745, 759.) 

“The defendant’s first-step burden is to identify the activity each challenged claim rests on and demonstrate that that activity is protected by the anti-SLAPP statute. A ‘claim may be struck only if the speech or petitioning activity¿itself¿is the wrong complained of, and not just evidence of liability or a step leading to some different act for which liability is asserted.’ [Citation.]  To determine whether a claim arises from protected activity, courts must ‘consider the elements of the challenged claim and what actions by the defendant supply those elements and consequently form the basis for liability.’ [Citation.] Courts then must evaluate whether the defendant has shown any of these actions fall within one or more of the four categories of ‘act[s]’ protected by the anti-SLAPP statute. [Citations.]”  (Wilson v. Cable News Network, Inc.¿(2019) 7 Cal.5th 871, 884 [italics in original].) 

“In the first step of the analysis, the trial court determines whether the cause of action ‘arises from’ an ‘‘‘act in furtherance of a person's right of petition or free speech under the United States or California Constitution in connection with a public issue.’’’ [Citation] The first step of the anti-SLAPP analysis ‘turns on two subsidiary questions: (1) What conduct does the challenged cause of action ‘arise[] from’; and (2) is that conduct ‘protected activity’ under the anti-SLAPP statute?’ [Citation]” (Laker, supra, 32 Cal.App.5th. at p.760.)  

“The Supreme Court has clarified that ‘arising from’ means ‘based on.’ [Citation] This element of the first step of the anti-SLAPP analysis is sometimes referred to as the ‘nexus’ requirement. [Citation] Conduct constitutes ‘protected activity,’ if it falls within one of the categories set out in section 425.16, subdivision (e). Section 425.16, subdivision (e), in turn, applies to (1) ‘any written or oral statement or writing made before a legislative, executive, or judicial proceeding, or any other official proceeding authorized by law’; (2) ‘in connection with an issue under consideration or review by a legislative, executive, or judicial body, or any other official proceeding authorized by law’; (3) ‘in a place open to the public or a public forum in connection with an issue of public interest’; or (4) ‘any other conduct in furtherance of the exercise of the constitutional right of petition or the constitutional right of free speech in connection with a public issue or an issue of public interest.’ [Citation] The defendant bringing the anti-SLAPP motion to strike must make a prima facie showing that the allegations that form the basis of the plaintiff's claims arise from conduct that falls under one of these categories. [Citation]” (Ibid.) 

“If the defendant prevails in this step of the analysis, the trial court must then assess the merits of the plaintiff's claim. The Supreme Court has described this second step of the SLAPP analysis as a ‘summary-judgment-like procedure.’ [Citation.]  The plaintiff carries the burden of demonstrating that its claim has ‘at least ‘minimal merit.’’ [Citation.] If the plaintiff is unable to demonstrate that his or her claim has at least minimal merit, then the trial court should deem the cause of action a SLAPP and should strike it. [Citation.]” (Ibid.)   

At this “second stage of an anti-SLAPP hearing, the court may consider affidavits, declarations, and their equivalents if it is reasonably possible the proffered evidence set out in those statements will be admissible at trial. Conversely, if the evidence relied upon cannot be admitted at trial, because it is categorically barred or undisputed factual circumstances show inadmissibility, the court may not consider it in the face of an objection. If an evidentiary objection is made, the plaintiff may attempt to cure the asserted defect or demonstrate the defect is curable.” (Sweetwater Union High School Dist. v. Gilbane Building Co. (2019) 6 Cal.5th 931, 949.) 

The trial court properly considers the evidentiary submissions of both the plaintiff and the defendant, but it may not weigh the credibility or comparative strength of the evidence and must instead simply determine whether the plaintiff’s evidence would, if believed by the trier of fact, be sufficient to result in a judgment for plaintiff. (McGarry v. Univ. of San Diego (2007) 154 Cal.App.4th 97, 108-09.) The court “accept[s] as true the evidence favorable to the plaintiff [citation] and evaluate[s] the defendant’s evidence only to determine if it has defeated that submitted by the plaintiff as a matter of law.’ [Citations.]” (Flatley v. Mauro (2006) 39 Cal.4th 299, 326.) Further, whether the evidence is in conflict, in the context of a motion to strike under the anti-SLAPP statute, if the plaintiff has presented a sufficient pleading and has presented evidence showing that a prima facie case will be established at trial, the plaintiff is entitled to proceed. (Moore v. Shaw (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 182, 193.) Only a minimal showing of merit is required. (Robinzine v. Vicory (2006) 143 Cal.App.4th 1416, 1421.)

V.                DISCUSSION

Fitness filed the instant anti-SLAPP motion as to paragraphs 16, 32, 67, 74, 86, 87 in the

FAC.

1.      First Prong: Protected Activity

“To prevail on an anti-SLAPP motion, the movant must first make ‘a threshold showing the challenged cause of action’ arises from an act in furtherance of the right of petition or free speech in connection with a public issue.” (Varian Medical Systems, Inc. v. Delfino (2005) 35 Cal.4th 180, 192.)  “[T]he focus is on determining what ‘the defendant’s activity [is] that gives rise to his or her asserted liability—and whether that activity constitutes protected speech or petitioning.’ [Citation.]”  (Park v. Board of Trustees of California State University (2017) 2 Cal.5th 1057, 1063.) 

In determining whether an entire cause of action should be stricken from a complaint: “[T]the statutory phrase ‘cause of action ... arising from’ means simply that the defendant's act underlying the plaintiff's cause of action must itself have been an act in furtherance of the right of petition or free speech.” (City of Cotati v. Cashman (2002) 29 Cal.4th 69, 78.) “In the anti-SLAPP context, the critical point is whether the plaintiff's cause of action itself was based on an act in furtherance of the defendant's right of petition or free speech.” (Ibid.) “In terms of whether relief is sought based on allegations of both protected and unprotected activity, “the unprotected activity is disregarded at this stage. If the court determines that relief is sought based on allegations arising from activity protected by statute, the second step is reached. There, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to demonstrate that each challenged claim based on protected activity is legally sufficient and factually substantiated … If not, the claim is stricken.” (Baral v. Schnitt¿(2016) 1 Cal.5th 376, 396.)

“‘The only means specified in section 425.16 by which a moving defendant can satisfy that [‘arising from’] requirement is to demonstrate that the defendant’s conduct by which plaintiff claims to have been injured falls within one of the four categories described in subdivision (e) . . . .’ [Citation.]” (Ibid.) Code of Civil Procedure Section 425.16 subdivision (e), in pertinent part, defines protected acts to include “(1) any written or oral statement or writing made before a legislative, executive, or judicial proceeding, or any other official proceeding authorized by law, (2) any written or oral statement or writing made in connection with an issue under consideration or review by a legislative, executive, or judicial body, or any other official proceeding authorized by law, (3) any written or oral statement or writing made in a place open to the public or a public forum in connection with an issue of public interest, or (4) any other conduct in furtherance of the exercise of the constitutional right of petition or the constitutional right of free speech in connection with a public issue or an issue of public interest.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16(e)(1-4).)   

Pursuant to Baral, “[a]t the first step, the moving defendant bears the burden of identifying all allegations of protected activity, and the claims for relief supported by them.” (Baral, supra, 1 Cal. 5th at p, 396.)

On the first step of the anti-SLAPP analysis, a defendant may introduce extrinsic evidence to meet its initial burden in demonstrating whether the disputed claims arise from protected activity. (Gunn v. Drage (9th Cir. 2023) 65 F.4th 1109, 1118 [“Under California's anti-SLAPP statute, defendants are entitled to rely on their own proffered evidence to show that they have met the prima facie burden of demonstrating protected activity. Indeed, California's anti-SLAPP statute requires courts to consider evidence outside the pleadings at both steps of the analysis.”]; Navellier v. Sletten (2002) 29 Cal.4th 82 [examining declarations and other documents at first prong to determine whether each of the acts “about which plaintiffs complain falls squarely within the plain language of the anti-SLAPP statute”].) The defendant does not have to solely rely on the pleadings, as “[s]uch conclusive deference would be difficult to reconcile with the statutory admonition that courts must look beyond the pleadings to consider any party evidentiary submissions as well.” (Wilson v. Cable News Network, Inc. (2019) 7 Cal.5th 871, 887; see Stewart v. Rolling Stone LLC (2010) 181 Cal.App.4th 664 [“[W]e do not evaluate the first prong of the anti-SLAPP test solely through the lens of a plaintiff's cause of action.”].)

            Here, Fitness contends that Plaintiffs seek damages against it for engaging in the activities of litigation, particularly engaging in written discovery, deposition discovery and the preparation and filing of motions such as the summary judgment motion that was filed by Fitness in this case. Fitness contends that paragraphs 16, 32, 67, 74, 86, 87 allege that Fitness obtained signatures from plaintiff, Berta Mirano, in discovery and used these signature samples to create or alter documents submitted to the Court.

            The allegations at issue provide that “[d]uring this litigation, Defendant and its agents obtained approximately forty (40) specimen signatures from Plaintiffs in discovery, including approximately twenty (20) signature exemplars from 2019, the year Plaintiffs joined the Facility. . . . Plaintiffs never actually signed as such waivers and then presented and relied on those fabricated or altered documents as genuine waivers in support of a motion for summary judgment.” (FAC ¶ 16.) “Defendant’s use of Plaintiffs’ discovery signature specimens to fabricate or alter purported waivers, and its reliance on those documents to attempt to deprive an injured senior of her day in court[.]” (Id. ¶ 32.) “In addition, Defendants, through their officers, directors, managing agents, and authorized representatives, acted with recklessness, oppression, fraud, and malice by, among other things, using Plaintiffs’ signature specimens obtained in discovery to create or alter purported waivers and releases that Berta never knowingly signed, and then relying on those documents in an attempt to bar her claims and deprive an injured elder of her day in court.” (Id. ¶ 67.) “Defendants’ acts and practices were fraudulent in that they misled reasonable consumers by . . . inducing Berta to sign in a small tablet box without disclosing that Defendants would later treat that signature as a broad waiver of liability for Defendants’ own negligence and statutory violations; and fabricating or altering purported waivers using Plaintiffs’ signature specimens and then presenting those documents as genuine in court to defeat an injured elder’s claims.” (Id. ¶ 74.) “During this litigation, Defendants and their agents obtained numerous signature specimens from Plaintiffs and, on information and belief, used those specimens to create or alter purported “waiver” or “release” forms that Berta never knowingly signed as such waivers. Defendants then presented and relied upon those fabricated or altered documents as genuine waivers in support of a motion for summary judgment, in an effort to bar the claims of an injured elder and to deprive her of her day in court.” (Id. ¶ 86.)

            To reiterate, subdivision (e)(1) of the Anti-SLAPP statute protects “any written or oral statement or writing made before a legislative, executive, or judicial proceeding, or any other official proceeding authorized by law.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (e)(1).) There is no denying that Fitness’ activities regarding obtaining discovery and reliance on such discovery in the motion for summary judgment in this case is protected activity. These allegations relate to Fitness’ activities of filing a motion for summary judgment based upon information obtained through pretrial discovery. (Rusheen v. Cohen (2006) 37 Cal.4th 1048, 1056, 39 Cal.Rptr.3d 516, 128 P.3d 713 [anti-SLAPP statute protects “communicative conduct such as the filing, funding, and prosecution of a civil action,” including such acts when “committed by attorneys in representing clients in litigation”].)

Thus, Fitness’ statements made in connection with this case, specifically engaging in pretrial discovery, consequently obtaining Plaintiffs’ “signature specimen,” and subsequently relying on such signatures in Fitness’ motion for summary judgment in this action, are clearly “made before a … legislative … proceeding, or any other official proceeding authorized by law.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (e)(1).) Any statements or conduct in litigation conduct constitute petitioning activity within the meaning of the anti-SLAPP statute. (Briggs v. Eden Council for Home & Opportunity (1999) 19 Cal. 4th 1106, 1116 [“Under the plain terms of the statute it is the context or setting itself that makes the issue a public issue: all that matters is that the First Amendment activity take place in an official proceeding or be made in connection with an issue being reviewed by an official proceeding.”].)

However, the analysis does not end here.

a.       Mixed Causes of Action

While the Court finds that the alleged conduct relating to Fitness’ litigation activities to

be protected activity, Plaintiffs allege other conduct not subject to the instant anti-SLAPP motion, such as Fitness’ failure to adopt and enforce safety protocols for gym equipment, including the subject treadmill that malfunctioned while Berta was using it. (See FAC ¶¶ 18-24.)

However, “a plaintiff [can] not deprive a defendant of anti-SLAPP protection by bringing a complaint based upon both protected and unprotected conduct.” (Club Members for an Honest Election v. Sierra Club (2008) 45 Cal.4th 309, 319.) Thus, the Court must determine whether such allegations are merely “incidental” to each cause of action or “could be the sole an adequate basis for liability under the cause[s] of action[.]” (Haight Ashbury Free Clinics, Inc. v. Happening House Ventures (2010) 184 Cal.App.4th 1539, 1551; Peregrine Funding, Inc. v. Sheppard Mullin Richter & Hampton LLP (2005) 133 Cal.App.4th 658, 672 [first prong of SLAPP analysis met where the allegations of loss resulting from protected activity were not merely incidental or collateral to unprotected activity]; see also Fox Searchlight Pictures, Inc. v. Paladino (2001) 89 Cal.App.4th 294, 308 [“a plaintiff cannot frustrate the purposes of the SLAPP statute through a pleading tactic of combining allegations of protected and nonprotected activity under the label  of one ‘cause of action’ ”].)

“In the anti-SLAPP context, the critical point is whether the plaintiff's cause of action itself was based on an act in furtherance of the defendant's right of petition or free speech.” (Cotati, supra, 29 Cal.4th at p. 78.) “A defendant meets this burden by demonstrating that the act underlying the plaintiff's cause fits one of the categories spelled out in section 425.16, subdivision (e) . . . .” (Ibid.) In terms of whether relief is sought based on allegations of both protected and unprotected activity, “the unprotected activity is disregarded at this stage. If the court determines that relief is sought based on allegations arising from activity protected by statute, the second step is reached. There, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to demonstrate that each challenged claim based on protected activity is legally sufficient and factually substantiated … If not, the claim is stricken.” (Baral, supra, 1 Cal.5th at p. 396.)

Thus, “[i]t does not matter that other unprotected acts may also have been alleged within what has been labeled a single cause of action; these are disregarded at this stage. So long as a ‘court determines that relief is sought based on allegations arising from activity protected by the statute, the second step is reached; with respect to these claims.” (Bonni v. St. Joseph Health System (2021) 11 Cal.5th 995, 1010 (quoting Baral, supra, 1 Cal.5th at p. 396); Laker, supra, 32 Cal.App.5th. at p. 772 [“When relief is sought based on more than one allegation, some of which arise from activity protected by the anti-SLAPP statute and some of which do not, the allegations that arise from unprotected activity are ‘disregarded.’”].)    

And relief is being sought based on the allegations related to Fitness’ protected activity. Specifically, related to Plaintiffs’ elder abuse cause of action, Plaintiffs are seeking to impose liability related to Fitness’ use of “Plaintiffs’ signature specimens obtained in discovery to create or alter purported waivers and releases that Berta never knowingly signed, and then relying on those documents in an attempt to bar her claims and deprive an injured elder of her day in court.” (FAC ¶ 67; see Optional Capital, Inc., supra, 18 Cal.App.5th at p. 111 [“The ‘gravamen is defined by the acts on which liability is based, not some philosophical thrust or legal essence of the cause of action’ ”].) Particularly, Plaintiffs rely on this allegation to support that Fitness “acted with recklessness, oppression, fraud, and malice” in order to obtain exemplary and punitive damages. (FAC ¶ 86.)

Further, to support Plaintiffs’ UCL claim and demonstrate that Fitness’ acts and practices were “fraudulent,” Plaintiffs allege that Fitness “fabricat[ed] or alter[ed] purported waivers using Plaintiffs’ signature specimens and then present[ed] those documents as genuine in court to defeat an injured elder’s claims.” (FAC ¶ 74.)

Therefore, relief is explicitly being sought based on allegations of protected activity, specifically Fitness engaging in discovery and relying on such discovery to move for summary judgment. This activity is what forms the basis of Plaintiffs’ injury of her, “an injured senior” being “deprive[d]. . .of her day in court.” (FAC ¶ 32, 67, 86.) The protected activity is, at least partly, the injury-producing conduct. (Area 51 Prods., Inc. v. City of Alameda (2018) 20 Cal.App.5th 581, 599 [“Under the rule in Baral, we must disregard the unprotected conduct and focus on whether any nonincidental protected conduct is charged, even just in part.”].)

To the extent that Plaintiffs allege other unprotected conduct to support Plaintiffs’ claims, such acts are disregarded at this stage. (Sandoval v. Pali Inst., Inc. (2025) 113 Cal.App.5th 616, 628 [“If through this analysis it becomes clear a cause of action is a mixed cause of action, meaning “one that rests on allegations of multiple acts, some of which constitute protected activity and some of which do not,” we disregard the unprotected acts and reach the second step of the anti-SLAPP analysis with respect to the claims arising from protected activity.”]; Park, supra, 2 Cal.5th at p. 1060 [“a claim may be struck only if the speech or petitioning activity itself is the wrong complained of, and not just evidence of liability or a step leading to some different act for which liability is asserted.”].)

Because Fitness has sufficiently presented how the challenged allegations, concerning Fitness’ litigation activities related to its motion for summary judgment in this case, supply a basis for Plaintiffs’ relief, Fitness has sufficiently satisfied its burden under the first prong.

2.      Second Prong: Minimal Merit

For the second prong of the anti-SLAPP analysis, Plaintiff bears the burden of establishing a probability of succeeding on the merits. (See Kyle v. Carmon (1999) 71 Cal.App.4th 901, 907.)  However, Plaintiff is “not required ‘to prove the specified claim to the trial court;’ rather, so as to not deprive the plaintiff of a jury trial, the appropriate inquiry is whether the plaintiff has stated and substantiated a legally sufficient claim.”  (Whitehall v. County of San Bernardino (2017) 17 Cal.App.5th 352, 364.) “Put another way, the plaintiff must demonstrate that the complaint is both legally sufficient and supported by a sufficient prima facie showing of facts to sustain a favorable judgment if the evidence submitted by the plaintiff is credited.” [Citation.] (Navellier v. Sletten (2002) 29 Cal. 4th 82, 88–89.)

“In opposing an anti-SLAPP motion, the plaintiff cannot rely on the allegations of the complaint, but must produce evidence that would be admissible at trial. [Citation.] Thus, declarations may not be based upon ‘information and belief’ [Citation] and documents submitted without the proper foundation will not be considered.” (HMS Capital, Inc. v. Lawyers Title Co. (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 204, 212.) The complaint, even if verified, is insufficient to carry the plaintiff’s shifted burden.  (Roberts v. Los Angeles County Bar Association (2003) 105 Cal.App.4th 604, 614; Karnazes v. Ares (2016) 244 Cal.App.4th 344, 354 [“pleadings do not constitute evidence”].)

“The court considers the pleadings and evidence submitted by both sides, but does not weigh credibility or compare the weight of the evidence. Rather, the court's responsibility is to accept as true the evidence favorable to the plaintiff [citation] and evaluate the defendant's evidence only to determine if it has defeated that submitted by the plaintiff as a matter of law. [Citation.] The trial court merely determines whether a prima facie showing has been made that would warrant the claim going forward.” (HMS Capital, Inc., supra, 118 Cal.App.4th at p. 212.)

a.       Litigation Privilege

“‘The usual formulation is that the [litigation] privilege applies to any communication (1) made in judicial or quasi-judicial proceedings; (2) by litigants or other participants authorized by law; (3) to achieve the objects of the litigation; and (4) that [has] some connection or logical relation to the action.’¿[Citation.]  The privilege “is not limited to statements made during a trial or other proceedings, but may extend to steps taken prior thereto, or afterwards.’ [Citation.]”  (Action Apartment Assn., Inc. v. City of Santa Monica¿(2007) 41 Cal.4th 1232, 1241.)  “[T]he litigation privilege was founded on defamation actions, and has been applied primarily to provide absolute immunity from¿tort¿liability for communications with ‘some relation’ to judicial proceedings.” (Feldman v. 1100 Park Lane Associates¿(2008) 160 Cal.App.4th 1467, 1494.) 

Moreover, the Supreme Court noted that it had previously “directed that¿section 47(b) be applied broadly to bar tort actions based on privileged communications, excepting only the tort of malicious prosecution.” (Hagberg v. California Fed. Bank (2004) 32 Cal.4th 350, 358.) “The privilege applies to communications relative to the defense of an action as well as those relative to its filing and prosecution.” (Cabral v. Martins¿(2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 471, 485.) 

The threshold issue in determining whether the litigation privilege applies is whether the defendant's conduct was communicative or noncommunicative. (Herterich v. Peltner (2018) 20 Cal. App. 5th 1132, 1139.) The “[p]leadings and process in a case are generally viewed as privileged communications.” (Navellier v. Sletten (2003) 106 Cal.App.4th 763, 770.) “The privilege extends to any publication required or permitted by law in the course of a judicial proceeding to achieve the objects of the litigation, even if the publication occurs outside the courtroom in the absence of a court function or the court's officers. The privilege also applies to noncommunicative acts that are necessarily related to privileged communicative conduct.” (Herterich, supra, 20 Cal.App.5th at p. 1139 (internal citations omitted).)

“The principal purpose of Civil Code section 47, subdivision (b) is to afford litigants and witnesses the utmost freedom of access to the courts without fear of being harassed subsequently by derivative tort actions. The litigation privilege also promotes the effectiveness of judicial proceedings by encouraging attorneys to zealously protect their clients' interests without being concerned with subsequent derivative actions. Additionally, the privilege gives finality to judgments and avoids unending litigation.” (Ibid. (internal citations omitted).)

Here, the challenged allegations are entirely premised on Fitness participating in litigation activities of requesting and obtaining pretrial discovery and later using discovery produced as evidence to support Fitness’ motion for summary judgment in this case. (See Optional Capital, Inc., supra, 18 Cal.App.5th at p. 118 [“the litigation privilege applies because the communicative conduct at issue—as established by the pleadings and documents submitted in connection with motions—was made in judicial or quasi-judicial proceedings (i.e., the state court action, the federal forfeiture action, and the private mediation in the state court action) by attorneys [] to achieve the object of the proceedings and had some connection or logical relation to the action.”].) The communicative acts underlying the challenged allegations, filing a motion for summary judgment after obtaining the specimen signatures in discovery, were clearly made to further Fitness’ litigation efforts in this action. (Rothman v. Jackson (1996) 49 Cal.App.4th 1134, 1146 [“the communicative act—be it a document filed with the court, a letter between counsel or an oral statement—must function as a necessary or useful step in the litigation process and must serve its purposes.”].) Therefore, the litigation privilege applies. (See Bergstein v. Stroock & Stroock & Lavan LLP (2015) 236 Cal.App.4th 793, 814 [“A plaintiff cannot establish a probability of prevailing if the litigation privilege precludes the defendant's liability on the claim.”].)

Nonetheless, even if the Court were to address the second prong on the merits, Plaintiffs failed to submit any competent and admissible evidence to support the probability of prevailing on the challenged claim or the truth of the allegations at issue, and thus Plaintiff failed to establish its burden on step two of the anti-SLAPP analysis. (See Wisner v. Dignity Health (2022) 85 Cal. App. 5th 35, 43 [“the plaintiff cannot meet this burden by relying on the allegations of the complaint. The plaintiff must submit admissible evidence.”].)

VI.             CONCLUSION

Thus, Fitness’ anti-SLAPP motion is GRANTED. The Court strikes paragraphs 16, 32, 67,

74, 86, 87 from the FAC. When a court grants an anti-SLAPP motion, the unsuccessful party is not entitled to leave to amend. (Simmons v. Allstate Ins. Co. (2001) 92 Cal.App.4th 1068, 1074 [“[W]e believe that granting leave to amend the complaint after the court finds the defendant had established its prima facie case would be jamming a procedural square peg into a statutory round hole.”].)

I.                   INTRODUCTION

This case arises from alleged habitability issues with the residential rental property

located at 2105 W 152nd St, Gardena, CA 90249-4220 (“the Premises”) from on or about June 3, 2022, to on or about November 28, 2023.

On October 21, 2025, Plaintiff Shannon McClain Robertson initiated this

action.

            On October 28, 2025, Plaintiffs Shannon McClain Robertson, Tatum Robertson, Niall Robertson and Lennox McClain Robertson (“Plaintiffs”) filed a first amended complaint (“FAC”) against Defendants Phyllis Whitaker, as an Individual and as Trustee of the Lillian J Pawsey Living Trust, William Barnard, and the Estate of Lilian Pawsey (“Defendants”), alleging causes of action for (1) Breach of Contract, (2) Tortious Breach of Implied Warranty of Habitability, (3) Premises Liability, (4) Negligence, (5) Constructive Eviction, (6) Wrongful Eviction under California Tenant Protection Act, (7) Tenant Harassment, (8) Wrongful Retention of Security Deposit, and (9) Unfair Business Practices.

            On April 13, 2026, Defendant William Barndard (“Barnard”) filed the instant demurrer.

            No opposition was filed.

II.                LEGAL STANDARD

A demurrer is an objection to a pleading, the grounds for which are apparent from either the face of the complaint or a matter of which the court may take judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.30, subd. (a); see also Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) The purpose of a demurrer is to challenge the sufficiency of a pleading “by raising questions of law.” (Postley v. Harvey (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 280, 286.) “In the construction of a pleading, for the purpose of determining its effect, its allegations must be liberally construed, with a view to substantial justice between the parties.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 452.) The court “‘treat[s] the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law . . ..’” (Berkley v. Dowds (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 518, 525.) 

When a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Ibid.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.)¿¿¿ 

III.             DISCUSSION

            Barnard demurs to all causes of action alleged in the FAC.

1.      Breach of Contract

 

Barnard demurs to the cause of action for Breach of Contract on the ground that he acted as merely a property manager of the Premises and is not a signatory to the Subject Lease. (FAC, Ex. A [residential lease].)

To state a cause of action for breach of contract, Plaintiff must be able to establish “(1) the existence of the contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance, (3) defendant’s breach, and (4) the resulting damages to the plaintiff.” (Oasis West Realty, LLC v. Goldman (2011) 51 Cal.4th 811, 821.) If a breach of contract claim “is based on alleged breach of a written contract, the terms must be set out verbatim in the body of the complaint or a copy of the written agreement must be attached and incorporated by reference.” (Harris v. Rudin, Richman & Appel (1999) 74 Cal.App.4th 299, 307.)

Here, the Subject Lease identifies that the parties to the Lease are Plaintiff Shannon McClain (with her three minor children) as tenants and Lillian Pawsey as the landlord/owner. (FAC, Ex. A.) The FAC further alleges that Phyliss Whitaker as Trustee of the Lillian J. Pawsey Living Trust is the successor owner of the subject residential premises. (Id. ¶ 5.) Barnard is identified in the F AC as managing the Premises from early 2022. (Id. ¶¶ 6, 28.)

Given that Barnard is not a signatory to the Subject Lease, the Breach of Contract claim fails. (Burnett v. Chimney Sweep (2004) 123 Cal.App.4th 1057, 1071 [property manager “is entitled to judgment on the pleadings as to the cause of action for breach of contract because it was not a party to the lease”].)

The demurrer to the first cause of action is sustained.

2.      Tortious Breach of Implied Warranty of Habitability, Constructive Eviction, Wrongful Eviction, Wrongful Retention of Security Deposit

 

Barnard demurs to the causes of action for Breach of the Implied Warranty of

Habitability, Constructive Eviction, Wrongful Eviction, and Wrongful Retention of Security Deposit the similar ground that Barnard is not alleged to have been acting as Plaintiff’s landlord.

“[A] warranty of habitability is implied by law in residential leases.” (Green v. Superior

Court (1974) 10 Cal.3d 616, 637.) To establish a breach of the implied warranty of habitability, Plaintiffs must establish (1) “the existence of a material defective condition affecting the premises’ habitability,” (2) “notice to the landlord of the condition within a reasonable time after the tenant’s discovery of the condition,” (3) “the landlord was given a reasonable time to correct the deficiency, and” (4) “resulting damages.” (Erlach v. Sierra Asset Servicing, LLC (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 1281, 1297; Stoiber v. Honeychuck (1980) 101 Cal.App.3d 903, 918–919 [‘... assuming appropriate pleadings of fact, a tenant may state a cause of action in tort against his landlord for damages resulting from a breach of the implied warranty of habitability’].)

“An eviction is actual when a landlord takes direct action to physically expel the tenant from the premises. An eviction is constructive if the landlord engages in acts that render the premises unfit for occupancy for the purpose for which it was leased, or deprive the tenant of the beneficial enjoyment of the premises.” (Cunningham v. Universal Underwriters (2002) 98 Cal.App.4th 1141, 1152.) “A tenant who is wrongfully evicted by his landlord before the expiration of the lease term may maintain a wrongful eviction action for tort damages and punitive damages[.]” (Nativi v. Deutsche Bank Nat'l Tr. Co. (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 261, 294.)

Civil Code section 1950.5 governs the “security for a rental agreement for residential property that is used as the dwelling of the tenant.” (Civ. Code § 1950.5(a).) “Any security shall be held by the landlord for the tenant who is party to the lease or agreement.” (Id. § 1950.5(d).)

Each of these claims flows within a landlord-tenant relationship. Plaintiff alleges that Barnard was in the position of property manager of the Premises. (Id. ¶¶ 6, 28.)  There is simply no allegation that Barnard was Plaintiff’s landlord.  

            The demurrer to the second, fifth, sixth and eighth causes of action is sustained.

3.      Premises Liability, Negligence, Harassment, Unfair Business Practices

Barnard demurs to the causes of action for Premises Liability, Negligence, and

Harassment on the ground that they are barred by the applicable statute of limitations.

“ ‘A demurrer on the ground of the bar of the statute of limitations will not lie where the action may be, but is not necessarily barred.’ [Citations.] It must appear clearly and affirmatively that, upon the face of the complaint, the right of action is necessarily barred. [Citations.] This will not be the case unless the complaint alleges every fact which the defendant would be required to prove if he were to plead the bar of the applicable statute of limitation as an affirmative defense. [Citation.]” (Lockley v. Law Office of Cantrell, Green, Pekich, Cruz & McCort (2001) 91 Cal.App.4th 875, 881.)

Claims for Premises Liability, Negligence, and Harassment are subject to the two-year

statute of limitations delineated in Code of Civil Procedure section 335.1 for personal injury claims. (Code Civ. Proc., § 335.1.)

The statute of limitations begins running “when the cause of action is complete with all of its elements.” (Norgart v. Upjohn Co. (1999) 21 Cal.4th 383, 389.) “A plaintiff has reason to discover a cause of action when he or she ‘has reason at least to suspect a factual basis for its elements.’ [Citations.] Under the discovery rule, suspicion of one or more of the elements of a cause of action, coupled with knowledge of any remaining elements, will generally trigger the statute of limitations period. [Citations.] ... [B]y [previously] discussing the discovery rule in terms of a plaintiff's suspicion of ‘elements’ of a cause of action, [we were] referring to the ‘generic’ elements of wrongdoing, causation, and harm.” (Fox v. Ethicon Endo-Surgery, Inc. (2005) 35 Cal.4th 797, 807.) Put differently, under the discovery rule, “the statute of limitations begins to run when the plaintiff suspects or should suspect that her injury was caused by wrongdoing.” (Jolly v. Eli Lilly & Co. (1988) 44 Cal.3d 1103, 1110.) “By statute, the discovery rule applies to fraud actions.” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accts., Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1318.)

In California, the default rule for accrual is that “an action accrues on the date of injury.” (Jolly v. Eli Lilly & Co. (1988) 44 Cal.3d 1103, 1109.)

Here, Plaintiffs allege that once Barnard received management responsibilities, Plaintiff’s “repair requests were either ignored or completely disregarded.” (FAC ¶ 28.) From January 2022 onward, Barnard “failed to address critical habitability issues, including electrical hazards, broken cabinetry, malfunctioning garbage disposal, and plumbing issues.” (Ibid.) “By September 2023, [Plaintiff’s] children were experiencing chronic respiratory issues, which had been formally diagnosed as mold toxicity following blood tests.” (Id. ¶ 35.) These allegations form the basis for Plaintiff’s claims. Thus, on the face of Plaintiff’s FAC, her claims arising from these allegations accrued latest September 2023, when the mold toxicity injuries were discovered and formally diagnosed. Plaintiff filed her original complaint on October 21, 2025, over two years later. To the extent that these claims are premised on other potential alleged violations, such as failure to resolve issues regarding termite infestation or attempt to enter the Premises without proper notice, both violations allegedly occurred prior to September 2023. (Id. ¶¶ 43-45, 49.)

            The demurrer to the third, fourth, and seventh causes of action is sustained.

            Specifically, for the UCL claim, the UCL statute of limitations is “governed by common law accrual rules.” (Aryeh v. Canon Bus. Sols., Inc. (2013) 55 Cal.4th 1185, 1195.) “[T]he underlying nature of the claim, not its form, should control.” (Ibid.; see Jefferson v. J.E. French Co. (1960) 54 Cal.2d 717, 718 [“[T]he nature of the right sued upon, not the form of action or the relief demanded, determines the applicability of the statute of limitations.”].) Consequently, “just like common law claims challenging fraudulent conduct, a UCL deceptive practices claim should accrue ‘only when a reasonable person would have discovered the factual basis for a claim.’” (Aryeh, supra, 55 Cal.4th at p. 1195.)

Here, the harm underlying Plaintiff’s UCL claim against Barnard is the same harm underlying Plaintiff’s Premises Liability, Negligence, and Harassment claims. Therefore, similarly with the above causes of action, the UCL claim accrued when the alleged harm was completed, which is when Plaintiff is alleged to have discovered the injuries stemming from the toxic mold, resulting from Barnard failure to take action “to address [Plaintiff’s] concerns regarding the HVAC system or contamination within the Premises.” (FAC ¶ 31.)

The demurrer to the ninth cause of action is sustained.

4.      Exemplary Damages

Plaintiff’s claim for “exemplary damages” fails because it is not cognizable cause of

action. It is a remedy, for which a Plaintiff must establish by clear and convincing evidence that a defendant acted with fraud, malice, or oppression. Nevertheless, the Court does not find that Plaintiff has alleged “oppression, fraud, or malice” with the requisite specificity. (Grieves v. Superior Court (1984) 157 Cal.App.3d 159, 166 [“[t]he mere allegation an intentional tort was committed is not sufficient to warrant an award of punitive damages. Not only must there be circumstances of oppression, fraud, or malice, but facts must be alleged in the pleading to support such a claim”].) Furthermore, given that the Court otherwise sustained the demurrer in its entirety, there is no viable cause of action an award of punitive damages can be based upon. (See 569 E. Cnty. Boulevard LLC v. Backcountry Against the Dump, Inc. (2016) 6 Cal.App.5th 426, 429 fn.3 [“a claim for punitive damages is merely an additional remedy that is dependent on a viable cause of action for an underlying tort.”].)

            the demurrer to the tenth cause of action is sustained.

IV.             CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, Barnard’s demurrer is SUSTAINED in its entirety with thirty (30)

days leave to amend.
