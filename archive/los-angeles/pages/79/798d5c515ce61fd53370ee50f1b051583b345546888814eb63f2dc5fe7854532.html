(¶114 (a), (c)).)

 

(Plaintiffs made their investment with Li in August 2023.) Turning to the merits of this motion, Specially Appearing Defendant Alteri Enterprises, Inc. (“Alteri”) brings the motion “solely for the purpose of challenging this Court’s personal jurisdiction to enjoin Alteri…. Alteri seeks no affirmative relief and raises only the threshold question of this Court’s power to enjoin it.” (Notice p. 1.)[3]

 

Accordingly, the issue presented is whether California has specific jurisdiction over a Delaware corporation.[4] (According to Alteri, their role is as a Texas-based operator and asset holder.)  For reasons to be discussed below—namely as Plaintiffs have not met their burden of showing that Alteri has purposefully availed itself of the privilege of conducting activities within California or have purposefully directed its conduct into the forum State—controlling precedent compels this court to grant the motion. The court will begin its analysis with an overview of specific jurisdiction then followed by an examination of the respective arguments and evidence.

 

Specific Jurisdiction

 

The specific jurisdictional analysis is “intensely fact-specific.” (Rivelli v. Hemm (2021) 67 Cal.App.5th 380, 393.) Both the United States Supreme Court and the California high court have cautioned that the “‘minimum contacts’ test ... is not susceptible of mechanical application; rather, the facts of each case must be weighed to determine whether the requisite ‘affiliating circumstances’ are present.” (Kulko v. California Superior Court (1978) 436 U.S. 84, 92, 98 S.Ct. 1690, 56 L.Ed.2d 132 (Kulko.)

 

“California courts routinely apply a three-part test to determine whether a court may exercise specific jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant: (1) the defendant has purposefully availed himself or herself of forum benefits, (2) the controversy is related to or arises out of the defendant’s purposeful contacts with the forum,[5] and (3) the assertion of personal jurisdiction would comport with fair play and substantial justice. (Pavlovich v. Superior Court (2002) 29 Cal.4th 262;[6] see also Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc. (1984) 465 U.S. 770, 774 (Keeton).)

 

A plaintiff has the initial burden of demonstrating facts to support the first two factors, which establish the requisite minimum contacts with the forum state. The burden then shifts to the defendant to show that the exercise of jurisdiction would be unreasonable under the third factor. (Snowney v. Harrah’s Entertainment, Inc. (2005) 35 Cal.4th 1054, 1062; see also see also Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz (1985) 471 U.S. 462, 477 (Burger King) [“where a defendant who purposefully has directed his activities at forum residents seeks to defeat jurisdiction, he must present a compelling case that the presence of some other considerations would render jurisdiction unreasonable.”].)

 

As the Pavlovich court summarized, specific jurisdiction “depends upon the quality and nature of [the defendant’s] activity in the forum in relation to the particular cause of action…the fact that a defendant’s actions in some way set into motion events which ultimately injured a California resident cannot, by itself, confer jurisdiction over that defendant.” (Pavlovich, supra, Cal.4th at p. 276, emphasis added.)

 

1.     Purposeful Availment 

 

“[P]urposeful availment exists whenever the defendant purposefully and voluntarily directs its activities toward the forum state in an effort to obtain a benefit from that state.” (Snowney, supra, 35 Cal.4th at p. 1067; see also Yahoo! Inc. v. La Ligue Cantra Le Racisme Et L’Antisemitism (9th Cir. 2006) 433 F.3d 119, 1206 [“Despite its label, this prong includes both purposeful availment and purposeful direction. It may be satisfied by purposeful availment of the privilege of doing business in the forum; by purposeful direction of activities at the forum; or by some combination thereof.”], emphasis added.) The purposeful availment requirement ensures that a defendant will not be haled into a jurisdiction solely as a result of random, fortuitous, or attenuated contacts. (Burger King, supra, 471 U.S. at p. 475.) Put differently, the purposeful availment inquiry focuses on the defendant's intentionality. (Pavlovich, supra, 29 Cal.4th at p. 269.)

 

For example, California courts have consistently concluded that a foreign corporation purposefully avails itself of the benefits of the California forum when it knowingly sells and ships its products to California businesses for use in California. (See Jayone Foods, Inc. v. Aekyung Industrial Co. Ltd. (2019) 31 Cal.App.5th 543, 556–557 (Jayone Foods) [South Korean company that “engaged in a number of direct sales transactions with multiple California distributors of its consumer products” purposefully availed itself of the benefits of doing business in California], emphasis added; see also Luberski, Inc. v. Oleficio F.LLI Amato S.R.L. (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 409, 419 [Italian company that sold olive oil to California businesses and delivered it to California “had the expectation that the goods ... would be utilized in California” and thus “purposefully availed itself of the [California] forum”].)

 

Or a defendant does purposefully avail itself of the privilege of conducting business activity in California as exhibited by nature of the transaction, the economic implications, and the ongoing relationship and mutual obligations established thereby. (Rivelli v. Hemm (2021) 67 Cal.App.5th 380, 390.) (Rivelli is cited by Plaintiffs in their supplemental brief.) In Rivelli, the issue on appeal was whether a Swiss company, Institut Straumann AG, and its executive vice president, Frank Hemm who is a Swiss citizen and resident, were subject to suit in California court for claims related to the purchase by the Swiss company of shares of a privately held California corporation, Rodo (a medical device company). (Id. at pp. 385-386.) In 2014, the Swiss company acquired a 12 percent minority interest in Rodo. A couple years later, Hemm joined Rodo's board of directors. (Id. at p. 387.) In 2016, Rodo received approval from the United States Food & Drug Administration (FDA) to sell its dental device such that Rodo's management began to negotiate with the Swiss company the terms of a stock purchase agreement. The transaction resulted in the following: (i) increase Straumann's equity interest in Rodo from 12 percent to 30 percent; (ii) Straumann would gain an additional seat on the Rodo board of directors (for a total of two seats); (iii) Straumann would became Rodo's exclusive worldwide distributor under an exclusive distributorship and license agreement; (iv) Straumann agreed to establish a revolving $5 million line of credit between Straumann and Rodo; and (v) Straumann gained “considerable influence over the long-term viability of” Rodo. (Id. at pp. 395-397.) Effectively, the appellate court found a showing of “proactive and purposeful availment” (id. at p. 395, emphasis added) because “the transaction—proposed by Straumann, negotiated by Hemm on behalf of Straumann, and consummated with significant and continuing benefits and consequences—represents conduct by Straumann that forged a ‘substantial economic connection.’” (Id. at p. 398, emphasis added.) (And this further underscored by the very nature of the “operational realty” of the relationship which was fulfilling the requirements of the distributorship agreement (id. at p. 397).) In short, the court identified a “lattice of contacts” linking the nonresident defendant to California. (Ibid, citing Checker Motors Corp. v. Superior Court (1993) 13 Cal.App.4th 1007, 1018.) (In Rivelli, despite a finding of purposeful availment, the motion to quash was granted for lack of forum-relatedness.)[7]

 

Or when a foreign defendant comes to California to negotiate agreement, and the foreign defendant company executes an agreement, that may certainly constitute purposeful availment. (See Epic Communications, Inc. v. Richwave Technology, Inc. 2009) 179 Cal.App.4th 314.) By contrast, merely sending an agreement to California and communicating with a California resident over the agreement is insufficient. (See Supp. Reply p. 4, citing R.E. Sanders & Co. v. Lincoln-Richardson Enterprises (1980) 108 Cal.App.3d 71, 78 (Sanders)[8] [“Defendants' intrusions into this state were few in number, consisting only of two letters and six or seven telephone calls to plaintiff's agents. The quality of these was insubstantial; none dealt with formation of the terms of the agency relationship or with directions of plaintiff's activities.”].)

 

Also, merely receiving payment from California investors is insufficient to evidence purposeful availment was applied in ParaFi Digital Opportunities LP v. Egorov (2025) 108 Cal.App.5th 124 (ParaFi). The court will discuss ParaFi at length as it involved cryptocurrency such that the context (i.e., “operational reality” supra) is instructive. In ParaFi, a dispute arose from a Swiss defendant’s (Egorov) cancellation of the plaintiffs’ investment in a decentralized cryptocurrency trading platform, Curve, developed by the Swiss defendant. The plaintiffs, who claimed to be among the most prominent investment funds in the decentralized finance (“DeFi”) space, were excited about the opportunity to help grow the defendant’s platform and subsequently provided Egorov with time, money, and other resources in reliance on the defendant’s promise that ParaFi would be able to invest in the platform. (Id. at p. 130.) But then the defendant cancelled the investment and offered to refund everyone who wasn't an investor in the company. (Id. at p. 131.) The plaintiffs sued Egorov alleging that Egorov engaged in a “multi-faceted scheme to defraud Plaintiffs” by “falsely promising plaintiffs a stake in Curve to leverage plaintiffs’ ‘networks, reputation, and other assistance to generate interest and legitimize Curve,’ without any intent to distribute CRV tokens, and related voting rights, to plaintiffs.” (Id. at pp. 131-132.) Both the trial and appellate court determined that the plaintiffs’ record did not purposeful availment. The court noted how there was no indication that Egorov solicited California investors; Egorov never visited California to market Curve, to seek out investors, or sought to meet with plaintiffs during negotiations.

 

As for the forum benefits test, purposeful availment may be found where the defendant purposefully “benefits in the forum by reaching out to forum residents to create an ongoing’ relationship.” (Id. at p. 136.) The court noted how (i) Egorov sought to avoid engaging in economic activities in California (by moving); (ii) the “funds plaintiffs transmitted to Egorov were cryptocurrency sent to Swiss Stake's digital wallet. While ParaFi and Framework initiated the transactions from California, they are both Delaware corporations and do not state where the United States currency underlying the USDC originated”; (iii) and that the messages between the parties “relate[d] to a single transaction, with the only relationship to California being ParaFi's and Framework's presence in the state.” (Id. at pp. 137-138.)

 

As for the effects test (which is used in cases involving intentional torts), specific personal jurisdiction “may be exercised over a defendant who has caused an effect in the forum state by an act or omission occurring elsewhere.” (Id. at p. 138.) But the effects test “requires express aiming at the forum (not necessarily at the plaintiff)…Thus, under the effects test, specific jurisdiction lies “only if [the defendant's] suit-related conduct creates a substantial connection with that state”; it is not sufficient that a defendant “targeted conduct at a plaintiff he knows resides in the forum state… The proper question under the effects test is not where the plaintiff experienced a particular injury or effect but whether the defendant's conduct connects him to the forum in a meaningful way.” (Id. at p. 138, italics original, internal citations omitted.)  

In ParaFi, the court determined that Egorov's only relationship to California is based on the plaintiffs’ “incidental presence in the state, which is insufficient to establish purposeful availment.” (Id. at p. 139, all emphasis added.)

 

As for the contracts test, the court observed that “the agreements themselves did not contemplate any ongoing California-based activity.” (Id. at p. 140)

 

Another case discussing how the acceptance of payments alone is insufficient to create specific jurisdiction was Elkman v. National States Ins. Co. (2009) 173 Cal.App.4th 1035. There, the plaintiff purchased a policy plan whilst at her residence in Florida, but she then relocated to California. When the defendant insurance company, a Missouri corporation, denied certain benefits, the plaintiff sued and the defendant filed a motion to quash. The Elkman court concluded that National did not subject itself to specific jurisdiction in California merely by accepting premium payments from California and by processing and paying claims submitted by its insureds for services rendered in this state. The court’s reasoning was that “National did not ‘come here’ voluntarily, no matter how many insureds did. It was the unilateral decisions of [the plaintiff] and other insureds to relocate to California which caused National to accept payments from this state and to process and pay claims for services rendered in this state.” (Id. at p. 1321, emphasis added.)

 

In contrast to the foregoing, purposeful availment was found in Gilmore Bank v. AsiaTrust New Zealand Ltd. (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 1558, which is relied upon by Plaintiffs in their supplemental brief. In Gilmore, in short, the defendant (Cindy) began to form trusts and other entities to avoid judgment in a certain proceeding. (Id. at pp. 1563-1564.) One of the entities was a New Zealand Trust, AsiaTrust, which agreed to serve as trustee for a trust that would be created to hold the annuity. (Id. at p. 1564.) (The Swiss annuity certificate reflected that the insured person was the individual defendant, a resident of California and that the annuity pays about $67,500 every quarter.) (Id. at p. 1566.) In the lawsuit for fraudulently transferring and sequestering Cindy's assets, AsiaTrust filed a motion to quash “stress[ing] that [their general manager] never visited California.” (Id. at p. 1572.) The court was not persuaded. This was a case wherein the foreign defendant was receiving “compensation for accepting, investing, managing, disbursing, and shielding the assets of a California judgment debtor in a scheme that contemplates an ongoing contractual relationship.” (Id. at p. 1572; see also p. 1567 [“The flow of money originates from the annuity, moves to the New Zealand Trust, then to Cindy's Retirement Trust, and ultimately to Cindy herself.”].) Accordingly, the case was more than a “single telephone call or piece of correspondence” but a “continuing stream of payments” from the foreign defendant to California and a “continuing receipt” of compensation. (Id. at pp. 1572-1573.) Key word from Gilmore: continuing.

 

Synthesizing the rules above, the purposeful availment requirement requires a latticework of intentional, meaningful, and proactive contacts targeting the forum state that begets significant and continuing benefits for the foreign defendant. Here, by contrast, what the evidence simply provides is that one of the Plaintiffs lives in California and invested with a California resident who later sent funds to Alteri. None of the cases Plaintiffs cite support their position but instead showcase how much is required for purposeful availment, which is lacking here. From what the court has gathered, there is only one “agreement” between Li and Alteri and that was the for one transaction of certain mining equipment; there is no evidence of an ongoing transactional relationship, let alone evidence of Alteri soliciting California investors. With that, the court adheres to much of its original tentative but will address some of the additional points and authorities raised in the supplemental brief. 

 

A. Merits

 

Plaintiffs set forth six arguments as to why purposeful availment is ostensibly satisfied. (See Opp. pp. 9-11; see also Supp. Opp. p. 5:26-28 [“[Alteri] knowing it was dealing with a Californian, sent offering documents and invoices into the state, took $2.66 million traceable to an identified California account, and booked the Californian as an equity holder.”]; see also Supp. Opp. pp. 10-11 [“Alteri knew it was dealing with a California investor and then sent the offering documents and invoices into California, collected approximately $2.66 million traceable to a California bank account and California wallets, used California counsel to structure the acquisition that money funded, and booked the investor on its own cap table; all within the precise window in which Plaintiffs' fraudulent-transfer claims arose.”].)

For reasons to be discussed, the court disagrees.

 

Summary of Plaintiffs’ arguments: Plaintiffs aver that: (1) Alteri's Board formally designated its "principal executive office" in Beverly Hills by written consent signed by both directors; (2) Alteri "acts through its officers and agents" HealthMarkets, Inc. v. Superior Court (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1160, 1167, and Alteri's California-resident CEO, Younis, has performed his Alteri duties from California as evidenced most recently by executing the declaration submitted with this motion in Irvine on March 16, 2026; (3) Alteri operated through California-licensed counsel; (4) Alteri solicited business in California; and (5) just five days after this Court enjoined Li’s equity interest in Alteri, Alteri publicly launched a $30 million tokenization, a securities offering marketed broadly, including to California investors. And in their supplemental brief, Plaintiffs argue that Alteri entered into a multimillion-dollar commercial relationship with Auradine.

 

First, to the extent that Plaintiff argues that the acts of a corporation's agents within California may establish jurisdiction over the corporation, not necessarily for a couple of reasons. First and foremost, and as noted in reply, contacts for specific jurisdiction are evaluated at the time the plaintiff’s cause of action arose. (DVI, Inc. v. Superior Court (2002) 104 Cal.App.4th 1080, 1100-1102 [“As noted above, the [conduct/activity] occurred after the [claim], and therefore it is irrelevant to establishing specific jurisdiction.”], emphasis added.) Applied here, the referenced June 21, 2022 Unanimous Consent stating that Alteri’s “principal executive office” is in Beverly Hills, California or that Alteri has used transactional counsel located in California in the past are insufficient evidence.

 

Second, the personal residence of a director or officer does not impute jurisdiction over a corporation. (See Reply p. 6, citing Thomson v. Anderson (2003) 113 Cal.App.4th 258, 271.)[9]

 

Third, the same goes for Plaintiffs’ attempt at arguing purposeful availment by Alteri’s use of a California-based lawyer. (Reply p. Aquila, Inc. v. Superior Court (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 556, 572 (2007) [“[I]t is not enough for a nonresident defendant to become subject to the specific jurisdiction of this state simply by entering into a contract with a California resident.”)].)

 

To the extent that in their supplemental brief Plaintiffs rely upon Moncrief v. Clark (2015) 238 Cal.App.4th 1000 to support their proposition that “out-of-state lawyer's telephone and email communications into California concerning a single transaction were purposeful availment,” yes, but that case does not support Plaintiffs’ point. In Moncrief, a California partnership/company hired the plaintiff, Moncrief, a California law firm, to perform due diligence in connection the company’s purchase of farm equipment from a company in Arizona; that farm company was represented by Clark, an Arizona attorney. (Id. at p. 1003.) Despite Clark’s assurances that the farm equipment was free and clear, it wasn’t; as a result of the failed farm equipment purchase, the California company sued Moncrief for legal malpractice and the law firm in turn cross-complained against the Arizona attorney, Clark. The appellate court reversed the trial court’s order finding California did have personal jurisdiction over the Arizona attorney. As to the purposeful availment prong, Clark argued that he had “a single, fortuitous transaction” with the California law firm such that he did not “personally [sic] avail himself of the benefits of California.” (Id. at p. 1007.) In a rather brief opinion, the appellate court rejected that court but did so because Clark relied upon a distinguishable case. (Id. at p. 1007 [“The facts of this case are distinguishable from those in Shisler. Here, while Moncrief and Clark engaged in a single transaction, Clark targeted Moncrief with the specific purpose of inducing Moncrief's client to finalize the purchase of farm equipment from Texas Hill Farms. Clark's contacts with California are not akin to maintaining a passive Web site available to California residents as the defendant did in Shisler.”].) Here, by contrast, Alteri has advanced much analogous authority to find that Alteri did not purposefully avail itself of California’s benefits.

 

Fourth, Plaintiffs argue that Alteri solicited business in California.

 

Younis’s own declaration admits that Alteri sent Li (a California resident) a Subscription Agreement and Private Placement Memorandum (Younis Decl. ¶ 5), and 8 mining equipment invoices. (Younis Decl. ¶8; Jeon Decl., ¶17 & Ex. K) Alteri subsequently accepted at least $2,662,802.50 from California-sourced wire transfers and cryptocurrency transfers between August 2023 and August 2024. (Jeon Decl., ¶¶ 4, 15, 16.) Soliciting forum residents and accepting their investments is the paradigm of purposeful availment. Anglo Irish Bank, supra, 165 Cal.App.4th at p. 983 ("By purposefully and successfully soliciting the business of California residents, defendants could reasonably anticipate being subject to litigation in California . . . ."); Snowney, supra, 35 Cal.4th at p. 1068 (defendant's deliberate marketing efforts directed at California established purposeful availment). (Opp. p. 10.)

 

But as set forth in Reply, Younis met Li on the server Discord in late 2023 and discussed cryptocurrency mining with him, without knowing his identity or his location. (Younis Decl. ¶ 4; Supp. Younis Decl. ¶5.) In any event, Alteri has never requested that Younis solicit Li or anyone in California to invest in the company. (Supp. Younis Decl. ¶5.)[10] And without seeking out investors or even meeting with plaintiffs during negotiations in California, a finding of purposeful availment is attenuated. (See e.g., ParaFi, supra, 108 Cal.App.5th at pp. 135-136; see also Pavlovich, supra, 29 Cal.4th at p. 274, citing Jewish Defense Organization, Inc. v. Superior Court (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 1045 [posting a passive website on the internet is not by itself sufficient to subject the defendant to the jurisdiction of California].)[11]

 

Plaintiffs also argue that Alteri sent a Subscription Agreement and Private Placement Memorandum to Li’s email address, not to any physical location (Opp. at 10), just as it did for all 40 putative investors throughout the country and in Canada. (Supp. Younis Decl. ¶ 6.) In opposition, Plaintiffs cite to Beckman v. Thompson (1992), 4 Cal.App.4th at p. 488 for the proposition that “sending communications into California that are "the instrumentality of the tort" establishes jurisdiction. (Opp. p. 12.) Beckman involved a breach of contract. In that case, the court actually found that California had no jurisdiction over Delaware corporation with its principal place of business in New York. And the portion Plaintiffs cite to in Beckman actually refers to Abbott Power Corp. v. Overhead Electric Co. (1976) 60 Cal.App.3d 272 (Abbott). But neither Beckman nor Abbott necessarily discussed purposeful availment. The Abbott court primarily focused on the second prong of how the tort of which the defendant was accused of directly related to and, for the most part, rose out of that local conduct of the defendant. (The reply did not address Beckman.)

 

To the extent Plaintiffs rely upon Snowney and Anglo Irish Bank, those cases militate against purposeful availment. In Snowney, looking at the scale analysis described in Zippo Mfg. Co. v. Zippo Dot Com, Inc. (W.D.Pa.1997) 852 F.Supp.1119, the court examined whether the Nevada hotels that had websites were subject to California’s jurisdiction. The court maintained they did because they had: an “interactive” website that provided directions from California to its Nevada hotels; “advertised extensively in California through billboards, newspapers, and radio and television stations located in California”; and regularly sent mailings advertising its hotels to California residents. (Snowney, supra, 35 Cal.4th at pp. 1063-1067.) Similarly, Anglo Irish Bank Corp. does not support a finding of purposeful availment. There, a court found jurisdiction over an Irish bank, the Isle of Man bank, and the trust company as they purposefully directed their activities at California residents by and through the individuals who visited California on their behalf based upon evidence that certain individuals met with “potential investors at the specific request of the Irish bank.” (Id. at p. 984.) In contrast to those two cases, here, Plaintiffs offer no evidence that Alteri caused Younis to solicit Li or anyone else in California, let alone that it engaged in the coordinated marketing campaigns like the ones in Snowney or Anglo Irish. (Reply pp. 7-8.)

 

Also, Plaintiffs argue that after this court enjoined Li’s equity interest in Alteri, Alteri publicly launched a $30 million tokenization, a securities offering marketed broadly, including to California investors. “That post-injunction California-directed marketing is itself purposeful availment, and it independently demonstrates that Alteri ‘could reasonably anticipate being subject to litigation in California.’ Anglo Irish Bank, supra, 165 Cal.App.4th at p. 983.” (Opp. p. 11:1-6.) But Anglo Irish Bank did involve post-injunction activity. That aside, in reply, Alteri argues that it never did so and the website that Plaintiffs claim is Alteri’s is unauthenticated; it was not created or authorized by Alteri. (Reply p. 8, citing Supp. Younis Decl. ¶¶ 7-8.).

 

Finally, Plaintiffs argue that Alteri maintains a public, multimillion-dollar commercial relationship with Auradine, Inc., a Santa Clara, California supplier of bitcoin miners. As set forth in the opposition, Younis publicly described the “partnership” on LinkedIn—“hundreds of units over the past months in our pilot order, and thousands to come”—and his personal visit to Auradine's California headquarters, and on May 7, 2025, Auradine filed a UCC-1 financing statement naming Alteri as its debtor. (Supp. Brief p. 10.) But the fact that Alteri bought machines from Auradine in 2025 has nothing to do with Plaintiffs’ claims, all of which arose from August 2023 to August 2024.[12]

 

All in all, much more is required than what Plaintiffs have offered for a showing of purposeful availment. Effectively, the court need not address the other two prongs.

 

B. Miscellaneous Arguments

 

Plaintiffs also advance other arguments separate from personal jurisdiction. Those too do not change the outcome.

 

First, Plaintiffs argue that Alteri has waived jurisdiction by filing the instant motion on March 16, 2026 before it filed its motion to quash service of summons on March 27, 2026. But this motion is clear that it only seeks to address jurisdiction.[13]

 

Second, Plaintiffs argue that the “personal-jurisdiction question Alteri presses is not ripe” since it only becomes an issue if “Plaintiffs later seek to enforce the orders against Alteri through contempt, the Court can address personal jurisdiction and concert- actor status on a developed record at that time. (Opp. p. 13.) The court will turn to one of Plaintiffs’ own argument when it asked this court to modify its junction: “Defendants position is akin to arguing that a plan to bulldoze a historic landmark cannot be enjoined until the site has been razed. It is axiomatic that Plaintiffs are not obligated to wait for irreparable harm to actually take place before seeking to enjoin that harm based on the new facts they have uncovered.” (12/10/25 Reply Re: Modify Injunction p. 6.) Similarly, here, Alteri need not wait for a “hypothetical future contempt proceeding.” (Opp. p. 13.) In any event, as noted in reply, lack of jurisdiction may be raised at any time. (Reply p. 10.)

 

Based on the foregoing, the motion as to Alteri is GRANTED.

Specially Appearing Defendant Alteri Enterprises Inc.’s Motion to Quash Third-Party Subpoenas to Fifth Third Bank and JP Morgan Chase Bank N/A and Request for Sanctions

 

As set forth in the motion, on February 5, 2025, Plaintiffs served nonparty Alteri with a subpoena, to which Alteri

timely served responses and documents; On February 23, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a SAC; and on March 3, 2026, Plaintiffs issued the Subpoenas to Fifth Third and Chase, seeking financial records related to Alteri. The Fifth Third Subpoena demands all account statements and “[a]ll DOCUMENTS RELATED TO” all deposits and credits, withdrawals (with supporting instruments), KYC and CDD materials, and records of freezes or closures and all documents related thereto from January 1, 2023, to the present. Similarly, the Chase Subpoena requests “[a]ll DOCUMENTS RELATED TO” all accounts associated with

Alteri, including transaction histories, device and access metadata, trading and product usage records, communications, and tax reports, covering the same timeframe.

 

Alteri argues that the Subpoenas are grossly overbroad because the requests effectively call for every single financial document regarding Alteri over a three-year time period that Fifth Third and Chase possess; these requests are not confined to specific transactions or relevant timeframes, resulting in an undue burden on the financial institutions and an unwarranted invasion of Alteri’s privacy. (Motion p. 4.) Not only that, but Alteri avers that the Subpoenas are duplicative and cumulative of previous third-party discovery served on Alteri.

 

Plaintiffs argue that the records “bear directly on whether Li and the Aion defendants routed investor money into the Alteri-related accounts, how those transfers were characterized, whether anything of value came back and whether and how funds moved between those accounts and cryptocurrency exchanges such as Coinbase.” (Opp. pp. 5-6.) After all, the complaint alleges that Li and the Aion defendants solicited and diverted investor funds.

 

After a review of the arguments, the court is inclined to grant the motion in part by way of modification, but will perhaps use the hearing as an informal discovery conference.

 

Under California Code of Civil Procedure Section 1985, the subpoenaing party must establish that there is “good cause for the production of the matters and things described in the subpoena, specifying the exact matters or things desired to be produced, setting forth in full detail the materiality thereof to the issues involved in the case[.]” (Reply p. 2.) Moreover, pursuant to CCP section 1985.8(i), 2020.220(i), hat the “court shall limit the frequency or extent of discovery of electronically stored information” if “It is possible to obtain the information from some other source that is more convenient, less burdensome, or less expensive” or if “The discovery sought is unreasonably cumulative or duplicative.”

 

Plaintiffs seek they information they do because of the “evidence show[ing] financial links among Li, Aion and Alteri.” They argue that Alteri’s complete records are necessary to trace the “upstream source of those funds” and that these “requests are tied to a concrete, legitimate need to trace investor funds.” (Opp. p. 8.) But, as noted in reply, even if Plaintiffs sent Li funds from August 2023 to September 2024; even if Li allegedly transferred $2.6

million to Alteri and Alteri TX Deployment LLC; and even if approximately $852,802.50 was used to purchase cryptocurrency mining equipment located in Texas, Plaintiffs fail to demonstrate how subpoenaing all of Alteri’s bank records will show whether or not Li used Plaintiffs’ funds to make his transfers to Alteri or whether Li has any claim to equity in Alteri. (See Reply p. 3, citing See County of L.A. v. Superior Court (2021) 65 Cal.App.5th 621, 637 [nonparty discovery requires “specific facts justifying the discovery sought”].) If there are answers, those would be readily available in records obtainable from Li and his financial institutions to determine if funds from Li and his entities were moved to Alteri (or vice versa).

 

Additionally, as explained by Alteri, Plaintiffs already served Alteri with a third-party subpoena before it was a party, and the opposition acknowledges as much.[14] That subpoena asked, inter alia, for “All financial records showing revenue generated from the TX MINING SITE; All bank statements showing receipt of funds for the purchase of the TX MINING SITE; and All DOCUMENT RELATING TO funds transfers in fiat and cryptocurrency. between YOU and DEFENDANTS RELATING TO TX MINING SITE.” Now, Plaintiffs seek the same information from Alteri’s financial providers such that they are duplicative and cumulative of previous third-party discovery served on Alteri and should be quashed for this additional reason. (See also Reply p. 3 [“Alteri timely responded to two different subpoenas served on it before it was a party and produced extensive documents, including all invoices for Li’s cryptocurrency machine purchases and records evidencing corporate records and documents and other financial records.”].)

 

Notwithstanding, the court is inclined to order Plaintiffs to narrow their discovery to the specific transfers from Li and his entities.

 

The court will hear from the parties.
