Case Number: 25STCV07029    Hearing Date: August 12, 2026    Dept: 511

Tentative Ruling

 

Judge Upinder S. Kalra, Department 511

 

HEARING DATE:   August 12, 2026                                             

 

CASE NAME:           Dane Wheaton, LLC v. Paul Morris, et al.

 

CASE NO.:                25STCV07029

 

MOTION TO CHARGE JUDGMENT DEBTORS’ INTERESTS  WITH THE JUDGMENT AND TO FORECLOSE ON SUCH INTERESTS

 

MOVING PARTY:  Plaintiff/Judgment Creditor Dane Wheaton, LLC

 

RESPONDING PARTY(S): None

 

REQUESTED RELIEF:

 

1.      An order charging the interests of Judgment Debtors in the following companies: Forward Helms, LLC; Forward Keith, LLC; Forward Group Holdings, LLC; Forward Capital Holdings, LLC; Forward Management, LLC; Greentree Rd. Associates, LP; Alderson-Forward Properties, LLC; and Forward-Brentwood Properties, LLC (collectively the Limited Entities);

2.      An order directing the Limited Entities to pay any and all money or property due or to become due to Judgment Debtors to Plaintiff/Judgment Creditor until the Judgment, plus all accrued interest thereon, is paid in full; and

3.      An order granting Plaintiff/Judgment Creditor permission to immediately foreclose on such charging order to satisfy the Judgment in this action.

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

1.      Motion for charging order is GRANTED.

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

 

On March 12, 2025, Plaintiff Dane Wheaton, LLC (Plaintiff) filed a complaint against Defendants Paul Morris (Morris) and Edwards Krifcher (Krifcher) (sometimes collectively Defendants) with causes of action for: (1) guaranty [Morris]; and (2) guaranty [Krisfcher].

 

According to the complaint, Plaintiff and non-party Forward Studio City, Inc. entered a lease of commercial real property located at 4061 Laurel Canyon Blvd., Studio City, California 91604. Plaintiff alleges that Defendants separately executed personal guaranties pertaining to the lease. They allege that Forward Studio City, Inc. subsequently breached the lease, and Defendants breached their guaranties by failing to pay amounts due under the lease.

 

On October 30, 2025, default was entered against Defendants.

 

On December 3, 2025, default judgment was entered against Defendants in the amount of $292,095.58, with $437.25 in costs, and $4,810.95 in attorneys’ fees.

 

LEGAL STANDARD:

 

If a money judgment is rendered against a partner or member but not against the partnership or limited liability company, the judgment debtor's interest in the partnership or limited liability company may be applied toward the satisfaction of the judgment by an order charging the judgment debtor's interest pursuant to Section 15907.03, 16504, or 17705.03 of the Corporations Code.

(Code Civ. Proc. (CCP) § 708.310.)

 

“[A] court may enter a charging order against the transferable interest of the judgment debtor for the unsatisfied amount of the judgment.” (Corps. Code § 17705.03 [application to limited liability companies].) “Upon a showing that distributions under a charging order will not pay the judgment debt within a reasonable time, foreclose the lien and order the sale of the transferable interest.” (Id. at subd. (b)(3).)

 

ANALYSIS:

 

Request for Judicial Notice

 

The court GRANTS Plaintiff/Judgment Creditor’s request for judicial notice of Exhibits 2-9 to show the Limited Entities’ registered agent for service of process. (Evid. Code § 452(c), (h); See Kalnoki v. First American Trustee Servicing Solutions, LLC (2017) 8 Cal.App.5th 23,37.) However, the court only takes judicial notice of the foregoing documents only as to “the existence, content and authenticity of public records and other specified documents”; it does not take judicial notice of the truth of the factual matters asserted in those documents. (Dominguez v. Bonta (2022) 87 Cal. App. 5th 389, 400.)

 

Charging Order

 

Plaintiff/Judgment Creditor contends a charging order is appropriate because Defendants/Judgment Debtors have membership/partnership interests in the Limited Entities who are not also named as Judgment Debtors. They add that foreclosure of these interests is necessary because the small amounts of distributions (if any) from the Limited Entities will never satisfy the judgment, let alone within a reasonable amount of time.

 

The court has received no oppositions.

 

Here, relief is warranted. First, Plaintiff/Judgment Creditor established a judgment against Defendants/Judgment Debtors. (Declaration of Norman H. Levine ¶ 2.) Second, although their evidence concerning the Limited Entities is solely based on a brief declaration of counsel (Declaration of Nicolette R. Kumar ¶¶ 2-3) without an accompanying transcript of Morris’ debtor’s examination, the showing satisfies their burden.

 

Accordingly, the court GRANTS the motion for charging order without prejudice.

 

CONCLUSION:

 

            For the foregoing reasons, the Court decides the pending motion as follows:

 

1.      Motion for charging order is GRANTED.

Moving party is to give notice.

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Case Number: 25STCV20069    Hearing Date: August 12, 2026    Dept: 511

Tentative Ruling

 

Judge Upinder S. Kalra, Department 511

 

HEARING DATE:   May 12, 2026                                     

 

CASE NAME:           Charlene Egbe v. Jason Hamilton, et al.

 

CASE NO.:                25STCV20069

 

DEMURRER TO FIRST AMENDED COMPLAINT WITH MOTION TO STRIKE

 

MOVING PARTY:  Defendant Integrative Surgical Associates Group, LLC

 

RESPONDING PARTY(S): Plaintiff Charlene Egbe

 

REQUESTED RELIEF:

 

1.      Demurrer to the entire FAC as uncertain and for failing to state sufficient facts;

2.      Demurrer to the first, second, third, fourth, and fifth causes of action (separately) for failing to state sufficient facts to constitute a cause of action and for uncertainty.

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

1.      Demurrers to the FAC are OVERRULED in full;

2.      Motion to strike is DENIED without prejudice;

3.      ISAG to file their answer within 14 days of this ruling.

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

 

On July 8, 2025, Plaintiff Charlene Egbe (Plaintiff) filed a complaint against Defendant Jason Hamilton (Defendant) with causes of action for medical battery. According to the complaint, Defendant performed unauthorized surgical procedures at his office on October 16, 2023 and December 10, 2024. Plaintiff alleges that Defendant performed the procedures without proper disclosure, without informed consent or preoperative planning, and other circumstances that violated Plaintiff’s rights. 

 

On December 3, 2025, Plaintiff filed the operative first amended complaint (FAC) changing Defendant Hamilton to Jason Hamilton, M.D., adding Defendants Osborne Head & Neck Institute, Inc., Integrative Surgical Associates Group, and Hamilton Facial Plastic Surgery, Inc. Plaintiff also added causes of action for: (2) negligence; (3) violation of Cal. Confidentiality of Medical Information Act; (4) invasion of privacy (public disclosure of private facts); and (5) intentional infliction of emotional distress. 

 

On December 4, 2025, the court entered a protective order pursuant to the parties’ request. 

 

On January 6, 2026, Defendant Jason Hamilton, M.D. (Dr. Hamilton) filed the instant demurrer with motion to strike which the court OVERRULED and DENIED.

 

On March 18, 2026, Dr. Hamilton filed an answer to the FAC.

 

On April 24, 2026, Defendants Dr. Hamilton, Hamilton Facial Plastic Surgery, Inc., and Osborne Head and Neck Specialty Group, Inc. (erroneously sued as Osborne Head and Neck Institute, Inc.) filed an answer to the FAC.

 

On June 12, 2026, Defendant Integrative Surgical Associates Group, LLC (ISAG) filed the instant demurrer to the FAC.[1] On July 30, 2026, Plaintiff filed oppositions to the demurrer and to a motion to strike. On August 5, 2026, ISAG filed replies in support of their demurrer and a motion to strike.

 

LEGAL STANDARD:

 

Meet and Confer 

 

Prior to filing a demurrer, the demurring party is required to satisfy their meet and confer obligations pursuant to Code of Civ. Proc. §430.41, and demonstrate that they so satisfied their meet and confer obligation by submitting a declaration pursuant to Code of Civ. Proc. §430.41(a)(2) & (3). ¿The meet and confer requirement also applies to motions to strike. (CCP § 435.5.)¿

 

Here, ISAG sent one meet and confer correspondence to Plaintiff on May 4, 2026. (Declaration of Joseph Broughton ¶¶ 5-6.) One letter is hardly sufficient meet and confer efforts. Still, insufficient meet and confer alone is not grounds to sustain or overrule a demurrer or the grant or deny a motion to strike. (CCP §§ 430.41(a)(4), 435.5(a)(4).)

 

Demurrer – Sufficiency of Facts 

 

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action.¿(Hahn v. Mirda¿(2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context.¿In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice.¿(Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)¿“A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. …. The only issue involved in a demurrer hearing is whether the complaint, as it stands, unconnected with extraneous matters, states a cause of action.”¿(Hahn¿147 Cal.App.4th at 747.)¿¿ 

¿ 

When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context, accepting the alleged facts as true. (Nolte v. Cedars-Sinai Medical Center (2015) 236 Cal.App.4th 1401, 1406.) Courts also consider exhibits attached to the complaint and incorporated by reference. (See Frantz v. Blackwell (1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 91, 94 (Frantz).)¿ 

 

Demurrer – Uncertainty

 

CCP § 430.10(f) provides that a party may demurrer to a pleading because: “The pleading is uncertain. As used in this subdivision, “uncertain” includes ambiguous and unintelligible.”

 

However, “demurrers for uncertainty are disfavored, and are granted only if the pleading is so incomprehensible that a defendant cannot reasonably respond.” (Morris v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A. (2022) 78 Cal.App.5th 279, 292.) “A demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Chen v. Berenjian (2019) 33 Cal.App.5th 811, 822.) “A demurrer for uncertainty should be overruled when the facts as to which the complaint is uncertain are presumptively within the defendant’s knowledge.” (Ibid.)

 

Motion to Strike 

 

The court may, upon a motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (CCP § 436(a).) The court may also strike all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Id., § 436(b).) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (Id.¿§¿437.)¿“When the defect which justifies striking a complaint is capable of cure, the court should allow leave to amend.” (Vaccaro v. Kaiman¿(1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 761, 768.)¿¿¿¿

 

ANALYSIS:

 

Request for Judicial Notice

 

The court GRANTS ISAG’s request for judicial notice of the Civil Restraining Order filed June 12, 2025 in Los Angeles Superior Court Case No. 25STRO03715. (Evid. Code § 452(d); See Kalnoki v. First American Trustee Servicing Solutions, LLC (2017) 8 Cal.App.5th 23,37.) However, the court only takes judicial notice of the foregoing documents only as to “the existence, content and authenticity of public records and other specified documents”; it does not take judicial notice of the truth of the factual matters asserted in those documents. (Dominguez v. Bonta (2022) 87 Cal. App. 5th 389, 400.)

 

Demurrer

 

First Cause of Action: Medical Battery

 

ISAG contends this claim is uncertain because it refers collectively to all Defendants and that it is otherwise insufficiently plead as to them because there are no facts as to ISAG’s involvement in a medical battery.

 

Plaintiff argues that ISAG inappropriately raises factual challenges inappropriately resolved now. She also argues that the uncertainty argument fails because ISAG clearly understands the claims asserted against it and the conduct at issue.[2] Finally, she argues that she alleged Dr. Hamilton was ISAG’s agent, therefore ISAG is vicariously liable for his actions.

 

ISAG reiterates their uncertainty argument on reply.

 

Like a claim for civil battery, the elements of a claim for medical battery are: 1. The defendant intentionally did an act which resulted in a harmful or offensive contact with the plaintiff’s person; 2. The plaintiff did not consent to the contact; and 3. The harmful or offensive contact caused injury, damage, loss or harm to the plaintiff. (Piedra v. Dugan (2004) 123 Cal.App.4th 1483, 1495 (Piedra).) In the medical context, battery lies when the doctor “obtains consent of the patient to perform one type of treatment and subsequently performs a substantially different treatment for which consent was not obtained.” (Ibid.)  

 

“[O]ne who gives informed consent to a surgery cannot recover for resulting harm under a theory of battery.” (Id. at p.1497.) However, a patient can place limits on the consent. (Ibid.) If the doctor exceeds the terms or conditions of the consent, then the doctor cannot rely on consent as a defense to battery. (Ibid.)  

 

Medical battery due to violation of conditional consent also has three elements: 1. Consent was conditional, (2) the doctor intentionally violated the condition while providing treatment, and 3. The patient suffered resulting harm. (Id. at p. 1497-1498.) 

 

Here, Plaintiff sufficiently stated this claim for pleading purposes.

 

Notably, Plaintiff alleges an agency relationship between all Defendants. First, Plaintiff alleges that ISAG “is a California entity involved in surgical services, patient intake, consent processing, and medical record handling for Hamilton’s procedures.” (FAC ¶ 10.) Second, she alleges she “signed additional consent documents under ISA[G]’s name based on Hamilton’s representations.” (Ibid.) Third, Plaintiff also alleges, generally, that “each Defendant was the agent, employee, representative, partner, joint venturer, or ostensible agent of each Defendant and acted within the course and scope of that relationship.” (FAC ¶ 16.) Additionally, she alleges that ISAG “controlled, directed, supervised, authorized, ratified, or failed to supervise the acts of Hamilton and the DOE Defendants.” (FAC ¶ 17.) Finally, she alleges each corporate Defendant “established, implemented, or negligently failed to implement policies relating to: a) surgical protocols; b) informed consent; c) staff participation in procedures; d) patient privacy and PHI protection; e) handling, storing, transmitting and releasing medical records; and f) preparation and review of legal filings containing patient information.” (FAC ¶ 18.) These allegations are sufficient to defeat ISAG’s uncertainty demurrer because there are no affirmatively plead specific allegations contradicting these general allegations. (Garton v. Title Ins. & Trust Co. (1980) 106 Cal.App.3d 365, 376 [“Generally, an allegation of agency is an allegation of ultimate fact and is, of itself, sufficient to avoid a demurrer.”][3]

 

Next, Plaintiff alleged that Dr. Hamilton, an agent of ISAG, exceeded the conditional consent for her procedures. (FAC ¶¶ 20, 21, 24, 25, 45, 46, 49, 50.) Whether Dr. Hamilton turns out to be ISAG’s agent is a factual dispute for future proceedings. This claim therefore survives the demurrer stage.

 

Accordingly, the court OVERRULES ISAG’s demurrer to the first cause of action.

 

Second Cause of Action: Negligence (including failure to obtain informed consent)

 

ISAG reiterates their uncertainty argument. They add that there are no facts that ISAG owed a duty to her or breached their duty.

 

Plaintiff argues this is a factual dispute. She also argues that she alleged ISAG participated in identified healthcare and administrative functions and that ISAG and the other corporate defendants independently failed to implement or follow policies governing consent, staff participation, supervision, records, and patient privacy. Finally, she argues that ISAG owed her a duty of care as a medical facility or medical corporation and breached that duty in various ways.

 

ISAG reiterates their uncertainty argument on reply.

 

The basic elements of a¿negligence¿claim¿are:¿a duty on the part of the defendant towards the plaintiff; defendant’s breach of that duty; causation; and harm to plaintiff. (Kesner v. Sup. Ct. (Pneumo¿Abex, LLC)¿(2016 1 Cal.5th 1132, 1142.)¿

 

Here, Plaintiff sufficiently stated a negligence claim. First, she alleged ISAG owed a duty of care as a healthcare provider, medical facility, and medical corporation. (FAC ¶¶ 10, 60.) Second, she alleged ISAG breached this duty in various ways ranging from exceeding consent, failing to obtain consent, withholding records, negligently handling records, and failing to protect her private information. (FAC ¶ 61.) These are the only two elements at issue. They survive the pleadings stage.

 

Accordingly, the court OVERRULES ISAG’s demurrer to the second cause of action.

 

Third Cause of Action: Violation of California Confidentiality of Medical Information Act (CMIA)

 

ISAG contends this claim fails for uncertainty, ISAG played no role in disclosing Plaintiff’s private information, and there are no facts ISAG was a provider of health care as contemplated by the CMIA.[4]

 

Plaintiff argues she sufficiently stated this claim and ISAG otherwise improperly makes factual challenges.

 

ISAG reiterates their uncertainty argument on reply as well as reiterating that the judicially noticed documents show they had no role in the public court filing serving as the basis of this claim.

 

“No provider of health care shall disclose medical information regarding a patient of the provider without first obtaining an authorization, except as provided in subdivision (b) or (c)” (Heller v. Norcal Mutual Ins. Co. (1994) 8 Cal.4th 30, 38 (Heller) [citing Civ. Code § 56.10(a)].) 

 

“Considered together, the statutory provisions require a health care provider to hold confidential a patient's medical information unless the information falls under one of several exceptions to the act.” (Heller, supra, 8 Cal.4th at p. 38.) “ 

 

“Thus, in order to violate the act, a provider of health care must make an unauthorized, unexcused disclosure of privileged medical information. A provider is relieved from liability under the act if it can show that the disclosure is excepted either by the mandatory (§ 56.10, subd. (b)) or permissive (§ 56.10(c)(4)) provisions of the act, allowing disclosure of medical information under specified circumstances.” 

(Heller, supra, 8 Cal.4th at p. 38.) 

 

Medical Information includes the patient's name, address, electronic mail address, telephone number, or social security number, or other information that, alone or in combination with other publicly available information, reveals the identity of the individual. (Civ. Code § 56.05(j)(1).) 

 

Here, Plaintiff sufficiently stated this claim. First, she alleged that ISAG was a provider of health care under the statute. (FAC ¶ 66.) Second, she alleged ISAG made at least five unauthorized disclosures in public court filings without her authorization. (FAC ¶¶ 67-69.) As discussed above, whether Dr. Hamilton’s disclosure was in the scope of his alleged agency is a future factual dispute inappropriate for resolution now.

 

Accordingly, the court OVERRULES ISAG’s demurrer to the third cause of action.

 

Fourth Cause of Action: Invasion of Privacy (public disclosure of private facts)

 

The arguments here are the same as for the third cause of action.

 

Generally, invasion of privacy requires: (1) a legally protected privacy interest; (2) reasonable expectation of privacy under the circumstances; and (3) defendant’s serious invasion of privacy. Four distinct kinds of activities have been found to violate this privacy protection and give rise to tort liability: (1) intrusion into private matters; (2) public disclosure of private facts; (3) publicity placing a person in a false light; and (4) misappropriation of a person’s name or likeness. To prevail on an invasion of privacy claim, the plaintiff must have conducted himself or herself in a manner consistent with an actual expectation of privacy. (Moreno v. Hanford Sentinel, Inc. (2009) 172 Cal.App.4th 1125, 1129; Lachtman v. Regents of University of California (2007) 158 Cal.App.4th 187, 213.)¿ 

 

The elements of a claim for public disclosure of private facts are: (1) public disclosure; (2) of a private fact; (3) which would be offensive and objectionable to the reasonable person; and (4) which is not of legitimate public concern. (Nelson v. Tucker Ellis LLP (2020) 48 Cal.App.5th 827, 843.)

 

For similar reasons articulated in the third cause of action, the court OVERRULES ISAG’s demurrer to the fourth cause of action.

 

Fifth Cause of Action: Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress (IIED)

 

ISAG contends there are no facts of their conduct supporting this claim.

 

Plaintiff argues that she sufficiently stated this claim because ISAG engaged in a continuing course of conduct that extended beyond her medical treatment, including publicly disclosing her confidential medical information in publicly accessible court filings without authorization.

 

ISAG reiterates their uncertainty argument on reply as well as reiterating that the judicially noticed documents show they had no role in the public court filing serving as the basis of this claim.

 

In order to properly allege a cause of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress, a plaintiff must allege the following elements: “(1) extreme and outrageous conduct by the defendant with the intention of causing, or reckless disregard of the probability of causing, emotional distress; (2) the plaintiff suffered severe emotional distress; and (3) the defendant's extreme and outrageous conduct was the actual and proximate cause of the severe emotional distress.”¿ (Crouch v. Trinity Christian Center of Santa Ana, Inc.¿(2019) 39 Cal.App.5th 995, 1007.)¿ “Conduct, to be¿‘ “outrageous,” ’¿must be so extreme as to exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated in civilized society.”¿ (Trerice¿v. Blue Cross California¿(1989) 209 Cal.App.3d 878, 883.)¿ “Liability for intentional infliction of emotional distress¿‘ “does not extend to mere insults, indignities, threats, annoyances, petty oppressions, or other trivialities.” ’¿”¿ (Hughes v. Pair¿(2009) 46 Cal.4th 1035, 1051.)¿ “In order to avoid a demurrer, the plaintiff must allege with ‘great[¿] specificity’ the acts which he or she believes are so extreme as to exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated in a civilized community.”¿ (Vasquez¿v. Franklin Management Real Estate Fund, Inc.¿(2013) 222 Cal.App.4th 819, 832.)¿¿¿ 

 

Here, Plaintiff sufficiently stated a claim for IIED. The only issue is whether Plaintiff alleged extreme and outrageous conduct by ISAG. For pleading purposes, she did. Plaintiff alleges both medical battery and disclosure of her private medical information for which ISAG is allegedly vicariously liable. (FAC ¶ 89.) Thus, this is sufficient for the pleadings stage.

 

Accordingly, the court OVERRULES ISAG’s demurrer to the fifth cause of action.

 

Motion to Strike

 

As noted above, the court has not received the purported motion to strike. Without it, the court cannot evaluate the relief sought to render a coherent ruling.

 

Accordingly, the court DENIES the motion to strike without prejudice.

 

CONCLUSION:

 

            For the foregoing reasons, the Court decides the pending motion as follows:

 

1.      Demurrers to the FAC are OVERRULED in full;

2.      Motion to strike is DENIED without prejudice;

3.      ISAG to file their answer within 14 days of this ruling.

Moving party is to give notice.

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Case Number: 25STCV36833    Hearing Date: August 12, 2026    Dept: 511

Tentative Ruling

 

Judge Upinder S. Kalra, Department 511

 

HEARING DATE:   August 12, 2026                                             

 

CASE NAME:           Michelle Olivares Ramirez v. Din Tai Fung Century City Restaurant, LLC, et al.

 

CASE NO.:                25STCV36833

 

MOTION TO COMPEL ARBITRATION

 

MOVING PARTY:  Defendants Din Tai Fung Century City Restaurant, LLC; Din Tai Fung; Din Tai Fung North America; and Hector Zeledon

 

RESPONDING PARTY(S): Plaintiff Michelle Olivares Ramirez

 

REQUESTED RELIEF:

 

1.      An order compelling this matter to arbitration and issuing a stay pending arbitration.

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

1.      Motion to compel arbitration is GRANTED;

2.      This matter is ordered STAYED pending arbitration.

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

 

On December 17, 2025, Plaintiff Michelle Olivares Ramirez (Plaintiff) filed a complaint against Defendants Din Tai Fund Century City Restaurant, LLC; Din Tai Fung; Din Tai Fung North America; and Hector Zeledon (Defendants) with nine causes of action for: (1) discrimination in violation of FEHA; (2) hostile work environment in violation of FEHA; (3) retaliation in violation of FEHA; (4) failure to prevent discrimination, harassment, or retaliation in violation of FEHA; (5) failure to provide reasonable accommodation in violation of FEHA; (6) failure to engage in the interactive process in violation of FEHA; (7) interference with and retaliation for exercising pregnancy disability leave rights; (8) retaliation for using paid sick leave; and (9) wrongful termination of employment in violation of public policy.

 

According to the complaint, Plaintiff was a strong-performing Barista/Expo who became pregnant and sought pregnancy-related accommodations, including medically excused absences. After notifying multiple managers of her pregnancy and experiencing pregnancy-related illness and an emergency room visit, she received warnings tied to the company’s attendance points system despite providing doctor’s notes. On October 1, 2024—just weeks after disclosing her pregnancy—Plaintiff was terminated, allegedly under pretextual “attendance” grounds, which she claims were actually motivated by discrimination, retaliation, and failure to accommodate her pregnancy and related conditions.

 

On February 23, 2026, Defendants filed the instant motion to compel arbitration. On July 30, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition. On August 5, 2026, Defendants filed a reply.

 

LEGAL STANDARD:

 

The Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”), while a federal statute, applies in California courts and requires state courts to enforce arbitration agreements as required by the federal common law developed under the FAA. (See¿Southland Corp. v. Keating¿(1984) 465 U.S. 1, 15-16 (Southland Corp.);¿Broughton v. Cigna¿Healthplans¿(1999) 21 Cal.4th 1066, 1074-78, superseded by statute on another ground as¿stated¿in¿Ferguson v. Corinthian Colleges, Inc.¿(9th Cir. 2013) 733 F.3d 928, 937.).) The FAA preempts and invalidates state law and state judicial decisions that disfavor arbitration or require arbitration provisions to pass higher scrutiny. (Southland Corp.,¿supra, at p. 12;¿Perry v. Thomas¿(1987) 482 U.S. 483, 490.) If the parties¿designate¿the FAA applies, then California arbitration law is preempted. (See, e.g.,¿Rodriguez v. American Techs., Inc.¿(2006) 136 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1121-1122.)¿However, courts have found that where the FAA is found not to apply, the California Arbitration Act (Code Civ. Proc. § 1280 et seq.) applies. (See¿Valencia v. Smyth¿(2010) 185 Cal.App.4th 153, 178 (Valencia).)¿¿¿¿ 

¿¿¿ 

¿A court’s inquiry is limited to a determination of (1) whether a valid arbitration agreement exists and (2) whether the arbitration agreement covers the dispute. (9 U.S.C. § 4;¿Chiron Corp. v. Ortho Diagnostics Systems, Inc.¿(9th Cir. 2000) 207 F.3d 1126, 1130;¿Howsam v. Dean Witter Reynolds, Inc.¿(2002) 537 U.S. 79, 84; see¿Simula, Inc. v. Autoliv, Inc.¿(9th Cir. 1999) 175 F.3d 716, 720 [if the finding is affirmative on both counts the FAA requires the Court to enforce the¿arbitration agreement in accordance with its terms]; see¿Omar v. Ralphs Grocery Co.¿(2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 955, 961 [In determining the enforceability of an arbitration agreement, the court first considers “two ‘gateway issues’ of arbitrability: (1) whether there was an agreement to arbitrate between the parties, and (2) whether the agreement covered the dispute at issue”]¿Lacayo v. Cataline Restaurant Group Inc.¿(2019) 38 Cal.App.5th 244, 257 (Lacayo) [Where moving party meets initial burden, “the party opposing arbitration must prove by a preponderance of the evidence any defense to the petition”].)¿¿¿¿ 

¿ 

The general rule is that the FAA governs all agreements to arbitrate in contracts “involving interstate commerce.” (Higgins v. Superior Court¿(2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 1238, 1247.) The term “involving” commerce “is broad and is indeed the functional equivalent of “affecting’ commerce.” (Allied-Bruce Terminix Companies, Inc. v. Dobson¿(1995) 513 U.S. 265, 273-274.) The U.S. Supreme Court has held that this broad interpretation includes employment contracts. (See¿Circuit City Stores v. Adams¿(2001) 532 U.S. 105, 106.) The defendant bears the burden of proving applicability of the FAA by showing that its activities¿constitute¿interstate commerce. (Hoover v. Am. Income Life Ins. Co.¿(2012) 206 Cal.App.4th 1193, 1207.) Failure to¿demonstrate¿that the employment agreement affects interstate commerce¿renders¿the FAA inapplicable. (See¿Lane v. Francis Capital Management LLC¿(2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 676, 687-688 (Lane);¿Woolls v. Superior Court¿(2005) 127 Cal.App.4th 197, 212.)¿¿ 

¿ 

Even where the FAA governs the interpretation of arbitration clauses, California law governs whether an arbitration agreement has been formed in the first instance. (Baker v. Osborne Development Corp.¿(2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 884, 893.)¿ 

 

As a threshold matter, the parties do not dispute that the FAA applies to this matter. It otherwise applies because the 2024 Agreement expressly states it does and Defendants engage in interstate commerce. (Declaration of Ryann Hanna ¶¶ 4, 7, Exhibit A, item 6.) Thus, the FAA applies.

 

ANALYSIS:

 

Evidentiary Objections

 

The court is unaware of any legal authority which requires a court to rule on evidentiary objections on a motion, except as to a motion for summary motion/adjudication or a special motion to strike. ([CCP § 437c (q); CCP § 425.16 (b)(2); see also, Sweetwater Union High School Dist. v. Gilbane Building Co. (2019) 6 Cal.5th 931, 947-949.) Thus, the court declines to rule on these objections.

 

Request for Judicial Notice

 

The court GRANTS Plaintiff’s request to take judicial notice of Government Code section 12940(j)(4)(C)  and DENIES Plaintiff’s request for judicial notice as to remaining matters.[1]

 

Compel Arbitration

 

Defendants seek to enforce the February 5, 2024 Arbitration Agreement (Agreement). (Mot. 9:13-21; Reply 6, n. 1.)

 

i.                    Existence of Arbitration Agreement 

¿“The party¿seeking¿to compel arbitration bears the burden of proving the existence of an arbitration agreement, while the party opposing the petition bears the burden of¿establishing¿a defense to the agreement’s enforcement.” ’”¿ (Beco v. Fast Auto Loans¿(2022) 86 Cal.App.5th 292, 302.)¿ To¿determine¿the existence of an arbitration agreement, the court uses “a three-step burden-shifting process.”¿ (Iyere¿v. Wise Auto Group¿(2023) 87 Cal.App.5th 747, 755.)¿ “The arbitration proponent must first recite verbatim, or provide a copy of, the alleged agreement.¿ [Citations.]¿ A movant can bear this¿initial¿burden ‘by attaching a copy of the arbitration agreement purportedly bearing the opposing party’s signature.’”¿ (Ibid.¿[internal citations omitted].)¿ “If the movant bears its initial burden, the burden shifts to the party opposing arbitration to identify a factual dispute as to the agreement’s existence¿. . . .”¿ (Ibid.)¿ If the opposing party meets its burden to “submit sufficient evidence to create a factual dispute” as to the existence of the agreement, the burden shifts back to the arbitration proponent, who¿retains¿the ultimate burden of proving its existence by a preponderance of the evidence.¿ (Ibid.)¿ 

 

Here, Defendants met their initial burden to show an arbitration agreement exists. Notably, they attach the two arbitration agreements Plaintiff signed on June 19, 2023 and February 5, 2024. (Declaration of Ryann Hanna (Hanna Decl.) ¶ 7, Exhibits A, B.)

 

“If the moving party meets its initial prima facie burden and the opposing party disputes the agreement, then in the second step, the opposing party bears the burden of producing evidence to challenge the authenticity of the agreement.” (Gamboa v. Northeast Community Clinic (2021) 72 Cal. App. 5th 158, 165 (Gamboa).) The evidence must be sufficient to create a factual dispute to shift the burden back to the arbitration proponent who retains the ultimate burden of proving, by a preponderance of the evidence, the authenticity of the signature. (Iyere supra, 87 Cal.App.5th at p. 755.)

 

Here, Plaintiff sufficiently challenged her signature. She avers under penalty of perjury that she was physically presented with documents to sign and does not recall signing documents electronically. (Declaration of Michelle Olivares Ramirez (Ramirez Decl.) ¶¶ 4, 5.) Under Gamboa, this is sufficient to return the burden back to Defendant since they are seeking to enforce an electronic signature.

 

Once Plaintiff challenges the validity of the signature, “defendants were then required to establish by a preponderance of the evidence that the signature was authentic.” (Espejo v. Southern California Permanente Medical Group (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 1047, 1060 (Espejo).)¿Espejo¿dealt with an electronic signature. In¿Espejo, the supplemental declaration of the systems consultant provided the necessary information to¿establish¿the authenticity of the document, specifically how the unique username and password were only accessible to that specific individual. (Id.¿at 1062). In¿Gamboa, in contrast, the trial court determined the employer¿failed to¿sustain its burden of proof because it presented no admissible evidence rebutting the employee’s claim denying remembering signing any arbitration document. (Gamboa, supra,¿72 Cal.App.5th at 169.) The¿Gamboa¿panel found no error in the trial court’s order.¿¿ 

 

Here, Defendant met their burden to authenticate Plaintiff’s electronic signature by a preponderance of the evidence. First, Defendants provides evidence that they do not have a practice of distributing physical copies of onboarding documents, updated policies, or employment-related documents for acknowledgement or signature. (Supplemental Declaration of Ryann Hanna (Supp Hanna Decl.) ¶ 5.) Second, Defendants have no copies of any documents physically signed by Plaintiff. (Id. at ¶ 6.) Instead, they only have electronically signed employment documents, including the two arbitration agreements, her Form I-9, and Form W-4. (Id. at ¶ 7.) Third, Defendants articulate that, through the Paycom system, the employee creates a unique username and password that is only accessible to them to complete employment paperwork. (Hanna Decl. ¶¶ 9, 10, 11.) The system cannot be accessed except by the employee. (Ibid.) Considered together, this sufficiently authenticates Plaintiff’s electronic signatures on the 2024 agreement Defendants seek to enforce.

 

ii.                  Application to Current Dispute – Ending Forced Arbitration of Sexual Harassment/Assault (EFAA) Applicability

Plaintiff argues the Agreement is barred under EFAA due to her pregnancy harassment claim. Specifically, she argues that she pleads termination and other adverse actions due to her pregnancy since the termination was a hostile message concerning her pregnancy.

 

Defendants reply that Plaintiff has not plausibly alleged a pregnancy harassment (rather than a pregnancy discrimination) claim, her allegations are merely conclusions, and there are no allegations of a hostile message.

 

The term “sexual harassment dispute” means a dispute relating to conduct that is alleged to constitute sexual harassment under applicable Federal, Tribal, or State Law. (9 U.S.C.A. § 401(d).) “[A]t the election of the person alleging conduct constituting a sexual harassment dispute . . . no¿predispute¿arbitration agreement . . . shall be valid or enforceable with respect to a case which is filed under Federal, Tribal, or State law¿and relates¿to the . . . sexual harassment dispute.” (Id.¿at § 402(a).) (italics¿added.) “An issue as to whether this chapter applies with respect to a dispute shall be determined under Federal Law.” (Id.¿at subd. (b).)¿“Under the EFAA, when a plaintiff's lawsuit contains at least one claim that fits within the scope of the act, the arbitration agreement is unenforceable as to all claims asserted in the lawsuit.”¿(Liu v.¿Miniso¿Depot CA, Inc.¿(2024) 105 Cal.App.5th 791, 800¿(Liu).)¿¿¿¿ 

 

Under FEHA, an employer cannot harass an employee because of sex, which includes sexual harassment, gender harassment, and harassment based on pregnancy, childbirth, or related medical conditions. (Gov. Code §§ 12940(j)(1) and (j)(4)(C).)

 

An employee “claiming harassment based upon a hostile work environment must demonstrate that the conduct complained of was¿severe enough or sufficiently pervasive¿to alter the conditions of employment and create a work environment that qualifies as hostile or abusive to employees¿because of their sex.” (Lyle v. Warner Brothers Television Productions (2006) 38 Cal.4th 264, 279 (Lyle).) The employee must be subjected to “sexual advances, conduct, or comments” that were: “(1) unwelcome;” “(2) because of sex;” and “(3) sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of [their] employment and create an abusive work environment.” (Ibid.) Such conduct must also be “imputable to [their] employer” (Ibid.) 

 

Courts look at “all the circumstances” including “the frequency of the discriminatory conduct; its severity; whether it is physically threatening or humiliating, or a mere offensive utterance; and whether it unreasonably interferes with an employee’s work performance.” (Holmes v. Petrovich Development Co., LLC (2011) 191 Cal.App.4th 1047, 1059.) The environment “must be both objectively and subjectively offensive, one that a reasonable person would find hostile or abusive, and one that the victim in fact did perceive to be so.” (Ibid.) There is no recovery for harassment that is “occasional, isolated, sporadic, or trivial.” (Id. at p. 1061.) “Rather, a plaintiff must show a concerted pattern of harassment that is repeated, routine, or generalized in nature.” (Ibid.)

 

“[H]arassment often does not involve any official exercise of delegated power on behalf of the employer.” (Roby v. McKesson Corp. (2009) 47 Cal.4th 686, 706.) “[H]arassment focuses on situations in which the social environment of the workplace becomes intolerable because the harassment (whether verbal, physical, or visual) communicates an offensive message to the harassed employee.” (Ibid.) Commonly necessary personnel management actions do not come within the meaning of harassment even though they may retrospectively be found discriminatory if based on improper motives. (Id. at p. 707 [discussing promotions by one supervisor of only women as conveying hostile message to non-favored employees that women were sexual playthings].) Official employment actions “can also have a secondary effect of communicating a hostile message” when “the actions establish a widespread pattern of bias.” (Id. at p. 709.)

 

Here, Plaintiff’s hostile work environment harassment claim based on her pregnancy is insufficiently alleged to invoke EFAA. First, Plaintiff does not allege repeated, routine, or generalized harassment based on her pregnancy. Instead, she alleges her managers congratulated her upon sharing the news, informed her she needed a doctor’s note for accommodations beyond what the law already provided, she was not required to share pregnancy test results to receive accommodations since this was personal information, she was covered by law once pregnant, and to communicate with leaders or her team when she needed breaks or to sit. (Compl. ¶¶ 14(f), (j).) Second, Plaintiff’s only allegation supporting this claim is Defendant Zeledon’s October 1, 2024 decision to terminate her due to attendance. (Compl. ¶ 17(a).) She alleges that Defendant Zeledon referred to “the recent messages” on the Manager Hotline in connection with the termination decision which meant the termination was based on her pregnancy-related call-outs. (Ibid.) There are no allegations that this one interaction with Defendant Zeledon conveyed a hostile message through official actions establishing a widespread pattern of bias. Indeed, Plaintiff alleged pre-pregnancy attendance issues and being informed in August of 2024 that her attendance points were close to zero. (Compl. ¶ 14(d).) Taken together, these allegations do not support a claim for hostile work environment harassment.

 

Therefore, EFAA does not apply.

 

The parties do not otherwise argue the Agreement does not cover the subject dispute. In fact, the Agreement covers claims for “wrongful termination, discrimination, harassment, retaliation, . . . and torts . . . .” (Hanna Decl., Exhibit A.)

 

Accordingly, an enforceable arbitration agreement exists.[2]

 

iii.                Who is Bound – Third Party Enforcement

The Agreement is between Din Tain Fund (Century City) Restaurant L, defined as “the Company,” and Plaintiff. (Hanna Decl., Exhibit A, item 1.) Defendants contend that the remaining Defendants may enforce the Agreement as third party beneficiaries since Plaintiff alleges joint employment/alter ego theories and the Agreement covers claims against the Company’s “parent, subsidiaries, affiliates. . .  as well as against owners, directors, officers, managers, employees, agents, . . . .” (Hanna Decl., Exhibit A, item 3.)

 

Plaintiff did not directly address this in her opposition. Rather, she challenges the lack of any signature by Defendants on the Agreement.

 

Generally, “only a party to an arbitration agreement is bound by or may enforce the agreement.” (Thomas v. Westlake (2012) 204 Cal.App.4th 605, 613.) One exception to this general rule provides that “when a plaintiff alleges a defendant acted as an agent of a party to an arbitration agreement, the defendant may enforce the agreement even though the defendant is not a party thereto.” (Id. at p. 614.)

 

Here, the exception allowing all Defendants to compel arbitration applies. Notably, Plaintiff alleged that all Defendants are alter egos of one another, are agents, and directly employed her. (Compl. ¶¶ 4, 5.)

 

Therefore, all Defendants may enforce the Agreement.

 

iv.                Defenses to Enforceability

In¿Armendariz,¿the California Supreme Court¿stated¿that when¿determining¿whether an arbitration agreement was unconscionable, there is both a procedural and a substantive element. (Armendariz v. Foundation Health¿Psychcare¿Service, Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 82, 114 (Armendariz)).¿ 

¿ 

a.      Procedural Unconscionability¿ 

Plaintiff argues the Agreement is procedurally unconscionable because it was a requirement for her employment, no one explained it to her, and she was rushed through executing it.

 

Defendants reply that the Agreement was voluntary, not a condition of employment, and they are not required to explain the Agreement to her.

 

Courts¿determine¿whether an agreement is procedurally unconscionable by looking at surprise and oppression. Oppression is an “inequality of bargaining power, when one party has no real power to negotiate or a meaningful choice. Surprise occurs when the allegedly unconscionable provision is¿hidden.”¿(Carmona v. Lincoln Millennium Car Wash, Inc.¿(2014) 226 Cal.App.4th¿74, 84 (Carmona).) Examples of contracts that are procedurally unconscionable are contracts of adhesion, which is a “standardized contract, which, imposed and drafted by the party of superior bargaining strength, relegates to the subscribing party only the opportunity to adhere to the¿contract or reject it.” (Armendariz, supra, at p. 113). “The circumstances relevant to establishing oppression include, but are not limited to (1) the amount of time the party is given to consider the proposed contract; (2) the amount and type of pressure exerted on the party to sign the proposed contract; (3) the length of the proposed contract and the length and complexity of the challenged provision; (4) the education and experience of the party; and (5) whether the party’s review of the proposed contract was aided by an attorney. (OTO, L.L.C. v. Kho¿(2019) 8 Cal.5th 111, 126-27 (OTO).)¿¿¿¿ 

 

Here,¿there is¿minimal procedural unconscionability. “When arbitration is a condition of employment, there is inherently economic pressure on the employee to accept arbitration. This alone is a¿fairly low¿level of procedural unconscionability.” (Cisneros Alvarez¿v.¿Altamed¿Health Services Corporation¿(2021) 60 Cal.App.5th 572, 591). Despite Defendants’ assertions, there are no opt-out provisions in the Agreement and no other terms indicating it was not a condition of Plaintiff’s continued employment. The only purported language appears above Plaintiff’s electronic signature where she “confirms” that she “voluntarily agree[s]” to be bound by the Agreement. (Hanna Decl., Exhibit A.) The court is hard-pressed to conclude this represents an ability to refuse the Agreement while remaining employed. Next, there is no evidence as to how much time Plaintiff had to review and sign the Agreement aside from her characterization that she felt rushed when called in to the HR office to review and sign it. (Ramirez Decl. ¶ 5.) This is thus distinguishable from OTO, where the employee would be financially penalized by spending time to review the agreement and the agreement was selected by a low-level employee which created the impression that any requests for explanation would be unavailing. (OTO, supra, 8 Cal.5th at p. 127-128.) Like, OTO, though, Plaintiff was not presented with a copy of the agreement that he signed. (See Id. ¶ 6.) Still, considered together, these factors weighs against surprise or oppression.¿Nonetheless, it is an adhesive contract as it was an employment condition. 

¿ 

Thus, the Agreement has some procedurally unconscionability.¿ 

 

b.      Substantive Unconscionability

Plaintiff argues the Agreement is substantively unconscionable because Defendants seek to enforce multiple agreements, it lacks mutuality without Defendants signature, provides Defendants with an unfair advantage in the arbitrator-selection process, and contains an illegal PAGA waiver.[3]

 

Defendants reply that the Agreement is not substantively unconscionable because they only seek to enforce the 2024 Agreement, providing their ability to initiate the strike-first process is not unconscionable, and there is no PAGA waiver.

 

“Substantive unconscionability pertains to the fairness of an agreement's actual terms and to assessments of whether they are overly harsh or one-sided.” (Carmona, supra, at p. 85). There are five minimum substantive requirements to an enforceable arbitration agreement: (1) neutral arbitrators, (2) more than minimal discovery, (3) written award sufficient for judicial review, (4) all types of relief otherwise available in court, and (5) no unreasonable costs or fees as a condition of access. (Armendariz, supra, at p.102.) When there is little procedural unconscionability, a party opposing arbitration must show substantial substantive unconscionability. (Id. at 114.)¿¿ 

 

Here, the Agreement is not substantively unconscionable. First, Defendants seek to enforce only one agreement – the 2024 Agreement. Therefore, there are no inconsistent terms. Second, Defendants’ initiation of a strike procedure is not substantively unconscionable because the process still provides for the selection of a neutral arbitrator. (Epstein v. Vision Service Plan (2020) 56 Cal.App.5th 223, 240.) Put differently, the Agreement allows for the selection of a neutral arbitrator. Finally, there is no PAGA waiver. Instead, the Agreement provides that non-individual PAGA claims “will be stayed in court pending completion of the arbitration of any concurrently raised arbitrable disputes and individual claims covered by this Agreement.” (Hanna Decl., Exhibit A, item 4.) Thus, there is no substantive unconscionability.

 

Accordingly, the court GRANTS Defendants’ motion to compel arbitration.

 

CONCLUSION:

 

            For the foregoing reasons, the Court decides the pending motion as follows:

 

1.      Motion to compel arbitration is GRANTED;

2.      This matter is ordered STAYED pending arbitration.

Moving party is to give notice.

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

CASE NAME:           Vilma Orellana v. Music Express, Inc.

 

CASE NO.:                26STCV06728

 

MOTION TO STRIKE

 

MOVING PARTY:  Defendant Music Express, Inc.

 

RESPONDING PARTY(S): Plaintiff Vilma Orellana

 

REQUESTED RELIEF:

 

1.      An order striking various portions of the complaint pertaining to punitive damages.

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

1.      Motion to strike is GRANTED with leave to amend;

2.      Plaintiff shall file her amended complaint within 21 days of this ruling.

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

 

On March 2, 2026, Plaintiff Vilma Orellana (Plaintiff) filed a complaint against Defendant Music Express, Inc. (Defendant) with six causes of action for: (1) discrimination in violation of FEHA; (2) retaliation in violation of FEHA; (3) failure to prevent discrimination, harassment and retaliation in violation of FEHA; (4) failure to provide reasonable accommodation in violation of FEHA; (5) failure to engage in a good faith interactive process in violation of FEHA; and (6) wrongful termination in violation of public policy.

 

According to the complaint, Plaintiff, a Hispanic billing clerk with a documented kidney-related disability, took approved FMLA leave from February to May 2024 and repeatedly provided medical documentation to Defendant. Upon returning and requesting additional medically related time off, her requests were denied while similarly situated white, non-Hispanic employees were allegedly granted such accommodations, and she was told her job was no longer protected. Five days after being denied sick time to take her son to a doctor’s appointment, she was terminated for “prolonged absence and schedule adjustments,” which she alleges were pretextual and motivated by discrimination and retaliation.

 

On May 27, 2026, Defendant filed the instant motion to strike. On July 30, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition. On August 5, 2026, Defendant filed a reply and a notice of errata correcting the request to strike paragraph 40, 53, and 84 as paragraphs 42, 54, and 87.

 

LEGAL STANDARD:

 

Meet and Confer 

 

Prior to filing a demurrer, the demurring party is required to satisfy their meet and confer obligations pursuant to Code of Civ. Proc. §430.41, and demonstrate that they so satisfied their meet and confer obligation by submitting a declaration pursuant to Code of Civ. Proc. §430.41(a)(2) & (3). ¿The meet and confer requirement also applies to motions to strike. (CCP § 435.5.)¿

 

Here, the parties met and conferred via telephone on May 22, 2026. (Declaration of Daniel Ho ¶ 5.) This requirement is therefore met.

 

Motion to Strike 

 

The court may, upon a motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (CCP § 436(a).) The court may also strike all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Id., § 436(b).) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (Id.¿§¿437.)¿“When the defect which justifies striking a complaint is capable of cure, the court should allow leave to amend.” (Vaccaro v. Kaiman¿(1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 761, 768.)¿¿¿¿

 

ANALYSIS:

 

Defendant moves to strike the following portions of the complaint:

1.      Paragraph 31;

2.      Paragraph 42;

3.      Paragraph 54;

4.      Paragraph 60;

5.      Paragraph 68;

6.      Paragraph 76;

7.      Paragraph 87; and

8.      Prayer for Relief paragraph 2: “for punitive damages, pursuant to Civil Code §§ 3294 in amounts sufficient to punish Defendants for the wrongful conduct alleged herein and to deter such conduct in the future.”

Defendant contends Plaintiff insufficiently alleged facts supporting punitive damages because there are no factual allegations of ratification or authorization by a qualifying corporate officer of the corporate defendant.

 

Plaintiff argues Defendant incorrectly demands she plead her evidence supporting her claim for punitive damages before conducting any discovery. She adds that the complaint provides dated, specific, factual allegations of wrongdoing that, taken as true, support a reasonable inference that Defendant’s decisionmakers acted with knowledge of Plaintiff’s protected medical condition, treated her differently because of her race and disability, and punished her for exercising rights.

 

Defendant replies that Plaintiff failed to plead specific facts demonstrating authorization or ratification. In fact, there is no employee identified in the complaint at all even though she alleges she was told she could not use her sick leave and informed of the reasons for her termination.

 

To¿obtain punitive damages, a plaintiff must plead sufficient facts in support of punitive damages.¿ (See¿Hilliard v. A.H. Robins Co.¿(1983) 148 Cal.App.3d 374, 391-92.)¿ In addition,¿punitive damages are allowed only where “it is proven by clear and convincing evidence that the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice.”¿ (Civ. Code, § 3294(a).)¿ Courts have viewed despicable conduct as conduct “so vile, base, contemptible, miserable,¿wretched¿or loathsome that it would be looked down upon and despised by ordinary decent people. (Scott v. Phoenix Schools, Inc.,¿(2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 702, 715.) Further, Civil Code § 3294(c) provides the definition of malice, oppression, and fraud. Malice is “conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.” (Ibid.) Oppression is “despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person's rights.” (Ibid.) Fraud is “an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known to the defendant with the intention on the part of the defendant of thereby depriving a person of property or legal rights or otherwise causing injury.”¿(Ibid.)¿¿¿

 

Subsection (b) of Civil Code § 3294 states:¿¿

¿

An employer shall not be liable for damages pursuant to subdivision (a), based upon acts of an employee of the employer, unless the employer had advance knowledge of the unfitness of the employee and employed him or her with a conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others or authorized or ratified the wrongful conduct for which the damages are awarded or was personally guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice. With respect to a corporate employer, the advance knowledge and conscious disregard, authorization, ratification or act of oppression, fraud, or malice must be on the part of an officer, director, or managing agent of the corporation.¿¿

 

Here, Plaintiff insufficiently alleged facts supporting punitive damages. Notably absent are allegations by any individuals at all. (See, e.g., Compl. ¶¶ 19, 20, 21 [Plaintiff was informed that any absences . . . would jeopardize her employment.” and “Plaintiff was told . . . her ‘job was no longer protected.’”], denied requests, “was told”) Thus, this request is insufficiently plead because it does not allege conduct by anyone, let alone a managing agent or corporate officer. (See, e.g., Nargizyan v. State Farm General Ins. Co. (2026) 120 Cal.App.5th 581, 604-605 [discussing managing agents].) It is a curable defect, though.

 

Accordingly, the court GRANTS Defendant’s motion to strike with leave to amend.
