Case Number: 23PSCV02916    Hearing Date: September 24, 2026    Dept: G

Plaintiffs Chenxu Wang and Wei Lu’s Motion for Sanctions to Enforce Compliance with Court’s Order

Respondent: NO OPPOSITION

TENTATIVE RULING

Plaintiffs Chenxu Wang and Wei Lu’s Motion for Sanctions to Enforce Compliance with Court’s Order is GRANTED IN PART and DENIED IN PART.

BACKGROUND

This is a Song-Beverly action arising out of the lease of an allegedly defective motor vehicle.  On August 11, 2021, plaintiffs Chenxu Wang and Wei Lu (collectively, Plaintiffs) leased an SUV manufactured by defendant Volkswagen Group of America, Inc. (VW).  Defendant Mesa Imports, LLC (the Dealership) allegedly failed to repair the vehicle.

On September 20, 2023, Plaintiffs filed the Complaint, alleging causes of action for (1) breach of express warranty, (2) breach of implied warranty, (3) breach of Civil Code section 1793.2, and (4) negligent repair.

On September 22, 2025, the parties informed the court that they settled the case.

On July 10, 2026, Plaintiffs filed this motion to enforce order and for sanctions, which is unopposed.  On September 2, 2026, the court continued the hearing on the motion for supplemental briefing.  On September 11, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a supplemental brief.

On August 26, 2026, Plaintiffs dismissed the action with prejudice.

The motion is set for a continued hearing on September 24, 2026.

MOTION TO ENFORCE ORDER

Plaintiffs move to enforce the court’s March 19, 2026 order awarding attorney’s fees and costs.  For the following reasons, the motion is GRANTED.

Legal Standard

The court has the authority to compel obedience to its judgments, orders, and process, and to the orders of a judge out of court, in an action or proceeding pending therein.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 128, subd. (a)(4).)

Discussion

Here, the court ordered VW to pay plaintiffs $85,505.62 in attorney’s fees and costs.  (See 3/19/2026 Min. Order, p. 1; see also 3/19/2026 Order Re: Court’s Final Ruling, p. 1.)  The court notes that its tentative ruling originally awarded $87,215.62 in attorney’s fees and costs, but the court adjusted the award at the hearing.  (See 3/19/2026 Min. Order, p. 1; compare 3/19/2026 Order Re: Court’s Final Ruling, p. 1 with 3/19/2026 Order Re: Court’s Final Ruling, p. 9.)  The court also notes that Plaintiffs’ motion to enforce order cites the original award from the tentative ruling and not the amended award from the final order.  (See Mot., pp. 2, 4, 6, 8–9.)  According to Plaintiffs, VW failed to comply with the court’s order to pay attorney’s fees and costs.  (See Mot., pp. 4, 6.)  VW did not file an opposition arguing that it paid attorney’s fees and costs.  (See Sexton v. Super. Ct. (1997) 58 Cal.App.4th 1403, 1410, applying a local rule to hold that “[t]he failure to file opposition creates an inference that the motion or demurrer is meritorious.”)

Therefore, the motion to enforce the court’s order is GRANTED.  VW is ORDERED to PAY Plaintiffs $85,505.62 in attorney’s fees and costs.

MOTION FOR SANCTIONS

Plaintiffs move for contempt sanctions and request additional attorney’s fees.  For the following reasons, the motion is GRANTED IN PART and DENIED IN PART.

Legal Standard

“A trial court may order a party, the party’s attorney, or both, to pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney’s fees, incurred by another party as a result of actions or tactics, made in bad faith, that are frivolous or solely intended to cause unnecessary delay.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 128.5, subd. (a).)  “Actions or tactics” include, but are not limited to, “the making or opposing of motions or the filing and service of a complaint, cross-complaint, answer, or other responsive pleading.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 128.5, subd. (b)(1).)  “Frivolous” means “totally and completely without merit or for the sole purpose of harassing an opposing party.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 128.5, subd. (b)(2).)  The trial court may also “impose reasonable money sanctions, not to exceed fifteen hundred dollars . . . payable to the court, for any violation of a lawful court order by a person, done without good cause or substantial justification.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 177.5.)  These sanctions may only be imposed after giving notice and the opportunity to be heard.  (See Code Civ. Proc., §§ 128.5, subd. (c); 177.5)

Discussion

As a preliminary matter, the court raised the issue at a previous hearing on this motion whether it retains jurisdiction to rule on the sanctions motion after Plaintiffs dismissed the underlying action.  (See 9/2/2026 Min. Order, p. 1.)  Although a voluntary dismissal terminates the trial court’s jurisdiction over the dismissed causes of action and parties, California courts recognize an exception for collateral statutory matters, including attorney’s fees, costs, and sanctions.  (See Pittman v. Beck Park Apartments Ltd. (2018) 20 Cal.App.5th 1009, 1022–1023, ruling that the court retains the jurisdiction to determine and enforce “collateral statutory rights” post-dismissal or post-judgment, including motions for attorney’s fees and sanctions.)  Thus, the court finds that it has the jurisdiction to impose sanctions post-dismissal.

As stated above, the court ordered VW to pay Plaintiffs for attorney’s fees and costs, but VW failed to comply with the order.  (See Mot., pp. 4, 6.)  Further, VW failed to oppose the motion which gave notice that sanction would be sought pursuant to Code Civ. Proc., § 177.5.  Additionally, Plaintiffs provide an email from VW’s counsel, admitting that the failure to comply was due to his “inadvertence.”  (Castruita Decl., Exh. C, p. 3.)  Counsel’s inadvertence, however, does not constitute “good cause or substantial justification.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 177.5)  Nevertheless, the court finds that counsel’s inadvertence does not rise to the level of “bad faith” to award Plaintiffs’ reasonable expenses.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 128.5, subd. (a).)

Therefore, the motion is GRANTED IN PART as to sanctions under CCP 177.5 and DENIED IN PART as to reasonable expenses.  VW is ORDERED to PAY the court $250.00 in sanctions.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the motion for sanctions to enforce compliance with court’s order is GRANTED IN PART as to the request to enforce the court’s order and impose contempt sanctions, DENIED IN PART as to the request for reasonable expenses, and HELD IN ABEYANCE IN PART as to the requests for prospective monetary sanctions and for an order to show cause re: contempt.

Defendant Volkswagen Group of America, Inc. is ORDERED to PAY $85,505.62 to plaintiffs Chenxu Wang and Wei Lu FORTHWITH, based on the court’s March 19, 2026 order.

Defendant Volkswagen Group of America, Inc. is further ORDERED to PAY $250.00 in sanctions to the court pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 177.5 within thirty (30) days.

Case Number: 26PSCV02059    Hearing Date: September 24, 2026    Dept: G

Defendant Hyundai Motor America’s Motion to Compel Binding Arbitration

Respondent: NO OPPOSITION

TENTATIVE RULING

Defendant Hyundai Motor America’s Motion to Compel Binding Arbitration is DENIED.

BACKGROUND

This is a Song-Beverly action arising out of the purchase of an allegedly defective motor vehicle.  On October 22, 2022, plaintiff Alan Del Rosario (Del Rosario) allegedly bought an SUV manufactured by defendant Hyundai Motor America (HMA).  Del Rosario allegedly brought the SUV to HMA’s authorized service and repair facility, but they allegedly failed to repair the vehicle.

On June 11, 2026, Del Rosario filed the Complaint, alleging causes of action for (1) breach of express warranty obligations, (2) breach of implied warranty obligations, (3) unfair business practices, and (4) negligent repair.

On July 20, 2026, HMA filed this motion to compel arbitration, which is unopposed.

The motion is set for hearing on September 24, 2026.

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

HMA requests that the court take judicial notice of the Complaint.  The court may take judicial notice of the records of any California court, including its own.  (See Evid. Code, § 452, subd. (d)(1).)  Thus, the court takes judicial notice of the Complaint.

ANALYSIS

HMA moves to compel arbitration pursuant to the arbitration provision of the warranty handbook.  For the following reasons, the motion is DENIED.

Legal Standard

“A written agreement to submit to arbitration an existing controversy or a controversy thereafter arising is valid, enforceable and irrevocable, save upon such grounds as exist for the revocation of any contract.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.)  The court must grant a petition to compel arbitration unless it finds no written agreement to arbitrate exists, the right to compel arbitration has been waived, grounds exist for revocation of the agreement, or litigation is pending that may render the arbitration unnecessary or create conflicting rulings on common issues.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2.)

In a motion or petition to compel arbitration under both California and federal law, “the moving party bears the burden of producing prima facie evidence of a written agreement to arbitrate the controversy.”  (Gamboa v. Northeast Community Clinic (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 158, 165, internal quotation marks omitted.)  Once the court finds an arbitration agreement exists and that the dispute is covered by the agreement, the party opposing arbitration bears the burden of establishing a defense to enforcement by preponderance of the evidence.  (See Rosenthal v. Great Western Fin. Securities Corp. (1996) 14 Cal.4th 394, 413.)  In interpreting an arbitration agreement, courts apply the same principles used to interpret contractual provisions with the fundamental goal of giving effect to the parties’ mutual intentions and applying contractual language if clear and explicit.  (See Valencia v. Smyth (2010) 185 Cal.App.4th 153, 177.)  Because public policy strongly favors arbitration, “any doubts regarding the arbitrability of a dispute are resolved in favor of arbitration.”  (Coast Plaza Doctors Hospital v. Blue Cross of California (2000) 83 Cal.App.4th 677, 686.)

Discussion

The moving party’s burden is twofold: first, provide prima facie evidence that an arbitration agreement exists, and second, that agreement applies to the dispute.  (See Rosenthal, supra, 14 Cal.4th at 413.)

HMA did not meet its burden of demonstrating that the parties agreed to arbitrate their dispute in the owner’s handbook.  HMA provides a “true and correct copy of [the owner’s handbook].”  (Ameripour Decl., ¶ 4; see Ameripour Decl., Exh. 2.)  The owner’s handbook states:

If you purchased or leased your Hyundai vehicle in the State of California, you and [HMA] each agree that any claim or disputes between us (including between you and any of our affiliated companies) related to or arising out of your vehicle purchase, advertising of the vehicle, use of your vehicle, the performance of the vehicle, any service relating to the vehicle, the vehicle warranty, representations in the warranty, or the duties contemplated under the warranty. . . shall be resolved by binding arbitration. . . .  (Ameripour Decl., Exh. 2, section 4, p. 12.)

The handbook was produced by HMA and evidences its assent to arbitration.  (See Ameripour Decl., Exh. 2.)  However, there is no evidence that Del Rosario agreed to the arbitration provision in the owner’s handbook.  Neither his name nor his signature appear in the handbook, and there is no evidence that Del Rosario agreed to the handbook’s contents.  (See Ameripour Decl., Exh. 2.)  Based on its lacking evidence, HMA fails to meet its burden to demonstrate that it made an agreement to arbitrate with Del Rosario.

Since HMA failed to provide sufficient evidence of an arbitration agreement with Del Rosario, the court need not examine whether this dispute is covered by the arbitration provision or whether there are any defenses to enforcement.

Accordingly, the motion to compel arbitration is DENIED.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the motion to compel binding arbitration is DENIED.
