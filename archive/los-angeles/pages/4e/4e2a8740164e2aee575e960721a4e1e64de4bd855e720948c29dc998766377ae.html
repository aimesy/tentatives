DEPARTMENT 511 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 25STCV24097    Hearing Date: September 28, 2026    Dept: 511

Tentative Ruling

 

Judge Upinder S. Kalra, Department 511

 

HEARING DATE:   September 28, 2026                                       

 

CASE NAME:           David Kay v. Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC, et al.

 

CASE NO.:                25STCV24097

 

MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT, OR ALTERNATIVELY, SUMMARY ADJUDICATION

 

MOVING PARTY:  Plaintiff David Kay

 

RESPONDING PARTY(S): Defendants Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC and Mercedes-Benz of Southbay

 

REQUESTED RELIEF:

 

1.      An order granting summary judgment in Plaintiff’s favor on the complaint; or

2.      An order granting summary adjudication in Plaintiff’s favor on the following issues:

a.       First cause of action because it is undisputed that a warrantable defect caused the new, warranted Vehicle to spontaneously ignite and destroy itself, the presentation requirement is excused as a matter of law, and MBZ refused to repurchase or replace the Vehicle;

b.      Second cause of action because a new motor vehicle that spontaneously combusts is not fit for the ordinary purpose of providing safe transportation, and the implied-warranty claim requires no repair presentation;

c.       Fifth cause of action because it is derivative of, and rises and falls with, Plaintiff’s state-law warranty claims, which are established as a matter of law;

d.      Sixth cause of action because it is undisputed that the Vehicle contained a manufacturing defect that caused it to catch fire while parked, unplugged, and not being driven – a failure no ordinary consumer would expect – an no external cause contributed to the fire; and

e.       That MBZ’s failure to comply with the Song-Beverly Act was “willful” entitling Plaintiff to a civil penalty.

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

1.      Motion for summary judgment, or alternatively summary adjudication, is DENIED.

 

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

 

On August 15, 2025, Plaintiff David Kay (Plaintiff) filed a complaint against Defendants Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC (Mercedes) and Mercedes-Benz of Southbay (Southbay) (sometimes collectively Defendants) with six causes of action for: (1) violation of lemon law and express warranty; (2) breach of implied warranty of merchantability; (3) negligent repair; (4) misrepresentation; (5) violation of Magnuson-Moss Warranty Federal Trade Comission Improvement Act; and (6) strict products liability. 

 

According to the complaint, Plaintiff leased a 2023 Mercedes Benz EQE350v (the Vehicle) from Defendants that developed defects during the warranty period. Specifically, Plaintiff alleges that the Vehicle had a defective battery that overheated, ignited, and destroyed property aside from the Vehicle. Plaintiff alleges that he had previously presented the Vehicle for maintenance/repairs and that Defendants did not disclose this defect. 

 

On October 30, 2025, Plaintiff filed a request for dismissal of the third cause of action for negligent repair and the fourth cause of action for misrepresentation. 

 

On November 4, 2025, Mercedes and Southbay (appearing as Carwell LLC dba Mercedes-Bena of Southbay) each filed an answer. 

 

On November 7, 2025, Defendants filed a stipulation and order to strike and dismiss the third and fourth causes of action as well as punitive damages claims from the complaint which the court GRANTED. 

 

On June 10, 2026, Plaintiff filed a motion for relief from jury waiver and posted jury fees which the court GRANTED.

 

On July 1, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion for summary judgment, or alternatively, summary adjudication. On September 8, 2026, Defendants filed an opposition. On September 10, 2026, Plaintiff filed a reply.

 

LEGAL STANDARD:

 

“[T]he party moving for summary judgment bears the burden of persuasion that there is no triable issue of material fact and that he is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850, fn. omitted (Aguilar).) “Once the [movant] has met that burden, the burden shifts to the [other party] to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to that cause of action . . . .” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2); see Aguilar, supra, at p. 850.) The party opposing summary judgment “may not rely upon the mere allegations or denials of its pleadings,” but rather “shall set forth the specific facts showing that a triable issue of material fact exists . . . .” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) A triable issue of material fact exists where “the evidence would allow a reasonable trier of fact to find the underlying fact in favor of the party opposing the motion in accordance with the applicable standard of proof.” (Aguilar, supra, at p. 850.) 

 

When a plaintiff seeks summary judgment, he/she must produce admissible evidence on each element of each cause of action on which judgment is sought.¿ (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c(p)(1).)¿ When a defendant seeks summary judgment, he/she has the “burden of showing that a cause of action has no merit if the party has shown that one or more elements of the cause of action, even if not separately pleaded, cannot be established, or that there is a complete defense to the cause of action.”¿ (Code of Civ. Proc. § 437c(p)(2).)¿¿¿ 

 

The opposing party on a motion for summary judgment is under no evidentiary burden to produce rebuttal evidence until the moving party meets his or her initial movant’s burden.¿ (Binder v. Aetna Life Insurance Company (1999) 75 Cal.App.4th 832, 840.)¿ Once the initial movant’s burden is met, then the burden shifts to the opposing party to show, with admissible evidence, that there is a triable issue requiring the weighing procedures of trial.¿ (Code Civ. Proc. § 437c(p).)¿ The opposing party may not simply rely on his/her allegations to show a triable issue but must present evidentiary facts that are substantial in nature and rise beyond mere speculation.¿ (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68¿Cal.App.4th 151, 162.)¿ Summary judgment must be granted “if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.”¿ (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7¿Cal.App.4th¿1110, 1119.)¿¿¿¿ 

 

The pleadings dictate the scope of a motion for summary judgment. “The materiality of a disputed fact is measured by the pleadings [citations], which ‘set the boundaries of the issues to be resolved at summary judgment.’ [Citations.]” (Hutton v. Fid. Nat'l Title Co. (2013) 213 Cal. App. 4th 486, 493.) The pleadings here are challenging to decipher. 

 

ANALYSIS:

 

Evidentiary Objections

 

The court OVERRULES Plaintiff’s evidentiary objections in full.

 

Summary Judgment/Adjudication

 

Plaintiff contends summary judgment or adjudication is proper because there are no disputed facts that a battery defect caused the fire and resulting damages.

 

Defendants argue summary judgment is inappropriate because there are triable issues concerning causation, there was no attempts to repair, and no evidence of a manufacturing defect.

 

Plaintiff replies that Defendants concede all the facts, they do not need expert testimony to show a defect, and Defendants’ engineer does not create a triable issue.

 

First Cause of Action: Breach of Express Warranty

 

“A plaintiff pursuing an action under the Song–Beverly Act has the burden to prove the following elements: (1) the product had a defect or nonconformity covered by the express warranty; (2) the product was presented to an authorized representative of the manufacturer for repair; and (3) the manufacturer or its representative did not repair the defect or nonconformity after a reasonable number of repair attempts.” (Robertson v. Fleetwood Travel Trailers of Cal., Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 785, 799.) 

 

Here, the only elements in dispute are the existence of a defect or nonconformity and whether the reasonable number of attempts to repair element is excused.[1]

 

i.        Defect

Because Song-Beverly is remedial in nature, the plaintiff “is not obligated to identify or prove the cause of the car’s defect.” (Donlen v. Ford Motor Co. (2013) 217 Cal.App.4th 138, 149.) “Rather, he is required only to prove the car did not conform to the express warranty.” (Ibid.)

 

Here, Plaintiff met their initial burden that a defect or nonconformity covered by the express warranty existed. The provided evidence that the Vehicle was parked, not plugged in, was not charging, sent an alert via text to Plaintiff that the battery was overheating, and shortly thereafter spontaneously overheated and ignited. (UMF Nos. 6, 7, 8, 9.) This is circumstantial evidence of a defect.

 

Defendants, in turn, met their burden. Defendants provide evidence that the cause of the fire is undetermined. (UMF No. 16 [Declaration of Eric Wierman ¶ 8.)

 

But there is more.

 

ii.                  Repair Attempts

Plaintiff argues this element is met because it is unreasonable to force them to demand repair when the defect destroyed the vehicle. Defendants argue the statute clearly requires at least one repair attempt.

 

Defendants are correct.

 

“The statute does not require the manufacturer to make restitution of replace a vehicle if it has had only one opportunity to repair that vehicle.” (Silvio v. Ford Motor Co. (2003) 109 Cal.App.4th 1205, 1208.) “A single attempt does not meet the statutory threshold, so that there is no need for a trier of fact to determine its reasonableness.” (Id. at p. 1209.) “If anything, the statute indicates that even where the defect is life-threatening, one opportunity to repair is not enough.” (Ibid.) Based on this logic, at least one attempt is required.[2]

 

Here, Plaintiff did not meet his initial burden that he presented the Vehicle for repairs a reasonable number of times. In fact, there are none because the first manifestation of the defect destroyed the Vehicle. (UMF Nos. 8, 9, 10, 11.)

 

Accordingly, the court DENIES Plaintiff’s motion for summary judgment or adjudication on the first cause of action.

 

Second Cause of Action: Breach of Implied Warranty of Merchantability

 

“Unless specific disclaimer methods are followed, an implied warranty of merchantability accompanies every retail sale of consumer goods in the state.” (Carver v. Volkswagen Group of America, Inc. (2024) 107 Cal.App.5th 864, 878 [quoting Civ. Code § 1792].) “This warranty includes a guarantee that the particular item is fit for the ordinary purposes for which such goods are used.” (Ibid. [citing Civ. Code § 1791.1(a)(2)].) “Breach of the implied warranty of merchantability means the product did not possess even the most basic degree of fitness for ordinary use.” (Ibid. [internal quotation marks omitted].) There is no requirement to provide the manufacturer an opportunity to repair in an implied warranty claim. (Ibid.)

 

The elements of a claim for breach of the implied warranty of merchantability are: lack of merchantability, causation, and damages. (Carver, supra, 107 Cal.App.5th at p. 888-889.) “An unmerchantable vehicle is typically unsafe or not suitable for ordinary use.” (Id. at p. 889.)

 

As discussed above and below, there is a triable issue of fact as to whether the Vehicle had a defect rendering it unmerchantable. (UMF Nos. 6-9, 16.)

 

Accordingly, the court DENIES Plaintiff’s motion for summary judgment or adjudication on the second cause of action.

 

Fifth Cause of Action: Magnusson-Moss Warranty Act

 

The parties agree that the same reasoning as the first and second causes of action applies to this claim (i.e., the fifth cause of action stands or falls with the prior two). Since summary judgment or adjudication is inappropriate for the first two claims, it is inappropriate here.

 

Accordingly, the court DENIES Plaintiff’s motion for summary judgment or adjudication on the fifth cause of action.

 

Sixth Cause of Action: Strict Products Liability

 

A product is defective if it: (a) contains a manufacturing defect when the product departs from its intended design even though all possible care was exercised in the preparation and marketing of the product; (b) is defective in design when the foreseeable risks of harm posed by the product could have been reduced or avoided by the adoption of a reasonable alternative design by the seller or other distributor, or a predecessor in the commercial chain of distribution, and the omission of the alternative design renders the product not reasonably safe; and (c) is defective because of inadequate instructions or warnings when the foreseeable risks of harm posed by the product could have been reduced or avoided by the provision of reasonable instructions or warnings by the seller or other distributor, or a predecessor in the commercial chain of distribution, and the omission of the instructions or warnings renders the product not reasonably safe.  (Brady v. Calsol, Inc. (2015) 241 Cal.App.4th 1212, 1218-1219.)

 

Here, Plaintiff’s claim is based on a manufacturing defect that differed from the manufacturer’s design. (Compl. ¶¶ 147, 152.) So, to establish the subject vehicle contained a manufacturing defect, Plaintiff needs to establish Vehicle had a defect when it left Defendant’s possession and that the defect was a substantial factor in causing Plaintiff’s harm. Plaintiff did not do so.

 

Critically absent is evidence of a defect in the battery that departs from the intended design. Instead, Plaintiff provided circumstantial evidence of a defect due to the temporal closeness between notification of overheating and the fire. (UMF Nos. 6-9.) The court is not convinced this is sufficient to satisfy his burden.

 

Accordingly, the court DENIES Plaintiff’s motion for summary judgment or adjudication on the sixth cause of action.

 

CONCLUSION:

 

            For the foregoing reasons, the Court decides the pending motion as follows:

 

1.      Motion for summary judgment, or alternatively summary adjudication, is DENIED.

Moving party is to give notice.

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Case Number: 25STCV36299    Hearing Date: September 28, 2026    Dept: 511

Tentative Ruling

 

Judge Upinder S. Kalra, Department 511

 

HEARING DATE:   September 28, 2026                                       

 

CASE NAME:           Allen Shay v. Coldwell Banker Residential Brokerage, et al.

 

CASE NO.:                25STCV36299

 

MOTION TO STRIKE, OR ALTERNATIVELY, MOTION FOR JUDGMENT ON THE PLEADINGS

 

MOVING PARTY:  Defendants Levene, Neale, Bender, Yoo & Golubchik LLP and Anthony A. Friedman

 

RESPONDING PARTY(S): Plaintiff Allen Shay

 

REQUESTED RELIEF:

 

1.      An order striking the first amended complaint and for judgment on the pleadings based on this court’s lack of jurisdiction.

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

1.      Motion to strike or for judgment on the pleadings due to lack of subject matter jurisdiction is GRANTED without leave to amend.

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

 

On December 12, 2025, Plaintiff, in pro per, Allen Shay (Plaintiff) filed a complaint against Defendants Coldwell Banker Residential Brokerage, Anthony A. Friedman, Thomas Bluemel, Alfred H. Siegel, Daniel Zakaria, and Marisa Zakaria (Defendants) with causes of action for: (1) fraud; (2) conversion; (3) intentional misrepresentation; (4) promissory fraud; (5) violation of Penal Code § 496; (6) violation of CCP § 338; (7) violation of FEHA; (8) quiet title; (9) fraud in the inducement; (10) abuse of process; (11) intention infliction of emotional distress; (12) negligence; and (13) declaratory relief.

 

This dispute concerns Plaintiff’s 2012 bankruptcy filing and valuations of Plaintiff’s home on Pine Bluff Drive. Plaintiff alleges that Defendants falsely inflated the value of his home in 2015 to make it appear that the property had substantial equity, even though earlier valuations during the bankruptcy showed a steep market decline and no equity. Based on those allegedly false declarations, the bankruptcy court reversed its prior abandonment of the home and forced a sale, leading to Plaintiff’s eviction and the distribution of sale proceeds to the Defendants through commissions and fees. Plaintiff claims he later discovered the fraud and suffered severe financial, emotional, and property losses as a result.

 

On January 6, 2026, Plaintiff filed the operative first amended complaint (FAC). The FAC is against Coldwell Banker Realty, Anthony A. Friedman, Law Firm of Levene, Neale, Bender, Yoo & Brill, Thomas Bluemel, Alfred H. Siegel, Daniel Zakaria, and Marisa Zakaria. It has the same thirteen causes of action.[1]

 

On June 29, 2026, Defendants Daniel and Marisa Zakaria (Zakaria) filed a demurrer to the FAC.

 

On July 8, 2026, Defendant Coldwell Banker Realty (Coldwell Banker) filed a demurrer to the FAC.

 

On August 10, 2026, Defendants Anthony A. Friedman and Levene, Neale, Bender, Yoo & Golubchik LLP (collectively Attorneys) filed the instant motion to strike, or alternatively, motion for judgment on the pleadings.

 

On September 16, 2026, Plaintiff filed an omnibus opposition to Attorneys’ motion to strike, Zakaria’s demurrer, and Coldwell Banker’s demurrer. The opposition was untimely as to all motions. On September 21, Attorneys timely filed a reply and the Zakarias timely filed a reply.

 

LEGAL STANDARD:

 

Motion to Strike¿¿ 

¿¿ 

The court may, upon a motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it¿deems¿proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (CCP § 436(a).) The court may also strike¿all¿or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Id., § 436(b).) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (Id.¿§¿437.)¿“When the¿defect which justifies striking a complaint is capable of cure, the court should allow leave to amend.” (Vaccaro v. Kaiman¿(1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 761, 768.)¿ 

 

Judgment on the Pleadings¿¿¿ 

¿¿¿ 

A defendant may move for judgment on the pleadings if (i) the court does not have¿jurisdiction¿of the subject of the cause of action alleged in the complaint, or (ii) the complaint does not¿state¿facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action alleged in the complaint. (Cal. Code Civ. Proc. (CCP) § 438(b)(2), and subd. (c).) “The standard of review for a motion for judgment on the pleadings is the same as that for a general demurrer: We treat the pleadings as admitting¿all of¿the material facts properly pleaded, but not any contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law¿contained¿therein. We may also consider matters subject to judicial notice.” (Dunn v. County of Santa Barbara¿(2006) 135 Cal. App. 4th 1281, 1298.)¿¿¿¿¿¿ 

¿¿¿¿¿ 

The grounds for a demurrer must appear on the face of the pleading or from judicially noticeable matters.¿ (CCP¿§ 430.30(a);¿Blank v. Kirwan¿(1985) 39 Cal. 3d 311, 318.) A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint¿states¿a cause of action. (Hahn v.¿Mirda¿(2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) The only issue involved in a demurrer hearing is whether the complaint¿states¿a cause of action. (Ibid.)¿¿¿¿¿¿ 

¿¿¿¿¿ 

A demurrer assumes the truth of all factual, material allegations properly pled in the challenged pleading. (Blank v. Kirwan, supra,¿39 Cal. 3d at p. 318.) No matter how unlikely or improbable, the plaintiff’s allegations must be accepted as true for the purpose of ruling on the demurrer. (Del E. Webb Corp. v. Structural Materials Co.¿(1981) 123 Cal.¿ App. 3d 593, 604.) But this does not include contentions; deductions; conclusions of fact or law alleged in the complaint; facts impossible in law; or allegations contrary to facts of which a court may take judicial notice.¿ (Blank,¿supra, 39 Cal. 3d at p. 318.)¿¿

 

Meet and Confer

 

Prior to filing a motion to strike, the parties are required to meet and confer. (CCP § 435.) This also applies to motions for judgment on the pleadings. (CCP § 439.)

 

Here, no meet and confer occurred despite counsel’s request to Plaintiff to do so via email. (Declaration of Allen L. Michel ¶¶ 2-5.) Still, failure to meet and confer is insufficient to grant or deny a motion to strike. (CCP § 435.5(a)(4).)

 

ANALYSIS:

 

Request for Judicial Notice

 

The court GRANTS Attorneys’ request for judicial notice of the FAC and various bankruptcy court filings. (Evid. Code § 452(d).) The court GRANTS Plaintiff’s request for judicial notice of a bankruptcy court filing. (Ibid.) Finally, the court GRANTS Attorneys’ supplemental request for judicial notice of the bankruptcy court docket and a court order denying the notice of abandonment. (Ibid.)

 

Motion to Strike/MJOP

 

Attorneys contend relief is proper because this matter, against them, is barred under the Barton doctrine since Plaintiff did not receive permission from the bankruptcy court to file this action against Attorneys.

 

Plaintiff argues that the property was outside of the bankruptcy court’s jurisdiction in September 2013 via an abandonment so the Barton doctrine does not apply.

 

Attorneys reply that Plaintiff’s arguments miss the mark, he selectively applies the bankruptcy record to support his position, and the Barton doctrine applies in full.

 

Bankrupty estate property may be abandoned “in one of three ways” including “a trustee may proactively abandon property that is burdensome or of inconsequential value after notice and a hearing. . . .” (Martin v. Gladstone (2023) 96 Cal.App.5th 681, 690 [emphasis added].)

 

The Barton doctrine provides, as summarized, “that a party must first obtain leave of the bankruptcy court before it initiates an action in another forum against a bankruptcy trustee or other officer appointed by the bankruptcy court for acts done in the officer’s official capacity.” (Martin, supra, 96 Cal.App.5th at p. 697.) Without such leave, the other forum lacks subject matter jurisdiction over the suit. (Ibid.) The Barton doctrine applies when: “(1) the plaintiff is attempting to initiate an action in another forum;” “(2) the action is against a bankruptcy trustee or other officer appointed by the bankruptcy court;” and “(3) the action is for acts done in the officer’s official capacity.”[2] (Ibid. [internal quotation marks omitted].)

 

After reviewing the FAC, the Barton doctrine applies. First, the plaintiff is attempting to initiate an action in another forum – here. Second, the action is against a bankruptcy trustee (Siegel) and his Attorneys (Attorneys/Moving Parties). Third, the action is for actions done in the trustee’s official capacity, and by extension Attorneys’ actions as trustee’s representatives. (See, e.g., FAC ¶¶ 13 [Siegl’s valuation in December 2012], 14 [Friedman prepared a settlement agreement], 16 [Friedman retaining Bluemel], 18 [Friedman received attorneys’ fees].) Therefore, the Barton doctrine applies.

 

Next, Plaintiff’s abandonment argument fails because the property was not abandoned in 2013. While there was a notice to abandon the property, the bankruptcy court denied that request. (Attorneys’ Supplemental RJN, Exhibit 9.) So, as identified by Martin, the property was not abandoned because the court ruled otherwise at a hearing.

 

Additionally, there are no allegations that Plaintiff obtained leave from the bankruptcy court to file this action against Attorneys. Therefore, the court lacks subject matter jurisdiction of this matter.

 

Finally, leave to amend is not warranted because Plaintiff has not articulated facts that this defect is reasonable capable of curing.

 

Accordingly, the court GRANTS Attorneys’ motion to strike and/or motion for judgment on the pleadings without leave to amend.

 

CONCLUSION:

 

            For the foregoing reasons, the Court decides the pending motion as follows:

1.      Motion to strike or for judgment on the pleadings due to lack of subject matter jurisdiction is GRANTED without leave to amend.

Moving party is to give notice.

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Case Number: 26STCV19107    Hearing Date: September 28, 2026    Dept: 511

Tentative Ruling

 

Judge Upinder S. Kalra, Department 511

 

HEARING DATE:   September 28, 2026                                       

 

CASE NAME:           Juan Pablo Abaraca-Reyes v. Hyundai Motor America

 

CASE NO.:                26STCV19107

 

MOTION TO COMPEL ARBITRATION

 

MOVING PARTY:  Defendant Hyundai Motor America

 

RESPONDING PARTY(S): Plaintiff Juan Pablo Abaraca-Reyes

 

REQUESTED RELIEF:

 

1.      An order compelling Plaintiff to arbitration his claims and to stay the action pending arbitration.

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

1.      Motion to compel arbitration is DENIED.

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

 

On June 15, 2026, Plaintiff Juan Pablo Abaraca-Reyes (Plaintiff) filed a Song-Beverly complaint against Defendant Hyundai Motor America (Hyundai) with causes of action for: (1) violation of Civ. Code § 1793.2(d); (2) violation of Civ. Code § 1793.2(b); (3) violation of Civ. Code § 1793.2(a)(3); (4) breach of express written warranty; (5) breach of implied warranty of merchantability; and (6) violation of Tanner Consumer Protection Act.

 

According to the complaint, Plaintiff leased a 2026 Hyundai Ioniq 5 (the Vehicle) from Hyundai which developed defects during the warranty period that Hyundai’s representative could not repair.

 

On August 11, 2026, Hyundai filed the instant motion to compel arbitration. On September 14, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition. On September 21, 2026, Hyundai filed an untimely reply.

 

LEGAL STANDARD:

 

A written provision in any contract¿evidencing¿a transaction involving commerce to settle by arbitration a controversy thereafter arising out of such contract shall be valid, irrevocable, and¿¿ 

enforceable, save upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract.¿ (9 U.S.C. § 2.)¿ The Federal Arbitration Act (FAA) requires courts to direct parties to¿proceed¿to arbitration on issues covered by an arbitration agreement upon a finding that the making of the arbitration agreement is not in¿issue.¿ (9 U.S.C. § 4;¿Chiron Corp. v. Ortho Diagnostic Sys.¿(9th Cir. 2000) 207 F.3d 1126, 1130.)¿ “The court’s role under the [FAA] is therefore limited to determining (1) whether a valid agreement to arbitrate exists and, if it does, (2) whether the agreement encompasses the dispute at issue.”¿ (Chiron Corp., supra,¿207 F.3d¿at p.¿1130.)¿ The FAA reflects “both a ‘liberal federal policy favoring arbitration,’ [citation], and the ‘fundamental principle that arbitration is a matter of contract,’ [citation].”¿ (AT&T Mobility LLC v. Concepcion¿(2011) 563 U.S. 333, 339.)¿¿¿¿ 

¿¿ 

“‘ “The party¿seeking¿to compel arbitration bears the burden of proving the existence of an arbitration agreement, while the party opposing the petition bears the burden of¿establishing¿a defense to the agreement’s enforcement.” ’”¿ (Beco v. Fast Auto Loans¿(2022) 86 Cal.App.5th 292, 302.)¿ The burden of production as to¿this finding shifts¿in a three-step process.¿ (Gamboa v. Northeast Community Clinic¿(2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 158, 165.)¿ First, the moving party bears the burden of producing prima facie evidence of a written agreement to arbitrate, which can be met by attaching a copy of the arbitration agreement purporting to bear the opponent’s signature. (Ibid.)¿ If the moving party meets this burden, the opposing party bears, in the second step, the burden of producing evidence to challenge its authenticity.¿ (Ibid.)¿ If the opposing party produces¿evidence sufficient¿to meet this burden, the¿final step¿requires the moving party to¿establish, with admissible evidence, a valid arbitration agreement between the parties.¿ (Ibid.)¿¿

 

ANALYSIS:

 

Request for Judicial Notice

 

The court GRANTS Hyundai’s request for judicial notice of Plaintiff’s complaint. (Evid. Code § 452(d).) The court DENIES Plaintiff’s request for judicial notice of excerpts of the legislative history of certain Federal regulations and reports as immaterial to this ruling.

 

Compel Arbitration

 

  1. Existence of Arbitration Agreement 

 

The burden of production as to¿this finding shifts¿in a three-step process. (Gamboa v. Northeast Community Clinic¿(2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 158, 165.) First, the moving party bears the burden of producing prima facie evidence of a written agreement to arbitrate, which can be met by attaching a copy of the arbitration agreement purporting to bear the opponent’s signature or by setting forth the agreement’s provisions. (Id.) If the moving party meets this burden, the opposing party bears, in the second step, the burden of producing evidence to challenge its authenticity. (Ibid.) If the opposing party produces evidence sufficient to meet this burden, the third and¿final step¿requires the moving party to¿establish, with admissible evidence, a valid arbitration agreement between the parties. (Ibid.) 

 

Hyundai met their initial burden to establish an arbitration agreement exists. Notably, the produced a copy of the agreement and stated its terms verbatim in their motion (Declaration of Ali Ameripour (Ameripour Decl.) ¶ 4, Exhibit 2 [Owner’s Handbook & Warranty Information]). This is sufficient to meet the initial burden and to shift the burden to Plaintiff. (Kostandian v. American Honda Motor Co., Inc. (2026) 120 Cal.App.5th 872, 882.)

 

Next, the court addresses the equitable estoppel argument.

“[I]f a plaintiff relies on the terms of an agreement to assert his or her claims against a nonsignatory defendant, the plaintiff may be equitably estopped from repudiating the arbitration clause of that very agreement.” (Goldman v. KPMG, LLP (2009) 173 Cal.App.4th 209, 220.)

 

After reviewing the motion, the supporting declarations, and the complaint, Plaintiff is estopped from refuting the arbitration agreement contained in the warranty. First, his claims against Hyundai are based on breach of the express warranty.[1] (See, e.g., Compl. ¶¶ 11, 12, 13, 27-29.) Indeed, he confirms his claim is based on the warranty. (Declaration of Juan Pablo Abarca-Reyes ¶ 1 [“regarding my warranty claims” against Hyundai . . . .]) Second, as in Kostandian, Plaintiff has no evidence supporting an assertion of lack of mutual assent.[2] In short, Plaintiff cannot avoid the arbitration provisions in the warranty while simultaneously seeking to hold Hyundai liable for failing to conform to that warranty.

 

Therefore, an arbitration agreement exists.

 

      ii.            Applicability to Current Dispute 

Plaintiff argues the Agreement does not cover this matter because it is carved out via the Magnuson Moss Warranty Act. However, Plaintiff does not bring such a claim. His breach of express warranty claim is rooted in Song-Beverly, Civ. Code § § 1791.2. (Compl. ¶¶ 26-29.)

 

Other than that, the Agreement covers the subject dispute. It specifically covers any and all claims or disputes:

related to or arising out of your vehicle purchase, advertising for the vehicle, use

of your vehicle, the performance of the vehicle, any service relating to the vehicle, the vehicle warranty, representations in the warranty, or the duties contemplated under the warranty, including without limitation claims related to false or misleading advertising, unfair competition, breach of contract or warranty, the failure to conform a vehicle to warranty, failure to repurchase or replace your vehicle, or claims for a refund or partial refund of your vehicle's purchase price (excluding personal injury claims)

(Ameripour Decl., Exhibit 2, Warranty Section 4.)

 

As discussed above, Plaintiff’s complaint is based on Hyundai’s failure to conform the Vehicle to warranty, breach of the warranty, and failure to repurchase or replace the Vehicle.

 

Therefore, the dispute is covered by the arbitration agreement.

 

    iii.            Defenses to Enforceability

In¿Armendariz,¿the California Supreme Court¿stated¿that when¿determining¿whether an arbitration agreement was unconscionable, there is both a procedural and a substantive element. (Armendariz v. Foundation Health¿Psychcare¿Service, Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 82, 114 (Armendariz)).¿ 

 

                                i.            Procedural Unconscionability

Plaintiff argues the Agreement is procedurally unconscionable because it is a post-sale adhesion contract that he had no opportunity to negotiate.

 

Hyundai contends there is no procedural unconscionability because there is an opt-out provision and they have no obligation to direct Plaintiff to the agreement.

 

Courts¿determine¿whether an agreement is procedurally unconscionable by looking at surprise and oppression. Oppression is an “inequality of bargaining power, when one party has no real power to negotiate or a meaningful choice. Surprise occurs when the allegedly unconscionable provision is¿hidden.”¿(Carmona v. Lincoln Millennium Car Wash, Inc.¿(2014) 226 Cal.App.4th¿74, 84 (Carmona).) Examples of contracts that are procedurally unconscionable are contracts of adhesion, which is a “standardized contract, which, imposed and drafted by the party of superior bargaining strength, relegates to the subscribing party only the opportunity to adhere to the¿contract or reject it.” (Armendariz, supra, at p. 113). “The circumstances relevant to establishing oppression include, but are not limited to (1) the amount of time the party is given to consider the proposed contract; (2) the amount and type of pressure exerted on the party to sign the proposed contract; (3) the length of the proposed contract and the length and complexity of the challenged provision; (4) the education and experience of the party; and (5) whether the party’s review of the proposed contract was aided by an attorney. (OTO, L.L.C. v. Kho¿(2019) 8 Cal.5th 111, 126-27 (OTO).)

 

Here, there is minimal procedural unconscionability. First, the Warranty is adhesive since Plaintiff had no meaningful ability to negotiate it after he purchased his vehicle. (See Abarca-Reyes Decl. ¶¶ 5, 6, 7.) Second, the inclusion of an opt-out provision “does not insulate an arbitration agreement from a finding of procedural unconscionability.” (Swain v. LaserAway Medical Group, Inc. (2020) 57 Cal.App.5th 59, 69.) Plaintiff provided evidence that the transaction occurred “in a high-pressure, in-dealership environment” which weighs towards oppression. (Abarca-Reyes Decl. ¶ 7.) But this is not evidence showing lack of time to consider the warranty. (Swain, supra, 57 Cal.App.5th at p. 69.) Still, there is evidence of surprise since it is undisputed the Warranty was presented after the transaction concluded and no one advised Plaintiff there was an opt-out provision.

 

As such, there is minimal procedural unconscionability.

 

                              ii.            Substantive Unconscionability

Plaintiff argues the Agreement is substantively unconscionable because the applicability to claims is one-sided (lacks mutuality) since Plaintiff is the only party realistically to file warranty claims against Hyundai. He also argues it includes an unconscionable delegation clause.

 

Hyundai contends no authority holds that an agreement to arbitrate a Song-Beverly violation is unconscionable and the delegation clause argument is inapplicable to this dispute.

 

“Substantive unconscionability pertains to the fairness of an agreement's actual terms and to assessments of whether they are overly harsh or one-sided.” (Carmona, supra, at p. 85). There are five minimum substantive requirements to an enforceable arbitration agreement: (1) neutral arbitrators, (2) more than minimal discovery, (3) written award sufficient for judicial review, (4) all types of relief otherwise available in court, and (5) no unreasonable costs or fees as a condition of access. (Armendariz, supra, at p.102.) When there is little procedural unconscionability, a party opposing arbitration must show substantial substantive unconscionability. (Id. at 114.)

 

Here, there is substantial substantive unconscionability. Notably, the Agreement lacks mutuality. On its face, the Agreement states it applies to both Plaintiff and Hyundai. (Ameripour Decl., Exhibit 2.) However, on closer inspection, the claims subject to arbitration are really only claims Plaintiff (as a consumer) would bring against Hyundai. (Ibid.) This is heavily unconscionable. (Baker v. Osborne Development Corp. (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 884, 896-897.)

 

Therefore, the Agreement is substantially substantively unconscionable.

 

Accordingly, the court DENIES Hyundai’s motion to compel arbitration.

 

CONCLUSION:

 

            For the foregoing reasons, the Court decides the pending motion as follows:

 

1.      Motion to compel arbitration is DENIED.

Moving party is to give notice.
