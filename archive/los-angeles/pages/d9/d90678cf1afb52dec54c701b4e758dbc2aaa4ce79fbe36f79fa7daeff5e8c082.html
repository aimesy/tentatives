DEPARTMENT 224 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 20STCV31424    Hearing Date: July 7, 2026    Dept: 224

Maria Moreno and Benito Tranquilino v. Kia Motors America Inc. et al.,

TENTATIVE RULING

The motion to strike or tax costs is GRANTED in part and DENIED in part. The Memorandum of Costs is taxed in the amount of $3,975.00.

Introduction

On August 18, 2020, Plaintiffs Maria Moreno and Benito Tranquilino filed the Complaint in this action against Defendant Kia Motors America, Inc.

On June 24, 2025, following trial, the Court granted KA's motion for judgment pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 631.8.

On November 19, 2025, the Court entered judgment in favor of KA and against Plaintiffs, with costs to be determined upon the filing of a memorandum of costs.

On November 20, 2025, KA filed its Memorandum of Costs, seeking a total of $16,909.24.

On December 8, 2025, Plaintiffs filed the instant Motion to Strike and/or Tax Defendant's Costs.

On June 23, 2026, KA filed its Opposition.

No reply has been filed.

Motion to Strike or Tax Costs

A prevailing party in litigation may recover costs, including but not limited to filing fees. (Code of Civ. Proc. §1033.5, subd. (a)(1).) “Prevailing party” includes the party with a net monetary recovery, a defendant in whose favor a dismissal is entered, a defendant where neither plaintiff nor defendant obtains any relief, and a defendant as against those plaintiffs who do not recover any relief against that defendant. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1032, subd. (a)(4).)

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5, subdivision (c)(2), allowable costs are only recoverably if they are “reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation.” Even mandatory costs, when incurred unnecessarily, are subject to section 1033.5, subdvision (c)(2). (Perko’s Enterprises, Inc. v. RRNS Enterprises (1992) 4 Cal.App.4th 238, 245.)

The memorandum of cost is a verified statement by the party, attorney, or agent that the costs are correct and were necessarily incurred in the case. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1700(a)(1).) If the items on a verified cost bill appear proper charges they are prima facie evidence that the costs, expenses, and services listed therein were necessarily occurred. (Oak Grove School Dist. v. City Title Ins. Co. (1963) 217 Cal.App.2d 678, 698 (Oak Grove).) “The trial court’s first determination is whether the statute expressly allows the particular item and whether it appears proper on its face; if so, the burden is on the objecting party to show the costs to be unnecessary or unreasonable.” (Foothill-DeAnza Community College Dist. v. Emerich (2007) 158 Cal.App.4th 11, 29 (Foothill-DeAnza).) The burden of showing that an item is not properly chargeable or is unreasonable is upon the party challenging the costs. (Wilson v. Nichols (1942) 55 Cal.App.2d 678, 682-683 (Wilson).)

Where the items are properly objected to, they are put at issue, and the burden of proof is on the party claiming them as costs. (Oak Grove, supra, 217 Cal.App.2d at p. 698.) In other words, the burden is initially on the objecting party to show that the costs are not proper on their face. Then, the burden shifts to the moving party to justify its costs and expenses. If the costs are not facially proper, that is, the costs are not expressly allowed under a statute, then the moving party must justify the requests for the expenses.

In Lemon Law cases, like here, Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d), “authorizes an award of expert witness fees in addition to costs.” (Jensen v. BMW of North America, Inc. (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 112, 119.) Thus, costs normally not allowed by Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5 can be allowed to the prevailing party under Civil Code section 1794. (Id. at p. 137.)

Pursuant to Civil Code section 1794, “If the buyer prevails in an action under this section, the buyer shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs¿and expenses¿... determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.” (Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).)

Motion to Strike or Tax Costs

Defendant moves to strike or tax a number of costs in Plaintiff’s Memorandum of Costs on the grounds that they are excessive, unreasonable and/or not recoverable under the statute.

Item No. 1: Filing and Motion Fees

Defendant moves to strike or tax $3,659.88 in filing and motion fees on the grounds that the amount includes “self-imposed convenience costs” and “administrative research costs” that cannot be recovered.

CCP Section 1033.5(a)(1) provides that “filing, motion, and jury fees” are allowable as costs under Section 1032. Further, evidence is generally required in order to support an objection, and the mere submission of conclusions or bare allegations typically does not justify taxing costs. (See County of Kern v. Ginn (1983) 146 Cal.App.3d 1107) (bare allegation that depositions were neither necessary nor reasonable insufficient to overcome right to costs).

Defendant has not met its burden to challenge these costs. Bare allegations that certain charges are administrative in nature are insufficient to overcome the prima facie showing established by KA's verified Memorandum of Costs. The Court finds the costs to be reasonably incurred.

Accordingly, the Court declines to tax costs on this basis.

Item No. 4: Deposition Costs

Defendant moves to strike $4,931.35 for deposition costs on the grounds that the amount includes various unrelated and nonrecoverable “preparation charges.”

Deposition costs are expressly allowable under section 1033.5, subdivision (a)(3), which permits recovery of costs for taking, video recording, and transcribing necessary depositions, as well as travel expenses to attend depositions, including hotel, car rental, gas, and parking. (Chaaban v. Wet Seal, Inc. (2012) 203 Cal.App.4th 49, 55.)

Plaintiffs have identified specific entries within this category that are not deposition costs at all, but rather expert preparation time and attorney work-product expenses labeled as deposition costs. These include charges described as "Complete Defense Expert Opinions Report $675," "Expert depo prep $200," and "Review expert deposition transcript, complete ERRATA sheet $250." These are the professional time charges of an expert for consulting, report-drafting, and transcript review. The professional fees of expert witnesses retained by a party, as distinct from the logistics of taking a deposition, are expressly not recoverable under section 1033.5, subdivision (b)(1), regardless of whether they are incurred in connection with a deposition. (Gorman v. Tassajara Development Corp. (2009) 178 Cal.App.4th 44, 74; McGarity v. Dept. of Transportation (1992) 8 Cal.App.4th 677.) The Court will not allow recovery of expert preparation time repackaged as deposition costs.

The Court taxes $1,125.00 from this item, representing the identified expert preparation charges ($675 + $200 + $250.)

Item No. 5: Service of Process

Plaintiffs challenge certain surcharges within this item as luxury preferences not reasonably necessary to the litigation.

Costs of service of process by a registered process server or other means are allowable as a matter of right under section 1033.5, subdivision (a)(4).

Defendant does not provide any evidence to show that service of a John Doe is an unreasonably incurred cost. Nor has Defendant provided a basis for the contention that special handling or priority service are unreasonably incurred costs.

The Court will not tax costs on this basis.

Item No. 8(b): Witness Fees

The recoverability of the costs claimed under this item requires careful analysis under both section 1033.5 and Code of Civil Procedure section 998.

As a threshold matter, the professional fees of expert witnesses not ordered by the court are expressly not recoverable as costs under section 1033.5, subdivision (b)(1). (Gorman v. Tassajara Development Corp. (2009) 178 Cal.App.4th 44, 74.) However, Code of Civil Procedure section 998 provides a separate statutory basis for expert fee recovery where a prevailing party's offer to compromise was not beaten by the other side.

KA served its first section 998 Offer to Compromise on December 12, 2021. Plaintiffs obtained no monetary recovery. Plaintiffs failed to obtain a result more favorable than KA's offer, the Court has discretion under section 998, subdivision (c)(1), to award KA reasonable costs for expert witness services. (Chaaban, supra, 203 Cal.App.4th at p. 55.) This statutory authorization under section 998 supplies the basis for expert fee recovery that would otherwise be prohibited under section 1033.5, subdivision (b)(1), and the Court exercises its discretion to allow reasonable expert witness fees within this item.

However, the Court's discretion under section 998 extends only to the reasonable fees of the experts for their services, not to all expenses associated with the expert's retention. Meals are not recoverable as deposition-related travel or litigation costs. (Ladas v. California State Auto. Assn. (1993) 19 Cal.App.4th 761, 774.) The statute's only express provision for meals as recoverable costs applies to juror food during trial and deliberations. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (a)(2).) Section 998 does not independently authorize the recovery of meal expenses; it provides discretion to award costs for expert witness services. Restaurant charges, food delivery charges, and similar personal subsistence expenses are therefore not recoverable within this item regardless of the section 998 basis.

Similarly, per diem charges and remote-connection technology fees not tied to a specific deposition or court proceeding are not established on this record as reasonable expert witness fees within the meaning of section 998. KA's Opposition does not separately justify these components, and the Court declines to award them.

The Court taxes $1,231.50 from this item, representing meal charges, food delivery charges, and unsubstantiated per diem and technology fees.

Item No. 16: Other Expenses

Plaintiffs challenge the following specific charges: hotel charges at the Omni Los Angeles during trial, restaurant charges and Uber Eats food delivery orders, valet parking, expert mileage, internal travel costs for counsel or experts to attend trial, and a $925.00 interpreter fee for a deposition interpreter who was not court-ordered.

As to the hotel, meals, and food delivery charges, personal expenses of counsel during trial are not enumerated as allowable costs under section 1033.5, subdivision (a). Meals in particular are expressly not recoverable as litigation costs. (Ladas, supra, 19 Cal.App.4th at p. 774.) To the extent hotel costs are claimed as a discretionary cost under subdivision (c), KA has provided no evidentiary basis, such as a declaration establishing that counsel or experts traveled from out of town, demonstrating that lodging was reasonably necessary rather than merely convenient. Vague references to attending trial are insufficient.

As to the interpreter fee of $925.00: Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5, subdivision (a)(3) expressly authorizes recovery of fees for a certified or registered interpreter for a non-English-speaking deponent as part of deposition costs. However, this authorization is limited to interpreters retained for depositions of non-English-speaking deponents. KA has not established on this record that the interpreter at issue was retained in connection with a deposition rather than for some other purpose, that the deponent was non-English-speaking, or that the interpreter was certified or registered as required by the statute. No supporting documentation is identified in the Opposition. This charge is not recoverable on this record.

As to valet parking and expert mileage: parking and mileage incurred to attend depositions may be recoverable as travel expenses under subdivision (a)(3).

(Chaaban, supra, 203 Cal.App.4th at p. 55.) However, KA has not identified which specific deposition or proceeding these charges relate to or established that they were reasonable in amount and reasonably necessary. Undifferentiated parking and mileage charges unsupported by any documentation or explanation in the Opposition are not allowable on this record.

The Court taxes the full $1,618.50 claimed under this item.

Conclusion

The motion to strike or tax costs is GRANTED in part and DENIED in part. The Memorandum of Costs is taxed in the amount of $3,975.00.

Case Number: 25STCV04788    Hearing Date: July 7, 2026    Dept: 224

Bungay v. Blue Jay Transit CFM, LLC

TENTATIVE RULING

Defendant has not met its burden of proving, by a preponderance of the evidence, the existence of a valid arbitration agreement authenticated as the act of Plaintiff.

The motion is DENIED.

Introduction

On February 19, 2025, Plaintiff filed the Complaint in this action.

On July 3, 2025, Defendant Blue Jay Transit CFM, LLC filed the Motion to Compel Arbitration.

On June 23, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition.

On June 29, 2026, Defendant filed a reply.

Evidentiary Objections

Plaintiff submits objections to the Declaration of James Havel in support of Defendant’s Motion to Compel Arbitration.

Defendant submits objections to the Declaration of Elaine Bungay in support of Plaintiff’s Opposition to Defendant’s Motion to Compel Arbitration.

Plaintiff’s evidentiary objections 1-5 are OVERRULED.

Defendant’s evidentiary objections 1-7 are OVERRULED.

Motion to Compel Arbitration

Defendant moves the Court for an order: (i) compelling Plaintiff to submit the claims set forth in her Complaint to binding arbitration in lieu of court proceedings; and (ii) staying the instant lawsuit pending completion of such arbitration.

Legal Standard

In a motion to compel arbitration, the moving party must prove by a preponderance of evidence the existence of the arbitration agreement and that the dispute is covered by the agreement. The burden then shifts to the resisting party to prove by a preponderance of evidence a ground for denial (e.g., fraud, unconscionability, etc.). (Rosenthal v. Great Western Fin. Securities Corp. (1996) 14 Cal.4th 394, 413-414; Hotels Nevada v. L.A. Pacific Center, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 754, 758.)

Generally, on a petition to compel arbitration, the court must grant the petition unless it finds either (1) no written agreement to arbitrate exists; (2) the right to compel arbitration has been waived; (3) grounds exist for revocation of the agreement; or (4) litigation is pending that may render the arbitration unnecessary or create conflicting rulings on common issues. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2; Condee v. Longwood Management Corp. (2001) 88 Cal.App.4th 215, 218-219.)

“California has a strong public policy in favor of arbitration and any doubts regarding the arbitrability of a dispute are resolved in favor of arbitration.” (Coast

Plaza Doctors Hospital v. Blue Cross of California (2000) 83 Cal.App.4th 677, 686.) “This strong policy has resulted in the general rule that arbitration should be upheld unless it can be said with assurance that an arbitration clause is not susceptible to an interpretation covering the asserted dispute.” (Ibid. [internal quotations omitted].) This is in accord with the liberal federal policy favoring arbitration agreements under the Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”), which governs all agreements to arbitrate in contracts “involving interstate commerce.” (9 U.S.C. § 2, et seq.; Higgins v. Superior Court (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 1238, 1247.)

The Existence of an Agreement

A party to an arbitration agreement may seek a court order compelling the parties to arbitrate a dispute covered by the agreement. (CCP § 1281.2) The party seeking arbitration has the initial “burden of proving the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by a preponderance of the evidence, while a party opposing the petition bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence any fact necessary to its defense.” (Ruiz v. Moss Bros. Auto Group, Inc. (2014) 232 Cal.4th 836, 842.) A moving party meets its initial burden by showing the agreement at issue exists, and need only establish its validity on challenge by opponent. (HM DG, Inc. v. Amini (2013) 219 Cal.App.4th 1100, 1114; Espejo v. Southern Calif. Permanente Med. Group (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 1047, 1060.)

Defendant submits the Mutual Agreement to Arbitrate as Exhibit A to the Declaration of James E. Havel. (Havel Decl., Exh. A.) An electronic signature, by Plaintiff, is recorded in the document. Thus, Defendant has met its initial burden of showing the existence of a document.

Then, if the plaintiff challenges the authenticity of the signature, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to produce evidence sufficient to create a factual dispute regarding the signature's authenticity. A plaintiff's declaration stating that they do not recall signing the agreement can be sufficient to meet this burden, as it challenges the authenticity of the signature. In Ruiz v. Moss Bros. Auto Group, Inc., the plaintiff declared he did not recall signing the arbitration agreement and stated he would not have signed it had it been presented to him. The court held that this was enough to shift the burden back to the employer to prove the authenticity of the electronic signature (Ruiz v. Moss Bros. Auto Group, Inc., 232 Cal.App.4th 836, 846 (2014)).

“An electronic record or electronic signature is attributable to a person if it was the act of the person. The act of the person may be shown in any manner, including a showing of the efficacy of any security procedure applied to determine the person to which the electronic record or electronic signature was attributable.” (Civ. Code, § 1633.9, subd. (a).) “The effect of an electronic record or electronic signature attributed to a person under subdivision (a) is determined from the context and surrounding circumstances at the time of its creation, execution, or adoption, including the parties' agreement, if any, and otherwise as provided by law.” (Id., subd. (b).)

Under the California Uniform Electronic Transactions Act (UETA), an electronic signature has the same legal effect as a handwritten signature. (Civ. Code, §1633.7, subd. (a) [“[a]…signature may not be denied legal effect or enforceability solely because it is in electronic form”].) The UETA permits enforcement of electronic signatures when the evidence shows that a signing party “agreed to conduct the transaction by electronic means.” (Civ. Code, § 1633.5, subd. (b).)

Plaintiff argues that Defendant fails to meet its burden to authenticate Plaintiff's signature, and to present evidence that the signature was "the act" of Plaintiff. In support of her argument, Plaintiff submits her declaration that she does "not recall ever being presented with, reviewing, or signing an arbitration agreement" during her employment, and that she "rebut[s] that [her] signature is affixed to any purported arbitration agreement." (Bungay Decl., ¶ 6.) Thus, Plaintiff has met her burden of challenging the authenticity of the signature.

In the face of a Plaintiff’s disputing that he actually signed a digitally signed arbitration agreement, Ruiz provides that the defendant has “the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence that the electronic signature was authentic.” Namely, it is defendant’s burden to show that the electronic signature “was ‘the act’” of the plaintiff. (See Ruiz v. Moss Bros. Auto Group, Inc. (2014) 232 Cal.App.4th 836, 846.) The authorities that provide the goalposts for this this issue are Ruiz, supra, 232 Cal.App.4th 836 and Espejo v. Southern California Permanente Medical Group (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 1047. In Ruiz, the declaration seeking to authenticate the plaintiff’s electronic signature on an arbitration agreement was deemed insufficient where the declarant “summarily asserted” plaintiff electronically signed the agreement and “did not explain how she arrived at that conclusion or inferred [the plaintiff] was the person who electronically signed the agreement” or that the electronic signature “was ‘the act’” of the plaintiff. (Ruiz, supra, 232 Cal.App.4th at pp. 844-845.) Thus, the court found the arbitration agreement at issue was unenforceable. (Id. at p. 846.)

By contrast, in Espejo the “declaration offered the critical factual connection that the declarations in Ruiz lacked,” by detailing “security precautions regarding transmission and use of an applicant’s unique username and password, as well as the steps an applicant [or employee] would have to take to place his or her name on the signature line.” (Espejo, supra, 246 Cal.App.4th at p. 1062.)

Here, unlike in Espejo, Defendant's evidence more closely resembles the deficient showing in Ruiz. The sole declaration addressing the formation and execution of the Arbitration Agreement is that of James E. Havel, Defendant's Legal Director. Mr. Havel avers only that the personnel file maintained by Blue Jay in the ordinary course of business contains “a fully executed version of the Mutual Agreement to Arbitrate,” and that a “true and correct copy of the Arbitration Agreement signed by Elaine Bungay is attached hereto as Exhibit A.” (Havel Decl., ¶ 3.) This is precisely the sort of summary assertion that Ruiz found insufficient. Mr. Havel does not explain how he arrived at the conclusion that Plaintiff was the person who electronically signed the agreement, nor does he offer any evidence that the electronic signature was “the act” of Plaintiff.

The declaration is devoid of the “critical factual connection” required by Espejo. Mr. Havel does not describe any security procedures used to transmit the agreement to Plaintiff, to verify her identity, or to ensure that only Plaintiff could have affixed the electronic signature. He does not describe any unique username, password, or login credentials required to access or sign the document, nor does he detail the steps a signatory would have had to take to place her name on the signature line. Indeed, the certification page attached to Exhibit A reflects that the document was requested by a third party, Sara Dick, whose knowledge is not accounted for; Defendant submits no declaration from Ms. Dick, and Mr. Havel does not mention her. Mr. Havel likewise assumed his role in May 2024 - more than five years after the agreement was purportedly signed in November 2018 - and offers no basis for personal knowledge of the 2018 execution process. On this record, Defendant has not carried its burden of demonstrating, by a preponderance of the evidence, that the electronic signature was “the act” of Plaintiff.

To the extent Defendant relies on the Employment Change Letter (Exhibit B) as corroboration that Plaintiff entered into the Arbitration Agreement, that document suffers from the same authentication deficiency. This document is also supported only by Mr. Havel's declaration. Plaintiff disputes the signature, and the certification page appended to Exhibit B reflects a 2018 signing event rather than the 2024 execution the letter bears. It therefore cannot supply the showing the Ruiz/Espejo requires.

Because Plaintiff has challenged the authenticity of her signature and Defendant has failed to authenticate it, Defendant has not established the existence of a valid agreement to arbitrate. The motion is therefore subject to denial on this ground alone. (Ruiz, supra, 232 Cal.App.4th at p. 846.)

Having concluded that Defendant failed to establish the existence of an enforceable agreement to arbitrate, the Court need not reach Plaintiff's remaining arguments, including whether Defendant, as a nonsignatory, may enforce the Agreement, and whether the Agreement is procedurally or substantively unconscionable.

Conclusion

Defendant has not met its burden of proving, by a preponderance of the evidence, the existence of a valid arbitration agreement authenticated as the act of Plaintiff.

The motion is DENIED.

Case Number: 26STCV00896    Hearing Date: July 7, 2026    Dept: 224

Estrada et al., v. Rodriguez et al.,

TENTATIVE RULING

The Demurrer is sustained with 20 days leave to amend. A CMC is Set for September 20, 2026 at 8:30 a.m.

ANALYSIS

Plaintiffs filed an Amended Complaint on May 29, 2026. 

Defendants filed the instant Demurrer to the First Amended Complaint on June 12, 2026.

On June 22, 2026, Plaintiff filed an Opposition. 

On June 24, 2026, Defendant filed a Reply.

Request for Judicial Notice

Defendants' request for judicial notice is GRANTED.

Discussion

Plaintiffs Josefina Estrada and Angelina Estrada, as Co-Trustees of the Ramiro C. Iniguez Trust, seek possession of the subject property from Defendants Lucero Rodriguez and Fernando Rodriguez. Plaintiffs allege Defendants resided at the property with Ramiro C. Iniguez. It is undisputed that Mr. Iniguez is not a party to this action.

According to the First Amended Complaint ("FAC"), Plaintiffs served Defendants with a 30-Day Notice to Quit on June 13, 2024, and allege Defendants' right to possession terminated 30 days later. (FAC ¶¶ 2, 4.) Mr. Iniguez subsequently died on January 31, 2026. (Id.) The Court notes that the FAC is substantially identical to a complaint Plaintiffs filed in 2024, except that Plaintiffs now allege the property was exempt from the City of Los Angeles Just Cause for Eviction Ordinance ("JCEO"), Los Angeles Ordinance No. 187737, because Mr. Iniguez resided at the property with Defendants.

Defendants Demurrer on grounds that the FAC fails to allege that Defendants are being evicted on any ground authorized by the JCEO.

The JCEO applies once a tenant has continuously occupied residential property for six months or the initial lease term has expired, whichever occurs first. (LAMC § 165.04.) When the ordinance applies, "[a] landlord shall not terminate a tenancy unless it is based upon" one of the fourteen enumerated grounds set forth in LAMC section 165.03(A). Termination of a tenancy under Civil Code section 789 is not among those permissible grounds. Moreover, noncompliance with the JCEO may be asserted as an affirmative defense in an unlawful detainer action. (LAMC § 165.07.)

The FAC alleges the property is located within the City of Los Angeles and that Defendants have resided there since 2019—well beyond the six-month threshold. Accordingly, the JCEO applies unless Plaintiffs establish a statutory exemption.

The FAC's allegations establish that Plaintiffs are "landlords" and Defendants are "tenants" within the meaning of the ordinance. The JCEO defines a "landlord" as "[a]n owner, lessor, or sublessor... who is entitled to... maintain an action for possession of residential real property, or the agent, representative or successor of any of the foregoing," and defines a "tenant" as "[a] tenant, subtenant, lessee, sublessee or any other person entitled to use or occupancy of residential real property." (LAMC § 165.02.) These definitions encompass the parties alleged in the FAC. (See Roxbury Lane LP v. Harris (2023) 88 Cal.App.5th Supp. 9, 15 [holding that a tenancy at will is "presumably" covered by the materially identical definition of "tenant" contained in the Los Angeles Rent Stabilization Ordinance].)

Plaintiffs contend the property falls within the owner-occupancy exemption set forth in LAMC section 165.04(E), which exempts "[h]ousing accommodations in which the tenant shares bathroom or kitchen facilities with the owner who maintains their principal residence at the residential real property."

That argument is not supported by the FAC. Plaintiffs are the only owners identified in the pleading. They allege they are the Co-Trustees of the Ramiro C. Iniguez Trust, which owned the property, and they do not—and cannot—allege that they shared bathroom or kitchen facilities with Defendants or maintained their principal residence at the property.

Plaintiffs instead argue the exemption applies because Mr. Iniguez resided at the property when the 30-Day Notice to Quit was served. That contention is inconsistent with both the FAC and the notice itself. The Notice to Quit is signed by "Josefina Estrada and Angelina Estrada, as Co-Trustees of the Ramiro C. Iniguez Trust," not by Mr. Iniguez individually, or by them alleging he is the owner and they are his agents. (FAC, Ex. 1; FAC ¶ 4.) The FAC likewise alleges the Trust—not Mr. Iniguez—owned the property. Under Plaintiffs' own allegations, Mr. Iniguez was an occupant of the property, not the owner identified as the landlord for purposes of the eviction.

Because the FAC alleges the property is subject to the JCEO and does not plead that Defendants are being evicted for any of the fourteen permissible grounds enumerated in LAMC section 165.03(A), it fails to state a cause of action for unlawful detainer.

Leave to amend is granted only if Plaintiffs can truthfully allege facts establishing that Mr. Iniguez, rather than the Trust acting through its co-trustees, was the owner of the property for purposes of the owner-occupancy exemption in LAMC section 165.04(E). As presently pleaded, neither the FAC nor the attached Notice to Quit identifies Mr. Iniguez as the owner or landlord.

Any amendments have to include, and be consistent with, the recorded title and governing trust documents, if applicable.

Conclusion

The Demurrer is sustained with 20 days leave to amend. A CMC is Set for September 20, 2026 at 8:30 a.m.
