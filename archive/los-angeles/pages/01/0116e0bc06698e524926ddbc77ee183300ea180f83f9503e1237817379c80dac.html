DEPARTMENT 400 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 19STCV19306    Hearing Date: August 19, 2026    Dept: 400

MOTION TO DISMISS

 

The Court tenders the following tentative decision in the matter Guven Uzun, et al. v. Stewart Baker Jefferson Powell, et al., Los Angeles County Superior Court case number 19STCV19306, set for hearing on August 19, 2026. Stewart Baker Jefferson Powell and Yacoubian & Powell, LLP (collectively, Defendants) move to dismiss the present action filed by Guven Uzun, Infoneuro Group, Playa Advance Surgical Institute, LLC, and Playa Advance Urgent Care, Inc. (collectively, Plaintiffs) with prejudice as to all defendants. Defendants’ motion is granted except the case is dismissed without prejudice.


A.    Legal Standard


“An action shall be brought to trial within five years after the action is commenced against the defendant.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 583.310.) A party may move to dismiss an action pursuant to this provision, and such dismissal is mandatory. (Id., § 583.360.) Additionally, “[t]he court may in its discretion dismiss an action for delay in prosecution pursuant to this article on its own motion or on motion of the defendant if to do so appears to the court appropriate under the circumstances of the case.” (Id., § 583.410, subd. (a).) A dismissal for delay in prosecution for failure to bring an action to trial only lies where the action is not tried within two years of commencement. (Id., § 583.420, subd. (a)(2)(B); Rules of Court, rule 3.1340(a).)

 

B.     Discussion


Defendants move to dismiss the present action on the basis that it was not brought to trial within five years of commencement. Such a dismissal is mandatory under section 583.360. The 5-year rule is tolled while prosecution of the action is stayed. (Code Civ. Proc., § 583.340, subd. (b).) The stay must apply to the action as a whole and not include carve-outs for certain matters. (Gaines v. Fidelity National Title Insurance Co. (2016) 62 Cal.4th 1081, 1097 (Gaines).) The five-year deadline may also be extended by written stipulation or oral agreement in open court. (Code Civ. Proc., § 583.330.) But the emergency rule adopted by the Judicial Council of California in response to the COVID-19 pandemic merely extended the deadline and did not toll the period. (Ables v. A. Ghazale Brothers, Inc. (2022) 74 Cal.App.5th 823, 828, fn. 5 (Ables).) Specifically, “[n]otwithstanding any other law, including Code of Civil Procedure section 583.310, for all civil actions filed on or before April 6, 2020, the time in which to bring the action to trial is extended by six months for a total time of five years and six months.” (Id. at p. 825.)

 

The relevant timeline is as follows:

 

 

6/4/19   Complaint filed   (5 years expires 6/4/24)

 

4/6/20   Covid emergency rule extends 6 months   (5 years expires 12/4/24)

 

7/29/22   Court stays entire case

 

8/29/23   Stay lifted   (adds 13 months to prior deadline; 5 years now expires 1/4/26)

 

1/8/25   Trial continued to 11/3/25 per ex parte and stipulation (no change to 5 year deadline)

 

9/3/25   Trial continued to 4/20/26 based on P’s unopposed ex parte application;  (no change to 5 year deadline)

 

2/24/26   Stipulation to continue trial to 12/28/26   (stipulation to waive 5 years except no agreement if period already expired prior to 5/3/26)

 

Plaintiffs filed the present action on June 4, 2019. At that time, the five-year rule was set to expire on June 4, 2024. The special COVID-19 emergency rule then extended this period by 6 months to December 4, 2024, as this case was filed “on or before April 6, 2020.” (Ables, supra, 74 Cal.App.5th at p. 828.) On July 29, 2022, the Court stayed the present case “in its entirety.” This properly tolled the deadline pursuant to Gaines. The stay was lifted by Court order on August 28, 2023, effective the next day. Thus, the five-year rule was tolled for an additional 13 months, extending it to January 4, 2026. The parties agreed to another trial continuance in the interim which did not exceed the 5-year period due to the COVID emergency rule. Importantly, Plaintiffs dispute this interpretation, seeming to argue that the COVID extension does not apply until after all tolling periods are considered. Under this interpretation, this continuance would have extended the five-year rule period further because trial was continued until after the five-year limit if the COVID-19 extension was ignored. But the Court does not adopt this interpretation. On January 8, 2025 the Court continued the trial date to November 3, 2025 (a date prior to the then-existing 5 year deadline of January 4, 2026).  Then, on September 3, 2025, trial was again continued to April 20, 2026. Defendants’ counsel was present in Court for this continuance and did not file an opposition. But Defendants did not stipulate in writing to an extension of the five-year rule or orally agree to such in open court as reflected in the minute order from that proceeding. Thus, the five-year rule was not tolled pursuant to section 583.330. It expired in January 2026. Then, on February 24, 2026, the parties stipulated to a further trial continuance and agreed to extend the five-year deadline further, setting the current trial date of December 28, 2026. However, Defendants reserved their objection to Plaintiffs’ calculation of the five-year rule, stating that they did not waive or extend any five-year period that had already expired before May 3, 2026. Thus, Plaintiffs’ action has not been brought to trial within five years of commencement, even after taking all applicable tolling periods into consideration. This makes dismissal appropriate.

 

Plaintiffs argue for relief as permitted in Tresway Aero, Inc. v. Superior Court (1971) 5 Cal.3d 431. There, the California Supreme Court held that trial courts are vested with discretion in applying the five-year rule, “exercised in accordance with the spirit of the law and with a view of subserving, rather than defeating, the ends of substantial justice.” (Id. at p. 437.) Indeed, a defendant may be estopped from seeking dismissal under the five-year rule. (Id. at p. 438.) However, the Court does not exercise its discretion to deny Defendants’ motion to dismiss in this instance. The first stipulation to continue trial did not set a trial date past the five-year rule after considering adjustments from the COVID-19 emergency extension. And in the most recent stipulation, Defendants’ counsel agreed to an ostensible extension of the five-year rule based on Plaintiffs’ interpretation of the COVID-19 extension, but they reserved their right to challenge that interpretation as they have here. The Court does not find that any justification exists to deny the present motion. Accordingly, Defendants’ motion is granted.

 

Conclusion:

 

The Court grants Defendant’s motion to dismiss the case without prejudice.


Case Number: 23STCV25022    Hearing Date: August 19, 2026    Dept: 400

MOTION TO COMPEL

 

            The Court tenders the following tentative decision in the matter Chengbao Dong v. General Motors, LLC, Los Angeles County Superior Court case number 23STCV25022, set for hearing on August 20, 2026. Ramon Rincon (Plaintiff) moves for an order compelling General Motors, LLC (Defendant) to produce its person most knowledgeable (PMK) for deposition and produce the records identified in the deposition notice. Defendant opposes the motion. Plaintiff’s motion is denied but the Court requires Defendant to make the PMK available in the next ten days for a remote deposition.

 

A.    Legal Standard

 

An oral deposition must be scheduled for at least 10 days after the service of the deposition notice. (Id., § 2025.270, subd. (a).) “If, after service of a deposition notice, a party to the action . . . without having served a valid objection under Section 2025.410, fails to appear for examination, or to proceed with it, or to produce for inspection any document . . . the party giving the notice may move for an order compelling the deponent’s attendance and testimony, and the production for inspection of any document . . . described in the deposition notice.” (Id., 2025.450, subd. (a).) The motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration or declaration stating that the petitioner has contacted the deponent to inquire about their nonappearance. (Id., § 2025.450, subd. (b)(2).)

 

B.     Discussion

 

Plaintiff initially served a notice of deposition on Defendant’s PMK on April 11, 2024, setting an initial deposition date of May 7, 2024. (Helo Decl. ¶ 2.) Over the next two years, the parties conferred numerous times in an attempt to secure a deposition date. (Id. ¶¶ 3–13.) On June 16, 2026, Plaintiff served a second amended notice of deposition along with a request for production of documents. (Id. ¶¶ 14–15.) Deposition was set for June 25. (Ibid.) Two days before the deposition, Defendant objected to the deposition notice on the grounds that the deposition would be conducted in-person rather than remotely. (Id. ¶ 21.) Defendant’s PMK did not appear for deposition on June 25 or produce any documents. (Id. ¶¶ 24–25.)

 

Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s motion fails for three reasons. First, it argues that Plaintiff failed to meet and confer. A motion to compel deposition pursuant to section 2025.450 “shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040, or, when the deponent fails to attend the deposition and produce the documents, electronically stored information, or things described in the deposition notice, by a declaration stating that the petitioner has contacted the deponent to inquire about the nonappearance.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subd. (b)(2).) A meet and confer declaration “shall state facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt at an informal resolution of each issue presented by the motion.” (Id., § 2016.040.) Here, Plaintiff has demonstrated sufficient meet and confer efforts. Plaintiff has produced evidence that the parties have repeatedly communicated regarding the present deposition, including just days before the present motion was filed.

 

Second, Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s motion should be denied because they seek to compel irrelevant testimony and information. Plaintiff seeks testimony regarding repairs, diagnostics, and warranty claims concerning the subject vehicle, as well as information regarding internal records, further details of the vehicle’s Retail Sales Installment Contract, known defects and service bulletins, and the procedure by which Defendant sought to procure that information. Such categories of examination are not irrelevant given the facts of the present case. Nor does the requested information contain trade secrets.

 

Third, Defendant argues that the motion should be denied because Plaintiff did not file a separate statement. Rule of Court 3.1345 requires that a party submit a separate statement in support of a motion involving the content of a discovery request or responses thereto. The present motion seeks to compel Defendant’s deposition but also concerns the content of discovery responses. Indeed, Plaintiff deems Defendant’s objections to various document requests improper yet does not offer a separate statement in support of the present motion. (Helo Decl., Ex. E.) A separate statement is required because the present motion relates to the content of discovery responses. Accordingly, the present motion is denied.

 

C.     Conclusion

 

The Court denies Plaintiff’s motion. Nevertheless, the Court will require the PMK deposition to be taken within the next ten days.  It may proceed remotely.  Defendant should come to Court prepared to offer specific dates and times or the Court will set it.

 

           

 


Case Number: 23STCV31301    Hearing Date: August 19, 2026    Dept: 400

MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT OR SUMMARY ADJUDICATION

 

The Court tenders the following tentative decision in the matter Eugene Ward v. General Motors LLC, Los Angeles County Superior Court case number 23STCV31301, set for hearing on August 19, 2026. General Motors LLC (Defendant) moves for summary judgment against Eugene Ward (Plaintiff). Separately, Defendant moves for summary adjudication as to each cause of action individually. Plaintiff opposes the motion. The motion is denied.


A.    Factual Summary


The following is undisputed. Plaintiff purchased a 2020 Chevrolet Impala from Antelope Valley Chevrolet, Inc. (AVC) on June 25, 2023. (Plaintiff’s Separate Statement (PSS) ¶¶ 1–2; Roybal Decl., Ex. A.) At the time, the vehicle had been driven 61,070 miles. (PSS ¶ 2; Roybal Decl., Ex. A.) Plaintiff was not the original owner of the vehicle. (PSS ¶ 4; Jensen Decl. ¶ 6, Ex. B.) The original owner received the vehicle on August 29, 2019, when it had been driven only 9 miles. (Ibid.) Defendant issued limited warranties in connection with the original transaction. (PSS ¶ 5; Jensen Decl. ¶¶ 7–8, Exs. C, D.) The coverage of those warranties began on August 29, 2019. (PSS ¶ 6; Jensen Decl., Ex. C.)


B.     Legal Standard


The function of a motion for summary judgment or adjudication is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party can show evidentiary support for a claim or defense and if not to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) Code of Civil Procedure Section 437c, subdivision (c) “requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.)

 

As to each claim as framed by the complaint, a moving defendant “has met that party’s burden of showing that a cause of action has no merit if the party has shown that one or more elements of the cause of action, even if not separately pleaded, cannot be established, or that there is a complete defense to the cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) Courts “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.” (Dore v. Arnold Worldwide, Inc. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 384, 389 (Dore).) Once the defendant has met that burden, the burden shifts to the opposing plaintiff “to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence. (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

 

C.     Discussion

 

As a threshold matter, Plaintiff argues that Defendant’s motion for summary adjudication is procedurally deficient. Specifically, Plaintiff notes that Defendant’s separate statement of undisputed facts does not comply with the Rules of Court. If summary adjudication is sought as an alternative to the motion for summary judgment, “the specific cause of action, affirmative defense, claims for damages, or issues of duty must be stated specifically in the notice of motion and be repeated, verbatim, in the separate statement of undisputed material facts.” (Rules of Court, rule 3.1350(b).) The separate statement must separately identify each cause of action that is the subject of the motion and “[e]ach supporting material fact claimed to be without dispute with respect to the cause of action.” (Id., rule 3.1350(d)(1).)

 

Here, Defendant’s separate statement complies with rule 3.1350 with respect to Defendant’s motion for summary adjudication. It identifies each cause of action and the supporting material facts for each, by specifying “facts common to all issues.” Accordingly, Defendant’s motion for summary adjudication is procedurally proper.

 

To obtain summary judgment, Defendant must first meet its initial burden of demonstrating that all Plaintiff’s causes of action fail as a matter of law. Plaintiff brings his second cause of action under Commercial Code Section 2313. Under that provision, express warranties by the seller are created through “[a]ny affirmation of fact or promise made by the seller to the buyer,” “[a]ny description of the goods which is made part of the basis of the bargain,” and “[a]ny sample or model which is made part of the basis of the bargain.” (Com. Code, § 2313.) A buyer is one who “buys or contracts to buy goods” and the seller is “a person who sells or contracts to sell goods.” (Id., § 2103.)

 

Here, Defendant offers evidence that it did not sell Plaintiff the subject vehicle and is thus not a “seller” as defined in the Commercial Code. (Defendant’s Separate Statement (DSS) ¶ 2; Roybal Decl. ¶¶ 3–4.) But this ignores the fact that unexpired express warranty protections may be transferred to a subsequent purchaser if the warranty term has not fully run — it is only the plaintiff’s rights under the Song-Beverly Act which are extinguished. (See Dagher v. Ford Motor Co. (2015) 238 Cal.App.4th 905, 927.) Indeed, Defendant’s new vehicle limited warranty explicitly states that it extends to any subsequent owners. (Ex. C.) However, that warranty’s period had already expired by the time Plaintiff purchased the subject vehicle, as it only lasted for 60,000 miles. (Ibid.) Thus, Defendant has met its burden of demonstrating that the subject vehicle was not covered by an express warranty.

 

The burden thus shifts to Plaintiff to identify a triable issue of material fact. Plaintiff identifies additional express warranties related to emissions control systems. These include a 7-year or 70,000-mile emissions warranty and an 8-year or 80,000-mile emissions warranty for light vehicles. (Jensen Decl., Ex. C., p. 22.) These warranties do not contain the same assurance that they extend to subsequent owners, and involve further limitations related to the owner’s consistent service of the vehicle. Additionally, Plaintiff presents evidence of special coverage related to his vehicle’s evaporative emissions purge valve, which covers the part for 15 years or 150,000 miles, regardless of ownership. (Derderian Decl. ¶ 4, Ex. 2.) Plaintiff has presented evidence that Defendant offered certain warranties as a seller that extended to subsequent purchasers or buyers, such as Plaintiff. Thus, triable issues of material fact exist as to Defendant’s second cause of action, and summary adjudication is denied. Summary judgment is denied as well.

 

Plaintiff brings his third and fourth causes of action for breach of the implied warranties of merchantability and fitness for a particular purpose. A warranty that goods shall be merchantable is implied in a contract for their sale if the seller is a merchant. (Com. Code, § 2314.) And “[w]here the seller at the time of contracting has reason to know any particular purpose for which the goods are required and that the buyer is relying on the seller’s skill or judgment to select or furnish suitable goods, there is unless excluded or modified under the next section an implied warranty that the goods shall be fit for such purpose.” (Id., § 2315.) Where there are no direct dealings between a manufacturer and purchaser, the manufacturer’s implied warranties generally fall outside the scope of the UCC. (Ballesteros v. Ford Motor Co. (2025) 109 Cal.App.5th 1196, 1217.)

 

Here, it is undisputed that there were no direct dealings between Defendant and Plaintiff. Unlike Defendant’s express warranties, which contemplated subsequent purchasers as discussed ante, any implied warranties from the original sale are inapplicable because Plaintiff did not purchase the vehicle from Defendant. Thus, Defendant has met its burden of demonstrating that Plaintiff’s implied warranty claims fail as a matter of law.

 

Plaintiff argues that the implied warranties should still apply under U.S. Roofing, Inc. v. Credit Alliance Corp. (1991) 228 Cal.App.3d 1431. There, the Court recognized that while vertical privity is a prerequisite for recovery on a theory of breach of implied warranties, such privity may exist between parties other than the seller and direct buyer. (Id. at p. 1442–1443.) Specifically, where the direct purchaser of goods acted as an intermediary and leased the use of those goods directly to the plaintiff, the plaintiff could still bring an implied warranty claim against the manufacturer. (Ibid.) Such privity might exist here.

 

“If it appears from the affidavits submitted in opposition to a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication, or both, that facts essential to justify opposition may exist but cannot, for reasons stated, be presented, the court shall deny the motion, order a continuance to permit affidavits to be obtained or discovery to be had, or make any other order as may be just.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (h).) Plaintiff argues that AVC acts as an authorized dealer of Defendant and that certain agreements between those parties may establish vertical privity. And Plaintiff argues that Defendant has withheld these documents. Specifically, Defendant has failed to produce dealer agreements which are expected to contain “evidence regarding the relationship between Defendant and its authorized dealers, the dealers’ authority to act on Defendant’s behalf, and the contractual obligations relating to the sale, warranty administration, and servicing of Defendant's vehicles.” (Derderian Decl. ¶ 6.) Under section 437c, subdivision (h), the Court finds it appropriate to deny Defendant’s motion with respect to these causes of action to allow Plaintiff to conduct further discovery on the issue of whether privity exists between Plaintiff and Defendant. Accordingly, Defendant’s motion is denied without prejudice with respect to the third and fourth causes of action as well.

 

Finally, Plaintiff’s first cause of action is brought pursuant to the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act. (15 U.S.C. § 2301–2312.) The parties agree that this cause of action is derivative of Plaintiff’s other warranty claims. Thus, Defendant has not met its burden of demonstrating that it is entitled to judgment as a matter of law, because Plaintiff’s other warranty claims have survived summary adjudication. Accordingly, Defendant’s motion is denied with respect to Plaintiff’s first cause of action as well.

 

Conclusion:

Defendant’s motion for summary judgment and adjudication is denied.


Case Number: 24STCV02014    Hearing Date: August 19, 2026    Dept: 400

MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER

 

            The Court tenders the following tentative decision in the matter Bryhana Monegain v. Anschutz Entertainment Group, Inc., et al., Los Angeles County Superior Court case number 24STCV02014, set for hearing on August 19, 2026. G-Unit Touring, Inc. (Defendant) moves for an order compelling further response to its requests for admissions, set one (RFAs), propounded upon Bryhana Monegain (Plaintiff). Defendant also requests a total $1,219 in monetary sanctions. Plaintiff opposes the motion. Defendant’s motion is granted in part. The Court awards $920 in sanctions.


A.    Legal Standard

 

A party may move for an order compelling further responses to requests for admissions if it shows that an answer is evasive or incomplete or an objection is meritless. (Id., § 2033.290, subd. (a).) “For discovery purposes, information is relevant if it ‘might reasonably assist a party in evaluating the case, preparing for trial, or facilitating settlement.’ [Citation]. Admissibility is not the test and information, unless privileged, is discoverable if it might reasonably lead to admissible evidence. [Citation] These rules are applied liberally in favor of discovery.” (Gonzales v. Superior Court (1995) 33 Cal.App.4th 1539, 1546.) “If the court determines that the answer or production sought is subject to discovery, it shall order that the answer be given, or the production be made on the resumption of the deposition.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.480, subd. (i).)

 

B.     Discussion

 

Defendant seeks further responses to RFAs 18 and 19, which ask Plaintiff to admit or deny that she suffered from anxiety or depression prior to August 30, 2023. These RFAs were initially propounded on November 24, 2025. (Bowen Decl. ¶ 3, Ex. A.) After receiving multiple extensions, Plaintiff provided initial responses objecting to the terms anxiety and depression as ambiguous on March 18, 2026. (Id. ¶ 4, Ex. C.) Defendant then filed the present motion on May 26. Plaintiff served further responses on August 6, providing substantive denials along with explanation. (Hultin Decl. ¶ 2, Ex. 1.) Thus, further responses are no longer warranted.

 

However, sanctions are warranted pursuant to section 2033.290, subdivision (d), as Plaintiff’s initial responses were deficient. She has thus unsuccessfully opposed Defendant’s motion to compel further responses. Defendant’s counsel alleges an hourly rate of $230. (Bowen Decl. ¶ 6.) Counsel further testifies that it took him 3.8 hours to review discovery and draft the present motion and would take a further 1.5 hours to review the opposition, draft a reply, and attend the hearing on this matter. (Ibid.) Because of the simplicity of the present motion and its coverage of only two RFAs, the Court only credits counsel with 2.5 hours for drafting the motion and 1.5 hours for drafting the reply and attending the hearing on this matter. Accordingly, the Court awards Defendant sanctions in the amount of $920 ([$230 per hour] x [4 hours] = $920), payable by Plaintiff’s counsel.

 

C.     Conclusion


            The Court grants Defendant’s motion in part. Plaintiff is not required to provide further responses but her counsel is sanctioned for $920, payable to Defendant within 30 days..

Case Number: 24STCV33682    Hearing Date: August 19, 2026    Dept: 400

MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER

 

The Court tenders the following tentative decision in the matter Yesenia Zacarias, et al. v. FCA US, LLC, et al., Los Angeles County Superior Court case number 24STCV33682, set for hearing on August 19, 2026. FCA US LLC (Defendant) moves for an order compelling Yesenia Zacarias (Plaintiff) to provide further responses to its requests for production of documents, set one (RFPs). Defendant also requests sanctions. Plaintiff has not filed a brief in opposition. The motion is granted


A.    Procedural History

 

Plaintiff filed the present suit alleging claims against Defendant under the Song-Beverly Act on December 19, 2024. On April 7, 2026, Defendant propounded the relevant RFPs. (Wang Decl. ¶ 4, Ex. A.) Plaintiff provided allegedly noncompliant and evasive responses on May 11. (Id. ¶ 5, Ex. B.) Defendant filed the present motion to compel further responses on July 16 after Plaintiff failed to respond to Defendant’s meet and confer correspondence. (Id. ¶ 6.) Plaintiff has still failed to provide further responses. (Id. ¶ 8.)

 

B.     Legal Standard

 

A party may move for an order compelling further responses to an inspection demand if: (1) a statement of compliance is incomplete; (2) a representation of inability to comply is inadequate, incomplete, or evasive; or (3) an objection is meritless or overly general. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (a).) Such a motion must set forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought and be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under section 2016.040. (Id., § 2031.310, subd. (b).) Any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel further responses is subject to monetary sanctions, unless they acted with substantial justification. (Id., § 2031.310, subd. (h).)

 

C.     Discussion

 

Defendant has set forth good cause justifying the discovery sought, explaining that the relevant RFPs relate to Plaintiff’s claims and involve discoverable information. Specifically, Defendant seeks further responses to RFPs 49–64. These involve documents in support of Plaintiff’s causes of action, the attorney’s fees incurred in prosecuting them, documents related to incidental damages claimed, and documents supporting the essential elements of Plaintiff’s claims. Plaintiff offered the same boilerplate objections with respect to every request. In failing to oppose the present motion, Plaintiff has not substantiated any of these objections. Thus, the Court finds these objections improper and further responses are therefore warranted pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.310, subdivision (a)(3). Accordingly, Defendant’s motion is granted.

 

Defendant seeks sanctions pursuant to section 2031.310, subdivision (h). Sanctions are appropriate as Plaintiff has unsuccessfully opposed the present motion to compel further responses and not offered any substantial justification for her failure. Defendant’s counsel alleges hourly rates of $450, $375, and $200 which the Court credits. (Wang Decl. ¶ 9.) Counsel additionally alleges 3.4 hour spent preparing the present motion. (Ibid.) However, counsel fails to explain which billing rate is properly used for those hours. Adopting the $375 rate and acknowledging the simplicity of the present motion, the Court credits Defendant’s counsel with 2 hours spent drafting the motion, declaration, and separate statement. The Court additionally recognizes the $60 filing fee. (Ibid.) Accordingly, Defendant is awarded $810 in sanctions, payable by Plaintiff’s counsel ([$375 per hour] x [2 hours] + [$60 filing fee] = $810).

 

D.    Conclusion

 

Defendant’s motion is granted. Plaintiff’s counsel is ordered to pay $810 in sanctions to Defendant.


Case Number: 25STCV33912    Hearing Date: August 19, 2026    Dept: 400

MOTION TO COMPEL

 

            The Court tenders the following tentative decision in the matter Jesus Flores v. Eugene la Pietra, Los Angeles County Superior Court case number 25STCV33912, set for hearing on August 20, 2026. Jesus Flores (Plaintiff) moves for an order compelling further responses to his special interrogatories, set one (SPROGs) from Eugene la Pietra (Defendant). Defendant opposes the motion on the grounds that it is untimely.

 

A party may move for an order compelling further responses to interrogatories if the propounding party deems that responses are incomplete, evasive, or contain meritless objections. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (a).) A party waives its right to compel further responses to interrogatories unless “notice of this motion is given within 45 days of the service of the verified response, or any supplemental verified response, or on or before any specific later date to which the propounding party and the responding party have agreed in writing.” (Id., § 2030.300, subd. (c).)

 

Here, Plaintiff propounded his SPROGs on January 6, 2026. (Fox Decl. ¶ 3.) Defendant served verified responses on February 10. (Id. ¶ 4.) Under section 2030.300, Plaintiff had 45 days, or until March 27, to file the present motion. Plaintiff did not file the motion until June 24, 2026. Plaintiff does not provide any evidence of an agreement in writing between the parties that the motion to compel deadline would be extended. Thus, the motion is untimely under section 2030.300, subdivision (c).

 

Plaintiff requests that the Court disregard this delay for several reasons. First, Plaintiff argues that he was engaged in settlement discussions. This did not prevent the timely filing of the present motion. Second, Plaintiff points to an attempt at informal resolution. This too is irrelevant. Third, Plaintiff notes that Defendant raised the possibility of relating this case to another proceeding before later admitting that the cases were not related. Even if the case had been deemed related, the same deadline would have existed. Thus, none of these reasons excuse the heavily delayed filing. Accordingly, Plaintiff’s motion is denied.

 


Case Number: 26STCV05536    Hearing Date: August 19, 2026    Dept: 400

MOTION TO COMPEL ARBITRATION

 

The Court tenders the following tentative decision in the matter Adam Bullock v. Shye West, Inc., Los Angeles County Superior Court case number 26STCV05536, set for hearing on August 19, 2026. Shye West, Inc. (Defendant) moves for an order compelling Adam Bullock (Plaintiff) to submit to binding arbitration and staying these proceedings in the interim. Plaintiff opposes the motion. The motion is denied.

 

A.    Legal Standard

 

“On petition of a party to an arbitration agreement alleging the existence of a written agreement to arbitrate a controversy and that a party thereto refuses to arbitrate such controversy, the court shall order the petitioner and the respondent to arbitrate the controversy if it determines that an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists, unless it determines that: (a) The right to compel arbitration has been waived by the petitioner; or (b) Grounds exist for the revocation of the agreement.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 1281.2, subds. (a), (b).)

 

A proceeding to compel arbitration is in essence a suit in equity to compel specific performance of a contract. (Freeman v. State Farm Mutual Auto Insurance Co. (1975) 14 Cal.3d 473, 479.) Such enforcement may be sought by a party to the arbitration agreement. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1280, subd. (e)(1).) A motion to compel arbitration requires the facts to be proven by affidavit or declaration and documentary evidence with oral testimony taken only in the court’s discretion. (Code Civ. Proc., §1290.2; Rosenthal v. Great Western Fin. Securities Corp. (1996) 14 Cal.4th 394, 413–414.) The motion must set forth the provisions of the written agreement and the arbitration clause verbatim, or such provisions must be attached and incorporated by reference. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1330; see Condee v. Longwood Mgmt. Corp. (2001) 88 Cal.App.4th 215, 218.) 

 

            Once the moving party alleges that an arbitration agreement exists, the burden shifts to the responding party to prove the falsity of the purported agreement, and no evidence or authentication is required to find the arbitration agreement exists. (See Condee, supra, 88 Cal.App.4th at p. 219.) However, if the existence of the agreement is challenged, “[movant] bears the burden of proving [the arbitration agreement’s] existence by a preponderance of the evidence.” (Rosenthal, supra, p. 413; see also Espejo v. Southern California Permanente Medical Group (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 1047, 1058–1060.)


B.     Discussion

 

Defendant moves to compel Plaintiff to submit to binding arbitration pursuant to a signed employment agreement. Defendant argues that the agreement is governed by the Federal Arbitration Act (FAA) and that enforcement is thus mandatory. But Defendant must still demonstrate that the parties have entered into a valid agreement to arbitrate. (Davis v. Nordstrom, Inc. (9th Cir. 2014) 755 F.3d 1089, 1092.) To that end, Plaintiff argues that Defendant has failed to establish the existence of an agreement, that the purported agreement has expired by its own terms, that any agreement was illusory, and that any agreement was unconscionable with provisions that should not be severed. The Court finds that Defendant has failed to establish the material terms of the agreement and therefore denies the Motion.

 

 

Plaintiff argues that Defendant has not demonstrated that an arbitration agreement exists. For a trial court to compel arbitration under Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.2, the moving party first bears the burden of producing “prima facie evidence of a written agreement to arbitrate the controversy.” (Rosenthal, supra, 14 Cal.4th 394 at p. 413.) This burden can be met by attaching a copy of the agreement purporting to be signed by the opposing party. (Gamboa v. Northeast Community Clinic (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 158, 165 (Gamboa).) Alternatively, the moving party may set forth the agreement’s provisions in the motion. (Ibid.) If the opposing party disputes the agreement, it bears the burden of producing evidence to challenge its authenticity. (Ibid.) The opposing party may “testify under oath or declare under penalty of perjury that the party never saw or does not remember seeing the agreement, or that the party never signed or does not remember signing the agreement.” (Ibid.) In that case, the moving party must then prove the existence of the agreement by a preponderance of the evidence. (Id. at pp. 165–166.)

 

Here, Defendant offers evidence that Plaintiff entered a negotiated employment agreement (“Agreement”) with it that contained an arbitration clause. (Keep Decl. ¶ 4.) The employment agreement includes section 4.6, titled “Arbitration.” (Id., Ex. 1.) It states that “[a]ny controversy or dispute arising out of, based upon, or relating to this Agreement, its enforcement or interpretation, or because of an alleged breach, default, or misrepresentation in connection with any of its provisions, or arising out of, based upon, or relating in any way to Employee’s employment or association with Imagine This, or termination of the same, including, without limiting the generality of the foregoing, any questions regarding whether a particular dispute is arbitrable, and any alleged violation of statute, common law or public policy, including, but not limited to, any state or federal statutory claims, shall be submitted to final and binding arbitration.” (Ibid.) Defendant’s President testifies that Plaintiff signed a copy of the agreement on February 14, 2019, and the agreement presented by Defendant bears Plaintiff’s signature. (Id. ¶ 4, Ex. 1.)  

 

Plaintiff challenges the agreement’s authenticity by identifying that the agreement is missing a page, page 3. Indeed, the attached arbitration agreement skips from page 2 to 4. (Id., Ex. 1.) Defendant argues that it has submitted the entirety of the agreement and that the absence of Page 3 is solely the result of misnumbered pagination.  Defendant’s Reply Brief states that the “missing page” is therefore a “red herring” because it “can be seen from the fact that the final sentence on Page 2 of section 4.6 continues from Page 2 to Page 4.”  But this explanation is incorrect as the missing page 3 actually covers portions of Section 1.5.   Page 2 ends with section 1.5(a) while page 4 begins with section 1.5(e). Thus, it is apparent that sections 1.5(b) through 1.5(d) appeared on the missing Page 3, not section 4.6 as Defendant argues.  The Court cannot tell what provisions might be in the missing sections of the agreement and is therefore unable to assess whether any missing terms might be unenforceable or unconscionable.  This is further complicated by Defendant’s incorrect argument that the entirety of the agreement has been submitted. The Court finds that Defendant has failed to establish the material terms of the Agreement.

 

                  Plaintiff further argues that the agreement expired upon his termination and is thus not binding. The agreement states that “[n]otwithstanding any terms stated herein to the contrary, this Agreement shall commence and be effective on the Effective Date and shall exist for the duration of [Plaintiff’s] employment with [Defendant].” (Id., Ex. 1, p. 5.) Plaintiff’s employment with Defendant ended on January 8, 2026. (Bullock Decl. ¶ 3.) Thus, the employment agreement terminated on that date. Under the agreement, certain provisions were slated to “survive and continue after any termination of this Agreement.” (Keep Decl., Ex. 1, p. 8.) These were limited to “Section 1.5 (Nondisclosure; Confidentiality; Trade Secrets; & Works for Hire), and Section 3 (Release & Waiver).” (Ibid.)   Because Defendant has not attached the portion of the agreement covering sections 1.5 (b) through (d) it is not clear what provisions in that section are intended to survive.  Moreover, the other section which is said to survive termination is “Section 3 (Release and Waiver).”  But Section 3 of the agreement says nothing about “Release and Waiver” so it appears the internal reference is incorrect.  This is not a minor point because Defendant is contending that the agreement is clear on what is to survive termination and what is not.  Yet, the two items specified to survive are (1) missing terms from the missing page 3 and (2) “Release and Waiver” provisions which are stated in the agreement to be found in section 3 but which are not there (nor, apparently, are they anywhere else).  Furthermore, the “survival” provision follows section 4.6, the arbitration provision, yet it is not included as a provision which survives termination of the agreement. Defendant argues that a construction which does not include survival of the arbitration provision is logically inconsistent, because that provision contemplates arbitration of disputes related to the termination of Plaintiff’s employment. But it is conceivable that disputes surrounding Plaintiff’s potential termination could arise before his termination became effective; such a construction would not render the portion of the arbitration provision related to termination disputes surplusage.

 

            In sum, Defendants have not established the material terms of the Agreement, and their explanation of  why it is allegedly in fact complete is demonstrably incorrect.  Moreover, the portions of the Agreement which are included contain incomplete and incorrect internal references on the key issue of what obligations survive termination and which do not.  Given this degree of uncertainty, the Court finds Defendants have not satisfied their obligation to prove the material terms of an agreement to arbitrate.

 

C.     Conclusion


Defendant’s motion to compel arbitration and stay these proceedings is denied.

MOTIONS TO DEEM REQUEST FOR ADMISSIONS

 

The Court tenders the following tentative decision in the matter Dr. Iman Sadeghi v. Pinscreen Inc., et al., Los Angeles County Superior Court case number BC709376, set for hearing on August 19, 2026. On January 23, 2026, Dr. Iman Sadeghi (Plaintiff) propounded requests for admissions, set two (RFAs), on Dr. Hao Li and Pinscreen, Inc. (collectively, Defendants). (Zaffos Decl. ¶ 7.) After Plaintiff granted an extension, responses were due on March 6, 2026. (Id. ¶ 9.) Unverified responses were served on March 7. (Id. ¶ 10.) On July 2, 2026, Plaintiff moved to deem RFAs admitted as to Defendants. Defendants served further verified responses on August 6. (Serpik Decl. ¶ 6.)

 

“If a party to whom requests for admission are directed fails to serve a timely response . . . [that] party waives any objection to the requests.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (a).) “The requesting party may move for an order that the genuineness of any documents and the truth of any matters specified in the requests be deemed admitted.” (Id., § 2033.280, subd. (b).) The Court shall grant an order deeming RFAs admitted unless the responding party serves substantially compliant responses before the hearing on the motion. A finding of substantial compliance depends on “whether answers in a proposed response are ‘as complete and straightforward’ as reasonably possible, as explicitly required by section 2033.220.” (Katayama v. Continental Investment Group (2024) 105 Cal.App.5th 898, 907.) A party not in possession of information or knowledge necessary to respond to a request for admission must state in the answer that “a reasonable inquiry concerning the matter in the particular request has been made, and that the information known or readily obtainable is insufficient to enable that party to admit the matter.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.220, subd. (c).)

 

Here, Defendants have served substantially compliant responses to Plaintiff’s RFAs in advance of this hearing. (Serpik Decl. ¶¶ 6, 7.) Thus, the RFAs are not properly deemed admitted pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.280. However, sanctions are still warranted and mandatory under that section due to Defendants’ failure to serve an initial timely response. Counsel alleges hourly rates of $330 and $350, which the Court credits. (Zaffos Decl. ¶ 16.) Counsel further alleges having spent 2.5 hours drafting the present motions at $330 per hour, 1 hour spent drafting a reply at the same rate, and anticipates spending 0.5 hours attending the hearing on this matter at $350 per hour. (Id. ¶ 17.) Counsel additionally alleges a $60 filing fee with respect to each of the two motions. (Ibid.) Because of the near identical nature and simplicity of the two motions, the Court finds 1.5 total hours drafting them a more appropriate estimate. Thus, Plaintiff is awarded $1,120 in sanctions ([$330 per hour] x [2.5 hours] + [$350 per hour] x [0.5 hours] + 2 x [$60 filing fee] = $1,120), payable by Defendants’ counsel.

 

Conclusion:

 

            The Court grants the motion in part. The matters are not deemed admitted but Plaintiff is still awarded $1,120 in sanctions payable by Defendants’ counsel within 30 days.
