Case Number: 25TRCV03511    Hearing Date: August 13, 2026    Dept: B

Moving Parties:                       Defendant Lote MD LLC dba Blink Fitness

Responding Party:                  Plaintiff Giselle Nuno

Motion for Summary Judgment

 

           

INTRODUCTION

            Defendant Lote MD LLC dba Blink Fitness requests summary judgment in its favor and against Plaintiff Giselle Nuno on the ground that no triable issue of material fact exists and Defendant is entitled to summary judgment as a matter of law.  The Court considered the moving, opposition, and reply papers.  The motion for summary judgment is GRANTED.

PROCEDURAL HISTORY

On October 14, 2025, Plaintiff Giselle Nuno filed a form complaint against Defendant asserting one cause of action for premises liability.

On March 13, 2026, Defendant filed its motion for summary judgment.

On July 23, 2026, Plaintiff filed her opposition.

On July 31, 2026, Defendant filed a reply.

OBJECTIONS AND REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

Plaintiff’s objection Nos. 1 and 6 are OVERRULED.  Objection Nos. 2-5 are SUSTAINED.

Defendant’s objection nos. 1 and 2 are SUSTAINED.  Objection No. 3 is OVERRULED.

Defendant’s request for judicial notice is GRANTED.

FACTS

            The form complaint alleges that on February 10, 2025, Plaintiff was injured when she stepped onto a moving treadmill at Defendant’s Blink Fitness facility located in Gardena, California.  Defendant’s Undisputed Material Facts (“DUMF”) 1.  The entirety of the narrative allegations in the form complaint state:  “Plaintiff was injured on Defendant’s premises when she stepped onto a treadmill that was running at a high speed.  Defendant knew or should have known that the treadmill was operating at a high speed and failed to take measures to maintain a [sic] safe premises.  Defendant should have conducted reasonable inspections of the premises and prevented this dangerous condition.”  Complaint.

Before Plaintiff was injured, at the time she became a member of Defendant’s gym on July 19, 2021, she voluntarily signed a Membership Agreement, which included a Waiver and Release of Liability provision.  DUMF 5, 13.  This provision states:

IN CONSIDERATION OF THE MEMBERSHIP PRIVILEGES PROVIDED TO YOU HEREUNDER, AND ON BEHALF OF YOUR HEIRS, BENEFICIARIES, DISTRIBUTEES, LEGAL REPRESENTATIVES, SUCCESSORS, ASSIGNS AND GUESTS, YOU HEREBY VOLUNTARILY AND KNOWINGLY, FOREVER WAIVE, RELEASE, COVENANT NOT TO SUE, DISCHARGE AND HOLD HARMLESS BLINK, ITS PARENTS, SUBSIDIARIES, AND OTHER AFFILIATES, AND ITS AND THEIR RESPECTIVE OFFICERS, DIRECTORS, EMPLOYEES, CONTRACTORS, AGENTS, SUCCESSORS AND ASSIGNS (THE “BLINK PARTIES”) FROM, AND SUCH BLINK PARTIES WILL NOT BE LIABLE TO YOU OR ANY OTHER PERSON FOR, ANY LOSSES OR DAMAGES, WHETHER DIRECT, INDIRECT, INCIDENTAL, CONSEQUENTIAL, SPECIAL, PUNITIVE, OR EXEMPLARY, INCLUDING WITHOUT LIMITATION FOR ANY PROPERTY LOSS OR DAMAGE, LOSS OF EARNINGS OR EARNING CAPACITY, PERSONAL INJURY, ILLNESS, DISEASE (INCLUDING AN INFECTIOUS DISEASE), INFECTION, IMPAIRMENT, PHYSICAL PAIN, MENTAL ANGUISH, PARALYSIS, HEART ATTACK OR DEATH, ARISING OUT OF, IN CONNECTION WITH OR RELATED TO THIS AGREEMENT, YOUR MEMBERSHIP, THE USE OR NON-USE OF ANY SERVICE, PRODUCT OR EQUIPMENT PROVIDED OR OFFERED HEREUNDER, WHETHER RELATED TO EXERCISE OR NOT AND REGARDLESS OF LEGAL THEORY OR WHETHER ARISING IN OR BY STATUTE, TORT, CONTRACT, STRICT LIABILITY, BREACH OF WARRANTY OR OTHERWISE, INCLUDING THOSE LOSSES OR DAMAGES RESULTING FROM OR CAUSED BY, IN WHOLE OR IN PART, THE NEGLIGENCE OF ANY BLINK PARTY AND REGARDLESS OF WHETHER SUCH LOSSES OR DAMAGES ARE KNOWN OR UNKNOWN TO YOU OR ANY OTHER PERSON; PROVIDED THAT THE FOREGOING RELEASE AND WAIVER OF LIABILITY SHALL NOT APPLY TO ANY LOSSES OR DAMAGES TO THE EXTENT CAUSED BY OR RESULTING FROM THE WILLFUL OR WANTON MISCONDUCT OF ANY BLINK PARTY OR TO THE EXTENT PROHIBITED BY LAW. NONETHELESS, THIS RELEASE IS INTENDED BY BOTH PARTIES TO BE AS BROAD IN EFFECT AS ALLOWED BY LAW AND SHALL COVER OR INCLUDE ANY CLAIM OR DEMAND YOU HAVE, HAD OR EVER WILL HAVE.

Defendant’s Ex. A, ¶ 3.

STANDARD OF REVIEW

            The purpose of a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication “is to provide courts with a mechanism to cut through the parties’ pleadings in order to determine whether, despite their allegations, trial is in fact necessary to resolve their dispute.”  Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.  “Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (c), requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.”  Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.

“On a motion for summary judgment, the initial burden is always on the moving party to make a prima facie showing that there are no triable issues of material fact.”  Scalf v. D. B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1519.  A defendant moving for summary judgment or summary adjudication “has met his or her burden of showing that a cause of action has no merit if the party has shown that one or more elements of the cause of action . . . cannot be established, or that there is a complete defense to the cause of action.”  CCP § 437c(p)(2).  “Once the defendant . . . has met that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff . . . to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.” CCP § 437c(p)(2).

“To meet its burden of proof, a plaintiff must introduce evidence which affords a reasonable basis for the conclusion that it is more likely than not that the conduct of the defendant was a cause in fact of the result.  A mere possibility of such causation is not enough; and when the matter remains one of pure speculation or conjecture, or the probabilities are at best evenly balanced, it becomes the duty of the court to direct a verdict for the defendant.”  Peralta v. The Vons Companies, Inc. (2018) 24 Cal.App.5th 1030, 1035 (quotation marks and citation omitted).

“When deciding whether to grant summary judgment, the court must consider all of the evidence set forth in the papers (except evidence to which the court has sustained an objection), as well as all reasonable inferences that may be drawn from that evidence, in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment.” Avivi v. Centro Medico Urgente Medical Center (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 463, 467; CCP §437c(c).

“[A]ny doubts as to the propriety of granting a summary judgment motion should be resolved in favor of the party opposing the motion.”  Reid v. Google, Inc. (2010) 50 Cal.4th 512, 535 (citation omitted).

ANALYSIS

            Plaintiff alleges a cause of action for premises liability.  The elements of premises liability mirror those for negligence:  a legal duty of care, breach of that duty, and proximate cause resulting in injury. Kesner v. Superior Court (2016) 1 Cal.5th 1132, 1158.  “Premises liability is grounded in the possession of the premises and the attendant right to control and manage the premises; accordingly, mere possession with its attendant right to control conditions on the premises is a sufficient basis for the imposition of an affirmative duty to act.”  Id. (citations omitted).  “Those who own, possess, or control property generally have a duty to exercise ordinary care in managing the property in order to avoid exposing others to an unreasonable risk of harm.”  Annocki v. Peterson Enterprises, LLC (2014) 232 Cal.App.4th 32, 37.

Defendant argues that it has a complete defense because there is a valid agreement signed by Plaintiff that releases Defendant from liability for ordinary negligence.  “An exculpatory contract releasing a party from liability for future ordinary negligence is valid unless it is prohibited by statute or impairs the public interest.  Numerous cases have upheld the validity of such agreements waiving ordinary negligence claims in the context of the use of gymnasiums and fitness facilities.”  Joshi v. Fitness Int’l, LLC (2022) 80 Cal.App.5th 814, 826 (quotation marks and citations omitted).  Defendant has carried its burden of showing that Plaintiff cannot succeed on her claim based in ordinary negligence because she waived such claims.  Defendant’s Ex. A; DUMF 5, 13.  Plaintiff does not contest the validity of the waiver.  Plaintiff argues, however, that Defendant failed to carry its initial burden on gross negligence, which cannot be waived.  See Joshi, supra, 80 Cal.App.5th 814, 827 (explaining that an agreement that purports to release liability for future gross negligence violates public policy and is unenforceable).

“Ordinary negligence—an unintentional tort—consists of a failure to exercise the degree of care in a given situation that a reasonable person under similar circumstances would employ to protect others from harm.  Gross negligence long has been defined in California and other jurisdictions as either a want of even scant care or an extreme departure from the ordinary standard of conduct.”  Jimenez v. 24 Hour Fitness USA, Inc. (2015) 237 Cal.App.4th 546, 555 (quotation marks and citations omitted). 

“In the context of a motion for summary judgment, where a complaint alleges facts demonstrating gross negligence in anticipation of a release, the initial burden remains on the moving defendant asserting the release as a defense to produce evidence refuting the allegations constituting gross negligence.  But when the plaintiff fails to allege in his or her pleading facts sufficient to support a theory of gross negligence, the defendant satisfies its burden by asserting a release as a complete defense, and the burden then shifts to the plaintiff to produce evidence that there is a triable issue of fact supporting gross negligence to defeat summary judgment.”  Joshi v. Fitness Int’l, LLC, supra, 80 Cal.App.5th 814, 828 (quotation marks and citations omitted). 

Defendant did not have the initial burden of refuting allegations constituting gross negligence because there are none in the complaint.  Plaintiff alleged that Defendant failed to discover that a treadmill was operating at high speed.  “[C]onduct demonstrating the failure to guard against, or warn of, a dangerous condition typically does not rise to the level of gross negligence.”  Ibid.  Having shown that the release is a complete defense, the burden shifts to Plaintiff to produce evidence that there is a triable issue of fact supporting gross negligence.  “[A]lthough the existence of gross negligence is a mater generally for the trier of fact, it may be determined as a matter of law on summary judgment in an appropriate case.”  Ibid.  This is such a case.

Plaintiff failed to produce evidence that there is a triable issue of material fact supporting gross negligence.  Plaintiff declares that as she stepped on the treadmill, the belt was already moving.  She had not turned the treadmill on, and she had not set its speed. The belt was moving quickly, faster than a running pace.  The moving belt immediately threw her off balance; she fell and was injured.  No one from Blink warned her that a treadmill might be left running while no one was using it.  Plaintiff’s Dec., ¶¶ 3-7.  Nothing in her declaration supports that Defendant was grossly negligent.  See Anderson v. Fitness Int’l, LLC (2016) 4 Cal.App.5th 867, 881 (“Mere nonfeasance, such as the failure to discover a dangerous condition or to perform a duty, amounts to ordinary negligence.” (quotation marks and citation omitted)). 

 Plaintiff argues that Defendant was grossly negligent because it did not verify the automatic shut-off safety feature on the treadmill worked, did not inspect the treadmill for malfunction, and did not check equipment daily or the subject treadmill on the day of the incident.  These arguments are immaterial because Plaintiff produced no evidence that the treadmill malfunctioned.  Plaintiff further argues that Defendant had no safety procedures or procedures of any kind aimed at unattended running treadmills.  This argument falls short because there is no evidence that Defendant departed from any manufacturer’s safety directions or an industry standard in failing to do so.  Cf. Joshi, supra, 80 Cal.App.5th 814, 828 (“Evidence of conduct that evinces an extreme departure from manufacturer’s safety directions or an industry standard also could demonstrate gross negligence.”).  Finally, Plaintiff argues that Defendant disregarded a safety instruction, yet cites no safety instruction that was purportedly disregarded.   

Plaintiff relies on Jimenez v. 24 Hour Fitness USA, Inc., supra, 237 Cal.App.4th 546, and Chavez v. 24 Hour Fitness USA, Inc. (2015) 238 Cal.App.4th 632, to no avail.  In Jimenez, a plaintiff was injured when she fell backwards off a moving treadmill and hit her head on an exercise machine placed approximately four feet behind the treadmill.  Jimenez, supra, 237 Cal.App.4th 546, 549.  The court found that there were triable issues of fact because the plaintiff presented evidence of a possible industry standard on treadmill safety zones, including the treadmill manufacturer’s owner’s manual that instructed that the minimum space required for user safety is six feet deep;  the manufacturer’s treadmill assembly guide that instructed the treadmill requires a minimum six-foot-deep clearance behind it for user safety; and an expert declaration stating that, for user safety, it is important that there be a safety zone behind the treadmill that is kept clear of all objects.  Id. at 556.

In Chavez, supra, 238 Cal.App.4th 632, 634, a plaintiff was injured when the back panel of a cross-trainer machine struck her in the head.  The defendant presented evidence that it employed a technician to routinely inspect and perform preventative maintenance on its equipment.  Id. at 635.  The plaintiff pointed out, however, that despite employing such technician, there was no evidence that anyone actually performed preventative maintenance on the cross-trainer machine.  Id. at 636.  Plaintiff also presented evidence that the owner’s manual called for maintenance, along with a declaration from a consultant and supplier specializing in physical conditioning equipment opining that it is the custom and practice in the fitness industry to perform preventative maintenance on all machinery in compliance with the owner’s manual. Ibid.  Finally, the plaintiff submitted an expert declaration of a mechanical engineer specializing in engineering design of exercise equipment who declared that he inspected the cross-trainer and stated that the back panel should be held in place by four metal brackets and magnetic strips, but the upper right bracket and upper left magnetic strips were missing, and the three remaining brackets were bent and separating from the machine.  Ibid.  On this record, the court found that there were triable issues of fact regarding whether the defendant was grossly negligent with respect to preventative maintenance.  Id. at 642. 

Unlike those cases, there is no evidence here that Defendant “violated something like an industry standard, or manufacturer’s safety directions or otherwise made an extreme departure from the ordinary standard of conduct.”  Jimenez, supra, 237 Cal.App.4th 546, 561.     

Finally, Plaintiff argues that Defendant is not entitled to summary judgment because it did not preserve any video footage of the incident, which Plaintiff alleges would have shown how long the treadmill was running before the incident.  There is no evidence that Defendant’s video cameras captured the incident.  In fact, there is evidence that the cameras did not capture it.  At his deposition, the gym’s General Manager, Brian Lopez, testified that he checked to see if any video footage captured the incident, he checked eight different angles, and the incident was not captured on video because it was in a “blind spot” in the gym.  Farzan Dec., Ex. 1 (Lopez Depo.) at PDF 19-21 (the deposition transcript pages are unnumbered).  Lopez declared that he did not save any footage because no camera angle showed the incident.  Id. at PDF 21.

The Court finds that Plaintiff has not carried her burden of producing evidence sufficient to raise a triable issue of fact of gross negligence.

CONCLUSION

            The motion for summary judgment is GRANTED.

            Defendant is ordered to file a separate proposed judgment.

            Defendant is ordered to give notice of ruling.


Case Number: 26TRCV00714    Hearing Date: August 13, 2026    Dept: B

Moving Parties:                      Defendants Internet Law Center Ltd. and Bennet Kelley

Responding Party:                  Plaintiff Gai Toi Williams

Demurrer and Motion to Strike

            The Court considered the moving, opposition, and reply papers.

RULING

            See below.

BACKGROUND

            On February 25, 2026, plaintiff Gai Toi Williams filed a complaint against defendants Bennet Kelley and Internet Law Center Ltd for (1) fraud or deceit in concealment, (2) professional negligence (legal malpractice), and (3) breach of fiduciary duty.

LEGAL AUTHORITY

Demurrer

When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context.  Taylor v. City of Los Angeles Dept. of Water and Power (2006) 144 Cal. App. 4th 1216, 1228.  “A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters.  Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed.”  SKF Farms v. Superior Court (1984) 153 Cal. App. 3d 902, 905.  “The only issue involved in a demurrer hearing is whether the complaint, as it stands, unconnected with extraneous matters, states a cause of action.”  Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal. App. 4th 740, 747.

Strike

“The court may, upon a motion . . . , or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper:  (a) Strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading.  (b) Strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court.”  CCP §436(b).

            CCP §431.10 states:  “(a) A material allegation in a pleading is one essential to the claim or defense and which could not be stricken from the pleading without leaving it insufficient as to that claim or defense. 

(b) An immaterial allegation in a pleading is any of the following:  (1) An allegation that is not essential to the statement of a claim or defense.  (2) An allegation that is neither pertinent to nor supported by an otherwise sufficient claim or defense.  (3) A demand for judgment requesting relief not supported by the allegations of the complaint or cross-complaint. 

(c) An ‘immaterial allegation’ means ‘irrelevant matter’ as that term is used in Section 436.”

The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice.  CCP §437.

DISCUSSION

            Demurrer

Defendants Bennet Kelley and Internet Law Center Ltd. (self-represented) demur to the first, second, and third causes of action in the complaint on the grounds that they fail to state sufficient facts to constitute a cause of action and are uncertain.

            The complaint alleges that defendant Bennet Kelley is a licensed California attorney.  Complaint, ¶6.  In the early 2000s, Kelley first had a spam email case against Dan Balsam.  Id., ¶12.  Kelley’s website states that since 2003, Kelley has “sparred with Balsam over California’s spam law.”  Id., ¶13.  In early 2018, plaintiff hired Balsam and another attorney named Jacob Harker (“B&H”) to represent her in various spam email matters.  Id., ¶15.  From early 2018 through 2021, plaintiff had referred several of her friends and family members to B&H.  Id., ¶16.  Some of the people who plaintiff referred to B&H ended up hiring B&H to file malpractice actions against their former spam attorneys Sheehan, Heit, and Feaver (“SFH”).  Id., ¶17.  On October 28, 2021, SFH filed a lawsuit against plaintiff and B&H in retaliation for B&H representing SFH’s unhappy former clients in the malpractice actions and in retaliation for plaintiff’s referrals of some of those unhappy former clients to B&H (“SFH malicious SLAPP action”).  Id., ¶18.  Unbeknownst to plaintiff, in 2021, defendant Kelley provided material assistance to SFH to help them prepare and litigate the SFH malicious SLAPP action.  Id., ¶20.  On January 10, 2022, B&H filed an anti-SLAPP motion.  At that time, plaintiff had not been served in the SFH malicious SLAPP action and did not join in the anti-SLAPP motion.  Id., ¶21.  On June 8, 2022, the anti-SLAPP motion was granted.  Id., ¶22.  As of the filing date of the herein complaint, the SFH malicious SLAPP action is still pending against plaintiff.  Id., ¶24.

The complaint further alleges that in early 2023, plaintiff was referred to Kelley to investigate any claims that she may have against B&H.  Id., ¶25.  Since Kelley still held animus towards Balsam, Kelley agreed to represent plaintiff (“B&H representation”).  The scope of the B&H representation was expressly limited to plaintiff’s potential claims against B&H.  Id., ¶26.  Kelley never told plaintiff that he had assisted SFH in the SFH malicious SLAPP action.  Id., ¶27.  Kelley ultimately terminated the B&H representation.  Id., ¶28. 

The complaint further alleges that in early 2023, during the course of the B&H representation, Kelley contacted plaintiff and informed her that Sheehan and Heit (“SH”) wanted to discuss purchasing damaging testimony against B&H from plaintiff (“SH witness tampering scheme”).  Kelley told plaintiff that he would represent her in negotiations with Sheenan and Heit (“SH representation”).  Id., ¶29.  Kelley and plaintiff did not have a written agreement for the SH representation.  Id., ¶30.  Plaintiff participated in a telephone call with Kelley and SH wherein SH proposed paying plaintiff to provide a declaration which, in effect, would state that plaintiff had committed a crime along with B&H.  Id., ¶31.  SH memorialized this proposal in an email to Kelley, which Kelley forwarded to plaintiff.  Id., ¶32.  At that time, the SFH malicious SLAPP action was ongoing and Harker was plaintiff’s attorney of record.  Id., ¶33.  Kelley never informed plaintiff that the SH witness tampering scheme was likely illegal.  Id., ¶34.  Kelley never informed plaintiff that it could be damaging to her credibility if she accepted money to provide testimony instead of simply responding to any discovery in the SFH malicious SLAPP action.  Id., ¶35.  Kelley never informed plaintiff of the potential dangers associated with signing a declaration, under penalty of perjury, stating that she committed a crime.  Id., ¶36.  Kelley never attempted to settle SH’s claims against plaintiff in the SFH malicious SLAPP action.  Id., ¶37.  Kelley never did any of these things because he did not care about plaintiff.  Instead, he cared about harming Balsam and was willing to assist SH and throw plaintiff “under the proverbial bus” in order to damage Balsam.  Id., ¶38.  Kelley never terminated the SH representation and plaintiff never discharged him.  Id., ¶39.

The complaint further alleges that on May 29, 2024, Heit filed for bankruptcy.  Id., ¶40.  On February 25, 2025, plaintiff first learned of Kelley’s participation in the SFH malicious SLAPP action.  Id., ¶42.  Plaintiff has suffered from emotional distress after learning that Kelley assisted SFH in litigating the SFH malicious SLAPP action against her.  Id., ¶43.

            First cause of action for fraud or deceit in concealment

“’The required elements for fraudulent concealment are (1) concealment or suppression of a material fact; (2) by a defendant with a duty to disclose the fact to the plaintiff; (3) the defendant intended to defraud the plaintiff by intentionally concealing or suppressing the fact; (4) the plaintiff was unaware of the fact and would not have acted as he or she did if he or she had known of the concealed or suppressed fact; and (5) plaintiff sustained damage as a result of the concealment or suppression of fact.’”  Hambridge v. Healthcare Partners Medical Group, Inc. (2015) 238 Cal. App. 4th 124, 162 (citation omitted).  “One who willfully deceives another with intent to induce him to alter his position to his injury or risk, is liable for any damage which he thereby suffers.”  Civil Code §1709.

“[T]o establish fraud through nondisclosure or concealment of facts, it is necessary to show the defendant ‘was under a legal duty to disclose them.’”  OCM Principal Opportunities Fund v. CIBC World Markets Corp. (2007) 157 Cal. App. 4th 835, 845.  Nondisclosure or concealment may constitute actionable fraud when:  (1) there is a fiduciary relationship between the parties; (2) the defendant had exclusive knowledge of material facts not known to the plaintiff; (3) the defendant actively conceals a material fact from the plaintiff; and (4) the defendant makes partial representations but also suppresses some material facts.  Los Angeles Memorial Coliseum Commission v. Insomniac, Inc. (2015) 233 Cal. App. 4th 803, 831.

The complaint alleges that defendants intentionally failed to disclose to plaintiff that Kelley assisted SFH in the SFH malicious SLAPP action.  Complaint, ¶46.  Plaintiff did not know that Kelley had assisted SFH.  Id., ¶47.  Defendants intended to deceive plaintiff by concealing the fact that Kelley assisted SFH.  Id., ¶48.  Had the omitted information been disclosed, plaintiff reasonably would have behaved differently.  Plaintiff would never have hired Kelley to represent her in the SH representation.  Id., ¶49.  Plaintiff was harmed.  Specifically, plaintiff lost the opportunity to negotiate a settlement of the SFH malicious SLAPP action and will have to incur costs to continue litigating that action.  Id., ¶50.  Defendants’ concealment was a substantial factor in causing Williams’ harm.  Id., ¶51.

Defendants argue that the allegations are conclusory and that the complaint fails to plead with specificity, including as to “material assistance” and as to how plaintiff purportedly became aware on February 25, 2025.  Defendants further assert that plaintiff cannot allege damage and causation as plaintiff alleges that she was represented in the course of the “SFH malicious SLAPP action” by B&H and that it “defies logic” that a “brief tri-party discussion” between SH, Kelley, and plaintiff “somehow deprived” plaintiff’s own counsel of the ability to reach a settlement.  Defendants also note that the allegation at para. 50 that “plaintiff lost the ability to negotiate a settlement” is false because on November 1, 2023, plaintiff reached a “664.6 enforceable settlement agreement” with Sheehan Group, citing to Request for Judicial Notice, Exh. B.

In opposition, plaintiff argues that the claim is not uncertain and that the allegations are sufficient.

The Court finds that the allegations are insufficient.  The allegations are conclusory as to “material assistance,” which is what the complaint is based on and what plaintiff alleges that Kelley concealed.  Also, the allegations are insufficient as to causation and damages.  Plaintiff alleges that had she known that Kelley provided assistance to SFH in the SFH action, she would not have hired him to represent her in the SH matter, and that because she hired Kelley in the SH matter, she lost the opportunity to negotiate a settlement of the SFH action in which she was not represented by Kelley.

The demurrer is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

Second cause of action for professional negligence/legal malpractice

The elements of a claim for professional negligence are (1) the duty to use such skill, prudence, and diligence as members of the profession commonly possess; (2) a breach of that duty; (3) a proximate causal connection between breach and damages, and (4) actual damages. Schultz v. Harney (1994) 27 Cal. App. 4th 1611, 1621.  An attorney’s breach of the ethical duties of good faith and fidelity amounts to malpractice and is actionable.  Id.  “In a litigation malpractice action, the plaintiff must establish that but for the alleged negligence of the defendant attorney, the plaintiff would have obtained a more favorable judgment or settlement in the action in which the malpractice allegedly occurred.  The purpose of this requirement, which has been in use for more than 120 years, is to safeguard against speculative and conjectural claims.  It serves the essential purpose of ensuring that damages awarded for the attorney’s malpractice actually have been caused by the malpractice. . . . [T]he crucial causation inquiry is what would have happened if the defendant attorney had not been negligent.  This is so because the very idea of causation necessarily involves comparing historical events to a hypothetical alternative.”  Viner v. Sweet (2003) 30 Cal.4th 1231, 1241-1242.

The complaint alleges that defendant’s wrongful acts began some time in or around early 2023 when they agreed to the SH representation without disclosing Kelley’s participation in the SFH malicious SLAPP action, and continued through the present day because the SH representation was never terminated.  Plaintiff learned of the fraud on February 25, 2025.  Id., ¶53.  Plaintiff suffered actual injury when defendants, with respect to the SH representation acted against plaintiff’s interests in the SH witness tampering scheme, failed to advise plaintiff on the dangers of participating in the SH witness tampering scheme, and failed to assist in negotiating a settlement of the SFH malicious SLAPP action.  Id., ¶54.

Defendants argue that the claim is barred by the one-year statute of limitation under CCP §340.6.  Defendants assert that there is no allegation of defendants performing any further work beyond 2023.  Defendants contend that tolling under the continued representation exception does not depend on formal termination or withdrawal.  Defendants also argue that plaintiff cannot establish damage or causation as plaintiff was represented by B&H, and not Kelley, and that on November 1, 2023, plaintiff entered into a settlement agreement, citing to RJN.

In opposition, plaintiff argues that she sufficiently pled delayed discovery and that the representation was continuing.  Plaintiff also argues that “injury” is a question of fact and that “emotional distress” damages are properly pleaded.  Plaintiff further disputes that there was a binding settlement of the underlying action.

The Court finds that the allegations are insufficient and the claim appears barred by the one-year statute of limitations.  Under CCP §340.6, “[a]n action against an attorney for a wrongful act or omission, other than for actual fraud, arising in the performance of professional services shall be commenced within one year after the plaintiff discovers, or through the use of reasonable diligence should have discovered, the facts constituting the wrongful act or omission, or four years from the date of the wrongful act or omission, whichever occurs first.”  The allegations are conclusory as to how and under what circumstances plaintiff discovered that defendants had “materially helped” SFH in the “SFH malicious SLAPP action.”  Further, the allegations are insufficient as to continued representation to toll the statute of limitations.  The complaint alleges that in 2023, Kelley agreed to represent plaintiff in the “B&H representation”; the scope of the B&H representation was expressly limited to plaintiff’s potential claims against B&H; and Kelley ultimately terminated the B&H representation.  See Complaint, ¶¶25-28.  The complaint states that Williams told Kelley “that he would represent her in negotiations” with Sheehan and Heit on the proposed criminal witness tampering scheme.  Id., ¶29.  The parties did not have a written agreement for this representation, which consisted of a phone call and some emails in 2023.  Id., ¶¶ 29-39.  Plaintiff asserts that the attorney-client relationship on the SH matter is still ongoing because it was never terminated, even though no work has been performed in three years.  See id. ¶39.  Further, the allegations are insufficient as to breach and damages.  Plaintiff alleges that she suffered actual injury when Kelley acted against her interests in the SH matter by failing to advise her of the dangers of participating in the witness tampering scheme (there are no allegations that she ultimately participated), and failed to assist her in negotiating a settlement of the SFH action, in which he did not represent her.

The demurrer is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

            Third cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty

The complaint alleges the same as under the second cause of action.

Defendants argue the same as under the second cause of action that the claim is barred by the one-year statute of limitations and cannot establish damage or causation.

The demurrer is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND for the same reasons as under the second cause of action.

            Motion to Strike

            Defendants request an order striking the following paragraphs:  3, 12, 13, 14, 26, 33, 34, 35, 36, 38, 54, 66, 72, 75, and 76.

            The Court notes that defendants improperly combined the demurrer and motion to strike.  A motion to strike is required to be filed separately along with a filing fee.

            Defendants contend that the first category of allegations relate to the past history and animus between Kelley and Balsam (who is not counsel or a party) are speculative and inflammatory.  Defendants assert that the second category of allegations are inflammatory characterizations relating to the declaration that the Sheehan Group proposed but plaintiff did not sign and any potential liability stemming therefrom.

            In opposition, plaintiff argues that the challenged allegations are relevant.

            The Court finds that there is no ground for striking the allegations.  The motion is DENIED.

ORDER

            The demurrer is SUSTAINED WITH TWENTY DAYS LEAVE TO AMEND as to the first, second, and third causes of action in the complaint.

            The motion to strike is DENIED.

            Defendants are ORDERED to pay a $60 filing fee for the motion to strike.

            Defendants are to give notice of the ruling.
