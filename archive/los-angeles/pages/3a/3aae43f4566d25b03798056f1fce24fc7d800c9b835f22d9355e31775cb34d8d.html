If the parties wish to submit on the tentative ruling and avoid a court appearance on the matter, the moving party must contact the opposing party and all other parties who have appeared in the action and confirm that each will submit on the tentative ruling. Please call the court no later than 4:30 p.m. on the court day before the hearing, leave a message with the court clerk at (310) 260-3629 advising her that all parties will submit on the tentative ruling and waive hearing, and finally, serve notice of the Court's ruling on all parties entitled to receive service. Case Number: 19SMCV01008    Hearing Date: June 25, 2026    Dept: I

This is an ex parte application to set a contempt hearing for defendant’s failure to execute an assignment as it had promised to do.  We were here a few months ago on a similar issue, and the court thought it had worked things out.  But apparently not.  By way of thumbnail background, this arises from the settlement of a civil suit brought by plaintiff against defendant claiming that defendant was using his image and licensing it without his permission.  The matter went to the jury and it is fair to say that defendant did not do well.  Before the punitive damages phase could begin, the parties settled the matter.  The settlement was agreed upon in open court.  The agreement was based on an exchange of emails and was to be replaced by a “long form” agreement.  However, the parties intended that until and unless the long form was signed, the exchange of emails would be binding.  One of the terms of the settlement was that defendant would assign its copyright interest in the images of Jaeschke to Jaeschke.  However, defendant was concerned that Jaeschke would sue those who were using the image, and those entities would turn around and sue defendant.  As part of the settlement, the parties agreed to establish protocols to protect defendant from such an action.  As the court understands it, the general nature would be that Jaeschke would agree not to sue any third party for the prior use of the image but would only sue the third party if the image was not taken down after Jaeschke (or perhaps defendant) told the entity to do so.  That agreement, though, was not a reduction in Jaeschke’s rights under the assignment; rather it was an agreement separate from (although related to) the assignment agreement but one that would bind Jaeschke nonetheless.  After the last hearing, where the court expressed its view that the settlement essentially meant what the court just said, the court granted the motion to enforce.  The court did not craft the language to be used, however.  Plaintiff claims that the court’s order granting the motion to enforce the settlement (which was essentially a motion to compel defendant to assign the copyright) is the one being breached.  Plaintiff contends that it cleaned up the proposed assignment to be consistent with the court’s order and sent it to the defense.  The defense seems to have ignored the communication.  Plaintiff says it followed up but was again met with silence.  Plaintiff now seeks contempt.  In opposition, defendants state a concern about images that include not only plaintiff, but others.  They are worried that assigning the entire copyright to plaintiff could lead to suits by those others, or something like that.  The solution they propose is that as to images that include others as well as plaintiff, they assign 90% of the copyright to plaintiff and retain 10% so that they have some control.  They claim that this is the way to achieve the protocol and protection for which they bargained in the email exchange such that they would not be sued unless a party refused to take down plaintiff’s image. 

 

The court is inclined to DENY the request.  Procedurally, this ought to be as a technical matter a request that the court issue an OSC re: Contempt.  Plaintiff has provided no such document, and there are specific forms for this.  An OSC re: Contempt is a charging document, like an indictment.  It has to set forth the counts of contempt and other matters just like an indictment or information would.  Plaintiff did not do that.  For that reason alone, the request is denied.  But the court also thinks plaintiff does not really want to march down this path, although plaintiff thinks he does.  This seems to be a one-count OSC.  That means that if plaintiff prevails, defendant will pay a relatively small fine or do a bit of community service.  (There is the theoretical possibility of jail time, but it is unlikely here, and even were the court to order it, there is real doubt as to whether the Sheriff would actually lock someone up over this given overcrowding.)  That’s it.  In the meantime, this would be viewed as a criminal matter.  Defendant would have the right to counsel and all of the rights that a criminal defendant would have.  Because it is one count, there would be no jury, but otherwise, that is where we are.  In the meantime, everything freezes because the defendant has the right against self-incrimination.  This is a ham-fisted way to get compliance. 

 

What the court thinks plaintiff is really after is an order that defendant sign a particular assignment document with particular language and, if defendant does not sign, then a signature by the clerk that actually assigns the rights (assuming that the court can issue such an order—that has not been briefed).  In the meantime, the court needs to hear from the defense why it seems to continue to ignore prior rulings and agreements.  Defendants avoided what could have been significant punitive damages by virtue of this settlement.  The jury’s verdict was very much in plaintiff’s favor and the jury had found punitive damages were appropriate.  Defendants got the benefit of the bargain.  The trial ended, the jury went home, and no judgment was entered against it.  But defendants must honor their end as well. 

 

At the last hearing, the court said “all means all.”  In their opposition, defendants agree on the one hand—they say so—but then they argue that all means 90%.  The problem is that they agreed to something that they now regret.  They were under the gun, to be sure.  But they agreed nonetheless.  All means all.  It does not mean 90%.  If defendants are worried that plaintiff will wrongfully exploit the pictures of third parties, then they can build a protection against that into the protocol.  If they are concerned that someone will refuse to take down a picture of plaintiff because there is a third party in it and then they will be sued for it, then they either need to accept that risk or build a safety into the protocols.  But what they cannot do is keep part of the copyright they agreed to assign.

 

For now, the application for an OSC re: Contempt is DENIED.  But the court expects it will soon see a more particular request for a specific order concerning the assignment.  Better for all would be for the parties to reach an accord.


Case Number: 23SMCV03186    Hearing Date: June 25, 2026    Dept: I

This is a motion for reconsideration.  In it, Hart-Flaherty seeks reconsideration of the court’s order regarding collateral estoppel, which was made on April 21, 2026.  Hart-Flaherty complains that the court had not read all of the papers she had submitted at the last substantive hearing.  Part of the problem is that papers tend to be filed at the last minute.  At the most recent hearing, it happened again.  Literally the morning of the hearing, Hart-Flaherty’s reply papers were uploaded.  But that time, it was not really her fault.  Late opposition papers were filed by plaintiff a few days before.  Those untimely papers were what caused the response, and the court cannot blame Hart-Flaherty for that; rather, the fault there lies with plaintiff.  However, to deal with that issue, the court continued the hearing and ordered that no further papers be filed relating to the now-fully-briefed motion for reconsideration.  That is where we now find ourselves.  The court believes it has now read all of the papers that have been filed.

 

At the prior hearing, plaintiff moved for a finding that Hart-Flaherty was collaterally estopped from arguing that she in fact owned the property.  That matters for two reasons.  First, of course, if Hart-Flaherty owns the property, then plaintiff cannot evict her.  Second, under a relatively long line of cases, the UD timeline is not proper where the defendant claims that it is the defendant who actually owns the property in question.  When that occurs, there is in essence a debate between the plaintiff and the defendant as to which is the true owner.  Our courts have squarely held that the 20-days-to-trial UD timeline is not proper to strip away a person’s claim of ownership and title to property.  The court notes that this rule applies only where the defendant claims to be the owner.  Where the defense is that the plaintiff is not the owner because some third party really owns the property, the UD process goes forward and the written title generally will be enough.  (Here, the written title is from the trustee who sold the property.)  Because Hart-Flaherty claimed to own the property, this court refused plaintiff’s repeated requests to treat this as a UD case and set it for immediate trial.  The case was filed on December 27, 2023, and trial is now set for July 2026—two and a half years after filing.  It has proceeded along a normal litigation time line, and nothing has (for the most part) stopped Hart-Flaherty from litigating the case in the normal course. 

 

But during that period, Hart-Flaherty has been involved in a number of other suits.  It is a little bit complicated because the parcel at issue is one of three somewhat related parcels originally owned by Hart-Flaherty’s former spouse, Flaherty (who is in default in the instant case).  Hart-Flaherty has no written title, but she claims that she has ownership rights due to a marriage settlement with Flaherty entered into some years ago.  To the best of the court’s knowledge, though, that settlement was never reduced to a recorded or formal change in title.  In any event, there have been a number of cases in which Hart-Flaherty has claimed some ownership interest in the property.  The court has considered all of those cases and the court believes that Hart-Flaherty has had her day in court.  She has tried to intervene claiming the ownership interest she claims here.  She has been denied on the merits each time, and she has had no success in the appellate courts either.  She has sought reconsideration in some courts, but to no avail.  The court emphasizes that Hart-Flaherty’s attempt to intervene was not denied on procedural grounds, such as an untimely filing.  It was denied on the basis that she had no legal ownership interest.  (That case was Flaherty v. Thayer, Case No. 23SMCV02778.)  There was also a bankruptcy proceeding that raised the issue and that has been affirmed on appeal.  The court is aware that it might still be pending on certiorari to the US Supreme Court.  But under federal law, a petition for certiorari does not deprive a lower court’s final judgment of collateral estoppel effect and this court will give the federal court judgment no less force that would the federal court that issued it.  That said, and this court has said it a lot, if the US Supreme Court grants the petition—meaning that it elects to hear the case on the merits—the court will stay the instant case immediately and await the Court’s merits ruling.  In other words, if the Supreme Court decides to take the matter up, that will do.  The court will hold the case until the Court’s merits ruling.  As of now, though, the court in its prior order ruled that Hart-Flaherty was now collaterally estopped from claiming that she in fact owns the property.

 

A motion for reconsideration is governed by CCP section 1008.  It requires that the moving party demonstrate new law or facts or circumstances not reasonably available to it at the prior hearing.  The court does not see any of that.  What the court sees, for the most part, is Hart-Flaherty making the same arguments that she has been making for a long time now and that have been repeatedly rejected by other courts.  The court is aware of Hart-Flaherty’s claim that her prior opposition was wrongly rejected for filing and thus not considered at the prior hearing.  Part of the problem is that the prior opposition does not appear to have been timely filed.  But whether or not it was properly filed then, the court has read it now, and the court does not reach a different conclusion. 

 

It comes down to this.  UD is the proper remedy for one claiming ownership of land to evict one who allegedly is wrongfully in possession.  In the typical UD case, the defendant—the person in possession—cannot be heard to question the plaintiff’s title if the plaintiff can produce title that appears proper on its face.  That is a powerful doctrine.  The argument that plaintiff got title improperly from another party does not matter in the UD context; the defendant is still evicted.  The exception to that rule is where the defendant claims that defendant in fact is the rightful owner of the property.  Where the defendant is able to claim that not only does plaintiff lack true title, but that defendant is the true title holder, the law will allow the case to develop along a normal litigation line rather than the extremely limited and streamlined UD framework.  Here, Hart-Flaherty claims she is the true title holder, but there are some problems.  The first is that she claims her title not by right of any recorded document in the County Recorder’s office, but rather through a dissolution order that purports to give her a 50% interest in 17474 Tramonto Drive.  If that order had been reduced to a deed or some other recorded document—as would typically be the case—then we might not be here.  Because Flaherty would only have had a 50% recorded undivided interest in the property (the court assumes it is undivided), he could only borrow money against that interest, not the property entire.  Fraudulently obtained or not, a deed of trust purportedly signed only by him would be inadequate to convey title (or the right to obtain title later) as to the whole property.  But that did not happen.  Instead, at least insofar as the court can tell, Flaherty had 100% official title in 17474 Tramonto at the time of the purported deed of trust.  And that was the question that was at issue and considered in the case where Hart-Flaherty sought to intervene.  The court is also aware that the intervention motion was denied in part on the theory that Hart-Flaherty had not provided the prenuptial agreement or dissolution agreement giving her the 50% interest, whereas she claims she did provide that.  But the court later did consider the issue.  In its later order, the court considered both the prenuptial agreement and the dissolution agreement.  The court was aware of the dissolution agreement, which predated the deed of trust, but found that it was not sufficient to overcome the presumption indicated on the recorded instruments giving Flaherty complete ownership of 17474 Tramonto.  That is a decision on the merits, and it is collateral estoppel.  The court also noted that the property was sold at the trustee’s sale, but that Hart-Flaherty did not redeem the property, which would be the way allowed by law to regain title.  The court also made reference to the attempt to stop the trustee’s sale by way of bankruptcy, and that the bankruptcy action was dismissed as frivolous.

 

The court has read and considered Hart-Flaherty’s reply.  In it, she claims that these other cases involved a different parcel, so they could not have adjudicated her rights concerning the property here in question.  The court remains unconvinced.  The gap in Hart-Flaherty’s reasoning is this.  It could well be that the earlier case involved 17470 Tramonto.  But it was a quiet title case and it appears that part of the issue was an alleged easement held by 17474 Tramonto, which is the parcel Hart-Flaherty claims she owns (or at least owns in part), and from which plaintiff seeks to evict her.  In a quiet title action involving an easement, one proper party is the owner of the property with the alleged easement.  That is why when Hart-Flaherty attempted to intervene, her ownership in 17474 Tramonto was at issue even though the quiet title was for 17470 Tramonto, a parcel she agrees she does not own.  Only the property owner of the dominant estate (allegedly 17474 Tramonto) had standing to intervene in that action to protect or be heard on the easement.  If Hart-Flaherty did have title to 17474 Tramonto, then she would have standing to be heard in the 17470 Tramonto case and it would be error to exclude her from attempting to enforce her easement, but not otherwise.  The court notes that the case is on appeal, but the court was not aware that Hart-Flaherty’s intervention issue remained open on appeal.  And none of that really addresses the bankruptcy case, which raises similar concerns. 

 

Therefore, the motion for reconsideration is DENIED.  However, the court will make one note.  In various filings, Hart-Flaherty states that she can produce the notary public who notarized the various trust deeds pursuant to which the sale of the property occurred in which plaintiff obtained title.  Hart-Flaherty says that this notary will testify that she (the notary) committed perjury because although she notarized the deeds, they were not actually signed.  If in fact Hart-Flaherty can produce the notary, and the notary confesses that the notarization that she signed was perjurious, that is a different fact.  It will have to be done at trial, and a declaration will not suffice; the notary needs to be live and in person and subject to cross examination.  But if that is the case, and if plaintiff here was a party to the potentially fraudulent notarization, then there is at least an arguable claim of extrinsic fraud on the court, which might very well be a ground to determine some of the prior rulings or judgments void (at least in state court).  That is a steep hill to climb, but that is what Hart-Flaherty says she can do.  If she can do it, that has heft.  The court is aware of plaintiff’s position that even if the deed of trust was forged, it still is not enough because all that means is that Flaherty owns the property, not that Hart-Flaherty does.  Perhaps.  But if the whole thing is stacked and built upon a perjurious document and if plaintiff was aware of that perjury at the time the notarization occurred, that at least piques the court’s interest.

Case Number: 25SMCV06475    Hearing Date: June 25, 2026    Dept: I

These are similar special motions to strike brought in two similar cases.  Plaintiff in these cases is a tenant who sues the former landlord and property manager.  There was a previous UD case in which the landlord successfully evicted plaintiff.  In the instant case, plaintiff claims a number of torts.  The underlying alleged facts are that on November 4, 2025, defendant wrote and delivered a letter asking to talk to plaintiff concerning various complaints that defendant had received.  This was a first step toward investigating issues that could lead to a Measure RR letter and potential eviction.  The eventual Measure RR letter was written on official letterhead and was addressed to “Semen.”  Measure RR is a part of the Santa Monica rent control structure.  It extends protection against eviction in a number of ways.  It requires landlords to give tenants written notice to correct a lease violation before starting eviction proceedings, and also limits the landlord’s ability to evict tenants who are terminally ill or who have lived in the unit for at least five years or who are disabled or who are at least 62.  The notice here accused plaintiff of abusing, harassing, and threatening others, which would be cause for eviction if true.  It accused plaintiff of being a nuisance.  On December 5, defendants sent plaintiff a cure or quit notice.  The notice was allegedly verified by management.  Plaintiff also claims that defendant disseminated the letter to the Housing Authority and to two individuals: Dominique Thurman and Jonathan Campbell.  Plaintiff alleges that the foregoing conduct constituted defamation by discussing the alleged behaviors without plaintiff’s permission and communicating the accusations to others.  Plaintiff also claims that defendants did not respond to her requests for accommodation made by herself and her representative Jonathan Campbell to have meetings through Zoom or a similar method or to use Zoom to inspect the premises rather than conduct a physical inspection.  She also alleges that the foregoing conduct constituted intentional infliction of emotional distress.  The third cause of action is for negligence, and again, it is based on the same set of facts.  The fourth cause of action is for the public disclosure of private facts.  It is based on the same allegations as set forth above—there is no additional detail.  The fifth cause of action is for intrusion into private affairs, again by discussing the issues raised in the Measure RR notice and failing to accommodate plaintiff’s requests.  The sixth and final cause of action is for false light, and is again based on the same set of facts.  The other case is similar, but it alleges sexual harassment.  However, the supporting facts are the same.

 

A Special Motion to Strike (SMS) is a California response to what the Legislature concluded were a series of lawsuits designed to chill speech.  The underlying problem was that a person would engage in free speech—such as picketing or the like—and would be met with a lawsuit claiming that the speech was tortious in some way.  True, the defendant would ultimately prevail if the speech was truly protected speech, but only after litigating the issue—potentially through summary judgment or trial.  In the meantime, the defendant would be forced to run the litigation gauntlet and retain counsel.  The effect, the Legislature concluded, was that people were afraid to engage in protected speech or activity due to this improper and heavy-handed mis-use of the legal process.  The response was Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16—the Special Motion to Strike.  (It is often referred to as an anti-SLAPP motion.  The court prefers SMS.)  This is a very powerful tool that is given to defendants who believe that they are being sued for engaging in this kind of constitutionally-protected conduct.  It allows an early resolution of the case on the merits.  The motion is a speaking motion, meaning that the court goes beyond the allegations in the complaint, and once brought, the motion freezes discovery.  A plaintiff is generally not allowed to amend if the motion is successful, and the motion—win or lose—is appealable.  A successful defendant is entitled to recover attorneys’ fees as a matter of right as well.  The same is not true of a party successfully defeating the motion unless the motion was brought in a way that (essentially) violates CCP section 128.5. 

 

The SMS sets forth a two step process to balance the desire to get a resolution early against the need to be sure that due process is maintained.  The first step is to determine whether the suit really alleges a tort based on the exercise of a constitutionally protected right.  After all, most torts having nothing to do with the constitution.  The burden on establishing this prong lies with the defense, and the court looks primarily to the complaint to answer the question.  The court can look beyond the complaint, but generally, plaintiff is the master of the complaint and a plaintiff is entitled to plead around constitutionally protected conduct.  Further, an allegation of constitutionally protected activity will not trigger the statute if it is only background.  It has to be a part of the charging allegations—meaning it needs to form a part of the actual basis for the cause of action.  If the defendant can meet the first prong, then the burden shifts to the plaintiff to defeat the motion under the second prong.  The statute states that it is up to the plaintiff to show a reasonable probability of success.  But those words are deceiving.  Recall that this is the start of the case.  The plaintiff has not had the benefit of discovery, and the point of the SMS is not to do away with the fact-finder’s role.  Rather, this prong has been interpreted as a test to see whether the case has “minimal merit,” which is a standard akin to that needed to oppose successfully a motion for summary judgment.  If the plaintiff can present evidence—not just allegations—that (if believed) would lead to a plaintiff’s verdict, that is sufficient.  The court can consider the evidence, but it is not to weigh the evidence.  If there is a triable issue of fact, the motion fails. 

 

The court turns to the first prong.  The allegations there are that Leguizamon, who was employed by Community Corporation as a Property Supervisor for the relevant property, wrote to plaintiff to request a call to discuss certain complaints that had been received.  He provided his contact information.  He mistakenly began the letter “Dear Semen.”  The defense has put in evidence that this was a mistake.  He took the heading from a recent letter he had written to a tenant whose first name was “Semen” (pronounced Simon).  Plaintiff did not call, so a formal Measure RR letter was sent to plaintiff.  Under that measure, such a letter is required before eviction proceedings can begin.  That ultimately led to an inspection of plaintiff’s residence in December 2025, which ultimately led to a new Measure RR letter and then eviction proceedings.  Therefore, the November letter was a predicate to the start of potential eviction procedures, which is constitutionally protected.  The same is true of the December cure or quit notice.

 

All of that conduct is protected.  Measure RR is the predicate that leads to an unlawful detainer action.  That is an action in court, and as such, it involves the right to petition government.  Suing someone for taking a required first step toward bringing a legal action is a suit that does involve a constitutionally protected right.  While plaintiff does not allege that the UD action itself is the wrong, the things about which she does sue are necessary predicates to that action.  (That is true even if the ultimate grounds for the UD action were different from those in the original Measure RR notice.  The fact is that the original notice was a predicate to suit; the fact that the landlord ultimately brought the UD on different grounds after learning more information does not change the character of the claim.)  That is sufficient, and the burden shifts.  The court notes that while it is plaintiff’s burden to present evidence, the defense here has raised the litigation privilege codified in Civil Code section 47(b).  That statute makes conduct in litigation absolutely privileged as against tort claims (other than malicious prosecution).  It is often called the “absolute” privilege, although pre-litigation conduct is not really absolutely privileged.  Even so, it is very potent and does extend to core conduct necessary to pursue an eventual claim in court.  Here, plaintiff has not opposed the motion, so there is no evidence to support her claims at all.  But even if there were, pretty much everything done was done in furtherance of the eventual or contemplated UD claim.  The defamation assumes a false statement, but there is no evidence that any statement was false.  (Further, to the extent that the statement was an allegation of complaints by others, if the defendant really received the complaints, the statements are true.)  The other causes of action are all derivative of the fact that defendant was starting to take steps that eventually led to plaintiff’s eviction.  Further, it is possible that plaintiff’s action here is barred by res judicata from the UD action, but even if not plaintiff has failed to show minimal merit or how her claims will survive the section 47 privilege.

 

Because defendant has demonstrated that the lawsuits involve constitutionally protected conduct, and because plaintiff has presented no evidence suggesting that there is even a triable issue of fact on the merits, the motions succeed.  Therefore, the Special Motions to Strike are GRANTED.  The complaint is STRICKEN.  Defendants will prepare a written order for the court’s signature.

Case Number: 26SMCP00219    Hearing Date: June 25, 2026    Dept: I

This is a motion brought by petitioner to quash a subpoena.  Petitioner was involved in an accident and claims to be eligible for insurance benefits under the underinsured motorist coverage with the carrier.  Petitioner began this action in aid of the arbitration to establish the coverage.  The way it works is that under Insurance Code section 11580, where this sort of claim is made, it will be determined by arbitration.  But in aid of that, a petitioner can open a Superior Court file in order to use the court to enable discovery.  The respondent is also entitled to use court process for that purpose.  Respondent has issued a subpoena to another carrier seeking the production of the claim files for three separate losses occurring over a two month period in 2022.  The records sought include medical bills, medical records, medical reports, earning information, settlements, and similar materials.  Petitioner contends that this is overbroad but that attempts to work with respondent to narrow the scope or have the subpoena withdrawn have been unsuccessful, leading to this motion to quash.

 

The thrust of petitioner’s argument is that whatever claims were made back in 2022 have nothing to do with the claim at issue here, given that the incident here took place in 2024.  Petitioner notes that these records likely contain confidential medical information that petitioner has not put at issue in this case.  In support, petitioner notes the right to privacy, which is generally waived in a case like this only to the extent that the claimant puts the matter at issue.  In opposition, respondent claims that medical records already obtained in this case concerning the 2024 incident demonstrate that there were several prior accidents involving the same body parts.  Respondent contends that it presently does not have the records pertaining to those injuries, and that is what it now seeks.  Respondent also notes that in discovery to petitioner, petitioner failed to disclose the 2022 accidents but rather disclosed only an earlier accident in 2018.  That has led respondent to be concerned that some of the injuries claimed here actually stem from the 2022 incidents.  And even if there is still coverage because the 2024 incident aggravated the injury, respondent argues that it still needs the information.  As to overbreadth, respondent claims that petitioner wrongly puts the burden on it to establish that the records involve the same body parts or the like.  But respondent contends that is not proper because it does not have any details about those incidents—that is why it seeks discovery.  And respondent notes that petitioner has not even alleged that the 2022 information is for different body parts.  In reply, plaintiff notes that the subpoena is not narrowly tailored and that it is respondent’s burden to do so given petitioner’s privacy interests.  Further, petitioner states that the injuries in 2022 had all resolved fully well before 2024. 

 

Respondent ought to narrow the subpoena to the body parts at issue in this case.  But other than putting that limit on the subpoena, the court believes that the subpoena is proper.  Respondent does not need to take petitioner’s word for it that everything was done and over shortly after 2022. 

The court suspects that this might lead to more discovery, but the court will not borrow trouble by opining on that now.  The court also suspects that respondent suspects that there might be more here to explore, but the court will not borrow trouble on that question either.

 

The motion to quash is therefore GRANTED IN PART AND DENIED IN PART in that the subpoena will be limited to claims that involve the same body parts as are now at issue in the instant case.  The sanctions request is DENIED.
