Hamada Abdulla v. Askar Ranjbar, et al.

 

Wednesday, September 16, 2026

 

 

 

CASE NUMBER: 22STCV14401

 

OPPOSED

___________________________________________________________________

 

Cross-Defendant Aleksan Gezalian’s Motion to Quash Service of Summons or Dismiss Claims of Cross-Complainants Askar Ranjbar, Mohamed Jafar, and Mohammad Ranjbar ___________________________________________________________________

 

Facts: This is an action for breach of contract and fraud. The First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) alleges as follows. Plaintiff Hamada Abdulla (“Plaintiff”) entered into an agreement with Defendant Askar Ranjbar to purchase from him the business known as All United Auto Wrecking, Inc. (FAC ¶¶ 2, 7–9.) Askar Ranjbar has repudiated the agreement and, with Defendants Mohammad Ranjbar and Mohamed Jafar, stolen various goods from premises belonging to Plaintiff. (FAC ¶¶ 13–22.) 

Defendants’ Cross-Complaint (“XC”) alleges as follows. Instead of Defendants agreeing to sell their business to Plaintiff, it was Plaintiff who agreed to sell their premises and lease to Defendants. (XC ¶¶ 13–19.) But Plaintiff refused to vacate the  premises, forged documents indicating that Defendant had sold their business to Plaintiff, and  entered Defendants’ premises by deception in order to attempt to sell Defendants’ inventory. (XC ¶¶ 20–32.) The landlord of the property, Cross-Defendant Aleksan Gezalian, has breached the lease by failing to afford Defendants quiet enjoyment of the property. (XC ¶¶ 38–43.)

Procedural History: Plaintiff filed the original Complaint on May 2, 2022, and filed the FAC September 21, 2022, alleging four causes of action:

1.      Breach of Contract

2.      Conversion

3.      Fraud

4.      Declaratory Relief

Defendants filed a Cross-Complaint on November 14, 2022, alleging ten causes of action:

1.      Breach of Contract

2.      Breach of Covenant of Fair Dealing

3.      Conversion

4.      Trespass

5.      Aiding and Abetting

6.      False Promise

7.      Intentional Misrepresentation

8.      Negligent Misrepresentation

9.      Promissory Estoppel

10.  Unjust Enrichment

On March 22, 2023, this court granted Doron F. Eghbali’s motions to be relieved as counsel for Defendants and Cross-Complainants Askar Ranjbar, Mohamed Jafar, Mohammad Ranjbar.

 

On September 2 and 9, 2026, Askar Ranjbar in pro per filed successive cross-complaints, without leave of court.

 

Cross-Defendant Aleksan Gezalian filed the present motion to quash service of summons and to dismiss action on October 13, 2025.

 

No opposition was filed by the July 15, 2026 hearing date. On that date, this court continued the present hearing to require submission of a proof of service with the motion. Said proof of service was filed on July 29, 2026, indicating service on the same date.

 

Cross-Complainants filed an opposition on August 24, 2026.

 

Cross-Defendant Gezalian filed a reply on September 2, 2026.

 

Analysis

I.                   MOTION TO QUASH SERVICE OF SUMMONS OR DISMISS CASE

Code of Civil Procedure section 418.10, subd. (a)(1) states: “A defendant, on or before the last day of his or her time to plead or within any further time that the court may for good cause allow, may serve and file a notice of motion for one or more of the following purposes . . . (1) To quash service of summons on the ground of lack of jurisdiction of the court over him or her.”

“‘[C]ompliance with the statutory procedures for service of process is essential to establish personal jurisdiction.” (Ellard v. Conway (2001) 94 Cal.App.4th 540, 544.) Mere notice of litigation does not confer personal jurisdiction absent substantial compliance with the statutory requirements for service of summons. (MJS Enterprises, Inc. v. Superior Court (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 555, 557.)

 “The court may in its discretion dismiss an action for delay in prosecution pursuant to this article on its own motion or on motion of the defendant if to do so appears to the court appropriate under the circumstances of the case.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 583.410,, subd. (a).) Dismissal may be granted upon the following applicable conditions:

(1) Service is not made within two years after the action is commenced against the defendant.

The action is not brought to trial within the following times:

(A) Three years after the action is commenced against the defendant unless otherwise prescribed by rule under subparagraph (B).

(B) Two years after the action is commenced against the defendant if the Judicial Council by rule adopted pursuant to Section 583.410 so prescribes for the court because of the condition of the court calendar or for other reasons affecting the conduct of litigation or the administration of justice.

(Code Civ. Proc. § 583.420, subd. (a)(1), (2).) The California Rules of Court (“CRC”)permit discretionary dismissal under this latter subdivision “if the action has not been brought to trial or conditionally settled within two years after the action was commenced against the defendant.” (CRC Rule 3.1340, subd. 9a).) The rules prescribe consideration of the following non-exclusive factors:

(1) The court's file in the case and the declarations and supporting data submitted by the parties and, where applicable, the availability of the moving party and other essential parties for service of process;

(2) The diligence in seeking to effect service of process;

(3) The extent to which the parties engaged in any settlement negotiations or discussions;

(4) The diligence of the parties in pursuing discovery or other pretrial proceedings, including any extraordinary relief sought by either party;

(5) The nature and complexity of the case;

(6) The law applicable to the case, including the pendency of other litigation under a common set of facts or determinative of the legal or factual issues in the case;

(7) The nature of any extensions of time or other delay attributable to either party;

(8) The condition of the court's calendar and the availability of an earlier trial date if the matter was ready for trial;

(9) Whether the interests of justice are best served by dismissal or trial of the case; and

(10) Any other fact or circumstance relevant to a fair determination of the issue.

The court must be guided by the policies set forth in Code of Civil Procedure section 583.130.[1]

(CRC Rule 3.1342, subd. (e).)

The above statutes describe the standard for discretionary dismissal of an action for failure to prosecute or serve summons within a reasonable time. But service of summons is required within three years of the filing of a complaint or cross-complaint (Code Civ. Proc. §§ 583.110, subd. (b), 583.210), and failing to serve the summons and complaint within this time renders dismissal mandatory, not discretionary. (Code Civ. Proc. § 583.250, subd. (a).)

Cross-Defendant Aleksan Gezalian (“Gezalian”) moves to quash service of summons upon him of the Cross-Complaint of Defendants Askar Ranjbar, Mohamed Jafar, and Mohammad Ranjbar, on the grounds that no service of the operative cross-complaint, filed on November 14, 2022, has been made. Gezalian argues that the only service that has occurred was of a cross-complaint later filed without leave on September 2 or September 9, 2026, which were filed by Askar Ranjbar in pro per, without indications of serialization. (Motion at pp. 3–6.) Gezalian alternately moves to dismiss the Cross-Complaint against him because service was not effectuated until more than two years after the Cross-Complaint was filed. (Motion at pp. 6–7.)

The court previously continued hearing on the present motion to permit the filing of a proof of service, and in that time, Defendants have filed an opposition, acknowledging that the cross-complaints filed in September 2025 were filed without leave of court, and that the service accomplished upon Gezalian around the same time was defective because no summons accompanied the cross-complaint, which was in any event not the operative cross-complaint in this action. (Opposition at pp. 2–3.) But Defendants nonetheless request that, if service is to be quashed, that they be given an order clarifying the operative pleading and outlining a timetable in which service is to be accomplished, in consideration of their status as self-represented parties. (Opposition at pp. 3–4.)

Defendants acknowledge that an order quashing service of summons is required. Service of summons upon a defendant is mandatory in the absence of a general appearance (Code Civ. Proc. § 410.50, subd. (a)), and the filing of the present motion to quash or dismiss does not constitute a general appearance by Gezalian. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 583.220, subd. (b).) Thus at present, there has been no service of summons, and the attempted service indicated in Defendants’ September 12, 2025 proof of service of summons is properly ordered quashed.

What remains in dispute is whether Defendants’ cross-claims against Gezalian ought to be dismissed, under either the discretionary provisions for failure to serve summons within two years, or mandatory provisions for failure to serve summons within three years. 

Dismissal here is mandatory under Code of Civil Procedure § 583.230 for failure to serve Gezalian within three years of the filing of the cross-complaint. Defendants’ cross-complaint was filed on November 14, 2022, and service of summons was therefore required by November 14, 2025. It is undisputed that no such service has been made.

Defendants in opposition note only that the statutory computation of time excludes periods when “[t]he validity of service was the subject of litigation by the parties,” and thus argue that the running of the three-year period has been tolled since the filing of this motion on October 13, 2025, about a month before the three-year deadline otherwise would have run. (Opposition at p. 4.)

This statute does not benefit Defendants, however, because the validity of service has not been the subject of litigation — i.e. a matter of dispute between the parties. Defendants acknowledge in their opposition that their September 2025 cross-complaints were invalid as being filed without leave of court, and that no valid service of any cross-complaint ever occurred. The invalidity of their service is thus undisputed, and has not been the subject of litigation. What is disputed is only the propriety of dismissal, which is now mandatory under Code of Civil Procedure § 583.230.

Accordingly, the motion to quash is GRANTED, and the motion to dismiss is GRANTED.

Case Number: 25STCP00869    Hearing Date: September 16, 2026    Dept: 732


Kimberly Swartz v. Pacific Global Investment Management Co.

 

Wednesday, September 16, 2026

 

 

 

CASE NUMBER: 25STCP00869

 

OPPOSED

___________________________________________________________________

 

Petitioner Kimberly Swartz, as Trustee of the Third Bruce L. Safman Amended and Restated Liv Tr Dtd 9/23/2016’s Motion to Vacate Trial Date and Confirm Arbitration Award against Respondent Pacific Global Investment Management Co.  ___________________________________________________________________

 

Facts: This is a petition to confirm arbitration award. Petitioner Kimberly Swartz, as Trustee of the Third Bruce L. Safman Amended and Restated Liv Tr Dtd 9/23/2016 (“Petitioner”) seeks to confirm an arbitration award against Respondent Pacific Global Investment Management Co. (“Respondent”) in the amount of $2.25 million, based on claims of breach of fiduciary duty and breach of an investment agreement.

Procedural History: Petitioner filed the petition on March 6, 2025.

Respondent filed a response on March 19, 2025.

Petitioner filed a reply on July 3, 2025.

No hearing date was set for the petition, and at an August 27, 2025, a trial date was set on the petition.

On November 7, 2025, this court denied Petitioner’s ex parte application to vacate trial date and confirm arbitration award.

Petitioner filed the present motion to vacate trial date and confirm arbitration award on January 20, 2026.

Respondent filed an opposition on August 3, 2026.

Petitioner filed a reply on September 8, 2026.

Analysis

I.       MOTION TO CONFIRM ARBITRATION AWARD

“Any party to an arbitration in which an award has been made may petition the court to confirm, correct or vacate the award. The petition shall name as respondents all parties to the arbitration and may name as respondents any other persons bound by the arbitration award.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 1285.) A petition must include (a) the substance of or have attached a copy of the agreement to arbitrate unless the petitioner denies the existence of such an agreement, (b) the names of the arbitrators, and (c) a copy of the award and the written opinion of the arbitrators, if any. (Code Civ. Proc. § 1285.4.)

 

A petition to confirm an arbitration award must be filed and served within four years of the date of the service of the signed copy of the award on the petition, but the petition may not be filed until at least ten days after service of signed copy of the award upon the petitioner. (Code Civ. Proc. §§ 1288, 1288.4.) Service of a petition must be “in the manner provided in the arbitration agreement for the service of such petition and notice.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 1290.4.) If the arbitration agreement does not provide the manner for service, “[s]ervice within this State shall be made in the manner provided by law for the service of summons in an action.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 1290.4, subd. (b)(1).)

 

Petitioner Kimberly Swartz, as Trustee of the Third Bruce L. Safman Amended and Restated Liv Tr Dtd 9/23/2016 (“Petitioner”) seeks to vacate the trial date presently set for February 2, 2027, on her petition to confirm arbitration award against Respondent Pacific Global Investment Management Co. (“Respondent”) in the amount of $2.25 million. Petitioner argues that no trial date is necessary because petitions to confirm arbitration awards are heard in a summary manner, and further that no grounds exist to vacate the ward. (Motion at pp. 3–10.)

 

Petitioner is entitled to have the trial date vacated and a decision rendered on her petition. “A petition under this title shall be heard in a summary way in the manner and upon the notice provided by law for the making and hearing of motions, except that not less than 10 days' notice of the date set for the hearing on the petition shall be given.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 1290.2.) Petitions to confirm arbitration are therefore not generally subject to trial, and the trial date is properly vacated to permit a decision on Petitioner’s motion to confirm.

 

Respondent in opposition argues that the present motion seeks reconsideration of this court’s November 7, 2025 order denying Petitioner’s ex parte application for the same relief. (Opposition at p. 1.) This argument is unpersuasive, as the ex parte application was not denied on the merits, but “pursuant to CRC 3.1202(c),” which rule requires an evidentiary showing of “irreparable harm, immediate danger, or any other statutory basis for granting relief ex parte.” (CRC Rule 3.1202, subd. (c).) The prior order considered only the availability of ex parte relief, and the request made in the present motion has not been considered on the merits.

 

The petition includes the arbitration agreement, the name of the arbitrator, and a copy of the award, and the petition was personally served in the manner provided for summons, in conformity with Code of Civil Procedure §§ 1285.4 and 1290.4.)

 

Respondent in its opposition argues that the arbitration award cannot be confirmed because it is a different entity than that which had the arbitration award entered against it. (Opposition at p. 1.) The basis for the argument is not clear. The petition, agreement, award, and proposed judgment are all in accord that the award to be confirmed is against “Pacific Global Investment Management Company,” not some other entity. In its response to the petition, Respondent argues that “Pacific Global Investment Company, [which was served with the Petition on March 13, 2025], is not a proper party to this proceeding.” (Response at p. 1.) But the argument is a non sequitur, as the petition does not seek any relief against “Pacific Global Investment Company.”

 

In opposition to the present motion, Respondent changes tack, presenting the declaration of Catherine Henning, who states that although she was served with the petition in this matter, she is not an officer of Respondent Pacific Global Investment Management Company, but of non-Respondent Pacific Global Investment Management Co., Inc.” (Henning Decl. ¶ 2, italics added.)  But this claim is not convincing. Henning’s signature appears for Respondent on the arbitration agreement attached to the petition, the arbitrator listed her as an intended recipient of a copy of the award, and she is listed in the award as a witness who testified at the arbitration. (Petition Exhs. A, B.) The Glendale address she gives for her office is the same as the address listed for Respondent on the arbitration agreement. (Henning Decl. ¶ 2; Petition Exh. B.) Respondent offers no evidence except Henning’s unreliable testimony that its legal name is as Henning characterizes it, let alone that a “Respondent, Inc.” exists as a different entity separate from the Respondent named in the petition and which answers it here. The argument is meritless.

 

Respondents next arguments concern grounds for vacating the arbitration award under Code of Civil Procedure § 1286.2.  Courts may vacate an arbitration award under the following circumstances:

 

(1) The award was procured by corruption, fraud or other undue means.

(2) There was corruption in any of the arbitrators.

(3) The rights of the party were substantially prejudiced by misconduct of a neutral arbitrator.

(4) The arbitrators exceeded their powers and the award cannot be corrected without affecting the merits of the decision upon the controversy submitted.

(5) The rights of the party were substantially prejudiced by the refusal of the arbitrators to postpone the hearing upon sufficient cause being shown therefor or by the refusal of the arbitrators to hear evidence material to the controversy or by other conduct of the arbitrators contrary to the provisions of this title.

(6) An arbitrator making the award either: (A) failed to disclose within the time required for disclosure a ground for disqualification of which the arbitrator was then aware; or (B) was subject to disqualification upon grounds specified in Section 1281.91 but failed upon receipt of timely demand to disqualify himself or herself as required by that provision. However, this subdivision does not apply to arbitration proceedings conducted under a collective bargaining agreement between employers and employees or between their respective representatives.

(Code Civ. Proc., § 1286.2, subd. (a)(1)–(6).)

California public policy supports the use of private arbitration to resolve disputes. To promote this alternative means of dispute resolution, the law minimizes judicial intervention in the proceedings, in part, by the doctrine of arbitral finality. Thus, the general rule is that ‘an arbitrator's decision cannot be reviewed for errors of fact or law. Both because it vindicates the intentions of the parties that the award be final, and because an arbitrator is not ordinarily constrained to decide according to the rule of law, it is the general rule that, [t]he merits of the controversy between the parties are not subject to judicial review. More specifically, courts will not review the validity of the arbitrator's reasoning. Further, a court may not review the sufficiency of the evidence supporting an arbitrator's award.”

 

(Jones v. Humanscale Corp. (2005) 130 Cal.App.4th 401, 407–408, internal citations, quotation marks, and alterations omitted.)

In both its response and opposition to the present petition, Respondent offers two grounds for vacating the arbitration award. First, they argue that the arbitrator declined to permit Respondent to conduct voir dire concerning his prior decisions in order to assess bias in the proceedings, which failure to disclose amounts to corruption and misconduct under Code of Civil procedure § 1282.6, subd. (a)(1)–(3) and (4). (Opposition at pp. 1–3; Response at pp. 1, 4.) Respondent also argues that the arbitrator’s decision was legally incorrect, as the arbitrator placed the liability for investment handling decisions upon Respondent, rather than upon Petitioner, who under various provisions of the Probate Code owed a duty to supervise the investments herself. (Opposition at pp. 3–4.)

There is no basis for finding arbitrator corruption, misconduct, or grounds for disqualification. Although Respondent argues that the arbitrator failed to disclose conflicts under Code of Civil Procedure § 1286.2, subd. (a)(6), it offers no evidence that any conflict or other grounds for disqualification actually existed. Rather, Respondent presents a belated email to the arbitrator — sent after arbitration had concluded and the award served upon the parties — which sought, “[g]iven the unexpected adverse award,” disclosure of the arbitrator’s prior decisions and affiliations. (Response Exh. 1.) The response to this belated request was that arbitration had concluded, the arbitrator’s administrative jurisdiction had ended, and that communications to the arbitrator were limited to requests for modification, interpretation, or correction. (Response Exh. 1.) Respondent presents no evidence any conflict actually existed, that information concerning grounds for disqualification was not made available during or prior to the arbitration, or that any request for same was timely made.

Respondent’s arguments concerning legal error in the award offer no grounds for denying Petitioner’s request for confirmation. The contention that the arbitrator failed to take into account Petitioner’s duties under the Probate Code amount only to a contention that the arbitrator committed legal error. “Because courts review neither the award for legal error nor the merits of the controversy itself, a party to an arbitration may not challenge the award for insufficiency of the evidence or flaws in the arbitrator's reasoning.” (Oaktree Capital Management, L.P. v. Bernard (2010) 182 Cal.App.4th 60, 68.)

Respondent finally argues that the failure to account for Petitioner’s duties under the Probate Code violates Code of Civil Procedure § 1283.4, which states that an award “shall include a determination of all the questions submitted to the arbitrators the decision of which is necessary in order to determine the controversy.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 1283.4; Response at pp. 2–3.) But this provision does not require the ultimate arbitration award to expressly address all of the arguments made by the parties to arbitration, but merely requires that any arbitration award resolve all issues pending before the arbitrator before the court enters any order confirming or vacating an award. “If an arbitration “ ‘award’ does not qualify as an award under section 1283.4, then the [trial] court is deprived of jurisdiction to confirm or vacate it.” (Ortiz v. Elmcrest Care Center, LLC (2024) 106 Cal.App.5th 594, 606.) Moreover, it “is for the arbitrators to determine which issues were actually ‘necessary’ to the ultimate decision.” (d. at p. 606.) The arbitrator here clearly determined the award at issue to be a final resolution of all issues between the parties, stating: “This Final Award is in full settlement of all claims submitted to this arbitration.” (Petition Exh. A, Final Arbitration Award, ¶ 13.) No issues remained to be determined to render the award effective, and no relief is therefore appropriate to Respondent under Code of Civil procedure § 1283.4.

Accordingly, Petitioner’s motion to vacate trial date and confirm arbitration award is GRANTED.

Erin O. Guthrie v. 660 Spring Street Owner, LLC, et al.

 

Wednesday, September 16, 2026

 

 

 

CASE NUMBER: 25STCV10146

 

OPPOSED

___________________________________________________________________

 

Plaintiff Erin O. Guthrie’s Motion to Compel Responses to Form Interrogatories, Special Interrogatories, and Requests for Production from Defendant 650 Spring Owner, LLC ___________________________________________________________________

Facts: This is an action for assault, negligence, and premises liability. Plaintiff Erin O. Guthrie (“Plaintiff”) resided in residential property owned and controlled by Defendants 660 S Spring Owner LLC, SBDTLA 1, LLC, SBDTLA 2, LLC, SBDTLA 3, LLC, SBDTLA 4, LLC, FPI Management, Inc., and Avenue 5 Residential Inc. (“Property Defendants”). (Complaint ¶ 21.) Plaintiff was injured in an unprovoked attack by another tenant of the property, Defendant Ron Billups (“Billups”), who thereafter continued to intimidate and assault her in the building’s common areas and near her unit. (Complaint ¶¶ 26–29.) The security guard employed by Property Defendants and Defendant Newton Patrol, Inc. (“NPI”) was present during the attack and did not attempt to deescalate, but rather recorded the attack on his phone. (Complaint ¶ 26.) Property Defendants had advance knowledge of the necessity of security on the premises as well as the unfitness of Defendant NPI for the task. (Complaint ¶¶ 60–61.)

Procedural History: Plaintiff filed the Complaint on April 4, 2026, alleging six causes of action:

1.    Assault

2.    Battery

3.    Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress

4.    Negligent Hiring, Supervision, Training, Retention

5.    Negligence

6.    Premises Liability

On June 10, 2026, this court overruled the demurrer of Defendants SBDTLA 1, LLC, SBDTLA 2, LLC, SBDTLA 3, LLC and SBDTLA 4, LLC.

Plaintiff filed the present motion to compel responses from Defendant 650 Spring Owner on May 27, 2026.

Defendant 650 Spring Owners filed an opposition on September 1, 2026.

Analysis

 

I.                   MOTIONS TO COMPEL

 

A propounding party may demand a responding party to produce documents that are in their possession, custody or control. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.010.) A party may likewise conduct discovery by propounding interrogatories to another party to be answered under oath. (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.010, subd. (a).) The responding party must respond to the production demand either by complying, by representing that the party lacks the ability to comply, or by objecting to the demand. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.210.) The responding party must respond to the interrogatories by answering or objecting. (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.210, subd. (a).) If the responding party fails to serve timely responses, the propounding party may move for an order compelling responses to the production demand and interrogatories. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.290, 2031.300.)

 

Plaintiff Erin O. Guthrie (“Plaintiff”) moves to compel responses to form and special interrogatories and requests for production from Defendant 650 Spring Owner, LLC (“Defendant”). The discovery was served on August 26, 2025. (Chung Decl. ¶ 3.) No responses were timely served. In subsequent meet-and-confer exchanges, Defendant’s counsel indicated that responses had been served by email on November 24, 2025, but (1) Plaintiff’s counsel did not receive these responses on the date indicated; (2) the proof of service provided by Defendant for these responses listed defective email addresses, (3) the responses contained objections that had been waived even by the date of ostensible service, and (4) the responses belatedly given to Plaintiff are not verified. (Chung Decl. ¶¶ 5–8.)

 

Defendant in opposition essentially confirms the above narrative of facts, but claims only that verifications of the discovery at issue are forthcoming. (Opposition at p. 9.) It does not explain why verification were not served in the period between the filing of the motion on May 27, 2026 and the filing of its opposition on September 1, 2026. But in any event, the responses contain objections which have long been waived under Code of Civil Procedure § 2030.290, subd. (a) and 2031.300, subd. (a). Defendant does not contend that the waiver has not occurred, but states only that it reserves the right to bring a motion for relief from waiver, which motion has yet to be filed. (Opposition at pp. 8–9.) Defendant’s reservations of rights and assurance of future performance provide no basis to deny the motion.

 

The motion is GRANTED. Responses without objections shall be served within 20 days of this order. 

 

II.    SANCTIONS

The prevailing party on a motion to compel is generally entitled to monetary sanctions, unless the court “finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (Code Civ. Proc. §§ 2030.290, 2031.300.) Sanctions are also mandatory against a party whose failure to serve responses to requests for admission makes the motion necessary. (Code Civ. Proc. § 2033.280, subd. (c).)

 

Plaintiff seeks $1,560.00 in sanctions, representing two hours of attorney work at $750 per hour, plus a $60 filing fee. (Chung Decl. ¶ 10.)

 

Sanctions are awarded against Defendant and its counsel in the amount of $1,560.00, to be paid within 20 days of this order.

Beauty Rehab LLC v. Lux Beauty, LLC, et al.

 

Wednesday, September 16, 2026

 

 

 

CASE NUMBER: 25STCV30838

 

OPPOSED

___________________________________________________________________

 

Defendants Lux Beauty, LLC, Neal Flowers, and Kate Jung’s Motion to Quash Service of Summons and Stay or Dismiss Case for Forum Non Conveniens ___________________________________________________________________

 

Facts: This is an action for breach of contract and fraud. The Complaint alleges as follows. Plaintiffs Beauty Rehab, LLC, Keith H. Harris, and Chad G. Hett (“Plaintiffs”) entered into an agreement with Defendants Lux Beauty, LLC, Neal Flowers, and Kate Jung (“Defendants”) whereby the latter agreed to purchase the assets of Plaintiff Beauty Rehab for $105,000, consisting of one large initial payment followed by eleven monthly installments. (Complaint ¶¶ 2–4.) Defendants entered into the contract with no intention of performing, and despite acquiring the agreed assets, have failed to make any payment. (Complaint ¶¶ 6–7.) 

Procedural History: Plaintiffs filed the Complaint on October 22, 2025, alleging two causes of action:

1.      Breach of Contract

2.      Fraud

Defendants filed the present motion to quash and forum non coveniens motion on January 8, 2026.

Plaintiffs filed an opposition on August 31, 2026.

Defendants filed a reply on September 9, 2026.

Analysis:

I.                   MOTION TO QUASH SERVICE OF SUMMONS

Code of Civil Procedure section 418.10, subd. (a)(1) states: “A defendant, on or before the last day of his or her time to plead or within any further time that the court may for good cause allow, may serve and file a notice of motion for one or more of the following purposes . . . (1) To quash service of summons on the ground of lack of jurisdiction of the court over him or her.”

A defendant may serve and file a motion to quash service of summons on the grounds of a lack of jurisdiction over him or her. (Code Civ. Proc., § 418.10 subd. (a)(1).) A plaintiff opposing a motion to quash service for lack of personal jurisdiction “has the initial burden to demonstrate facts establishing a basis for personal jurisdiction.” (HealthMarkets, Inc. v. Superior Court (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1160, 1167.) If satisfied, the burden then shifts to defendant to show that exercise of jurisdiction would be unreasonable. (Id.)

A court may exercise jurisdiction on any basis not inconsistent with the Constitution of California or of the United States. (Code Civ. Proc., § 410.10.) The exercise of personal jurisdiction is constitutionally permissible only “if the defendant has sufficient ‘minimum contacts’ with the forum state so that the exercise of jurisdiction ‘does not offend “traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.” [Citations.]’ ” (HealthMarkets, Inc. v. Superior Court (“HealthMarkets, Inc.”) (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1160, 1166 [citing International Shoe Co. v. Washington (1945) 326 U.S. 310, 316].) A defendant’s conduct with the forum state must be such that the defendant has “fair warning” that its activities might subject it to personal jurisdiction. (HealthMarkets, Inc., supra, 171 Cal.App.4th at p. 1166–1167; Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz (1985) 471 U.S. 462, 472.)

“Minimum contacts exist where the defendant's conduct in the forum state is such that he should reasonably anticipate being subject to suit there, and it is reasonable and fair to force him to do so. [Citations.] In contrast, contacts that are random, fortuitous, or attenuated do not rise to the minimum level, and general jurisdiction cannot be exercised under these circumstances.” (F. Hoffman-La Roche, Inc. v. Superior Court (“F. Hoffman-La Roche”) (2005) 130 Cal.App.4th 782, 795.)

A defendant that has substantial, continuous, and systematic contacts with the forum state is subject to general jurisdiction in the state, meaning jurisdiction on any cause of action. Absent such extensive contacts, a defendant may be subject to specific jurisdiction, meaning jurisdiction in an action arising out of or related to the defendant's contacts with the forum state. Specific jurisdiction depends on the quality and nature of the defendant's forum contacts in relation to the particular cause of action alleged.

 

(HealthMarkets, Inc. v. Superior Court (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1160, 1167.)

 

In determining whether specific jurisdiction exists over a given defendant, “the appropriate inquiry is whether the plaintiff's cause of action “arises out of or has a substantial connection with a business relationship defendant has purposefully established with California.” (Vons Companies, Inc. v. Seabest Foods, Inc. (1996) 14 Cal.4th 434, 448.) “[A]s the relationship of the defendant with the state seeking to exercise jurisdiction over him grows more tenuous, the scope of jurisdiction also retracts, and fairness is assured by limiting the circumstances under which the plaintiff can compel him to appear and defend. The crucial inquiry concerns the character of defendant's activity in the forum, whether the cause of action arises out of or has a substantial connection with that activity, and upon the balancing of the convenience of the parties and the interests of the state in assuming jurisdiction.” (Ibid.)

 

Defendants Lux Beauty, LLC, Neal Flowers, and Kate Jung (“Defendants”) move to quash service of summons upon them on the grounds that they are a Nevada corporate entity and Nevada residents who are being sued over a contract to purchase the assets of Plaintiff Beauty Rehab, LLC, another Nevada corporation, and as such never purposefully availed themselves of the benefits of California sufficient to create general or specific jurisdiction in this state. (Motion at pp. 5–9.)

 

Plaintiffs Beauty Rehab, LLC,  Keith H. Harris, and Chad G. Hett (“Plaintiffs”) present evidence to show that Defendants purposefully availed themselves of the benefits of dealing with the forum state sufficient to create specific jurisdiction in California. Although this matter concerns a contract for the purchase by Nevada-based parties of the assets of a Nevada corporation (Flowers Decl. Exh. A), Plaintiffs Keith Harris and Chad Hett are also parties to the agreement in their individual capacities, and both are California residents. (Complaint ¶¶ 10–11; Harris Decl. ¶ 2.)) Plaintiff Harris testifies that it was Defendants who approached Plaintiffs with the proposed transaction and negotiated with the California-based Plaintiffs by telephone and email. (Harris Decl. ¶¶ 3–4.) Moreover, the terms of the agreement contemplate not a one-off transaction, but a series of installment payments to be made to Plaintiffs over the course of a year. (Harris Decl. ¶ 5.) Harris states that Defendants understood that the individual Plaintiffs resided in California, and that the payments were to be directed to Plaintiffs there. (Harris Decl. ¶ 4.)

 

The present case is like Hall v. LaRonde (1997) 56 Cal.App.4th 1342, 1347, in which the court found purposeful availment where a California resident and a New York resident electronically negotiated and executed a software provision agreement whereby the Californian agreed to provide continuing support and service in exchange for continuing royalty payments from New York to California, thus creating a “continuing obligation between himself and a resident of California.” (Hall, supra, 56 Cal.App.4th at p. 1347, internal quotation marks and alterations omitted.) Defendants’ continuing obligation to pay installment payments over eleven months to California residents is analogous, and moreover, Plaintiffs present evidence that Defendants specifically solicited their business and initiated the transaction at issue.

 

Also analogous is the case of  Luberski, Inc. v. Oleificio F.LLI Amato S.R.L. (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 409, 419, in which the court found purposeful availment where an Italian vender (“Amato”) was alleged to have breached a contract to deliver olive oil to California. The court stated: “Amato received the purchase order and was presented with the option of completing a substantial transaction with a California entity in California, which required delivery of goods to California. Amato opted to accept this order. The totality of facts supports a finding that Amato purposefully availed itself of the benefits of the forum.” (Id. at p. 419.) Defendants here similarly entered into a substantial transaction with California residents with the understanding that they were required to send payment to California residents over a continuing duration. 

 

Defendants argue that fair play and substantial justice require adjudicating the matter in Nevada, where they are residents. (Motion at pp. 10–11.) Yet they submit no concrete evidence of prejudice to be suffered by litigation here, save conclusory statements offered in their declarations. While Plaintiffs’ objections to these declarations are OVERRULED, the statements themselves are not persuasive. And while Defendants make arguments arising from the Nevada forum selection clause in the operative agreement, the existence of this agreement bears upon the separate matter of its enforceability, not this court’s possession of the constitutional prerequisites for personal jurisdiction over Defendants.  The forum selection clause shall be addressed below, in connection with Defendants’ motion to enforce that agreement.   

 

II.                INCONVENIENT FORUM

 “When a court upon motion of a party or its own motion finds that in the interest of substantial justice an action should be heard in a forum outside this state, the court shall stay or dismiss the action in whole or in part on any conditions that may be just.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 410.30, subd. (a).)

In determining whether to grant a motion based on forum non conveniens, a court must first determine whether the alternate forum is a “suitable” place for trial. If it is, the next step is to consider the private interests of the litigants and the interests of the public in retaining the action for trial in California. The private interest factors are those that make trial and the enforceability of the ensuing judgment expeditious and relatively inexpensive, such as the ease of access to sources of proof, the cost of obtaining attendance of witnesses, and the availability of compulsory process for attendance of unwilling witnesses. The public interest factors include avoidance of overburdening local courts with congested calendars, protecting the interests of potential jurors so that they are not called upon to decide cases in which the local community has little concern, and weighing the competing interests of California and the alternate jurisdiction in the litigation.

(Stangvik v. Shiley Inc. (1991) 54 Cal.3d 744, 751.) The party moving for forum non conveniens bears the initial burden of establishing the propriety of dismissal. (Ibid.) “If the plaintiff is a California resident, the ‘plaintiff's choice of a forum should rarely be disturbed unless the balance is strongly in favor of the defendant.” (National Football League v. Fireman’s Fund Insurance Company (2013) 216 Cal.App.4th 902, 917.)

However, Defendants move for stay or dismissal based on the operation of a forum selection clause. (Motion at pp. 11–12.) This involves a different analysis:

The law in this area is well-established. In California, the procedure for enforcing a forum selection clause is a motion to stay or dismiss for forum non conveniens, but a motion based on a forum selection clause is a special type of forum non conveniens motion. The factors that apply generally to a forum non conveniens motion do not control in a case involving a mandatory forum selection clause. When a case involves a mandatory forum selection clause, it will usually be given effect unless it is unfair or unreasonable. Moreover, a court will normally reject any claims that the chosen forum is unfair or inconvenient. Also, a court will usually honor a mandatory forum selection clause without extensive analysis of factors.

(Richtek USA, Inc. v. uPI Semiconductor Corporation (2015) 242 Cal.App.4th 651, 661.)

Defendants here cite the following language from the contract that Plaintiffs seek to enforce:

Each party irrevocably submits to the exclusive jurisdiction of the federal and state courts of Clark County, Nevada, for the purposes of any action, proceeding, suit, or claim arising out of this Agreement. Each party agrees to commence any such action, proceeding, suit, or claim in the state or federal courts of Clark County., Nevada. Each party irrevocably and unconditionally waives any objection to the laying of venue of any action, proceeding, suit, or claim arising out of this Agreement or the transaction contemplated hereby in the state or federal courts of Clark County, Nevada, and hereby further irrevocably and unconditionally, waives and agrees not to plead or claim in any such court that any such action, proceeding, suit, or claim brought in any such court has been brought in an inconvenient forum.

(Flowers Decl. Exh. A, ¶ 7.14.) The agreement also includes a choice-of-law provision requiring application of Nevada law. (Id. at ¶ 7.13.)

The forum selection clause applies here. The clause includes not merely a consent to jurisdiction in Clark County, but states that this jurisdiction shall be exclusive and mandatory. The clause applies to any claims “arising out of this agreement.” (Flowers Decl. Exh. A, ¶ 7.14.) Plaintiffs claims are for breach of the agreement, and promissory fraud, based on the contention that the promises of the agreement were made without intent to perform, and that Plaintiff was damaged by rendering the consideration contemplated by the agreement. (Complaint ¶¶ 25–27.) The claims here arise out of the agreement, and thus fall within the ambit of the forum selection clause.

Plaintiffs in opposition argue that Defendants should have brought their inconvenient forum motion as a separate motion from their motion to quash. (Opposition at pp. 11–12.) They cite no authority for this proposition save the fact that such a motion is authorized under Code of Civil Procedure § 418.10, subd. (a)(2), while a motion to quash is governed by Code of Civil Procedure § 418.10, subd. (a)(1). (Opposition at pp. 11–12.) But these separate subdivisions do not indicate that a motion made under one subdivision cannot include a request for relief under the other, as Defendants do here. Indeed, the statute expressly permits a motion to be brought “for one or more of the following purposes,” including to quash service of summons or to raise objections based on inconvenient forum. (Code Civ. Proc. § 418.10, subd. (a).)

Plaintiffs briefly argue that their fraud claim does not arise out of the agreement, and is not subject to the forum selection clause. (Opposition at p. 12.) But this argument is unconvincing. The fraud that Plaintiffs allege is that they were induced to execute the agreement and deliver their company’s assets because of the promises made in the agreement to pay a certain price. No characterization of the facts permits the conclusion that their fraud claim does not arise from the agreement.

This matter is therefore properly stayed pending resolution of the matter in the forum contemplated by the agreement. Although Defendants seek alternately dismissal or stay of the present action, dismissal here would be improper because an “action brought by a California resident may not be dismissed on grounds of forum non conveniens except in extraordinary circumstances.”  (Century Indemnity Co. v. Bank of America (1997) 58 Cal.App.4th 408, 411.) “The trial court, however, has considerably wider discretion to grant stays precisely because under a stay California retains jurisdiction.” (Ibid.)

Accordingly, the motion to stay this matter on forum non conveniens grounds is GRANTED.

CASE NUMBER: 26STCV10813

 

OPPOSED

___________________________________________________________________

 

Defendant Fast Home Services, LLC’s Motion to Compel Arbitration ___________________________________________________________________

 

Facts: This is an action for unpaid wages and retaliatory discharge. The Complaint alleges as follows. Plaintiff  Richard Hikal Sweeting III (“Plaintiff”) was discharged from his employment with Defendant Fast Home Services, LLC (“Defendant”) shortly after raising complaints about the non-payment of legally owed commissions and seeking to review the contract pursuant to which they were promised. (Complaint ¶¶ 12–14.)

 

Procedural History: Plaintiff filed the Complaint on April 3, 2026, alleging three causes of action:

 

1.      Labor Code § 98.6

2.      Labor Code § 1102.5

3.      Wrongful Termination

Defendant filed the present motion to compel arbitration on May 19, 2026.

 

Plaintiff filed an opposition on April 3, 2026.

 

Defendant filed a reply on September 2, 2026.

 

Analysis:

I.                   MOTION TO COMPEL ARBITRATION

On petition of a party to an arbitration agreement to arbitrate a controversy, a court must order the petitioner and respondent to arbitrate the controversy if it determines the arbitration agreement exists, unless (1) the petitioner has waived its right to arbitrate; (2) grounds exist for the revocation of the agreement; or (3) “[a] party to the arbitration agreement is also a party to a pending court action or special proceeding with a third party, arising out of the same transaction or series of related transactions and there is a possibility of conflicting rulings on a common issue of law or fact.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2.)

 

“[T]he party moving to compel arbitration bears the burden of establishing the existence of a preponderance of the evidence any fact necessary to its defense. The role of the trial court is to sit as a trier of fact, weighing any affidavits, declarations, and other documentary evidence, together with oral testimony received at the court's discretion, to reach a determination on the issue of arbitrability.” (Hotels Nevada v. L.A. Pacific Center, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 754, 758.)

 

Defendant Fast Home Services, LLC (“Defendant”) moves to compel arbitration of Plaintiff Richard Mikal Sweetings III’s (“Plaintiff”) claims based on an arbitration agreement executed by Plaintiff on May 24, 2026. That agreement requires arbitration of all claims “relating to the Employee's employment relationship with the Company and/or the termination of the Employee's employment relationship with the Company, to the extent permitted by law.” (McGavran Decl. Exh. 2.)

 

Plaintiff in opposition argues that the arbitration agreement is unconscionable for, among other things, lack of mutuality, once it is considered in connection with a concurrently executed confidentiality, non-solicitation, and non-disparagement agreement omitted from Defendant’s motion. (Opposition at pp. 8–20.)

 

Consideration of the concurrently executed confidentiality agreement is proper when addressing the enforceability of the arbitration agreement here sought to be enforced. “Several contracts relating to the same matters, between the same parties, and made as parts of substantially one transaction, are to be taken together.” (Civ. Code § 1642.) The agreements here were delivered to Plaintiff in the same Docusign envelope and executed concurrently. (Sweeting Decl. ¶¶ 9–13; Pletcher Decl. ¶ 4.) Although Defendant argues that the agreements ought not to be considered together in the absence of express incorporation (Reply at pp. 7–8), binding authority suggests consideration of the concurrently executed employment documents together is proper in order to assess the enforceability of the arbitration agreement, regardless of whether the agreements expressly reference or incorporate each other. (See Fuentes v. Empire Nissan, Inc. (2026) 19 Cal.5th 93, 108.)

 

“Unconscionability requires a showing of both procedural unconscionability and substantive unconscionability.” (Ajamian v. CantorCO2e, L.P. (2012) 203 Cal.App.4th 771, 795.) The oppression that creates procedural unconscionability arises from an inequality of bargaining power that results in no real negotiation and an absence of meaningful choice.” (Grand Prospect Partners, L.P. v. Ross Dress for Less, Inc. (2015) 232 Cal.App.4th 1332, 1347–1348.) “Substantive unconscionability is not susceptible of precise definition. It appears the various descriptions—unduly oppressive, overly harsh, so one-sided as to shock the conscience, and unreasonably favorable to the more powerful party—all reflect the same standard. Substantive unconscionability is not concerned with a simple old-fashioned bad bargain.” (Id. at p. 1349, citations omitted.)

Here, Plaintiff presents a basis for finding a high degree of procedural unconscionability, because arbitration contracts presented to employees on a take-it-or-leave-it basis are at least minimally procedurally unconscionable. (See Armendariz v. Foundation Health Psychcare Services, Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 83, 113.) Here, not only were the agreements presented in such a fashion, but they were sent to Plaintiff in the middle of his workday, with express instructions that he could not take further jobs until they were signed, forcing Plaintiff to choose either immediate signature, essentially without review, or to take the time to review the documents, and lose work in the meantime. (Sweeting Decl. ¶¶  8–9.) Thus the element of procedural unconscionability is met here.

Plaintiff also demonstrates a high degree of substantive unconscionability arising from a lack of mutuality. Although the arbitration agreement is outwardly mutual in its requirement of arbitration, the concurrently executed confidentiality agreement indicates that Defendant retained for itself the prerogative of bringing an action in court to enforce  its confidentiality, non-solicitation, and non-disparagement agreement. While the arbitration agreement expressly applies to claims for “breach of contract” (McGavran Decl. Exh. 2), the confidentiality agreement does not appear to contemplate arbitration of claims to enforce its provisions. It makes no mention of arbitration, and includes special provision for attorney fees incurred in “litigation, including upon appeal and in bankruptcy proceedings.” (Pletcher Decl. Exh. 4, ¶ 6.) It includes severability clauses specifically referring to determinations by a “court” or “court of competent jurisdiction.” (Id. at ¶¶ 3(e), 5.) Similar textual indicators contained in confidentiality agreements, executed concurrently with an arbitration agreement but themselves lacking any provision for arbitration, have been held to suggest that the confidentiality claims were intended to be excluded from arbitration: “The confidentiality agreements’ silence about the forum for dispute resolution therefore indicates that the parties did not intend disputes to be subject to mandatory arbitration.” (Fuentes v. Empire Nissan, Inc. (2026) 19 Cal.5th 93, 109.) The agreements together thus create a broad exception to arbitration in favor of the employer’s claims under the confidentiality agreement, evidencing a lack of mutuality.

The confidentiality agreement also permits Defendant to obtain equitable remedies upon evidentiary presumptions and procedural advantages not extended to its employees.  The confidentiality agreement contains a provision creating a presumption in Defendant’s favor that violation of the agreement creates irreparable harm, thus absolving it of the necessity of proving damages, and further states that no bond need be posted in the pursuit of injunctive relief. (Pletcher Decl. Exh. 4, ¶ 6.) .) Such a provision is one-sided in Defendant’s favor and unconscionable.  (See Alberto v. Cambrian Homecare (2023) 91 Cal.App.5th 482, 492 [“[A]dditional provisions that waive the employer's need to obtain a bond before seeking an injunction, waive the employer's need to show irreparable harm, and require an employee to consent to an immediate injunction are unconscionable. They exceed the legitimate ‘margin of safety’ for the employer and are not mutual.”].)

The arbitration agreement itself also contains substantively unconscionable provisions. It permits the prevailing party in any action “to compel arbitration” to their “reasonable attorney fees and costs.” (McGavran Decl. Exh. 2, ¶ 12.) The clause thus threatens employees who oppose arbitration of their employment-related claims with penalties that contradict the statutory apportionment of fees for certain statutory claims, including that of Labor Code § 1102.5, as Plaintiff brings here. (Lab. Code § 1102.5, subd. (j).) Similar clauses which violate “asymmetric rule[s] regarding awards of costs and fees” mandated by statute have been held unconscionable, as they impose fees upon plaintiffs bringing unwaivable statutory claims greater than they would otherwise have to bear in the absence of an agreement to arbitrate.  (Ramirez v. Charter Communications, Inc. (2024) 16 Cal.5th 478, 507.)

The arbitration agreement also contains an unconscionable waiver of Plaintiff’s right to bring non-individual PAGA claims. (McGavran Decl. Exh. 2, ¶ 5.) Representative PAGA claims are not arbitrable, and the arbitration of individual PAGA claims does not deprive the employee of standing to pursue representative PAGA claims in court. (See Adolph v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (2023) 14 Cal.5th 1104, 1123.) The waiver of representative PAGA claims is thus another substantively unconscionable provision.

Notwithstanding the severability clauses in the confidentiality and arbitration agreements, the above  provisions permeate the arbitration agreement with unconscionability, and demonstrate an intent by Defendant to funnel employee claims into arbitration, while arrogating to itself the privilege of bringing actions in court, subject to favorable procedures and evidentiary presumptions not available to employees like Plaintiff. As such, the offending provisions are not severable, and the arbitration agreement is not enforceable  (See Carmona v. Lincoln Millennium Car Wash, Inc. (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 74, 90.)

The motion is therefore DENIED.
