MOTION TO AUGMENT EXPERT DESIGNATION 

(CCP §§ 2024.050, 2034.610)

 

TENTATIVE RULING:                                                                       

 

Defendant Alpha Construction Co., Inc.’s Motion to Reopen Discovery and to Augment Expert Designation is DENIED.

 

Moving party is ordered to give notice.

ANALYSIS:

 

I.                Background

 

On October 4, 2022, Plaintiff Aryand Arshadi (“Plaintiff”) filed the instant action against Defendants Alpha Construction Co., Inc. (“Alpha Construction”); McCadden Plaza Tay Housing, LP (“McCadden Plaza”); and Los Angeles LGBT Center (“LA LGBT Center”) (“Defendants”) and Does 1-100, alleging causes of action for Negligence; Premises Liability; Negligent Hiring, Supervision, Training and/or Retention; and Negligence Per Se. (Compl., p. 1.)

 

On March 1, 2023, Defendants filed Answers.

 

On August 13, 2026, Alpha Construction filed the instant Motion to Reopen Discovery and to Augment Expert Designation (the “Motion”).

 

On August 26, 2026, Plaintiff filed an Opposition.

 

On September 1, 2026, Alpha Construction filed a Reply.

 

II.              Legal Standard

 

Reopening of Discovery

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2024.050 states:

 

On motion of any party, the court may grant leave … to reopen discovery after a new trial date has been set. This motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.

(b) In exercising its discretion to grant or deny this motion, the court shall take into consideration any matter relevant to the leave requested, including, but not limited to, the following:

(1) The necessity and the reasons for the discovery.

(2) The diligence or lack of diligence of the party seeking the discovery or the hearing of a discovery motion, and the reasons that the discovery was not completed or that the discovery motion was not heard earlier.

(3) Any likelihood that permitting the discovery or hearing the discovery motion will prevent the case from going to trial on the date set, or otherwise interfere with the trial calendar, or result in prejudice to any other party.

(4) The length of time that has elapsed between any date previously set, and the date presently set, for the trial of the action.

(c) The court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to extend or to reopen discovery, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.

 

 (Code Civ. Proc., § 2024.050.)

 

Expert Designation Augmentation

 

“On motion of any party who has engaged in a timely exchange of expert witness information, the court may grant leave to . . . (1) Augment that party’s expert witness list and declaration by adding the name and address of any expert witness whom that party has subsequently retained.” (Civ. Proc. Code, § 2034.610, subd. (a)(1).) A motion under this subdivision “shall be made a sufficient time in advance of the time limit for the completion of discovery . . . to permit the deposition of any expert to whom the motion relates . . . .” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2034.610, subd. (b)(1).) Finally, “[t]he motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2034.610, subd. (c).)

 

Further:

 

The court shall grant leave to augment or amend an expert witness list or declaration only if all of the following conditions are satisfied:

(a) The court has taken into account the extent to which the opposing party has relied on the list of expert witnesses.

(b) The court has determined that any party opposing the motion will not be prejudiced in maintaining that party’s action or defense on the merits.

(c) The court has determined either of the following:

(1) The moving party would not in the exercise of reasonable diligence have determined to call that expert witness or have decided to offer the different or additional testimony of that expert witness.

(2) The moving party failed to determine to call that expert witness, or to offer the different or additional testimony of that expert witness as a result of mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect, and the moving party has done both of the following:

(A) Sought leave to augment or amend promptly after deciding to call the expert witness or to offer the different or additional testimony.

(B) Promptly thereafter served a copy of the proposed expert witness information concerning the expert or the testimony described in Section 2034.260 on all other parties who have appeared in the action.

(d) Leave to augment or amend is conditioned on the moving party making the expert available immediately for a deposition under Article 3 (commencing with Section 2034.410), and on any other terms as may be just….

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 2034.620.)

 

Finally, Code of Civil Procedure section 2034.630 states that “[t]he court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to augment or amend expert witness information, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2034.630.)

 

Sanctions

 

“A request for a sanction shall, in the notice of motion, identify every person, party, and attorney against whom the sanction is sought, and specify the type of sanction sought. The notice of motion shall be supported by a memorandum of points and authorities, and accompanied by a declaration setting forth facts supporting the amount of any monetary sanction sought.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.040.)

 

III.            Discussion

 

Alpha Construction moves for an order to reopen discovery and serve supplemental written discovery requests upon Plaintiff and to allow a second deposition of Plaintiff on the grounds that it has been nearly two years since fact discovery closed, and “Defendants must have access to the past two years of Plaintiff’s medical history to learn whether his condition has improved or worsened, and to verify the extent to which he has sought medical treatment.”

 

Alpha Construction also moves for an order permitting Alpha Construction to designate a retained expert neuropsychologist after Alpha Construction “electively de-designated neuropsychology expert Dr. Schaeffer” in October 2024 “based upon representations from opposing counsel that their own neuropsychologist, Dr. Tervo, had not conducted an IME of Plaintiff and had not formed any opinions. Yet after the close of expert discovery, and after trial was continued, Plaintiff’s counsel … arranged an IME of Plaintiff, conducted by Dr. Tervo on October 30, 2024. Although Dr. Tervo submitted to a deposition in 2025, Defendants had already been deprived of an opportunity to designate an expert in that field due to Plaintiff’s discovery gamesmanship.”

 

The Court has reviewed all evidence and briefing filed by the parties and determines that the moving party has not met its burden of proof for relief requested.

 

First, the Court finds there is no mandatory meet and confer declaration accompanying the Motion as required by Code of Civil Procedure sections 2024.050 and 2034.610.

 

Next, the Court does not find Alpha Construction has demonstrated necessity for reopening written discovery to allow additional interrogatories and requests for production to be propounded on Plaintiff, and Alpha Construction has shown a lack of diligence in making this request. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2024.050.) Indeed, the parties do not dispute that the parties had an understanding that formal fact discovery closed on or about October 12, 2024 (see Motion; Opp.; Reply); however, Alpha Construction fails to set forth any evidence demonstrating why, for nearly two years, Alpha Construction failed to move to reopen discovery and/or to obtain updated medical information on Plaintiff until the instant Motion. This is despite the fact that the moving party appeared before the Court on several occasions yet did not raise this very issue at any time. Further, Plaintiff provides undisputed evidence that the parties were informally engaging in discovery in 2025 beyond the formal discovery cut-off deadline (see e.g., Opp., Farahi Decl., Exh. O) and yet Alpha Construction failed to request additional written discovery.

 

While the Court acknowledges the Motion states “the reason that this motion was not made earlier was because trial was only continued for a few months at a time, for each continuance, and the motion did not become necessary until the trial date was pushed so far out that the evidence obtained during discovery would be 2+ years old at the time of trial” (Motion, p. 8), the Court does not find this unsupported rationale to be persuasive or sufficient to demonstrate diligence in the circumstances of this action.

 

Furthermore, the Court finds the Motion fails to demonstrate specific topics and/or questions that would be posed in additional written discovery thereby making it difficult for the Court to ascertain necessity for reopening written discovery.

 

Third, the Court finds Alpha Construction’s cursory request in the Notice of Motion to depose Plaintiff for a second time is legally unsupported in the Motion. Indeed, there is no legal authority included in the Motion at all to support this request. While the Court acknowledges that Alpha Construction raises Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.610 for the first time in the Reply brief, the Court does not find Alpha Construction has demonstrated good cause under that code provision for an additional deposition of Plaintiff, especially since the evidence presented by Plaintiff shows that “Plaintiff has already sat for five examinations and depositions in this case: his deposition on October 18, 2023, examinations by Dr. Ludwig on April 25, 2024, Dr. Macyszyn on June 18, 2024, and Dr. Schaeffer on August 5, 2024, and Dr. Tervo's five-and-a-half-hour evaluation on October 30, 2024. (Exs. A, B, C, I; Ex. H, 20:3-5; Farahi Decl., ¶ 12.)” (Opp., p. 13.) Further, Alpha Construction has not demonstrated there has been a significant change in Plaintiff’s circumstances since the prior depositions/examinations to warrant an additional deposition. Indeed, Alpha Construction merely states that during the three years since Plaintiff’s deposition, “Plaintiff’s circumstances may have changed.” (Reply, p. 8, emphasis added.)

 

The Court also finds it notable here that there is no evidence before this Court that Alpha Construction attempted to meet and confer and/or stipulate with Plaintiff for additional written discovery and/or a second deposition of Plaintiff limited solely to updates in Plaintiff’s medical condition and/or medical history since 2023.

 

Finally, the Court does not find that Alpha Construction satisfied the requirements of Code of Civil Procedure section 2034.620 for augmenting Alpha Construction’s expert designation list. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2034.610, 2034.620.) Specifically, Alpha Construction fails to show either that Apha Construction “would not in the exercise of reasonable diligence have determined to call that expert witness or have decided to offer the different or additional testimony of that expert witness” or “failed to determine to call that expert witness, or to offer the different or additional testimony of that expert witness as a result of mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2034.620, subd. (c)(1)-(2).)

 

Further, to the extent Alpha Construction contends its lack of neuropsychology expert is due to mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect, Alpha Construction has not demonstrated it has done both of the following: “Sought leave to augment or amend promptly after deciding to call the expert witness or to offer the different or additional testimony” and “Promptly thereafter served a copy of the proposed expert witness information concerning the expert or the testimony described in Section 2034.260 on all other parties who have appeared in the action.” (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2034.620, subd. (c)(2)(A)-(B).)

 

Indeed, Alpha Construction states it “de-designated neuropsychology expert, Dr. Schaeffer” on October 11, 2024, but then did not file the instant Motion until August 13, 2026. (See Motion, pp. 6-7; Code Civ. Proc., § 2034.620, subd. (c)(2)(A).) Further, Alpha Construction fails to provide any mention, let alone evidence, of a copy of the proposed expert witness information that has also been served on all parties who have appeared to the action. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2034.620, subd. (c)(2)(B).)

 

            For all these reasons, the Motion is DENIED.

 

As to sanctions, the Court finds the Notice of Opposition fails to comply with Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.040. Thus, Plaintiff’s request for sanctions is denied.

 

IV.            Conclusion & Order

 

For the foregoing reasons, Defendant Alpha Construction Co., Inc.’s Motion to Reopen Discovery and to Augment Expert Designation is DENIED.

 

Moving party is ordered to give notice.

ANALYSIS:

 

I.                Background

 

On February 10, 2026, Plaintiff Elizabeth Mora, by and through her guardian ad litem, Robert Mora (“Plaintiff”) filed the instant action against Defendant Cedars-Sinai Meical Center (“Defendant”) and Does 1-40.

On June 18, 2026, Plaintiff filed a First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) against Defendant and Does 1-40, alleging causes of action for Negligence, and Reckless and Willful Neglect in Violation of the Elder Abuse and Dependent Adult Civil Protection Act.

 

On July 2, 2026, Robert Mora substituted in as Plaintiff in this action in place of deceased Plaintiff Elizabeth Mora (“Plaintiff”).

 

On July 20, 2026, Defendant filed the instant Motion to Strike Portions of Plaintiff’s FAC (the “Motion”).

 

On August 26, 2026, Plaintiff filed an Opposition.

 

On September 1, 2026, Defendant filed a Reply.

 

II.              Legal Standard

 

California law authorizes a party’s motion to strike matter from an opposing party’s pleading if it is irrelevant, false, or improper. (Code Civ. Proc. §§ 435, 436.) Motions may also target pleadings or parts of pleadings that are not filed or drawn in conformity with applicable laws, rules, or orders. (Code Civ. Proc. § 436, subd. (b).)

 

The Code of Civil Procedure also authorizes the Court to act on its own initiative to strike matters, empowering the Court to enter orders striking matter “at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 436.)

 

Finally, Code of Civil Procedure section 435.5 requires that “[b]efore filing a motion to strike pursuant to this chapter, the moving party shall meet and confer in person or by telephone with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to the motion to strike for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that resolves the objections to be raised in the motion to strike.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 435.5, subd. (a), emphasis added.)

 

Punitive damages are authorized by Civil Code section 3294 in non-contract cases “where the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice, express or implied . . . .”  (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (a).) Malice means conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others. (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (c)(1).) Oppression means despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person’s rights. (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (c)(2).) Fraud means an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known to the defendant with the intention on the party of the defendant of thereby depriving a person of property or legal rights or otherwise causing injury. (Civil Code, § 3294, subd. (c)(3).)

 

III.            Discussion

 

Defendant moves to strike the FAC’s claim for punitive damages. (Motion, pp. 3-7.) Defendant contends that that the FAC fails to establish oppression, fraud, or malice, and Plaintiff failed to obtain a court order pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 425.13 before including a claim for punitive damages against Defendant, which is a healthcare provider. (Motion, pp. 4-5.)

 

In Opposition, Plaintiff contends the Motion should be denied on the grounds that the FAC adequately alleges facts to support a punitive damages request, and Code of Civil Procedure section 425.13 does not apply to claims arising under the Elder Abuse and Dependent Adult Civil Protection Act. (Opp., pp. 2-8.)

 

In Reply, Defendant only addresses and emphasizes the argument that the FAC contains insufficient facts to support a punitive damages request and does not address the argument raised in the Motion regarding Code of Civil Procedure section 425.13. (Reply, pp. 2-3.)

 

            Meet and Confer

 

As an initial matter, Defendant submits a meet and confer declaration in support of the Motion that states the parties’ counsels had two telephone calls regarding the issues raised in the Motion to no avail. (Motion, Gonzalez Decl.)

 

Thus, the Court finds the meet and confer requirement is satisfied. (Code Civ. Proc. § 435.5, subd. (a).)

 

Punitive Damages

 

            As to Defendant’s contention that the FAC failed to comply with Code of Civil Procedure section 425.13, the Court finds the FAC involves an Elder Abuse claim, and the California Supreme Court held that Code of Civil Procedure section 425.13 does not apply to an action under the Elder Abuse Act. (See Covenant Care, Inc. v. Superior Court (2004) 32 Cal.4th 771, 790.)

 

Turning to whether the FAC contains sufficient facts to support the punitive damages claim, the FAC states among other claims “Defendants neglected and abused Elizabeth Mora through neglect and by deprivation by a care custodian of goods or services necessary to avoid physical harm.” (FAC, ¶ 16.) Specifically, Defendants “failed to provide medical care for Elizabeth Mora’s physical needs, failed to protect her from health and safety hazards, and deprived her of goods and services necessary to avoid physical harm…. When admitted to defendants’ facility, Elizabeth Mora had no serious skin conditions or disorders…. Defendants’ neglect caused Elizabeth Mora to develop pressure ulcers, in particular an ulcer to the sacral area. This was despite the warning of a plastic surgery consultant that her wounds needed to be treated and monitored very closely as otherwise ‘it will worsen given the patient’s overall condition.’ ” (FAC, ¶ 16.) Defendants “failed to take steps necessary to prevent the development of pressure ulcers,” including turning and repositioning Elizabeth Mora, regularizing mobilizing Elizabeth Mora, providing continence care, maintaining adequate skin moisture, obtaining regular skin assessments, and providing regular wound checks. (FAC, ¶ 16.)

 

Further, Defendants “routinely understaffed and undertrained their facility… technicians and other classes of care providers.” (FAC, ¶ 18.) The FAC states that Defendants knew of this insufficient training and staffing and ratified/authorized the wrongful conduct of employees. (FAC, ¶¶ 19-21.) “The conduct of Defendants, and each of them, described in this complaint imposed a new injury posing additional serious risks to Elizabeth Mora’s fragile health and well-being. The conduct of the Defendants, and each of them, acting directly or through their agents, was intentional and/or oppressive, and/or reckless, and evinced a conscious disregard for Elizabeth Mora’s life, health, and safety. Plaintiff is entitled to punitive damages.” (FAC, ¶ 24.)

 

As to corporate entity, Complaint pleads ratification to managing agents through the “plans of correction … could only be made by officers, directors and managing agents” theory and failure to discipline any staff. (FAC, ¶¶ 20-22.)

 

The Court finds the FAC contains sufficient facts to allege malice and/or oppression to support the claim for punitive damages in the FAC because there are facts detailing how Defendants are alleged to have failed to follow Elizabeth Mora’s care plan for maintaining her health and thereby caused Elizabeth Mora to develop serious skin conditions or disorders, including pressure ulcers. (FAC, ¶¶ 16-24; Civ. Code, § 3294, subds. (a), (c); see Sababin v. Superior Court (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 81, 84.)

 

For all these reasons, the Motion is DENIED.

 

IV.            Conclusion & Order

 

For the foregoing reasons, Defendant Cedars-Sinai Medical Center’s Motion to Strike Portions of Plaintiff’s First Amended Complaint for Damages is DENIED.

 

Moving party is ordered to give notice.

ANALYSIS:

 

I.                Background

 

On February 27, 2026, Plaintiff A.A. Rocha Construction (“Plaintiff”) filed the instant action against Defendants Kaitlin Zemet and James R. Zemet (“Defendants”), alleging causes of action for breach of oral and written contracts; work, labor, and services rendered/agreed price; work, labor, and services rendered/reasonable value; unjust enrichment; and foreclosure of mechanic’s lien. (Compl.)

 

On March 10, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Lis Pendens.

 

On April 24, 2026, Defendants filed the instant Demurrer to Plaintiff’s Complaint (the “Demurrer”).

 

On June 16, 2026, Plaintiff filed an Opposition.

On June 24, 2026, Defendants filed a Supplemental Declaration in support of the Demurrer.

 

On August 3, 2026, Defendants filed a Reply and Supplemental Declaration.

That same day, Defendants filed and served Notice of Ruling.

 

II.              Procedural Issues

 

As an initial matter, the Court finds the previous notice of hearing defects have been remedied.

 

III.            Legal Standard

 

“As a general matter, in a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) “A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Ibid.) The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)

A general demurrer may be brought under Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (e) if insufficient facts are stated to support the cause of action asserted or under section 430.10, subdivision (a), where the court has no jurisdiction of the subject of the cause of action alleged in the pleading. All other grounds listed in Section 430.10, including uncertainty under subdivision (f), are special demurrers.

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Id.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.) However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245).

III.            Discussion

 

A.    Meet and Confer

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41 requires that “[b]efore filing a demurrer pursuant to this chapter, the demurring party shall meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a).) The parties are to meet and confer at least five days before the date the responsive pleading is due. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(2).) Thereafter, the demurring party shall file and serve a declaration detailing their meet and confer efforts. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(3).) 

Here, Defendants submit a declaration stating defense counsel attempted to meet and confer with Plaintiff’s counsel by way of email and attached letter to no response. (Demurrer, Croddy Decl.)

 

However, Defendants submitted a supplemental declaration stating the parties spoke telephonically on June 30, 2026, regarding the issues raised in the Demurrer to no avail. (Reply, Supp. Croddy Decl.)

 

While the Court finds the requirement to meet and confer prior to filing the Demurrer was not satisfied, the Court acknowledges the parties met and conferred prior to the instant hearing date.

 

Further, the Court notes it may not overrule a demurrer for failure to adequately meet and confer. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41(a)(4).)

 

B.    Demurrer

 

Statute of Frauds

 

For breach of a written contract, the statute of limitations is four years. (Code. Civ. Proc., § 337, subdivision (a).) For breach of a contract “not founded upon an instrument of writing” absent certain exceptions, the statute of limitations is two years. (Code Civ. Proc., § 339(1).)

 

Civil Code section 1624 states that certain contracts are invalid unless they, or some note or memorandum thereof, are in writing and subscribed by the party to be charged or by the party’s agent. These contracts include “[a]n agreement that by its terms is not to be performed within a year from the making thereof.” (Civ. Code, § 1624, subd. (a)(1).)

 

Here, the Complaint alleges the parties entered into a written agreement in April 2025 and an accompanying oral agreement sometime thereafter, and a written agreement in October 2025 and a separate oral agreement. (See Compl., pp. 3-6.) The Complaint does not allege when the breach of the written or oral agreements occurred. Further, the Court also does not find that the kind of contracts alleged in the Complaint could not be completed in one year. (Civ. Code, § 1624, subd. (a)(1).)

 

Thus, the Complaint as pled does not demonstrate that the instant action is barred by the Statute of Frauds as a matter of law.

 

Causes of Action Nos. 1-4 – Breach of Contract (Written and Oral); Work, Labor, and Services Rendered

 

A cause of action for breach of contract must plead “(1) the contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for non-performance, (3) defendant’s breach, and (4) damage to plaintiff therefrom.” (Acoustics, Inc. v. Trepte Constr. Co. (1971) 14 Cal.App.3d 887, 913.) A written contract can be pled by alleging its making and attaching a copy to the complaint or by alleging the making and the substance of the relevant terms. (Construction Protective Services, Inc. v. TIG Specialty Ins. Co. (2002) 29 Cal.4th 189, 198-199.) The terms of an “oral contract may be pleaded generally as to its effect, because it is rarely possible to allege the exact words.” (Scolinos v. Kolts (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 635, 640.) Finally, an implied-in-fact contract can be alleged based on the promisor’s conduct. (Gomez v. Lincare, Inc. (2009) 173 Cal.App.4th 508, 525.) 

 

Here, the Complaint states the parties entered into a written contract the terms of which became effective on or around April 16, 2025 (the “April Agreement”). (Compl., ¶ 11,) The April Agreement stated Plaintiff was to construct an additional dwelling unit (“ADU”) on Defendants’ property of 3526 and 3528 7th Avenue, Los Angeles CA 90018 (the “Property”) and provide related labor, material, equipment, and services for the ADU; in exchange, Defendants were to pay $418,132.00 for Plaintiff’s services. (Compl., ¶¶ 4, 11-12.) “Plaintiff has performed all conditions and obligations to be performed on its part, except for those excused due to the actions and/or inactions of Defendants.” (Compl., ¶ 14.) However, Defendants breached the April Agreement by failing to pay Plaintiff for all services, materials, labor, and equipment Plaintiff provided to the ADU in the amount of at least $134,892.10. (Compl., ¶ 16.)

 

The Court finds these facts suffice to plead breach of a written agreement for Cause of Action No. 1.

 

In relation to the April Agreement, the Complaint states the parties entered into an oral agreement whereby Plaintiff was to supply certain additional labor, materials, and supplies to Defendants for the ADU on Defendants’ Property in exchange for Defendants’ agreement to pay; Plaintiff performed all conditions and covenants to be performed, except those that were excused; and Defendants’ breached the agreement by “failing and refusing and continuing to fail and refuse to pay Plaintiff all amounts due.” (Compl., ¶¶ 17-21.)

 

The Court finds these facts suffice to plead breach of an oral agreement for Cause of Action No. 2.

 

The Complaint states the parties entered into a written contract on or around October 29, 2025, whereby Plaintiff was to remodel the main house on the Property in exchange for $12,650.00 (the “October Agreement”). (Compl., ¶¶ 4, 23-24.) “Plaintiff has performed all conditions and obligations to be performed on its part with respect to the MAIN HOUSE AGREEMENT, except for those excused due to the actions and/or inactions of Defendants.” (Compl., ¶ 26.) However, Defendants breached the October Agreement by failing to pay Plaintiff for all services, materials, labor, and equipment provided by Plaintiff, and Plaintiff has suffered damages therefrom in the minimum amount of $1,200.10, plus interest. (Compl., ¶ 28.)

 

The Court finds these facts suffice to plead breach of a written agreement for Cause of Action No. 3.

 

The Complaint also pleads the parties entered into an oral agreement whereby Plaintiff was to supply certain labor, materials, and supplies to Defendants to Defendants in exchange for Defendants’ agreement to pay; Plaintiff performed all conditions and covenants to be performed, except those that were excused; and Defendants’ breached the agreement by “failing and refusing and continuing to fail and refuse to pay Plaintiff all amounts due it.” (Compl., ¶¶ 29-33.) However, given the generality of the facts pled regarding this cause of action, the Court is unable to ascertain whether this cause of action is duplicative of the second cause of action.

 

As such, the Court finds these facts are insufficient to plead a non-duplicative breach of oral agreement for Cause of Action No. 4.

 

Thus, the Demurrer is OVERRULED as to the first, second, third, and fifth causes of action and SUSTAINED as to the fourth cause of action.

 

Cause of Action Nos. 5, 6 – Work, Labor, and Services Rendered; Common Count

 

The required elements of a common count claim are “ ‘(1) the statement of indebtedness in a certain sum, (2) the consideration, i.e., goods sold, work done, etc., and (3) nonpayment. A cause of action for money had and received is stated if it is alleged the defendant is indebted to the plaintiff in a certain sum for money had and received by the defendant for the use of the plaintiff.’ ” (Farmers Insurance Exchange v. Zerin (1997) 53 Cal.App.4th 445, 460, citations omitted.) 

 

“A common count is not a specific cause of action, however; rather, it is a simplified form of pleading normally used to aver the existence of various forms of monetary indebtedness, including that arising from an alleged duty to make restitution under an assumpsit theory. When a common count is used as an alternative way of seeking the same recovery demanded in a specific cause of action, and is based on the same facts, the common count is demurrable if the cause of action is demurrable.” (McBride v. Boughton (2004) 123 Cal.App.4th 379, 394.)

 

            Here, the Complaint states Defendants are indebted to Plaintiff for approximately $134,892.10 for the use of Plaintiff’s services, and Defendants have not paid Plaintiff the amount owed despite demand. (See Compl., ¶¶ 37-39.)

 

Further, the Court finds the common count is seeking the same recovery demanded in the breach of contract cause(s) of action and is based on the same facts, and the Demurrer is overruled as to three of those causes of action. (See McBride, supra, 123 Cal.App.4th at p. 394.)

 

Thus, the Demurrer is OVERRULED as to the sixth cause of action.

 

However, the Court interprets the fifth cause of action for “Work, Labor, and Services/Agreed Price” to be duplicative of the sixth cause of action, and the Opposition fails to demonstrate otherwise.

 

Thus, the Demurrer is SUSTAINED as to the fifth cause of action.

 

Cause of Action No. 7 – Unjust Enrichment

 

There is a split of authority in California courts regarding whether unjust enrichment is a cause of action. (See Ghirardo v. Antonioli (1996) 14 Cal.4th 39, 50, 52, 53-55 [recognizing split in authority]; compare Durell v. Sharp Healthcare (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 1350, 1370 [“ ‘[T]here is no cause of action in California for unjust enrichment’ ”] with Lyles v. Sangadeo-Patel (2014) 225 Cal.App.4th 759, 769 [analyzing the elements for an unjust enrichment cause of action].)

 

This Court follows the line of cases holding that unjust enrichment is a general principal synonymous with restitution, which can be sought as a remedy for various causes of action, instead of as a separate cause of action. (See Rutherford Holdings, LLC v. Plaza Del Rey (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 221, 231.) 

 

“As a matter of law, an unjust enrichment claim does not lie where the parties have an enforceable express contract.” (Durell v. Sharp Healthcare (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 1350, 1370.)

 

However, “a party to an express contract can assert a claim for restitution based on unjust enrichment by ‘alleg[ing in that cause of action] that the express contract is void or was rescinded.’ [Citation.] A claim for restitution is permitted even if the party inconsistently pleads a breach of contract claim that alleges the existence of an enforceable agreement.” (Rutherford Holdings, LLC v. Plaza Del Ray (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 221, 231.) 

 

Here, the Court finds the Complaint alleges express contracts that allows for unjust enrichment. (See also McBride v. Boughton (2004) 123 Cal.App.4th 379, 388.)

 

Thus, the Demurrer is OVERRULED as to the seventh cause of action.

 

Cause of Action No. 8 – Foreclosure of Mechanic’s Lien

 

Article XIV, section 3, of the California Constitution states that “[m]echanics, persons furnishing materials, artisans, and laborers of every class, shall have a lien upon the property upon which they have bestowed labor or furnished material for the value of such labor done and material furnished; and the Legislature shall provide, by law, for the speedy and efficient enforcement of such liens.”

 

            Under Civil Code section 8400, a “person that provides work authorized for a work of improvement, including, but not limited to, the following persons, has a lien right under this chapter: Direct contractor… Material supplier,” amidst others. (Civ. Code, § 8400, subds. (a), (c).)

 

            “A claimant may enforce a lien only if the claimant has given preliminary notice to the extent required by Chapter 2 (commencing with Section 8200) and made proof of notice.” (Civ. Code, § 8410.)

 

Under Civil Code section 8412, a direct contractor may not enforce a lien unless the contractor records a claim of lien after the contractor completes the direct contract, and before the earlier of the following times: “(a) Ninety days after completion of the work of improvement. (b) Sixty days after the owner records a notice of completion or cessation.” (Civ. Code, § 8412.)

 

Under Civil Code section 8414, the claimant other than a direct contractor may not enforce a lien unless the claimant records a claim of lien within the following times: 

 

(a) After the claimant ceases to provide work. 

(b) Before the earlier of the following times: 

(1) Ninety days after completion of the work of improvement. 

(2) Thirty days after the owner records a notice of completion or cessation. 

 

(Civ. Code, § 8414.) Subdivision (a) is the earliest date a lien can be recorded, and subdivision (b) is the last date a lien can be recorded.

 

Here, the Complaint states that Plaintiff performed construction work related to an ADU and main house on Defendants’ Property, and Plaintiff filed a lien, with instrument number 20250864478, on the Property on December 2, 2025. (Compl., ¶¶ 43-50.)

 

However, the Complaint fails to specify whether Plaintiff is a direct contractor or a claimant other than a direct contractor, and whether the lien was timely filed under either Civil Code section 8412 or 8414.

 

Thus, the Court finds the Complaint fails to allege sufficient facts to state a foreclosure of mechanic’s lien cause of action.

 

The Demurrer is SUSTAINED as to the eighth cause of action.

 

Leave to Amend

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman, supra, 18 Cal.3d at p. 348.)

As this is the first demurrer to the operative complaint, and Plaintiff requests leave to amend in the Opposition, the Court finds leave to amend appropriate at this time.

Thus, the Demurrer is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND as to the fourth, fifth, and eighth causes of action.

            The Demurrer is otherwise OVERRULED.

IV.            Conclusion & Order

 

For the foregoing reasons, Defendants Kaitlin Zemet and James R. Zemet’s Demurrer to Plaintiff’s Complaint is OVERRULED IN PART and SUSTAINED IN PART.

 

The Demurrer is SUSTAINED WITH 30 DAYS’ LEAVE TO AMEND as to the fourth, fifth, and eighth causes of action. The Demurrer is otherwise OVERRULED.  

 

Moving parties are ordered to give notice.
