DEPARTMENT M LAW AND MOTION RULINGS



Case Number: 23SMCV04063    Hearing Date: September 17, 2026    Dept: M

CASE NAME:           Sabia, et al., v. Rubin, et al.

CASE NO.:                23SMCV04063

MOTION:                  Motion for Summary Judgment

HEARING DATE:   9/17/2026

 

Legal Standard

 

A party may move for summary judgment in any action or proceeding if it is contended the action has no merit or that there is no defense to the action or proceeding. (CCP, § 437c(a).) “The purpose of the law of summary judgment is to provide courts with a mechanism to cut through the parties' pleadings in order to determine whether, despite their allegations, trial is in fact necessary to resolve their dispute.” (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.)  

 

“A party may move for summary adjudication as to one or more causes of action within an action, one or more affirmative defenses, one or more claims for damages, or one or more issues of duty, if the party contends that the cause of action has no merit, that there is no affirmative defense to the cause of action, that there is no merit to an affirmative defense as to any cause of action, that there is no merit to a claim for damages, as specified in¿Section 3294 of the Civil Code, or that one or more defendants either owed or did not owe a duty to the plaintiff or plaintiffs.”¿(CCP,¿§ 437c(f)(1).)¿If a party seeks summary adjudication as an alternative to a request for summary judgment, the request must be clearly made in the notice of the motion. (Gonzales v. Superior Court¿(1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 1542, 1544.)¿ “[A] party may move for summary adjudication of a legal issue or a claim for damages other than punitive damages that does not completely dispose of a cause of action, affirmative defense, or issue of duty pursuant to” subdivision (t). (CCP,¿§ 437c(t).)¿ 

 

To prevail, the evidence submitted must show there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.¿(CCP, §¿437c(c).)¿The motion cannot succeed unless the evidence leaves no room for conflicting inferences as to material facts; the court has no power to weigh one inference against another or against other evidence. (Murillo v. Rite Stuff Food Inc. (1998) 65 Cal.App.4th 833, 841.) In determining whether the facts give rise to a triable issue of material fact, “[a]ll doubts as to whether any material, triable, issues of fact exist are to be resolved in favor of the party opposing summary judgment…” (Gold v. Weissman (2004) 114 Cal.App.4th 1195, 1198-99.) “In other words, the facts alleged in the evidence of the party opposing summary judgment and the reasonable inferences there from must be accepted as true.” (Jackson v. County of Los Angeles (1997) 60 Cal.App.4th 171, 179.) However, if adjudication is otherwise proper the motion “may not be denied on grounds of credibility,” except when¿a material fact is the witness’s¿state of mind and “that fact is sought to be established solely by the [witness’s] affirmation thereof.” (CCP, § 437c(e).)¿ 

 

Once the moving party has met their burden, the burden shifts to the opposing party “to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to that cause of action or a defense thereto.” (CCP § 437c(p)(1).) “[T]here¿is no obligation on the opposing party... to establish anything by affidavit unless and until the moving party has by affidavit stated facts establishing every element... necessary to sustain a judgment in his favor.”¿(Consumer Cause, Inc. v.¿SmileCare¿(2001) 91 Cal.App.4th 454, 468.)¿ 

¿ 

“The pleadings play a key role in a summary judgment motion. The function of the pleadings in a motion for summary judgment is to delimit the scope of the issues and to¿frame¿the outer measure of materiality in a summary judgment proceeding.” (Hutton v. Fidelity National Title Co.¿(2013) 213 Cal.App.4th 486, 493, quotations and citations omitted.) “Accordingly, the burden of a defendant moving for summary judgment only requires that he or she negate plaintiff's theories of liability¿as alleged in the complaint; that is, a moving party need not refute liability on some theoretical possibility not included in the pleadings.” (Ibid.)¿ 

 

Analysis

 

Defendant Alyssa B. Zackler M.D. moves for summary judgment against Plaintiffs Meryem Sabia and Raed Odem’s First Amended Complaint (“FAC”). The FAC states two causes of action for medical negligence and loss of consortium.

 

The elements of medical malpractice are:¿ “(1) the duty of the professional to use such skill, prudence, and diligence as other members of his profession commonly possess and exercise; (2) a breach of that duty; (3) a proximate causal connection between the negligent conduct and the resulting injury; and (4) actual loss or damage resulting from the professional's negligence.”¿(Simmons v. West Covina Medical Clinic (1989) 212 Cal.App.3d 696, 701-02 citations omitted.)¿ “Both the standard of care and defendants’ breach must normally be established by expert testimony in a medical malpractice case.”¿ (Avivi v. Centro Medico Urgente Medical Center (2008) 159 Cal. App. 4th 463, 467.) 

 

A defendant moving for summary judgment in a medical malpractice action must “present evidence that would preclude a reasonable trier of fact from finding it was more likely than not that their treatment fell below the standard of care.”¿ (Johnson v. Superior Court (2006) 143 Cal.App.4th 297, 305.) “Both the standard of care and defendants’ breach must normally be established by expert testimony in a medical malpractice case.”¿(Avivi v. Centro Medico Urgente Medical Center (2008) 159 Cal. App. 4th 463, 467.) “When a defendant moves for summary judgment and supports his motion with expert declarations that his conduct fell within the community standard of care, he is entitled to summary judgment unless the plaintiff comes forward with conflicting expert evidence.”¿(Munro v. Regents of University of California (1989) 215 Cal. App. 3d 977, 984-985.)¿ An expert declaration, if uncontradicted, is conclusive proof as to the prevailing standard of care and the propriety of the particular conduct of the health care provider.¿(Starr v. Mooslin (1971) 14 Cal. App. 3d 988, 999.)¿¿ 

  

A married person may recover for loss of consortium when his or her spouse has been injured by the negligence of a third party. (Zwicker¿v. Altamont Emergency Room Physicians Medical Group¿(2002) 98 Cal.App.4th 26, 31.) The elements for a loss of consortium cause of action are: (1) a valid and lawful marriage between the plaintiff and the person injured at the time of the injury; (2) a tortious injury to the plaintiff’s spouse; (3) loss of consortium suffered by the plaintiff; and (4) the loss was proximately caused by the defendant’s act. (Vanhooser¿v. Superior Court¿(2012) 206 Cal.App.4th 921, 927.) “‘A cause of action for loss of consortium is, by its nature, dependent on the existence of a cause of action for tortious injury to a spouse.’” (Id.)

 

In her motion, Defendant presents expert opinion testimony through the declaration of expert Joel Kessler, M.D., that Dr. Zackler’s care and treatment of plaintiff always complied with the applicable standard of care. As such, Defendant presents undisputed evidence that she met the standard of care and that no act of hers harmed Plaintiffs to a reasonable degree of medical certainty.

 

Defendant first provides the complete background of Sabia’s treatment. (UMF 1-22.) Defendant provides foundation for Dr. Kessler’s expertise. (UMF 23.) Based on a review of the record, Dr. Kessler opines that Dr. Zackler was well within the standard of care in her evaluation, care, and treatment of plaintiff. (UMF 24.) In sum, Plaintiff was admitted to Henry Mayo Newhall Hospital on October 7, 2022 following persistent flu like symptoms which pointed to infection. (UMF 25.) Dr. Zackler thoroughly evaluated plaintiff and evaluated the imaging that had been performed up to that point. (Id.) She was placed on antibiotics three antibiotics for aggressive medical management. (Id.) She continued to evaluate plaintiff from October 7, 2022 to October 10, 2022 and called for consultations by infectious disease, gynecologic surgery, internal medicine, interventional radiology, and obstetrics and gynecology. (Id.) These consults were made to definitively diagnose plaintiff’s complaint, obtain imaging, and begin the process of any surgical intervention to treat the abscess. (Id.) This was within the standard of care since surgical interventions and infectious disease management are best treated by specialists. (Id.) Nonetheless, Dr. Zackler began plaintiff on ceftriaxone, doxycycline, and metronidazole (flagyl) to treat the infection and abscess. (Id.) It was within the standard of care to manage plaintiff’s condition with antibiotics before opting for or proceeding with surgical management. (Id.) Dr. Zackler started plaintiff on three different antibiotics to aggressively tackle the infection. (Id.) Her choice of antibiotic treatment was appropriate. (Id.) Triple antibiotic therapy was necessary here as an initial treatment in order to treat the infection, inflammation, and swelling in plaintiff’s abdomen. (Id.) Only if this failed, would surgery be indicated. (Id.) The standard of care dictates 72 hours of antibiotic treatment prior to proceeding with any surgical intervention. (Id.) It was necessary to reduce plaintiff’s symptoms from her sepsis before surgical intervention was initiated, otherwise it could have resulted in significant complications including overwhelming sepsis and possible death. (Id.) Immediate surgery was not indicated, rather antibiotic treatment was necessary, warranted, and essential. Plaintiff needed to be medically stabilized first prior to surgery. (Id.)

 

To a reasonable degree of medical probability, Dr. Kessler further opines that nothing Dr. Zackler did or did not do caused or contributed to plaintiff’s injuries. (UMF 26.) Dr. Zackler’s initiation of initial antibiotic treatment was warranted before proceeding with surgical treatment. (Id.) The nature of Plaintiff’s infection was severe upon her admission. (Id.) Rushing to surgical intervention would have carried unnecessary significant risks including a probability of overwhelming sepsis and possible death. (Id.) Hence, Dr. Zackler ordering antibiotics and exploring initial conservative methods of treatment was within the standard of care and appropriate. (Id.) Dr. Zackler’s refusal to rush to surgery was appropriate and did not cause or contribute to plaintiff’s alleged injuries in any way. (Id.) Rather, it prevented further harm. (Id.)

 

The above evidence meets Defendant’s initial burden of production. Plaintiffs have the burden present expert testimony that Dr. Zackler’s treatment fell below the standard of care. Plaintiffs have not opposed and therefore fail to meet this burden.

 

Accordingly, the motion is GRANTED.

Case Number: 25SMCV06694    Hearing Date: September 17, 2026    Dept: M

CASE NAME:           Green v. Oakmont Management Group, LLC, et al. 

CASE NO.:                25SMCV06694

MOTION:                     Petition/Motion to Compel Arbitration 

HEARING DATE:    9/17/2026 

 

LEGAL STANDARD 

 

Under California and federal law, public policy favors arbitration as an efficient and less expensive means of resolving private disputes. (Moncharsh v. Heily & Blase (1992) 3 Cal.4th 1, 8-9; AT&T Mobility LLC v. Concepcion (2011) 563 U.S. 333, 339.) Accordingly, whether an agreement is governed by the California Arbitration Act (“CAA”) or the Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”), courts resolve doubts about an arbitration agreement’s scope in favor of arbitration.  (Moncharsh, supra, 3 Cal.4th at 9; Comedy Club, Inc. v. Improv West Assocs. (9th Cir. 2009) 553 F.3d 1277, 1284; see also Engalla v. Permanente Med. Grp., Inc. (1997) 15 Cal.4th 951, 971-972 [“California law incorporates many of the basic policy objectives contained in the Federal Arbitration Act, including a presumption in favor of arbitrability [citation] and a requirement that an arbitration agreement must be enforced on the basis of state law standards that apply to contracts in general”].) “[U]nder both the FAA and California law, ‘arbitration agreements are valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, save upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract.’ ” (Higgins v. Superior Crout (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 1238, 1247.) 

 

“Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.2 requires a trial court to grant a petition to compel arbitration if the court determines that an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists.” (Avery v. Integrated Healthcare Holdings, Inc. (2013) 218 Cal.App.4th 50, 59, quotations omitted.) Accordingly, “when presented with a petition to compel arbitration, the court’s first task is to determine whether the parties have in fact agreed to arbitrate the dispute.”  (Ibid.) A petition to compel arbitration is in essence a suit in equity to compel specific performance of a contract. (Id. at 71.) As with any other specific performance claim, “a party seeking to enforce an arbitration agreement must show the agreement’s terms are sufficiently definite to enable the court to know what it is to enforce.” (Ibid. [internal citations omitted].) “Only the valid and binding agreement of the parties, including all material terms well-defined and clearly expressed, may be ordered specifically performed.” (Ibid.) An arbitration agreement “must be so interpreted as to give effect to the mutual intention of the parties as it existed at the time of contracting, so far as the same is ascertainable and lawful.” (Civ. Code, § 1636.) The language of the contract governs its interpretation if it is clear and explicit. (Civ. Code, § 1368.) If uncertainty exists, “the language of a contract should be interpreted most strongly against the party who caused the uncertainty to exist.” (Civ. Code, § 1654.)  

 

The party seeking to compel arbitration bears the burden of proving the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by the preponderance of the evidence. (Engalla v. Permanente Medical Group, Inc. (1997) 15 Cal.4th 951, 972.) It would then be plaintiff’s burden, in opposing the motion, to prove by a preponderance of the evidence any fact necessary to her opposition. (See Ibid.) “In these summary proceedings, the trial court sits as a trier of fact, weighing all the affidavits, declarations, and other documentary evidence, as well as oral testimony received at the court’s discretion, to reach a final determination.” (Ibid.) 

 

As with any contract, mutual assent or consent is necessary for the formation of a valid arbitration agreement. (Civ. Code, §§ 1550, 1565.) “Consent is not mutual, unless the parties all agree upon the same thing in the same sense.” (Civ. Code, § 1580.) The moving party bears the initial burden of showing the existence of an agreement to arbitrate by a preponderance of the evidence. (Mitri v. Arnel Mgmt. Co. (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 1164, 1169 [“Because the existence of the agreement is a statutory prerequisite to granting the petition, the petitioner bears the burden of proving its existence by a preponderance of the evidence.”].)   

 

ANALYSIS 

 

Defendants Welltower Inc., Oakmont Management Group LLC, and Ivy Living LLC move to compel Plaintiff Ora Green’s complaint to arbitration.

 

Valid Arbitration Agreement 

 

Defendants assert that Plaintiff signed an arbitration agreement covering her claims and the Court should order these claims to arbitration. Defendants provide a declaration from its Executive director at its Assisted Living Memory Care facility stating that Plaintiff and Plaintiff’s granddaughter, Jaquita Ta’le, signed the Residence and Services Agreement with an arbitration provision. (Thorton Decl., ¶ 2, Ex. 2.) Section 21 of the Agreement provides, in relevant part:

 

“…[T]he parties agree that if informal resolution is not possible, any and all claims or disputes arising from or related to this Agreement or to your rights obligations, care or services at the community shall be resolved by submission to neutral, binding arbitration in accordance with the Federal Arbitration Act. This agreement to arbitrate applies regardless of whether the claim is made august us, you, or any other individual or entity, and it includes, without limitation, personal injury and wrongful death claims….”

 

            The arbitration agreement also contains a clear and unmistakable delegation provision. (Thorton Decl., ¶ 2, Ex. 2, at ¶ 21.b.) Parties may agree to arbitrate gateway questions of arbitrability such as the enforceability of an arbitration agreement and whether claims are covered by the arbitration agreement.  (Rent-A-Center, West, Inc. v. Jackson (2010) 561 U.S. 63, 68-69.) To establish this exception, it must be shown by clear and unmistakable evidence that the parties intended to delegate the issue to the arbitrator. (First Options of Chicago, Inc. v. Kaplan (1995) 514 U.S. 938, 944.) The delegation must not be revocable under state contract defenses such as fraud, duress, or unconscionability. (Tiri v. Lucky Changes, Inc. (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 231, 242.) Delegation clauses are treated as separate agreements from the underlying arbitration agreement for purposes of enforcement. (Malone v. Superior Court¿(2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 1551, 1559-60.) Where the parties clearly and unmistakably incorporate a delegation clause, the parties clearly evidence their intention to accord the arbitrator the authority to determine issues of arbitrability. (Rodriguez v. American Technologies, Inc. (2006) 136 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1123.)  

 

In opposition, Plaintiff does not dispute that she and her granddaughter signed the agreement. Plaintiff raises several defenses to the arbitration agreement, including her capacity to contract, her granddaughter’s authority to sign on her behalf, and unconscionability. As discussed below, Plaintiff does not show any defense to the delegation clause aside from the capacity issue.

 

Capacity

 

Plaintiff claims that she lacked capacity at the time that she agreed to arbitration due to her diagnosed Alzheimer’s disease and associated dementia. “All persons are capable of contracting, except minors, persons of unsound mind, and persons deprived of civil rights.” (Civ. Code § 1556.) The lack of capacity to contract are governed by Civ. Cod sections 38-39 and Prob. Code sections 810-812. There is a rebuttable presumption affecting the burden of proof that all persons have the capacity to make decisions and to be responsible for their acts or decisions. (Prob. Code § 810(a).)

 

Contracts created by persons of unsound mind, who are not entirely without understanding, are voidable. (Civ. Code § 39.) Civil Code section 39 provides in full:

 

            (a) A conveyance or other contract of a person of unsound mind, but not entirely without understanding, made before the incapacity of the person has been judicially determined, is subject to rescission, as provided in Chapter 2 (commencing with Section 1688) of Title 5 of Part 2 of Division 3.

 

(b) A rebuttable presumption affecting the burden of proof that a person is of unsound mind shall exist for purposes of this section if the person is substantially unable to manage his or her own financial resources or resist fraud or undue influence. Substantial inability may not be proved solely by isolated incidents of negligence or improvidence.”

 

Generally, the test for mental capacity set forth in section 39 is whether the person said to have entered a contract understood the nature, purpose, and effect of what they did. (Smalley v. Baker (1968) 262 Cal.App.2d 824, 832.)

 

A determination that a person lacked the capacity to contract must be “supported by evidence of a deficit in at least one of the following mental functions, subject to subdivision (b), and evidence of a correlation between the deficit or deficits and the decision or acts in question.” (Prob. Code, § 811(a).) The four functions include, alertness and attention, information processing, thought processes, and ability to modulate mood and affect. (Id.) A deficit counts only where it “significantly impairs the person’s ability to understand and appreciate the consequences of his or her actions with regard to the type of act or decision in question.” (Prob. Code, § 811(b).) A person lacks capacity unless he can communicate the decision and “understand and appreciate, to the extent relevant,” the rights and responsibilities affected, the probable consequences, and the significant risks, benefits, and reasonable alternatives involved. (Prob. Code, § 812.) Notably, the mere diagnosis of a mental disorder does not by itself support a determination that a person is of unsound mind. (Prob. Code, § 811(d).) Once the presumption of lack of capacity is established, the party claiming capacity must prove that a person unable to manage his financial resources or resist fraud or undue influence was “nevertheless still capable of contracting being of sound mind as defined by Probate Code section 811.” (In re Marriage of Greenway (2013) 217 Cal.App.4th 628, 642-643.) Probate Code sections 811 and 812 provide the baseline for capacity to contract, and Civil Code section 39(b) provides “more specific guidelines for determining the capacity to contract.” (Id. at 642.)

 

Plaintiff’s daughter and granddaughter present evidence demonstrating that Plaintiff’s Alzheimer’s disease and associated dementia significantly affected her ability to understand and appreciate the consequences of her actions regarding the subject contract. It is undisputed that Plaintiff was diagnosed with Alzheimer’s on August 31, 2023. (Ta’le-Sekani Decl., ¶¶ 2-4; Ta’le Decl., ¶¶ 2-4.) Plaintiff had cognitive decline as her memory, thinking skills, and the ability to carry out simple tasks were noticeably diminishing. (Id.) During her lucid moments, Plaintiff confided that she did not know what was going on with her memory. (Id., ¶ 4.) Plaintiff also showed poor financial judgment. Sometime in 2022 or early 2023, employees at her banking institution persuaded her to place a substantial sum and significant portion of her savings into a certificate of deposit for a set term. (Ta’le Decl., ¶ 5.) The family became concerned over her ability to manage her bills. (Id.) Plaintiff became obsessed with her money and sought to take out large amounts of cash to have on hand, only to misplace the cash in her home, and began missing regular monthly bill payments. (Id.) Both Ta’le’s father and Ta’le were added to all of Plaintiff’s banking and investment accounts to allow them to manage her financial resources, preserve her financial stability, and ensure all her bills were timely paid. (Id.) Plaintiff had Home Aide daily to assist her with daily personal care and living activities, and to monitor her so that she did not wander. (Id., ¶ 6.)

 

The family began looking into assisted living in early 2024 due to Plaintiff’s gradual decline in both physical health and memory, which made independent living dangerous for her. (Id., ¶ 7.) They visited several facilities but Plaintiff was unable to secure placement or was not satisfied with the facilities for various reasons. (Id.) In July 2024, Plaintiff’s children began staying overnight with her, as her Alzheimer’s and associated dementia were worsening and she could not be left alone. (Id., ¶8.) She began to forget who her children were and would often get agitated. (Id.) She also would try to leave her house at odd hours of the night to wander the streets wearing her mink coat. (Id.) She began to believe that her home was assisted living. (Id.) By December 2024, Plaintiff often could not distinguish family members from strangers and was unaware of her surroundings. (Id., ¶ 9.)  After a December 2024 wandering incident which required police assistance, it was clear that Plaintiff  required aid, assistance, and supervision at a level that her family could not provide. (Id.) Defendants assessed Plaintiff and determined that she needed to be placed in the Memory Care Program, which provides specialized care for residents with Alzheimer’s, dementia, and other cognitive impairments. (Id., ¶ 10.) Ta’le instructed Plaintiff where to sign and she signed the agreement as indicated. (Id., ¶ 13.) However, Ta’le claims that Plaintiff was not cognizant of her surroundings, nor could she meaningfully read or understand the terms of the agreement and attached addenda, and that it was “impossible” for Plaintiff to have understood what an arbitration agreement was, much less what she was signing and that by signing, she would be giving up her right to a jury trial. (Id.) Ta’le only directed her to sign because she felt pressured to do so under the circumstances and so that Plaintiff would not lose her spot at Ivy Park. (Id.)

 

The weight of the evidence demonstrates that Plaintiff lacked the ability to understand and appreciate the consequences of her actions regarding the arbitration agreement. Aside from the undisputed diagnosis of Alzheimer’s, and the worsening dementia throughout 2024, Ta’le provides specific testimony that, at the time Plaintiff signed the contract, Plaintiff could not understand her surroundings or meaningfully understand the terms of the agreement. Plaintiff therefore raises a presumption that she lacked capacity to contract at the relevant time. Defendant does not show with any specific evidence that, despite her mental deficit, Plaintiff was nevertheless still capable of contracting being of sound mind. (Prob. Code § 811.) In reply, Defendant fails to submit substantial authenticated evidence that she could communicate the decision, or understand /appreciate the rights and responsibilities, the probable consequences, or the risks, benefits, and reasonable alternatives involved in the decision.

 

Ta’le’s Agency

 

Additionally, Defendant fails to demonstrate that Ta’le was Plaintiff’s actual or ostensible agent. When written authorization is absent, agency relationship may arise by oral consent or by implication from the conduct of the parties. (Flores v. Evergreen at San Diego, LLC (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 581, 587-88.) "Agency can be founded on ostensible authority, that is, some intentional conduct or neglect on the part of the alleged principal creating a belief in the minds of third persons that an agency exists, and a reasonable reliance thereon by such third persons.'" (Ibid.) “Ostensible agency cannot be established by the representations or conduct of the purported agent; the statements or acts of the principal must be such as to cause the third party to believe that the agency exists. (Milrot v. Stamper Medical Corp. (1996) 44 Cal.App.4th 182, 187; see also Young v. Horizon West, Inc. (2013) 220 Cal.App.4th 1122 [no direct evidence that principal’s conduct demonstrated ostensible authority to bind him]; Pagarigan v. Libby Care Center, Inc. (2002) 99 Cal.App.4th 298 [finding no agency where patient, who was “comatose and mentally incompetent,” had done nothing that “caused [the defendants] to believe [that] either of her daughters was authorized to act as her agent in any capacity”].) Defendant submits no evidence that Plaintiff indicated by any means that Ta’le was her agent. Defendant’s evidence is silent on this point. (Thorton Decl., ¶¶ 1-2; Fodrini Decl., ¶¶ 1-4.) Moreover, Plaintiff’s evidence denies this position. (Ta’le Decl., ¶ 15.)  

 

Conclusion 

 

Defendant fails to show that Plaintiff had the capacity to contract, or that Ta’le had ostensible authority to bind Plaintiff. Thus, the arbitration agreement is voidable. Thus, Plaintiff cannot be compelled to arbitration. Accordingly, the motion is DENIED.


Case Number: 26STCV19307    Hearing Date: September 17, 2026    Dept: M

CASE NAME:           PW001, et al., v. Coopersurgical Inc., et al.

CASE NO.:                26STCV19307

MOTION:                  Motion to Seal

HEARING DATE:   9/17/2026

 

 

Legal Standard

 

The sealing of trial court records is governed by California Rules of Court (“CRC”) rules 2.550 and 2.551. (Mercury Interactive Corp. v. Klein (2007) 158 Cal.App.4th 60, 68.) Pleadings, motions, discovery documents, and other papers may not be filed under seal merely by stipulation of the parties—a prior court order must be obtained. (CRC, Rule 2.551(a); see H.B. Fuller Co. v. Doe (2007) 151 Cal.App.4th 879, 888.)

 

To seal a record, the following requirements are imposed: (1) the party must file a motion or application for an order sealing the record, which must be accompanied by a memorandum and a declaration containing facts sufficient to justify the sealing; (2) the party must serve a copy of the motion on all parties who have appeared in the case; and (3) the party requesting that a record be filed under seal must lodge it with the court when the motion or application is made unless the record has previously been lodged. (CRC, Rule 2.551(b).)  

 

The Court must make the following express factual findings in order to seal records: (1) an overriding interest exists that overcomes the right of public access to the record; (2) the overriding interest supports sealing the records; (3) a substantial probability exists that the overriding interest will be prejudiced if the record is not sealed; (4) the proposed sealing is narrowly tailored; and (5) no less restrictive means exist to achieve the overriding interest. (CRC, Rule 2.550(d).) These findings embody constitutional requirements for a request to seal court records, protecting the First Amendment right of public access to civil trials. (NBC Subsidiary (KNBC-TV), Inc. v. Superior Court (1999) 20 Cal.4th 1178, 1217-1218.)

 

An order sealing the record must specifically state the facts that support the findings and direct the sealing of only those pages and documents or, if reasonably practicable, portions of those documents and pages, that contain the material that needs to be placed under seal, and all other portions must be included in the public file. (CRC, rule 2.550(e).) 

 

Once sealed, a record can only be unsealed by order of court. (CRC, Rule 2.551(h)(1).) So long as it remains under seal, all parties must refrain from filing anything not under seal that would disclose the sealed matter. (Id., Rule 2.551(c).) If a party files a new document referring to sealed matter, it must submit an unredacted version of the document under seal and a redacted one for the public record. (Id., Rule 2.551(b)(5).)

 

ANALYSIS

 

Plaintiffs PW011 and PW012 move to seal their Minor’s Compromise Petition and related documents and information. Plaintiffs demonstrate an overriding interest which overcomes the right of public access to the record. The plaintiffs in this action are parents of eight children whose embryos developed in Defendants’ embryo culture media in late 2023. (Wolf Decl., ¶ 3.) Plaintiffs’ claims, including the claims of their minor children, were resolved as part of a global settlement. (Id.) The Minor’s Compromise Petition and related documents are replete with identifying information of the petitioner and minor, health status of the minor, and the reproductive decisions of Plaintiffs. (Id., ¶ 4.) The parties’ settlement agreements also contain a strict confidentiality provision in which the terms (including the amount) of the settlement must not be disclosed. (Id., ¶ 5.) This overriding interest supports sealing the records. Absent sealing, there would be a substantial probability that the privacy interests stated will be prejudiced. Plaintiffs show that the proposed sealing is narrowly tailored and that no less restrictive means exist to achieve the overriding interest under the circumstances. The entirety of the petitions should be sealed, since all the information placed on the form petitions would be subject to the above privacy interests.

 

Accordingly, the motion is GRANTED.
