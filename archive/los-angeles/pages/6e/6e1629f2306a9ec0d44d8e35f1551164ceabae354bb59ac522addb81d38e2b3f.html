DEPARTMENT 414 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 22STCV21977    Hearing Date: September 11, 2026    Dept: 414

22STCV21977 Marc Alan Corbin, et al. v. 720 Normandie LP, et al.

Friday, September 11, 2026

(1) [TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING PLAINTIFFS’ JOINT MOTION TO COMPEL DEFENDANT PRANA HOLDING COMPANY LLC RESPONSES FOR FORM INTERROGATORIES-GENERAL SET ONE

(2) [TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING PLAINTIFFS’ JOINT MOTION TO COMPEL DEFENDANT PRANA INVESTMENTS RESPONSES FOR FORM INTERROGATORIES-GENERAL SET ONE

(3) [TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING PLAINTIFFS’ JOINT MOTION TO COMPEL DEFENDANT STATEWIDE ENTERPRISES, INC. RESPONSES FOR FORM INTERROGATORIES-GENERAL SET ONE

 

                                                                                         I.         BACKGROUND

    The complaint alleges Defendants leased residential premises to Plaintiffs that suffered from multiple habitability issues.  The operative complaint is the First Amended Complaint filed on March 17, 2023 alleging (1) tortious breach of the implied warranty of habitability; (2) breach of the implied warranty of habitability; (3) nuisance (negligent); (4) nuisance (intentional); (5) negligence; (6) intentional infliction of emotional distress; (7) breach of the implied covenant of quiet use and enjoyment; and (8) premises liability.

    On May 26, 2026, Plaintiffs in pro per filed three motions to compel initial responses to form interrogatories from (1) Defendant Prana Holding Company LLC; (2) Defendant Statewide Enterprises, Inc.; and (3) Defendant Prana Investments (collectively “Defendants”).  On August 24, 2026, Defendants each filed separate oppositions to the motions to compel.  On September 3, 2026, Plaintiffs filed separate joint replies to each of the oppositions.  On September 8, 2026, Defendants each filed supplemental declarations in support of their original oppositions.  On September 9, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a joint objection to the unauthorized supplemental declarations.

   

                                                                                       II.        LEGAL STANDARDS

      When a party to whom interrogatories are directed fails to respond, a party propounding the interrogatories may move for an order compelling a response.  (Code of Civ. Proc. §2030.290, subd. (b).)  A party who fails to provide a timely response waives any objection, including one based on privilege or work product. (Id. at subd. (b).)  For a motion to compel initial responses, no meet and confer is required.  All that must be shown is that a set of interrogatories was properly served on the opposing party, that the time to respond has expired, and that no response has been served.  (Leach v. Sup. Ct. (1980) 111 Cal.App.3d 902, 905-906.)

                                                                                           III.       DISCUSSION

A.  Motion to Compel Initial Responses from Defendant Prana Holding Company, LLC (Res. No. -7534)

      Plaintiffs move to compel initial responses to form interrogatories served on Defendant Prana Holding Company, LLC on December 7, 2023.  Plaintiffs claim Defendant failed to serve any responses, much less timely responses.  Plaintiffs request sanctions in the amount of $1,396.00 pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 128.5 and 2030.290. 

      In opposition, Defendant argues the motion is moot because it served verified responses on June 2, 2026.  Defendant argues it served verified supplemental responses on August 24, 2026 without objections.  Defendant argues Plaintiffs are in pro per and can only recover costs, not fees.  Defendant argues Plaintiffs cannot recover for the time required to prepare the briefing, nor may it recover for filing fees on reply.  Defendant argues replies do not require a filing fee.

      In reply, Plaintiffs argue the responses served on August 24, 2026 were incomplete.  Plaintiffs argue Defendant failed to respond at all to FR 1.1, 3.1-3.7 and approximately 29 subparts.  Plaintiffs argue they have submitted a declaration in support of the sanctions request and that is sufficient.  Plaintiffs maintain they attempted to meet and confer in good faith despite not being required to do so.

      In a supplemental declaration, Defense counsel restates the facts attested to in the original declaration in support of the opposition and explains the failure to supplement FR 1.1, 3.1-3.7.  Defendant testifies that FR 1.1, 3.1-3.7 did not require supplemental responses, because full responses were served to those interrogatories on July 2, 2026. 

      As discussed in Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pacific Healthcare Consultants (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, the Court has several options if untimely responses are served once a motion to compel is filed.  “The trial court might compel responses without objection if it finds no legally valid responses have been provided to one or more interrogatories; it might deny the motion to compel responses as essentially unnecessary, in whole or in part, and just impose sanctions; it might treat the motion as one under section 2030.300 and either determine that further answers are required, or order the propounding party to “meet and confer” (§ 2030.300, subd. (b)) and file a separate statement (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1020(a)(2), (c)); or it might take the motion off calendar, thereby requiring the propounding party to file a motion under section 2030.300 [statute governing motions to compel further responses to interrogatories].”  (Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc., supra, 148 Cal.App.4th at 409.)

      Defendant’s responses served on July 2, 2026, contained general objections applicable to all of the interrogatories and specific objections to certain interrogatories.  (Opposition, Feldstein Dec., Ex. A.)  The July 2, 2026 responses therefore did not moot Plaintiffs’ motion to compel responses without objection. 

      Defendant’s August 24, 2026 responses were also defective, because they are prefaced by general objections and fail to fully respond to certain interrogatories.  A number of Defendant’s supplemental responses refer to Bates numbers, rather than provide full substantive answers, or fail to fully respond to all subparts, including requests for contact information of witnesses or persons holding certain evidence.  (See e.g., Feldstein Supp. Dec., Ex. C, FR 4.1, 12.1, 12.4, 12.7, 15.1, 16.2, 16.3, 16.5.)

      Thus, based on a review of the Defendant’s July 2, 2026 and August 24, 2026 responses, Plaintiffs’ motion to compel full verified responses without objection to Plaintiff’s Form Interrogatories (Set One) is not moot.  Defendant failed to serve timely responses thereby waiving all objections.  Defendant served defective responses containing specific and general objections on July 2 and August 24, 2026.  Defendant’s supplemental responses also failed to fully respond to a number of the interrogatories.  Plaintiffs’ motion to compel is therefore granted. 

      Plaintiffs also request sanctions in the amount of $1,360. Plaintiffs are self-represented. “A pro se litigant cannot recover attorney's fees as a discovery sanction, but he can recover the ‘reasonable expenses’ he has ‘incurred,’ including photocopying, computer-assisted legal research, and other identifiable and allocable costs.” (Kravitz v. Superior Court (2001) 91 Cal.App.4th 1015, 1017.) Although a self-represented litigant may recover attorney’s fees actually incurred for legal assistance provided in connection with a discovery dispute, the litigant must establish that such fees were in fact incurred. (Dalessandro v. Mitchell (2019) 43 Cal.App.5th 1088, 1093; Argaman v. Ratan (1999) 73 Cal.App.4th 1173, 1181.)

      Here, Plaintiffs seek $1,000 based upon four hours of “Legal research and assistance $250 per hour.” (Motion, Megan Corbin Decl., ¶ 7.) The declaration, however, does not identify an attorney who provided the claimed assistance, state that Plaintiffs retained or paid an attorney, attach an invoice or billing record, or otherwise establish that Plaintiffs actually incurred an obligation to pay $250 per hour for legal services. Nor is there evidence establishing the source or basis for the asserted $250 hourly rate. The bare characterization of time spent on “legal research and assistance,” without evidence that the claimed expense was actually incurred, is insufficient to support an award of attorney’s fees or other legal-assistance expenses. The Court therefore declines to award the requested $1,000. Plaintiffs have, however, established $336 in recoverable costs actually incurred in connection with the discovery dispute. Accordingly, the Court awards Plaintiffs monetary sanctions consisting solely of costs in the amount of $336.

      Plaintiffs’ alternative request for sanctions pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 128.5 is denied.  Plaintiffs fail to satisfy either the procedural or substantive requirements for such sanctions.  (Code of Civ. Proc., §128.5, subd. (a)(trial court may award sanctions based on frivolous, bad faith actions or tactics or actions or tactics solely intended to cause unnecessary delay by party, party attorney or both), (f)(1)(A)(“motion or sanctions under this section shall be made separately from other motions or requests….”)) 

      Plaintiffs’ Motion to Compel Responses to Form Interrogatories from Defendant Prana Holding Company, LLC is GRANTED.  Plaintiffs’ request for sanctions is GRANTED in the amount of $1,360.  Defendant Prana Holding Company, LLC is ordered to serve full responses to each form interrogatory, including subparts, without objection within 20 days.  

 

B.  Motion to Compel Initial Responses from Defendant Prana Investments (Res. No. -8801)

      Plaintiffs move for an order compelling verified responses without objection to form interrogatories served on Defendant Prana Investments on July 14, 2023.  Plaintiffs argue Defendant served untimely, evasive, unverified answers on November 3, 2023 that are tantamount to no response at all.  Plaintiffs request sanctions in the amount of $1,409 pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 128.5 and 2030.300 subdivision (d).

      In opposition, Defendant Prana Investments argues the motion is moot.  Defendant argues it served verifications of this November 3, 2025 responses on June 2, 2026 and served verified supplemental responses without objection on August 24, 2026.  Defendant argues Plaintiffs are not entitled to the requested sanctions as pro per litigants.

      In reply, Plaintiffs raise the same arguments raised in its reply to Prana Holding Company, LLC’s opposition.  Plaintiffs argue the responses served after the filing of the motion are defective and fail to fully respond to the interrogatories. 

      In a supplemental declaration, defense counsel explains that supplemental responses were not served to FR 1.1 through 3.7 because the original November 3, 2023 responses fully responded to those interrogatories.

      The substantive November 3, 2023 responses were also unverified and therefore tantamount to no responses at all.  (Appleton v. Superior Court (1988) 206 Cal.App.3d 632, 636.)  Verifications were only sent on July 2, 2026, after this motion was filed.  Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.290 for initial responses, as opposed to section 2030.3100 for further responses, is the applicable code section.

      As discussed in connection with the motion to compel responses from Prana Holding Company, LLC’s, the Court has multiple options where responses are served after a motion to compel is filed under Sinaiko.  Having reviewed the unverified November 3, 2023 responses and the August 24, 2026 supplemental responses, they are defective for the same reasons stated in connection with Prana Holding Company, LLC’s responses.  Both sets of responses contain objections, even though Prana Investment did not serve timely responses to the July 14, 2023 form interrogatories.  The August 24, 2026 supplemental responses are defective because they do not fully respond to the certain interrogatories.  The November 3, 2023 and August 24, 2026 responses do not moot the instant motion.  Plaintiffs’ motion to compel responses from Prana Investments to form interrogatories (set one) is granted.

      Plaintiffs also request monetary sanctions. Plaintiffs are self-represented. “A pro se litigant cannot recover attorney's fees as a discovery sanction, but he can recover the ‘reasonable expenses’ he has ‘incurred,’ including photocopying, computer-assisted legal research, and other identifiable and allocable costs.” (Kravitz v. Superior Court (2001) 91 Cal.App.4th 1015, 1017.) Although a self-represented litigant may recover attorney’s fees actually incurred for legal assistance provided in connection with a discovery dispute, the litigant must establish that such fees were in fact incurred. (Dalessandro v. Mitchell (2019) 43 Cal.App.5th 1088, 1093; Argaman v. Ratan (1999) 73 Cal.App.4th 1173, 1181.)

      Here, to the extent Plaintiffs seek sanctions based upon “legal research and assistance” calculated at an hourly rate, the evidence does not identify an attorney who provided the claimed assistance, establish that Plaintiffs retained or paid an attorney, or otherwise demonstrate that Plaintiffs actually incurred an obligation to pay for such legal services. Nor is there evidence establishing the source or basis for the asserted hourly rate. The Court therefore declines to award sanctions based upon the claimed legal research and assistance.

Plaintiffs have, however, established $409 in recoverable costs actually incurred in connection with this discovery dispute. Accordingly, the Court awards Plaintiffs monetary sanctions consisting solely of costs in the amount of $409.

      Plaintiffs’ Motion to Compel Responses to Form Interrogatories from Defendant Prana Investments is GRANTED.  Plaintiffs’ request for sanctions is GRANTED in the amount of $1,409.  Defendant Prana Investments is ordered to serve full responses to each form interrogatory, including subparts, without objection within 20 days.  

C.  Motion to Compel Initial Responses from Defendant Statewide Enterprises, Inc. (Res. No. -4899)

      The briefing in support of and in opposition to Plaintiffs’ Motion to Compel Initial Responses from Defendant Statewide Enterprises, Inc. is identical to that submitted in connection with Plaintiffs’ Motion to Compel Initial Responses from Defendant Prana Investments.  For the same reasons stated in connection with Plaintiffs’ motion to compel responses from Defendants Prana Holding Company, LLC and Defendant Prana Investments is granted.  Plaintiffs’ request for sanctions is granted in the amount of $1,409 for the same reasons stated in connection with Plaintiffs’ motions as to those Defendants as well.   

      Plaintiffs’ Motion to Compel Responses to Form Interrogatories from Defendant Statewide Enterprises, Inc. is GRANTED.  For the reasons discussed above, the Court declines to award Plaintiffs sanctions based upon the claimed “legal research and assistance.” Plaintiffs are self-represented, and the evidence does not establish that they actually incurred attorney’s fees or other legal-assistance expenses. Plaintiffs have, however, established $409 in recoverable costs actually incurred in connection with this discovery dispute. Accordingly, the Court awards Plaintiffs monetary sanctions consisting solely of costs in the amount of $409.

 

                                                                                         IV.       CONCLUSION

      Plaintiffs’ Motion to Compel Responses to Form Interrogatories from Defendant Prana Holding Company, LLC is GRANTED.  Plaintiffs’ request for sanctions is GRANTED in the amount reflected in the order.   Defendant Prana Holding Company, LLC is ordered to serve full responses to each form interrogatory, including subparts, without objection within 20 days.  

      Plaintiffs’ Motion to Compel Responses to Form Interrogatories from Defendant Prana Investments is GRANTED.  Plaintiffs’ request for sanctions is GRANTED in the amount reflected in the order. Defendant Prana Investments is ordered to serve full responses to each form interrogatory, including subparts, without objection within 20 days.  

      Plaintiffs’ Motion to Compel Responses to Form Interrogatories from Defendant Statewide Enterprises, Inc. is GRANTED.  Plaintiffs’ request for sanctions is GRANTED in the amount of reflected in the order.  Defendant Statewide Enterprises, Inc. is ordered to serve full responses to each form interrogatory, including subparts, without objection within 20 days.  

     

 


Case Number: 24STCV01254    Hearing Date: September 11, 2026    Dept: 414

24STCV01254 Kids Empire Pomona, LLC v. Royal Oak, L.P., et al.

Friday, September 11, 2026

(1) [TENTATIVE] ORDER DENYING DEFENDANT RELIABLE PROPERTIES’ MOTION FOR PROTECTIVE ORDER (Res. No. -4062) AND DENYING REQUEST FOR SANCTIONS

(2) [TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING IN PART PLAINTIFF’S MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES TO AND COMPLIANCE WITH FIFTH SET OF INSPECTION DEMANDS TO DEFENDANTS; REQUEST FOR SANCTIONS [Res. No. -4819]

(3) [TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING IN PART PLAINTIFF’S MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES TO FIRST SET OF REQUESTS FOR PRODUCTION PROPOUNDED TO DEFENDANTS; REQUEST FOR SANCTIONS [Res. No. -4356]

 

I. BACKGROUND

        Plaintiff Kids Empire Pomona, LLC (“Kids Empire”) leased commercial premises from Defendants Royal Oak, L.P., Twin Flower Properties, L.P., Clear Mountain Properties, L.P., Skymeadow Properties, L.P. and Reliable Properties (“Landlord Defendants”) pursuant to a written Lease Agreement (“Lease Agreement”). Plaintiff alleges David Gold of Reliable Properties fraudulently misrepresented that the amount of common area maintenance fees (“CAM Fees”) due per month would be $4,795. Plaintiff alleges after it commenced occupancy in June 2021, Landlord Defendants informed Plaintiff that the monthly CAM charge was $8,096.90.

        On January 17, 2024, Plaintiff filed complaint alleging (1) fraud; (2) rescission (mistake); and (3) declaratory relief.  On May 22, 2026, Defendant Reliable Properties filed a Motion for Protective Order. On August 28, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition to the Motion for Protective Order. On September 3, 2026, Defendant filed a reply.

        On July 22, 2026, Plaintiff filed (1) a Motion to Compel Further Responses to First Set of Requests for Production (Res. no. 4356) and (2) a Motion to Compel Further Responses to and Compliance with Fifth Set of Inspection Demands (Res. no. 4819). On August 28, 2026, Defendants filed oppositions to both Motions to Compel Further Responses. On September 3, 2026, Plaintiff filed a consolidated reply as to both Motions to Compel Further Responses.

II. ARGUMENTS

A. Motion for Protective Order filed on May 22, 2026

        Defendant Reliable Properties moves for a protective order under Code of Civil Procedure sections 2031.060 and 2017.020. and asks that the Court issue a protective order limiting the electronically stored information it must produce in response to Plaintiff’s RFP Nos. 13-18 and Plaintiff’s Request to Inspect Email Accounts issued on April 23, 2026.  Defendant argues parties agreed after an IDC with the Court on October 8, 2025, that Defendant would run a keyword search of terms relating to CAM, common area maintenance, services provided, and synonyms of the services provided. Defendant argues it understood the search terms would only relate to the specific property leased by Plaintiff and at most, the shopping center in which that property was located.

        Defendant argues Plaintiff has unreasonably refused to limit the keyword searches to the leased premises within a reasonable time period. Defendant suggests that any keyword search of its emails be limited in time to two years (instead of ten years) focusing on the one subject shopping center in which Plaintiff’s unit is located.

        Defendant argues Plaintiff’s proposed search would also be unreasonably time consuming and expensive. Defendant argues the cost of the proposed document review would be approximately $150,000 to more than $400,000.  Defendant asks that the Court impose sanctions in the amount of $25,155.95.

B. Opposition filed August 28, 2026

            Plaintiff  argues that discovery concerning Defendants’ handling of CAM expenses at other properties is relevant because one of the central issues in this action is why Plaintiff’s CAM expenses increased so dramatically after execution of the Lease. Plaintiff points out that the cause of that increase may have arisen from decisions or practices affecting multiple properties, not merely the subject Shopping Center. By way of example, Plaintiff identifies evidence that Defendants procured no-deductible insurance policies covering multiple properties and passed the increased costs through to tenants as CAM expenses. Communications concerning such decisions may not identify Plaintiff’s property by street address or otherwise expressly reference the subject Shopping Center yet could still bear directly on the reasons Plaintiff’s CAM charges increased and on Defendants’ intent in providing the substantially lower CAM estimate during lease negotiations. Plaintiff therefore contends that restricting the search from the outset to communications expressly tied to the subject Shopping Center risks excluding otherwise relevant evidence concerning Defendants’ CAM practices and the reasons for the increase alleged here. Plaintiff argues Defendant failed to meet and confer prior to filing the motion for protective order. Plaintiff argues Defendant violated the parties’ stipulation when it refused to perform the keyword search without “proximity” modifiers. Plaintiff argues it has already established that Defendant was withholding documents based on Defendant’s document production in a different lawsuit by a different tenant for CAM overcharges.

        To address Defendant’s overbreadth concerns, Plaintiff suggested a two-step process to run the search and produce responsive emails. Plaintiff suggested that the unlimited keyword search be run first and a report listing all responsive documents be generated, a “hit report” listing responsive documents by search term. Plaintiff suggested that modifiers limiting search results would only be applied to those terms that generated an inordinate number of hits. Plaintiff also offered to pay for generation of the hit report.

        Plaintiff argues the searches should not be limited to the shopping center in question. Plaintiff argues the search should not be so limited, because the definition of relevance under Code of Civil Procedure section 2017.010 is broad. Plaintiff argues the reason for the dramatic increase in CAM charges could apply to multiple properties owned by Defendants and emails discussing those reasons may not necessarily contain references to the shopping center in question.

            Plaintiff argues Defendant’s bad faith is evidenced by their filing the motion for protective order six days before the scheduled hearing date and after expiration of the statutory deadline. Plaintiff argues Defendant refused to stipulate to a trial continuance despite its conduct necessitating the continuance. Plaintiff argues Defendant should be forced to pay for the entire process of running the keyword search, generating the hit report and reviewing the documents to respond to the discovery.

        Plaintiff asks that the Court impose sanctions against Defendant and defense counsel.

C.  Reply filed September 3, 2026 

            Defendant argues the keyword search it has proposed is reasonable. Defendant maintains the agreement arrived at during the October 2025 IDC focused on the subject shopping center, not every retail property owned by Defendant, and the list of search terms would be created by reference to the list of tenants and service providers at the subject shopping center. Defendant argues it was Plaintiff who impermissibly deviated from the parties’ agreement by refusing to limit the search to the subject shopping center.

        Defendant argues its motion for protective order was filed in response to Plaintiff’s Fifth Set of Inspection Demands (RFP Nos. 13-18), which were not limited in time or scope. Defendant argues the parties’ agreement to limit production to a keyword search of any kind justifies granting this motion for protective order. Defendant argues it has already conducted the proposed unlimited keyword search as to one of its custodians and the results were inordinately high, just as Defendant suspected it would be. Defendant argues it would be more efficient to run a proximity-limited search first and then if need be, proposed additional discovery or increasing the proximity modifiers.

        Defendant also denies purposefully concealing documents. Defendant argues any failure to produce responsive documents was inadvertent. Defendant argues any disparity in the hit report from employee Hubie Byrnes’ inbox and those emails produced in discovery is attributable to the hit report covering all emails up to the present.

        Defendant argues its price estimates to perform the keyword search, generate the hit report and review the documents is not exaggerated. Defendant argues it is not required to use the third-party suggested by Plaintiff to perform these tasks.

III. LEGAL STANDARDS

        “When an inspection, copying, testing, or sampling of documents, tangible things, places, or electronically stored information has been demanded, the party to whom the demand has been directed, and any other party or affected person, may promptly move for a protective order. This motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.” (Code of Civ. Proc., §2031.060, subd. (a).)

        “The court, for good cause shown, may make any order that justice requires to protect any party or other person from unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression, or undue burden and expense. This protective order may include, but is not limited to, one or more of the following directions: (1) That all or some of the items or categories of items in the demand need not be produced or made available at all…” (Code of Civ. Proc., §2031.060, subd. (b).)

        The scope of discovery is broad but it is not unlimited. (Haniff v. Supr. Ct. (2017) 9 Cal.App.5th 191, 205-206.) “The court shall limit the scope of discovery if it determines that the burden, expense, or intrusiveness of that discovery clearly outweighs the likelihood that the information sought will lead to the discovery of admissible evidence. The court may make this determination pursuant to a motion for protective order by a party or other affected person.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2017.020, subd. (a).)

 

IV. DISCUSSION

A. Procedural Issues

        The underlying discovery at issue are the Requests for Production Nos. 13-18 (Set One). The discovery was served on September 13, 2024. (Hayes Dec. ISO Plaintiff’s Opposition, Ex. 1.) Defendant served responses on November 12, 2024. (Tate Dec., ¶9.) Defendant filed supplemental responses to the RFPs (Set One) on March 14, 2025. (Id. at ¶10.) Defendant produced 48,000 pages of documents in response to the RFPs on November 13, 2025. (Id. at ¶11.) After parties met and conferred on the supplemental responses and production, Defendant served further supplemental responses on May 21, 2025. (Id. at ¶13.)

        Parties then engaged in an IDC on October 8, 2025, and agreed to utilize mutually agreed-upon keyword searches to narrow the universe of documents Defendant would be required to review. (Hayes Decl., ¶ 8, Ex. 6, pp. 494–495 [IDC Tr. 21:28–22:17].) The parties were to meet and confer further regarding the specific search terms and implementation of that procedure. Their subsequent discussions ultimately resulted in a disagreement concerning the scope and manner of conducting the keyword searches.

            On April 23, 2026, Plaintiff served its Fifth Set of Inspection Demands formally seeking the disputed ESI search. (Id. at ¶ 21, Ex. 6.) Defendant responded with a meet-and-confer communication on May 18, 2026, and thereafter filed the present motion for protective order.

            The Court finds the meet-and-confer requirement satisfied. The parties engaged in extensive discussions concerning this discovery, participated in an IDC, and continued their efforts after disagreement arose concerning implementation of the keyword-search procedure. Under these circumstances, Defendant sufficiently complied with Code of Civil Procedure sections 2016.040 and 2031.060 before filing the motion.

 

B. Defendant Has Not Established Good Cause for the Requested Protective Order

            The scope of discovery is broad and encompasses matters relevant to the subject matter involved in the pending action or reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence. (Code of Civ. Proc., § 2017.010.) The Court may nevertheless limit discovery where the burden, expense, or intrusiveness clearly outweighs the likelihood that the information sought will lead to the discovery of admissible evidence. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2017.020, subd. (a).) On a motion for protective order, Defendant bears the burden of establishing good cause for the requested restriction.

 

1. IDC Stipulation

            At the IDC, the parties agreed to utilize mutually agreed-upon keyword searches as a means of identifying potentially responsive electronically stored information. The IDC discussion, however, was directed to CAM-related discovery concerning the subject Shopping Center, including CAM information relating to other tenants at that location. The Court does not read the IDC transcript as reflecting an agreement that Plaintiff could obtain CAM discovery concerning tenants at every property owned or managed by Defendants. At the same time, the parties did not expressly agree upon the precise mechanics by which the keyword search would be limited, including whether proximity modifiers or other property-specific restrictions would be applied before the initial search was run.

            The present dispute therefore is not properly characterized as Defendant attempting to rewrite an agreement that plainly authorized an unrestricted search of all properties. Rather, the parties agreed upon a keyword-search process within the context of the subject Shopping Center, but did not resolve how that process would be implemented if particular search terms generated communications concerning other properties. Defendant contends that property-specific limitations were implicit in the IDC discussion; Plaintiff contends that the search terms should first be run without such limitations and then refined based upon the resulting hit counts. The transcript supports Defendant’s position that the IDC discussion generally concerned CAM discovery relating to the subject Shopping Center, but it does not establish that the parties agreed to property-specific search restrictions or Defendant’s subsequently proposed proximity protocol.

            The parties’ conduct following the IDC is consistent with that narrower reading. On October 21, 2025, defense counsel described the compromise as one under which, “instead of looking for every document responsive to the overbroad requests my client would instead run search terms.” (Hayes Decl., ¶ 9, Ex. 7, p. 516.) That statement confirms the parties’ agreement to use keyword searches as a substitute for unrestricted document-by-document review, but it does not resolve whether every search term had to be paired from the outset with a property-specific modifier. Thereafter, Plaintiff proposed search terms on December 22, 2025. When defense counsel expressed concern that those terms might generate an excessive volume of ESI, Plaintiff proposed obtaining a hit report first so the parties could determine which particular terms actually required refinement. According to Plaintiff’s evidence, defense counsel agreed during the January 20, 2026, conference to consult with his e-discovery vendor concerning whether such a report could be generated. (Hayes Decl., ¶ 11.) Defendant later proposed, on February 23, 2026, applying proximity modifiers before generation of the hit report. (Hayes Decl., ¶ 12, Ex. 9.)

            Upon consideration of the IDC transcript, the parties’ subsequent conduct, and the present record, the Court concludes that Defendant reasonably understood the IDC discussion to concern CAM discovery relating to the subject Shopping Center rather than every property owned or managed by Defendants. The Court therefore rejects any broader interpretation of the stipulation.

 

2. Plaintiff has established a sufficient basis for the initial search without proximity modifiers

 

            With that said, Defendant has not established good cause for imposing its proposed proximity restrictions before the initial search is conducted.  The IDC did not resolve that particular implementation issue, and Plaintiff has separately made a sufficient showing that some communications concerning multi-property CAM practices may be relevant even if they do not expressly identify the subject Shopping Center. The appropriate course is therefore to enforce the agreed keyword-search process within that clarified scope and address overbreadth through the staged hit-report and refinement procedure.

            The Court also finds Plaintiff has sufficiently established the relevance of the discovery beyond communications expressly identifying the subject Shopping Center. Plaintiff alleges that Defendants represented during the negotiation of the September 2018 Lease that monthly CAM charges would be approximately $4,795 but subsequently charged approximately $8,096.90 after Plaintiff commenced occupancy. Plaintiff is entitled to discovery reasonably calculated to reveal why the CAM estimate provided during lease negotiations differed so substantially from the CAM charges subsequently imposed.

            That inquiry is not necessarily confined to documents expressly mentioning Plaintiff or the subject Shopping Center. Evidence concerning Defendants’ calculation, estimation, allocation, budgeting, or treatment of CAM expenses at other properties during the relevant period could bear upon whether the CAM figure provided to Plaintiff reflected an ordinary estimating error, a particular methodology or business practice, or an intentional understatement designed to induce execution of the Lease. Such evidence may also bear upon intent and the credibility of Defendants’ explanation for the discrepancy. The Court need not determine at the discovery stage whether such evidence will ultimately be admissible. Plaintiff has demonstrated a sufficient factual nexus to permit discovery directed toward determining whether such evidence exists.

 

3. Burden and Overbreadth

            Nor does the potential breadth of the search require the Court to determine prospectively which search terms are too broad or which proximity modifiers should be imposed before any hit-count information is available. Plaintiff has proposed a practical mechanism for addressing that concern: run the search terms without proximity modifiers, generate a hit report, and then refine those terms that actually produce an inordinate number of results. Plaintiff’s evidence establishes that it repeatedly proposed that procedure and ultimately offered to bear the expense of generating the initial hit report. Although the Court does not find that this particular two-stage procedure was itself part of the IDC stipulation, the Court finds it a reasonable means of implementing the agreed keyword-search process while addressing Defendant’s legitimate concern that some of the search terms may prove overly broad. The federal ESI authorities cited by Plaintiff likewise recognize the utility of obtaining hit-count information and testing proposed terms before determining whether particular search terms require modification.

            Nor is the Court persuaded that the number of anticipated keyword hits, standing alone, establishes undue burden sufficient to require Defendant’s proposed proximity restrictions at the outset. Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.060 authorizes a protective order upon a showing of “unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression, or undue burden and expense,” and section 2017.020 requires limitation where the burden, expense, or intrusiveness clearly outweighs the likelihood that the information sought will lead to the discovery of admissible evidence. Defendant has established that an unrestricted search may generate a substantial number of hits, but that does not establish that Defendant must review and produce every resulting document or that the appropriate solution is to impose Defendant’s proposed restrictions before the parties know what the searches actually produce.

Importantly, Plaintiff does not propose that Defendant review and produce every document generated by the initial keyword search without further limitation. Plaintiff proposes a two-stage procedure. Defendant will first run the search terms and generate a hit report. The parties will then use the actual results to identify search terms producing an inordinate number of hits and meet and confer regarding appropriate refinements. Plaintiff has offered to bear the expense associated with generating the initial hit report.

            That procedure substantially addresses Defendant’s asserted burden while preserving the possibility that the initial search will identify relevant communications that Defendant’s proposed proximity modifiers would exclude. As discussed above, the Court agrees with Defendant that the IDC did not authorize unrestricted discovery concerning every tenant at every property owned or managed by Defendants. Plaintiff has nevertheless independently established that certain communications concerning multi-property CAM practices may be relevant to the claims here, even though those communications may not expressly identify the subject Shopping Center. Imposing a property-specific proximity requirement at the outset could therefore exclude relevant information before the parties have any basis to determine the practical effect of the limitation.

            The hit-report procedure also avoids requiring the Court to speculate in advance about which search terms or communications will ultimately warrant further review. The breadth of a particular search term can be evaluated more intelligently after the parties know whether that term generates dozens, hundreds, or tens of thousands of hits and, where appropriate, the nature of those hits. The potentially large universe of electronically stored information therefore does not, on this record, establish that Defendant’s proposed proximity modifiers must be imposed before the initial search is conducted.

            The Court is likewise unpersuaded that Defendant’s proposed approach should be adopted merely because it may be more efficient. Efficiency is relevant to the burden analysis, but it does not independently define the permissible scope of discovery. The relevant questions are whether the information sought falls within the scope of permissible discovery and whether Defendant has demonstrated that the burden, expense, or intrusiveness of the proposed procedure clearly outweighs the likelihood that it will lead to the discovery of admissible evidence. Plaintiff has established a sufficient basis for conducting the initial search without Defendant’s proposed proximity modifiers, and Defendant has not demonstrated that generating the initial hit report—particularly where Plaintiff has offered to bear that expense—creates undue burden or expense warranting the protective order requested.

            The Court does, however, find a temporal limitation appropriate. The alleged misrepresentation occurred in connection with negotiation of the September 2018 Lease. Discovery beginning two years before those negotiations provides a reasonable period from which Plaintiff may investigate Defendants’ CAM practices preceding the alleged representation, including whether the CAM estimate supplied during the negotiations was consistent with Defendants’ contemporaneous practices. To the extent CAM-related decisions or practices affecting the subject Shopping Center were made on a multi-property basis, relevant communications concerning those decisions are not excluded merely because they also concern other properties. Discovery thereafter is relevant to Plaintiff’s allegation that the CAM charges ultimately imposed substantially exceeded the amount represented during the negotiations and to Defendants’ explanation for that increase. Accordingly, the initial keyword search shall encompass the period beginning September 1, 2016, through the present.

            Defendant shall conduct the agreed keyword searches without the proximity modifiers proposed in Defendant’s February 23, 2026, email and shall generate a hit report identifying the results by search term and custodian. After generation of the hit report, the parties shall promptly meet and confer regarding any search terms producing an inordinate number of results and whether reasonable refinements are appropriate. The Court is not presently ordering Defendant to conduct substantive review or produce every document identified by the initial search. The purpose of the initial search is to provide the parties with objective information from which reasonable refinements, if necessary, can be made rather than imposing speculative restrictions before the universe of potentially responsive documents is known. Defendant’s Motion for Protective Order is therefore DENIED.

 

C. Sanctions

“Except as provided in subdivision (i), the court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion for a protective order, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (Code of Civ. Proc., §2031.060, subd. (h).)

            Although Defendant does not prevail on the Motion for Protective Order, the Court finds Defendant acted with substantial justification in seeking judicial resolution of the parties’ genuine dispute concerning the scope of the October 2025 IDC agreement and the burden associated with the proposed searches. The parties’ competing interpretations of the agreement and the appropriate scope of ESI discovery were reasonably subject to dispute. Accordingly, the requests for sanctions are DENIED.

D. Conclusion

Defendant’s Motion for Protective Order is DENIED. Defendant’s Request for Sanctions is DENIED.

 

(2) PLAINTIFF’S MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES TO AND COMPLIANCE WITH FIFTH SET OF INSPECTION DEMANDS TO DEFENDANTS; REQUEST FOR SANCTIONS [Res. No. -4819]

 

I. ARGUMENTS

A. Plaintiff’s Motion

        Plaintiff moves to compel compliance and for further responses to its Fifth Set of Inspection Demands pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.240, which is also the subject of Defendant’s Motion for Protective Order discussed above. Plaintiff argues Defendant was obligated to comply with the Fifth Set of Inspection Demands based on parties’ stipulation entered into at the October 8, 2025, IDC. Plaintiff argues Defendant instead filed a Motion for Protective Order without first meeting and conferring. Plaintiff asks that the Court order Defendant to comply with section 2031.240 in responding to the Fifth Set of Inspection Demands. Plaintiff asks that the Court order Defendant to perform the search of the 12 custodians’ email accounts based on the 78 keywords identified by Plaintiff without proximity limitation. Plaintiff asserts the same arguments raised in Plaintiff’s opposition to the Motion for Protective Order.

 

B. Opposition filed on August 28, 2026

        Defendant denies refusing to comply with the stipulation to do a keyword search of its emails. Defendant argues it disagreed with Plaintiff’s characterization of the terms of the stipulation. Defendant argues it never agreed to keyword search the emails without regard to the location. Defendant argues its understanding from the October 8, 2025, IDC was that the Shopping Center and the Premises were at issue only and any keyword search would therefore be limited to those locations and the tenants and service providers at those locations. Defendant argues Plaintiff’s refusal to limit the keyword search with proximity modifiers impermissibly expands the discovery to cover other properties owned by Defendant, which are not relevant to this action.

        Defendant argues Plaintiff has not established good cause for the discovery sought. Defendant raises the same arguments raised in support of its Motion for Protective Order.

C. Consolidated Reply filed on September 3, 2026

        Plaintiff argues that it filed two Motions to Compel Further on July 22, 2026, and as to both Motions Plaintiff only seeks two orders: (1) an order governing the keyword search submitted on August 28, 2026, with Plaintiff’s opposition to Defendants’ motion for protective order and (2) an order requiring Defendants to strictly comply with Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.240. Plaintiff refers the Court to its opposition to the Motion for Protective Order for its reply arguments.

II. LEGAL STANDARDS

       Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 2031.310, subdivision (a), parties may move for a further response to interrogatories where an answer to the requests are evasive or incomplete or where an objection is without merit or too general. (Code of Civ. Proc., §2031.310, subd. (a).) The moving party is entitled to sanctions if it prevails, unless the court finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust. (Code of Civ. Proc., §2031.310, subd. (c).)

        Where a party is seeking to compel further responses to requests for document production, the burden is on the moving party to show both relevance to the subject matter and specific facts justifying discovery. (Code of Civ. Proc., §2031.310, subd. (b)(1); Kirkland v. Supr. Ct. (2002) 95 Cal.App.4th 92, 98 (“Once good cause was shown, the burden shifted to Kirkland to justify his objection”); Glenfed Develop. Corp. v. Supr. Ct. (1997) 53 Cal.App.4th 1113, 1117.) Once good cause is established by the moving party, the burden then shifts to the responding party to justify any objections made to document disclosure. (Hartbrodt v. Burke (1996, 2nd Dist.) 42 Cal.App.4th 168, 172-174.)

        Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.240 sets forth the standards for responses to requests for production. Section 2031.240 provides:

“(a) If only part of an item or category of item in a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling is objectionable, the response shall contain a statement of compliance, or a representation of inability to comply with respect to the remainder of that item or category.

(b) If the responding party objects to the demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling of an item or category of item, the response shall do both of the following:

(1) Identify with particularity any document, tangible thing, land, or electronically stored information falling within any category of item in the demand to which an objection is being made.

(2) Set forth clearly the extent of, and the specific ground for, the objection. If an objection is based on a claim of privilege, the particular privilege invoked shall be stated. If an objection is based on a claim that the information sought is protected work product under Chapter 4 (commencing with Section 2018.010), that claim shall be expressly asserted.

(c)(1) If an objection is based on a claim of privilege or a claim that the information sought is protected work product, the response shall provide sufficient factual information for other parties to evaluate the merits of that claim, including, if necessary, a privilege log.

(2) It is the intent of the Legislature to codify the concept of a privilege log as that term is used in California case law. Nothing in this subdivision shall be construed to constitute a substantive change in case law.” (Code of Civ. Proc., §2031.240.)

 

III. DISCUSSION

A. Timeliness

        Plaintiff moves to compel further responses to the RFPs (Set Five) served on April 23, 2026. Defendants served objections on May 22, 2026. (Hayes Dec., Ex. 3.) Defendants agreed to extend the deadline to file any motions to compel to these responses to July 22, 2026. (Id. at Ex. 11.) The motion was timely filed on July 22, 2026.

 

B. Request for Judicial Notice

        Plaintiff’s Request for Judicial Notice is GRANTED as to Exhibit 4 and DENIED as to Exhibits 6 and 15. Exhibit 4 is a copy of the complaint in 23PSCV01848 and judicially noticeable under Evidence Code sections 451 and 452. Exhibits 6 and 15 are transcripts from the October 8, 2025, IDC and from a hearing in 23PSCV01848. Neither documents falls within any category of judicially noticeable under Evidence Code sections 451 and 452.

 

C. Plaintiff Has Established Good Cause to Compel Further Responses

            For the same reasons discussed in connection with the protective order, Plaintiff has established good cause for the discovery sought. The alleged discrepancy between the CAM amount represented during the lease negotiations and the amount subsequently charged supplies a sufficient factual basis for discovery concerning Defendants’ CAM practices, calculations, and communications during the relevant period, including practices involving other properties that may bear upon Defendants’ intent, methodology, or explanation for the CAM figure provided to Plaintiff.

            The Court’s ruling does not rest upon Plaintiff’s broader interpretation of the October 2025 IDC agreement. As discussed above, the IDC concerned CAM discovery relating to the subject Shopping Center, including other tenants at that location, and did not constitute an agreement authorizing discovery concerning every property owned or managed by Defendants. The IDC likewise did not resolve whether Defendant could impose its proposed proximity modifiers before conducting the initial keyword search. Plaintiff has independently established good cause for conducting the initial search without those modifiers because certain communications concerning multi-property CAM practices may bear upon the claims in this action even though they do not expressly identify the subject Shopping Center.

            Plaintiff’s proposed staged procedure provides the appropriate mechanism for addressing overbreadth. The initial search will identify the number and distribution of hits. Search terms producing an inordinate number of results may thereafter be refined through the meet-and-confer process. This permits refinement based upon actual information rather than assumptions concerning what the searches may produce or what information may ultimately prove relevant.

            Plaintiff’s motion is therefore GRANTED IN PART to the extent it seeks implementation of the keyword-search procedure described above. Defendant shall conduct the search for the period September 1, 2016, through the present and generate the initial hit report. The parties shall thereafter meet and confer regarding appropriate refinement of search terms producing an inordinate number of hits and Defendant’s resulting review and production obligations. Any production or withholding of responsive documents following that process shall comply with Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.240, including the provision of an appropriate privilege log for responsive documents withheld on grounds of privilege or work product.

 

D. Sanctions

            The Court finds the parties’ disagreement concerning the scope and implementation of the IDC agreement was substantially justified. The requests for monetary sanctions are DENIED.

 

E. Conclusion

            Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel Further Responses to the Fifth Set of Inspection Demands and Compliance with Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.240 is GRANTED IN PART as set forth above. Plaintiff’s Request for Sanctions is DENIED.

25STCV04554 Marrian Myungok Lee v. Spark Family Operations, LLC, et al.

Friday, September 11, 2026

TENTATIVE ORDER GRANTING MOTION FOR LEAVE TO AMEND COMPLAINT [Res. No. -5886]

 

                                                                                         I.         BACKGROUND

      The complaint alleges that Defendant Spark Family Operations, LLC dba Sunny Hills Assisted Living (“Sunny Hills”), a residential care facility, neglected caring for its patient, Plaintiff Marrian Myungok Lee (“Lee”), causing her to develop severe health conditions and pass away. This action is brought by Lee (through her Successor-in-Interest and daughter Plaintiff Joanne Chu (“Chu”)), and Chu in her individual capacity (collectively, “Plaintiffs”).

      On February 18, 2025, Plaintiffs filed their Complaint against Sunny Hills and Does 1 through 40, inclusive, asserting causes of action for: (1) elder abuse/neglect (Welf. Inst. Code 15600, et seq.); (2) negligence; and (3) wrongful death.

      On April 24, 2025, Sunny Hills filed its Answer.

      On March 4, 2026, Plaintiffs amended their Complaint to substitute Defendant Steve Yong Jin Cho (“Cho”) for Doe 1 and Select Care Home Health Services, Inc. (“Select Care”) for Doe 21.

      On March 24, 2026, Plaintiffs dismissed the first cause of action.

      Plaintiffs now move for leave to file a First Amended Complaint (“FAC”).

                                                                                          II.        ARGUMENTS

A.     Motion filed July 9, 2026

      The proposed FAC adds additional facts concerning the conduct of Cho and Select Care. Counsel for Sunny Hills stated that the defendant will not oppose the motion. Defendants failed to file an opposition. 

 

                                                                                        III.       LEGAL STANDARD

      “The court may …, in its discretion, after notice to the adverse party, allow, upon any terms as may be just, an amendment to any pleading or proceeding in other particulars; and may upon like terms allow an answer to be made after the time limited by this code.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (a)(1).)

      California Rules of Court, rule 3.1324(a) states: “A motion to amend a pleading before trial must: ¶ (1) Include a copy of the proposed amendment or amended pleading, which must be serially numbered to differentiate it from previous pleadings or amendments; ¶ (2) State what allegations in the previous pleading are proposed to be deleted, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the deleted allegations are located; and ¶ (3) State what allegations are proposed to be added to the previous pleading, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the additional allegations are located.”

      “A separate declaration must accompany the motion and must specify: ¶ (1) The effect of the amendment; ¶ (2) Why the amendment is necessary and proper; ¶ (3) When the facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered; and ¶ (4) The reasons why the request for amendment was not made earlier.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1324(b).)

      “‘Courts must apply a policy of liberality in permitting amendments at any stage of the proceeding, including during trial, when no prejudice to the opposing party is shown.... “However, ‘“even if a good amendment is proposed in proper form, unwarranted delay in presenting it may—of itself—be a valid reason for denial.”’”’ [Citations.]” (Duchrow v. Forrest (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 1359, 1377.)

                                                                                                IV.       DISCUSSION

      Plaintiffs have attached a copy of the proposed FAC, which signals the changes in bold font. (Motion, Declaration of Destiny M. Verdugo, Esq. (“Verdugo Decl.”), ¶ 6; Exh. A.)

      In addition, Plaintiffs’ counsel attests to the following facts. The “proposed FAC does not add any new causes of action, does not change the parties as already substituted by Doe amendment, and do [sic] not alter the fundamental nature of the action. Rather, the amendments clarify CHO’s and SELECT CARE’s roles, knowledge, duties, conduct, agency relationships, and involvement in the care and oversight at issue, and conform the pleadings to information obtained after the initial filing. The amendments also make non-substantive edits for clarity and to correct typographical and formatting issues. Leave to amend is necessary and proper to ensure the operative pleading accurately reflects the facts discovered regarding CHO and SELECT CARE and to promote resolution on the merits. Plaintiffs could not have included the proposed factual clarifications earlier because CHO and SELECT CARE were unidentified at the time of the original Complaint filed on February 18, 2025. Upon learning CHO' s and SELECT CARE’s identities and roles, promptly filed Doe amendments and now seek to conform factual allegations accordingly. No party will suffer prejudice.” (Verdugo Decl., 7.)

      Based on the above, the Court finds that Plaintiffs have complied with the California Rules of Court requirements. No opposition has been filed arguing against the filing of the FAC. In addition, Plaintiffs have not unduly delayed in bringing the motion.

Therefore, the Court grants Plaintiffs’ request to file the FAC.

Notwithstanding the above, for clarity purposes, the Court denies Plaintiffs’ request to deem the proposed FAC filed. Instead, the Court will require Plaintiffs to file the pleading separately.

                                                                                          V.        CONCLUSION

      Based on the foregoing, the Motion for Leave to File First Amended Complaint is GRANTED IN PART and DENIED IN PART as follows. The request to file the First Amended Complaint is GRANTED. The request to deem the proposed First Amended Complaint filed is DENIED. Plaintiffs are ordered to separately file and serve their First Amended Complaint within five (5) days of this ruling.


Case Number: 25STCV24344    Hearing Date: September 11, 2026    Dept: 414

25STCV24344 Taskey v. Benjamin Boston, et al.

Friday, September 11, 2026

TENTATIVE ORDER DENYING PLAINTIFF’S MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES FROM DEFENDANTS, BENJAMIN BOSTON, LEXINGTON PLAZA LLC, ROXBURYLANE, L.P., KATHY SMALL DBA L.A. RESIDENCE, AND ELLIOT HYLAND, TO FORM INTERROGATORIES, SET ONE; REQUEST FOR SANCTIONS  [Res. No. -0466]

 

                                                                                         I.         BACKGROUND

      The third amended complaint alleges that Defendants Benjamin Boston, Lexington Plaza LLC, Roxburylane, L.P., Kathy Small dba L.A. Residence, and Elliot Hyland are debtors liable on a claim and judgment Plaintiff obtained in a prior action. Plaintiff alleges that Defendants have improperly routed judgment-debtor-derived rent proceeds and rent-payment rights to Sky Properties Inc., obstructing Plaintiff’s ability to collect on the judgment allegedly owned. Plaintiff alleges 3 causes of action for actual voidable transfer, constructive voidable transfer, and declaratory relief.      

                                                                                                 II.        DISCUSSION

      A motion to compel further responses to form interrogatories is proper where the requesting party believes the responses are evasive and incomplete, that an objection is without merit, or an exercise of the option to produce documents is unwarranted. (Code Civ. Proc., sections 2030.300 subd. (a).) The motion must be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration. (Id. subd. (b).)      

      Plaintiff moves to compel further responses from Defendants Benjamin Boston, Lexington Plaza LLC, Roxbury Lane, L.P., Kathy Small dba L.A. Residence, and Elliot Hyland to Form Interrogatories, Set One, Nos. 15.1 and 17.1 and to compel further responses from Defendant Elliot Hyland to Form Interrogatories, Set One, No. 2.5.

A.     Improperly Combined Motion

      As a preliminary matter, the Court notes that there is no legal authority or rule of Court permitting Plaintiff to improperly combine five separate motions to be heard together absent a Court order permitting such.  Multiple motions should not be combined into a single filing.¿(See¿Govt. Code,¿§ 70617(a)(4) [setting forth the required filing fee for each motion, application, or any other paper or request requiring a hearing];¿see¿also¿Weil & Brown, Civil Procedure Before Trial, [8:1140.1] at 8F-60 (The Rutter Group 2011)¿[“Motions to compel compliance with separate discovery requests ordinarily should be filed separately.”].) The California Rules of Court only permit the combining of the papers in support of the same motion.  (Rules of Court, Rule 3.1112(c).)  Rather, each motion requires a separate notice, a motion, and memorandum in support.  (Rules of Court, Rule 3.1112(a).) 

      Combining multiple motions under the guise of one motion with one hearing reservation manipulates the Court Reservation System and unfairly jumps ahead of other litigants. Moreover, Plaintiff does not dispute that the Court has previously admonished her that discovery motions must be filed separately. (Reply, p. 13:9-12.) This alone is grounds to deny the motion.

 

B.     Timeliness

      “Unless notice of this motion is given within 45 days of the service of the verified response, or any supplemental verified response, or on or before any specific later date to which the propounding party and the responding party have agreed in writing, the propounding party waives any right to compel a further response to the interrogatories.” (Code Civ. Proc., sections 2030.300 subd. (c).)

      Here, Defendants served responses to the Form Interrogatories, Set One, on March 30, 2026. (Taskey Decl., Exs. A-E.) However, notice of the instant motion was not given until August 17, 2026, well after the statutory 45-day deadline. Although Plaintiff asserts that the March 30, 2026, responses were not verified, in the opposition, Defendants submit evidence demonstrating that verifications were served along with the substantive responses on March 30, 2026. (Mozer Decl., Exs. B-F.) Thus, the motion is untimely, and Plaintiff has waived any right to compel a further response to the interrogatories. (Code Civ. Proc., sections 2030.300 subd. (c).) Based on the foregoing, the motion is DENIED.

 

C.     Request for Sanctions

      As the motion has been denied, Plaintiff’s request for sanctions is likewise DENIED. Defendants also request sanctions against Plaintiff for unsuccessfully bringing this motion. (Opp., p. 7:5-7.) However, Defendants make the request only in passing and do not specify the amount of sanctions sought or provide any description of the time incurred in opposing the motion. Under these circumstances, Defendants’ request for sanctions is DENIED.

                                                                                              III.       CONCLUSION

      Plaintiff’s motion to compel further responses to form interrogatories is DENIED.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING MOTION TO DEEM REQUESTS FOR ADMISSION ADMITTED (Res. No. -9844)

 

I.       BACKGROUND

The complaint alleges Defendants, Plaintiff’s neighbors, deprived Plaintiff from utilizing public roads and sideways, engaged in overly loud nuisance activities, improperly operated a mobile car wash, defamed Plaintiff and generally harassed Plaintiff. Plaintiff moves for an order deeming Requests for Admission (Set Two), RFA Nos. 29 through 49 admitted against Defendant Lizette Navarro.

      A response to requests for admission is due within 30 days after service. (Code Civ. Proc., §2033.250, subd. (a).) “If a party to whom requests for admission are directed fails to serve a timely response, the following rules apply:  (a) the party to whom the requests for admission are directed waives any objection to the requests. . .(b) [t]he requesting party may move for an order that the genuineness of any documents and the truth of any matters specified in the  requests be deemed admitted. . .” (Code Civ.       Based on the foregoing, Plaintiff’s Motion to Deem Requests for Admission Admitted is GRANTED.
