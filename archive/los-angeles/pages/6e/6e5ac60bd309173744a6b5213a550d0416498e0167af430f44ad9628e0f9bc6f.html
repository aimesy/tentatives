Case Number: 22STCV37608    Hearing Date: July 1, 2026    Dept: 515

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: Hearing on Plaintiff’s Motion to Vacate Stipulated Protective Order

 

Plaintiff’s Motion to Vacate Stipulated Protective Order is denied.

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff John Doe J.R. (Plaintiff) filed this action against Los Angeles Unified School District (LAUSD), former LAUSD employee Darryl Lott, and Doe defendants. Plaintiff alleges that Lott sexually abused him while he was a minor student at one of LAUSD’s schools.

The causes of action are: (1) negligent hiring, supervision, and retention; (2) negligent supervision of a minor; and (3) failure of a mandated reporter to report suspected child abuse under Penal Code section 11166.

On May 2, 2024, the Court entered the parties’ Stipulated Protective Order (Protective Order) to facilitate the exchange of discovery. Under the Protective Order, LAUSD designated Lott’s personnel records and the identities and employee numbers of certain school site administrators as confidential.

The Court granted LAUSD’s motion for summary judgment and entered judgment. The Court of Appeal affirmed on June 2, 2026, and Plaintiff filed a petition for rehearing the same day. The remittitur is scheduled to issue on August 3, 2026. (Carelli Decl. ¶¶ 2-4.)

Plaintiff filed a Motion to Vacate Stipulated Protective Order. LAUSD filed an Opposition.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

The perfecting of an appeal stays proceedings in the trial court upon the matters embraced in or affected by the judgment or order appealed from. (Code Civ. Proc., § 916, subd. (a).) During the pendency of the appeal, the trial court is divested of jurisdiction over any matter embraced in or affected by the appeal and may not proceed as to that matter until the appeal is determined. (Varian Medical Systems, Inc. v. Delfino (2005) 35 Cal.4th 180, 196-197.) Whether a matter is embraced in or affected by the appeal depends on whether the trial court proceeding would affect the effectiveness of the appeal. (Elsea v. Saberi (1992) 4 Cal.App.4th 625, 629.)

 

ANALYSIS

Plaintiff seeks to vacate the Protective Order on the grounds that this action has concluded and that continued confidentiality impairs his use of the discovery materials on appeal. The motion fails for two independent reasons: (1) the appeal remains pending, so the Court lacks jurisdiction to vacate or modify the Protective Order; and (2) even if the Court had jurisdiction, Plaintiff has not shown good cause to disturb it.

I. Jurisdiction During the Pending Appeal

Here, the appeal remains pending. Although the Court of Appeal affirmed the judgment on June 2, 2026, Plaintiff filed a petition for rehearing that same day, the remittitur is not set to issue until August 3, 2026, and Plaintiff may still seek review in the Supreme Court. (Carelli Decl. ¶ 4.)

The Protective Order governs the use of materials produced in discovery in this action, and those materials would again be at issue were the judgment disturbed on review. Vacating or modifying the Protective Order is therefore a matter embraced in or affected by the pending appeal. Accordingly, the Court is without jurisdiction to grant the relief Plaintiff seeks while the appeal remains pending. (Code Civ. Proc., § 916, subd. (a).)

II. Good Cause to Vacate the Protective Order

Even if the Court had jurisdiction, Plaintiff’s asserted grounds do not warrant vacating the Protective Order. Plaintiff’s premise that this action has concluded is incorrect, for the reasons stated above.

The Protective Order also provides its own procedures for the relief Plaintiff seeks. A party that disputes a confidential designation may challenge it, and a party that must file protected materials may file them under seal. (Protective Order ¶¶ 6, 17.) Plaintiff has not invoked those procedures or shown that they are inadequate. Accordingly, Plaintiff has not shown good cause to vacate or modify the Protective Order.

CONCLUSION

Plaintiff’s Motion to Vacate Stipulated Protective Order is denied.

Case Number: 24STCV31377    Hearing Date: July 1, 2026    Dept: 515

Below are tentative rulings on Defendant’s 7 motions in limine, which Defendant timely filed ahead of the initial FSC on 6/26/2026. These motions were timely filed pursuant to the Court’s Trial Preparation Order and CCP Section 1005.

Plaintiffs' seven motions in limine, some of which were filed after the FSC on 6/26/2026, are all untimely under the TPO and Section 1005. The Court therefore exercises its discretion and denies them as untimely.

 

Defendant’s MIL No. 1. To exclude evidence of insurance is denied. The motion is not specific enough for the Court to understand what insurance evidence Defendant seeks to exclude. Instead of identifying the specific evidence that the motion seeks to exclude, this motion in limine amounts to little more than a restatement of the law and general legal principles.  The moving party fails to demonstrate that there are no circumstances which would permit admission of this evidence.  The Court declines to rule on this motion in limine in a vacuum.  The moving party may at trial assert an objection to the introduction of specific evidence if and when this issue arises.

 

Defendant’s MIL No. 2 to exclude medical testimony and/or opinions from lay witnesses is denied. The motion is not specific enough for the Court to understand what lay testimony regarding medical issues Defendant seeks to exclude. Instead of identifying the specific evidence that the motion seeks to exclude, this motion in limine amounts to little more than a restatement of the law and general legal principles.  The moving party fails to demonstrate that there are no circumstances which would permit admission of this evidence.  The Court declines to rule on this motion in limine in a vacuum.  The moving party may at trial assert an objection to the introduction of specific evidence if and when this issue arises.

 

Defendant’s MIL No. 3 to exclude Plaintiff’s submission of hearsay documents including medical/legal reports, narratives, and billings is denied. The motion is not specific enough for the Court to understand what specific evidence Defendant seeks to exclude. Instead of identifying the specific evidence that the motion seeks to exclude, this motion in limine amounts to little more than a restatement of the law and general legal principles regarding hearsay.  The moving party fails to demonstrate that there are no circumstances which would permit admission of this evidence.  The Court declines to rule on this motion in limine in a vacuum.  The moving party may at trial assert an objection to the introduction of specific evidence if and when this issue arises.

 

Defendant’s MIL No. 4 to exclude evidence of healthcare medical bills other than the amount accepted by Plaintiffs’ healthcare providers as payment in full of said bills is deferred. As set forth in the Pebley case, while a plaintiff who elects to treat with lien providers may be considered an uninsured plaintiff and provide evidence of bills for past medical services, such a plaintiff needs to proffer expert testimony on the reasonable value of the services rendered. Pebley v. Santa Clara Organics, LLC (2018) 22 Cal. App. 5th 1266, 1278. It is unclear to this Court whether Plaintiff has any such evidence to provide at trial, and so the Court expects Plaintiffs’ counsel to provide an offer of proof of such evidence at the continued FSC.

Defendant’s MIL No. 5 to exclude Plaintiffs from referencing, commenting upon or introducing into evidence documents not produced in discovery is denied. The motion fails to identify any potential evidence Plaintiffs plan on introducing that was not disclosed in discovery.  Nor has the moving party attached any responses to interrogatories or other written discovery.  Thus, the record is devoid of evidence of what witnesses were disclosed and what witnesses were not disclosed in discovery.

Defendant’s MIL No. 6 to limit expert testimony of Plaintiffs’ experts to those opinions and conclusions testified to at deposition and to exclude testimony based upon materials not reviewed or provided at deposition is premature.

The Court understood from the parties’ counsel that Plaintiff has no designated retained experts in this case. Assuming that is correct, Plaintiff only has non-retained treating physicians testifying at trial. Defendant’s Kennemur objection is premature at this juncture.  The Court cannot anticipate at this time whether each and every possible opinion that Plaintiff may attempt to elicit from any non-retained expert will fall within the scope of their deposition testimony or potentially extend beyond opinions rendered during deposition.  The Court will discuss the procedure for dealing with Kennemur objections with counsel at the continued FSC.

Defendant’s MIL No. 7 to preclude testimony, evidence, and argument based upon the “reptile theory”  is granted in part and denied in part. Parties must avoid arguments that ask jurors to put themselves in the shoes of Plaintiffs or to act on the basis of some ill-defined community interests. As to specific questions to witnesses or arguments made at trial, the Court denies the motion and directs counsel to raise specific objections at trial.


Case Number: 25STCV09320    Hearing Date: July 1, 2026    Dept: 515

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: Hearing on Motion for Summary Adjudication

 

The Motion for Summary Adjudication filed by Ford Motor Company is denied.

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff John T Tuttle filed this action against Ford Motor Company (Ford) and Santa Monica Ford Lincoln (collectively "Defendants"), alleging that the 2021 Ford Explorer he purchased on May 20, 2021 (Subject Vehicle) had a defective 10R80 transmission that was susceptible to sudden and premature failure, and that Ford knew of the transmission defect before the sale but failed to disclose it.

The causes of action are: (1) Violation of Civil Code Section 1793.2, subdivision (d); (2) Violation of Civil Code Section 1793.2, subdivision (b); (3) Violation of Civil Code Section 1793.2, subdivision (a)(3); (4) Breach of the Implied Warranty of Merchantability; (5) Negligent Repair; and (6) Fraudulent Inducement – Concealment. The first four and the sixth causes of action are alleged against Ford; the fifth cause of action is alleged against Santa Monica Ford Lincoln.

Ford Motor Company filed a Motion for Summary Adjudication of the sixth cause of action for fraudulent inducement – concealment. Plaintiff filed an Opposition.

 

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

Plaintiff requests judicial notice of nineteen exhibits offered in support of the Opposition. (Evid. Code, §§ 451, 452, 459.)

Exhibits 1 through 17 are internal Ford documents and excerpts of the deposition testimony of Ford personnel, unsealed on February 24, 2026, in Avila v. Ford Motor Co. (N.D.Cal. 2026) 2026 WL 508364. The Court takes judicial notice of these exhibits as records of a court of the United States. (Evid. Code, § 452, subd. (d).) Exhibit 18 is a set of Ford technical service bulletins, and Exhibit 19 is Ford Safety Recall 22S27; both are publicly accessible records, and the Court takes judicial notice of them as well. (Evid. Code, § 452, subds. (c), (h).)

The Court takes judicial notice of the existence of these documents, but not of the truth of the matters asserted in them. (Lockley v. Law Office of Cantrell, Seipp & Anderson (2001) 91 Cal.App.4th 875, 882 [judicial notice of a court record establishes the record’s existence, not the truth of the matters asserted in it].)

 

LEGAL STANDARD

“A party is entitled to summary adjudication of a cause of action if there is no triable issue of material fact and the party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subds. (c) & (f)(1).) ‘A motion for summary adjudication shall be granted only if it completely disposes of a cause of action … .’ (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (f)(1).) A defendant is entitled to summary adjudication of a cause of action if she demonstrates that the plaintiff cannot establish one or more elements of the cause of action. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) [¶] A court considering a motion for summary adjudication must view the evidence and reasonable inferences from the evidence in the light most favorable to the opposing party, as on a motion for summary judgment. [Citation.]” (Borman v. Brown (2021) 59 Cal.App.5th 1048, 1055.)

To meet this burden of showing a cause of action cannot be established, a defendant must show not only “that the plaintiff does not possess needed evidence” but also that “the plaintiff cannot reasonably obtain needed evidence.”(Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 854.) The supporting evidence can be in the form of affidavits, declarations, admissions, depositions, answers to interrogatories, and matters of which judicial notice may be taken. (Id. at p. 855.) “Once the defendant . . . has met that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff . . . to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) The plaintiff may not merely rely on allegations or denials of its pleadings to show that a triable issue of material fact exists, but instead, “shall set forth the specific facts showing that a triable issue of material fact exists as to the cause of action.” (Ibid.) “If the plaintiff cannot do so, summary [adjudication] should be granted.” (Avivi v. Centro Medico Urgente Medical Center (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 463, 467.)

 

ANALYSIS

Ford moves for summary adjudication of the sixth cause of action for fraudulent inducement –concealment on three grounds: that Plaintiff cannot establish Ford’s pre-sale knowledge of a transmission defect; that Plaintiff cannot establish a transactional relationship giving rise to a duty to disclose; and that the economic loss rule bars the claim. None of these grounds entitles Ford to summary adjudication.

I. Late-Filed Opposition

As a preliminary matter, Plaintiff’s Opposition was due June 11, 2026, and was filed June 15, 2026, two court days late. Plaintiff’s counsel has submitted a declaration under Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b), taking responsibility for this oversight. Ford has filed an objection to the late Opposition, asking the Court either to decline to consider it or, in the alternative, to continue the hearing and permit Ford to file a reply. (Ford’s Obj. to Pl.’s Untimely Opp., filed June 18, 2026.)

The Court has broad discretion to consider a late-filed opposition, and the law favors the disposition of cases on their merits. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1300(d).) The authority on which Ford relies confirms that the decision is within the Court’s discretion. (Bozzi v. Nordstrom, Inc. (2010) 186 Cal.App.4th 755, 765 [recognizing the trial court’s broad discretion to refuse papers filed beyond the deadline absent a prior finding of good cause].) The delay here was brief, Plaintiff’s counsel has explained it, and Ford has not shown prejudice. Ford’s argument that the late filing deprived it of the full time to reply under Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (b)(4), is not persuasive. Reply papers were due no later than five days before the July 1, 2026 hearing, and Ford had until June 26, 2026 to file them. On this record, the Court exercises its discretion to consider the Opposition, declines to continue the hearing, and overrules the objection.

II. Pre-Sale Knowledge of the Transmission Defect

The elements of fraudulent concealment are (1) concealment or suppression of a material fact; (2) by a defendant with a duty to disclose the fact to the plaintiff; (3) the defendant intended to defraud the plaintiff by intentionally concealing or suppressing the fact; (4) the plaintiff was unaware of the fact and would not have acted as he did if he had known of the concealed or suppressed fact; and (5) the plaintiff sustained damage as a result of the concealment or suppression of the fact. (Graham v. Bank of America, N.A. (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 594, 606.)

Ford first argues that Plaintiff cannot establish that Ford knew of a transmission defect at the time of sale. Ford’s main argument, that Plaintiff never presented the Subject Vehicle for any transmission-related issue, does not establish the absence of a triable issue. A repair presentation is not an element of a fraudulent concealment claim. The claim turns on the manufacturer’s pre-sale knowledge and concealment, not on the buyer’s post-sale conduct. (Anderson v. Ford Motor Co. (2022) 74 Cal.App.5th 946, 966-967 [a fraud claim rests on the manufacturer’s pre-sale conduct, whereas a Song-Beverly claim rests on post-sale conduct].) Whether Plaintiff later presented the Subject Vehicle for transmission repairs is therefore immaterial to this cause of action.

Plaintiff offers Ford’s own internal communications, technical service bulletins, and the deposition testimony of Ford personnel, produced by Ford and unsealed in the Avila litigation, as evidence that Ford was generally aware of the defect in the 10R80 transmission. Although the Court does not take judicial notice of the truth of these documents, they are competent evidence as Ford’s own records and the statements of its own employees, offered against Ford as admissions of a party opponent. (Evid. Code, § 1220.) This evidence indicates that Ford was aware of recurring defects in the 10R80 transmission as early as 2012, years before the May 20, 2021 sale. (Pl.’s Sep. Stmt. Nos. 1-19; RJN Exs. 1-19.) Viewing this evidence and the reasonable inferences from it in the light most favorable to Plaintiff, a triable issue of material fact exists as to whether Ford knew of the transmission defect before the sale and concealed it. Summary adjudication is not warranted on this ground.

III. Duty to Disclose and the Transactional Relationship

Ford next argues that Plaintiff cannot establish a transactional relationship sufficient to establish a duty to disclose, because Plaintiff purchased the Subject Vehicle from an independent dealership rather than from Ford. A duty to disclose a material fact can arise “(1) when the defendant is in a fiduciary relationship with the plaintiff; (2) when the defendant had exclusive knowledge of material facts not known to the plaintiff; (3) when the defendant actively conceals a material fact from the plaintiff; and (4) when the defendant makes partial representations but also suppresses some material facts.” (LiMandri v. Judkins (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 326, 336.) The latter three grounds require a transactional relationship between the parties. (Id. at pp. 336-337.)

Transactional relationships include “seller and buyer, employer and prospective employee, doctor and patient, or parties entering into any kind of contractual agreement.” (Hoffman v. 162 North Wolfe LLC (2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 1178, 1187.) Also, under California law, a vendor has a duty to disclose material facts not only to immediate purchasers, but also to subsequent purchasers when the vendor has reason to expect that the item will be resold. (OCM Principal Opportunities Fund, L.P. v. CIBC World Markets Corp. (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 835, 859.)

Ford relies, as it did on demurrer, on Bigler-Engler v. Breg, Inc. (2017) 7 Cal.App.5th 276, 312, for the proposition that a duty to disclose arises only where there is already a sufficient relationship or transaction between the parties. However, unlike the medical device manufacturer in Bigler-Engler, Ford had a direct transactional relationship with Plaintiff because it made express warranties regarding the Subject Vehicle. Although a manufacturer’s warranty that comes with the sale of a vehicle is independent of the sale contract between the buyer and the dealer (Davis v. Nissan North America, Inc. (2024) 100 Cal.App.5th 825, 837), that does not mean the warranty cannot be a direct transactional relationship. Unlike the manufacturer in Bigler-Engler, which had no direct contact with the patient who rented the device, Ford has direct contact with the recipients of its express warranties, and Ford admits that it issued an express warranty to Plaintiff. (Mot. at pp. 16-17.)

Ford’s reliance on Ford Motor Warranty Cases (2025) 17 Cal.5th 1122 does not change this conclusion. That decision held only that a manufacturer cannot compel arbitration under the arbitration clause in a dealer’s sales contract, because the buyer’s warranty and fraud claims “flow not from the contracts but from separate statutory requirements and conventional fraud theories.” (Id. at p. 1138.) It addressed the formation of a contractual relationship for purposes of equitable estoppel and arbitration, not the existence of a duty to disclose for purposes of fraud.

The Court overruled Ford’s demurrer to this cause of action on this same transactional-relationship ground. Here, Ford offers no evidence negating the warranty-based relationship. A moving defendant bears the burden of negating the theories of liability as alleged in the complaint. (Nativi v. Deutsche Bank National Trust Co. (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 261, 290.) Because it is undisputed that Ford issued an express warranty to Plaintiff, Ford has not shown, as a matter of law, the absence of a duty to disclose. Accordingly, summary adjudication is not warranted on this ground.

IV. The Economic Loss Rule

Finally, Ford argues that the economic loss rule bars the claim because Plaintiff seeks only economic losses from disappointed expectations. The economic loss rule requires a contracting party to recover in contract for purely economic loss due to disappointed expectations, unless the party can show harm above and beyond a broken contractual promise. (Robinson Helicopter Co. v. Dana Corp. (2004) 34 Cal.4th 979, 988.) Robinson, however, decided only the case of an affirmative intentional misrepresentation and expressly declined to address fraudulent concealment. (Id. at p. 991.)

As the Court explained in overruling Ford’s demurrer, this issue is controlled by Dhital, which held that the economic loss rule does not bar a claim for fraudulent inducement by concealment. (Dhital v. Nissan North America, Inc. (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 828, 840.) The California Supreme Court granted review of Dhital pending its decision in Rattagan, and after deciding Rattagan, it dismissed review without ordering depublication; Dhital therefore remains binding authority. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 8.528(b).) Indeed, Rattagan expressly declined to address the application of the economic loss rule to claims of fraudulent inducement by concealment. (Rattagan v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1, 41, fn. 12.) Accordingly, the economic loss rule does not bar the claim, and summary adjudication is not warranted on that ground.

 

CONCLUSION

Ford Motor Company’s Motion for Summary Adjudication is denied.


Case Number: 25STCV30618    Hearing Date: July 1, 2026    Dept: 515

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: Hearing on Motion to Enforce Settlement

 

Plaintiffs’ Motion to Enforce Settlement is granted.

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiffs Bryan Albright and Tracy Germain (collectively “Plaintiffs”) filed this action against Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC, a Delaware limited liability company (MBUSA), asserting claims under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act (the Act) arising from an allegedly defective Mercedes-Benz vehicle. The merits of those claims are not before the Court on this motion.

On February 3, 2026, MBUSA accepted Plaintiffs’ Offer to Compromise under Code of Civil Procedure section 998 (998 Offer), which resolved all claims. (Rotman Decl., ¶ 2, Ex. 1.) The 998 Offer required MBUSA to repurchase the vehicle and to pay $85,000 in settlement funds. The parties stipulated that the Court would retain jurisdiction to enforce the settlement under Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6. (Rotman Decl., Ex. 1, ¶ 10.) Plaintiffs requested dismissal on that basis, and on March 24, 2026, the Court entered dismissal without prejudice and retained jurisdiction under section 664.6.

Under the 998 Offer, Plaintiffs were to provide MBUSA with the current registration and payoff information upon MBUSA’s receipt of the executed offer, and Plaintiffs did so on February 3, 2026, the date of acceptance. (Rotman Decl., ¶ 3, Ex. 2.) Paragraph 5 of the 998 Offer required MBUSA to pay the settlement funds, less any payoff paid directly to the lienholder, within thirty days, “[r]egardless of the surrender scheduling,” subject to an exception for delay caused by Plaintiffs or an agreement of counsel. (Rotman Decl., Ex. 1, ¶ 5.) Thirty days after February 3, 2026, was March 5, 2026. Paragraph 6 provided that, if MBUSA did not timely perform, it would pay interest at ten percent per annum on the settlement funds from the date of acceptance until Plaintiffs’ counsel received the funds. (Rotman Decl., ¶ 4, Ex. 1, ¶ 6.)

MBUSA did not pay the settlement funds by March 5, 2026. The vehicle was surrendered on March 26, 2026, and Plaintiffs’ counsel received the settlement funds on March 31, 2026. (Rotman Decl., ¶¶ 10-12.) Plaintiffs demanded interest of $1,327.40, which MBUSA declined to pay, offering instead $256.19. (Rotman Decl., ¶¶ 13-17, Ex. 4.)

Plaintiffs filed a Motion to Enforce Settlement. MBUSA filed an Opposition.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6 authorizes the Court, on noticed motion, to enter judgment pursuant to the terms of a settlement and to retain jurisdiction to enforce the settlement until performance in full. (Code Civ. Proc., § 664.6, subd. (a).) The statute provides a summary means of enforcing a settlement as written; it does not authorize the Court to create material terms the parties did not agree to or to rewrite the agreement the parties made. (Weddington Productions, Inc. v. Flick (1998) 60 Cal.App.4th 793, 810.) A settlement agreement is a contract and is interpreted under the rules governing contracts generally. (Ibid.) The Court ascertains and gives effect to the parties’ mutual intention as expressed in the writing, and the language governs if it is clear and explicit. (Civ. Code, §§ 1636, 1638.) The contract is read as a whole, with each part given effect so that none is rendered meaningless. (Civ. Code, § 1641.)

 

ANALYSIS

I. Jurisdiction to Enforce the Settlement

The parties stipulated in the 998 Offer that the Court would retain jurisdiction under section 664.6 to enforce the settlement, and the Court retained that jurisdiction when it entered the dismissal Plaintiffs requested. (Rotman Decl., Ex. 1, ¶ 10.) The Court therefore has authority to enforce the settlement’s terms.

II. The Payment Deadline

The agreement set distinct timing obligations. Paragraph 3 required Plaintiffs to surrender the vehicle within thirty days after MBUSA’s acceptance of the 998 and Plaintiff’s production of payoff information, subject to delay caused by Plaintiffs. Paragraph 4 required MBUSA to overnight the settlement funds within three business days after surrender. Paragraph 5 then imposed a separate payment deadline: “[r]egardless of the surrender scheduling,” MBUSA was to pay the settlement funds, less any payoff paid directly to the lienholder, within thirty days, subject to an exception for delay caused by Plaintiffs or an agreement of counsel. (Rotman Decl., Ex. 1, ¶ 5.) Plaintiffs provided the payoff information and current registration on February 3, 2026, the date of acceptance, which fixed the payment deadline at March 5, 2026, regardless of the status of the car surrender. (Rotman Decl., ¶ 3, Ex. 2.)

Paragraph 6 sets out the agreed consequence for late payment. If MBUSA did not timely perform, it agreed to pay interest at ten percent per annum on the settlement funds from the date of acceptance until Plaintiffs’ counsel received the funds. (Rotman Decl., ¶ 4, Ex. 1, ¶ 6.) MBUSA did not pay by March 5, 2026, and Plaintiffs’ counsel did not receive the funds until March 31, 2026. (Rotman Decl., ¶¶ 11-12.) On its face, the agreement states that the late payment triggered the interest term.

III. MBUSA’s Arguments

MBUSA raises several arguments, none of which alter this result.

First, MBUSA contends the thirty-day payment period could not begin before it received Plaintiffs’ Form W-9 and preferred surrender location, which Plaintiffs did not provide until after business hours on February 12, 2026. (Opp. at pp. 5-6; White Decl., ¶¶ 6-8.) Paragraph 5 forecloses that argument. By its terms, payment was due “regardless of the surrender scheduling.” The surrender location bears on surrender logistics, and the Form W-9 bears on the mechanics of issuing the check, not on the existence or amount of the payment obligation. Construing the payment period to await those items would nullify the “regardless of the surrender scheduling” language. In any event, MBUSA had both items by February 12 or 13, more than three weeks before the March 5 deadline, and does not explain why they prevented payment by that date.

Second, MBUSA invokes the agreement’s exception for delay caused by Plaintiffs and the prevention doctrine, arguing that Plaintiffs’ late production excuses MBUSA’s late payment. (Opp. at pp. 5-8.) Both the contractual exception and the prevention doctrine require a causal link between Plaintiffs’ conduct and the delay in performance. (Civ. Code, § 1511, subd. (1).) That link is absent. MBUSA’s own evidence shows it paid late because it treated payment as following surrender. The vehicle was surrendered on March 26, 2026, and MBUSA sent the check three business days later, on March 30, 2026. (White Decl., ¶ 11.) Because Paragraph 5 decoupled the payment deadline from surrender scheduling, it was MBUSA’s decision to pay only after surrender, and not Plaintiffs’ February 12 production of routine documents, that caused the funds to arrive on March 31 rather than by March 5.

Third, MBUSA argues that time was not of the essence and that no statutory interest is available under Civil Code section 3287 or Code of Civil Procedure section 685.010. (Opp. at p. 7.) These arguments do not fit the relief sought. A delay in performance is a material failure of consideration only if time is of the essence, that is, if prompt performance is, by the express language of the contract or by its very nature, a vital matter. (Johnson v. Alexander (1976) 63 Cal.App.3d 806, 813.) Materiality would bear only on a claim to rescind or abandon the settlement; in contrast, a breach that can be compensated in damages does not permit rescission. (Integrated, Inc. v. Alec Fergusson Electrical Contractor (1967) 250 Cal.App.2d 287, 295-296) Plaintiffs do not seek to rescind the settlement for late performance, so the time-is-of-the-essence doctrine is inapposite. Nor do Plaintiffs seek statutory prejudgment interest on an unliquidated claim. They seek the interest the parties bargained for in Paragraph 6 as the agreed consequence of late payment. The certainty requirement of section 3287 and the money-judgment requirement of section 685.010 govern statutory interest, not a contractual interest term.

Fourth, MBUSA points to a February 2, 2026, email in which Plaintiffs’ counsel stated that, although she had written thirty days into the offer, she could make the previously discussed sixty-day period work if MBUSA needed it. (Opp. at pp. 2, 8; White Decl., ¶ 5, Ex. B.) That email was a conditional offer to revise the writing, not an agreement to do so. MBUSA chose to accept the offer as written, with its thirty-day term, rather than request the sixty-day revision. (White Decl., ¶¶ 5-6.) The accepted writing controls, and the only contractual avenue for extending the deadline, an agreement of counsel, did not occur. (Rotman Decl., Ex. 1, ¶ 5.) To the extent MBUSA contends the term is ambiguous and should be construed against Plaintiffs as the drafter, that rule applies only when an ambiguity remains after the ordinary rules of interpretation, and no ambiguity remains here. (Civ. Code, § 1654.)

Finally, MBUSA argues the motion was unnecessary and contrary to the civility guidelines because MBUSA performed and proposed a compromise. (Opp. at pp. 9-10.) Performance of the principal obligations does not extinguish the separately bargained-for interest term, and MBUSA’s refusal to pay an amount owed is what made the motion necessary. The civility guidelines do not supply a defense to an otherwise enforceable term.

IV. Amount of Interest

Plaintiffs calculated interest of $1,327.40 using fifty-seven days from February 3, 2026, to March 31, 2026, at a daily rate of $23.29. (Rotman Decl., ¶ 13.) MBUSA correctly observes that the period from February 3 to March 31 is fifty-six days, not fifty-seven. (Opp. at p. 8:26-27.) Applying the agreement’s ten percent per annum rate to the $85,000 settlement for fifty-six days yields $1,304.11 ($85,000 multiplied by 0.10, multiplied by 56, divided by 365). The Court awards interest in that amount.

 

CONCLUSION

Plaintiffs’ Motion to Enforce Settlement is granted. The Court orders MBUSA to pay Plaintiffs interest of $1,304.11 within fourteen days of this order.


Case Number: 26STCP01150    Hearing Date: July 1, 2026    Dept: 515

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: Hearing on Demurrer - without Motion to Strike

 

The Demurrer filed by Defendant is sustained with leave to amend.

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Esperanza Mercado (Plaintiff) filed this action against Mahyar Zarabian (Defendant), her landlord, on March 23, 2026. The Complaint alleges that Defendant has failed to maintain Plaintiff’s rental unit at 1117 1/2 West 38th Street, Los Angeles, in a habitable condition; that the code enforcement and health departments have cited the unit and has exposed Plaintiff to mold; that Defendant has refused to make repairs and to accept rent, and rejected Plaintiff’s attempt to cure a three-day notice; that Defendant mishandled relocation assistance and an escrow account and attempted an at-fault eviction that jeopardized Plaintiff’s relocation assistance; and that the Los Angeles Housing Department has since offered a reduced relocation amount.

The Complaint does not separately identify or label any cause of action.

Defendant filed a Demurrer. Plaintiff did not file an Opposition.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (Wilson v. Transit Authority of City of Sacramento (1962) 199 Cal.App.2d 716, 720-21.) In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) “‘A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or the facts alleged.’ [Citation.]” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Services (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, courts assume the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Ibid.) However, they do not accept as true deductions, contentions, or conclusions of law or fact. (Stonehouse Homes LLC v. City of Sierra Madre (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 531, 538.)

 

ANALYSIS

I. Failure to State Facts Sufficient to Constitute a Cause of Action

Defendant first demurs on the ground that the Complaint does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. The Court agrees. The Complaint lists a variety of grievances against Defendant: that the unit is uninhabitable and has been cited by code enforcement, that Plaintiff was exposed to mold, that Defendant refused to accept rent and rejected an attempt to cure a three-day notice, that Defendant mishandled relocation assistance and an escrow account, and that Defendant attempted an at-fault eviction. The Complaint does not, however, identify any cause of action, separate its theories, or allege the ultimate facts required to establish the elements of any recognized claim.

For example, to the extent Plaintiff intends to assert a claim for breach of the implied warranty of habitability, she must allege the existence of a material defective condition affecting the habitability of the premises, notice to the landlord within a reasonable time after Plaintiff discovers the condition, that the landlord was given a reasonable time to correct the deficiency, and resulting damages. (Erlach v. Sierra Asset Servicing, LLC (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 1281, 1297.) The Complaint references uninhabitable conditions and code citations, but does not allege these elements in support of any identified claim. The Complaint’s other apparent theories, including violations of the Rent Stabilization Ordinance and the Ellis Act, retaliation, and fair housing violations, are likewise unsupported by allegations of the facts required for each such claim.

Because the Complaint does not allege facts establishing the elements of any cause of action, it is subject to demurrer under subdivision (e). (Rakestraw v. California Physicians’ Service, supra, 81 Cal.App.4th at p. 43.)

II. Uncertainty

Defendant also demurs on the ground that the Complaint is uncertain. Although a demurrer for uncertainty is disfavored, it is properly sustained where the Complaint is so incomprehensible that the defendant cannot reasonably respond. (Khoury v. Maly’s of California, Inc., supra, 14 Cal.App.4th at p. 616.) Here, the Complaint mixes several potential theories without separating or labeling them, and does not state the legal basis for the relief sought or the facts supporting Plaintiff’s claimed damages. Defendant, therefore, cannot reasonably determine which claims are asserted against him or frame a responsive pleading. The demurrer is sustained with leave to amend.

 

CONCLUSION

Defendant’s Demurrer is sustained. Plaintiff has twenty days leave to amend.
