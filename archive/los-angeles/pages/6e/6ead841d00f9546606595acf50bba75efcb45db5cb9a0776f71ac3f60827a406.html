  1. Motion for New Trial
    1. Procedural Considerations

                                          i.    Timeliness

Paraphrased, under Code of Civil Procedure section¿659, subdivision (a), a party who intends to move for a new trial must file and serve a notice of intention that identifies the grounds for the motion and states whether it will be based on affidavits, the court’s minutes, or both. This notice must be filed either after the decision but before entry of judgment, or—if judgment has already been entered—within the earliest of: (1) 15 days after entry of judgment for an unrepresented party under section¿664.5; or (2) 180 days after entry of judgment. (Ibid.) After filing the notice, the moving party must serve and file a supporting memorandum within 10 days, and any opposing party has 10 days thereafter to file a reply. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1600(a); see also Code Civ. Proc., § 659a.) If the moving party fails to timely file its memorandum, the court may deny the motion without a hearing on the merits. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1600(b).)

Here, on August 12, 2026, seven days after notice of entry of judgment, Defendant Melanie Middien as Trustee filed a notice of intent to move for a partial new trial, specifically as to the third cause of action, filing supporting papers that same day.

                                         ii.    75-Day Rule

Paraphrased, under Code of Civil Procedure section¿660(c), the court has a strict and limited period in which it may rule on a motion for a new trial, ending 75 days after the clerk mails notice of entry of judgment under section¿664.5, or 75 days after any party serves the moving party with written notice of entry of judgment—whichever happens first.

Here, the Clerk noticed entry of judgment on August 5, 2026, 75 days from which is Monday, October 19, 2026. The Court is thus empowered to hear this motion on September 3, 2026.

b.     Legal Standard

An aggrieved party may move for new trial may under seven grounds prescribed by Code of Civil Procedure 657. A party seeking reversal of an adverse summary judgment ruling may also make a motion for new trial on the grounds that there are triable issues of material fact or that there is newly discovered evidence favoring reversal of the summary judgment ruling. (See Doe v. United Air Lines, Inc. (2008) 160 Cal.App.4th 1500, 1504-1505.)

The seven grounds are:

(1) Irregularity in the proceedings of the court, jury or adverse party, or any order of the court or abuse of discretion by which either party was prevented from having a fair trial.

(2) Misconduct of the jury; and whenever any one or more of the jurors have been induced to assent to any general or special verdict, or to a finding on any question submitted to them by the court, by a resort to the determination of chance, such misconduct may be proved by the affidavit of any one of the jurors.

(3) Accident or surprise, which ordinary prudence could not have guarded against.

(4) Newly discovered evidence, material for the party making the application, which he could not, with reasonable diligence, have discovered and produced at the trial.

(5) Excessive or inadequate damages.

(6) Insufficiency of the evidence to justify the verdict or other decision, or the verdict or other decision is against law.

(7) Error in law, occurring at the trial and excepted to by the party making the application.

(Code Civ. Proc., § 657, subds. (1)-(7).)

To grant a new trial, the Court must determine that there exists in this action an insufficiency of evidence at trial to justify the verdict because, after weighing the evidence, the court is not convinced from the entire record, including reasonable inferences, that the judge or jury clearly should have reached a different verdict or decision. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 657; Lauren H v. Kannappan (2002) 96 Cal.App.4th 834, 839; see Dominguez v. Pantalone (1989) 212 Cal.App.3d 201, 216 [A judge may not grant a new trial even where the judge would have found differently from the jury].) The standard is instead one requiring the conclusion “that the verdict was obviously and clearly wrong.” (Cal. Judges Benchbook: Civil Proceedings-After Trial (2021 Supp.) Insufficient Evidence to Justify Verdict or Decision (CCP § 657(6)), § 2.56.)

In ruling on a motion for new trial that specifically relates to a non-jury trial, the court may, on such terms as may be just, change or add to the statement of decision, modify the judgment, in whole or in part, vacate the judgment, in whole or in part, and grant a new trial on all or part of the issues, or, in lieu of granting a new trial, may vacate and set aside the statement of decision and judgment and reopen the case for further proceedings and the introduction of additional evidence with the same effect as if the case had been reopened after the submission thereof and before a decision had been filed or judgment rendered. (Code Civ. Proc., § 662.) Any judgment thereafter entered shall be subject to the provisions of sections 657 and 659. (Ibid.)

“[T]he [denial] of a motion for a new trial rests so completely within the discretion of the trial court that an appellate court will not interfere with his action unless” there is an “‘affirmative showing of a gross, manifest or unmistakable abuse of discretion.’ [Citation.]” (Dunford v. General Water Heater Corp. (1957) 150 Cal.App.2d 260, 264.) “The only relevant limitation on this discretion is that the trial court must state its reasons for granting the new trial, and there must be substantial evidence in the record to support those reasons. [Citation.]” (Lane v. Hughes Aircraft Co. (2000) 22 Cal.4th 405, 412.)

    1. Discussion

After review, the Court finds merit to Plaintiff’s motion but, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 662, the Court limits relief to vacatur of the August 5, 2026, judgment, with the parties to meet and confer on a proposed amended judgment.

                                          i.    Failure to Establish Compliance with Rule 1.8.1 as Basis for New Trial on Third Cause of Action

The third cause of action in the Complaint sought enforcement of liens secured by Plaintiff on October 27, 2020, as against interests held by the Decedent pursuant to Family Code section 2033. (See Complaint, ¶¶ 15, 20-24, Exs. E-G.) Subdivision (e) of section 2033 provides that “[a]n attorney for whom a family law attorney’s real property lien is obtained shall comply with Rule 3-300 of the Rules of Professional Conduct of the State Bar of California.” In turn, former rule 3-300, now rule 1.8.1. of the Rules of Professional Conduct mandates that a lawyer shall not enter into a business transaction with a client, or knowingly acquire an ownership, possessory, security or other pecuniary interest adverse to a client, unless the terms and the lawyer’s role are fully disclosed in writing and understandable to the client, the client has independent counsel or a written opportunity to seek it, and the client then gives informed written consent. (Cal. Rules of Prof. Conduct, rule 1.8.1.) Relevant here, an attorney’s charging lien is a “security interest” in the proceeds of the litigation and is therefore adverse to the interests of a client. (Fletcher v. Davis (2004) 33 Cal.4th 61, 67.) Failure to comply with rule 1.8.1 (formerly rule 3-300) is a proper basis for a court to decline to enforce a charging lien. (See, e.g., id. at pp. 71-72.) In such circumstances, while the attorney cannot enforce the lien against the judgment, the attorney may still enforce a valid, severed (as applicable) contingent fee agreement to recover their contractually agreed-upon fees. (See, e.g., Shopoff & Cavallo LLP v. Hyon (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 1489, 1524-1525.)

Here, the record shows that at trial, over objections by Plaintiff (Motion, Notice of Lodging, Attach., 121:11-26), the Court accepted the totality of the circumstances regarding the Decedent’s knowledge of how charging liens operated as providing support for enforcement of the lien pursuant to Family Code section 2033. (Ibid. at 123:16-125:18.) However, under the above authorities, compliance with Rules of Professional Conduct, rule 1.8.1 is a mandatory prerequisite to enforcement of a charging lien under Family Code section 2033. (See, e.g., BGJ Assocs. V. Wilson (2003) 113 Cal.App.4th 1217, 1226-1227 [tracking steps of former rule 3-300, current rule 1.8.1, and noting that the steps are sequential and not offered in the alternative, where the “rule clearly contemplates a situation in which a client does consult with counsel, then consents in writing to the terms of the agreement”].)

Critically, as pointed out in Defendant’s reply (Reply, 3:8-9), Plaintiff’s opposition admits that in this proceeding, Plaintiff failed to comply with rule 1.8.1. (Opposition, 6:4-7 [“Plaintiff’s only error in respect to Rule 1.8.1 compliance was his failure to include in a letter to Rader that Rader should seek the advice of an independent lawyer of his choice concerning the proposed FLARPLs”].)

Under these circumstances, there is clear non-compliance with rule 1.8.1, and the charging lien was not enforceable as a matter of law. Consequently, there is merit to Plaintiff’s motion.

However, rather than granting new trial, the Court VACATES the August 5, 2026, judgment as described below.

  1. Motion to Set Aside/Vacate Judgment
    1. Procedural Requirements

                                          i.    Timeliness

Paraphrased, under Code of Civil Procedure section 663a, subdivision (a), a party who intends to move to set aside and vacate a judgment under section 663 must file and serve a notice of intention that specifies the legal grounds and factual inconsistencies with the decision or special verdict. This notice must be filed either after the decision but before entry of judgment, or—if entry has occurred—within the earliest of: (1)¿15 days after the clerk mails the notice of entry under section¿664.5, (2)¿15 days after receiving written notice of entry from any party, or (3)¿180 days after entry of judgment. (Ibid.)

Here, on August 11, 2026, six days after notice of entry of judgment, Defendant Melanie Middien as Trustee filed a notice of intent to move for an order vacating the August 5, 2026, judgment, filing supporting papers that same day.

                                         ii.    75-Day Rule

Paraphrased, under Code of Civil Procedure section¿663a, subdivision (b), the court’s authority to rule on a motion to set aside and vacate a judgment is strictly time-limited, expiring 75 days after the clerk mails notice of entry of judgment under section¿664.5, or 75 days after any party serves the moving party with written notice of entry—whichever occurs first. (Ibid.)

Here, the Clerk noticed entry of judgment on August 5, 2026, 75 days from which is Monday, October 19, 2026. The Court is thus empowered to hear this motion on September 3, 2026.

b.     Legal Standard

A judgment or decree, when based upon a decision by the court, or the special verdict of a jury, may, upon motion of the party aggrieved, be set aside and vacated by the same court, and another and different judgment entered, for either of two reasons, materially affecting the substantial rights of the party and entitling the party to a different judgment: (1) an incorrect or erroneous legal basis for the decision, not consistent with or not supported by the facts; and in such case when the judgment is set aside, the statement of decision shall be amended and corrected; and (2) a judgment or decree not consistent with or not supported by the special verdict.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 663.)

A motion pursuant to section 663 may only be brought “when the trial court draws an incorrect legal conclusion or renders an erroneous judgment upon the facts as found by the court or as found by the jury in a special verdict,” such that, for example, a judgment that is consistent with a special verdict cannot be attacked pursuant to section 663 and must instead be challenged via a motion for new trial pursuant to section 657. (Acosta v. Los Angeles Unified School Dist. (1995) 31 Cal.App.4th 471, 479, fn. 7, citations omitted; see Garibotti v. Hinkle (2015) 243 Cal.App.4th 470, 477 [The motion “may only be brought when the ‘trial judge draws an incorrect legal conclusion or renders an erroneous judgment upon the facts found by it to exist,’” “e.g., [when] the evidence is insufficient to support the findings or verdict” (citations omitted)]; Hassell v. Bird (2016) 247 Cal.App.4th 1336, 1350, reversed on other—section 230 immunity—grounds in Hassell v. Bird (2018) 5 Cal.5th 522, 548 [“[R]elief is available under section 663 only where a different judgment is compelled by the facts found by a judge or jury” (citations omitted)].)

As a result, a motion brought under section 663 must be based on uncontroverted evidence. (Simac Design, Inc. v. Alciati (1979) 92 Cal.App.3d 146, 153; see Shapiro v. Prudential Property & Casualty Co. (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 722, 728-30 [trial court did not err in refusing to correct an ambiguous verdict under section 663]; see also Plaza Hollister Ltd. Partnership v. County of San Benito (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 1, 14 [A motion under section 663 is not applicable to stipulated judgments because “[a] stipulated judgment does not rest upon facts established by evidence” such that “it is impossible for a court to say that the judgment is not consistent with or not supported by the facts and it is impossible to substitute the correct judgment which is consistent with and supported by the facts”].)

“In ruling on a section 663 motion, the court cannot in any way change any finding of fact,” nor can the motion “challenge to the sufficiency of the evidence to support the judgment” or “be used to secure additional findings that were not made before judgment was entered.” (Hassell v. Bird, supra, 247 Cal.App.4th at p. 1350, citations omitted; Knapp v. City of Newport Beach (1960) 186 Cal.App.2d 669, 682 [The Court is not authorized to make supplemental findings under Code of Civil Procedure section 663, and any such findings are void].)

“[O]ral comments of the trial judge do not form a part of the final judgment and cannot be used to impeach the finding and judgment.” (In re Swanson’s Estate (1959) 171 Cal.App.2d 437, 443.)

    1. Discussion

                                          i.    Bases for Relief Nos. 2, 3, 6, and 9

The Court initially notes that relief related to bases for relief numbers 2, 3, 6, and 9 is MOOTED by the Court’s order in relation to the motion for new trial.

This is because these bases for relief center around the Complaint’s third cause of action, in relation to which the Court now finds in favor of Plaintiff, rendering relief here moot.

                                         ii.    Basis for Relief No. 1

The Court finds a basis for vacatur of the August 5, 2026, judgment to clarify that the damages at issue for causes of action 1, 2, and 4 reflect a single recovery for $100,000.00.

An injured person is entitled to only one satisfaction of judgment for a single harm, and full payment of a judgment by one tortfeasor discharges all others who may be liable for the same injury. (Tikosky v. Yehuda (2018) 19 Cal.App.5th 1224, 1231.) Under California law, where the causes of action arise from the same primary right and injury, the judgment represents a single, undivided amount, such that the per-count figures are alternative or overlapping components of one recovery, not sums to be added. (See Tavaglione v. Billings (1993) 4 Cal.5th 1150, 1159.) California courts have reversed judgments that contain ambiguity as to whether separate awards are meant to be cumulative or a single undivided liability. (See, e.g., West v. Duncan (1962) 205 Cal.App.2d 140, 143-144 [reversing and remanding for new trial based on judgment’s ambiguity as to allocation of liability between two defendants, which was required by statute].)          

                                        iii.    Basis for Relief No. 4

The Court also finds a basis for vacatur of the August 5, 2026, judgment based on naming of Decedent, Matthew Rader, as one of the defendants against whom the $100,000.00 award is rendered. (See, e.g., Aug. 5, 2026, Judgment, ¶¶ 1, 2, 5.)

Judgment cannot be rendered for or against a decedent, nor can it be rendered for or against a personal representative of a decedent's estate, until the representative has been made a party by substitution. (Sacks v. FSR Brokerage, Inc. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 950, 957.)

                                        iv.    Basis for Relief No. 5

The Court next finds insufficient merit in Basis for Relief No. 5.

Defendant is correct in noting that a claim based on a contract entered into by a trustee in the trustee’s representative capacity, on an obligation arising from ownership or control of trust property, or on a tort committed in the course of administration of the trust may be asserted against the trust by proceeding against the trustee in the trustee’s representative capacity, whether or not the trustee is personally liable on the claim (Prob. Code, § 18004), and that a judgment on such a claim is enforced against trust property by proceeding against the trustee in her representative capacity. (Portico Management Group, LLC v. Harrison (2011) 202 Cal.App.4th 464, 473-474.)

However, the judgment is sufficiently clear in limiting liability to Melanie Middien as Successor Trustee of the Middien-Rader Family Trust, necessarily excluding Melanie Middien as an individual, and limiting itself to trust corpus.

                                         v.    Basis for Relief No. 7

Plaintiff provides another nominal basis for vacating the August 5, 2026, judgment based on the judgment’s omission of findings by the Court during trial releasing two of the liens alleged in the pleadings. (See Complaint, ¶¶ 14 [Peach Street lien], 16 [Highbury and Columbia liens]; see also Motion, Notice of Lodging, 126:7-10 [liens to be released other than as to Columbia Avenue].)

Proposed amended judgment(s) to be prepared by the parties shall address this issue, as well as necessary execution instructions, e.g., re: execution of recordable instruments by Plaintiff.

                                        vi.    Basis for Relief No. 8

Basis for relief number 8, challenging the judgment based on the listing of an incorrect zip code, addresses a clerical error more so than providing a basis for a motion pursuant to section 663, which the moving papers themselves recognize. Relief therefore cannot be granted as to Basis for Relief No. 8. The Court nevertheless permits any proposed amended judgment to respond to this deficiency.

(2) DENIED as relates to Bases for Relief No. 5 and 8.

  1. Background Facts

This action arises from a dispute concerning residential habitability and Defendants’ alleged failure to properly maintain an apartment unit at 3655 Westwood Blvd., Unit 7, Los Angeles, California.

    1. Pleadings

Plaintiffs Kimberly Gordon (“Gordon”), Lee Quinones (“Lee”), and Madison Quinones (“Madison”) (collectively “Plaintiffs”) sue Westwood Blvd Properties, LLC (“Westwood Properties”), Optimus Properties, LLC (“Optimus Properties”), and Optimus Property Management, LLC (“Optimus Management”) (collectively, “Defendants”) pursuant to a March 13, 2025, First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) alleging tortious breach of implied warranty of habitability, breach of contract, negligence, unfair business practices, violations of various Los Angeles Municipal Code provisions, and violations of California Civil Code sections governing landlord obligations.

The claims arise from Plaintiffs’ long-term tenancy at the subject property and Defendants’ alleged failure, beginning in mid-2021, to address escalating habitability problems, including repeated water leaks, floods, sewer overflows, excess moisture, mold, mites, termite infestation, faulty plumbing, electrical hazards, lack of heating, and structural defects. Multiple City inspections allegedly documented code violations, while Defendants refused to perform adequate remediation, environmental testing, or required habitability-plan and relocation measures. Plaintiffs assert Defendants continued collecting rent despite official repair orders, engaged in harassment—including improper demands related to emotional-support animals—and caused injuries through unsafe conditions. Based on these alleged failures, Plaintiffs seek damages, restitution, and punitive relief.

    1. Motion Before the Court

Now before the Court is Plaintiff’s motion to compel the further deposition of a person most qualified (“PMQ”) for Westwood Properties on questions relating to the time period of August 2022 through August 2024, arguing that at the prior two deposition sessions, Westwood Properties only supplied a PMQ qualified to speak as to matters between 2020 and August 2022 and August 2024 to the present. The motion also seeks monetary sanctions in the modest amount of $1,573.41.

Defendants initially opposed Plaintiffs’ motion on June 25, 2026, arguing chiefly that the Westwood Properties PMQ who Plaintiff deposed on April 23, 2026, and purportedly again on May 29, 2026—Jerome Mikelson—is the only PMQ available for Westwood Properties, that Mr. Mikelson discharged the PMQ duties for Westwood Properties by testifying on those dates, and that Plaintiffs can depose the other Defendants’ PMQs for the periods of 2022 to 2024.

Plaintiff’s July 1, 2026, reply disputes the position that Mr. Mikelson discharged the PMQ duties for Westwood Properties where his testimony was limited to “2020 to 2022 and the 2024 to current” [sic].

On July 8, 2026, the hearing on Plaintiff’s motion was continued to September 17, 2026, based on the lack of an assigned judicial officer over this Department.

On September 2, 2026, Defendants filed a supplemental opposition to Plaintiffs’ motion.

On September 9, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a supplemental reply to Defendants’ supplemental opposition, arguing among other things that the supplemental opposition is not authorized by law or court order.

Plaintiffs’ motion is now before the Court.

 

  1. Motion to Compel Deposition; Request for Sanctions
    1. Global Preliminary Considerations

                                          i.    Opposition and Evidence in Support Thereof

The Court declines to consider any factual propositions advanced in Defendants’ June 25, 2026, opposition.

A memorandum supporting a motion must include a concise statement of the law, arguments, and evidence relied on and a discussion of the statutes, cases, and textbooks cited in support of the movant's position. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1113(b), emphasis added; see, e.g., Nationwide Ins. v. Tipton (2023) 91 Cal.App.5th 1355, 1365 [trial court had discretion to ignore arguments that defendants did not properly present in their memorandum of points and authorities in opposition to plaintiff's petition].)

Here, no supporting declaration or documents accompany Defendants’ June 25, 2026, opposition.

Accordingly, the Court declines to consider any factual propositions raised in that paper.

                                         ii.    Supplemental Opposition and Reply

The Court declines to consider the supplemental opposition and reply to the extent that these papers (1) go beyond describing updated factual background for the motion and litigation before the Court and (2) are not supported by evidence.

    1. Motion to Compel Deposition

                                          i.    Mootness

The Court declines to find that Plaintiffs’ motion was mooted by the alleged taking of a second PMQ deposition session after Plaintiffs’ motion was filed.

Plaintiffs filed the motion on May 27, 2026. Defendants assert that a further deposition session occurred on May 29, 2026. However, Defendants’ opposition does not contain a supporting declaration establishing the facts necessary to determine that the subsequent deposition fully resolved the issues presented by the motion.

Moreover, Plaintiffs’ July 1, 2026 reply makes clear that the central dispute remains whether Westwood Blvd Properties has complied with its obligation to provide a PMQ capable of answering questions concerning the period from August 2022 through August 2024.

Whether subsequent discovery has satisfactorily resolved the issues raised by a motion is a matter within the trial court’s discretion. (Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pacific Healthcare Consultants (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, 409.)

The Court therefore finds that the motion is not moot.

                                         ii.    Preliminary Note re: Nature of Motion

Here, Plaintiffs’ motion is noticed pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.450 (see Motion, p. 3), with Plaintiffs arguing that Jerome Mikelson did not answer certain questions at deposition (see Motion, 8:3-10), and Defendants arguing that Mr. Mikelson did in fact respond to questions at the deposition sessions (Opposition, pp. 2-3, 3-5).  The substance of the requested relief, however, concerns further answers at a deposition.

 

A trial court may construe a motion bearing one label as a different type of motion. (Austin v. Los Angeles Unified School Dist. (2016) 244 Cal.App.4th 918, 930.) “‘The nature of a motion is determined by the nature of the relief sought, not by the label attached to it. The law is not a mere game of words. … The principle that a trial court may consider a motion regardless of the label placed on it by a party is consistent with the court’s inherent authority to manage and control its docket.’ [Citation.]” (Ibid.)

Here, it is clear from the face of the record and the nature of the relief sought that Plaintiffs are seeking to compel further answers at a third deposition session for the Westwood Properties PMQ, specifically because at the past deposition sessions, the PMQ was unable to address questions relating to a period from 2022 to 2024.

Consequently, the Court reads this motion as being brought pursuant to a counterpart of the statute pursuant to which Plaintiff brought this motion, i.e., Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.480 as opposed to section 2025.450.

                                        iii.    Meet and Confer

A motion to compel deposition answers or production pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.480 must be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Code of Civil Procedure section 2016.040. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.480, subd. (b).) A meet and confer declaration in support of a motion shall state facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt at an informal resolution of each issue presented by the motion. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2016.040.)

Here, the Court determines that sufficient meet and confer efforts preceded the filing of Plaintiffs’ motion. (Motion, Burton Decl., ¶¶ 3-14, Exs. 1-7.)

                                        iv.    Timeliness

A motion to compel pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.480 shall be made no later than 60 days after the completion of the record of the deposition. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.480, subd. (b).) The record is completed once the entire inspection at issue is finished, including the raising of objections during that process. (See Unzipped Apparel, LLC v. Bader (2007) 156 Cal.App.4th 123, 134 (Unzipped).) This 60-day timeframe is jurisdictional and if it is not met, the court is in excess of its jurisdiction to act other than to deny the motion. (Id. at pp. 134-136.)

Here, even if the completion of the record took place on April 23, 2026, when the first deposition session took place and Defendants took the ‘only 2020 to 2022 and 2024 to the present’ position, Plaintiffs’ motion was filed on May 27, 2026, well within the 60-day limit.

                                         v.    Separate Statement

If the proposed deponent made some response to the deposition notice, then a separate statement must accompany any motion to compel deposition attendance or production. (See Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1345(a)(4).) A separate statement is not required when no response has been provided to discovery and when a court has allowed the moving party to submit—in place of a separate statement—a concise outline of the discovery request and each response in dispute. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1345(b)(1)-(2).)

Here, though no separate statement accompanies Plaintiffs’ motion, the Court finds that rule 3.1345(b) is sufficiently satisfied insofar as (1) the Court permits the moving papers to serve in lieu of a separate statement, where (2) those papers confirm that any questions for the period of August 2022 to August 2024 were unanswerable at the deposition, given that Westwood Properties only offered the PMQ for the periods of 2020 to 2022 and 2024 to the present.

                                        vi.    Legal Standard

Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.480 authorizes a motion to compel when a deponent fails to answer a question or produce documents, electronically stored information, or tangible things identified in a deposition notice or subpoena, and subdivision (a) expressly provides that the party seeking discovery may request an order compelling such an answer or production. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.480, subd. (a).)

In the event that the court determines that the answer or production sought is subject to discovery, it shall order that the answer be given or the production be made on the resumption of the deposition. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.480, subd. (i).)

                                       vii.    Discussion

After review, the Court finds in favor of Plaintiffs.

Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.480 authorizes a party to move to compel an answer when a deponent fails to answer a question identified in a deposition notice or subpoena. (§ 2025.480, subd. (a).) Where the Court determines that the requested answer is subject to discovery, the Court shall order that the answer be given on resumption of the deposition. (§ 2025.480, subd. (i).)

The record establishes that, on April 23, 2026, Westwood Properties did not provide a PMQ capable of answering questions concerning the period from August 2022 through August 2024. Defendants contend that the PMQ subsequently testified again and that no other PMQ is available for Westwood Properties. Plaintiffs, however, maintain that the PMQ remained unable to answer questions concerning the disputed period.

The evidence before the Court establishes that Westwood Properties qualified its PMQ testimony to periods before August 2022 and after August 2024. Westwood Properties has not demonstrated why it should be relieved of its obligation to provide a PMQ capable of responding to questions concerning the duly noticed period from August 2022 through August 2024. Nor did Westwood Properties obtain a protective order relieving it of that obligation.

The fact that other defendants may have PMQs who can testify concerning the same period does not, standing alone, discharge Westwood Properties’ obligation to produce its own PMQ for matters within the scope of the deposition notice.

The Court also finds that the requested testimony is relevant. The deposition notice and the pleadings establish that testimony concerning Plaintiffs’ claims and Defendants’ defenses during the period from August 2022 through August 2024 bears on issues of liability. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2017.010.)

Accordingly, the Court finds that the PMQ failed to answer questions concerning a relevant period identified in the deposition notice, and that no valid protective order relieved Westwood Properties of its obligation to provide responsive testimony.

A further deposition session is therefore warranted.

    1. Request for Sanctions

                                          i.    Legal Standard

The court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel an answer or production, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.480, subd. (j).)

The court may award sanctions under the Discovery Act in favor of a party who files a motion to compel discovery, even though no opposition to the motion was filed, or opposition to the motion was withdrawn, or the requested discovery was provided to the moving party after the motion was filed. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1348, subd. (a).)

                                         ii.    Discussion

After review, the Court finds in favor of Plaintiffs as to sanctions of $1,573.41 against Westwood Properties and counsel of record.

These sanctions are modest and supported by a declaration from counsel. (Motion, Burton Decl., ¶¶ 16-17.)

Moreover, the sanctions are supported by a basis for sanctionable conduct: failure to respond or submit to an authorized method of discovery re: the 2022 to 2024 time period. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.010, subd. (d).)

Sufficient grounds thus exist to grant sanctions.

Last, the Court notes that sanctions are limited to Westwood Properties and not to counsel of record as the notice of motion seeks sanctions against Westwood Properties and “RESNICK & LOUIS, PC” without identifying a specific counsel of record against whom sanctions are sought. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.040 [“A request for a sanction shall, in the notice of motion, identify every person, party, and attorney against whom the sanction is sought, and specify the type of sanction sought”].)

    1. Motion to Compel Deposition

                                          i.    Legal Standard

After a deposition notice has been properly served, if the party or designated deponent does not serve a valid objection pursuant to section 2025.410 and then fails to appear, fails to proceed with the deposition, or fails to produce the documents or other materials identified in the notice, the noticing party may move for a court order compelling the deponent to appear, testify, and produce the requested materials. Section 2025.450 contains no time limit for a motion to compel where a party fails to attend the deposition or produce any documents after valid service of the deposition notice and no objections have been made. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subds. (a)-(i)(2).)

    1. Motion to Compel Further

The moving papers also satisfy the requirement to attach a separate statement to a motion for an order compelling further responses to production requests. (See Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1345, subd. (a)(1).)

                                        iii.    Merits

1.     Legal Standard

A motion to compel a further response is used when a party gives unsatisfactory answers or makes untenable objections to interrogatories. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (a); Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pacific Healthcare Consultants (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, 403 (Sinaiko) [interrogatories and demands to produce].)

To compel a further response to interrogatories, the movant can show that: (1) the responding party’s answer to a particular interrogatory is evasive or incomplete (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (a)(1)); (2) the responding party’s exercise of the option to produce documents in response to an interrogatory was unwarranted or the required specification of those documents was inadequate (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (a)(2)); and (3) the responding party’s objection to an interrogatory is without merit or too general (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (a)(3); see, e.g., Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 550 [defendant’s argument that plaintiff was required to establish good cause or prove merits of underlying claim before propounding interrogatories without merit]).

2.     Discussion

After review, the Court finds in favor of Plaintiff in part and in favor of Defendant Guo in part.

The motion lacks merit as to SI, Set One No. 26. As shown in the separate statement and in Exhibit F attached to the Ferris Declaration of the moving papers, in response to an interrogatory asking how Plaintiff calculated lost income in the amount of $1.5 million, Plaintiff responded by identifying a loss of approximately $500,000.00 per year. While sparse, this information is responsive to the interrogatory by identifying the math supporting the $1.5 million lost income projection: $500,000.00 per year in lost income, necessarily times three years. Any additional or contradictory information raised later in the litigation may be addressed via impeachment or, if proper, via a motion related to evidence and issue sanctions or motions in limine, but for present purposes, the response is sufficient.

In contrast, the response to SI, Set Two, No. 29 is evasive in the extreme, asking Plaintiff to identify dates on which he applied for employment with any employer since May 1, 2022, and Plaintiff responding, “I have not maintained that information.” This objectively evasive response fails to comply with the text and spirit of Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.220, requiring, at the very least at subdivision (c), that Plaintiff “state” he “does not have personal knowledge sufficient to respond fully to an interrogatory” while also making “a reasonable and good faith effort to obtain the information by inquiry to other natural persons or organizations, except where the information is equally available to the propounding party,” which would not be the case here. If Plaintiff has not mitigated damages, he should so state. If he has, he needs to make an attempt to obtain responsive information rather than simply relying on the passive statement that he has not maintained that information. If responsive mitigation information is found after a reasonable and good faith effort, then that information would necessarily go to SI, Set One, No. 29 as far as dates applied. In sum, relief is warranted for outright evasiveness in response to SI, Set One, No. 29.

Similarly, relief is warranted as to SI, Set Two, Nos. 37-38, which ask that Plaintiff provide identifying contact information for possible witnesses in this action, with Plaintiff limiting responses to responses tantamount to ‘I do not have present address or phone number’ and then providing a phone number, but without providing full names, last known addresses, last known telephone numbers, or last known email addresses, where the phone numbers provided by Plaintiff in the supplemental responses appears to refer to “present” contract information, as limited in Plaintiff’s initial response to the these interrogatories.

(2) GRANTED as to SI, Set Two, Nos. 29 and 37-38.

This action involves a dispute over right to portions of the attorneys’ fees obtained pursuant to a resolved civil litigation involving Defendant Chanda Kellogg (no assigned case number), in which action Ms. Kellogg was first represented by Defendant Law Offices of Ramin Younessi before changing representation to Plaintiff Downtown LA Law Group LLP.

  1. Demurrer
    1. Meet and Confer

The Court determines that while the meet and confer efforts that preceded Defendants’ demurrer were insufficient (Demurrer, Ortiz Decl., ¶ 4 [meet and confer limited to March 2, 2026, well before filing of FAC on June 15, 2026]; cf. Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)), such a finding is not a basis to overrule the demurrer. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(4) [“A determination by the court that the meet and confer process was insufficient shall not be grounds to overrule or sustain a demurrer”].)

b.     Legal Standard

                                          i.    Uncertainty

A demurrer to a pleading lies where the pleading is uncertain, ambiguous, or unintelligible. (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.10, subd. (f).)

“A demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Khoury v. Maly’s of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616, disapproved on other grounds in Quelimane Co. v. Stewart Title Guar. Co. (1998) 19 Cal.4th 26, 46 [holding claims for unfair business practices need not be pled specifically, impliedly disapproving Khoury].) As a result, a special demurrer for uncertainty is not intended to reach failure to incorporate sufficient facts in the pleading but is directed only at uncertainty existing in the allegations already made. (People v. Taliaferro (1957) 149 Cal.App.2d 822, 825, disapproved on other grounds in Jefferson v. J.E. French Co. (1960) 54 Cal.2d 717, 719-720 [statute of limitations question].)

Where complaint is sufficient to state a cause of action and to apprise defendant of issues he is to meet, it is not properly subject to a special demurrer for uncertainty. (See ibid.; see also Gressley v. Williams (1961) 193 Cal.App.2d 636, 643 [“A special demurrer [for uncertainty] should be overruled where the allegations of the complaint are sufficiently clear to apprise the defendant of the issues which he is to meet”].)

                                         ii.    Sufficiency

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747; see Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (e).)

To sufficiently allege a cause of action, a complaint must allege all the ultimate facts—that is, the facts needed to establish each element of the cause of action pleaded. (Committee on Children’s Television, Inc. v. General Foods Corp. (1983) 35 Cal.3d 197, 212, superseded by statute as stated in Branick v. Downey Savings & Loan Assn. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 235, 242.) “[E]ach evidentiary fact that might eventually form part of the plaintiff’s proof need not be alleged.” (C.A. v. William S. Hart Union High School Dist. (2012) 53 Cal.4th 861, 872.)

In testing the sufficiency of the cause of action, the demurrer admits the truth of all material facts properly pleaded. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 966-67.) Courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (Taylor v. City of Los Angeles Dept. of Water and Power (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 1216, 1228, disapproved on other grounds, Jones v. Lodge at Torrey Pines Partnership (2008) 42 Cal.4th 1158, 1162.) A demurrer, however, “does not admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Daar v. Yellow Cab Co. (1967) 67 Cal.2d 695, 713.)

    1. Discussion

                                          i.    FAC, First Cause of Action, Interpleader

As to the interpleader claim, the Court finds in favor of Defendant.

For interpleader purposes, “stakeholder” is any person, firm, corporation, association, or other entity who holds money or property (i.e., the “stake”) and who is or may be subject to inconsistent claims to that property. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 386, subds. (a), (b).) The stakeholder may be (1) a plaintiff who initiates the interpleader action by filing a complaint or (2) a defendant who institutes the interpleader proceeding in an ongoing suit by filing a cross-complaint in interpleader, a motion for substitution and discharge, or a motion for discharge and dismissal. (See ibid.)

A “disinterested stakeholder” puts the stake into the possession of the court, is dismissed from the proceeding, and leaves the competing claimants to litigate among themselves for ownership of the stake. (See Code Civ. Proc., §§ 386, 386.5; Virtanen v. O'Connell (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 688, 698; see, e.g., Hustead v. Superior Court (1969) 2 Cal.App.3d 780, 784 [garnishee subject to conflicting claims for rent due].)

An “interested stakeholder” claims an interest in the stake, puts the stake into the possession of the court, and litigates with the claimants for ownership of the stake. (See Code Civ. Proc., §§ 386.) When an interested stakeholder is involved, the proceeding is usually referred to as “partial interpleader.” (See City of Morgan Hill v. Brown (1999) 71 Cal.App.4th 1114, 1123.)

A “claimant” is a person or entity who asserts an interest in the stake. (See City of Morgan Hill v. Brown (1999) 71 Cal.App.4th 1114, 1123.) The various claimants must assert a claim to the same stake and be adverse to one another. (Code Civ. Proc., § 386, subd. (b); City of Morgan Hill, supra, 71 Cal.App.4th at p. 1123.)

The first claim in the FAC alleges an interpleader claim against Defendant.

In its demurrer, Defendant argues that the first count is not sufficiently alleged because Plaintiff is not a disinterested stakeholder but instead claims an interest in the disputed funds and as of the filing of this demurrer, Plaintiff has not deposited the disputed funds with the Court. (Motion, p. 4.) Defendant also highlights contradictions in the FAC, which render it uncertain, such as Plaintiff claiming to be a disinterested stakeholder to deposit funds with the Court and Defendants to litigate over the stake, while simultaneously seeking relief in favor of Plaintiff as to the same stake. (See, e.g., Motion, 5:1-5.)

The Court finds the latter point availing for demurrer purposes.

Specifically, Plaintiff uses language reading like language relating to a disinterested stakeholder while simultaneously seeking relief for an interested stakeholder, imbuing the pleading with uncertainty as to the nature of Plaintiff’s involvement in the case. (Compare, e.g., FAC, ¶¶ 24-27 [seeking to litigate this action, connoting interested stakeholder], Prayer for Relief, ¶¶ 2 [praying for Defendants to litigate amongst themselves as to stake], 5 [also praying for determination of its portion of stake].)

The interpleader claim thus fails on demurrer.

                                         ii.    FAC, Second Cause of Action, Declaratory Relief

However, the demurrer fails as to the FAC’s declaratory relief claim.

A complaint for declaratory relief must demonstrate: (1) a proper subject of declaratory relief and (2) an actual controversy involving justiciable questions relating to the rights or obligations of a party. (Brownfield v. Daniel Freeman Marina Hosp. (1989) 208 Cal.App.3d 405, 410.) The fundamental basis of declaratory relief is a present and actual controversy between the parties over a proper subject. (City of Cotati v. Cashman (2002) 29 Cal.4th 69, 80; see Code Civ. Proc., §1060.)

Here, the FAC alleges a dispute over right to portions of the attorneys’ fees obtained in the underlying action related to Defendant Kellogg (FAC, ¶¶ 11-23), facts which are incorporated into the second count (FAC, ¶ 28), and are there repeated within a declaratory relief framing (FAC, ¶¶ 29-32), with a prayer for a declaration of their respective rights and money judgment (FAC, Prayer for Relief, ¶¶ 6-7).

The Court does not credit the demurrer arguments against the claim.

The duplicativeness argument fails because the Court sustained Defendant’s demurrer to the interpleader claim, eliminating any duplicativeness.

The ‘no controversy’ argument fails because there is a clearly alleged controversy here regarding proceeds from the underlying action relating to Chanda Kellogg.

The demurrer thus fails as to this claim.

d.     Conclusion

Defendant Law Offices of Ramin Younessi’s demurrer is accordingly:

(1) SUSTAINED, with leave to amend, as to the Interpleader count (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (e)(1));

(2) OVERRULED as to the Declaratory Relief count.

(Motion, Tojarieh Decl., ¶¶ 14-15.) The Court instead limits relief to a reasonable fee rate of $450.00 per hour and 3 hours reasonably expended on these proceedings, yielding relief in the amount of $1,350.00. (Morris v. Hyundai Motor America (2019) 41 Cal.App.5th 24, 40 [When a voluminous fee application is made the court may make across-the-board percentage cuts either in the number of hours claimed or in the final lodestar figure], 41 [A judge may reasonably reduce an attorney’s requested rate if the judge finds that the invoiced work could have been done by a lower billing attorney].)

Last, the Court notes that the sanctions are granted jointly and severally as against AGC and its counsel of record, James Moss, Jr.

Notice:                          OK

 

  1. Background Facts

Now before the Court is Defendant Forward Networks, Inc.’s opposed motion to compel into arbitration all Fair Employment and Housing Act (“FEHA”), Labor Code, and wrongful termination claims alleged against it in Plaintiff Robert Welch’s March 23, 2026, Complaint.

 

  1. Motion to Compel Arbitration
    1. Preliminary Considerations

                                          i.    Requests for Judicial Notice

The Court takes judicial notice of the JAMS Employment Arbitration Rules & Procedures. (Motion, RJN, Ex. A [copy of rules]; see Evid. Code, §§ 452, subd. (h), 453, subds. (a)-(b).)

The Court also takes judicial notice of Labor Code section 432.6. (Reply, RJN, Ex. A [Westlaw printout of Lab. Code, § 432.6; see Evid. Code, § 451, subd. (a).)

                                         ii.    Arguments/Evidence Raised for First Time on Reply; FAA v. CAA Applicability

Court’s have discretion to permit arguments or evidence raised for the first time on reply. (See Nordstrom Com. Cases (2010) 186 Cal.App.4th 576, 583 [Courts generally need not accept arguments raised for the first time on reply without good cause explanation as to why points were not raised earlier]; Hahn v. Diaz-Barba (2011) 194 Cal.App.4th 1177, 1193 [While additional evidentiary matter submitted with the reply ordinarily should not be allowed, the court has discretion to consider it when it poses no prejudice to the opposing party].)

Here, Plaintiff argues that because Defendant’s motion points and authorities do not address interstate commerce, Defendant’s motion, which invokes the FAA, should be denied for failure to meet an essential element of Defendant’s burden. Plaintiff also preemptively argues that any evidence of interstate commerce on reply should not be considered.

The Court, however, agrees with the reply position that evidence of interstate commerce in Defendant’s business and Plaintiff’s employment should be considered. Here, there is no prejudice apparent to Plaintiff other than having to argue the merits of whether his employment involved interstate commerce. Under these circumstances, the Court accepts evidence relating to interstate commerce.

The Court discusses merits below.

b.     Legal Standard

The Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”), while a federal statute, applies in California courts and requires state courts to enforce arbitration agreements as required by the federal common law developed under the FAA. (See Southland Corp. v. Keating (1984) 465 U.S. 1, 15-16 (Southland Corp.); Broughton v. Cigna Healthplans (1999) 21 Cal.4th 1066, 1074-78, superseded by statute on another ground as stated in Ferguson v. Corinthian Colleges, Inc. (9th Cir. 2013) 733 F.3d 928, 937.).) The FAA preempts and invalidates state law and state judicial decisions that disfavor arbitration or require arbitration provisions to pass higher scrutiny. (Southland Corp., supra, at p. 12; Perry v. Thomas (1987) 482 U.S. 483, 490.) If the parties designate the FAA applies, then California arbitration law is preempted. (See, e.g., Rodriguez v. American Techs., Inc. (2006) 136 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1121-1122.) However, courts have found that where the FAA is found not to apply, the California Arbitration Act (Code Civ. Proc. § 1280 et seq.) applies. (See Valencia v. Smyth (2010) 185 Cal.App.4th 153, 178 (Valencia).)

 A court’s inquiry is limited to a determination of (1) whether a valid arbitration agreement exists and (2) whether the arbitration agreement covers the dispute. (9 U.S.C. § 4; Chiron Corp. v. Ortho Diagnostics Systems, Inc. (9th Cir. 2000) 207 F.3d 1126, 1130; Howsam v. Dean Witter Reynolds, Inc. (2002) 537 U.S. 79, 84; see Simula, Inc. v. Autoliv, Inc. (9th Cir. 1999) 175 F.3d 716, 720 [if the finding is affirmative on both counts the FAA requires the Court to enforce the arbitration agreement in accordance with its terms]; see Omar v. Ralphs Grocery Co. (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 955, 961 [In determining the enforceability of an arbitration agreement, the court first considers “two ‘gateway issues’ of arbitrability: (1) whether there was an agreement to arbitrate between the parties, and (2) whether the agreement covered the dispute at issue”] Lacayo v. Cataline Restaurant Group Inc. (2019) 38 Cal.App.5th 244, 257 (Lacayo) [Where moving party meets initial burden, “the party opposing arbitration must prove by a preponderance of the evidence any defense to the petition”].)

Even where the FAA governs the interpretation of arbitration clauses, California law governs whether an arbitration agreement has been formed in the first instance. (Baker v. Osborne Development Corp. (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 884, 893.) 

    1. Discussion

                                          i.    Defendant’s Burden

1. Existence of an Arbitration Agreement

The Court finds that the parties entered into an agreement to arbitrate.

     “Parties are not required to arbitrate their disagreements unless they have agreed to do so. [Citation.] A contract to arbitrate will not be inferred absent a ‘clear agreement.’ [Citation.]” (Davis v. Nordstrom, Inc. (9th Cir. 2014) 755 F.3d 1089, 1092-1093.) California applies ordinary principles of contract formation in determining whether an agreement to arbitrate exists.

     Defendant submits a declaration from its Senior Vice President concerning Plaintiff's execution of an offer letter containing an arbitration provision and the electronic signature associated with that agreement. (Motion, P Decl., ¶¶ 2-4, 8, 11, 13-18, 19-20, 21, 22-23, Exs. A-C; agreement at Ex. C, § 12.)

     Plaintiff does not dispute that he entered into the agreement. Instead, Plaintiff contends that his claims fall outside the agreement's scope or that the agreement should not be enforced. (Opposition, pp. 3-15.)

The Court therefore finds that an agreement to arbitrate exists.

2. Interstate Commerce

     The Court next considers whether Defendant has established that the FAA applies.

     A party seeking to compel arbitration under the FAA bears the burden of establishing FAA coverage, including by demonstrating that the relevant employment relationship involves interstate commerce. (Hoover v. American Income Life Ins. Co. (2012) 206 Cal.App.4th 1193, 1207; Lane v. Francis Capital Management LLC (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 676, 687-688.)

     Plaintiff argues that his employment was exclusively California-based because he physically worked in California. That fact, standing alone, does not resolve whether his employment involved interstate commerce. The relevant inquiry concerns the nature of Defendant's business and Plaintiff's employment.

     Defendant's reply evidence establishes that Plaintiff's employment involved communications and work concerning persons or activities outside California, including work involving other states and a self-review apparently referencing work related to Japan. (Reply, pp. 5-6; Reply, P Decl., ¶¶ 2-10, Exs. A-C.) The evidence also establishes the interstate nature of Defendant's business and the nexus between that business and Plaintiff's employment.

     On this record, the Court finds that Defendant has established that the employment relationship involved interstate commerce. The FAA therefore applies.

 

3. Scope of the Arbitration Agreement

     The Court nevertheless finds that Plaintiff's claims fall outside the scope of the arbitration agreement.

     The arbitration provision states that the parties agree to submit to mandatory binding arbitration “any and all claims arising out of or related to your employment with the Company and the termination thereof,” subject to specified carveouts. (Motion, P Decl., Ex. C, § 12(a)(i).)

     One such carveout excludes Labor Code and FEHA claims “for so long as AB 51 is in effect.”

     The parties principally dispute the meaning of the phrase “in effect.”

     The Ninth Circuit has held that the Federal Arbitration Act preempts AB 51 “as a whole to the extent it applies to arbitration agreements.” (Chamber of Commerce of the United States of America v. Bonta (9th Cir. 2023) 62 F.4th 473, 490.) The court explained that AB 51's provisions, taken together, burdened the formation of arbitration agreements in a manner inconsistent with the FAA. (Ibid.) Thus, where the FAA governs an arbitration agreement, AB 51 does not provide a basis for invalidating an otherwise enforceable arbitration agreement. See also Lab. Code, § 432.6, subd. (f) [“Nothing in this section is intended to invalidate a written arbitration agreement that is otherwise enforceable under the Federal Arbitration Act.”].

     That holding, however, does not answer the contractual question presented here.

     The issue before the Court is not whether AB 51 may be enforced against Defendant or whether AB 51 renders the parties' arbitration agreement invalid. Rather, the issue is what the parties meant when they agreed to exclude FEHA and Labor Code claims “for so long as AB 51 is in effect.”

     The agreement does not define “in effect.” Nor does it state that the exclusion ceases to apply if AB 51 is preempted, unenforceable, enjoined, or otherwise incapable of being enforced against the parties. The agreement instead uses the phrase “in effect” without further qualification.

     Defendant asks the Court to construe that language as effectively meaning “for so long as AB 51 is enforceable against the parties.” The Court declines to add that qualification to the agreement.

     The Ninth Circuit's decision in Chamber of Commerce addressed the legal effect of AB 51 under the FAA. It did not interpret the parties' private agreement or determine what the parties intended by the phrase “for so long as AB 51 is in effect.” The FAA's application and the scope of the parties' contractual agreement are therefore analytically distinct questions.

     Nor does the agreement state that the FEHA and Labor Code carveout is limited to claims excluded from arbitration by a presently enforceable provision of California law. Had the parties intended that result, they could have drafted the provision to exclude such claims only “to the extent required by AB 51,” “to the extent AB 51 is enforceable,” or using similar language. They did not do so.

     The Court must enforce the agreement according to its terms and may not rewrite it to achieve a different allocation of arbitrable claims. (Ritzenthaler v. Fireside Thrift Co. (2001) 93 Cal.App.4th 986, 991.)

     The Court therefore concludes that the phrase “for so long as AB 51 is in effect” does not mean “for so long as AB 51 is enforceable against Defendant under the FAA.” The agreement contains no such limitation.

     The Court further concludes that the existence of federal preemption does not, by itself, modify the contractual language selected by the parties. The parties agreed to a particular allocation of claims, and that allocation expressly excludes FEHA and Labor Code claims subject to the stated temporal condition.

     On the record and briefing before the Court, Defendant has not established that the contractual condition has ceased to exist under the terms of the agreement. Accordingly, the FEHA and Labor Code claims alleged in the Complaint fall within the contractual carveout and outside the scope of claims Defendant agreed to arbitrate.

     Because Defendant has failed to establish that the arbitration agreement encompasses the claims asserted in this action, the motion to compel arbitration must be denied.

d.     Conclusion

Defendant Forward Networks, Inc.’s motion to compel arbitration is DENIED.

Now before the Court is Defendants The Dragon Logistics LLC and Douglas Lopez’s demurrer to Plaintiff’s initial April 6, 2026, Complaint.

After review, the Court finds that Defendants’ demurrer is MOOT and the corresponding hearing is TAKEN OFF CALENDAR.

A party may amend its pleading once as a matter of course before an answer, demurrer, or motion to strike is filed, or after such a motion is filed but before it is heard, provided the amendment is filed and served no later than the deadline for filing an opposition to the motion. (Code Civ. Proc., § 472, subd. (a).) That opposition deadline requires that all papers opposing a motion be filed and served at least nine court days before the hearing. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1005, subd. (b).) When a party timely files an amended complaint, the amendment moots any motion directed to the prior complaint. (JKC3H8 v. Colton (2013) 221 Cal.App.4th 468, 477; Sylmar Air Conditioning v. Pueblo Contracting Services, Inc. (2004) 122 Cal.App.4th 1049, 1054.) As a result, a demurrer directed to the superseded pleading should be taken off calendar once the amended complaint is filed. (People ex rel. Strathmann v. Acacia Research Corp. (2012) 210 Cal.App.4th 487, 506.) The right applies only to the original complaint initiating the action, and a plaintiff cannot amend an already amended complaint as of right even if a demurrer is pending against that operative pleading. (Hedwall v. PCMV, LLC (2018) 22 Cal.App.5th 564, 572–579.)

Here, in response to Defendants’ demurrer, on September 3, 2026, i.e., nine court days prior to the September 17, 2026, hearing, Plaintiff filed a First Amended Complaint. Consequently, under the above authority, Defendants’ demurrer is directed toward a moot pleading and is taken off calendar.
