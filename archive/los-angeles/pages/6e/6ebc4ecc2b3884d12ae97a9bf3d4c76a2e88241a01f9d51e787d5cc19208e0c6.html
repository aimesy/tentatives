TENTATIVE RULING

HEARING DATE:                 September 16, 2026

CASE NUMBER:                   22SMCV01737 consolidated with Case No.: 21STCV18885

CASE NAME:                        Joseph Daneshrad, et al v. Daniel Davidovitz, et al                         

MOVING PARTY:                 Defendant Daniel Davidovitz

OPPOSING PARTY:             Plaintiff Joseph Daneshrad

PROCEEDING:                      Motion to strike or, in the alternative, tax costs

RULING SUMMARY:          The Motion to strike is denied. The alternative motion to tax is granted in part as follows: the Court taxes $2,107.23, and allows the remaining amount of costs in the Memorandum of Costs.

                                                                                                                                               

Background

 

I.         Legal Standard

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5 sets forth the costs recoverable by the prevailing party. To recover a cost, it must be reasonably necessary to the litigation and reasonable in amount. (Perko’s Enterprises, Inc. v. RRNS Enterprises (l992) 4 Cal.App.4th 238, 244.)  "If the items appearing in a cost bill appear to be proper charges, the burden is on the party seeking to tax costs to show that they were not reasonable or necessary. (Ladas v. California State Automotive Assoc. (1993) 19 Cal.App.4th 761, 773-74.) “On the other hand, if the items are properly objected to, they are put in issue and the burden of proof is on the party claiming them as costs.” (Ibid.)¿ 

¿ 

The losing party may dispute any or all of the items in the prevailing party’s memorandum of costs by a motion to strike or tax costs. (CRC, rule 3.1700(b).) A motion to strike challenges the entire costs memorandum, whereas a motion to tax challenges particular items or amounts.

 

II.        Discussion

 

Judgment in the amount of $10,000.00, was issued on May 13, 2026, in favor of Plaintiffs.  On the Davidovitz parties’ claims against Daneshrad, the Court found for Daneshrad. Plaintiffs now seek $4,103.34 in costs. Defendants move to strike or reduce the entire memorandum of costs as follows:

 

A.    Strike Costs

 

Defendants argue that the motion to strike costs should be granted under Code Civ. Proc., §1033(a) because Plaintiffs recovered only $10,000, which falls within the limited civil jurisdiction. Plaintiffs elected to litigate this matter as an unlimited civil case but recovered an amount that could have been awarded in a limited civil action.

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 1033(a) applies only when “the prevailing party recovers a judgment that could have been rendered in a limited civil case.” Here, the judgment resolved two actions tried together, including Defendants’ own claim for $350,000, an amount far exceeding the $35,000 limited-civil ceiling. Therefore, the motion to strike costs is denied.

 

B.    Tax Costs

 

1.     Item 1(c) – Appellate filing ad record costs

 

Plaintiffs concede this item because the appellate disposition awarded costs on appeal to Defendants (Opposition, p.17.) The Court taxes $877.25

 

2.     Item 14 – Electronic- Filing Fees.

 

Filing fees are recoverable under Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5 subdivision (a)(1).

 

Plaintiffs concede a reduction of $49.53, which includes $25.02 in appeal related charges and $24.51 they cannot reconcile to the receipts, leaving $255.75. The Court taxes $49.53.

 

3.     Items 1(e) and 1(f) – Summary Judgment Filing Fees

 

As discussed above, filing fees are recoverable. Defendants claim the filing fees for the two motions for summary judgment filed in this matter exceed ordinary costs.

 

The Court does not find that the motions were necessary and therefore taxes the costs for the motions for summary judgment (Items 1(e) and 1(f)) in the amount of $1,030.45.  On February 11, 2025, the Court found the second motion for summary judgment to be procedurally improper.  On June 6, 2024, the Court found that Daneshrad had failed to address all of the Davidovitz parties’ claims and therefore, could not show Daneshrad was entitled to a ruling in his favor on the first motion for summary judgment. 

 

4.     Item 2 – Jury Fees

 

Jury Fees are recoverable costs under code of civil procedure section 1032(a)(1). Defendants argue that Plaintiffs are only entitled to $150 out of the $300 sought. Section 631, subdivision (b) requires “[a]t least one party demanding a jury on each side of a civil case to pay a non-refundable fee of one hundred fifty dollars ($150). Because Daneshrad could have filed his claim as a cross-complaint against the Davidovitz parties, rather than as a separate action, and the case cases were ultimately consolidated, the Court taxes one of the jury fees deposited, in the amount of $150. 

 

5.     Item 13 – Models/enlargements/photocopies of exhibits

 

Defendants take issue with Item 13 stating Plaintiff provides no itemization, invoices, or proof of payment.

 

Plaintiff states that to comply with Department 37’s trial preparation order he had to prepare five identical sets of consolidated exhibit records containing 506 pages. However, Plaintiff declares in his declaration that the binders were prepared in his office and cost $0.20 per page.

 

Code Civ. Proc. Section 1033.5 states that “[m]odels, the enlargements of exhibits and photocopies of exhibits, and the electronic presentation of exhibits, including costs of rental equipment and electronic formatting, may be allowed if they were reasonably helpful to aid the trier of fact” are allowable costs under Section 1032.  

 

The Court finds that exhibit binders were necessary for the non-jury trial conducted and the fees sought are reasonable. As a result, Item 13 is fully recoverable in the amount of $540.52.

 

6.     Unchallenged Items – Item 1(a), 1(b), 1(d), and 5

 

Defendants’ motion does not object to any of these costs. Each are costs allowed under Code Civ. Proc Section 1033.5 and are supported by receipts.

 

Conclusion

 

The Motion to strike is denied. The alternative motion to tax is granted in part: the court taxes $877.25 (item c) and $49.53(item 14) as conceded by plaintiffs. Costs are awarded to the Plaintiff in the amount of $3,176.56

Case Number: 24STCV21056    Hearing Date: September 16, 2026    Dept: 413

TENTATIVE RULING

HEARING DATE:                 September 16, 2026

CASE NUMBER:                   24STCV21056 

CASE NAME:                        John Jezzini v. Hillcrest Davidson and Associates, LLC, et al.

MOVING PARTY:                 Plaintiff and Cross-Defendant JLS Capital, LLC

JOINDERS IN MOTION:      Cross-Defendants John Jezzini, Elevate Holdings, First Defense Security, Inc., High Note, Inc., and 15916 High Knoll Road, LLC

OPPOSING PARTY:             Cross-Complainant Hillcrest Davidson and Associates, LLC

PROCEEDING:                      Special Motion to Strike (Code Civ. Proc. § 425.16) 

RULING SUMMARY:          JLS’s special motion to strike the first through fifth causes of action of the Cross-Complaint and the other Cross-Defendants’ joinders in the motion is GRANTED.  JLS may file a motion for attorneys’ fees pursuant to section 425.16(c) of the Code of Civil Procedure.  The Court continues the Case Management Conference to _____________, 2026,at 8:30AM. 

                                                                                                                                               

Background

 

On August 15, 2024, John Jezzini (“Plaintiff”) filed a Complaint against Hillcrest Davidson and Associates, LLC (“Hillcrest”), Kenny Williams (“Williams”) (collectively “Defendants”), and Does 1 to 50. The Complaint alleges causes of action for Libel and Civil Harassment.  

 

On June 17, 2025, Defendants filed a cross-complaint against Plaintiff, JLS Capital LLC (“JLS”), Sean Atwood, Elevate Manufacturing, Inc., Elevate Holdings, 15916 High Knoll Road LLC, Canna Pharmaceuticals, Inc., Green Healing, High Note, Inc., WHHD, Inc., First Defense Security, Inc. (collectively, “Cross-Defendants”), and Roes 1 to 10.  The Cross-Complaint alleges causes of action for (1) Abuse of Process, (2) Malicious Prosecution, (3) Fraud, (4) Civil Conspiracy, (5) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress, (6) Common Count for Money Had and Received, and (7) Common Count for Account Stated.

 

On May 4, 2026, JLS filed a Special Motion to Strike under CCP § 425.16 (“anti-SLAPP”).  The same day, Cross-Defendants John Jezzini, Elevate Holdings, First Defense Security, Inc., High Note, Inc., and 15916 High Knoll Road, LLC each filed a notice of joinder, adopting the motion on the ground that the allegations against them are identical to those against JLS. 

 

Hillcrest opposes the Motion.

 

Special Motion to Strike (CODE cIV. pROC., § 425.16) 

 

I.         Legal Standard 

  

Section 425.16 of the Code of Civil Procedure sets forth the procedure governing anti-SLAPP motions.¿ In pertinent part, it provides:

 

A cause of action against a person arising from any act of that person in furtherance of the person’s right of petition or free speech under the United States Constitution or the California Constitution in connection with a public issue shall be subject to a special motion to strike, unless the court determines that the plaintiff has established that there is a probability that the plaintiff will prevail on the claim.

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16(b)(1).) “[A]n anti-SLAPP motion, like a conventional motion to strike, may be used to attack parts of a count as pleaded.” (Baral v. Schnitt¿(2016) 1 Cal.5th 376, 393.)  The purpose of the statute is to identify and dispose of lawsuits brought to chill the valid exercise of a litigant’s constitutional right of petition or free speech. (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16,(a); Sylmar Air Conditioning v. Pueblo Contracting Services, Inc. (2004) 122 Cal.App.4th 1049, 1055-1056.)¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿¿ 

¿¿¿¿¿¿ 

Courts employ a two-step process to evaluate anti-SLAPP motions. (Equilon Enterprises v. Consumer Cause, Inc. (2002) 29 Cal.4th 53, 61.) To invoke the statute's protections, the defendant must first show that the challenged lawsuit arises from protected activity, such as an act in furtherance of the right of petition or free speech. (Ibid.) From this fact, courts “‘presume the purpose of the action was to chill the defendant’s exercise of First Amendment rights.¿ It is then up to the plaintiff to rebut the presumption by showing a reasonable probability of success on the merits.’” (Ibid.)  In determining whether the plaintiff has carried this burden, the trial court considers “the pleadings, and supporting and opposing affidavits stating the facts upon which the liability or defense is based.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16(b)(2); see Soukup v. Law Offices of Herbert Hafif (2006) 39 Cal.4th 260, 291.)¿¿¿¿¿¿ 

 

II.        Discussion

 

JLS moves to strike the Cross-Complaint’s first through fifth causes of action on the grounds that each cause of action is based primarily on statements made in the Complaint in this action and interferes with JLS’s statutorily protected speech. (Mot., p. 1.)

 

a.     Timeliness and Joinders

 

Hillcrest argues the motion and joinders must be denied on the ground that they are untimely because filed more than 60 days after service of the Cross-Complaint. (Opp., p. 3.)

 

A special motion to strike “may be filed within 60 days of the service of the complaint or, in the court’s discretion, at any later time upon terms it deems proper.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16(f).)

 

Cross-Defendants were served with the Cross-Complaint on March 6, 2026. (See 040/3/2026 Proof of Service.)  JLS’s anti-SLAPP motion was filed on May 4, 2026.  Measured from the March 6 service date, JLS’s motion, and Cross-Defendants Elevate Holdings, First Defense Security, High Note, and 15916 High Knoll Road’s joinders, were filed 59 days later. Therefore, JLS’s motion, and the previously mentioned Cross-Defendants' joinders, are timely.

 

Cross-Defendant Jezzini answered the Cross-Complaint on July 15, 2025, but did not file his joinder until May 4, 2026, well beyond the 60-day time limit.  The Court nonetheless exercises its discretion under section 425.16(f) to hear Jezzini’s joinder. (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16(f) [“may be filed….in the court’s discretion, at any later time upon terms it deems proper.”].)

 

In Six4Three, LLC v. Facebook, Inc., the Court of Appeal found the trial court had not abused its discretion where the trial court considered Facebook’s untimely motion after granting other defendants’ timely motion “based on largely the same allegations and same causes of action,” because it served “both judicial economy and the public policy behind the anti-SLAPP statute.” (Six4Three, LLC v. Facebook, Inc. (2025) 109 Cal.App.5th 635, 646.)  Similar to Facebook, here Cross-Defendant Jezzini’s joinder is based on the same allegations and same causes of action as the other Cross-Defendants’ joinders, discovery is still open, and no trial date has been set.  Permitting Cross-Defendant Jezzini’s joinder advances the purpose of the anti-SLAPP statute.

 

JLS’s motion is thus timely, and Cross-Defendants' joinders are permitted.

 

b.     Prong One – Protected Activity  

 

As noted above, “[i]n ruling on a defendant's anti-SLAPP motion, the trial court engages in a two-step analysis.”  (Navellier v. Sletten (2002) 29 Cal.4th 82, 88.)  First, the court determines “whether the defendant has made a threshold showing that the challenged cause of action is one arising from protected activity,” which includes the defendants’ right of petition, or free speech, under a constitution, in connection with issues of public interest. (Ibid.; Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16.) “[T]he moving defendant must identify the acts alleged in the complaint that it asserts are protected and what claims for relief are predicated on them. In turn, a court should examine whether those acts are protected and supply the basis for any claims.” (Bonni v. St. Joseph Health System (2021) 11 Cal.5th 995, 1010.)¿¿¿

 

JLS argues the common thread in the Cross-Complaint is that each claim is primarily based on statements made in the Complaint, which are statutorily protected speech. (Mot., p. 1.)

 

An “act in furtherance of a person’s right of petition or free speech under the United States or California Constitution in connection with a public issue,” includes “any written or oral statement or writing made before a legislative, executive, or judicial proceeding, or any other official proceeding authorized by law.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16(e)(1).)  The constitutional right of petition encompasses “the basic act of filing litigation.”  (Navellier, supra, 29 Cal.4th at p. 90, citing Briggs v. Eden Council for Hope Opportunity (1999) 19 Cal.4th 1106, 1115.)  Cross-complaints “alleging a cause of action arising from the plaintiff’s act of filing the complaint against the defendant and the subsequent litigation,” may qualify as an anti-SLAPP action.  (Raining Data Corp. v. Barrenechea (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 1363, 1373 [finding a person may attempt to bring an anti-SLAPP suit alleging that libelous allegations or statements were contained in the complaint itself].)  Causes of action for “malicious prosecution and abuse of process,” by definition arise from the protected activity of filing a complaint. (Id., at p. 1368.)

 

“A claim arises from protected activity when that activity underlies or forms the basis for the claim.”  (Park v. Board of Trustees of California State University (2017) 2 Cal.5th 1057, 1062.)  The speech or petitioning activity itself must be “the wrong complained of.”  (Id. at p. 1060.)  “Allegations of protected activity that merely provide context, without supporting a claim for recovery, cannot be stricken under the anti-SLAPP statute.”  (Baral v. Schnitt (2016) 1 Cal.5th 376, 394.) 

 

Here, the first and second causes of action allege Cross-Defendants “filed a groundless lawsuit or legal proceeding in this action,” without “sufficient legal and factual basis and with intent to abuse the cross-complainant system based on fraudulent allegations.” (Cross-Complaint, ¶¶ 1, 11.)  The filing of the complaint is the basis of both the first and second causes of action, and is the wrong complained of in both claims. Therefore, both the first and second causes of action arise from protected activity.

 

The third cause of action alleges the Cross-Defendants “knew that the representations were false when they made them and made the representations recklessly and without regard for its truth.” (Cross-Complaint, ¶ 21.) The basis of the claim is the content of the pleading, and therefore the third cause of action arises from protected petitioning activity.

 

The fourth and fifth causes of action allege and incorporate each allegation of the first through third causes of action, and rest on the same conduct, i.e., the filing of the Complaint and the statements within.  (Cross-Complaint, ¶¶ 28-41.)

 

To the extent the first and second causes of action allege Cross-Defendants orally uttered false allegations concerning the content of the Complaint, these allegations do not bar the action from being subjected to section 425.16. (See Cross-Complaint, ¶¶ 2, 12.)  “[W]here a cause of action alleges both protected and unprotected activity, the cause of action will be subject to section 425.16 unless the protected conduct is ‘merely incidental’ to the unprotected conduct.” (Raining Data Corp., supra, 175 Cal.App.4th at p. 1369, citing Mann v. Quality Old Time Service, Inc. (2004) 120 Cal.App.4th 90, 103.)

 

Here, because the filing of the Complaint is not “merely incidental” to the causes of action in the Cross-Complaint, any oral communications regarding the contents of the Complaint do not save the Cross-Complaint from JLS’s anti-SLAPP motion.

 

Based on the foregoing, the Court finds JLS has met its moving burden, as the basis of the claims, i.e., the filing of the Complaint and the content of the Complaint, constitute protected petitioning activity.

 

c.      Prong Two – Probability of Prevailing   

 

Cross-Defendants¿having met their burden¿on the first prong, the burden shifts to Cross-Complainant to¿demonstrate¿a probability of prevailing on the merits.¿¿(Equilon, supra, 29 Cal.4th at 67.)¿ The second prong is framed by the complaint (or cross-complaint), but Cross-Complainant cannot rely on the Cross-Complaint.¿¿(Martin v. Inland Empire Utilities Agency¿(2011) 198 Cal.App.4th¿611, 630.)¿ Cross-Complainant must produce evidence showing at least part of the cause of action has merit.¿¿(Id.;¿A.F. Brown Electrical Contractor, Inc. v. Rhino Elec. Supply, Inc.¿(2006) 137 Cal.App.4th 1118, 1124.) 

 

Hillcrest fails to meet its burden.  Hillcrest argues there is a showing of probability that Hillcrest will prevail in this action based on the face of the pleadings and Cross-Defendants’ own admissions. (Opp., p. 7.)  Hillcrest argues that Cross-Defendants have not yet filed a verified answer to the cross-complaint, and that Cross-Defendants’ defenses are vague and do not make a showing of any credible defense. (Opp., pp. 7-8.)  Hillcrest argues that a triable issue of fact exists. (Opp., p. 8.)

 

Hillcrest submits only the declaration of Kenny Williams, which recites the legal argument of Hillcrest’s opposition brief.  Hillcrest does not submit any evidence showing that the causes of action have merit. A party cannot meet the second prong by resting on the allegations of the pleading or conclusory statements. Hillcrest has not made a prima facie evidentiary showing, and thus has not met its burden.

 

Further, the first, third, fourth, and fifth causes of action are barred as a matter of law.  The litigation privilege of Civil Code section 47 (b), is absolute and applies to “all torts except malicious prosecution,” having been held to immunize defendants from liability for abuse of process, intentional infliction of emotional distress, and fraud.” (Silberg v. Anderson (1990) 50 Cal.3d 205, 212.)  It attaches to any communication “(1) made in judicial or quasi-judicial proceedings; (2) by litigants or other participants authorized by law; (3) to achieve the objects of the litigation; and (4) that have some connection or logical relation to the action.” (Ibid.)

 

The filing of the Complaint and the contents of the Complaint satisfy each element.  Therefore, the first, third, fourth, and fifth causes of action are barred under Civil Code section 47, subdivision (b), and Hillcrest cannot show a probability of prevailing on them.  As to the malicious prosecution cause of action, Hillcrest cannot show a favorable termination on the claims in the Complaint; therefore, it cannot show a probability of prevailing on that claim. 

 

 

Conclusion

 

JLS’s special motion to strike the first through fifth causes of action of the Cross-Complaint is GRANTED.

 

The joinders of John Jezzini, Elevate Holdings, First Defense Security, Inc., High Note, Inc., and 15916 High Knoll Road, LLC are GRANTED.

 

The first, second, third, fourth, and fifth causes of action are stricken as to JLS Capital, LLC and each joining Cross-Defendant.

TENTATIVE RULING

HEARING DATE:                 September 16, 2026

CASE NUMBER:                   25VECV02593

CASE NAME:                        Farmers Insurance Exchange v. Karen Shainsky, et al.    

MOVING PARTY:                Defendant and Cross-Complainant Karen Shainsky  

MOTION:                               Defendant and Cross-Complainant’s Motion to Compel Responses to Requests for Production, Set One

OPPOSITION:                        None  

REPLY:                                  None  

                                                                                                                                                           

RULING SUMMARY:          Shainsky’s motion is granted; Farmers shall produce the responsive documents that its Response stated would be produced, within 10 days of this Ruling.  Shainsky’s request for sanctions is granted in the amount of $660.00. Shainsky is to give notice.

 

                                                                                                                                                           

 

Background

On January 3, 2025, Karen Shainsky (Shainsky) filed a petition to confirm appraisal award against Farmers Insurance Exchange (Farmers) under lead case number 25STCP00038 and on March 18, 2025, the Court confirmed the award acknowledging that the award did not address coverage issues and that any issues could be resolved in a subsequent lawsuit. 

On May 9, 2025, Farmers filed a Complaint under case number 25VECV02593 against Shainsky for declaratory relief seeking a judicial declaration of rights, duties and obligations under the Policy (Declaratory Relief Action).  Shainsky filed a Cross-Complaint for breach of contract, breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, and unfair business practices (Breach Action) against Farmers. 

On January 28, 2026, the cases were consolidated.  

On June 1, 2026, Shainsky filed a motion to compel Farmers to comply with its own written verified responses to her Requests for Production, in which it stated that it would produce responsive documents, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.320.

No opposition has been filed.

Discussion

“If a party filing a response to a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling under Sections 2031.210, 2031.220, 2031.230, 2031.240, and 2031.280 thereafter fails to permit the inspection, copying, testing, or sampling in accordance with that party's statement of compliance, the demanding party may move for an order compelling compliance.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.320, subd. (a).)  

 

Shainsky asserts she propounded her Request for Production of Documents, Set No. One, on Farmers, and Farmers served its written responses on April 21, 2026. (Mot., p. 3.)  Shainsky contends Farmers explicitly agreed to produce documents responsive to Request Nos. 1-7, but has failed to tender the documents. (Mot., p. 3.)  Shainsky argues the Court must compel compliance under Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.320. (Mot., pp. 4-5.)

 

Having committed itself to production, Farmers is obligated to tender the documents in accordance with its statement of compliance.  Shainsky asserts Farmers has produced no documents, and Farmers has not filed an opposition.  Nothing before the Court excuses the failure to produce documents.

 

Accordingly, Shainsky’s motion is granted.

 

Monetary Sanctions

The Court “shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel compliance with a demand, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.320, subd. (b).)

 

Shainsky seeks monetary sanctions in the amount of $1,260.00, consisting of 1.5 hours drafting the motion, an anticipated 1.5 hours reviewing the opposition and drafting a reply brief, and preparing for and attending the hearing, at an hourly rate of $400, plus the $60 filing fee. 

 

The Court finds the amount requested unreasonable, as the motion is straightforward and unopposed.  The Court thus awards monetary sanctions to Shainsky against Farmers and its counsel in the reduced amount of $660.00, consisting of 1 hour drafting the motion and 0.5 hours attending the hearing at an hourly rate of $400, plus the $60 filing fee.

 

Conclusion

 

Shainsky’s motion is granted; Farmers shall produce the responsive documents that its Response stated would be produced, within 10 days of this Ruling. Shainsky’s request for sanctions is granted in the amount of $660.00. Shainsky is to give notice.
