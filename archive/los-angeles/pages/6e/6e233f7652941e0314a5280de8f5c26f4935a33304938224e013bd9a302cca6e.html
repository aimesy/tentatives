DEPARTMENT 406 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 24STCV07459    Hearing Date: September 16, 2026    Dept: 406

 

MARIA BRIZUELA,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

PACIFIC ENERGY NETWORK LLC, et al.,

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  24STCV07459

  Hearing Date:  September 16, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

defendant sunlight financial llc’s motion to compel arbitration

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On March 25, 2024, Plaintiff Maria Brizuela filed this action against Defendants Pacific Energy Network LLC (Pacific), Cross River Bank (CRB), and Business Alliance Insurance Company (BAIC). The dispute involves two contracts: (1) an installation contract between Plaintiff and Pacific for the installation of solar panels on Plaintiff’s property; and (2) a loan agreement between Plaintiff and CRB to finance the installation. The latter agreement contains an arbitration provision. 

On July 1, 2024, the Court granted CRB’s motion to compel arbitration and stayed the case as to CRB only.

On November 6, 2025, Plaintiff filed an amendment, naming Sunlight Financial LLC (Sunlight) as Doe 1.

On December 2, 2025, the Court granted a stipulation between Plaintiff and BAIC for Plaintiff to file a First Amended Complaint.

On December 15, 2025, Plaintiff filed the FAC.

On February 20, 2026, BAIC was dismissed pursuant to Plaintiff’s request.

On June 16, 2026, Sunlight filed the instant motion to compel arbitration. Plaintiff filed an opposition on September 2, 2026. Sunlight filed a reply on September 9, 2026.

LEGAL STANDARD

“On petition of a party to an arbitration agreement alleging the existence of a written agreement to arbitrate a controversy and that a party to the agreement refuses to arbitrate that controversy, the court shall order the petitioner and the respondent to arbitrate the controversy if it determines that an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists….” (Code Civ. Proc, § 1281.2.) “The party seeking arbitration bears the burden of proving the existence of an arbitration agreement, and the party opposing arbitration bears the burden of proving any defense, such as unconscionability.” (Pinnacle Museum Tower Assn. v. Pinnacle Market Development (US), LLC (2012) 55 Cal.4th 223, 236.)

DISCUSSION

I. Existence of Agreement

            a. Proof of Agreement

“The moving party ‘can meet its initial burden by attaching to the motion or petition a copy of the arbitration agreement purporting to bear the opposing party's signature.’” (Gamboa v. Northeast Community Clinic (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 158, 165.) “At this step, a movant need not ‘follow the normal procedures of document authentication’ and need only ‘allege the existence of an agreement and support the allegation.’” (Iyere v. Wise Auto Group (2023) 87 Cal.App.5th 747, 755.)  

Here, Sunlight relies on the arbitration provision in Plaintiff’s loan agreement with CRB. The agreement contains an arbitration provision stating that “either party may elect to require arbitration of any Claim under this Provision.” (Ex. 1 at p. 13.) The provision defines a covered “Claim” in the following manner:

 

any claim, dispute or controversy between you and us (or any Related Party) that arises from or relates in any way to this Note (including any amendment, modification or extension of this Note), the Contractor Agreement, the work performed by the Contractor or a subcontractor; the System, including maintenance and servicing of the System; the arrangements between and among us, Sunlight and the Contractor; any of our marketing, advertising, solicitations and conduct relating to your request for credit or the System; our collection of any amounts you owe; or our disclosure of or failure to protect any information about you.

(Ibid.)

            The provision further defines “Related Parties” as “all our parent companies, subsidiaries and affiliates, as well as the Contractor and Sunlight Financial, LLC (which has provided services to Lender and Contractor in connection with this Note) and their parent companies, subsidiaries and affiliates, and our and their employees, directors, officers, shareholders, governors, managers and members.” (Ibid.)

            This agreement facially covers the claims asserted by Plaintiff against Sunlight. Sunlight is entitled to enforce the agreement as a named third-party beneficiary. (See Ford Motor Warranty Cases (2023) 89 Cal.App.5th 1324, 1339.) Thus, Sunlight has satisfied its initial burden on the motion.

            b. Fraud in Execution

            Fraud in the execution of a contract occurs when a party “is deceived as to the nature of his act, and actually does not know what he is signing, or does not intend to enter into a contract at all.” (Rosenthal v. Great Western Fin. Securities Corp. (1996) 14 Cal.4th 394, 415.) In such a case, “mutual assent is lacking, and [the contract] is void.” (Ibid.) Fraud in the inducement, by contrast, occurs when “the promisor knows what he is signing but his consent is induced by fraud.” (Ibid.) In such a case, “mutual assent is present and a contract is formed, which, by reason of the fraud, is voidable.” (Ibid.)

            “[C]laims of fraud in the execution of the entire agreement are not arbitrable under either state or federal law.” (Rosenthal, supra, 14 Cal.4th at p. 416.) “If the entire contract is void ab initio because of fraud, the parties have not agreed to arbitrate any controversy.” (Ibid.) “In the absence of a contrary agreement, parties to a predispute arbitration agreement are presumed to have intended arbitration of controversies, including allegations of fraud in the inducement of the contract generally, that may allow rescission or reformation of the contract or part of it. They cannot, however, have intended arbitration under a contract wholly void for fraud in its execution.” (Id. at p. 417.) “[T]he question of fraud in the execution is for the trial court to decide.” (Ibid.) The party asserting fraud bears the burden of proving it by a preponderance of the evidence. (Id. at p. 413.)

            Here, Plaintiff argues that the agreement void for fraud in the execution because “the entire nature and source of this transaction was concealed from Plaintiff. She was not informed that SUNLIGHT was the party with whom they were negotiating, that they were paying SUNLIGHT’s fees in the HIC, that SUNLIGHT predetermined their lender and financial terms, or that PACIFIC and CRB were the partners of SUNLIGHT. The entire project is the product of SUNLIGHT’s solar lending program.” (Opp. 6:23-28.) Plaintiff further contends she was “unaware that, even though she spoke with only one salesperson for both the PVS and financing, the defendants’ intent was to disassociate the Loan from the PVS. The Loan terms require repayment regardless of whether Plaintiff receives a functioning PVS. The terms make the PVS ancillary to selling a Loan and paying SUNLIGHT a Dealer Fee.” (Opp. 7:1-5.)

            None of these facts are reflected in the evidence submitted by Plaintiff. There is no evidence that Plaintiff did not know what she was signing or did not intend to enter a contract at all. To the contrary, Plaintiff’s declaration shows that she understood she was contracting for the installation of solar panels and additionally signing an associated loan agreement with CRB (through Sunlight) to finance the installation. (See Brizuela Decl. ¶¶ 4-5.) Plaintiff avers that the salesman “did not tell me that Sunlight was charging a fee that was included in the price of the system. If I knew that, I would not have signed the Installation Contract or the Loan.” (Id., ¶ 8.) However, there is no evidence that anyone misrepresented the total price to Plaintiff, or that Plaintiff was unaware of the amount of the loan she was signing. Plaintiff’s ignorance of the precise breakdown of the total amount is not evidence of fraud in the execution.

Moreover, Plaintiff’s contention that she would not have signed the loan had she known the price included a fee to Sunlight, at most describes fraud in the inducement, which does not void the arbitration provision. (See Rosenthal, supra, 14 Cal.4th at pp. 415-17.) Not being told that a fraction of the price included a fee to Sunlight is not evidence that Plaintiff was deceived as to the nature of the contract or did not intend to enter a contract at all. Plaintiff still knew she was signing a loan, knew what the loan was for, knew the amount, and intended to sign it.  

            The remaining claims in Plaintiff’s declaration similarly do not describe fraud in the execution. Plaintiff avers that she “had never heard of Sunlight or CRB and did not know that financing was available through them.” (Brizuela Decl. ¶ 6.) Plaintiff further claims that the salesman “did not tell me who he was applying to. He did not give me an option to choose another lender or an opportunity to shop around for loans. He did not give me an opportunity to negotiate the terms. He had a link for the Loan sent to my phone and told me where to sign.” (Id., ¶ 7.) None of this shows that Plaintiff was unaware of what she was signing when she executed the loan agreement, nor does it show that Plaintiff did not intend to enter a contract at all. Plaintiff may not have been aware of other lending options, and may not have been able to negotiate the terms of the loan, but she still knew what she was signing and intended to sign it.

            The declaration of Plaintiff’s counsel is equally unhelpful. The only documentary evidence consists of a rate sheet and an investment brochure. (Valdez Decl., Ex. 4, 5.) This does not demonstrate fraud in the execution of the particular loan agreement at issue. Plaintiff’s counsel also notes that Sunlight has been sued in other states in connection with its lending program. (Id., ¶ 5.) However, these unrelated and nonbinding cases have no bearing on the particular loan agreement at issue in this case. Lastly, Plaintiff’s counsel avers that there was no itemization of costs and that the price was, in his assessment, “extremely high.” (Id., ¶ 6.) This has no bearing on fraud in the execution. An unfair price is not evidence that Plaintiff was deceived as to the nature of the contract or did not intend to enter the contract. Plaintiff does not contend that the amount was misrepresented.  

            In sum, Plaintiff has failed to prove fraud in the execution by a preponderance of the evidence. As a result, the Court finds that an arbitration agreement exists which covers the claims against Sunlight.

II. Unconscionability

Unconscionability has both a procedural and a substantive element. (Aron v. U-Haul Co. of California (2006) 143 Cal.App.4th 796, 808.) Both elements must be present for a court to invalidate a contract or clause. (Ibid.) However, the two elements need not be present in the same degree; courts use a sliding scale approach in assessing the two elements. (Carbajal v. CWPSC, Inc. (2016) 245 Cal.App.4th 227, 242.)

a. Procedural Unconscionability

Procedural unconscionability “focuses on two factors: ‘oppression’ and ‘surprise.’ ‘Oppression’ arises from an inequality of bargaining power which results in no real negotiation and ‘an absence of meaningful choice.’ ‘Surprise’ involves the extent to which the supposedly agreed-upon terms of the bargain are hidden in the prolix printed form drafted by the party seeking to enforce the disputed terms.” (Zullo v. Superior Court (2011) 197 Cal.App.4th 477, 484, internal citations omitted.)

            Plaintiff argues that the agreement was procedurally unconscionable because she had no opportunity to negotiate it. However, Plaintiff has no evidence that she could not negotiate the loan terms. Plaintiff’s claim that the salesman “did not give me an opportunity to negotiate the terms” is vague. (See Brizuela Decl. ¶ 7.) There is no indication that Plaintiff attempted to negotiate or was prevented from doing so. Moreover, Plaintiff did not have to enter into the loan agreement. The fact that the salesman did not affirmatively identify alternative options for Plaintiff does not mean Plaintiff was prevented from seeking other lending options. Plaintiff also did not have to obtain a loan at all or purchase solar panels at all. Lastly, even if the contract were adhesive, that would only present a minimal degree of procedural unconscionability. (See Serpa v. California Surety Investigations, Inc. (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 695, 704.)

            Plaintiff also argues that material terms of the loan were concealed from her. However, as discussed above, Plaintiff lacks evidence of these contentions. Moreover, this has no bearing on the validity of the arbitration provision. On a motion to compel arbitration, unconscionability must pertain to the arbitration provision. (Moncharsh v. Heily & Blase (1992) 3 Cal.4th 1, 29.) Disputes over the validity of the larger contract are themselves subject to arbitration. (Ibid.)

            In sum, the Court finds no procedural unconscionability.

b. Substantive Unconscionability

Substantive unconscionability focuses on the actual terms of the agreement and evaluates whether they create overly harsh or one-sided results as to shock the conscience. (Suh v. Superior Court (2010) 181 Cal.App.4th 1504, 1515.)

            Plaintiff’s only point here is that the Court already found a small degree of substantive unconscionability in the same agreement when it granted CRB’s motion to compel arbitration. But the Court also found no procedural unconscionability in the prior motion and does so again on this motion. Therefore, there is insufficient unconscionability to invalidate the agreement, and the agreement remains enforceable.

III. Reconsideration

            Plaintiff’s opposition contains a request to reconsider the Court’s order granting CRB’s motion to compel arbitration. Such a request is not properly made in opposition to a different party’s motion. Moreover, Plaintiff presents no new facts, circumstances, or law to justify reconsideration. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 1008.) Therefore, reconsideration is unwarranted.

IV. Discovery

            Plaintiff argues that the motion should be continued to permit additional discovery. However, Plaintiff fails to identify any discovery that would alter the result of this motion. The existing record is dispositive. Therefore, a continuance is not warranted.  

CONCLUSION

            Defendant Sunlight Financial LLC’s motion to compel arbitration is GRANTED.

 


Case Number: 25STCV10841    Hearing Date: September 16, 2026    Dept: 406

 

ARI BAER,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

SHOMER INSURANCE SERVICES, AN ALERA GROUP AGENCY, LLC, et al.,

                       

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  25STCV10841

  Hearing Date:  September 16, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

plaintiff’s motion for summary adjudication

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On April 11, 2025, Plaintiff Ari Baer filed this action against Defendants Shomer Insurance Services, an Alera Group Agency, LLC (Shomer); Alera Group Ultimate Holdings, LLC (Alera Holdings); and Alera Group Holdings, Inc. (Alera Corp.) The complaint asserts causes of action for (1) declaratory relief, (2) unfair business practices, and (3) breach of contract. The dispute stems from the following allegations.

            In May 2019, Plaintiff began his employment with Shomer, which was founded as part of an acquisition by Alera Corp. Alera Holdings owns or controls both Shomer and Alera Corp. Plaintiff terminated his employment at Shomer effective September 2024. Plaintiff alleges that his Employment Agreement with Shomer unlawfully restrains his ability to engage in a trade, business, or profession. Plaintiff also alleges that he acquired substantial equity in Alera Holdings as a result of his work at Shomer. Alera Holdings exercised its right under its Operating Agreement to purchase Plaintiff’s equity. However, Alera Holdings allegedly failed to perform its obligations under the Operating Agreement by paying the contractual price of $10.3 million.

            Through this action, Plaintiff seeks a judicial declaration that the restrictive covenants and noncompete clauses in the Employment Agreement constitute an unlawful restraint of trade in violation of Business and Professions Code section 16600. Plaintiff further alleges that Defendants’ attempts to enforce the covenants are acts of unfair competition in violation of Business and Professions Code section 17200. Lastly, Plaintiff seeks $10.3 million for Defendants’ breach of the equity purchase provisions of the Operating Agreement.

            On January 7, 2026, the Court stayed the third cause of action due to an overlapping declaratory relief action in Delaware filed by Alera Holdings.

            On June 15, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion for summary adjudication of the first cause of action. Defendants filed an opposition on August 27, 2026. Plaintiff filed a reply on September 4, 2026.

EVIDENTIARY OBJECTIONS

            Plaintiff’s objections are overruled.

LEGAL STANDARD

The function of a motion for summary judgment or adjudication is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party cannot show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) Code of Civil Procedure section 437c(c) “requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.)

“[T]he party moving for summary judgment bears an initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing of the nonexistence of any triable issue of material fact; if he carries his burden of production, he causes a shift, and the opposing party is then subjected to a burden of production of his own to make a prima facie showing of the existence of a triable issue of material fact.” (Leyva v. Garcia (2018) 20 Cal.App.5th 1095, 1101.) To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce “substantial responsive evidence.” (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.) Courts “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.” (Dore v. Arnold Worldwide, Inc. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 384, 389.)

DISCUSSION

I. Plaintiff’s Allegations

“Any person interested under a written instrument . . . or under a contract . . . may, in cases of actual controversy relating to the legal rights and duties of the respective parties, bring an original action or cross-complaint in the superior court for a declaration of his or her rights and duties . . . arising under the instrument or contract.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1060.) A declaratory relief claim has “two essential elements: ‘(1) a proper subject of declaratory relief, and (2) an actual controversy involving justiciable questions relating to the rights or obligations of a party.’” (Lee v. Silveira (2016) 6 Cal.App.5th 527, 546, quoting Brownfield v. Daniel Freeman Marina Hospital (1989) 208 Cal.App.3d 405, 410.) 

            Here, the complaint seeks declaratory relief “as to the validity of the Restrictive Covenant Agreement and the Additional Noncompete Clauses.” (Compl. ¶ 22.) The Restrictive Covenant Agreement (hereinafter “Employment RCA”) is a part of the Employment Agreement and allegedly “contains both a ‘Non-Solicitation Clause’ (Section 2.1) and an expansive ‘Confidential Information Clause’ (Section 1), both of which restrict and restrain Plaintiff’s ability to engage in any insurance brokerage-related business for at least two years after the termination of his employment with Shomer.” (Id., ¶ 7.) The Additional Noncompete Clauses are defined in the complaint as “similar provisions relating to alleged ‘Confidential Information’ or ‘Non-Solicitation’ in one or more documents relating to the grant of bonus compensation or other aspects of Plaintiff’s relationship with Shomer or Alera Holdings.” (Id., ¶ 19.)

            In the present motion, Plaintiff challenges provisions in the May 2019 Employment RCA, an August 2021 Merger Letter, and June 2023 Unit Award Agreements. 

II. Mootness

            On February 12, 2024, Defendants notified Plaintiff in writing that “any provision in your Restrictive Covenant Agreement which, after termination of your employment, prohibits your solicitation of the Company’s clients, employees or contractors, to the extent that such solicitation does not involve acquisition, use, and/or disclosure of the Company’s trade secret and/or Confidential Information, is void.” (Marathas Decl., Ex. 1.)

            On August 26, 2026, Defendants sent another notice to Plaintiff confirming that “Alera irrevocably waives and disclaims any right to enforce against you, and covenants that it will not seek to enforce against you, any post-employment non-competition covenant, non-solicitation covenant, non-interference covenant, customer restriction, employee restriction, or business-partner restriction contained in the” Employment RCA and Unit Award Agreements. (Marathas Decl., Ex. 2.)

            Defendants argue that because they have expressly waived enforcement of the restrictive covenants in the Employment RCA and Unit Award Agreements, there is no present controversy for the Court to resolve. Defendants also point out that the restrictive provisions in the Merger Letter expired after five years, or on August 25, 2026, thus mooting any controversy over that instrument as well. (Marathas Decl. ¶ 16.)

            However, the February 2024 notice states that “if you sold your business to Alera, any non-compete or other restrictive covenants you may have signed in connection with the sale of your business to Alera, including but not limited to those contained in a Purchase Agreement, are not impacted by this recent change in California law, and they remain in force through the restricted period stated therein.” (Marathas Decl., Ex. 1.) This is a reference to the exception enumerated in Business and Professions Code section 16601, which permits restrictive covenants in association with the sale of a business. Defendants contend that the challenged provisions fall under the section 16601 exception, effectively asserting that the provisions are enforceable. Accordingly, a present controversy exists over the enforceability of the challenged provisions.  

            The August 2026 notice does not compel a different result. The notice expressly states that “[n]othing in this letter shall be construed as . . . an admission that any challenged provision is invalid, unenforceable, or otherwise unlawful” and preserves “any claim or defense that Alera has asserted or may assert in the lawsuit currently pending against Mr. Baer in the Delaware Court of Chancery.” (Marathas Decl., Ex. 2.) Defendants allege in the Delaware action that they had no obligation to pay Plaintiff $10 million for the equity purchase “[g]iven Baer’s breaches of . . . the restrictive covenants in his employment agreement.” (Plntf.’s RJN, Ex. A, ¶ 6.) Thus, Defendants have retained the right to assert, and have in fact asserted, the very provision being challenged by Plaintiff’s declaratory relief action. Accordingly, the declaratory relief claim is not moot.

            The remaining issue is whether Plaintiff is entitled to declaratory relief in his favor as a matter of law.  

III. Entire Cause of Action

            “A motion for summary adjudication shall be granted only if it completely disposes of a cause of action, an affirmative defense, a claim for damages, or an issue of duty.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c(f)(1).)

            Plaintiff’s complaint challenges Section 2.1 and Section 1 of the Employment RCA, but the motion only addresses Section 2.1. Moreover, the motion challenges the 2021 Merger Letter as one of the “Additional Noncompete Clauses.” (See Compl. ¶¶ 19, 22.) However, Plaintiff does not dispute that the restrictive covenants in the Merger Letter expired by their own terms on August 25, 2026. Plaintiff does not articulate a present controversy over the Merger Letter. Lastly, for the reasons discussed below, there are also triable issues as to the validity of the Employment RCA. Therefore, the motion fails to dispose of the entire cause of action.

IV. Business Exception

“Except as provided in this chapter, every contract by which anyone is restrained from engaging in a lawful profession, trade, or business of any kind is to that extent void.” (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 16600(a).) “This section shall be read broadly, in accordance with Edwards v. Arthur Andersen LLP (2008) 44 Cal.4th 937, to void the application of any noncompete agreement in an employment context, or any noncompete clause in an employment contract, no matter how narrowly tailored, that does not satisfy an exception in this chapter.” (Id., § 16600(b)(1).) One such exception permits agreements restricting the seller of a business “from carrying on a similar business within a specified geographic area in which the business so sold, or that of the business entity, division, or subsidiary has been carried on.” (Id., § 16601.) 

Whether a restrictive covenant is valid under section 16601 does not depend on the document in which the covenant appears. (Hilb, Rogal & Hamilton Ins. Services v. Robb (1995) 33 Cal.App.4th 1812, 1825-27.) Thus, a covenant may fall under the business sale exception even if it appears in an employment agreement rather than the merger agreement. (Ibid.) Where an employment agreement and a merger agreement are part of the same transaction, they should be read together. (Ibid.; Civ. Code, § 1642.)  

Here, Section 2.1 of the Employment RCA provides that Plaintiff will not use Confidential Information to solicit, sell to, provide services to, consult for, accept a broker of-record letter with respect to, or accept certain requests involving a Client or Active Prospective Client. (Plntf.’s Separate Stmt. of Undisputed Facts (UF) 16.) The RCA defines Confidential Information to include client identities and the identities of markets or companies from which insurance coverage, commitments, benefits, or services for clients are obtained. (UF 17.)  

A reasonable trier of fact may find that this provision is a valid restrictive covenant under section 16601 because it is tailored to prevent the use of Confidential Information to solicit defined Clients. This is arguably designed to protect the goodwill of the business that Plaintiff sold to Defendants. (See also Marathas Decl. ¶ 6 [“the restrictive covenants in the Employment Agreement were aimed at protecting the goodwill purchased by Alera in 2019 in the event of Mr. Baer’s departure from Alera”].)  

Plaintiff relies on Fillpoint, LLC v. Maas (2012) 208 Cal.App.4th 1170 for the proposition that a restrictive covenant which goes beyond protecting the goodwill of a business remains invalid under section 16600. In Fillpoint, the court found that a stock purchase agreement’s three-year covenant not to compete was sufficient to fulfill the purposes of section 16601. (Id. at p. 1182.) “In contrast, the employment agreement's covenant not to compete was much broader” and “targeted an employee's fundamental right to pursue his or her profession.” (Id. at p. 1183.) Thus, even reading the stock purchase agreement and employment agreement together, the court still found the restrictive covenants in the employment agreement to be invalid. (Id. at p. 1180.)

The challenged provision in Fillpoint is distinguishable from the one in this case. The provision in Fillpoint prevented the employee from “(1) making sales contacts or making actual sales to anyone who was Crave's customer or potential customer during the two years preceding the termination of Maas's employment, or assisting others in doing so; (2) working for or owning an interest in any business that was in the same business as, or would compete with, Crave; or (3) employing or soliciting for employment any of Crave's employees or consultants.” (Fillpoint, supra, 208 Cal.App.4th at p. 1183.) Moreover, the employer conceded that “the two covenants not to compete were intended ‘to deal with the different damage Maas might do wearing the separate hats of major shareholder and key employee.’” (Ibid.) Thus, the restrictive covenant in the employment agreement was clearly not meant to protect the goodwill of the business which was sold.

By contrast, the Employment RCA is limited to the use of Confidential Information and does not outright prevent solicitation of clients. Nor does the Employment RCA prevent Plaintiff from working for or owning a competing business. A reasonable trier of fact may find that the provision is sufficiently tailored to the purposes of section 16601. Plaintiff contends that the Employment RCA’s definition of “Confidential Information” is so broad that it effectively accomplishes the result prohibited in Fillpoint. (See UF 17 [“The Employment Agreement defines ‘Confidential Information’ to include client identities and the identities of markets or companies from which insurance coverage, commitments, benefits, or services for clients are obtained”].) However, a reasonable trier of fact may conclude otherwise. The definition is not so facially overbroad that the Court can find it illegal as a matter of law.

Moreover, unlike the employer in Fillpoint, Defendants here do not concede that the restrictive covenants in the Employment RCA and Asset Purchase Agreement serve different purposes. To the contrary, Defendants explain how the Employment RCA was intended to preserve the goodwill of the business Plaintiff sold. (See Marathas Decl. ¶¶ 6-7.) Ultimately, the evidence is sufficient to raise a triable issue over the validity of the Employment RCA.

Because triable issues exist regarding multiple contractual provisions challenged in the complaint and motion, summary adjudication is unwarranted.

CONCLUSION

            Plaintiff’s motion for summary adjudication is DENIED. 


Case Number: 25STCV18714    Hearing Date: September 16, 2026    Dept: 406

 

AMERICAN MODERN PROPERTY AND CASUALTY INSURANCE COMPANY,

                       

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

3 LA OZ FUND I, LLC, et al.,

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  25STCV18714

  Hearing Date:  September 16, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

plaintiff’s motion to tax costs

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On June 27, 2025, Plaintiff American Modern Property and Casualty Insurance Company (American) filed this action against Defendants 3 LA Oz Fund I, LLC (3 LA Oz Fund) and Steven Chang, M.D. (Chang). The complaint asserts claims for recission and fraud.

            The dispute stems from an insurance policy that American issued to Chang and 3 LA Oz Fund. Brenda King (King) and First Steps to Success, Inc. (First Steps) sued Chang and 3 La Oz Fund for private nuisance in the case Brenda King, et al. v. Steven Chang, M.D., et al. (24STCV05714). King and First Steps also sued Steven Fleming (Fleming), 3 LA Ventures, LLC (3 LA Ventures), God’s Property Sober Living Foundation (God’s Property), Aldo Rodriguez (Rodriguez), and Winssen Transport, LLC (Winssen). The King complaint alleged that the insured property was being operated as a sober living home or halfway house.

Chang and 3 LA Oz Fund demanded that American defend and indemnify them for the King lawsuit under the policy. However, American alleges that the policy is subject to recission based on the mistaken belief that the property would not be operated as a halfway home or similar occupancy. American further alleges that Chang and 3 LA Oz Fund fraudulently represented that the property would not be used for such purposes.  

            On May 15, 2025, American filed the operative First Amended Complaint, adding King, First Steps, Fleming, 3 LA Ventures, and God’s Property as defendants. These additional defendants allegedly claim an interest in the proceeds of the insurance policy issued to Chang and 3 LA Oz Fund.

            Following mediation, the King action resolved in a stipulated judgment dated May 8, 2026. Judgment was entered: (a) for King against Winssen in the amount of $1.2 million; and (b) for Chang, 3 LA Oz Fund, 3 LA Ventures, Rodriguez, Fleming, and God’s Property against King. American contributed 56% of the settlement on behalf of its insureds, Chang and 3 LA Oz.

            On June 11, 2026, this action was dismissed with prejudice pursuant to American’s request.

            On June 11, 2026, King and First Steps filed a Memorandum of Costs (MOC) seeking $938 in filing and motion fees.

            On June 29, 2026, American filed the instant motion to tax costs. King and First Steps filed an opposition on August 26, 2026. American filed a reply on September 8, 2026.

LEGAL STANDARD

“Except as otherwise expressly provided by statute, a prevailing party is entitled as a matter of right to recover costs in any action or proceeding.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1032(b).) Section 1033.5 sets forth the types of costs that are recoverable to the prevailing party as follows: (1) costs that are expressly allowed; (2) costs that are expressly disallowed; and (3) costs that are allowed in the court’s discretion. (Id., § 1033.5.) Even where costs are deemed allowable, such costs are only recoverable if they are: (1) reasonably necessary to the litigation and not merely convenient or beneficial to its preparation; and (2) reasonable in amount. (Ibid.)

“If the items appearing in a cost bill appear to be proper charges, the burden is on the party seeking to tax costs to show that they were not reasonable or necessary. On the other hand, if the items are properly objected to, they are put in issue and the burden of proof is on the party claiming them as costs.” (Sanford v. Rasnick (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 1121, 1128.) The mere filing of a motion to tax costs can be a proper objection if the challenged costs appear doubtful or improper on their face. (Nelson v. Anderson (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 111, 131.)

DISCUSSION

I. Prevailing Party

            A prevailing party is “the party with a net monetary recovery, a defendant in whose favor a dismissal is entered, a defendant where neither plaintiff nor defendant obtains any relief, and a defendant as against those plaintiffs who do not recover any relief against that defendant.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1032(a)(4).)

            Here, King and First Steps are the prevailing parties because dismissal was entered in their favor, and American recovered nothing against King and First Steps. However, American argues that it was the prevailing party because it “enjoyed net monetary recovery to the extent that it was able to contribute 43% less to settlement of this matter than it would have in the absence of the rescission action.” (Mtn. 3:2-3.)

            American’s position is unsupported by law. American cites no authority suggesting that a party’s prevailing status may be determined by reference to a settlement in a separate action. In this action, King and First Steps obtained a dismissal in their favor. American sought recission of the policy and did not achieve that result. American obtained no monetary recovery from any defendant, including King and First Steps.

            American alternatively argues that the Court should exercise its discretion to find American prevailed by obtaining relief “other than monetary relief.” (See Code Civ. Proc., § 1032(a)(4).) However, American obtained no relief in this action, monetary or otherwise, because it dismissed the action without obtaining the sought-after recission. Nothing in the complaint suggests that American sought to reduce the amount paid towards settlement of the King action. Instead, American sought to void the policy entirely based on fraud or mistake. Because American dismissed its lawsuit without accomplishing that result, it is not the prevailing party.  

            In sum, King and First Steps are the prevailing parties entitled to recover costs.

II. Amount

            The MOC claims $938 for filing and motion fees, consisting of the following filings: answer to complaint ($906); CMC statement ($12); and the MOC ($20).

American challenges the $906 for filing the answer, arguing that it should be reduced to $870. American cites no evidence for this reduction. By contrast, defense counsel presents evidence that the filing fee for the answer was $905.69. (Wilens Decl., Ex. A.) The Court finds that the cost was reasonably incurred and reasonable in amount. 

CONCLUSION

            Plaintiff American Modern Property and Casualty Insurance Company’s motion to tax costs is DENIED.

 


Case Number: 25STCV20825    Hearing Date: September 16, 2026    Dept: 406

 

RENATO ALESIANI,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

PACIFIC6 ENTERPRISES, LLC, et al.,

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  25STCV20825

  Hearing Date:  September 16, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

plaintiff’s motions to compel further responses (CRS# 9117, 4159, 9645)

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On July 15, 2025, Plaintiff Renato Alesiani filed this action against Defendants Pacific6 Enterprises, LLC (P6); 6 Founders, LLC (6F); John Molina (Molina); and Todd Lemmis (Lemmis). Plaintiff filed the operative First Amended Complaint on August 21, 2026, asserting claims for (1) whistleblower retaliation, (2) breach of oral contract, (3) fraudulent misrepresentation, (4) negligent misrepresentation, (5) breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, (6) waiting time penalties, and (7) accounting.

            Plaintiff alleges that he worked for P6 as the Director of Real Estate starting April 2018. Beginning January 2023, Plaintiff allegedly complained that the contractor retained by P6 was not properly licensed and raised related safety concerns. Plaintiff also alleges that his compensation was inconsistent with the agreement reached between the parties. Plaintiff alleges that he was excluded from meetings and responsibilities, and eventually terminated, as retaliation for his whistleblowing. Plaintiff alleges that he was not provided with the requisite severance upon his termination in September 2024. Defendants allegedly claimed that this was because Plaintiff was terminated “for cause.” Plaintiff alleges that P6 and 6F operate as a single enterprise and are alter egos of one another. Molina and Lemmis are allegedly officers, directors, and/or managing agents of P6.

            On August 24, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant three motions to compel P6 and 6F to provide further responses to Requests for Production, Set One and Special Interrogatories, Set One. Defendants have not filed an opposition.

LEGAL STANDARD

Upon receiving responses to its discovery requests, the propounding party may move for an order compelling further responses if the responses are incomplete or evasive, or objections are without merit or too general. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300(a), 2031.310(a), 2033.290(a).)

A party “is entitled to demand answers to its interrogatories, as a matter of right . . . [and] the burden of justifying any objection and failure to respond remains at all times with the party resisting an interrogatory.” (Williams v. Sup. Ct. (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 541.) By contrast, the party seeking production of documents bears the initial burden of showing good cause through a fact-specific showing of relevance. (Kirkland v. Superior Court (2002) 95 Cal.App.4th 92, 98.) Once this showing is made, the burden shifts to the responding party to justify any objections. (Ibid.)

MEET AND CONFER

A motion to compel further must be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration demonstrating an attempt to resolve the matter informally. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300(b)(1), 2031.310(b), 2033.290(b).) The Court finds that Plaintiff has satisfied the meet and confer requirement. (See Holcomb Decl.)

DISCUSSION

            Plaintiff’s motions and separate statements set forth good cause for the requests, as well as the deficiencies in Defendants’ responses. Defendants have not filed an opposition to justify their failure to provide proper responses. Thus, an order compelling further responses is warranted.

            Sanctions are also warranted as Defendants present no substantial justification. Plaintiff requests $4,735 for each motion, reflecting $835 per hour for 5.5 hours, plus a $60 filing fee. This is excessive given the simplicity of the motions and lack of opposition. The Court awards a total of $3,780, reflecting $450 per hour for 8 hours, plus $180 in filing fees.

CONCLUSION

            Plaintiff’s motions to compel further responses are GRANTED. Defendants Pacific6 Enterprises, LLC and 6 Founders, LLC shall provide further responses to the subject discovery within 20 days of today. The Court sanctions Defendants, jointly and severally, in the total amount of $3,780, to be paid within 20 days of today.


Case Number: 26STCV10860    Hearing Date: September 16, 2026    Dept: 406

 

MIKAH LOPEZ,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

PRISCILLA VOTION, et al.,

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  26STCV10860

  Hearing Date:  September 16, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

defendants’ demurrer to complaint

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On April 2, 2026, Plaintiff Mikah Lopez filed this action against Defendants Priscilla Votion and Yvette Aragon Herrera for libel and false light. The complaint alleges that Defendants posted false statements online accusing Plaintiff of: (1) being involved in an incident causing injury to a journalist; (2) abusing Defendant Votion by choking her; and (3) being a molester.

            On August 17, 2026, Defendants filed the instant demurrer to the complaint. Plaintiff filed an opposition on August 28, 2026.

LEGAL STANDARD

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (Taylor v. City of Los Angeles Dept. of Water and Power (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 1216, 1228.) In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or by proper judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.30(a).) A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. (SKF Farms v. Superior Court (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 902, 905.) “California law emphasizes ultimate fact pleading (with some exceptions, notably for fraud and related torts) ‘in ordinary and concise language,’ and the test for adequacy is not absolute but ‘whether the pleading as a whole apprises the adversary of the factual basis of the claim.’” (Lim v. The.TV Corp. Internat. (2002) 99 Cal.App.4th 684, 690.)

MEET AND CONFER

Before filing a demurrer or a motion to strike, the demurring or moving party is required to meet and confer with the party who filed the pleading demurred to or the pleading that is subject to the motion to strike for the purposes of determining whether an agreement can be reached through a filing of an amended pleading that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 430.41, 435.5.) The Court finds that Defendants have satisfied the meet and confer requirement. (See Reeves Decl.)

DISCUSSION

The elements of a defamation claim are: (1) publication that is (2) false, (3) defamatory, (4) unprivileged, and (5) has a natural tendency to injure or cause special damage. (Wong v. Jing (2010) 189 Cal.App.4th 1354, 1369.) Libel is defamation in written form. (Edward v. Ellis (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 780, 790.) A complaint alleging defamation “must set forth ‘either the specific words or the substance of’ the allegedly defamatory statements.” (Barker v. Fox & Associates (2015) 240 Cal.App.4th 333, 343, quoting Lipman v. Brisbane Elementary Sch. Dist. (1961) 55 Cal.2d 224, 235.)

Here, the complaint makes the following allegation:

 

Defendant Priscilla Votion falsely posted a statement on the Internet that Lopez was involved in an incident causing injury to journalist Julianna LaCoste. Additionally, Votion falsely posted that Lopez abused her by choking her for a minute. Defendant Aragon Herrera falsely posted that Lopez is a molester.

(Compl. ¶ 2.)

This sufficiently sets forth the substance of the alleged defamatory statements as required by the law. The demurrer acknowledges this legal standard but then demands additional detail not required by the law: the exact words of the publications; the dates of the publications; the websites or other locations where the publications appeared; the audience to whom the publications were directed; whether the statements were in the form of posts, comments, messages, etc.; whether the statements were part of the same or separate publications; and the circumstances surrounding the publications. (Dem. 5:1-9.) These are evidentiary facts not required for pleading. The existing allegations adequately “give[] notice of the issues sufficient to enable preparation of a defense.” (See Okun v. Superior Court (1981) 29 Cal.3d 442, 458.)

The complaint also alleges the remaining elements of defamation. The statements are allegedly false. (Compl. ¶¶ 2-3, 13, 19.) The defamatory effect of the statements is self-evident, as they charge Plaintiff with committing abuse, assault, and molestation. (See Burrill v. Nair (2013) 217 Cal.App.4th 357, 383 [“false statements charging the commission of crime or tending directly to injure a plaintiff in respect to his or her profession by imputing dishonesty or questionable professional conduct are defamatory per se”].) The complaint alleges that Plaintiff was damaged in the form of “loss of business opportunities, loss of reputation, shame, mortification and hurt feelings.” (Compl. ¶ 14.) These are ultimate facts which must be assumed true on a demurrer.

In sum, the complaint sufficiently alleges a defamation claim. “When a false light claim is coupled with a defamation claim, the false light claim is essentially superfluous, and stands or falls on whether it meets the same requirements as the defamation cause of action.” (Jackson v. Mayweather (2017) 10 Cal.App.5th 1240, 1264.) Because the defamation claim survives, so does the derivative false light claim.

 

CONCLUSION

            Defendants’ demurrer is OVERRULED.
