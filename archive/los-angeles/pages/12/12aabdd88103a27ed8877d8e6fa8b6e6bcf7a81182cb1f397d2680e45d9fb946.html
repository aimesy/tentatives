DEPARTMENT 514 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 26STCV07950    Hearing Date: September 11, 2026    Dept: 514

Lopez v. FCA US, LLC
26STCV07950
Defendants’ Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings

Tentative Ruling

The memorandum of points and authorities exceeds the permissible page length.  The Court disregards the arguments made on or after page 16 of the memorandum.

The motion is denied.

Background

This case arises out of the acquisition of a Certified Pre-Owned 2018 Jeep Grand Cherokee (the “Vehicle”) by Plaintiff Cecilia Lopez (“Plaintiff”) in November 2021.

On February 6, 2025, Plaintiff filed the complaint in this action in San Diego Superior Court.  Plaintiff named as defendants FCA US, LLC (“FCA”) and Autonation Chrysler Dodge Jeep Ram Valencia (“Autonation Valencia”) and asserted causes of action for: (1) violation of subdivision (d) of Civil Code section 1793.2, (2) violation of subdivision (b) of Civil Code section 1793.2, (3) violation of subdivision (a)(3) of Civil Code section 1793.2, (4) breach of implied warranty of merchantability (Civil Code sections 1791.1, 1794, 1795.5), (5) negligent repair, and (6) fraudulent concealment.

On April 25, 2025, FCA filed an answer.

On May 2, 2025, Autonation Valencia filed an answer.

In January 2026, the case was transferred to Los Angeles County Superior Court.

On May 21, 2026, FCA and Autonation Valencia (collectively, “Defendants”) filed this motion for judgment on the pleadings.  Plaintiff filed an opposition on July 16, and Defendants filed a reply on July 30.

On August 11, 2026, the Court continued the hearing to September 11 and ordered counsel to meet and confer.

On August 20, 2026, Defendants filed a declaration of counsel regarding the meet and confer.

Trial is set for June 5, 2028.

Legal Standard

California practice recognizes two types of motions for judgment on the pleadings.  

First, there is a statutory motion.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 438.)  A defendant may bring a statutory motion for judgment on the pleadings when “the complaint does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action against that defendant.”  (Id., subd. (c)(1)(B)(ii).)  A statutory motion for judgment on the pleadings must be filed within certain time limits, and it is subject to a statutory meet-and-confer requirement.  (Id., subd. (e); Code Civ. Proc., § 439.)  The grounds for a statutory motion for judgment on the pleadings must “appear on the face of the challenged pleading or from any matter of which the court is required to take judicial notice.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 438, subd. (d).)

Second, there is a non-statutory motion for judgment on the pleadings, which “may be made at any time either prior to the trial or at the trial itself.”  (Ion Equipment Corp. v. Nelson (1980) 110 Cal.App.3d 868, 877; see also Smiley v. Citibank (South Dakota) (1995) 11 Cal.4th 138, 145 fn. 2; Tarin v. Lind (2020) 47 Cal.App.5th 395, 402; Stoops v. Abbassi (2002) 100 Cal.App.4th 644, 650.)    

A motion for judgment on the pleadings is the functional equivalent of a demurrer.  (People ex rel. Harris v. Pac Anchor Transp. (2014) 59 Cal.4th 772, 77; City of Rancho Palos Verdes v. State (2025) 114 Cal.App.5th 13, 23.)  “The standard for granting a motion for judgment on the pleadings is essentially the same as that applicable to a general demurrer, that is, under the state of the pleadings, together with matters that may be judicially noticed, it appears that a party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.”  (Bezirdjian v. O'Reilly (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 316, 321-322; accord Los Angeles Unified School Dist. v. Torres Construction Corp. (2020) 57 Cal.App.5th 480, 494; Templo v. State (2018) 24 Cal.App.5th 730, 735; Southern California Edison Co. v. City of Victorville (2013) 217 Cal.App.4th 218, 227; see also 1 Weil & Brown, California Practice Guide: Civil Procedure Before Trial [2026] ¶ 7:275.)  

Discussion

The Court notes, at the outset, that Defendants’ memorandum in support of their motion exceeds the permissible page length.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1113.)  Defendants have not sought or obtained a court order permitting them to file an overly long brief. 

The Court exercises its discretion to consider only pages 1 through 15 of the memorandum of points and authorities and to disregard the arguments made by Defendants in the remainder of the memorandum, commencing at the top of page 16. 

Defendants move for judgment on the pleadings on all six causes of action in the complaint.  Defendants also seek to strike the punitive damages allegations in the complaint.  The Court considers each of Defendants’ arguments in turn.

First and Second Causes of Action (Civil Code Sections 1793.2, Subdivisions (d) & (b))

Defendants’ first argument is that the First and Second Causes of Action in the complaint, brought under subdivisions (d) and (b) of Civil Code section 1793.2, are time-barred.  (Mem., at pp. 4-5.)

Because a general demurrer lies where the complaint, or facts judicially noticeable, are insufficient to state a cause of action, a general demurrer may lie where the dates alleged show the cause of action is barred by the statute of limitations. (See Saliter v. Pierce Bros. Mortuaries (1978) 81 Cal.App.3d 292, 300; Iverson, Yoakum, Papiano & Hatch v. Berwald (1999) 76 Cal.App.4th 990, 995; Vaca v. Wachovia Mortg. Corp. (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 737, 746.)  “A demurrer based on a statute of limitations will not lie where the action may be, but is not necessarily, barred.  In order for the bar of the statute of limitations to be raised by demurrer, the defect must clearly and affirmatively appear on the face of the complaint; it is not enough that the complaint shows that the action may be barred.” (Geneva Towers Ltd. Partnership v. City of San Francisco (2003) 29 Cal.4th 769, 781 [citations omitted]; see also Committee for Green Foothills v. Santa Clara County Bd. of Supervisors (2010) 48 Cal.App.4th 32, 42; Stueve Bros. Farms, LLC v. Berger Kahn (2013) 222 Cal.App.4th 303, 321.)

Defendants argue that under AB 1755, signed into law on September 29, 2024, and effective as of January 1, 2025, actions brought under Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivisions (b) and (d), must be filed no later than six years after the date of original delivery of the motor vehicle.  (Civ. Code, § 871.21, subd. (b).)

Defendants contend that because the case involves a 2018 model year vehicle, the original delivery date of the vehicle must have been in 2018 – and that therefore any lawsuit under subdivisions (b) and (d) of section 1793.2 must have been filed before the end of 2024 (six years later) to be timely.  (Mem., at 5:12-18.)

Nowhere in the complaint, however, does the Plaintiff allege the date of the original delivery of the vehicle.  The statute of limitations, moreover, is a defensive matter.  The Court may sustain a demurrer (or, here, grant a motion for judgment on the pleadings) on this basis only if it appears from the face of the pleadings that the action is time-barred.  A plaintiff is under no obligation to anticipate potential affirmative defenses and plead facts sufficient to defeat them.

On this record, the Court cannot determine whether the First and Second Causes of Action are time-barred as a matter of law. 

The Court need not reach, and does not reach, Plaintiff’s other arguments on this issue.

The motion for judgment on the pleadings is denied as to the First and Second Causes of Action.

Third Cause of Action (Civil Code Section 1793.2, Subdivision (a)(3))

Under Civil Code section 1792.3, subdivision (a)(3), FCA is required to “[m]ake available to authorized service and repair facilities sufficient service literature and replacement parts to effect repairs during the express warranty period.”

Defendants argue that Plaintiff has not alleged sufficient facts to support this cause of action.  (Mem., at pp. 5-6.)  But Plaintiff alleges a violation of this statutory provision.  (Compl., ¶¶ 52-53.)  And she alleges that an authorized dealer was unable to complete repairs on the Vehicle.  (Compl., ¶¶ 60-62.)  Viewing the complaint in the light most favorable to the Plaintiff, and drawing all reasonable inferences in her favor, this is sufficient at the pleading stage.

The motion for judgment on the pleadings is denied as to the Third Cause of Action.

Fourth Cause of Action (Breach of Implied Warranty of Merchantability)

Defendants argue that the Fourth Cause of Action, for breach of the implied warranty of merchantability is barred by a four-year statute of limitations.  (Mem., at pp. 6-7.)  As Plaintiff alleges that she acquired the vehicle (along with the implied warranty of merchantability) in November 2021 and filed suit less than four years later, it is unclear how this claim would be time-barred.  In any event, where, as here, a warranty “extends to future performance of the goods,” the cause of action for breach of the implied warranty of merchantability “accrues when the breach is or should have been discovered.”  (Comm. Code, § 2725, subd. (2); see also, e.g., Mexia v. Rinker Boat Co. (2009) 174 Cal.App.4th 1297, 1306; Krieger v. Nick Alexander Imports, Inc. (1991) 234 Cal.App.3d 205, 213-214.)  In the complaint, Plaintiff alleges that FCA’s authorized repair facilities continued to attempt to repair the vehicle after she purchased it in November 2021.

The motion for judgment on the pleadings is denied as to the Fourth Cause of Action.

Fifth Cause of Action (Negligent Repair)

Defendants contend that Plaintiff fails to allege facts sufficient to support a cause of action for negligent repair.  (Mem., at p. 11.)  But Plaintiff alleges that she delivered the Vehicle to Defendant Autonation Valencia for repair on at least one occasion, and that Autonation breached the duty of ordinary care in failing to repair the Subject Vehicle. (Compl., ¶¶ 60-62.)  Viewing the complaint in the light most favorable to the Plaintiff, and drawing all reasonable inferences in her favor, this is sufficient at the pleading stage.

The motion for judgment on the pleadings is denied as to the Fifth Cause of Action.

Sixth Cause of Action (Fraudulent Inducement/Concealment)

In the Sixth Cause of Action, Plaintiff asserts a cause of action for fraudulent concealment.  Defendants argue that this cause of action is time-barred (Mem., at pp. 10-11, 12-15) and that Plaintiff does not allege facts sufficient to state this cause of action (Mem., at pp. 7-10).

Beginning with the statute of limitations, an action for fraud must be brought within three years.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 338, subd. (d).)  The statute expressly provides, however, that the “cause of action … is not deemed to have accrued until the discovery, by the aggrieved party, of the facts constituting the fraud ….” (Ibid.)

In the complaint, Plaintiff alleges that she acquired the Vehicle in November 2021 and that at some time thereafter brought the Vehicle to an authorized dealer for repairs.  The complaint does not clearly and affirmatively establish that Plaintiff discovered the facts constituting the fraud on or before February 6, 2022, the date that is three years before the complaint was filed.

The motion for judgment on the pleadings as to the Sixth Cause of Action on this basis is denied.

Next, Defendants argue that Plaintiff does not sufficiently allege the elements of her cause of action for fraudulent concealment.

“One who willfully deceives another with intent to induce him to alter his position to his injury or risk, is liable for any damage which he thereby suffers.” (Civ. Code, § 1709.)

The Civil Code recognizes four types of fraud: (1) an intentional misrepresentation; (2) a negligent misrepresentation; (3) concealment; and (4) promissory fraud. (Civ. Code, § 1710.) “California case law … has viewed fraud by concealment on equal footing with fraud by affirmative misrepresentation.” (Rattagan v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1, 39.)

The elements of a cause of action for fraudulent concealment are:

“(1) concealment or suppression of a material fact; (2) by a defendant with a duty to disclose the fact; (3) the defendant intended to defraud the plaintiff by intentionally concealing or suppressing the fact; (4) the plaintiff was unaware of the fact and would have acted differently if the concealed or suppressed fact was known; and (5) plaintiff sustained damage as a result of the concealment or suppression of the material fact.”

(Rattagan, supra, 17 Cal.5th at 1, 40; see also Boschma v. Home Loan Center (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 230, 248; Blickman Turkus, LP v. MF Downtown Sunnyvale, LLC (2008) 162 Cal.App.4th 858, 868.)

Fraudulent concealment is actionable only when there is a duty to disclose.

“A duty to disclose a material fact can arise if (1) it is imposed by statute; (2) the defendant is acting as plaintiff's fiduciary or is in some other confidential relationship with plaintiff that imposes a disclosure duty under the circumstances; (3) the material facts are known or accessible only to defendant, and defendantknows those facts are not known or reasonably discoverable by plaintiff (i.e., exclusive knowledge); (4) the defendant makes representations but fails to disclose other facts that materially qualify the facts disclosed or render the disclosure misleading (i.e., partial concealment); or (5) defendant actively conceals discovery of material fact from plaintiff (i.e., active concealment). Circumstances (3), (4), and (5) presuppose a preexisting relationship between the parties, such as between seller and buyer, employer and prospective employee, doctor and patient, or parties entering into any kind of contractual agreement. All of these relationships are created by transactions between parties from which a duty to disclose facts material to the transaction arises under certain circumstances. Such a transaction must necessarily arise from direct dealings between the plaintiff and the defendant; it cannot arise between the defendant and the public at large.”

(Rattagan, supra, 17 Cal.5th at pp. 40–41 [citations omitted]; see also, e.g., Bigler-Engler v. Breg, Inc. (2017) 7 Cal.App.5th 276, 312; SCC Acquisitions v. Central Pacific Bank (2012) 207 Cal.App.4th 859, 860; LiMandri v. Judkins (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 326, 336–37.)

A plaintiff must plead fraud with particularity. (Robinson Helicopter Co. v. Dana Corp. (2004) 34 Cal.4th 979, 993; Lazar v. Super Ct. (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 645; Glaski v. Bank of America (2013) 218 Cal.App.4th 1079, 1090-1092.) This rule applies to causes of action for fraudulent concealment. As our Supreme Court has explained:

“If the duty [to disclose] allegedly arose by virtue of the parties' relationship and defendant's exclusive knowledge or access to certain facts …, the complaint must also include specific allegations establishing all the required elements, including (1) the content of the omitted facts, (2) defendant's awareness of the materiality of those facts, (3) the inaccessibility of the facts to plaintiff, (4) the general point at which the omitted facts should or could have been revealed, and (5) justifiable and actual reliance, either through action or forbearance, based on the defendant's omission.”

(Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 347.) “[M]ere conclusionary allegations that the omissions were intentional and for the purpose of defrauding and deceiving plaintiff[ ] ... are insufficient.” (Ibid; see also Rattagan, supra, 17 Cal.5th at pp. 43–44.)

Reviewing the Complaint, Plaintiff alleges that FCA acquired knowledge of the Engine Defect through “pre-production and postproduction testing data; early consumer complaints about the Engine Defect made directly to FCA and its network of dealers; aggregate warranty data compiled from FCA's network of dealers; testing conducted by FCA in response to these complaints; as well as warranty repair and part replacements data received by FCA from FCA's network of dealers, amongst other sources of internal information.” (Compl., ¶ 19; see also id., ¶¶ 16, 22-26, 67.) Even with this knowledge, FCA failed to disclose this information to its sales representatives and Plaintiff at the time of sale and thereafter. (Compl., ¶¶ 20, 25, 48.)  Plaintiff alleges that if she had known of the defect, she would not have purchased the Vehicle.  (Compl., ¶¶ 21, 24, 69-71.)

Based on these allegations in the complaint, and others, the Court determines that Plaintiff has alleged sufficient facts to state a cause of action for fraudulent concealment.

The motion for judgment on the pleadings is denied as to the Sixth Cause of Action.

Punitive Damages

Defendants also seek a motion for judgment on the pleadings as to the punitive damages allegations.  This request is denied for three independent reasons.  First, the argument appears almost entirely on pages 16 through 18 of the memorandum, which the Court is disregarding.  Second, a motion for judgment on the pleadings functions like a demurrer and must be directed to a cause of action; a demurrer (or motion for judgment on the pleadings) is not properly directed to a portion of a cause of action or a claim for relief.  Third, Plaintiff has, as set forth below, sufficiently alleged a cause of action for fraud.

The motion for judgment on the pleadings as to the punitive damages allegations in the complaint is denied.

Conclusion

The Court DENIES the motion of Defendants FCA US, LLC and Autonation Chrysler Dodge Jeep Ram Valencia for judgment on the pleadings. 

Moving Party is ORDERED to give notice.


Case Number: 26STCV14059    Hearing Date: September 11, 2026    Dept: 514

Baxter v. Sony Pictures Television, Inc.
26STCV14059
Defendant’s Motion to Compel Litigation

Tentative Ruling

The motion is granted.

Background

This case arises out of an employment relationship.

On May 1, 2026, Wendy Baxter (“Plaintiff”) filed the complaint in this action against Sony Pictures Television, Inc. (“Defendant”) and Does 1 through 10 for (1) age discrimination, (2) sex/gender discrimination, (3) retaliation, (4) failure to prevent discrimination and retaliation, (5) retaliation in violation of Labor Code section 1102.5, (6) wrongful termination in violation of public policy, and (7) violation of the California Equal Pay Act.

On July 23, 2026, Defendant filed this motion to compel arbitration.  Plaintiff filed an opposition on August 13, and Defendant filed a reply on August 19.

The hearing was initially set for August 26 and was continued to September 11.

Legal Standard

California law and the Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”) share many of the same policy objectives with regard to arbitration, including a presumption in favor of arbitrability. (See Engalla v. Permanente Medical Group, Inc. (1997) 15 Cal.4th 951, 971-72.) Under the FAA:

“A written provision in … a contract evidencing a transaction involving commerce to settle by arbitration a controversy thereafter arising out of such contract or transaction, or the refusal to perform the whole or any part thereof, or an agreement in writing to submit to arbitration an existing controversy arising out of such a contract, transaction, or refusal, shall be valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, save upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract ….” 

(9 U.S.C., § 2.)  Under Code of Civil Procedure section 1281, a “written agreement to submit to arbitration an existing controversy or a controversy thereafter arising is valid, enforceable and irrevocable, save upon such grounds as exist for the revocation of any contract.”

Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.2 provides, in pertinent part, the following:

“On petition of a party to an arbitration agreement alleging the existence of a written agreement to arbitrate a controversy and that a party thereto refuses to arbitrate such controversy, the court shall order the petitioner and the respondent to arbitrate the controversy if it determines that an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists, unless it determines that: 

(a) The right to compel arbitration has been waived by the petitioner; or 

(b) Grounds exist for the revocation of the agreement. 

(c) A party to the arbitration agreement is also a party to a pending court action or special proceeding with a third party, arising out of the same transaction or series of related transactions and there is a possibility of conflicting rulings on a common issue of law or fact ....

(d) … If the court determines that a party to the arbitration is also a party to litigation in a pending court action or special proceeding with a third party as set forth under subdivision (c), the court (1) may refuse to enforce the arbitration agreement and may order intervention or joinder of all parties in a single action or special proceeding; (2) may order intervention or joinder as to all or only certain issues; (3) may order arbitration among the parties who have agreed to arbitration and stay the pending court action or special proceeding pending the outcome of the arbitration proceeding; or (4) may stay arbitration pending the outcome of the court action or special proceeding.”

(Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2.) 

“[A]rbitration agreements are creatures of contract.”  (Ford Motor Warranty Cases (2025) 17 Cal.5th 1122, 1125.)  Arbitration is “a matter of consent, not coercion,” and the Federal Arbitration Act “does not require parties to arbitrate when they have not agreed to do so.  (Volt Information Sciences v. Board of Trustees of Stanford University (1989) 489 U.S. 468, 478-479.)

“In ruling on a motion to compel arbitration, the court must first determine whether the parties actually agreed to arbitrate the dispute.  General principles of contract law guide the court in making this determination.”  (Ford Motor Warranty Cases, supra, 17 Cal.5th at p. 1128.) 

The party seeking to compel arbitration bears the burden of proving the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by the preponderance of the evidence. (Engalla, supra, 15 Cal.4th at p. 972; Giuliano v. Inland Empire Personnel, Inc.¿(2007) 149 Cal.App.4th 1276, 1284.)  This is usually done by presenting a copy of the signed, written agreement to the court. (See Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1330 [“A petition to compel arbitration or to stay proceedings pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 1281.2 and 1281.4 must state, in addition to other required allegations, the provisions of the written agreement and the paragraph that provides for arbitration. The provisions must be stated verbatim or a copy must be physically or electronically attached to the petition and incorporated by reference.”].) ¿

As our Supreme Court has explained:

“[W]hen a petition to compel arbitration is filed and accompanied by prima facie evidence of a written agreement to arbitrate the controversy, the court itself must determine whether the agreement exists and, if any defense to its enforcement is raised, whether it is enforceable. Because the existence of the agreement is a statutory prerequisite to granting the petition, the petitioner bears the burden of proving its existence by a preponderance of the evidence. If the party opposing the petition raises a defense to enforcement—either fraud in the execution voiding the agreement, or a statutory defense of waiver or revocation (see [Code Civ. Proc.] § 1281.2(a), (b))—that party bears the burden of producing evidence of, and proving by a preponderance of the evidence, any fact necessary to the defense.”

(Rosenthal v. Great Western Fin. Securities Corp. (1996) 14 Cal.4th 394, 413.)

The party seeking to compel arbitration “bears the burden of proving the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by the preponderance of the evidence.” “In determining whether an arbitration agreement applies to a specific dispute, the court … should attempt to give effect to the parties' intentions, in light of the usual and ordinary meaning of the contractual language and the circumstances under which the agreement was made.”¿¿(Weeks v. Crow¿(1980) 113 Cal.App.3d 350, 353 [citations omitted].) “To determine whether a contractual arbitration clause requires arbitration of a particular controversy, the controversy is first identified and the issue is whether that controversy is within the scope of the contractual arbitration clause.”¿(Titolo¿v. Cano¿(2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 310, 316.)

In ruling on a petition or motion to compel arbitration, “the trial court sits as a trier of fact, weighing all the affidavits, declarations, and other documentary evidence, as well as oral testimony received at the court's discretion, to reach a final determination.” (Giuliano, supra, 149 Cal.App.4th at p. 1284.)

Discussion

Defendant moves to compel arbitration and to stay the action.

In support of its motion, Defendant provides the declaration of Jacqui Ngo, Director, Operations in People & Organization for Sony Pictures Entertainment, Inc., which provides for Defendant. (Ngo Decl., ¶ 1.) Ms. Ngo states that she has access to Defendant’s personnel files, records, policies, electronic signature programs, and associated records. (Ngo Decl., ¶ 3.) To access work emails, employees are required to enter a login credential, including a password that is created by each employee. (Ngo Decl., ¶¶ 4-5.) Defendant uses Adobe Sign to obtain electronic signatures on contracts, including employment agreements. (Ngo Decl., ¶ 6.)  Ms. Ngo states that, according to the records she reviewed, Plaintiff was sent the Employment Agreement (Ngo Decl., Exhibit B) on April 1, 2022, and signed it that same day.  (Ngo Decl., ¶ 8.)  Exhibit A to the Employment Agreement is a printed form entitled, “Standard Terms and Conditions of Employment,” and paragraph 12 of the Standard Terms contains an arbitration provision.  Specifically, paragraph 12 provides:

“(a) Except as provided below, the substantive laws (as distinguished from the choice of law rules) of the State of California will govern (i) the validity and interpretation of this Agreement, (ii) the performance by the parties of their respective duties and obligations under this Agreement and (iii) all other causes of action (whether sounding in contract or in tort) arising out of or relating in any fashion to Employee’s employment by the Company or the termination of such employment. Notwithstanding the foregoing, the enforcement, interpretation and application of the agreement to arbitrate (set forth in subparagraphs (b) and (c), immediately below) shall be governed by the Federal Arbitration Act, 9 U.S.C. Section 1, et seq.

(b) Any controversy or claim arising out of or relating to this Agreement, its enforcement, arbitrability or interpretation, or because of an alleged breach, default or misrepresentation in connection with any of its provisions, or arising out of or relating in any way to Employee’s employment or termination of employment, including, for example, any alleged violation of statute, common law or public policy, will be submitted to final and binding arbitration, to be held in Los Angeles County, California, before a single arbitrator, in accordance with the then-current JAMS Arbitration Rules and Procedures for Employment Disputes, as modified by the terms and conditions contained in this Paragraph. The arbitrator will be selected by mutual agreement of the parties or, if the parties cannot agree, then by striking from a list of arbitrators supplied by JAMS. The arbitrator will issue a written opinion stating the essential findings and conclusions upon which the arbitrator’s award is based. The Company will pay the arbitrator’s fees and arbitration expenses and any other costs unique to the arbitration hearing (recognizing that each side bears its own deposition, witness, expert and attorneys’ fees and other expenses to the same extent as if the matter were being heard in court). If, however, any party prevails on a statutory claim, which affords the prevailing party attorneys’ fees and costs, then the arbitrator may award reasonable fees and costs to the prevailing party. Any dispute as to who is the prevailing party and/or the reasonableness of any fee or cost will be resolved by the arbitrator.

(c) All claims, defenses and proceedings (including, without limiting the generality of the foregoing, the existence of a controversy and the fact that there is an arbitration proceeding) will be treated in a confidential manner by the arbitrator and all those involved in the proceeding. Any controversy relating to the arbitration presented to a court will be filed under seal to the extent permitted by law.”

(Ngo Decl., Exh. B.)

With this evidence, Defendant has met its initial burden of presenting evidence of an arbitration agreement between the parties that covers the claims in the complaint.  This shifts the burden to Plaintiff to identify a dispute regarding the existence or scope of the agreement or some other defense to enforcement.  (See Garcia v. Stoneledge Furniture LLC (2024) 102 Cal.App.5th 41, 51-52; Espejo v. Southern California Permanente Medical Group (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 1047.)

Plaintiff opposes this motion, arguing that the arbitration provision in the Employment Agreement is unconscionable. 

As noted above, an agreement in writing to arbitrate is valid and enforceable “save upon such grounds as exist in law or in equity for the revocation of any contract.”  (9 U.S.C., § 2; see also Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.)  One such ground is unconscionability.  (E.g., Ramirez v. Charter Communications (2024) 16 Cal.5th 478, 492; Armendariz v. Foundation Health Psychcare Services (2000) 24 Cal.4th 83, 113-114.)

“The general principles of unconscionability are well established. A contract is unconscionable if one of the parties lacked a meaningful choice in deciding whether to agree and the contract contains terms that are unreasonably favorable to the other party.  Unconscionability has both a procedural and a substantive element. …

Procedural unconscionability addresses the circumstances of contract negotiation and formation, focusing on oppression or surprise due to unequal bargaining power.  This element is generally established by showing the agreement is a contract of adhesion, i.e., a standardized contract which, imposed and drafted by the party of superior bargaining strength, relegates to the subscribing party only the opportunity to adhere to the contract or reject it. …

Substantive unconscionability looks beyond the circumstances of contract formation and considers the fairness of an agreement’s actual terms,” focusing on whether the contract will create unfair or one-sided results. Substantively unconscionable contractual clauses reallocate risks in an objectively unreasonable or unexpected manner.

Both procedural and substantive elements must be present to conclude a term is unconscionable, but these required elements need not be present to the same degree.  Courts apply a sliding scale analysis under which the more substantively oppressive a term, the less evidence of procedural unconscionability is required to come to the conclusion that the term is unenforceable, and vice versa.”

 

(Ramirez, supra, 16 Cal.5th at pp.492-493.)

The unconscionability standard is, and must be, “the same for arbitration and nonarbitration agreements.”  (Sanchez v. Valencia Holding Co. (2015) 61 Cal.4th 899, 912.)

Beginning with procedural unconscionability, the Court finds that there is some degree of procedural unconscionability here.  Although Plaintiff does not present any evidence regarding the circumstances under which the Employment Agreement was signed, the arbitration provision is contained in a printed form attached to the agreement itself; the arbitration provision is buried on page 3 and 4 of the “Standard Terms and Conditions of Employment,” without any bold type, highlighting, or other emphasis to draw the reader’s attention to such a significant term.  Moreover, there is no indication or suggestion (for example) that Defendant specifically brought the arbitration provision to Plaintiff’s attention; that Plaintiff could have engaged in negotiation regarding the “Standard Terms and Conditions of Employment” of her Employment Agreement; or that Plaintiff could have opted out of the arbitration provision (and was so advised).

Turning to substantive unconscionability, Plaintiff argues that the following terms in the Employment Agreement are unconscionable: (1) a delegation clause (Opp. at pp. 3-4); (2) a one-sided carve-out for injunctive relief (Opp. at pp. 5-7); (3) a limitation on the employee’s right to serve as an expert witness (Opp. at p. 7); and (4) a non-solicitation provision (Opp. at p. 8.)  The Court considers each in turn.

First, Plaintiff argues that JAMS Rule 11 (which would apply to an arbitration under the Employment Agreement) is “independently unconscionable” because it requires an arbitrator, and not a court, to rule on arbitrability.  Plaintiff does not explain why that is so, and it is not immediately clear to the Court.  The provisions of Rule 11(b) may well be unenforceable, but that’s a separate issue.  Substantive unconscionability examines whether the terms are (for example) one-sided or unfair, and there does not appear to be anything one-sided or unfair in having a neutral arbitrator, as opposed to a neutral judicial officer, make a decision regarding arbitrability.

Second, Plaintiff argues that paragraph 11 of the Standard Terms and Conditions creates a one-way exception to arbitration for Defendant.  It does not.  Paragraph 11(b) provides:

“Employee acknowledges, and stipulates to a legal finding, that the services to be rendered by Employee under this Agreement, and the rights and privileges granted by Employee to the Company under this Agreement, are of a special, unique, extraordinary and intellectual character which gives them a peculiar and special value, the loss of which cannot be reasonably or adequately compensated in damages in an action at law, and a breach by Employee will cause the Company great and irreparable injury. Employee acknowledges that the Company will, therefore, be entitled, in addition to any other remedies which it may have under this Agreement or at law, to seek injunctive and other equitable relief (including without limitation specific performance) to enforce any of the rights and privileges of the Company or any of the covenants or obligations of Employee under this Agreement.”

Although this provision benefits Defendant by making it potentially less burdensome for Defendant to obtain specific performance or other equitable relief in the event of a breach of contract, this provision does not exempt Defendant from arbitration or prohibit Plaintiff from seeking equitable relief (whether from a judge or an arbitrator).  This provision is not, on this record, substantively unconscionable.

Third, paragraph 13(a) of the Standard Terms and Conditions provides that an employee may not serve as an expert witness in a proceeding against Defendant without Defendant’s consent.  This, of course, has nothing to do with arbitration.  Nor has Plaintfif shown that it is the type of unfair or burdensome provision that shocks the conscience or is otherwise substantively unconscionable.

Fourth, and finally, paragraph 8(i) of the Standard Terms and Conditions bars Plaintiff from soliciting any other person to terminate their employment with Defendant.  Plaintiff recognizes that “this provision is unlikely to be dispositive,” and the Court agrees.  Whether standing alone or in conjunction with the other provisions identified by Plaintiff, this term does not establish that the Employment Agreement is substantively unconscionable.

In sum, some degree of procedural unconscionability appears in the record, but Plaintiff has not shown substantive unconscionability.  The Court concludes that the Employment Agreement is not unconscionable.

Accordingly, the motion to compel arbitration is granted.

Conclusion

The Court GRANTS motion of Defendant Sony Pictures Television, Inc. to compel arbitration.

The Court ORDERS the parties to arbitrate their dispute in accordance with the terms of their contract.

The Court STAYS this matter in its entirety pending completion of the arbitration (or further order of the Court).

The Court SETS a post-arbitration status conference for July 14, 2027 at 8:30 am in Department 514.

The Court ORDERS the parties to file a written report regarding the status of the arbitration by no later than June 30, 2027.

Moving Party is ORDERED to give notice.
