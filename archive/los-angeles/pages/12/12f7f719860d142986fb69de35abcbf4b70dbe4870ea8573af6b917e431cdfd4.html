            [TENTATIVE] ORDER: The Court DENIES Moving Defendant’s Motion for Summary Judgment.

Background

This is a medical malpractice case. On October 12, 2022, Plaintiff Anthony Sanchez was severely injured when his girlfriend drove her car into him, pinning his legs against a second, stationary vehicle. Plaintiff was admitted to Moving Defendant Providence Holy Cross Medical Center for treatment for his injuries. Plaintiff was treated by multiple doctors, including Non-Moving Defendant Ramin Ganjianpour.

Plaintiff underwent multiple separate surgeries during his admission to Providence Holy Cross. First, on October 13, 2022, doctors performed a femoral bypass graft on Plaintiff’s left knee in an attempt to restore blood flow to Plaintiff’s foot. On October 18, 2022, doctors once again operated on Plaintiff’s left leg. Then, on October 22, 2022, doctors “revascularized” Plaintiff’s left leg, as it was still not receiving enough blood flow. Finally, on October 23, 2022, doctors performed a vascular bypass surgery on Plaintiff’s left leg. Unfortunately, the blood flow to Plaintiff’s leg was not restored. As a result, it had to be amputated. Moving Defendant Valle was present for multiple operations on Plaintiff, including his initial admission to the emergency room.

On January 2, 2024, Plaintiff brought suit against Moving Defendant Providence Holy Cross Medical Center, and six other doctors who treated him. Plaintiff alleges a single cause of action for professional negligence.

Now, Moving Defendant Providence Holy Cross Medical Center moves for summary judgment as to this claim of professional negligence.

            Discussion 

Moving Defendant Providence Holy Cross Medical Center moves for summary judgment, or in the alternative, summary adjudication as to this claim of professional negligence. (MSJ, pp. 7:10-8:10.) 

In analyzing a motion for summary judgment, courts must, 1) determine whether the moving party has negated the opponent's claims; and 2) determine whether the opposition has demonstrated the existence of a triable, material factual issue.” (Hinesley v. Oakshade Town Center (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 289, 294.) Thus, summary judgment is granted when, after the Court’s consideration of the evidence set forth in the papers and all reasonable inferences accordingly, no triable issues of fact exist, and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. (CCP § 437(c); Villa v. McFarren(1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 733, 741.) 

i)               Moving Defendant’s Initial Burden

Moving Defendant makes three arguments in an attempt to rebut Plaintiff’s claim of medical malpractice. First, Moving Defendant argues that there is no dispute of fact as to whether the treatment rendered by their nurses or employees fell below the standard of care. (MSJ, pp. 19:27-20:6.) Second, Moving Defendant argues there is no dispute of fact as to whether their nurses or employees caused Plaintiff’s injuries. (Id. p. 22:1-9.) Third, Moving Defendant argues that they cannot be held liable for the actions of the doctors who treated Plaintiff because these doctors were all independent contractors. (Id. pp. 22:19-23:4.)

a)     Standard of Care and Causation

In support of these first two arguments, Moving Defendant provides the declaration of Alexis Chaudron, a registered nurse, and expert on the topic of the relevant standard of care for hospital staff in the state of California. (Chaudron Decl.) When a plaintiff alleges professional negligence, whereby the defendant is accused of failing to adhere to accepted standards of practice, the issues of standard of care and causation may be established only by qualified expert testimony. (Stevenson v. Kaiser Foundation Hosp. (1962) 203 Cal.App.3d 631 [emphasis added].) This is a professional negligence case. (Compl. p. 1.) Therefore, Stevenson applies.

Here, Moving Defendant’s expert establishes her credibility as an expert on this subject, and concludes that, to a reasonable degree of medical probability, Moving Defendant’s staff both complied with the relevant standard of care, and did not cause Plaintiff’s injuries. (Chaudron Decl. ¶¶ 44, 45.)

Notably, Plaintiff does not dispute this argument in his Opposition. Plaintiff states that, “Plaintiff does not contend that the nurses or non-physicians were negligent. Rather, as alleged in the Complaint, Plaintiff’s theory of liability against the hospital is based on the negligence of its physician-agents, including Dr. Ramin Ganjianpour.” (Opp. p. 1:21-24.) Given that Plaintiff has conceded the issue of Moving Defendant’s nurses and employees, the outcome of this motion rests entirely on Moving Defendant’s independent contractor argument.

b)    Independent Contractor Liability

Next, Moving Defendant argues that they cannot be held liable for the actions of the doctors who treated Plaintiff because these doctors were all independent contractors. (Id. pp. 22:19-23:4.) In support of this third argument, Moving Defendant provides the declaration of Sonia Ramos. Ms. Ramos works for Moving Defendant as the Senior Manager for Medical Staff Services. (Ramos Decl. ¶ 1.) Ms. Ramos explains that Moving Defendant does not employ any physicians. (Id. ¶ 3.) Ms. Ramos also states that none of the physicians who treated Plaintiff, including Dr. Ganjianpour, were Moving Defendant’s employees. (Id. ¶ 5.)

Next, Moving Defendant preemptively argues that Plaintiff cannot raise an argument for “ostensible agency” for these physicians. Under CIV § 2300, “[a]n agency is ostensible when the principal intentionally, or by want of ordinary care, causes a third person to believe another to be his agent who is not really employed by him.” (CIV § 2300.) In order to raise an argument for ostensible agency, a plaintiff must establish two elements: “(1) conduct by the hospital that would cause a reasonable person to believe that the physician was an agent of the hospital, and (2) reliance on that apparent agency relationship by the plaintiff.” (Mejia v. Community Hospital of San Bernadino (2002) 99 Cal.App.4th 1448, 1453.)

As to this first element, “a hospital is generally deemed to have held itself out as the provider of care, unless it gave the patient contrary notice.” (Id. p. 1454 [emphasis added].) As to the second element, “reliance . . . is established when the plaintiff ‘looks to’ the hospital for services, rather than to an individual physician . . . However, reliance need not be proven by direct testimony . . . In fact, many courts presume reliance, absent evidence that the plaintiff knew or should have known the physician was not an agent of the hospital.” (Ibid. [emphasis added].) Thus, even though there are two elements, only one factual analysis is required here – namely, whether the patient had reason to know that the treating physicians were not agents of the hospital. (Ibid.)

Moving Defendant asserts that Plaintiff had reason to know that none of the physicians treating him were agents of the hospital because, on October 12, 2022, Plaintiff signed a Conditions of Admission form which stated that, “PHYSICIANS ARE INDEPENDENT CONTRACTORS: The undersigned recognizes that all physicians, physician assistants, and surgeons furnishing service to the patient, including the radiologist, pathologist, anesthesiologist, emergency room physician, physician assistants, and the like, are independent contractors and are not employees or agents of the hospital.” (Sep. Statement, Issue 3, ¶¶ 4, 5; see also, Exhibit List, Ex. JJ, p. 4.) This form was purportedly signed by Plaintiff on October 12, 2022. (Exhibit List, Ex. JJ, p. 6.)

Under California case law, clear and unequivocal Conditions of Admission language like this can establish that a patient had reason to know that their physicians were not agents of the hospital. (Wicks v. Antelope Valley Healthcare Dist. (2020) 49 Cal.App.5th 866, 883.) However, the patient must be able to understand and act on the information provided. (Wicks, supra, 884 [distinguishing Whitlow v. Rideout Memorial Hospital (2015) 237 Cal.App.4th 631, where a patient who signed a similar form was vomiting and in intense pain].) Here, Moving Defendant asserts that the language of the Conditions of Admission form is clear, and that Plaintiff was not incapacitated when he signed the form. Moving Defendant bases this assertion on the declaration of Sonia Ramos, who states that, “[o]n October 12, 2022, Mr. Sanchez signed the Conditions of Admission form, which acknowledged that he reviewed the form, and he was offered the opportunity to ask any questions.” (Ramos Decl. ¶ 6.)

Based on this evidence, the Court finds that Moving Defendant has met their initial burden to negate Plaintiff’s claim of medical negligence, as the evidence presented suggests that Plaintiff was given notice that his treating physicians were not agents of Moving Defendant. This means that Plaintiff’s ostensible agency theory fails.

ii)             Plaintiff’s Shifted Burden

The burden now shifts to Plaintiff to establish a triable issue of fact as to the issue of ostensible agency. In his Opposition, Plaintiff argues that: 1) the evidence of the signed Conditions of Admission form is inadmissible, as it was not properly authenticated; and 2) even if it were admissible, there is a dispute of fact as to whether he was incapacitated when he signed the form. (Opp. pp. 2:24-3:12.) 

a)     Admissibility of the Ramos Declaration

First, Plaintiff raises the issue of admissibility. Moving Defendant’s evidence that Plaintiff signed the Conditions of Admission form comes from the declaration of Sonia Ramos, an employee of Moving Defendant. Ms. Ramos states that, “[o]n October 12, 2022, Mr. Sanchez signed the Conditions of Admission form, which acknowledged that he reviewed the form, and he was offered the opportunity to ask any questions.” (Ramos Decl. ¶ 6.) Ms. Ramos asserted to have, “personal knowledge of all facts contained within this Declaration.” (Id. ¶ 2.)

Plaintiff argues that Ms. Ramos lacks the foundation to make these assertions. In support of this argument Plaintiff provides excerpts from the deposition transcript of Ms. Ramos. There, Ms. Ramos states that she was not physically present in the ER on the night that Plaintiff was admitted. (Opp. Compendium of Evidence, Ex. C, p. 28:20-24.) She also states that she does not personally know whether Plaintiff was presented with, or read, the Conditions of Admission form she authenticated. (Id. p. 29:3-22; see also, Id. p. 29:8-11 [“Q: Okay. And your statement that the form was presented before any services was rendered is not based on anything you personally observed, right?  A: Correct”].) Based on these deposition statements, it appears as though Ms. Ramos lacks the foundation to state how Plaintiff was presented with these documents when he was admitted to the ER, and that he was given an opportunity to ask questions about the form. Thus, this evidence is arguably inadmissible.

In their Reply, Moving Defendant asserts that this foundation argument, “conflates Ms. Ramos’ lack of personal recollection of Mr. Sanchez’s individual admission with the distinct question whether an electronically maintained hospital admission record may be authenticated through appropriate testimony concerning the hospital’s recordkeeping and admission practices.” (Reply, p. 9:8-11.) Moving Defendant also argues that Plaintiff is mistaken when he asserts that, because Ms. Ramos did not personally observe Plaintiff’s intake, she cannot testify to the fact that physicians are not typically employees of the hospital. (Id. p. 9:2-7.)

The Court agrees for the most part. Ms. Ramos, in her role as Moving Defendant’s Senior Manager for Medical Staff Services, has the foundation to state that, as a general rule, the physicians working for Moving Defendant are independent contractors. She also has the ability to authenticate electronically stored documents like the signed form presented here. However, she does not have the foundation to rebut the elements of an ostensible agency claim. The primary dispute in this motion is whether 1) Plaintiff was presented with clear notice that his physicians were not agents of the hospital, and 2) whether Plaintiff was able to understand the information provided. Given that Ms. Ramos was not present when Plaintiff first signed these forms, she could, at most, testify as to the date of the electronic signature on this form. She cannot, however, testify as to the circumstances of the signature. Thus, her statement that Plaintiff, “was offered the opportunity to ask any questions” cannot be used to establish that Plaintiff was capable of understanding the implications of this agreement. (Ramos Decl. ¶ 6.)

Thus, the Court agrees that Moving Defendant has failed to present admissible evidence showing that Plaintiff understood the information provided, as required under Wicks. 

b)    Dispute of Fact as to Plaintiff’s Incapacitation

Setting aside the issue of admissibility, Plaintiff also raises a dispute of material fact as to whether they were able to understand the Conditions of Admission language presented to them on October 12, 2022.

Under California case law, clear and unequivocal Conditions of Admission language like this can establish that a patient had reason to know that their physicians were not agents of the hospital. (Wicks v. Antelope Valley Healthcare Dist.(2020) 49 Cal.App.5th 866, 883.) However, the patient must be able to understand and act on the information provided. (Wicks, supra, p. 884.)

Here, Plaintiff provides multiple pieces of evidence that suggest he was unable to understand the information presented to him in the Conditions of Admission form. First, Plaintiff points to Exhibit E of Moving Defendant’s motion – a summary of his patient notes. There, it states that Plaintiff, “was given fentanyl in route and states he has no relief with that.” (Exhibit List, Ex. E, p. 152.) Additionally, Plaintiff points out that, shortly after he was admitted, he was administered 100 milligrams of ketamine. (Kvamme Depo. 36: 19-23; see also, Compendium of Evidence, Ex. E, p. 172.) Given the strong dose of pain meds Plaintiff was administered, it is possible that at the time he signed the Conditions of Admission form, he could not understand them, or even remember signing them. This argument is reinforced by Plaintiff’s own deposition testimony, where he was shown a copy of the Conditions of Admission form. Plaintiff stated on the record that he never received this document, did not sign it, and did not recognize the signature. (Opp. Ex. 2. pp. 96:17-97:7.) Taken together, these facts raise a dispute of material fact as to Plaintiff’s ability to understand the terms of the Conditions of Admission form. Thus, per Wicks, Moving Defendant cannot defeat Plaintiff’s claim of ostensible agency at the summary judgment stage.

In their Reply, Moving Defendant argues that this dispute as to the signing of the first form does not defeat their argument, because Moving Defendant signed more than a dozen other Consent for Surgery forms while admitted, all of which also contained similar “not agents” language. (Reply, p. 8:9-18.) As evidence of these forms, Moving Defendant submits the declaration of William Sulentor, who verifies and attaches multiple consent forms signed by Plaintiff, all of which include language clarifying that the physicians treating him are not Moving Defendant’s agents. (See generally, Sulentor Decl. Ex. A.) 

Normally, this would be very compelling evidence that Plaintiff had notice of his physicians’ relationship to Moving Defendant. However, this evidence was improperly brought via a Reply. Under CCP § 437c(b)(4), “[a] reply to the opposition [for a motion for summary judgment] shall be served and filed by the moving party not less than 11 days preceding the noticed or continued date of hearing, unless the court for good cause orders otherwise. The reply shall not include any new evidentiary matter, additional material facts, or separate statement submitted with the reply and not presented in the moving papers or opposing papers.” (CCP § 437c(b)(4) [emphasis added].) Moving Defendant’s initial motion did not provide these consent forms as evidence. (See generally, MSJ; see also, Exhibit List.) In fact, Moving Defendant’s separate statement only discussed the initial October 12, 2022, Conditions of Admission form. (Sep. Statement, Issue 3, ¶¶ 4-5.) Thus, this form is the only one that Moving Defendant has put forth as necessary to negate Plaintiff’s claim. 

When a moving party includes a "fact" in the separate statement, the moving party concedes that fact's materiality. (See, Nazir v. United Airlines, Inc. (2009) 178 Cal.App.4th 243, 252.) Thus, Moving Defendant has conceded that the validity of the initial Conditions of Admission form is material to the dispute in this case. Moreover, Moving Defendant cannot introduce new evidence of other relevant documents at this late stage to defeat Plaintiff’s argument. Thus, Moving Defendant’s argument fails.

iii)           Summary

In summary, Moving Defendant met their initial burden to rebut Plaintiff’s ostensible agency argument by establishing that they provided Plaintiff with actual notice of the fact that his physicians were not agents of Moving Defendant. Plaintiff then met their shifted burden by disputing the material facts raised by Moving Defendant, thereby undermining the validity of that notice. Thus, there is a triable issue of material fact as to Plaintiff’s ostensible agency argument.

For these reasons, the Court DENIES Moving Defendant’s Motion for Summary Judgment.

Conclusion

The Court DENIES Moving Defendant’s Motion for Summary Judgment.

Background
This is a negligence and premises liability case. Plaintiff is Bruce Kelly, an employee of Commercial Tech Services (CTS), an electrical subcontractor. Plaintiff’s employer was hired by Defendant CSI as a subcontractor, installing electrical wiring for a modification at the Westfield Topanga mall. Defendant CSI, in turn, was hired by Defendant Westfield Topanga Owner, LLC as the general contractor on the project. The remaining Defendant in this case is Westfield DDC, LLC.
Plaintiff alleges that on January 24, 2020, he fell off a ladder while installing wiring in the walls. As he fell, Plaintiff hit his head on the concrete floor, knocking him unconscious.
On January 21, 2021, Plaintiff brought suit against Defendants alleging negligence, premises liability, and product liability.
In early 2022, Plaintiff was examined by both a neurologist and neuropsychologist.
In March of 2023, this Court granted Defendants Motion for Summary Judgment. Plaintiff successfully appealed this order, and in 2025, the case was remanded back to this Court.
On July 8, 2026, following meet and confer efforts, Defendants unilaterally noticed a subsequent physical and mental examination of Plaintiff.
Plaintiff now moves for a protective order as to both requests.

Plaintiff argues that these requests are improper as Defendants did not request leave to conduct these examinations. Under CCP § 2032.310, “[i]f any party desires to obtain discovery by a physical examination other than that described in Article 2 (commencing with Section 2032.210), or by a mental examination, the party shall obtain leave of court.” (CCP § 2032.310(a).) In other words, a party must obtain leave of court to request a subsequent physical or mental examination. (Carpenter v. Superior Court (2006) The court, “shall grant a motion for a physical or mental examination under Section 2032.310 only for good cause shown.” (CCP § 2032.320(a).)
Here, Plaintiff has already undergone both a physical and mental examination by doctors Edwin Amos and David Lechuga. (Henriks Decl. ¶ 2.) Thus, per CCP § 2032.310(a), Defendants needed to request leave of court to request a subsequent examination. Defendants did not request leave. For these reasons, the Court agrees that these demands are void.
In their Opposition, Defendants assert that there is good cause for these subsequent examinations. First, Defendants point out that it has been four years since the initial examinations of Plaintiff. (Opp. p. 5:13-15.) Additionally, Defendants note that Plaintiff has characterized their own condition as “deteriorating”. (Id. p. 16-18.) Given the long length of time between the original examinations of Plaintiff and now, as well as Plaintiff’s deteriorating condition, the Court agrees that there is good cause for these subsequent examinations to proceed.
Additionally, in a parallel motion, Plaintiff argued that Defendants should be denied the right to depose him personally, because the topic of his health is better addressed by experts. Here, however, Plaintiff moves to prevent experts from examining his physical and mental health. The
Court does not believe that Plaintiff can request relief from both of these discovery avenues simultaneously. Thus, the Court finds these examinations are likely proper.
That being said, this is a motion for a protective order, not a motion for leave to conduct subsequent examinations. Defendants cannot use their Opposition to transmute one motion into another. The ultimate question here is whether Defendants properly moved for leave as to these examinations. The Court has found that they haven’t. Therefore, the Court GRANTS Plaintiff’s request for a protective order as to the August 4, and August 19 examinations unilaterally scheduled by Defendants. The Court explicitly does not grant this protective order as to these examinations in general.
As explained above, the Court agrees that there is good cause for these examinations. The Court strongly encourages the parties to work together informally to reach a mutually agreeable time for these examinations to take place. In order to ensure this collaboration, the Court schedules an OSC Re: Subsequent Examination Scheduling for October 12, 2026. The Court expects the parties to have scheduled mutually agreeable dates for these examinations by then.
ii)
Sanctions
Plaintiff also requests sanctions against Defendants for opposing this protective order. (Mot. for Protective Order, p. 7:4-18.)
Under CCP § 2017.020, “[t]he court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion for a protective order, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (CCP § 2017.020(b).)


As explained above, the Court believes that Defendants’ request for subsequent examinations was procedurally improper but correct on the merits. Thus, the Court finds Defendants acted with substantial justification in opposing this motion, and that the circumstances make the imposition of sanctions here unjust. Therefore, the Court denies Plaintiff’s request for sanctions.
Conclusion
The Court GRANTS Plaintiff’s request for a protective order as to the August 4, and August 19 examinations unilaterally scheduled by Defendants. The Court explicitly does not grant this protective order as to these examinations in general.
The Court also schedules an OSC Re: Subsequent Examination Scheduling for October 12, 2026.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER: The Court GRANTS Plaintiff’s motion to enforce the April 13, 2026, joint stipulation as to the 21 stipulated subpoena custodians. The Court also GRANTS Plaintiff’s request for discovery sanctions in the modified amount of $1,050.00.
Background
This is a negligence and premises liability case. Plaintiff is Bruce Kelly, an employee of Commercial Tech Services (CTS), an electrical subcontractor. Plaintiff’s employer was hired by

Defendant CSI as a subcontractor, installing electrical wiring for a modification at the Westfield Topanga mall. Defendant CSI, in turn, was hired by Defendant Westfield Topanga Owner, LLC as the general contractor on the project. The remaining Defendant in this case is Westfield DDC, LLC.
Plaintiff alleges that on January 24, 2020, he fell off a ladder while installing wiring in the walls. As he fell, Plaintiff hit his head on the concrete floor, knocking him unconscious.
On January 21, 2021, Plaintiff brought suit against Defendants alleging negligence, premises liability, and product liability.
On February 24, 2026, Defendants served Plaintiff with 83 third-party subpoenas for the production of various medical records.
On March 25, 2026, Plaintiff objected to all 83 subpoenas.
On April 13, 2026, the parties entered into a joint stipulation. Plaintiff agreed to withdraw all 83 objections to these third-party subpoenas, and Defendants agreed that they would provide Plaintiff with copies of all of these documents at no cost. The Court granted this stipulation on April 15, 2026.
Now, Plaintiff moves to enforce this joint stipulation.
Discussion
i)
Motion to Enforce Stipulation
Plaintiff moves to enforce the terms of the April 13, 2026, joint stipulation as to the third-party subpoenas for medical information. (Mot. to Enforce Stipulation, p. 6:16-24.)
Under the terms of the April 13, 2026, stipulation, Plaintiff agreed to withdraw his objections to 83 third-party subpoenas, “on the condition that Defendants provide Plaintiff’s


counsel with all copies of all documents produced pursuant to the subpoenas within five (5) days of receipt at no cost to Plaintiff.” (Joint Stipulation Order, 4/15/26.)
According to Plaintiff’s notice of narrowed scope, Plaintiff specifically moves to compel Defendant into providing copies of the following twenty-one subpoena documents: 1. Claremont Imaging Associates (Radiology Records); 2. Doctors of Hearing, Inc.; 3. University Spine and Orthopedics; 4. Vincent L. Gumbs, M.D.; 5. Michael Klein, M.D.; 6. Ved Prakash, M.D.; 7. Stanley G. Katz, M.D.; 8. Patricia Penny-Fichtner, LVN; 9. Jin Lee, M.D.; 10. Andrew Hsiao, M.D.; 11. Dr. Paul Gilbert; 12. UCLA Health Systems (Medical Records); 13. UCLA Health, HIT Team (Billing Records); 14. UCLA Health (Radiology Records); 15. UC Irvine Medical Center (Medical Records); 16. Marcia R. Gump, MFT; 17. Martin Levine, M.D.; 18. Medata, Inc.; 19. Collage Rehabilitation Partners; 20. Gordon Tam, M.D.; 21. Uthaiah Kokkalera, M.D. (Notice of Narrowed Scope, p. 2:12-20.)
Under CCP § 2016.030, parties “may by written stipulation modify the procedures provided by this title for any method of discovery.” (CCP § 2016.030.) Moreover, “both the court and the opposing litigants must be able to rely upon the validity of stipulated resolutions of discovery disputes and to have them enforceable by order.” (Young v. Rosenthal (1989) 212 Cal.App.3d 96, 117.) Thus, “[a] stipulation in proper form is binding upon the parties if it is within the authority of the attorneys.” (Bowden v. Green (1982) 128 Cal. App. 3d 65, 72.) Based on this case law, the Court has both the power and the obligation to enforce this stipulation.
Here, Plaintiff has provided evidence of a valid stipulation agreement and has asserted that Defendant has failed to comply with this agreement as to 21 of the 83 relevant subpoenas. (Henriks Decl. Ex. A; see also, Henriks Decl. ¶ 5.) As explained above, litigants must be able to rely on the validity of discovery stipulations. Thus, the Court is inclined to grant this motion.

Notably, Defendants never filed an Opposition. This appears to be a clerical mistake rather than a strategic one, as Plaintiff’s Reply addresses arguments supposedly raised in opposition to this motion. (See, e.g., Reply, p. 3:27-28.) Given that Defendants never filed this Opposition, the Court will not consider it.
For these reasons, the Court GRANTS Plaintiff’s motion to enforce the April 13, 2026, joint stipulation as to the 21 stipulated subpoena custodians.
ii)
Sanctions
Additionally, Plaintiff requests $7,800.00 in discovery sanctions for misuse of the discovery process.
Under CCP § 2023.030(a), “[t]he court may impose a monetary sanction ordering that one engaging in the misuse of the discovery process, or any attorney advising that conduct, or both pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney's fees, incurred by anyone as a result of that conduct. . . If a monetary sanction is authorized by any provision of this title, the court shall impose that sanction unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (CCP § 2023.030(a).)
Under CCP § 2023.010, disobeying a court order to provide discovery amounts to a misuse of the discovery process. (CCP § 2023.010(g).) And, under California case law, a granted stipulation is tantamount to a court order. (Mileikowsky v. Tenet Healthsystem (2005) 128 Cal. App. 4th 262, 279.) Moreover, there are no facts to suggest that Defendants acted with substantial justification, as Defendants never filed their Opposition. Thus, the Court agrees that sanctions are appropriate.
However, the Court finds Plaintiff’s requested amount unreasonable. Plaintiff’s attorney estimates her reasonable fees at $7,800.00 – based on an estimated 12 hours of work, billed at $650
an hour. (Henriks Decl. ¶ 8.) Given the simplicity of this motion, the Court does not find that either 12 hours of work, or an hourly rate of $650 an hour is reasonable. This was a simple motion to enforce a stipulated discovery agreement between the parties.
“[T]he court's discretion in awarding attorney fees is … to be exercised so as to fully compensate counsel for the prevailing party for services reasonably provided to his or her client.” (Horsford v. Board of Trustees of California State University (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 359, 395 [emphasis added].) The trial court may reduce the award where the fee request appears unreasonably inflated, such as where the attorneys’ efforts are unorganized or duplicative. (Serrano v. Unruh (1982) 32 Cal.3d 621, 635, fn. 21.) Moreover, courts have repeatedly stated that the trial court is in the best position to value the services rendered by the attorneys in his or her courtroom, and this includes the determination of the hourly rate that will be used in the lodestar calculus. (East County Boulevard LLC v. Backcountry Against the Dump, Inc. (2016) 6 Cal.App.5th 426, 436-437.)
Given the simplicity of this motion, the Court will exercise its discretion and reduce Ms. Henriks’ hourly rate to $350 an hour, and reduce the hours requested to 8. This brings Plaintiff’s total reasonable fees to $2,800.00.
For these reasons, the Court GRANTS Plaintiff’s request for discovery sanctions in the modified amount of $2,800.00.
Conclusion
The Court GRANTS Plaintiff’s motion to enforce the April 13, 2026, joint stipulation as to the 21 stipulated subpoena custodians. The Court also GRANTS Plaintiff’s request for discovery sanctions in the modified amount of $2,800.00.

Background
This is a negligence and premises liability case. Plaintiff is Bruce Kelly, an employee of Commercial Tech Services (CTS), an electrical subcontractor. Plaintiff’s employer was hired by Defendant CSI as a subcontractor, installing electrical wiring for a modification at the Westfield Topanga mall. Defendant CSI, in turn, was hired by Defendant Westfield Topanga Owner, LLC as the general contractor on the project. The remaining Defendant in this case is Westfield DDC, LLC.
Plaintiff alleges that on January 24, 2020, he fell off a ladder while installing wiring in the walls. As he fell, Plaintiff hit his head on the concrete floor, knocking him unconscious.
On January 21, 2021, Plaintiff brought suit against Defendants alleging negligence, premises liability, and product liability.
In March of 2022, Plaintiff was deposed for the first time. Then, in July of 2022, Plaintiff was deposed an additional time. Cumulatively, Plaintiff has been deposed for 5.5 hours.
In March of 2023, this Court granted Defendants Motion for Summary Judgment. Plaintiff successfully appealed this order, and in 2025, the case was remanded back to this Court.
Now, Defendants request leave to once again depose Plaintiff.
Discussion
Defendants request leave to depose Plaintiff for a second1 time. (Mot. for Leave, p. 1:3-6.)
Under CCP § 2025.610, “[o]nce any party has taken the deposition of any natural person, including that of a party to the action, neither the party who gave, nor any other party who has been served with a deposition notice pursuant to Section 2025.240 may take a subsequent
1 According

deposition of that deponent.” (CCP § 2025.610(a).) Thus, the general rule is that a natural person can only be deposed once. However, “for good cause shown, the court may grant leave to take a subsequent deposition.” (CCP § 2025.610(a).
Here, Defendants argue there is good cause for a further deposition of Plaintiff, as nearly four years have passed since he was initially deposed, and the facts surrounding Plaintiff’s damages have evolved since then. (Mot. for Leave, p. 1:7-14.)
Defendants argues that this deposition will be limited in scope to the following topics: 1) the factual bases for Plaintiff’s updated and expanded damage contentions as reflected in his recent responses to Special Interrogatories, Set Two; 2) Plaintiff’s current subjective symptoms and functional limitations; and 3) Plaintiff’s employment history and functional capacity during the time period between his last deposition and now. (Id. p. 1:18-21.)
i)
New Discovery Responses
Defendants’ first argument is that, on June 3, 2026, Plaintiff provided responses to Special Interrogatories, Set Two. (Id. p. 5:5-6.) Therein, Plaintiff apparently expanded the scope of the damages he claims to have suffered. (Id. p. 5:8-17.) Defendants provide a copy of Plaintiff’s responses to these SROGs. (Ross Decl. Ex. E.) However, Defendants do not point to or cite any specific answer from Plaintiff that went beyond the scope of their initial claims. (See Mot. for Leave, p. 5:5-25.) Having reviewed Plaintiff’s responses, the Court notes that Plaintiff’s responses to questions about their damages appear to direct Defendants to medical records that have already been produced, as well as point out that this information will be provided via expert disclosure. (See e.g., Ross Decl. Ex. E, p. 12:11-18.) Thus, unless Defendants can point to a more specific expansion of Plaintiff’s earlier claim for damages, the Court does not find this argument compelling.

Additionally, the Court has other reasons to believe that Plaintiff’s responses to these interrogatories are not actually relevant to Defendants’ request. Defendants began the process of requesting this subsequent deposition before Plaintiff even submitted these responses. Plaintiff’s responses to these interrogatories were served on June 3, 2026. (Ross Decl. Ex. E, p. 46.) However, Defendants began to meet and confer with Plaintiff about a subsequent deposition on June 1, 2026. (Ross Decl. ¶ 1.) Thus, it seems like Plaintiff’s responses to these SROGs are an after-the-fact justification for this request, rather than an inciting cause.
ii)
Four-Year Gap
Next, Defendants argue that a second deposition is necessary because substantial time has passed since Plaintiff’s initial deposition. (Mot. for Leave, pp. 7:25-8:5.) Specifically, Defendants state that, “[t]he passage of significant time, combined with a party’s claim that his condition has changed, is a well-recognized basis for good cause under section 2025.610.” (Id. p. 8:1-3.) Defendants proport to support this assertion with two citations: Fairmont Ins. Co. v. Superior Court, and Beverly Hospital v. Superior Court. (Id. p. 8:3-4.) However, neither of these cases stand for this assertion, as neither case addresses the issue of subsequent depositions. (Beverly Hospital v. Superior Court (1993) 19 Cal. App. 4th 1289, 1291; see also, Fairmont Ins. Co. v. Superior Court (2000) 22 Cal. 4th 245, 247.) Instead, these cases address the trial court’s general discretion on matters of discovery. Given that Defendants have provided no direct case law on this point and have not cited any specific change in Plaintiff’s claims, the Court does not find this argument convincing.
iii)
Deteriorating Health
Finally, Defendants argue that Plaintiff’s deteriorating health establishes good cause for a subsequent deposition. Specifically, Defendants state, “Plaintiff cannot claim ongoing and

worsening neurological and neuropsychological deficits—and contend that he requires future treatment across eleven medical specialties—while simultaneously denying Defendant the right to question him about those very deficits under oath.” (Mot. for Leave, p. 8:21-23.)
The Court’s issue with this argument is that questions of fact about Plaintiff’s worsening medical condition are more readily addressed by medical experts, not Plaintiff himself. Defendants preemptively argue that the questions they have for Plaintiff are matters unique to his personal knowledge. (Id. p. 9:17-19.) Defendants state, “Plaintiff’s subjective experience of his current symptoms, his daily functional limitations, his personal assessment of his condition, and the factual bases for his expanded damage contentions—are not documented in medical records or interrogatory responses.” (Id. p. 9:14-17.) The Court agrees, but only in part. Plaintiff’s updated assessment of his daily functional limitations is relevant to the case at hand and is something only he could testify to. However, topics like his current symptoms or the assessment of his condition is something better left to experts.
Unfortunately, Defendants’ requested topics for this deposition go beyond the scope of the narrow issue of Plaintiff’s daily functional limitations. Defendants wish to discuss topics such as, “Plaintiff’s current medical condition and treatment received since 2022; (b) Plaintiff’s current subjective symptoms and functional limitations in daily life; (c) Plaintiff’s employment history and functional capacity since 2022; and (d) the factual basis for Plaintiff’s expanded damage contentions as reflected in his responses to Special Interrogatories, Set Two.” (Id. p. 9:6-10.) Moreover, as discussed above, the Court does not find that Defendants have established good cause for a subsequent deposition.
Given that Defendants’ request is so much broader than the Court feels is appropriate, the Court is tentatively inclined to DENY Defendants’ request.

Alternatively, the Court may be inclined to allow for Plaintiff’s deposition, but solely on the subject of Plaintiff’s daily functional limitations, and potentially his employment history. Notably, this deposition would be limited to 1.5 hours. As Plaintiff points out in his Opposition, he has already been deposed for 5.5 hours. (Opp. p. 3:7-17.) Under CCP 2025.290(a), “[e]xcept as provided in subdivision (b), or by any court order, including a case management order, a deposition examination of the witness by all counsel, other than the witness' counsel of record, shall be limited to seven hours of total testimony.” (CCP § 2025.290(a).) Thus, any time spent in deposition beyond this point would exceed the seven-hour statutory limit.
iv)
Addendum Analysis
The above represents this Court’s analysis of the merits of this motion. However, the Court would also like to note that, on July 13, 2026, after this motion was initially filed, Plaintiff filed a Second Amended Complaint, alleging a new cause of action for negligent hiring retention and supervision. (SAC p. 1.) This amendment was made with leave of court. (Minute Order, 7/10/26.) However, on September 8, 2026, Plaintiff dropped this claim as to both Defendant Westfield Topanga Owner, LLC, and Defendant Westfield DDC, LLC. (Request for Dismissal, 9/8/26.) If Plaintiff has raised an entirely new cause of action, then Defendants are almost assuredly entitled to a subsequent deposition. However, the fact that this claim was dropped as to multiple Defendants suggests the issue might be moot. Given the timings of these filings, these procedural complications are not addressed in the motion itself. Thus, the Court intends to discuss these topics further with the parties at the hearing on this motion. The Court will reserve its ruling on this motion until then.
Conclusion

The Court TENTATIVELY DENIES Defendants’ Motion for Leave to Take Subsequent Deposition of Plaintiff.
In the alternative, the Court GRANTS the motion but limits the deposition to the topics of Plaintiff’s daily functional limitations and potentially his employment history. The duration of this deposition is also limited to 1.5 hours
IT IS SO ORDERED, Plaintiff TO GIVE NOTICE.

Background
This is a medical malpractice and wrongful death case. Plaintiffs are Brooke and Brady Poppinga, two parents suing individually, and as successors in interest to their deceased 17-year-old son, Julius Poppinga (herein “decedent”).
Plaintiffs allege that on August 27, 2023, their son, decedent, was admitted to the emergency department at Defendant Providence Cedars-Sinai Tarzana Medical Center due for difficulty breathing and chest pain. Decedent was admitted to the Pediatric ICU with a diagnosis of asthma exacerbation. There, Decedent was cared for by numerous doctors, including Moving Defendant Ira Wardono.
Given decedent’s difficulty breathing, and his history of asthma, he was given oxygen and monitored. Decedent was also given a chest x-ray, which showed low lung volumes. Decedent’s history was taken the next day, where he explained that his symptoms started on August 14, but had been mild. Decedent was then admitted for inpatient treatment and was given fluids. Decedent was also given multiple abuterol treatments to relax his airways. Over the rest of the day, decedent was treated and evaluated by numerous doctors.
On August 29, 2023, decedent was still having difficulty breathing and appeared anxious. He also temporarily lost consciousness. At the time, decedent was being cared for by Non-Moving Defendant Dr. Arielle Czerwinski. Dr. Czerwinski briefly left the room to place multiple treatment orders. When she came back, she was notified that decedent had stopped breathing and had flat-lined. Decedent’s doctors intubated him and performed chest compressions in an attempt to

resuscitate him. Tragically, they were unable to stabilize decedent, and he died shortly thereafter. A subsequent autopsy concluded that decedent had died from a pulmonary embolism.
On November 25, 2024, Plaintiffs brought suit against Defendant Providence Cedars-Sinai Tarzana Medical Center, Moving Defendant Oren Hirsch, as well as numerous other doctors, alleging four causes of action: 1) wrongful death due to medical negligence, 2) medical negligence survival action, 3) NIED, and 4) loss of consortium. Plaintiffs argue that Defendants were medically negligent in their misdiagnosis and treatment of decedent, and that decedent died as a result of this negligence.
On May 27, 2026, Plaintiffs added Moving Defendant Dr. Ira Wardono to this case via a doe amendment.
Now, Moving Defendant Ira Wardono demurs to Plaintiff’s first two causes of action – the only two claims alleged against Moving Defendant.
Meet and Confer
Before filing a demurrer, the demurring party shall meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer. (CCP § 430.41(a) [emphasis added].) A declaration must be filed with a demurrer regarding the results of the meet and confer process. (CCP § 430.41(a)(3).)
Here, Moving Defendant provides the declaration of Sara Mores, who states that on July 13, 2026, the parties met and conferred over the phone and discussed the issue raised in this pleading. (Mores Decl. ¶ 4.) Unfortunately, the parties were unable to reach an informal agreement. (Ibid.) For these reasons, the Court finds that Moving Defendant has met their meet and confer obligations.

Discussion
Moving Defendant demurs to the first and second causes of action for wrongful death and medical negligence, arguing that both claims are time-barred. (Demurrer, p. 2:6-11.)
A demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of other pleadings. It raises issues of law, not fact, regarding the form or content of the opposing party’s pleading (complaint, answer or cross-complaint). (CCP §§ 422.10, 589; see, Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) It is not the function of the demurrer to challenge the truthfulness of the complaint; and for purposes of the ruling on the demurrer, all facts pled in the complaint are assumed to be true. (Id. at 994.) This means that, in order for Moving Defendant’s demurrer to succeed, Plaintiff’s first two causes of action must be facially time-barred.
Under CCP § 340.5, “[i]n an action for injury or death against a health care provider based upon such person's alleged professional negligence, the time for the commencement of action shall be three years after the date of injury or one year after the plaintiff discovers, or through the use of reasonable diligence should have discovered, the injury, whichever occurs first.” (CCP § 340.5.)
Here, it is undisputed that Plaintiffs’ original lawsuit was timely filed. Decedent Julius Poppinga died on August 29, 2023. (Compl. ¶ 20.) Plaintiffs’ initial Complaint was filed on November 25, 2024. (Compl. p. 1.) Plaintiffs served the known Defendants with Section 364 Notice of Intent letters, which tolled the statute of limitations for an additional 90 days. (Opp. p. 5:23-25.) Instead, Moving Defendant challenges the timeliness of Plaintiff’s May 27, 2026, doe amendment, which added Moving Defendant Ira Wardono to the lawsuit. (Opp. Schumacher Decl. ¶ 3.)
Typically, a doe amendment is affected by the “relation back” doctrine, which, “deems a later-filed pleading to have been filed at the time of an earlier complaint which met the applicable

limitations period.” (Quiroz v. Seventh Ave. Center (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 1256, 1278.) However, Moving Defendant argues that the relation back doctrine does not apply in this case.
In order for the relation-back doctrine to apply, "the amended complaint must (1) rest on the same general set of facts, (2) involve the same injury, and (3) refer to the same instrumentality, as the original one." (Ibid.)1 Additionally, the plaintiff must be genuinely ignorant of the doe defendant at the time the original Complaint was filed. (Woo v. Superior Ct., (1999) 75 Cal. App. 4th 169, 177.)
Here, Moving Defendant argues that Plaintiffs cannot meet this final requirement, because they were not genuinely ignorant of his involvement at the time they originally filed the Complaint. (Demurrer, pp. 6:21-7:2.) In support of this claim, Moving Defendant points to the fact that his name appears seven separate times in Decedent’s medical records. (Ibid.) Thus, Moving Defendant concludes that Plaintiffs were not ignorant of his name and role in decedents treatment, and therefore the relation back doctrine does not apply. (Ibid.)
In Opposition, Plaintiffs dispute that they were aware of Moving Defendant’s role in Decedent’s treatment. Plaintiffs assert that the first time they learned of Moving Defendant’s involvement was on May 4, 2026, when Defendant, PCSTMC, served Supplemental Responses to Plaintiffs’ Requests for Production of Documents (Set One), which included concurrently produced copies of the PICU Staff Sheet/Schedule for August 27, through August 30, 2023. (Opp. Schumacher Decl. ¶ 8.)
Plaintiffs also argue that six of the “seven times” Moving Defendant’s name is mentioned in Decedent’s medical records are all references to a single order for Pepcid, issued on August 28,

2023. (Opp. p. 10:5-7; see also, Opp. Ex. D.) Thus, while Moving Defendant’s name was technically present, Plaintiffs provide compelling that they did not know the extent of her role until recently.
Additionally, Plaintiffs point out that Moving Defendant, “was never once identified or referenced by name – whether as a witness or as a healthcare provider involved in or consulted with during and/or in connection with the Decedent’s care and treatment at PCSTMC – in any of the verified responses to written discovery received from any of the Defendants.” (Opp. Schumacher Decl. ¶¶ 9-10.)
In summary, Moving Defendant’s demurrer rests on the assertion that the relation back doctrine does not apply to her. Moving Defendant’s argument as to the relation back doctrine is based almost entirely on the fact that her name appears multiple times in Decedent’s medical records – meaning Plaintiffs must have known about her. However, Plaintiffs have explained that the presence of Moving Defendant’s name was insufficient to put them on notice of her involvement in Decedents treatment. Additionally, Plaintiffs have provided other evidence to suggest that they were ignorant of Moving Defendant’s role in this case. Thus, the Court finds that Plaintiffs were ignorant of Moving Defendant’s role, meaning the relation back doctrine does apply, and therefore these claims are not time-barred.
For these reasons, the Court OVERRULES Moving Defendant’s demurrer to the first and second causes of action.
Conclusion
The Court OVERRULES Moving Defendant’s demurrer to the first and second causes of action.
