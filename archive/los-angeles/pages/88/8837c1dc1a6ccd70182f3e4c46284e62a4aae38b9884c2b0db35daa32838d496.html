DEPARTMENT 509 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 25STCV25541    Hearing Date: July 10, 2026    Dept: 509

Liduvina Flores v. Daniel Sanchez, et al. 

(1) DEMURRER TO FIRST AMENDED COMPLAINT

(2) MOTION TO STRIKE
 

MOVING PARTY: Defendants Daniel Sanchez and Linda Calderon

RESPONDING PARTY(S): Plaintiff Liduvina Flores

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

This is a habitability action. Plaintiff Liduvina Flores (Plaintiff) brings this action against her landlord Daniel Sanchez (Sanchez) and property manager Linda Calderon (Calderon, collectively, Defendants) regarding habitability of her former residential rental unit located at 4141 Michigan Avenue, Los Angeles, California 90063. Plaintiff brings causes of action for: (1) violation of Los Angeles County Rent Stabilization & Tenant Protections Ordinance (L.A. County Code ch. 8.52); (2) unlawful rent increase and unauthorized fees (Civil Code § 827; Los Angeles County Code §§ 8.52.040,; (3) breach of the implied warranty of habitability; (4) retaliatory eviction/retaliatory acts (Civil Code § 1942.5); (5) negligence per se; (6) private nuisance; (7) breach of the covenant of quiet enjoyment; (8) defamation (slander per se); and (9) unfair business practices (Business and Professions Code § 17200 et seq.).

Defendants now demur to the first, second, fourth, fifth, sixth, eighth, and ninth causes of action in the first amended complaint (FAC) and move to strike portions within. Calderon, individually, demurs to the third and seventh causes of action. Plaintiff opposed. 

TENTATIVE RULING:

Calderon’s Demurrer to the Third and Seventh Causes of Action is SUSTAINED WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND. 

Defendants’ Demurrer to the First and Second Causes of Action is SUSTAINED WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND.

Defendants’ Demurrer to the Fourth Cause of Action is OVERRULED.

Defendants’ Demurrer to the Fifth Cause of Action is SUSTAINED WITH 30 DAYS LEAVE TO AMEND.

Defendants’ Demurrer to the Sixth, Eighth, and Ninth Causes of Action is SUSTAINED. Whether leave to amend is granted will depend on whether Plaintiff can make an adequate offer of proof at the hearing that a successful amendment is reasonably possible.

Defendants’ Motion to Strike is DENIED.

Moving parties are ordered to give notice, unless waived. 

DISCUSSION:

Demurrer to First Amended Complaint

I. Meet and Confer

The Declaration of attorney Anthony O. Egbase does not reflect that the meet and confer requirement was satisfied. The Egbase Declaration only attests to sending an email with no telephonic meet and confer taking place. This is insufficient to satisfy the requirements to meet either telephonically or in person as required by Code of Civil Procedure, section 430.41. However, in the interest of judicial economy, the Court exercises its discretion to address the motion on its merits. The parties are admonished to comply with all meet and confer obligations going forward.

II. Judicial Notice

Defendants seek judicial notice of Exhibit A, the “Stipulated Judgment and Settlement Agreement” entered June 20, 2025, in Linda Calderon v. Liduvina Flores, LASC No. 25STUD03610. Defendants request for judicial notice is GRANTED.

III. Improper Filings

Defendants’ demurrer is 19 pages long and their reply is 13 pages long, both of which  are over the applicable page limits. (See Cal. Rule of Court, Rule 3.1113(d).) Defendants did not seek ex-parte relief to submit a longer memorandum or reply. (See Cal. Rule of Court, Rule 3.1113(e).) Defendants’ motion does not contain a table of contents nor a table of authority. (See Cal. Rule of Court, Rule 3.1113(f).)

Nevertheless, its discretion, the Court considers this improperly filed demurrer and reply. Defendants are admonished to comply with all Rules of Court going forward.

IV. Legal Standard

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (Taylor v. City of Los Angeles Dept. of Water and Power (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 1216, 1228.) In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or by proper judicial notice. (Code Civ. Pro. § 430.30, subd. (a).) A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. (SKF Farms v. Superior Court (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 902, 905.) Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed. (Ibid.) The only issue involved in a demurrer hearing is whether the complaint, as it stands, unconnected with extraneous matters, states a cause of action. (Hahn, at p. 747.) “We treat the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Serrano v. Priest (1971) 5 Cal.App.3d 584, 591.) A “demurrer does not, however, admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law alleged in the pleading, or the construction of instruments pleaded, or facts impossible in law.” (S. Shore Land Co. v. Petersen (1964) 226 Cal.App.2d 725, 732 (internal citations omitted).)  

V. Analysis

Defendants demur to all causes of action in the FAC.

As an initial note, the Court OVERRULES Defendants’ demurrer for uncertainty on the grounds that (a) demurrers for uncertainty are disfavored and should only be sustained where the complaint is so uncertain that the demurring defendant cannot reasonably respond thereto (see, e.g., Khoury v. Maly’s of Calif., Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616); and (b) here, Plaintiff’s complaint is not so uncertain that Defendants cannot reasonably respond thereto. Moreover, even if the pleading is somewhat vague, “ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Ibid.)  

Second, in their demurrer, Defendants argue the FAC “contains allegations that directly contradict a judicially noticeable prior judgment. The stipulated judgment in the prior unlawful-detainer action conclusively established that possession was awarded to the landlord, the tenant's lease rights were forfeited, and vacatur was required by August 12, 2025. The First Amended Complaint alleges that Plaintiff retained possession and that the unlawful-detainer action was baseless-allegations that are factually and legally inconsistent with the judgment's operative terms.” (Mot. at p. 6:8-13.) Defendants argue the “Court should strike all allegations in the First Amended Complaint that contradict the judgment’s operative terms, including but not limited to statements that Plaintiff retained possession or that the unlawful-detainer was baseless.” (Mot. at p. 13:13-15.) First, nowhere does Defendants address which causes of action are barred by the prior proceeding nor do the Defendants clarify where the Court should look in the FAC for such allegations. Second, “[a] demurrer must dispose of an entire cause of action to be sustained.” (Fremont Indemnity Co. v. Fremont General Corp. (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 97, 119.) This request appears incompatible with the instant motion type.

The Court addresses the remaining arguments as to each cause of action in turn.

A. First Cause of Action (Violation of Los Angeles County Rent Stabilization & Tenant Protections Ordinance (LA County Code Ch. 8.52.)

There is no private cause of action under Los Angeles County Code Chapter 8.52. Instead, “The Department is authorized to take any and all appropriate steps it deems necessary to enforce this Chapter.” (Chapter 8.52.145, emphasis added.) Section 8.52 only allows for enforcement by county counsel.

Accordingly, Defendants’ demurrer to the first cause of action is SUSTAINED WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND.

B. Second Cause of Action (Unlawful Rent Increase and Unauthorized Fees)

Plaintiff brings this cause of action for unlawful rent increase and unauthorized fees pursuant to Los Angeles County Code, Chapter 8.52 and Civil Code, section 827. As discussed above, Chapter 8.52 does not create a private cause of action. Therefore, Plaintiff’s cause of action is based solely on Civil Code, section 827. 

However, Civil Code, section 827, contains no express or implied private right of action. A statute does not create a private cause of action absent legislative intent. (Lu v. Hawaiian Gardens Casino, Inc. (2010) 50 Cal.4th 592, 597.) Civil Code, section 827, merely makes any change set forth in a notice, as outlined by the section, a part of the lease when a tenant remains in possession of the premises after such notice becomes effective. A violation of this section does not create a new cause of action itself. Instead, this section is a defensive mechanism to avoid collections of improperly noticed rent.

 Accordingly, Defendants’ demurrer to the second cause of action is SUSTAINED WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND. 

C. Third and Seventh Causes of Action (Breach of the Implied Warranty of Habitability and Breach of the Covenant of Quiet Enjoyment)

Calderon demurs to the third and seventh causes of action arguing that as a disclosed agent of the landlord, she is immune to contractual obligations. The Court agrees.

Both causes of action arise out of the contractual relationship (the lease agreement) between Plaintiff and defendant Sanchez. The implied warranty of habitability and quiet enjoyment are not a separate tort duty owed to¿tenants,¿but a contractual obligation implied by law in every residential lease. (Green v. Superior Court¿(1974) 10 Cal.3d 616, 637;¿Allstate Ins. Co. v. Loo¿(1996) 46 Cal.App.4th 1794, 1798; Andrews v. Mobile Aire Estates (2005) 125 Cal.App.4th 578, 588, [“every lease contains an implied covenant of quiet enjoyment.”]) 

However, “the agent defendants may not be held liable under the implied warranty theory, causes of action may be stated against them in tort. [¶] Since the suit for breach of the implied warranty is essentially a contractual one, the trial court correctly ruled the agents could not be held liable on the breach of warranty because an agent is ordinarily not liable on the contract when he acts on behalf of a disclosed principal.” (Stoiber v. Honeychuck (1980) 101 Cal.App.3d 903, 928.) Indeed, Plaintiff’s opposition confirms that the “First Amended Complaint alleges that Calderon exercised actual control over the tenancy, maintenance, rent collection, tenant communications, and the conduct at issue.” (Opp. at p. 6:23-25.) Therefore, Calderon was a known agent acting on behalf of a disclosed principal. These causes of action are therefore improperly pled against Calderon and fail as a matter of law. 

Calderon’s demurrer to the third and seventh cause of action is SUSTAINED WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND.

D. Fourth Cause of Action (Retaliatory Eviction / Retaliatory Acts (Civil Code § 1942.5)

“[I]t is unlawful for a lessor to increase rent, decrease services, cause a lessee to quit involuntarily, bring an action to recover possession, or threaten to do any of those acts, for the purpose of retaliating against the lessee because the lessee has lawfully organized or participated in a lessees’ association or an organization advocating lessees’ rights or has lawfully and peaceably exercised any rights under the law. In an action brought by or against the lessee pursuant to¿this subdivision, the lessee shall bear the burden of producing evidence that the lessor’s conduct was, in fact,¿retaliatory.”¿(Civ. Code, §¿1942.5, subd. (d).)¿

Defendants argue Plaintiff “fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action due to temporal defects.” (Mot at p. 19:9-10.)  

Here, Plaintiff alleges that, “[b]eginning in or about 2019, and continuing thereafter, Plaintiff made repeated complaints to Defendants regarding serious habitability defects, including infestations, mold, water intrusion, and broken locks. Plaintiff also demanded repairs in writing, including by certified letter on or about January 6, 2025, which placed Defendants on notice of their statutory obligations.” (FAC ¶68.) “In retaliation for Plaintiff’s lawful exercise of her rights, Defendants engaged in a pattern of retaliatory conduct, including but not limited to: (1) increasing Plaintiff’s monthly rent by $80 without legal authority in December 2023; (2) imposing an unauthorized late fee in January 2024; (3) rejecting Plaintiff’s partial rent tender in March 2025 made in good faith due to continuing habitability violations; (4) serving an inflated and unlawful Three Day Notice to Pay Rent or Quit on March 11, 2025; and (5) filing a baseless unlawful detainer action on March 28, 2025, alleging that Plaintiff owed “two years’ rent,” despite having accepted regular payments and Plaintiff having obtained rental assistance.” (FAC ¶69.)

These allegations do not have temporal defects. For example, Plaintiff alleges that “Plaintiff also demanded repairs in writing, including by certified letter on or about January 6, 2025.” (FAC ¶68.) Within a couple months, Defendants retaliated by “serving an inflated and unlawful Three Day Notice to Pay Rent or Quit on March 11, 2025; and (5) filing a baseless unlawful detainer action on March 28, 2025, alleging that Plaintiff owed “two years’ rent,” despite having accepted regular payments and Plaintiff having obtained rental assistance.” (FAC ¶69.) These allegations properly support a cause of action for retaliation in violation of Civil Code, section 1942.5.

Defendants make a further argument that “[a]llegations regarding conduct outside the 180-day window should be stricken or moved to a separate common-law retaliation claim with appropriate temporal allegations.” (Mot. at p. 13-15.) However, “[a] demurrer must dispose of an entire cause of action to be sustained.” (Fremont Indemnity Co. v. Fremont General Corp., supra, 148 Cal.App.4th at p. 119.) A demurrer would be improper for this requested relief.

Accordingly, Defendants’ demurrer to the fourth cause of action is OVERRULED.

E. Fifth Cause of Action (Negligence Per Se)

Defendants argue that the fifth cause of action “should be sustained on demurrer as a non-cognizable cause of action.” (Mot. at p. 21:6.) However, negligence is a recognized cause of action. 

In opposition, Plaintiff concedes that “that Evidence Code section 669 generally codifies negligence per se as an evidentiary doctrine or presumption that may establish duty and breach within a negligence claim.” (Opp. at p. 7:18-20.) Plaintiff argues that “even if the negligence per se cause of action is technically mislabeled, the factual allegations support a viable negligence/ statutory violation theory… Accordingly, the demurrer should be overruled or, alternatively, Plaintiff should be granted leave to amend to restate this cause of action as ordinary negligence with negligence per se pleaded as a theory” (Opp. at pp. 7:23-8:2.) The best course of action, which appears agreeable to both parties, is to sustain the demurrer, and allow Plaintiff to clarify her cause of action. 

Accordingly, Defendants’ demurrer to the fifth cause of action is SUSTAINED. Plaintiff is given 30-days leave to amend.

F. Sixth Cause of Action (Private Nuisance)

When a nuisance claim is based on negligence, it is treated as a negligence claim rather than a distinct cause of action. (El¿Escorial Owners’¿Ass’n¿v. DLC Plastering, Inc., (2007) 154 Cal.App.4th 1337, 1349.)¿In¿Melton,¿the¿court¿sustained a demurrer to a nuisance claim because it merely restated the party’s negligence¿claims,¿“using a different label.” (Melton v. Boustred (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 521, 543.)¿¿¿ 
¿¿ 
Here, to support her cause of action for private nuisance, Plaintiff¿alleges: “(1) chronic rat and roach infestations that persisted despite repeated complaints and extermination efforts; (2) mold and dampness caused by water intrusion; (3) broken and unsafe window locks; (4) nonfunctional and rodent contaminated appliances, including the oven and refrigerator, rendering the kitchen unsafe for food preparation and storage; (5) diversion of common-area electricity and water-heating systems to Plaintiff’s utility account, unlawfully increasing Plaintiff’s utility expenses; (6) denial of access to the driveway without legal justification; and (7) extended periods of displacement and loss of use of the kitchen during incomplete and delayed repair efforts.” (FAC ¶85.) Plaintiff’s private nuisance claim therefore merely restates Plaintiff’s negligence claim with a different label. 
¿¿ 
Accordingly, Defendants’ demurrer to the sixth cause of action is SUSTAINED. Generally, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy¿(1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.)¿While¿this cause of action is only available if¿Plaintiff¿withdraws¿their claim for¿negligence,¿Plaintiff could amend their FAC to successfully allege this cause of action¿instead. Plaintiffs must demonstrate such desire at the hearing if they seek leave to amend.¿ 

G. Eighth Cause of Action (Defamation (Slander per se))

Slander per se requires: (1) a false and unprivileged publication; (2) orally uttered to a  
person; and (3) naturally tending directly to injure a person, in respect to office, profession,  
trade, or business (slander per se), or special damages. (Mann v. Quality Old Time Service, Inc, (2004) 120 Cal.App.4th 90, 106, disapproved on other grounds by Baral v. Schnitt (2016) 1 Cal.5th 376, 392.) 

Here, Plaintiff does not sufficiently allege slander per se. 

Plaintiff alleges: “[s]pecifically, Manager told neighbors and others that Plaintiff was “behind on rent” and “owed two years’ rent,” despite Manager’s acceptance of payments and initiation of litigation over disputed amounts, and further asserted that Plaintiff kept dogs at the Property “without authorization” and that the dogs were “vicious,” while falsely claiming a lack of knowledge of the dogs’ presence.” (FAC ¶100.) “Plaintiff suffered reputational harm, humiliation, emotional distress, and loss of community standing.” (FAC ¶104.) 

Slander per se is actionable without proof of special damages (Gonzalez v. Fire Ins. Exchange¿(2015) 234 Cal.App.4th 1220, 1240), however, Plaintiff must sufficiently alleged harm to her in her occupation or other unique circumstances inapplicable here. (Civ Code, §46; Regalia v. The Nethercutt Collection¿(2009) 172 Cal.App.4th 361, 369.) These statements “must be evaluated to determine if “upon their face” they “clearly convey a meaning” that fits within subdivision (3) of Civil Code section 46, and therefore, if defamatory, are slander per se.” (Id. at p. 369-370, internal citations omitted.) These statements do not clearly convey a meaning that is actionable under Civil Code, section 46. 

Accordingly, Defendants’ demurer to the eighth cause of action is SUSTAINED.

Generally, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Ibid.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.) Whether leave to amend is granted will depend on whether Plaintiff can make an adequate offer of proof at the hearing that a successful amendment is reasonably possible.

H. Ninth Cause of Action (Violation of Business and Professions Code, Section 17200)

Business and Professions Code, section 17200 prohibits “any unlawful, unfair or fraudulent business act or practice.” (Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200; see Clark v. Superior Court (2010) 50 Cal.4th 605, 610.) A violation of other laws is deemed independently actionable under the UCL. (See Law Offices of Mathew Higbee v. Expungement Assistance Services (2013) 214 Cal.App.4th 544, 554.) “Virtually any law, federal, state or local, can serve as a predicate for a section 17200 action.” (Ibid. (quoting Troyk v. Farmers Group, Inc. (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1305, 1335).) To establish a fraudulent practice under the UCL, the plaintiff must show that members of the public are likely to be deceived. (See West v. JP Morgan Chase Bank, N.A. (2013) 214 Cal.App.4th 780, 806.) “A business practice is unfair within the meaning of the UCL if it violates established public policy or if it is immoral, unethical, oppressive or unscrupulous and causes injury to consumers which outweighs its benefits.” (McKell v. Washington Mutual, Inc. (2006) 142 Cal.App.4th 1457, 1473.) Further, statutory claims must be pled with particularity. (Carter v. Prime Healthcare Paradise Valley LLC (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 396, 410.) A cause of action for unfair competition “is not an all-purpose substitute for a tort or contract action.” (Cortez v. Purolator Air Filtration Products Co. (2000) 23 Cal.4th 163, 173.) 

Here, Plaintiff alleges, “Defendants engaged in unlawful, unfair, and fraudulent business practices in the management and operation of the Property. Such practices included, without limitation: (1) failing to register the Property with the Los Angeles County Rent Stabilization Program in violation of Los Angeles County Code Chapter 8.52; (2) imposing unlawful rent increases and unauthorized late fees in violation of Civil Code section 827 and Los Angeles County Code sections 8.52.040 and 8.52.060; (3) interfering with and diverting Plaintiff’s utilities in violation of Civil Code section 789.3; (4) maintaining the Property in uninhabitable and unsafe condition in violation of Civil Code section 1941.1; (5) retaliating against Plaintiff for exercising her lawful rights in violation of Civil Code section 1942.5; and (6) filing an unlawful detainer action based on inflated and unlawful rent demands, thereby misusing judicial process for improper purposes.” (FAC ¶108.) 

These allegations are deficient. All violations are merely restatements of prior causes of action. Plaintiff is attempting to use this cause of action as a “all-purpose substitute for a tort or contract action” instead of alleging an independent cause of action. (Cortez v. Purolator Air Filtration Products Co., supra, 23 Cal.4th at p. 173.) Therefore, the complaint does not allege sufficient facts to support a cause of action for violation of Business and Professions Code, section 17200. 

Accordingly, Defendants’ demurer to the ninth cause of action is SUSTAINED.

Generally, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Ibid.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.) Whether leave to amend is granted will depend on whether Plaintiff can make an adequate offer of proof at the hearing that a successful amendment is reasonably possible.

Motion to Strike
  
I. Legal Standard  
  
A motion to strike lies either (1) to strike any irrelevant, false or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or (2) to strike any pleading or part thereof not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule or order of court. (Code Civ. Pro § 436.)  

II.  Analysis  
  
Defendants seek to strike four categories of allegations in the FAC. As a result of the concurrent demurrer, the request to strike: (1) the fifth cause of action; and (2) relief sought under the UCL cause of action, are MOOT.

Defendants have two remaining requests to strike: (1) “Allegations Contradicted by Judicially Noticeable Court Records;” and (2) “All Prayers for Prospective Injunctive Relief.”

However, nowhere does Defendants address which allegations are barred by the prior proceeding nor do the Defendants clarify where the Court should look in the FAC for such allegations. “Where the motion to strike is based on matter of which the court may take judicial notice . . . such matter shall be specified in the notice of motion, or in the supporting points and authorities, except as the court may otherwise permit.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437, subd. (b), emphasis added.) “A notice of motion to strike a portion of a pleading must quote in full the portions sought to be stricken except where the motion is to strike an entire paragraph, cause of action, count, or defense. Specifications in a notice must be numbered consecutively.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1322(a).) By failing to comply with the Rules of Court, Defendants’ motion fails to identify what Defendants seek to strike from the FAC.

Accordingly, Defendants’ motion to strike is DENIED.

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Case Number: 26STCV07352    Hearing Date: July 10, 2026    Dept: 509

First Legal Buyer, Inc. v. Strategic Legal Practices, APC

DEMURRER TO CROSS-COMPLAINT
 

MOVING PARTY: Plaintiff/Cross-Defendant, First Legal Buyer, Inc.

RESPONDING PARTY(S): Defendant/Cross-Complainant Strategic Legal Practices, APC 

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:
Plaintiff First Legal Buyer, Inc. is a litigation support services company. First Legal Buyer, Inc., alleges that law firm Strategic Legal Practices, APC failed to pay more than $4 million for services rendered under a contract. First Legal Buyer, Inc., alleges causes of action for: (1) breach of contract; (2) open book account; (3) account stated; and (4) quantum meruit.

Strategic Legal Practices, APC (Cross-Complainant) filed a cross-complaint against First Legal Buyer, Inc., (Cross-Defendant) alleging that the unpaid invoices result from Cross-Defendant’s breach of the agreement. Cross-Complainant alleges causes of action for: (1) breach of contract; (2) breach of the covenant of good faith and fair dealing; (3) intentional misrepresentation; (4) negligent misrepresentation; (5) professional negligence; and (6) equitable accounting.

Cross-Defendant now demurs to the second, third, and fifth causes of action in the cross-complaint. Cross-Complainant opposed.

TENTATIVE RULING:

Cross-Defendant’s demurrer to the third cause of action (intentional misrepresentation) is OVERRULED.

Cross-Defendant’s demurrer to the second and fifth causes of action is SUSTAINED.

Whether leave to amend is granted will depend on whether Cross-Complainant can make an adequate offer of proof at the hearing that a successful amendment is reasonably possible.

Moving party is ordered to give notice, unless waived.

DISCUSSION:

Demurrer to Cross-Complaint

I. Meet and Confer
The Declaration of attorney Thomas Dreblow reflects that the meet and confer requirement was satisfied. 

II. Legal Standard

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (Taylor v. City of Los Angeles Dept. of Water and Power (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 1216, 1228.) In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or by proper judicial notice. (Code Civ. Pro. § 430.30, subd. (a).) A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. (SKF Farms v. Superior Court (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 902, 905.) Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed. (Ibid.) The only issue involved in a demurrer hearing is whether the complaint, as it stands, unconnected with extraneous matters, states a cause of action. (Hahn, at p. 747.) “We treat the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Serrano v. Priest (1971) 5 Cal.App.3d 584, 591.) A “demurrer does not, however, admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law alleged in the pleading, or the construction of instruments pleaded, or facts impossible in law.” (S. Shore Land Co. v. Petersen (1964) 226 Cal.App.2d 725, 732 (internal citations omitted).)  

III. Analysis

Cross-Defendant demurs to the cross-complaint on grounds that the second, third, and fifth causes of action fail to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. (Not. of Mot.)

1. Second Cause of Action (Breach of Good Faith and Fair Dealing)

“A breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing involves something beyond breach of the contractual duty itself and it has been held that bad faith implies unfair dealing rather than mistaken judgment.” (Careau & Co. v. Security Pacific Business Credit, Inc. (1990) 222 Cal.App.3d 1371, 1394.) “If the allegations do not go beyond the statement of a mere contract breach and, relying on the same alleged acts, simply seek the same damages or other relief already claimed in a companion contract cause of action, they may be disregarded as superfluous as no additional claim is actually stated … [T]he only justification for asserting a separate cause of action for breach of the implied covenant is to obtain a tort recovery.” (Id. at pp. 1394-1395.) To recover in tort for breach of the implied covenant, the defendant must “have acted unreasonably or without proper cause.” (Id. at p. 1395, citations and italics omitted.) 

Here, Plaintiff alleges, “SLP paid all of FLB’s invoices as proscribed per the terms of the Agreement from 2017 through September 2025, when it discovered FLB’s erroneous invoicing.” (Cross-Compl. ¶40.) “FLB’s erroneous invoicing and subsequent refusal to rectify such improper billing prevented SLP from receiving the benefits under the contract.” (Cross-Compl. ¶41.) “By doing so, FLB did not act fairly and in good faith.” (Cross-Compl. ¶42.) Because Cross-Complainant’s allegations do not go beyond the statement of a mere contractual breach and simply relies on the same acts and seeks the same damages already claimed in the breach of contract cause of action, they may be disregarded as superfluous as no additional claim is stated. (Cross-Compl. ¶¶34-36; see Careau & Co. v. Security Pacific Business Credit, Inc., supra, 222 Cal.App.3d at p. 1394.)

Accordingly, Cross-Defendant’s demurrer to the second cause of action is SUSTAINED.

Generally, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Ibid.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.) Whether leave to amend is granted will depend on whether Cross-Complainant can make an adequate offer of proof at the hearing that a successful amendment is reasonably possible.

2. Third Cause of Action (Intentional Misrepresentation)

Cross-Defendant demurs to the third cause of action for intentional misrepresentations on the grounds that Cross-Complainant has not alleged facts with the required specificity that would allow for recovery under this cause of action.

The elements of fraud are: “(a) misrepresentation (false representation, concealment, or nondisclosure); (b) knowledge of falsity (or ‘scienter’); (c) intent to defraud, i.e., to induce reliance; (d) justifiable reliance; and (e) resulting damage.” (Charnay¿v.¿Cobert¿(2006) 145 Cal.App.4th 170, 184.)¿In California, fraud, including misrepresentation, must be pled¿with specificity. (Small v. Fritz Companies, Inc.¿(2003) 30 Cal.4th 167, 184.) “The particularity demands that a plaintiff plead facts which show how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were tendered.” (Cansino¿v. Bank of America¿(2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 1462, 1469.)¿¿ 

Cross-Complainant alleges, “FLB consistently represented to SLP that it had performed and was performing the Services as outlined in their invoices, per the rates defined in the Rate Cards, pursuant to the Agreement.” (Cross-Compl. ¶45.) “In actuality, FLB’s invoices systemically contained dubious billings for unauthorized expenses.” (Id. at ¶46.) “When FLB invoiced SLP, FLB either knew that the invoices it submitted contained erroneous charges at the time FLB submitted them, or was reckless as to the generation of those invoices.” (Id. at ¶48.) “FLB intended that SLP rely on its misrepresentations and erroneous invoices.” (Id. at ¶49.) Cross-Complainant provides one example of a fraudulent invoice. (Id. at ¶¶23-26.) In short, Cross-Complainant alleges “many of the invoices FLB had issued had one or more billing defects, including unauthorized service categories, unverifiable surveillance charges, fabricated billing categories, and payments of unauthorized charges including appearance fees for both the plaintiff and defendant in certain matters.” (Id. at ¶17.) 

These allegations are sufficient because the “how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were tendered” is sufficiently alleged. (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 645.) While Cross-Defendant is correct that the allegations do not include any “First Legal employee, officer, manager, or representative who allegedly made any misrepresentation,” the Court still finds the allegations sufficient. (Mot. at p. 7:9-10.) The precise identities of the natural persons who prepare the invoices need not be alleged, as such matters presumptively lie more within Cross-Defendant’s knowledge. (Tenet Healthsystem Desert, Inc. v. Blue Cross of California (2016) 245 Cal.App.4th 821, 837-38; see also West v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A. (2013) 214 Cal.App.4th 780, 793; Boschma v. Home Loan Center, Inc. (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 230, 248, [“While the precise identities of the employees responsible ... are not specified in the loan instrument, defendants possess the superior knowledge of who was responsible for crafting these loan documents.”]) Therefore, Cross-Complainant has sufficiently alleged a cause of action for intentional misrepresentation.

Accordingly, Cross-Defendant’s demurrer to the third cause of action is OVERRULED.

3. Fifth Cause of Action (Professional Negligence)

“The elements of a cause of action for professional negligence are (1) the existence of the duty of the professional to use such skill, prudence, and diligence as other members of the profession commonly possess and exercise; (2) breach of that duty; (3) a causal connection between the negligent conduct and the resulting injury; and (4) actual loss or damage resulting from the professional negligence.” (Oasis West Realty, LLC v. Goldman (2011) 51 Cal.4th 811, 821.) 

Here, Cross-Complainant alleges, “FLB had a duty to use such skill, prudence and diligence as other legal service providers commonly possess and exercise. FLB breached its duty by failing to monitor and control the propriety of its invoices, and failing to properly track charges that it would force SLP to incur.” (Cross-Compl. ¶¶61-62.)

 However, the Cross-Complaint does not allege Cross-Defendant failed to exercise any skill, prudence, or diligence as other members of this profession commonly possess and exercise. Instead, this appears to be duplicative of the breach of contract action as any duty to provide accurate billing statements would be rooted in the agreement between the parties, not in an independent professional duty to abide by the terms of the agreement. In opposition, Cross-Complainant cites no authority that demonstrates a duty for Cross-Defendant to “monitor and control the propriety of its invoices.” (Cross-Compl. ¶62.) Instead, Cross-Complainant argues “that FLB failed to perform services that it represented it had performed at all, and in many cases, performed professional services that SLP had not authorized or requested, including surveillance and unauthorized rush filing.” (Opp. at p. 4:5-7.) This is merely the allegations supporting a breach of contract in a new cause of action. 

Accordingly, Cross-Defendant’s demurrer to the fifth cause of action for professional negligence is SUSTAINED.

Generally, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Ibid.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.) Whether leave to amend is granted will depend on whether Cross-Complainant can make an adequate offer of proof at the hearing that a successful amendment is reasonably possible.

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Case Number: 26STCV08809    Hearing Date: July 10, 2026    Dept: 509

Safeway Insurance Company, et al. v. Dong Eun Kim

DEFENDANT’S MOTION FOR LEAVE TO FILE CROSS-COMPLAINT
 

MOVING PARTY: Defendant Dong Eun Kim

RESPONDING PARTY(S): None

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

Plaintiffs Safeway Insurance Company and Karen Pisciotto (Pisciotto, collectively, Plaintiffs) bring this action against Dong Eun Kim (Defendant) for property damage and personal injury sustained in a motor vehicle accident. Plaintiffs bring causes of action for: (1) negligence; and (2) motor vehicle liability. 

Defendant now moves for leave to file a cross-complaint. No opposition was filed.

TENTATIVE RULING:

Defendant’s motion for leave to file a Cross-Complaint is GRANTED.

The standalone Cross-Complaint must be filed within 10-days and timely served on all parties as required by law.  

Moving party is ordered to give notice, unless waived.

DISCUSSION:

Motion for Leave to File Cross-Complaint

Defendant moves for leave to file a cross-complaint against Pisciotto. (Cho Decl. ¶2, Exh. A.) Defendant’s proposed cross-complaint will assert a cause of action for negligence alleging that, in fact, Pisciotto was at fault for the motor vehicle accident at issue in this action. (Ibid.)

Cross-claims against complainants arising from the same accident, existing at the time of filing an answer, are compulsory. (See, e.g., Code Civ. Pro. §426.30, subd. (a); Al Holding Co. v. O’Brien & Hicks, Inc. (1999) 75 Cal.App.4th 1310, 1313-14.) Leave to file compulsory cross-complaints must be granted where moving parties acted in good faith. (Code Civ. Pro. § 426.50; Silver Organizations, Ltd. v. Frank (1990) 217 Cal.App.3d 94, 98-99.) The “principle of liberality requires that a strong showing of bad faith be made in order to support a denial of the right to file a cross-complaint under this section.” (Foot's Transfer & Storage Co. v. Superior Ct. (1980) 114 Cal.App.3d 897, 902 [emphasis added].) “The legislative mandate is clear. A policy of liberal construction of section 426.50 to avoid forfeiture of causes of action is imposed on the trial court. A motion to file a cross-complaint at any time during the course of the action must be granted unless bad faith of the moving party is demonstrated where forfeiture would otherwise result.” (Silver Organizations, Ltd. v. Frank (1990) 217 Cal.App.3d 94, 98-99.)

Here, because the complaint and proposed cross-complaint are based on the same incident (the motor vehicle accident) the cross-complaint is compulsory. Therefore, the opposing party must make a strong showing of bad faith to oppose Defendant’s request for leave. By failing to oppose, no party has made such showing.

The Court need not address the merits of the proposed cross-complaint at this time. Rather, the “better course of action” is to permit the filing “and then let the parties test its legal sufficiency in other appropriate proceedings,” such as by demurrer or motion to strike. (Atkinson v. Elk Corp. (2003) 109 Cal.App.4th 739, 760.) 

Accordingly, Defendant’s motion for leave to file a Cross-Complaint is GRANTED.

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Case Number: BC710638    Hearing Date: July 10, 2026    Dept: 509

Hsiang Ling Liou v. Anthony De La Sota, et al.

DEFENDANT’S MOTION FOR ATTORNEY’S FEES, COSTS, AND EXPENSES
  

MOVING PARTY: Defendant/Cross-Complainant Anthony De La Sota

RESPONDING PARTY(S): None

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

Plaintiff Hsiang Ling Liou (Plaintiff) brought this action against defendant Anthony De La Sota (Defendant) for: (1) nuisance, (2) trespass, and (3) negligence. Plaintiff and Defendant own adjacent real properties in Los Angeles. Plaintiff alleged that Defendant’s property utilizes a concrete walkway, roof eaves, and an air conditioning unit, that encroach on Plaintiff’s Property. Plaintiff asked Defendant to remove the encroachments so that Plaintiff can construct a new fence on the property line for Plaintiff’s Property and develop her property with a new house; Defendant refuses.

On November 21, 2023, Plaintiff filed a Notice of Settlement of Entire Case.

On April 18, 2025, Defendant moved to enforce the settlement and enter judgment pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure, section 664.6. Plaintiff opposed. The Court granted Defendant’s motion and entered judgment pursuant to the settlement agreement.

On June 13, 2025, Plaintiff filed an appeal challenging the final judgment.

The Court of Appeals dismissed the appeal and on April 28, 2026, issued the remittitur.

On June 8, 2026, Defendant filed this instant motion to recover their reasonable attorney’s fees and costs on appeal. Plaintiff did not file an opposition.  Plaintiff was properly served, via email, with this motion through his attorneys of record.  (See, POS, 6/8/26;  see also, Notice of Change of Firm Address, 1/7/26)
  
TENTATIVE RULING:

Defendant’s Motion for Attorney’s Fees is GRANTED in the amount of $9,263.50.

Defendant’s Motion for Costs is GRANTED in the amount of $470.44.

Defendant may file and serve a proposed amended judgment, consistent with this ruling.

Defendant is ordered to give notice.  

DISCUSSION: 

Motion for Attorney’s Fees, Costs, and Expenses

I. Analysis

A. Entitlement to Fees 

On November 21, 2023, the parties entered into a settlement agreement. This Agreement stated: “If there is litigation or arbitration regarding this Agreement, the prevailing Party will be entitled to recover reasonable attorneys’ fees and costs.” (Kassouni Decl., Exh. 1, ¶ 7.3)

 Further, the Court of Appeal stated that “Respondent(s) shall recover costs on appeal.” (See 4/28/26 Remittitur (B347229) at p. 1.) Thus, the Court is bound to follow the Court of Appeal’s determination on this subject.¿ (Benson v. Greitzer (1990) 220 Cal.App.3d 11; Ramirez v. St. Paul Fire & Marine Ins. Co. (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 473.)

Accordingly, Defendant is entitled to an award of reasonable attorney fees and costs.  

B. Reasonableness of Fees

The determination of reasonable amount of attorney fees is within the sound discretion of trial courts. (PLCM Group v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095; Akins v. Enterprise Rent-A-Car Co. (2000) 79 Cal.App.4th 1127, 1134.) “The determination of what constitutes a reasonable fee generally ‘begins with the ‘lodestar,’ i.e., the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate….’”  “[T]he lodestar is the basic fee for comparable legal services in the community; it may be adjusted by the court based on factors including, as relevant herein, (1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, (4) the contingent nature of the fee award….” (Graciano v. Robinson Ford Sales, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 140, 154.) In setting the hourly rate for an attorney fees award, courts are entitled to consider the rate of “‘fees customarily charged by that attorney and others in the community for similar work.’” (Bihun v. AT&T Information Systems, Inc. (1993) 13 Cal.App.4th 976, 997 [affirming rate of $450 per hour], overruled on other grounds by Lakin v. Watkins Associated Indus. (1993) 6 Cal.4th 644, 664.) The burden is on the party seeking attorney fees to prove reasonableness of the fees. (Center for Biological Diversity v. County of San Bernardino (2010) 188 Cal.App.4th 603, 615.)
 
The Court has broad discretion in determining the amount of a reasonable attorney’s fee award which will not be overturned absent a “manifest abuse of discretion, a prejudicial error of law, or necessary findings not supported by substantial evidence.” (Bernardi v. County of Monterey (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 1379, 1393-94.) The Court need not explain its calculation of the amount of attorney’s fees awarded in detail; identifying the factors considered in arriving at the amount will suffice. (Ventura v. ABM Industries Inc. (2012) 212 Cal.App.4th 258, 274-75.)

Here, Defendant seeks $9,263.50 in attorney fees as well as costs in the amount of $470.44. Although, upon a cursory review, the claimed fees appear to be somewhat excessive, by failing to oppose, Plaintiff has not formally challenged the reasonableness of Defendant’s attorney’s fees or costs.  It was their burden to do so. (See, e.g., Lunada Biomedical v. Nunez (2014) 230 Cal.App.4th 459, 488 [“[I]t is the burden of the challenging party to point to the specific items challenged [within the moving party’s verified billing invoice], with a sufficient argument and citations to evidence”].)

Accordingly, Defendant’s Motion for Attorney’s Fees is GRANTED, as requested, in the amount of $9,263.50. 

Defendant’s Motion for Costs is GRANTED in the amount of $470.44.

IT IS SO ORDERED.
