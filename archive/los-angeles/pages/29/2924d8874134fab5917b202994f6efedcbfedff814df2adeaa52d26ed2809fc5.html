            [TENTATIVE] ORDER: The Court DENIES Moving Defendant’s Motion for Summary Judgment.

Background

This is a medical malpractice case. On October 12, 2022, Plaintiff Anthony Sanchez was severely injured when his girlfriend drove her car into him, pinning his legs against a second, stationary vehicle. Plaintiff was admitted to Moving Defendant Providence Holy Cross Medical Center for treatment for his injuries. Plaintiff was treated by multiple doctors, including Non-Moving Defendant Ramin Ganjianpour.

Plaintiff underwent multiple separate surgeries during his admission to Providence Holy Cross. First, on October 13, 2022, doctors performed a femoral bypass graft on Plaintiff’s left knee in an attempt to restore blood flow to Plaintiff’s foot. On October 18, 2022, doctors once again operated on Plaintiff’s left leg. Then, on October 22, 2022, doctors “revascularized” Plaintiff’s left leg, as it was still not receiving enough blood flow. Finally, on October 23, 2022, doctors performed a vascular bypass surgery on Plaintiff’s left leg. Unfortunately, the blood flow to Plaintiff’s leg was not restored. As a result, it had to be amputated. Moving Defendant Valle was present for multiple operations on Plaintiff, including his initial admission to the emergency room.

On January 2, 2024, Plaintiff brought suit against Moving Defendant Providence Holy Cross Medical Center, and six other doctors who treated him. Plaintiff alleges a single cause of action for professional negligence.

Now, Moving Defendant Providence Holy Cross Medical Center moves for summary judgment as to this claim of professional negligence.

            Discussion 

Moving Defendant Providence Holy Cross Medical Center moves for summary judgment, or in the alternative, summary adjudication as to this claim of professional negligence. (MSJ, pp. 7:10-8:10.) 

In analyzing a motion for summary judgment, courts must, 1) determine whether the moving party has negated the opponent's claims; and 2) determine whether the opposition has demonstrated the existence of a triable, material factual issue.” (Hinesley v. Oakshade Town Center (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 289, 294.) Thus, summary judgment is granted when, after the Court’s consideration of the evidence set forth in the papers and all reasonable inferences accordingly, no triable issues of fact exist, and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. (CCP § 437(c); Villa v. McFarren(1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 733, 741.) 

i)               Moving Defendant’s Initial Burden

Moving Defendant makes three arguments in an attempt to rebut Plaintiff’s claim of medical malpractice. First, Moving Defendant argues that there is no dispute of fact as to whether the treatment rendered by their nurses or employees fell below the standard of care. (MSJ, pp. 19:27-20:6.) Second, Moving Defendant argues there is no dispute of fact as to whether their nurses or employees caused Plaintiff’s injuries. (Id. p. 22:1-9.) Third, Moving Defendant argues that they cannot be held liable for the actions of the doctors who treated Plaintiff because these doctors were all independent contractors. (Id. pp. 22:19-23:4.)

a)     Standard of Care and Causation

In support of these first two arguments, Moving Defendant provides the declaration of Alexis Chaudron, a registered nurse, and expert on the topic of the relevant standard of care for hospital staff in the state of California. (Chaudron Decl.) When a plaintiff alleges professional negligence, whereby the defendant is accused of failing to adhere to accepted standards of practice, the issues of standard of care and causation may be established only by qualified expert testimony. (Stevenson v. Kaiser Foundation Hosp. (1962) 203 Cal.App.3d 631 [emphasis added].) This is a professional negligence case. (Compl. p. 1.) Therefore, Stevenson applies.

Here, Moving Defendant’s expert establishes her credibility as an expert on this subject, and concludes that, to a reasonable degree of medical probability, Moving Defendant’s staff both complied with the relevant standard of care, and did not cause Plaintiff’s injuries. (Chaudron Decl. ¶¶ 44, 45.)

Notably, Plaintiff does not dispute this argument in his Opposition. Plaintiff states that, “Plaintiff does not contend that the nurses or non-physicians were negligent. Rather, as alleged in the Complaint, Plaintiff’s theory of liability against the hospital is based on the negligence of its physician-agents, including Dr. Ramin Ganjianpour.” (Opp. p. 1:21-24.) Given that Plaintiff has conceded the issue of Moving Defendant’s nurses and employees, the outcome of this motion rests entirely on Moving Defendant’s independent contractor argument.

b)    Independent Contractor Liability

Next, Moving Defendant argues that they cannot be held liable for the actions of the doctors who treated Plaintiff because these doctors were all independent contractors. (Id. pp. 22:19-23:4.) In support of this third argument, Moving Defendant provides the declaration of Sonia Ramos. Ms. Ramos works for Moving Defendant as the Senior Manager for Medical Staff Services. (Ramos Decl. ¶ 1.) Ms. Ramos explains that Moving Defendant does not employ any physicians. (Id. ¶ 3.) Ms. Ramos also states that none of the physicians who treated Plaintiff, including Dr. Ganjianpour, were Moving Defendant’s employees. (Id. ¶ 5.)

Next, Moving Defendant preemptively argues that Plaintiff cannot raise an argument for “ostensible agency” for these physicians. Under CIV § 2300, “[a]n agency is ostensible when the principal intentionally, or by want of ordinary care, causes a third person to believe another to be his agent who is not really employed by him.” (CIV § 2300.) In order to raise an argument for ostensible agency, a plaintiff must establish two elements: “(1) conduct by the hospital that would cause a reasonable person to believe that the physician was an agent of the hospital, and (2) reliance on that apparent agency relationship by the plaintiff.” (Mejia v. Community Hospital of San Bernadino (2002) 99 Cal.App.4th 1448, 1453.)

As to this first element, “a hospital is generally deemed to have held itself out as the provider of care, unless it gave the patient contrary notice.” (Id. p. 1454 [emphasis added].) As to the second element, “reliance . . . is established when the plaintiff ‘looks to’ the hospital for services, rather than to an individual physician . . . However, reliance need not be proven by direct testimony . . . In fact, many courts presume reliance, absent evidence that the plaintiff knew or should have known the physician was not an agent of the hospital.” (Ibid. [emphasis added].) Thus, even though there are two elements, only one factual analysis is required here – namely, whether the patient had reason to know that the treating physicians were not agents of the hospital. (Ibid.)

Moving Defendant asserts that Plaintiff had reason to know that none of the physicians treating him were agents of the hospital because, on October 12, 2022, Plaintiff signed a Conditions of Admission form which stated that, “PHYSICIANS ARE INDEPENDENT CONTRACTORS: The undersigned recognizes that all physicians, physician assistants, and surgeons furnishing service to the patient, including the radiologist, pathologist, anesthesiologist, emergency room physician, physician assistants, and the like, are independent contractors and are not employees or agents of the hospital.” (Sep. Statement, Issue 3, ¶¶ 4, 5; see also, Exhibit List, Ex. JJ, p. 4.) This form was purportedly signed by Plaintiff on October 12, 2022. (Exhibit List, Ex. JJ, p. 6.)

Under California case law, clear and unequivocal Conditions of Admission language like this can establish that a patient had reason to know that their physicians were not agents of the hospital. (Wicks v. Antelope Valley Healthcare Dist. (2020) 49 Cal.App.5th 866, 883.) However, the patient must be able to understand and act on the information provided. (Wicks, supra, 884 [distinguishing Whitlow v. Rideout Memorial Hospital (2015) 237 Cal.App.4th 631, where a patient who signed a similar form was vomiting and in intense pain].) Here, Moving Defendant asserts that the language of the Conditions of Admission form is clear, and that Plaintiff was not incapacitated when he signed the form. Moving Defendant bases this assertion on the declaration of Sonia Ramos, who states that, “[o]n October 12, 2022, Mr. Sanchez signed the Conditions of Admission form, which acknowledged that he reviewed the form, and he was offered the opportunity to ask any questions.” (Ramos Decl. ¶ 6.)

Based on this evidence, the Court finds that Moving Defendant has met their initial burden to negate Plaintiff’s claim of medical negligence, as the evidence presented suggests that Plaintiff was given notice that his treating physicians were not agents of Moving Defendant. This means that Plaintiff’s ostensible agency theory fails.

ii)             Plaintiff’s Shifted Burden

The burden now shifts to Plaintiff to establish a triable issue of fact as to the issue of ostensible agency. In his Opposition, Plaintiff argues that: 1) the evidence of the signed Conditions of Admission form is inadmissible, as it was not properly authenticated; and 2) even if it were admissible, there is a dispute of fact as to whether he was incapacitated when he signed the form. (Opp. pp. 2:24-3:12.) 

a)     Admissibility of the Ramos Declaration

First, Plaintiff raises the issue of admissibility. Moving Defendant’s evidence that Plaintiff signed the Conditions of Admission form comes from the declaration of Sonia Ramos, an employee of Moving Defendant. Ms. Ramos states that, “[o]n October 12, 2022, Mr. Sanchez signed the Conditions of Admission form, which acknowledged that he reviewed the form, and he was offered the opportunity to ask any questions.” (Ramos Decl. ¶ 6.) Ms. Ramos asserted to have, “personal knowledge of all facts contained within this Declaration.” (Id. ¶ 2.)

Plaintiff argues that Ms. Ramos lacks the foundation to make these assertions. In support of this argument Plaintiff provides excerpts from the deposition transcript of Ms. Ramos. There, Ms. Ramos states that she was not physically present in the ER on the night that Plaintiff was admitted. (Opp. Compendium of Evidence, Ex. C, p. 28:20-24.) She also states that she does not personally know whether Plaintiff was presented with, or read, the Conditions of Admission form she authenticated. (Id. p. 29:3-22; see also, Id. p. 29:8-11 [“Q: Okay. And your statement that the form was presented before any services was rendered is not based on anything you personally observed, right?  A: Correct”].) Based on these deposition statements, it appears as though Ms. Ramos lacks the foundation to state how Plaintiff was presented with these documents when he was admitted to the ER, and that he was given an opportunity to ask questions about the form. Thus, this evidence is arguably inadmissible.

In their Reply, Moving Defendant asserts that this foundation argument, “conflates Ms. Ramos’ lack of personal recollection of Mr. Sanchez’s individual admission with the distinct question whether an electronically maintained hospital admission record may be authenticated through appropriate testimony concerning the hospital’s recordkeeping and admission practices.” (Reply, p. 9:8-11.) Moving Defendant also argues that Plaintiff is mistaken when he asserts that, because Ms. Ramos did not personally observe Plaintiff’s intake, she cannot testify to the fact that physicians are not typically employees of the hospital. (Id. p. 9:2-7.)

The Court agrees for the most part. Ms. Ramos, in her role as Moving Defendant’s Senior Manager for Medical Staff Services, has the foundation to state that, as a general rule, the physicians working for Moving Defendant are independent contractors. She also has the ability to authenticate electronically stored documents like the signed form presented here. However, she does not have the foundation to rebut the elements of an ostensible agency claim. The primary dispute in this motion is whether 1) Plaintiff was presented with clear notice that his physicians were not agents of the hospital, and 2) whether Plaintiff was able to understand the information provided. Given that Ms. Ramos was not present when Plaintiff first signed these forms, she could, at most, testify as to the date of the electronic signature on this form. She cannot, however, testify as to the circumstances of the signature. Thus, her statement that Plaintiff, “was offered the opportunity to ask any questions” cannot be used to establish that Plaintiff was capable of understanding the implications of this agreement. (Ramos Decl. ¶ 6.)

Thus, the Court agrees that Moving Defendant has failed to present admissible evidence showing that Plaintiff understood the information provided, as required under Wicks. 

b)    Dispute of Fact as to Plaintiff’s Incapacitation

Setting aside the issue of admissibility, Plaintiff also raises a dispute of material fact as to whether they were able to understand the Conditions of Admission language presented to them on October 12, 2022.

Under California case law, clear and unequivocal Conditions of Admission language like this can establish that a patient had reason to know that their physicians were not agents of the hospital. (Wicks v. Antelope Valley Healthcare Dist.(2020) 49 Cal.App.5th 866, 883.) However, the patient must be able to understand and act on the information provided. (Wicks, supra, p. 884.)

Here, Plaintiff provides multiple pieces of evidence that suggest he was unable to understand the information presented to him in the Conditions of Admission form. First, Plaintiff points to Exhibit E of Moving Defendant’s motion – a summary of his patient notes. There, it states that Plaintiff, “was given fentanyl in route and states he has no relief with that.” (Exhibit List, Ex. E, p. 152.) Additionally, Plaintiff points out that, shortly after he was admitted, he was administered 100 milligrams of ketamine. (Kvamme Depo. 36: 19-23; see also, Compendium of Evidence, Ex. E, p. 172.) Given the strong dose of pain meds Plaintiff was administered, it is possible that at the time he signed the Conditions of Admission form, he could not understand them, or even remember signing them. This argument is reinforced by Plaintiff’s own deposition testimony, where he was shown a copy of the Conditions of Admission form. Plaintiff stated on the record that he never received this document, did not sign it, and did not recognize the signature. (Opp. Ex. 2. pp. 96:17-97:7.) Taken together, these facts raise a dispute of material fact as to Plaintiff’s ability to understand the terms of the Conditions of Admission form. Thus, per Wicks, Moving Defendant cannot defeat Plaintiff’s claim of ostensible agency at the summary judgment stage.

In their Reply, Moving Defendant argues that this dispute as to the signing of the first form does not defeat their argument, because Moving Defendant signed more than a dozen other Consent for Surgery forms while admitted, all of which also contained similar “not agents” language. (Reply, p. 8:9-18.) As evidence of these forms, Moving Defendant submits the declaration of William Sulentor, who verifies and attaches multiple consent forms signed by Plaintiff, all of which include language clarifying that the physicians treating him are not Moving Defendant’s agents. (See generally, Sulentor Decl. Ex. A.) 

Normally, this would be very compelling evidence that Plaintiff had notice of his physicians’ relationship to Moving Defendant. However, this evidence was improperly brought via a Reply. Under CCP § 437c(b)(4), “[a] reply to the opposition [for a motion for summary judgment] shall be served and filed by the moving party not less than 11 days preceding the noticed or continued date of hearing, unless the court for good cause orders otherwise. The reply shall not include any new evidentiary matter, additional material facts, or separate statement submitted with the reply and not presented in the moving papers or opposing papers.” (CCP § 437c(b)(4) [emphasis added].) Moving Defendant’s initial motion did not provide these consent forms as evidence. (See generally, MSJ; see also, Exhibit List.) In fact, Moving Defendant’s separate statement only discussed the initial October 12, 2022, Conditions of Admission form. (Sep. Statement, Issue 3, ¶¶ 4-5.) Thus, this form is the only one that Moving Defendant has put forth as necessary to negate Plaintiff’s claim. 

When a moving party includes a "fact" in the separate statement, the moving party concedes that fact's materiality. (See, Nazir v. United Airlines, Inc. (2009) 178 Cal.App.4th 243, 252.) Thus, Moving Defendant has conceded that the validity of the initial Conditions of Admission form is material to the dispute in this case. Moreover, Moving Defendant cannot introduce new evidence of other relevant documents at this late stage to defeat Plaintiff’s argument. Thus, Moving Defendant’s argument fails.

iii)           Summary

In summary, Moving Defendant met their initial burden to rebut Plaintiff’s ostensible agency argument by establishing that they provided Plaintiff with actual notice of the fact that his physicians were not agents of Moving Defendant. Plaintiff then met their shifted burden by disputing the material facts raised by Moving Defendant, thereby undermining the validity of that notice. Thus, there is a triable issue of material fact as to Plaintiff’s ostensible agency argument.

For these reasons, the Court DENIES Moving Defendant’s Motion for Summary Judgment.

Conclusion

The Court DENIES Moving Defendant’s Motion for Summary Judgment.

            [TENTATIVE] ORDER: The Court GRANTS Plaintiff’s Motion for Attorney Fees, in the modified amount of $11,499.12.

 

Background

This is a lemon law case. Plaintiffs Jose Contreras and Isabel Laina allege that on February 27, 2022, they purchased a 2020 Ford F-150 that was manufactured by Defendant Ford Motor Company. Plaintiffs allege that the vehicle exhibited numerous defects that could not be cured after a reasonable number of repair attempts.

On October 18, 2024, Plaintiffs brought suit, alleging a single claim for Breach of Express Warranty under the Song-Beverly act.

On March 26, 2026, the parties in this case entered into a signed settlement agreement for $105,939.00 in damages.

Now, Plaintiff moves for an award of attorney fees.

            Discussion

Plaintiff requests a total of $37,135.87 in attorney fees and costs. (Mot. for Attorney Fees, p. 14:13.) Specifically, Plaintiff requests $22,231.50 in reasonable fees, $11,115.75 as a 1.5x lodestar multiplier, and $3,788.62 in costs. (Id. p. 14:10-13.)

Plaintiff bases their request for fees on fee-shifting provision of the Song-Beverly Act, which states that a prevailing buyer can, “recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution.” (Civ. Code § 1794(d).)

i)                   Fee Motion Analysis

“[T]he fee setting inquiry in California ordinarily begins with the ‘lodestar,’ i.e., the number of hours¿reasonably expended¿multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate. … The reasonable hourly rate is that prevailing in the community for similar work. The lodestar figure may then be adjusted, based on consideration of factors specific to the case,¿in order to¿fix the fee at the¿fair market value¿for the legal services provided.” ((PLCM Group, Inc. v. Drexler¿(2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095 [internal citations omitted]); (see¿Robertson v. Fleetwood Travel Trailers of California, Inc.¿(2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 785, 818 [applying the lodestar method to¿determine¿attorneys’ fees in Song-Beverly action].) Thus, the Court will analyze the reasonableness of:¿i) Plaintiffs’ lodestar multiplier request, ii) Plaintiffs’ hourly rates, iii) Plaintiffs’ hours, and iv) Plaintiffs’ costs.

A.    Lodestar Multiplier

First, the Court will analyze Plaintiffs’ request for a 1.5x lodestar multiplier ($11,115.75). While the baseline lodestar reflects the basic fee for comparable legal services in the community, it may be adjusted based on various factors, including “(1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, and the skill displayed in presenting them; (2) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys; (3) the contingent nature of the fee award[;]” and (4) the success achieved. (Serrano v. Priest (1977) 20 Cal.3d 25, 49.) “[A] trial court should award a multiplier for exceptional representation only when the quality of representation far exceeds the quality of representation that would have been provided by an attorney of comparable skill and experience billing at the hourly rate used in the lodestar calculation. Otherwise, the fee award will result in unfair double counting and be unreasonable.” (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1138-1139.)

The Court finds that the facts of this case don’t support the application of a multiplier. There is no indication that this lemon law case was complex or presented any challenging legal issues, particularly considering that Plaintiffs’ counsel specializes in these types of cases. Additionally, there is no evidence that Plaintiffs’ counsel was precluded from taking other cases because of the nature of this case. Lemon law firm take on these cases hundreds at a time, so the opportunity cost of taking on one more is negligible. Nor is the success achieved by Plaintiff’s counsel exceptional. The Court also notes that counsel’s skill and experience is also being factored into the reasonableness of their fees. Thus, it would be unreasonable to award an enhancement here. (See Holguin v. Dish Network LLC (2014) 229 Cal.App.4th 1310, 1333 [“Where, as here, the court determines that the lodestar itself constitutes a reasonable fee for the action at issue, no enhancement is warranted.”].)

For these reasons, the Court declines to grant this multiplier.

B.     Plaintiffs’ Hourly Rates

Second, the Court will evaluate the reasonableness of Plaintiffs’ hourly rates. Plaintiffs bill for the time of ten separate lawyers, a single paralegal, and an eleventh lawyer from a separate law firm. Their fee schedule is as follows:

(Kirnos Decl. Ex. A, p. 8.)

“[T]he court's discretion in awarding attorney fees is … to be exercised so as to fully compensate counsel for the prevailing party for services reasonably provided to his or her client.” (Horsford v. Board of Trustees of California State University (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 359, 395 [emphasis added].)  The trial court may reduce the award where the fee request appears unreasonably inflated, such as where the attorneys’ efforts are unorganized or duplicative. (Serrano v. Unruh (1982) 32 Cal.3d 621, 635, fn. 21.) Moreover, courts have repeatedly stated that the trial court is in the best position to value the services rendered by the attorneys in his or her courtroom, and this includes the determination of the hourly rate that will be used in the lodestar calculus. (East County Boulevard LLC v. Backcountry Against the Dump, Inc. (2016) 6 Cal.App.5th 426, 436-437.)

Here, the Court has determined that most of Plaintiffs’ requested rates are too high. The Court does not believe that high-volume, boilerplate lemon law cases like this one justify such high rates. The Court has seen enough suits brought under Song-Beverly to know that every paper filed in these cases, from the Complaint, to the discovery requests, to the Demurrer oppositions are copied and pasted from templates, with minimal personalization to each client. What’s more, the issues presented in this case were not novel or complex. Thus, there was no special skill or insight required to litigate this case. 

For similar reasons, the Court also takes issue with Plaintiffs charging significantly higher hourly rates for some of their more experienced lawyers. The logic for why a more senior lawyer can charge higher rates is that they will bring more knowledge to the table, work more efficiently, and produce better outcomes for their clients. That logic does not hold in this context. The only motion practice in this case beyond discovery motions (that were denied) was a Motion for Summary Judgment filed by the Defendant just four days before the case settled. Additionally, this case also did not involve any complex issues of law. Whatever documents that were filed were based on templates and needed next to no specific factual personalization. Thus, charging significantly higher rates is not reasonable, as this work could have been done by a much less experienced attorney. (See Mikhaeilpoor v. BMW of North America (2020) 48 Cal.App.5th 240, 256 [Court of Appeals held that it was not an abuse of discretion for the trial court to reduce the hourly rates of every lawyer in a lemon lawsuit to $350.00].)

            For these reasons, the Court reduces the hourly rates of Armando Lopez, Colby Meagle, Debra Reed, Jacob Cutler, Russel Higgins, Timothy Lupinek, Zachary Lodmer, and Zachary Powell to $350.

C.     Plaintiffs’ Hours Billed

Next, the Court will assess the reasonableness of the hours billed by Plaintiffs.

            The Song-Beverly Act, “requires the trial court to make¿an initial¿determination of the actual time expended; and then to¿ascertain¿whether under all the circumstances of the case the amount of actual time expended and the monetary charge being made for the time expended are reasonable. These circumstances may include, but are not limited to, factors such as the complexity of the case and procedural demands, the skill¿exhibited¿and the results achieved. If the time expended or the monetary charge being made for the time expended are not reasonable under all the circumstances, then the court must take this into account and award attorney fees in a lesser amount.” (Goglin v. BMW of North America, LLC¿(2016) 4 Cal.App.5th 462, 470 [quoting¿Nightingale v. Hyundai Motor America¿(1994) 31 Cal.App.4th 99, 104].)¿

 

Here, Plaintiffs bill for 54.7 hours of time, across 11 different lawyers, and a single paralegal. (Kirnos Decl. Ex. A, p. 8.) Plaintiffs also submit an 8-page billing record with over 150 individual billing entries. (Ibid.) By spreading their billing across so many lawyers and entries, Plaintiffs make it difficult for the Court to evaluate the reasonableness of each individual entry. “When a ‘voluminous fee application’ is made, the court may … ‘make across-the-board percentage cuts either in the number of hours claimed or in the final lodestar figure.’” (Kerkeles v. City of San Jose (2015) 243 Cal.App.4th 88, 102.) Moreover, “trial courts need not, and indeed should not, become green-eyeshade accountants. The essential goal in shifting fees (to either party) is to do rough justice, not to achieve auditing perfection. So trial courts may take into account their overall sense of a suit, and may use estimates in calculating and allocating an attorney's time.” (Fox v. Vice (2011) 563 U.S. 826, 838.)

             Here, Plaintiffs’ billings are voluminous, and thus are subject to across-the board cuts in hours. As explained, the Court’s overall sense of this suit is that it was a typical lemon law case that involved no serious motion practice beyond minor discovery disputes. Thus, this volume of billing across so many lawyers is unreasonable. For these reasons, the Court will reduces Plaintiffs’ requested hours by approximately 60%. The exact reductions are as follows:

D.    Plaintiffs’ Costs

Finally, the Court will evaluate Plaintiffs' request for $3,788.62 in costs. Under CIV § 1794(d), when a buyer prevails in a Song-Beverly action, “the buyer shall be allowed . . . to recover . . .  the aggregate amount of costs and expenses . . . determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.” (CIV § 1794(d).) Thus, Plaintiffs are entitled to recover any costs they reasonably incurred as part of this litigation.

Defendant argues that all of these costs should be denied because they are unrecoverable, and lack evidentiary support. (Opp. p. 15:2-3.) Specifically, Defendant argues that because Plaintiffs provide no explanation, invoice, or receipts for their claims, the Court cannot determine whether they were reasonably incurred. (Id. p. 15:13-18.)

In Reply, Plaintiffs argue that Defendant is attempting to improperly shift the burden of proving costs. (Reply, p. 10:6-14.) Plaintiffs then assert that, under Hadley v. Krepel, once costs are established by a verified memorandum, “the burden then shifts to the opposing party to show that the costs were unnecessary.” (Reply, p. 10:11-12 [pin cite to Hadley v. Krepel (1985) 167 Cal.App.3d 677, 682].) This is incorrect. According to Hadley, costs presented on a verified cost bill are, “prima facie evidence the costs, expenses and services listed were necessarily incurred, and when they are properly challenged the burden of proof shifts to the party claiming them as costs. (Hadley v. Krepel (1985) 167 Cal.App.3d 677, 682 [emphasis added].) Thus, the burden rests with Plaintiffs to justify their costs as reasonably incurred.

Notably, Plaintiffs do provide verified copies of their invoices and receipts for their requested costs. (See generally, Swanson Decl.) For example, Plaintiffs provide an invoice for $1,748.75 for two deposition transcripts, paid on January 9, 2026. (Id. Ex. A.) These costs match the costs requested in Plaintiffs’ original memorandum of costs. (Memo of Costs, ¶ 4.) Having reviewed these receipts and invoices, the Court is satisfied that these costs were reasonably incurred.

In their Opposition, Defendant pre-emptively argues that Plaintiffs should not be allowed to introduce this new evidence in their Reply, as it deprives Defendant of the opportunity to respond. (Opp. p. 15:19-26.) The Court disagrees. Plaintiffs filed their initial memorandum of costs in conjunction with this motion, which acted as “prima facie evidence” of their costs. (Hadley, supra, p. 682.) Defendant then challenged the validity of these costs in their Opposition. This shifted the burden back to Plaintiffs to provide evidence of these costs. (Ibid.) Thus, the evidence provided by Plaintiffs in their Reply is the end result of Defendant challenging the validity of these costs. For this reason, the Court finds it is proper to consider this evidence.

For these reasons, the Court declines to strike any of Plaintiffs’ $3,788.62 in costs.

E.     Summary

In summary, the Court declines to apply a 1.5 lodestar multiplier to this case. Additionally, the Court reduces the hourly rate of most of Plaintiffs’ attorneys to $350. Next, the Court reduces the total hours billed by Plaintiffs from 54.7 to 25.4 hours. Based on Plaintiffs adjusted hours and hourly rates, the Court recalculates Plaintiffs’ reasonable attorney fees at $7,710.50. Finally, the Court grants Plaintiffs’ request for $3,788.62 in costs.

In total, the Court GRANTS Plaintiffs’ Motion for Attorney Fees in the modified amount of $11,499.12.

Conclusion

The Court GRANTS Plaintiff’s Motion for Attorney Fees, in the modified amount of $11,499.12.

            [TENTATIVE] ORDER:

 

Background

This is a lemon law case. Plaintiffs Angela Pesnilyan and Macrown Transportation, Inc. allege that on March 5, 2024, they purchased a vehicle manufactured by Moving Defendant General Motors, LLC. Plaintiffs allege that the vehicle exhibited numerous defects that could not be cured after a reasonable number of repair attempts.

On December 30, 2024, Plaintiffs filed suit, alleging multiple violations of the Song Beverly Act.

On December 4, 2025, the parties settled this case out of court.

On May 29, 2026, Plaintiffs filed two memoranda of costs, one for each law firm they retained for this case.

On June 9, 2026, Moving Defendant General Motors LLC filed a motion to tax costs as to both memoranda.

            Discussion

Moving Defendant moves to tax the costs requested by both of Plaintiffs’ law firms. (Mot. to Tax, p. 2:2-11.)

i)                   Procedural Issues

Before the Court can analyze this motion on the merits, it must address two threshold procedural issues.

First, Moving Defendant argues that Plaintiffs’ motion is untimely. (Id.  p. 3:3-11.) Under CRC Rule 3.1700, “[a] prevailing party who claims costs must serve and file a memorandum of costs within 15 days after the date of service of the notice of entry of judgment or dismissal by the clerk under Code of Civil Procedure section 664.5 or the date of service of written notice of entry of judgment or dismissal, or within 180 days after entry of judgment, whichever is first.” (CRC Rule 3.1700(a)(1).)

Moving Defendant argues that because dismissal was requested on December 11, 2025, that Plaintiffs’ May 29, 2026, filing is untimely. (Mot. to Tax, p. 2:9-11.) The Court disagrees. The fifteen day timer under CRC Rule 3.1700 does not start until service of notice of entry of judgment, not the request for dismissal. Moving Defendant provides no evidence that this service actually occurred. (Mot. to Tax, p. 2:2-11.) Having reviewed the docket, the Court finds that this case was not dismissed until April 7, 2026. (Minute Order 4/7/26.) Further, there is no indication that the clerk served this dismissal on Plaintiffs. Thus, the 180-day timer applies. This means that Plaintiffs’ memoranda, filed May 29, 2026, are timely.

Second, Plaintiffs argue that Moving Defendant’s motion is procedurally improper because it does not contain a notice of the hearing for the motion. Under CRC Rule 3.1112, “[u]nless otherwise provided by the rules in this division, the papers filed in support of a motion must consist of at least the following: (1) A notice of hearing on the motion.” (CRC Rule 3.1112(a)(1).) Here, Moving Defendant’s motion is limited to the motion itself. (See generally, Mot. to Tax.)

The Court agrees that Moving Defendant should have included this notice of the hearing as a separate part of this filing. However, the Court also notes that Moving Defendant’s papers substantially comply with the purpose of the notice requirement, as the first page indicates the date, time, and location of the hearing on the motion. Additionally, Plaintiffs were able to file a full Opposition to this motion, meaning they have not been prejudiced by this minor procedural error. For these reasons, the Court will consider the motion on the merits.

ii)                 The Merits of the Motion to Tax Costs

Moving Defendant moves to tax $1,789.47 of Plaintiffs’ $2,322.32 requested costs. (Mot.to tax, p. 2:1-4.)

a)      Costs are Awardable

First, the Court must determine whether costs are awardable. Plaintiff in this case is the prevailing party in a Song-Beverly action. Under CIV § 1794(d), “[i]f the buyer prevails in an action under this section, the buyer shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses.” (CIV § 1794(d).) Thus, costs are awardable here.

b)      Analysis

Next, the Court will analyze the motion itself. Typically, in a ruling on a motion to tax costs, the trial court’s first determination is whether the statute expressly allows the particular item and whether it appears proper on its face; if so, the burden is on the objecting party to show the costs to be unnecessary and unreasonable. Where costs are not expressly allowed by statute, the burden is on the party claiming the costs to show that the charges were reasonable and necessary. (Foothill-De Anza Comm College Dist. v Emerich (2007) 158 Cal.App.4th 11, 29.) In other words, the Court has the power to disallow costs that are “allowable as a matter of right” when they are not “reasonably necessary.” (Perko’s Enterprises Inc. v. RRNS Enter. (1992) 4 Cal.App.4th 238, 245.)

However, the case at hand is a Song-Beverly case. This means the controlling statute is CIV § 1794(d). Under CIV § 1794(d), “[i]f the buyer prevails in an action under this section, the buyer shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs . . . determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.” (CCP § 1794(d).) Thus, the only analysis this Court must engage in is whether the costs requested were “reasonably incurred.”

As Plaintiffs point out in their Opposition, "A verified memorandum of costs is prima facie evidence of the propriety' of the items listed on it, and the burden is on the party challenging these costs to demonstrate that they were not reasonable or necessary." (Adams v. Ford Motor Co. (2011) 199 Cal.App.4th 1475, 1486.) However, “if the items appearing in a cost bill appear to be proper charges, the burden is on the party seeking to tax costs to show that they were not reasonable or necessary. On the other hand, if the items are properly objected to, they are put in issue and the burden of proof is on the party claiming them as costs.” (Melnyk v. Robledo (1976) 64 Cal.App.3d 618, 624.) Here, Moving Defendant has raised multiple objections to Plaintiff’s requested costs. Thus, the burden is on Plaintiff to establish that these costs were reasonably incurred.

1)      Wirtz Law Costs

Moving Defendant objects to all $1,454.72 in costs requested by Plaintiffs’ counsel Wirtz Law. (Mot. to Tax, p. 4:11-12.)

First, Moving Defendant objects to Plaintiffs’ request for $60 for the filing fee associated with Plaintiffs’ Motion for Attorney Fees. (Id. p. 4:14-16.) At the time this motion to strike was filed, Plaintiffs had not yet filed their Motion for Attorney Fees. However, it has now been filed. Filings are required of every litigant in a case, as documents need to be filed with this Court. Thus, these are standard fees that should be expected of litigation and are therefore reasonably incurred. Additionally, the Court notes that filing costs are allowable under CCP § 1033.5(a)(1). While this statute does not control in the instant motion, the inclusion of filing fees suggests that California courts presume these costs as reasonably incurred. Additionally, Plaintiffs provide copies of bills and invoices for these costs. (Rotman Decl. Exs. 2-7.) For these reasons, the Court finds these costs reasonably incurred.

Second, Moving Defendant objects to Plaintiffs’ request for $625 for an anticipated court reporter fee for the upcoming motion for attorney fees. (Mot. to Tax, p. 4:17-20.) While the Motion for Attorney Fees has not been heard yet, the Court recognizes the need for attorneys to use court reporters to establish a record for their substantive motions. Additionally, the Court notes that court reporter fees are allowable costs under CCP § 1033.5(a)(11). While this statute does not control in the instant motion, the inclusion of court reporter fees suggests that California courts presume these costs as reasonably incurred. For these reasons, the Court finds these cost to be reasonably incurred for now. However, if the Court determines after the October hearing on this motion that this cost was not, in fact, incurred, the Court will modify this order.

Third, Moving Defendant objects to Plaintiffs’ request for $426.50 in electronic filing fees. (Mot. to Tax, p. 4:21-26.) The Court disagrees. Electronic filings are required of every litigant in a case, as documents need to be filed with this Court. Thus, these are standard fees that should be expected of litigation and are therefore reasonably incurred. Additionally, the Court notes that filing costs are allowable under CCP § 1033.5(a)(14). While this statute does not control in the instant motion, the inclusion of e-filing fees suggests that California courts presume these costs as reasonably incurred. Additionally, Plaintiffs provide copies of bills and invoices for these costs. (Rotman Decl. Exs. 2-7.) That said, Plaintiffs in their Opposition agree to reduce these costs by $64.70 for an un-incurred e-filing cost related to one of the memoranda of costs. (Opp. p. 6:2-4.) Thus, the Court will strike these requested costs by $64.70. The remaining costs are found to have been reasonably incurred.

Fourth, Moving Defendant objects to Plaintiffs’ request for $343.22 in “other” costs, explained as $280 in courtesy copies, and $63.22 for “research”. (Mot. to Tax, pp. 4:27-5:3.) The Court finds these costs were reasonably incurred. This Court requires parties to file courtesy copies, thus these costs were reasonably incurred. Additionally, Plaintiffs provide an invoice they received from Westlaw for research they conducted when researching the Motion to Enforce Settlement that was filed, but later withdrawn in this case. (Rotman Decl. Ex. 7.) Based on this evidence, the Court finds these costs were reasonably incurred.

In summary, the Court GRANTS the Motion to Tax as to the Wirtz Law Memorandum of Costs in the amount of $64.70.

2)      Norman Taylor and Associates Costs

Next, Moving Defendant objects to $334.75 in costs requested by Plaintiffs’ counsel Normal Taylor and Associates. (Mot. to Tax, p. 5:6-7.)

            First, Moving Defendant objects to Plaintiffs’ request for $150 in jury fee costs. (Id. p. 5:8-13.) Moving Defendant argues that because no jury was called in this matter, this cost was not reasonably incurred. (Ibid.) Moving Defendant cites to Perko’s Enterprises Inc. v. RRNS Enterprises in support of this argument. (Ibid.)

The Court disagrees. To begin, Defendant misstates the law. Perko’s in no way holds that jury fees are recoverable only when a trial actually occurs. In fact, the only mention of “jury fees” in Perko’s is in a footnote that quotes the following language from CCP § 1033.5(a): “[t]he following items are allowable as costs under Section 1032: "(1) Filing, motion, and jury fees.” (Perko’s Enterprises Inc. v. RRNS Enterprises (1992) 4 Cal.App.4th 238, fn. 5.) Thus, Defendant’s misleading citation holds no weight here. Additionally, under CCP § 631, jury fees must be paid by the first case management conference in the action. (CCP § 631.) Thus, if Plaintiff wished to be entitled to a jury trial, they had to pay this fee early in the case’s lifespan. Therefore, the fee was reasonably necessary for the litigation of the case. For these reasons, the Court declines to tax these costs.

Second, Moving Defendant objects to Plaintiffs’ request for $85.07 in service of process costs. (Mot. to Tax, p. 5:14-19.) Moving Defendant argues that the cost of serving a subpoena on a non-party dealership Casa De Cadillac did nothing to advance this case. (Ibid.) The Court disagrees. Dealership Casa De Cadillac is a named Defendant in this case. Additionally, service of process costs are listed in CCP § 1033.5 as recoverable costs. (CCP § 1033.5(a)(4).) While this statute does not control in the instant motion, the inclusion of service fees suggests that California courts presume these costs are reasonably incurred. Thus, the Court declines to strike these costs.

Third, Moving Defendant objects to Plaintiffs’ request for $99.68 in electronic filing fees. (Mot. to Tax, p. 5:20-25.) As explained above, the Court notes that electronic filings are required of every litigant in a case, as documents need to be filed with this Court. Thus, these are standard fees that should be expected of litigation and are therefore reasonably incurred. Additionally, the Court notes that filing costs are allowable under CCP § 1033.5(a)(14). While this statute does not control in the instant motion, the inclusion of e-filing fees suggests that California courts presume these costs as reasonably incurred. Thus, the Court Declines to strike these costs.

In summary, the Court DENIES the Motion to Tax as to the Norman Taylor & Associates Memorandum of Costs.

Conclusion

The Court GRANTS the Motion to Tax as to the Wirtz Law Memorandum of Costs in the amount of $64.70. The Court finds all other costs reasonably incurred.

            [TENTATIVE] ORDER: The Court TENTATIVELY GRANTS Cross-Defendant’s Anti-SLAPP Motion to Strike the Cross-Complaint. The Court TENTATIVELY GRANTS Cross-Defendant’s request for $3,365.02 in costs.

 

Background

This is a FEHA case. Plaintiff/Cross-Defendant Mia Camillah alleges that on September 26, 2024, she applied to live at a property owned and controlled by Defendant/Cross-Complainant A.K. Group, LLC. Cross-Defendant alleges that she qualifies for Section 8 Housing Choice Voucher rental assistance. Cross-Defendant further alleges that Cross-Complainant discriminated against Cross-Defendant by denying her application due to her status as a Section 8 recipient.

On March 12, 2026, Cross-Defendant filed suit, alleging five causes of action: 1) Fair Employment and Housing Act, Cal. Gov’t Code § 12955 subdivs. (a), (d), (g) and (k); 2) Unruh Civil Rights Act, Cal. Civ. Code § 51 et seq.; 3) Negligence, Cal. Civil Code §1714; 4) Los Angeles Municipal Code, Chapter IV, Article 5.6.1; and 5) Unfair Business Practices, Business and Professions Code § 17200.

On May 14, 2026, Defendant (now Cross-Complainant) filed a Cross-Complaint against Plaintiff (now Cross-Defendant). The Cross-Complaint raises claims of both negligent and intentional misrepresentation. Specifically, Cross-Complainant alleges that Cross-Defendant misrepresented her Section 8 qualifications so that she could file a lawsuit against Cross-Complainant.

Now, Cross-Defendant brings this Anti-SLAPP Motion against Cross-Complainant’s Cross-Complaint.

            Discussion

Cross-Defendant moves to strike both claims raised in Cross-Complainant’s Cross-Complaint under CCP § 425.16.

Anti-SLAPP Analysis

CCP § 425.16 (the anti-SLAPP statute) is “a mechanism through which complaints that arise from the exercise of free speech rights can be evaluated at an early stage of the litigation process and resolved expeditiously.” (Simmons v. Allstate Ins. Co. (2001) 92 Cal.App.4th 1068, 1073 [internal quotations omitted].) Courts use a two-prong approach to determine whether an action is a strategic lawsuit against public participation, or a SLAPP. First, the court determines whether the defendant has established that the challenged claim arises from protected activity. (Equilon Enterprises v. Consumer Cause, Inc. (2002) 29 Cal.4th 53, 67.) Second, the court, “determines whether the plaintiff has demonstrated a probability of prevailing on the claim.” (Ibid.)

i)                   Prong One: Arising from Protected Activity

The first question this Court must answer is whether Cross-Complainant’s claims arises from protected activity. To satisfy this first step, Cross-Complainant must show two things: a) that the defendants engaged in protected activity; and b) that the causes of action arose from that protected activity. (Colyear v. Rolling Hills Community Assn. of Rancho Palos Verdes (2017) 9 Cal.App.5th 119, 130.)

a)      Did Cross-Defendant Engage in Protected Activity?

Under CCP § 425.16(b)(1), an Anti-SLAPP motion applies to, “[a] cause of action against a person arising from any act of that person in furtherance of the person's right of petition or free speech under the United States Constitution.” (CCP § 425.16(b)(1) [emphasis added].) Under California case law, "[t]he filing of a lawsuit is an exercise of the First Amendment right to petition the government." (Bonni v. St Joseph Health System (2021) 11 Cal.5th 995, 1024; see also Navellier v. Sletten (2002) 29 Cal.4th 82, 90.) Thus, Cross-Defendant engaged in protected activity when she filed the underlying FEHA claim against Cross-Complainant.

b)      Do Cross-Complainant’s Claims Arise out of that Protected Activity?

Next, the Court concludes that Cross-Complainant’s claims for negligent and intentional misrepresentation arise out of this protected activity.

Under California case law, "[t]he filing of a lawsuit is an exercise of the First Amendment right to petition the government. Consequently, claims that arise out of the filing of a suit arise from protected activity for purposes of the anti-SLAPP statute." (Bonni v. St Joseph Health System (2021) 11 Cal.5th 995, 1024.)

Here, Cross-Defendant’s nominal infraction was that they allegedly misrepresented their Section 8 status to Cross-Complainants. (Cross-Complaint ¶¶ 14-15, 20-21.) However, Cross-Defendant alleges that, “Cross-Defendant made said representation with the intent to induce Cross-Complainant to respond in a manner that could later be used to support a claim of housing discrimination.” (Cross-Complaint ¶ 10.) Later, Cross-Complainant also alleges that, “Cross-Defendant made said representations with the intent to induce Cross-complainant's to act or respond in reliance thereon. So she could immediately file a lawsuit against cross complainant causing damage to cross complainant's reputation and causing cross complainant to incur costs and attorney fees.” (Id. ¶ 21.) The specific language of these allegations – “with the intent to induce . . . so she could immediately file a lawsuit” – is crucial, as they allege that the act of making a misrepresentation was done in contemplation of bringing the underlying lawsuit. Thus, these claims of misrepresentation arise out of the filing of Cross-Defendant’s lawsuit.[1]

In Opposition, Cross-Complainant argues that because the misrepresentations at issue were made before Cross-Defendant brought the underlying suit, these counter claims are independent from the judicial proceedings, and thus do not arise from protected activity. (Opp. p. 5:17-28.) The Court disagrees with this argument for multiple reasons. First, Cross-Complainant provides no case law in support of this argument. (Opp. p. 5:17-28.) Second, as discussed above, Cross-Complainant specifically alleges that Cross-Defendant made these misrepresentations with the intention of filing a lawsuit afterwards. (See e.g. Cross-Complaint ¶ 21.) Thus, as alleged, these actions were taken in anticipation of engaging in litigation.

c)      Summary

In summary, both of Cross-Defendant’s claims arise out of protected activity.

ii)                 Prong Two: Likely to Succeed on the Merits

Now, the burden shifts to Cross-Defendant to show that both of these actions have merit. Once the burden shifts, the “[non-moving party] must demonstrate that the complaint is both legally sufficient and supported by a sufficient prima facie showing of facts to sustain a favorable judgment if the evidence submitted by the plaintiff is credited.” (Premier Medical Management Systems, Inc. v. California Ins. Guarantee Assn. (2006) 136 Cal.App.4th 464, 476 [internal quotations omitted].) In making the prong two determination, “the court shall consider the pleadings, and supporting and opposing affidavits stating the facts upon which the liability or defense is based.” (CCP § 425.16(b)(2).)

In their Opposition, Cross-Complainant provides only the sworn declaration of Niloo Bedrood, the owner of Cross-Complainant AK Group LLC. (Opp. Bedrood Decl.) Mr. Bedrood states that he received contradictory information from Cross-Defendant. Specifically, Mr. Bedrood states that, “Plaintiff represented that she qualified for Section 8 housing assistance.” (Id. ¶ 8.) However, Mr. Bedrood also states that, “I reviewed a Zillow rental inquiry in which Plaintiff disclosed annual income of approximately $87,500. Based upon that information, I concluded Plaintiff's stated income appeared inconsistent with eligibility for Section 8 housing assistance.” (Ibid.) Thus, Cross-Complainant has provided sworn evidence that Cross-Defendant misrepresented their Section 8 assistance status. This suggests that Cross-Complainant’s claims have merit.

In their Reply, Cross-Defendant objects to this statement by Mr. Bedrood, asserting it is an improper lay opinion, lacks foundation, and is irrelevant. (Evid. Objections, p. 3:16-25.) The Court overrules these objections. Mr. Bedrood is the owner of Defendant AK Group LLC, an apartment rental business. Thus, it follows that Mr. Bedrood would be familiar with Section 8 vouchers and their requirements. This assumption is further evidenced by Mr. Bedrood’s sworn statement that he has previously rented to other Section 8 tenants. (Bedrood Decl. ¶ 7.) Thus, the Court does not find this statement to be an improper lay opinion or lacking in foundation.

That being said, Cross-Complainant’s declaration does raise a separate issue as to damages. Damages are a necessary element of any claim for intentional or negligent misrepresentation. (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal. 4th 631, 638; West v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A. (2013) 214 Cal.App.4th 780, 792.) Here, Cross-Complainant has alleged that as a result of Cross-Defendant’s misrepresentations, they “sustained damages according to proof.” (Cross-Complaint ¶ 18.) More specifically, Cross-Complainant alleges that, “As a direct and proximate result of Cross-Defendant's conduct, Cross-Complainant has been forced to incur damages, including but not limited to costs of defending this action, attorney's fees, and other related expenses.” (Id. ¶ 12.)

In their Reply, Cross-Defendant argues that these allegations and evidence show that Cross-Complainant has not suffered any damages. (Reply. p. 5:17-25.) The Court is inclined to agree. Costs and fees related to litigation are not recoverable as damages, absent a statute or contractual provision. (Kummeth v. Atkisson (1913) 23 Cal.App. 401.) Cross-Complainant provides no evidence or allegations of either. (See generally, Cross-Complaint.) Moreover, Mr. Bedrood states that, “Plaintiff never submitted a rental application for the subject property.” (Bedrood Decl. ¶ 9.) If there was never a rental application for Cross-Complainant to review, it is unclear what incidental damages Cross-Complainant suffered as a result of these misrepresentations.

Additionally, Mr. Bedrood’s statement that, “Plaintiff never submitted a rental application for the subject property” conflicts with their allegation that, “Cross-Complainant relied on Cross-Defendant's representations in evaluating her application and in responding to her inquiries.” (Bedrood Decl. ¶ 9; Cross-Complaint ¶ 22.) Thus, it is also unclear how Cross-Complainant can establish reasonable reliance, a necessary element of both intentional and negligent misrepresentation. (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal. 4th 631, 638; West v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A. (2013) 214 Cal.App.4th 780, 792.) Therefore, Cross-Complainant appears to be unable to establish multiple elements of both of their claims as a matter of law.

In their Opposition, Cross-Complainant argues that this issue is not relevant because, “whether additional damages are ultimately recoverable is a question for later proceedings and is not properly resolved on an anti-SLAPP motion.” (Opp. p. 7:12-13.) This argument misses the point. The second prong of an Anti-SLAPP analysis analyzes the merit of a potential claim. In this case, the existence of damages is a required element of both challenged claims. Thus, it is Cross-Complainant’s burden to show that they have evidence or sufficient allegations to support this element.

Therefore, the Court’s tentative conclusion is that Cross-Complainant cannot establish that they were damaged by Plaintiff’s misrepresentations. However, the Court intends to ask Cross-Complainants for clarification on this issue at the hearings. If Cross-Complainants can explain how they were harmed beyond the inconvenience and costs of being taken to court, the Court may exercise its discretion and continue the hearing on this motion to allow for further evidentiary briefing.

For these reasons, the Court TENTATIVELY GRANTS Cross-Defendant’s Anti-SLAPP Motion to Strike the Cross-Complaint.

Costs

Next, Cross-Defendant requests $3,365.02 in costs associated with this motion. (Anti-SLAPP, pp. 6:23-7:9.)

A prevailing defendant on a special motion to strike “shall be entitled” to recover

attorney’s fees and costs. (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16(c).) The award is mandatory: “[A]ny SLAPP defendant who brings a successful motion to strike is entitled to mandatory attorney fees.” (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1131.) Given that this Court has tentatively granted Cross-Defendant’s motion, an award of costs is mandatory.

            In Opposition, Cross-Complainant does not challenge Cross-Complainant’s costs in any way. (See generally, Opp.) Therefore, the Court GRANTS Cross-Defendant’s request for $3,365.02 in costs.

Conclusion

The Court TENTATIVELY GRANTS Cross-Defendant’s Anti-SLAPP Motion to Strike the Cross-Complaint. The Court TENTATIVELY GRANTS Cross-Defendant’s request for $3,365.02 in costs.

Background

This is a habitability, stalking, and eviction case arising out of an unlawful detainer case. Plaintiff April Johnson alleges that she entered into an oral lease agreement with Defendant David Levi to rent an ADU from him for $2,700 a month. Plaintiff alleges that, after she moved in, Defendant began to sexually harass and stalk Plaintiff. Defendant’s alleged behavior is extensive. Defendant allegedly frequently commented on Plaintiff’s appearance, sent her sexual or suggestive text messages, and attempted to coerce Plaintiff into using his shower by shutting off her hot water. Defendant also allegedly withheld Plaintiff’s mail in order to force her into face-to-face discussions. Additionally, Defendant allegedly drilled a hole in Plaintiff’s bathroom wall in order to spy on her. In December of 2025, Plaintiff filed a police report against Defendant based on these intrusions. Plaintiff alleges that, in retaliation for her filing this report, Defendant filed an unlawful detainer suit against Plaintiff in an attempt to evict her.

            On March 26, 2026, Plaintiff filed suit against Defendant, alleging ten causes of action: 1) intrusion into private affairs; 2) constructive eviction; 3) breach of the implied covenant of quiet enjoyment; 4) retaliatory eviction; 5) malicious prosecution; 6) violation of Los Angeles Municipal Code Article 5.3 (tenant anti-harassment ordinance); 7) violation of CIV § 789.3 – interruption of utility services; 8) violation of CIV § 1940.2 – threats or menacing conduct; 9) violation of CIV § 1708.7 – stalking; and 10) intentional infliction of emotional distress.

            On April 20, 2026, Defendant filed a general denial.

            On June 2, 2026, Plaintiff filed a motion to strike this general denial, due to lack of proof of service.

            On June 22, 2026, Defendant filed a demurrer to the entirety of Plaintiff’s Complaint based on res judicata.

            Now, the Court hears both motions.

            Discussion

Motion to Strike General Denial

First, Plaintiff moves to strike Defendant’s general denial, filed on April 20, 2026. (See, General Denial, p. 1.) Plaintiff correctly points out that Defendant’s filing includes no proof of service. (Ibid.)

Proof of service is required for a general denial under both CCP § 465, as well as per the text of the form itself, which states, “[t]he original of this General Denial must be filed with the clerk of this court with proof that a copy was served on each plaintiffs attorney and on each plaintiff not represented by an attorney.” (Ibid [emphasis added]; see also CCP § 465.)

Notably, Defendant does not oppose this motion. (Notice of Non-Opposition; see also, Supp. Cohen Decl. ¶ 4.) Thus, Defendant has failed to file a proof of service with their general denial.

For these reasons, the Court GRANTS Plaintiff’s Motion to Strike Defendant’s general denial.

Demurrer

Next, Defendant demurs to the entirety of Plaintiff’s Complaint, arguing that the claims are barred under theories of res judicata and claim preclusion. (Demurrer, p. 2.)

i)                   Meet and Confer

First, the Court notes that Defendant has failed to meet and confer properly. Before filing a demurrer, the demurring party shall meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer. (CCP § 430.41(a) [emphasis added].) A declaration must be filed with a demurrer regarding the results of the meet and confer process. (CCP § 430.41(a)(3).)

Here, Defendant provides no meet and confer declaration whatsoever. (See generally, Demurrer.) Thus, Plaintiff has failed to meet and confer properly over this motion.

Generally, the Court would be inclined to continue this motion and order the parties to meet and confer before proceeding. However, the Court will opt to hear this motion on the merits.

ii)                 Proof of Service

Additionally, the Court notes that there is a dispute as to the proof of service for this demurrer. Defendant has attached proof of service to this demurrer, dated June 22, 2026. (Demurrer, p. 5.) However, Plaintiff asserts in their Opposition that they never received this proof of service and that they have yet to receive any documents whatsoever from Defendant. (Opp. p. 4, fn. 1; see also, Cohen Decl. ¶ 3.) Plaintiff explains that they have been following the filings in this case by checking the Court’s docket daily and by purchasing filings from the Court website. (Cohen Decl. ¶ 3.)

            Given that this proof of service issue arose as part of Plaintiff’s Motion to Strike Defendant’s general denial, the Court is interested in getting to the bottom of why Plaintiff has received no documents from Defendant, despite there being proof of service. The Court intends to use the hearing on this motion to ask both parties about this issue. If necessary, the Court will order the parties to meet and confer further.

iii)               Analysis of the Merits

Finally, the Court will address the merits of Defendant’s demurrer. Defendant demurs to the entirety of Plaintiff’s Complaint, arguing that the claims are barred under theories of claim and issue preclusion. (Demurrer, p. 2.)

In support of this argument, Defendant points to three recent hearings: Case No. 25VERO02C50 (Demurrer, Ex. A); Case No. 26VERO00029 (Demurrer, Ex. B); and Case No. 26VERO00162 (Demurrer, Ex. C). All three of these cases were based on the same underlying facts, and were dismissed with prejudice. (Demurrer, Exs. A-C.) Notably, Defendant engages in no analysis as to how claim or issue preclusion applies beyond pointing out these factual similarities. (Id. p. 3:8-27.)

The Court does not find that the existence of these cases gives rise to a claim or issue preclusion defense. In order to assert that claim preclusion bars these claims, Defendant must establish that, “[the] second suit involves (1) the same cause of action (2) between the same parties (3) after a final judgment on the merits of the first suit.” (DKN Holdings LLC v. Faerber (2015) 61 Cal.4th 813, 824 [emphasis added].) Similarly, “issue preclusion applies (1) after final adjudication (2) of an identical issue (3) actually litigated and necessarily decided in the first suit and (4) asserted against one who was a party in the first suit or one in privity with that party.” (Id. at p. 825 [emphasis added].)

 Here, none of these cases involve the “same causes of action” or “an identical issue”. The three cases cited by Defendant were civil harassment petitions brought under CCP § 527.6. (Demurrer, Exs. A-C.) These claims sought restraining orders against Defendant, not monetary relief. (Ibid.) By contrast, Plaintiff’s Complaint alleges ten civil causes of action, ranging from stalking to wrongful eviction. (Compl. p. 1.) Plaintiff seeks monetary damages from these claims, not a restraining order. (Id. p. 15:10-23.) Thus, nothing about these earlier cases precludes Plaintiff from bringing the present claims.

Additionally, the only issue that was litigated as a part of these hearings was whether Plaintiff had met her burden of proof to apply for an order for protection. (Demurrer, Ex. A.) The extend of the analysis in these hearings was that, “the Court finds the party requesting the order of protection did not sustain the applicable burden of proof and accordingly the request is denied.” (Ibid.) Thus, the only issue that has been litigated is whether Plaintiff met their burden of proof as to this type of relief. Given that Plaintiff’s current lawsuit does not seek an order of protection, issue preclusion does not apply here.

For these reasons, the Court OVERRULES the demurrer as to the entire Complaint.

Conclusion

The Court GRANTS Plaintiff’s Motion to Strike Defendant’s general denial. Additionally, the Court OVERRULES the demurrer as to the entire Complaint.
