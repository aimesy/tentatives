DEPARTMENT I LAW AND MOTION RULINGS

If the parties wish to submit on the tentative ruling and avoid a court appearance on the matter, the moving party must contact the opposing party and all other parties who have appeared in the action and confirm that each will submit on the tentative ruling. Please call the court no later than 4:30 p.m. on the court day before the hearing, leave a message with the court clerk at (310) 260-3629 advising her that all parties will submit on the tentative ruling and waive hearing, and finally, serve notice of the Court's ruling on all parties entitled to receive service. If any party declines to submit on the tentative ruling, then no telephone call is necessary, and all parties should appear at the hearing.

Case Number: 23SMCV05956    Hearing Date: July 10, 2026    Dept: I

This is a motion by defendant to compel plaintiff’s deposition.  There is a motion for protective order set for September, but this motion will moot out that one.

 

Defendant contends that plaintiff has refused to be deposed and is just delaying things.  Plaintiff argues that such is not the case.  Plaintiff says that plaintiff first tried to depose defendants but could not get dates.  Plaintiff would also like to be sure that there is only one plaintiff’s deposition even though there are two related cases—in other words, all defendants would depose him at the same time rather than doing it twice.  Plaintiff initially said that no dates would be set for deposition unless the defense agreed to those terms.  In any event, after the motion was filed, plaintiff agreed to be deposed on July 31, 2026, and there are no issues with any of the other dates.

 

The court is inclined to agree with plaintiff that the motion is moot to a point.  The court will not start borrowing trouble when the date has been set, but the motion is GRANTED to the extent that plaintiff is ORDERED TO BE DEPOSED ON JULY 31, 2026.  The court issues the order so that if plaintiff fails to appear, plaintiff will be in violation of the court’s order.  The court notes, though, that whether the court would actually sanction plaintiff should plaintiff not appear might well depend on whether the depositions set for the prior dates go forward. 

 

The court has some sympathy for the one deposition not two argument.  And the court cannot really fault plaintiff for wanting to insist on that if possible.  As to priority, while the Code of Civil Procedure does not recognize it, the Litigation Guidelines discourage stealing a march by noticing a deposition to pre-date a near-term deposition already on the calendar.  And plaintiff wanted to get responses to certain written discovery before being deposed.  While plaintiff does not have such a right, again, orderly conduct suggests that it is not unreasonable for plaintiff to want to have the benefit of discovery already served before being deposed.  But, thankfully, it appears that the issue has been solved.  

 

Everyone wants sanctions.  But no one’s position was unjustified.  Because all parties had substantial justification for their respective positions, the sanctions requests are all DENIED.  The motion now set for September 17, 2026, is VACATED AS MOOT.  The court is hoping that this will be the last discovery motion the court will hear, at least on procedural stuff.

Case Number: 25SMCV00577    Hearing Date: July 10, 2026    Dept: I

This is a motion to compel and a motion to deem admitted.  Defendant states that it served discovery on April 26, 2026, but responses to that discovery were never served.  A follow up request allegedly went unanswered.  These motions followed.  After the date by which the opposition was due, defendant filed a notice of non-opposition.  In an untimely opposition, plaintiff states that all that was needed was a phone call.  And plaintiff stated that responses have been served without objections.  Plaintiff also noted that when a request for a short continuance of the discovery response date was made, it was refused.  Plaintiff also pointed out that the non-opposition notice did not mention that responses had been served.    In reply, defendant states that the opposition should be disregarded as untimely.  Defendant also notes that one of the exhibits in the declaration was missing.  As to the refusal to give the extension, defendant notes that the request was for an extension until June 2, 2026, but no responses were actually served until June 4, 2026, and the verified objection-free responses were not served until June 8.  And further, defendant points out, defendant did not file the motion until June 4, 2026—after the requested extension would have expired.  Defendant also notes that a number of the documents produced are illegible and that there is no privilege log.  Defendant seeks sanctions of $3975 for the motion to compel and $3125 for the motion to deem admitted.  That is a total of $7100.  (The request for increased fees made in the reply is improper).

 

The court is troubled by everyone.  Defendant should have granted the extension when requested—and should not have required a waiver of objections.  Routine extensions are routinely granted as professional courtesies, and the Litigation Guidelines encourage that behavior.   On the other hand, defendant did wait the requested week before filing this motion and still received nothing.  More troubling, the notice of non-opposition failed to mention that responses had been served.  As to this, the misconduct is material.  The Code of Civil Procedure expressly states that if responses to requests for admission are served before the hearing, the motion shall be denied (other than sanctions).  Defendants, knowing that the motion to deem admitted must be denied and that it is error not to do so, nonetheless urged the court to commit prejudicial error and grant the motion anyway.  While many mistakes can be viewed as sloppy, deliberately attempting to lead the court into prejudicial error by hiding facts from the court is misconduct of a different sort.  Next, plaintiff’s opposition was late.  Next, the eventual production apparently included illegible materials, suggesting that no one did a quality check.  (It is also possible that there exists no legible copy, but the court has no way of knowing.) 

 

What the court will do is this.  The motion to deem admitted is MOOT.  The motion to compel is GRANTED.  No later than 10 days from today, plaintiff will provide verified responses without objection to the form and special interrogatories.  To the extent plaintiff believes that has already been done, plaintiff may stand on the responses already given.  The request for sanctions as to the RFA’s is DENIED based on plaintiff’s counsel’s misconduct in attempting to lead the court into erroneously deeming the RFA’s to be admitted.  As to the motion to compel, the court will award sanctions in the reduced amount of $1000.  The reduction is because counsel’s misconduct in attempting to lead the court into error on the RFAs causes the court to distrust the fee declarations and so the court does not believe it can put weight on the amounts set forth therein.  The $1000 reflects a minimum amount that just about anyone would have to spend.  The amount is further reduced because the court believes that counsel’s hardball tactics of refusing a professional courtesy of following up with a phone call might have obviated the need for the motion.  While such a call is not required under the Code, the Code does require that fees be both reasonable and necessary.  These were likely not necessary.  That $1000 is assessed against plaintiff, but not counsel, and is payable within 30 days.

 

The court also has before it plaintiff’s counsel’s ex parte application to shorten time on its motion to withdraw as counsel.  Right now, the motion is set for September 15, 2026.  (The court apologizes for the long delay in setting hearings.  It is a function of a somewhat lengthy leave the court must take.)  The underlying motion was filed on July 8, 2026.  In the supporting declaration, counsel states that there has been a breakdown in the attorney/client relationship.  The court’s concern is this.  Plaintiffs have moved to disqualify defense counsel, and that hearing is set for September 1, 2026.  That is not a good motion for plaintiff to appear and argue in a self represented capacity.  What the court will do is this.  The court will GRANT the application to specially set the withdrawal hearing, but it will have to be after the court gets back from leave.  No later than 5 court days before the hearing, the court ORDERS plaintiffs themselves—not their current counsel—to file a declaration stating their position on this motion.  They can support it, oppose it, state that they take no position on it.  They need not provide any information that is privileged, and they do not even need to provide reasoning.  The court just wants to know if plaintiffs support, oppose, or take no position on counsel’s motion to withdraw.  The court does warn plaintiffs that it will not postpone any hearings based on a lack of counsel should current counsel be allowed to withdraw.  The court also notes the discovery motion set for September 18, 2026.

DISCOVERY RELATED GUIDELINES FOR DEPARTMENT I

 

The guidelines below are not “rules” and they are not universally applicable in all cases.  Rather, they are guidelines as to the court’s general views.  Each case, of course, will be decided on its own facts and circumstances, and some of the guidelines below may not apply to particular circumstances.  That said, the court does take these views seriously.

 

Discovery Responses

 

The court provides the following guidance concerning its general views on some common issues regarding responses to written objections.  The court has noticed that many attorneys view objections and the manner of making them in a way different than does the court.  To avoid these common pitfalls, the court offers the following in addition to the provisions of the Litigation Guidelines appended to the Los Angeles County Superior Court Local Rules.

 

First, “General Objections” are not permitted.  The Code of Civil Procedure does not provide for them, and therefore they are disregarded by the Court.  Any response that “incorporates” the “General Objections” is improper by definition. 

 

Second, the  court often views boilerplate objections as being in bad faith.  A bad faith response is no response at all in the court’s view.  Therefore, making boilerplate objections does not preserve those objections nor does it constitute a good faith response to written discovery.  A bad faith response is viewed by the court for what it is: bad faith.  The objections will be stricken and deemed waived.  In the context of a Request for Admission, such objections may lead to a finding that the request is deemed admitted, although that will depend on the facts of the particular case and the specific discovery at hand.

 

Objections should be tailored to each specific request.  If a request truly is overbroad, then an objection to that effect is proper.  But such an objection is best accompanied by some reasonable limitation by the responding party that will narrow the request appropriately and (as narrowed) provide a substantive response rather than a mere statement that the request is overbroad.  The same is true as to an objection that a request is unduly burdensome.  The court also notes that the party asserting that a request is unduly burdensome has the obligation at some point to provide an evidentiary basis for the objection, including evidence as to what the burden of compliance would be.  (West Pico Furniture Co. v. Superior Court (1961) 56 Cal.2d 407.)  Often such an objection will include a statement by the responding party as to a narrower request that would not be unduly burdensome and provide substantive responses as so limited.  Objections that a request is “vague and ambiguous” should set forth the vague and ambiguous term as well as the responding party’s reasonable interpretation of that term as well as a statement that, so interpreted, the response will provide the discovery requested.  If there is no reasonable interpretation possible (which is a rare situation), then the responding party should so state.  Objections as to privilege must set forth the basis explaining why the information sought is in fact privileged.  Where a privilege is asserted in the context of a document request, a privilege log should accompany the answer or be provided within a short and reasonable time after the answer, and the log will serve as the explanation.  Where the objection is made in the context of an interrogatory, it must be clear from the objection the scope of the information being withheld.  If there is no log, there should be no privilege objection to a document request (meaning that a prophylactic privilege objection is the equivalent of no objection; it preserves nothing).  There are some rare exceptions, such as where the entire point of the discovery is to get allegedly privileged information or where compliance would require a log that is in essence an index of counsel’s file.  In that situation, the log is unnecessary, but the assertion should be made that the request is in that rare context.

 

Third, if an objection is made to the discovery but a response is being given, it must be clear whether information or documents are in fact being withheld on the basis of the objections.  If the objections are clear and done in the manner set forth above, with statements in the objection as to a narrowing that will make the request proper, this is usually a simple task.  The objections themselves state the limit and the response will be full and complete as limited.  But where the objections are not so clear, the response must clearly state whether any information or document is being withheld on the basis of the objection and, if so, the extent of the withholding.  Accordingly, in those situations, phrases like “Notwithstanding and subject to the foregoing objections, responding party states as follows” are generally improper.  Those sorts of phrases make the verification useless, as the verifier can always fall back on the ”objections” as the reason why a document was not produced or information was not disclosed.  On the other hand, where the line of demarcation is clear, the verification will still serve its purpose.

 

Fourth, for document requests, the substantive response must conform to the Code of Civil Procedure.  There are relatively tight rules as to what the response must say, and the response must say it.  For example, where a responding party is not producing documents because they are not in the party’s possession, custody, or control, the responding party must verify that a diligent search has been made and must further provide the information set forth in the Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.230 in such cases.  In the case of interrogatories, the responses must also conform to the Code of Civil Procedure and must be made after diligent inquiry.  It is not proper to refuse to respond because the responding party has no personal knowledge.  If the knowledge is hearsay, it must still be disclosed, although it can be qualified to make it clear that it is not based on the verifier’s personal knowledge.

 

Fifth, the court frowns on responses that do not conform to the foregoing rules being served with the view that the responses will moot themselves out in the meet and confer process.  That is not how the process works.  A good faith response is required before the meet and confer process begins.  The meet and confer process will (hopefully) bridge the gaps between the parties’ respective positions.  Further, where a response to a request for documents is made and documents are to be produced subject to certain objections (with the documents withheld properly delineated), the documents should be turned over irrespective of the meet and confer.  The documents are to be produced with alacrity to the extent that there is no objection to them, but the court recognizes that there is often a lag between the date responses are served and the date that the documents are produced.

 

What this means is that the response to a discovery request is not a trivial undertaking.  Nabbing the response from the form file is a generally bad idea and can lead to all objections being waived.  The point is that the boilerplate often renders the remainder of the response useless.  The only exception is where it is clear that the substantive response is not in any way limited by the objections.  In that case, the objections do no harm, although they also do no good.

 

Meet and Confer

 

The Code of Civil Procedure requires that before a motion to compel further responses or a motion for a protective order is filed, the parties engage in a good faith attempt to resolve their differences.  They are to “meet and confer” for that purpose.  Only if that effort fails can a motion be brought. 

 

Sadly, many litigants view the meet and confer process as just another procedural hoop through which they must jump in order to bring the motion, similar to the need to include an actual demurrer with the demurrer papers.  The Code of Civil Procedure requires it, so they do it, but no one’s heart is really in it.  That is not sufficient.

 

Given that, the court believes it appropriate to set forth how the court views the meet and confer requirement.  Failure to abide by the guidelines below may well justify denial of the motion or a continuance of it to allow the process to take place.  If one party but not the other refuses to participate as set forth, more likely than not the party refusing to participate will find itself on the losing end of the motion.  The following quotation fairly sums up the court’s own view.

 

“[W]e feel compelled to observe that resort to the courts easily could have been avoided here had both parties actually taken to heart Justice Stone's admonitions in Townsend that ‘the statute requires that there be a serious effort at negotiation and informal resolution.’ (Townsend, supra, 61 Cal.App.4th at p. 1438.)  Perhaps after 11 years it is necessary to remind trial counsel and the bar once again that ‘[a]rgument is not the same as informal negotiation’ (id at p. 1437); that attempting informal resolution means more than the mere attempt by the discovery proponent ‘to persuade the objector of the error of his ways’ (id. at p. 1435); and that ‘a reasonable and good faith attempt at informal resolution entails something more than bickering with [opposing] counsel. . . .  Rather, the law requires that counsel attempt to talk the matter over, compare their views, consult, and deliberate.’ (Id. at p. 1439.)”  (Clement v. Alegre (2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 1277, 1294, emphasis in original, parallel citations omitted.) 

 

In practical terms, it means as follows.  It is entirely appropriate to begin the process with a letter or other correspondence.  The letter should not include a demand in the form of an ultimatum, but it can certainly include the relief that is being sought with an invitation to meet and confer.  It is also entirely appropriate for the other party to respond with a letter or other correspondence to set forth its position on the issues.  Such an initial exchange can often be helpful to narrow the dispute or at least articulate plainly the boundaries.  Whether further exchanges make sense will depend on each case and each dispute.

 

However, at some point before impasse is reached the process must generally include an actual “meet.”  The letters might well suffice to “confer,” but an exchange of correspondence is not a meeting.  In the COVID-19 era, the “meeting” can be virtual or telephonic, but a meeting there should be.  The meeting must be attended by a person from each side with the authority to agree to the other side’s position without getting further permission from anyone, including the client.  If only the client can give the needed authority, then the client must be available instantaneously at all times during the meeting.  This does not mean that one side or the other must concede, but it does mean that the people meeting have the ability in real and practical terms to strike a deal.

 

The parties should approach the meet and confer process as a problem-solving exercise.  The purpose of the meet and confer is not to convince the other side of the bankruptcy of its position; rather the purpose is to reach an agreement by which the party seeking discovery is able to obtain the information that it reasonably needs and the party providing discovery is not put to an undue burden or forced to provide unnecessary information that might infringe on a privacy interest or disclose a competitive trade secret or the like.

 

At the conclusion of the meet and confer, the parties should have an agreed statement as to the outcome.  If the outcome is a total impasse, then they should agree on that.  If they have resolved some or all of their differences, then they should state—in writing—what that agreement is so that there will be no confusion later.  Often, an agreement will be without prejudice to a further request by the propounding party for more information after that party receives the further responses.

 

If a motion is still required, the declaration in support should describe with some detail the meet and confer process.  While the court is not interested in the back and forth (and indeed describing it can be counter-productive), the court is interested in when the meeting took place, who was there, and how long it lasted.

 

All communications—in writing or oral—must be civil, polite, and professional.  Name-calling and accusations are devices that undermine the process; they do not further it.  A party engaging in such activity generally is not acting in a good faith effort to reach an agreement.

 

Informal Discovery Conferences

 

The court generally prefers an Informal Discovery Conference (IDC) before any party files a motion to compel further responses, and the failure to use this avenue of resolution can greatly affect the amount of sanctions that might be awarded because sanctions are limited to fees that are reasonable and necessary.  The goal of the IDC is to “get to yes.”  To achieve that goal, the court adopts the following.

 

  1. Five court days before the IDC, the parties will submit a JOINT statement no more than eight pages total (on pleading paper, but excluding the caption page) setting forth their respective positions.  Where a similar issue is raised that will resolve many requests, it can be dealt with as a single issue. 
  2. The IDC follows the good faith meet and confer.  It is not a substitute for the meet and confer.  The court’s time is to be spent only on issues after the parties have tried to narrow the dispute as much as possible.
  3. As stated above, the goal is to “get to yes.”  This means a few things.  One is that each party must be represented by a person who has the authority to agree to the other side’s position without getting further permission.  It need not be lead counsel and that authority need not be exercised.  But the people at the conference must have that authority.  If only the client has that authority, then the client will be present physically with counsel in a adjacent room without distraction after initial introductions are made.
  4. All IDC’s are “off the record.”  That means that whatever anyone says at the IDC will not be binding or quoted back at the party unless an agreement is struck.  It is very bad form to quote the court’s comments at an IDC during a later hearing unless the comments were part of a stipulated order.
  5. All parties have the right to change position if no deal is reached.  The court, for example, might strongly indicate that it believes one side or the other has the better position but rule in a completely different way if there is a formal motion.  Partial agreements that are contingent upon a full resolution are not binding unless a full resolution is reached.  Partial resolutions that are not contingent on a full resolution are binding.  Parties who are suggesting a resolution should make it clear whether their proposal depends upon other resolutions.
  6. Parties should be especially willing to give up sanctions at an IDC if it will resolve the dispute.  That is not a requirement, but the court’s experience is such that if a party is really going to insist on a sanction, the likelihood of a resolution is very low.
  7. It is likely that no party will get everything that it wants.  The goal is to abide the Code of Civil Procedure, but at the same time make sure that the party seeking discovery is not creating busy-work or demanding a lot of work for no benefit (or virtually no benefit).  The responding party might well have the right (should it come to motion practice) to avoid some aspect of the discovery, but in order to get to a resolution it might be in the responding party’s interest to be willing to offer something.  Conversely, the propounding party might have the right (should it come to motion practice) to get some aspect of discovery, but in order to get to a resolution it might be in the propounding party’s interest to forego aspects of the discovery that are not really needed, perhaps reserving the right to seek that information at a later time should it prove necessary.  The point is not to have the court simply hammer away at the opponent.  That might happen at a motion, but it is not the goal of the IDC.
  8. No orders will be made except on stipulation.  However, if there is an agreement, the court may well want to make an appropriate order or ask the parties to confirm it in writing.
  9. Parties should take careful notes on any agreements.  Because there are no Court Reporters, it is hard to put an agreement on the record.  However, unless the agreement is confirmed quickly, people have a tendency to forget or to have “buyer’s remorse.”  Therefore, it is best to have one party send an immediate confirming email to the other and obtain an agreement.  The court would prefer not to get into the specific wording of the agreement during the IDC unless absolutely necessary.
  10. The IDC process does not work if people view it as just a necessary step to filing a motion.  The court does not see it that way—if it did, then the court would abandon the IDC as a waste of time.  Rather, this is an off-ramp to avoid motion practice and the attendant costs and time.  Hopefully, it is also a way to get discovery more quickly than through formal motion practice.
  11. At the IDC, no matter how hot tempers have run in the past, the court expects all counsel to be polite and civil in tone to one another.  The court will not tolerate rudeness or unnecessary accusations.  They do not move the ball forward and they make it nigh impossible to reach an agreement.

Case Number: 25SMCV03160    Hearing Date: July 10, 2026    Dept: I

The court has before it the trustee’s motion for authority to accept the offer of sale as to two of the properties.  The motion is supported by the defense but opposed by plaintiffs.

 

The gist of the issue is this.  There is a buyer, Hankey, who has offered to purchase two properties for a total of $110 million.  Of that, $80 million will be paid up front.  The remaining $30 million will be a carryback mortgage.  The $80 million will be used for two purposes: (1) to pay any existing debts on the properties, including the JPMorgan loan and the PMF loan; and (2) to complete the construction of the four properties still not completed (two of which are the ones being sold).  The $80 million will bear interest in favor of the buyer at a below market rate until such time as the deal closes.  The trustee’s view is that this proposal minimizes risk to the properties because it assures a ready and liquid source of cash sufficient to pay off all debts (other than Bobs) and also to complete construction.  While there are contingencies, the receiver does not believe that they are risky ones.  For example, the receiver is confident that the work can in fact be completed properly and timely, and also that the required permit for the tennis courts will be forthcoming.  The remaining two properties are not part of the deal, but they will be available for sale once completed.  The combination of the sale proceeds from the other two homes, anything left unspent of the $80 million, and the $30 million carryback as it is repaid will be available to repay Bobs and any later incurred debts, with the final balance going to the 600 Spring.  The receiver has stated that if the court does not approve the Hankey deal, Hankey intends to rescind the offer.  The court is not sure what, if any, provision is being made to set aside any money for Bobs relating to these two properties.  The court will inquire as to that.

 

Bobs proposes an alternative deal.  Bobs has agreed to fund the remaining construction, including any overruns beyond any budgeted amounts.  Bobs will fund that construction at no interest and will not even add those costs to its principal loan balance—in essence, Bobs agrees that it will complete the work on its own nickel.  However, Bobs has two conditions that remain sticking points.  The first is that Bobs will have an initial fund of $65 million that it will control.  It will then use $3 million of that to a fund that can be used for construction.  If and when that fund dips below $500,000, Bobs will add another $2 million from the $65 million (or what remains of it), and it will continue funding in that way until the work is done.  Bobs has agreed that if it fails to replenish the construction account for more than 59 days, it will pay a $2 million penalty and if still not funded, the receiver can issue Receiver Certificates.  However, it will not front-load the entire $65 million.  (The court is not confident that Bobs is stuck at $3 million/$500,000 as the specific numbers, but Bobs is clear that it is not just going to turn over $65 million up front.)  Second, Bobs insists that its general contractor, Live Happy, replace the current general contractor, Unvarnished, who is defendants’ preferred contractor.  Related to that, Live Happy will warrant its own work or work done under its subcontracts but will not warrant work done by Unvarnished or any subcontractor before Live Happy comes on board.  Bobs’ theory is that the project can be completed for much less money than the receiver or defendants believe and that it can be completed quickly.  Bobs further believes that the two parcels Hankey wants to buy will sell on the market for tens of millions of dollars more than Hankey is offering.  But to make sure, Bobs is willing to guarantee that the ultimate sale price of the two parcels will at least equal what Hankey is offering, by agreeing that any shortfall will come out of plaintiffs’ share of the proceeds.

 

Defendants have also made an offer, but neither plaintiffs nor the receiver believe it is the best offer available.  The court agrees.

 

The parties criticize the various deals.  Defendants contend that Bobs is not acting out of altruism; rather, they argue, Bobs is trying to delay the completion and sale of the property in order to keep racking up interest on the loan, which defendants state is usurious.  As a result, they argue, this is not “free money,” as Bobs contends.  To the contrary, they assert, the costs of construction will be far less than the additional interest that will accrue to Bobs in the interim.  Further, defendants suggest that the guarantee is really no guarantee at all because it will stand behind the Bobs loan, which will eat up whatever equity might otherwise exist.  Defendants also don’t like that they will lose control of the project.  They contend that plaintiffs, and Live Happy as well, have no experience building luxury super-high-end homes like these and do not know what they are talking about or getting themselves into.  And they are unhappy that they will have no power over the construction.  While they will have a non-voting seat at the table, if Bobs and the Receiver agree on something, defendants cannot stop it from occurring without running to court. 

 

The receiver (joined by defendants) points out that the $3 million is not adequate.  Instead, the receiver states that while there will be money in the ether, in fact there will be precious little actually available to do any work.  That means, the receiver worries, that Bobs will have the ability essentially to call all of the shots, which means people will be running to court all the time, which will delay the project further.  The concern is that Bobs will have a practical stranglehold on the project, giving Bobs effective total control.  (The court notes, as does the receiver, that part of this issue is based on the very different budgets proposed by the receiver and by Bobs.  That is, in turn, partially based on the notion that the receiver’s budget is for the global project, not just the cost to complete the two properties Hankey offers to buy.  Further, apparently Bobs’ budget (as is defendants’ budget) is more of a hard cost budget, but not the full cost-to-completion budget that the receiver discusses.)  The receiver also notes that the lack of a single general contractor could lead to problems on sale.  Usually when a buyer buys property from a developer and something goes wrong, the buyer can look to the builder to fix the problem.  But here, Live Happy will (so it goes) blame Unvarnished for the problem and refuse to take responsibility, and the developer, 600 Spring, will be long gone or judgment proof.  That problem will lead to a significant reduction in the purchase price because there is really no effective new home warranty. 

 

Bobs responds to these concerns by suggesting that there will be no money in this project no matter what; Bobs has resigned itself to the reality that it will lose money—it is just trying to cut its losses.  And as to the GC, Bobs states that if it is going to fund and pay for the rest of the project, it only stands to reason that it not be saddled with a contractor that it thinks has not been honest.  Rather, Bobs argues, Live Happy has every incentive to finish the project quickly and efficiently.  As to the funding issue, Bobs argues that no one puts all of the construction loan out there immediately.   When a bank loans money, it is doled out in construction draws that have to meet specific standards; this is not very different from that.  True, Hankey is putting the full amount out at once, but Hankey is a buyer, not a lender.  Bobs argues that the $2 million penalty provision if Bobs delays is incentive enough to get the job done quickly.  The receiver counters that up front funding is actually very common in the receivership world.  Also, in the context of bank funding, the money is there and easily accessible because the bank is not an adverse party.  Here, Bobs will control the money, and it is a party to a lawsuit.  The incentives are therefore just a bit different.  As to the warranty, Bobs does not disagree with what the receiver argues, at least in concept.  But Bobs states that it is unreasonable to require it to warrant the work of others—especially those it does not trust (like Unvarnished).  And to the extent that this warranty issue actually does have an impact on the purchase price, Bobs argues that it is plaintiffs, not defendants, who will bear the burden through the guarantee that the deal will generate no less than the Hankey deal.

 

On the other side, Bobs argues that it has gone above and beyond to try and meet everyone’s needs.  It notes that it is willing to fund the construction—including overruns—without even adding to the principal balance.  Bobs suggests that this is not really a common offer under any scenario.  Bobs states that it has looked at all of the concerns raised by the court and addressed them all—essentially by conceding every point.  Bobs also reiterates that the best test of what property is worth is the market.  And there is no real market value here by the receiver, Bobs states.  There is no appraisal report put forth by the receiver showing some methodologically sound valuation and there has been no attempt to get any actual market reaction at all.  Bobs, in contrast, has put forth detailed and reasoned appraisals showing, it claims, that there is significantly more value in the property than Hankey is willing to pay.  Bobs is not asserting that the receiver is in defendants’ pocket (or Hankey’s pocket), Bobs believes that defendants are simply trying to keep their own contractor and therefore themselves in practical control. 

 

And plaintiffs say defendants are not good people, and defendants say plaintiffs are really not good people.  At present, no one is attacking the receiver.

 

The court is somewhat flummoxed.  It would seem that everyone ought to be on the same page.  The receiver’s duty is to protect the property and sell it with minimum risk and maximum return, and quickly.  The court would think that plaintiffs and defendants would have the same interest.  Maybe there will be money for the equity holders and maybe not.  But the more money that comes in and the more quickly it comes in, the more likely it will be that the project will turn a profit (or at least less of a loss).  As to Bobs, once the other debts are paid, Bobs will get paid what it is owed (it is, after all, a lender).  If the interest rate is usurious, there are legal doctrines to deal with that.  But again, the more money that comes in—and quickly—the more of its loan will be repaid with appropriate interest.  And no one is arguing (to the best of the court’s knowledge) that money actually paid out by Bobs is somehow suspect or should stand behind the equity.  The court is just beyond unhappy that three parties with the same interests have such divergent views.

 

So what is a poor trial court to do?  Pretty simply this: go back to basics.  And the basics are these.  First, the court is not a contractor, property manager, or real estate developer.  Worse, the court is out of time (because Hankey is threatening to walk) and cannot hold an evidentiary hearing to take evidence or hear experts.  Second, the court does not want to become a property manager or contractor.  Third, the court appointed a neutral receiver who does have experience and whose job it is to look at issues dispassionately and without favor to one side or the other.  Fourth, risk is bad; hard money is good.  Fifth, the resolution of this motion is committed to this court’s discretion looking to the surrounding circumstances and facts, as well as fairness, justice, and the rights of the parties.  (People v. Riverside University (1973) 35 Cal.App.3d 572.)  Relatedly, the court has attempted to consider all of the material facts and evidence.  (Martin v. Alcoholic Beverage Appeals Board (1961) 55 Cal.2d 867.) 

 

All of those factors tilt generally the same way: to approve the sale.  True, there are dueling financial models as to which option will provide the best return.  While the court is not an economist, even the court can tell that much depends on the particular assumptions being made, many of which are hotly contested.  The receiver has no interest in the Hankey deal per se.  The court knows of no issues or links that would cause the receiver to favor the defense over plaintiffs or a low ball offer from Hankey.  The whole point of appointing the receiver was to have a person who could devote the time necessary as well as specialized skills in order to advise the court as to the best avenue to maximize value and minimize risk.  While there are debates one could have with the receiver’s methodology, the court puts weight on the receiver’s advice, and that is as the law should be.  That is not to say that the court is relieved of exercising its full and independent judgment.  The receiver is not infallible, but neither are plaintiffs or defendants (or the court, for that matter).  And the receiver’s office is an arm of the court, not the other way ‘round.  But in the exercise of the court’s independent judgment, it is not improper for the court to recognize the receiver’s expertise and independence.  In addition, time matters.  The Hankey deal is a firm deal now.  Subject to very few contingencies, the sale will close.  (And the receiver is holding most of the money.)  Under plaintiffs’ proposal, the property will have to be built and marketed before that happens, and even then there are the inevitable delays.  It might close higher; but then again, it might not.  And unforeseen events can occur to disrupt the process or the value.  And finally, the court is risk averse.  True, the receiver needs to establish or demonstrate that consummating a sale at present is in the parties’ best interest.  (Cal-American Income Property Fund VII v. Brown Development Corp. (1982) 138 Cal.App.3d 268.)  But the receiver does not need to demonstrate the need for a sale in general because the whole point of the enterprise is to sell the property.  Further, the court is very concerned that if the court adopts Bobs’ approach, it will become embroiled with ex parte applications over aspect after aspect of the construction.  And the court is also not sure that Bobs has really taken the warranty issue into account.  To be sure, 600 Spring can still be sued by a buyer.  But, as set forth above, few buyers will be comforted by a warranty issued by an LLC that is dissolving or in the process of doing so.

 

To be clear, if the receiver were on the other side of this, the court would likely go with the other side.  And the court is making no finding of bad faith by plaintiffs.  Quite the contrary, the court discounts defendants’ assertion that this is just a bad faith way to wrest control of the project away from them.  It does have that effect, but at a significant potential cost.  The court believes plaintiffs have made a good faith and honest attempt to reach a deal that plaintiffs believe will benefit all.  But at days’ end, and after considering the various briefs and factors raised by the parties, the court will defer to the expertise of the expert it appointed to look at these issues and recommend an outcome that is in the best interest of both parties.  The receiver’s recommendation appears to be sound.  And therefore, the motion is GRANTED.  To be clear, defendants will need to sign a declaration under penalty of perjury as discussed at the last hearing: that they will not benefit directly or indirectly from the Hankey deal (other than through their ownership of 600 Spring).  That means no winks and nods about future sweetheart deals or the like.  And the court expects such a declaration to be enforceable.  In this respect, the court treats defendants as fiduciaries at least of 600 Spring.  And we all know what happens when a fiduciary makes a secret profit.

 

The court also notes that it might well condition this ruling on some provision being made for Bobs as it relates to these two properties.  The court is aware that there is a dispute as to whether the Bobs loan is usurious.  But the court does not believe that defendants are suggesting that Bobs did not actually lend any money.  To the extent the Hankey purchase would wipe out Bobs’ asserted Deed of Trust, the court will need to be sure that money is set aside for the Bobs loan, at least as to the principal attributable to these two properties.  The failure to do that could well change the court’s view.

For the parties’ mutual aid, the court attaches its Guidelines on Discovery.

 

 

DISCOVERY RELATED GUIDELINES FOR DEPARTMENT I

 

The guidelines below are not “rules” and they are not universally applicable in all cases.  Rather, they are guidelines as to the court’s general views.  Each case, of course, will be decided on its own facts and circumstances, and some of the guidelines below may not apply to particular circumstances.  That said, the court does take these views seriously.

 

Discovery Responses

 

The court provides the following guidance concerning its general views on some common issues regarding responses to written objections.  The court has noticed that many attorneys view objections and the manner of making them in a way different than does the court.  To avoid these common pitfalls, the court offers the following in addition to the provisions of the Litigation Guidelines appended to the Los Angeles County Superior Court Local Rules.

 

First, “General Objections” are not permitted.  The Code of Civil Procedure does not provide for them, and therefore they are disregarded by the Court.  Any response that “incorporates” the “General Objections” is improper by definition. 

 

Second, the  court often views boilerplate objections as being in bad faith.  A bad faith response is no response at all in the court’s view.  Therefore, making boilerplate objections does not preserve those objections nor does it constitute a good faith response to written discovery.  A bad faith response is viewed by the court for what it is: bad faith.  The objections will be stricken and deemed waived.  In the context of a Request for Admission, such objections may lead to a finding that the request is deemed admitted, although that will depend on the facts of the particular case and the specific discovery at hand.

 

Objections should be tailored to each specific request.  If a request truly is overbroad, then an objection to that effect is proper.  But such an objection is best accompanied by some reasonable limitation by the responding party that will narrow the request appropriately and (as narrowed) provide a substantive response rather than a mere statement that the request is overbroad.  The same is true as to an objection that a request is unduly burdensome.  The court also notes that the party asserting that a request is unduly burdensome has the obligation at some point to provide an evidentiary basis for the objection, including evidence as to what the burden of compliance would be.  (West Pico Furniture Co. v. Superior Court (1961) 56 Cal.2d 407.)  Often such an objection will include a statement by the responding party as to a narrower request that would not be unduly burdensome and provide substantive responses as so limited.  Objections that a request is “vague and ambiguous” should set forth the vague and ambiguous term as well as the responding party’s reasonable interpretation of that term as well as a statement that, so interpreted, the response will provide the discovery requested.  If there is no reasonable interpretation possible (which is a rare situation), then the responding party should so state.  Objections as to privilege must set forth the basis explaining why the information sought is in fact privileged.  Where a privilege is asserted in the context of a document request, a privilege log should accompany the answer or be provided within a short and reasonable time after the answer, and the log will serve as the explanation.  Where the objection is made in the context of an interrogatory, it must be clear from the objection the scope of the information being withheld.  If there is no log, there should be no privilege objection to a document request (meaning that a prophylactic privilege objection is the equivalent of no objection; it preserves nothing).  There are some rare exceptions, such as where the entire point of the discovery is to get allegedly privileged information or where compliance would require a log that is in essence an index of counsel’s file.  In that situation, the log is unnecessary, but the assertion should be made that the request is in that rare context.

 

Third, if an objection is made to the discovery but a response is being given, it must be clear whether information or documents are in fact being withheld on the basis of the objections.  If the objections are clear and done in the manner set forth above, with statements in the objection as to a narrowing that will make the request proper, this is usually a simple task.  The objections themselves state the limit and the response will be full and complete as limited.  But where the objections are not so clear, the response must clearly state whether any information or document is being withheld on the basis of the objection and, if so, the extent of the withholding.  Accordingly, in those situations, phrases like “Notwithstanding and subject to the foregoing objections, responding party states as follows” are generally improper.  Those sorts of phrases make the verification useless, as the verifier can always fall back on the ”objections” as the reason why a document was not produced or information was not disclosed.  On the other hand, where the line of demarcation is clear, the verification will still serve its purpose.

 

Fourth, for document requests, the substantive response must conform to the Code of Civil Procedure.  There are relatively tight rules as to what the response must say, and the response must say it.  For example, where a responding party is not producing documents because they are not in the party’s possession, custody, or control, the responding party must verify that a diligent search has been made and must further provide the information set forth in the Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.230 in such cases.  In the case of interrogatories, the responses must also conform to the Code of Civil Procedure and must be made after diligent inquiry.  It is not proper to refuse to respond because the responding party has no personal knowledge.  If the knowledge is hearsay, it must still be disclosed, although it can be qualified to make it clear that it is not based on the verifier’s personal knowledge.

 

Fifth, the court frowns on responses that do not conform to the foregoing rules being served with the view that the responses will moot themselves out in the meet and confer process.  That is not how the process works.  A good faith response is required before the meet and confer process begins.  The meet and confer process will (hopefully) bridge the gaps between the parties’ respective positions.  Further, where a response to a request for documents is made and documents are to be produced subject to certain objections (with the documents withheld properly delineated), the documents should be turned over irrespective of the meet and confer.  The documents are to be produced with alacrity to the extent that there is no objection to them, but the court recognizes that there is often a lag between the date responses are served and the date that the documents are produced.

 

What this means is that the response to a discovery request is not a trivial undertaking.  Nabbing the response from the form file is a generally bad idea and can lead to all objections being waived.  The point is that the boilerplate often renders the remainder of the response useless.  The only exception is where it is clear that the substantive response is not in any way limited by the objections.  In that case, the objections do no harm, although they also do no good.

 

Meet and Confer

 

The Code of Civil Procedure requires that before a motion to compel further responses or a motion for a protective order is filed, the parties engage in a good faith attempt to resolve their differences.  They are to “meet and confer” for that purpose.  Only if that effort fails can a motion be brought. 

 

Sadly, many litigants view the meet and confer process as just another procedural hoop through which they must jump in order to bring the motion, similar to the need to include an actual demurrer with the demurrer papers.  The Code of Civil Procedure requires it, so they do it, but no one’s heart is really in it.  That is not sufficient.

 

Given that, the court believes it appropriate to set forth how the court views the meet and confer requirement.  Failure to abide by the guidelines below may well justify denial of the motion or a continuance of it to allow the process to take place.  If one party but not the other refuses to participate as set forth, more likely than not the party refusing to participate will find itself on the losing end of the motion.  The following quotation fairly sums up the court’s own view.

 

“[W]e feel compelled to observe that resort to the courts easily could have been avoided here had both parties actually taken to heart Justice Stone's admonitions in Townsend that ‘the statute requires that there be a serious effort at negotiation and informal resolution.’ (Townsend, supra, 61 Cal.App.4th at p. 1438.)  Perhaps after 11 years it is necessary to remind trial counsel and the bar once again that ‘[a]rgument is not the same as informal negotiation’ (id at p. 1437); that attempting informal resolution means more than the mere attempt by the discovery proponent ‘to persuade the objector of the error of his ways’ (id. at p. 1435); and that ‘a reasonable and good faith attempt at informal resolution entails something more than bickering with [opposing] counsel. . . .  Rather, the law requires that counsel attempt to talk the matter over, compare their views, consult, and deliberate.’ (Id. at p. 1439.)”  (Clement v. Alegre (2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 1277, 1294, emphasis in original, parallel citations omitted.) 

 

In practical terms, it means as follows.  It is entirely appropriate to begin the process with a letter or other correspondence.  The letter should not include a demand in the form of an ultimatum, but it can certainly include the relief that is being sought with an invitation to meet and confer.  It is also entirely appropriate for the other party to respond with a letter or other correspondence to set forth its position on the issues.  Such an initial exchange can often be helpful to narrow the dispute or at least articulate plainly the boundaries.  Whether further exchanges make sense will depend on each case and each dispute.

 

However, at some point before impasse is reached the process must generally include an actual “meet.”  The letters might well suffice to “confer,” but an exchange of correspondence is not a meeting.  In the COVID-19 era, the “meeting” can be virtual or telephonic, but a meeting there should be.  The meeting must be attended by a person from each side with the authority to agree to the other side’s position without getting further permission from anyone, including the client.  If only the client can give the needed authority, then the client must be available instantaneously at all times during the meeting.  This does not mean that one side or the other must concede, but it does mean that the people meeting have the ability in real and practical terms to strike a deal.

 

The parties should approach the meet and confer process as a problem-solving exercise.  The purpose of the meet and confer is not to convince the other side of the bankruptcy of its position; rather the purpose is to reach an agreement by which the party seeking discovery is able to obtain the information that it reasonably needs and the party providing discovery is not put to an undue burden or forced to provide unnecessary information that might infringe on a privacy interest or disclose a competitive trade secret or the like.

 

At the conclusion of the meet and confer, the parties should have an agreed statement as to the outcome.  If the outcome is a total impasse, then they should agree on that.  If they have resolved some or all of their differences, then they should state—in writing—what that agreement is so that there will be no confusion later.  Often, an agreement will be without prejudice to a further request by the propounding party for more information after that party receives the further responses.

 

If a motion is still required, the declaration in support should describe with some detail the meet and confer process.  While the court is not interested in the back and forth (and indeed describing it can be counter-productive), the court is interested in when the meeting took place, who was there, and how long it lasted.

 

All communications—in writing or oral—must be civil, polite, and professional.  Name-calling and accusations are devices that undermine the process; they do not further it.  A party engaging in such activity generally is not acting in a good faith effort to reach an agreement.

 

Informal Discovery Conferences

 

The court generally prefers an Informal Discovery Conference (IDC) before any party files a motion to compel further responses, and the failure to use this avenue of resolution can greatly affect the amount of sanctions that might be awarded because sanctions are limited to fees that are reasonable and necessary.  The goal of the IDC is to “get to yes.”  To achieve that goal, the court adopts the following.

 

  1. Five court days before the IDC, the parties will submit a JOINT statement no more than eight pages total (on pleading paper, but excluding the caption page) setting forth their respective positions.  Where a similar issue is raised that will resolve many requests, it can be dealt with as a single issue. 
  2. The IDC follows the good faith meet and confer.  It is not a substitute for the meet and confer.  The court’s time is to be spent only on issues after the parties have tried to narrow the dispute as much as possible.
  3. As stated above, the goal is to “get to yes.”  This means a few things.  One is that each party must be represented by a person who has the authority to agree to the other side’s position without getting further permission.  It need not be lead counsel and that authority need not be exercised.  But the people at the conference must have that authority.  If only the client has that authority, then the client will be present physically with counsel in a adjacent room without distraction after initial introductions are made.
  4. All IDC’s are “off the record.”  That means that whatever anyone says at the IDC will not be binding or quoted back at the party unless an agreement is struck.  It is very bad form to quote the court’s comments at an IDC during a later hearing unless the comments were part of a stipulated order.
  5. All parties have the right to change position if no deal is reached.  The court, for example, might strongly indicate that it believes one side or the other has the better position but rule in a completely different way if there is a formal motion.  Partial agreements that are contingent upon a full resolution are not binding unless a full resolution is reached.  Partial resolutions that are not contingent on a full resolution are binding.  Parties who are suggesting a resolution should make it clear whether their proposal depends upon other resolutions.
  6. Parties should be especially willing to give up sanctions at an IDC if it will resolve the dispute.  That is not a requirement, but the court’s experience is such that if a party is really going to insist on a sanction, the likelihood of a resolution is very low.
  7. It is likely that no party will get everything that it wants.  The goal is to abide the Code of Civil Procedure, but at the same time make sure that the party seeking discovery is not creating busy-work or demanding a lot of work for no benefit (or virtually no benefit).  The responding party might well have the right (should it come to motion practice) to avoid some aspect of the discovery, but in order to get to a resolution it might be in the responding party’s interest to be willing to offer something.  Conversely, the propounding party might have the right (should it come to motion practice) to get some aspect of discovery, but in order to get to a resolution it might be in the propounding party’s interest to forego aspects of the discovery that are not really needed, perhaps reserving the right to seek that information at a later time should it prove necessary.  The point is not to have the court simply hammer away at the opponent.  That might happen at a motion, but it is not the goal of the IDC.
  8. No orders will be made except on stipulation.  However, if there is an agreement, the court may well want to make an appropriate order or ask the parties to confirm it in writing.
  9. Parties should take careful notes on any agreements.  Because there are no Court Reporters, it is hard to put an agreement on the record.  However, unless the agreement is confirmed quickly, people have a tendency to forget or to have “buyer’s remorse.”  Therefore, it is best to have one party send an immediate confirming email to the other and obtain an agreement.  The court would prefer not to get into the specific wording of the agreement during the IDC unless absolutely necessary.
  10. The IDC process does not work if people view it as just a necessary step to filing a motion.  The court does not see it that way—if it did, then the court would abandon the IDC as a waste of time.  Rather, this is an off-ramp to avoid motion practice and the attendant costs and time.  Hopefully, it is also a way to get discovery more quickly than through formal motion practice.
  11. At the IDC, no matter how hot tempers have run in the past, the court expects all counsel to be polite and civil in tone to one another.  The court will not tolerate rudeness or unnecessary accusations.  They do not move the ball forward and they make it nigh impossible to reach an agreement.

Case Number: 26SMCV01195    Hearing Date: July 10, 2026    Dept: I

This is a motion to compel arbitration.  Plaintiff does not really oppose the motion for the most part.  The issue is this.  There are two potential contracts.  One is the Motor Vehicle Order Agreement (MVOA).  It states that any dispute will be determined by AAA.  There is also the later signed Retail Installment Sale Contract (RISC), which is required for purchases on credit.  The RISC states that either party may choose to have any dispute covered therein (which this would) will be arbitrated.  The RISC further provides that plaintiff “may choose the American Arbitration Association . . . or any other organization to conduct the arbitration subject to our approval.”  Defendant moves to have the matter arbitrated before AAA.  Plaintiff apparently would prefer a different organization, but the court is not sure which one.  Defendant apparently likes AAA.

 

The court will certainly GRANT the motion to compel arbitration.  The issue is the entity.  The court believes that the later-signed RISC supersedes the MVOA.  The question is whether the provision in the RISC that plaintiff may choose AAA or any other organization subject to approval means that AAA is automatically approved but defendant has the sole and absolute discretion to reject any other entity, or whether that discretion must be reasonable.  The court will begin, though, by asking plaintiff what specific organization plaintiff proposes.  Defendant might be ok with that, and if so, that’s the end of the inquiry.  If there is a disagreement, then the court will need to understand what the contract means and perhaps the reason why defendant still prefers AAA.  The court is hoping that the parties can agree on an agency to conduct the arbitration.   The court notes that plaintiff has not articulated any reason why AAA would not be a reasonable organization for the arbitration, so, absent some strong reason along those lines, and given that the court reads the RISC to the extent it is reasonable to do so to be consistent with the MVOA, the court would be inclined to GRANT the request that AAA be the organization overseeing the arbitration as well if push came to shove.
