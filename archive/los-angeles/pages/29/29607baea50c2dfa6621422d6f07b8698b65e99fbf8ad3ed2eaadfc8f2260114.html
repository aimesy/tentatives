25VECV02844 PARRA V TESLA

TENTATIVE] ORDER: Defendant Tesla Inc.’s Motion to Compel Arbitration and Stay the Action are GRANTED.

Defendants Sunrun Installation Services Inc. and Sunrun Inc.’s Request for Joinder is GRANTED.  However, Defendant Tesla Inc.’s Motion to Compel Arbitration and Stay the Action as related to Defendants Sunrun Installation Services Inc. and Sunrun Inc. is DENIED.  The action against Sunrun is not stayed.

The action as to Tesla only is stayed.  Arbitration Status Conference is scheduled for __________.

Introduction

Defendant Tesla Inc. (Defendant or Tesla) moved to compel arbitration and stay Plaintiff Leonardo Parra’s (Plaintiff) Complaint.  The claims alleged against Defendant are found in the second cause of action (COA) for breach of contract, the fourth COA for negligence, the sixth COA for breach of express warranty, the eighth COA for breach of the implied duty of good faith and fair dealing, the ninth COA for negligent misrepresentation, and the tenth COA for declaratory relief.

[There is a typo in the motion.  Defendant cited to “Hyundai Amco America, Inc. v S3H Inc. (2014) 242 Cal.4th 574”.  (Mtn. pg. 3:11-12.)  The correct citation is 342 Cal.App.4th 574. To avoid sanctions for AI hallucinations, it is important to proofread citations.]

Procedure

Defendants Sunrun Installation Services Inc. and Sunrun Inc. (collectively, Sunrun) requested joinder to Defendant Tesla’s motion to compel arbitration and motion to stay the action.  The request for joinder is granted.  However, the compelling of arbitration and the staying of the action, as applied to Sunrun, is DENIED because Sunrun is not seen as a contracting party to the agreement entered between Plaintiff and Tesla.

Discussion

Whether decided under Federal law or California law, the trial courts must decide first whether an enforceable arbitration agreement exists between the parties and then determine the second gateway issue whether the claims are covered within the scope of the agreement.  (9 U.S.C., sec. 2 et seq.;Code Civ. Proc., sec. 1281 et seq.; Howsamv. Dean Witter Reynolds, Inc. (2002) 537 U.S. 79, 84;  Hyundai Amco America, Inc. v. S3H, Inc. (2014) 232 Cal.App.4th 572, 577.)  Once it is determined that an enforceable arbitration agreement exists, the burden is on the party opposing arbitration to demonstrate that an arbitration agreement is unenforceable or invalid. (Rent-A-Center, West, Inc. v. Jackson (2010) 561 U.S. 63, 70-71; Buckhorn v. St. Jude Heritage Medical Group (2004) 121 Cal.App.4th 1401, 1406.) 

Defendant provided that the contract to install solar panels with Plaintiff (Tesla Contract) entered on January 24, 2023, included an arbitration agreement.  (Compl. par. 40, Exh. D at par. 14 and Daniell Newman Decl. par. 3, Exh A.)   Defendant provided a sufficient showing that an arbitration agreement exists between Defendant and Plaintiff.  The burden shifts to Plaintiff to provide that the arbitration agreement is unenforceable.

Plaintiff asserted that the tort claims are based upon non-contractual duties and the tort claims do not have their “roots” in the contractual relationship between the parties, specifically the fourth COA for negligence and the ninth COA for negligent misrepresentation.  Plaintiff further argued that tort claims do not arise out of the contract.  The claims alleged against Tesla involve failure to properly install the solar panel (i.e., water intrusion from improper solar panel installation and damage to roof tiles, driveway, and copper rain gutters) and involve the failure of the system to provide the benefits promised (i.e., a reduction in amount charged on Plaintiff’s electrical bills).  (Compl. pars. 40-56.)  However, the arbitration agreement is seen to be broadly worded because it includes disputes with Defendant “arising out of or relating to any aspect of this Agreement or your purchase of the System”.  Generally, the scope of an arbitration agreement is narrow when the phrase “relating to this agreement” is excluded from the terms of the arbitration agreement.  (Rice v. Down (2016) 248 Cal.App.4th 175, 186 & 189 (Rice).)  When the arbitration agreement only includes language stating, “arising out of”, the phrase have been interpreted as to only encompassing disputes relating to the interpretation or performance of the agreement.  Because Defendant’s arbitration agreement included “relating to” language, the provision is interpreted as being broad, to include the alleged tort claims.  Plaintiff’s reliance on the Rice opinion is misplaced, and the opinion does not support Plaintiff’s contention.  Plaintiff’s argument as to the tort claims not being covered by the arbitration agreement is not persuasive. 

Further, the Rice Court reviewed the arbitration agreement in context with the whole agreement, which involved an operating agreement for a property development company.  The Rice Court opined that an operating agreement for a property development company does not include contractual obligations that involve legal services.  The legal malpractice claims at issue in the Rice case were found not to be within the scope of the operating agreement but within the scope of a separate attorney-client relationship.  Based upon this distinction, the Rice Court found that the legal malpractice claims were not within the scope of the operating agreement’s arbitration agreement.  In the instant action, the tort claims for damages are expressly caused by the faulty installation of the solar panels. The express context of the purchase and installation agreement is for the same solar panels.  Because the tort damages are directly related to the purchase/installation contract at issue, the tort claims are within the scope of the arbitration agreement.  Plaintiff’s argument attempting to separate out the tort COAs is not persuasive.

Plaintiff further argued that the arbitration agreement acknowledged that tenth COA for declaratory relief is non-arbitrable.  However, Plaintiff failed to present the specific language that shows that the arbitration agreement carved out an exception for declaratory relief claims.  A plain reading of the arbitration agreement showed that there was a potential for the Court or an arbitrator to determine that declaratory relief cannot be enforced.  However, the arbitration agreement did not expressly carve out of the agreement.  Plaintiff failed to present supportive facts or law to support their contention that declaratory relief claims are carved out.  The argument is unpersuasive.

Plaintiff argued that there is potential for a conflict in rulings because the remaining claims alleged against Sunrun and Defendant LA Roofing & Builder Group Inc. dba Bumble Roofing (Bumble) are to be tried before this Court.  The Court previously denied Sunrun/Bumble’s motion to compel arbitration.  The Court is not convinced that sending the claims against Tesla to arbitration and keeping the claims against Sunrun with this Court presents a potential for conflicting rulings.  The allegations against Sunrun and Tesla are distinct as to the alleged breaches and alleged timing of the disputes.  The contract and tort claims against Sunrun arose in 2022.  The contract and tort claims against Tesla arose in 2023.  It is undisputed that both Sunrun and Tesla allegedly caused damage to Plaintiff’s roof (and other property) and caused water intrusion.  However, the location of the damage is different.  Sunrun allegedly failed to seal the roof’s ridge line from one end of the roof to the other.  (Compl. par. 29.)  Tesla is alleged to have failed to seal the hole/bolts that connected the solar panels to the roof.  (Compl. par. 46.)  It is noted that Sunrun never installed the agreed upon solar panels.  The claims against Sunrun are for negligent preparation of the roof to install the solar panels but no solar panels were ever installed.  The alleged breaches are different.  Common issues may arise when causation and/or damages are reviewed.  But the facts, as alleged in the Complaint, show that causation and damages are distinctly alleged.  Plaintiff expressly alleged an approximately nine-month gap between the water intrusion caused by Sunrun and Tesla (i.e., December 2022 and fall 2023.)  (Compl. pars. 32, 47, and 48.)  Further, the damage caused by Tesla in the fall of 2023 was to Plaintiff’s “new wood flooring, wood ceilings, carpets, and brand-new furniture.”  (Compl. par. 47 (emphasis added).)  The express allegation of the newness of the property gives an inference showing that the damage previously done by Sunrun was repaired and the damages caused by Tesla is different from the damages caused by Sunrun.  Plaintiff’s fact allegations as to the causation and damages are sufficient to show that there is a low likelihood of conflicting rulings. 

Plaintiff argued that there is no evidence to show Plaintiff’s intent to arbitrate all his claims.  However, Defendant’s initial burden on the motion is to only present the existence of an arbitration agreement.  The burden transferred to Plaintiff to show that the arbitration agreement is unenforceable.  With the transfer of the burden, it is Plaintiff who must show that there was no intent to arbitrate all his claims.  Plaintiff failed to present any evidence to support the contention that Plaintiff lacked intent.  Because there is no evidence to support the contention, Plaintiff’s argument is unpersuasive. 

Plaintiff failed to meet his burden to show that the Tesla arbitration agreement is unenforceable.

Tesla’s motion to compel arbitration and stay the action is GRANTED. Sunrun's request to join the arbitration is DENIED.

IT IS SO ORDERED, CLERK TO GIVE NOTICE.

25VECV03709 Rivas Diaz v VW

[TENTATIVE] ORDER: Plaintiff Erika Yamilet Rivas Diaz’s Motion for Attorneys’ Fees is GRANTED at the reduced amount of $11,251.50.

The Court sets an OSC re Monetary Sanctions of $750.00 to be levied against  Attorney Kevin Jacobson, individually, and $750.00 to be levied against Attorney Alex McKeon, individually.  Sanctions to be payable to the Court.  The OSC is scheduled for  ______________, 2026. Opposition per Code.

Introduction

Plaintiff Erika Yamilet Rivas Diaz (Plaintiff) moved for attorneys’ fees against Defendant Volkswagen Group of America, Inc. (Defendant).  The total in attorneys’ fees requested is $22,650.50, which includes: (1) $14,920.00 in attorneys’ fees; (2) $3,730.00 for a 1.25 multiplier; and (3) an additional $4,000.00 for fees to review the opposition, draft the reply and appear at the hearing. 

Procedure

In the motion, Plaintiff cited to “Avila v. Ford Motor Company 2024 WL 311081 (C.D. Cal. May 15, 2024).”  (Motion, pgs. iv:2-3 and 9:18.)  However, the Court could not locate the opinion either by searching the case name or the case citation.  The citation appears to be a hallucination.  Further, Plaintiff cited to “Nightingale v. Hyundai Motor America (1994) 31 Cal.App.4th 807”.  (Emphasis added.)  (Motion pgs. v:4-5 and 8:16-17.)  The citation is incorrect and the correct citation is “31 Cal.App.4th 99”.  The incorrect citation is seen as a hallucination also. 

In the opposition, Defendant also cited to “Nightingale, 4 Cal.App.5th at 462”.  (Emphasis added.)  (Opposition pg. 14:16.)  However, this citation also is incorrect.  As stated above the correct citation is “31 Cal.App.4th 99”.  The Court finds that the incorrect citation to the Nightingale case to be a hallucination.  Further, Defendant cited to “Donahue v. Donahue (2010) 184 Cal.App.4th 259, 271”.  (Emphasis added.)  (Opposition pg.14:17-18.)  The correct citation to the Donahue opinion is “182 Cal.App.4th 259”.  The Court finds the incorrect citation to the Donahue opinion to be a hallucination. 

For the above hallucinations, the Court sets an OSC re: Sanctions of $750.00 to be levied against Attorney Kevin Jacobson, individually, and $750.00 to be levied against Attorney Alex McKeon, individually.  (Noland v. Land of the Free LP (2025) 114 Cal.App.5th 426, 444-448.)  The sanctions if levied are payable to the Court.

Discussion

Plaintiff asserted entitlement to attorneys’ fees as the prevailing party under Civil Code, section 1794(d).  There is no dispute that Plaintiff is the prevailing party. 

Statutory attorney fees are ordinarily determined by the court pursuant to the “lodestar” or “touchstone” method. However, the Court’s discretion is limited in fee awards under the SBA.  The statute takes a different approach from customary statutory or contractual provisions.  The statute requires an “initial determination of the actual time expended; and then to ascertain whether under all the circumstances of the case the amount of actual time expended and the monetary charge being made for the time expended are reasonable.  These circumstances may include, but are not limited to, factors such as the complexity of the case and procedural demands, the skill exhibited and the results achieved. If the time expended or the monetary charge being made for the time expended is not reasonable under all the circumstances, then the court must take this into account and award attorney fees in a lesser amount.” In the situation of a contingency fee arrangement, the court in Nightingale stated, “for purposes of section 1794, subdivision (d), a prevailing buyer represented by counsel is entitled to an award of reasonable attorney fees for time reasonably expended by his or her attorney.”  (Robertson v. Fleetwood Travel Trailers of California, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 785, 817-818 (citing to Nightingale v. Hyundai Motor America (1994) 31 Cal.App.4th 99, 104-105, fn. 6.)  In making a lodestar calculation of proper hourly rates to apply, “the court may rely on its own knowledge and familiarity with the legal market, as well as the experience, skill, and reputation of the attorney requesting fees (Heritage Pacific Financial, LLC v. Monroy (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 972, 1009), the difficulty or complexity of the litigation to which that skill was applied (Syers Properties [III, Inc. v. Rankin (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 691,] at p. 700) . . . and affidavits [from plaintiffs’ attorneys and] from other attorneys regarding prevailing fees in the community and rate determinations in other cases. (Heritage, at p. 1009.)” (569 East County Boulevard LLC v. Backcountry Against the Dump, Inc. (2016) 6 Cal.App.5th 426, 437.)

Plaintiff asserted that representation was retained on a contingency basis.  (Kevin Jacobson (Jacobson) Decl. pars. 72 and 93.)  Plaintiff asserted that fees would have been calculated at 32 hours for time charged by several attorneys: (1) Donald Mahnke (8.2 hours), (2) Stephen Basinger (7.7 hours), (3) Ningjing Jiang (7.1 hours), (4) Joshua Kohanoff (.6 hours), (5) Gary Sobel (1.9 hours), (6) Solange Tadros (1.2 hours), and (7) Derek Chipman (5.3 hours).  (Jacobson Decl. pars. 48-61, Exh. 43.)  The Court finds that the time expended by the seven attorneys to be excessive because the action was not heavily litigated with law and motion work specific to Defendant.  The Court was only called upon to resolve a singular law & motion matter, which did not involve Defendant.  A demurrer was filed by Co-Defendant DCH Calabasas-A LLC.  Because the demurrer did not involve Defendant, the Court strikes the request for fees related to Co-Defendant DCH Calabasas-A LLC’s demurrer (5.9  hours total or $3,510.00).  The action also involved three motions to compel furthers filed against Defendant but became moot due to the settlement.  It is noted that Plaintiff prepared the discovery motions mid-October 2025, while Plaintiff was in possession of Defendant’s Code of Civil Procedure section 998 Offer to Compromise (998 Offer).  Defendant served their 998 Offer on September 18, 2025, and Plaintiff’s invoice showed that the 998 Offer appears to have been first reviewed on October 3, 2025.  (Jacobson Decl., Exh. 43 at pg. 6; Alex McKeon Decl. par. 5, Exh. A.)  The Court finds it unreasonable that Plaintiff prepared the discovery motions while Defendant’s 998 Offer was pending and/or possibly was not reviewed/communicated to Plaintiff when received in September 2025.  The time expended on the discovery motions after receipt of the 998 Offer is stricken from the fees’ calculation (7.1 hours total or $2,485.00).  Further, this action also was resolved within four months of filing and/or two months from Defendant’s first appearance.  The action was filed on July 3, 2025.  The Answer was filed on August 13, 2025.  The notice of settlement was filed on October 31, 2025.  Because action was settled relatively quickly, the Court finds the total amount requested is unreasonable.  The Court reduces the requested fees of $11,410.00 by a total of $2,485.00.  The total fees are $8,925.00.

Plaintiff further requested an adjusted, $2,326.50 for reviewing the opposition, drafting the reply, and appearing at the hearing.  The Court finds the adjusted amount in fees to be reasonable.

Plaintiff’s total fees awardable is $11,251.50.

Plaintiff lastly requested a 1.25 multiplier, which amounted to $3,730.00.  In determining to augment the fees by a multiplier the Court takes into consideration “various relevant factors into account, including (1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved and the skill displayed in presenting them; (2) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys; and (3) the contingent nature of the fee award, based on the uncertainty of prevailing on the merits and of establishing eligibility for the award.”  (Robertson v. Fleetwood Travel Trailers of California, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 785, 819; Serrano v. Priest (Serrano III) (1977) 20 Cal.3d 25, 49.)  Plaintiff has the burden of proof to show that the enhancement is primarily to compensate counsel at a rate reflecting the risk of nonpayment in contingency cases as a class.  (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1138 (Ketchum).)  The aim is to compensate attorneys at fair market value as an inducement to accept these cases.  (Id.)  “A trial court should award a multiplier for exceptional representation only when the quality of representation far exceeds the quality of representation that would have been provided by an attorney of comparable skill and experience billing at the hourly rate used in the lodestar calculation. Otherwise, the fee award will result in unfair double counting and be unreasonable.  Nor should a fee enhancement be imposed for the purpose of punishing the losing party.”  (Ketchum, supra, 24 Cal.4th at p. 1139.)  The Court further has the discretion to adjust the lodestar downward where appropriate.  (Ketchum, supra, 24 Cal.4th at p. 1132, Chavez v. Netflix, Inc. (2008) 162 Cal.App.4th 43, 64.)  The representation of Plaintiff did not exceed the quality of representation that would have been provided by an attorney of comparable skill and experience billing at the hourly rates charged.  There was no presentation of any new or novel legal theory to promote Plaintiff’s position.  Litigation with Defendant was not contentious.  The Court file does not show much in the way of law and motion disputes with Defendant, other than the unreasonable discovery motions which became moot.  Further, the fact that this action took about four months to prosecute, defend, and/or settle also shows the non-complex nature of the action.  Lastly, the fact that seven attorneys could seamlessly work on the case and take on different sections of prosecuting the action further shows the non-complexity of the case.  On these grounds, the Court declines to award a fee enhancement. 

The requested multiplier of 1.25 ($3,730.00) is DENIED.

Plaintiff’s Motion for Attorneys’ Fees is GRANTED at the reduced amount of $11,251.50.

IT IS SO ORDERED, CLERK TO GIVE NOTICE.
