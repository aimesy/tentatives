DEPARTMENT 509 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 22STCV29823    Hearing Date: September 16, 2026    Dept: 509

Otter Lake Farm, LLC v. Al Mizrahie, et al.

JUDGMENT CREDITOR’S MOTION FOR ATTORNEY’S FEES, COSTS, AND EXPENSES
  

MOVING PARTY: Judgment Creditor Otter Lake Farm, LLC

RESPONDING PARTY(S): Judgment Debtor Al Mizrahie

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

Otter Lake Farm, LLC, (Judgment Creditor) brought this unlawful detainer action against defendants Al Mizrahie and Alma Mizrahie (Judgment Debtors) for possession of commercial property. 

On February 9, 2023, Judgment Creditor obtained a stipulated unlawful detainer judgment.

In September 2025, the parties entered into a Conditional Post Judgment Occupancy Agreement that allowed Judgment Debtors to continue their occupancy while preserving the stipulated judgment. 

On March 25, 2026, Judgment Creditor sought issuance of a writ of possession to enforce the judgment. Judgment Debtors opposed, arguing, among other things, that Judgment Creditor had created a new tenancy through the Agreement, that Judgment Creditor was required to prosecute a new unlawful detainer action. 

On May 18, 2026, Judgment Debtors’ Request to Stay Writ of Possession was denied, and this Court lifted the stay of execution.

On June 15, 2026, Judgment Creditor filed this instant motion to recover their reasonable attorney’s fees and costs.

On July 8, 2026, the Court continued this motion and ordered Judgment Creditor to properly serve both Judgment Debtors. On July 10, 2026, Judgment Creditor properly served both Judgment Debtors in the manner prescribed in the July 8, 2026, Minute Order. (7/13/26 Notice.) Al Mizrahie opposed. Alma Mizrahie did not file an opposition.

The Court now rules considering the entire record before it.
  
TENTATIVE RULING:

Judgment Creditor’s Motion for Attorney’s Fees is GRANTED in the amount of $8,100.00.

Judgment Creditor’s Motion for Costs is GRANTED in the amount of $163.50.

Judgment Creditor is ordered to give notice.  

DISCUSSION: 

Motion for Attorney’s Fees, Costs, and Expenses

I. Analysis

A. Entitlement to Fees 

On September 30, 2025, the parties entered into a Conditional Post-Judgment Occupancy Agreement (Agreement). This Agreement stated that “In any action or proceeding arising out of or related to this Agreement, the prevailing party shall be entitled to recover its reasonable attorney s fees and costs.” (Pena Decl. Exh. 2 at §10.) 

Judgment Creditor now moves for an order awarding attorney’s fee of $8,100 and $163.50 in costs arising from the enforcement of the writ of possession opposed by the Judgment Debtors. 

Code of Civil Procedure, section 685.080, allows for post-judgment collection of fees and here, the Agreement governs such collection. In opposing the writ of possessions, Judgment Debtors argued, in part that “Plaintiff and Defendants Created A New Periodic Tenancy that Requires a New Unlawful Detainer Action to Terminate.” (4/8/26 Defendants’ Memorandum of Points and Authorities at p. 6:8-9.) As this proceeding related to the interpretation of the Agreement, the attorney’s fees provision in the Agreement applies. Further, there can be no dispute that Judgment Creditor was the prevailing party in the subsequent proceedings. (See 5/19/26 Order.)

Accordingly, Judgment Creditor is entitled to an award of reasonable attorney fees as the prevailing party under the Agreement.  

B. Reasonableness of Fees (Lodestar)

The determination of reasonable amount of attorney fees is within the sound discretion of trial courts. (PLCM Group v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095; Akins v. Enterprise Rent-A-Car Co. (2000) 79 Cal.App.4th 1127, 1134.) “The determination of what constitutes a reasonable fee generally ‘begins with the ‘lodestar,’ i.e., the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate….’”  “[T]he lodestar is the basic fee for comparable legal services in the community; it may be adjusted by the court based on factors including, as relevant herein, (1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, (4) the contingent nature of the fee award….” (Graciano v. Robinson Ford Sales, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 140, 154.) In setting the hourly rate for an attorney fees award, courts are entitled to consider the rate of “‘fees customarily charged by that attorney and others in the community for similar work.’” (Bihun v. AT&T Information Systems, Inc. (1993) 13 Cal.App.4th 976, 997 [affirming rate of $450 per hour], overruled on other grounds by Lakin v. Watkins Associated Indus. (1993) 6 Cal.4th 644, 664.) The burden is on the party seeking attorney fees to prove reasonableness of the fees. (Center for Biological Diversity v. County of San Bernardino (2010) 188 Cal.App.4th 603, 615.)
 
The Court has broad discretion in determining the amount of a reasonable attorney’s fee award which will not be overturned absent a “manifest abuse of discretion, a prejudicial error of law, or necessary findings not supported by substantial evidence.” (Bernardi v. County of Monterey (2008) 167 Cal. App. 4th 1379, 1393-94.) The Court need not explain its calculation of the amount of attorney’s fees awarded in detail; identifying the factors considered in arriving at the amount will suffice. (Ventura v. ABM Industries Inc. (2012) 212 Cal.App.4th 258, 274-75.)

Here, Judgment Creditor seeks $8,100 in attorney’s fees for 21.6 hours of work at $375 per hour as well as costs in the amount of $163.50. (Peña Decl. ¶19, Ex 12.)

Judgement Debtors did not formally challenge the reasonableness of Judgment Creditor’s attorney fees. It was their burden to do so. (See, e.g., Lunada Biomedical v. Nunez (2014) 230 Cal.App.4th 459, 488 [“[I]t is the burden of the challenging party to point to the specific items challenged [within the moving party’s verified billing invoice], with a sufficient argument and citations to evidence.”]) Instead, the opposition generally discusses Al Mizrahie’s financial condition and the burden such an award would place on his finances. (Opp. at pp. 1-3.) However, even had Judgment Debtors sufficiently challenged the reasonableness of the fees or costs requested, the Court would still find Judgment Creditor’s requested fees to be a reasonable time spent on all tasks in the matter including the time spent in support of this instant motion and the anticipated time spent at the hearings.

Further, Judgment Debtors have not challenged the reasonableness of Judgment Creditor’s costs, and all costs appear recoverable on their face.

Accordingly, Judgment Creditor’s Motion for Attorney’s Fees is GRANTED in the amount of $8,100. 

Judgment Creditor’s Motion for Costs is GRANTED in the amount of $163.50.

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Case Number: 24STCV18083    Hearing Date: September 16, 2026    Dept: 509

Jose R. Solano v. Orange Kangaroo, LLC, et al.

(1) MOTION REQUIRING PLAINTIFF TO FURNISH SECURITY

(2) DEMURRER TO COMPLAINT

(3) MOTION TO STRIKE

(4) DEMURRER TO COMPLAINT
 

MOVING PARTY: (1): Defendants Defendant Orange Kangaroo, LLC; Harrison S. Won; Yoomi Won; and Yosemite Lopez
(2) & (3): Defendant Orange Kangaroo, LLC; Harrison S. Won; and Yoomi Won
(4): Defendant Yosemite Lopez

RESPONDING PARTY(S): (1)-(3): Plaintiff Jose R. Solano; (4) None

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

Plaintiff Jose R. Solano (Plaintiff) brings this action against defendants Orange Kangaroo, LLC, Harrison S. Won, Yoomi Won, and Yosemite Lopez, (collectively, Defenadants) seeking quiet title to the property at 5502 Dobbs St. #74, Los Angeles, CA 90032. Plaintiff brings causes of action for: (1) quiet title; (2) fraud; (3) slander of title; (4) cancellation of written instrument; and (5) declaratory judgment.

There are three actions currently pending between the parties:
-Unlawful Detainer Action: Orange Kangaroo, LLC v. Jose R. Solano, et al. (LASC No. 24STUD06273.)

-Civil Quiet Title Action: Jose R. Solano v. Orange Kangaroo, LLC (LASC No. 25STCV23323.) filed August 8, 2025, during the stay in this action.

-Federal Action: Jose R. Solano v. Orange Kangaroo, LLC, et al. (US Central District, Case No: 8:24-cv-01587-DMG-PP.)

Defendants Orange Kangaroo, LLC, Harrison S. Won, and Yoomi Won (Orange Kangaroo) brought a demurrer to the Complaint. Plaintiff opposed. 

On December 19, 2024, this Court stayed this matter in the entirety pending the Plaintiff’s bankruptcy proceedings.

On June 22, 2026, this Court lifted the stay and rescheduled this motion to July 2, 2026. 

On July 2, 2026, Defendants noted that Plaintiff has been declared a vexatious litigant and they requested Plaintiff post bond. The Court continued the hearing to allow Defendants to file further briefing on the issue of whether Plaintiff is required to furnish the bond. 

On August 8, 2026, the Court heard argument on the request for Plaintiff to post bond. The Court deemed all “papers filed by defendant regarding the issue of plaintiff posting a bond to be a motion” and allowed Plaintiff’s supplemental opposition to be filed by September 11, 2026. (8/4/26 Minute Order.) 

On September 10, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition to Defendants’ Motion Requiring Plaintiff to Furnish Security.

The Court now considers the entirety of the record in addressing the motions.

TENTATIVE RULING:

Defendants’ Motion to Furnish Security is GRANTED. 

The amount of security in the instant action is set as $20,000.00 to be furnished within 30 days of this order. If Plaintiff fails to adequately furnish said security within 30 days, the action shall be dismissed pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure, section 391.4.

An OSC Re: Dismissal for Plaintiff’s Failure to Post the $20,000.00 Bond is scheduled for October 19, 2026; 8:30 a.m.

Orange Kangaroo’s Demurrer and Motion to Strike and Yosemite Lopez’s Demurrer are CONTINUED to October 19, 2026. If Plaintiff posts the required bond, both demurrers will be calendared for hearing. 

Defendants to give notice.

DISCUSSION:

Motion Requiring Plaintiff to Furnish Security

I. Judicial Notice

Orange Kangaroo requests judicial notice of: The October 21, 2025, Minute Order in, Jose R. Solano v. Orange Kangaroo, LLC (LASC No. 25STCV23323), the Dismissal of the bankruptcy of Jose Solano, Judgment and Findings which duplicate the allegations against these Defendants in this case but filed in the United States District Court, Additional Court Findings by District Court Judge Bernal, and the Vexatious Litigation Order (7/20/26 RJN.)

Pursuant to Orange Kangaroos’ request and without objection, the Court GRANTS Orange Kangaroos’ request for judicial notice. 

Yosemite Lopez requests judicial notice of a further thirty-two exhibits. (7/28/26 RJN.)

Pursuant to Yosemite Lopez’s request and without objection, the Court GRANTS Yosemite Lopez’s request for judicial notice. 


II. Legal Standard 
 
Code of Civil Procedure, section 391.1(a) provides: 
 
In any litigation pending in any court of this state, at any time until final judgment is entered, a defendant may move the court, upon notice and hearing, for an order requiring the plaintiff to furnish security or for an order dismissing the litigation pursuant to subdivision (b) of Section 391.3. The motion for an order requiring the plaintiff to furnish security shall be based upon the ground, and supported by a showing, that the plaintiff is a vexatious litigant and that there is not a reasonable probability that they will prevail in the litigation against the moving defendant. 
 
In determining whether the plaintiff has a reasonable probability of prevailing, the “court shall consider any evidence, written or oral, by witnesses or affidavit, as may be material to the ground of the motion.” (Code of Civ. Pro. § 391.2.) “[I]f, after hearing the evidence upon the motion, the court determines that the plaintiff is a vexatious litigant and that there is no reasonable probability that the plaintiff will prevail in the litigation against the moving defendant, the court shall order the plaintiff to furnish, for the benefit of the moving defendant, security in such amount and within such time as the court shall fix.” (Code of Civ. Pro. § 391.3(a).) 
 
Thus, “[i]n pending litigation, a defendant may have the plaintiff declared a vexatious litigant and, if the plaintiff has no reasonable probability of prevailing, ordered to furnish security. If the plaintiff fails to furnish the security, the action will be dismissed.” (Shalant v. Girardi (2011) 51 Cal.4th 1164, 1171 [citing §§ 391.1–391.6].)  
 
In moving for an order requiring a vexatious litigant to post security, the defendant has the burden of showing there is no reasonable likelihood plaintiff will prevail in the action. (Golin v. Allenby (2010) 190 Cal.App.4th 616, 640, 642.) “A court’s decision that a vexatious litigant does not have a reasonable chance of success in the action is based on an evaluative judgment in which the court weighs the evidence.” (Id. at p. 636.) 
 
III. Analysis 
 
A. Background 
 
Defendants move to require Plaintiff furnish security in the amount of $20,000.00. 

This is, in essence, a quiet title action in which Plaintiff alleges that on May 1, 2024, Defendants recorded a fraudulent “Trustee Deed Upon Sale.” Plaintiff alleges the “Trustee’s Deed Upon Sale is wholly void due to fraud, irregularity in foreclosure procedures, lack of proper notice, and fictitious or fabricated consideration.” (Compl. at p.3)
 
There are two other actions between the parties dealing with the same real property Plaintiff seeks ownership in this action:
-Civil Quiet Title Action: Jose R. Solano v. Orange Kangaroo, LLC (LASC No. 25STCV23323.) filed August 8, 2025, during the stay in this action.

-Federal Action: Jose R. Solano v. Orange Kangaroo, LLC, et al. (US Central District, Case No: 8:24-cv-01587-DMG-PP.)

In Jose R. Solano v. Orange Kangaroo, LLC (LASC No. 25STCV23323), the court dismissed the action after Plaintiff failed to post bond pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure, section 391.4. (RJN Ex., 1.)

In Jose R. Solano v. Orange Kangaroo, LLC, et al. (US Central District, Case No: 8:24-cv-01587-DMG-PP) the action was ordered dismissed pursuant to Orange Kangaroo’s Motion to Dismiss. (RJN Ex., 2.) 

As a threshold issue, Plaintiff has already been declared a vexatious litigant in Jose R. Solano v. Orange Kangaroo, LLC (LASC No. 25STCV23323). (See 10/21/25 Minute Order and Prefiling Order in 25STCV23323; see Orange Kangaroo RJN Ex. 4.) That element is met. (Hupp v. Solera Oak Valley Greens Assn. (2017) 12 Cal.App.5th 1300, 1311 [“Where a plaintiff has already been declared vexatious, a defendant moving under section 391.7 need not again establish the plaintiff’s status.”])

As a result, the Court turns to Plaintiff’s probability of prevailing in this action. 
 
B. Plaintiff’s Probability of Prevailing 
 
Defendants argue it is highly likely that they, and not Plaintiff, will prevail in the underlying case. The Court agrees.
 
First, the issue presented in Plaintiff’s complaint was examined in Jose R. Solano v. Orange Kangaroo, LLC, et al. (US Central District, Case No: 8:24-cv-01587-DMG-PP) in which the Federal Court dismissed Plaintiff’s quiet title claim. Then Plaintiff filed the action in Jose R. Solano v. Orange Kangaroo, LLC (LASC No. 25STCV23323) in which Plaintiff sought the exact same relief. That action was dismissed after the court there found Plaintiff’s claims were unlikely to succeed. Indeed, Plaintiff had two separate opportunities to fully litigate this proceeding and in both, Plaintiff’s action did proceed beyond the challenges to the pleadings. There is no evidence that Plaintiff’s action will lend itself to a different result in this action and it appears from those records, that Defendants are highly likely to succeed in defense of this action.

Second, Plaintiff’s complaint and subsequent filings are devoid of any factual allegations. Plaintiff broadly alleges terms such as “fraud” and “civil conspiracy” without any facts giving rise to such. There is currently nothing alleged in the complaint that would give rise to a reasonably viable claim nor does any of the documents filed in this action suggest any claim is viable. 

In opposition, Plaintiff alleges that:

The documentary packet contains multiple recorded instruments that form the asserted foreclosure chain, including the original 2006 Deed of Trust, a 2012 Assignment of Deed of Trust recorded as Instrument No. 20120373737, a Notice of Default, a Notice of Trustee's Sale recorded December 19, 2023, and the May 1, 2024 Trustee’s Deed Upon Sale. The Trustee's Deed Upon Sale is followed by post-foreclosure possession documents. A three/ninety-day notice to quit dated May 10, 2024 was directed to Jose R. Solano, Soledad M. Solano, and other occupants of the premises, and identified Orange Kangaroo, LLC as owner. This sequence shows that Orange Kangaroo’s claim to possession flowed from the title it asserted through the Trustee’s Deed Upon Sale. These documents create a direct evidentiary bridge between the disputed foreclosure instruments, the resulting Trustee’s Deed Upon Sale, Orange Kangaroo’s claimed ownership, and the effort to remove Plaintiff from the property. Because Plaintiff challenges the legal validity of that bridge, the action presents a live and material title controversy for determination on the merits.

(Opp. at pp. 5:22-6:11.)

As in the complaint, Plaintiff makes the same accusations without any facts supporting the allegation that the trustee sale was invalid. Plaintiff has filed numerous actions, opposed an unlawful detainer, and filed this complaint without ever presenting a single factual allegation, let alone evidence, to support his claim that the Trustee Sale is void. The Court must reasonably conclude that there exists no factual basis to support these claims. Therefore, it is highly likely that Defendants prevail in this action.

Third, and most persuasive, Defendants have put forth overwhelming evidence that Plaintiff’s allegations in the complaint are baseless. (See e.g. Yosemite Lopez RJN, Exs. 1-14.) Defendants have provided substantial evidence that the property transfer at was done in compliance with applicable laws and Plaintiff has no grounds to seek quiet title to the property or for damages flowing from the transaction. Despite the numerous hearings, Plaintiff has provided no evidence in support of his claims but rather relies solely on legal jargon and general allegations of misconduct as discussed above. Plaintiff was given the right to present evidence and has been given numerous opportunities to do. (See Bravo v. Ismaj (2002) 99 Cal.App.4th 211, 223.) Instead, Plaintiff has presented none. 

In the end, Defendants have provided overwhelming evidence in support of their defense to this action while Plaintiff has failed to demonstrate any viability to his complaint. The Court finds that it is highly likely that Defendants, and not Plaintiff, will prevail in this action.

Accordingly, Defendants’ Motion to Furnish Security is GRANTED. 

The amount of security in the instant action is set as $20,000.00. If Plaintiff fails to adequately furnish said security within 30 days, the action “shall be dismissed as to the defendant for whose benefit it was ordered furnished.” (Golin v. Allenby (2010) 190 Cal.App.4th 616, 634.)

An OSC Re: Dismissal for Plaintiff’s Failure to Post the $20,000.00 Bond is therefore scheduled for October 19, 2026.

Orange Kangaroo’s Demurrer and Motion to Strike and Yosemite Lopez’s Demurrer

“If the court issues an order to furnish security, the action is automatically stayed from the time the motion was filed until 10 days after plaintiff posts the required security.” (Golin v. Allenby, supra, 190 Cal.App.4th at p. 634; Code Civ. Pro. §391.6.) 

Accordingly, Orange Kangaroo’s Demurrer and Motion to Strike and Yosemite Lopez’s Demurrer are CONTINUED to October 19, 2026. If Plaintiff posts the required bond, both demurrers will be calendared for hearing. 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Case Number: 25STCV19410    Hearing Date: September 16, 2026    Dept: 509

Christine Rasho, et al. v. General Motors, LLC

PLAINTIFFS’ MOTION FOR ATTORNEY’S FEES, COSTS, AND EXPENSES
  
MOVING PARTY: Plaintiffs Christine Rasho and Banipal Shamoon

RESPONDING PARTY(S): Defendant General Motors, LLC

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

Plaintiffs Christine Rasho and Banipal Shamoon (collectively, Plaintiffs) allege they purchased a defective vehicle from defendant General Motors, LLC (Defendant). Plaintiff filed this action for breach of warranty under the Song-Beverly Act.

On March 5, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a Notice of Settlement of Entire Case. 

On May 4, 2026, the Court ordered the complaint dismissed without prejudice and reserved jurisdiction in accordance with Code of Civil Procedure, section 664.6 for enforcement purposes, including motions.

On August 11, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a Motion for Attorney Fees and a Memorandum of Costs. Defendant opposed.
  
TENTATIVE RULING:

Plaintiffs’ Motion for Attorney’s Fees is GRANTED in the amount of $13,649.48.

Plaintiffs’ Motion for Costs is GRANTED in the amount of $2,629.28.

Plaintiffs are ordered to give notice, unless waived.  

DISCUSSION: 

Motion for Attorney’s Fees, Costs, and Expenses

I. Analysis

A. The Motion is Timely

Plaintiffs move for an award of attorney’s fees, costs and expenses. In Opposition, Defendant argues that this attorney’s fees motion is untimely and should be denied in its entirety. The Court disagrees.

On March 5, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a Notice of Settlement. On May 4, 2026, the Court ordered the complaint dismissed without prejudice and reserved jurisdiction in accordance with Code of Civil Procedure, section 664.6. (May 4, 2026, Minute Order.) In the May 4, 2026, hearing, both parties waived notice. (Ibid.) Plaintiff received no notice of entry of dismissal from the Court nor Defendant.

The issue of timeliness for attorney fees motions in Song-Beverly actions has been recently addressed by the Court of Appeal. 

Rule 3.1702 of the California Rules of Court governs motions for attorney fees in civil cases. A motion for fees incurred up to the time of judgment in the trial court “must be served and filed within the time for filing a notice of appeal under rules 8.104 and 8.108 in an unlimited civil case.” With exceptions not relevant to this appeal, the time for filing a notice of appeal is the earlier of 60 days after service of notice of entry of judgment or 180 days after entry of judgment. A voluntary dismissal is “effectively a ‘judgment’” within the meaning of the rule prescribing the time to appeal. Thus, “the clock starts to run [on the time to move for attorney fees] from either the service of notice of entry of judgment or dismissal (starting a 60-day clock), or if no such notice is given, the entry of judgment or dismissal (starting a 180-day clock).”

(Hatlevig v. General Motors LLC (2026) 118 Cal.App.5th 644, 649, internal citations omitted.)  

While Defendant is correct that the parties waived notice in the hearing, this waiver does not begin the 60-day clock under rule 8.104(1)(A) or (B). Instead, the 180-day clock under rule 8.104(a)(1)(C) applies. California Rules of Court, rule 8.104(a)(3) states: 

If the parties stipulated in the trial court under Code of Civil Procedure section 1019.5 to waive notice of the court order being appealed, the time to appeal under (1)(C) applies unless the court or a party serves notice of entry of judgment or a filed-endorsed copy of the judgment to start the time period under (1)(A) or (B).

“Indeed, the court rules expressly provide that a party’s waiver of notice of ruling does not automatically trigger the shorter 60-day jurisdictional time limits for filing notices of appeal, even where the party’s waiver of notice occurs in open court and is entered into the minutes, as allowed under Code of Civil Procedure section 1019.5. Notwithstanding the statutory waiver of notice, the prevailing party still must comply with the service provisions in the court rules in order to shorten the appeals period from 180 days to 60 days.” (In re Marriage of Lin (2014) 225 Cal.App.4th 471, 476; E.M. v. Los Angeles Unified School Dist. (2011) 194 Cal.App.4th 736, 744 [“following the July 31, 2009 order of dismissal, neither the superior court clerk nor any of the parties served a document entitled “Notice of Entry” of the order of dismissal. Therefore, plaintiff had 180 days within which to file notice of appeal from the July 31, 2009 order of dismissal.”])

Here, there is no dispute the parties waived notice of the dismissal under Code of Civil Procedure, section, 1019.5. Further, there is no dispute that neither the Court nor Defendant served notice of entry of dismissal on Plaintiffs. Therefore, the 180-day clock of California Rules of Court, rule 8.104(a)(1)(C) applies as instructed by rule 8.104(a)(3).

As the dismissal was entered on May 4, 2026, Plaintiffs were required to file this instant motion by November 4, 2026. Plaintiffs filed this instant motion on August 11, 2026, and is therefore timely.

B. Entitlement to Fees 

On January 23, 2026, the parties resolved this case. (Barry Decl. ¶8.) Pursuant to the settlement agreement, Defendant agreed that Plaintiffs were the prevailing party and entitled to attorney’s fees, costs, and expenses. (Barry Decl. ¶8.) 

Plaintiffs now move for an order awarding attorney’s fees and costs totaling $32,427.18. This amount consists of: (1) $27,089.00 in attorney fees; (2) $2,629.28 in costs and expenses; and (3) a 1.1 multiplier enhancement on the attorney fees (or $ $2,708.90). (See Mot. at p. 1.)

Civil Code, section 1794(d) provides:
 
(d) If the buyer prevails in an action under this section, the buyer shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.

(Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d) [emphasis added].)

Here, there appears no dispute that Plaintiffs are the prevailing party under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act and the Settlement Agreement between the parties allows Plaintiffs to recover their reasonable fees. Accordingly, Plaintiffs are entitled to an award of reasonable attorney fees as the prevailing party.  

C. Reasonableness of Fees (Lodestar)

The determination of reasonable amount of attorney fees is within the sound discretion of trial courts. (PLCM Group v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095; Akins v. Enterprise Rent-A-Car Co. (2000) 79 Cal.App.4th 1127, 1134.) “The determination of what constitutes a reasonable fee generally ‘begins with the ‘lodestar,’ i.e., the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate….’”  “[T]he lodestar is the basic fee for comparable legal services in the community; it may be adjusted by the court based on factors including, as relevant herein, (1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, (4) the contingent nature of the fee award….” (Graciano v. Robinson Ford Sales, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 140, 154.) In setting the hourly rate for an attorney fees award, courts are entitled to consider the rate of “‘fees customarily charged by that attorney and others in the community for similar work.’” (Bihun v. AT&T Information Systems, Inc. (1993) 13 Cal.App.4th 976, 997 [affirming rate of $450 per hour], overruled on other grounds by Lakin v. Watkins Associated Indus. (1993) 6 Cal.4th 644, 664.) The burden is on the party seeking attorney fees to prove reasonableness of the fees. (Center for Biological Diversity v. County of San Bernardino (2010) 188 Cal.App.4th 603, 615.)
 
The Court has broad discretion in determining the amount of a reasonable attorney’s fee award which will not be overturned absent a “manifest abuse of discretion, a prejudicial error of law, or necessary findings not supported by substantial evidence.” (Bernardi v. County of Monterey (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 1379, 1393-94.) The Court need not explain its calculation of the amount of attorney’s fees awarded in detail; identifying the factors considered in arriving at the amount will suffice. (Ventura v. ABM Industries Inc. (2012) 212 Cal.App.4th 258, 274-75.)

It should be noted that state trial court opinions are unpublished and have no precedential value. (Neary v. Regents of Univ. of Calif. (1992) 3 Cal.4th 273, 282.) Likewise, “[w]hile unpublished federal district court opinions are citable, they do not constitute binding authority.” (Gong v. City of Rosemead (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 363, 375.) 

1. Reasonable Hourly Rate

Plaintiffs were represented by The Barry Law Firm, which staffed five attorneys on the matter. The attorney’s billed between $375 and $695 per hour. (Barry Decl., Exh. 2.)

Plaintiff is seeking 51.6 hours for a total of $27,089.00. (Mot. at p. 14.) This gives a blended hourly rate of $524.98 per hour.  [FN 1] Here, under the totality of the circumstances and based upon general prevailing rates in the Los Angeles area for this type of litigation, this Court finds that a blended rate of $524.98 per hour is reasonable given this Court’s considerable experience in Song-Beverly cases, many of which culminate with an attorney’s fees motion. This determination considers the complexity of the case, the quality of services provided, and the attorneys’ experience. An award at this rate comes with the expectation that the attorney works in an efficient manner that reflects the premium paid for his or her services. The Court considers this fact in addressing the reasonableness of the hours expended.

2. Number of Hours Reasonably Expended

Beginning to end, Plaintiff seeks compensation for a total of 51.6 hours on this case. (Mot. at p. 12.) Of those hours, a staggering 20.7 hours includes this instant motion, the anticipated response to a motion to tax costs, as well as anticipated expenses at this hearing.

“It is the burden of the challenging party to point to the specific items challenged [within the moving party’s verified billing invoice], with a sufficient argument and citations to evidence. General arguments that fees claimed are excessive, duplicative, or unrelated do not suffice.” (Lunada Biomedical v. Nunez (2014) 230 Cal.App.4th 459, 488.)

In opposition, Defendant contends the hours purportedly spent by Plaintiffs’ counsel are excessive. Defendant highlights multiple entries in the billing records it contends are excessive and duplicative.  This Court agrees is considerable part.
 
Here, this action was filed June 30, 2025. The docket reflects very little motion practice consisting of Defendant’s motion for protective order and Plaintiff’s motion for reconsideration of the same. This action lasted just seven months. Further, there was nothing particularly complex or unique about this case. The issues involved were applicable to other consumers’ vehicles, thereby triggering economies of scale in terms of Plaintiffs’ counsel’s efficiency in litigating this type of lemon law case. Litigating this matter should not have required anything more than slight factual modification to existing boilerplate. Consistent with these observations, Counsel’s billing records reflect a series of repetitive administrative-type tasks. Further, Plaintiffs’ counsel has brought similar attorney’s fees motions routinely demonstrating proficiency in filing such motions. (Barry Decl. ¶¶22-61.) Therefore, this instant motion should have taken minimal effort given Plaintiff counsel’s extensive experience in bringing these motions for attorney fees.

Considering these facts, this Court ultimately agrees that in some instances, the time quoted is excessive under the circumstances. In light of the foregoing discussion, and in view of the totality of the circumstances, the Court finds that the total amount of reasonable attorney’s fees in this case, using a lodestar methodology, is $13,649.48. This was calculated by multiplying the hourly rate of $524.98 by 26 hours, which this Court deems to be the reasonable time spent on all tasks in the matter including the time spent in support of this instant motion and the anticipated time spent at the hearing. 
 
Plaintiff seeks a multiplier of 1.1. “The award of a multiplier is in the end a discretionary matter largely left to the trial court.” (Yes in My Back Yard v. City of Culver City (2023) 96 Cal.App.5th 1103, 1122 [citation and internal quotations omitted].) The Court in its discretion declines to apply a multiplier given this was a relatively standard Song-Beverly case with no novel or complex issues of law.   

Accordingly, Plaintiffs’ Motion for Attorney’s Fees is GRANTED in the amount of $13,649.48.

3. Costs and Expenses

Plaintiffs seek costs in the amount of $2,629.28. (Memo of Costs.)

First, the Court finds Defendant’s opposition to this memorandum of costs untimely. Motions to tax costs must be filed within 15 days’ service of the memorandum of costs, plus any applicable extensions based on method of service. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1700, subd. (b)(1).) This memorandum of costs was filed on August 11, 2026. A timely motion to tax should be filed by August 28, 2026. However, this opposition was not filed until September 2, 2026. Therefore, it is untimely.
 
Even if the Court were to consider the motion on its merits, the Court would still deny striking Plaintiffs costs. The scope of recoverable costs under Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d), is broader than the scope of recoverable costs under Code of Civil Procedure, section 1033.5. “[I]n enacting Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d) the Legislature intended the phrase ‘costs and expenses’ to cover items not included in ‘the detailed statutory definition of ‘costs’’ set forth in Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5. [Citation.]” (Warren v. Kia Motors Am., Inc. (2018) 30 Cal.App.5th 24, 42.) Thus, Plaintiff’s claimed costs are not limited to those set forth in Code of Civil Procedure, section 1033.5 and are recoverable on their face. Defendants disputed categories such as jury fees, court reporter fees, filing fees, travel, and mediation costs are reasonable in the prosecution of a lemon law case and appear to have been reasonably incurred. (Code Civ. Pro., §1794, subd. (d); See Jensen v. BMW of N. Am., Inc. (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 112, overruled on other grounds in Rodriguez v. FCA US LLC (2024) 17 Cal.5th 189.)  
 
Accordingly, Plaintiff’s Motion for Costs is GRANTED IN ITS ENTIRETY in the amount of $2,629.28.

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Case Number: 26STCV15410    Hearing Date: September 16, 2026    Dept: 509

Wound Care Solutions Management Company, Inc., et al. v. Raymond Manuel Aguirre, et al.

(1) MOTION TO SET ASIDE DEFAULT

(2) MOTION TO SET ASIDE DEFAULT

(3) MOTION TO SET ASIDE DEFAULT

(4) MOTION TO SET ASIDE DEFAULT
 

MOVING PARTY: (1) Defendant Raymond Manuel Aguirre
(2) Defendant Christina Abad-Aguirre
(3) Defendant Rovih Bryan Herrera
(4) Defendant Harbor Wound Care P.C

RESPONDING PARTY(S): (1)-(4) Plaintiffs Wound Care Solutions Management Company, Inc. and Empire Wound Care Corp.

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

Plaintiffs Wound Care Solutions Management Company, Inc. and Empire Wound Care Corp. (Plaintiffs) alleges that defendants Raymond Manuel Aguirre; Christina Abad-Aguirre; Rovih Bryan Herrera; and Harbor Wound Care P.C (Defendants) formed a competing business, in direct violation of the duties of loyalty, honesty and good faith owed to Plaintiffs. Plaintiffs allege eleven causes of action against Defendants.

On July 13, 2026, pursuant to Plaintiffs’ request, each defendants’ default was entered.

Now, each defendant moves to set aside the default. Plaintiff opposed each motion.

As the motions are materially identical, the Court considers all Defendants’ motions together.

TENTATIVE RULING

Each Defendant’s Motion to Set Aside Default is GRANTED. The Court orders the defaults entered on July 13, 2026, set aside and vacated. A stand-alone copy of the answer is to be filed no later than five days after the date of this order. All other requests for relief are DENIED, without prejudice.

Plaintiffs’ request for sanctions is DENIED.

Moving parties to give notice, unless waived.

DISCUSSION

Motion to Set Aside Default

I. Analysis 

A. Motion to Set Aside Default

Defendants now moves to set aside the default entered against them on July 13, 2026, under Code Civil Procedure section 473(b). In support of relief, Defendants’ counsel demonstrates he was actively engaged in settlement negotiations with Plaintiffs when default was entered. 

“Section 473, subdivision (b) provides for two distinct types of relief…Under the mandatory relief provision, on the other hand, upon a showing by attorney declaration of ‘mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect,’ the court shall vacate any ‘resulting default judgment or dismissal entered.’” (Leader v. Health Indus. of Am., Inc. (2001) 89 Cal. App. 4th 603, 615–16.) Here, Defendants do not request mandatory relief. 

Instead, under the discretionary provisions of section 473(b), the court “may, upon any terms as may be just, relieve a party or his or her legal representative from a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against him or her through his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect.” (Code Civ. Pro. § 473, subd. (b).) To obtain relief under this provision, the application for relief must be made “within a reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months, after the judgment, dismissal, order, or proceeding.” (Ibid.)

This Court is mindful of the policy favoring relief from default where there is no prejudice to the opposing party. (See Even Zohar Constr. & Remodeling, Inc. v. Bellaire Townhouses, LLC, (2015) 61 Cal. 4th 830, 839 [“The general underlying purpose of section 473(b) is to promote the determination of actions on their merits”]; see also Austin v. Los Angeles Unified Sch. Dist. (2016) 244 Cal. App. 4th 918, 929 [“[b]ecause the law favors disposing of cases on their merits, ‘any doubts in applying section 473 must be resolved in favor of the party seeking relief from default [citations].’”].) As an initial matter, Defendants’ counsel’s declaration is sufficient to demonstrate excusable neglect under a totality of the circumstances. While Plaintiffs allege “Defendant’s showing is manifestly inadequate under these principles,” the Court disagrees. Defense counsel has a sufficient showing of excusable neglect. Further, the Court is not persuaded by Plaintiffs allegation they would suffer any prejudice should these defaults be set aside. As will be discussed below, Plaintiffs knew responsive pleadings were forthcoming yet chose to delay proceedings by requesting the default to be entered. 

Finally, as an observation, even if these instant motions were to be denied, Plaintiffs would likely need to file an amended complaint. If Plaintiffs seeks monetary damages, which it appears they are, Plaintiffs failed to demand a specified monetary amount in the complaint. (See Code Civ. Pro. §580; Dhawan v. Biring (2015) 241 Cal.App.4th 963.) As a result, it appears opposing this motion is merely academic as Defendants would likely respond to any amended complaint.

Accordingly, Defendants’ Motion to Set Aside Default is GRANTED. The court orders the defaults entered on July 13, 2026, set aside and vacated. A stand-alone copy of the answer is to be filed no later than five days after the date of this order.

B. Other Requested Relief

“The entry of a default terminates a defendant’s rights to take any further affirmative steps in the litigation until either its default is set aside or a default judgment is entered. “A defendant against whom a default has been entered is out of court and is not entitled to take any further steps in the cause affecting plaintiff’s right of action; he cannot thereafter, until such default is set aside in a proper proceeding, file pleadings or move for a new trial or demand notice of subsequent proceedings.” (Devlin v. Kearny Mesa AMC/Jeep/Renault, Inc. (1984) 155 Cal.App.3d 381, 385–386, internal citations omitted.)

Here, Defendants request various other forms of relief ranging from challenging the sufficiency of the allegations in the complaint to compelling arbitration. (Not. of Mot.) Ruling on such matters on a motion to vacate is improper. Defendants may file appropriate motions once entry of default is vacated.

The only request the Court could reasonably entertain is a motion to quash. However, Defendants request the Court quash “service of the Summons for lack of personal jurisdiction pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure § 418.10(a)(1), because none of the Defendants resides in, maintains a principal place of business in, or has constitutionally sufficient minimum contacts with Los Angeles County.” (Not. of Mot.) This appears to be mislabeled because an individual is subject to general jurisdiction in the state of their domicile. (Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown (2011) 564 U.S. 915, 924) Whether Defendants reside in Los Angeles County is irrelevant to personal jurisdiction.

Further, “[a] general appearance is one in which the defendant participates in the action in a manner which recognizes the court’s jurisdiction. [Citation.] If the defendant raises an issue for resolution or seeks relief available only if the court has jurisdiction, then the appearance is a general one. [Citation.]” (Factor Health Management v. Superior Court (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 246, 250.) By requesting this Court to send the matter to arbitration, a relief only available if the Court has jurisdiction, the Defendants have made a general appearance in filing this motion. Any challenge to personal jurisdiction has been waived.

Accordingly, all additional requested relief is DENIED, without prejudice. Defendants may bring noticed motions should they choose. 

C. Sanctions

In opposition, Plaintiffs “respectfully request an award of their reasonable attorneys’ fees and costs. (Opp. at p. 14:14-15.)

Plaintiffs move for sanctions under Code of Civil Procedure section 473(b) which states that “the court shall, whenever relief is granted based on an attorney’s affidavit of fault, direct the attorney to pay reasonable compensatory legal fees and costs to opposing counsel or parties.” As stated above, defense counsel did not move for mandatory relief. Therefore, the award of legal fees and costs are not mandatory in this particular case. Instead, Code of Civil Procedure section 473(c)(1)(a) allows the court discretion to impose a penalty of up to $1,000 whenever “the court grants relief from a default, default judgment, or dismissal based on any of the provisions of this section.” (Code Civ. Proc.,¿§ 415.20. subd. (c)(1)(a).) The Court, in its discretion, declines to do so.

Indeed, to be perfectly blunt, Plaintiff’s attorney, Mr. Ian A. Wright (SBN 271957) should not have requested entry of default and may have violated his ethical duty in doing so.

The evidence before this Court demonstrates that Mr. Wright and defense counsel, Mr. Grant were actively discussing mediation, settlement, and other procedural issues during the period these defaults were entered. (Grant Decl., Exs. 2-3.) On July 13, 2026, at 2:05pm, Mr. Wright sent an email to Mr. Grant stating in part: “Although I did agree to 15-day extensions on the responsive pleading deadline, I can’t hold off on filing a request for entry of default.” (Grant Decl., Ex. 3.) Just nine minutes later, on July 13, 2026, at 2:14pm, Mr. Grant sent an email to Mr. Wright stating in part: “Sorry. I picked up COV’D during my vacation and have been out. I will be filing a response for all Parties by the end of the week.” (Grant Decl., Ex. 3.) 

Just forty minutes later, at 2:54pm on July 13, 2026, Mr. Wright filed request for default. (7/13/26 Request for Default.) This Court does not deny that Plaintiffs may have had the statutory right to enter a default on June 13th, when Mr. Wright did so. Be that as it may, one could reasonably assert that this filing is explicitly not to be condoned. (See Lasalle v. Vogel (2019) 36 Cal.App.5th 127, 135, [“The State Bar Civility Guidelines deplore the conduct of an attorney who races opposing counsel to the courthouse to enter a default before a responsive pleading can be filed.”])

Indeed, “it is now well-acknowledged that an attorney has an ethical obligation to warn opposing counsel that the attorney is about to take an adversary’s default.” (Lasalle v. Vogel, supra, 36 Cal.App.5th at p. 135.) “In that regard we heartily endorse the related admonition found in The Rutter Group practice guide, and we note the authors’ emphasis on reasonable time: “Practice Pointer: If you’re representing plaintiff, and have had any contact with a lawyer representing defendant, don’t even attempt to get a default entered without first giving such lawyer written notice of your intent to request entry of default, and a reasonable time within which defendant’s pleading must be filed to prevent your doing so.” (Weil & Brown, Cal. Practice Guide: Civil Procedure Before Trial (The Rutter Group 2008) § 5:73, p. 5-19 (rev. #1, 2008) as quoted in Fasuyi, supra, 167 Cal.App.4th at p. 702, 84 Cal.Rptr.3d 351.)” (Ibid.)

Instead, Mr. Wright sought the entry of default less than fifty minutes from notice of intent to file a default despite being informed that Mr. Grant intended to file a responsive pleading “by the end of the week.” (See Grant Decl., Ex. 3.) It appears Mr. Wright rushed to file his request for default in time to beat Mr. Wright’s responsive pleading. This action cannot be condoned.

While “Counsel’s breach of ethics is subject to reproval [it] does not in itself compel reversal of the order denying [the] motion to set aside the default and default judgment.” (Shapell Socal Rental Properties, LLC v. Chico’s FAS, Inc. (2022) 85 Cal.App.5th 198, 216.) Instead, the Court addresses such concerns as it is required to consider taking “appropriate corrective action” regarding the breach of ethical and statutory duties. (See Id. at p. 216, fn. 3.) At this time, the Court declines to take any further such action, other than this admonishment.   As such, Mr. Wright should consider himself fortunate.

Accordingly, Plaintiffs’ request for sanctions is DENIED.  

IT IS SO ORDERED.
