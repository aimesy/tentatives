I.            INTRODUCTION

On January 25, 2023, plaintiff Francyne Carrillo (“Plaintiff”), individually and as successor in interest of Francyne Saucedo (“Saucedo”), filed this action against defendants Monterey Park Hospital AHMC (“Monterey Park Hospital”), AHMC Healthcare Inc. (“AHMC”), Shuo Wang, M.D. (“Defendant”, erroneously sued as “Wang Shuo, M.D.”), Arthur H. Williams, M.D. (“Dr. Williams”), and Yau Mug Tang, M.D. (“Dr. Tang”) (collectively, “Defendants”).

Plaintiff alleges that on or about January 25, 2021, Saucedo was admitted to the hospital’s Intensive Care Unit for abdominal pain. (Compl., ¶ 20.) Saucedo was released from the hospital on January 26, 2021, against Plaintiff’s wishes, requests, and demands, even though Monterey Park Hospital was given notice that Plaintiff was Saucedo’s power of attorney and Plaintiff did not give consent for Saucedo’s release. (Compl., ¶¶ 21, 23-24.)

Saucedo subsequently fell into a critical state and taken to LAC + USC Medical Center. (Compl., ¶ 25.) She was pronounced deceased by cardiac arrest on January 27, 2021. (Compl., ¶ 26.) Plaintiff alleges Defendants “performed their treatment so poorly, and nonetheless released [Saucedo] from their care.” (Compl., ¶ 27.)

Plaintiff asserts thirteen causes of action, including wrongful death, medical battery, battery, lack of informed consent, breach of fiduciary duty, negligence, medical malpractice, intentional infliction of emotional distress, intentional misrepresentation, negligent misrepresentation, conspiracy to commit fraud, fraud, and fraudulent concealment.

On August 17, 2026, Defendant filed a demurrer and motion to strike. Defendant demurs to all of Plaintiff’s causes of action other than the First and Seventh Causes of Action for wrongful death and medical malpractice on the grounds that they fail to state facts sufficient to constitute causes of action and are uncertain. Defendant moves to strike Plaintiff’s allegations of oppressive, fraudulent, and malicious conduct as well as Plaintiff’s prayer for punitive damages. Defendant additional moves to strike Plaintiff’s request for attorneys’ fees.

Both the demurrer and motion to strike are unopposed.

II.          DISCUSSION

A.   Demurrer

California Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (e), provides that a demurrer is proper where “[t]he pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action.” A demurrer tests not only the sufficiency of the actual allegations in the complaint, but also whether those facts are pleaded with sufficient certainty and particularity. (Banerian v. O’Malley (1974) 42 Cal.App.3d 604.) On demurrer, the Court accepts all material facts that are properly pleaded, but does not assume the truth of “contentions, deductions, or conclusions of fact or law.” (Stansfield v. Starkey (1990) 220 Cal.App.3d 59, 72.) The Court may also “disregard any allegations that are contrary to the law or to a fact of which judicial notice may be taken.” (Leyva v. Nielsen (2000) 83 Cal.App.4th 1061.)

A demurrer may also be sustained for uncertainty where the complaint is ambiguous and unintelligible such that the defendant cannot reasonably respond. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (f).) The objection of uncertainty concerns doubt as to what the pleader means by the facts alleged, such that the defendant cannot reasonably determine what issues must be admitted or denied or what claims or causes of action are directed against him. (Brea v. McGlashan (1934) 3 Cal.App.2d 454.)

Defendant demurs to all causes of action other than the First Cause of Action for Wrongful Death and the Seventh Cause of Action for Medical Malpractice.

1.   Battery Claims (Second and Third Causes of Action)

Defendant argues that their demurrer to the Second and Third Causes of Action for Battery and Medical Battery should be sustained because the Complaint fails to specify whether these causes of action are brought on behalf of Saucedo or by Plaintiff in her individual capacity. The Complaint also does not state any facts alleging harmful or offensive contact as required to state a battery claim. (Ashcraft v. King (1991) 228 Cal.App.3d 604, 611.) The Complaint only alleges that Defendants committed medical battery and battery by “poorly treating and . . . releasing [Saucedo] from their care” without Plaintiff’s consent, causing “permanent injuries”. (Compl., ¶¶ 40, 51-52.)

The demurrer to the Second and Third Causes of Action is therefore SUSTAINED.

2.   Lack of Informed Consent, Breach of Fiduciary Duty, and Negligence (Fourth through Sixth Causes of Action)  

Defendant argues that Plaintiff fails to state a claim for lack of informed consent because Plaintiff does not identify the particular treatment whose inherent and substantial risks were not disclosed or the treatment allegedly administered that neither of them consented to. (Compl., ¶¶ 61, 68.) Thus, the demurrer to the Fourth Cause of Action is SUSTAINED.

As for the Complaint’s Fifth Cause of Action for Breach of Fiduciary Duty and Sixth Cause of Action for Negligence, Defendant’s demurrer is SUSTAINED because the allegations are duplicative of the Seventh Cause of Action for Medical Malpractice. The Fifth and Sixth Causes of Action allege that Defendant “negligently, unprofessionally, and carelessly” treated Saucedo, injured her during treatment, and released her from the hospital. Since duplicative pleading that adds nothing by way of fact or theory is subject to demurrer, Defendant’s demurrer to the Fifth and Sixth Causes of Action is SUSTAINED. (Award Metals, Inc. v. Superior Court (1991) 228 Cal.App.3d 1128, 1135.)

3.   Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress (Eighth Cause of Action)

To prevail on a claim for intentional infliction of emotional distress, the plaintiff must prove: “(1) extreme and outrageous conduct by the defendant with the intention of causing, or reckless disregard of the probability of causing, emotional distress; (2) the plaintiff's suffering severe or extreme emotional distress; and (3) actual and proximate causation of the emotional distress by the defendant's outrageous conduct.” (Hughes v. Pair (2009) 46 Cal.4th 1035, 1050.)

The Complaint fails to allege extreme and outrageous conduct; it merely repeats claims that Defendant provided substandard medical care. (Compl., ¶ 95.) The Complaint is also uncertain because the allegations do not specify whether the alleged tortious conduct was directed towards Plaintiff or Saucedo.

Accordingly, the demurrer to the Eighth Cause of Action is SUSTAINED.   

Fraud Claims (Ninth through Thirteenth Causes of Action)

Fraud claims must be pleaded with specificity. (Goldrich v. Natural Y Surgical Specialties, Inc. (1994) 25 Cal.App.4th 772, 782-783.) The tort of fraud (deceit) requires: “(a) misrepresentation (false representation, concealment, or non-disclosure); (b) knowledge of falsity (or “scienter”); (c) intent to defraud, i.e., to induce reliance; (d) justifiable reliance; and (e) resulting damage.” (Rattagan v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1, 32 [citing Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 638].)

Plaintiff’s fraud claims fail because the Complaint alleges that the misrepresentations were made on September 16, 2022, while Saucedo was treated and died in January 2021. The Complaint also fails to state the specific misrepresentations that were made, the speaker who made those misrepresentations, and when or how the misrepresentations were made.

Therefore, the demurrer to the Ninth through Thirteenth Causes of Action is SUSTAINED.

B.   Motion to Strike

On noticed motion, the Court may strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in a pleading and may strike any pleading or portion thereof that has not been drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the Court. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 435-436.) Here, Defendant moves to strike Plaintiff’s punitive-damages allegations and attorney-fee request as improper.

Defendant’s motion to strike Plaintiff’s prayer for punitive damages and all allegations of oppression, fraud, and malice pursuant to Civil Code section 3294 is GRANTED. Defendant is a medical provider and Plaintiff must first seek leave before pleading a claim for punitive damages. (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.13, subd. (a).)

Defendant’s motion to strike Plaintiff’s prayer for attorneys’ fees is also GRANTED. The Complaint fails to identify any statute or contract which provides a basis for recovering attorneys’ fees.  

III.        CONCLUSION

Defendant’s demurrer is SUSTAINED in its entirety. As the demurrer is unopposed, the Court declines to grant leave to amend.

Defendant’s motion to strike is GRANTED without prejudice to a later noticed motion to add a claim for punitive damages pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 425.13.

Plaintiff Jerry Easley moves for an award of attorneys’ fees, costs, and expenses pursuant to Civil Code § 1794, subdivision (d). Defendant General Motors, LLC (“Defendant”) did not oppose the motion.

Under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act (“SBA”), prevailing buyers have the burden to show that the fees incurred were reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation and were reasonable in amounts. (Doppes v. Bentley Motors, Inc. (2009) 174 Cal.App.4th 967, 998 (Doppes).) The SBA requires the trial court to make an initial determination of the actual time expended; and then to ascertain whether under all the circumstances of the case the amount of actual time expended, and the monetary charge being made for the time expended are reasonable.” (Tidrick v. FCA US LLC (2025) 112 Cal.App.5th 1147, 1158.) The question whether attorneys’ fees were reasonably incurred may depend on circumstances including, “factors such as the complexity of the case and procedural demands, the skill exhibited and the results achieved.” (Goglin v. BMW of North America, LLC (2016) 4 Cal.App.5th 462, 470. The appellate court reviews awards of attorney fees under Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d), for abuse of discretion. (Doppes, supra, 174 Cal.App.4th at p. 998.)

Plaintiff’s counsel, Joseph A. Kaufman, requests a base lodestar of $31,038, a 1.2 multiplier of $6,207.60, and $2,703.89 in costs and expenses, for a total award of $39,949.49. Mr. Kaufman declares that his hourly rate for 2026 is $615 and $595 from 2023 to 2025. Other attorneys on this matter have 2.5 years’ experience and billed at hourly rates ranging from $375 to $395, while a paralegal with 3 years’ experience billed at hourly rates of $175 in 2025 and $250 in 2026.

Mr. Abraham declares that his rate is based on factors such as 23 years of litigation experience, which include decades of defense work, and his involvement in thousands of lemon law cases/claims, he is also a regular presenter and author with many presentations and publications. (Kaufman Decl., ¶ 4.) He states that his hourly rate is less expensive than many consumer-side consumer protection attorneys and has been approved in 14 cases. (Kaufman Decl., ¶ 6.)

The motion is unopposed and Defendant does not dispute the reasonableness of Plaintiff’s counsel’s hourly rates. However, the Court notes that the billing records supporting Plaintiff’s counsel’s lodestar amount includes time entries and filing expenses for “anticipated” work in reviewing and responding to any opposition motion. The motion is unopposed, therefore, the Court reduces the base lodestar by 4.5 hours ($1,777.50) and Plaintiff’s counsel’s expenses by $24.62, for a total reduction of $1,802.12. The Court declines to award a multiplier because there is no showing that the case required any exceptional skill, beyond that possessed by counsel and already reflected in the hourly billing rate, or that it required Plaintiff’s counsel to turn down other work.

Accordingly, the Court GRANTS Plaintiff’s motion in part and awards $29,260.50 in attorney fees and $2,679.27 in expenses, for a total award of $31,939.77.
