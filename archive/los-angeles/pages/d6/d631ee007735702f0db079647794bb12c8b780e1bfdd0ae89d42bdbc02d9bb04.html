DEPARTMENT 509 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 26STCV00211    Hearing Date: August 12, 2026    Dept: 509

Brian Rattliff v. Lyft, Inc., et al.

MOTION TO COMPEL ARBITRATION
 

MOVING PARTY: Defendant Lyft Inc.

RESPONDING PARTY(S): Plaintiff Brian Rattliff

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

Plaintiff Brian Rattliff (Plaintiff) hired defendant Abbas Jammal (Jammal) for motor vehicle transportation on the Lyft Inc. (Lyft) app. While Plaintiff was a passenger in Jammal’s motor vehicle, Jammal rear-ended another vehicle. Plaintiff now brings causes of action for: (1) negligence; (2) negligence per se; (3) vicarious liability; (4) negligent hiring, retention, and supervision; and (5) carrier of persons for reward.

Lyft now moves to compel the matter to arbitration. Plaintiff opposed.

TENTATIVE RULING:

Lyft’s Motion to Compel Arbitration is GRANTED. The action is stayed pending the results of the arbitration.

A Status Review/OSC re: Dismissal is set for August 12, 2027, at 8:30 a.m.

Moving Party is ordered to give notice.

DISCUSSION:

Motion to Compel Arbitration

1. Legal Standard

“[T]he petitioner bears the burden of proving the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by the preponderance of the evidence . . . .” (Giuliano v. Inland Empire Personnel, Inc. (2007) 149 Cal.App.4th 1276, 1284). “In determining whether an arbitration agreement applies to a specific dispute, the court may examine only the agreement itself and the complaint filed by the party refusing arbitration [citation]. The court should attempt to give effect to the parties’ intentions, in light of the usual and ordinary meaning of the contractual language and the circumstances under which the agreement was made.” (Weeks v. Crow (1980) 113 Cal.App.3d 350, 353.) “Doubts as to whether an arbitration clause applies to a particular dispute are to be resolved in favor of sending the parties to arbitration. The court should order them to arbitrate unless it is clear that the arbitration clause cannot be interpreted to cover the dispute.” (California Correctional Peace Officers Ass'n v. State (2006) 142 Cal.App.4th 198, 205.) 

“[A] party opposing the petition bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence any fact necessary to its defense. [Citation.] In these summary proceedings, the trial court sits as a trier of fact, weighing all the affidavits, declarations, and other documentary evidence, as well as oral testimony received at the court's discretion, to reach a final determination.” (Giuliano v. Inland Empire Personnel, Inc. (2007) 149 Cal.App.4th 1276, 1284.)

“If a court of competent jurisdiction, whether in this State or not, has ordered arbitration of a controversy which is an issue involved in an action or proceeding pending before a court of this State, the court in which such action or proceeding is pending shall, upon motion of a party to such action or proceeding, stay the action or proceeding until an arbitration is had in accordance with the order to arbitrate or until such earlier time as the court specifies. . . .” (Code Civ. Pro. §1281.4.)

2. Analysis

California has a strong public policy in favor of arbitration as an expeditious and cost-effective way of resolving disputes. “Even so, parties can only be compelled to arbitrate when they have agreed to do so.” (Avila v. S. California Specialty Care, Inc. (2018) 20 Cal. App. 5th 835, 843.) “The party seeking to compel arbitration bears the burden of proving the existence of a valid arbitration agreement.” (Ibid.)

An arbitration agreement is a contractual agreement. “General contract law principles include that ‘[t]he basic goal of contract interpretation is to give effect to the parties’ mutual intent at the time of contracting. [Citations.] ... The words of a contract are to be understood in their ordinary and popular sense.” [Citations.] (Garcia v. Expert Staffing W., 73 Cal. App. 5th 408, 412–13.)

A. Delegation Clause

“Parties to an arbitration agreement may agree to delegate to the arbitrator, instead of a court, questions regarding the enforceability of the agreement. [Citation.] They ‘can agree to arbitrate almost any dispute—even a dispute over whether the underlying dispute is subject to arbitration.’” (Tiri v. Lucky Chances, Inc. (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 231, 241.) “There are two prerequisites for a delegation clause to be effective. First, the language of the clause must be clear and unmistakable. [Citation.] Second, the delegation must not be revocable under state contract defenses such as fraud, duress, or unconscionability.” (Id. at p. 242.)

Here, the Agreement contains a clear and unmistakable delegation clause. “All disputes concerning the arbitrability of a Claim (including disputes about the scope, applicability, enforceability, revocability or validity of the Arbitration Agreement) shall be decided by the arbitrator, except as expressly provided below.” (Sniegowski Decl., ¶ 7, Exhibit 7, p. 23.) This is a clear and unmistakable delegation clause. Further, as discussed below in detail, this Agreement is not voidable as a result of fraud, duress, or unconscionability.

Therefore, this action against Lyft must be initiated in arbitration. Whether the action continues in arbitration is left to the discretion of the arbitrator. 

Further, even if the delegation clause was inapplicable or insufficient, the Court would find a valid and binding arbitration agreement between the parties. 

B. Existence of an Arbitration Agreement 
 
The party moving to compel arbitration must establish the existence of a written arbitration agreement between the parties. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2.) The moving party can meet this initial burden by attaching to the petition a copy of the arbitration agreement purporting to bear the respondent’s signature. (Bannister v. Marinidence Opco, LLC (2021) 64 Cal.App.5th 541, 541-543.) Alternatively, the moving party can meet its initial burden by setting forth the agreement’s provisions in the motion. (See Condee v. Longwood Management Corp. (2001) 88 Cal.App.4th 215, 219; see also Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1330.) 
 
Here, Lyft has met their initial burden to establish the existence of a written arbitration agreement between the parties. The Declaration of Alex Sniegowski provides the Terms of Service that Plaintiff agreed to which includes a valid arbitration provision applicable to this current action. Plaintiff has agreed to Lyft’s terms of service, each containing an arbitration provision, six times. (Sniegowski Decl., ¶12.)
 
The Terms of Service contain an arbitration provision on its first page, which is further elaborated later in the Terms of Service. The first page arbitration provision states: 
 
PLEASE BE ADVISED: THIS AGREEMENT CONTAINS PROVISIONS THAT GOVERN HOW CLAIMS BETWEEN YOU AND LYFT CAN BE BROUGHT (SEE SECTION 17 BELOW). THESE PROVISIONS WILL, WITH LIMITED EXCEPTION, REQUIRE YOU TO: (1) WAIVE YOUR RIGHT TO A JURY TRIAL, AND (2) SUBMIT CLAIMS YOU HAVE AGAINST LYFT TO BINDING AND FINAL ARBITRATION ON AN INDIVIDUAL BASIS, NOT AS A PLAINTIFF OR CLASS MEMBER IN ANY CLASS, GROUP OR REPRESENTATIVE ACTION OR PROCEEDING.
 
(Sniegowski Decl., ¶ 7, Exhibit 7, p. 1.) 

Except as expressly provided below, ALL DISPUTES AND CLAIMS BETWEEN US (EACH A “CLAIM” AND COLLECTIVELY, “CLAIMS”) SHALL BE EXCLUSIVELY RESOLVED BY BINDING ARBITRATION. These Claims include, but are not limited to, any dispute, claim or controversy, whether based on past, present, or future events, arising out of or relating to: this Agreement and prior versions thereof (including the breach, termination, enforcement, interpretation or validity thereof), the Lyft Platform, the Rideshare Services, the Lyft Services, Lyft promotions, gift card, referrals or loyalty programs, the Lyft Tablet, any other goods or services made available through the Lyft Platform by Lyft or a third-party provider, your relationship with Lyft . . . 

(Sniegowski Decl., ¶ 7, Exhibit 7, p. 23.) 

“Plaintiff affirmatively accepted Lyft’s Terms of Service within the Lyft App on six (6) separate dates, both before and after the subject accident.” (Sniegowski Decl., ¶ 7, Exh. 1.) Therefore, Lyft has put forth a signed arbitration agreement that generally covers the claims here. 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues as to the sufficiency of the authentication of the documents presented. However, Plaintiff provides no declaration attesting that Plaintiff did not agree to the Lyft Terms of Service, including the arbitration provisions, presented by Lyft. There is no evidence to rebut Lyft’s evidence that Plaintiff agreed to Lyft’s Terms of Service and the arbitration provision within. Therefore, the arbitration agreement is valid and enforceable.

C. Defenses to Enforcement

As previously stated supra, it is this Court’s expressed finding that the Arbitrator shall decide whether the arbitration agreement is enforceable or not, since that task has been expressly delegated to him or her.  This Court’s role in this case was merely to determine whether or not there was an arbitration agreement between the parties, which it has been determined that there is such an agreement to arbitrate this dispute.

That being said, the following analysis is provided in the alternative, in the event that somehow this task was also supposed to be adjudicated by this Court. [FN 1] 

Plaintiff argues the Agreement should be disregarded based on principles of unconscionability. Unconscionability has “both a procedural and a substantive element, the former focusing on oppression or surprise due to unequal bargaining power, the latter on overly harsh or one-sided results. (Sanchez v. Valencia Holding Company, LLC (2015) 61 Cal.4th 899, 910.) Under California law, an arbitration agreement must be in some measure both procedurally and substantively unconscionable in order for the agreement to be unenforceable. (Armendariz v. Foundation Health Psychcare Services, Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 83, 114 (Armendariz); De La Torre v. CashCall, Inc. (2018) 5 Cal.5th 966, 982.) “But they need not be present in the same degree. . . . [T]he more substantively oppressive the contract term, the less evidence of procedural unconscionability is required to come to the conclusion that the term is unenforceable, and vice versa.” (Armendariz, supra, 24 Cal.4th at p. 114.)

a. Procedural Unconscionability

First, Plaintiff argues the agreement is procedurally unconscionable because it was a contract of adhesion—it was offered on “take-it-or-leave it-basis” as a condition to hail a Lyft taxi and he had no ability to negotiate its terms. Plaintiff alleges that this contract of adhesion should invalidate the Agreement. “The term [contract of adhesion] signifies a standardized contract, which, imposed and drafted by the party of superior bargaining strength, relegates to the subscribing party only the opportunity to adhere to the contract or reject it.” [Citation]. (Armendariz, supra, 24 Cal.4th at p. 113.)

Here, the Court agrees with Plaintiff that the dynamic here represents a classic contract of adhesion, but only to the degree that is typical in modern digital transactions. Therefore, the “take it or leave it” nature of the agreement is sufficient to establish “some degree of procedural unconscionability.” (Sanchez v. Valencia Holding Co., LLC (2015) 61 Cal.4th 899, 915). This means the substantive terms of the agreement must be scrutinized to ensure they are not manifestly unfair or one-sided. (Ibid.) 

b. Substantive Unconscionability

Plaintiff also contends the Agreement is substantively unconscionable. “‘Substantive unconscionability’ focuses on the terms of the agreement and whether those terms are ‘so one-sided as to ‘shock the conscience.’” (Kinney v. United HealthCare Services, Inc. (1999) 70 Cal.App.4th 1322, 1330.)

“In assessing substantive unconscionability, the paramount consideration is mutuality.” (Pinela v. Neiman Marcus Group, Inc.¿(2015) 238 Cal.App.4th 227, 241, internal quotes and citations omitted.) “A contractual provision is not substantively unconscionable simply because it provides one side a greater benefit. The party with the greater bargaining power is permitted to require contractual provisions that provide it with additional protections if there is a legitimate commercial need for those protections, but the stronger party may not require additional protections merely to maximize its advantage over the weaker party. ‘As has been recognized ‘unconscionability turns not only on a ‘one-sided’ result, but also on an absence of ‘justification’ for it.’” (Carbajal v. CWPSC, Inc. (2016) 245 Cal.App.4th 227, 248, internal citations omitted.)

Plaintiff argues, “[w]hile facially mutual, they exempt from arbitration only discrete categories of claims that do not meaningfully burden Lyft, while sweeping in the broadest category of claims a consumer is likely to bring – personal-injury tort claims of exactly the type at issue here.” (Opp. at p. 10:24-27.) 

However, these provisions are not so one-sided as to shock the conscious of the Court. While these provisions certainly favor the Lyft, they do not appear to be unjustifiable constraints designed to maximize its advantage over the weaker party. The terms leave a minimal finding of substantive unconscionability.

This, therefore, leaves us with only a minimal finding of both procedural and substantive conscionability. This is not enough under the sliding scale approach to invalidate the agreement as a whole.

c. Waiver

Finally, Plaintiff briefly addresses the issue of waiver alleging “Lyft waited more than six months after Plaintiff’s operative complaint” to file this instant motion. (Opp. at p. 11:18-19.) However, waiver occurs “when the party seeking to enforce a known contractual right has intentionally relinquished or abandoned that right.” (Quach v. California Commerce Club, Inc. (2024) 16 Cal.5th 562, 569.) “To establish waiver under generally applicable contract law, the party opposing enforcement of a contractual agreement must prove by clear and convincing evidence that the waiving party knew of the contractual right and intentionally relinquished or abandoned it.” (Id. at p. 584.) “The waiver inquiry is exclusively focused on the waiving party’s words or conduct; neither the effect of that conduct on the party seeking to avoid enforcement of the contractual right nor that party’s subjective evaluation of the waiving party’s intent is relevant.” (Id. at p. 585.) This brief delay, plus the significant evidence of Lyft’s meet and confer efforts during this time, does not rise to waiver. There is no evidence of an abandonment of Lyft’s right to arbitration from this delay alone.

Accordingly, Defendants’ Motion to Compel Arbitration is GRANTED. The action is stayed pending the results of the arbitration.

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Case Number: 26STCV14932    Hearing Date: August 12, 2026    Dept: 509

Jane Doe v. Polytechnic School, et al.

MOTION TO STRIKE
 

MOVING PARTY: Defendants Polytechnic School and Meredith Robbins

RESPONDING PARTYS:   Plaintiff Jane Doe

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

This is an employment action. Plaintiff Jane Doe (Plaintiff) filed this complaint against defendants Polytechnic School and Meredith Robbins (collectively, Defendants) arising from an employment relationship. Plaintiffs assert causes of action for: (1) discrimination in violation of the FEHA; (2) hostile work environment harassment in violation of the FEHA; (3) retaliation in violation of the FEHA; (4) failure to prevent discrimination, harassment, or retaliation in violation of FEHA; (5) negligent hiring, supervision, and retention; (6) intentional infliction of emotional distress; (7) wrongful constructive termination of employment in violation of public policy; (8) whistleblower retaliation (Labor Code §1102.5 et seq.); and (9) violation of Labor Code § 2802.

Defendants now move to strike Plaintiff’s use of the Jane Doe designation in the complaint. Plaintiff opposed. 

TENTATIVE RULING:

Defendants’ Motion to Strike is DENIED WITHOUT PREJUDICE.

An OSC Re: Plaintiff’s Use of Pseudonym is scheduled for September 15, 2026, which is the date of the initial Case Management Conference. Any declarations and/or briefing on this issue are to be filed and served by any party on or before September 8, 2026, at 4:00 pm.

Defendant is ordered to give notice, unless waived. 

DISCUSSION:

Motion to Strike
 
I. Meet and Confer

Before filing a motion to strike, the moving party “shall meet and confer…with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to the motion to strike for the purpose of determining if an agreement can be reached that resolves the objections to be raised in the motion to strike…” (Code Civ. Pro. § 435.5, subd. (a).) The moving party “shall file and serve with the motion to strike a declaration” attesting to the meet and confer. (Id. § 435.5(a)(3).)

The Declaration of attorney Matthew W. Burris reflects the meet and confer requirement was satisfied.

II. Legal Standard 
 
A motion to strike lies either (1) to strike any irrelevant, false or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or (2) to strike any pleading or part thereof not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule or order of court. (Code Civ. Pro. § 436.) 
 
III. Analysis 
 
Defendants move to strike Plaintiff’s use of the Jane Doe pseudonym from the complaint. 
 
Before a party to a civil action can be permitted to use a pseudonym, the trial court must conduct a hearing and apply the overriding interest test: A party’s request for anonymity should be granted only if the court finds that an overriding interest will likely be prejudiced without use of a pseudonym, and that it is not feasible to protect the interest with less impact on the constitutional right of access. In deciding the issue the court must bear in mind the critical importance of the public’s right to access judicial proceedings. Outside of cases where anonymity is expressly permitted by statute, litigating by pseudonym should occur ‘only in the rarest of circumstances.’

(Department of Fair Employment and Housing v. Superior Court of Santa Clara County (2022) 82 Cal.App.5th 105, 111–112, emphasis added.)

Because resolution requires an evidentiary hearing, a motion to strike is not the appropriate avenue for relief. Further, Plaintiff has provided no evidence in support of her opposition but rather relies upon generalized arguments. Considering this observation, Plaintiff should be given the opportunity to present specific evidence supporting her need for a pseudonym.

Accordingly, Defendant’s Motion to Strike is DENIED WITHOUT PREJUDICE.

Instead, on its own motion, the Court sets an OSC Re: Plaintiff’s Use of Pseudonym for September 15, 2026. An evidentiary hearing will be conducted to determine whether Plaintiff has sufficient evidence supporting her need for a pseudonym. 


IT IS SO ORDERED.

Case Number: BC674584    Hearing Date: August 12, 2026    Dept: 509

Vickers Holding & Finance Inc. v. Jossiv Kim, et al.

MOTION TO MODIFY THE COURT’S MARCH 20, 2020, ASSIGNMENT ORDER  

MOVING PARTY: Judgment Creditor Vickers Holding

RESPONDING PARTY(S): Judgment Debtors Jossiv Kim and Angelina Kim

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

Vickers Holding and Finance, Inc., (Judgment Creditor) brought this action against Jossiv Kim and Angelina Kim (Judgment Debtors) seeking to recognize and enforce a judgment entered against them in the Netherlands. 

In November 2018, this Court entered a judgment against Judgment Debtors of which an outstanding balance of over $10 million remain.

On March 20, 2020, this Court granted an assignment order in favor of Judgment Creditor.

Judgment Creditor now moves for an order modifying the March 20, 2020, assignment order. Judgment Debtors opposed.

TENTATIVE RULING:

Judgment Creditor’s Motion to Modify the Court’s March 20, 2020, Assignment Order is CONTINUED to December 11, 2026. 

By November 16, 2026, at 4pm, Judgment Creditor is to file a supplemental briefing.

Judgment Debtors may file a supplemental response no later than December 7, 2026, at 4pm.

Judgment Creditor is ordered to give notice to all interested parties or entities, as may be required by law.

DISCUSSION:

Modify Assignment Order

I. Legal Standard

“Either the judgment creditor or the judgment debtor may apply to the court on noticed motion for an order to modify or set aside the assignment order.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 708.560, subd. (a).) The moving party must give notice of the motion to the other party personally or by mail. (Ibid.) “The court shall make an order modifying or setting aside the assignment order upon a showing that there has been a material change in circumstances since the time of the previous hearing on the assignment order.” (Id. at subd. (b).) “The court may order a reassignment of the rights to payments as necessary.” (Ibid.) “The order shall state whether and to what extent it applies to payments already made.” (Ibid.)  

II. Analysis

Judgment Creditor moves for an order modifying the March 20, 2020, Assignment Order on grounds that the Judgment Debtors have stopped collecting money from the assigned payors and have begun utilizing alternative means of receiving income. (See Mot., generally.)

As an initial matter, the motion discusses various violations of the March 20, 2020, Assignment Order. To any extent the existing assignment order was violated is not at issue in this motion. Such determinations are better left to other motion types.

Instead, the Court has authority to modify its previous assignment order due to a material change in circumstances since the time of the original hearing. (Code of Civ. Pro. § 708.560, subd. (b).) 

The Court turns to Judgment Creditor’s proposed order. It requests, among other things:

To the extent necessary to satisfy the unpaid November 6, 2018 Judgment in favor of Vickers (the “Judgment”), including accrued interest and costs, the Assigned Payment Rights – defined as all present and future non-exempt, legally enforceable rights to payment due or to become due to Judgment Debtors within the scope of Cal. Code of Civ. P. section 708.510 -- are assigned to Vickers, subject to any applicable exemption or statutory limitation established by Judgment Debtors through competent evidence.

([Proposed] Order at ¶3.) 

From the Proposed Order, it appears that Judgment Creditor is seeking a blanket assignment order that it can utilize against any entity it discovers makes payments to Judgment Debtors going forward. Indeed, it appears that the law allows for such a blanket order. In Philippine Export & Foreign Loan Guarantee Corp. v. Chuidian (1990) 218 Cal.App.3d 1058 the Court granted an assignment order against “all debts owing or to become owing to the Philippine Export [Debtor] from individuals or entities located in the United States.” (Id. at pp. 1099-1100, emphasis added.)  

However, this Court is hesitant to provide such a blanket assignment order. The utilization of such a broad order may reach beyond the anticipated scope and bring about unforeseen consequences such as reaching into protected categories of income despite Judgment Creditor’s diligence to avoid as such. While the Court acknowledges Judgment Creditor’s arguments, there is nothing to suggest that such a blanket order is necessary at this time. 

Here, the only change in circumstance demonstrated is that the current entities captured by the March 20, 2020, Assignment Order no longer make payments to Judgment Debtors. [FN 1]  (Stolyar Decl. ¶9.) However, beyond allegations that he “received substantial cash gifts from overseas” or in 2023, worked “unofficially for cash,” there is no change in circumstances that indicate a need to add new entities to the prior assignment order. (Stolyar Decl. ¶¶12-13.) There is no evidence to support that Judgment Debtors have any additional rights to payments due or to becoming due in the future. However, Section 708.510 requires “a right to payment due, or to become due.” (Code of Civ. Pro. § 708.510, subd. (a); see Landstar Global Logistics, Inc. v. Robinson & Robinson, Inc. (2013) 216 Cal.App.4th 378, 390, [“Statutory provisions governing these remedies are subject to strict construction.”]) Simply, there is no evidence that Judgment Debtors have any additional payment rights that would warrant a modification of the current order.

Further, this modification does not remedy the underlying issues Judgment Creditor presents in its moving papers. This instant motion does nothing to compel Judgment Debtors to comply with the March 20, 2020, Assignment Order nor does it require Judgment Debtors to inform Judgment Creditor as to the identity of any potential new payors. However, the Court notes that Judgment Creditor does not indicate that any discovery has been completed regarding the source of Judgment Debtors new income since the recent closure of bankruptcy proceedings. Therefore, this motion appears premature. Judgment Creditor has not discovered the entities which may be reasonably captured by this proposed assignment order, to the extent any exists. 

Accordingly, Judgment Creditor’s Motion to Modify the Court’s March 20, 2020, Assignment Order is CONTINUED to December 11, 2026

IT IS SO ORDERED.
