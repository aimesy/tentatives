Case Number: 25PSCV01312    Hearing Date: July 28, 2026    Dept: G

Plaintiff Pete Mejia’s Amended Motion for Attorney’s Fees for Time Actually Expended and Reasonably Incurred

Respondent: Defendant General Motors LLC

TENTATIVE RULING

Plaintiff Pete Mejia’s Amended Motion for Attorney’s Fees for Time Actually Expended and Reasonably Incurred is GRANTED in a reduced amount. 

The court AWARDS Plaintiff Pete Mejia a total of $12,788.36.

BACKGROUND

This is a Song-Beverly action arising out of the lease of an allegedly defective motor vehicle.  On October 6, 2022, plaintiff Pete Mejia (Mejia) leased a truck manufactured by defendant General Motors LLC (GM).  Defendant Chevrolet of Puente Hills allegedly failed to repair the truck.

On April 14, 2025, Mejia filed the Complaint, alleging causes of (1) breach of express warranty in violation of the Song-Beverly Act, (2) breach of implied warranty in violation of the Song-Beverly Act, (3) failure to maintain service and repair facilities in violation of the Song-Beverly Act, and (4) failure to service and repair in a workmanlike manner in violation of Civil Code section 1796.5.

On November 20, 2025, the parties informed the court that they settled the case.  On January 20, 2026, the court granted Mejia’s dismissal of the entire action and all parties.

On March 18, 2026, Mejia filed the original motion for attorney’s fees.  On May 13, 2026, GM filed the opposition, and on May 18, 2026, Mejia filed the reply.  On May 26, 2026, the court issued a tentative ruling continuing the hearing on the motion to allow Mejia to provide a copy of the written settlement agreement.  When Mejia failed to appear at the hearing, the court did not adopt its tentative ruling and instead denied the motion without prejudice.

On May 28, 2026, Mejia filed this amended motion for attorney’s fees.  (The court notes that the amended motion for attorney’s fees is identical to the original motion for attorney’s fees, except that Mejia now includes a copy of the written settlement agreement in the supporting documents, as the court requested.  Since the amended motion is identical to the original motion, the court considers the opposition and reply from the original motion in its analysis of the amended motion.

The motion is set for hearing on July 28, 2026.

ANALYSIS

Mejia moves for an award of attorney’s fees and costs in the amount of $17,112.68.  For the following reasons, the motion for attorney’s fees is GRANTED in the reduced amount of $11,666.68.

Legal Standard

“If the buyer prevails in an action under this section, the buyer shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.”  (Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).)  “[T]he negotiation of a possible settlement does not excuse the failure to proceed with due diligence in the filing of pleadings, absent some understanding that pleadings need not be filed while negotiations are in process.”  (Turley v. Turley (1967) 254 Cal.App.2d 169, 173.)  In the context of an award of attorneys’ fees “the time expended by attorneys in obtaining a reasonable fee is justifiably included in the attorneys’ fee application, and in the court’s fee award.”  (Serrano v. Unruh (1982) 32 Cal.3d 621, 631.)  “[I]t is inappropriate and an abuse of a trial court’s discretion to tie an attorney fee award to the amount of the prevailing buyer/plaintiff’s damages or recovery in a Song-Beverly Act action.”  (Hanna v. Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC (2019) 36 Cal.App.5th 493, 510.)

In assessing attorney’s fees under the Song-Beverly Act, a trial court must make an “initial determination of the actual time expended; and then to ascertain whether under all the circumstances of the case the amount of actual time expended and the monetary charge being made for the time expended are reasonable.  These circumstances may include, but are not limited to, factors such as the complexity of the case and procedural demands, the skill exhibited and the results achieved.  If the time expended or the monetary charge being made for the time expended is not reasonable under all the circumstances, then the court must take this into account and award attorney fees in a lesser amount.”  (Robertson v. Fleetwood Travel Trailers of Cal., Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 785, 817.)

“It is well established that the determination of what constitutes reasonable attorney fees is committed to the discretion of the trial court, whose decision cannot be reversed in the absence of an abuse of discretion.”  (Melnyk v. Robledo (1976) 64 Cal.App.3d 618, 623.)  The fee setting inquiry in California ordinarily “begins with the ‘lodestar’ [method], i.e., the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate.”  (Graciano v. Robinson Ford Sales, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 140, 154.)  “The reasonable hourly rate is that prevailing in the community for similar work.”  (Margolin v. Regional Planning Com. (1982) 134 Cal.App.3d 999, 1004.)  “Under the lodestar method, a party who qualifies for a fee should recover for all hours reasonably spent unless special circumstances would render an award unjust.”  (Vo v. Las Virgenes Municipal Water Dist. (2000) 79 Cal.App.4th 440, 446.)

“[A] computation of time spent on a case and the reasonable value of that time is fundamental to a determination of an appropriate attorneys’ fee award.”  (Margolin v. Regional Planning Com., supra, 134 Cal.App.3d 999, 1004.)  The lodestar figure may then be adjusted, based on consideration of factors specific to the case, in order to fix the fee at the fair market value for the legal services provided.  (See Serrano v. Priest (1977) 20 Cal.3d 25, 49, discussing factors relevant to proper attorneys’ fees award.)  Such an approach anchors the trial court’s analysis to an objective determination of the value of the attorney’s services, ensuring that the amount awarded is not arbitrary.  (Id. at 48, fn. 23.)  The factors considered in determining the modification of the lodestar include “(1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, [and] (4) the contingent nature of the fee award.”  (Mountjoy v. Bank of America (2016) 245 Cal.App.4th 266, 271.)  “[T]he burden is on the party seeking attorney fees to prove that the fees it seeks are reasonable.”  (Gorman v. Tassajara Development Corp. (2009) 178 Cal.App.4th 44, 98.)  “[A]n award of attorney fees may be based on counsel’s declarations, without production of detailed time records.”  (Raining Data Corp. v. Barrenechea (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 1363, 1375.)  “[P]aralegal fees may be awarded as attorney’s fees if the trial court deems it appropriate. . . .”  (Roe v. Halbig (2018) 29 Cal.App.5th 286, 312.)

Where a party is challenging the reasonableness of attorney’s fees as excessive that party must “attack the itemized billing[] with evidence that the fees claimed were not appropriate, or obtain the declaration of an attorney with expertise in the procedural and substantive law to demonstrate that the fees claimed were unreasonable.”  (Premier Medical Management Systems, Inc. v. Cal. Ins. Guarantee Assn. (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 550, 563–564.)  “[I]t is the burden of the challenging party to point to the specific items challenged, with a sufficient argument and citations to the evidence and arguments that fees claimed are excessive, duplicative, or unrelated do not suffice.”  (Id. at 564.)  “General arguments that fees claimed are excessive, duplicative, or unrelated do not suffice.”  (Ibid.)

A “court should defer to the winning lawyer’s professional judgment as to the tasks completed in an action because he won, and might not have, had he been more of a slacker.”  (Moreno v. City of Sacramento (9th Cir. 2008) 534 F.3d 1106, 1111.)  A losing party cannot litigate tenaciously then be heard to complain about the time spent or tasks performed by the prevailing party in response.  (City of Riverside v. Rivera (1986) 477 U.S. 561, 580, fn. 11.)  Where a defendant does not produce evidence contradicting the reasonableness of counsel’s hourly rates, the court will deem an attorney’s hourly rate as reasonable.  (Goglin v. BMW of North America, LLC (2016) 4 Cal.App.5th 462, 473.)

Discussion

As the prevailing party, Mejia is entitled to attorney’s fees.  To decide the award, the court considers the reasonableness of the claimed hours, the reasonableness of the rates charged, whether to apply a lodestar multiplier, and the amount of costs and expenses.

Reasonableness of the Claimed Hours

The Song-Beverly Act provides for the recovery of “costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer.”  (Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).)  A verified fee bill is “prima facie evidence the costs, expenses and services listed were necessarily incurred.”  (Hadley v. Krepel (1985) 167 Cal.App.3d 677, 682.)  However, counsel bears the burden to demonstrate the reasonableness of charges.  (Mikhaeilpoor v. BMW of North America, LLC (2020) 48 Cal.App.5th 240, 247.)  Fees should be denied where the court finds the tasks unreasonable or unnecessary since “padding in the form of inefficient or duplicative efforts is not subject to compensation.”  (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132.)

Mejia’s counsel claims 32.5 hours of legal work.  (See 5/28/2026 Acosta Decl., ¶ 31; 5/28/2026 Acosta Decl., Exh. A, p. 6.)  The verified fee bill is prima facie evidence of the legal services necessarily incurred.  (See 5/28/2026 Acosta Decl., Exh. A; see also Hadley, supra, 167 Cal.App.3d at 682.)  To determine the reasonableness of the claimed hours, the court considers the arguments from GM’s opposition.  The court categorizes the challenged billing entries with which GM takes issue: (1) excessive and inefficient litigation, (2) clerical work completed by attorneys, and (3) anticipated time.  (See Opp., pp. 12–19.)

Excessive and Inefficient Litigation

GM argues a reduction in the attorney’s fee award is justified for “extensive non-compensable clerical work and routine communications” and “excessive and unreasonable” review of initial disclosures.  (Opp., pp. 12, 16, capitalization changed.)  The court agrees.

The court may reduce an attorney’s fee award if “plaintiff’s counsel failed to act efficiently.”  (Mikhaeilpoor, supra, 48 Cal.App.5th at 256.)  “In evaluating whether the attorney fee request is reasonable, the trial court should consider whether the case was overstaffed, how much time the attorneys spent on particular claims, and whether the hours were reasonably expended.”  (Morris v. Hyundai Motor America (2019) 41 Cal.App.5th 24, 38, cleaned up.)  Additionally, the court does not award attorney’s fees for work that is unnecessarily duplicative.  (See Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d); see also Premier Medical Management Systems, Inc., supra, 163 Cal.App.4th at 564.)

The court reviewed the specific entries GM challenged and found one that is appropriately characterized as inefficient and duplicative; the other entries, such as those for client communications, the court finds were necessarily incurred in litigation.  On July 22, 2025, Anthony Greco included two billing entries totaling 2.0 hours to review GM’s initial disclosures, even though a paralegal had already completed a review of the same initial disclosures that same day.  (See 5/28/2026 Acosta Decl., Exh. A, pp. 2–3.)

Thus, the court reduces the award of attorney’s fees for inefficient litigation by 2.0 hours (2.0 hours from Anthony Greco).

Clerical Work Completed by Attorneys

GM argues a reduction in the attorney’s fee award is justified for “work that could and should have been performed by paralegals or support staff.”  (Opp., p. 14, capitalization changed.)  The court disagrees.

On one hand, a trial court may reduce attorney’s fees for clerical work completed by an attorney.  (See Save Our Uniquely Rural Community Environment v. County of San Bernardino (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 1179, 1187, finding that the trial court properly reduced attorney’s fees where attorneys billed clerical work at partners’ rates.)  On the other hand, courts have also found that non-attorney work may be included in an award of attorney’s fees.  (See City of Oakland v. McCullough (1996) 46 Cal.App.4th 1, 7, finding that support services provided to attorneys may be included in an award of attorney’s fees.)  In either case, it is within the trial court’s discretion to determine whether clerical work should be included in the award of attorney’s fees.  (Roe, supra, 29 Cal.App.5th at 312.)

Here, the court reviewed the entries cited as legal assistant tasks but found that they were reasonably incurred in the prosecution of the action.

Thus, the court declines to reduce the award of attorney’s fees for legal assistant tasks.

Anticipated Time

GM argues a reduction in the attorney’s fee award is justified for “preparing the instant motion” because “anticipated time should not be awarded.”  (Opp., p. 18, capitalization changed.)

The court must base its award of attorney’s fees on “actual time expended.”  (Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).)  The plain language of Section 1794, subdivision (d) necessarily precludes an award based on speculative fees.

The motion anticipates 5.5 hours in connection with reviewing the opposition, drafting the reply, and attending the hearing.  (See 5/28/2026 Acosta Decl., Exh. A, p. 6.)  Anticipated fees are speculative and not recoverable as “actual time expended.”  (Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).)

Thus, the court reduces the award of attorney’s fees for anticipated time by 5.5 hours (5.5 hours from Christopher Urner).

Therefore, the court finds that Mejia substantiated 25.0 of the 32.5 hours claimed by counsel in this litigation.

Reasonableness of Counsel’s Rates

The trial court is the ultimate arbiter of what rates are reasonable.  (Warren v. Kia Motors America, Inc. (2018) 30 Cal.App.5th 24, 36, stating, “experienced trial judges are the best judges of the value of professional services rendered in their courts.”)  To determine if an hourly rate is reasonable, courts consider the rates of similar attorneys in the community as well as “the experience, skill, and reputation of the attorney requesting fees.”  (Heritage Pacific Financial, LLC v. Monroy (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 972, 1009.)

Here, Mejia’s counsel request hourly rates as outlined by this table:

Counsel’s declaration provides an overview of the experience and credentials for each listed attorney and legal staff member.  (See 5/28/2026 Acosta Decl., ¶¶ 2–6, 9–12.)  The court considered counsel’s declaration, the United States Consumer Law Attorney Fee Survey Report, and the Laffey Matrix.  (See 5/28/2026 Acosta Decl., ¶¶ 21–23.)  Based on the court’s review of this case compared to other Los Angeles-area counsel in similar Song-Beverly cases, the court finds that the rates charged by some attorneys are unreasonable. 

Attorneys’ rates over $500 for this type of case are excessive, so the court reduces the rates of Anthony Greco and Christopher Urner to $500.  Anthony Greco billed 15.8 hours and substantiated 13.8 hours at a rate of $595, so the court reduces the award by $2,501.00.  Christopher Urner billed 7.8 hours and substantiated 2.3 hours at a rate of $525, so the court reduces the award by $2,945.00.

Therefore, adjusting counsel’s rates for reasonableness, the appropriate award of attorney’s fees is $11,666.68 before considering any multiplier.

Lodestar Multiplier

The factors that courts look at to determine if a multiplier is reasonable are: “(1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, [and] (4) the contingent nature of the fee award.”  (Ketchum, supra, 24 Cal.4th at 1132.)  “[T]he purpose of a fee enhancement is primarily to compensate the attorney for the prevailing party at a rate reflecting the risk of nonpayment in contingency cases.”  (Id. at 1138.)  When a case is not complicated, “the request for a multiplier [is] specious.”  (Mikhaeilpoor, supra, 48 Cal.App.5th at 255.)

Here, Mejia does not request a lodestar multiplier.

Therefore, the court does not elect to apply a lodestar multiplier to the award of reasonable attorney’s fees of $11,666.68.

Costs and Expenses

Mejia is entitled to all $1,121.68 in claimed costs and expenses.  A prevailing plaintiff has a right to recover all costs and expenses that were reasonably incurred in prosecuting the case.  (Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).)  Allowable costs must be reasonably necessary to conduct the litigation and reasonable in amount.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subds. (c)(2)–(c)(3).)

Mejia requests $1,121.68 in costs and expenses in the motion and the memorandum of costs.  (See 5/28/2026 Mot., pp. 15–16; 5/28/2026 Memo. of Costs, p. 1.)  Based on the memorandum of costs and the expenses described therein, the court finds that all claimed costs were reasonably incurred.  (See 5/28/2026 Memo. of Costs.)

Therefore, Mejia is entitled to all $1,121.68 in claimed costs and expenses.

Accordingly, the motion for attorney’s fees and costs is GRANTED in the reduced amount of $12,788.36.68.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the amended motion for reasonable attorney’s fees and costs is GRANTED in a reduced amount. 

The court AWARDS Plaintiff Pete Mejia a total of $12,788.36.

Case Number: 25PSCV03582    Hearing Date: July 28, 2026    Dept: G

Plaintiff Adrian Torres’ Motion to Quash Subpoena and for Protective Order

Respondent: Defendant Avendano P&D

TENTATIVE RULING

Plaintiff Adrian Torres’ Motion to Quash Subpoena is DENIED.

Plaintiff Adrian Torres’ Motion for Protective Order is GRANTED.

BACKGROUND

This is an employment action for wrongful termination.  On August 4, 2025, defendant Avendano P&D (Avendano) allegedly hired plaintiff Adrian Torres (Torres) as a driver.  On August 18, 2025, Torres allegedly fell ill and left work before his shift ended.  On August 19, 2025, Torres allegedly sought treatment at the hospital, which allegedly diagnosed him with a spinal disc condition limiting his ability to life and bend.  That day, Torres allegedly informed Avendano about his diagnosis, and Avendano allegedly told him to take the rest of the week off to recover.  On August 24, 2025, Avendano allegedly terminated Torres.

On October 3, 2025, Torres filed the Complaint, alleging causes of action for (1) disability discrimination; (2) failure to provide reasonable accommodation; (3) failure to engage in the interactive process; (4) retaliation for requesting accommodation; (5) failure to prevent discrimination, harassment, and retaliation; (6) retaliation for using sick leave; (7) wrongful termination; and (8) failure to provide employment records.

On June 23, 2026, Torres filed this motion to quash subpoena and for protective order.  On July 15, 2026, Avendano filed the opposition, and on July 21, 2026, Torres filed the reply.

This case is set for a case management conference/status conference re: ADR and a hearing on the motion on July 28, 2026.

ANALYSIS

Torres seeks to quash the subpoena that Avendano served on his medical provider and requests a protective order.  For the following reasons, the motion is GRANTED IN PART and DENIED IN PART.

Legal Standard

Any party whose records are subject to subpoena may challenge the validity of the subpoena by way of a motion to quash.  (See Code Civ. Proc., §§ 1985.6; 2025.410, subd. (c).)  Under Code of Civil Procedure section 1987.1, a court “may make an order quashing the subpoena entirely, modifying it, or directing compliance with it upon such terms or conditions as the court shall declare.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.1, subd. (a).)  The court may also make any other order as may be appropriate to protect the person from unreasonable or oppressive demands such as unreasonable violations of the right of privacy.  (Ibid.)

Discussion

Torres argues that the subpoena served on his medical provider violates his right to privacy.  The court disagrees.

An individual’s right of privacy is protected by the California Constitution.  (Cal. Const., Art. I, § 1.)  In ruling on discovery motions, the court must balance the privacy claims of the responding party with the requesting party’s need for the information.  (Schnabel v. Super. Ct. (1993) 5 Cal.4th 704, 718–722.)  The responding party, as the party asserting the right of privacy, bears the initial burden of demonstrating (1) a legally protected privacy interest, (2) an objectively reasonable expectation of privacy in the given circumstances, and (3) a threatened intrusion that is serious.  (Williams v. Super. Ct. (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 552.)  If this standard is met, requesting party must then show that the requested documents are “directly relevant” to the litigation.  (Tylo v. Super. Ct. (1997) 55 Cal.App.4th 1379, 1387.)

Responding Party’s Burden

Torres’ privacy interest satisfies all three requirements under Williams.  For the first Williams requirement, medical records are protected by the constitutional right of privacy.  (See Britt v. Super. Ct. (1978) 20 Cal.3d 844, 862, commenting that patients have a right of privacy over confidential communications with their physicians and over their medical histories.)  For the second element, the court finds that Torres has an objectively reasonable expectation of privacy in his own medical records apart from his spinal disc diagnosis.  (See Bd. of Medical Quality Assurance v. Gherarhini (1979) 93 Cal.App.3d 669, 678, highlighting that “[a] person’s medical profile is an area of privacy infinitely more intimate, more personal in quality and nature than many areas already judicially recognized and protected,” disapproved of on other grounds by Williams, supra, 3 Cal.5th at 557, fn. 8.)  For the third Williams requirement, Torres cites the subpoena’s demand for “[a]ny and all medical records . . . relating to any care, treatment, diagnosis, prognosis, consultation and/or findings” without limiting the request to the medical condition at issue.  (Mot., p. 2, referencing Silva-Guzman Decl., Exh. 2, Attach. 3.)  Reviewing the language of the subpoena, the court finds that they are overbroad, threatening a serious intrusion into Torres’s private records.  Thus, the court finds that Torres met his burden under Williams.

Since Torres demonstrates that the subpoena implicates his right to privacy, the burden now shifts to Avendano to demonstrate that the requested records are directly relevant to the litigation.  (See Tylo, supra, 55 Cal.App.4th at 1387.)

Requesting Party’s Burden

The court finds the requested documents are directly relevant to this litigation.  Torres placed his physical health at issue with allegations that he left work during a shift to seek medical treatment, received medical treatment and a diagnosis the next day, and required a medical accommodation at work.  (See Compl., ¶¶ 11–14.)  Furthermore, the Complaint prays for “compensatory damages for emotional distress, humiliation, and mental anguish.”  (Compl., Prayer, ¶ 1.)  Torres’ medical records are probative and relevant to the litigation and the claims asserted by Torres.  Thus, the court finds that Avendano met its burden to obtain the requested documents.

Therefore, the motion to quash subpoena is DENIED.

Protective Order

Even though the court denies the motion to quash subpoena, it considers the request for a protective order.

“The court shall limit the scope of discovery if it determines that the burden, expense, or intrusiveness of that discovery clearly outweighs the likelihood that the information sought will lead to the discovery of admissible evidence.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2017.020, subd. (a).)  “The court may make this determination pursuant to a motion for protective order by a party or other affected person.”  (Ibid.)  The burden of proof is on the party seeking the protective order to show “good cause” for the order they seek.  (Fairmont Ins. Co. v. Super. Ct. (2000) 22 Cal.4th 245, 255.)

As discussed above, even though the information sought is relevant, the court finds the subpoena is overbroad because it seeks medical records that are not related to Torres’ spinal disc condition, his emotional distress, humiliation, or mental anguish.  (See Silva-Guzman Decl., Exh. 2, Attach. 3.)

Therefore, the motion for a protective order is GRANTED.  The subject subpoena is limited to records pertaining to Torres’ emotional distress, humiliation, mental anguish, and the relevant body parts for which Torres claims injury, namely the back and spine.  The court does not find that the request for records dating back to January 1, 2023 is overbroad, so the court declines to issue a protective order further limiting the timeframe for the subpoena.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the motion to quash subpoena is DENIED, and the motion for protective order is GRANTED.  A PROTECTIVE ORDER is ISSUED limiting the subpoena to any medical records dating back to January 1, 2023 dealing with plaintiff Adrian Torres’ (1) emotional distress, humiliation, or mental anguish and (2) back and spine complaints and/or treatment.

Case Number: 26PSCV00867    Hearing Date: July 28, 2026    Dept: G

Defendant Howard Tse-Ho Hsu’s Demurrer with Motion to Strike to Plaintiff City of Diamond Bar’s Complaint

Respondent: Plaintiff City of Diamond Bar

TENTATIVE RULING

Defendant Howard Tse-Ho Hsu’s Demurrer with Motion to Strike to Plaintiff City of Diamond Bar’s Complaint is CONTINUED.

BACKGROUND

This is a nuisance abatement action.  Defendants Howard Tse-Ho Hsu and Cecil Tse Hsu (collectively, the Hsus) allegedly own residential real property within the jurisdiction of plaintiff City of Diamond Bar (the City).  Defendants Homebridge Financial Services, Inc.; Mortgage Electronic Registration Systems, Inc.; and Interfaith Federal Credit Union each allegedly have a recorded interest in the real property.  On September 27, 2023, the City allegedly inspected the real property from the public right-of-way and observed a deteriorated wall, outdoor storage of items and debris, a vehicle encroaching into the public right-of-way, a vehicle parked on an unpaved surface, poor landscaping, and a commercial vehicle; all of which allegedly constitute violations of the City’s Municipal Code.  That same day, the City issued a Courtesy Notice to Correct Violation.  On October 4, 2023, the City allegedly re-inspected the real property and observed that the Hsus failed the correct the violations.  On October 5, 2023, the City allegedly informed the Hsus that they needed a zoning clearance to properly address the violations.  On March 18, 2024, the City allegedly re-inspected the real property and observed that the Hsus failed to complete the abatement work authorized by the City despite the Hsus obtaining a zoning clearance.  On April 9, 2024, the City allegedly issued a Civil Citation citing the storage of debris and trash storage receptacles in public view, maintenance of a deteriorated wall, and failure to maintain landscaping in proper condition; directing the Hsus to correct the violations; and ordering the Hsus to pay a fine.  On May 9, 2024; June 26, 2024; and September 30, 2024, the City allegedly issued additional Civil Citations citing the same violations, directing the Hsus to correct the violations, and ordering the Hsus to pay a fine.  Between June 2024 and March 2026, the City allegedly re-inspected the real property nine times and observed the same violations.

On March 10, 2026, the City filed the Complaint, alleging causes of action for (1) maintaining a public nuisance, (2) municipal code violations, and (3) declaratory and injunctive relief.

On April 13, 2026, Howard Tse-Ho Hsu filed this demurrer with motion to strike.  On May 6, 2026, the City filed the opposition, and there is no reply.  On May 19, 2026, the court continued the demurrer with motion to strike to allow the parties to properly meet and confer.

This case is set for a case management conference, an order to show cause re: failure to file proof of service, and a continued hearing on the demurrer and motion to strike on July 28, 2026.

ANALYSIS

Howard Tse-Ho Hsu generally demurs to the Complaint based on the failure to state sufficient facts and uncertainty and moves to strike the requests for injunctive and declaratory relief.  For the following reasons, the court CONTINUES the demurrer and motion to strike.

Legal Standard

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 430.41 and 435.5, prior to filing a demurrer or motion to strike, “the demurring party shall meet and confer in person or by telephone with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer.”  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 430.41, subd. (a); 435.5, subd. (a).)  They further provide that the demurring or moving party “shall identify all of the specific causes of action that it believes are subject to [demurrer or being stricken] and identify with legal support the basis of the deficiencies.”  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 430.41, subd. (a)(1); 435.5, subd. (a)(1).)

While Code of Civil Procedure sections 430.41 and 435.5 make clear failing to meet and confer is not grounds to overrule a demurrer, courts “are not required to ignore defects in the meet and confer process” and if the court determines “no meet and confer has taken place, or concludes further conferences between counsel would likely be productive, it retains discretion to order counsel to meaningfully discuss the pleadings with an eye toward reducing the number of issues or eliminating the need for a demurrer, and to continue the hearing date to facilitate that effort.”  (Dumas v. Los Angeles County Bd. of Supervisors (2020) 45 Cal.App.5th 348, 355 & fn. 3.)

Discussion

The court previously found that “Howard Tse-Ho Hsu’s counsel failed to provide a declaration that attests that they complied with the meet and confer requirement.”  (5/19/2026 Order Re: Court’s Final Ruling, p. 2, citing Dem. w/ MTS.)  The court ordered that Howard Tse-Ho Hsu’s counsel meet and confer with the City’s counsel and to file a supplemental meet and confer declaration.  (See 5/19/2026 Order Re: Court’s Final Ruling, p. 2.)  However, Howard Tse-Ho Hsu did not provide a supplemental meet and confer declaration.

Therefore, the court CONTINUES the demurrer with motion to strike and ORDERS Howard Tse-Ho Hsu’s counsel to MEET AND CONFER with the City’s counsel by telephone, video conference, or in person and FILE a SUPPLEMENTAL DECLARATION describing such meet and confer efforts, including whether the attempts were made by telephone, video conference, or in person, at least nine (9) court days before the next scheduled hearing on the demurrer and motion to strike.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the demurrer with is CONTINUED to a date to be determined at the hearing in Department G (Pomona).

Counsel for defendant Howard Tse-Ho Hsu is ORDERED to MEET AND CONFER with plaintiff City of Diamond Bar’s counsel regarding the present demurrer with motion to strike and to FILE a SUPPLEMENTAL DECLARATION describing such meet and confer efforts, including whether the attempts were made by telephone, video conference, or in person, at least nine (9) court days before the next scheduled hearing on the demurrer and motion to strike.
