Case Number: 24SMCV02786    Hearing Date: September 16, 2026    Dept: I

This is a last-second application filed to move the trial or the MSJ hearing on a motion that was also filed at the last second.  This is part of a disturbing trend of filing MSJ motions at the last possible second and then moving to have them heard or the trial continued.  It is a bad practice.  Last week, a party attempted this but there was a problem with the service so the motion was untimely.  The court therefore denied that application.  

 

But that said, the law is clear.  If the motion is timely filed—meaning that it is filed at least 81 days before the hearing plus additional time depending on service, and the earliest possible hearing date is at least 30 days before the trial—then the motion must be heard.  This motion was filed on September 3, 2026.  81 days later is November 23, 2026.  Adding two court days for electronic service, the earliest hearing date is November 25, 2026—the day before the Thanksgiving holiday.  The trial is December 28, 2026.  Had the motion been filed and served even one day later, it would be untimely (as the earliest possible hearing date would be November 30).  But the fact is that the motion is timely.  The court will therefore GRANT the motion to the extent that the court will exercise its discretion to hear the motion, presently set for December 3, just four court days after the earliest possible hearing date.  The trial and FSC remain on calendar.

 

The court will not hear argument.

Case Number: 25SMCV03338    Hearing Date: September 16, 2026    Dept: I

This is a demurrer reflecting an attack on plaintiff’s third attempt to state a viable cause of action.  Plaintiff opposes.

 

The SAC alleges that plaintiff was walking on the sand at Will Rogers Beach in a recreational sand area.  As plaintiff was going past unattended life guard tower number 12, he tripped and fell on an exposed, unsecured, and unmarked rock or rocks.  He claims he was severely injured.  He alleges that defendants negligently maintained, managed, operated, controlled, and safeguarded the premises such that an exposed or unmarked rock was allowed to exist.  Specifically, he alleges that defendant purportedly cleans and rakes public beaches daily using specialized equipment that cleans 30,000 square meters of sand per hour.  Plaintiff states that the coastline is cleaned daily and that over 10,000 tons of trash are removed annually such that the “main objective of these maintenance activities is to remove debris like glass and metals, as well as natural materials like seaweed, wood, and rocks, from recreational sand areas.”  (SAC at par. 7.)

 

The court has previously sustained a similar demurrer twice before and much of the court's analysis was set forth there and will not be repeated here, although it still applies.  The court warned that it was unlikely to grant leave to amend again if this demurrer succeeds.  Defendant asserts that the complaint still fails to state sufficient facts.  In particular, defendant states that plaintiff failed to show a dangerous condition.  The court notes that although a public entity does have a duty to protect against dangerous conditions on public property generally pursuant to Government Code section 835, it has no duty to protect against minor or trivial or insignificant risks; the risk must be substantial.  (Mohler v. County of Santa Clara (2023) 92 Cal.App.5th 418.)  Here, plaintiff claims he tripped over a rock in the sand.  Even were the court to accept that defendant actually rakes every inch of coastline, the court does not believe that an exposed rock on a beach, without more, poses a significant risk of injury or that defendant has a duty to remove all rocks.  A sufficiently dangerous condition exists when public property is damaged or has deteriorated in a way that foreseeably endangers those using it or possesses characteristics in its design or feature that leads to the danger.  (Cerna v. City of Oakland (2008) 161 Cal.App.4th 1340.)  A condition is not dangerous, though, if even viewing the condition most favorably to the plaintiff, the court determines as a matter of law that the risk was trivial, meaning that no reasonable person would conclude that the condition created a substantial risk when used with due care in a manner it was reasonably foreseeable it would be used.  (Sambrano v. City of San Diego (2001) 94 Cal.App.4th 225.)  The fact that an area is not inspected is therefore not enough.

 

Plaintiff here has not alleged anything about the rock over which he tripped other than to say that it was exposed and unmarked.  But a rock on a beach, without more, is a trivial risk at best to one who is looking where one is going even reading the complaint in the most favorable manner to plaintiff.  There is no allegation that the rock was hidden or posed some kind of unusual threat or danger.  It was just a rock on a beach.  Plaintiff suggests that the court might be making unfair inferences against it, in that perhaps the rock was partially concealed.  That makes no sense.  Plaintiff is the one who tripped over the rock.  If it was concealed or partially concealed, he would have so alleged.  The fact that it was unsecured or unmarked does not change that.  Indeed one wonders how the County would secure a rock or mark it unless it was hidden in some unusual and strange way. 

 

Relatedly, there is no sufficient allegation of notice.  Plaintiff suggests that the beach is regularly raked and cleaned, but that is not notice.  Notice would require either actual notice—a showing that someone else was hurt or that the condition was known and sufficiently obvious that the risk was apparent and actually seen by the defendant or its agents—or an allegation that defendant created the condition.  (Martinez v. City of Beverly Hills (2021) 71 Cal.App.5th 508.)  None of that is adequately alleged here.  A statement of routine sand-raking is not sufficient.  The court is aware that Martinez was a summary judgment case, but the legal principles are the same.  Plaintiff must at least allege with reasonable detail that this particular rock was known to the defendant in a way where the danger was readily apparent.  The fact that there is a rock on a beach is not enough.  And there is no reason to believe that there were prior complaints or that defendant created the condition.  Plaintiff does note that the rock was visible, but the fact that the condition is visible is not enough.  (Heskel v. City of San Diego (2014) 227 Cal.App.4th 313.)  Indeed, if anything, it suggests that had plaintiff been exercising due care, he would not have tripped on the rock at all.

 

Defendant also claims immunity.  Government Code section 831.21 provides for immunity from dangerous condition liability relating to public beaches if the danger is part of the natural condition.  There is no allegation or suggestion that the defense put the rock in the location in question.  It would seem that the immunity applies.  The court also notes the laudable policy underlying this statute.  It is not possible for the government to become the guarantor or insurer of nature.  Where we are talking about a street or something, we are not talking about nature.  But a beach is different.  The public benefits from access to beaches in their natural condition.  As a result, if there is an injury resulting from that natural condition, the government is not liable for it.  There is nothing in the complaint that would lead the court to believe that the rock in question was not a naturally occurring natural feature.

 

The court does not believe that further leave to amend would be fruitful.  Plaintiff has not suggested how a fourth attempt to state a claim would succeed.  Therefore, the demurrer is SUSTAINED WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND.  Defendant to lodge an order of dismissal.


Case Number: 25SMCV04580    Hearing Date: September 16, 2026    Dept: I

Plaintiffs sued their carrier and the claims administrator for damages resulting from a water leak.  Plaintiffs not only claim a breach of contract and bad faith, but also fraud.  The demurrer is only as to the fraud-type causes of action and is brought only by defendant Wilhite. 

 

Plaintiffs allege that when they returned home from a vacation on June 25, 2023, they discovered that a pipe had burst and water had flooded the property.  They retained a restoration company for the next several days, but after remediation was completed, they discovered that the damage was more extensive than they had believed.  Plaintiffs reported the loss to Bamboo (the administrator), which handled the claim under a reservation of rights.  Plaintiffs submitted to recorded examinations with Bamboo’s investigators about the incident and the property.  On February 23, 2024, Bamboo wrote, over Wilhite’s signature, and stated that it had completed the investigation and would accept the claim.  Plaintiffs were offered $168,237.06.  However, on March 18, 2024, plaintiffs retained a public adjuster, and they inquired as to the payment status on certain elements of their claim.  They demanded that the property be inspected by a licensed contractor.  Bamboo then demanded plaintiffs produce tax records to justify their request for loss of rental income.  Bamboo did retain a licensed contractor who inspected the property and determined that it would cost $237,944.53 to repair.  Bamboo told plaintiffs on May 14, 2024, that it accepted that estimate and would issue a supplemental check.  However, plaintiffs wrote to Bamboo on August 22, 2024, and said that they believed that the claim was worth $1.42 million, and they provided an itemized list detailing that amount.  They assert that they requested mediation, but rather than mediate, Bamboo took another examination of plaintiffs.  After that, plaintiffs state Bamboo went silent aside from rote letters.  On March 31, 2025, Bamboo told plaintiffs that the claim was denied due to false material statements and concealment by plaintiffs during presentation of the claim.  Plaintiffs say that they asked for information about what was misrepresented, but Bamboo provided no substantive response.  Before the court is Chelsea Wilhite’s demurrer.  She is the Bamboo employee who wrote the February 2024 letter.  Plaintiffs oppose.

 

Wilhite asks the court to take judicial notice of the SAC and various RFA responses that authenticate documents.  The court will take judicial notice of the SAC and the RFA responses.  To that extent, the request is GRANTED.

 

The gist of the demurrer is that the fraud causes of action (fifth and sixth causes of action) are not pled with the requisite specificity.  The specificity requirement applies to both fraud and to negligent misrepresentation.  Further, reliance, an element of both causes of action, must also be pled with sufficient specificity.  (National Union Fire Ins. Co. v. Cambridge Integrated Services Group, Inc. (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 35.)  The misrepresentations are specifically alleged.  They are that Bamboo had completed its investigation when it allegedly had not, and that it had accepted the claim when it allegedly had not, and that the letter so stating went out over Wilhite’s signature.  That is enough to satisfy the requisite pleading standards as to the nature of the fraud or misrepresentations.  A more difficult question is whether those statements can lead to personal liability against Wilhite, who acted as an agent for Bamboo.  The court has some doubts about that, given that the general rule is that an agent is not responsible for most torts that are attributable to the principal and that were done where the agency was disclosed and in the course and scope of the agency.  But where the agent engages in intentional misconduct, there can be personal liability.  While the court has some doubts as to the negligent misrepresentation tort (meaning the court has some doubts that Wilhite can be personally liable for negligent misrepresentation), it appears that this tort is also covered in the exception.  (Peredia v. HR Mobile Services, Inc. (2018) 25 Cal.App.5th 680.)  To the extent the demurrer is based on the agency issue, then, it is unsuccessful.  Reliance is also a basis for the demurrer.  Wilhite argues that even if the letter was false, plaintiffs did not rely on it.  Plaintiffs do allege that they allege that they hired a public adjuster rather than just accept the money and call it a day.  But that is a different issue.  There is no reason to believe that they would have acted differently had the offending language not been in the letter.  But plaintiffs allege more than that.  In paragraph 85 of the SAC, plaintiffs state that they “made certain decisions as to how the subject water loss and the claim should be managed.  For example, plaintiffs began renovation at the Subject Property expecting that portion of the costs will be covered under the Policy, Plaintiffs decided to dispose some of their personal property expecting compensation for those items under the Policy, and similarly, Plaintiffs rented storage units to store their remaining personal property expecting payments under the Policy for the costs incurred in renting the units.”  That is enough.  Whether this is truly material, or whether there is truly reliance, or whether Wilhite can be responsible for it personally will need to be decided on summary judgment or trial. 

 

The demurrer is OVERRULED.  Wilhite has 30 days to answer.


Case Number: 26SMCV02267    Hearing Date: September 16, 2026    Dept: I

This is a medical malpractice case, but it also involves alleged battery.  Plaintiff sues the surgeon who performed the procedure and also the hospitalist, who demurs.  Plaintiff opposes the demurrer.

 

As to the cause of action for battery, the demurrer is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE, although the court doubts that any amendment will work.  The demurring defendant is the hospitalist.  This defendant had nothing to do with the surgery; this defendant came into the picture after the surgery was over.   The court just does not understand the battery issue.  Indeed, plaintiff comes about as close as possible to conceding the issue without actually admitting that the demurrer is insurmountable in this regard.

 

There is also a claim for fraud.  The theory is that the hospitalist was aware of the problem, part of which was that the surgery was allegedly elective surgery but the chart was changed to suggest that it was emergency surgery.  The argument is that the hospitalist was aware of the false chart but initially said nothing to plaintiff, although plaintiff does agree that it was the hospitalist who later disclosed the alleged impropriety.  The problem here is that the court does not understand reliance.  In other words, there was at least allegedly a period of time where the hospitalist purportedly knew of the false entry but did not disclose it.  But so what?  For plaintiff to sue under this theory, plaintiff needs to establish that because the hospitalist did not disclose the entry sooner, plaintiff, in reliance, took some action or refrained from taking some action to plaintiff’s detriment.  There is nothing like that alleged here.  The actual injury itself was not hidden, and there is no allegation that due to the hospitalist’s statements (or lack thereof) any treatment changed.  Accordingly, the demurrer as to this issue is also SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

Thus, the demurrer is SUSTAINED WITH 30 DAYS’ LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

As to the motion to strike, the court agrees with the defense that the allegations of willfulness should be stricken (without prejudice to a motion at a later date to add a request for punitive damages upon a proper showing.  Thus, that aspect of the motion to strike is GRANTED.  The reservation of the right to amend is also stricken.  There is no right to amend; there is a right to bring a motion for leave to amend.  The motion to strike is therefore GRANTED.
