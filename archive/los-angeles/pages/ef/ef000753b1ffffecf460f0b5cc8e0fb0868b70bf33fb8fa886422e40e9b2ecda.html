Ruling:                                    The Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings is GRANTED.

 

Cross-Defendants Matthew Eanet and Eanet P.C. to give notice.

 

The Court encourages all parties to appear remotely via LA CourtConnect. If submitting on the Court's tentative ruling, please follow the instructions provided above.

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On November 14, 2019, Plaintiff Leonard Babaie (“Plaintiff”) filed the operative Complaint against Defendants Law Offices of Armand Tinkerian (“Tinkerian Firm”), Armand Tinkerian (“Tinkerian”) (jointly, “Tinkerian Defendants”), and DOES 1 to 10 (collectively, “Defendants”). The Complaint asserts a sole cause of action for Legal Malpractice.

            On August 24, 2020, Tinkerian Defendants filed a Motion to Compel Arbitration. The matter came for hearing on October 26, 2020; the motion was granted.

            On May 17, 2022, Tinkerian filed the operative First Amended Cross-Complaint (“FACC”) against Cross-Defendants Richard Lee; Salisian Lee, LLP; Matthew Eanet; Eanet, P.C.; Reza Iraj Gharakhani; Rostow & Auster, LLP; and ROES 1 through 100 (collectively, “Cross-Defendants”), asserting a sole cause of action for Equitable Indemnity.

            On May 20, 2022, Cross-Defendants Matthew Eanet and Eanet, P.C. (jointly, “Eanet Cross-Defendants”) filed their Answer to the FACC. On June 20, 2022, Cross-Defendants Richard Lee and Salisian Lee, LLP (jointly, “Lee Cross-Defendants”) filed their Answer to the FACC. On November 28, 2022, Cross-Defendants Reza Iraj Gharakhani and Rostow & Auster, LLP (jointly, “Gharakhani Cross-Defendants”) filed their Answer to the FACC.

            On April 21, 2026, Eanet Cross-Defendants filed the instant Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings. Any opposition would have been due June 24, 2026. To date, no opposition has been filed.

DISCUSSION

Applicable Law

A defendant may move for judgment on the pleadings on the grounds that: (1) the court has no jurisdiction of the subject of the cause of action alleged in the complaint; or (2) the complaint does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action against that defendant. (Code Civ. Proc. § 438, subd. (c)(1)(B).)¿¿¿ 

The standard for ruling on a motion for judgment on the pleadings is essentially the same as that applicable to a general demurrer, that is, under the state of the pleadings, together with matters that may be judicially noticed, it appears that a party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. (Bezirdjian v. O'Reilly (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 316, 321-322 [citing Schabarum v. California Legislature (1998) 60 Cal.App.4th 1205, 1216].)¿Any defects must either appear on the face of the pleading, or else be taken by judicial notice. (Id. at 321-22.) The parties’ ability to prove their respective claims is of no concern. (Cloud, supra, 67 Cal.App.4th 995 at 999.) Though the Court must accept the allegations of the complaint and answer as true (Gerawan Farming, Inc. v. Lyons (2000) 24 Cal.4th 468, 515), it will not do so for “conclusions of law or fact, opinions, speculation, or allegations contrary to law or [judicially noticed] facts….” (Stevenson Real Estate Servs., Inc. v. CB Richard Ellis Real Estate Servs., Inc. (2006) 138 Cal.App.4th 1215, 1219–20.)¿¿

Meet and Confer

Before filing a judgment on the pleadings pursuant to this chapter, the moving party shall meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by videoconference with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to the motion for judgment on the pleadings for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the motion for judgment on the pleadings...” (Code Civ. Proc., § 439, subd. (a).)

Eanet Cross-Defendants’ counsel of record, Rinat Klier Erlich submits a declaration in support of the present motion. (Mot. at 8:1-16 – Erlich Decl.) However, Counsel Erlich’s declaration makes no mention of compliance with the meet and confer requirement prior to the filing of the instant motion. (Id.) Nevertheless, the Court will exercise its discretion and rule on the merits below.

Application to Facts

Eanet Cross-Defendants move for judgment on the pleadings regarding the FACC on the grounds that the Court granted its Motion to Deem Request for Admissions (Set One) Admitted against Tinkerian, thus Tinkerian has admitted Eanet Cross-Defendants were not negligent to Babaie in their representation of him in the JM Van Nuys LLC, et al. v. Leonard Babaie, Case No. BC540867 (the “Underlying Action”) and has no evidence to support his claims for equitable indemnity against Eanet Cross-Defendants.

Request for Judicial Notice

Eanet Cross-Defendants’ request for judicial notice of the Request for Admissions (Set One) nos. 1, 15, 17-19, 22-30 deemed admitted against Tinkerian is GRANTED per Evidence Code Section 452, subdivision (h). (See Pang v. Beverly Hosp., Inc. (2000) 79 Cal.App.4th 986, 989–990 [“In addition to the facts pleaded, we may consider matters that may be judicially noticed, including a party’s admissions or concessions which cannot reasonably be controverted.”].)

Equitable Indemnity

“Equitable indemnity, which ‘requires no contractual relationship,’ ‘is premised on a joint legal obligation to another for damages’; it is ‘subject to allocation of fault principles and comparative equitable apportionment of loss.’ ” (C.W. Howe Partners Inc. v. Mooradian (2019) 43 Cal.App.5th 688, 700.) “ ‘The elements of a cause of action for [equitable] indemnity are (1) a showing of fault on the part of the indemnitor and (2) resulting damages to the indemnitee for which the indemnitor is ... equitably responsible.’ ” (Id.)

The FACC alleges, inter alia, that during all relevant times Eanet Cross-Defendants represented Plaintiff concurrently alongside Tinkerian in the Underlying Action. (FACC, ¶¶13-20.) Contrary to the information provided and/or plan of success devised by Tinkerian and Eanet Cross-Defendants, Eanet Cross-Defendants failed to follow or execute the plan resulting in a less favorable settlement or recovery in the Underlying Action. (Id., ¶¶21-22, 27.) Eanet Cross-Defendants must reimburse Tinkerian for the damages purported incurred by Plaintiff, which he is required to pay based on shared responsibility. (Id., ¶26.)

However, on April 10, 2026, this Court deemed Eanet Cross-Defendants’ Request for Admissions (“RFA”) (Set One) admitted against Tinkerian. (Min. Order, 4/10/26 at p. 1; RJN, nos. 1-13.) Accordingly, Tinkerian admits that he was the sole attorney of record for Plaintiff in the Underlying Action and solely responsible for the legal work performed; has no facts supporting Eanet Cross-Defendants’ actions or inactions were the cause of any damage claimed by Plaintiff; he has no evidence and/or facts supporting that Eanet Cross-Defendants’ breached any duty of care owed to Plaintiff regarding his representation in the Underlying Action; he has no evidence and/or facts supporting that Eanet Cross-Defendants’ had any supervisory authority over his representation of Plaintiff in the Underlying Action; he has no evidence and/or facts supporting that Eanet Cross-Defendants’ received any portion of legal fees paid by Plaintiff for his representation in the Underlying Action; he showed up thirty minutes late to Plaintiff’s deposition in the Underlying Action; and California law bars indemnity claims against successor attorneys like Eanet Cross-Defendants.

            Therefore, as a matter of law Tinkerian cannot support his claim for equitable indemnity against Eanet Cross-Defendants.

CONCLUSION

            The Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings is GRANTED.

            Cross-Defendants Matthew Eanet and Eanet P.C. to give notice.

Case Number: 24STCV00957    Hearing Date: July 8, 2026    Dept: 310

Tentative Ruling

Judge Kevin C. Brazile

Department 310

Hearing Date:                                July 8, 2026

Case Name:                            Martin Ansoorian v. Tesla Motors, Inc., et al.

Case No.:                                24STCV00957

Motion:                                  Motion to Compel Enforcement of Records Subpoena

Moving Party:                         Defendant Tesla Motors, Inc.

Responding Party:                  None.

Notice:                                    OK

 

 

Ruling:                                             The Motion is granted.

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

The Court encourages all parties to appear remotely via LA CourtConnect.  If submitting on the Court's tentative ruling, please follow the instructions provided above.

 

 

BACKGROUND

            This is an employment action filed by Plaintiff Martin Ansoorian (“Plaintiff”) against Defendants Tesla Motors, Inc. (“Tesla”), Diana Affre (“Affre”), and Does 1 through 25 (collectively, “Defendants”).

            On January 12, 2024, Plaintiff filed his complaint, asserting the following causes of action: (1) disability discrimination in violation of the Fair Employment and Housing Act (“FEHA”), (2) unlawful retaliation in violation of FEHA, (3) failure to accommodate, (4) failure to engage in interactive process, (5) failure to prevent discrimination and harassment from occurring in violation of FEHA, (6) FEHA violations based upon sexual harassment, (7) FEHA violations based upon sex and gender discrimination, (8) violation of Labor Code sections 98.6, 6310, and 1102.5(c), (9) wrongful termination in violation of public policy, (10) violation of Civil Code section 43, (11) violation of Civil Code section 51.7, (12) intentional infliction of emotional distress, and (13) negligent hiring, supervision, retention, and/or training.

            On May 26, 2026, Tesla filed the instant motion to compel enforcement of records subpoena against non-party Brain Health USA (“Brain Health”).

No opposition was filed.

DISCUSSION

Applicable Law

A party seeking discovery from a person who is not a party to the action may obtain discovery by oral deposition, written deposition, or deposition for production of business records.¿ (Code Civ. Proc. § 2020.010.)¿ Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1987.1, “the court, upon motion reasonably made [by a party] . . . may make an order . . . directing compliance with [a subpoena] upon those terms or conditions as the court shall declare . . .” (Code Civ. Proc. § 1987.1(a).) “If a deponent fails to answer any question or to produce any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing under the deponent’s control that is specified in the deposition notice or a deposition subpoena, the party seeking discovery may move the court for an order compelling that answer or production.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.480, subd. (a).) Further, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure § 2017.010, “any party may obtain discovery regarding any matter, not privileged, that is relevant to the subject matter involved in the pending action.” “Discovery may relate to the claim or defense of the party seeking discovery or of any other party to the action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2017.010.)

Discussion

            Tesla moves for an order from the Court compelling Brain Health to release Plaintiff’s medical and therapy records in response to a records subpoena.

Timing

A motion to compel compliance with a records subpoena must be “made no later than 60 days after the completion of the record of the deposition....” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.480, subd. (b).)

Here, Tesla served Plaintiff with a notice to consumer or employee and objection, attaching a copy of the deposition subpoena for production of business records to Brain Health on February 28, 2025. (Reed Decl., ¶ 3; Exh. A.) Then, Tesla served the subpoena on Brain Health, requesting production by March 26, 2025. (Reed Decl., ¶ 4.) On February 25, 2026, Tesla served Plaintiff with a revised notice to consumer and copy of the revised subpoena to Brain Health. (Reed Decl., ¶ 8; Exh. C.) Under this subpoena, Tesla requested production from Brain Health by March 24, 2026. (Reed Decl., ¶ 9.) Thus, the 60-day deadline fell on May 23, 2026, a Saturday, so the deadline extended to the next court day, May 26, 2026. Tesla filed the instant motion on May 26, 2026. Therefore, Tesla complied with the timing requirements under Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.480, subdivision (b).

Separate Statement

“Any motion involving the content of a discovery request or the responses to such a request must be accompanied by a separate statement.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1345(a).)

Here, Tesla filed a separate statement with the Court in compliance with this requirement.

Meet and Confer

A motion to compel compliance with a records subpoena must “be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.480, subd. (b).)

Tesla’s counsel details numerous phone calls and emails to Brain Health on numerous occasions regarding the underlying records subpoena. (Reed Decl., ¶¶ 11-12.) Thus, this requirement is met.

Analysis

            As mentioned, Tesla served Plaintiff with a notice to consumer and a copy of the subpoena on February 28, 2025, requesting production of Plaintiff’s medical and therapy records. (Reed Decl., ¶ 3; Exh. A.) Thereafter, Tesla served the subpoena on Brain Health. (Reed Decl., ¶ 4.) Then, on March 14, 2025, Plaintiff executed a written authorization for the release of his “medical records concerning [his] treatment as specified in the attached subpoena for records.” (Reed Decl., ¶ 5; Exh. B.) On March 26, 2025, Tesla’s counsel provided Plaintiff’s authorization to Brain Health. (Reed Decl., ¶ 6.) Brain Health neglected to produce Plaintiff’s therapy records because Plaintiff’s authorization did not expressly mention release of those records. (Reed Decl., ¶ 6.) Tesla’s counsel explained to Brain Heath that Plaintiff’s authorization encompassed release of the therapy records, but Brain Health responded that release of the therapy records required express language. (Reed Decl., ¶ 7.)

On February 25, 2025, Tesla served Plaintiff with a revised notice to consumer, attaching a revised subpoena to Brain Health. (Reed Decl., ¶ 8; Exh. C.) Tesla served the subpoena on Brain Health, requesting production by March 24, 2026. (Reed Decl., ¶ 9.) However, on February 27, 2026, Plaintiff’s counsel sent a notice to Brain Health asserting objections to the subpoena and directing Brain Health to not produce the requested documents. (Reed Decl., ¶ 10; Exh. D.) On March 24, 2024, Brain Health produced documents but did not include the therapy records. (Reed Decl., ¶ 11.) Brain Health indicated that it needed Plaintiff’s signed release of his medical records to proceed. (Reed Decl., ¶ 11.) On April 23, 2026, after Tesla sent a letter further demanding all responsive documents, Brain Health transmitted further documents and stated it was willing to share the medial records and meet compliance, but patient consent was required under HIPAA regulations. (Reed Decl., ¶¶ 12-13.)

On April 29, 2026, a paralegal at Tesla’s counsel’s offices sent to Brain Health the prior signed authorization by Plaintiff allowing release of his medical and therapy records. (Reed Decl., ¶ 14; Exh. F.) In response, Brain Health again stated it was willing to share but was bound by HIPAA regulations and requested Plaintiff complete and sign a new 2026 authorization form. (Reed Decl., ¶ 15; Exh. G.) As of the filing of this motion, Tesla has not received Plaintiff’s therapy records from Brain Health. (Reed Decl., ¶ 16.)

Tesla argues Brain Health waived objection to the subpoena by failure to follow statutory procedures under Code of Civil Procedure section 1985.3. A “nonparty whose personal records are sought by a subpoena duces tecum may, prior to the date of production, serve on the subpoenaing party, the witness, and the deposition officer, a written objection that cites the specific grounds on which production of the personal records should be prohibited. Notice of the bringing of that motion shall be given to the witness and deposition officer at least five days prior to production.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1985.3, subd. (g).)

Here, Brain Health failed to provide notice of written objection to the subpoena at least five days prior to the production date. Under both the initial and revised subpoena, Brain Health provided document production without the therapy records requested and did not provide an explanation for its failure to comply until after the production date. Therefore, Brain Health did not comply with Code of Civil Procedure section 1985.3.

Tesla argues the therapy records should be compelled because the therapy records are relevant to Plaintiff’s claims and Tesla’s defenses. Because Plaintiff alleges he was subject to harassment, retaliation, and discrimination due to his sex/gender and disability, as well as intentional infliction of emotional distress, the Court finds the therapy records relevant to Plaintiff’s claimed mental injuries and emotional distress pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure § 2017.010.

Tesla further argues Plaintiff’s objections sent to Brain Health are meritless because he waived his privacy rights and the psychotherapist-patient privilege under the patient-litigant exception. The psychotherapist-patient privilege does not apply to “a communication relevant to an issue concerning the mental or emotional condition of the patient if such issue has been tendered by: (a) The patient; (b) Any party claiming through or under the patient; ….” (Evid. Code, § 1016.) The patient-litigation exception to the psychotherapist-patient privilege applies when a plaintiff places his mental or emotional condition at issue in litigation. (In re Lifschutz (1970) 2 Cal.3d 415, 434-435.) Additionally, a “[p]laintiff … may not withhold information which relates to any physical or mental condition which they have put in issue by bringing lawsuit.” (Britt v. Superior Court (1978) 20 Cal.3d 844, 864.) Plaintiff’s complaint alleges Tesla caused him emotional distress, psychological distress, anxiety, anguish, mental harm, and feelings of hopelessness. (Compl., ¶¶ 48, 63, 77, 78, 90, 96, 106, 113, 121, 133, 142, 149, 154, 161.)  Further, Plaintiff previously executed a written authorization for the release of medical records concerning his treatment pursuant to the subpoena. (Reed Decl., ¶ 5; Exh. B.) Specifically, Plaintiff’s authorization stated:

I specifically authorize the release of all documents (including any computer-recorded, handwritten, audiotape or videotape materials) that relate or refer to any psychiatric, psychological, emotional or mental condition, diagnosis, counseling, treatment and/or examination, including but not limited to billing statements, notes, diaries, journals, audio or videotapes of any office visits or other treatment, memoranda, insurance forms, treatment plans, laboratory or other test results, diagnoses, prognoses, psychological evaluations, records of any hospitalizations, prescriptions, records of treatment performed, appraisals of patient status, documents (including any photographs or other materials) that I have submitted, records listing all appointments scheduled, rescheduled and/or cancelled by either me, my physician or other health care professional, any authorizations for me to miss work, or releases for me to return to work, and any documents listing any work restrictions, as specified in the attached subpoena for records, from January 1, 2018 to the present. This authorization constitutes an express waiver of the physician-patient privilege. I expressly waive, release, and discharge any and all claims and liability that I may have arising from the release of such information, documents, and records pursuant to this Authorization.

(Reed Decl., Exh. B.)  

            The Court finds this evidence as sufficient to waive the psychotherapist-patient privilege under the patient-litigant exception.

Notably, Plaintiff has not filed an opposition to the instant motion, nor did Plaintiff file a formal objection with Tesla following the notices to consumer regarding the subpoena.

Thus, the motion to compel enforcement of records subpoena is granted.

Sanctions

Tesla requests the Court award monetary sanctions against Brain Health in the amount of $3,698.90.

The Court may award sanctions against a party “engaging in conduct that is a misuse of the discovery process.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030.) “The court may impose a monetary sanction ordering that one engaging in the misuse of the discovery process, or any attorney advising that conduct, or both pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney’s fees, incurred by anyone as a result of that conduct.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030; subd. (a).) Misuse of the discovery process includes “Failing to respond or to submit to an authorized method of discovery.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.010, subd. (d).)

Here, the Court finds an award of sanctions against Brain Health, a non-party, who stated multiple times that it sought to act in accordance with HIPAA regulations, as unnecessary. (See Reed Decl., ¶¶ 13, 15.) While the Court ultimately grants the instant motion against Brain Health, Brain Health expressed willingness to comply, after Plaintiff provided renewed authorization. (Reed Decl., ¶ 15.) Therefore, the Court, in its discretion, declines to award sanctions.

CONCLUSION

            The Motion to Compel Enforcement of Records Subpoena is GRANTED.

            Moving party to give notice.

Moving Party:                         Defendants King Taco Restaurants, Inc. and Raquel Martinez

Responding Party:                  Plaintiff Cindy Contreras

Notice:                                    OK

 

 

Ruling:                                             The Demurrer to the Second Amended Complaint is overruled. Defendants are ordered to answer in 20 days.

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

The Court encourages all parties to appear remotely via LA CourtConnect.  If submitting on the Court's tentative ruling, please follow the instructions provided above.

 

 

BACKGROUND

            Plaintiff Cindy Contreras was employed by Defendant King Taco Restaurant, Inc. (“King Taco”) from July 28, 2008 through October 28, 2024.  Defendant Raquel Martinez was Defendant King Taco’s General Manager during Plaintiff’s employment.  Plaintiff alleges Defendant Martinez asked her to participate in an internal investigation of Plaintiff’s direct supervisor, Lizbeth Gutierrez.  Plaintiff participated in the investigation and confirmed and reported Gutierrez’s use of discriminatory and homophobic remarks at work.  Plaintiff later reported Defendant Martinez to Gutierrez for creating an unsafe work environment after Plaintiff witnessed an altercation between Defendant Martinez and a vendor.  Plaintiff alleges that after reporting Defendant Martinez, Defendant Martinez retaliated against her and divulged to her confidential statements regarding Gutierrez’s homophobic remarks to Gutierrez.  Plaintiff alleges the work environment became so hostile, intimidating and unbearable that she felt compelled to resign on October 28, 2024 after sixteen (16) years of service.

            On July 31, 2025, Plaintiff filed this action against Defendants King Taco and Martinez.  On November 7, 2025, Plaintiff filed a First Amended Complaint.  On March 18, 2026, the Court sustained demurrer without leave to amend to Plaintiff’s sixth and seventh causes of action for retaliation (Labor Code §98.6) and retaliation (Labor Code §§1102.5, 1102.6) and sustained demurrer with 30 days leave to amend to the first through fifth and eight causes of action.

            On April 17, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”) alleging (1) Discrimination in Violation of Gov’t Code §§ 12940 et seq.; (2) Harassment in Violation of Gov’t Code §§ 12940 et seq.; (3) Retaliation in Violation of Gov’t Code §§ 12940 et seq.; (4) Failure to Prevent Discrimination, Harassment, and Retaliation in Violation of Gov’t Code §§ 12940(k); (5) Declaratory Judgment; and (6) Constructive Wrongful Discharge in Violation of Public Policy.

            On May 19, 2026, Defendants filed a demurrer to the SAC.  On June 24, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition.  On June 30, 2026, Defendant filed a reply.

DISCUSSION

Applicable Law

            “The primary function of a pleading is to give the other party notice so that it may prepare its case [citation], and a defect in a pleading that otherwise properly notifies a party cannot be said to affect substantial rights.”  (Harris v. City of Santa Monica (2013) 56 Cal.4th 203, 240.)  A general demurrer may be brought under Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (e) if insufficient facts are stated to support the cause of action asserted in a complaint or under section 430.10, subdivision (a), where the court has no jurisdiction of the subject of the cause of action alleged in the pleading.

“A¿demurrer¿tests the legal sufficiency of the factual allegations in a complaint.” (Ivanoff v. Bank of America, N.A.¿(2017) 9 Cal.App.5th 719, 725.)  The Court looks to whether “the complaint alleges facts sufficient to state a cause of action or discloses a complete defense.” (Id.) The Court does not “read passages from a complaint in isolation; in reviewing a ruling on a demurrer, we read the complaint ‘as a whole and its parts in their context.’ [Citation.]” (West v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A. (2013) 214 Cal.App.4th 780, 804.)  The Court “assume[s] the truth of the properly pleaded factual allegations, facts that reasonably can be inferred from those expressly pleaded and matters of which judicial notice has been taken.”  (Harris, supra, 56 Cal.4th p. 240.) “The court does not, however, assume the truth of contentions, deductions or conclusions of law. [Citation.]” (Durell v. Sharp Healthcare (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 1350, 1358.)

Application to Facts

            Defendants demur to the SAC on grounds that (1) Plaintiff fails to establish an adverse employment action under FEHA; (2) Plaintiff fails to establish a causal link for retaliation; (3) Plaintiff fails to establish a protected characteristic for her discrimination and harassment claims; (4) Plaintiff fails to establish that she suffered “severe and pervasive” harassment within FEHA; (5) Plaintiff’s derivative FEHA claims fail as a matter of law; and (6) Plaintiff fails to establish constructive discharge.

            Plaintiff sufficiently alleges adverse employment action

            An adverse employment action is one that materially affects the terms, conditions, or privileges of employment.  (Yanowitz v. L'Oreal USA, Inc. (2005) 36 Cal.4th 1028, 1036, 1051.)  What constitutes an adverse employment action “is not, by its nature, susceptible to a mathematically precise test,” and, as a result, the “significance of  particular types of adverse actions must be evaluated by taking into account the legitimate interest of both the employer and employee.”  (Id. at 1054; Brown v. Los Angeles Unified School Dist. (2021) 60 Cal.App.5th 1092, 1106.)  “The inquiry as to whether an employment action is adverse requires a case-by-case determination based upon objective evidence.”  (Thomas v. Dept. of Corrections (2000) 77 Cal.App.4th 507, 510.) 

“Minor or relatively trivial adverse actions or conduct by employers or fellow employees that, from an objective perspective, are reasonably likely to do no more than anger or upset an employee cannot properly be viewed as materially affecting the terms, conditions, or privileges of employment and are not actionable, but adverse treatment that is reasonably likely to impair a reasonable employee's job performance or prospects for advancement or promotion” qualifies as adverse employment action for purposes of FEHA.  (Yanowitz, supra, 36 Cal.4th at 1054–1055.) 

            Where the alleged adverse employment action consists of multiple acts, each act need not separately constitute an adverse employment action.  (Id. at 1055-1056.)  The actions must be considered “collectively through consideration of a totality of the circumstances.”  (Id. at 1036.)  “[A]lthough an adverse employment action must materially affect the terms, conditions, or privileges of employment to be actionable, the determination of whether a particular action or course of conduct rises to the level of actionable conduct should take into account the unique circumstances of the affected employee as well as the workplace context of the claim.”  (Id. at 1052 (“Retaliation claims are inherently fact specific, and the impact of an employer’ action in a particular case must be evaluated in context.”))

“In the case of an institutional or corporate employer, the institution or corporation itself must have taken some official action with respect to the employee, such as hiring, firing, failing to promote, adverse job assignment, significant change in compensation or benefits, or official disciplinary action.  An adverse employment action refers not only to ultimate employment actions such as termination or demotion, but also actions that are reasonably likely to adversely and materially affect an employee's job performance or opportunity for advancement.”  (Doe v. Department of Corrections & Rehabilitation (2019) 43 Cal.App.5th 721, 734.) 

            Plaintiff alleges multiple acts by Defendant Martinez, Defendant King Taco’s General Manager, that rose to the level of adverse employment action.  Plaintiff alleges that once she reported Martinez to Gutierrez for creating an unsafe working environment, Defendant Martinez (1) divulged and misrepresented Plaintiffs’ confidential statements made during Defendants’ investigation of Gutierrez for homophobic and discriminatory remarks, with the intention of creating hostility between Gutierrez and Plaintiff and making Plaintiff the target of hostile treatment for associating with and defending her gay co-worker; (2) ignored Plaintiff and gave her the “silent treatment”; (3) spoke to Plaintiff with overt hostility and aggression, making sarcastic and belittling remarks to Plaintiff in front of colleagues and glaring at her in an intimidating manner; (4) revoked Plaintiff’s parking privileges; (5) deliberately changed Plaintiff’s schedule without business justification and with the intent of imposing hardship on Plaintiff by increasing her commute time and travel costs; and (6) caused Plaintiff to be ostracized by her peers due to Martinez’s prominent position and influence at Defendant King Taco.  (SAC, ¶¶32, 36.) 

            Defendants rely on authority involving appeals of motions for summary judgment or jury verdicts.  (Yanowitz, supra, 36 Cal.4th at 1036 (affirming Court of Appeal’s reversal of trial court’s order granting defense summary judgment); Light v. Dept. of Parks & Recreation (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 75, 92 (reversing in part and affirming in part trial court’s order granting defense summary judgment); McRae v. Dept. of Corrections & Rehabilitation (2006) 142 Cal.App.4th 377, 386-388 (reversing jury finding that plaintiff had been subject to an adverse employment action as unsupported by the evidence).) 

Whether a course of conduct amounts to adverse employment action is generally a question of fact best resolved on summary judgment or at trial, where evidence can be presented, not demurrer.  However, courts have resolved the issue on demurrer. 

For example, in Thomas, supra, l77 Cal.App.4th at 512, demurrer was sustained without leave to amend on grounds that plaintiff “failed to allege material changes in the terms of her employment to cause employment injury.”  (Thomas, supra, 77 Cal.App.4th at 512 (affirming trial court order sustained demurrer without leave to amend).)  However, Thomas was decided prior to Yanowitz, which made clear that a totality of the circumstances must be considered in determining whether conduct qualifies as adverse employment action. 

In addition, the conduct alleged in Thomas is factually distinguishable from that alleged here.  In Thomas, the plaintiff relied on acts that “were one time events, such as a delayed check, an early job change and failure to receive one overtime check” and “other allegations, such as the general assertion that Thomas was assigned more duties than other employees in the same unit, are not accompanied by facts which evidence both substantial and detrimental effect on her employment.”  (Thomas, supra, 77 Cal.App.4th at 512.)  Here, Plaintiff alleges more than “one time events.”  Plaintiff alleges a course of conduct that changed the conditions of employment and would likely impair a reasonable employee's job performance. 

            The Court of Appeals in Brown v. Los Angeles Unified School Dist. (2021) 60 Cal.App.5th 1092 sustained demurrer without leave to amend to the plaintiff’s retaliation and discrimination claims for failure to allege any adverse employment action.  The plaintiff’s allegations of adverse employment action were based on defendant’s decision not to reasonably accommodate her complaints.  (Brown, supra, 60 Cal.App.5th at 1105.)  Plaintiff alleged that defendant’s decision not to accommodate was based on the findings of a third-party consultant.  (Id. at 1105.)  The Court of Appeals found such allegations did not plead adverse employment action because those actions were “actions taken in by [defendant] in response to the complaint,” not “to harm her because of her complaint.”  (Id.)  Without more, a defendant’s failure to accommodate and failure to engage meaningfully in the interactive process does not amount to adverse employment action.  (Id. at 1107.) 

            As with Thomas, the alleged adverse employment action in Brown is factually distinguishable from the circumstances alleged here.  The acts alleged against Gutierrez were not legitimate responses to any complaints.  Plaintiff alleges Gutierrez’s conduct was without business justification and undertaken purely to create hostility towards Plaintiff.

            Plaintiff also alleges constructive discharge, which itself would qualify as an adverse employment action.  “Constructive discharge, like actual discharge, is a materially adverse employment action.”  (St. Myers v. Dignity Health (2019) 44 Cal.App.5th 301, 315.)  “Constructive discharge occurs when the employer's conduct effectively forces an employee to resign. Although the employee may say, ‘I quit,’ the employment relationship is actually severed involuntarily by the employer's acts, against the employee's will. As a result, a constructive discharge is legally regarded as a firing rather than a resignation.” (Turner v. Anheuser-Busch, Inc. (1994) 7 Cal.4th 1238, 1244.) 

“Under the cases, an employee cannot simply ‘quit and sue,’ claiming he or she was constructively discharged. The conditions giving rise to the resignation must be sufficiently extraordinary and egregious to overcome the normal motivation of a competent, diligent, and reasonable employee to remain on the job to earn a livelihood and to serve his or her employer. The proper focus is on whether the resignation was coerced, not whether it was simply one rational option for the employee.” (Id. at 1246.) 

“Although situations may exist where the employee's decision to resign is unreasonable as a matter of law, whether conditions were so intolerable as to justify a reasonable employee's decision to resign is normally a question of fact.”  (Vasquez v. Franklin Management Real Estate Fund, Inc. (2013) 222 Cal.App.4th 819, 827.)  For the reasons stated above, the Court cannot find as an issue of law that Plaintiff fails to allege adverse working conditions “so intolerable that any reasonable employee would resign rather than endure such conditions.”  (Turner, supra, 7 Cal.4th at 1247.) 

Defendants cite to Kruitbosch v. Bakersfield Recovery Services, Inc. (2025) 114 Cal.App.5th 200, 225 for the proposition that a constructive discharge claim is properly subject to demurrer.  However, Kruitbosch was only certified for partial publication.  Defendants cite to the unpublished portion of the Kruitbosch opinion discussing the constructive discharge claim.  Pursuant to California Rules of Court Rule 8.1115 subdivision (a), an opinion of the California Court of Appeal that is not certified for publication or ordered published must not be cited or relied on by a court or a party in any other action.  (Ca. Rules of Ct. Rule 8.115, subd. (a).)

            When viewed collectively and against the totality of the circumstances, Plaintiff sufficiently alleges Defendants’ adverse employment action against her.  In combination, a reasonable juror could conclude that these acts materially changed the terms of her employment  and were likely to impair a reasonable employee's job performance or prospects for advancement or promotion.  Given the standard applied on demurrer and the fact-specific inquiry required to determine whether a course of conduct qualifies as an adverse employment action, the Court cannot conclude that the alleged conduct was trivial or minor as a matter of law and did not materially change the terms of Plaintiff’s employment. 

            Plaintiff alleges a causal link for retaliation

            To establish a prima facie case of retaliation under the FEHA, “a plaintiff must show (1) he or she engaged in a ‘protected activity,’ (2) the employer subjected the employee to an adverse employment action, and (3) a causal link existed between the protected activity and the employer's action. Once an employee establishes a prima facie case, the employer is required to offer a legitimate, nonretaliatory reason for the adverse employment action.” (Yanowitz v. L’Oreal USA, Inc. (2005) 36 Cal.4th 1028, 1042.)

            A plaintiff “must produce evidence sufficient to show that an illegitimate criterion was a substantial factor in the particular employment decision.”  (Harris v. City of Santa Monica (2013) 56 Cal.4th 203, 232 (quoting Price Waterhouse v. Hopkins (1989) 490 U.S. 228, 278).  “The retaliatory motive is proved by showing that plaintiff engaged in protected activities, that his employer was aware of the protected activities, and that the adverse action followed within a relatively short time thereafter.   The causal link may be established by an inference derived from circumstantial evidence, such as the employer's knowledge that the employee engaged in protected activities and the proximity in time between the protected action and allegedly retaliatory employment decision.  Essential to a causal link is evidence that the employer was aware that the plaintiff had engaged in the protected activity.”  (Morgan v. Regents of University of Cal. (2000) 88 Cal.App.4th 52, 69–70.) 

Defendants argue there is no causal link between the alleged protected activity— participation in Defendants’ investigation of Gutierrez’s use of homophobic slurs in August 2023—and the alleged adverse employment actions.  Defendants argue there is no causal link alleged, because the adverse employment actions only began immediately after Plaintiff reported Martinez’s altercation with a vendor to Gutierrez in September 2023.  (SAC, ¶¶25, 29, 31, 32.)  Defendants rely in part on the Court’s order sustaining demurrer to the FAC, which stated “the FAC fails to allege a causal link between the reporting of the homophobic slur and the alleged adverse employment action because it alleges mistreatment only after September of 2023, when Plaintiff reported Martinez’s altercation to Gutierrez.”  (3/18/26 Minute Order.) 

Upon reexamination of the allegations and the applicable law, the Court finds Plaintiff sufficiently alleges a causal link between her participation in the investigation of Gutierrez and the adverse employments actions for purposes of demurrer.  The Court is not bound by its earlier ruling and Defendants’ decision to demur reopens entirely the question of whether a cause of action is stated.  (Carlton v. Dr. Pepper Snapple Group, Inc. (2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 1200, 1211.)

The timing of the adverse employment action and the protected activity does not establish as an issue of law that the protected activity could not have been a substantial factor in Defendant Martinez’s adverse employment actions.  The protected conduct took place in August 2023.  The adverse employment action occurred in September 2023, only a month later.  The timing of the events does not make it impossible or legally untenable that Plaintiff’s participation in the investigation of Gutierrez was a substantial factor in Defendant Martinez’s adverse employment actions against Plaintiff.  Plaintiff’s participation in the investigation of Gutierrez,  Plaintiff’s complaint to Gutierrez about Defendant Martinez and the onset of the adverse employment actions all occurred within a month or two of one another.  (SAC, ¶31.)  The Court cannot determine based on timing alone that Plaintiff’s participation in the investigation of Gutierrez was not a substantial factor in Defendants’ adverse employment actions against her as an issue of law. 

In fact, if anything the close temporal proximity of the alleged protected conduct and the onset of the adverse employment actions support an inference of causation.  “[C]lose temporal proximity between a plaintiff’s protected activity and the alleged retaliatory conduct against the plaintiff has been found to support a prima facie case of causation.”  (LeMere v. Los Angeles Unified School Dist. (2019) 35 Cal.App.5th 237, 243).  In LeMere, the court acknowledged that federal courts have held that “intervals of more than a few months were too long to support causation.”  (Id.)  The court found a “gap of two years is not sufficient as a matter of law to support an inference of causation” and sustained the demurrer to plaintiff’s retaliation for failure to plead a causal link between the protected conduct and the adverse employment action.  (Id.) 

Defendants also rely on Plaintiff’s allegation that the adverse employment action began “immediately after Plaintiff’s report in September 2023.”  (SAC, ¶31.)  The allegation is not an admission that the adverse employment action was triggered solely by Plaintiff’s report to Gutierrez about Defendant Martinez’s altercation with the vendor. 

            In addition, Plaintiff alleges the protected conduct was “participating as a witness in an internal investigation” and “reporting inappropriate behavior in the workplace that made Plaintiff feel uncomfortable.”  (SAC, ¶76.)  Plaintiff alleges the adverse employment actions were motivated by her “participation as a witness in an internal investigation, reporting inappropriate behavior in the workplace and/or some combination of these factors.”  (SAC, ¶78.)  Plaintiff alleges facts from supporting the allegation that Defendants were retaliating against her for participating in the investigation, including Defendant Martinez’s knowledge of the protected activity, her punitive disclosure of Plaintiff’s confidential participation in the investigation to create hostility in the workplace against her and her anger towards Plaintiff for reporting her altercation with the vendor and closely associating with and defending a gay coworker.  (SAC, ¶¶30 and 31.)  While the adverse employment action could well have been motivated solely by Martinez’s anger at being reported, Plaintiff plausibly alleges circumstantial facts from which a jury could reasonably conclude that her participation in the investigation and association with and defense of a gay coworker were also motivating factors.

            Plaintiff alleges a protected characteristic for discrimination and harassment claims

            Defendants argue Plaintiff fails to allege any facts to support her claim that she was discriminated against and harassed on the basis of her sex or gender.  Defendants argue Plaintiff also fails to allege facts to support a protected characteristic based on associational discrimination and harassment.

            The SAC is devoid of any allegations that Plaintiff suffered any discrimination or harassment due to her sex or gender.  The SAC alleges her sex/gender as the basis for discrimination and harassment but there are no specific allegations of any conduct that would qualify as discrimination or harassment based on her sex/gender.

            The SAC alleges Plaintiff was discriminated against and harassed because she associated with her gay male coworker and regularly associated with, supported and defender her gay coworker.  (SAC, ¶27.)  “Race, religious creed, color, national origin, ancestry, physical disability, mental disability, medical condition, genetic information, marital status, sex, age, sexual orientation, reproductive health decisionmaking, or veteran or military status” includes any of the following:…(3) A perception that the person is associated with a person who has, or is perceived to have, any of those characteristics or any combination of those characteristics.”  (Gov. Code, §12926, subd. (o)(3).)  Contrary to Defendants’ characterization of Plaintiff’s allegations, Plaintiff does not allege that she “merely observed” the purported workplace hostility against a homosexual male colleague.  Plaintiff served as a witness in an investigation of Gutierrez for homophobic remarks and reported Gutierrez’s use of homophobic slurs in the workplace to refer to and harass male coworkers.  (SAC, ¶26.)  Plaintiff actively participated in an investigation into Gutierrez’s use of homophobic slurs and reported her for using them.  Plaintiff “regularly associated with, supported, and defender her gay coworker, who was the target of Gutierrez’s homophobic slurs.  (SAC, ¶27.)  Plaintiff alleges sufficient facts to establish a protected characteristic based on associational standing under Government Code section 12926 subdivision (o)(3).

            Plaintiff’s failure to allege any facts to support her sex or gender discrimination claims within the first and second causes of action for discrimination and harassment are insufficient grounds to sustain demurrer to those causes of action. Plaintiff alleges discrimination and harassment based on association with a person with a protected characteristic based on sexual orientation.  “A demurrer will not lie to a part of a cause of action or count.”  (5 Witkin, Cal. Proc. (6th ed. 2021) Pleading, § 956, p. 355.)

            Plaintiff sufficiently alleges severe and pervasive harassment

            Pursuant to Government Code §12940(j)(1), “It is an unlawful employment practice…For an employer…or any other person, because of…sexual orientation….”  Pursuant to Government Code §12940(j)(3), “[a]n employee of an entity subject to this subdivision is personally liable for any harassment prohibited by this section that is perpetrated by the employee, regardless of whether the employer or covered entity knows or should have known of the conduct and fails to take immediate and appropriate corrective action.” 

“[D]iscrimination refers to bias in the exercise of official actions on behalf of the employer, and harassment refers to bias that is expressed or communicated through interpersonal relations in the workplace.”  (Roby v. McKesson Corp. (2009) 47 Cal.4th 686, 707.)  “[H]arassment consists of a type of conduct not necessary for performance of a supervisory job. Instead, harassment consists of conduct outside the scope of necessary job performance, conduct presumably engaged in for personal gratification, because of meanness or bigotry, or for other personal motives. Harassment is not conduct of a type necessary for management of the employer's business or performance of the supervisory employee's job.”  (Reno v. Baird (1998) 18 Cal.4th 640, 645–646.)

            “The law prohibiting harassment is violated when the workplace is permeated with discriminatory intimidation, ridicule and insult that is sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of the victim's employment and create an abusive working environment…And the issue of whether an employee was subjected to a hostile environment is ordinarily one of fact.”  (Nazir v. United Airlines, Inc. (2009) 178 Cal.App.4th 243, 263–64; see also Galvin v. Dameron Hospital Assn. (2019) 37 Cal.App.5th 549, 564 (citing Miller v. Dept. of Corrections (2005) 36 Cal.4th 446, 462).) Whether harassment is sufficiently severe or pervasive is ordinarily one of fact for the trier of fact and a totality of the circumstances must be considered.  (Caldera v. Dept. of Corrections and Rehabilitation (2018) 25 Cal.App.5th 31, 38-39.) 

            Defendants argue a single homophobic slur made in Plaintiff’s presence is insufficiently severe and pervasive as a matter of law.  However, Plaintiff’s harassment claim is not limited to being present when a single homophobic slur was made.  Plaintiff alleges harassment based on the use of “homophobic slurs, ostracism, hostile communications, removal of parking privileges and punitive schedule change.”  (SAC, ¶66.)  Plaintiff alleges harassment based on witnessing the sexual harassment of her gay coworker, Defendants’ disclosure of her participation in the investigation of Gutierrez to Gutierrez, her immediate supervisor, divulging and misrepresenting to Gutierrez what Plaintiff said during the investigation, Defendant Martinez’s refusal to greet or interact with Plaintiff, Defendant Martinez’s overt hostility and aggression towards Plaintiff whenever interaction could not be avoided, glaring at Plaintiff in an intimidating manner and making sarcastic and belittling remarks about Plaintiff’s work in front of colleagues whenever interaction could not be avoided.  (SAC, ¶¶36, 38.)  Plaintiff alleges Defendant Martinez’s conduct, including her disclosure of Plaintiff’s participation in the investigation to Gutierrez, her shunning of Plaintiff and her public insults of Plaintiff, was intended to create hostility between Plaintiff and her immediate supervisor, Gutierrez, and other employees.  Plaintiff alleges these acts were “frequent,” “severe and pervasive,” and altered the conditions of Plaintiff’s employment and created an abusive and hostile working environment.  (SAC, ¶41.) 

Plaintiff also alleges harassment based on the removal of parking privileges and punitive schedule changes.  However, such conduct is likely of the type necessary for management of the employer's business or performance of the supervisory employee's job.  Defendants do not address this issue, however, because they argue the harassing conduct is limited to a single homophobic slur. 

Accepting these allegations as true, reasonable minds could conclude that the harassment was severe and pervasive based on the totality of the circumstances.  Plaintiff therefore sufficiently alleges severe and pervasive harassing conduct to state a claim for harassment.

Plaintiff’s derivative claims do not fail because they underlying FEHA discrimination, harassment and retaliation claims are sufficiently pled

            Defendants argue the derivative claims for failure to prevent discrimination, harassment or retaliation and for a FEHA-based declaratory judgment fail because the underlying causes of action for FEHA discrimination, harassment and retaliation fail.  However, as stated above, Plaintiff sufficiently states causes of action for FEHA discrimination, harassment and retaliation are sufficiently stated.

CONCLUSION

Defendants’ Demurrer is overruled.  Defendants are ordered to answer in 20 days.
