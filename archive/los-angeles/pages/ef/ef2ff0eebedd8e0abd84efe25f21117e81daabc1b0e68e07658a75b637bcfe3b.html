Superior Court of California

DEPARTMENT 309

 

TENTATIVE RULING

 

EVERWIN INTERNATIONAL, LLC

 

         vs.

 

CASSELL’S HAMBURGERS DTLA,

LLC, et al.

 

 Case No.: 24STCV07475  

 

 

 

 Hearing Date: September 15, 2026

 

 

Plaintiff’s motion for summary judgment is GRANTED

 

On 3/25/2025, Plaintiff Everwin International, LLC (Plaintiff) filed suit against Cassell’s Hamburgers DTLA, LLC and Cassell’s Hamburgers, LLC (collectively, Defendants), alleging: (1) breach of contract (lease); and (2) breach of contract (guaranty).

 

            On 6/15/2026, Plaintiff moved for summary judgment or, in the alternative, summary adjudication of its claims.

 

            The motion is unopposed.

 

Discussion

 

            Plaintiff argues that summary judgment is appropriate here because no triable dispute exists as to any of its claims.

 

            In support, Plaintiff notes that Defendant failed to respond to any of the requests for admission (or to any other discovery) propounded on them, and its requests for admission were deemed admitted. Accordingly, Defendant has admitted that: (1) Everwin and Cassell’s DTLA entered into a lease; (2) Everwin fully performed all of its obligations under the lease; (3) Cassell’s DTLA breached the lease; and (4) As a result of Cassell’s DTLA’s breaches of the lease, Everwin has suffered damages. Moreover, Defendant has admitted that (1) Cassell’s executed the guaranty; (2) Everwin fully performed all its obligations under the guaranty; (3) Cassell’s breached the guaranty; and (4) As a result of Cassell’s breaches of the guaranty, Everwin has suffered damages.

 

            Accordingly, no triable issue exists as to the material facts. The Court takes Defendant’s non-opposition to be a concession to the motion on the merits.

 

            Based on the foregoing, Plaintiff’s motion for summary judgment is granted.

 

It is so ordered.

Case Number: 24STCV20900    Hearing Date: September 15, 2026    Dept: 309

Superior Court of California

County of Los Angeles

 

DEPARTMENT 309

 

TENTATIVE RULING

 

HRATCH MINASSIAN

                          

         vs.

 

AMERICAN HONDA MOTOR CO.

INC.

 

 Case No.: 24STCV20900

 

 

 

 Hearing Date: September 22, 2026

 

Plaintiff is awarded $22,000 in attorney fees and $805.05 in costs.

 

On 8/19/2024, Plaintiff Hratch Minassian (Plaintiff) initiated this action against Defendant American Honda Motor Co. Inc. (Defendant) alleging violations of the Song Beverly Consumer Warranty Act.

 

On 6/4/2026, Plaintiff moved for attorney fees and costs totaling $40,622.79.

 

Legal Standard

 

The party claiming attorneys’ fees must establish entitlement to such fees and the reasonableness of the fees claimed.  (Civic Western Corporation v. Zila Industries, Inc. (1977) 66 Cal.App.3d 1, 16.) “Except as attorney’s fees are specifically provided for by statute, the measure and mode of compensation of attorneys and counselors at law is left to the agreement, express or implied, of the parties[.]” (CCP § 1021.)

 

“It is well established that the determination of what constitutes reasonable attorney fees is committed to the discretion of the trial court, whose decision cannot be reversed in the absence of an abuse of discretion.”  (Melnyk v. Robledo (1976) 64 Cal.App.3d 618, 623.)  In exercising its discretion, the court should consider a number of factors, including the nature of the litigation, its difficulty, the amount involved, the skill required in handling the matter, the attention given, the success or failure, and the resulting judgment.  (Id.)

 

In determining what constitutes a reasonable compensation for an attorney who has rendered services in connection with a legal proceeding, the court may and should consider the nature of the litigation, its difficulty, the amount involved, the skill required and the skill employed in handling the litigation, the attention given, the success of the attorneys’ efforts, their learning, their age, and their experience in the particular type of work demanded the intricacies and importance of the litigation, the labor and necessity for skilled legal training and ability in trying the cause, and the time consumed. (Stokus v. Marsh (1990) 217 Cal.App.3d 647, 657.)

 

In determining the proper amount of fees to award, courts use the lodestar method.  The lodestar figure is calculated by multiplying the total number of reasonable hours expended by the reasonable hourly rate.  “Fundamental to its determination … [is] a careful compilation of the time spent and reasonable hourly compensation of each attorney … in the presentation of the case.”  (Serrano v. Priest (1977) 20 Cal.3d 25, 48 (Serrano III).)  A reasonable hourly rate must reflect the skill and experience of the attorney.  (Id. at 49.)  “Prevailing parties are compensated for hours reasonably spent on fee-related issues.  A fee request that appears unreasonably inflated is a special circumstance permitting the trial court to reduce the award or deny one altogether.”  (Serrano v. Unruh (1982) 32 Cal.3d 621, 635 (Serrano IV); see also Weber v. Langholz (1995) 39 Cal.App.4th 1578, 1587 (“The trial court could make its own evaluation of the reasonable worth of the work done in light of the nature of the case, and of the credibility of counsel’s declaration unsubstantiated by time records and billing statements.”)

 

Reasonable attorney fees should be based on an objective standard of reasonableness, i.e., the market value of services rendered, not on some notion of cost incurred. (PLCM Group, Inc. v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1090.)  The value of legal services performed in a case is a matter in which the trial court has its own expertise.  (Id. at 1096.)  The trial court may make its own determination of the value of the services contrary to, or without the necessity for, expert testimony.  (Id.)  The trial court makes its determination after consideration of a number of factors, including the nature of the litigation, its difficulty, the amount involved, the skill required in its handling, the skill employed, the attention given, the success or failure, and other circumstances in the case.  (Id.)

 

Discussion

 

            Plaintiff seeks $40,622.79 in attorney fees and costs consisting of attorney fees of $39,817.74 and $805.05 in costs.

 

In Morris v. Hyundai Motor Am. (2019) 41 Cal. App. 5th 24, 41, the trial court reduced the rates of Bryan Altman of the Altman Law Group, who has 30-plus years of litigation experience with over 100 jury trials, of which 20-plus were as lead trial counsel in lemon law cases, from $650 to $500 per hour, reduced Steve Mikhov of the Knight Law Group from $500 to $400, and reduced all the associates’ rates that ranged from $450 to $350 to $300 per hour. (Id. at 8-9.) In support of the lower court decision, the Court of Appeal held that:

 

[E]ven if Morris established that her attorneys’ rates were generally commensurate with other consumer law attorneys with the same level of experience and skill, Morris ignores that there are a number of factors that the trial court may have taken into consideration in determining that reductions in the attorneys’ hourly rates were warranted. The court reasonably could have reduced the rates based on its finding that the matter was not complex; that it did not go to trial; that the name partners were doing work that could have been done by lower-billing attorneys; and that all the attorneys were doing work that could have been done by paralegals.

 

            (Id. at pp. 23-24.)

 

            Similarly, Judge Randolph Hammock of the Los Angeles County Superior Court recently found rates of $350 per hour appropriate for an “experienced” lawyer to take a lemon law case all the way through trial. (See Mikhaeilpoor v. BMW of N. Am., LLC (2020) 48 Cal.App.5th 240, 245-46 [“The court found the requested fee amount “was just not reasonable.”… The court decided that $350 “is a reasonable hourly rate for the services that were done.”].)

 

Here, the Court reduces counsels’ hourly rates to a blended rate of $550, consistent with Mikhaeilpoor. In support of this reduction, the Court notes that the matter was not complex, the case did not go to trial, and discovery was rote.

 

Hours

 

A review of the billings records suggest excessive billing and padding. For example, there are considerable billings for review and correspondence.   

 

Given the Court’s power to make “across-the-board percentage cuts either in the numbers of hours claimed or in the final lodestar figure,” the Court finds only 40 hours reasonably spent. (Gonzalez v. City of Maywood (9th Cir. 2013) 729 F.3d 1196, 1203 (Gonzalez). ($550 x 40 hrs = $22,000.)

 

Based on the foregoing, Plaintiff is awarded $22,000 in attorney fees, and $805.05 in costs.

 

 

It is so ordered.

Case Number: 25STCP04987    Hearing Date: September 15, 2026    Dept: 309

Superior Court of California

County of Los Angeles

 

DEPARTMENT 17

 

TENTATIVE RULING

 

R. WESTERN MONROE

 

         vs.

 

CITY OF SANTA CLARITA

 

 Case No.: 25STCP04987

 

 

 

 Hearing Date: September 15, 2026

 

 

 

Petitioner’s motion to vacate judgment and enter a different judgment is DENIED.

 

            On 12/24/2025, pro per Plaintiff R. Weston Monroe (Plaintiff) filed a petition for writ of mandate against City of Santa Clarita (the City).

 

            On 8/11/226, Petitioner moved to vacate judgment and enter a different judgment.

 

Discussion

 

            Petitioner argues that an alternative judgment is necessary “to correct a single, purely legal defect in the August 4, 2026 signed order of dismissal, on documentary facts no party disputes.” (Motion, 1: 20-21.) More specifically, Petitioner argues:

 

The order’s operative sentence rests on Petitioner’s asserted failure to name the project applicant within “the 30-day window following the NOD.” No provision of CEQA contains that deadline. Public Resources Code section 21167 allows 30 days to file the petition; Petitioner filed within that period. Public Resources Code section 21167.6.5 governs the naming and service of the real party, and it states no 30-day naming window measured from the notice of determination. And under Citizens for a Better Eureka v. City of Eureka (2025) 111 Cal.App.5th 1114, 1130–1131 — authority the order itself cites — whether the absent person is indispensable “remains a case-by-case discretionary determination.” The judgment therefore rests on a rule of law that does not exist, applied in place of a discretionary determination that has not yet been made.

 

            (Motion, 1:22-2: 3.)

 

            However, the Court’s 8/4/2026 ruling granting the motion to dismiss was based on a determination that the CEQA required Petitioner to name as a real party in interest the applicant identified in the City’s Notice of Determination. Petitioner named a completely unrelated third party LLC (Royal Clark Development Company LLC) in the caption and failed to name any real party in the body of his original Petition. Petitioner’s ex parte “Incorrect Name” Amendment was ineffectual and could not cure the omission once the limitations period had run, and Wiley was a necessary and indispensable real party in interest whose absence compelled dismissal. (8/4/2026 Ruling, pp. 2-3.)

 

            Accordingly, the issue identified by Petitioner is not dispositive.

 

            Moreover, as noted by Defendants in opposition, the 8/4/2026 Ruling is not the first time this Court resolved the dispositive questions. In its 4/16/2026 Minute Order ruling on the City’s demurrer, the Court held: that Public Resources Code section 21167.6.5 requires a CEQA petitioner to name and serve the person identified in the Notice of Determination; that persons so identified “are necessary parties for purposes of Code of Civil Procedure section 389”; and that in a CEQA challenge the project applicant “is an indispensable party whose absence requires dismissal” (Citizens for a Better Eureka v. City of Eureka (2025) 111 Cal.App.5th 1114, 1126-1127, 1129-1132; Beresford Neighborhood Assn. v. City of San Mateo (1989) 207 Cal.App.3d 1180, 1187-1190).

 

            Finally, because Plaintiff argues that the Court should have reached an alternative ruling, this motion is one of reconsideration regardless of its caption. The name of a motion is not controlling, and, regardless of the name, a motion asking the trial court to decide the same matter previously ruled on is a motion for reconsideration under Code of Civil Procedure section 1008.” (Powell v. County of Orange (2011) 197 Cal.App.4th 1573, 1577.) Plaintiff does not identify any new facts or law to justify a different outcome.

 

            Based on the foregoing, Petitioner’s motion to vacate judgment and enter a different judgment is denied.

 

 

It is so ordered.

Case Number: 25STCV02535    Hearing Date: September 15, 2026    Dept: 309

Superior Court of California

County of Los Angeles

 

DEPARTMENT 17

 

TENTATIVE RULING

 

TOMAS MONTES DE OCA

 

         vs.

 

CARINA HERNANDEZ, et al.

 

 Case No.: 25STCV02535  

 

 

 

 Hearing Date: September 15, 2026

 

Defendant Line Crew Investments, LLC’s motion to dismiss is DENIED, WITHOUT PREJUDICE.

 

On 1/29/2025, Plaintiff Tomas Montes De Oca (Plaintiff) filed suit against Carina Hernandez, Synergia Investments, LLC, Line Crew Investments, LLC, Jesus Montes Martinez, and Century 21 Synergia Realty, alleging: (1) cancellation of instrument; (2) cancellation of instrument; (3) cancellation of instrument; (4) breach of fiduciary duty; (5) negligence; (6) fraud and deceit; and (7) accounting.

 

            On 6/12/2026, Defendant Line Crew Investments, LLC (Defendant) moved to dismiss.

 

Discussion

 

            Defendant moves to dismiss this action on the grounds that Plaintiff failed to file a second amended complaint (SAC), following the Court’s 4/15/2026 ruling sustaining Defendant Jesus Montes Martinez’s demurrer, with 20 days leave to amend.

 

            However, by its own admission, Defendant filed an answer to Plaintiff’s FAC. The 4/15/2026 demurrer was based on a determination that the claims against Defendant Martinez were insufficiently pled. Defendant advances no such argument that the claims against it were insufficiently pled, or that it joined in the Martinez demurrer.

 

            The appropriate action for Defendant is to move for terminating sanctions based on failure to prosecute.

 

            Based on the foregoing, Defendant Line Crew Investments, LLC’s motion to dismiss is denied, without prejudice.

 

It is so ordered.
