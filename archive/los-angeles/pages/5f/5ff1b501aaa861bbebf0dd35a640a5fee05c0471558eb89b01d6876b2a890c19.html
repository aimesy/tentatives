MOTION IN LIMINE

 

MOVING PARTY: Flyer Next, LLC, Oded Nechushtan, and Amit Nechushtan

RESPONDING PARTY: Marvin Engineering Co., Inc.

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Motion in Limine No. 1

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

Oded Nechushtan and Amit Nechushtan are the founders of Flyer Technologies, LLC. In 2000, Flyer Technologies entered into a joint venture agreement with Marvin Engineering Co., Inc. for a project involving the development and sale of lightweight tactical all-terrain vehicles to the United States Marine Corps. As part of the joint venture, Flyer Technologies licensed its intellectual property for the manufacture of Flyer vehicles to Marvin and transferred its subsidiary to become a wholly owned Marvin subsidiary named Flyer Defense. Marvin and Flyer Technologies had equal voting power and an equal 50-50 split of royalties from the operation of Flyer Defense.

 

After the head of Marvin became less involved due to health issues, the parties entered into a new agreement in 2018 to restructure the Flyer business. Flyer Defense was transferred from Marvin to a new limited liability company, Flyer Next, LLC, which was jointly and equally owned by Marvin and Flyer Technologies. Marvin was to contribute, assign, and transfer to Flyer Next all the intellectual property and other property rights that existed at the time with respect to the Flyer business. While the parties retained the 50-50 economic structure between Marvin and Flyer Technologies, the Limited Liability Company Agreement for Flyer Next designated Oded as Manager and gave him “the sole power to manage the business, property, and affairs of Flyer Next and its wholly owned subsidiary, Flyer Defense.” Flyer Technologies also agreed to give Marvin an $18 million “priority return” over Flyer Technologies upon the occurrence of “specified liquidity events.”

 

On May 17, 2023, Plaintiffs Flyer Defense, Flyer Next, and Flyer Technologies filed a Second Amended Complaint against Defendants Marvin and its President and Chief Operating Officer, David Gussman, as well as its Chief Executive Officer, Howard Gussman. Following voluntary dismissal of certain claims, the remaining causes of action are for declaratory relief, breach of the Contribution and Assignment Agreement, breach of the Services Agreement, breach of the Line of Credit Agreement, fraudulent misrepresentation, trespass to chattels, conversion, and unauthorized access to computers. Plaintiffs allege that while negotiating the 2018 restructuring, Flyer Technologies relied on Marvin’s misrepresentations of material facts in providing it 50% ownership of Flyer Next and a priority return of $18 million if Flyer Next is ever sold for $100 million or more. Plaintiffs further allege breaches of different agreements and a controversy as to distributions.

On May 24, 2023, the Court consolidated the case with related Case No. 22STCV30079, with the instant case as the lead case.

 

RULING: Granted.

Flyer Next, LLC, Oded Nechushtan, and Amit Nechushtan (Flyer Parties) move for an order excluding any argument regarding, reference to, or evidence concerning the bonus allegedly paid to Oded and Amit in 2023. They argue that such evidence is not relevant because Marvin’s complaint (filed in the related case) does not contain any allegations related to the 2023 bonus. They contend that introduction of the bonus would prejudicially expand the scope of the case and lead to substantial danger of conflating issues related to the 2021 and 2023 bonuses. The Flyer Parties also contend that any dispute relating to the 2023 bonus is subject to arbitration. Marvin opposes the motion, arguing that the 2023 bonus is relevant because its breach of fiduciary duty claim encompasses the bonus even though it occurred after the complaint was filed. Marvin additionally contests the applicability of the arbitration agreement and the propriety of the Flyer Parties’ attempt to use it to exclude evidence. The Flyer Parties’ motion is granted.

 

Marvin is precluded from introducing evidence or argument or referencing the 2023 bonus. The bonus was not put in issue in its complaint. “One of the functions of pleadings is to limit the issues and narrow the proofs.” (Fuentes v. Tucker (1947) 31 Cal.2d 1, 4.) “Evidence which is not pertinent to the issues raised by the pleadings is immaterial, and it is error to allow the introduction of such evidence.” (Id. at p. 4.) Even in equity, post-filing facts are permitted if they “bear as evidence upon the facts in issue in the original cause.” (Collins v. Sargent (1928) 89 Cal.App. 107, 112-13.) The 2023 bonus involves a separate bonus approved by a board, of which Flyer Parties represent Amit was no longer a member of, consisting of at least 3 new board members. [Ashley Suppl. Reply Decl. ¶ 14.] These differences indicate that the 2023 bonus does not bear upon the complaint’s allegations of prior use of power over Flyer Next to give excessive bonuses. The Flyer Parties’ motion is thus granted.

 

The Court declines to use the instant motion as a vehicle to decide the merits of the pending motion to compel arbitration.

 

Moving parties to give notice.

MOTION IN LIMINE

 

MOVING PARTY: Flyer Next, LLC, Oded Nechushtan, and Amit Nechushtan

RELIEF REQUESTED

Motion in Limine No. 2

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

Oded Nechushtan and Amit Nechushtan are the founders of Flyer Technologies, LLC. In 2000, Flyer Technologies entered into a joint venture agreement with Marvin Engineering Co., Inc. for a project involving the development and sale of lightweight tactical all-terrain vehicles to the United States Marine Corps. As part of the joint venture, Flyer Technologies licensed its intellectual property for the manufacture of Flyer vehicles to Marvin and transferred its subsidiary to become a wholly owned Marvin subsidiary named Flyer Defense. Marvin and Flyer Technologies had equal voting power and an equal 50-50 split of royalties from the operation of Flyer Defense.

 

After the head of Marvin became less involved due to health issues, the parties entered into a new agreement in 2018 to restructure the Flyer business. Flyer Defense was transferred from Marvin to a new limited liability company, Flyer Next, LLC, which was jointly and equally owned by Marvin and Flyer Technologies. Marvin was to contribute, assign, and transfer to Flyer Next all the intellectual property and other property rights that existed at the time with respect to the Flyer business. While the parties retained the 50-50 economic structure between Marvin and Flyer Technologies, the Limited Liability Company Agreement for Flyer Next designated Oded as Manager and gave him “the sole power to manage the business, property, and affairs of Flyer Next and its wholly owned subsidiary, Flyer Defense.” Flyer Technologies also agreed to give Marvin an $18 million “priority return” over Flyer Technologies upon the occurrence of “specified liquidity events.”

 

On May 17, 2023, Plaintiffs Flyer Defense, Flyer Next, and Flyer Technologies filed a Second Amended Complaint against Defendants Marvin and its President and Chief Operating Officer, David Gussman, as well as its Chief Executive Officer, Howard Gussman. Following voluntary dismissal of certain claims, the remaining causes of action are for declaratory relief, breach of the Contribution and Assignment Agreement, breach of the Services Agreement, breach of the Line of Credit Agreement, fraudulent misrepresentation, trespass to chattels, conversion, and unauthorized access to computers. Plaintiffs allege that while negotiating the 2018 restructuring, Flyer Technologies relied on Marvin’s misrepresentations of material facts in providing it 50% ownership of Flyer Next and a priority return of $18 million if Flyer Next is ever sold for $100 million or more. Plaintiffs further allege breaches of different agreements and a controversy as to distributions.

On May 24, 2023, the Court consolidated the case with related Case No. 22STCV30079, with the instant case as the lead case.

 

RULING: Granted.

Flyer Next, Oded Nechushtan, and Amit Nechushtan (Flyer Parties) move for an order excluding any evidence or argument that Flyer Next breached the LLC Agreement by not providing access to books and records and requesting injunctive relief. The Flyer Parties argue that Marvin’s complaint (filed in the related case) does not include any claim based on this theory nor a request for injunctive relief. They also argue that the books and records claim is subject to arbitration. Marvin opposes the motion, contending that the evidence is relevant to its breach of fiduciary duty claim and its request for punitive damages. The Flyer Parties’ motion is granted.

 

Marvin is precluded from introducing evidence or argument regarding Flyer Next’s alleged failure to provide it access to books and records for the purposes of showing a breach of the LLC Agreement and entitlement to injunctive relief on this basis. Neither of these issues are raised in Marvin’s complaint. “One of the functions of pleadings is to limit the issues and narrow the proofs.” (Fuentes v. Tucker (1947) 31 Cal.2d 1, 4.) “Evidence which is not pertinent to the issues raised by the pleadings is immaterial, and it is error to allow the introduction of such evidence.” (Id. at p. 4.) Marvin’s complaint bases its claim for breach of the LLC Agreement on Flyer Next and Oded’s failure to pay royalty proceeds, not on a lack of access to books and records. [Compl. ¶¶ 57-62.] The complaint also does not request injunctive relief to require Flyer Next to provide access to books and records nor is there any indication that the Flyer Parties were put on notice of Marvin’s intention to seek such a remedy until their meet and confer efforts. (North Coast Village Condominium Assn. v. Phillips (2023) 94 Cal.App.5th 866, 881.) The Flyer Parties’ motion is therefore granted.

 

The Court declines to use the instant motion as a vehicle to decide the merits of the pending motion to compel arbitration.

 

Moving parties to give notice.

SUMMARY JUDGMENT

 

MOVING PARTY: Defendant, Vivian Yamaguchi

RESPONDING PARTY: Unopposed/Defendant, Patricia Munoz

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Motion for Summary Judgment

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

On July 26, 2023, Plaintiff Integrated Lender Services filed a complaint in interpleader against Defendants Sharon Munoz, Patricia Munoz, and Vivian Yamaguchi. Plaintiff alleged the complaint arises from a surplus balance of $344,954.56 following the August 3, 2022, non-judicial foreclosure sale of 732-734 Leonard Ave., Los Angeles, CA 90022. Plaintiff alleged the complaint in interpleader was necessary due to competing claims for the surplus funds amongst Defendants.

 

Yamaguchi answered the complaint on September 26, 2023. Particia Munoz filed an answer and amended answer on October 23, 2023, and December 14, 2023. Default was entered against Sharon Munoz on October 10, 2023. Plaintiff was dismissed from the action on November 6, 2024.

 

RULING: Granted.

Yamaguchi moves for summary judgment, arguing that no triable issue of material fact exists that she is the rightful owner of the interpleaded funds. Patricia Munoz does not oppose the motion. Yamaguchi’s motion is granted.

 

The pleadings frame the issues for motions, “since it is those allegations to which the motion must respond. (Citation.)” (Scolinos v. Kolts (1995) 37 Cal. App. 4th 635, 640-641; FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 367, 382-83; Jordan-Lyon Prods., LTD. v. Cineplex Odeon Corp. (1994) 29 Cal.App.4th 1459, 1472.) The purpose of a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication “is to provide courts with a mechanism to cut through the parties’ pleadings in order to determine whether, despite their allegations, trial is in fact necessary to resolve their dispute.” (Aguilar v. Atl. Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) “Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (c), requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.”  (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.) 

 

“On a motion for summary judgment, the initial burden is always on the moving party to make a prima facie showing that there are no triable issues of material fact.” (Scalf v. D.B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1519.) A defendant moving for summary judgment “has met his or her burden of showing that a cause of action has no merit if the party has shown that one or more elements of the cause of action . . . cannot be established.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) “Once the defendant . . . has met that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff . . . to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.” (Ibid.)

 

“When deciding whether to grant summary judgment, the court must consider all of the evidence set forth in the papers (except evidence to which the court has sustained an objection), as well as all reasonable inferences that may be drawn from that evidence, in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment.” (Avivi v. Centro Medico Urgente Medical Center (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 463, 467; see also Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c).) “An issue of fact can only be created by a conflict in the evidence. It is not created by speculation, conjecture, imagination or guesswork.”  (Lyons v. Security Pacific National Bank (1995) 40 Cal.App.4th 1001, 1041 (citation omitted)

 

Yamaguchi contends that no triable issue of material fact exists as to her ownership of the funds because Edward and Patricia Munoz transferred the property to her via grant deed.

 

“A deed does not transfer title to the grantee until it has been legally delivered.” (Luna v. Brownell (2010) 185 Cal.App.4th 668, 673; Code Civ. Proc., § 1054.) “Delivery is a question of intent.” (Luna, supra, 185 Cal.App.4th at p. 673.) “A valid delivery of a deed depends upon whether the grantor intended that it should be presently operative, and a manual transfer is not conclusive evidence of such intention.” (Ibid.) “The trier of fact must determine intent by reviewing all of the surrounding circumstances of the transaction.” (Ibid.) “In addition, acceptance by the grantee is necessary to make a delivery effective and the deed operative. Whether the deed was accepted by the grantee so as to complete a transfer of title to him is likewise a question of fact for the trial court.” (Ibid.)

 

Yamaguchi has satisfied her burden. On January 31, 2011, Edward and Patricia Munoz gave Yamaguchi a grant deed to the property in exchange for monthly mortgage payments. [Yamaguchi Decl. ¶¶ 3-5.] Patricia has admitted the genuineness of the notarized grant deed containing both Edward and Patricia’s signatures. [Creekmore Decl. ¶¶ 8, 11, Exs. 7, 10.] Yamaguchi made mortgage payments until October 2015. [Ibid.; Yamaguchi Decl. ¶ 6.] These facts show the requisite intent for immediate transfer of title via the grant deed and Yamaguchi’s acceptance through monthly mortgage payments. The unopposed motion is therefore granted.

 

The July 6, 2026 Final Status Conference and trial date are vacated. Yamaguchi to submit a judgment.

 

Yamaguchi to give notice.

ATTORNEYS’ FEES

 

MOVING PARTY: Defendant, Marc H. Garelick

RESPONDING PARTY: Plaintiff, Ernest Calhoon

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Motion for Attorneys’ Fees

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

On May 6, 2024, Plaintiff Ernest Calhoon filed a First Amended Complaint for defamation and intentional infliction of emotional distress against Defendant Marc H. Garelick, Judge Pro Tem. The complaint arises from an alleged accusation of Plaintiff “being on drugs,” damaging his law practice. On October 17, 2024, the Court granted Defendant’s special motion to strike the entire complaint. Judgment was entered in favor of Defendant on May 29, 2025.  

 

RULING: Granted.

Defendant moves for an award of $12,608.24 in attorneys’ fees and costs following his successful special motion to strike Plaintiff’s First Amended Complaint. Defendant also requests a lodestar enhancement in the amount of $475/hour as opposed to the discounted $175/hour rate charged to Defendant given the “complex nature of the constitutional issues raised by the First Amended Complaint.” Plaintiff filed a “cursory” opposition to the motion,[1] challenging the Court’s exercise of its discretion to consider the untimely filed anti-SLAPP motion and contending certain orders of the prior judge are void due to a purported failure to timely respond to a disqualification challenge. Defendant’s motion is granted and Defendant is awarded $10,880.24 in fees and costs.

 

The “prevailing defendant” on an anti-SLAPP motion “shall be entitled to recover that defendant’s attorney’s fees and costs.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (c)(1).) “Such entitlement includes fees and costs ‘incurred in connection with’ the anti-SLAPP motion itself, but not ‘fees and costs incurred for the entire action.’” (Six4Three, LLC v. Facebook, Inc. (2025) 109 Cal.App.5th 635, 660.)

 

A motion for attorneys’ fees must be served and filed within the time for the filing of a notice of appeal in a civil case. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1702(b)(1).) The time for the filing of a notice of appeal in an unlimited action is 60 days if a “Notice of Entry” of Judgment was served by the court clerk or a party to the action, or within 180 days of entry of judgment. (Id. rule 8.104.) Judgment was entered on May 29, 2025 and Notice of Entry of Judgment was served on the same day. Defendant served and filed his motion 4 days later on June 2, 2025. The motion is therefore timely.

 

Defendant also timely filed and served a memorandum of costs on June 11, 2025. Plaintiff presents no challenge to the claimed costs. The Court finds no improperly listed items and the items appear reasonable. The Court thus awards all unchallenged costs.

 

The lodestar method is used “when determining an award of attorney fees under the anti-SLAPP statute.” (Frym v. 601 Main Street LLC (2022) 82 Cal.App.5th 613, 620.) “‘[T]he lodestar is the basic fee for comparable legal services in the community; it may be adjusted by the court based on factors including, as relevant herein, (1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, (4) the contingent nature of the fee award. [Citation.] The purpose of such adjustment is to fix a fee at the fair market value for the particular action. In effect, the court determines, retrospectively, whether the litigation involved a contingent risk or required extraordinary legal skill justifying augmentation of the unadorned lodestar in order to approximate the fair market rate for such services.’” (Graciano v. Robinson Ford Sales, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 140, 154.)

 

The reasonableness of attorney fees lies within the discretion of the trial court. (PLCM Group v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1096.) The Court makes it determination based on the consideration of a number of factors, including, “the nature of the litigation, its difficulty, the amount involved, the skill required in its handling, the skill employed, the attention given, the success or failure, and other circumstances in the case.” (Ibid.) The Court should apply an objective standard of reasonableness. (Id. at p. 1098.) “A fee request that appears unreasonably inflated is a special circumstance permitting the trial court to reduce the award or deny one altogether.” (Serrano v. Unruh (1982) 32 Cal.3d 621, 635.) The Court adheres to the “market rate” approach for contingency cases, and finds the “Laffey” charts general and only suggestive. (Pasternack v. McCullough (2021) 65 Cal.App.5th 1050, 1057; Center for Biological Diversity v. County of San Bernardino (2010) 188 Cal.App.4th 603, 619-20.)

 

Counsel lists a total of 55.9 billable hours, charged at a rate of $175/hour, with a total billable listed amount of $9,660. [McCormick Decl. ¶ 10, Ex. D.] The Court finds the billable rate reasonable given counsel’s over 40 years of experience and his usual $475/hour fee for similar work. [Id. ¶¶ 3, 6, 10, Ex. B.]

 

The Court next considers the listed entries. “In referring to ‘reasonable’ compensation, we indicated that trial courts must carefully review attorney documentation of hours expended; ‘padding’ in the form of inefficient or duplicative efforts is not subject to compensation.” (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132; Levy, supra, 4 Cal.App.4th at p. 817.) The Court considers the listings rather than engage in a categorical determination of entitlement. (Morris, supra, 41 Cal.App.5th at p. 40; Mountjoy v. Bank of America, N.A. (2016) 245 Cal.App.4th 266, 280-81; Christian Research Institute v. Alnor (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 1315, 1329.)

 

Plaintiff does not challenge any individual entries but presents an inappropriate collateral attack on the underlying order. The Court nonetheless notes certain entries for unrecoverable administrative tasks for calendaring and archiving documents and emails. [See McCormick Decl. ¶ 10, Ex. D.] (See Zargarian v. BMW of North America, LLC (C.D. Cal. 2020) 442 F.Supp.3d 1216, 1229.) Absent further elaboration or objection from the parties, the Court presumes that such entries were excluded as “non-billable” tasks identified in the billing record. The Court further excludes certain entries not incurred “in connection with” the special motion to strike for a total amount of $95. [McCormick Decl. ¶ 10, Ex. D: 3/28-4/4/24.] Given the lack of a reply, the Court only awards $350 ($175 x 2 hours) for reviewing Plaintiff’s opposition to the fee motion and preparing for and attending the fee hearing. [See id. ¶ 11.]  This reflects the reasonable time spent on these tasks. The Court otherwise finds the remaining entries reasonable.  

 

Defendant requests a lodestar enhancement in the amount of $475/hour. When considering a lodestar enhancement request, the Court must consider a series of factors. “[T]he lodestar is the basic fee for comparable legal services in the community; it may be adjusted by the court based on factors including, as relevant herein, (1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, (4) the contingent nature of the fee award.” (Ketchum, supra, 24 Cal.4th at p. 1132.) “Contingency and delay factors” apply in consideration of the application of a lodestar enhancement in order to reflect the “semiguaranteed” recovery of fees upon a successfully prevailing party, but any consideration must still acknowledge the “contingency” nature of representation in general. (Horsford v. Board of Trustees of California State University (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 359, 400, fn. 11.)

 

“In Serrano IV, applying the same principles to the statutory fee award under Code of Civil Procedure section 1021.5, we reiterated that fee awards should be fully compensatory. We approved the calculation of attorney fees beginning with a lodestar figure based on the reasonable hours spent, multiplied by the hourly prevailing rate for private attorneys in the community conducting noncontingent litigation of the same type. (Citation.) [¶] We held in Serrano IV that, absent circumstances rendering the award unjust, an attorney fee award should ordinarily include compensation for all the hours reasonably spent, including those relating solely to the fee. (Citation.) We explained that the purpose behind statutory fee authorizations—i.e., encouraging attorneys to act as private attorneys general and to vindicate important rights affecting the public interest—'will often be frustrated, sometimes nullified, if awards are diluted or dissipated by lengthy, uncompensated proceedings to fix or defend a rightful fee claim.’ (Citation.) [¶] We acknowledged the discretion of the trial court in setting attorney fees, but emphasized that because the determination of the lodestar figures is so fundamental to arriving at an objectively reasonable amount, ‘the exercise of that discretion must be based on the lodestar adjustment method.’ (Ibid.) We also reiterated that the lodestar figure may be increased by application of a fee enhancement, or reduced as appropriate, after the trial court has considered other factors concerning the lawsuit, including the contingent nature of the fee award.” (Ketchum, supra, 24 Cal.4th at pp. 1133-34.)

 

The Court declines to award a lodestar enhancement on the subject action. “The lodestar amount is presumptively the reasonable fee amount, and thus a multiplier may be used to adjust the lodestar amount upward or downward only in “ ‘rare’ and ‘exceptional’ cases, supported by both ‘specific evidence’ on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts” that the lodestar amount is unreasonably low or unreasonably high.” (Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mut. Life Co. (9th Cir. 2000) 214 F.3d 1041, 1045.) The Court finds the lodestar determination sufficiently addresses the fee here as the case involved a 5-page amended complaint and a relatively straightforward immunity defense.

 

In sum, Defendant is awarded $9,915 in attorneys’ fees and the full requested $965.24 in costs for a total amount of $10,880.24.

 

Because the amount of fees exceeds $5,000, the order is immediately appealable. (Code Civ. Proc., § 904.1, subd. (a)(12); Doe v. Luster (2006) 145 Cal.App.4th 139, 146.)

 

Defendant to give notice.
