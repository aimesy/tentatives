Case Number: 23AHCV02079    Hearing Date: September 8, 2026    Dept: P

[TENTATIVE] ORDER DENYING IN PART AND GRANTING IN PART DEFENDANT JULIA ZHU’S MOTION FOR SUMMARY ADJUDICATION

I.          INTRODUCTION

            This action arises out of an alleged fraudulent investment scheme. Plaintiffs Guangyu Zhao (“Zhao” or “Plaintiff”) and JZ Group USA, Inc. allege that Defendants Bowei Zhang (“Zhang”) and Julia Yali Zhu (“Zhu”) lured Plaintiff Zhao into making an investment in JZ Group which was set up to acquire and resell luxury vehicles to third parties in China. Defendants Zhang and Zhu acquired six vehicles and sold them to Hong Kong Zhengfang International Trade Co., Ltd. (“HK Zhengfang”) through the shell company Gua Investments, Inc. (“Gua Investments”) but HK Zhengfang did not receive the six vehicles it purchased. HK Zhengfang sued Plaintiffs, Defendant Zhang, and Gua Investments in case number 19STCV43714. Plaintiffs defended that case, but Defendant Zhang and Gua Investments did not appear. The Court entered a judgment against Plaintiff in the amount of $1,024,344. Through the litigation of this action, Plaintiff Zhao discovered that Defendants Zhu and Zhang opened corporate accounts in JZ Group’s names, transferred corporate funds to Defendants Zhu, Wenia Yang, and Thang Nguyen, and other third parties without Plaintiff’s knowledge.

The Complaint alleges causes of action for (1) fraud and deceit, (2) breach of fiduciary duty, (3) breach of covenant of good faith and fair dealing, (4) unfair competition, (5) equitable indemnity, (6) unjust enrichment, (7) imposition of constructive trust, (8) imposition of resulting trust, (9) willful misconduct, and (10) declaratory relief.

On June 5, 2026, Defendant Zhu filed the instant motion for summary judgment or, in the alternative, summary adjudication. On August 19, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition. On August 28, 2026, Defendant Zhu filed a reply.

The Court GRANTS Plaintiff’s request for judicial notice of Exhibits 1-19, consisting of various government, corporate, property, and court records.

  • Nos. 6 MOOT

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

            The purpose of a motion for summary judgment “is to provide courts with a mechanism to cut through the parties’ pleadings in order to determine whether, despite their allegations, trial is in fact necessary to resolve their dispute.” (Aguilar v. Atl. Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) “Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (c), requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.) 

“On a motion for summary judgment, the initial burden is always on the moving party to make¿a prima facie showing that there are no triable issues of material fact.” (Scalf v. D. B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1519.)  A defendant moving for summary judgment “has met his or her burden of showing that a cause of action has no merit if the party has shown that one or more elements of the cause of action . . . cannot be established.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c(p)(2).) “Once the defendant . . . has met that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff . . . to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.” (Id.) “If the plaintiff cannot do so, summary judgment should be granted.” (Avivi v. Centro Medico Urgente Med. Ctr. (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 463, 467.) 

“When deciding whether to grant summary judgment, the court must consider all of the evidence set forth in the papers (except evidence to which the court has sustained an objection), as well as all reasonable inferences that may be drawn from that evidence, in the light most favorable to the party opposing summary judgment.” (Avivi, supra, 159 Cal.App.4th at p. 467; see also Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c).) 

III.      ANALYSIS

            Zhu moves for summary judgment or alternatively summary adjudication of each of the ten causes of action alleged against her, on the grounds that each cause of action lacks merit. (Notice, pp. 2-3.)

Zhu categorizes Plaintiff’s claims as resting on two principal theories: (1) that Zhu, together with Zang “set up” Zhao to bear liability in the prior action (hereinafter “Prior Action”), such that Zhu should bear responsibility for the resulting judgment; and (2) that Zhu misappropriated funds belonging to Zhao and/or Hong Kong Zhengfang International Trade Co., Ltd. and used those funds to acquire real property in Massachusetts. (MSJ, p. 6.)

Collateral estoppel

As an initial matter, Zhu argues that the findings made in the Prior Action collaterally estop Zhao from pursuing his claims against her. According to Zhu, the jury in the Prior Action found Zhao liable for the intentional torts of conversion and intentional interference with contractual relations and awarded $526,435 in compensatory damages, $197,869 in prejudgment interest, and $300,000 in punitive damages based on a finding of malice, oppression, or fraud. Zhu contends that the jury’s findings and resulting judgment against Zhao render the entire Complaint subject to summary judgment under the doctrine of collateral estoppel. (MSJ, pp. 7-8.)

Zhao opposes, arguing that Zhu’s collateral estoppel argument fails for two reasons: two of the findings Zhu relies on were never made, and the findings that were made did not concern Zhu’s conduct. Zhao argues that the Prior Action never addressed, and the jury never decided, whether Zhu defrauded Zhao, breached any duties owed to him, or misappropriated Zhao’s or JZ’s property. Thus, Zhao contends that collateral estoppel does not bar his claims against Zhu. (Opposition, pp. 17–19.)

The Court is unpersuaded that collateral estoppel applies here to determine whether Zhao’s claims against Zhu are barred. Although the Prior Action resulted in findings concerning Zhao’s own conduct and liability, it did not determine whether Zhu defrauded Zhao, breached any duties owed to him or misappropriated Zhao’s or JZ’s property. Those issues were not decided by the jury in the Prior Action. Thus, the prior judgment does not establish that the issues raised by Zhao’s claims against Zhu were actually litigated and necessarily decided for claim preclusion.

Based on the foregoing, the Court DENIES summary adjudication on this ground. 

First cause of action: fraud & deceit

Zhu argues that the first cause of action for fraud and deceit fails because the findings in the Prior Action establish Zhao’s knowing participation with Zhang in the conduct involving the six vehicles. Zhu further argues there is no evidence that she personally engaged in any misconduct, and that she cannot be held vicariously liable for Zhang’s conduct. (MSJ, pp. 13-15.)

The burden therefore shifts to Zhao. Zhao presents evidence that Zhu served as JZ’s CEO and registered agent, maintained JZ’s address at her residence, registered the six vehicles, communicated with counsel regarding the vehicle dispute, and participated in filing a Statement of Information bearing Zhao’s name that he denies signing. Zhao argues this evidence supports a finding that Zhu concealed her involvement while inducing his continued investment. (Opposition, p. 20.)

The Court finds that Zhao has raised a triable issue as to Zhu’s involvement in the alleged fraud. Zhao presents third-party bank records and deposition testimony indicating otherwise. Zhao also declares that Zhu confirmed she personally went to the Department of Motor Vehicles and registered all six vehicles. (DMF, Nos. 4, 5, 8; Zhao Decl., ¶ 22, Exhs. Z11–Z22.) Thus, a triable issue exists as to Zhu’s involvement in the underlying transactions. 

The Court DENIES summary adjudication as to this cause of action.

Second cause of action: breach of fiduciary duty

Zhu argues that the breach of fiduciary duty claim fails because she was not Zhao’s business partner through JZ and had no fiduciary relationship with him. Zhu maintains that she owed Zhao no contractual or other obligation and cannot be held vicariously liable for any acts or omissions by Zhang. (MSJ, pp. 14-15.) 

            As discussed above, Zhao has raised a triable issue as to Zhu’s involvement in JZ. Zhao presents evidence that Zhu served as JZ’s incorporator, officer and registered agent, obtained federal tax identification number using her information, opened its bank account as president, and participated in the vehicle transactions. Zhao contends that, by assuming these responsibilities over the company in which he invested, Zhu occupied a position of trust and confidence. (Opposition, pp. 21-22.)

            Based on this evidence, the Court cannot determine as a matter of law that no fiduciary relationship existed. The Court finds that there is a triable issue of fact as to whether a fiduciary relationship existed between Zhu and Zhao. Thus, summary adjudication as to this cause of action is DENIED.

Third cause of action: breach of implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing

As to this cause of action, Zhu argues that it fails because the judgment in the Prior Action precludes Zhao’s claim and because there is no evidence that Zhu owed or breached any express or implied contractual obligation to Zhao or JZ. Zhu further argues that she cannot be held vicariously liable for Zhang’s conduct. (MSJ, pp. 15-16.)

In opposition, Zhao argues that his investment in JZ and the arrangement by which he was added as a co-signatory on JZ’s bank account to protect that investment support the existence of a contractual or quasi-contractual relationship. Zhao contends that Zhu’s alleged concealment of the GUA-Hong Kong transaction, diversion of proceeds, and participation in filing the disputed Statement of Information breached the good-faith obligations arising from that arrangement. (Opposition, p. 22.)

The Court finds that Zhao has raised a triable issue of fact as to whether the parties arrangement concerning Zhao’s investment in JZ and his addition as a co-signatory on JZ’s bank account gave rise to an implied contractual relationship between Zhao and Zhu. Thus, summary adjudication as to this cause of action is DENIED.

Fourth cause of action: unfair competition

Zhu moves for summary adjudication as to this cause of action, raising the same arguments concerning the preclusive effect of the Prior Action and the lack of evidence of any actionable misconduct by Zhu.   

The Court finds that there is a triable issue of fact here. As discussed above, Zhao has presented evidence raising a triable issue as to Zhu’s involvement in the underlying transactions. Based on this evidence, the Court finds a triable issue of fact as to whether Zhu engaged in an unlawful, unfair, or fraudulent business practice. Thus, summary adjudication as to the fourth cause of action is DENIED.

Fifth cause of action: equitable indemnity

Zhu moves for summary adjudication as to the fifth cause of action, arguing that the claim is premature because Zhao has not paid any portion of the judgment entered in the Prior Action. Zhu further argues that she was not a party to the Prior Action, not a judgment debtor and shares no joint obligation with Zhao. (MSJ, pp. 18-19.)

A fundamental prerequisite to a claim for equitable indemnity is an actual monetary loss through payment of a judgment or settlement. (Western Steamship Lines, Inc. v. San Pedro Peninsula Hospital (1994) 8 Cal.4th 100, 107; Christian v. County of Los Angeles (1986) 176 Cal.App.3d 466, 471.)

Here, it is undisputed that Zhao has not paid any portion of the judgment entered in the Prior Action. (See Opposition, p.22; U/DMF No. 14.) As such, the claim for equitable indemnity does not accrue until the indemnitee suffers an actual loss through payment of a judgment or settlement. Zhao’s claim is premature as a matter of law.

 Thus, summary adjudication as to the fifth cause of action is GRANTED. 

Sixth, seventh and eighth causes of actions: unjust enrichment, constructive trust and resulting trust

As to these causes of action, Zhu argues that each fails as a matter of law because they are remedies rather than independent causes of action. Zhu further argues that Zhao cannot trace any funds belonging to Zhao, JZ, or Hong Kong to Zhu or to any specific property owned by her. (MSJ, pp. 19-23.)

            In opposition, Zhao acknowledges that they are equitable remedies rather than standalone causes of action but argues that the underlying allegations may nevertheless support the imposition of such remedies. (Opposition, pp. 22-23.)

            The Court agrees with Zhu. Because these causes of action do not constitute independent causes of action, they cannot survive as separately pleaded claims. This ruling does not preclude Zhao from seeking appropriate equitable remedy based upon any surviving substantive cause of action.

            Thus, summary adjudication as to these causes of action is GRANTED.

Ninth cause of action: willful misconduct

            Zhu moves for summary adjudication as to this cause of action arguing that willful misconduct is again not an independent cause of action and that Zhao otherwise lacks evidence of actionable misconduct by Zhu. (MSJ, pp. 23-25.)

            In opposition, Zhao acknowledges not a separate cause of action. However, but argues that it constitutes an aggravated form of misconduct applicable to his remaining claims.(Opposition, p.24.)

The Court agrees that willful misconduct does not constitute an independent cause of action. To the extent Zhu’s alleged willful conduct is relevant to Zhao’s surviving claims, Zhao may pursue that theory in connection with those claims. 

Thus, summary adjudication as to this cause of action is GRANTED.

Tenth cause of action: declaratory relief

            Zhu moves for summary adjudication as to this cause of action arguing that fails because no actual controversy exists between Zhao and Zhu because Zhao cannot establish that Zhu received or possesses any funds or property belonging to Zhao, JZ, GUA or Hong Kong. Zhu further argues that the judgment in the Prior Action does not create a controversy between Zhao and Zhu because Zhu was not a party to that action nor a judgment debtor. (MSJ, p. 26; Reply p. 13.)

            In opposition, Zhao argues that an actual controversy exists as to whether Zhu may retain proceeds traceable to transfers from JZ, GUA, or Zhang and whether she must account for those proceeds. (Opposition, p. 24.)

            Here, as discussed above, Zhao has raised triable issues concerning Zhu’s involvement in the underlying transactions and whether she received or controls proceeds traceable to JZ, GUA, or Zhang. On the present record, the Court cannot determine as a matter of law that no actual controversy exists between Zhao and Zhu.

            Thus, summary adjudication as to this cause of action of action is DENIED.

IV.  CONCLUSION & ORDER

               The Court DENIES Zhu’s motion for summary judgment as to the entire Complaint. The Court GRANTS summary adjudication as to the fifth, sixth, seventh, eighth, and ninth causes of action, and DENIES summary adjudication as to the first, second, third, fourth, and tenth causes of action.

              Zhu is to give notice.

Case Number: 23AHCV02287    Hearing Date: September 8, 2026    Dept: P

[TENTATIVE] ORDER DENYING CROSS-DEFENDANT’S YUSHAN HAN AND LI LIU’S MOTION FOR ATTORNEY’S FEES

I.          INTRODUCTION

This is an action for breach of contract. Plaintiff Express Restoration Corp. alleges that on January 9, 2023, it entered into an agreement with Defendant 1927 Strathmore Townhouse Homeowners Association to perform emergency work at property located at 1927 Strathmore Avenue, San Gabriel, CA 91776, after there was a fire on the property, including water mitigation, mold mitigation and fire mitigation work. Plaintiff alleges that Defendant breached the agreement by failing to pay Plaintiff for work performed. The complaint alleges the following causes of action: (1) to foreclose mechanic’s lien; (2) breach of contract; (3) for work labor and materials furnished; and (4) unjust enrichment. On November 7, 2023, Defendant filed a cross-complaint against Plaintiff, Yushan Han, and Li Liu, alleging causes of action for (1) breach of contract; (2) fraud; (3) violation of Business and Professions Code section 17200, and (4) indemnity. 

On December 3, 2025, after a non-jury trial, Plaintiff dismissed its first cause of action, and the Court found that there was no contract and entered judgment in favor of Defendant. The Court specifically found that Plaintiff’s witness, Tammy Woloski, was not credible and that Plaintiff had intentionally and fraudulently overbilled Defendant for many thousands of dollars for work that was never performed. As to the cross complaint, the Court found Defendant suffered no financial damages. The Court granted Plaintiff’s motion pursuant to CCP section 631.8 and found judgment in favor of Plaintiff as to the cross-complaint.

On August 7, 2026, Cross-Defendants filed the instant motion for attorney’s fees. On August 14, 2026, Cross-Complainant HOA filed an opposition. No reply has been filed.

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

The court, upon notice and motion by a party, shall determine the prevailing party and shall fix, as an element of the costs of suit, the reasonable attorney fees. (Civ. Code § 1717 subd. (a), (b).) Any notice of motion to claim attorney fees as an element of costs under shall be served and filed before or at the same time the memorandum of costs is served and filed; if only attorney fees are claimed as costs, the notice of motion shall be served and filed within the time specified in California Rules of Court 3.1700 for filing a memorandum of costs. (California Rules of Court 3.1702; Gunlock Corp. v. Walk on Water, Inc. (1993) 15 Cal.App.4th 1301, 1303, fn. 1.) 

“It is well established that the determination of what constitutes reasonable attorney fees is committed to the discretion of the trial court, whose decision cannot be reversed in the absence of an abuse of discretion. [Citation.]” (Melnyk v. Robledo (1976) 64 Cal.App.3d 618, 623-624.) The fee setting inquiry in California ordinarily “begins with the ‘lodestar’ [method], i.e., the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate.” (Graciano v. Robinson Ford Sales, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 140, 154.) “[A] computation of time spent on a case and the reasonable value of that time is fundamental to a determination of an appropriate attorneys’ fee award.” (Margolin v. Reg’l Planning Comm’n (1982) 134 Cal.App.3d 999, 1004.)

The lodestar figure may then be adjusted, based on consideration of factors specific to the case, in order to fix the fee at the fair market value for the legal services provided. (See Serrano v. Priest (1977) 20 Cal.3d 25, 49 [discussing factors relevant to proper attorneys’ fees award].) Such an approach anchors the trial court’s analysis to an objective determination of the value of the attorney’s services, ensuring that the amount awarded is not arbitrary. (Id. at 48, fn. 23.) The factors considered in determining the modification of the lodestar include “(1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, (4) the contingent nature of the fee award.” (Mountjoy v. Bank of Am. (2016) 245 Cal.App.4th 266, 271.) 

III.      ANALYSIS

Cross-Defendants move for attorney fees pursuant to Civil Code section 1717 and California Rules of Court, rule 3.1702, based on the fees they incurred in defending against the HOA’s cross-complaint in this action.

Cross-Defendants argue that, following trial, the HOA’s cross-complaint against them was dismissed with prejudice, and, therefore, they are the prevailing parties. They further contend that they are entitled to recover their attorney’s fees pursuant to section 14.03 of the HOA’s CC&Rs. That section permits an award of reasonable attorney’s fees and costs to the prevailing party in an action arising from an alleged breach or default under the Declaration. (Motion, pp. 4-6.)

Further, Cross-Defendants acknowledge that instant motion for attorney’s fees is untimely because it was filed on August 7, 2026, which is well beyond the applicable 180-day period. They nevertheless request that the Court extend the time to file arguing that the delay resulted from counsel’s mistake, inadvertence, and excusable neglect. Specifically, counsel states that she mistakenly calendared March 6, 2026, as the date of entry of judgment and, as a result, believed the deadline to file the motion was September 6, 2026. (Motion, p.6.)

Untimeliness

The HOA opposes the motion as untimely, arguing that counsel’s claimed inadvertence is undermined by the fact that Cross-Defendants filed a Memorandum of Costs on January 30, 2026, which indicates that they were aware judgment had been entered before that date. (Opposition, p. 2.)

The Court declines to extend the filing deadline. Cross-Defendants have not demonstrated good cause under rule 3.1702(d). Although counsel attributes the untimely filing to her mistaken belief that judgment was entered on March 6, 2026, Cross-Defendants filed a Memorandum of Costs on January 30, 2026. That filing is inconsistent with counsel’s asserted belief regarding the date of entry of judgment. Cross-Defendants provide no explanation for this discrepancy, and no reply was filed addressing the HOA’s argument. Under these circumstances, the Court is not persuaded that counsel’s calendaring mistake constitutes good cause to extend the filing deadline under rule 3.1702(d).Further the judgment entered on 1/9/26 indicates that “The Court retains jurisdiction to hear and determine the entitlement to and the amount of any costs and/or attorney’s fees upon timely application by the parties.”

Thus, the motion is DENIED as untimely.

Prevailing party

Even if the motion was timely, Cross-Defendants have not established their entitlement to attorney’s fees. 

The HOA argues that Civil Code section 1717 does not apply because there was no contract between the HOA and Cross-Defendants upon which the underlying action was based. The HOA contends that its claims against Cross-Defendants were brought under the indemnity provisions of the CC&Rs and that the CC&Rs merely govern the relationship between the HOA and its members, rather than constitute a separately negotiated contract between the parties. (Opposition, pp. 2-4.)

Cross-Defendants contend that they are the prevailing parties for two reasons. First, they argue that they qualify as prevailing parties under Code of Civil Procedure section 1032, subdivision (a)(4), because the HOA’s cross-complaint against them was dismissed with prejudice. Second, they contend that they are prevailing parties under section 14.03 of the HOA’s CC&Rs, which permits the Court to award reasonable attorney’s fees and costs to the prevailing party in an action based on an alleged breach or default under the Declaration.

Following the bench trial, the Court found that no contract existed between Plaintiff ERC and the HOA and entered judgment in favor of the HOA. (4/13/26 Minute Order, p. 1.) As a result, the HOA had no liability to ERC for which it could seek indemnification from Cross-Defendants, and the HOA’s cross-claims against Cross-Defendants were subsequently dismissed with prejudice. 

The Court agrees that Cross-Defendants qualify as prevailing parties for purposes of CCP section 1032. Section 1032, subdivision (a)(4) defines a prevailing party to include “a defendant in whose favor a dismissal is entered,” and for purposes of the statute, a defendant includes a cross-defendant. Moreover, the fact that the HOA’s indemnity claims became moot after the HOA prevailed on Plaintiff’s complaint does not preclude Cross-Defendants from qualifying as prevailing parties for purposes of costs. (City of Long Beach v. Stevedoring Services of America (2007) 157 Cal.App.4th 672.)

However, prevailing-party status under section 1032 does not, by itself, establish entitlement to attorney’s fees. Cross-Defendants must demonstrate an independent statutory or contractual basis for such an award. Cross-Defendants rely on section 14.03 of the CC&Rs, which permits the Court to award reasonable attorney’s fees to the prevailing party “[i]n any action on account of any alleged breach or default of any Owner or other party hereto under this Declaration.” (Han Decl., Exh. A.)

The Court is not persuaded that Cross-Defendants have established that section 14.03 applies here. Although the HOA’s indemnity claim referenced section 4.04 of the CC&Rs, the claim sought indemnification from Cross-Defendants in the event the HOA was found liable to ERC and expressly invoked equitable principles as the basis for such relief. By its plain language, section 14.03 applies to an action “on account of any alleged breach or default” under the Declaration. Cross-Defendants have not shown that the HOA’s indemnity claim falls within that language merely because it referenced an obligation contained in the CC&Rs. No reply was filed addressing the HOA’s argument. No reply was filed addressing the HOA’s argument on this issue.

Thus, even if the motion were timely, Cross-Defendants have not established that section 14.03 provides a basis for an award of attorney’s fees.

IV.       CONCLUSION AND ORDER

            The Court DENIES Cross-Defendants’ motion for attorney’s fees.

Cross-Defendants’ are to give notice.
