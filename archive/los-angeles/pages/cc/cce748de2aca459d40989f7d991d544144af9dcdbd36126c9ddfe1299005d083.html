Case Number: 25SMCV04824    Hearing Date: July 30, 2026    Dept: O

TENTATIVE RULING

July 30, 2026

25SMCV04824 (Steinberg, et. al. v. Peterson, et. al.)

 

RELEVANT BACKGROUND

 

            On September 16, 2025, plaintiffs Gail Steinberg (Steinberg) and Alexa Jacobs f/k/a/ Alexa Steinberg (Jacobs) (collectively, Plaintiffs) filed their complaint against defendants Diane Peterson (Peterson), Bernard Alexander (Alexander), and Does 1-100 (collectively, Defendants). Plaintiffs allege two causes of action: (1) fraud and deceit (suppression of fact); and (2) intentional misrepresentation.         

 

            On or about November 3, 2022, Plaintiffs became the owners of a condominium unit located at 26664 Seagull Way, Unit A121, Malibu, California, County of Los Angeles (Unit A121 or the Property). (Compl. ¶ 4.) Unit A121 is located in the Tivoli Cove condominium complex at 26664 Seagull Way, Malibu, California (Tivoli Cove). (Id.) David Storrs (Storrs) was the prior owner of the Property. (Id. ¶ 9.)

 

             Plaintiff alleges that Alexander served on the Tivoli Cove HOA Board. (Id. ¶ 6.) In such capacity, he had access to and knowledge of information regarding the condition of Tivoli Cove and potential work that would be needed that would result in future assessments. (Id. ¶ 25.) Plaintiff further alleges that, notwithstanding that Alexander was privy to and had knowledge of the many repairs that were being discussed by Tivoli Cove HOA, including studies, inspections, reports and estimates related to such work, when asked about these things he failed to disclose any of this information to Plaintiff. (Id.)

                       

INSTANT MOTION

 

             On April 8, 2026, Alexander filed the instant demurrer and motion to strike. Plaintiff filed an opposition. No reply has been filed.

 

MEET AND CONFER

 

             The Court finds that Alexander has satisfied the meet and confer requirement. (Zumstein Decl. ¶ 1-9.)

 

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

 

             Alexander’s request for judicial notice of the complaint in this action, although unnecessary, is granted.

 

DISCUSSION

 

I.    DEMURRER

 

Fraud and Deceit

 

            “[T]he elements of an action for fraud and deceit based on concealment are: (1) the defendant must have concealed or suppressed a material fact, (2) the defendant must have been under a duty to disclose the fact to the plaintiff, (3) the defendant must have intentionally concealed or suppressed the fact with the intent to defraud the plaintiff, (4) the plaintiff must have been unaware of the fact and would not have acted as he did if he had known of the concealed or suppressed fact, and (5) as a result of the concealment or suppression of the fact, the plaintiff must have sustained damage.” (Lovejoy v. AT&T Corp. (2004) 119 Cal.App.4th 151, 157–158.)

 

            A duty to disclose arises when “[1] a defendant owes a fiduciary duty to a plaintiff … [2] when the defendant has exclusive knowledge of material facts not known to the plaintiff; [3] when the defendant actively conceals a material fact from the plaintiff; or [4] when the defendant makes partial representations but also suppresses some material facts.” (Jones v. ConocoPhillips Co. (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 1187, 1199 [internal citations and quotation marks omitted; cleaned up].)

 

            “Each of the [latter] three circumstances in which nondisclosure may be actionable presupposes the existence of some other relationship between the plaintiff and defendant in which a duty to disclose can arise.” (LiMandri v. Judkins (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 326, 336–337.) “[S]uch a relationship can only come into being as a result of some sort of transaction between the parties.” (Id. at p. 337.) “Thus, a duty to disclose may arise from the relationship between seller and buyer, employer and prospective employee, doctor and patient, or parties entering into any kind of contractual agreement.” (Ibid.)

 

Intentional Misrepresentation

 

            “The elements of a cause of action for intentional misrepresentation are (1) a misrepresentation, (2) with knowledge of its falsity, (3) with the intent to induce another’s reliance on the misrepresentation, (4) actual and justifiable reliance, and (5) resulting damage.” (Daniels v. Select Portfolio Servicing, Inc. (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 1150, 1166.)

 

            The facts constituting the alleged fraud must be alleged factually and specifically as to every element of fraud, as the policy of “liberal construction” of the pleadings will not ordinarily be invoked. (Lazar v. Superior Court (1996) 12 Cal.4th 631, 645.) “[Fraud’s] particularity requirement necessitates pleading facts which ‘show how, when, where, to whom, and by what means the representations were tendered.’ [Citation.]” (Stansfield v. Starkey (1990) 220 Cal.App.3d 59, 73.)

 

            To properly allege fraud against a corporation, the plaintiffs must plead the names of the persons allegedly making the false representations, their authority to speak, to whom they spoke, what they said or wrote, and when it was said or written. (Tarmann v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. (1991) 2 Cal.App.4th 153, 157.)

 

           Here, Alexander argues that both the first and second causes of action fail because the Complaint does not allege facts showing that Alexander acted with the intent to induce Plaintiffs' reliance. Alexander contends that intent to induce reliance is an essential element of fraud, deceit by suppression, and intentional misrepresentation, and it must be pleaded with factual specificity.

 

           In opposition, Plaintiffs highlight that Alexander’s demurrer improperly isolates the single element of intent while ignoring the factual allegations establishing his knowledge, duty, and non-disclosure. Plaintiffs contend that, when the Complaint is read as a whole, it adequately states claims for fraud and intentional misrepresentation. The Court agrees with Plaintiffs.

 

           The Court notes that no reply has been filed.

 

           The Court finds that the Complaint alleges facts sufficient to state causes of action for fraud and intentional misrepresentation, including facts sufficient to warrant an inference of intent to induce reliance. The Complaint alleges that Alexander: served on the HOA Board in leadership roles; had access to and knowledge of material information regarding structural conditions and anticipated assessments; was specifically identified as the person to address Plaintiffs' inquiries regarding HOA conditions and assessments; and failed to disclose those material facts despite direct inquiry. (See Compl. ¶ 6, 25, 28, 38, 41, 48, 50-57, 69, 71-73, 76-82, 87-94.) The Complaint alleges that Alexander, a member of the HOA Board with superior exclusive knowledge of material structural issues and anticipated assessments, was specifically identified as the person to address Plaintiffs’ inquiries and failed to disclose those facts. (Id.) As for particularity, the Complaint identifies: who (Alexander); what (failure to disclose material facts regarding structural issues and assessments); when (during escrow and prior to closing); and how (by remaining silent in response to direct inquiries despite knowledge of material facts). (Id.) The allegations in the Complaint are adequate at the pleading stage.

 

           Accordingly, Alexander’s demurrer to the first and second causes of action alleged in the complaint is OVERRULED.

 

II.    MOTION TO STRIKE

 

Based on the foregoing, Defendant’s motion to strike is DENIED, as the Complaint alleges a cause of action for fraud.


Case Number: 25SMCV04926    Hearing Date: July 30, 2026    Dept: O

TENTATIVE RULING

July 30, 2026

25SMCV04926 (Oritz v. Stache West Hollywood, et. al.)

 

RELEVANT BACKGROUND

 

            On September 22, 2025, plaintiff Jeremiah Oritz (Plaintiff) filed his complaint against Stache West Hollywood (Stache); Arc Group Agency Corp Dba Arc Lifestyle & Protection (Arc); Robin Liott (Liott) individually as Trustee of the Robin Liott Revocable Trust (Trust); Joanne Nathan (Nathan); and Pinherio Da Silva (Da Silvia). Plaintiff alleges five causes of action: assault; battery; intentional infliction of emotional distress (IIED); negligence; negligent hiring; supervision; and retention.

 

            This case arises from a physical altercation that allegedly occurred on the patio area of a restaurant located at 8941 Santa Monica Blvd., West Hollywood, on September 29, 2023. (Complaint, ¶¶ 7, 8.) Plaintiff alleges he was in the patio area of the premises when he was notified that his food order was ready from the restaurant next door. (Id. ¶ 8). According to Plaintiff, he left to pick up his order and, when he returned to the patio area, defendant Da Silva, a security guard, asked him to leave. (Id. ¶ 8). Plaintiff alleges that defendant Da Silva approached him and leaned forward within inches of him and loudly threatened to touch and/or grab Plaintiff. (Id. ¶ 15). Plaintiff further alleges that as he was leaving, Da Silva physically attacked him. (Id. ¶ 8). Plaintiff also claims that he did not batter De Silva before, during, or after the alleged attack. (Id. ¶ 10.)

                       

INSTANT MOTION

 

             On February 20, 2026, Stache, Liotta, Trust, and Nathan (Defendants) filed the instant demurrer. Plaintiff filed an opposition, and Defendants filed a reply.

 

MEET AND CONFER

 

             The Court finds that Defendants have satisfied the meet and confer requirement. (Thomson Decl.)

 

DISCUSSION

 

I.       DEMURRER

 

A.    Assault, Battery, and IIED

 

            “The essential elements of a cause of action for assault are: (1) defendant acted with intent to cause harmful or offensive contact, or threatened to touch plaintiff in a harmful or offensive manner; (2) plaintiff reasonably believed [he or] she was about to be touched in a harmful or offensive manner or it reasonably appeared to plaintiff that defendant was about to carry out the threat; (3) plaintiff did not consent to defendant’s conduct; (4) plaintiff was harmed; and (5) defendant’s conduct was a substantial factor in causing plaintiff’s harm.” (So v. Shin (2013) 212 Cal.App.4th 652, 668-669.)

 

            “The essential elements of a cause of action for battery are: (1) defendant touched plaintiff, or caused plaintiff to be touched, with the intent to harm or offend plaintiff; (2) plaintiff did not consent to the touching; (3) plaintiff was harmed or offended by defendant’s conduct; and (4) a reasonable person in plaintiff’s position would have been offended by the touching.” (So, 212 Cal.App.4th at pp. 668-669.)

 

            “The elements of a prima facie case for the tort of intentional infliction of emotional distress are: (1) extreme and outrageous conduct by the defendant with the intention of causing, or reckless disregard of the probability of causing, emotional distress; (2) the plaintiff’s suffering severe or extreme emotional distress; and (3) actual and proximate causation of the emotional distress by the defendant’s outrageous conduct. Conduct to be outrageous must be so extreme as to exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated in a civilized community.” (Wilson v. Hynek (2012) 207 Cal.App.4th 999, 1009, citation and ellipses omitted.) “Whether a defendant’s conduct can reasonably be found to be outrageous is a question of law that must initially be determined by the court; if reasonable persons may differ, it is for the jury to determine whether the conduct was, in fact, outrageous.” (Berkley v. Dowds (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 518, 534.)

 

            “Under the common law doctrine of respondeat superior, a principal or employer is vicariously liable for the acts of an agent or employee committed in the course of employment.” (Lathrop v. HealthCare Partners Medical Group (2004) 114 Cal.App.4th 1412, 1421; accord Lisa M. v. Henry Mayo Newhall Mem'l Hosp. (1995) 12 Cal.4th 291, 297; Myers v. Trendwest Resorts, Inc. (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 1403, 1427.) Employers may be liable for intentional or malicious torts or criminal acts, done outside the scope of employment, where they arise from the work and are a predictable risk of the work. (Flores v. Autozone W. (2008) 161 Cal.App.4th 373, 379, 380 [“an employee's willful, malicious and even criminal torts may fall within the scope of his or her employment for purposes of respondeat superior, even though the employer has not authorized the employee to commit crimes or intentional torts.”].) “‘An employer may … be vicariously liable for the employee's tort—even if it was malicious, willful, or criminal—if the employee's act was an outgrowth of his employment, inherent in the working environment, typical of or broadly incidental to the employer's business, or, in a general way, foreseeable from his duties.’” (Unruh-Haxton v. Regents of University of California (2008) 162 Cal.App.4th 343, 368.)

 

            Here, Defendants argue that Plaintiff fails to allege facts sufficient to establish that they are liable for De Silva’s conduct because the alleged acts were not within the course and scope of Da Silva’s employment. Defendants state that there are no facts pleaded that suggest that physically assaulting and battering another was an outgrowth of Da Silva’s duties as a security guard. Defendants cite Lisa M., supra, 12 Cal.4th 291. Further, Defendants argue that the conduct was not foreseeable, because there are no facts in the complaint showing that Da Silva had a prior incident of misconduct.

 

            Additionally, Defendants assert that Plaintiff fails to sufficiently allege who employed Da Silva, as the complaint alleges only that Da Silva was a “direct employee” of the defendants. (Complaint, ¶ 4.)

 

            Further, Defendants argue that Plaintiff failed to plead sufficient facts to establish that the Defendants ratified Da Silva’s alleged misconduct, because the complaint merely states in a conclusory manner that “Plaintiff is further informed and believes, and thereon alleges, that each of the Defendants herein gave consent to, ratified, and authorized the acts alleged herein to each of the remaining Defendants.” (Complaint, ¶ 6.)

 

            With respect to Stache, Defendants highlight that the complaint does not identify a managing agent with knowledge of the conduct who possessed a degree of discretion over decisions that would ultimately determine corporate policy, citing White v. Ultramar, Inc. (1999) 21 Cal.4th 563, 571 and Agarwal v. Johnson (1979) 25 Cal.3d 932, 952.

 

            Lastly, with respect to the IIED claim, Defendants argue that it is entirely duplicative of his assault-and-battery claims and “adds nothing to the complaint by way of fact or theory of recovery,” citing Rodrigues v. Campbell Industries (1978) 87 Cal.App.3d 494, 501.  

 

            The Court agrees with Defendants that the complaint fails to allege facts sufficient to establish Defendants’ liability for the Da Dilva’s alleged intentional misconduct. First, it is unclear from the allegations whether De Silva was employed by Defendants, and if so, which Defendant(s) employed him. Additionally, Plaintiff has not alleged sufficient facts to show that Defendants had any knowledge of the possibility of the alleged conduct by Da Silva or that Defendants failed to act in some manner for which they had authority after learning of the incident.  

 

            The Court is not persuaded by Plaintiff’s argument in opposition. Plaintiff cites paragraphs 22-23, 32-33, 42-43, 55 in the complaint. But none of those allegations show that Defendants had any knowledge of the possibility of the alleged conduct by Da Silva or that Defendants failed to act in some manner for which they had authority after learning of the incident. (See Compl. ¶¶ 22-23, 32-33, 42-43, 55.) Additionally, Plaintiff contends in opposition that “[w]hat makes this conduct especially outrageous is that it was committed by a security guard – an individual entrusted with the duty to protect, observe, and report” and that he failed “to fulfill[] [his] responsibilities.” (Opp. at p. 13.) This is consistent with Defendants’ argument that the alleged assault could not have been within the scope of his employment, and the allegations in the complaint fail to show otherwise. 

 

            The Court finds that leave to amend is appropriate because it is reasonably possible for Plaintiff to cure the defects through amendment.

 

            Accordingly, Defendants’ demurrer to the first, second, and third causes of action are SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

B.     Negligence

 

            In order to state a claim for negligence, a plaintiff must allege the elements of (1) “the existence of a legal duty of care,” (2) “breach of that duty,” and (3) “proximate cause resulting in an injury.” (McIntyre v. Colonies-Pacific, LLC (2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 664, 671.)

 

            California law requires landowners to maintain land in their possession and control in a reasonably safe condition. (Civ. Code, § 1714.) In cases involving third parties, businesses (because they generally stand in a special relationship with their tenants, patrons, or invitees) have an affirmative duty to take reasonable steps to secure their premises, as well as adjacent common areas within their control, against reasonably foreseeable criminal acts of third parties. (Delgado v. Trax Bar & Grill (2005) 36 Cal.4th 224, 235.) “[F]oreseeability is of primary importance in establishing the element of duty” for the wrongdoing of third parties giving rise to premises liability.” (Issacs v. Huntington Mem. Hosp. (1985) 38 Cal.3d 112, 123.)

 

            Here, Defendants assert that, as stated above, Plaintiff alleges no facts to suggest that the alleged incident should have been foreseeable to Defendants. Plaintiff’s opposition fails to address this argument regarding foreseeability. (See Opp. at p. 16-17.) The Court agrees with Defendants that the Complaint fails to allege facts showing that the alleged incident was foreseeable to Defendants or that Defendants had any knowledge of the possibility of the alleged conduct by Da Silva. But the Court finds that leave to amend is appropriate because it is reasonably possible to cure the defects through amendment.

 

            In opposition, Plaintiff cites paragraphs 9, 11, 24, 25, 27, and 28 in the complaint. But all of these allegations are conclusory and do not address the above deficiencies. (See Compl. ¶ 9, 11, 24, 25, 27, and 28.)

 

            Accordingly, the demurrer to the fourth cause of action is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

C.    Negligent Hiring, Supervision, and Retention

 

            The elements of a cause of action for negligent hiring, retention, or supervision are: (1) the employer’s hiring, retaining, or supervising an employee; (2) the employee was incompetent or unfit; (3) the employer had reason to believe undue risk of harm would exist because of the employment; and (4) harm occurs. (Evan F. v. Hughson United Methodist Church (1992) 8 Cal.App.4th 828, 836-837.)

 

            “An employer may be liable to a third person for the employer's negligence in hiring or retaining an employee who is incompetent or unfit. [Citation.]” (Roman Catholic Bishop v. Superior Court (1996) 42 Cal.App.4th 1556, 1564–1565.) “Liability for negligent hiring . . . is based upon the reasoning that if an enterprise hires individuals with characteristics which might pose a danger to customers or other employees, the enterprise should bear the loss caused by the wrongdoing of its incompetent or unfit employees.” (Mendoza v. City of Los Angeles (1998) 66 Cal.App.4th 1333, 1339.)

 

            The Court finds that the complaint fails to allege facts sufficient to state a cause of action for negligent hiring, supervision, and retention. As set forth above, the Court it is unclear from the allegations whether De Silva was employed by Defendants, and if so, which Defendant(s) employed him. Additionally, Plaintiff has not alleged sufficient facts to show that Defendants had any knowledge of the possibility of the alleged conduct by Da Silva or that Defendants failed to act in some manner for which they had authority after learning of the incident. But the Court finds that leave to amend is appropriate because it is reasonably possible to cure the defects through amendment.

 

            In opposition, Plaintiff cites paragraphs 54 and 55 of the complaint, but these allegations are conclusory and are devoid of any supporting facts. (Compl. ¶ 54 [“Plaintiff is further informed and believes, and based thereon alleges, that Defendants knew or should have known, through the exercise of reasonable care, that Defendant PINHERIO DA SILVA was unfit or incompetent, and that this unfitness created a particular risk of harm to others, including Plaintiff.”]; 55 [“Despite having actual knowledge, or being in a position where they should have known through the exercise of reasonable care, Defendants…failed to take appropriate action…”].)

 

            Accordingly, the demurrer to the fifth cause of action is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.
