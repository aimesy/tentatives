[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: MOTION FOR ATTORNEY FEES

 

Plaintiff Barry Rosen (“Plaintiff”) moves for attorney fees and costs from defendants Clark Drive Homeowners Association (“Association”), Deborah Bushell (“Bushell”), Elizabeth Muller (“Muller”), and Payam Yashar (“Yashar”) (collectively, “Defendants”) under Civil Code section 5145, and requests statutory penalties.

Although the parties have attempted to turn this fee dispute into a broader, accusatory exchange, the legal issue before the Court is straightforward. The motion is resolved solely on the governing timeliness rules. On that basis, the motion is denied.

I.                Background

On September 12, 2024, Plaintiff filed his verified complaint, initiating this action.

On February 11, 2025, the Court heard the matter and granted Plaintiff mandamus relief. On April 10, 2025, the Court entered judgment in favor of Plaintiff. On April 14, 2025, the Court issued a writ of mandate against Defendants.

On May 27, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant attorney fee motion. On July 15, 2026, Defendants filed their opposition. On July 28, 2026, Plaintiff filed his reply. On July 29, 2026, Defendants filed a brief styled as objections to evidence in Plaintiff’s reply, or alternatively, a proposed sur-reply. [1]

II.             Discussion

Plaintiff seeks an award of $104,927, composed of $103,927 in attorney fees and $1,000 for two $500 statutory penalties for violations of election statutes. (Mot. at 11-12.)

As Defendants argue in opposition (Opp. at 2), the motion is untimely.

California Rules of Court (“CRC”), rule 3.1702, subdivision (b)(1) provides that a motion claiming fees for services up to and including trial court judgment must be filed and served “within the time for filing a notice of appeal under rule[] 8.104 and 8.108.” [2] (CRC 3.1702(b)(1).) CRC rule 8.104 provides that a party must file a notice of appeal on or before the earlier of (A) 60 days after the clerk serves the party with a notice of entry of judgment or a filed-endorsed copy of the judgment; (B) 60 days after the party is served by another party with the same; or (C) 180 days after entry of judgment. (CRC rule 8.104.)

Here, the Court entered judgment in favor of Rosen on April 10, 2025. Consequently, the time to appeal the Court’s judgment expired, at latest, 180 days later, or October 7, 2025. (CRC rule 8.104.) Plaintiff filed his motion on May 27, 2026, or 232 days beyond the window to timely file. (CRC rules 3.1702, 8.104.)

In reply, Plaintiff argues that the Court’s writ “was never properly issued,” and therefore “the motion is timely, and the time to bring the motion for attorney fees was not subject to any limitations period.” (Reply at 3.)

CRC rule 3.1702 is clear. The time to file a motion claiming attorney fees is the same as the time to file a notice of appeal. (CRC rule 3.1702.) CRC rule 8.104 is also clear. The 180-day limitations period to file a notice of appeal begins with the entry of judgment. (CRC rule 8.104.) Plaintiff does not argue that any of the explicit extensions or exceptions apply; provides no legal authority supporting his position that the limitations period does not begin until a writ issues; and provides no legal authority suggesting that improper issuance of a writ tolls the limitations period. Furthermore, Plaintiff provides no evidence or legal basis to support his contention that the writ “was never properly issued.” (Reply at 2-3.)

The deadlines in CRC rule 3.1702(b) are mandatory, not discretionary, and the Court has no authority to enlarge or disregard them absent a statutory or rule-based exception. Even accepting Plaintiff’s factual assertions for the sake of argument, they would not alter the legal deadlines. The time to file a fee motion runs from entry of judgment, not from the issuance or service of a writ, and Plaintiff identifies no authority establishing otherwise. Plaintiff also did not seek any extension under CRC rule 3.1702(d) nor identify any basis for additional time under CRC rule 8.108. No extension applies here.

Accordingly, the motion is untimely.

III.           Conclusion

Because the motion is untimely, the Court does not reach the parties’ remaining arguments, including those concerning the reasonableness of the hours claimed, the hourly rate, or Plaintiff’s request for statutory penalties.

The motion is DENIED.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: DEMURRER

III.           Legal Standard

A demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the pleading alone and will be sustained where the pleading is defective on its face.

A demurrer may be asserted on any one or more of the following grounds: (a) The court has no jurisdiction of the subject of the cause of action alleged in the pleading; (b) The person who filed the pleading does not have legal capacity to sue; (c) There is another action pending between the same parties on the same cause of action; (d) There is a defect or misjoinder of parties; (e) The pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action; (f) The pleading is uncertain (“uncertain” includes ambiguous and unintelligible); (g) In an action founded upon a contract, it cannot be ascertained from the pleading whether the contract is written, is oral, or is implied by conduct; (h) No certificate was filed as required by Code of Civil Procedure (“CCP”) section 411.35 or (i) by CCP section 411.36. CCP § 430.10. Accordingly, a demurrer tests the sufficiency of a pleading, and the grounds for a demurrer must appear on the face of the pleading or from judicially noticeable matters. (CCP § 430.30(a); Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) The face of the pleading includes attachments and incorporations by reference (Frantz v. Blackwell (1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 91, 94); it does not include inadmissible hearsay. (Day v. Sharp, (1975) 50 Cal.App.3d 904, 914.)

IV.           Analysis

Respondents demur to the petition on the grounds that (1) the FAP is barred by absolute quasi-judicial immunity; (2) the Court lacks subject matter jurisdiction; (3) the FAP is moot; (4) the FAP is barred by the Government Claims Act; and (5) the FAP fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action.

1.               Jurisdiction

The court must sustain a demurrer where the court does not have subject matter jurisdiction. (CCP § 430.10(a).) The principle of subject matter jurisdiction relates to the inherent authority of the court involved to deal with the case or matter before it. (Barry v. State Bar of California (2017) 2 Cal.5th 318, 324) Without subject matter jurisdiction, a trial court has no power to hear a case. (Ibid.)

Only this state’s Supreme Court and the courts of appeal may review proceedings in trial court. (CCP § 43.) “One department of the superior court cannot enjoin, restrain, or otherwise interfere with the judicial act of another department of the superior court. … A judgment rendered in one department of the superior court is binding on that matter upon all other departments until such time as the judgment is overturned.” (Glade v. Glade (1995) 38 Cal.App.4th 1441, 1450; Silverman v. Superior Court (1988) 203 Cal.App.3d 145, 151 [“Glade”].) “Even between superior courts of different counties, having coequal jurisdiction over a matter, the first court of equal dignity to assume and exercise jurisdiction over a matter acquires exclusive jurisdiction.” (Id. at 151.)

Petitioner and Respondents spend considerable effort arguing whether Glade, People v. Davis, (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 1353 (“Mandamus or prohibition may be issued only by a court to another court of inferior jurisdiction”), and other cases prevent this Department of this Court from ordering Respondents to comply with a ministerial duty related to their judicial functions. The Court need not reach that issue to decide the matter at hand.

Respondents refused to enter default in the Prior Action against RPIs as Petitioner requested. (FAP, ¶¶ 2-4, 6.) Petitioner, seeking remedy, applied to the court in the Prior Action for an order directing Respondents to enter default. (FAP, Ex. A.) That department heard and ruled on Petitioner’s application. (FAP, Ex. A.) This Department cannot affirm, reverse, or modify any judgment or order by that department. (CCP § 43; Glade, supra, 38 Cal.App.4th at 1450.)

Accordingly, this department lacks jurisdiction to hear the FAP.[1]

An OSC re: dismissal is set for August 20, 2026 at 9:30 a.m. If a judgment of dismissal has been entered by that date, the OSC will be taken off calendar.

III.           Discussion

In determining whether to issue a preliminary injunction, the trial court considers two factors: (1) the reasonable probability that the plaintiff will prevail on the merits at trial (Code Civ. Proc. [“CCP”] §526(a)(1)), and (2) a balancing of the “irreparable harm” that the plaintiff is likely to sustain if the injunction is denied as compared to the harm that the defendant is likely to suffer if the court grants a preliminary injunction. (CCP §526(a)(2); 14859 Moorpark Homeowner’s Assn. v. VRT Corp. (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 1396, 1402; Pillsbury, Madison & Sutro v. Schectman (1997) 55 Cal.App.4th 1279, 1283; Davenport v. Blue Cross of California (1997) 52 Cal.App.4th 435, 446; Abrams v. St. John’s Hospital (1994) 25 Cal.App.4th 628, 636.) Thus, a preliminary injunction may not issue without some showing of potential entitlement to such relief. (Doe v. Wilson (1997) 57 Cal.App.4th 296, 304.)

1.               Probability of Success

“The trial court's determination must be guided by a ‘mix’ of the potential-merit and interim-harm factors; the greater the plaintiff's showing on one, the less must be shown on the other to support an injunction.” (Butt v. State of California (1992) 4 Cal.4th 668, 678.) However, “[a] trial court may not grant a preliminary injunction, regardless of the balance of interim harm, unless there is some possibility that the plaintiff would ultimately prevail on the merits of the claim. (Ibid.)

Petitioner seeks relief under both Code of Civil Procedure (“CCP”) section 1085 (traditional mandamus) and CCP section 1094.5 (administrative mandamus). [3] (Pet., ¶¶ 81-101.) Petitioner’s principal theory is that its charter renewal was already approved “by operation of law” under California Code of Regulations, title 5, section 11966.4 (“section 11966.4”) because LAUSD failed to issue timely written factual findings within 60 days of receiving the renewal petition. (5 Cal. Code Regs. § 11966.4(c).) Petitioner contends that, as a result, LAUSD has a ministerial duty to recognize the renewal and set aside its May 12, 2026 denial. (Pet., ¶¶ 108-109; Mot. at 13-14.)

Education Code section 47607 governs the renewal of existing charter schools. (Today’s Fresh Start Charter School v. Inglewood Unified School Dist. (2018) 20 Cal.App.5th 276, 282-283 (“TFS (2018)”).) Subdivision (b) of section 47607 states that “renewals and material revisions of charters are governed by the standards and criteria described in Section 47605,” and also require a reasonably comprehensive description of new legal requirements. (Ed. Code, § 47607(b).)

The implementing regulation for charter renewals is section 11966.4. It provides, in relevant part:

If within 60 days of its receipt of a petition for renewal, a district governing board has not made a written factual finding as mandated by Education Code section 47605(b), the absence of written factual findings shall be deemed an approval of the petition for renewal. (5 Cal. Code Regs., § 11966.4(c).)

The parties’ disagreement centers on whether section 11966.4(c)’s 60-day deemed-approval rule remains valid after AB 1505’s amendment to Education Code section 47605(b). Before AB 1505, section 47605(b) required a hearing within 30 days and a decision within 60 days for new charter petitions. After AB 1505, section 47605(b) requires a hearing within 60 days and a decision within 90 days. (Ed. Code, § 47605(b).)

Section 11966.4 has not been amended to reflect AB 1505’s longer timeline for new charter petitions. LAUSD therefore argues that section 11966.4 is now invalid because section 47607(b) incorporates section 47605(b) “by reference,” and section 47605(b) is now inconsistent with a 60-day renewal deadline. Petitioner responds that section 47607(b) incorporates only the “standards and criteria” of section 47605, not its timelines, and that AB 1505 did not amend the portions of section 47605 incorporated into the renewal statute.

TFS (2018) squarely addresses the interaction between sections 47605 and 47607. The Court of Appeal explained that although the substantive “standards and criteria” of section 47605 apply to new petitions, renewals, and material revisions, “the procedure applicable to each is different.” (TFS (2018), supra, 20 Cal.App.5th at pp. 282-283.) Section 47605 supplies the procedure for new charter petitions; section 47607 supplies the procedure for renewals. (Ibid.)

Under that interpretation, the phrase “standards and criteria” in section 47607(b) refers to the substantive evaluation factors in section 47605, not its procedural timelines. [4] This reading is dispositive of the parties’ dispute. AB 1505’s changes to section 47605(b) altered only procedural deadlines for new petitions, not substantive evaluation criteria. Because procedural timelines are not incorporated into section 47607(b), the amendment does not displace section 11966.4’s 60-day renewal rule.

The operative facts are not in dispute. Petitioner submitted its renewal petition on March 5, 2026. (Parker Decl., ¶ 27; Willmott Decl., ¶ 18.) Under section 11966.4(c), LAUSD had until May 4, 2026 to issue written factual findings supporting denial. (§ 11966.4(c).) LAUSD did not issue written findings during that period and did not act until May 12, 2026. (Parker Decl., ¶¶ 19–20; Willmott Decl., ¶¶ 21-26.)

On this record, the renewal petition was deemed approved by operation of law as of May 5, 2026.

LAUSD raises the defense that Petitioner failed to exhaust administrative remedies before filing this action. LAUSD notes that Petitioner may appeal the denial of its charter renewal petition to the County Board of Education and, in fact, has already initiated such an appeal. The difficulty with this argument is that the County’s administrative appeal process addresses only whether the chartering authority’s May 12, 2026 denial was supported by substantial evidence. By contrast, Petitioner’s traditional mandamus claim does not seek review of LAUSD’s May 12 decision. Rather, Petitioner seeks a determination that the Charter renewal was already deemed approved as of May 5, 2026, under section 11966.4(c), because LAUSD failed to issue timely written factual findings.

Education Code section 47607.5 provides that when a district “does not grant a renewal to a charter school,” the school “may appeal the decision” pursuant to the procedures governing the denial of a new charter petition under section 47605(k). (Ed. Code, § 47607.5(a).) Under those procedures, the County Board reviews whether the district’s stated findings are supported by substantial evidence. (Ed. Code, § 47605(k); see also Ed. Code, § 47607(i) [County Board may reverse “if the county board of education determines that the findings made by the chartering authority … are not supported by substantial evidence.”].)

Here, LAUSD identifies no administrative avenue permitting the County Board to consider, or remedy, the question of whether renewal occurred automatically by operation of section 11966.4(c) before the May 12 denial. The County’s review authority is limited to evaluating the merits of LAUSD’s May 12 decision; it does not include the power to determine, or to enforce, a renewal that became effective earlier by operation of regulation. Because the administrative process cannot adjudicate Petitioner’s deemed-approval theory, Respondent has not shown that an administrative remedy exists that Petitioner failed to exhaust.

Accordingly, Petitioner has shown a probability of success on at least its traditional mandamus claim.

2.               Balance of Harms

The court must balance the irreparable harm that Petitioner is likely to sustain if the injunction is denied as compared to the harm that Respondent is likely to suffer if the court grants a preliminary injunction. (CCP §526(a)(2); 14859 Moorpark Homeowner’s Assn. v. VRT Corp., supra, 63 Cal.App.4th at 1402; Pillsbury, Madison & Sutro v. Schectman, supra, 55 Cal.App.4th at 1283.)

Petitioner has shown that without relief, it will not be able to operate for the 2026-2027 school year. (Parker Decl., ¶¶ 40.) Petitioner also provides declarations from parents, faculty, and staff showing these individuals will be harmed, including the students who would need to enroll in new schools (e.g. Phillips Decl., ¶¶ 4-6), students who would lose access to nutritional programs (Fahmy Decl., ¶ 7), parents who would have difficulty finding quality schools in which to enroll their children (e.g. Fargnoli Decl., ¶ 17; Lopez Decl., ¶ 7), and teachers and staff losing jobs (Fargnoli Decl., ¶ 24).

Respondent’s opposition raises no specific harm it will suffer to balance against Petitioner’s identified harm, only asserting without evidence that granting the injunction would be “significantly harmful and burdensome.” (Opp. at 17-19, emphasis in original.) Respondent vaguely alludes to costs it might incur in overseeing Petitioner but provides no evidence and does not show how such expenses outweigh the harm to the students, parents, and staff should an injunction not issue.

Accordingly, the balance of harms weighs in favor of Petitioner.

3.               Other Issues

The purpose of a preliminary injunction is to preserve the status quo pending final resolution upon a trial. (See Scaringe v. J.C.C. Enterprises, Inc. (1988) 205 Cal.App.3d 1536; Grothe v. Cortlandt Corp. (1992) 11 Cal.App.4th 1313, 1316; Major v. Miraverde Homeowners Assn. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 618, 623.) The status quo has been defined to mean the last actual peaceable, uncontested status which preceded the pending controversy. (Voorhies v. Greene (1983) 139 Cal.App.3d 989, 995, quoting United Railroads v. Superior Court (1916) 172 Cal. 80, 87; 14859 Moorpark Homeowner’s Assn. v. VRT Corp. (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 1396, 1402.)

LAUSD argues the motion does not preserve the status quo because Petitioner’s charter expired on June 30, 2026 and Petitioner now seeks to operate under a new authorizer. In Respondent’s view, the “status quo” is Petitioner’s loss of authorization. This frames the status quo as the legal posture existing at the moment the lawsuit was filed. But this is incorrect. For example, a foreclosure sale scheduled before suit is filed does not redefine the status quo as “sale imminent.” (See, e.g., Baypoint Mortgage Corp. v. Crest Premium Real Estate etc. Trust (1985) 168 Cal.App.3d 818, 825 [given drastic implications of foreclosure, courts frequently find granting preliminary injunctions to forestall the remedy while court considers a case testing whether it is justified under the facts and law].) Similarly, a charter expiration date does not redefine the pre-controversy status quo as “school closed.” LAUSD cites no authority to support that the Court must adopt such a view.

Here, the last uncontested status was Petitioner operating as a public charter school serving enrolled students. The requested relief seeks to maintain that condition during litigation. Preserving that status does not constitute an impermissible alteration of rights that were already disputed.

Respondent also contends that CUSD is a necessary party and that any injunction must be framed as an extension of Petitioner’s CUSD charter. This argument rests on the same misunderstanding of the status quo. Here, Petitioner seeks to prevent only LAUSD from enforcing its May 12 denial while this action proceeds. No relief is requested against CUSD, and LAUSD cites no authority requiring joinder of the former authorizer to preserve the pre-controversy operation of the school.

The District further asserts that the doctrine of laches bars relief because Petitioner waited too long to pursue renewal and appeal. The record does contain indicia that Petitioner contributed to the compressed timeline now presented to the Court. Petitioner stated as early as July 2025 that it intended to submit a renewal petition to LAUSD in September 2025 but did not do so until March 2026. The minutes of Petitioner’s board reflect authorization to submit a renewal petition on January 30, 2026 (Resp. RJN Ex. G), yet the petition was not submitted until several weeks later. Standing alone, that latter interval is not extreme, but it does reflect that Petitioner was not moving with any particular urgency despite knowing that its existing charter would expire at the end of June 2026 and that LAUSD had questioned Petitioner’s authority to operate the Vernon/Crenshaw site for years.

The existing record also shows that LAUSD contributed to the procedural misalignment. LAUSD declined to schedule Petitioner’s initial intake meeting, and its June 30, 2025 letter declared Petitioner’s request “moot” based on an erroneous belief about site availability, rather than issuing an approval or denial. These circumstances reasonably created confusion about the appropriate path forward. The renewal petition, once filed, triggered LAUSD’s obligation under section 11966.4(c), and LAUSD does not dispute that it acted on the petition outside the regulatory 60-day period.

Ultimately, the laches inquiry at the preliminary injunction stage is not whether Petitioner acted with perfect diligence over the preceding year, but whether any delay by Petitioner so prejudiced LAUSD that interim relief should be barred. On this record, although Petitioner’s timing plainly contributed to the present urgency, LAUSD has not shown that Petitioner’s delay was so unreasonable or that LAUSD suffered prejudice sufficient to warrant denial of provisional relief, particularly where LAUSD’s actions also contributed to the procedural posture now before the Court. Moreover, the fact of Petitioner’s delay does not alter LAUSD’s statutory obligation to act within 60 days once the renewal petition was submitted.

Because the purpose of the preliminary injunction is to preserve the last peaceable, uncontested status pending adjudication, and because laches has not been established on this record, the Court finds that Petitioner’s request for provisional relief is not barred by laches at this stage.

4.               Bond

In setting the bond, the court must assume that the preliminary injunction was wrongly issued. (Abba Rubber Co. v. Seaquist (1991) 235 Cal.App.3d 1, 15.) The attorney fees necessary to successfully procure a final decision dissolving the injunction also are damages that should be included in setting the bond. (Id. at 15-16.) While Abba reasoned that the plaintiff’s likelihood of prevailing is irrelevant to setting the bond, a more recent case disagreed, stating that the greater the likelihood of the plaintiff prevailing, the less likely the preliminary injunction will have been wrongly issued, and that is a relevant factor for setting the bond. (Oiye v. Fox (2012) 211 Cal.App.4th 1036, 1062.)

The parties have not addressed the issue of bond. The Court will discuss the matter with counsel at hearing.

IV.           Conclusion

Petitioner has shown a probability of success on its claim for traditional mandamus, and the balance of harms favors Petitioner. The preliminary injunction is therefore GRANTED.

Pending final resolution of this matter, Respondent, and all persons acting in concert with it, are restrained from giving legal, administrative, or practical effect to Respondent’s May 12, 2026 denial of Petitioner’s renewal petition in any manner that prevents, impairs, delays, or interferes with Petitioner’s ability to prepare for, open, operate, enroll scholars, employ and retain staff, prepare classrooms, provide student services, report attendance, or receive public funding for its public charter school site at 4476 Crenshaw Boulevard, Los Angeles, California for the 2026-2027 school year.
