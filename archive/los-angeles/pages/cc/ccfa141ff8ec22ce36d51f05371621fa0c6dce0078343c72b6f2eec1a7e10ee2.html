Case Number: 23SMCV03537    Hearing Date: August 4, 2026    Dept: N

TENTATIVE RULING

Plaintiff Goldrich Kest, LLC’s Motion to Strike Costs is GRANTED in part and DENIED in part. Defendant Homeland Insurance Company of New York’s costs are TAXED in the amount of $8,868.75. Defendant Homeland Insurance Company of New York is entitled to recover $28,304.27 in costs.

Plaintiff Goldrich Kest, LLC to give notice. 

REASONING

“[A] prevailing party is entitled as a matter of right to recover costs in any action or proceeding.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1032, subd. (b).) A prevailing party is entitled to recover only those costs “necessarily incurred in the case” by that party or on that party’s behalf in prosecuting or defending the action. (See Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1700(a); Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (c)(1).) The non-prevailing party may contest any items in the prevailing party’s memorandum of costs by a motion to strike or tax costs. (See Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1700(b); Code Civ. Proc., § 1034, subd. (a).) Under Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5, allowable costs include costs that were “reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation rather than merely convenient or beneficial to its preparation,” and the costs must be “reasonable in amount.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subds. (c)(2), (c)(3).)

“If the items appearing in a cost bill appear to be proper charges, the burden is on the party seeking to tax costs to show that they were not reasonable or necessary. On the other hand, if the items are properly objected to, they are put in issue and the burden of proof is on the party claiming them as costs.” (Ladas v. California State Automobile Association (1993) 19 Cal.App.4th 761, 774.) “Whether a cost item was reasonably necessary to the litigation presents a question of fact for the trial court,” but “because the right to costs is governed strictly by statute, a court has no discretion to award costs not statutorily authorized.” (Ibid.)

Plaintiff Goldrich Kest, LLC (“Plaintiff”) moves to strike Defendant Homeland Insurance Company of New York (“Defendant”)’s requests for costs of $20,286.00 relating to the discovery referee and private mediation in Defendant’s Memorandum of Costs filed on February 17, 2026. Plaintiff does not dispute here that Defendant is entitled to recover costs or that Defendant was the prevailing party; rather, Plaintiff argues that the Court appointed the discovery referee because the parties jointly proposed and agreed to such an appointment, and the Court already divided the payment obligations in accordance with this understanding, such that no further order or award is necessary. Defendant argues that the prevailing party’s share of referee costs is allowed as an item of costs. As to private mediation, Plaintiff argue that Defendant is not entitled to recover costs it incurred from a privately hired mediator, as it is a mere convenience to the parties. The Court addresses each cost in turn.

As to the discovery referee, Defendant relies on Winston Square Homeowner’s Association v. Centex West, Inc. (1989) 213 Cal.App.3d 282, a case in which the trial court had appointed a special master to control discovery over the objection of the party later seeking costs. (Id. at p. 292.) The appellate court concluded it was within the trial court’s discretion to award the fees paid to the special master as an item of costs. (Id. at p. 293.) The parties agree that this is a discretionary cost governed by Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5, subdivision (c)(4), which states that costs not expressly identified as allowable in the statute “may be allowed or denied in the court’s discretion” if the cost is reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation and reasonable in amount. The Court finds here that the discovery referee was necessary to the conduct of the litigation, specifically because the number of discovery disputes between the parties could not have been quickly addressed by this Court with its limited resources, i.e., the appointment of the referee was not the result of any bad conduct by any party, and the cost for the referee was reasonable.

As to the private mediation expenses, the parties again agree that this is a discretionary cost. Defendant argues that it was obligated to participate in mediation proceedings for this action even though Plaintiff’s properly calculated loss never would have reached Defendant’s coverage layer even if Plaintiff had been entitled to any award of damages in this lawsuit. Notably, Defendant provides no evidence that it was required to participate in private mediation. It follows that the Court cannot conclude that private mediator costs were reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation such that they should be recoverable. Thus, Defendant’s costs are TAXED in the amount of $8,868.75.

Accordingly, Plaintiff Goldrich Kest, LLC’s Motion to Strike Costs is GRANTED in part and DENIED in part. Defendant Homeland Insurance Company of New York’s costs are TAXED in the amount of $8,868.75. Defendant Homeland Insurance Company of New York is entitled to recover $28,304.27 in costs.

Case Number: 23SMCV04158    Hearing Date: August 4, 2026    Dept: N

TENTATIVE RULING

Plaintiff/Cross-Defendant Mike Khattab’s Motion to Compel Deposition Appearance of Defendant Ahmedshah Patail is GRANTED. The parties shall meet and confer regarding the deposition of Defendant Ahmedshah Patail to occur within  () days of entry of this order.

Plaintiff/Cross-Defendant Mike Khattab’s Request for Monetary Sanctions is GRANTED in the reduced amount of $1,575, payable by Defendant Ahmedshah Patail to Plaintiff/Cross-Defendant Mike Khattab and Plaintiff’s counsel within thirty (30) days of entry of this order.

Plaintiff/Cross-Defendant Mike Khattab to give notice. 

REASONING

Plaintiff/Cross-Defendant Mike Khattab (“Plaintiff”) moves the Court for an order compelling Defendant Ahmedshah Patail (“Defendant”) to appear for his deposition in accordance with the deposition notice served on her. Plaintiff represents that he served Defendant with a Notice of Deposition on May 22, 2026, for the deposition to occur on June 5, 2026, and Defendant failed to appear on that date. (Mot., Pomerantz Decl. ¶¶ 5, 9, Exs. C, G.) Plaintiff represents that subsequent attempts to reschedule the deposition have failed. (Mot., Pomerantz Decl. ¶¶ 10-15.) Defendant has not filed an opposition to the motion.

“If, after service of a deposition notice, a party to the action . . . , without having served a valid objection under Section 2025.410, fails to appear for examination, or to proceed with it, or to produce for inspection any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing described in the deposition notice, the party giving the notice may move for an order compelling the deponent’s attendance and testimony, and the production for inspection of any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing described in the deposition notice.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subd. (a).) A motion to compel a party’s deposition “shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040, or, when the deponent fails to attend the deposition and produce the documents, electronically stored information, or things described in the deposition notice, by a declaration stating that the petitioner has contacted the deponent to inquire about the nonappearance.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subd. (b)(2).) Code of Civil Procedure section 2016.040 describes that “[a] meet and confer declaration in support of a motion shall state facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt at an informal resolution of each issue presented by the motion.”

Plaintiff is entitled to take Defendant’s deposition, and Plaintiff provides evidence that Defendant failed to appear for examination without having served any valid objection to the notice of deposition, and no alternative dates have been provided. Accordingly, Plaintiff/Cross-Defendant Mike Khattab’s Motion to Compel Deposition Appearance of Defendant Ahmedshah Patail is GRANTED. The parties shall meet and confer regarding the deposition of Defendant Ahmedshah Patail to occur within () days of entry of this order.

Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.450, subdivision (g)(1), provides that if a motion to compel a deposition is granted, the Court shall impose monetary sanctions “in favor of the party who noticed the deposition and against the deponent or the party with whom the deponent is affiliated, unless the court finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” The Court finds that monetary sanctions are warranted here, as any issues with scheduling were due to Defendant’s conduct alone, rather than that of former defense counsel. However, the Court reduces the monetary sanctions to $1,575, representing one-and-a-half hours of attorney time relating to the failure to appear for deposition and three hours preparing the present motion and appearing at the hearing on the same, at a reduced hourly rate of $350 per hour. Thus, Plaintiff/Cross-Defendant Mike Khattab’s Request for Monetary Sanctions is GRANTED in the reduced amount of $1,575, payable by Defendant Ahmedshah Patail to Plaintiff/Cross-Defendant Mike Khattab and Plaintiff’s counsel within thirty (30) days of entry of this order.

Case Number: 25SMCV06527    Hearing Date: August 4, 2026    Dept: N

TENTATIVE RULING

Plaintiffs Deborah Hodges and Eve Perez’s Application of David E. Gevertz to Appear as Counsel Pro Hac Vice is GRANTED.

Plaintiffs Deborah Hodges and Eve Perez’s Application of Kyle D. Brumbaught to Appear as Counsel Pro Hac Vice is GRANTED.

Defendant High Rise Financial LLC’s Motion for Reconsideration of Order Denying Motion to Dismiss or Stay for Forum Non Conveniens is DENIED.

Moving parties to give notice.

REASONING

Plaintiffs Deborah Hodges and Eve Perez’s Application of David E. Gevertz to Appear as Counsel Pro Hac Vice and Plaintiffs Deborah Hodges and Eve Perez’s Application of Kyle D. Brumbaught to Appear as Counsel Pro Hac Vice
Counsel who are not active members of the California State Bar and have not been granted permission to appear pro hac vice are prohibited from representing a party in California courts. (Gentis v. Safeguard Bus. Systems, Inc. (1998) 60 Cal.App.4th 1294, 1308.) Counsel licensed in another state may, in the court’s discretion, be permitted to appear as counsel pro hac vice if counsel is associated with an attorney of record who is an active member of the California bar. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(a).) Appearance pro hac vice is a privilege and not a right under the United States Constitution. (Leis v. Flynt (1979) 439 U.S. 438, 441.) 

An application to appear in California as counsel pro hac vice must state: 

(1) The applicant’s residence and office address;
(2) The courts to which the applicant has been admitted to practice and the dates of admission;
(3) That the applicant is a member in good standing in those courts;
(4) That the applicant is not currently suspended or disbarred in any court;
(5) The title of court and cause in which the applicant has filed an application to appear as counsel pro hac vice in this state in the preceding two years, the date of each application, and whether or not it was granted; and
(6) The name, address, and telephone number of the active member of the State Bar of California who is attorney of record.

(Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(d).) 

David E. Gevertz and Kyle D. Brumbaugh represent that they are members in good standing of all bars and with all courts in which they are admitted to practice, Kyle D. Brumbaugh has not appeared in any other actions in California in the preceding two years, and while David E. Gevertz has appeared in one other action in the preceding two years, this is not so many applications as to indicate that he has exceeded what is proper under pro hac vice regulations. They have met the requirements of California Rules of Court, rule 9.40(d), they appear qualified and ready to appear pro hac vice, and there are no other facts or circumstances to show that their appearance in this action would cause a significant disruption of orderly justice. Accordingly, Plaintiffs Deborah Hodges and Eve Perez’s Application of David E. Gevertz to Appear as Counsel Pro Hac Vice and Plaintiffs Deborah Hodges and Eve Perez’s Application of Kyle D. Brumbaught to Appear as Counsel Pro Hac Vice are GRANTED.

Defendant High Rise Financial LLC’s Motion for Reconsideration of Order Denying Motion to Dismiss or Stay for Forum Non Conveniens
Defendant High Rise Financial LLC (“Defendant”) moves the Court for an order reconsidering the Court’s April 8, 2026 order denying Defendant’s Motion to Dismiss or Stay Action for Forum Non Conveniens. Defendant argues that new facts support reconsidering the order; specifically, Defendant points to a filing by Plaintiff Eve Perez in a pleading in United States District Court for the Eastern District of Tennessee stating that she “does not oppose a stay in favor of arbitration” of her dispute with Defendant, thereby negating the Court’s basis for denying the motion, as there will not be two nearly identical trials in two different states because Perez cannot and will not litigate in any courtroom.

Code of Civil Procedure section 1008, subdivision (a), allows a Court to reconsider a prior order as follows:

When an application for an order has been made to a judge, or to a court, and refused in whole or in part, or granted, or granted conditionally, or on terms, any party affected by the order may, within 10 days after service upon the party of written notice of entry of the order and based upon new or different facts, circumstances, or law, make application to the same judge or court that made the order, to reconsider the matter and modify, amend, or revoke the prior order. The party making the application shall state by affidavit what application was made before, when and to what judge, what order or decisions were made, and what new or different facts, circumstances, or law are claimed to be shown.

In the Court’s order denying Defendant’s motion to dismiss or stay this action for forum non conveniens reasons, the Court considered whether the alternate forum, here, Kentucky or Tennessee, was a suitable place for trial, and if it was, whether the private interests of the litigations and the interests of the public in retaining the action for trial in California. Hodges is a resident of Kentucky, and Perez is a resident of Tennessee, and the agreement between each plaintiff and Defendant stated that the parties consent to jurisdiction in Kentucky or Tennessee, respectively. However, the Court noted that moving this action to Kentucky or Tennessee would require each other plaintiff to submit to jurisdiction in another states, which they had not indicated they would not do, and this weighed heavily against litigating this action outside of California, as Plaintiffs’ claims would be split, thus requiring two nearly identical trials in two different states. Witnesses would have to participate in two actions, which is of particular concern where some witnesses may not be willing to participate and must be compelled to do so, particularly where the witnesses may be based in California, as Defendant is a California resident. Moreover, Plaintiffs’ claims asked the trier of fact to determine whether Defendant violated California law, and the communities of potential jurors in Kentucky and Tennessee have little concern over such a question. For these reasons, the Court found that both the private and public interest factors weighed in favor of litigating this action in California, and it was not clear that there is a single state that could hear this action given the jurisdictional issues.

Defendant now argues that Perez’s statement in a Tennessee filing that she did not oppose a stay of that action in favor of arbitration eliminates the Court’s “sole basis for denying the original motion,” that basis being that there would be two nearly identical trials in two different states if the Court were to grant Defendant’s motion to dismiss. However, it is not clear to the Court why an agreement to arbitrate should cause the Court to reconsider its prior order. Put simply, the Court did not consider any arbitration agreement in ruling on the motion to dismiss or stay, and it was Defendant’s responsibility to ask the Court to consider the effects of arbitration or an arbitration agreement if it would have been dispositive. The arbitration agreement is not new even if Perez’s statement is, and it is well established that “[the burden under [Code of Civil Procedure] section 1008 is comparable to that of a party seeking a new trial on the ground of newly discovered evidence: the information must be such that the moving party could not, with reasonable diligence, have discovered or produced it at the trial.” (New York Times Co. v. Superior Court (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 206, 212-213.) Defendant simply chose not to raise the issue of arbitration with the prior motion. A motion for reconsideration is not a chance to raise a new argument after other arguments failed, thereby giving a party a second bite at the proverbial apple. Moreover, this does not change the Court’s conclusion that there was no single state that could hear this action, in arbitration or otherwise, and private and public interest factors weighed in favor of litigating this action in California. The Court finds no reason to reconsider its prior order. Accordingly, Defendant High Rise Financial LLC’s Motion for Reconsideration of Order Denying Motion to Dismiss or Stay for Forum Non Conveniens is DENIED.
