DEPARTMENT 529 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 20STCV46605    Hearing Date: June 30, 2026    Dept: 529

ALANNA FOUTZ, et al. vs AFTON PROPERTIES, INC. et al.

 

motion to tax defendants’ cost memorandum

 

Date of Hearing:        June 30, 2026                                     Trial Date:       N/A   

Department:              529                                                     Case No.:        20STCV46605

 

Moving Party:            Plaintiff Ed Baran

Responding Party:     Defendants Afton Properties, Inc., Harold Herskowitz, and My Management Co., Inc.

 

 

[Tentative] Ruling

 

Plaintiff Ed Baran’s Motion to Tax Defendants’ Memorandum of Costs is GRANTED IN PART.

 

discussion

 

Plaintiff Ed Baran moves this court for an order taxing and/or striking items from Defendants’ Memorandum of Costs filed February 17, 2026 in the total amount of $74,438.59. Plaintiff makes the motion on the grounds Defendants Harold Herskowitz and My Management Co., Inc. are not prevailing parties; the court did not order a court reporter for trial; expert witness fees are impermissible because the court denied Defendants' claim for costs under Code of Civil Procedure section 998; deposition costs for settled co-plaintiffs Maria McKenna, John Bassett, Lauren Mintzer, Greg Clark, and Josh Petker are improper as each settled their claims and waived any entitlement to costs; and certain electronic filing fees, remote appearance costs, and other "Other" costs were incurred for all defendants collectively, and any allowable amount must be apportioned to reflect only legitimate costs attributable to the sole prevailing defendant, Afton Properties, Inc.

 

“In ruling upon a motion to tax costs, the trial court’s first determination is whether the statute expressly allows the particular item and whether it appears proper on its face.¿If so, the burden is on the objecting party to show the costs to be unnecessary or unreasonable.” (Foothill De Anza Cmty. College Dist. v. Emerich (2007) 158 Cal.App.4th 11, 2930.) “Whether a cost item was reasonably necessary to the litigation presents a question of fact for the trial court.” (Ladas v. Cal. State Auto. Assn. (1993) 19 Cal.App.4th 761, 774.) 

 

Prevailing Parties

 

Plaintiff Baran first argues that Defendants Herskowitz and My Management Co., Inc. are not prevailing parties and therefore not entitled to costs.

 

The term prevailing party is defined by statute to include: The party with a net monetary recovery; a defendant who is dismissed from the action; a defendant where neither plaintiff nor defendant recovers anything; and a defendant as against those plaintiffs who do not recover any relief against that defendant.  (CCP § 1032(a)(4).) 

 

Plaintiff argues as to Defendant Herskowitz, the court’s Fee Order expressly held that Herskowitz is not the prevailing party. The jury found Herskowitz liable for breach of the warranty of habitability, awarded $2,000 in damages against him, and abated approximately $50,000 in back rent that Herskowitz was owed. Because plaintiff obtained a “net monetary recovery” of roughly $52,000 from Herskowitz, Herskowitz may claim no costs against Ed Baran. As to My Management Co., Inc., although the jury assessed zero dollars in separate damages against My Management—apportioning 100% of damages to Herskowitz—the jury nonetheless found My Management liable. A defendant who is adjudicated liable on a cause of action has not obtained a favorable judgment within the meaning of section 1032(a)(4). (See, e.g., Michell v. Olick (19960 49 Cal. App. 4th 1194, 1198.)

 

Defendants do not address this contention in their opposition.

 

The court agrees Defendants Herskowitz and My Management Co., Inc. are not entitled to costs. The court has previously found that Afton was the prevailing party and awarded it $500 in attorney fees.  The court, therefore, must separately consider Afton’s request for costs.  (The court does not disturb its earlier ruling that none of the defendants are entitled to costs or fees under Section 998.) 

 

California courts have consistently held that a prevailing party may only recover costs actually incurred by that party or on that party's behalf in prosecuting or defending the action. (Fennessy v. Deleuw-Cather Corp. (1990) 218 Cal.App.3d 1192.) Accordingly, the court “’must apportion the costs between the parties’ [based on the reason the costs were incurred and whether they were reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation by the jointly represented party who prevailed].’ [Citations.]” (Charton v. Harkey (2016) 247 Cal.App.4th 730, 744.) “In allocating costs between jointly represented parties, however, the trial court may not make an across-the-board reduction based on the number of jointly represented parties because such an allocation fails to consider the necessity or reasonableness of the costs as required by section 1033.5, subdivision (c).” (Id. at p. 744-745.)

 

Here, based on the declaration and invoices provided, the interests of the parties completely aligned and all the work done was reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation as to Defendant Afton.

 

Reporter Fees

 

Plaintiff moves to tax the entire $31,497.50 claimed for court reporter fees on the grounds the court reporter was not authorized by this court.

 

Court reporter fees are recoverable when established by statute.¿(CCP § 1033.5(a)(11).) Plaintiff argues the court did not appoint or order a court reporter for the trial. The court's own Minute Order for January 20, 2026 reflects "CSR: None[]" and the trial transcript confirms that no official court reporter was present. Instead, Defendants chose to retain a private court reporting service, Magna Legal Services, at their own expense and for their own convenience. Accordingly, such private court reporter fees are not recoverable under section 1033.5(a)(9) or (11) because they are not “established by statute.”

 

In opposition, Defendants argues they required trial transcripts to adequately prepare for witnesses and to prepare for closing arguments throughout the course of trial and in order to preserve the trial record. Accordingly, Defendants did in fact incur reasonable court reporter fees in the amount of $31,497.50.

 

The court did not appoint or order a court reporter in this case.  Accordingly, the court taxes the entirety of the court reporter fees sought, or $31,497.50. 

 

Expert Fees

 

Plaintiff moves to tax the $23,675 in expert witness fees because the court denied Defendants’ 998 Offer.

 

Expert fees are generally not allowable as costs except when expressly authorized by law. (CCP § 1033.5(b)(1).) However, expert fees are recoverable when a defendant conveys a settlement offer, it is rejected, and the plaintiff fails to obtain a more favorable judgment or award, the court has discretion to require the plaintiff to cover those costs. (CCP § 998(c)(1).)

 

Plaintiff argues the court has already ruled, in its January 20, 2026 Fee Order, that Defendants are not entitled to section 998 costs. Plaintiff contends the court applied the analysis from Kahn v. The Dewey Group (2015) 240 Cal. App. 4th 227, holding that the joint, undifferentiated offer to compromise submitted by all three defendants for $50,000 must be compared to the total judgment against all offering defendants collectively, not against any single defendant in isolation. Because Plaintiff's total recovery against Herskowitz and Afton together was approximately $52,000—exceeding the $50,000 offer—the judgment was more favorable to Plaintiff than the offer and no section 998 costs are owed.

 

In opposition, Defendants argue because Plaintiff was unable to obtain a more favorable judgment at trial, Defendants should be awarded their post-offer costs, including their expert witness fees, in the amount of $23,675. (Exh. E.)

 

The court agrees Defendants are not entitled to expert fees. The court has expressly found “neither of the defendants is entitled to an award of costs under Section 998 because the total judgment against Herskowitz and Afton Property was $52,000 (even though Afton was not required to pay any of it).” (January 20, 2026 Minute Order.)  Plaintiff has obtained a more favorable judgment.

 

Based on the foregoing, the court strikes the $23,675.00 in expert fees.

 

Deposition Costs

 

Plaintiff seeks to tax the deposition costs of settled co-Plaintiffs. Plaintiff argues Defendants' cost worksheet includes deposition costs for multiple individuals who are not the remaining plaintiff in this action—specifically, costs for depositions of Maria McKenna ($586.70), John Bassett ($167.64), Lauren Mintzer ($175.70), Greg Clark ($185.20), and Josh Petker ($203.90). Plaintiff contends the costs are not recoverable for two reasons -- each settling plaintiff's settlement extinguished the claims and obligations running between that plaintiff and Defendants and the depositions were not "reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation as they were taken in connection with those individuals' personal claims (claims that never went to trial against Ed Baran’s trial).

 

In opposition, Defendants argue the costs are recoverable as each of these plaintiffs testified at trial as ordinary witnesses. Moreover, Defendants attended their depositions and incurred costs related to their depositions.

 

The court finds Defendants are entitled to costs. Under Nelson v. Anderson (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 111, 129, the court noted the non-settling plaintiff could not benefit from cost-waiver provisions in settlement agreements to which she was not a party, even when those agreements include broad mutual releases. Instead, the deposition costs must still meet the statutory requirements of being actually incurred, reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation at the time they were taken. At the time the depositions were noticed of the settling Plaintiffs, Afton could not have known which ones would settle and which ones would have proceeded to trial.

 

Accordingly, the court denies the motion to tax deposition costs. 

 

Remaining Costs

 

Plaintiff argues the remaining cost items must be apportioned, and to the extent attributable to non-prevailing Defendants, taxed.

 

i.                    Jury Fees

 

Plaintiff first argues the jury fees ($1,398.40) should be taxed on the grounds the $1,398.40 claimed reflects fees advanced on behalf of all defendants collectively. Plaintiff contends given that Afton's role in the litigation was secondary and that Herskowitz was the primary defendant responsible for the judgment, it would be inequitable to hold Plaintiff responsible for the entirety of jury fees attributable to Herskowitz's defense.

 

The court agrees the jury fees should be taxed as each Defendant had to pay for the jury fees. (See CCP §631.) Accordingly, the court strikes $932.26.

 

ii.                  Models, Enlargements and Photocopies of Exhibits

 

Next, Plaintiff argues the $4,950.23 in “preparation of exhibit binders” must be taxed as Defendants have made no showing that these exhibits were reasonably necessary or that they related to Afton's defense specifically as opposed to Herskowitz's defense. In opposition, Defendants argue these exhibits (Exh. G) were in preparation for trial. Exhibit G is one invoice.  The court finds that the proper apportionment of these costs is again to allocate one third of the cost to the Afton defendant, striking $3267.15.

 

iii.                Fees for electronic filing or service

 

As for the electronic filing fees, Plaintiff argues many of the filings relate to all three defendants—including the Motion for Attorneys' Fees filed after trial. Accordingly, because it is impossible to apportion these fees, the court denies the motion to tax costs. 

 

iv.                Other

 

As for the Other Costs, the "Other" category encompasses "Remote appearance, Subpoena Records, Mediation, Document Production" totaling $6,761.15. Plaintiff argues mediation costs are expressly disallowed under section 1033.5(b)(8). Moreover, remote appearance fees are permissible only if those appearances were ordered or required by the court. Plaintiff contends Defendants have provided no itemization of this catch-all category, making it impossible to evaluate which specific items (if any) are legitimate. The entire $6,761.15 should be disallowed unless Defendants provide a detailed, item-by-item breakdown demonstrating that each expense was court-ordered or otherwise allowable, and attributable to Afton Properties' defense of Baran's specific claims.

 

In opposition, Defendants provide copies of the invoices pertaining to the remote appearance, subpoenaing records, mediation, and document production that were disclosed on section 15 of Defendants’ Memorandum of Costs worksheet under “other.” (See Exh. I.)

 

Regarding mediation, “mediation fees incurred for mediation that was not ordered by the court are not categorically nonrecoverable as ‘not reasonably necessary to the conduct of litigation.’ The question whether mediation fees should be awarded as costs in a particular matter must be determined based on the facts and circumstances of the particular action.” (Berkeley Cement, Inc. v. Regents of University of California (2019) 30 Cal.App.5th 1133, 1143.) In at least one case, the court has held parties who wish to preserve the right to seek cost recovery of shared mediation fees must expressly say so in their agreement. (See Anthony v. Li (2020) 47 Cal.App.5th 816, 825.) There is nothing before the court that the parties reserved the right to later seek recovery after dividing the mediators fees. The court taxes $3,925.00.

 

As for remote appearances, remote appearance fees are not specifically enumerated as either allowable or disallowable costs under Section 1033.5. Defendant has not provided any reason for the remote appearance. Accordingly, the court strikes $15.00. Looking at the subpoena records and document production invoices, Defendants provide no real explanation. The court also strikes these costs.

 

Accordingly, the court strikes $6,761.15 in Other Costs.

 

$500 Attorney Fees

 

The court has already ruled on the request for attorney fees and strikes the request from the Memorandum of Costs.

 

Accordingly, the motion to tax Defendants’ Cost Memorandum is GRANTED IN PART.


Case Number: 22STCV33422    Hearing Date: June 30, 2026    Dept: 529

SAJDAH GOLDE V. NETFLIX, INC.

 

PLAINTIFF’S MOTION TO VACATE OR ALTER ARBITRATION AWARD

 

Date of Hearing:         June 30, 2026                                                  Trial Date:       N/A 

Department:                529                                                                  Case No.:22STCV33422

 

Moving Party:             Plaintiff Sajdah Golde  

Responding Party:       Netflix, Inc.  

 

BACKGROUND

 

This is a wrongful termination action, which was compelled to arbitration on May 11, 2023.   The arbitration resulted in an award in favor of defendant on June 2, 2025.   On May 21, 2026, plaintiff filed the instant motion to vacate or alter the award. 

 

[TENTATIVE] RULING:

 

 The motion to alter or vacate the arbitrator’s decision is denied. 

 

DISCUSSION

 

The grounds for judicial review of an arbitration award are limited by the California Arbitration Act (Code Civ. Proc., § 1280 et seq.) and the Federal Arbitration Act (9 U.S.C. § 10 et seq.), which authorize a court to vacate an award only if it was (1) procured by corruption, fraud, or undue means; (2) issued by a corrupt arbitrator; (3) affected by prejudicial misconduct on the part of the arbitrator; or (4) in excess of the arbitrator's powers. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1286.2, subd. (a); 9 U.S.C. § 10(a).) An award may be corrected for (1) evident miscalculation or mistake; (2) issuance in excess of the arbitrator's powers; or (3) imperfection in the form. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1286.6; 9 U.S.C. § 11.)  In addition, Section 1286.2(a)(5) permits vacatur where a party’s rights were “substantially prejudiced by the refusal of the arbitrators to postpone the hearing upon sufficient cause being shown therefor or by the refusal of the arbitrators to hear evidence material to the controversy.”

 

“Generally, courts cannot review arbitration awards for errors of fact or law, even when those errors appear on the face of the award or cause substantial injustice to the parties.” [Citation omitted.] “This is true even where . . . an arbitration agreement requires an arbitrator to rule on the basis of relevant law, rather than on principles of equity and justice. (Cable Connection Inc. v. Direct TV, Inc. (2008) 44 Cal.4th 1334, 1360] … [‘A provision requiring arbitrators to apply the law leaves open the possibility that they are empowered to apply it “wrongly as well as rightly.”’]; see City of Richmond v. Service Employees Intern. Union, Local 1021 (2010) 189 Cal.App.4th 663, 669, fn. 1, 118 Cal.Rptr.3d 315 [‘The arbitration provision here, reciting generally that the arbitrator “shall ... make no decisions in violation of existing law’ is a standard arbitration provision that does not provide for [judicial] review.”].)’” (Richey v. AutoNation, Inc. (2015) 60 Cal. 4th 909, 916, 341 P.3d 438, 442.)

 

 

In her motion to set aside the arbitrators’ award, which was filed on May 21, 2026, Golde contends that the matter went to final disposition by summary judgment before she had been able to obtain critical comparator information or to take the deposition of a witness who became ill. 

 

Defendant takes the position that the motion is untimely, arguing that Golde’s deadline to vacate the Final Award lapsed in September 2025. “A petition to vacate an award or to correct an award shall be served and filed not later than 100 days after the date of the service of a signed copy of the award on the petitioner.” (Cal. Civ. Proc. Code § 1288; Law Finance Group, LLC v. Key (2023) 14 Cal. 5th 932, 948.) Here, the Final Award was issued and served on the Parties on June 2, 2025 through JAMS Access, JAMS’s electronic filing system. (Su Decl. ¶ 29.) From the email sent later that day by Golde to JAMS, it is apparent that Golde received the decision on that date.   (See Su Decl, Exh. 17.)  Accordingly, Golde’s deadline to move to vacate the Final Award was September 10, 2025.  Therefore, because Golde’s deadline has passed, the Court must confirm the award. (See Darby v. Sisyphian, LLC (2023) 87 Cal. App. 5th 1100, 1114 (2023), disapproved on other grounds by L. Fin. Grp., LLC v. Key, 14 Cal. 5th 932 (2023); Cal. Civ. Proc. Code § 1286.)

 

Assuming arguendo that the motion is nonetheless timely, the court finds that plaintiff has not met the heavy burden of establishing a basis to vacate the award under Section 1286.  Plaintiff has not presented any evidence to indicate that the arbitrator was corrupt, acted in excess of their powers, or engaged in prejudicial misconduct.  With respect to her claim that she was prevented from presenting critical evidence, plaintiff relies only on her own declaration.  She does not explain what evidence witness Lang would have provided that could not have been provided by other witnesses or plaintiff herself.   Golde’s bare boned declaration merely states that she sought discovery from Lang and other witnesses about comparator information, and when she learned that Lang was on a medical leave, she requested a continuance of deadlines or to exclude Lang’s testimony.   Critically, she does not present any evidence as to what information Lang might have supplied or what showing she made before the arbitrator to justify the continuance.   She therefore has not met her burden of establishing that the arbitrator denied her material evidence.  (Royal Alliance Assoc. Inc.v. Liebhaber (2016) 2 Cal. App. 5th 1092, 1107; SWAB Financial LLC v. E*Trade Securities (2007) 150 Cal. App. 4th 1181, 1198-2000.)

 

Moreover, the defendant has presented substantial evidence to support its position that the arbitrator gave plaintiff a fair opportunity to engage in discovery and to oppose the motion for summary judgment; it was plaintiff who failed to marshal the evidence needed to oppose that motion.   The court notes the following evidence presented by defendant: The Arbitrator gave each side five depositions and twice extended the deposition deadline, from January 15 to February 28 to March 7, 2025. (Su Decl. ¶¶ 8, 24, Exs. 5, 14.) Netflix produced all five witnesses Golde noticed and, at her request, lined them up for a single day on March 4, 2025. (Id. ¶¶ 14, 15, Ex. 8.) When Lang took medical leave, Netflix disclosed it at once and offered to produce her after she returned. (Id. ¶ 20, Ex. 8.) Golde’s response was to cancel all five depositions. (Id. ¶¶ 21, 25, Exs. 8, 15.) The Arbitrator did not refuse a postponement on “sufficient cause.” In Order No. 10, he declined to continue every deadline in a case that had been pending since October 2023, after he had already extended the deposition cutoff twice at Golde’s request.  The

Arbitrator preserved Golde’s opportunity to take testimony from Lang, stating that “[i]f

Ms. Lang has recovered by the time of the arbitration hearing, Ms. Golde may seek to call her as a witness.” (Su Decl. ¶ 24, Ex. 14.)  Golde cancelled four confirmed depositions of other Netflix witnesses because she was “interested in receiving all witness testimonies on the same date.” (Id. ¶ 25, Ex. 15.) Golde did not object to the scheduling of the motion for summary judgment.   She

declined to oppose Netflix’s request for leave to file the motion, and the Arbitrator granted that unopposed request in Order No. 6. Although she had the ability to file an opposition, she elected to file one without any declarations or exhibits and without a response to Netflix’s separate statement. The court agrees that choosing to resolve the case on an authorized motion, after

briefing and a hearing, is a procedural call committed to the arbitrator and beyond review.

(Moncharsh v. Heily & Blasé (1992) 3 Cal. 4th 1,11; SWAB, 150 Cal. App. 4th at 1200.)

 

The remainder of her arguments., which challenge the reasoning of the arbitrator’s award, are also unavailing.  Golde cannot challenge the sufficiency of the evidence and the Arbitrator’s reasoning, as those issues are not subject to judicial review on the grounds raised by plaintiff.  A court will not review the sufficiency of the evidence, the validity of the arbitrator’s reasoning, or alleged errors of law. (Moncharsh, 3 Cal. 4th at 11.) “The arbitrator’s decision should be the end, not the beginning, of the dispute.” (Id. at 10; see also Pearson Dental Supplies v. Superior Court

(2010) 48 Cal.4th 665, 669 (emphasizing the limited nature of judicial review of contractual

arbitration awards).

 


Case Number: 24STCV03808    Hearing Date: June 30, 2026    Dept: 529

DENNIS DEVAUGHT v. TURNING POINT NETWORK, INC.

 

MOTIONS TO COMPEL FURTHER DISCOVERY

 

Date of Hearing:        June 30, 2026                         Trial Date:       November 9, 2026

Department:              529                                         Case No.:        24STCV03808

 

Moving Party:            Plaintiffs

Responding Party:     Defendant Turning Point Network, LLC (No opposition received)     

 

BACKGROUND

 

On  February 14, 2024, plaintiff Dennis Devaughn and others filed a complaint against Defendant Turning Point Network, LLC, and several individuals for breach of their partnership agreement.  On March 20, 2024, the other individual partners filed a cross-complaint against plaintiffs for injunctive and declaratory relief. 

 

Ruling

 

I.                    Plaintiffs’ Motion to Compel Further Responses to Plaintiffs’ Request for Production (Set One) is GRANTED in part.

II.                  Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel Further Responses to Plaintiffs’ Requests for Production (Set Two) is GRANTED in part.

III.                Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel Initial Disclosures is GRANTED. 

IV.                

DISCUSSION

 

I.                    PLAINTIFFS’ MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES TO PLAINTIFFS’ REQUESTS FOR PRODUCTION

 

Plaintiffs move for further responses to Plaintiffs’ First Set of Requests for Production, specifically Nos. 1, 2, 12, 15, 18, 19-21, 25-28.  The court has received no opposition.   The court rules as follows on this unopposed motion:

 

Requests Nos. 1 and 2:  These requests seek tax returns filed by the corporation.  The court finds such returns privileged and not discoverable.   However, the court agrees that any K-1s issued by the corporation should be produced and orders those documents produced within 30 days.  

 

Request No. 12.  The court finds the requested materials relevant and subject to discovery.  Defendant is ordered to provide further responses, without objection, and to produce the requested documents within 30 days. 

 

Request No. 15.  The court finds defendant’s response confusing.  If the company’s position is that it has no responsive documents (e.g., financial statements of any kind), it should state so clearly.  Further responses without objection are ordered within 30 days.  

 

Request Nos. 18 and 19.   The court finds the documents sought to be relevant or likely to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence and orders further responses without objection, as well as all responsive documents within 30 days.

 

Request Nos. 20-21.  The court agrees that the profits distributed to the individual defendants are relevant and discoverable.  Defendant is ordered to produce all responsive documents within 30 days without objection.

 

Request Nos. 25-28.   The court agrees that the governance documents for these entities are relevant and discoverable.  Defendant is ordered to produce all responsive documents without objection within 30 days. 

 

Plaintiffs’ request for sanctions is denied as the court finds defendants’ position to be reasonably justified at least with respect to the tax returns. 

 

II.                  PLAINITFF’S MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONES TO PLAINTIFFS’ REQUEST FOR PRODUCTION (SET 2)  

 

Plaintiffs move to compel further responses to their Request for Production (Set 2), specifically Requests Nos. 71, 72, 75 and 76.  The court has received no opposition.   The court rules as follows on this unopposed motion: 

 

With respect to Requests Nos. 71 and 72, they appear to seek the same privileged tax returns as were sought in the first set of requests for production.  As stated above, the court finds that these documents are privileged, with the exception of any K-1 forms (and attachments thereto) that were issued by the company.  Further responses are required, producing the K-1 forms within 30 days, if any exist.   With respect to Requests Nos. 75 and 76, the court finds the documents sought are relevant and discoverable.  Defendants are ordered to provide further responses without objection, as well as all responsive documents within 30 days.

 

Plaintiffs’ request for sanctions is denied as the court finds defendants’ position to be reasonably justified at least with respect to the tax returns. 

 

III.                MOTION TO COMPEL INITIAL DISCLOSURES PURSUANT TO C.C.P. SECTION 2016.090

 

Plaintiffs served a timely request for initial disclosures pursuant to C.C.P. Section 2016.090.  Provided that plaintiffs themselves complied with the section’s mutual disclosure requirements, plaintiffs are entitled to verified responses from defendants. If those have not been provided, defendants are ordered to provide complete verified responses, and all responsive documents, as required by the code, within 30 days.   Further, the court orders a sanction of $1000 against defense counsel and the entity defendant, jointly and severally, payable within 30 days, for failure to provide the required disclosures without substantial justification.

 

 


Case Number: 25STCV16604    Hearing Date: June 30, 2026    Dept: 529

DEAN D CARSON vs GENERAL MOTORS, LLC

 

defendant general motors llc’s motion for compliance pursuant to ccp § 871.26 and request for sanctions

 

Date of Hearing:       June 30, 2026                                                  Trial Date:       None set

Department:             529                                                                   Case No.:        25STCV16604

 

Moving Party:            Defendant General Motors LLC

Responding Party:     Plaintiff Deann D Carson

 

BACKGROUND

 

This is a lemon law action. On June 9, 2025, Plaintiff Deann D Carson filed a complaint against General Motors LLC. On November 24, 2025, Plaintiff filed a First Amended Complaint for various violations of the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act and Fraudulent Inducement – Concealment.

 

[Tentative] Ruling

 

Defendant General Motors LLC’s Motion for Compliance Pursuant to CCP §871.26 and Request for Sanctions is GRANTED IN PART.

 

discussion

 

Defendant General Motors LLC (“GM”) moves this court for an order for compliance requiring Plaintiff Deann D Carson to provide the complete production of documents and written disclosure of information required under Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26(b) and (f). GM also moves this court for an order imposing monetary sanctions against Plaintiff’s attorneys of record, Strategic Legal Practices, APC, in the sum of $1,500.00. GM makes the motion on the grounds Plaintiff’s counsel has made clear on multiple occasions they will not produce the required materials.

 

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26(b), all parties are required to provide enumerated documents within 60 days of filing an Answer or responsive pleading to Plaintiff’s Complaint.

 

Defendant GM argues on June 9, 2025, Plaintiff filed suit against GM alleging violations of the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act, breach of warranties, and fraud relating to a 2018 Chevrolet Cruze that Plaintiff purchased. (Lasater Decl. ¶2.) On September 19, 2025, GM filed its responsive pleading in the form of its demurrer to the complaint. (Lasater Decl. ¶3.) Accordingly, Plaintiff’s deadline to produce documents in this matter was November 13, 2025. (Lasater Decl. ¶5.) However, Plaintiff has not produced the sales contract and repair orders. (Lasater Decl. ¶6.) Without Plaintiff’s repair orders and sales contract, GM is unable to adequately prepare for the Code-mandated mediation that is to occur 150 days after GM filed its responsive pleading under Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues the motion is moot because as of September 19, 2025, Plaintiff produced and served Defendant with initial disclosures. Plaintiff claims it provided all documents within Plaintiff’s possession and Plaintiff contends that Defendant could obtain this information from Defendant’s own records. Accordingly, GM cannot show noncompliance warranting sanctions.

 

Defendant persuasively argues that it has not received a single document from plaintiff.   The court finds that based on the record before it, plaintiff has not complied with Section 871.26.   Plaintiff is ordered to provide all of the required documents within 30 days.  Moreover, the court finds Plaintiff has not established good cause for failing to timely produce these documents. Plaintiff’s contention that GM could obtain this information through their own records is inadequate; Section 871.26 mandates Plaintiff produce the documents. Moreover, the absence of prejudice does not, by itself, demonstrate Plaintiff acted with good cause.

 

Accordingly, the request for sanctions in the amount of $1,500.00 is GRANTED, payable by plaintiff’s counsel within 30 days.     

 

 

 


Case Number: 26STCV02799    Hearing Date: June 30, 2026    Dept: 529

VICTOR CABRERA VS KIA AMERICA, INC.

 

MOTION FOR ENTRY OF PROTECTIVE ORDER TO GOVERN PRODUCTION OF CONFIDENTIAL MATERIALS

 

Date of Hearing:        June 30, 2026                                     Trial Date:       None set.

Department:              529                                                     Case No.:        26STCV02799

 

Moving Party:            Defendant Kia America, Inc.

Responding Party:     Plaintiff Victor Cabrera

 

BACKGROUND

 

This is a lemon law action. On January 17, 2026, Plaintiff Victor Cabrera filed a complaint against Defendant Kia America, Inc. for various violations of the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act.

 

[TENTATIVE] RULING

 

Defendant Kia America, Inc.’s Motion for Entry of Protective Order to Govern Production of Confidential Materials is GRANTED.

 

DISCUSSION

 

Defendant Kia America, Inc. (“KA”) requests this court to enter a Protective Order applicable to the production of materials pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26.

 

“The court, for good cause shown, may make any order that justice requires to protect any party or other person from unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, oppression, or undue burden and expense,” including that “a trade secret or other confidential research, development, or commercial information not be disclosed, or be disclosed only to specified persons or only in a specified way.” (CCP § 2031.060(b)(5).) A party may request entry of an order to protect its confidential and proprietary business information at any time. (Stadish v. Sup. Ct. (Southern Calif. Gas Co.) (1999) 71 Cal.App.4th 1130, 1144 [finding that “upon a proper showing a party may – even after it has waived its right to object to the production of documents, and has produced most of the documents requested – seek a protective order restricting dissemination of the documents.”].) 

 

Defendant KA argues entry of a Protective Order is necessary as the documents at issue here contain KA’s confidential and proprietary business information, and they reveal methods and processes that KA independently developed and has maintained as confidential and secret to KA. KA contends a document that was previously designated as confidential and subject to a protective order should not lose its designation simply because it is filed under the new statutory scheme that happens to be silent on the issue.

 

The court GRANTS Defendant KA’s motion for protective order. This process will not relieve KA of its discovery obligations and the new mandatory disclosures under Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26.  As noted by Defendant KA, the proposed Protective Order is reasonably targeted and will allow the parties to continue litigating this case without disruption.  If Plaintiff wants to challenge a “Confidential” designation, they may do so by noticed motion.

 

Based on the foregoing, Defendant KA’s Motion for Protective Order is GRANTED.
