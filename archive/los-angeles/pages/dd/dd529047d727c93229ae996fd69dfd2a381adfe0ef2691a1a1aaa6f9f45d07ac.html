Case Number: 24NWCV02598    Hearing Date: August 6, 2026    Dept: P

BARRIOS v. GENERAL MOTORS LLC

CASE NO.: 24NWCV02598 

HEARING: 08/06/2026 @ 9:30 AM

 

#10

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Defendant General Motors LLC’s motion to dismiss is DENIED on procedural grounds.

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

Defendant General Motors LLC (Defendant) moves to dismiss this complaint with prejudice.

 

Background

 

On August 19, 2024, Plaintiff filed this lemon law action against Defendants General Motors LLC (Defendant) and Does 1 through 10, inclusive. On October 8, 2025, following the Court’s ruling granting Plaintiff’s motion for leave to amend, Plaintiff filed the operative first amended complaint (FAC).

 

The FAC alleges Plaintiff purchased a 2021 GMC Sierra 1500, VIN: 1GTU9EET4MZ286007 (Subject Vehicle) and received an express warranty from Defendant. (FAC, ¶¶ 6-7.) The FAC alleges the Subject Vehicle was delivered to Plaintiff with defects and nonconformities to warranty including engine, transmission, and electrical defects. (FAC, ¶ 8.) The complaint asserts two causes of action: (1) violation of the federal Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act (MMWA) and (2) breach of express warranty under the Commercial Code.

 

On March 19, 2026, the Court denied Defendant’s ex parte application to advance the instant motion. The Court granted Defendant’s alternative request, to continue the trial date from April 3, 2026 to September 21, 2026.

 

Discussion

 

Defendant moves to dismiss this action with prejudice on the grounds that (1) Plaintiff has no evidence that he bought the Subject Vehicle and therefore lacks standing under the MMWA and Commercial Code; (2) Plaintiff has no evidence of the amount paid or payable for the Subject Vehicle and therefore lacks standing under the MMWA and Commercial Code; and (3) Plaintiff has failed to produce any evidence showing that the Subject Vehicle was worth less due to any alleged defects and therefore lacks standing under the MMWA.

 

This motion is procedurally improper.

 

First, as raised in Plaintiff’s opposition, Defendant’s motion fails to provide adequate notice for due process. Defendant’s Notice of Motion does not specify under what basis it moves to dismiss. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 1010 [“the notice of a motion…must state…the grounds upon which it will be made”].) The Notice of Motion states that the complaint should be dismissed for lack of standing, without specifying the statutory or nonstatutory grounds for dismissal.

 

Next, and compounded by the defective notice, is the fact that a motion to dismiss based on standing defects or lack of evidence is not recognized under California law. An issue of lack of standing may be addressed by demurrer. (See Cloud v. Northrop Grumman Corp. (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 995, 1004.) The Court does not construe this motion as a demurrer or motion for judgment on the pleadings because Defendant specifically moves on evidentiary grounds, which is beyond the scope of a demurrer or motion for judgment on the pleadings.

 

Defendant cites to Troyk v. Farmers Group, Inc. (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 1305, 1345, to argue that standing is a threshold issue and that standing requires establishing certain evidence and elements. Upon review, the Court does not find that Troyk provides authority for the instant motion to dismiss. The issue in Troyk was standing to bring a claim under the UCL pursuant to a motion for summary judgment. (See id., at p. 1350 [“Based on our independent review of the parties’ summary judgment papers, we conclude Troyk has not carried his burden to show there is no triable issue of fact regarding the element of causation for his standing to prosecute his UCL cause of action.”].) The Troyk court indeed states that “[b]ecause standing goes to the existence of a cause of action, lack of standing may be raised by demurrer or at any time in the proceeding, including at trial or in an appeal.” (Id., at p. 1345.) However, this Court does not construe this as creating a separate motion to dismiss as a procedural vehicle since the Troyk court specifically addressed standing via a motion for summary judgment.

 

Defendant also cites to Limon v. Circle K Stores Inc. (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 671, 704, to establish the standard for raising issues of standing. In Limon, the Court of Appeal reviewed an order of dismissal following a trial court’s ruling sustaining a demurrer without leave to amend on the issue of standing. (Id., at p. 685.) As stated above, this motion is neither a demurrer nor capable of being treated as a demurrer.

 

Here, Defendant appears to move on grounds typical of a motion for summary judgment in that Defendant argues that Plaintiff cannot put forth evidence to establish his standing. Motions for summary judgment are subject to strict statutory notice procedures which are not established here. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (a); see also San Diego Watercrafts, Inc. v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2002) 102 Cal.App.4th 308, 316.)

 

As the Court is without governing legal standards for this motion to dismiss, Defendant’s motion to dismiss is DENIED on procedural grounds

Case Number: 25NWCV00271    Hearing Date: August 6, 2026    Dept: P


MARTINEZ-CASTILLO v. FIGUEROA

CASE NO.: 25NWCV00271 

HEARING: 08/06/2026 @ 9:30 AM

 

#11

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Defendant Steve Figueroa’s motion set aside default is CONTINUED to Tuesday, August 25, 2026 at 9:30 AM in Dept. P only as to the new arguments raised in reply. Plaintiff’s sur-reply is due by August 17, 2026.

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

Defendant Steve Figueroa (Defendant) moves for relief from the default entered against him.

 

Background

 

On January 22, 2025, Plaintiff Armando Martinez-Castillo (Plaintiff) filed this personal injury action against Defendant and Does 1 through 50. The form complaint alleges that Plaintiff sustained injuries on August 3, 2024 while hired to work at Defendant’s property located at 11234 Cullman Ave., Whittier, CA 90603 after Defendant supplied a faulty and/or broken ladder to Plaintiff. The complaint asserts two causes of action: (1) general negligence and (2) premises liability.

 

On April 21, 2025, Plaintiff filed a request for Entry of Default against Defendant and Default was entered against Defendant on the same date (Default).

 

Legal Standard

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b) provides for discretionary and mandatory relief for mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect.

 

Discretionary relief is available to “relieve a party or his or her legal representative from a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against him or her through his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b).) An application for discretionary relief “shall be accompanied by a copy of the answer or other pleading proposed to be filed…and shall be made within a reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months, after the judgment, dismissal, order, or proceeding was taken.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b).) Mandatory relief is available “whenever an application for relief is made no more than six months after entry of judgment, is in proper form, and is accompanied by an attorney's sworn affidavit attesting to his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect…unless the court finds that the default or dismissal was not in fact caused by the attorney's mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b).)

 

“‘[W]hen relief under section 473¿is¿available, there is a strong public policy in favor of granting relief and allowing the requesting party his or her day in court.’” (Rappleyea v. Campbell (1994) 8 Cal.4th 975, 981-82, citations omitted.)

 

Discussion

 

Defendant moves to set aside the Default entered against him on April 21, 2025. Defendant moves for discretionary relief or, alternatively, mandatory relief under Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b).

 

Defendant argues that relief is warranted because the failure to not move for timely relief was based on (1) defense counsel’s mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect, (2) it is timely under the circumstances, and (3) it is accompanied by a proposed answer.

 

Defendant puts forth the declaration of defense counsel, Albert Robles (Mr. Robles). Mr. Robles states that the failure to not promptly move for relief after failing to timely answer is “the result of my mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect, due to my reliance on the various representations by Plaintiff of being open to setting aside the default via stipulation; because but-for these (mis)representations I would have immediately filed the CCP §473(b) Motion.” (Robles Decl., ¶ 4.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that relief under Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b) is jurisdictionally barred as untimely as Defendant moved for this relief eleven months after Entry of Default.

 

As to discretionary relief, this motion is untimely. A motion for relief under Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b) “shall be made within a reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months” after entry of default. (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b).) “The six-month period for granting relief under section 473, subdivision (b), ‘runs from entry of default, not entry of judgment.’” (Kramer v. Traditional Escrow, Inc. (2020) 56 Cal.App.5th 13, 39.)

 

Here, the Court agrees that discretionary relief is not available since it has been longer than six months since Entry of Default. As argued by Plaintiff, Defendant does not put forth authorities showing that negotiations regarding stipulations can toll this deadline. Defendant’s evidence shows that Plaintiff requested defense counsel call Plaintiff regarding setting aside the Default on August 15, 2025. (Robles Decl., Exh. 2.) There is no explanation why defense counsel did not then take action prior to November of 2025, after the deadline to move for relief passed in October of 2025. Moreover, this motion seeks relief from the six-month deadline, not the failure to file an answer. This is improper. (See Arambula v. Union Carbide Corp. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 333, 345 [“a court has no authority under section 473, subdivision (b), to excuse a party's noncompliance with the six-month time limit.”].)

 

As to mandatory relief, the Court notes that relief is not available as to the Default and relief is premature as to any default judgment. “Under the discretionary relief provision (§ 473(b), a motion to set aside a default judgment must be made no more than ‘six months’ after the default—not the default judgment—is entered [citation.] This is in contrast to the mandatory relief provision (§ 473(b)), which requires the underlying default to be vacated if a motion to vacate the default judgment is filed within six months of the date the default judgment was entered. (Jimenez v. Chavez (2023) 97 Cal.App.5th 50, 59–60, citing Sugasawara v. Newland (1994) 27 Cal.App.4th 294, 296–97.) Therefore, at this time, mandatory relief is not available.

 

For the first time on reply, Defendant argues that the Court should grant relief pursuant to its inherent authority. “[A] trial court may still vacate a default on equitable grounds even if statutory relief is unavailable.” (Rappleyea v. Campbell (1994) 8 Cal.4th 975, 981.) Courts have inherent equity powers to grant relief from a default or default judgment procured by extrinsic fraud or mistake. (Aheroni v. Maxwell (1988) 205 Cal.App.3d 284, 290.) “A party who seeks to have his default vacated under the court's equity power must make a stronger showing than is necessary to obtain relief under section 473.” (Id. at p. 291.) “ ‘To set aside a judgment based upon extrinsic mistake one must satisfy three elements. First, the defaulted party must demonstrate that it has a meritorious case. Second[ ], party seeking to set aside the default must articulate a satisfactory excuse for not presenting a defense to the original action. Last [ ], the moving party must demonstrate diligence in seeking to set aside the default once ... discovered.’”  (Rappleyea v. Campbell (1994) 8 Cal.4th 975, 982.)

 

As this argument is raised for the first time in reply, the Court finds a continuance warranted to allow Plaintiff the opportunity to file a sur-reply directed toward the new arguments.

 

Accordingly, Defendant’s motion to set aside default is CONTINUED to Tuesday, August 25, 2026 at 9:30 AM in Dept. P only as to the new arguments raised in reply. Plaintiff’s sur-reply is due by August 17, 2026.

 

Defendant’s motion to set aside is DENIED as to discretionary and mandatory relief under Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b).

DICKSON v. FCI LENDER SERVICES, INC., ET AL.

CASE NO.: 25NWCV00728

HEARING: 08/06/2026 @ 9:30 AM

 

#12

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

I.             Defendants FCI Lender Services and Coastal Capital Group, LLC’s motion to dismiss the first amended complaint as to Moving Defendants is GRANTED.

 

II.            The first amended complaint is DISMISSED with prejudice as to Defendants FCI Lender Services and Coastal Capital Group, LLC.

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

Defendants FCI Lender Services (FCI) and Coastal Capital Group, LLC (CCG) (collectively, Moving Defendants) move to dismiss with prejudice the first amended complaint (FAC) pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 581, subdivision (f)(2).

 

Background

 

On February 8, 2025, Plaintiff Gary Dickson (Plaintiff) filed a verified complaint against Defendants FCI, CCG, California TD Specialists (TD), PHH Mortgages (PHH), and Does 1 through 20. On Plaintiff filed a FAC against Defendants FCI, CCG, TD, PHH, Suresh Kamble, Suresh Kamble as Trustee of the Settler’s Living Trust, and Does 1 through 20. This action concerns the real property known as 6049 Pennswood Avenue, Lakewood, CA, 90712 (Subject Property). (FAC, ¶ 17.) The FAC asserts six causes of action: (1) wrongful foreclosure against FCI, CCG, and TD, (2) injunctive relief against all defendants expect Kamble, (3) violation of the California Homeowner Bill of Rights (HBOR) against FCI, CCG, and TD, (4) Unfair Fraudulent Business Practices (Business & Professions Code § 17200 et seq.) against FCI, CCG, and TD, (5) quiet title against FCI, CCG, TD, and Settler’s Living Trust, and (6) elder abuse against Kamble.

 

On January 15, 2026, the Court sustained with 30 days leave to amend Moving Defendants’ demurrer to the causes of action in the FAC alleged against them.

 

Legal Standard

 

“The court may dismiss the complaint as to that defendant when…after a demurrer to the complaint is sustained with leave to amend, the plaintiff fails to amend it within the time allowed by the court and either party moves for dismissal.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 581, subd. (f)(2).) “[N]o cases have held that a dismissal pursuant to section 581, subdivision (f)(2) may be without prejudice.” (Cano v. Glover (2006) 143 Cal.App.4th 326, 329.)

 

Discussion

 

Moving Defendants move to dismiss the FAC with prejudice as to Moving Defendants only on the grounds that Plaintiff failed to file an amended pleading within the time permitted for leave to amend. This motion is unopposed.

 

On January 15, 2026, the Court heard Moving Defendants’ demurrer to the FAC. The Court sustained the demurrer with 30 days leave to amend. (Order, 1/15/26.) Moving Defendants served a Notice of Ruling to Plaintiff via mail on January 28, 2026. (Hubbard Decl., ¶ 4.) Moving Defendants show that Plaintiff failed to file an amended pleading within 30 days from the date of the 1/15/26 Order and from the date of service of the Notice of Ruling. As of the date Moving Defendants filed this motion, Plaintiff had not filed an amended complaint. (Hubbard Decl., ¶ 6.)

 

Based on the foregoing, dismissal with prejudice is warranted as to Plaintiff’s claims against Moving Defendants pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 581, subdivision (f)(2) for failure to file an amended pleading in the time allotted for leave to amend.

 

Accordingly, Moving Defendants’ motion to dismiss the FAC as to Moving Defendants is GRANTED. The first amended complaint is DISMISSED with prejudice as to Moving Defendants.


Case Number: 26NWCV01282    Hearing Date: August 6, 2026    Dept: P

AGB MANAGEMENT, LLC v. FIRST CLASS ESCROW, INC., ET AL.

CASE NO.: 26NWCV01282 

HEARING: 08/06/2026 @ 9:30 AM

 

#ADD-ON

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

I.             Defendants First Class Escrow, Inc. and Angie Diaz’s demurrer to the complaint is SUSTAINED with 30 days LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

II.            Defendants’ motion to strike is MOOT.

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

Defendants First Class Escrow, Inc. (FCS) and Angie Diaz (Diaz) (collectively, Defendants) demur to the complaint and move to strike portions of the complaint.

 

Background

 

On April 10, 2026, Plaintiff AGB Management, LLC (Plaintiff) filed this breach of contract action and Defendant and Does 1 through 50. The complaint alleges that FCS, formerly known as La Costa Escrow, Inc., and Plaintiff entered into a written Management Services Agreement (Agreement) in November of 2024 in which AGB agreed to provide management, marketing, payroll accounting, administrative, compliance, and related services for FCS's escrow business.
(Complaint, ¶ 11.) Plaintiff also provided capital as part of its business arrangement. (Complaint, ¶ 15.) The complaint alleges that in March of 2025, Defendants unilaterally removed Plaintiff from operations, excluded Plaintiff from the business, and failed to reimburse Plaintiff in violation of the Agreement. (Complaint, ¶¶ 19-21.)

 

The complaint asserts seven causes of action against FCE and Does 1 through 20: (1) Breach of Written Contract; (2) Breach of the Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing; (3) Common Count – Money Had and Received; (4) Common Count – Open Book Account; (5) Accounting; (6) Declaratory Relief; and (7) Conversion against all Defendants.

 

On August 4, 2026, on the Court’s own motion, the Court continued the hearings on the instant demurrer and motion to strike to today’s date.

 

Legal Standard

 

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) In assessing a demurrer, the court “treat[s] the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of law.” (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) “The only issue involved in a demurrer hearing is whether the complaint, as it stands, unconnected with extraneous matters, states a cause of action.” (Hahn, supra, 147 Cal.App.4th at p. 747.) A complaint will be upheld against a demurrer if it pleads facts sufficient to place the defendant on notice of the issues sufficient to enable the defendant to prepare a defense. (Doe v. City of Los Angeles (2007) 42 Cal.4th 531, 549-50.)

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (f) provides for a demurrer when a pleading is uncertain. Demurrers for uncertainty are disfavored and are only sustained where a pleading is so incomprehensible a defendant cannot reasonably respond. (A.J. Fistes v. GDL Best Contractors, Inc. (2019) 38 Cal.App.5th 677, 695; Khoury v. Maly’s of California (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.)  

 

“Generally it is an abuse of discretion to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend if there is any reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment.” (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349.) A “[p]laintiff must show in what manner he can amend his complaint and how that amendment will change the legal effect of his pleading.” (Ibid.) 

 

Meet and Confer

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41 requires that the demurring party meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference at least five days before the responsive pleading is due. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a).) The demurring party shall file and serve a declaration stating either: (a) “The means by which the demurring party met and conferred…and that the parties did not reach an agreement” or (b) “[t]hat the party who filed the pleading subject to demurrer failed to respond to the meet and confer request of the demurring party or otherwise failed to meet and confer in good faith.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(3).)

 

Defense counsel sent a meet and confer letter to Plaintiff’s counsel but did not receive any response. (Coy Decl., ¶¶ 2-4.) Moving Defendants comply with the requirement. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(3).)

 

Discussion

 

FCE specially and generally demurs to each cause of action in the complaint on the grounds that the causes of action fail to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. Moving Defendants specially and generally demur to the seventh cause of action on the grounds that it fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. This demurrer is unopposed.

 

          First Cause of Action – Breach of Written Contract

 

To state a cause of action for breach of contract, Plaintiff must be able to establish “(1) the existence of the contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance, (3) defendant’s breach, and (4) the resulting damages to the plaintiffs.” (Oasis West Realty, LLC v. Goldman (2011) 51 Cal.4th 811, 821.) “A written contract may be pleaded either by its terms—set out verbatim in the complaint or a copy of the contract attached to the complaint and incorporated therein by reference—or by its legal effect.” (Heritage Pacific Financial, LLC v. Monroy (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 972, 993; Miles v. Deutsche Bank National Trust Co. (2015) 236 Cal.App.4th 394, 402.) “In order to plead a contract by its legal effect, plaintiff must ‘allege the substance of its relevant terms. This is more difficult, for it requires a careful analysis of the instrument, comprehensiveness in statement, and avoidance of legal conclusions.” (Heritage Pacific Financial, LLC, supra, 215 Cal.App.4th at p. 993.)

 

The complaint alleges Plaintiff and La Costa entered into the Agreement. (Complaint, ¶ 11.) Additionally, the complaint alleges FCE, “formerly known as LA COSTA ESCROW, INC. (“La Costa”…) is, and at all relevant times was, a California corporation engaged in the business of providing escrow services in California.” (Complaint, ¶ 2.) FCE argues that the complaint fails to establish a privity of contract and the allegation that FCE was formally known as La Costa is insufficient to allege privity. FCE argues that there are no allegations how La Costa allegedly “morphed into FCE,” assigned the Agreement to FCE, or under what circumstances did FCE assume La Costa’s privileges and obligations. (Dem., 9:5.) FCE refers to the factors in determining whether a purchaser assumed a seller’s liability and argues there are no supporting allegations here. (Ray v. Alad Corp. (1977) 19 Cal.3d 22, 28 [a “purchaser does not assume the seller's liabilities unless (1) there is an express or implied agreement of assumption, (2) the transaction amounts to a consolidation or merger of the two corporations, (3) the purchasing corporation is a mere continuation of the seller, or (4) the transfer of assets to the purchaser is for the fraudulent purpose of escaping liability for the seller's debts.”].)

 

The Court agrees with FCE that the complaint fails to allege FCE’s privity with Plaintiff or La Costa and thus its obligations under the Agreement. Further, as argued by FCE, the complaint does not provide a copy of the Agreement or put forth the terms verbatim. The complaint generally alleges certain contractual terms. However, the Court does not find these allegations sufficient to allege the effect of the Agreement, especially in light of the deficiency in alleging FCE’s obligations.

 

Accordingly, FCE’s demurrer to the first cause of action is SUSTAINED with 30 days LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

Second Cause of Action – Breach of the Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing

 

“A breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing involves something beyond breach of the contractual duty itself and it has been held that bad faith implies unfair dealing rather than mistaken judgment.” (Careau & Co. v. Security Pacific Business Credit, Inc. (1990) 222 Cal.App.3d 1371, 1394.) “If the allegations do not go beyond the statement of a mere contract breach and, relying on the same alleged acts, simply seek the same damages or other relief already claimed in a companion contract cause of action, they may be disregarded as superfluous as no additional claim is actually stated.” (Id. at pp. 1394-95.) To recover in tort for breach of the implied covenant, the defendant must “have acted unreasonably or without proper cause.” (Id. at p. 1395, citations and italics omitted.) Further, “because [the implied covenant] protects only the express terms of the agreement, [i]t cannot impose substantive duties or limits on the contracting parties beyond those incorporated in the specific terms of their agreement.” (McClain v. Octagon Place, LLC (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 784, 806.)

 

Here, as the Court finds that the complaint does not adequately allege the terms of the Agreement, the Court cannot discern whether the complaint alleges a breach of the implied covenant as to express terms of the Agreement. (McClain, supra, 159 Cal.App.4th at p. 806.)

 

Accordingly, FCE’s demurrer to the second cause of action is SUSTAINED with 30 days LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

Third, Fourth, Fifth, & Sixth Causes of Action – Common Count – Money Had and Received; Common Count – Open Book Account; Accounting; & Declaratory Relief

 

“‘A common count is not a specific cause of action…rather, it is a simplified form of pleading normally used to aver the existence of various forms of monetary indebtedness, including that arising from an alleged duty to make restitution under an assumpsit theory.’” (Korchemny v. Piterman (2021) 68 Cal.App.5th 1032, 1048.) “When a common count is used as an alternative way of seeking the same recovery demanded in a specific cause of action, and is based on the same facts, the common count is demurrable if the cause of action is demurrable. [Citations.]” (Id., at p. 1048-49, citations omitted.) “[A] cause of action for accounting need only state facts showing the existence of the relationship which requires an accounting and the statement that some balance is due the plaintiff.” (Kritzer v. Lancaster (1950) 96 Cal.App.2d 1, 7.)

 

“To qualify for declaratory relief, [a party] would have to demonstrate its action presented two essential elements: ‘(1) a proper subject of declaratory relief, and (2) an actual controversy involving justiciable questions relating to [the party’s] rights or obligations.’” (Jolley v. Chase Home Finance, LLC (2013) 213 Cal.App.4th 872, 909.) “A trial court may…dismiss a declaratory relief claim at the demurrer stage where it is “ ‘ “wholly derivative” ’ ” of other failed claims.” (Siskiyou Hospital, Inc. v. County of Siskiyou (2025) 109 Cal.App.5th 14, 53.)

 

Here, as the third through sixth causes of action are derivative of the contractual claims and require allegations of FCE’s obligations under the Agreement, the
Court finds that the complaint fails to state a cause of action as these claims.

 

Accordingly, FCE’s demurrer to the third through sixth causes of action is SUSTAINED with 30 days LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

          Seventh Cause of Action – Conversion

 

“Conversion is the wrongful exercise of dominion over the property of another. The elements of a conversion claim are: (1) the plaintiff’s ownership or right to possession of the property; (2) the defendant’s conversion by a wrongful act or disposition of property rights; and (3) damages.” (Lee v. Hanley (2015) 61 Cal.4th 1225, 1240.) “Money can be the subject of an action for conversion if a specific sum capable of identification is involved.” (Farmers Ins. Exchange v. Zerin (1997) 53 Cal.App.4th 445, 452.) “Neither legal title nor absolute ownership of the property is necessary. A party need only allege it is ‘entitled to immediate possession at the time of conversion. [Citations.]’” (Ibid., citations omitted.) “[A] mere contractual right of payment, without more, will not suffice.” (Ibid.)

 

Moving Defendants demur to the seventh cause of action on the grounds that the complaint does not allege conversion of tangible property. The complaint alleges “[w]ithin the last two years, AGB owned and had the right to immediate possession of specific, identifiable funds and property, including but not limited to: (a) net profits generated by [FCE] during the term of the…Agreement that, by agreement and practice, were to be paid to AGB; and (b) funds and economic interests traceable to AGB’s capital contributions and operational advances made for the acquisition and operation of the escrow business.” (Complaint, ¶ 48.) As the Court does not find the Agreement’s terms to be sufficiently pled or amounts owing as to Moving Defendants, the Court agrees that the complaint does not sufficiently allege a specific sum capable of identification.

 

Accordingly, Moving Defendants’ demurrer to the seventh cause of action is SUSTAINED with 30 days LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

While the Court sustains the demurrer as to sufficiency of allegations arguments, the Court does not find the complaint uncertain within the meaning of Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (f).

 

In conclusion, Moving Defendants’ demurrer to the first through seventh causes of action is SUSTAINED with 30 days LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

Motion to Strike

 

Moving Defendants move to strike certain alter ego allegations and claims for punitive damages from the complaint.

 

Based on the Court’s ruling on the demurrer, Moving Defendants’ motion to strike is MOOT.
