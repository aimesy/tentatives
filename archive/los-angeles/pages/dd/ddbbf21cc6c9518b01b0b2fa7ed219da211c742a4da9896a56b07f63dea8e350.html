Motions to Quash Service of Summons

 

Background

 

            On April 18, 2023, Plaintiff Hume Street Management Consultants Limited (“Plaintiff”) field the operative First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) against several entities and individual defendants including Specially Appearing Defendants Sheikh Hamad bin Khalifa bin Hamad bin Abdullah bin Jassim bin Mohammed Al-Thani (“HBK”), Sheikha Lulwah bint Hamad bin Khalifa Al-Thani (“Lulwah”), and Dilmon Services (U.K.) Limited (“Dilmon”), asserting causes of action for: (1) Breach of Contract; (2) Account Stated; (3) Quantum Meruit; (4) Promissory Estoppel; and (5) Unjust Enrichment.

 

            On May 4, 2026, HBK, Lulwah, and Dilmon (collectively, “HBK Defendants”) filed the instant Motions to Quash Service of Summons. On June 25, 2026, Plaintiff filed an Opposition to each motion. On July 1, 2026, HBK Defendants field their respective Reply briefs.

 

Evidentiary Objections

 

            HBK Defendants object to portions of the declarations of Patrick McMillen, Liam Cunningham, and Phillip Howard. Objection no. 1 is SUSTAINED as argumentative and improper legal conclusion. However, Objection nos. 2-19 are OVERRULED. Objections based on lack of foundation, hearsay, and lack of personal knowledge should be overruled unless there is a reason to believe that a witness, including an expert witness, would not be able to lay a foundation and authenticate these facts at trial. (See Sweetwater Union High School Dist. v. Gilbane Building Co. (2019) 6 Cal.5th 931, 947-948.) For the same reason, Plaintiff’s objections to portions of the declaration of Ziyad Clot are OVERRULED.

 

Legal Standard

 

      On a motion to quash service of the summons and complaint, the moving party must first present some admissible evidence, such as declarations or affidavits, to place the issue of minimum contacts before the Court. (School Dist. of Oskaloosa County v. Superior Court¿(1997) 58 Cal.App.4th 1126, 1131.) “When a motion to quash is properly brought, the burden of proof is placed upon the plaintiff to establish the facts of jurisdiction by a preponderance of the evidence.”¿ (Aquila, Inc. v. Sup. Ct.¿(2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 556, 568; see also¿Elkman v. National States Ins. Co.¿(2009) 173 Cal.App.4th 1305, 1312-13 [“Where a nonresident defendant challenges jurisdiction by way of a motion to quash, the plaintiff bears the burden of establishing by a preponderance of the evidence that minimum contacts exist between the defendant and the forum state to justify imposition of personal jurisdiction.”].) Evidence of the facts giving rise to personal jurisdiction or their absence may be in the form of declarations. (Arensen v. Raymond Lee Organization, Inc.¿(1973) 31 Cal.App.3d 991,¿995.) 

 

      California’s long-arm statute permits a court to exercise personal jurisdiction on any basis consistent with state or federal constitutional principles. (Code Civ. Proc. [“CCP”] §410.10.) 

“Personal jurisdiction may be either general or specific. A nonresident defendant may be subject to the general jurisdiction of the forum if his or her contacts in the forum state are ‘substantial . . . continuous and systematic.’” (Vons Companies, Inc. v. Seabest Foods, Inc. (1996) 14 Cal.4th 434, 445-446 (citations omitted).) 

 

      “If the nonresident defendant does not have substantial and systematic contacts in the forum sufficient to establish general jurisdiction, he or she still may be subject to the specific jurisdiction of the forum, if the defendant has purposefully availed himself or herself of forum benefits, and the ‘controversy is related to or ‘arises out of’ a defendant’s contacts with the forum.’” (Id. at pg. 446, citations omitted.) The purposeful availment test is only satisfied if the defendant purposefully and voluntarily directs its activities toward California so that the defendant should expect, because of the benefits it receives, to be subject to jurisdiction here based on its contacts with California. (Snowney v. Harrah’s Entertainment, Inc. (2005) 35 Cal.4th 1054, 1062.)¿¿Purposeful availment occurs when a nonresident defendant purposefully directs its activities at California residents, deliberately engages in significant activities here, or creates “continuing obligations” between itself and California residents.¿¿(Id.¿at 1063.) 

 

Analysis

 

            Here, HBK Defendants assert that there is no basis for exercising personal jurisdiction over them as they have no minimum contacts with California. Plaintiff opposes. For the reasons set forth below the Court finds that Plaintiff has not met its burden demonstrating that jurisdiction over Dilmon is proper but has met its burden of demonstrating that jurisdiction over HBK and Lulwah is proper.

 

General Jurisdiction 

 

General jurisdiction is an “exacting standard” which requires that the defendant's contacts with the forum state be both “substantial and continuous” as to “make it consistent with traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice to subject the defendant to the jurisdiction of the forum, even where the cause of action is unrelated to the contacts.” (Calvert v. Huckins¿(1995) 875 F. Supp. 674, 677.) 

 

First, Dilmon asserts and submits evidence showing that it is not subject to general jurisdiction. Dilmon is an English limited company with its principal place of business located in London, England. (Dilmon Mot., Clot Decl., ¶¶2-3.) It has no employees or board members located in California; does not receive mail outside of its London address; bank accounts are only in the United Kingdom; only pays taxes in the United Kingdom; and its parent company has an office only in Doha, Qatar but is a separate entity from Dilmon. (Id., ¶¶3-4.) Furthermore, Dilmon does not own any real estate in California, does not maintain any offices in California, does not provide services to California nor its residents, and has not entered into any contracts with California residents. (Id., ¶7.) Plaintiff’s argument that Dilmon is an agent of Beverly Hills Acquisition, LLC for purposes of general jurisdiction is unavailing. (See BBA Aviation PLC v. Superior Court (2010) 190 Cal.App.4th 421, 429 [“When the nonresident defendant is a parent corporation of a subsidiary which does business in California, the minimum contacts may be direct between the parent and the state, or imputed to the parent via its subsidiary. General jurisdiction over a local subsidiary extends to the foreign parent under an alter ego theory, general principles of agency, or under the representative services doctrine, a narrow species of agency.”].) There are no allegations in the FAC indicating that Dilmon is an agent or owner of Beverly Hills Acquisition, LLC (FAC/Compl., ¶23; Reply at 3:5-22) nor does Plaintiff produce any evidence via judicial notice showing that Dilmon has any affiliation with or equitable ownership interest in Beverly Hills Acquisition, LLC.

 

Next, HBK and Lulwah aver that they are domiciled in Doha, Qatar and do not own any real estate in California. (HBK Mot., Clot Decl.,¶¶4-5; Lulwah Mot., Clot Decl., ¶¶3-4.) Moreover, HBK asserts that he only has indirect ownership of Maybourne Beverly Hills (“MBH”) through his ownership in Beverly Hills Acquisition, LLC. (HBK Mot. at 7:5-17.) By contrast, Lulwah states that she has no ownership in MBH. (Lulwah Mot. at 6:6-9.) To the extent that Plaintiff argues that through Lulwah’s oversight of the MBH project on behalf of HBK, both HBK and Lulwah are subject to general jurisdiction, the Court disagrees. Plaintiff does not indicate that HBK and Lulwah traveled to California to enter into the contract, to oversee the MBH project in person, and/or to have meetings in California related to the MBH project. In fact, Plaintiff attests that Lulwah participated in a meeting with Patrick McKillen on HBK’s yacht in Doha, Qatar. (McMillen Decls., ¶11.)

 

Accordingly, the Court finds no evidence of activities in the forum pervasive enough to support the exercise of general jurisdiction. 

 

Specific Jurisdiction 

 

Specific jurisdiction involves a three-part test in California. “A court may exercise specific jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant only if: (1) ‘the defendant has purposefully availed himself or herself of forum benefits’ [citation]; (2) ‘the “controversy is related to or ‘arises out of’ [the] defendant's contacts with the forum” ’ [citation]; and (3) ‘the assertion of personal jurisdiction would comport with “fair play and substantial justice” ’ ” ’ [Citations].” (Pavlovich v. Superior Ct. (2002) 29 Cal. 4th 262, 269.) The “ ‘minimum contacts’ analysis looks to the defendant’s contacts with the forum [s]tate itself, not the defendant's contacts with persons who reside there.” [Citations.] “ ‘[T]he plaintiff cannot be the only link between the defendant and the forum. Rather, it is the defendant’s conduct that must form the necessary connection with the forum State that is the basis for its jurisdiction over him.’ ” [Citations.]¿“[T]o find specific jurisdiction, a court must look to the defendant's ‘own’ suit-related contacts with the forum to see if they create a ‘substantial connection with the forum State,’ not just ‘with persons who reside there.’ ” (Swenberg v. Dmarcian, Inc.¿(2021) 68 Cal.App.5th 280, 295 [emphasis in original].) 

 

1.¿¿¿¿¿Purposeful Availment 

 

[P]urposeful availment occurs where a nonresident defendant¿‘ “purposefully direct[s]” [its] activities at residents of the¿forum, ‘create[s] a “substantial connection” with the forum’ [citation], ‘ “deliberately” has engaged in significant activities within’ the forum [citation], or ‘has created “continuing obligations” between [itself] and residents of the forum’ [citation].” (Swenberg v. Dmarcian, Inc.¿(2021) 68 Cal.App.5th 280, 294-295.) “ ‘Purposeful availment’ requires that the defendant ‘have performed some type of affirmative conduct which allows or promotes the transaction of business within the forum state.’ ” [Citations.] In other words, “[t]he purposeful availment inquiry ... focuses on the defendant’s intentionality. This prong is only satisfied when the defendant purposefully and voluntarily directs his activities toward the forum so that he should expect, by virtue of the benefit he receives, to be subject to the court’s jurisdiction based on’ his contacts with the forum.” (LG Chem, Ltd. v. Superior Court of San Diego County (2022) 80 Cal.App.5th 348, 362 [internal quotation marks and citations omitted.].) 

 

            With respect to Dilmon, there is no evidence that Dilmon conducted business in the state of California; advertises or markets its investment advisory services to California residents; and/or contracted with a company doing business in California. (Dilmon Mot., Clot Decl., ¶7.) Nor was Dilmon involved in the day-to-day operations and management of MBH. (Id., ¶6.) Although Marc Socker and Nicolas Bouquay are employees of Dilmon, their roles have been limited to investment advice and/or real estate work for properties in Dilmon’s portfolio, respectively. (Id., ¶9.) Contrarily, Michele Faissola is employed by Dilmon’s parent company not Dilmon. (Id.) As such, Plaintiff assertions that Socker, Bouquay, and Faissola’s communications concerning the management, operations, and development of the MBH project cannot be attributed to Dilmon. (McKillen Decls., ¶¶17-22.)

 

            As to HBK and Lulwah, there is no evidence that either of them traveled to California for purposes related to the MBH project. As discussed earlier, Plaintiff admits the contract and meeting occurred electronically and/or in Qatar. Nonetheless, neither HBK nor Lulwah dispute that the contract was supposed to be performed in California, i.e., directly aimed at engaging in commercial activities within the state of California. Likewise, the communications related to the MBH project concerned approval for budget, the work to be performed, and renaming the businesses. (See generally McKillen Decls.; Howard Decls.; Cunningham Decls.) In Anglo Irish Bank Corp., PLC v. Superior Court (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 969 held that “[t]he proper jurisdictional question is not whether the defendant can be liable for the acts of another person or entity under state substantive law, but whether the defendant has purposefully directed its activities at the forum state by causing a separate person or entity to engage in forum contacts.” (Anglo Irish Bank Corp., PLC v. Superior Court (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 969, 983.)

Essentially, HBK and Lulwah purposefully and voluntarily directed their activities toward California for business purposes through Socker, Bouquay, and Faissola. (See generally McKillen Decls.; Howard Decls.; Cunningham Decls.)

 

Thus, Plaintiff has established that exercising jurisdiction over HBK and Lulwah would comport with the traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.

 

The Court briefly notes that HBK contests the validity of the service of process via mail.  However, the Court already found that service of process was proper via mail when it ruled HBK’s prior motion to quash. (See Min. Order, 4/28/23 at p. 20.)

Based on the foregoing, Defendant Dilmon Services (U.K.) Limited’s Motion to Quash Service of Summons is GRANTED.

Background

 

This is a medical malpractice case alleging the following:

 

Plaintiff Ashley Matthau sought medical care and treatment from Kristi Funk, M.D. in relation to a palpable right breast mass. On that same day, Dr. Funk examined Ms. Matthau and recommended the mass be removed. Dr. Funk understood at that time that Ms. Matthau was also a patient of Garth Fisher, M.D., a plastic surgeon. Dr. Funk and Plaintiff discussed that the mass could be excised at the same time as a procedure by Dr. Fisher. (Stevens Decl. ¶ 8(a).) Dr. Funk emailed Dr. Fisher, acknowledging that Dr. Fisher intended to excise the mass without the assistance or involvement of Dr. Funk. Dr. Funk indicated this plan was “fine.” (Id. ¶ 8(b).)

 

Plaintiff argues that the change in plan was not Dr. Funk’s choice. Rather, Dr. Fisher’s office unilaterally, and without informing Plaintiff beforehand, told Dr. Funk’s office that she was not needed. This decision was made by Dr. Fisher, without input or agreement by Dr. Funk. (Flagg Decl. ¶ 10(e).) Dr. Fisher used misrepresentations to exclude Dr. Funk, falsely telling her that Ms. Matthau wanted him to excise the tumor, which elicited a vague response “it’s fine,” and falsely telling Ms. Matthau that Dr. Funk agreed with the change. (Plaintiff’s Statement of Facts (“PSOF”) Nos. 53-54.)

 

Plaintiff confirmed she attended pre-surgical appointments with Dr. Fisher and was made aware of the surgical plan. Prior to the surgical procedure, the plaintiff signed informed consents indicating Dr. Fisher would perform the procedure and indicating which procedures would be performed by Dr. Fisher. The procedures to be performed were a right breast biopsy or lumpectomy and standard scar revision. (Id. ¶ 8(c).) Plaintiff argues that the informed consent was procured through fraud. Plaintiff claims that while she knew Dr. Fisher was performing the procedure rather than Dr. Funk, Dr. Fisher lied about Dr. Funk’s agreement to the change of plans. (PSOF 54.) Dr. Fisher did not disclose that he told Dr. Funk that Plaintiff wanted him to perform the procedure. (Ibid.) Plaintiff also claims that Dr. Fisher lied to her about where the procedure should be performed. Dr. Fisher’s office told Plaintiff that the insurance would not cover the procedure. (Id. ¶ 57.) However, Plaintiff claims that her insurance would have covered the procedure if it had been performed at a hospital, rather than Dr. Fisher’s surgical center. (Id. ¶¶ 64, 65.)

 

On August 30, 2022, Plaintiff underwent scar revision to the right forehead and excision of tumor from the right breast. The procedure was performed at Dr. Fisher’s surgical center. (Id. ¶ 8(d).) On September 1, 2022, a pathology report from Cedars-Sinai Medical Center indicates “phyllodes tumor approaches to than .1 cm of inked margin and is focally present at ink.” (Id. ¶ 8(e).) On September 19, 2022, Dr. Fisher phoned the pathologist with Plaintiff present. They discussed that there was positive tumor at the margin of the pathology specimen. (Id. ¶ 8(f).)

 

Plaintiff claims that the first surgical procedure was not successful because Dr. Fisher failed to take out sufficient margins, leaving cancer in Ms. Matthau’s body. This was particularly problematic because Dr. Funk had sent Dr. Fisher an email instructing him of the need to take clear margins. Despite admonition from a leading oncologist, Dr. Fisher did not remove a sufficient amount of tissue. Dr. Fisher later admitted his fault and apologized by text and saying he could not see the cancer cells. (PSOF 67, 71.)

 

On September 20, 2022, Plaintiff underwent right breast wide re-excision with specimen mammography. Findings during the procedure indicate grossly clear margins obtained with no clinically apparent residual tumor. Dr. Funk was the surgeon and Dr. Fisher was the assisting surgeon. (Id. ¶ 8(h).)

 

On January 16, 2023, Plaintiff returned to Dr. Fisher, complaining of breast rippling to the right breast. Dr. Fisher recommended changing the implants. (Id. ¶ 8(i).) Plaintiff signed multiple informed consents for the planned surgical procedure, which was noted as bilateral breast implant exchange (silicone) and bilateral breast augmentation. (Id. ¶ 8(j).)

 

Dr. Fisher performed the procedure on January 31, 2023. On a follow up visit, Plaintiff indicated she believed her old breasts looked better than the new breasts. Dr. Fisher told Plaintiff she could go back to her old breasts, but this might result in rippling which caused the plaintiff’s previous concerns. (Id. ¶ 8(l).)

 

On February 14, 2023, Plaintiff signed multiple informed consents for another planned surgical procedure, a bilateral breast implant exchange (silicone) and bilateral breast augmentation. Specifically, Plaintiff signed a High Risk Consent form detailing the increased risks for the planned procedure. (Id. ¶ 8(m).)

 

The procedure took place on February 28, 2023. In a follow up visit, Plaintiff said she loved her breasts. Dr. Fisher conducted an exam and instructed the plaintiff to return for further evaluation in one week. Plaintiff did not return to Dr. Fisher and sought care elsewhere. (Id. ¶ 8(o).)

 

Dr. Fisher’s medical expert, Dr. Grant Stevens, is a board-certified plastic surgeon, a professor at the USC School of Medicine, and an expert consultant for the Medical Board of California. He opines that Dr. Fisher acted at all relevant times within the standard of care related to his care and treatment of Ms. Matthau. (Id. ¶ 9.) According to Dr. Stevens, based upon Dr. Fisher's experience as a breast surgeon, it was acceptable for him to perform the tumor excision. He did not require assistance by Dr. Funk. (Id. ¶ 11.) And it was appropriate to perform the procedure at the surgical center, as Dr. Funk also testified. (Ibid.)

 

As to whether Dr. Fisher properly excised the tumor, Dr. Stevens opines that assuming “the margins were not clear, this is a known risk of the surgical procedure. In a surgical procedure like the one performed by Dr. Fisher on August 30, 2022, there is always the possibility that the margins will not be cleared and residual tumor will remain.” (Id. ¶ 13.) “This can and does occur in the absence of negligence. Further, there is always a risk a subsequent procedure will be required to address any residual tumor.” (Id. ¶ 14.)

 

As to the procedure to replace Plaintiff’s breast implants, Dr. Stevens opines that the timing of this procedure from the re-excision procedure by Dr. Funk was reasonable and appropriate and allowed Plaintiff time to heal from her prior procedure. According to Dr. Stevens, there is no indication the procedure was performed below the standard of care. (Id. ¶ 19.) Following a procedure like the one performed by Dr. Fisher, Dr. Stevens opines there is always the possibility the patient may not like the aesthetic appearance of the breasts and additional surgeries may be performed. This can and does in occur in the absence of negligence. (Ibid.)

 

Dr. Stevens concludes that Dr. Fischer’s medical treatment did not fall below the standard of care, and no act or failure to act on the part of Dr. Fisher, to a reasonable degree of medical probability, caused or contributed to the plaintiff's claimed injuries. (Id. ¶ 25.)

 

In contrast to Dr. Stevens, Plaintiff’s medical expert, Dr. Gwenevere Flagg opines that Dr. Fisher’s treatment of Ms. Matthau fell below the standard of care. Dr. Flagg opines that Dr. Fisher relied exclusively on his “unaided vision” to determine what tissue to excise and to set margins. Dr. Flagg opines that in doing so Dr. Fisher fell below the standard of care, which requires the use of diagnostic instruments, which would be available at a hospital setting but not necessarily at a surgery center. (Flagg Decl. ¶ 15.)

 

Dr. Flagg also attests that if the procedure had been performed at the hospital, the pathology report would have indicated that the margins were not clear and that additional tissue had to be removed. Performing the excision procedure in a hospital with immediate pathology available would have made the second surgery unnecessary, would have resulted in less tissue being removed, and would have made the restoration easier and more cosmetically appealing. (Id. ¶ 14(a).)

 

Dr. Flagg further opines that Dr. Fisher’s decision to exclude Dr. Funk, a noted oncologist, from the excision procedure fell below the standard of care as he should have deferred to a specialist like Dr. Funk when performing the procedure at issue. (Id. ¶ 13.) In addition, Dr. Flagg attests that by obtaining Ms. Matthau’s consent to the procedure, while omitting material facts, Dr. Fisher’s conduct fell below the standard of care. (Id. ¶ 10.)

Motion for Summary Judgment and Summary Adjudication Standard

 

The function of a motion for summary judgment or adjudication is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party cannot show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Ritchfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) Code Civ. Proc. §437c(c) “requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Adler v. Minor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.) “The function of the pleadings in a motion for summary judgment is to delimit the scope of the issues; the function of the affidavits or declarations is to disclose whether there is any triable issue of fact within the issues delimited by the pleadings.” (Juge v. County of Sacramento (1993) 12 Cal.App.4th 59, 67).

 

As to each claim as framed by the complaint, a defendant moving for summary judgment must satisfy the initial burden of proof by presenting facts to negate an essential element of the claim(s) or by establishing an affirmative defense. (Code Civ. Proc. § 437c(p)(2); Scalf v. D.B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1520.) Courts “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.” (Dore v. Arnold Worldwide, Inc. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 384, 389.)

 

Once the defendant has met its burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to that cause of action or a defense thereto. (Id.) To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence. (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

 

In a case involving a cause of action for medical malpractice, “[w]hen a defendant moves for summary judgment and supports his motion with expert declarations that his conduct fell within the community standard of care, he is entitled to summary judgment unless Plaintiff comes forward with conflicting expert evidence.” (Munro v. Regents of Univ. of Cal. (1989) 215 Cal.App.3d 977, 985.)

Analysis

 

Defendants move for summary judgment or, in the alternative, summary adjudication of all claims in the operative Second Amended Complaint (SAC) and Plaintiff’s request for punitive damages.

 

First Cause of Action for Medical Malpractice/Negligence

“‘[I]n any medical malpractice action, the plaintiff must establish: “(1) the duty of the professional to use such skill, prudence, and diligence as other members of his profession commonly possess and exercise; (2) a breach of that duty; (3) a proximate causal connection between the negligent conduct and the resulting injury; and (4) actual loss or damage resulting from the professional’s negligence.” [Citations.]’ [Citation.]” (Hanson v. Grode (1999) 76 Cal.App.4th 601, 606 (Hanson).)

“‘“California courts have incorporated the expert evidence requirement into their standard for summary judgment in medical malpractice cases. When a defendant moves for summary judgment and supports his motion with expert declarations that his conduct fell within the community standard of care, he is entitled to summary judgment unless the plaintiff comes forward with conflicting expert evidence.” [Citations.]’ [Citation.]” (Hanson, supra, 76 Cal.App.4th at pp. 606–607.)

The Court finds that Defendants have met their initial burden of showing that Plaintiff’s medical negligence claim lacks merit. As stated above, their expert, Dr. Stevens, testifies that at all relevant times, Dr. Fisher’s conduct fell within the standard of care.

However, Plaintiff has also met her burden of showing that triable issues of material fact exist regarding the medical malpractice claim. Her expert testifies that Dr. Fisher’s conduct fell below the standard of care.

Therefore, the request for summary judgment and for

summary adjudication of the first cause of action for medical malpractice/negligence is denied.

Second Cause of Action for Lack of Informed Consent

 

“Under the informed consent doctrine, ‘“‘[1] the patient must have the capacity to reason and make judgments, [2] the decision must be made voluntarily and without coercion, and [3] the patient must have a clear understanding of the risks and benefits of the proposed treatment alternatives or nontreatment, along with a full understanding of the nature of the disease and the prognosis.’” ’ [Citation.]” (Conte v. Girard Orthopaedic Surgeons Medical Group, Inc. (2003) 107 Cal.App.4th 1260, 1267.) The doctrine “imposes a duty on the physician to provide material information about any proposed treatment, such as risks and alternative procedures.” (Ibid.)

 

Here, the SAC alleges: “When Dr. Fisher obtained Ms. Matthau’s signature(s) on consent forms for the first surgery, that consent was obtained by fraudulently misrepresenting that Ms. Matthau’s oncologist agreed to withdraw from the surgery and to have Dr. Fisher perform the entire procedure. In fact, Dr. Fisher made the decision himself without consulting Ms. Matthau and against the wishes and recommendations of the oncologist.” (SAC, ¶ 44.) “A reasonable person in Ms. Matthau’s position, and Ms. Matthau herself, would have followed the recommendation of the oncologist, who specialized in the removal of cancerous tissue, had Dr. Fisher been truthful about the situation.” (SAC, ¶ 45.) “Based on this fraud and misrepresentation, Ms. Matthau’s execution of consent forms was not based on proper and necessary information and did not constitute the informed consent necessary for Dr. Fisher to perform the procedure to remove cancer tissue.” (SAC, ¶ 46.) “As a direct and legal result of the foregoing, Ms. Matthau’s execution of consent forms was not based on proper and necessary information and did not constitute the informed consent necessary for Dr. Fisher to perform the procedure to remove cancer tissue.” (SAC, ¶ 47.)

 

The Court finds that Defendants have met their initial burden of showing that Dr. Fisher obtained informed consent that he alone proceeds with the surgery on August 30, 2022. Dr. Fisher testifies as follows. “My long-time patient plaintiff Ashley Matthau was diagnosed with a breast lesion in 2022. Although Surgeon and colleague Dr. Kristi Funk was originally going to exercise the lesion, Ms. Matthau requested that I perform the breast excision, which I ultimately did on August 30, 2022. It was Ms. Matthau’s choice that I perform the breast excision, and this choice was acceptable to both Dr. Funk and me.” (Exh. 3 – Declaration of Garth Fisher, M.D., ¶ 2 [underlining removed]; see also Exh. 2 – Declaration of Kristi Funk, M.D., ¶ 3 [“Prior to the mass excision, my office was notified by Dr. Fisher’s office that Dr. Fisher intended on performing the surgical procedure to excise the right breast mass himself and did not require my assistance”]; id. ¶ 4 [“On July 18, 2022, I prepared an e-mail correspondence to Dr. Fisher wherein I acknowledged that his office informed my office that he intended to excise the mass without my assistance or involvement. In the e-mail correspondence, I indicated this plan was fine”].)

 

However, Plaintiff has met her burden of showing that triable issues of material fact exist regarding her lack of informed consent claim by testifying that, “[s]hortly before the procedure was to be performed, Dr. Fisher’s office unilaterally, and without informing [Plaintiff] beforehand, told Dr. Funk’s office that she was not needed for the surgery. The initial decision to cut out Dr. Funk was made entirely by Dr. Fisher with no consultation with [Plaintiff or her mother, Katherine Parnello].” (Declaration of Ashley Matthau, filed October 9, 2025, ¶ 16.) In addition, “[i]nstead of telling [Plaintiff or her] mother that he made a unilateral decision to replace Dr. Funk, Dr. Fisher mispresented to [them] that Dr. Funk had agreed for him to do the entire procedure without her. [Plaintiff and her mother] learned later that this statement was misleading, and Dr. Funk’s agreement was based on Dr. Fisher falsely telling her that [Plaintiff] wanted Dr. Fisher to repeal Dr. Funk.” (id. ¶ 17.) Plaintiff “was not pleased with [the] change.” (id. ¶ 19.) Indeed, it was Dr. Fisher’s office that had initially and highly recommended Dr. Funk to Plaintiff after she was diagnosed with breast cancer in 2022. (id. ¶ 6.) Plaintiff and her mother were told and ended up confirming that Dr. Funk co-founded the Pink Lotus Breast Center (PLBC), where she excels as an expert in minimally invasive diagnostic and treatment methods for all types of breast disease. (id. ¶ 6.) Therefore, Plaintiff selected Dr. Funk for the surgery “because of [the surgeon’s] expertise in removing breast cancer. [Plaintiff’s and her mother’s understanding] was that she, unlike Dr. Fisher, was experienced and qualified in that surgical procedure and had access to medical equipment designed to enhance the probability of a successful outcome.” (id. ¶ 19.) 

 

In their initial and supplemental reply papers, Defendants argue that there is no triable issue of material fact concerning the informed consent claim because “Plaintiff executed multiple written consent forms identifying the procedures to be performed, the physicians performing those procedures, and the associated risks and benefits. Dr. Funk’s declaration further explains the circumstances surrounding the July 18, 2022 email and confirms that she understood and agreed with the operative plan before surgery.” (Suppl. Reply, p. 10:18-22; Initial Reply, p. 7:20-25.)

 

However, Plaintiff’s declaration disputes consenting to Dr. Fisher proceeding with the August 30, 2022 surgery without Dr. Funk. Only the jury can decide whose version of the facts is credible.

 

Therefore, the request for summary adjudication of the second cause of action for lack of informed consent is denied.

 

Third Cause of Action for Breach of Contract

 

The SAC alleges that Dr. Fisher breached the parties’ agreement, that Dr. Funk would remove the cancerous tissue and Dr. Fisher would follow to cosmetically restore the breast appearance, by replacing Dr. Funk in the procedure. (SAC, ¶¶ 51, 54.)

 

In their motion, Defendants do not dispute the existence of the alleged contract. Instead, they argue that breach of contract claim lacks merit for the same reasons discussed above (i.e., Dr. Funk agreed with the plan, and Plaintiff knew before surgery that Dr. Fisher alone will proceed with the surgery on August 30, 2022).

 

“[E]ven if not modified by an executed oral agreement, ‘the parties may, by their words or conduct, waive contractual rights.’ [Citations.]” (Park v. NMSI, Inc. (2023) 96 Cal.App.5th 346, 357.) “Waiver is ordinarily a question of fact unless ‘“there are no disputed facts and only one reasonable inference may be drawn.”’ [Citation.]” (Id. at p. 358.)

 

Here, however, the Court has found that triable issues of material fact exist as to whether Plaintiff consented to Dr. Fisher proceeding with the August 30, 2022 surgery alone. Consequently, triable issues also exist as to whether Plaintiff waived the parties’ contract terms that Dr. Funk would be involved in the procedure.

 

Therefore, the request for summary adjudication of the third cause of action for breach of contract is denied.

 

Fourth Cause of Action for Medical Battery

 

The SAC alleges that Dr. Fisher committed medical battery because he performed the surgery to remove cancerous breast tissue from Plaintiff’s body after inducing Plaintiff’s consent for the surgery by fraudulently misrepresenting that Dr. Funk agreed to withdraw from the surgery. (SAC, ¶¶ 44, 58.)

 

“[B]attery and lack of informed consent are separate causes of action.” (Saxena v. Goffney (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 316, 324 (Saxena).)

 

“A claim based on lack of informed consent—which sounds in negligence—arises when the doctor performs a procedure without first adequately disclosing the risks and alternatives.” (Saxena, supra, 159 Cal.App.4th at p. 324.)

 

“In contrast, a battery is an intentional tort that occurs when a doctor performs a procedure without obtaining any consent. (See, e.g., Conte v. Girard Orthopaedic Surgeons Medical Group, Inc. (2003) 107 Cal.App.4th 1260, 1266, 132 Cal.Rptr.2d 855 [a battery ‘is an intentional and offensive touching of a person who has not consented to the touching’]; Perry v. Shaw (2001) 88 Cal.App.4th 658, 664, 106 Cal.Rptr.2d 70 [doctor committed battery—a claim distinct from professional negligence—when he ‘performed an operation to which [plaintiff] did not consent’]; Warren v. Schecter (1997) 57 Cal.App.4th 1189, 1194, 67 Cal.Rptr.2d 573 [discussing statute of limitations for ‘medical malpractice action arising out of a failure to obtain informed consent to surgery’]; Nelson v. Gaunt (1981) 125 Cal.App.3d 623, 634, 178 Cal.Rptr. 167 [battery ‘should be reserved for those circumstances where a doctor performs an operation to which the patient has not consented’].)” (Saxena, supra, 159 Cal.App.4th at pp. 324-325.)

 

Despite those differences, and even if the Court were to find that Defendants have met their initial burden of proving that Plaintiff’s medical battery claim lacks merit, Plaintiff has met her burden of showing that triable issues of material fact exist as to the battery claim as well for the following reasons.

 

“The element of lack of consent to the particular contact is an essential element of battery.” (Rains v. Superior Court (1984) 150 Cal.App.3d 933, 938 (Rains) [a case cited by Plaintiff’s opposition].)

 

“In Rains, the plaintiffs, psychiatric patients, agreed to beat each other up after defendants fraudulently represented to them this ‘sluggo therapy’ would have a therapeutic benefit.” (Ashcraft v. King (1991) 228 Cal.App.3d 604, 613.)

 

The patients sued the psychiatrists for battery and later appealed the trial court’s order sustaining the psychiatrists’ demurrer without leave to amend. (Rains, supra, 150 Cal.App.3d at p. 935.)

 

The issue before the Court of Appeal was: “Whether a viable cause for battery has been or might be pleaded turns upon whether plaintiffs’ consent to use of physical violence by defendants as a therapeutic treatment may be vitiated by allegations that defendant psychiatrists actually did not administer such violence for any therapeutic purposes but for the improper ulterior purpose of subjugating plaintiffs and controlling their behavior to defendants’ personal advantage.” (Rains, supra, 150 Cal.App.3d at p. 936.)

 

The appellate court answered in the affirmative, holding that “deceit as to the therapeutic purpose of contact vitiates consent for the same reason as does deceit as to the lack of professional capacity of the person effecting the purported therapeutic contact.” (Rains, supra, 150 Cal.App.3d at p. 940 [italics added].)

 

“‘To vitiate consent the mistake “must extend to the essential character of the act itself, which is to say that which makes it harmful or offensive ... rather than to some collateral matter which merely operates as an inducement...”’ [Citations.]” (Ashcraft v. King, supra, 228 Cal.App.3d at p. 614 [italics added].) “Where … the patient has expressly conditioned her consent on certain acts being performed by the doctor, the patient has made that condition or event a matter of primary importance. To label such a condition merely ‘collateral’ is to ignore the patient’s ‘right, in the exercise of control over his own body to determine whether or not to submit to lawful medical treatment.’ [Citation.]” (Ibid.)

 

Here, the Court finds that triable questions of material fact exist regarding whether (1) Dr. Funk’s participation in the surgery on August 30, 2022, was an essential character of the surgery that Plaintiff consented to, (2) Dr. Fisher used fraudulent misrepresentations to induce Plaintiff’s consent to only have him conduct the surgery; and (3) whether such misrepresentations vitiated Plaintiff’s consent. In their reply papers, Defendants do not address the above law. In addition, their arguments fail to convince the Court that there are no triable issues of material fact regarding the battery claim.

 

Therefore, the request for summary adjudication of the fourth cause of action for medical battery is denied.

 

Fifth Cause of Action for Fraud

 

The fraud claim alleges the following, among other things. “In or about July 2022, Dr. Fisher made a verbal representation to Ms. Matthau that he and Dr. Funk had decided together than [sic\ Dr. Fisher would perform the surgical procedure to remove Ms. Matthau’s breast cancer cells, that Dr. Funk would not participate in that surgical procedure, and that the procedure would be performed at Dr. Fisher’s surgical center rather than at a hospital.” (SAC, ¶ 65.) “At the time Dr. Fisher made these representations, he knew that they were false. The truth was that Dr. Funk was not consulted and did not agree to be excluded from the surgical procedure or for the procedure to be done at any location other than a hospital with the proper and necessary equipment. Dr. Fisher made the decision on his own, to maximize his profits, without regard to the risks, and dictated terms to Dr. Funk.” (SAC, ¶ 66.) “At the time Dr. Fisher made these representations, his intent was to induce reliance so that Ms. Matthau would not challenge his decision to excluded Dr. Funk from the surgical procedure and would not challenge his decision to perform the procedure in his surgical center rather than at a hospital.” (SAC, ¶ 67.) “Ms. Matthau actually relied on Dr. Fisher’s representation and abided by his decision to allow him to perform the procedure without Dr. Funk and in his surgical center rather than at a hospital.” (SAC, ¶ 68.)

 

In their motion, Defendants make the same arguments that Dr. Funk agreed to the surgery, Dr. Fisher believed that Dr. Funk had agreed to the plan, Plaintiff was aware that only Dr. Fisher would be conducting the surgery on August 30, 2022, and it was within the standard of care for Dr. Fisher to perform the surgery at his surgical center instead of a hospital. (Motion, p. 16:8-20.) Defendants further argue that Plaintiff was aware of the insurance issue before the surgical procedure, and Plaintiff’s mother was informed before the surgery that the payment will be out of pocket. (Id. at pp. 16:26-17:7.)

 

Even if Defendants have met their initial burden of showing that Plaintiff’s fraud claim lacks merit, the Court finds that triable issues of material fact exist regarding whether Dr. Fisher obtained Plaintiff’s consent through misrepresenting Dr. Funk’s lack of participation in August 30, 2022 surgery.

 

Therefore, the request for summary adjudication of the fifth cause of action for fraud is denied.  

 

Sixth Cause of Action for Breach of Fiduciary Duty

 

The breach of fiduciary duty claim alleges that “Dr. Fisher breached his fiduciary duties to Ms. Matthau when he took the steps previously alleged to replace Dr. Funk as the surgeon to remove the cancerous tissue and to change the location of the procedure from a hospital to his surgical center.” (SAC, ¶ 76.)

 

Defendants’ move for summary adjudication of that claim, making the same arguments as the ones above. (Motion, p. 17:19-24 [“As discussed above, the declaration of Dr. Funk directly refutes the plaintiffs fraud contentions. Further, Dr. Funk and Dr. Stevens agree it was appropriate to perform the procedure at a surgical center. Further Dr. Stevens opined it was appropriate for Dr. Fisher to performed the procedure without Dr. Funk's involvement. The plaintiff knew the surgical plan well before the procedure occurred as the surgical plan was discussed with her and she signed multiple informed consents prior to the same”].)

 

The Court has found triable issues of material fact exist regarding the allegations underlying the breach of fiduciary duty claim.

 

Therefore, the request for summary adjudication of the sixth cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty is denied.  

 

Seventh Cause of Action for Invasion of Privacy

 

The SAC alleges that: “Defendants violated Ms. Matthau’s privacy in at least the following two ways. First, Defendants allowed Ms. Medeiros, who had no medial [sic] basis to be present, to attend sensitive procedures, including surgical procedures. Second, Defendants transmitted images and information to a physician who was not necessary for Ms. Matthau’s care and treatment without Ms. Matthau’s consent.” (SAC, ¶ 83.) “As a direct and proximate result of this invocation of privacy, Ms. Matthau suffered, and continues to suffer, emotional distress, mental discomfort, embarrassment, humiliation, anxiety, and worry.” (SAC, ¶ 84.)

 

Defendants move for summary adjudication of that claim, arguing the following.

 

“The cause of action for invasion of privacy, as asserted by the plaintiff, is likely governed by either CACTI 1800 or CACTI 1801.

 

“As for CACI 1800 (intrusion into private affairs), the plaintiff must show: 1. She had a reasonable expectation of privacy in the pre-operative and operative setting; 2. That Dr. Fisher intentionally intruded in the pre-operative or operative setting; 3. that Dr. Fisher's intrusion would be highly offensive to a reasonable person; 4. The plaintiff was harmed; and 5. Dr. Fisher’s conduct was a substantial factor in causing the harm.

 

“As for CACI 1801 (public disclosure of private facts), the plaintiff must show: 1. Dr. Fisher publicized private information concerning the plaintiff; 2. A reasonable person in the plaintiff's position would consider the publicity highly offensive; 3. Dr. Fisher knew, or acted with reckless disregard of the fact, that a reasonable person in the plaintiff's position would consider the publicity highly offensive; 4. the private information was not of legitimate public concern; 5. The plaintiff was harmed; and 6. The conduct was a substantial factor in causing the plaintiff’ harm.”

 

(Motion, p. 18:7-19.)

 

Defendants argue that the allegation that Dr. Fisher transmitted Plaintiff’s image to another physician does not constitute invasion of privacy under the public disclosure theory because (1) the image was transmitted to only one individual, and (2) Plaintiff signed a photographic video release and consent form. (Motion, p. 19:3-12.)

 

As for Plaintiff’s claim that Ms. Medeiros was allowed to attend sensitive procedures, Plaintiff’s mother testified that both she and Plaintiff knew of Ms. Mederios involvement and presence in the surgical procedures. (Motion, p. 19:13-15.)

 

The Court finds triable issues of material fact exist as to the invasion of privacy claim at least with regard to Plaintiff’s allegation of Ms. Mederios. As Plaintiff argues, “[k]nowledge does not mean consent [of Mederios’s presence].” (Opposition, p. 15:16; see also Matthau Decl., 24, 25, 26 [testifying that Mederios’s presence during preparation of the procedure and during the surgery made Plaintiff uncomfortable as it reminded her of traumatic, sexual assault events that were similar to the ones Mederios was being accused of being associated with at the time].)

 

Therefore, the request for summary adjudication of the seventh cause of action for invasion of privacy is denied.  

 

Eighth Cause of Action for Negligent Infliction of Emotional Distress

 

“The law of negligent infliction of emotional distress [“NIED”] in California is typically analyzed, as it was in this case, by reference to two ‘theories’ of recovery: the ‘bystander’ theory and the ‘direct victim’ theory.” (Burgess v. Superior Court (1992) 2 Cal.4th 1064, 1071 (Burgess).)

 

“The distinction between the ‘bystander’ and ‘direct victim’ cases is found in the source of the duty owed by the defendant to the plaintiff.” (Burgess, supra, 2 Cal.4th at p. 1072.)

 

“[B]ystander liability is premised upon a defendant’s violation of a duty not to negligently cause emotional distress to people who observe conduct which causes harm to another.” (Burgess, supra, 2 Cal.4th at p. 1073 [emphasis added].)

 

The California Supreme Court has held that a plaintiff may recover NIED damages based on a bystander theory “only if … said plaintiff: (1) is closely related to the injury victim; (2) is present at the scene of the injury-producing event at the time it occurs and is then aware that it is causing injury to the victim; and (3) as a result suffers serious emotional distress-a reaction beyond that which would be anticipated in a disinterested witness and which is not an abnormal response to the circumstances.’ [Citation.]” (Bird v. Saenz (2002) 28 Cal.4th 910, 916–917, citing Thing v. La Chusa (1989) 48 Cal.3d 644, 667–668 (Thing) [italics added].) “Thing’s requirement that the plaintiff be contemporaneously aware of the injury-producing event has not been interpreted as requiring visual perception of an impact on the victim. [However,] [a] plaintiff may recover based on an event perceived by other senses so long as the event is contemporaneously understood as causing injury to a close relative. ([See e.g.] Wilks v. Hom (1992) 2 Cal.App.4th 1264, 1272–1273, 3 Cal.Rptr.2d 803 [plaintiff was in the living room speaking to her children in their bedrooms when she saw, heard and felt one bedroom explode from a gas leak].)” (Bird v. Saenz, supra, 28 Cal.4th at pp. 916–917.)

 

Direct victim cases, on the other hand, involve “cases where a duty arising from a preexisting relationship is negligently breached.” (Burgess, supra, 2 Cal.4th at p. 1074 [italics added].) For example, in Burgess, a mother sued an obstetrician, alleging that she suffered emotional distress as a result of the obstetrician’s negligence in delivering her baby. (Id. at p. 1070.) The California Supreme Court held that the mother was a direct victim (instead of a bystander) because the obstetrician had a physician-patient relationship with the mother and her fetus. (Id. at p. 1076 [“It is in light of both these physical and emotional realities that the obstetrician and the pregnant woman enter into a physician-patient relationship. It cannot be gainsaid that both parties understand that the physician owes a duty to the pregnant woman with respect to the medical treatment provided to her fetus. Any negligence during delivery which causes injury to the fetus and resultant emotional anguish to the mother, therefore, breaches a duty owed directly to the mother”]; see also id. at p. 1078 [“Under the facts of this case, Burgess is not a ‘bystander’ for purposes of bringing a claim for compensation for damages for her serious emotional distress”].)

 

Here, the NIED claim is brought by Plaintiff’s mother, Parnello, against Defendants and alleges the following. “By virtue of Dr. Fisher’s knowledge of the family relationship between Ms. Matthau and Ms. Parnello, Defendants’ duty of care extended to include Ms. Parnello.” (SAC, ¶ 88.) “As described [in the pleading], Defendants where [sic] negligent on numerous occasions while providing medical services to Ms. Matthau and while Ms. Parnello was present to observe and have personal knowledge of the negligence and severe physical and emotional injuries Ms. Matthau suffered as a result of that negligence.” (SAC, ¶ 89.) “Ms. Parnello suffered, and continues to suffer, emotional distress, suffering, anguish, fright, horror, nervousness, grief, anxiety, worry, shock, humiliation, mental discomfort, embarrassment, and worry. She has suffered damages in an amount not yet precisely attainable, but which she will establish at trial.” (SAC, ¶ 90.) “Defendants’ negligence was a substantial factor in causing Ms. Parnello’s serious emotional distress.” (SAC, ¶ 91.)

 

Defendants argue, and the Court agrees, that Parnello’s NIED claim is based on a bystander theory. Plaintiff’s argument that Parnello was a direct victim is unpersuasive. (See Opposition, pp. 16:13-17:15 [arguing that Parnello was a direct victim because she feared that cancer would remain in her daughter’s body, had advanced money to pay for the surgery, and was present at the appointments. However, unlike cases where a pregnant mother sued her obstetrician for the negligent delivery of her baby, there was no such pre-existing relationship between Dr. Fisher and Parnello here].) The Court declines to extend the direct victim theory in a case such as this one without any law directing it to do so.

 

The Court finds that Defendants have met their initial burden of proving that Parnello’s NIED claim lacks merit. As stated above, to prevail on a bystander theory, an NIED plaintiff must prove that she contemporaneously perceived and understood the event that caused injury to their close relative. Here, Parnello does not dispute that during her deposition, she testified that she did not witness the surgeries and did not witness any of the actual procedures. (Motion, p. 20:20-24.)

 

The Court also finds that Parnello has failed to meet her burden of showing that triable issues of material fact exist as to her bystander claim. Parnello argues: “[B]ystander liability is based on the negligence in Dr. Fisher’s delay in paying attention to the pathology report. [Citation.] It was negligent to fail to perform the procedure in a hospital where pathology reports would be contemporaneous. Ms. Parnello was present to witness the shock of learning Dr. Fisher waited approximately 3 weeks to learn that he did not leave clear margins and the scurry to schedule an emergency second excision procedure. She was present to see Dr. Fisher’s expression of horror when he finally read the pathology report. Her being on the scene to witness that negligence satisfies the requirements for a bystander action.” (Opposition, pp. 20:25-21:3.) However, it was not Dr. Fisher’s so-called expression of horror when he read the pathology report that injured Plaintiff. The SAC alleges that Plaintiff was injured by the medical practice of Dr. Fisher, including his failure to perform the surgery with the required standard of care. Therefore, Parnello has failed to meet her burden.

 

Accordingly, the request for summary adjudication of the eighth cause of action for NIED is granted.

 

Punitive Damages

 

Defendants do not present substantive arguments regarding Plaintiff’s request for punitive damages. They only argue that “if the Court grants summary adjudication as to causes of action that could rise to punitive damages [e.g., fraud], then summary adjudication must be granted as to plaintiffs’ claims for punitive damages.” (Motion, p. 21:14-16.)

 

The Court has not granted summary adjudication as to causes of action that could give rise to punitive damages.

 

Accordingly, the request for summary adjudication of Plaintiff’s request for punitive damages is denied.

 

Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, the Motion for Summary Judgment or, in the Alternative, Summary Adjudication is GRANTED IN PART and DENIED IN PART as follows.

 

The request for summary adjudication as to the eighth cause of action for negligent infliction of emotional distress is GRANTED.

 

The motion is otherwise DENIED.

On May 14, 2024, Plaintiff filed the operative First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) against Defendants Next Health, Inc. The matter came for hearing on July 16, 2024; the demurrer was sustained in part as the second cause of action without leave to amend, overruled in part as to the first cause of action, and the motion to strike was denied.

 

On July 30, 2024, NHI filed an Answer to the FAC.

Motion to Compel Deposition Standard

 

      Pursuant to the Code of Civil Procedure Section 2025.010, any party may obtain discovery (subject to the scope and restrictions provided by law) “by taking in California the oral deposition of any person, including any party to the action. The person deposed may be a natural person, an organization such as a public or private corporation, a partnership, an association, or a governmental agency.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.010.) A deposition subpoena is the only means by which discovery may be obtained from a nonparty. (Unzipped Apparel, LLC v. Bader¿(2007) 156 Cal.App.4th 123, 127.)¿¿ 

 

      “If, after service of a deposition notice, a party to the action or an officer, director, managing agent, or employee of a party, or a person designated by an organization that is a party under Section 2025.230, without having served a valid objection under Section 2025.410, fails to appear for examination, or to proceed with it, or to produce for inspection any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing described in the deposition notice, the party giving the notice may move for an order compelling the deponent's attendance and testimony, and the production for inspection of any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing described in the deposition notice.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subd. (a).) 

 

Meet and Confer

 

      “The motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040, or, when the deponent fails to attend the deposition and produce the documents, electronically stored information, or things described in the deposition notice, by a declaration stating that the petitioner has contacted the deponent to inquire about the nonappearance.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subd. (b)(2).) Specifically, “[a] meet and confer declaration in support of a motion shall state facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt, either in person, by telephone, or by videoconference, to informally resolve each issue presented by the motion.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2016.040, subd. (a).) “[A]ttempting informal resolution means more than the mere attempt by the discovery proponent ‘to persuade the objector of the error of his ways” and “a reasonable and good faith attempt at informal resolution entails something more than bickering with [opposing]counsel.... Rather, the law requires that counsel attempt to talk the matter over, compare their views, consult, and deliberate.” (Clement v. Alegre (2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 1277, 1294.) 

 

      Here, Plaintiff advances the declaration of its counsel of record, Alex Farzan, attesting that the parties engaged in meet and confer efforts between April 8, 2026 and April 14, 2026. (Farzan Decl., ¶¶11-12, Exs. G-H.) Thereafter, the parties were engaged in motion practice regarding NHI’s motion for protective order and attended an Informal Discovery Conference (“IDC”). (Id.) Furthermore, the Court expressly stated that Plaintiff had fulfilled his IDC obligation and was free to file the present motion. (Min. Order, 5/28/26 at p. 1 [“The Court finds that both parties have satisfied their IDC obligation and may file their discovery motion(s), if needed.”].)

 

      Thus, the Court finds that Plaintiff has satisfied his meet and confer requirement.

 

Analysis

 

      Plaintiff moves for an order compelling the depositions of Kevin Peake (President/Co-Founder of NHI) and Dr. Darshan Shah (Founder/CEO of NHI) on the grounds that NHI refused to produce them for depositions despite: (1) Mr. Peake personally verified NHI’s written discovery responses to Plaintiff’s Form Interrogatories (Set One) and Requests for Production of Documents (Set One) based on his personal knowledge of the facts of this case (Farzan Decl., ¶4, Exs. A-B); (2) NHI’s current person most knowledge (“PMK”), Amy Holman and former PMK, Kai Belveal identified Mr. Peake and Dr. Shah as the having personal knowledge of the material issues of this case (Farzan Decl., ¶¶6-7); (3) Plaintiff exhausted less intrusive discovery methods including written discovery, two PMK depositions, and meet and confer efforts (Farzan Decl., ¶¶3, 5-9, 12); and (4) this Court advised that these depositions would likely be compelled following the IDC.

 

      Here, Plaintiff served deposition notices for Mr. Peake and Dr. Shah on March 20, 2026 set for April 21, 2026 and April 22, 2026, respectively. (Farzan Decl., ¶10, Exs. E-F.) As briefly mentioned above, the parties engaged in meet and confer efforts regarding the taking of these depositions. (Id., ¶¶11-12, Exs. G-H.) The parties also engaged in motion practice pertaining to NHI’s motion for protective order, which was denied on June 26, 2026. (Farzan Decl., ¶12; Min. Order, 6/26/26.) Consistent with the Court’s advisement at the IDC and tentative ruling on the motion for protective order, Plaintiff has shown that the deposition testimonies of Mr., Peake and Dr. Shah are relevant to the material issues and reasonably calculated to discover facts to support any claims and defenses in this case. Specifically, Mr. Peake and Dr. Shah have been identified as material witnesses with personal knowledge of NHI’s decision to discontinue the use of the Vivo Wellness infrared sauna pods and what became of the Subject Light Bed; identity of the entity that sold or supplied the Subject Light Bed to NHI; and Dr. Shah’s medical oversight of the services at issue and his assessment of the subject incident. (Mot. at 8:24-10:13.) NHI does not dispute these facts, thus concedes on these issues. (See California Rules of Court Rule 3.1113(a); see also Rule 3.1320(f);¿Herzberg v. County of Plumas (2005) 133 Cal.App.4th 1, 20 [the party who fails to argue an issue abandons that issue].) 

 

            Request for Sanctions

 

“If a motion under [Code of Civil Procedure, section 2025.450, subdivision (a)] is granted, the court shall impose a monetary sanction . . . in favor of the party who noticed the deposition and against the deponent or the party with whom the deponent is affiliated, unless the court finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450, subd. (g)(1).) 

 

“The court may award sanctions under the Discovery Act in favor of a party who files a motion to compel discovery, even though no opposition to the motion was filed, or opposition to the motion was withdrawn, or the requested discovery was provided to the moving party after the motion was filed.” (Cal. Rules Ct., Rule 3.1348, subd. (a).) “[T]he burden is on the moving party to show (by declarations) the facts essential to an award of sanctions: e.g., the opposing party’s failure to make discovery, reasonable efforts to resolve the matter informally, etc. The burden then shifts to the party against whom sanctions are being sought to establish some excuse or justification for the conduct in question.”¿(Weil & Brown, Cal. Practice Guide:¿ Civ. Pro. Before Trial (The Rutter Group June 2021 Update) ¶ 8:2015.)¿ 

 

            Here, Mr. Farzan seeks discovery sanctions in the amount of $10,000.00 against NHI for having to bring the present motion, meet and confer correspondence, motion practice for the motion for protective order, preparing and filing Joint IDC statement, attending the IDC, reviewing an opposition to this present motion, preparing a reply for this present motion, and attending the hearing on the present motion for a total of 20.0 hours at an hourly rate of $500.00. I.      Requests for Production and Interrogatories

The requesting party may move for an order compelling further responses to request for production of documents if any of the following apply: (1) a statement of compliance with the demand is incomplete; (2) a representation of inability to comply is inadequate, incomplete, or evasive; or (3) an objection in the response is without merit or too general. The requesting party may move for an order compelling further responses to interrogatories if any of the following apply: (1) an answer to a particular interrogatory is evasive or incomplete; (2) an exercise of the option to produce documents under section 2030.230 is unwarranted or the required specification of those documents is inadequate; or (3) an objection to an interrogatory is without merit or too general. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (a).)

 

The motion must be filed within 45 days from responses, supplemental responses, or a specific later date agreed to in writing (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2031.310, subd. (c), 2030.300, subd. (c).) Failure to make motion within the specified period constitutes waiver of right to compel a further response. (Sexton v. The motion shall be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Code of Civil Procedure section 2016.040. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2031.310, subd. (b)(2), 2030.300, subd. (b)(1).) The purpose of the meet and confer requirement is to encourage the parties to make serious effort to work out differences informally and avoid a need for a formal order. (Stewart v. Colonial Western Agency, Inc. (2001) 87 Cal.App.4th 1006, 1016.) 

 

Under Rule of Court 3.1345, the movant must submit a separate statement along with the motion to compel further responses. Otherwise, the movant may submit a concise outline of the discovery request and its response in dispute. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2031.310, subd. (b)(1), 2030.300(b)(2).)

Background

 

            On January 22, 2026, Plaintiff the People of the State of California (“Plaintiff”) filed the operative Complaint against Defendants Hamid Enayati a/k/a Andrew Enayati a/k/a Andy Enayati, individually and as a Trustee of the ENA Property Trust dated 4/15/13; Nicole Massarat a/k/a Nicole Enayati; Nina Enayati; Jaleh Forouhar a/k/a Jaleh Enayati, individually and as a Trustee of the Marital Trust under the ENA1 Property Trust dated 4/15/13; Nina Property Management, Inc.; The 1028, LLC; 1111 11th Street, LLC; 1837 Glendon Avenue, LLC; ENA1, LLC; ENA2, LLC; ENA3, LLC; ENA4, LLC; ENA5, LLC; ENA6, LLC; ENA7, LLC; ENA8, LLC; ENA9, LLC; ENA10, LLC (jointly, “Enayati Defendants”); and DOES 1 through 10 (collectively, “Defendants”), asserting causes of action for: (1) Violation of Unfair Competition Law (“UCL”) and (2) Violation of the False Advertising Law.

 

            On May 4, 2026, Enayati Defendants filed the instant Demurrer to the Complaint. On June 8, 2026, Plaintiff filed an Opposition. On July 1, 2026, Enayati Defendants filed a Reply.

 

Request for Judicial Notice

 

            Enayati Defendants request judicial notice of the following documents and exhibits concerning emails, meeting minutes, commission reports, memoranda, and videos related to the enactment of Santa Monica Municipal Code Section 6.22. Plaintiff objects to the extent that Enayati Defendants seek the Court’s consideration of the truth of matters asserted therein.

 

“Regulations and legislative enactments issued by or under the authority of the United States or any public entity in the United States” may be judicially noticed. (Evid. Code, § 452, subd. (b); see also Montalvo v. Madera Unified Sch. Dist. Bd. of Education (1971) 21 Cal.App.3d 323, 329; U.S. v. Schneiderman (S.D. Cal. 1952) 106 F.Supp. 731, 734; People of State of Cal. v. Coast Federal Sav. & Loan Ass’n (S.D. Cal. 1951) 98 F.Supp. 311, 316.) Likewise, “courts must take judicial notice of the public and private official acts of the legislative, executive, and judicial departments of this state and of the United States government.” (Arnold v. Universal Oil Land Co. (1941) 45 Cal.App.2d 522, 529.) 

 

            The request for judicial notice is GRANTED per Evidence Code Section 452, subdivisions (b) and (c) except as to hearsay and facts in dispute. 

 

Legal Standard

 

      “The primary function of a pleading is to give the other party notice so that it may prepare its case [citation], and a defect in a pleading that otherwise properly notifies a party cannot be said to affect substantial rights.” (Harris v. City of Santa Monica (2013) 56 Cal.4th 203, 240.)¿ 

 

      “A¿demurrer¿tests the legal sufficiency of the factual allegations in a complaint.” (Ivanoff v. Bank of America, N.A.¿(2017) 9 Cal.App.5th 719, 725.) The Court looks to whether “the complaint alleges facts sufficient to state a cause of action or discloses a complete defense.” (Id.) The Court does not “read passages from a complaint in isolation; in reviewing a ruling on a demurrer, we read the complaint ‘as a whole and its parts in their context.’ [Citation.]” (West v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A. (2013) 214 Cal.App.4th 780, 804.) The Court “assume[s] the truth of the properly pleaded factual allegations, facts that reasonably can be inferred from those expressly pleaded and matters of which judicial notice has been taken.” (Harris, supra, 56 Cal.4th p. 240.) “The court does not, however, assume the truth of contentions, deductions or conclusions of law. [Citation.]” (Durell v. Sharp Healthcare (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 1350, 1358.)¿¿¿  

¿ 

      A general demurrer may be brought under Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (e) if insufficient facts are stated to support the cause of action asserted or under section 430.10, subdivision (a), where the court has no jurisdiction of the subject of the cause of action alleged in the pleading. All other grounds listed in Section 430.10, including uncertainty under subdivision (f), are special demurrers. Special demurrers are not allowed in limited jurisdiction courts. (Code Civ. Proc., § 92, subd. (c).)¿¿¿  

¿ 

      Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the complainant to show the Court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Id.)¿¿ 

 

Meet and Confer

 

      Prior to filing a demurrer, the demurring party is required to meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by videoconference with the party who filed the pleading demurred to for the purposes of determining whether an agreement can be reached through a filing of an amended pleading that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41.)¿¿ 

 

      Here, Enayati Defendants’ advance the declaration of their counsel of record, Joseph Trujillo attests to meeting and conferring with Plaintiff’s counsel via videoconference on April 28, 2026. (Trujillo Decl., ¶4.) The parties were unable to reach an informal agreement. (Id.) Thus, Enayati Defendants fulfilled the meet and confer requirement.

 

 

Analysis

 

            Here, Enayati Defendants argue that the Complaint fails entirely because it is premised on a violation of Santa Monica Municipal Code (“SMMC”) Section 6.22, which (1) is preempted by the Costa Hawkins Act under Civil Code Section 1954.50; (2) contains no exemption for preexisting lawful uses; and (3) fails to comply with the requirements set forth in the Coastal Act under California Public Resources Code Section 30000.

 

            Preemption

 

“Ruling on a facial challenge to a local ordinance, the court considers the text of the measure itself, not its application to any particular circumstances or individual.” (California Apartment Assn. v. City of Pasadena (2025) 117 Cal.App.5th 187, 233.) “The party claiming that general state law preempts a local ordinance has the burden of demonstrating preemption.” (Rental Housing Assn. of Northern Alameda County v. City of Oakland (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 741, 752.) “A conflict exists if the local legislation ‘duplicates, contradicts, or enters an area fully occupied by general law, either expressly or by legislative implication.’” (Id.)

 

Here, it is undisputed that the Complaint is premised on Enayati Defendants’ alleged violation of SMMC Section 6.22.050, 6.22.060, 6.22.070, and 6.22.090. (See Compl., ¶¶9-26, 29-30, 33-38, 40-49, 58, 69-74, 76, 78.) Under SMMC Section 6.22.090, “No person shall undertake, maintain, authorize, aid, facilitate or advertise any lease of a rental housing unit that does not comply with this Chapter.” (SMMC § 6.22.090.) Specifically, “[a] landlord and tenant may enter into a lease only if the tenant will use and occupy the rental housing unit as the tenant’s primary residence. No later than sixty days following the commencement of the lease, the tenant must provide and the landlord must receive documentation as specified in Section 6.22.020(c) that the rental housing unit is the tenant’s primary residence. Landlord shall retain this documentation throughout the duration of tenant’s lease and shall provide it to the Director of the Community Development Department or delegee on request.” (Id., § 6.22.050.) Furthermore, a landlord and tenant may enter into a lease “only if it is in writing and for an initial lease term of no less than one year” and “the rental housing unit is an unfurnished rental housing unit.” (Id., §§ 6.22.060-6.22.070.)

 

A “primary residence” is defined as “the usual place of return for housing of an owner or tenant, as documented by at least two of the following: motor vehicle registration; driver's license or California State identification card; voter registration; income tax return; proof of payment of resident tuition to a California public institution of higher education, including proof of payment in accordance with an exemption to payment of nonresident tuition under California Assembly Bill 540, the California DREAM Act; or a utility bill. An owner or tenant can only have one primary residence.” (SMMC § 6.22.020.) An “unfurnished rental housing unit” is defined as “a rental housing unit that is delivered by the landlord to the tenant without any furnishings, computer equipment, housewares, bed linens, towels, artwork, televisions, entertainment systems, or appliances except for a stove, a refrigerator, a microwave, a dishwasher, a clothes washing machine and/or a clothes dryer.” (Id.)

 

            To the extent that Enayati Defendants contend SMMC Section 6.22 is preempted by the Costa Hawkins Act, the Court disagrees. “The Costa-Hawkins Act was enacted in 1995 ‘to relieve landlords from some of the burdens of ‘strict’ and ‘extreme’ rent control, which the proponents of [the] Costa-Hawkins [Act] contended unduly and unfairly interfered with the free market.’ ” (California Apartment Assn., supra, 117 Cal.App.5th 231.) The alleged violations do not concern controlling rent prices initially set by landlords for vacant units nor does SMMC Section 6.22 make any mention of rent increases/levels. Similarly, Enayati Defendants assertion that the Santa Monica City Charter gave exclusive authority to the Rent Board to regulate rent-related matters disregards that this authority is limited to enacting replacement regulations where prior regulations have been invalidated or found to be unenforceable by a court, the State, or Federal legislation. (Santa Monica City Charter (“SMCC”) § 1802, subd. (e).) There is no indication that the City Council is prohibited from enacting initial regulations.

 

            Compliance with the Coastal Act

 

Next, the Coastal Act under California Public Resources Code Section 30000 et seq. applies to properties within the boundaries of a coastal zone. (See Sierra Club v. California Coastal Com. (2005) 35 Cal.4th 839, 853.) As such, Plaintiff argues that SMMC Section 6.22 is not invalid or preempted by the Coastal Act because the Complaint addresses only non-coastal properties and SMMC Section 6.22 applies exclusively to non-coastal properties. (Opp. at 1:24-2:1, 11:22-12:9, fn. 10.) Although Enayati Defendants contend that SMMC Section 6.22 extends to the coastal zone, it does not appear on the face of the Complaint, the judicially noticed facts, nor the statute itself that it applies to such properties.

           

            First Cause of Action for Violation of UCL

 

“To bring a UCL claim, a plaintiff must show either an (1) ‘unlawful, unfair, or fraudulent business act or practice,’ or (2) ‘unfair, deceptive, untrue or misleading advertising.’ ” (Adhav v. Midway Rent A Car, Inc. (2019) 37 Cal.App.5th 954, 970.) “ ‘Unlawful’ conduct includes any business practice or act forbidden by local, state or federal statutes or by regulations or case law. [Citation].” (Id.) “ ‘a UCL fraud plaintiff must allege he or she was motivated to act or refrain from action based on the truth or falsity of a defendant’s statement, not merely on the fact it was made.’ [Citation].” (Id.) A plaintiff alleging unfair business practices under these statutes must state with reasonable particularity the facts supporting the statutory elements of the violation.” (Khoury v. Maly's of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 619.) However, “[w]hether a practice is deceptive, fraudulent, or unfair is generally a question of fact which requires ‘consideration and weighing of evidence from both sides’ and which usually cannot be made on demurrer.” (Linear Technology Corp., supra, 152 Cal.App.4th 135.) 

 

The Complaint alleges, inter alia, that Enayati Defendants engaged in unlawful business acts or practices by entering into rental agreement knowing that the occupants did not intend to or would not use the rental housing as their primary residence in violation of SMMC Section 6.22.050; entering into rental agreements for initial terms of less than one year in violation of SMMC Section 6.22.060; renting furnished rental housing units in violation of SMMC Section 6.22.070; failing to provide mandatory notice as required under SMMC Section 6.22.080; and failing to submit vacancy registration forms to the Santa Monica Rent Control Board in violation of SMCC Section 1803, subdivision (q) and Santa Monica Rent Control Board Regulation 13001, subdivision (g). (Compl., ¶76A.) Furthermore, the Enayati Defendants engaged in fraudulent business acts or practices by knowing that the rental housing units were furnished, thus not unfurnished rental housing units as defined in SMMC Section 6.22.020 as asserted in the written lease agreement; knowing the Airbnb guests would not use the housing units as their primary residence as defined under SMMC Section 6.22.020; and knowing that Airbnb guests had booked the rental units for periods of less than one year violating the terms of the written lease stating otherwise. (Id., ¶76B.) Additionally, the Enayati Defendants engaged in unfair business acts or practices by executing written lease agreements purporting to comply with SMMC Section 6.22. (Id., ¶76C.)

 

Taking these allegations as true, Plaintiff sufficiently alleges that the Enayati Defendants engaged in unlawful and unfair business acts or practices that were violation of the provisions set forth in SMMC Section 6.22 by creating and entering into written lease agreements with Airbnb guests, which purported to comply with SMMC Section 6.22 when they in fact did not.

 

Accordingly, the demurrer to the first cause of action is OVERRULED.

 

            Second Cause of Action for Violation of False Advertising Law (“FAL”)

 

“California's FAL prohibits any ‘unfair, deceptive, untrue, or misleading advertising.’ ” (Ketayi v. Health Enrollment Group (S.D. Cal. 2021) 516 F.Supp.3d 1092, 1129.) “California laws prohibit not only advertising which is false, but also advertising which[,] although true, is either actually misleading or which has a capacity, likelihood or tendency to deceive or confuse the public.” (Id.)

 

 “To state a claim under the UCL, FAL, and CLRA, [Plaintiff] must allege facts satisfying the ‘reasonable consumer’ standard, i.e., that members of the public are likely to be deceived.” (LeGrand v. Abbott Laboratories (N.D. Cal. 2023) 655 F.Supp.3d 871, 890.) “ ‘Likely to deceive’ implies more than a mere possibility that the advertisement might conceivably be misunderstood by some few consumers viewing it in an unreasonable manner.” (Id.) “Rather, the phrase indicates that the ad is such that it is probable that a significant portion of the general consuming public or of targeted consumers, acting reasonably in the circumstances, could be misled.” (Id.) “In determining whether a statement is misleading under the statute, ‘the primary evidence in a false advertising case is the advertising itself.’ ” (Id.) “Generalized, vague, and unspecified assertions constitute ‘mere puffery’ upon which a reasonable consumer could not rely, and hence are not actionable.” (Oestreicher v. Alienware Corp. (N.D. Cal. 2008) 544 F.Supp.2d 964, 973.)

 

The Complaint alleges, inter alia, that the Enayati Defendants presented prospective short-term rental guests with a lease addenda, which stated the units were being delivered vacant and any furniture in the unit was for staging purposes even though this was untrue. (Compl., ¶78A.) The Enayati Defendants also told prospective short-term rental guests that the one-year minimum lease term provisions were only for purposes of preventing squatters, that booking through Airbnb counted as lawful notice of termination ending the lease before the one-year minimum term had passed even though this was misleading and untrue, in efforts to induce concerned guests to rent the units. (Id., ¶78B.) The Enayati Defendants also told prospective short-term rental guests that the primary residence provision of the written lease agreements were only for purposes of preventing squatters to mislead guests to rent the units. (Id., ¶78C.)

 

Taking these allegations as true, Plaintiff sufficiently alleges that the Enayati Defendants made false statements via the lease provisions provided to their Airbnb rental guests and made misleading statements to these guests to deceive them into renting the units when they expressed concerns. The Complaint identifies specific assertions made to these Airbnb rental guests. (See Compl., ¶49.)

 

Accordingly, the demurrer to the second cause of action is OVERRULED.

 

Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, the Enayati Defendants’ Demurrer to the Complaint is OVERRULED in its entirety.
