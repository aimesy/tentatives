Case Number: 24STLC03018    Hearing Date: June 25, 2026    Dept: 26

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

Defendant Trans Telecom Company, Inc. aka Trans Telecom Company Inc.’s Motion to Compel Mandatory Fee Arbitration and Stay Action is granted. The action is stayed pending arbitration.

 

Status Conference Re: Arbitration is scheduled for 12/18/2026 at 9:30 am in Department 26 at the Spring Street Courthouse.

 

 

Analysis:

 

On April 24, 2025, Plaintiff Creditors Adjustment Bureau, Inc. (“Plaintiff”) filed this action against Defendant Trans Telecom Company, Inc. aka Trans Telecom Company Inc. (“Defendant”). Plaintiff alleges that it is the assignee of Greenberg Glusker Fields Claman & Machtinger LLP (“Greenberg LLP”). (Compl., ¶1.) A proof of non-service was filed by Plaintiff on July 31, 2024. Proof of substitute service of the Summons and Complaint was filed on March 20, 2025. An amended proof of substitute service was filed on April 25, 2025 and the Court entered Defendant’s default on the same day.

 

On May 19, 2025, Defendant filed a Notice of Stay of Proceedings on the grounds of arbitration under Business & Professions Code, section 6201. Defendant then filed a Motion to Set Aside Entry of Default and/or Default Judgment on September 30, 2025. After taking the matter under submission, the Court granted the Motion to Set Aside on November 24, 2025 and vacated the default entered on April 25, 2025. (Minute Order, 11/24/25.)

 

Defendant filed an Answer to the Complaint on February 19, 2026, then another Answer through different counsel on February 24, 2026. Defendant filed the instant Motion to Compel Mandatory Fee Arbitration and Stay Action was filed on March 26, 2026. The next day, the Court granted Defendant’s ex parte application to stay the proceedings pending its Motion to Compel Mandatory Fee Arbitration and Stay Action. (Minute Order, 03/27/26.) Plaintiff filed its opposition on June 11, 2026 and Defendant replied on June 17, 2026.

 

Discussion

 

Background of the Parties’ Relationship

 

In 2022, Defendant hired a law firm, Greenberg LLP, to file a form complaint against a neighboring company in state court to preserve the statute of limitations and attempt to obtain a tolling agreement regarding toxic cleanup on a neighboring company’s property. (Motion, Karimi Decl., p. 1:20-25.) Greenberg LLP used the address of the neighboring company, 5977 West Washington Blvd., Culver City, CA 90232, on the engagement letter, instead of Defendant’s address, which was 5969 West Washington Blvd., Culver City, CA 90232. (Id. at p. 1:6-13.) On February 16, 2022, Defendant’s president sent Greenberg LLP a letter clarifying that its address was 5967-5969 West Washington Blvd., not 5977 West Washington Blvd. (Id. at p. 14-17.) Greenberg LLP then used the correct address when it filed the complaint on Defendant’s behalf on February 28, 2022. (Id. at p. 1:18-20.) Defendant terminated its relationship with Greenberg LLP on March 17, 2022. (Id. at p. 2:3-4.) Greenberg LLP mailed Defendant a Notice of Client’s Right to Fee Arbitration to the wrong address. (Id. at p. 2:9-12 and Exh. 1.)

 

Motion to Compel Arbitration

 

Defendant moves for an order compelling Plaintiff to arbitrate this matter pursuant to the Mandatory Fee Arbitration Act, set forth at Cal. Business & Professions Code section 6201 and the California Arbitration Act, set forth at Code of Civil Procedure section 1281, et seq.

 

“On petition of a party to an arbitration agreement alleging the existence of a written agreement to arbitrate a controversy and that a party thereto refuses to arbitrate such controversy, the court shall order the petitioner and the respondent to arbitrate the controversy if it determines that an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists, unless it determines that: (a) The right to compel arbitration has been waived by the petitioner; or (b) Grounds exist for the revocation of the agreement.”  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 1281.2, subds. (a)-(b).)  As with other types of agreements, “[t]he failure of the [party] to carefully read the agreement and the amendment is not a reason to refuse to enforce the arbitration provisions.”  (Powers v. Dickson, Carlson & Campillo (1997) 54 Cal.App.4th 1102, 1115.)  “California law, ‘like [federal law], reflects a strong policy favoring arbitration agreements and requires close judicial scrutiny of waiver claims.’”  (Wagner Const. Co. v. Pacific Mechanical Corp. (2007) 41 Cal.4th 19, 31.) If the court orders arbitration, then the court shall stay the action until arbitration is completed.  (Bus. & Profs. Code, § 6201, subd. (c).)

 

Under the MFAA, an attorney seeking to recover fees must provide the client with written notice of the right to arbitration and failure to provide this notice prevents the attorney from maintaining an action. (Bus. & Profs. Code, § 6201, subd. (a).) If notice is given as stated in the MFAA, the client may stay the action filing and serving a request for arbitration prior to filing an answer or response. (Bus. & Profs. Code, § 6201, subd. (b).) Failing to request arbitration within 30 days of receipt of the notice also waives the right to arbitration. (Bus. & Profs. Code, § 6201, subd. (b).)

 

Defendant contends that it did not receive the Notice of Arbitration, which was mailed to the wrong address, but upon receiving a copy of the Complaint in this action on April 1, 2025, it filed a request for fee arbitration with the Beverly Hills Bar Association on April 30, 2025 and paid the associated fee. (Motion, Karimi pp. 2:13-16, 2:25-28 and Exhs. 2-3.) Plaintiff’s opposition offers no evidence to demonstrate that the Notice of Arbitration was served to Defendant’s correct address. (Opp., Brown Decl., ¶¶1-2.) Instead, Plaintiff contends that Defendant’s former attorney, Dennis P. Block, Esq. acknowledged that Defendant was in possession of the Complaint, which attached a copy of the Notice of Arbitration. (Id. at ¶2 and Exh. 1.) Defendant replies that its possession of the Complaint following non-conforming service, as detailed in the ruling on the Motion to Set Aside Default dated November 24, 2025, cannot demonstrate Plaintiff’s compliance with the service requirements of the Notice of Arbitration.

 

The Court agrees that Plaintiff did not demonstrate initial service of the Notice of Arbitration in accordance with the requirements under Business & Professions section 6201. Defendant’s supporting declaration provides evidence that the Notice of Arbitration was served to the wrong address and Plaintiff fails to overcome the declaration and attached exhibits. Nor is the Court swayed by the decision of the Beverly Hill Bar Association to close the arbitration as untimely requested, to which Plaintiff points. As Defendant argues, that decision was made without the full record regarding improper service of the Summons and Complaint. (See Opp., pp. 3:15-4:1.) The mailing of the Notice of Arbitration to the wrong address does not satisfy the requirement for service under Business & Professions Code section 6201. 

 

Plaintiff also argues that under Business and Professions Code section 6201(b), the client’s right to stay the court action is lost if the client participates in the litigation without having filed a request for arbitration and without seeking a stay. Waiver of the right to arbitration exists under section 6201 if the client fails to request arbitration within 30 days after receipt of the notice, or before filing an answer if the Notice of Arbitration was served on the client as required. Plaintiff served Defendant with the Complaint and Notice of Arbitration in this action on January 20, 2026 and the Court deemed service of the Summons and Complaint on that day. (Minute Order, 01/20/26; Proof of Service of Summons, Complaint, filed 01/20/26.)

 

Defendant, however, had already requested arbitration with the Beverly Hills Bar Association eight months earlier, on April 30, 2026. It was Plaintiff who sought to close that arbitration proceeding after it was opened at Defendant’s request. (Reply, Karimi Decl., ¶¶22-23 and Exh. F.) Therefore, the Court finds no waiver of the right to arbitration by Defendant.

 

Conclusion

 

Defendant Trans Telecom Company, Inc. aka Trans Telecom Company Inc.’s Motion to Compel Mandatory Fee Arbitration and Stay Action is granted. The action is stayed pending arbitration.

 

Status Conference Re: Arbitration is scheduled for 12/18/2026 at 9:30 am in Department 26 at the Spring Street Courthouse.

Case Number: 25STLC02717    Hearing Date: June 25, 2026    Dept: 26

TENTATIVE RULING:  

           

Defendants Cheryl Aguilera and Steve Aguilera’s Motion for Attorney’s Fees and Costs is granted. Defendants are awarded attorney’s fees of $22,083.00 and costs of $1,003.80.

 

 

Analysis:

 

Plaintiff South Bay Design Center, Inc. (“Plaintiff”) filed the instant action for (1) breach of contract; (2) quantum meruit; (3) common count – account stated; (4) common count – open book; (5) prompt payment penalties; and (6) enforce mechanic’s lien, against Defendants Cheryl Aguilera and Steve Aguilera (“Defendants”) on April 4, 2025. Defendants filed an Answer on June 4, 2025.

 

On November 18, 2025, Plaintiff filed a Motion to Quash Deposition Subpoena for Production of Business Records. The Motion to Quash was set for hearing on January 29, 2026. On November 26, 2025, Defendant Cheryl Aguilera filed a Motion to Compel Further Responses to Special Interrogatories, Set One; Request for Monetary Sanctions, which was set for hearing on December 22, 2025. That Motion to Compel Further was taken off calendar by Defendant Cheryl Aguilera, then re-filed on December 12, 2025. Before the re-filed Motion to Compel Further came for hearing, however, Plaintiff filed a voluntary dismissal of the action on January 22, 2026. The dismissal was entered on the same day.

 

Defendants filed the instant Motion for Attorney’s Fees and Costs, and a Memorandum of Costs, on January 30, 2026. Plaintiff filed an opposition on April 10, 2026 and Defendants replied on April 16, 2026. The next day, Plaintiff filed an objection and a request to strike the reply and supporting declaration and evidence on the grounds that defense counsel’s reply declaration improperly includes new arguments. (Objection, filed 04/17/26, p. 2:12-13.)

 

At the hearing on April 23, 2026, the Court continued the matter to allow Plaintiff to file and serve a sur-reply to address the new evidence submitted with Defendants’ reply papers. (Minute Order, 04/23/26.) Plaintiff filed a sur-reply and supporting declaration on June 11, 2026.

 

 

 

Discussion

 

Timing of Motion for Attorney’s Fees

 

A motion for attorney’s fees must be filed and served within the time for filing a notice of appeal under Cal. Rules of Court Rule 8.822. (Cal. Rules of Court Rule 3.1702(a).) Cal. Rules of Court Rule 8.822 states that an appeal must be filed within the earlier of either (1) 30 days after service of the notice of entry of the judgment; or (2) 90 days after entry of the judgment. (Cal. Rules of Court 8.822(1).) The order dismissing the action was entered on January 22, 2026 and the instant Motion was timely filed eight days later.

 

Entitlement to Attorney’s Fees

 

Defendants move for attorney’s fees under the prompt payment statutes raised in Plaintiff’s fifth cause of action. Business and Professions Code section 7108.5 provides that “A prime contractor or subcontractor shall pay to any subcontractor, not later than seven days after receipt of each progress payment, unless otherwise agreed to in writing, the respective amounts allowed the contractor on account of the work performed by the subcontractors, to the extent of each subcontractor's interest therein.” (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 7108.5, subd. (a).) “In any action for the collection of funds wrongfully withheld, the prevailing party shall be entitled to his or her attorney’s fees and costs.” (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 7108.5, subd. (c).)

 

In James L. Harris Painting & Decorating, Inc. v. West Bay Builders, Inc. (2015) 239 Cal.App.4th 1214, the Court of Appeals explained that:

 

The determination of whether there is prevailing party is to be made ‘on a practical level’ after considering what each party accomplished via the litigation.” (ComputerXpress, supra, 93 Cal.App.4th at p. 1017, 113 Cal.Rptr.2d 625.) This means that “ ‘[t]he prevailing party determination is to be made only upon a final resolution of the contract claims and only by “a comparison of the extent to which each party ha[s] succeeded and failed to succeed in its contentions.” [Citation.]’ (Hsu v. Abbara (1995) 9 Cal.4th 863, 876 [39 Cal.Rptr.2d 824, 891 P.2d 804].)” (Brawley, supra, 161 Cal.App.4th at p. 1137, 74 Cal.Rptr.3d 832.)

 

(James L. Harris Painting & Decorating, Inc. v. West Bay Builders, Inc. (2015) 239 Cal.App.4th 1214, 1221-1222.) Plaintiff also points ComputerXpress, Inc. v. Jackson (2001) 93 Cal.App.4th 993, cited in James L. Harris, for the rule that a court has the discretion to find there is no prevailing party. (See ComputerXpress, Inc. v. Jackson (2001) 93 Cal.App.4th 993, 1017.) Finally, in Hsu v. Abbara (1995) 9 Cal.4th 863, the California Supreme Court held that this “practical level” determination, when brought pursuant to Civil Code section 1717, must be made only upon a final resolution of the contract claims. (Hsu v. Abbara (1995) 9 Cal.4th 863, 883.)

 

Although Plaintiff argues that the trial court determination of no-prevailing-party in James L. Harris Painting, which was upheld by the Court of Appeals, should dictate the same result here, the Court finds the circumstances of that case quite distinct from this action. In James L. Harris Painting, the plaintiff filed a complaint for breach of contract and violation of the prompt payment statutes, and the defendant likewise filed a cross-complaint for breach of contract against the plaintiff. (James L. Harris Painting, supra, 239 Cal.App.4th at 827.) The jury found both parties had failed to perform and refused to award damages to either side. (Ibid.) Under those facts, the trial court determined that there was no prevailing party and denied the defendant’s motion for attorney’s fees under the prompt payment statutes. (Ibid.)

 

In this action, only Plaintiff sought damages via its causes of action for breach of contract, common counts, and prompt payment penalties in the amount of at least $25,827.84. (Compl., p. 6.) Plaintiff also sought to enforce the mechanic’s lien it recorded against the subject property. Plaintiff did not achieve any of its litigation objectives as set out in the Complaint. Instead, after nine months of litigation, it dismissed the entire action. Although Defendants did not achieve a “defense verdict,” i.e., a determination by the finder of fact that it did not violate the prompt payment statutes, no authority holds that this is the only way to be a prevailing party. In this action, Defendants avoided any finding of fault, are not being held liable for any amount of damages, and did not have the mechanic’s lien enforced against their property. Therefore, the Court finds Defendants are the prevailing party in this action and are entitled to an award of attorney’s fees.

 

 Calculation of Reasonable Attorney’s Fees

 

The method for establishing the amount of reasonable attorney fees under California law is the lodestar method. (Thayer v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2001) 92 Cal.App.4th 819, 833.) The method multiplies the number of hours reasonably expended by counsel by a reasonable hourly rate. (Ibid.) Factors relevant to a lodestar calculation include

 

[T]he nature of the litigation, its difficulty, the amount involved, the skill required and the skill employed in handling the litigation, the attention given, the success of the attorney’s efforts, his learning, age, and experience in the particular type of work demanded [citation]; the intricacies and importance of the litigation, the labor and necessity for skilled training and ability in trying the cause, and the time consumed.

 

(In re Marriage of Cueva (1978) 86 Cal.App.3d 290, 296.)

 

Defendants seek an award of $25,023.00 based on 40.2 hours of attorney time spent litigating this action at an hourly rate of $600.00, plus 2.5 hours of paralegal time billed at $140.00 per hour, and 6.7 hours of legal assistant time billed at $90.00 per hour. (Motion, Julander Decl., ¶¶9-11 and Exh. A.) The attorney’s hourly rate is supported by the number of years he has practiced law and the comparable hourly rate of similarly positioned attorneys. (Id. at ¶13-14.)

 

Plaintiff argues that much of this time pertains to work unrelated to the prompt payment statute violations. Specifically, that none of the affirmative defenses in Defendants’ Answer addressed the fifth cause of action; the discovery motions were unrelated, procedurally defective, and never came for hearing; the subpoena that Plaintiff sought to quash related to the employment status of Plaintiff’s employees; and a cross-complaint that was never filed nor had any relation to the prompt payment statutes. Defendants reply that its litigation efforts revolved around the status of Plaintiff’s employees, which directly related to whether Plaintiff could bring and succeed on an action for violation of the prompt payment statutes.

 

The Complaint alleges that Plaintiff is a licensed general contractor that entered into a contract to perform certain work at the property owned by Defendants. (Compl., ¶¶3, 10.) Defendants allegedly breached the contract by failing to pay for work worth at least $25,827.84. (Id. at ¶11.) This factual basis underlies all the causes of action alleged in the Complaint. (See id. at ¶¶15-24, 28.) The fifth cause of action for violation of the prompt payment statutes incorporates those allegations and asserts that Defendants failed to make progress and/or retention payments within the time required by statute. (Id. at ¶¶24-25.) From these allegations, it is clear that the prompt payment violations arise out of Defendants’ alleged non-payment under the parties’ construction arrangement. It would be unnecessary for Defendants’ defense, as initially set forth in their Answer, to assert affirmative defenses specific to only the fifth cause of action. Similarly, Defendants’ discovery into the licensing status of Plaintiff’s employees pertained to Plaintiff’s right to bring these causes of action. Plaintiff’s insistence that Defendants articulate a separate, specific justification for the discovery with respect to the fifth cause of action disregards the Complaint’s common factual predicate.

 

Plaintiff also argues that the Answer did not assert any affirmative defense that it was not a properly licensed contractor, but the lack of such specific language in the Answer would not prohibit Defendants from seeking discovery on the issue, given the breadth of discoverable information. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2017.010 [“any party may obtain discovery regarding any matter, not privileged, that is relevant to the subject matter involved in the pending action or to the determination of any motion made in that action, if the matter either is itself admissible in evidence or appears reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence.”].)

 

Next, the parties disagree as to whether the discovery motions Defendants filed should be compensated. Plaintiff characterizes them as procedurally and statutorily defective; Defendants contend the relevant deadlines were not missed and the motions ultimately pushed Plaintiff to dismiss the case. The Court finds Defendants’ explanation sufficiently supported by the declaration of its counsel, and Plaintiff’s concession that it did not want to continue spending money on a limited civil case at the possible risk of its employees’ privacy. (Reply, Julander Decl., ¶6; Opp., Plumtree Decl., ¶12.)

 

Defendants lastly concede that reducing the award sought with respect to the time spent on their proposed Cross-Complaint, in the amount of 4.9 hours billed at $2,940.00, would be appropriate.

 

Based on the foregoing, Defendants are awarded attorney’s fees of $22,083.00 and costs of $1,003.80.

 

Conclusion

 

Defendants Cheryl Aguilera and Steve Aguilera’s Motion for Attorney’s Fees and Costs is granted. Defendants are awarded attorney’s fees of $22,083.00 and costs of $1,003.80.

Case Number: 25STLC04090    Hearing Date: June 25, 2026    Dept: 26

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

Plaintiff Geico General Insurance Company’s Motion to Vacate Dismissal is granted. The dismissal entered on 01/02/2026 is hereby vacated.

 

Order to Show Cause Re: Request for Default Judgment is scheduled for 07/30/2026 at 09:30 AM in Department 26 at Spring Street Courthouse.

 

 

Analysis:

 

Plaintiff Geico General Insurance Company (“Plaintiff”) filed the instant action for automobile subrogation against Defendant Angel Bruce Aguirre (“Defendant”) on May 23, 2025. Following Defendant’s failure to file a response to the Complaint, the Court entered their default on November 12, 2025. Plaintiff then dismissed the action on January 2, 2026.

 

Plaintiff filed the instant Motion to Vacate Dismissal on May 5, 2026. Plaintiff served the Motion on Defendant by first-class mail on May 4, 2026. No opposition has been filed to date.

 

Discussion

 

The Motion is brought pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure, section 473, subdivision (b). Under this statute, an application for relief must be made no more than six months after entry of the order from which relief is sought and must be accompanied by an affidavit of fault attesting to the party or attorney’s mistake, inadvertence, surprise or neglect. (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b); English v. IKON Business Solutions (2001) 94 Cal.App.4th 130, 143.) A timely request for relief from an entry of default, default judgment, or involuntary dismissal, based on attorney fault, must be granted. (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b).)

 

Plaintiff timely filed the Motion four months after dismissal of the action and included an attorney affidavit of fault explaining that Plaintiff’s counsel dismissed the action based on a misunderstanding that the parties had fully settled the action. (Motion, Yamamoto Decl., ¶¶2-5.) Based on the timely request for relief and supporting attorney affidavit of fault, the case must be reinstated pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (b).

 

Conclusion

 

Plaintiff Geico General Insurance Company’s Motion to Vacate Dismissal is granted. The dismissal entered on 01/02/2026 is hereby vacated.

 

Order to Show Cause Re: Request for Default Judgment is scheduled for 07/30/2026 at 09:30 AM in Department 26 at Spring Street Courthouse.

Case Number: 25STLC04882    Hearing Date: June 25, 2026    Dept: 26

TENTATIVE RULING: 

 

Defendant Melody Etezadi’s Motion for Leave to Amend Answer is granted.  Defendant is to file the Amended Answer within ten days.

 

 

Analysis:

 

On June 25, 2025, Plaintiff Allison Valentine (“Plaintiff”) filed the instant action against Defendant Melody Etezadi (“Defendant”) for (1) breach of contract; (2) conversion; (3) fraud; (4) violation of Pen. Code § 484; and (5) money had and received. The Complaint alleges that Defendant misrepresented her right to sub-lease her apartment to Plaintiff, and after Plaintiff transferred funds to Defendant, repudiated the sub-lease and refused to return the money. (Compl., ¶¶5-14.)

 

On December 5, 2025, Defendant, in propria persona, filed a verified Answer to the Complaint, verified Cross-Complaint, a Motion to Strike, and a Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings. The Answer and the Cross-Complaint both include the allegation that “[p]rior to entering into the Agreement, Cross-Complainant obtained written authorization from her landlord expressly permitting a two-month sublease for June and July 2025.” (Answer, ¶15; Cross-Compl., ¶1.) 

 

On February 3, 2025, the Court denied Defendant’s Motion to Strike; on February 19, 2026, the Court denied Defendant’s Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings. (Minute Order, 02/19/26.) On February 6, 2026, Defendant substituted in Attorney Arya Azizi, Esq. and on February 25, 2026, filed the instant Motion for Leave to Amend Answer. Plaintiff filed an opposition to the Motion on May 1, 2026 and Defendant replied with evidentiary objections on May 7, 2026.

 

The Motion initially came for hearing on May 14, 2026, at which time the Court continued the matter to give Plaintiff an opportunity to address new evidence and arguments raised in Defendant’s reply papers. Plaintiff filed a sur-reply on June 4, 2026.

 

Discussion

 

The request for leave to amend is brought pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 576 and 473, subdivision (a), which provides that “[t]he court may, in furtherance of justice and on any terms as may be proper, allow a party to amend any pleading…” (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (a)(1).) A motion for leave to amend a pleading must also comply with the procedural requirements of California Rules of Court, Rule 3.1324, which requires a supporting declaration to set forth explicitly what allegations are to be added and where, and when the new evidence was discovered warranting the amendment and why the amendment was not made earlier. The motion must also include (1) a copy of the proposed and numbered amendment, (2) specifications by reference to pages and lines of the allegations that would be deleted and added, and (3) a declaration specifying the effect, necessity and propriety of the amendments, date of discovery and reasons for delay. (Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1324, subds. (a), (b).)

 

The policy favoring amendments and resolving all matters in the same dispute is “so strong that it is a rare case in which denial of leave to amend can be justified. [Citation.]” (Howard v. County of San Diego (2010) 184 Cal.App.4th 1422, 1428.) “Although courts are bound to apply a policy of great liberality in permitting amendments to the complaint at any stage of the proceedings, up to and including trial [citations], this policy should be applied only ‘where no prejudice is shown to the adverse party . . . . [citation].  A different result is indicated ‘where inexcusable delay and probable prejudice to the opposing party’ is shown. [Citation].” (Magpali v. Farmers Group (1996) 48 Cal.App.4th 471, 487.) Where a proposed amendment opens an entirely new substantive area of injury on the eve of trial without any explanation for why the major change had not been made long before, denial of leave is appropriately ordered in the court’s discretion. (Id.)

 

Defendant moves for leave to amend the Answer by changing the allegation that Defendant obtained a written authorization to sublease the apartment, to a verbal authorization. Also, Defendant seeks to add two affirmative defenses that she did not have any intent to defraud Plaintiff and that any representations Defendant made were true and accurate. (Motion, Azizi Decl., ¶¶5-7; Exh. A at p. 3:18-24.)

 

The initial moving papers do not include a supporting declaration that sets forth explicitly what allegations are to be added and where, or the effect of the proposed amendment. (See Motion, Azizi Decl., ¶¶6-7.) Nor do the initial moving papers include specifications by reference to pages and lines of the allegations that would be deleted and added. In reply, Defendant’s declaration does address the effect, necessity and propriety of the amendments, date of discovery and reasons for delay of the proposed amendment. (Reply, Etezadi Decl., ¶¶4-8.) Specifically, Defendant seeks to change the initial allegation of a written authorization from the landlord to sublease the apartment to that of a verbal authorization. This is sufficient for purposes of complying with the mandatory Rules of Court.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that an amendment that seeks to contradict an earlier pleading may only be allowed “upon very satisfactory evidence that the party has been deceived or misled, or that his pleading was put in under a clear mistake as to the facts.” (Citing Tognazzi v. Wilhelm (1936) 6 Cal.2d 123, 127.) Plaintiff contends that their discovery demonstrates that no written authorization was provided to Defendant to sub-lease the apartment and that the document Defendant submitted with the Motion to Strike that purports to be a written authorization dated May 1, 2025, is a forgery of the landlord’s signature. (Opp., pp. 2:17-5:8.) In support, Plaintiff relies on the declaration of a handwriting expert regarding the May 1 document, to which Defendant brings evidentiary objections. The Court sustains the evidentiary objections to the Black declaration and makes no finding on the issue of whether the May 1 document is a forgery.

 

Defendant’s supporting reply declaration included new evidence regarding the effect, necessity and propriety of the proposed amendment, date of discovery of the new information, and the reasons for delay in seeking leave to amend. Specifically, Defendant declares that when she drafted the Answer, in propria persona and with the assistance of ChatGPT, she incorrectly used the term “written authorization” to describe the verbal permission she obtained from her landlord about subleasing the apartment, which was then later memorialized in the May 1 writing. (Reply, Etezadi Decl., ¶¶4-8.) Upon hiring an attorney, Defendant came to understand that the phrase she used in the Answer and other pleadings was not accurate and required amendment. (Id. at ¶¶9-10.)  Defendant also attaches deposition testimony to the same effect.  (Reply, Azizi Decl., Exh. 1.) 

 

The parties disagree whether these circumstances fall under the rule set forth in Tognazzi v. Wilhelm (1936) 6 Cal.2d 136, which holds that an amendment contradicting an admission made in the original pleadings may only be made “upon very satisfactory evidence that the party has been deceived or misled, or that his pleading was put in under a clear mistake as to the facts.” (Tognazzi v. Wilhelm (1936) 6 Cal.2d 123, 127.) The Court finds that for purposes of this Motion, Defendant has sufficiently provided evidence through her declaration and testimony of a mistake as to the facts based on her lay understanding of what constituted a “written authorization.”  Plaintiff is not precluded from challenging the credibility of Defendant’s explanations at trial. The Court is not prepared to treat the statements in the initial pleading as binding admissions.

 

The Court grants Defendant leave to amend the Answer as set forth in the proposed First Amended Answer.  

 

Conclusion

 

Defendant Melody Etezadi’s Motion for Leave to Amend Answer is granted.  Defendant is to file the Amended Answer within ten days.

Case Number: 26STCP00072    Hearing Date: June 25, 2026    Dept: 26

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

Petitioner Ricebray, Inc.’s Motion for an Award of Attorney’s Fees and Costs is granted. Petitioner is awarded attorney’s fees of $10,420.00 and costs of $492.03.

 

 

Analysis:

 

On January 6, 2026, Petitioner Ricebray, Inc. (“Petitioner”) filed the instant Petition for Permanent Injunctive Relief Pursuant to Civil Code section 798.88 against Respondents Martin Apolinar and Angels Rivas (“Respondents”). Petitioner personally served Respondents with the Petition and Notice of Hearing on Petition. (Proof of Personal Service, filed 01/20/26, ¶¶2-3.) Respondents filed no response to the Petition, and following the hearing on January 21, 2026, the Court granted the injunction in Petitioner’s favor. (Minute Order, 01/21/26; Order Granting Petition, 01/21/26.) Petitioner served Respondents with notice of the ruling on January 28, 2026. (Notice of Ruling, filed 01/28/26, p. 7.)

 

Petitioner filed a Memorandum of Costs on March 6, 2026 and the instant Motion for Attorney’s Fees and Costs on March 23, 2026. Respondents filed an opposition to the instant Motion on June 22, 2026.

 

Discussion

 

Timing of Motion for Attorney’s Fees and Memorandum of Costs

 

A motion for attorney’s fees must be filed and served within the time for filing a notice of appeal under Cal. Rules of Court Rule 8.822. (Cal. Rules of Court Rule 3.1702(a).) Cal. Rules of Court Rule 8.822 states that an appeal must be filed within the earlier of either (1) 30 days after service of the notice of entry of the judgment; or (2) 90 days after entry of the judgment. (Cal. Rules of Court 8.822(1).) The order granting the Petition was entered on January 21, 2026 and served on Respondent a week later. Although the document served on January 28, 2026 was not called “Notice of Entry of Judgment,” it operated as the judgment in this action. The instant Motion was timely filed less than 90 days later.

 

A memorandum of costs must be filed 180 days after entry of judgment, or within 15 days after the date of service of the notice of entry of judgment. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1700(a)(1).) The Memorandum of Costs was timely filed six weeks after the January 21, 2026 entry of judgment.

 

Entitlement to Attorney’s Fees and Costs

 

Petitioner moves for an award of attorney’s fees in the amount of $10,420.00 and $492.03 in costs pursuant to Civil Code sections 798.85 and 1717, subdivisions (a) and (b). A prevailing party is entitled to recover costs, including attorney fees, on a petition to enjoin violations of mobile home park rules. (Civ. Code, § 798.85.) Civil Code section 1717 provides that attorney fees and costs shall be awarded to the prevailing party in an action on a contract, where the contract specifically provides for attorney fees and costs. (Civ. Code, § 1717, subd. (a).) Petitioner is both the prevailing party on the instant Petition and is entitled to attorney fees and costs under the parties’ lease agreement. (Motion, Beam Decl., Exh. 1 at ¶26.) Therefore, Petitioner is entitled to an award of attorney fees and costs.

 

Reasonable Attorney’s Fees and Costs

 

Petitioner’s counsel billed its work between $325.00 and $375.00 per hour, and the paralegal’s time at $140.00 per hour. (Id. at ¶¶7-8 and Exh. 2.) Given the legal professionals working on this action have decades of experience, the Court finds these billing rates to be reasonable. (Ibid.) Petitioner also attaches its complete billing records, demonstrating that the attorneys spent 21.7 hours, and the paralegal spent ten hours, on this case. (Ibid.) The time was spent issuing two Seven-Day Notices to Respondents, preparing and filing the Petition, communicating with witnesses, preparing and filing the declarations in support of the Petition, attending the hearing and obtaining the issuance of the Injunction Order, and preparing the instant Motion. (Id. at ¶5 and Exh. B.)

 

Respondents oppose the fees sought as unsupported by contemporaneous billing records, disproportionate to the simplicity of an unopposed Petition, and involving duplicative and overcharged work. (Opp., pp. 1-3.) The opposition, however, cites no part of the evidence submitted with the Motion to demonstrate any of these points. The opposition incorrectly contends that the Motion does not include contemporaneous billing records, despite the invoices being attached at Exhibit B to the declaration of Attorney Beam. Respondents also do not provide any examples of the purported duplicative work performed by Petitioner’s counsel, or what administrative work was billed at an unreasonable rate. (Ibid.) Accordingly, the Court finds that the attorney’s fees sought are reasonable in light of the rate charged and the work performed in this action.

 

Petitioner seeks costs of $492.03 as set forth in its Memorandum of Costs filed on March 6, 2026. Respondents filed no motion to tax or strike costs by the 15-day deadline, nor does their opposition discuss the particular costs sought. (See Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1700(b)(1).)

 

Finally, to the extent Respondents ask the Court to take their financial and personal circumstances into account to reduce the award of fees and costs, they cite no legal authority for the Court’s ability to do so. (Opp., p. 4.)

 

Conclusion

 

Petitioner Ricebray, Inc.’s Motion for an Award of Attorney’s Fees and Costs is granted. Petitioner is awarded attorney’s fees of $10,420.00 and costs of $492.03.

Case Number: 26STLCOO700    Hearing Date: June 25, 2026    Dept: 26

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

Plaintiff Zhiwei Chen’s (1) Motion to Deem Requests for Admission Admitted; and (2) Motion to Compel Responses to Special Interrogatories and Requests for Production, are denied.

 

 

Analysis:

 

Plaintiff Zhiwei Chen (“Plaintiff”) filed the instant action against Defendant Five Points Towing, LLC (“Defendant”) on February 2, 2026. Defendant filed its Answer on March 12, 2026. On May 5, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant (1) Motion to Deem Requests for Admission Admitted; and (2) Motion to Compel Responses to Special Interrogatories and Requests for Production.

 

On June 18, 2026, Defendant filed an Ex Parte Application for Order Continuing Hearing on Motions, which the Court denied on June 23, 2026. (Minute Order, 06/23/26.) The Court ruled that it would consider Defendant’s arguments at the hearing on the instant Motions. (Ibid.) Plaintiff filed a Supplemental Notice with respect to the Motions on June 22, 2026.

 

Discussion

 

Plaintiff’s discovery Motions are supported by a proof of service stating that they were served on Defendant through Attorney Neil C. Evans, Esq. on May 18, 2026, by first-class mail. (Proof of Service, filed 06/10/26.) They are further supported by Plaintiff’s declaration attesting to service of the discovery requests on March 17, 2026, also by first-class mail to Attorney Evans. (Motions, Chen Decl., ¶¶2-3 and Exhs. A-B.) On April 22, 2026, Plaintiff sent Defendant a meet and confer letter to inquire about the lack of any response. (Id. at ¶4 and Exh. C.)

 

Initially, the Court notes that Plaintiff filed a combined motion to compel responses with respect to two separate sets of discovery: (1) Special Interrogatories; and (2) Requests for Production. Filing a request to compel responses to two separate sets of discovery as a single motion creates the following problems. First, it negatively impacts the Court’s calendar by placing more motions on the calendar than slots have been provided by the online reservation system. Second, it results in confusion in the motion papers regarding the arguments and evidence with respect to each request. Finally, combining multiple discovery sets into a single motion allows the moving party to avoid paying the requisite filing fees. Statutorily required filing fees are jurisdictional and “it is mandatory for the court clerks to demand and receive statutorily required filing fees.” (See Duran v. St. Luke’s Hospital (2003) 114 Cal.App.4th 457, 460.) Although Plaintiff has been granted a fee waiver in this action, they should still make separate motion reservations to address separate sets of discovery.

 

Additionally, the Court finds that the discovery requests served on Defendant do not comply with Code of Civil Procedure section 94, which allows only 35 written discovery requests in the Limited Civil Court unless otherwise authorized by court order or by stipulation. (Code Civ. Proc., § 94, subd. (a); § 95.) Plaintiff served Defendant with more than 35 requests for admission (14), special interrogatories (12), and requests for production (12) combined. (Motions, Chen Decl., Exhs. A.) The attached proofs of service also show that Plaintiff served Defendant with Form Interrogatories, which would further increase the number of requests. (Id. at Exh. B.) Based on the foregoing, the instant discovery Motions are denied. The Court will not grant motions to compel discovery, or motions to deem admissions admitted, if propounded in violation of the Code of Civil Procedure.

 

Finally, the Court does not find that Plaintiff’s Supplemental Notice, admitting to service of Defendant’s responses to the Requests for Admission prior to the hearing date, changes the outcome of the Motion to Deem Requests for Admission Admitted. Plaintiff asks for an award of sanctions in the Supplemental Notice, but the number of discovery requests remains in excess of that allowed by the Code of Civil Procedure and the Motion to Deem Requests for Admission Admitted itself does not include any notice of a request for sanctions, as required by Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.040.

 

Conclusion

 

Plaintiff Zhiwei Chen’s (1) Motion to Deem Requests for Admission Admitted; and (2) Motion to Compel Responses to Special Interrogatories and Requests for Production, is denied.
