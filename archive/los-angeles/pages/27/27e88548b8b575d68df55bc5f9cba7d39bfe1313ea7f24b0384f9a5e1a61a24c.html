I.               BACKGROUND 

Hua Ma (“Plaintiff”) filed this breach of contract and fraud action against Thien Kim Huynh Nguyen, aka Kim Huynh (“Kim”) and Phuc. T Huynh (“Phuc”) (jointly, “Defendants”). Plaintiff alleges she had agreements with Defendants wherein she agreed to lend them money and they would repay the amounts with interest.

Defendants move to set aside default and default judgment.

II.            PROCEDURAL HISTORY

On August 2, 2023, Plaintiff filed a Complaint alleging (1) Breach of Contract, (2) Fraud, (3) Breach of Contract, (4) Fraud, (5) Breach of Contract, (6) Fraud, (7) Breach of Contract, (8) Fraud, (9) Breach of Contract, (10) Fraud, (11) Breach of Contract, (12) Fraud, (13) Breach of Contract, (14) Fraud, (15) Breach of Contract, and (16) Fraud.

On May 21, 2024, the clerk entered default against Defendants.

On April 27, 2026, the Court entered judgment against Defendants.

On August 3, 2026, Defendants filed the instant Motions.

On August 25 and August 26, 2026, Plaintiff filed her Oppositions.

On September 1, 2026, Kim filed a Reply.

On September 8, 2026, Phuc filed a Reply.

III.         LEGAL STANDARD

A.             Lack of Actual Notice – Code of Civil Procedure § 473.5

Code of Civil Procedure § 473.5  permits the Court to vacate a default when service of a summons has not resulted in actual notice to a party in time to defend the action and a default or default judgment has been entered against him. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 473.5(a).) “The notice of motion shall be served and filed within a reasonable time, but in no event exceeding the earlier of: (i) two years after entry of a default judgment against him or her; or (ii) 180 days after service… of a written notice that the default or default judgment has been entered.” (Id.)

The phrase actual notice means genuine knowledge and does not include constructive or imputed notice to the client. (See Tunis v. Barrow (1986) 184 Cal.App.3d 1069, 1077.) “A defendant seeking vacation of a default judgment entered against him must further show that his lack of actual notice in time to defend the action was not caused by his inexcusable neglect or avoidance of service.” (Id. at 1077-1078, citing Code Civ. Proc. § 473.5(c).) Additionally, “the party shall serve and file with the notice a copy of the answer, motion, or other pleading proposed to be filed in the action.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 473.5(b).) It is well established the court is “not required to accept…self-serving evidence contradicting the process server’s declaration.” (Rodriguez v. Nam Min Cho (2015) 236 Cal.App.4th 742, 751.)

B.             Equitable Relief

Apart from any statutory authority, a court has inherent, equitable power to set aside a judgment for extrinsic fraud or mistake. (Bae, 245 Cal.App.4th at 97.)

Extrinsic mistake occurs when a mistake “extrinsic to the litigation… ‘led a court to do what it never intended…’ [Citation.]” (See Rappleyea v. Campbell (1994) 8 Cal.4th 975, 981.) Extrinsic fraud occurs when, “a party is denied a fair adversary hearing because he has been ‘deliberately kept in ignorance of the action or proceeding, or in some other way fraudulently prevented from presenting his claim or defense.’ [Citation.]” (Kulchar v. Kulchar (1969) 1 Cal.3d 467, 471.) To obtain equitable relief for a judgement based on extrinsic mistake or fraud, the defaulting party must demonstrate: (1) a meritorious defense; (2) a satisfactory excuse for not defending the original action; and (3) diligence in seeking to set aside the default once the default was discovered. (See Rappleyea, 8 Cal.4th at 884 [the Court did not determine whether this test applies to requests for equitable relief when a default judgment has not been entered].) “Prejudice to a plaintiff is obviously less if judgment has not been entered when a defendant seeks equitable relief.” (Id. at 984.)

IV.          ANALYSIS

A.             Judicial Notice

Defendants request judicial notice of the documents in this action’s docket. The Court grants the request.

B.             Kim’s Motion to Set Aside is Denied.

Kim argues the default and default judgment entered against her should be set aside because she did not receive actual notice of this action. (See Mot. at 5:11-15, Reply at 2:18-22.) Kim argues (1) the proof of service indicates “Thien Kim Nguyen, aka Kim Huynh” was served which is a name that does not match the name on the Complaint; (2) the proof of service shows “Thien Kim Nguyen – Wife” was served which is a name that is incorrect; (3) the request for entry of default is defective because it was purportedly mailed on April 10, 2014, which is incorrect and does not comply with Code of Civil Procedure § 587; and (4) none of the minute orders were served on her. (See Mot. at 3:8-17.) Kim also argues equitable relief from judgment is warranted based on this same evidence. (See Mot. at 7:28-8:3.)

Kim attests “I was not served with the summons and complaint on August 18, 2023, or on any other date, and I did not receive notice of the summons and complaint or this lawsuit prior to the issuance of the Abstract of Judgment on July 10, 2026.” (Declaration of Thieu Kim Huynh Nguyen filed August 3, 2026 (“Kim Decl.”), ¶ 3.) Kim also attests “I was not served with…the request for entry of default, or the request for court judgment, or the multiple notices of ruling as ordered by the multiple minute orders, or learn of this action or the Amended Judgment until after the Court issued the Abstract of Judgment.” (Id., at 16.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues Kim provides no evidence she lacked actual notice of this action aside from her self-serving declaration. (See Opp. at 4:1-6.) Plaintiff argues a process server served both Defendants. (See id., at 2:19-24.) Plaintiff explains the request for entry of default for each Defendant contains a typographical error on the date, as it states it was mailed April 10, 2014, instead of 2024. (See id., at 3:3-18, Exs. C-D.) Plaintiff argues both forms were mailed to Defendants. (See id.) Plaintiff argues the request for entry of default judgment and a memorandum of costs was mailed to Defendants on July 28, 2025, and September 5, 2024. (See id., Exs. E-F.) Plaintiff also argues the Motion is untimely because it was filed more than two years after entry of default. (See id., at 5:8-10.)

The Court finds insufficient evidence to set aside default and default judgment based on lack of actual notice. First, the Motion is timely as it was filed within two years after entry of default judgment on April 27, 2026. The 180-day clock under Code of Civil Procedure § 473.5(a) was not triggered as there is no evidence Plaintiff served Kim with written notice of entry of default. Second, the name on the proof of service filed August 29, 2023, Thien Kim Nguyen, aka Kim Huynh, matches the name on the Complaint, Thien Kim Huynh Nguyen, aka Kim Huynh. Third, once a default is entered, the defendant is no longer an active party in the litigation and is not entitled to notice of court hearings or proceedings. (See Code of Civ. Proc. § 1010 [no “notice or paper, other than amendments to the pleadings, or amended pleading, need be served”.] Thus, that Kim was not served with the minute orders after default was entered against her is not grounds for setting aside default.

Fourth, although the affidavit of mailing on judicial council form CIV-100 requesting entry of default against Kim states the incorrect year, based on the evidence before the Court, this does not necessarily constitute grounds for setting aside default judgment. (See CIV-100 filed May 21, 2024.) Noncompliance with the affidavit requirement does not support setting aside a default or default judgment absent a suitable showing of prejudice. (See Bae v T.D. Serv. Co. of Az. (2016) 245 Cal.App.4th 89, 107 [sufficient showing of prejudice]; Rodriguez v. Henard (2009) 174 Cal.App.4th 529 [“requirement of an affidavit of mailing under section 587 is not jurisdictional, and hence the failure to file one does not deprive the trial court of jurisdiction to render judgment.”].) An absent or defective affidavit of mailing is prejudicial when the defendant can credibly claim the request for entry of default would have been received if the plaintiff had, in fact, mailed the request to the defendant’s address, of which the plaintiff had knowledge. (See Murray & Murray v Raissi Real Estate Dev., LLC (2015) 233 Cal.App.4th 379, 386–388.) Any error as to the mailing of the affidavit, e.g., including an incorrect zip code or date of mailing, is not prejudicial when the defendant had knowledge of the default proceedings. (See Rodriguez, 174 CA4th at 536 (incorrect zip code did not make default judgment void); Taylor v Varga (1995) 37 CA4th 750, 759–760 (request for entry of default containing incorrect date of mailing did not make default judgment void because defendant acknowledged receiving request before actual entry of default), disapproved on other grounds in K.J. v Los Angeles Unified Sch. Dist. (2020) 8 C5th 875, 888 n6.)

Here, Kim merely attests she did not receive the request for entry of default. (See Kim Decl., ¶¶ 4, 15-16.) She does not attest to any other facts supporting her self-serving statement. Kim does not attest she did not reside or receive mail at the address where the request was mailed to, or other facts explaining why she would not have received the request at the address it was purportedly mailed to. Thus, Kim does not show the defective affidavit of mailing is prejudicial as she has not credibly shown that she did not receive the request in the first instance.

Fifth, a proof of service containing a declaration from a registered process server invokes a rebuttable presumption of valid service. (See American Express Centurion Bank v. Zara (2011) 199 Cal.App.4th 383, 390; see also Evid. Code § 647.) The party seeking to defeat service of process must present sufficient evidence to show that the service did not take place as stated. (See Palm Property Investments, LLC v. Yadegar (2011) 194 Cal.App.4th 1419, 1428.) Merely denying service took place without more is insufficient to overcome the presumption. (See id., at 1428; Bonzer v. City of Huntington Park (1993) 20 Cal.App.4th 1474, 1481, 25 Cal.Rptr.2d 278 [upon credible evidence that document was never received, presumption of receipt from mailing “ceased to exist”].) Here, Kim merely attests she did not receive notice of this lawsuit and was not served with the summons and complaint on August 18, 2023, or on any other date. (See Kim Decl., ¶¶ 3, 15-16.) This evidence, without more, is insufficient to overcome the presumption established by the registered process server’s declaration that Thien Kim Nguyen was served on August 18, 2023. (See Proof of Service filed August 29, 2023.)

Thus, the record shows Plaintiff served Kim with the summons and Complaint and the request for entry of default. (See Sporn v. Home Depot USA, Inc. (2005) 126 Cal.App.4th 1294 [“Examples of extrinsic fraud are: ... failure to give notice of the action to the other party…The essence of extrinsic fraud is one party's preventing the other from having his day in court.”] Accordingly, the Court finds Kim’s evidence does not show lack of actual notice or extrinsic fraud. Moreover, Kim’s affidavit does not attest that her lack of actual notice in time to defend the action was not caused by her avoidance of service or inexcusable neglect. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 473.5(b).) Thus, Kim hasn’t shown she is entitled to set aside default and default judgment based on lack of actual notice or on equitable grounds.

Accordingly, the Court DENIES Kim’s Motion to Set Aside Default and Default Judgment.

C.             Phuc’s Motion to Set Aside is Denied.

Phuc argues the default and default judgment entered against him should be set aside because he did not receive actual notice of this action. (See Mot. at 5:11-15, Reply at 2:17-21.) Phuc argues (1) he did not receive service of the summons and Complaint; (2) the request for entry of default is defective because it was purportedly mailed on April 10, 2014, which is incorrect and does not comply with Code of Civil Procedure § 587; and (3) none of the minute orders were served on him. (See Mot. at 3:8-17.) Phuc also argues equitable relief from judgment is warranted based on this same evidence. (See Mot. at 7:26-28.)

Phuc attests “he was not served with the summons and complaint on August 19, 2023, or on any other date, or by mail… No one by the name ‘Annie’ resided at the residence where she was purportedly served.” (Declaration of Phuc T. Huynh filed August 3, 2026, ¶ 3.)

The Court incorporates Plaintiff’s arguments set forth in her opposition to Kim’s Motion here, as they are the same.

Likewise, the Court incorporates its analysis concerning the defective declaration of mailing on the request for entry of default and lack of service of minute orders from above here.

Here, the proof of service shows substitute service was effected on “Annie (Doe) – Co-Occupant”. (See Proof of Service filed August 29, 2023.) The Court finds Phuc’s mere denial that no one named Annie resided at the service address is insufficient to overcome the presumption established by the registered process server’s declaration that this individual was served on August 19, 2023. There is no other evidence corroborating this denial. Thus, the record shows Phuc was served with the summons and complaint by substitute service. Accordingly, the Court finds none of the evidence indicates lack of actual notice or extrinsic fraud. Moreover, Phuc’s affidavit does not attest his lack of actual notice in time to defend the action was not caused by his avoidance of service or inexcusable neglect. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 473.5(b).) Thus, Phuc has not shown he is entitled to set aside default and default judgment based on lack of actual notice or on equitable grounds.

Accordingly, the Court DENIES Phuc’s Motion to Set Aside Default and Default Judgment.

V.             CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, the Court DENIES Defendants’ Motions to Set Aside Default and Default Judgment.

III.        LEGAL STANDARD

A.            Request for Production of Documents  

In responding to a request for production of documents, a party must provide one of the following: (1) a legally adequate statement of compliance; (2) a statement of inability to comply; or (3) an objection to the particular demand. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 2031.210(a).)[3] If any objections are made, they must: (1) “[i]dentify with particularity any document … falling within any category of item in the demand to which an objection is being made”; and (2) “[s]et forth clearly the extent of, and the specific ground for, the objection.”¿(Id. at § 2031.240(b).) A statement that the responding party is unable to comply with a particular demand “shall affirm that a diligent search and a reasonable inquiry has been made in an effort to comply with that demand”; “shall also specify whether the inability to comply is because the particular item or category has never existed, has been destroyed, has been lost, misplaced, or stolen, or has never been, or is no longer, in the possession, custody, or control of the responding party”;¿ and “shall set forth the name and address of any natural person or organization known or believed by that party to have possession, custody, or control of that item or category of item.” (Id. at § 2031.230.)¿ 

A party who believes a responding party has served (1) an incomplete statement of compliance, (2) an inadequate, incomplete, or evasive statement of compliance, or (3) an unmeritorious objection can move for an order compelling further responses but must “set forth specific acts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought by the demand.” (See id. at §§ 2031.310(a)-(b)(1).)  

B.            Interrogatories  

A propounding party may move for an order compelling further responses to interrogatories if the responding party produced an evasive or incomplete answer or a meritless or overly general objection in response to an interrogatory. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.300(a).) A motion to compel further responses to interrogatories lies where the party to whom the interrogatories were directed gave responses deemed improper by the propounding party; e.g., objections, or evasive or incomplete answers. (See id. at § 2030.300.)¿The moving party must also include reasons why further answers should be ordered: legal or factual arguments why the answers given were incomplete or nonresponsive, or the objections invalid.¿(See Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1345(c).)  

However, where a timely motion to has been filed, “the burden is on the responding party to justify any objection or failure to fully answer the interrogatories.”¿(Weil & Brown, Cal. Prac. Guide: Civil Pro. Before Trial (The Rutter Group 2017) ¶ 8:1179.)¿“The ruling usually is based on consideration of the following factors: [¶] the relationship of the information sought to the issues framed in the pleadings; [¶] the likelihood that disclosure will be of practical benefit to the party seeking discovery; [¶] the burden or expense likely to be encountered by the responding party in furnishing the information sought.”¿(Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc. v. Superior Court (Rolfe) (1968) 263 Cal.App.2d 12, 19.) 

IV.         MEET AND CONFER

Before bringing a motion to compel further responses to any discovery request, the moving party must make efforts to meet and confer in good faith and submit a declaration showing a reasonable and good faith attempt, either in person, by telephone, or by videoconference, to informally resolve each issue presented by the motion. (See Code Civ. Proc. §§ 2016.040, 2031.310(b)(2), 2030.300(b), & 2033.290(b).) Failure to make such attempt constitutes “misuse of discovery process”. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 2023.010(i) [amended eff. 1/1/26].)  

“A reasonable and good-faith attempt at informal resolution…requires that counsel attempt to talk the matter over, compare their views, consult, and deliberate.” (Townsend v. Superior Court (1998) 61 Cal.App.4th 1431, 1439.) In evaluating whether a meet and confer was reasonable and in good faith, “[a]n evaluation of whether, from the perspective of a reasonable person in the position of the discovering party, additional effort appeared likely to bear fruit, should also be considered.” (Clement v. Alegre (2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 1277, 1293-94.)  

El Sereno’s counsel, Andrew C. Jhun, attests he emailed Anbessaw’s counsel a meet and confer letter and twice requested availability for a telephonic meet and confer. (See Declaration of Andrew C. Jhun filed August 14, 2026 (“Jhun Decl.”), ¶¶ 14-15.) Jhun attests Anbessaw’s counsel did not respond. (See id.)

The Court will consider the merits of the Motion despite both counsel’s failure to comply with their meet and confer obligations. Future failures by either counsel could be met with sanctions or denial of the relief sought without addressing the merits.

V.            ANALYSIS

A.            El Sereno’s Motion FROGs is Granted.

El Sereno argues further response to Nos. 1.1 and 17.1 are warranted because Anbessaw served objection only responses, however because Anbessaw served untimely responses, it waived all objections. (See id.) El Sereno contends Anbessaw’s responses were due July 3, 2026, at noon and it untimely served its responses at 6:21 p.m. on that date. (See id.) El Sereno also argues the objections are inapplicable.

Anbessaw argues the noon deadline El Sereno imposed when confirming the deadline date was not discussed when the parties were deciding the deadline nor did Anbessaw accept it. (See Opp. at 2:6-10.) Moreover, Anbessaw argues §2030.270(a) only requires that parties agree to an extension date, not an hour. (See id., at 4:24-27.) Anbessaw also argues the Motion is moot because by the time of the hearing it will have served further responses. (See id., at 2:3-5.) In the alternative, Anbessaw requests relief from any waiver under section 2031.300(a). (See id., at 2:11-12.)

El Sereno argues it has not received further responses. (See Reply at 3:5.) El Sereno argues the Court need not reach whether the parties agreed to a noon deadline as the objections are meritless. (See id., at 3:24-25.) Moreover, El Sereno argues Anbessaw never objected to the noon deadline. (See id., at 4:18.)

The Court finds Anbessaw’s responses were timely. Per Anbessaw’s counsel’s June 26, 2026, email, there was no noon deadline memorialized as part of the extended deadline. (See Jhun Decl., ¶ 9, Ex. 2.) Only three days before the deadline did El Sereno add the noon deadline, which was never agreed upon by opposing counsel. (See id.) Since Anbessaw served responses by the deadline date, they are timely.

The Court finds further response to Nos. 1.1 and 17.1 are warranted. The objections are unsubstantiated and thus overruled. The Court GRANTS El Sereno’s Motion FROGs.

VI.         DISCOVERY SANCTIONS

The court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel further response to a demand, interrogatories, or requests for admission, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust. (See Code Civ. Proc. §§ 2031.310(h), 2030.300(d), 2033.290(c).) 

El Sereno requests discovery sanctions in the amount of $3,960.00 in connection with its Motion RFPs and $3,360.00 in connection with its Motion FROGs. Counsel attests he spent 5.5 hours preparing the Motion RFPs and will spend 1 hour preparing the reply and attending the hearing. (See RFPs. Jhun Decl., ¶ 21-22.) Counsel attests he spent 4.5 hours preparing the Motion FROGs and will spend 1 hour preparing the reply and attending the hearing. (See FROGs. Jhun Decl., ¶ 19-20.) Counsel’s hourly rate is $600.00. (See id.) Counsel also incurred the cost for two $60.00 filing fees.

The Court awards El Sereno’s counsel $2,820.00. The Court finds 1.5 hours to prepare each motion, 1 hour to prepare the substantially similar replies, and 0.5 hours to remotely attend the hearing at the requested hourly rate is reasonable. The Court also awards the filing fee amounts.

Accordingly, Anbessaw’s counsel is ordered to pay El Sereno’s counsel $2,820.00 on or before October 16, 2026.

VII.      CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, the Court GRANTS El Sereno’s Motion FROGs and RFPs. Anbessaw is ordered to provide further code complaint responses without objection on or before September 30, 2026.

Anbessaw’s counsel is ordered to pay El Sereno’s counsel $2,820.00 on or before October 16, 2026.

I.               BACKGROUND 

Plaintiff Shawn Partovi (“Plaintiff”) brings this habitability action against Defendants Yanna Sapozhnikov and Danil Sapozhnikov (jointly, “Defendants”). Plaintiff alleges he was the tenant of a single-family house located at 4121 Picasso Avenue, Woodland Hills, CA 91364 (“Property”). Plaintiff alleges the Property was unsafe, unsanitary, and uninhabitable. Plaintiff alleges Defendants owned and managed the Property but failed to cure the Property’s defects.

Defendants demur to and move to strike portions of the Complaint.

 

II.            PROCEDURAL HISTORY

On April 29, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Complaint alleging (1) Constructive Eviction; (2) Breach of Covenant of Quiet Enjoyment; (3) Tortious Breach of Implied Warranty of Habitability; (4) Statutory Breach of Warranty of Habitability (Civ. Code §§ 1941, 1941.1 and 1942.4)[1]; (5) Breach of Contract; (6) Breach of Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing; (7) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress; (9) Private Nuisance; (10) Unfair Business Practices; (11) Negligence; (12) Negligent Maintenance of the Premises; (13) Tenant Harassment and Retaliation; (14) Tenant Harassment; (15) Negligent Violation of Statutory Duty; and (16) Unlawful Retention of Security Deposit.

On July 30, 2026, Defendants filed the instant Demurrer with Motion to Strike.

On September 1, 2026, Plaintiff filed its Oppositions.

On September 4, 2026, Defendants filed a Reply to the Motion to Strike.

III.         MEET AND CONFER

Before filing a demurrer or motion to strike, the moving party shall meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer or motion to strike for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer or motion to strike. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41(a), §435.5(a).) A declaration must be filed with a demurrer regarding the results of the meet and confer process. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41(a)(3).) 

Defendants satisfied their meet and confer obligation. (See Declaration of Samantha R. Maixner filed July 30, 2026, ¶¶ 3-4.)

IV.          DEMURRER

A.             Legal Standard

A demurrer may be brought if insufficient facts are stated to support the cause of action asserted. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 430.10(e).) “[T]he court gives the complaint a reasonable interpretation, and treats the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded.”¿(Id.) Allegations are to be liberally construed. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 452.) In testing the sufficiency of the complaint, the court must assume the truth of (1) the properly pleaded factual allegations; (2) facts that can be reasonably inferred from those expressly pleaded; and (3) judicially noticed matters. (See Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. (See SKF Farms v. Superior Court¿(1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 902, 905.) A demurrer can only be sustained when it disposes of an entire cause of action.¿(See Poizner v. Fremont Gen. Corp. (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 97, 119; Kong v. City of Hawaiian Gardens Redev. Agency (2003) 108 Cal.App.4th 1028, 1046.)

Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (See Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the complainant to show the Court a pleading can be amended successfully. (See id.) However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245.)

B.             Analysis

1.               4th Cause of Action –Breach of Warranty of Habitability

(Civ. Code §§ 1941, 1941.1, & 1942.4)

Civil Code § 1941 establishes an implied warranty of habitability. (See Civ. Code § 1941.) Civil Code § 1941.1 enumerates standard characteristics a dwelling must possess to be deemed tenantable for purposes of § 1941. (See Civ. Code § 1941.1(a).) Civil Code § 1942.4 provides a statutory cause of action against a landlord who demands or collects rent, issues a notice of rent increase rent, or serves a 3-day notice to pay or quit if all of the following conditions exist prior to the landlord’s demand or notice: (1) the rental unit lacks the affirmative standard characteristics enumerated in § 1941.1, or violates Health and Safety Code § 17920.10, or is deemed and declared substandard as set forth in Health and Safety Code § 17920.3; (2) A public officer or employee who is responsible for the enforcement of any housing law, after inspecting the premises, has notified the landlord or its agent in writing of her obligations to abate the nuisance or repair the substandard conditions; (3) The conditions are not abated 35 days beyond service of the notice without good cause for the delay; and (4) The conditions were not caused by an act or omission of the tenant or lessee in violation of § 1929 or 1941.2. (See Civ. Code § 1942.2.)

Defendants argue the fourth cause of action fails to allege written notice from a public enforcement officer and the lapse of a 35-day cure period. (See Dem. at 4:13-15.) Defendants argue alleging Plaintiff’s complaints to Defendants does not satisfy the statutory notice requirement. (See id., at 6:1-2.)

Plaintiff argues any failure to meet the requirements set forth by § 1942.4 does not negate the allegations concerning Defendants’ failure to maintain the Property in compliance with Civil Code §§ 1941 and 1941.1. (See Opp. at 8:10-13.) Plaintiff argues because his habitability theories asserted under §§ 1941 and 1941.1 are sufficiently alleged, the demurrer should be overruled. (See id., at 8:25-28.)

The Court will overrule the demurrer. Here the Complaint alleges the Property was untenantable due to the lack of standard characteristics required by § 1941.1. (See Compl., ¶¶ 23, 83.) Plaintiff alleges these conditions persisted and caused him illness and damages. (See id., ¶¶ 43, 86-87.) The Court agrees these allegations fail to state a claim under § 1942.4 because there are no allegations satisfying the second and third requirements of § 1942.4. However, Plaintiff alleges the rental unit lacked the affirmative standard characteristics enumerated in § 1941.1 which is sufficient to state a claim pursuant to §§ 1941 and 1941.1. Practically speaking then, Plaintiff’s fourth cause of action presents alternate theories for his statutory breach of warranty of habitability, one of which – section 1942.4 – is defectively pled.

Thus, the Court SUSTAINS IN PART Defendant’s Demurrer to the Fourth Cause of Action for Statutory Breach of Warranty of Habitability to the extent it relies on Civil Code § 1942.4 WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

2.               10th Cause of Action –Business and Professions Code § 17200

To set forth a claim for a violation of Business and Professions Code § 17200 (“UCL”), Plaintiff must establish Defendant was engaged in an “unlawful, unfair or fraudulent business act or practice and unfair, deceptive, untrue or misleading advertising” and certain specific acts. (See Bus. & Prof. Code, § 17200.) A cause of action for unfair competition “is not an all-purpose substitute for a tort or contract action.” (Cortez v. Purolator Air Filtration Products Co. (2000) 23 Cal.4th 163, 173.) “Under the UCL’s ‘unfair’ prong, ‘a practice may be deemed unfair even if not specifically proscribed by some other law.’” (Beverage v. Apple, Inc. (2024) 101 Cal.App.5th 736, 748 [quoting Cel-Tech Communications, Inc. v. Los Angeles Cellular Telephone Co. (1999) 20 Cal.4th 163, 180].) “An action under the UCL ‘is not an all-purpose substitute for a tort or contract action.’” (That v. Alders Maintenance Assn. (2012) 206 Cal.App.4th 1419, 1427.) “Proposition 64 amended the UCL to provide a standing requirement for persons seeking relief under the UCL: a person bringing an action under the UCL must establish that he or she “has suffered injury in fact and has lost money or property as a result of the unfair competition.” (Morgan v. AT&T Wireless Services, Inc. (2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 1235; see also Gray v. Dignity Health (2021) 70 Cal.App.5th 225, 237.)

Defendants argue the restitution Plaintiff seeks, a retroactive reduction in the rent he paid for the Property, is not restitution within the meaning of the UCL. (See Dem. at 6:26-27.) Second, Defendants argue because Plaintiff has adequate legal remedies available to him, equitable relief under the UCL is foreclosed. (See id., at 7:7-15, citing Prudential Home Mortgage Co. v. Superior Court (1998) 66 Cal.App.4th 1236, 1249.) Third, Defendants argue to the extent the UCL claim relies on Civil Code § 1942.4, it fails for the same reasons the fourth cause of action fails. (See id., at 7:22-24.)

Plaintiff argues the alleged statutory habitability violations and Defendants’ alleged continued collection of rent despite knowledge of those conditions provide the predicate for the unlawful and unfair prongs of the UCL claim. (See Opp. at 10:1-4.) Plaintiff argues he seeks restitution, through the return of rental payments, not compensatory damages under this claim. (See id., at 10:5-17.) Plaintiff also argues the existence of other legal remedies does not preclude his UCL claim as UCL remedies are cumulative to remedies available under other laws. (See id., at 10:25-28, citing Cortez v. Purolator Air Filtration Products Co. (2000) 23 Cal.4th 163, 179.)

The Court finds the claim is sufficiently stated. Here, the Complaint alleges given the Property’s uninhabitable conditions including under Civil Code § 1941.1, Defendants’ decision to lease the Property to Plaintiff and collect rent from Plaintiff constitute unlawful, unfair, and fraudulent business practices. (See Compl., ¶ 126.) Plaintiff alleges he suffered illness and loss in his leasehold value. (See id., ¶¶ 119, 127.) Plaintiff seeks restitution of all sums of money obtained by Defendants during the lease term. (See id., ¶ 130.) On these allegations, Plaintiff has satisfied the unlawful prong of the UCL claim by alleging Defendants violated the breach of implied warranty of habitability by breaching the standards for residential tenantability in Civil Code § 1941.1, failing to cure the defects, and continuing to collect rent from Plaintiff while the Property remained untenantable. Plaintiff also sufficiently seeks restitution in the form of return of rental payments made while the Property was untenantable. (See Cortez, 23 Cal.4th 163, 177 [the object of restitution includes “money that once had been in the possession of the person to whom it was to be restored. The status quo ante to be achieved by the restitution order was to again place the victim in possession of that money.”].)

“UCL remedies are cumulative to remedies available under other laws (§17205) and, as section 17203 indicates, have an independent purpose –deterrence of and restitution for unfair business practices... The court's discretion is very broad. Section 17203 does not mandate restitutionary or injunctive relief when an unfair business practice has been shown.” (Cortez, 23 Cal.4th 163, 179-180.) Because the UCL remedies provision contemplates that UCL remedies will coexist with other legal remedies and are discretionary, the Court does not find the existence of adequate legal remedies necessarily precludes Plaintiff from seeking equitable remedies under his UCL claim at the pleadings stage. (See Bus. & Prof. Code § 17205; Zhang v. Superior Court (2013) 57 Cal.4th 364 [“UCL remedies are ‘cumulative ... to the remedies or penalties available under all other laws of this state’” and are discretionary]; see also Rader Co. v. Stone (1986) 178 Cal.App.3d 10, 29 [A plaintiff is entitled to plead facts or legal theories in the alternative, even ones that are inconsistent].)

Thus, the Court OVERRULES Defendant’s Demurrer to the Tenth Cause of Action for Unfair Business Practices.

V.             MOTION TO STRIKE

A.             Legal Standard

The court may, upon a motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 436(a).) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (See id., at § 437.)

A complaint including a request for punitive damages must also include allegations showing that the plaintiff is entitled to such an award. (See Clauson v. Superior Court (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1253, 1255.) “Malice” means conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others. (Civ. Code, § 3294(c)(1).) “Despicable conduct” is “conduct which is so vile, base, contemptible, miserable, wretched or loathsome that it would be looked down upon and despised by ordinary decent people.” (Tomaselli v. Transamerica Co. (1994) 25 Cal.App.4th 1269, 1286-1287.)

B.             Analysis

Defendants move to strike punitive damages allegations and prayer from the Complaint as follows:

(1)  Prayer for Relief, Paragraph 8: “For punitive and exemplary damages based on Defendants’ willful, malicious, wanton and fraudulent misconduct, in an amount according to proof at trial,” in its entirety;

(2)  Paragraph 47: “DEFENDANTS’ behavior simply shocks the conscience and falls well outside the standards of civilized decency, and as such, punitive damages are warranted in this case,” in its entirety;

(3)  Paragraph 71 in its entirety;

(4)  Paragraph 80 in its entirety;

(5)   Paragraph 105 in its entirety;

(6)  Paragraph 120 in its entirety;

(7)  Paragraph 142 in its entirety;

(8)  Paragraph 152 in its entirety; and

(9)  Paragraph 171 in its entirety.

Defendants moves to strike punitive damages allegations and prayer on grounds they make boilerplate allegations they acted willfully, maliciously, or oppressively without facts alleging conduct amounting to something beyond a mere habitability dispute. (See Mot. at 5:3-17, 6:20-27.)

Plaintiff argues the Complaint alleges sufficiently alleges facts supporting its punitive damages allegations and prayer including that the Property suffered from various defects, Plaintiff repeatedly notified Defendants of the defects and their effect on his health and safety, Defendants failed to cure the defects, and Defendants engaged in harassing and retaliatory conduct. (See Opp. at 4:4-9.) Plaintiff relies on Penner v. Falk (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 858, 867, to argue his allegations are sufficient. (See id., at 4:10-16.)

Defendants argue Penner concerned notice of repeated violent crimes, which is unlike the severity of the allegations here. (See Reply at 2:5-9.) Defendants argue here Plaintiff only alleges defects (a noisy water heater, discolored water, and an insect infestation), notice to Defendants, and their dismissive and hostile responses, which are unpleasant but not conduct sufficient to support punitive damages. (See id., at 3:12-20.)

The Court finds the Complaint alleges sufficient allegations to support the punitive damages allegations and prayer Defendants seek to strike. In Stoiber v. Honeychuck (1980) 101 Cal.App.3d 903, 921, the Court of Appeal found allegations “defendant had actual knowledge of defective conditions in the premises including leaking sewage, deteriorated flooring, falling ceiling, leaking roof, broken windows, and other unsafe and dangerous conditions” and that defendants. . . “acted with full knowledge of the consequences thereof and the damage being caused to plaintiff, and their conduct was willful, oppressive and malicious” were sufficient to support a prayer for exemplary damages.

Here, the Complaint alleges the Plaintiff occupied the Property from March 1, 2025, to January 1, 2026. (See Compl., ¶ 12.) The Complaint alleges the Property suffered from defects during this time including brown shower water, a water heater that emitted loud, persistent noises, and pest infestations of house centipedes, cockroaches, and spiders. (See id., ¶¶ 3, 27, 33.) Plaintiff alleges he had to shower elsewhere on at least one occasion and that house centipedes were on his bed. (See id., ¶¶ 31, 33.) Plaintiff alleges the Property was untenantable under Civil Code § 1941.1. (See id., ¶ 23.) Plaintiff alleges he notified Defendants of these hazardous conditions, as early as two weeks after occupying the Property, but they failed to correct the defects. (See id., ¶ 24, 28.) Plaintiff alleges Defendants informed him that the exterminator only came once a month and addresses the outside perimeter of the Property. (See id., ¶ 33.) Plaintiff alleges Defendants were dismissive of Plaintiff’s complaints, accused him of lying, and consciously disregarded his health and safety. (See id., ¶¶ 37, 41, 45-46.) The Court finds the allegations are similar to the allegations found sufficient in Stoiber, as Plaintiff likewise alleges notice to Defendants during a period of about nine months, conditions that didn’t satisfy the standard for tenantability for that same period, and Defendants’ disregard of Plaintiff’s complaints. The Court concludes a trier of fact could find this conduct amounts to despicable conduct warranting punitive damages. Thus, the Court DENIES Defendants’ Motion to Strike.

VI.          CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, the Court SUSTAINS IN PART Defendant’s Demurrer to the Fourth Cause of Action for Statutory Breach of Warranty of Habitability to the extent it relies on Civil Code § 1942.4 WITH LEAVE TO AMEND but OVERRULES it as to the Tenth Cause of Action for violation of Business & Professions Code § 17200.

The Court DENIES Defendants’ Motion to Strike.
