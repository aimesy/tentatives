ANALYSIS:

 

I.                Background

 

On July 17, 2023, Plaintiffs Kimberly Di Bonaventura and Boomin’ Real Estate (“Plaintiffs”) filed a Complaint for Breach of Settlement Agreement against Defendants Johnathan Lachman and Omerta Farms, LLC (“Defendants”) and Does 1-50.

 

On November 13, 2023, Defendants filed an Answer.

 

On June 26, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the instant Motion for Order to Substitute in Administrator Allison B. Margolin for the Estate of Jonathan Lachman (the “Motion”).

 

To date, no Opposition to the Motion has been filed.

 

II.              Legal Standard

 

Probate Code section 9370 states:

 

(a) An action or proceeding pending against the decedent at the time of death may not be continued against the decedent’s personal representative unless all of the following conditions are satisfied:

(1) A claim is first filed as provided in this part.

(2) The claim is rejected in whole or in part.

(3) Within three months after the notice of rejection is given, the plaintiff applies to the court in which the action or proceeding is pending for an order to substitute the personal representative in the action or proceeding. This paragraph applies only if the notice of rejection contains a statement that the plaintiff has three months within which to apply for an order for substitution.

(b) No recovery shall be allowed in the action against property in the decedent’s estate unless proof is made of compliance with this section.

 

 (Prob. Code, § 9370.)

 

III.            Discussion

 

Plaintiffs move to substitute in Administrator Allison B. Margolin as a defendant in this litigation for Lachman, who is deceased. (Motion, Clark Decl.) The Motion includes a claim rejected by Margolin, which states that notice of the rejected claim was given on March 30, 2026. (Motion, Clark Decl., Exh. 4.)

 

As an initial matter, the Court finds there is no proof of service filed demonstrating the Motion and notice of hearing have been served in any manner on Defendants or Allison B. Margolin.

 

Thus, the Motion is CONTINUED to allow Plaintiffs an opportunity to file proper proof of service demonstrating the Motion and notice of hearing were served on all parties to the case as well as Margolin.  

 

IV.            Conclusion & Order

 

For the foregoing reasons, Plaintiffs Kimberly Di Bonaventura and Boomin’ Real Estate, LLC’s Motion for Order to Substitute in Administrator Allison B. Margolin for the Estate of Jonathan Lachman is CONTINUED to Friday, September 18, 2026, at 9:00 a.m. in Department 512 of the Stanley Mosk Courthouse.

ANALYSIS:

 

I.               
Background
 

 

On March 19, 2026, Plaintiff Marcus
Byrd (“Plaintiff”) initiated the instant action against Defendants Michaels
Stores, Inc. (“Michaels”); EmployBridge, LLC (“EmployBridge”); and Suzy Kurt
(“Kurt”) (“Defendants”) and Does 1-100, alleging causes of action for
Disability Discrimination in Violation of FEHA; Hostile Work Environment
Harassment on the Basis of Disability; Failure to Prevent Discrimination,
Harassment, and Retaliation; Wrongful Termination in Violation of Public
Policy; Failure to Pay Wages Due and Owing Upon Discharge; Failure to Provide
Accurate Wage Statements; and Failure to Provide Personnel File for Inspection.
(Compl., p. 1.)

 

On April 16, 2026, Plaintiff amended
the Complaint to correct the name “Suzy Kurt” to “Suzanne Kury” (“Kury”).

 

            On June
10, 2026, Defendants filed the instant Motion to Compel Arbitration (the
“Motion”).

 

On August 6, 2026, Plaintiff filed an
Opposition and Evidentiary Objections to the Bredehoft and Huston declarations
filed in support of the Motion.

 

On August 12, 2026, Defendants filed a
Reply and Responses to Plaintiff’s Evidentiary Objections.

 

II.             
Evidentiary
Objections

 

In conjunction with the Opposition,
Plaintiff submits various objections to the Bredehoft and Huston declarations filed
in conjunction with the Motion. (Opp., Evid. Objections.) In conjunction with
the Reply, Defendants submit responses to all of Plaintiff’s objections.
(Reply, Reply to Plaintiff’s Objections to Evidence.)

 

The Court OVERRULES all of Plaintiff’s
objections to the Bredehoft and Huston declarations as the Court finds the
statements challenged contain sufficient personal knowledge and foundation, are
relevant, and do not constitute impermissible hearsay for the purpose(s)
offered.

 

III.           
Legal
Standard & Discussion

 

Defendants bring the instant Motion
seeking to compel Plaintiff to submit to arbitration based on the arbitration
clause (“Arbitration Agreement”) included in Plaintiff’s employment contract
with Defendants. (Motion, MPA, pp. 1-2, Bredehoft Decl., Exhs. A-B.)

 

A.    Governing Law

 

Here, the Arbitration Agreement
expressly states it is governed by the Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”).
(Motion, Bredehoft Decl., Exh.,
B, p. 1.)

 

The Federal Arbitration Act, set forth
at 9 USC, section 2, provides: 

 

A written provision in any maritime
transaction or a contract evidencing a transaction involving commerce to settle
by arbitration a controversy thereafter arising out of such contract or
transaction, or the refusal to perform the whole or any part thereof, or an
agreement in writing to submit to arbitration an existing controversy arising
out of such a contract, transaction, or refusal, shall be valid, irrevocable,
and enforceable, save upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the
revocation of any contract. 

 

      (9
U.S.C., § 2.)  

 

      Accordingly,
under both the FAA and California Law, arbitration agreements are valid,
irrevocable, and enforceable, except on such grounds that exist at law or
equity for voiding a contract. (Winter v. Window Fashions Professions, Inc.
(2008) 166 Cal.App.4th 943, 947.)

 

 Thus, the Court applies the FAA’s procedural
rules, and California law when appropriate and when not preempted by the FAA.
(See e.g., Western Bagel Co. v. Superior Court (Calderon) (2021) 66
Cal.App.5th 649, 664-666.)

 

B.    Existence of Arbitration Agreement

 

In deciding a motion to compel
arbitration, trial courts must first decide whether an enforceable arbitration
agreement exists between the parties, and then determine the second gateway
issue of whether the claims are covered within the scope of the agreement. (See
Chiron Corp. v. Ortho Diagnostic Systems, Inc. (2000) 207 F.3d 1126,
1130 [“The court’s role under the [FAA] is therefore limited to determining (1)
whether a valid agreement to arbitrate exists and, if it does, (2) whether the
agreement encompasses the dispute at issue”].)

 

“The burden of persuasion is always on
the moving party to prove the existence of an arbitration agreement with the
opposing party by a preponderance of the evidence.” (Gamboa v.
Northeast Community Clinic (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 158, 165.)

 

Defendants present an authenticated
copy of the Arbitration Agreement between EmployBridge and Plaintiff that bears
Plaintiff’s signature and thus meets its initial burden. (Motion, Bredehoft
Decl., Exhs. A-B; see Bannister v. Marinidence
Opco, LLC (2021) 64 Cal.App.5th 541, 543-544 [The moving party “can meet
its initial burden by attaching to the [motion or] petition a copy of the
arbitration agreement purporting to bear the [opposing party's] signature”].)

 

Plaintiff does not challenge the
existence of the Arbitration Agreement itself, Plaintiff’s signing of the
Arbitration Agreement, or that the Complaint’s claims as to EmployBridge and
Michaels are within the scope of the Arbitration Agreement.

 

However, in Opposition, Plaintiff states Kury
cannot enforce the Arbitration Agreement under principles of third-party
beneficiary or equitable estoppel as Kury has not satisfied the elements for
third-party beneficiaries; the Agreement contains merely boilerplate language
regarding third-party beneficiaries; and Plaintiff’s statutory FEHA claims
against Kury cannot be transformed into contract-based claims intertwined with
the Agreement. (Opp., pp. 10-11.)

 

In Reply, Defendants assert Kury may enforce
the Agreement as the Agreement expressly provides “that its provisions apply,
as explicit third-party beneficiaries, to Defendant EmployBridge’s clients
where Plaintiff may be sent to work.” (Reply, p. 5.) In the Complaint,
Plaintiff “alleges that Defendant EmployBridge placed him at Defendant
Michaels’ Lancaster facility and that Michaels was his joint employer. …
Plaintiff sues Kury solely for alleged conduct undertaken in her capacity as
Michaels’ Human Resources Manager, while ‘acting within the course and scope of
her employment and as an agent of Defendant Michaels…. Plaintiff also alleges
that in Kury’s capacity as a Human Resources Manager, she ‘was a managing agent
of Defendant Michaels with authority over employment decisions, including
hiring, termination, accommodation, and compliance with the FEHA.’ ” (Reply, p.
5; Compl.) Apart from the terms of the Agreement, Defendants also state that
“equitable estoppel independently supports arbitration.” (Reply, p. 6.) Defendants
assert Plaintiff’s claims are also so intertwined with the underlying contract
obligations that it warrants Kury’s invocation of the Arbitration Agreement.
(Reply, p. 6.)

 

“ ‘Generally speaking, one must be a party to
an arbitration agreement to be bound by it or invoke it.’ ” (Watson v.
Professional Business Management Corp. (2026) 120 Cal.App.5th 860, 868.)
There are limited exceptions to this general rule, including “incorporation by
reference; assumption; agency; veil-piercing or alter ego; estoppel; and third
party beneficiary.” (Ibid.)

 

In regard to the application of equitable
estoppel principles, “under both federal and California decisional authority, a
nonsignatory defendant may invoke an arbitration clause to compel a signatory
plaintiff to arbitrate its claims when the causes of action against the
nonsignatory are ‘intimately founded in and intertwined’ with the underlying
contract obligations.” (Boucher v. Alliance Title Co., Inc.
(2005) 127 Cal.App.4th 262, 271.) “[A]
party may not make use of a contract containing an arbitration clause and then
attempt to avoid the duty to arbitrate by defining the forum in which the
dispute will be resolved.” (Id., at p. 272.)

 

Here, the Arbitration Agreement is between
EmployBridge and Plaintiff, as well as Employbridge’s “related entities,
parents, affiliates and subsidiaries,” and the Agreement states that “all of
the provisions within this Agreement are intended to also apply to the
Company’s clients where you may be sent to work as explicit third-party
beneficiaries of this Agreement.” (Motion, Bredehoft Decl., Exh. B, p. 1.)

 

The parties do not dispute that Plaintiff
signed the Arbitration Agreement with EmployBridge and was sent to work at
Michaels, whereat Kury also worked. (See Compl.; Motion; Opp.; Reply.)

 

Given these circumstances, the Court
finds that Michaels and Kury are EmployBridge’s clients and thus members of the
classes of persons/entities for whose benefit the arbitration clause was, in
part, made. (See Watson, supra, 120 Cal.App.5th at p. 868; Ronay Family Limited Partnership v.
Tweed (2013) 216
Cal.App.4th 830, 838-839.) Defendants therefore made a sufficient showing to
enforce the arbitration agreement against Michaels and Kury as third-party
beneficiaries.

 

Moreover, the Complaint in this action
alleges that Michaels and EmployBridge were joint employers of Plaintiff, and
Kury was employed by Michaels and, at all times relevant to this action, was
acting within the course and scope of her employment and as an agent of
Michaels. (Compl., p. 2.)

 

As such, the Court also finds the
instant action’s “causes of
action against the nonsignator[ies] are ‘intimately founded in and intertwined’
with the underlying contract obligations,” and thus all Defendants are bound by
the Arbitration Agreement. (See Boucher, supra,
127 Cal.App.4th at pp. 272-273.)

 

            Given
these circumstances, the Court finds the Arbitration Agreement applies to all Defendants,
including Kury.  

 

Accordingly, the Court finds
Defendants met their burden to prove the existence of a valid arbitration
agreement between all the named parties that encompasses the claims raised in
the instant action.

 

C.    Enforceability – Unconscionability

 

“ ‘The prevailing view is that [procedural
and substantive unconscionability] must¿both¿be present in order for a
court to exercise its discretion to refuse to enforce a contract or clause
under the doctrine of unconscionability.’ [Citation] But they need not be
present in the same degree. “Essentially a sliding scale is invoked which disregards
the regularity of the procedural process of the contract formation, that
creates the terms, in proportion to the greater harshness or unreasonableness
of the substantive terms themselves.” . . . In other words, the more
substantively oppressive the contract term, the less evidence of procedural
unconscionability is required to come to the conclusion that the term is
unenforceable, and vice versa.” (Armendariz v. Foundation Health Psychcare
Services, Inc.¿(2000) 24 Cal.4th 83, 114 (“Armendariz”).) 

 

Procedural Unconscionability

 

Plaintiff contends there is a high
level of procedural unconscionability in this matter given that Defendants’
declarations in support of the Motion do not expressly state Plaintiff was free
to accept or decline the Arbitration Agreement; the Arbitration Agreement was
presented to Plaintiff in a bundle of other onboarding documents; the
Arbitration Agreement was a standardized, pre-printed form drafted unilaterally
by EmployBridge; there is inherent inequality of bargaining power between the
parties; Plaintiff was under pressure by Defendants to complete the paperwork; Defendants
did not explain the contents of the onboarding documents, including the
Arbitration Agreement, to Plaintiff; the Arbitration Agreement was presented as
a take-it-or-leave-it basis; and the Arbitration Agreement references the FAA
without “attaching, describing, or otherwise disclosing that referenced federal
procedure anywhere within the four corners of the Agreement itself.” (Opp., pp.
5-9.)

 

In Reply, Defendants highlight that
the top of the Agreement, written in large font that is a different color than
the body of the Agreement, expressly states that signing the Arbitration
Agreement is voluntary and not a condition of employment, and the bottom of the
Agreement provided Plaintiff with the option to accept or decline the
Arbitration Agreement. (Reply, pp. 3-4.) Further, Defendants were under no
obligation to provide Plaintiff with the Arbitration Agreement at a time
separate and apart from other onboarding documents, and Plaintiff’s failure to
read the Arbitration Agreement does not excuse Plaintiff from participating in
arbitration. (See Reply, p. 4.) Finally, Defendants highlight that Plaintiff’s
own declaration demonstrates Plaintiff was not pressured to sign the
Arbitration Agreement as Plaintiff states having six days to complete
onboarding documents, including the Arbitration Agreement, prior to Plaintiff’s
employment start date. (Reply, pp. 4-5.)

 

“ ‘Procedural unconscionability’ concerns the
manner in which the contract was negotiated and the circumstances of the
parties at that time. It focuses on factors of oppression and surprise. The
oppression component arises from an inequality of bargaining power of the
parties to the contract and an absence of real negotiation or a meaningful
choice on the part of the weaker party. The component of surprise arises when
the challenged terms are ‘hidden in a prolix printed form drafted by the party
seeking to enforce them.’ ” (Nyulassy v. Lockheed Martin Corp. (2004)
120 Cal.App.4th 1267, 1281.) 

 

As to procedural unconscionability,
the Court finds there is inequality in bargaining power between Plaintiff and
Defendants given the nature of their roles that makes it likely that Plaintiff
felt at least some pressure to sign the Arbitration Agreement. (See Sanchez
v. Western Pizza Enterprises, Inc. (2009) 172 Cal.App.4th 154, 174.)

 

Besides alleging inequality of
bargaining power, the Court finds Plaintiff’s other challenges to the
Arbitration Agreement do not demonstrate any level, let alone a moderate or
high level, of oppression or surprise.

 

The Court finds that the instant
Arbitration Agreement does not appear to be an adhesion contract as it
expressly provides that the Arbitration Agreement is “voluntary and not a
condition of employment” in large font at the top of the Arbitration Agreement
and in blue, which is a different font color than that used in the rest of the
Arbitration Agreement. (Motion, Bredehoft Decl., Exh. B.)

 

Further, the Court finds that Plaintiff’s
failure to read the Arbitration Agreement does not demonstrate a high level of
procedurally unconscionability. (See Ramirez, supra, Cal.App.3d
at p. 754; see also Iyere v. Wise Auto Group (2023) 87 Cal.App.5th 747,
759 [“It is hornbook law that failing to read an agreement before signing it
does not prevent formation of a contract”].) Moreover, Plaintiff was required to act diligently before
signing the Arbitration Agreement, and Defendants were not required to explain
the Arbitration Agreement to Plaintiff. (See Rowland, supra,
(1992) 4 Cal.App.4th at p. 286.)

 

As to Plaintiff’s challenge to the
Arbitration Agreement on the grounds that the FAA was not highlighted or
included in the Arbitration Agreement, the Court finds “Plaintiff does not
claim anything was hidden in the [FAA]. Rather, [Plaintiff] contests only
matters related to the agreement itself. Therefore… the failure to attach the
applicable [FAA] did not increase the procedural unconscionability of the…
arbitration provision.” (Nguyen, supra, 4 Cal.App.5th at p. 249.)

 

To the extent Plaintiff asserts he was
pressured to sign the Arbitration Agreement, the Court finds Plaintiff’s
evidence either fails to demonstrate any pressure by Defendants or refutes this
assertion. Indeed, Plaintiff’s declaration filed in support of the Opposition
states Plaintiff had multiple days to review the onboarding documents,
including the Arbitration Agreement, prior to Plaintiff’s employment start
date, and Plaintiff completed the onboarding documents electronically and
apparently outside the presence of any of Defendants. (Opp., Byrd Decl., ¶¶ 2-8.)
These circumstances demonstrate a lack of temporal pressure or any other kind
of pressure to sign the onboarding documents, including the Arbitration Agreement.

 

Notably, the Court also highlights
that Plaintiff does not dispute that the Arbitration Agreement itself was clear
and not difficult to read.

 

Thus, the Court finds that the
circumstances surrounding Plaintiff’s signing of this Agreement and Arbitration
Agreement contain a low level of procedural unconscionability.

 

The Court now proceeds to address
whether the Arbitration Agreement is substantively unconscionable.

 

Substantive Unconscionability

 

In Opposition, Plaintiff contends the
Arbitration Agreement is substantively unconscionable on the following grounds:
the Agreement’s discovery provision is ambiguous and discretionary, and the
Agreement includes an unconscionable representative action waiver. (Opp., pp.
4-5.)

 

In Reply, Defendants contend the Agreement’s discovery
provision is not substantively unconscionable as the Agreement “ensures
adequate discovery by entrusting discovery management to a neutral arbitrator and, rather than
imposing a one-sided limitation on Plaintiff, and applies equally to both
parties. The Agreement does not prohibit depositions, discovery requests,
expert witnesses or any category of proof Plaintiff contends he will need.”
(Reply, pp. 2-3.) Further, Defendants assert that Plaintiff does not plead any
PAGA or representative causes of action in the Complaint and, in any event, this
provision can be severed from the Agreement. (Reply, p. 3.)

 

Substantive unconscionability focuses on the
actual terms of the agreement and evaluates whether¿the terms¿create “overly
harsh” or “one-sided” results as to shock the conscience.¿ (Armendariz,
supra, 24 Cal.4th at p. 114.)

 

As to substantive unconscionability,
the Court finds the Agreement only holds a low or at most moderate level of
substantive unconscionability. 

 

First, the Court finds the discovery
provision in the Arbitration Agreement does not involve a high level of
unconscionability. As to discovery, the Arbitration Agreement states that “[t]he
arbitrator shall have the discretion to permit reasonable discovery necessary
to allow the parties to adequately arbitrate their claims and/or defenses.”
(Motion, Bredehoft Decl., Exh. B, p. 2.) The Court finds there is no compelling
reason to construe this provision that grants the arbitrator authority to
handle discovery as unconscionable, impermissibly limiting, or one-sided in any
way. (See Ramirez v. Charter Communications, Inc. (2024) 16 Cal.5th 478,
506.) Indeed, courts assume “the arbitrator will act reasonably and in
conformity with the law” (ibid.), and this provision fails to present
any reason this Court should assume the arbitrator will act otherwise. Furthermore,
Plaintiff fails to present any reason, legal authority, or evidence
demonstrating the Court should hold otherwise.

 

Finally, the Court finds there are no
other factors that suggest the Court should find a moderate, let alone high,
level of substantive unconscionability in the Arbitration Agreement’s discovery
provision. (See ibid. [“The assessment of whether a discovery clause is
unconscionable should focus on general factors that can be examined without
relying on subsequent developments. Those factors include the types of
claims covered by the agreement, the amount of discovery allowed, the degree to
which that amount may differ from the amount available in conventional
litigation, any asymmetries between the parties with regard to discovery, and
the arbitrator's authority to order additional discovery”].)

 

As to Plaintiff’s allegations
regarding an impermissible representative waiver in the Arbitration Agreement,
the parties do not dispute that there is not a representative or PAGA claim
alleged in the Complaint. (See Compl.; Opp.; Reply) In any event, the Court
finds that in the instant case, an impermissible representative or PAGA waiver
would be the sole surviving ground for Plaintiff’s assertion that the
Arbitration Agreement is substantively unconscionable which could be severed
from the Arbitration Agreement given that the Arbitration Agreement is not
permeated with unconscionability. (See Subcontracting Concepts (CT), LLC v.
De Melo (2019) 34 Cal.App.5th 201, 215; Viking River Cruises, Inc. v.
Moriana (2022) 596 U.S. 639.)

 

Under the circumstances, the Court finds
that, at most, the Arbitration Agreement contains a low or moderate level of
substantive unconscionability.

 

“As with any contract, the unconscionability
inquiry requires a court to examine the totality of the agreement's substantive
terms as well as the circumstances of its formation to determine whether the
overall bargain was unreasonably one-sided.” (Sonic-Calabasas A, Inc. v.
Moreno (2013) 57 Cal. App. 4th 1109, 1146.) 

 

Based on the foregoing, the Motion is
GRANTED.

Conclusion
& Order

 

Based on the
foregoing reasons, Defendants Michaels Stores, Inc.; EmployBridge, LLC; and
Suzy Kury’s Motion to
Compel Arbitration is GRANTED.
