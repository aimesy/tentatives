ANALYSIS:

 

I.                Background

 

On April 18, 2023, the Court issued a Final Statement of Decision in this matter, finding for Plaintiff Queens Land Builder, Inc. (“Plaintiff”) on the first and second causes of action alleged in the Complaint against Defendants Ming Gu and Cal Garden, Inc. (“Defendants”). (4/18/23 Final Statement of Decision.) The Court did not find for Plaintiffs on the third and fourth causes of action alleged in the Complaint, which Plaintiff effectively withdrew as moot. (4/18/23 Final Statement of Decision.)

 

On September 20, 2023, the Court granted Plaintiff’s Motion for Attorney’s Fees in the reduced amount of $455,053.50 and denied Plaintiff’s Motion to Increase Supersedeas Bond as Moot. (9/20/23 Order.) The Court also granted in part Defendants’ Motion to Tax Costs and taxed Plaintiff’s costs in the amount of $3,585.00. (9/20/23 Order.)  

 

            On March 23, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant Motion for Awad of Attorney Fees on Appeal in the Amount of $332,605.50 (the “Motion”).

 

On May 22, 2026, the Second District Court of Appeal issued a Remittitur affirming the Court’s 04/18/23 judgment and awarding Plaintiff costs on appeal. (Remittitur, filed 05/22/26.)

 

            On June 24, 2026, Defendants filed an Opposition to the Motion.

 

            On June 29, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Reply.

 

That same day, the Court continued the hearing on the Motion.

 

II.              Procedural Issues

 

The Court notes that the Opposition was filed and served one day late. However, given the timely Reply, the Court exercises its discretion to address the Opposition on the merits.

 

III.            Legal Standard

 

California rules of Court, rule 8.891 states that “[e]xcept as provided in this rule or by statute, the prevailing party in a civil appeal is entitled to costs on appeal.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 8891, subd. (a)(1). On appeal, the “prevailing party is the respondent if the appellate division affirms the judgment without modification or dismisses the appeal. The prevailing party is the appellant if the appellate division reverses the judgment in its entirety.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 8.891, subd. (a)(2).)

 

A party may recover only the costs of the following on appeal, if reasonable:

 

(A) Filing fees;

(B) The amount the party paid for any portion of the record, whether an original or a copy or both, subject to reduction by the appellate division under subdivision (e);

(C) The cost to produce additional evidence on appeal;

(D) The costs to notarize, serve, mail, and file the record, briefs, and other papers;

(E) The cost to print and reproduce any brief, including any petition for rehearing or review, answer, or reply;

(F) The cost to procure a surety bond, including the premium, the cost to obtain a letter of credit as collateral, and the fees and net interest expenses incurred to borrow funds to provide security for the bond or to obtain a letter of credit, unless the trial court determines the bond was unnecessary; and

(G) The fees and net interest expenses incurred to borrow funds to deposit with the superior court in lieu of a bond or undertaking, unless the trial court determines the deposit was unnecessary.

 

(2) Unless the court orders otherwise, an award of costs neither includes attorney’s fees on appeal nor precludes a party from seeking them under rule 3.1702.

 

(Cal. Rules of Court, rule 8.891, subd. (d).)

 

“A notice of motion to claim attorney’s fees on appeal … under a statute or contract requiring the court to determine entitlement to the fees, the amount of the fees, or both, must be served and filed within the time for serving and filing the memorandum of costs under rule 8.278(c)(1) in an unlimited civil case or under rule 8.891(c)(1) in a limited civil case.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1702, subd. (c)(1).) In an unlimited case, a memorandum of costs on appeal must be filed and served within 40 days after issuance of the remittitur. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 8.278, subd. (c)(1).)

 

California Rules of Court, rule 3.1702 allows a prevailing party to recover fees pursuant to a statute or contract. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1702, subd. (a) [“Except as otherwise provided by statute, this rule applies in civil cases to claims for statutory attorney's fees and claims for attorney's fees provided for in a contract”].) “Where a contract or a statute creates a right for the prevailing party to recover attorney fees, the prevailing party is also entitled to attorney fees on appeal.” (Villinger/Nicholls Development Co. v. Meleyco (1995) 31 Cal.App.4th 321, 329].)  

 

The Court’s objective is to award attorneys’ fee at the fair market value based on the particular action. (See Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132.) “The reasonable hourly rate is that prevailing in the community for similar work.” (PLCM Group v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095.) “ ‘[T]he fee setting inquiry in California ordinarily begins with the ‘lodestar,’ i.e., the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate.’ ” (Ketchum, supra, 24 Cal.4th at p. 1134.) The lodestar method is based on several factors, as relevant to each particular case: “(1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, (4) the contingent nature of the fee award.” (Id., at p. 1132.) “The ‘ “experienced trial judge is the best judge of the value of professional services rendered in his court, and while his judgment is of course subject to review, it will not be disturbed unless the appellate court is convinced that it is clearly wrong.” ’ ” (Ibid.) A negative multiplier is appropriate when duplicative work is performed. (Thayer v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2001) 92 Cal.App.4th 819, 834.)

 

IV.           Discussion

 

Parties’ Arguments

 

Plaintiff moves for an award of attorney’s fees on the grounds that Plaintiff is the prevailing party on appeal given that the Second District Court of Appeal affirmed the trial court judgment in Plaintiff’s favor. Plaintiff requests $332,605.50 in attorney fees on appeal, reflecting time incurred by two attorneys – 263.2 hours billed at $550.00 per hour, with a 1.5 multiplier, for counsel Steven P. Scandura, and 149 hours billed at $750.00 per hour for counsel Howard Posner, which includes a 1.25 multiplier. (Motion, Scandura Decl., Posner Decl., Exhs. C, D.)

 

In Opposition, Defendants do not dispute that Plaintiff is entitled to attorney’s fees on appeal; however, Defendants contend that the attorney fee request “is not grounded in the marketplace, the actual demands of the work, or any reasonable measure of what this appeal required.” (Opp., p. 1.) Defendants state that Plaintiff’s attorney fees claims are grossly excessive and four times Defendants’ estimate and are thus unreasonable, and the billing records are “riddled with block-billed entries” and charge or a “comprehensive re-review of a trial record [Scandura] generated as trial counsel.” (Opp., pp. 1-7.) Further, Defendants argue against the use of a multiplier at all as Plaintiff fails to make an adequate showing that a multiplier is warranted in this matter. (Opp., pp. 7-8.)

 

In Reply, Plaintiff contends the appeal was entirely fact-based in nature, which thus necessitated the extensive time and effort undertaken by Plaintiff’s counsel to review the record multiple times and prepare the respondent brief. (Reply, pp. 1-3.) As such, Plaintiff asserts the attorney’s fees requested are reasonable and warranted, especially given that any failure by Plaintiff to address Defendants’ contentions on appeal may have been taken as concessions by the Second District Court of Appeal. (Reply, pp. 1-4.) Further, Plaintiff states that the bill to Howard Posner has been paid, which is evidence of reasonableness, and “Defendants attack ‘block’ entries but are not willing to pay for the time needed to itemize each minute time entry.” (Reply, pp. 4-8.) Finally, Plaintiff contends the multiplier is well justified given the contingency nature of the matter and should at least be considered by the trial court. (Reply, pp. 8-10.)

 

Analysis

 

Plaintiff requests $332,605.50 in attorney fees on appeal, reflecting 263.2 hours billed at $550.00 per hour, with a 1.5 multiplier, for work done by Scandura, and 149 hours billed at $750.00 per hour for work done by Posner, which includes a 1.25 multiplier. (Motion, Scandura Decl., Posner Decl., Exhs. C, D.)

 

The parties do not dispute that Plaintiff is entitled to attorney’s fees on appeal pursuant to the remittitur and the Motion; however, the parties dispute the amount of attorney’s fees to which Plaintiff is entitled.

 

To address this issue, the Court first turns to California Rules of Court, rule 8.891. As an initial matter, the Court finds that Plaintiff is the prevailing party on appeal since Plaintiff was the respondent before the appellate division, and “the appellate division affirm[ed] the judgment without modification.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 8.891, subd. (a)(2).)

 

Next, the Court finds the Appellate Division’s award of costs does not automatically entitle Plaintiff to attorney’s fees. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 8.891, subd. (d) [“Unless the court orders otherwise, an award of costs neither includes attorney’s fees on appeal nor precludes a party from seeking them under rule 3.1702”].) Thus, Plaintiff must satisfy the requirements under California Rules of Court, rule 3.1702 for the Court to award attorney’s fees on appeal. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 8.891, subd. (d).)

 

 

Substantively, California Rules of Court, rule 3.1702 allows a prevailing party to recover fees pursuant to a statute or contract. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1702, subd. (a) [“Except as otherwise provided by statute, this rule applies in civil cases to claims for statutory attorney’s fees and claims for attorney’s fees provided for in a contract”].)

 

Here, the parties do not dispute that Plaintiff is the prevailing party and the underlying contract between the parties provides for attorney’s fees. The Court agrees. As such, Plaintiff is entitled to recover attorney’s fees on appeal.

 

In support of the Motion’s requested fees, Plaintiff submits two declarations from Plaintiff’s attorneys, Steven P. Scandura and Howard Posner. (Motion, Scandura Decl., Posner Decl.) Scandura acted as trial counsel in this matter, and Posner acted as appellate counsel. (Motion, Scandura Decl., ¶¶ 1-3, Posner Decl., ¶¶ 1-2.) Both attorneys provide timesheets of hours billed and time incurred in the appeal. (Motion, Scandura Decl., Posner Decl., Exhs. C, D.) Scandura provides timesheets detailing tasks including reviewing emails, conducting legal research regarding bond requirements, reviewing the trial court’s statement of decision, reviewing trial exhibits, reviewing defense exhibits, calls with Posner regarding appellate briefs and oral argument, appearing at appellate oral argument, redrafting/reviewing appellate briefs, and  helping prepare the instant Motion. (Motion, Scandura Decl., Exh. 3.) Posner provides timesheets detailing tasks including reviewing and drafting emails, reviewing trial transcripts and exhibits, reviewing and drafting appellate briefs, conferring with Scandura regarding appellate briefing and trial testimony, revising appellate briefing, preparing for appellate oral argument, and reviewing timesheets and preparing the declaration for the instant Motion. (Motion, Posner Decl., Exh. D.)

 

“[T]he verified time statements of the attorneys, as officers of the court, are entitled to credence in the absence of a clear indication the records are erroneous.” (Horsford v. Board of Trustees of California State University (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 359, 396.) If the motion is supported by evidence, the opposing party must respond with specific evidence showing that the fees are unreasonable. (Premier Med. Mgmt. Sys. v. California Ins. Guarantee Ass’n (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 550, 563.)

 

In Opposition, Defendants submit evidence of a declaration from appellate counsel David Zarmi, who states that, based on Zarmi’s experience handling appeals of comparable scope and complexity, a reasonable total estimate of Plaintiff’s attorney’s fees for this appeal would be approximately 108 hours. (Opp., Zarmi Decl., ¶¶ 1-7.) In Zarmi’s professional judgment, the amount requested by Plaintiff is not adequately justified or explained, and “it is highly unusual for the respondent’s side of a single appeal to employ two separate attorneys—a trial generalist and an appellate specialist—each billing substantial independent hours for the same record, the same brief, and the same argument.” (Opp., Zarmi Decl., ¶¶ 7-8.) In Zarmi’s experience as a Certified Appellate Specialist, the 263.2 hours claimed for trial counsel Scandura’s work is “independently unreasonable and well outside any reasonable range for this type of work. The role of trial counsel in appellate proceedings is typically limited to providing case-specific background and context to the appellate specialist, reviewing a draft of the brief for factual accuracy, and, if appropriate, attending oral argument (for which many trial counsel do not charge the client). In [Zarmi’s] experience, 20 to 30 hours would be a generous estimate for those functions in a case of this scope and complexity.” (Opp., Zarmi Decl., ¶ 9.)

 

In view of all the evidence presented, the Court finds that many of the billing descriptions paired with the dual representation of attorneys warrant a reduction of the fees requested. Indeed, the Court finds Defendants have provided evidence that show many of the fees incurred by Scandura are unreasonable and unnecessarily duplicative of the hours incurred by Posner. For instance, Scandura’s timesheets state Scandura spent over 46.5 hours billed at $550.00 per hour reviewing trial transcripts and trial documents separate and apart from Posner’s time incurred reviewing the trial transcripts and trial documents. (Motion, Scandura Decl., Posner Decl., Exhs. C-D.) The Court finds this work to be duplicative and unreasonable. Further, Posner was the appellate counsel on this matter, and it is unclear to this Court as to why more hours were incurred by Scandura, who served as trial counsel in this matter, than Posner. (Motion, Scandura Decl., Posner Decl., Exhs. C-D.) Zarmi estimates that reviewing the five volumes of Clerk’s transcript and six volumes of Reporter’s Transcript in this matter, researching applicable legal issues, and drafting/reviewing/filing the respondent brief should have taken 100 hours total. (Opp., Zarmi Decl., ¶ 6.) The Court agrees with Zarmi that Plaintiff’s counsel’s combined estimate of 412 hours between Posner and Scandura is excessive and duplicative.

 

Further, the Court notes that it is difficult to ascertain the exact number of hours incurred for many of Scandura’s tasks as Scandura’s timesheet attached to the Motion includes instances of bundled and block billing descriptions. To illustrate, a couple such billing descriptions in Scandura’s timesheet are as follows: 7.5 hours were incurred on “[r]eview[ing] of brief, legal research and reading cases, redrafting, proofreading and corrections,” and 3.5 hours were incurred on “[r]eview[ing] of email and draft[ing] oral argument notes from HP, review[ing] of transcript, review[ing] of Exhibit 26, [and] recalculation of interest to respond to DZ's interest miscalculation claim.” (Motion, Scandura Decl., Exh. C, pp. 4, 6.)

 

Additionally, the Court declines to award a multiplier. The Court is permitted, but not required, to apply a multiplier to an award for attorney’s fees. (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1138 [“the trial court is not required to include a fee enhancement to the basic lodestar figure for contingent risk, exceptional skill, or other factors, although it retains discretion to do so in the appropriate case; moreover, the party seeking a fee enhancement bears the burden of proof”], emphasis in original.) “[A] trial court should award a multiplier for exceptional representation only when the quality of representation far exceeds the quality of representation that would have been provided by an attorney of comparable skill and experience billing at the hourly rate used in the lodestar calculation. Otherwise, the fee award will result in unfair double counting and be unreasonable. Nor should a fee enhancement be imposed for the purpose of punishing the losing party.” (Id., at p. 1139.) Although this appeal involved a substantial record and required Plaintiff’s counsel to take on some financial risk, the Court finds the risks involved here do not warrant a multiplier of fees, let alone a 1.25 or 1.5 multiplier. Further, the Court does not find the appeal involved a particularly novel or difficult question or circumstances that warranted extensive work or exceptional skill justifying the application of a multiplier.

 

Finally, the Court also highlights that Plaintiff’s attorney fee request on appeal is approximately half the amount of hours that Plaintiff requested for attorney’s fees in the underlying action. (See 9/20/23 Order.)

Given the billing descriptions, nature of the appeal, and shared representation, the Court finds Plaintiff’s billed hours are excessive and inclusive of duplicative work.

Plaintiff requests a total of $240,362 in attorney fees for both Scandura and Posner absent the application of a multiplier. Defendants concede that 108 hours is reasonable. (Opp., Zarmi Decl., ¶ 7.) The Court finds an hourly rate of $550.00 or $750.00 to be reasonable.

Given all of the above, the amount of attorney’s fees to be awarded is $120,181.

Furthermore, the Court notes that this amount, while indeed half of the requested fees, also reflects a lodestar calculation of 149 hours billed by Posner at $750.00 per hour plus 15 hours billed by Scandura at $550.00 per hour. The Court finds this calculation to be reasonable.

Accordingly, the Motion is GRANTED in the reduced amount of $120,181.

 

V.             Conclusion & Order

 

For the foregoing reasons, Plaintiff Queens Land Builder, Inc.’s Motion for Award of Attorney Fees on Appeal in the Amount of $332,605.50 is GRANTED in the reduced amount of $120,181.

 

Moving party is ordered to give notice.

ANALYSIS:

 

I.                Background

 

On October 6, 2025, the Court entered judgment for costs in favor of all Defendants Farmers Insurance Exchange, Truck Insurance Exchange, and Frieda Swedelson and against Plaintiffs Zia Abhari and Donya Entertainment, Inc. (10/6/25 Order.)

 

On November 25, 2025, the Court, in pertinent part, denied Plaintiffs’ Motion for Clarification regarding the Court’s Statement of Decision. (11/25/25 Order.)

 

On January 6, 2026, the Court issued an amended judgment for costs in favor of all Defendants Farmers Insurance Exchange, Truck Insurance Exchange, and Frieda Swedelson and against Plaintiffs Zia Abhari and Donya Entertainment, Inc. (1/6/26 Amended Judgment.)

 

On April 27, 2026, Plaintiff Abhari filed the instant Motion for Clarification as to the Costs Award in Case Number 23STCV03400 (the “Motion”).

 

 On June 17, 2026, Defendants Farmers Group, Inc. dba Farmers Underwriters Association; Truck Underwriters Association; Farmers Insurance Exchange; and Truck Insurance Exchange (“Moving Defendants”) filed an Opposition and Request for Judicial Notice.

 

On July 6, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Reply.

 

II.              Procedural Issues

 

As an initial matter, the Court notes that the Motion appears to have been served to an incorrect email address of “[email protected]” rather than the correct email address of “cmaile@tharpe-howell.com” for defense counsel. (Emphasis added.) However, given the timely Opposition, the Court exercises its discretion to address the Motion on the merits.

 

III.            Request for Judicial Notice

 

In support of the Opposition, Moving Defendants request the Court take judicial notice of 21 documents filed in the instant matter. (Opp., RJN, Exhs. A-W.) The Court GRANTS the request pursuant to Evidence Code section 452, subdivision (d).

 

IV.           Legal Standard & Discussion

 

Abhari brings the instant Motion “for a definitive allocation to be made as to how the costs award is to be paid as to each of the three applicable dismissed Defendants, Defendant Farmers Insurance Exchange … Defendant Farmers Underwriters Association … and Defendant Farmers Group, Inc.” (Motion, p. 3.)

 

In Opposition, Moving Defendants state the Motion should be denied as Abhari raised the Motion in regard to the wrong case; the Motion misidentifies the parties that were awarded costs in the correct case; and the Motion is based on “an unsound premise” as the Court was and is not required “to parcel out the costs it awarded among the various defendants.” (Opp., pp. 5-9.)

 

California Rules of Court, rule 3.1113 requires that a supporting memorandum contain “a statement of facts, a concise statement of the law, evidence and arguments relied on, and a discussion of the statutes, cases, and textbooks cited in support of the position advanced.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rules 3.1113.)

 

Here, the Motion does not include a concise statement of the law or clear discussion of the authority supporting the Motion’s position. (See Cal. Rules of Court, rules 3.1113.) Plaintiff did not cite any overarching case law or statutory authority that authorizes this Court to “clarify” the Court’s prior costs award. The Court may not grant a motion absent supporting legal authority.

 

Further, the Motion does not address the correct parties who were previously awarded costs. (See Motion; 1/6/26 Amended Judgment.)

 

Thus, the Motion is DENIED.

 

V.             Conclusion & Order

 

For the foregoing reasons, Plaintiff Zia Abhari’s Motion for Clarification as to the Costs Award in Case No. 23STCV03400 is DENIED.

ANALYSIS:

 

I.                Background

 

On May 2, 2025, Plaintiff Gholwan Mechammil (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Defendant City of Los Angeles, acting by and through the Los Angeles Department of Water and Power (“Defendant”) and Does 1-50.

 

            On October 6, 2025, the Court granted Defendant’s Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings. (10/6/25 Order.)

 

On November 4, 2025, Plaintiff filed a First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) against Defendant.

 

On February 2, 2026, the Court sustained Defendant’s Demurrer to the second, third, and fourth causes of action in the FAC with leave to amend and overruled the Demurrer to the remaining cause of action. (2/2/26 Order.) The Court also granted Defendant’s Motion to Strike Plaintiff’s request for economic damages. (2/2/26 Order.)  

 

On March 4, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”) against Defendant and Does 1-50, alleging causes of action for Negligence, Breach of Written Contract, Breach of Oral Contract, and Negligent Interference with Prospective Economic Relations.

 

On April 10, 2026, Defendant filed the instant Demurrer to Plaintiff’s SAC (the “Demurrer”) and Motion to Strike Portions of Plaintiff’s SAC (the “Motion”).

 

On June 29, 2026, Plaintiff filed an Opposition to the Demurrer and an Opposition to the Motion.

 

On July 6, 2026, Defendant filed a Reply regarding the Demurrer and a Reply regarding the Motion.

 

II.              Request for Judicial Notice

 

In conjunction with the Demurrer, Defendant requests the Court take judicial notice of certain ordinances amending Article 1, Chapter 1, Division 10 of the Los Angeles Administrative Code. (Demurrer, RJN, pp. 1-2, Exhs. 1-2.)

 

The Court GRANTS the request. (Evid. Code, § 452, subd. (b); see also Madain v. City of Stanton (2010) 185 Cal.App.4th 1277, 1280, fn. 1.)

 

III.            Legal Standard

 

“As a general matter, in a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) “A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Ibid.) The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)

A general demurrer may be brought under Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (e) if insufficient facts are stated to support the cause of action asserted or under section 430.10, subdivision (a), where the court has no jurisdiction of the subject of the cause of action alleged in the pleading. All other grounds listed in Section 430.10, including uncertainty under subdivision (f), are special demurrers.

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Id.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.) However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245).

IV.           Discussion

 

A.    Demurrer

 

Meet and Confer

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41 requires that “[b]efore filing a demurrer pursuant to this chapter, the demurring party shall meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a).) The parties are to meet and confer at least five days before the date the responsive pleading is due. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(2).) Thereafter, the demurring party shall file and serve a declaration detailing their meet and confer efforts. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(3).) 

The Demurrer is accompanied by a meet and confer declaration as required by Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, subdivision (a). According to the declaration, defense counsel and Plaintiff’s counsel met and conferred by Teams videoconference regarding the issues raised in the Demurrer but were unable to reach a resolution. (Demurrer, Hsu Decl., ¶ 2.)

 

Given the circumstances, the Court finds the meet and confer requirement is satisfied.

 

            Analysis

 

Defendant demurs to all four causes of action in the SAC on the grounds the claim of breach of written contract fails because it is premised on contractual obligations that do not exist; the claim for breach of oral contract fails as it exceeds the scope of the Court’s prior order and is unenforceable as it does not comply with the city charter; the claim for negligent interference with prospective economic advantage fails as the SAC fails to plead the essential elements for this claim; and the claim for negligence fails because it hinges on a defective vicarious liability theory. (Demurrer, pp. 6, 10-18.) Defendant requests leave to amend be denied as Plaintiff “has already had three bites at the apple, and this Court should not give him a fourth.” (Demurrer, p. 19.)  

 

In Opposition, Plaintiff states the SAC sufficiently alleges each cause of action on the following grounds: SAC ¶ 64 provides for an enforceable written agreement between the parties that sufficiently pleads the breach of written contract claim; the claim for breach of oral contract does not exceed the scope of the Court’s order and SAC ¶ 75 provides adequate facts to state the claim; the claim for negligent interference with prospective economic relations is adequately pled in the SAC, particularly in SAC ¶¶ 27, 101, and 103; and the claim for negligence was previously found to be adequate by the Court. (Opp., pp. 8-19.) To the extent the Court finds the SAC deficient, Plaintiff requests leave to amend. (Opp., pp. 19.)

 

In Reply, Defendant emphasizes arguments raised in the Demurrer and states the Opposition fails to demonstrate that any of the causes of action in the SAC are adequately pled. (Reply, pp. 1-4.) Further, Defendant states that the Court’s prior ruling to overrule Defendant’s challenge to the negligence cause of action is superseded by the SAC and thus Defendant is within its rights to demur to the negligence claim. (Reply, pp. 5-7.) Finally, Defendant urges that Plaintiff has failed to meet the burden to show what facts could be pled to cure the defects in the SAC. (Reply, pp. 7-8.)

 

            First Cause of Action – Negligence

 

“The elements of a negligence cause of action are duty, breach, causation and damages.” (County of Santa Clara v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2006) 137 Cal.App.4th 292, 318.)

 

Government Code section 815.2 states, in pertinent part: “A public entity is liable for injury proximately caused by an act or omission of an employee of the public entity within the scope of his employment if the act or omission would, apart from this section, have given rise to a cause of action against that employee or his personal representative.” (Gov. Code, § 815.2, subd. (a).)

 

            Here, Plaintiff refers to Government Code section 815.2 (SAC, ¶ 35) and alleges that “[f]rom January 21, 2021 to October 1, 2024, Defendant LADWP, including, but not limited to its employee, Mark Navarro, unreasonably delayed, made mistakes and failed to complete within any reasonable time the design, construction, installation, maintenance, repair, replacement, calibration and improvement for the Subject Property.” (SAC, ¶ 46.) Further, Plaintiff alleges that Defendants “breached their duty of care by failing to exercise reasonable diligence and competence in the execution and completion of its responsibilities.” (SAC, ¶ 45.) Plaintiff also alleges: “The negligence of LADWP, as well as its employees, including, but not limited to, Mark Navarro, Roberto Jae Park, Jim Granados, Vasile Boboaca, Alvaro Escobar, Farhad Dormani, Mike Aguilar, Eric Maglasang, Rodolfo Monroy, Daniel Chavez and agents identified here as DOES 1-50, was a substantial factor causing Plaintiffs’ damages.” (SAC, ¶ 48.)

 

The Court finds these allegations are sufficient to allege a cause of action for negligence.

 

Thus, the Demurrer is OVERRULED as to the first cause of action.

 

Second and Third Causes of Action – Breach of Contract

 

A cause of action for breach of contract must plead “(1) the contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for non-performance, (3) defendant’s breach, and (4) damage to plaintiff therefrom.” (Acoustics, Inc. v. Trepte Constr. Co. (1971) 14 Cal.App.3d 887, 913.) A written contract can be pled by alleging its making and attaching a copy to the complaint or by alleging the making and the substance of the relevant terms. (Construction Protective Services, Inc. v. TIG Specialty Ins. Co. (2002) 29 Cal.4th 189, 198-199.) The terms of an “oral contract may be pleaded generally as to its effect, because it is rarely possible to allege the exact words.” (Scolinos v. Kolts (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 635, 640.) Finally, an implied-in-fact contract can be alleged based on the promisor’s conduct. (Gomez v. Lincare, Inc. (2009) 173 Cal.App.4th 508, 525.) 

 

            As to the second cause of action for breach of written contract, the Court finds the SAC alleges that a document entitled “Authority to Bill for Conduit Construction and Permit Fees” constitutes a written agreement between the parties. (SAC, ¶ 59.) A copy of the document is attached to the SAC. (SAC, ¶ 60, Exh. 1.) Plaintiff alleges that the document also incorporates by reference the Rules Governing Water and Electric Services through the following provision: “In consideration of the work and materials used for electric facilities… the undersigned hereby agrees to pay the LADWP, in accordance with the Rules Governing Water and Electric Services in the City of Los Angeles, the sum of $16,099.00.” (SAC, ¶ 61, Exh. 1.) The SAC then alleges that Defendant breached the Rules Governing Water and Electric Services. (SAC, ¶¶ 64-72, Exh. 2.)  

 

            The Court finds the written agreement referenced in and accompanying the SAC merely contains an agreement for Plaintiff to pay Defendant a specific amount in accordance with the Rules Governing Water and Electric Services and does not otherwise obligate by reference Defendant to comport with the Rules Governing Water and Electric Services. As such, the SAC fails to state sufficient facts to constitute the second cause of action.

 

            Thus, the Demurrer is SUSTAINED WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND as to the second cause of action. Leave to amend is not granted given that this is Plaintiff’s third attempt to allege this cause of action, and the Opposition fails to show Plaintiff has facts to plead this cause of action.

 

            As to the third cause of action for breach of oral contract, the Court first finds this cause of action was not beyond the scope of the Court’s prior order. Turning to the SAC’s allegations regarding the second cause of action, the SAC alleges that Defendant’s employees made oral promises to complete the electrical power installation for the Property within 3-6 months of the following dates while speaking with Plaintiff and Plaintiff’s property manager: January 21, 2021; February 23, 2021; January 10, 2022; March 10, 2022; April 12, 2022; April 20, 2022; April 21, 2022; May 25, 2022; and October 3, 2022 (SAC, ¶¶ 78-79), and Defendant’s employees made oral promises to complete the electric power lines within 30 days of the following dates while speaking with Plaintiff and Plaintiff’s property manager: January 29, 2023; March 28, 2023; April 5, 2023; April 17, 2023; July 25, 2023; August 26, 2023; December 14, 2023; and March 8, 2024. (SAC, ¶¶ 78, 80.) Plaintiff performed all terms and conditions of the agreement, including paying Defendant, but Defendant breached the agreement by failing to timely perform the work as promised. (SAC, ¶¶ 82-85.) As a direct result of breach, Plaintiff has suffered damages in no event less than $1,121,632.32. (SAC, ¶ 87.)

 

            The Court finds that the oral agreement directly contradicts the properly judicially noticed Los Angeles Administrative Code, Chapter 1, Article 1, Section 10.2, subdivision (a)(4), which states that contracts with consideration reasonably valued at more than $25,000, annually or in the aggregate, are required to be in writing. (Demurrer, RJN, Exhs. 1-2.) The SAC alleges that the contract between the parties was reasonably valued at more than $25,000 but was not in writing. As such, the Court finds the SAC fails to state sufficient facts to constitute the third cause of action. Plaintiff does not provide any argument or evidence to dispute the SAC’s pleaded violation of the pertinent Los Angeles Administrative Code.

 

            Thus, the Demurrer is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND as to the third cause of action.

 

            Fourth Cause of Action – Negligent Interference with Prospective Economic Relations

 

“The elements of negligent interference with prospective economic advantage are (1) the existence of an economic relationship between the plaintiff and a third party containing the probability of future economic benefit to the plaintiff; (2) the defendant’s knowledge of the relationship; (3) the defendant’s knowledge (actual or construed) that the relationship would be disrupted if the defendant failed to act with reasonable care; (4) the defendant’s failure to act with reasonable care; (5) actual disruption of the relationship; (6) and economic harm proximately caused by the defendant’s negligence.” (Redfearn v. Trader Joe’s Co. (2018) 20 Cal.App.5th 989, 1005, overruled in part on other grounds; see CACI No. 2204.) A cause of action for negligent interference with prospective economic relations also requires the defendant engaged in wrongful conduct. (See CACI No. 2204; National Medical Transportation Network v. Deloitte & Touche (1998) 62 Cal.App.4th 412, 440.)

 

            Here, the SAC alleges the existence of an economic relationship between Plaintiff and the third party tenants at the subject property; Defendant’s knowledge of the relationship; Defendant’s construed knowledge that the relationship would be disrupted if Defendant failed to act with reasonable care regarding the electrical power installation at the Property; Defendant’s failure to act with reasonable care and the resulting disruption of Plaintiff’s relationship with the third party tenants as Plaintiff was prevented from leasing the Property; and economic harm caused by Defendant’s negligence. (SAC, ¶¶ 88-114.) Further, the Court finds the SAC alleges the specific wrongful conduct of fraud or misrepresentation on behalf of Defendant’s employees regarding the status of the installation and the timeline to completion of the repairs. (See CACI No. 2204.)

 

            The Court finds these allegations suffice to plead a cause of action for negligent interference with prospective economic relations.

 

            Thus, the Demurrer is OVERRULED as to the fourth cause of action.

 

B.    Motion to Strike

 

Defendant moves to strike the irrelevant, false, or improper matter from the SAC – namely, portions of SAC ¶ 49 and ¶ 54 that reference economic damages and depreciation of value of the Property.

 

Legal Authority

 

California law authorizes a party’s motion to strike matter from an opposing party’s pleading if it is irrelevant, false, or improper. (Code Civ. Proc. §§ 435, 436.) Motions may also target pleadings or parts of pleadings that are not filed or drawn in conformity with applicable laws, rules, or orders. (Code Civ. Proc. § 436, subd. (b).)

 

The Code of Civil Procedure also authorizes the Court to act on its own initiative to strike matters, empowering the Court to enter orders striking matter “at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 436.)

 

Finally, Code of Civil Procedure section 435.5 requires that “[b]efore filing a motion to strike pursuant to this chapter, the moving party shall meet and confer in person or by telephone with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to the motion to strike for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that resolves the objections to be raised in the motion to strike.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 435.5, subd. (a).) (Emphasis added.)

 

Meet and Confer

 

As an initial matter, the Court finds Defendant has satisfied the meet and confer requirement of Code of Civil Procedure section 435.5, as defense counsel filed a declaration attesting that the parties’ counsels met and conferred via Teams videoconferencing regarding the issues raised in the Motion but were unable to arrive at a resolution. (Motion, Hsu Decl., ¶ 2.)

 

Analysis

 

Here, the Court finds the SAC adequately pleads a cause of action for negligent interference with prospective economic relations, which allows for economic recovery. As such, Defendant’s request to strike references to economic damages and depreciation of value of the subject Property on the grounds that the SAC fails to sufficiently plead facts to support these requests is meritless.

 

Thus, the Motion is DENIED.

 

IV.           Conclusion & Order

 

For the foregoing reasons, Defendant City of Los Angeles, acting by and through the Los Angeles Department of Water and Power’s Demurrer to Plaintiff’s Second Amended Complaint is OVERRULED in part and SUSTAINED in part.

 

The Demurrer is OVERRULED as to the first and fourth causes of action.

 

The Demurrer is SUSTAINED WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND as to the second cause of action.

 

The Demurrer is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND as to the third cause of action.

 

Defendant’s Motion to Strike Portions of Plaintiff’s Second Amended Complaint is DENIED.

 

Plaintiff’s Third Amended Complaint is to be filed within 20 days of this order.

 

Moving party is ordered to give notice.

ANALYSIS:

 

I.                Background

 

On August 22, 2025, Plaintiffs Meri Mkrtchyan and Artyom Aloyan (“Plaintiffs”) filed the instant action against Defendant Rustam Muratbek Uulu (“Defendant”) and Does 1-100, alleging Motor Vehicle and General Negligence causes of action. (Compl., pp. 1, 3.)  

 

On April 17, 2026, Plaintiffs filed proof of publication showing Defendant was served by publication.

 

On May 6, 2026, Non-Party Tesla Insurance Services (“Tesla”) filed a Motion for Leave to Intervene (the “Motion”). On June 4, 2026, Tesla filed a Reply stating no Opposition to the Motion had been filed.

 

On June 11, 2026, the Court continued the hearing on the Motion to allow Tesla to file and serve supplemental documents addressing the deficiencies noted by the Court or, alternatively, to file and serve a proposed answer prior to the next scheduled hearing. (6/11/26 Order.)

 

On June 15, 2026, Tesla filed an Amended Motion.

 

On June 23, 2026, Tesla filed the instant Second Amended Motion.

 

On June 29, 2026, the Court continued the hearing on the Second Amended Motion.

 

To date, no Opposition to the Second Amended Motion has been filed.

 

II.              Legal Standard

 

Code of Civil Procedure, section 387, subdivisions (c) and (d) provide:

(c) A nonparty shall petition the court for leave to intervene by noticed motion or ex parte application. The petition shall include a copy of the proposed complaint in intervention or answer in intervention and set forth the grounds upon which intervention rests.

 

(d) (1) The court shall, upon timely application, permit a nonparty to intervene in the action or proceeding if either of the following conditions is satisfied:

 

(A) A provision of law confers an unconditional right to intervene.

 

(B) The person seeking intervention claims an interest relating to the property or transaction that is the subject of the action and that person is so situated that the disposition of the action may impair or impede that person’s ability to protect that interest, unless that person’s interest is adequately represented by one or more of the existing parties.

 

(2) The court may, upon timely application, permit a nonparty to intervene in the action or proceeding if the person has an interest in the matter in litigation, or in the success of either of the parties, or an interest against both.

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 387, subds. (c), (d).)

 

Whether the petitioner has an interest in the matter in litigation is a question of fact that must be determined by the court before leave to file is granted.  (See Muller v. Robinson (1959) 174 Cal.App.2d 511, 515.) The burden rests on the one seeking to intervene to show that this is a proper case for intervention. (Id., at p. 515.)   

 

III.            Discussion

 

Here, “Tesla is the insurer for Defendant and maintains a direct interest in the litigation against its insured, including liability for a default judgment against the insured.” (Second Amended Motion, Kane Decl., ¶ 5.) Further, “[e]fforts to connect with Defendant Uulu have been unsuccessful; Defendant has not contacted Tesla or Tesla’s counsel to participate in this litigation to date.” (Second Amended Motion, Kane Decl., ¶ 5.) Finally, Tesla states that “[i]ntervention will not interfere with the rights of the original parties.” (Second Amended Motion, Kane Decl., ¶ 6.)

 

Tesla has an interest in the outcome of this action because it insures Defendant and will face direct liability if it is not permitted to intervene. (See Reliance Ins. Code v. Superior Court (2000) 84 Cal.App.4th 383, 386 [“An insurer’s right to intervene in an action against the insured, for personal injury or property damage, arises as a result of Insurance Code section 11580. Section 11580 provides that a judgment creditor may proceed directly against any liability insurance covering the defendant, and obtain satisfaction of judgment up to the amount of the policy limits”].) Tesla has been unable to connect with Defendant, which thus necessitated the instant Motion, and such circumstances are permissible grounds on which to grant a motion to intervene. (See id., at p. 387.) Finally, the Motion is timely as default judgment has not yet been entered against Defendant. (See id., at pp. 386-387 [“[W]here the insurer may be subject to a direct action under Insurance Code section 11580 by a judgment creditor who has or will obtain a default judgment in a third party action against the insured, intervention is appropriate”].)

 

Furthermore, the Second Amended Petition includes a proposed answer in intervention. (See Second Amended Motion, Attach.)

 

Accordingly, the unopposed Second Amended Motion is GRANTED.

For the foregoing reasons, Non-Party Tesla Insurance Services’ unopposed Second Amended Motion for Leave to Intervene is GRANTED.

I.                Background

 

On January 14, 2026, Plaintiff Danielle Turrentine (“Plaintiff”) initiated the instant action against Defendant HQ L.A. Inc. dba Hair Queen LA (“Defendant”) and Does 1-10, alleging 20 causes of action arising from Plaintiff’s employment with Defendant. (Compl., pp. 1-4.)

II.              Legal Standard

 

            Code of Civil Procedure section 284 states that “the attorney in an action…may be changed at any time before or after judgment or final determination, as follows: (1) Upon the consent of both client and attorney…; (2) Upon the order of the court, upon the application of either client or attorney, after notice from one to the other.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 284; Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362.)  “The determination whether to grant or deny a motion to withdraw as counsel lies within the sound discretion of the trial court.” (Manfredi & Levine v. Superior Court (1998) 66 Cal.App.4th 1128, 1133); see Lempert v. Superior Court (2003) 112 Cal.App.4th 1161, 1173.)

 

An application to be relieved as counsel must be made on Judicial Counsel Forms MC-051 (Notice of Motion and Motion), MC-052 (Declaration), and MC-053 (Proposed Order). (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362, subds. (a), (c), (e).) 

 

In addition, California Rules of Court, rule 3.1362 subsection (d) requires that the notice of motion and motion, declaration, and proposed order be served on the client and all other parties who have appeared in the case by personal service, electronic service, or mail. If the notice is served by electronic service, it must be accompanied by a declaration stating that the electronic service address is the client’s current electronic service address. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362, subd. (d)(2).) As used in this rule, “current” means:

 

“[T]he address was confirmed within 30 days before the filing of the motion to be relieved.

Merely demonstrating that the notice was sent to the client’s last known address and was not returned or no electronic delivery failure message was received is not, by itself, sufficient to demonstrate that the address is current.”

 

(Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362, subd. (d).)

 

III.            Discussion

 

On June 23, 2026, Counsel moved the Court in the Amended Motion to be relieved as attorney of record for Plaintiff. (MC-051.) Counsel filed MC-051, MC-052, and MC-053 Forms. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362, subds. (a), (c), (e).) 

 

Counsel seeks to be relieved under the permissive grounds of California Rules of Professional Conduct, rule 1.16, subdivision (b)(4), which states a lawyer may withdraw from representing a client if the client by other conduct renders it unreasonably difficult for the lawyer to carry out representation effectively. (MC-052, ¶ 2.)

 

The Court is satisfied with Counsel’s reasons for seeking to be relieved. (Cal. Rules of Professional Conduct, rule 1.16.) The Court also finds the Amended Motion satisfactorily complies with California Rules of Court, rule 3.1362.

 

            Thus, the Motion is GRANTED.
