[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING MOTIONS TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES TO PLAINTIFF’s REQUEST FOR PRODUCTION OF DOCUMENTS, SET THREE

 

 

I.           BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Luis Antonio Ortiz (“Ortiz”) was allegedly pretextually terminated from his employed with Defendants for ‘job abandonment’ during Ortiz's recovery following heart surgery. Ortiz sued Defendants for thirteen causes of action, including harassment and retaliation.

On July 29, 2026, Ortiz filed the instant motion to compel further responses to Defendant Elevance Health, Inc. (“EH”)’s request for production, set three. At the August 20, 2026, hearing on the motion, the Court ordered parties to submit supplemental briefing on remaining requests at issue. Ortiz filed a supplemental brief.

 

II.         LEGAL STANDARD

             A motion to compel further response to requests for production “shall set forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought by the inspection demand.” (Code Civ. Pro., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(1)[1].) To establish ‘good cause,’ the burden is on the moving party to show both: (1) relevance to the subject matter (e.g., how the information in the documents would tend to prove or disprove some issue in the case); and (2) specific facts justifying discovery (e.g., why such information is necessary for trial preparation or to prevent surprise at trial). (Glenfed Development Corp. v. Superior Court (1997) 53 Cal.App.4th 1113, 1117.)

            If ‘good cause’ is shown by the moving party, the burden is then on the responding party to justify any objections made to document disclosure. (Coy v. Superior Court (1962) 58 Cal.2d 210, 220-21.)

DISCUSSION

Ortiz supplemental brief requests the Court order EH provided further responses to Request for Production of Documents, Set Three, requests nos. 63, 65, 67-71, 74-78, and 82-83. The Court also notes Ortiz’s supplemental brief addresses “multiple other areas still subject to dispute” in a footnote, with no additional information. As this is not discussed in the text, despite the Court ordering briefing on remaining issues, the Court will not address these issues in the motion.

A.         Request No. 63

Request no. 63 requests “Any and all DOCUMENTS reflecting COMMUNICATIONS between Roxana Sadigh and Andrew Harrrell that mention or refer to any of the three candidates for the Senior Underwriting Consultant position that are identified in Roxana Sadigh’s email to Andrew Harrell dated January 3, 2023, which is Bates Stamped ELEVANCE_HEALTH_198.” (Mot. Helmer Decl. ¶ 2, Ex. 1.)

In response, EH provided “ELEVANCE_HEALTH_2230-2344,” which included e-mails concerning alternative candidates, with the names, resumes, and qualifications redacted. (Ibid.) Ortiz argues that, without designations, he cannot determine whether the same candidates are at issue, track each candidates’ treatment across documents, or determine which candidate EH was “moving forward with”. As none of these candidates are related to the claims in this action, and therefore should not lose their right to privacy, the Court orders EH to provide updated copies of the redacted documents that identify each candidate by pseudonyms (such as “Candidate A”) to allow Ortiz to better understand the context of the e-mails.

B.          Requests 68-70

Request no. 68 requests “Any and all notes, summaries and/or other DOCUMENTS (in any form, including electronic, digital, handwritten or otherwise) taken or prepared by Roxana Sadigh regarding her interviews of each of the three candidates for the Senior Underwriting Consultant position that are identified in Roxana Sadigh’s email to Andrew Harrell dated January 3, 2023, which is Bates Stamped ELEVANCE_HEALTH_198 (A true and correct copy of that email is attached hereto as Exhibit 2).” (Ibid.)

Request no. 69 requests “Any and all notes, summaries and/or other DOCUMENTS (in any form, including electronic, digital, handwritten or otherwise) taken or prepared by Andrew Harrell regarding his interview(s) of any the three candidates for the Senior Underwriting Consultant position that are identified in Roxana Sadigh’s email to Andrew Harrell dated January 3, 2023, which is Bates Stamped ELEVANCE_HEALTH_198 (A true and correct copy of that email is attached hereto as Exhibit 2).” (Ibid.)

Request no. 70 requests “Request no. 69 requests “Any and all notes, summaries and/or other DOCUMENTS (in any form, including electronic, digital, handwritten or otherwise) taken or prepared by Andrew Harrell regarding his interview(s) of any the three candidates for the Senior Underwriting Consultant position that are identified in Roxana Sadigh’s email to Andrew Harrell dated January 3, 2023, which is Bates Stamped ELEVANCE_HEALTH_198 (A true and correct copy of that email is attached hereto as Exhibit 2).” (Ibid.)

EH responded to all three requests as follows: “Subject to and without waiving the foregoing objections, Defendant responds as follows: Defendant has conducted a diligent search and reasonable inquiry in an effort to comply and it does not have any responsive documents in its possession, custody, or control. Defendant believes the requested item or category of documents never existed or has never been, or is no longer, in the possession, custody, or control of Defendant. Discovery is ongoing and Defendant reserves the right to supplement this response at a later date.” (Ibid.)

Ortiz takes issue with the exact language of EH’s responses, arguing that it does not strictly comply with section 2031.230, in part, because 1) belief is not affirmation; 2) it is unclear on what grounds EH is not in possession of the requested documents; 3) no third party is identified as having possession. The Court agrees, in part, that EH’s responses are unsatisfactory, in that they do not specify why EH is not in possession.

EH is ordered to provide supplemental responses addressing why each set of requested documents is not in EH’s possession. Unless EH is aware or believes a third-party to have access to or ownership of the requested documents, EH does not need to specify the third-party in possession.

C.          Request No. 74.

Request no. 74 requests “Any and all DOCUMENTS that relate or pertain to the reason(s) a decision was made not to offer a position of employment to the first of the three candidates for the Senior Underwriting Consultant position identified in Roxana Sadigh’s email to Andrew Harrell dated January 3, 2023, which is Bates Stamped ELEVANCE_HEALTH_198 (A true and correct copy of that email is attached hereto as Exhibit 2).” (Ibid.)

In response, EH provided “ELEVANCE_HEALTH_2389-2398.” (Ibid.) Ortiz argues this is non-responsive as it only indicates that “Candidate 1” is their ‘top pick’, who is ‘qualified and capable to handle the job’, with a ‘good demeanor’. (Mot., Helmer Decl. ¶ 4, Ex. 3.) The Court disagrees; this falls within the broad scope of documents relating to pertaining to decisions made to hire or not hire individuals. Absent evidence indicating there is additional written documentation that EH failed to produce, there is no basis for supplementary responses.

D.         Outstanding Requests: 65 – 67, 71, 75 – 78, 82-83.

No further briefing was provided on the subject requests, beyond stating these responses only were given objection-only responses. Therefore, the Court evaluates the original issues presented.

Requests nos. 65-67, 71, and 75- 78 all concern issues relating to the other candidates applying for the same position as Ortiz: primarily resumes, notes, and communications. The Court orders EH to produce responses, as the request is likely to result in the discovery of relevant and admissible evidence, as well as of probative value when determining if Oritz was unduly discriminated against compared to other candidates. (See Gupta v. Trustees of Cal. State Univ. (2019) 40 Cal. App. 5th 510, 519–521). However, as these candidates have not put themselves at issue, the Court orders all responses be subject to redaction, with each candidate referred to by a consistent pseudonym.

Requests nos. 82-83 request “Any and all DOCUMENTS (including Excel files) the reflect any input that Susan Millet Reilly provided for MR. ORTIZ’s 2019 performance appraisal (as she testified at page 58:21 – 60:18 of her deposition transcript)[,]” and “Any and all DOCUMENTS that support Susan Millet Reilly’s contention that MR. ORTIZ was not sufficiently responsive to sales partners (as she testified at pages 61:1-14 of her deposition transcript)[,]” respectively. The Court orders EH to provide further responses; the requests are not overly broad and specifically reference conclusions reached at Millet Reilly’s deposition. Documents allegedly barred by attorney-client privilege and/or attorney work product doctrine must be outlined via a privilege log, which the Court will review to determine the applicability of privilege to any responsive documents.

E.          Sanctions

Ortiz requests sanctions totaling $16,100.00 pursuant to section 2031.310, subdivision (h), on the basis that EH unsuccessfully opposed a motion to compel further responses without substantial justification. The Court does not find it is within the interests of justice to award sanctions, given EH generally acted reasonably in granting Ortiz extensions to file this motion, in order to allow parties to meet and confer. EH also provided further supplementary responses, resolving at least some of the issues previously identified. The Court also does not find a basis to impose sanctions for misuse of the discovery process; EH generally tried to comply with discovery. Therefore, the Court denies Ortiz’s request for sanctions.

 

IV.       CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, the motion to compel a further responses to Ortiz’s Request for Productions, Set Three, is GRANTED in limited scope as set forth in this ruling.

Ortiz is ordered to give notice.

ORDER GRANTING MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT

 

I.           BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Luis Antonio Ortiz (“Ortiz”) was allegedly pretextually terminated from his employed with Defendants for ‘job abandonment’ during Ortiz's recovery following heart surgery. Ortiz sued Defendants for thirteen causes of action, including harassment and retaliation.

On May 22, 2026, Defendants Anthem Blue Cross Life and Health insurance Company (“ABC”), Blue Cross of California (“BCC”), The Elevance Health Companies of California, Inc. (“TEHCC”) and Anthem Insurance Companies, Inc. (“AIC”) (collectively “Moving Defendants”) filed the instant motion for summary judgment. Ortiz filed an opposition. Moving Defendants filed a reply.

 

II.         OBJECTIONS

The Court does not rule on the issue of objections, as the Court need only rule on objections to evidence deemed material to its disposition of the motion. (Code of Civ. Proc. § 437c, subd. (q).)

 

III.       LEGAL STANDARD

A party may move for summary judgment “if it is contended that the action has no merit or that there is no defense to the action or proceeding.”  (Code Civ. Proc.[1] § 437c, subd. (a).) “[I]f all the evidence submitted, and all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law,” the moving party will be entitled to summary judgment. (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.) 

The moving party bears an initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing of the nonexistence of any triable issue of material fact, and if he does so, the burden shifts to the opposing party to make a prima facie showing of the existence of a triable issue of material fact. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850, citing to § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence.  (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

Neither a moving nor responding party may rely on the mere allegations or denials of its pleadings. A moving party must submit specific admissible evidence showing that the responding party cannot establish at least one element of his, her or its cause of action or defense. The responding party, to defeat the motion, must submit specific admissible evidence showing that a triable issue of material fact does exist as to that element of the cause of action or defense. (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

The “Golden Rule” on a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication is that “if [a fact] is not set forth in the separate statement, it does not exist.” (Zimmerman, Rosenfeld, Gersh & Leeds LLP v. Larson (2005) 131 Cal.App.4th 1466, 1477, citing United Community Church v. Garcin (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 327, 337.).

 

III.       DISCUSSION

Moving Defendants request the Court enter summary judgment as Moving Defendants did not employ Ortiz, nor are Moving Defendants alter egos or joint ventures of Ortiz’s employers.

A.         Statement of Facts

Defendant Elevance Health, Inc. (“EH”), formerly known as ‘Anthem, Inc.,’ employed Ortiz as a senior underwriting consultant at all times relevant to Ortiz’s claims. (UMF 1-2.) EH solely administered Ortiz’s performance reviews, medical leaves, extensions, disability and medical benefits, and 401(k) plan. (UMF 5-10.) EH had the sole right to control Ortiz’s wages, hours, working conditions, hire, transfer, promotions, discipline, termination, and scope of duties. (UMF 19-21.) When Ortiz reapplied for his job, he applied through EH. (UMF 73-77.) He went through an initial screening process solely with EH employees. (UMF 78-81.)

EH terminated Ortiz’s employment with EH on October 10, 2022. (UMF 10.) Susan Millet-Reilly (“Millet-Reilly”), Ortiz’s supervisor at the time of termination who made the decision to terminate Ortiz, was solely employed by EH. (UMF 11, 14.) Ortiz’s two previous supervisors were also solely employment by EH. (UMF 12-13.) Prior to deciding to terminate Ortiz, Millet-Reilly consulted with Kay Olangunju (“Olangunju”), a consultant solely employed by EH. (UMF 15-16.) Following termination, Millet-Reilly requested Ortiz return his equipment to EH’s IT team. (UMF 18.)

Defendant The Elevance Health Companies, Inc. (“TEHCI”) is a paymaster entity responsible for handling the administrative aspects of certain services for EH, including processing and disbursing wages and salaries, withholding taxes, and issuing W-2s or other tax documentations. (UMF 23.) TEHCI processed payroll for Ortiz and issued his wage statements and W-2 forms. (UMF 24-25.)

ABC is a subsidiary of a subsidiary of EH; ABC manages health and life insurance policies, but does not have, and has never had, any employees. (UMF 31-32.) ABC has never exercised any control over Ortiz's wages, hours, working conditions, hire, transfer, promotions, discipline, termination, or scope of duties. (UMF 33-35.) Ortiz was never told he worked for ABC, nor are there any documents supporting any connection between ABC and Ortiz, including payroll statements. (UMF 36-40.)

TEHCC is also a subsidiary of a subsidiary of EH; it is a common pay agent used only to process payroll and maintain payroll records. (UMF 41-42.) It has never had any employees. (UMF 43.) TEHCC has never exercised any control over Ortiz's wages, hours, working conditions, hire, transfer, promotions, discipline, termination, or scope of duties. (UMF 44-46.) Ortiz was never told he worked for TEHCC, nor are there any documents supporting any connection between TEHCC and Ortiz, including wage statements. (UMF 47-51.)

AIC is a subsidiary of EH; it underwrites insurance products. (UMF 52-53.) It has never had any employees. (UMF 54.) AIC has never exercised any control over Ortiz's wages, hours, working conditions, hire, transfer, promotions, discipline, termination, or scope of duties. (UMF 55-57.) Ortiz was never told he worked for AIC, nor are there any documents supporting any connection between AIC and Ortiz, including wage statements. (UMF 58-62.)

BCC is also a subsidiary of a subsidiary of EH; it is an entity that offers commercial, Medicare, and Medicaid health plans. (UMF 63-64.) It has never had any employees. (UMF 65.) Since at least January 1, 2019, through Ortiz’s termination, BCC has not exercised any control over Ortiz's wages, hours, working conditions, hire, transfer, promotions, discipline, termination, or scope of duties. (UMF 66-68.) Ortiz was never told he worked for BCC, nor are there any documents supporting any connection between BCC and Ortiz, including wage statements. (UMF 69-72.)

There is no agreement between EH and Moving Defendants to form a joint venture. (UMF 85.) There is no jointly owned business unit, division, or operation between EH and Moving Defendants that is related to Ortiz. (UMF 86.)

Ortiz’s complaint brings all 13 causes of action against Moving Defendants. Causes of action for intentional infliction of emotional distress and negligent infliction of emotional distress are based upon the alleged wrongful termination, discrimination, and other faults, done with conscious or negligent disregard for the rights and safety of Ortiz.

B.          Employment

Moving Defendants request the Court grant summary judgment as to all causes of action as Moving Defendants were not Ortiz’s employers, and thus cannot be liable for violations of FEHA or the labor code. Additionally, Moving Defendants request the Court grant summary judgment as to the intentional infliction of emotional distress (“IIED”) and negligent infliction of emotional distress (“NIED”) claims, as Moving Defendants were not Ortiz’s employers and thus could not have engaged in the extreme and outrageous acts attributable to Ortiz’s employer.

Pursuant to the Labor Code, an employer is a person or entity which employs any person or persons to perform services for a wage or salary. (LC § 1132.2.) A person employs another through three alternative means: 1) by exerting control over the wages, hours or working conditions of another person; 2) to suffer or permit to work; or 3) to engage another person, creating a common law employment relationship (Martinez v. Combs (2010) 49 Cal.4th 35, 64.) To establish a party ‘suffered’ or ‘permitted’ work, said party must have the power to either cause or prevent another from working under some sort of employment structure. (Futrell v. Payday California, Inc. (2010) 190 Cal.App.4th 1419, 1429.) A common law employment relationship generally evaluates whether the alleged employer had ‘control of details,’ such as tools, place of business, length of time, method of payment, and supervision. (Ibid.)

The evidence establishes, for purposes of FEHA, the labor code, and this action, Moving Defendants were not Ortiz’s employer at the time of termination. Moving Defendants did not control Ortiz’s wage, hours, or working conditions. Moving Defendants did not provide equipment or instructions, nor did Moving Defendants control the location or manner in which Ortiz worked. Precision could not hire, fire, promote, demote, discipline, or transfer Ortiz. Moving Defendants had no control of the details of Ortiz’s employment.

C.          Alter-Ego / Single Enterprise

Although Moving Defendants are subsidiaries of Ortiz’s employer, Moving Defendants would only be potentially liable for Ortiz’s claims if the ends of justice require the disregarding of the corporate form. (Laird v. Capital Cities/ABC, Inc. (1998) 68 Cal. App. 4th 727, 737.) Generally, this must be established via the alter ego doctrine if the entities are operating as a single enterprise. (Troyk v. Farmers Grp., Inc. (2009) 171 Cal. App. 4th 1305, 1341.)

For FEHA claims, court utilize the ‘integrated enterprise test’ to determine if the entities are effectively one enterprise. (Laird, 68 Cal. App 4th at 737.) The integrated enterprise test weighs four factors: “interrelation of operations, common management, centralized control of labor relations, and common ownership or financial control.” (Ibid.) These factors must display control that goes beyond the typical subsidiary-parent corporation relationship (Id. at 737-738.) ‘The critical question is, “[w]hat entity made the final decisions regarding employment matters related to the person claiming discrimination?” (Ibid.) Here, there is no evidence to support a finding beyond the typical subsidiary-parent corporation relationship; the subsidiaries serve distinct and limited functions and do not participate in Ortiz’s employment. Similarly, there is no evidence to suggest EH was an agent of Moving Defendants, or that Moving Defendants controlled Ortiz’s employment at the time of termination or rehiring.

In order to impose alter-ego liability, a plaintiff must demonstrate 1) there is a unity of interests and ownerships between two corporations removing all distinction of separate personalities; and 2) an inequitable result would follow if the parties were not held liable for the acts of the other. (F. Hoffman-La Roche, Ltd. v. Superior Court (2005) 130 Cal. App. 4th 782, 796.) In order to determine if a corporation satisfies the first prong, the Court reviews an array of factors: comingling of assets, use of corporate assets as personal funds, failure to issue stock, the holding out an individual that they are personally liable for the debts of the corporation, the failure to maintain minutes, sole ownership in a corporation by one individual or family, the use of the same office, identical equitable ownership of two entities, the use of the same office or business location, employment of the same employees or attorney, the failure to adequately capitalize a corporation, the absence of corporate assets, use of a corporation as a mere shell for a single venture, concealment of ownership or management, disregard of legal formalities, and the formation of the entity to transfer existing liability, among others. (Associated Vendors, Inc. v. Oakland Meat Co. (1962) 210 Cal.App.2d 825, 838-840.) 

Here, the Court does not find Moving Defendants have demonstrated facts showing distinction of separate personalities between Moving Defendants and EH. Moving Defendants have primarily shown that Moving Defendants serve distinct business functions on behalf of EH and did not exercise control over Ortiz. They have not provided any other evidence establishing a distinction between entities.

However, the Court does find the evidence shows there would be no inequitable result following absent application of the alter-ego doctrine. Ortiz was solely employed, in name, by EH. EH controlled Ortiz’s work, termination, and rehiring. As EH is the parent company of Moving Defendants, EH should be sufficiently funded as to be able to pay out any damages awarded from Ortiz’s claims. There is no indication Ortiz will be denied fair trial or judgment absent inclusion of Moving Defendants.

Finally, the Court notes Moving Defendants are not in joint venture with EH. To establish the existence of a joint venture, the following elements must be shown: “ ‘There are three basic elements of a joint venture: the members must have joint control over the venture (even though they may delegate it), they must share the profits of the undertaking, and the members must each have an ownership interest in the enterprise.’ ” (Cochrum v. Costa Victoria Healthcare, LLC (2018) 25 Cal.App.5th 1034, 1053, internal citation omitted.) Here, the evidence shows Moving Defendants did not have joint control over EH’s business, specifically in regard to employment. Mere fact there is some control over payroll or insurance does not demonstrate joint control over the entire venture.

Moving Defendants have provided sufficient evidence establishing 1) Moving Defendants did not employ Ortiz and 2) EH's actions are not attributable to Moving Defendants via vicarious liability. Therefore, Moving Defendants have met their burden. The burden shifts to Ortiz.

D.         Ortiz’s Opposition

Ortiz first argues he had more than one employer at the time of termination. Ortiz relies upon the fact Ortiz’s wage statements and W-2s were issued and maintained by TEHCI, and the fact Millet-Reilly signed off her correspondence as “RVP of Anthem Blue Cross and Blue Shield,” a fictitious business name of AIC. (PAMF 22, 24, 46, 54, 56, 66-67.) Ortiz also relies upon the ‘supervisory organization’ he applied to after his employment was identified as BCC, another subsidiary. (PAMF 54.) Ortiz points to a master services agreement (“MSA”) in which ABC pays EH for staffing that expressly includes underwriting, encapsulated by Ortiz’s job function. (PAMF 30.)

The Court does not find any of the presented evidence to be indicative of employment under any applicable theory. First, TEHCI is not a moving party, so any connection it had is irrelevant for purposes of employment. At most, Ortiz demonstrated Millet-Reilly also worked within the scope of employment for AIC but did not show Millet-Reilly was working within this scope when firing Ortiz. The evidence overwhelming demonstrates that the applied employment was through EH, not BCC. The MSA is also not indicative of employment or a lack of corporate formalities; in fact, it’s more indicative of the two entities being treated as two separate entities, as they are paying each other for services. Also, there is no evidence or claim Ortiz was assigned to ABC under this MSA agreement.

Ortiz also did not provide sufficient evidence to demonstrate EH’s actions are attributable to Moving Defendants. Ortiz relies upon the facts Moving Defendants renamed themselves following EH’s renaming, that all parties use the same code of conduct, and that Ortiz’s employment records indicate all short-term disability policy’s apply to all of EH’s companies, all parties used the same career portal, and use the same leave, payroll, and termination companies. (PAMF 2, 8, 21, 25, 43, 39, 50-51.) While the Court acknowledges this all demonstrates corporate affiliation, nothing establishes Moving Defendants controlled Ortiz in such a manner to make decisions pertaining to the alleged discriminatory behavior or in such a manner that would make alter-ego liability necessary to obtain a just result. At no point did Ortiz perform work at the direction of or for Moving Defendants.

Ortiz has failed to meet his shifted burden. Therefore, the Court grants summary judgment.

 

IV.        CONCLUSION

Moving Defendants’ motion for summary judgment is GRANTED.

Moving Defendants are to give notice.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER DENYING MOTION FOR RECONSIDERATION

 

I.           BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Faith Nathaniel Beck (“Beck”) reported believed misconduct by Defendant Charlotte Spainhower (“Spainhower”) to appropriate personnel.  Following this report, Spainhower and agents of Defendant Saugus Union School District (“SUSD”) allegedly engaged in a retaliatory campaign against Beck.  In addition, Defendants allegedly harassed Beck via misgendering and ‘deadnaming’ Beck.  Beck sued Defendants for sixteen causes of action, including whistleblower retaliation, defamation, and gender-based harassment and discrimination.

On April 13, 2026, the Court granted Defendants’ special motion to strike (“anti-SLAPP motion”), in part.

On June 18, 2026, Beck filed the instant motion “that the Court consider exercising its inherent authority to reconsider and correct discrete portions of the April 13, 2026, Anti-SLAPP order.”  Defendants opposed the motion.  Beck replied.

II.         LEGAL STANDARD

Section 1008, subdivision (a), requires that a motion for reconsideration be based on new or different facts, circumstances, or law.  A party seeking reconsideration also must provide a satisfactory explanation for the failure to produce the evidence at an earlier time.”  (New York Times Co. v. Superior Court (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 206, 212.)

The moving party “shall state by affidavit . . . what new or different facts, circumstances, or law are claimed to be shown.”  (§1008, subd. (a).)  These requirements set forth “the court’s jurisdiction with regard to applications for reconsideration of its orders . . . .”  (§1008, subd. (e).)  Otherwise, “a court acts in excess of jurisdiction when it grants a motion to reconsider that is not based upon ‘new or different facts, circumstances, or law.’”  (Ibid.)

Any party affected by the order may, within 10 days after service upon the party of written notice of entry of the order, file a motion for reconsideration.  (§1008, subd. (a).)

Whether “new” facts alleged are sufficient to satisfy the requirements of section 1008, subdivision (b), is a question confided to the sound discretion of the trial court, with which the appellate court will not interfere absent an obvious showing of abuse.  (Graham v. Hansen (1982) 128 Cal. App. 3d 965, 971.)

 

III.       discussion

Beck requests the Court ‘consider exercising its inherit authority to reconsider and correct discrete portions of the April 13, 2026, order’ on the basis the Court wrongfully excluded Beck’s evidence and allegedly misinterpreted records and documents.  Beck asserts that this is not a section 1008 motion for reconsideration, as this is not based upon new or different facts, circumstances, or law, but instead relies upon the Court’s authority recognized in Le Francois v. Goel (2005) 35 Cal.4th 1094 to “correct an interim ruling based on an identifiable mistake in the existing record.”  (Mot., p. 8, ¶¶ 16-18.)

However, Le Francois specifically prohibits this exact request: “a party may not file a written motion to reconsider that has procedural significance if it does not satisfy the requirements of section 437c, subdivision (f)(2), or 1008.”  (Id. at 1108.)  “Unless the requirements of section 437c, subdivision (f)(2), or 1008 are satisfied, any action to reconsider a prior interim order must formally begin with the court on its own motion.”  (Ibid.)  The Court does not find a basis to reconsider the anti-SLAPP motion, a motion with procedural significance, on its own motion.

This motion may only be considered if it is deemed a renamed motion for reconsideration.  However, Beck has no right to bring such a motion at this time.  Pursuant to section 1008, subdivision (a), any motion for reconsideration must be filed within 10 days of service of the order to be reconsidered upon the moving party.  Defendants served notice of the ruling on Beck on April 16, 2026, via electronic transmission.  Pursuant to section 1010.6, subdivision (a)(3)(B), any time period or date prescribed by statute shall be extended two court days upon service by electronic transmission.  Therefore, any motion for reconsideration needed to be filed and served by April 28, 2026.

Beck did not file and serve this motion until June 18, 2026.  As this motion was filed almost two months after the statutory deadline, the Court denies the motion.

 

IV.        CONCLUSION

Beck’s motion for reconsideration is therefore DENIED.

Defendants are ordered to give notice.

MAE MORGAN STAFFING COMPANY,

Plaintiff,

vs.

BRENDA PATRON KODANI, et al.,

Defendants.

Case No.:

26STCV02831

Hearing Date:

September 17, 2026

ORDER SUSTAINING DEMURRER TO CROSS-COMPLAINT AND denying motion to strike

 

I.           BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Mae Morgan Staffing Company (“Mae Morgan”) employed Defendants Brenda Patron Kodani (“Kodani”) as a manager for Mae Morgan’s elderly home-care referral business. Kodani allegedly violated Mae Morgan’s “Employee Proprietary Information and Non-Competition Agreement” (the “agreement”) by providing confidential information to Kodani’s new employers and Mae Morgan’s competitors Defendants Complete In-Home Care Services, Inc. (“Complete”) and Cuthkelvin (“Cuthkelvin”). Mae Morgan sued Defendants for five causes of action, including breach of written contract.

On December 12, 2025, Kodani filed a Cross-Complaint (“CC”) against Plaintiff and Cross-Defendant Jeannie Yoon (“Yoon”) for ten causes of action, primarily labor code violations. Kodani’s cross-complaint alleges that Mae Morgan and Mae Morgan’s owner, Yoon, did not properly pay Kodani for her work (including large amounts of overtime), denied Kodani required meal and rest breaks, failed to reimburse Kodani for expenses associated with Kodani’s cell-phone, and forced Kodani to sign an illegal non-compete, among other alleged wrongdoings.

On February 23, 2026, Yoon filed the instant demurrer to the first through sixth causes of action to Kodani’s Cross-Complaint with motion to strike. Kodani opposed. Yoon replied.

 

II.         LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10[1] states: “The party against whom a complaint or cross-complaint has been filed may object, by demurrer or answer as provided in Section 430.30, to the pleading on any one or more of the following grounds: (a) The court has no jurisdiction of the subject of the cause of action alleged in the pleading; (b) The person who filed the pleading does not have the legal capacity to sue; (c) There is another action pending between the same parties on the same cause of action; (d) There is a defect or misjoinder of parties; (e) The pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action; (f) The pleading is uncertain. As used in this subdivision, “uncertain” includes ambiguous and unintelligible; and (g) In an action founded upon a contract, it cannot be ascertained from the pleading whether the contract is written, is oral, or is implied by conduct.”

As a general matter, in a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice.  (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)  “A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.”  (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.)  As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations.  (Ibid.)  The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action.  (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment.  (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.)  The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully.  (Ibid.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.)  However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.”  (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245).

           

III.       DISCUSSION

Yoon demurs to the first through sixth causes of action for failure to state sufficient facts to establish the causes of action and on the basis that the claims are not proper as they do not arise out of the same transaction, occurrence, or series of transactions or occurrences as alleged in the complaint.

A.         Employment Relationship

Yoon first demurs to the first through sixth causes of action on the basis that Kodani insufficiently alleged Yoon was Kodani’s ‘employer’ within the meaning of the labor code. All relevant causes of action are pled under the Labor Code. (See LC §§ 200, 201, 202, 203, 226, 226.3, 226.6, 226.7 510, 512, 1174, 1174.5, and 2802.) These causes of action only allow an employee to recover for alleged wrongdoing by their employer. Therefore, a requisite condition to pleading any causes of action under the relevant Labor Code sections is alleging facts in support of an employment relationship between Kodani and Yoon.

Pursuant to the Labor Code, an employer is a person or entity which employs any person or persons to perform services for a wage or salary. (LC § 1132.2.) A person employs another through three alternative means: 1) by exerting control over the wages, hours or working conditions of another person; 2) to suffer or permit to work; or 3) to engage another person, creating a common law employment relationship (Martinez v. Combs (2010) 49 Cal.4th 35, 64.) To establish a party ‘suffered’ or ‘permitted’ work, said party must have the power to either cause or prevent another from working under some sort of employment structure. (Futrell v. Payday California, Inc. (2010) 190 Cal.App.4th 1419, 1429.) A common law employment relationship generally evaluates whether the alleged employer had ‘control of details,’ such as tools, place of business, length of time, method of payment, and supervision. (Ibid.)

Here, Kodani has not pled sufficient facts to establish any relationship in which Yoon employed Kodani. Yoon is identified in the Cross-Complaint as Mae Morgan’s agent and owner. (CC ¶¶ 4, 16.) Yoon is also identified as knowing about the various alleged labor code violations and requiring Kodani to sign the illegal non-compete agreement. (CC ¶¶ 14, 16, 17-20.) Yoon is not identified as an employer, but rather as “the owner of Mae Morgan” who was in a position to correct the various violations against Kodani, but failed to do so, “making her individually liable to Kodani.” (CC ¶¶ 40, 46, 54, 61, 68, 73.)

The allegations attributable to Yoon do not meet any definition of ‘employer’ as identified under the Labor Code or associated case law. There are no allegations that Yoon affirmatively had control over Yoon’s wages, hours, working conditions, or other details. Corporate officers and agents are not automatically liable for wage violations by virtue of their corporate role absent specific showing of personally exercised control. (Martinez at 49 Cal.4th 64.) Kodani has not pled any facts indicating personal control by Yoon over Kodani’s work or wages, beyond Yoon’s status as owner and agent of Mae Morgan. Therefore, these causes of action are subject to demurrer.

B.          Proper Cross-Claims

Yoon also demurs on the basis that the first through sixth causes of action are not proper cross-claims as they do not arise out of the same transaction or occurrence as the underlying allegation. Section 428.10 only allows a party to file a cross-claim against non-parties to the underlying action if the causes of action asserted arise out of the same transaction, occurrence, or series of transactions in the cause bought against the party OR if it asserts as claim, right, or interest in the property or controversy brought against the party.

 Align Technology, Inc. v. Tran (2009) 179 Cal.App.4th 949 is instrusctive. In Align, an employer sued its former employee for breach of non-disclosure agreement after the employee allegedly used the employer’s confidential information to assist a start-up competitor. (Id. at 954.) The employee demurred on the basis that the action was barred under the compulsory cross-complaint statute, as, in a prior action, the employee had filed a cross-complaint for wrongful termination, and the employer had failed to assert claims at this time. (Id. at 953.) The Align court agreed with the employee, finding that the employee’s wrongful termination claim was logically related to the breach of non-disclosure agreement claim as they arose out of the same employment relationship. (Id. at 962.) “In this regard, we find the reasoning of a case applying the federal compulsory counterclaim statute useful: ‘The word ‘transaction’ in Rule 13(a) cannot be read in such a restrictive sense as to limit compulsory counterclaims in actions for breach of contract to those addressed to the specific breach on which a plaintiff bases his claim for breach. The spirit and intent of Rule 13(a) requires that the entire contractual relationship be deemed to be included within the word ‘transaction’ in cases sounding in contract.’” (Ibid., citations omitted.)

 Mae Morgan’s underlying complaint alleges Kodani misappropriated information and trade secrets obtained via Kodani’s employment with Mae Morgan, or that Kodani breached her obligations as an employee to her employer. Kodani’s cross-complaint asserts that Mae Morgan, and Yoon (as owner), breached their obligations as employers to their employee, Kodani. Both concern the entire contractual relationship between Kodani and her employers. So long as Kodani sufficiently pleads Yoon was Kodani’s employer at all relevant times, Kodani’s claims are within the scope of the same transaction.

C.          Motion to Strike

Yoon requests the Court strike all requests for punitive damages, specifically identifying the claims for punitive damages in the ninth and tenth causes of action for intentional interference with prospective economic advantage and violation of Penal Code section 502. Whether a party may plead a request for punitive damages is based upon the allegations of a complaint. Facts must be alleged in the pleading to support a claim for oppression, fraud, or malice. (Grieves v. Superior Court (1984) 157 Cal.App.3d 159, 161.)

“‘Malice’ means conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of others.” (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (c)(1).) A plaintiff must establish the defendant was aware of the probable dangerous consequences of his conduct and that he willfully and deliberately failed to avoid those consequences to support an award of punitive damages based on conscious disregard of the safety of others. (Penner v. Falk (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 858, 867.) “‘Oppression’ means despicable conduct that subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person’s rights.” (Civ. Code § 3294, subd. (c)(2).) “‘Fraud’ means an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known to the defendant with the intention on the part of the defendant of thereby depriving a person of property or legal rights or otherwise causing injury.” (Id., § 3294, subd. (c)(3).)

Yoon argues neither cause of action presents allegations that Yoon acted with malice, oppression, or fraud. The Court disagrees. The ninth cause of action alleges Yoon sought to interfere with Kodani’s employment by threatening to sue Kodani and Kodani’s new employers over an allegedly illegal and void non-compete agreement. (CC ¶¶ 90, 92.) If the non-compete agreement is illegal and void, as alleged in the Cross-Complaint, threatening enforcement of an illegal agreement to interfere with a party’s livelihood may fit within the definition of either ‘oppression’ or ‘fraud’ for purposes of punitive damages.

The tenth cause of action for violation of Penal Code section 502 explicitly allows the recovery of punitive damages: “In any action brought pursuant to this subdivision for a willful violation of the provisions of subdivision (c), where it is proved by clear and convincing evidence that a defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice as defined in subdivision (c) of Section 3294 of the Civil Code, the court may additionally award punitive or exemplary damages.” (Penal Code § 502, subd. (e)(4).) Kodani is entitled to request punitive damages, by statute.

The Court denies the motion to strike.

D.         Leave to Amend

The burden is on the pleading party to show in what manner he can amend the complaint, and how that amendment will change the legal effect of the pleading. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349; Hendy v. Losse (1991) 54 Cal.3d 723, 742.) Kodani requests leave to amend, in part, on the basis that she can provide additional facts to demonstrate Yoon’s liability under Labor Code section 558.1. The Court notes that while liability under Labor Code section 558.1 was not pled within the Cross-Complaint, it does encapsulate the scope of the allegations. Therefore, the Court finds basis to grant leave to allow Kodani an attempt to present an actionable Cross-Complaint against Yoon.

 

IV.        CONCLUSION

Yoon's demurrer to the Cross-Complaint is SUSTAINED, with 30 days leave to amend. Yoon’s motion to strike is DENIED.

Yoon ordered to give notice.
