STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

           

            This is an action for breach of contract and tortious interference with contract. Plaintiff alleges that Defendant Marks intentionally undercapitalized and misused a company he founded and controlled, which had contracted with Plaintiff for technology services, so as to avoid financial obligations under the contract.

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

Defendants’ motion for summary judgment, or in the alternative, summary adjudication is CONTINUED. The parties must either move to seal the documents they have lodged instead of filed, and move to seal any redacted papers; or alternatively, file all of the evidence and file unredacted papers. The Judicial Assistant will provided continued hearing date.

 

            Defendants to give notice.

 

DISCUSSION:

 

Unless confidentiality is required by law, court records are presumed to be open to the public. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.550, subd. (c).) Consequently, pleadings, motions, evidence, and other papers may not be filed under seal merely by stipulation of the parties; rather, a prior court order is necessary. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.551, subd. (a).) (Emphasis added). California Rules of Court, rule 2.551(b)(1) states, “[a] party requesting that a record be filed under seal must file a motion or an application for an order sealing the record. The motion or application must be accompanied by a memorandum and a declaration containing facts sufficient to justify the sealing.” CRC 2.550(d) makes clear that records cannot be sealed without appropriate findings.   

 

Both parties have lodged with the Court certain evidence pursuant to a protective order. Further, the Motion for Summary Judgment, Opposition, and both parties’ Separate Statements are also redacted. A record must not be filed under seal without a court order, even if based on the agreement of the parties.  (See CRC 2.551(a); see also H.B. Fuller Co. v. Doe (2007) 151 Cal.App.4th 879, 888.) The Court is unable to locate any prior order authorizing all of the documents that are lodged, nor has there been any motion filed to seal all of the documents at issue.

 

Based on the foregoing, the Court will continue the motion for summary judgment in the interim to provide the parties an opportunity to properly file an application or motion for the requested portions of the documents to be sealed or alternatively for the parties to file the evidence that is lodged, and to file unredacted versions of their briefs and separate statements.

 

Defendants’ motion for summary judgment, or in the alternative, summary adjudication is CONTINUED. The parties must either move to seal the documents they have lodged instead of filed, and move to seal any redacted papers; or alternatively, file all of the evidence and file unredacted papers. Judicial assistant will provided continued hearing date.

 

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

CASE:                         Tamara Patlogar v. City of Azusa, et al.

MOTION FOR DISCOVERY OF PEACE OFFICER PERSONNEL RECORDS (PITCHESS MOTION) AND OTHER DOCUMENTS

 

MOVING PARTY:               Plaintiff Tamara Patlogar

 

RESPONDING PARTY:     Defendant City of Azusa

TENTATIVE RULING:

Plaintiff’s Pitchess Motion is GRANTED.

 

The Court sets an in camera hearing in Department 507, Stanley Mosk Courthouse. The custodian of records is to produce at the hearing all potentially responsive documents for in camera inspection by the Court. At the hearing, the Court will set an appropriate date.

BACKGROUND

           

            This is an employment action. Plaintiff alleges she was a Police Administrative Services Manager for Defendant City of Azusa. Plaintiff alleges in 2017, she suffered an ocular migraine triggered by stress. She took medical leave from work to recover. Prior to this incident, Plaintiff observed Lieutenant Robert “Bob” Chivas verbally attacking a female employee during a meeting and promptly reported it to her supervisor. Plaintiff alleges that in 2019, she again witnessed and reported inappropriate interaction involving Chivas and another female employee.

 

Plaintiff also alleges that in or around February 2021, during a command staff meeting, Chivas publicly belittled Plaintiff, undermining her professional abilities and explicitly stating that she was incapable of performing her job duties. This was the first time Plaintiff had witnessed such a direct and hostile confrontation in a meeting, and she believed it was a clear act of sexism as Chivas did not treat any male counterparts in this manner. Plaintiff felt that Chivas's actions were

motivated by a personal bias against her as a woman in a leadership role, compounded by

his retaliation for her prior involvement in reporting his misconduct toward female

employees.

 

Plaintiff further alleges that shortly after the February 2021 meeting, in the spring of the same year, Plaintiff attended another meeting where Chivas erupted in intense rage upon learning that a female co-worker had named him in a lawsuit.

 

Plaintiff alleges that in or around September 2022, Plaintiff was excluded by Chivas from being scheduled for basic peer support training. 

 

Plaintiff alleges that between June 2023 and August 2023, she reported an incident to Captain Landeros after Steven Rodriguez shared a concerning encounter with Sergeant Fernando Vasconcelos. Rodriguez explained that Vasconcelos had asked him to release booked evidence—a cell phone—outside of established policy and protocol for the property and evidence room, which Rodriguez oversaw. Rodriguez stated that when he refused to release the evidence without first consulting a supervisor, Vasconcelos responded in a threatening manner, telling Rodriguez, “You’ve crossed the line with me, bro,” as he walked out of his office. Rodriguez felt uncomfortable with this exchange, and since he was not willing to write it up himself, Plaintiff took the responsibility of submitting a formal memo to Captain Landeros regarding the incident since Plaintiff was Rodriguez’s supervisor.

 

Plaintiff alleges that shortly after she submitted the memo regarding Vasconcelos, he began to ignore her.

 

Plaintiff alleges in April 2023, Plaintiff filed a whistleblower complaint concerning the misuse of the California Law Enforcement Telecommunications System (“CLETS”). During an internal audit, Plaintiff alleges she discovered that the system had been improperly used in the course of an investigation. 

 

Further, Plaintiff alleges that on May 9, 2024, Plaintiff submitted a second memorandum addressing concerns about Lieutenant Chivas. In this memorandum, Plaintiff reported that Lieutenant Chivas had instructed her to destroy certain files that were part of pending litigation, which she refused to do.

 

            Plaintiff alleges her health condition worsened due to stress, and her accommodations that were put in place (including not working with Chivas or Landeros) were consistently ignored. Further, she alleges her supervisory duties were stripped away. Plaintiff alleges she was constructively discharged as a result of the workplace being a toxic and unsupportive environment.

 

            The complaint seeks causes of action for (1) discrimination on the basis of sex/gender and disability; (2) harassment on the basis of sex/gender and disability; (3) retaliation on the basis of sex/gender and disability; (4) failure to prevent discrimination, harassment, and retaliation; (5) failure to provide reasonable accommodations; (6) failure to engage in the interactive process; and (7) violation of Labor Code section 1102.5.

 

Plaintiff moves for discovery of peace officer personal records.

 

DISCUSSION:

 

Legal Standard

 

There is a special two-step procedure for securing disclosure of peace officer personnel records.  (Warrick v. Superior Court (City of Los Angeles Police Dept.) (2005) 35 Cal.4th 1011, 1019.)  First, the party seeking disclosure must file a motion that identifies the peace officer, the agency in possession of the records, a description of the records, who is seeking the records, as well as time and place of the hearing.  (Evid. Code, § 1043(b)(1).)  The motion must be accompanied by a declaration: showing “good cause” for disclosure of the records; setting forth the materiality of the records; and stating upon reasonable belief that the governmental agency has the requested documents.  (Evid. Code, § 1043(b)(3).)  This is a so-called “Pitchess” motion, derived from Pitchess v. Superior Court (1974) 11 Cal.3d 531.

 

The Evidence Code section 1043(b) “good cause” declaration must be sufficiently specific “to preclude the possibility of [the movant] simply casting about for any helpful information.”  (People v. Mooc (2001) 26 Cal.4th 1216, 1226.)  However, an attorney declaration based upon information and belief and containing hearsay may be used to evidence good cause in support of a Pitchess motion.  (Haggerty v. Superior Court (2004) 117 Cal.App.4th 1079, 1086.)  The moving party need show only a “plausible factual foundation” for discovery—i.e., a scenario of officer misconduct that might occur or could have occurred.  (Warrick, supra, 35 Cal.4th at 1026.)  All that is required is the presentation of a scenario that might have or could have occurred; i.e., a “relatively low threshold.”  (Uybungco v. Superior Court (San Diego Police Dept.) (2008) 163 Cal. App. 4th 1043, 1048; see also Blumberg v. Superior Court (2011) 197 Cal. App. 4th 1245, 1248 (the good cause requirement of section 1043(b) “embodies a ‘relatively low threshold' for discovery’ [citation], under which a defendant need demonstrate only ‘a logical link between the defense proposed and the pending charge’ and describe with some specificity ‘how the discovery being sought would support such a defense’”).) 

 

Second, if the court finds good cause, then an in camera hearing must be held.  (Slayton v. Sup.Ct. (Slayton) (2006) 146 Cal.App.4th 55, 61.)  After personally examining the records in camera, the trial court shall order disclosure of peace officer personnel records that are “relevant to the matter involved in the pending litigation.”  (Evid. Code §1045(a); People v. Mooc, supra, 26 Cal.4th at 1226.)  If disclosure is ordered, the court must also order that the disclosed information may not be used “for any purpose other than a court proceeding pursuant to applicable law.”  (Code Civ. Proc., §1045(e); see Alford v. Sup.Ct. (People) (2003) 29 Cal.4th 1033, 1039–1040.) 

 

Pitchess Motion

 

Plaintiff seeks the following documents which she contends will support her causes of action.

 

REQUEST FOR PRODUCTION NO. 1: Any and all documents (including e- mails, letters, text messages, investigative reports, etc.) that refer or relate to complaints of discrimination, harassment, or retaliation made by employees of Responding Party against Robert “Bob” Chivas from January 2016 to the present.

 

REQUEST FOR PRODUCTION NO. 2: Any and all documents (including e- mails, letters, text messages, investigative reports, etc.) that refer or relate to complaints of discrimination, harassment, or retaliation based on sex/gender made by employees of Responding Party against Robert “Bob” Chivas from January 2016 to the present.

REQUEST FOR PRODUCTION NO. 5: Any and all documents (including e- mails, letters, text messages, investigative reports, etc.) that refer or relate to any reprimand, notations on performance reviews, write ups, or disciplinary action against Robert “Bob” Chivas related to discrimination, harassment, or retaliation based on sex/gender from January 2016 to the present.

 

REQUEST FOR PRODUCTION NO. 7: Any and all documents (including e-mails, letters, text messages, investigative reports, etc.) that refer or relate to complaints of discrimination, harassment, or retaliation made by employees of Responding Party against Robert Landeros from January 2016 to the present.

 

REQUEST FOR PRODUCTION NO. 8: Any and all documents (including e-mails, letters, text messages, investigative reports, etc.) that refer or relate to complaints of discrimination, harassment, or retaliation based on sex/gender made by employees of Responding Party against Robert Landeros from January 2016 to the present.

 

REQUEST FOR PRODUCTION NO. 10: Any and all documents (including e-mails, letters, text messages, investigative reports, etc.) that refer or relate to any reprimand, notations on performance reviews, write ups, or disciplinary action against Robert Landeros related to discrimination, harassment, or retaliation from January 2016 to the present.

Step One: Good Cause

 

Plaintiff’s counsel submits a declaration stating that the requested documents will assist Plaintiff in proving her claims that Defendants discriminated against, retaliated against, and harassed her due to her sex/gender and for engaging in protected activity by pursuing her rights under the FEHA. Counsel declares that the requested documents will also assist Plaintiff in proving that Defendants not only condoned and ratified the violations, but also actively perpetrated such actions against her. (Sorejian Decl., ¶ 9.)

 

Counsel avers declares that the requested information is believed to be admissible under several theories: as evidence of Defendants’ FEHA violations; as evidence of Defendants’ failure to take appropriate corrective action, despite being on notice; evidence tending to show Defendants’ and one or more of its affiliated witnesses lack credibility, and/or have engaged in similar employment related misconduct and/or acts of dishonesty, and evidence of Defendants’ and/or officers’ pattern and practice of engaging in, encouraging, and/or failing to prevent or correct FEHA violations, as demonstrated by the inadequacy of the investigations. (Id., ¶ 10.)

 

Defendants expressly recognize the Pitchess procedure’s applicability here and that the “good cause” showing required by Evidence Code section 1043(b)(3) is a relatively low threshold. (See Riske v. Superior Court (2016) 6 Cal.App.5th 647 (“Riske”).) Defendants do not ask this Court to deny in-camera review outright as to records that bear a genuine relationship to sex/gender-based misconduct or to retaliation for Plaintiff's protected activity. However, Defendants argue that several of Plaintiff’s sixteen requests are not reasonably tailored to obtain such documents.

 

Defendants contend that as drafted, certain requests (1) reach back a full ten years without regard to the five- year limitation Plaintiff’s own brief invokes, and (2) sweep in complaints, if any, having nothing to do with sex/gender or retaliation at all, even though Plaintiff’s theory of the case is expressly limited to sex/gender discrimination, harassment and whistleblower retaliation.

 

As noted above, the requests seek documents from January 2016 to the present. Defendant contends that Plaintiff has made no attempt, by declaration or otherwise, to establish a plausible factual foundation of materiality for the additional five years (January 2016 through approximately early 2020).

 

However, Plaintiff’s complaint alleges that in 2017, Plaintiff witnessed Defendant Chivas verbally attacking a female employee. Plaintiff’s counsel has stated that they seek evidence of Defendants’ pattern and practice of engaging in, encouraging, and/or failing to prevent or correct FEHA violations. (Sorejian Decl., ¶ 10.)

 

Thus, there is good cause for the 10-year scope.

 

Next, Defendant argues that Request Nos. 1, 4, 7 and 10 should be limited to complaints and discipline bearing a nexus to sex/gender or to retaliation for protected activity. Defendant argues that Request Nos. 1 and 4 (as to Chivas) and Request Nos. 7 and 10 (as to Landeros) are too broad and must be limited to sex/gender. As drafted, Request Nos. 1, 4, 7, and 10 would reach complaints bearing no relationship whatsoever to Plaintiff’s own alleged protected activity/characteristics.

 

However, the scope of discovery is not so limited. (See Haggerty, supra, 117 Cal.App.4th at 1091 (“the issue here is not whether the report should or would be admissible at trial, but whether the factual information contained in the report could lead to the discovery of relevant and admissible evidence.”).) As Plaintiff points out, these documents are relevant to show past, similar misconduct of Chivas and Landeros. Further, Plaintiff argues the documents will show that these officers had a history of discriminatory, harassing, and retaliatory behavior and Entity Defendants did nothing about it. Additionally, Plaintiff's disability-related causes of action independently place at issue whether Chivas and Landeros discriminated against or harassed employees on the basis of disability, apart from sex/gender or whistleblowing.

 

As such, Plaintiff has shown good cause.

 

Step Two: In Camera Disclosure Determination Upon Review of All Potentially Relevant Documents 

  

[I]f “the trial court concludes the defendant has fulfilled these prerequisites and made a showing of good cause, the custodian of records should bring to court all documents ‘potentially relevant’ to the . . . motion. . . . The trial court ‘shall examine the information in chambers’ (Evid. Code, § 1045, subd. (b)), ‘out of the presence and hearing of all persons except the person authorized [to possess the records] and such other persons [the custodian of records] is willing to have present.’ … Subject to statutory exceptions and limitations . . . the trial court should then disclose to the [moving party] ‘such information [that] is relevant to the subject matter involved in the pending litigation.’ [Citations.]”  
 

(Haggerty, supra, 117 Cal.App.4th at 1086, bold emphasis added.) 

 

If disclosure is ordered, the court must also order that the disclosed information may not be used “for any purpose other than a court proceeding pursuant to applicable law.” (Evid. Code, § 1045, subd. (e);¿see Alford v. Superior Court (2003) 29 Cal.4th 1033, 1039-1040 [overruled on other grounds].)

 

Defendant also argues that before disclosure of any records to Plaintiff, the Court should permit redaction of third-party information. To the extent that information should be redacted or deemed confidential, the Court will make these determinations after the in camera hearing.  

 

CONCLUSION:

 

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s Pitchess Motion is GRANTED.

 

The Court sets an in camera hearing in Department 507, Stanley Mosk Courthouse. The custodian of records is to produce at the hearing all potentially responsive documents for in camera inspection by the Court. At the hearing, the Court will set the appropriate date.

Moving Party to give notice.

IT IS SO ORDERED.
