Case Number: 23SMCV05356    Hearing Date: July 6, 2026    Dept: P

Tentative Ruling

Oakhurst Income Fund II v. Don Caverhill, Case No. 23SMCV05356

Plaintiff’s Motion for Attorneys’ Fees

Hearing Date: July 6, 2026

Background

On November 14, 2023, Plaintiff Oakhurst Income Fund II, LP (“Oakhurst”) sued Defendant Don Caverhill for breach of three continuing guaranty agreements dated August 26, 2022. (Complaint, ¶ BC-1.) Oakhurst alleged that Caverhill owed it $1,087,459.48 under the guaranty agreements. (Id. at ¶ BC-4.)

On December 14, 2023, Oakhurst applied for a writ of attachment, right to attach order, and temporary protective order on Caverhill’s real property located at 2500 Briarcrest Rd., Beverly Hills, CA 90210. On February 2, 2024, the Court granted Oakhurst’s application.

On February 7, 2024, Oakhurst entered default against Caverhill.

On May 22, 2024, the Court granted Oakhurst default judgment against Caverhill, awarding Oakhurst $639,035.74 in damages, $11,266.60 in pre-judgment interest, $9,180.40 in attorneys’ fees, and $2,523.50 in costs.

On May 22, 2024, the Court found that this case was not related to LASC Case No. 24SMCV01151, Oakhurst Income Fund II, LP v. 2500 Briarcliff Road, LLC.

Since July 2024, there have been two hearings on applications for orders for appearance and examination.

On May 21, 2026, Oakhurst filed this Motion for Attorneys’ Fees. Oakhurst moves for an order under Code Civ. Proc §§ 685.040, 685.080, and 1033.5(a)(10)(A) awarding it attorneys’ fees and expenses incurred in enforcing the default judgment, in the amount of $55,766.50. (Motion for Attorneys’ Fees, p. 2.) This motion is unopposed.

Motion for Attorneys’ Fees

“The judgment creditor is entitled to the reasonable and necessary costs of enforcing a judgment. Attorney’s fees incurred in enforcing a judgment are not included in costs collectible under this title unless otherwise provided by law. Attorney’s fees incurred in enforcing a judgment are included as costs collectible under this title if the underlying judgment includes an award of attorney’s fees to the judgment creditor pursuant to subparagraph (A) of paragraph (10) of subdivision (a) of Section 1033.5.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 685.040.) Code Civ. Proc. § 1033.5(a)(10)(A) allows a prevailing party to recover attorneys’ fees authorized by contract.

A motion brought under § 685.040 must be made before the judgment is satisfied in full, but not later than two years after the costs have been incurred. (Code Civ. Proc. § 685.080.) Such a motion must be served on the judgment debtor and be supported by an affidavit of a person who has knowledge of the facts. (Code Civ. Proc. § 685.080.)

“A trial court assessing attorney fees begins with a touchstone or lodestar figure, based on the ‘careful compilation of the time spent and reasonable hourly compensation of each attorney ... involved in the presentation of the case.” (Christian Research Institute v. Alnor (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 1315, 1321.) “The reasonableness of attorney fees is within the discretion of the trial court, to be determined from a consideration of such factors as the nature of the litigation, the complexity of the issues, the experience and expertise of counsel and the amount of time involved. The court may also consider whether the amount requested is based upon unnecessary or duplicative work.” (Wilkerson v. Sullivan (2002) 99 Cal.App.4th 443, 448.) 

 

Analysis

The underlying judgment in this action was entered pursuant to three guarantee contracts, each of which contained an attorneys’ fees provision stating: “Guarantor agrees to pay Lender, on demand, reasonable attorneys’ fees and all other costs and expenses which may be incurred by Lender in the collection or attempted collection from Borrower of any Credit and/or in the interpretation, enforcement or attempted enforcement by Lender of this Guaranty. . .” (Complaint, Exhibit A, p. 010, ¶ 20; Complaint, Exhibit A, p. 022, ¶ 20; Complaint, Exhibit A, p. 034, ¶ 20.) Oakhurst’s default judgment in this case included an award of $9,180.40 in attorneys’ fees pursuant to this provision. The default judgment is therefore a qualifying underlying judgment including an award of attorneys’ fees “pursuant to subparagraph (A) of paragraph (10) of subdivision (a) of Section 1033.5,” under Code Civ. Proc. § 685.040.

This motion is timely because the judgment has not been satisfied and because it seeks attorneys’ fees for work to enforce the judgment in the past two years, between May 22, 2024 and May 21, 2026. (See Landau Decl., ¶ 7.)

a.      Lodestar Analysis

Oakhurst requests fees for its’ attorney in this matter, Lewis R. Landau. Mr. Landau specializes in bankruptcy law and has been in practice for more than thirty years. (Landau Decl., ¶ 2.) Mr. Landau’s rates for 2024, 2025, and 2026 are $745.00 per hour, $795.00 per hour, and $845.00 per hour. (Landau Decl., ¶ 8.) These rates are reasonable for an attorney of Mr. Landau’s experience and expertise in the Los Angeles area.

Oakhurst moves for fees for 68.10 hours of Mr. Landau’s work. (Landau Decl., ¶ 9.) Oakhurst’s billing records submitted in support of this motion, however, are primarily composed of billing entries for work on Oakhurst Income Fund II, LP v. 2500 Briarcliff Road, LLC, LASC Case No. 24SMCV01151, an unrelated case in which Mr. Landau also represents Oakhurst. Some billing entries reflect work on both cases. For example, Mr. Landau billed 2 hours on December 11, 2025 for “Prepar[ing] revised ORAP application for attempted service on Caverhill at Lancaster address and update B2500 discovery requests and subpoenas.” (Landau Decl., Exhibit A, p. 13.) This Court will not award fees or costs for work done on a different case before a different judge. The Court will also not award fees or costs for Mr. Landau’s time where it is not clear which case he was working on.

Based on the Court’s review of Mr. Landau’s billing records, Mr. Landau billed 3.5 hours on this action in 2024, 12.7 hours in 2025, and 5.3 hours in 2026. These hours are recoverable under Code Civ. Proc. § 635.040. (3.5 hours x $745.00 per hour ) + (12.7 hours x 795.00 per hour) + (5.3 hours x 845 per hour) = $17,182.50.

Oakhurst also requests costs of $1,317.00 (Landau Decl., ¶ 10.) Following a review of Oakhurst’s costs, $911.00 of Oakhurst’s requested costs were incurred in this case. The Court grants Oakhurst those costs.

Conclusion

Oakhurst’s Motion for Attorneys’ Fees is Granted in the amount of $17,182.50, plus $911.00 in costs.


Case Number: 25SMCV06676    Hearing Date: July 6, 2026    Dept: P

Tentative Ruling

Pellequr, LLC v. 136 Beverly Drive, LLC, Case No. 25SMCV06676

Defendant’s Motion to Set Aside Default

Hearing Date: July 6, 2026

Background

Plaintiff Pellequr, LLC, a skin care spa, sues Defendant 136 Beverly Drive, LLC, its landlord, for breach of lease, alleging that in January 2019, Defendant’s storm water pump failed, causing foul odors and black mold in the space Pellequr leased from Defendant at 136 Beverly Drive, Beverly Hills, CA. (Complaint, ¶ 21.) Plaintiff also alleges that Defendant’s construction in November 2023 caused Plaintiff’s clients to cancel appointments, obstructed Plaintiff’s parking spots, and caused power outages, carbon monoxide leaks, and water damage. (Id. at ¶ 24.)

On December 18, 2025, Plaintiff filed the complaint.

On February 5, 2025, Plaintiff filed proof of service by substituted service.

On March 12, 2025, Plaintiff entered default against Defendant.

On May 11, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Code Civ. Proc. § 585 prove-up packet.

On May 18, 2026, Defendant filed this motion to set aside default. On June 22, 2026, Plaintiff filed opposition. On June 25, 2025, Defendant filed a reply.

On June 29, 2026, Defendant filed a notice of related case.

Arguments

Defendant moves to set aside default pursuant to Code Civ. Proc. § 473, 473(d), 473.5, 473.6, 580(a), and on equitable grounds. Though Defendant moves to set aside default under five different code sections and on equitable grounds, his primary argument is under Code Civ. Proc. § 473.5(a), that he was not properly served. (Motion to Set Aside Default, pp. 5- 8.) Defendant also argues that default should be set aside because he was surprised by this lawsuit because he was not properly serves, and because Defendant has a defense to this case on the merits because the lease at issue was signed by Pellequere, not Plaintiff Pellequr. (Id. at pp. 4, 9.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that it changed its name from Pelle Cure to Pellequr after it signed the lease but before filing this litigation. (Opposition, p. 4.) Plaintiff argues that it properly served Defendant’s agent for service of process, Mr. Hakim, at his home, after Plaintiff’s process server was prevented from accessing Defendant’s office. (Id. at p. 2.) Plaintiff also argues that Defendant had actual notice because Plaintiff also emailed copies of the summons and complaint to Defendant’s attorney in another matter. Plaintiff also argues that several of Defendant’s arguments are irrelevant to setting aside default and that Defendant has a habit of evading service. (Id. at pp. 8-9.)

In reply, Defendant argues that Plaintiff improperly relies on imputed notice to Defendant’s counsel in another matter, that service on an entity’s agent’s home is not proper under Code Civ.Proc. § 415.20(b), and that subtstituted service was defected. (Reply, pp. 2-3.) Defendant also argues that Plaintiff’s allegations of evasion of service are hearsay and that it has a meritorious defense. (Id. at pp. 4-5.) In reply, Defendant abandons any argument it initially brought under Code Civ. Proc. § 473(b).

Motion to Set Aside Default

CCP section 473(b) provides, in relevant part, that “the court shall, whenever an application for relief is made no more than six months after entry of judgment, is in proper form, and is accompanied by an attorney’s sworn affidavit attesting to his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect, vacate any (1) resulting default entered by the clerk against his or her client, and which will result in entry of a default judgment, or (2) resulting default judgment or dismissal entered against his or her client, unless the court finds that the default or dismissal was not in fact caused by the attorney’s mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect.”  (Id.)

Code of Civ. Proc. § 473.5(a) also provides a mechanism for the court to set aside default judgment if service did not result in actual notice:¿¿ 

“When service of summons has not resulted in actual notice to the party in time to defend the action and a default or default judgment has been against him or her he or she may serve and file a notice of motion to set aside the default or default judgment and for leave to defend the action. The notice of motion shall be served and filed within a reasonable time, but in no event exceeding the earlier of: (i) two years after entry of a default judgment against him or her; or (ii) 180 days after service on him or her of a written notice that the default or default judgment has been entered.”  

In the first instance, the motion to set aside default must be brought within six months of entry of default. In the second instance, the motion must be brought within two years of entry of default.

“The policy of the law favors a hearing on the merits.” (Purdum v. Holmes (2010) 187 Cal.App.4th 916, 922.) Therefore, when a party in default moves promptly to request relief, very slight evidence is required to justify a trial court's order setting aside a default. (Id.)¿ 

Analysis

In lieu of personal service, a corporation may be served by substituted service by leaving a copy of the summons and complaint during usual office hours at the person’s office, and then mailing a copy of the summons and complaint to that address. (Code Civ. Proc. § 415.20(a).)

A summons may be served on a corporation or LLC by delivering a copy of the summons and the complaint to the person designated as agent for service of process. (Code Civ. Proc. § 416.10; Code Civ. Proc. § 416.40; Corp. Code § 17701.16(b).) A plaintiff may also serve a Defendant’s agent for service of process by substituted service by leaving a copy of the summons and complaint at the agent’s office, or, if no physical address is known, at that person’s usual mailing address, with a person at least 18 years old who is informed of the contents of the papers, and then mailing a second copy to that office by first class mail with pre-paid postage. (Code Civ. Proc. § 415.20(a), referencing Code Civ. Proc. § 416.10, 416.40.)

In arguing that they properly served Mr. Hakim, Defendant’s agent for service of process, Plaintiffs mistakenly rely on § 415.20(b), which describes the circumstances under which substituted service may be made on an individual defendant, or on individuals described under Code Civ. Proc. sections 416.60, 416.70, 416.80, or 416.90. As Defendant’s designated agent for service of process, Mr. Hakim is an individual who may be served on Defendant’s behalf under Code Civ. Proc. § 416.10 or § 416.40. Substituted service on Mr. Hakim is proper if it complies with Code Civ. Proc. § 416.20(a).

Here, service was proper under Section 415.20(a). Plaintiffs submit evidence that they initially attempted to make personal service or substituted service on Defendant itself under Section 415.20(a) at its place of business at 6100 Wilshire Blvd., Ste. 1100, Los Angeles, CA, 90048 but were refused access to the office space. (Trumpson Decl., ¶ 4.) Plaintiffs then decided to serve Defendant’s registered agent for service of process, Mr. Hakim. (Trumpson Decl., ¶ 5.) Mr. Since Plaintiffs had already failed to serve Defendant at the address listed for Mr. Hakim with the Secretary of State, 6100 Wilshire Blvd, Ste. 1100, they attempted service at his other known address, 1005 Summit Dr., Beverly Hills, CA. (Guerridos Decl., ¶ 3.) Plaintiff’s registered process made substituted service on Mr. Hakim at that address by serving an individual over 18 years of age and informing her of the contents of the package. (Guerridos Decl., ¶¶ 4-8.) Plaintiffs then mailed a copy of the summons and complaint to Mr. Hakim at the same address they attempted service on. (Guerridos Decl., ¶ 9.) Defendant argues that the only permissible location to serve a corporation is the address registered with the secretary of state. Defendant’s cited cases do not support this conclusion, nor does the plain text of Code Civ. Proc. § 415.20(a). Plaintiff’s service was proper under § 415.20(a). (See generally, Ramos v. Homeward Residential, Inc. (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 1434, 1412 [“it is axiomatic that strict compliance with the code’s provisions for service of process is not required.”].)

Though Mr. Hakim submitted a declaration that he did not receive the summons and complaint served on him at 1005 Summit Dr., Beverly Hills CA, he does not deny that this is his address. (See Hakim Decl., generally.) He also states that had he been served at the address where Plaintiff’s first attempted service, he would have received the documents. However, Plaintiff’s process server was denied entry at 6100 Wilshire Blvd. when he attempted to serve Defendant there.

Defendant’s argument that default should be set aside because of “surprise” due to the defects in service does not substantiate their effort to set aside default under § 473(b). To set aside default under that subsection, Defendant must submit some evidence of an error or neglect that created the surprise. Defendant submits no evidence of mistake or neglect other than the allegedly-improper service. Since service was proper, there is no evidence of mistake that would justify setting aside default.

Defendant’s argument that Plaintiff Pellequr is a completely different entity from the lease signatory, “Pellequere,” is completely unsupported by the evidence. Defendant submitted no evidence in support of this contention. In opposition, Plaintiff submitted a copy of the lease in question—signed by Pellequr, LLC. Pellequr’s CFO crossed out the name “Pelle Cure” and wrote in Pellequr. The entity “Pellequere” appears nowhere in the document. (Margaryan Decl. ISO Opposition to Defendant’s Motion to Set Aside Default, Exhibit C, p. 8.)

Suspected AI use

Among their cited code sections, Defendants claim that they move under Code Civ. Proc. § 473.6, which does not exist. In support of their Code Civ. Proc. § 473.6 argument, Defendants cite Goya v. P.E.R.U. Enterprises, et al. (1978) 87 Cal.App.3d 886, a real case concerning CCP § 473.5, not the non-existent CCP §473.6. As Plaintiffs point out, Defendants also cite Ramos v. Homeward Residential, Inc. (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 1434 for the proposition that “service at an address not associated with the corporation in SOS records is ineffective.” (Opposition, p. 6, fn. 7.)  Like Goya, Ramos is a real case that does not stand for the proposition that Defendant advances. Errors like this are typical of artificial intelligence hallucinations. Pursuant to Noland v. Land of the Free, L.P. (2025) 114 Cal.App.5th 426, the Court sets an OSC re: Sanctions for August 3, 2026.

 Conclusion

Defendant’s Motion to Set Aside Default is DENIED without prejudice.

The Court sets an OSC re: Sanctions for August 3, 2026. Defendant’s counsel is ordered to file a declaration by July 27, 2026 addressing the possible use of AI to craft pleadings.
