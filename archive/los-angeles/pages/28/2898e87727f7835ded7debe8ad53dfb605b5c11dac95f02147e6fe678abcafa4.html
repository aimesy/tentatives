Case Number: 24AHCV00046    Hearing Date: August 7, 2026    Dept: P

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING DEFENDANTS’ MOTION FOR LEAVE TO CONDUCT A SECOND PHYSICAL EXAMINATION OF PLAINTIFF BRADLEY ERWIN

I.         INTRODUCTION

            This action arises out of an alleged slip-and-fall incident that occurred on January 9, 2022, at 3329 South Bristol Street in Santa Ana, California. Plaintiff Bradley Erwin (“Plaintiff”) alleges that, while lawfully on the premises, he slipped and fell on an unknown liquid substance. Plaintiff claims that Defendants Trader Joe’s, Excel Realty Partners and Excel Realty Partners, L.P., negligently owned, operated, and maintained the subject premises.

As a result of the incident, Plaintiff alleges he sustained serious injuries requiring medical treatment. On January 8, 2024, Plaintiff filed this action asserting causes of action for (1) premises liability and (2) general negligence. On October 24, 2025, Plaintiff filed a First Amended Complaint.

On July 14, 2026, Defendants Trader Joe's Company ("Defendants") filed the instant motion for leave to conduct a second physical examination of Plaintiff. Plaintiff filed a late opposition on July 29, 2026, and Defendant filed a reply on July 31, 2026.

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

            “Any party may obtain discovery . . . by means of a physical or mental examination of (1) a party to the action, (2) an agent of any party, or (3) a natural person in the custody or under the legal control of a party, in any action in which the mental or physical condition … of that party or other person is in controversy in the action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2032.020, subd. (a).) 

In a personal injury action, the defendant may demand one physical examination of plaintiff as of right, without advance leave of the court.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 2032.220.)   

If a defendant seeks a further physical examination of plaintiff, or a mental examination, the defendant must first file a motion and “obtain leave of court.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2032.310, subd. (a).) Such a motion must “specify the time, place, manner, conditions, scope, and nature of the examination, as well as the identity and the specialty, if any, of the person or persons who will perform the examination.” (Id., subd. (b).) The court may grant such a motion “only for good cause shown.” (Id., § 2032.320, subd. (a).) A showing of good cause generally requires “that the party produce specific facts justifying discovery and that the inquiry be relevant to the subject matter of the action or reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence.” (Vinson v. Super. Ct. (1987) 43 Cal.3d 833, 840.)   

The moving party must support the motion with a meet and confer declaration. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2032.310, subd. (b).) A meet and confer declaration must state facts “showing a reasonable and good faith attempt at an informal resolution of each issue presented by the motion.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2016.040.) 

The examination will be limited to whatever condition is “in controversy” in the action.¿ (Code Civ. Proc., § 2032.020, subd. (a).)“An order granting a physical or mental examination shall specify the person or persons who may perform the examination, as well as the time, place, manner, diagnostic tests and procedures, conditions, scope, and nature of the examination.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2032.320, subd. (d).) “The court is to describe in detail who will conduct the examination, where and when it will be conducted, the conditions, scope and nature of the examination, and the diagnostic tests and procedures to be employed. The way to describe these ‘diagnostic tests and procedures’—fully and in detail—is to list them by name.”¿(Carpenter v. Super. Ct. (2006) 141 Cal.App.4th 249, 260.)

III.      ANALYSIS

            Defendant moves for an order granting leave to conduct a second physical examination of Plaintiff Bradley Erwin, limited to a neurosurgical examination of Plaintiff's lumbar spine and related lower-extremity neurological complaints. Specifically, Defendant seeks an examination by Paul E. Kaloostian, M.D., FAANS, FACS, a board-certified neurological surgeon, to take place on August 11, 2026, at 1:30 p.m., at 960 E. Green Street, Suite 320, Pasadena, California. Defendant represents that the examination will be a standard, non-invasive clinical examination lasting no more than two hours, will not include any painful, protracted, or intrusive procedures, imaging, or invasive testing, and will be limited to Plaintiff's lumbar spine and related neurological complaints. The examination will consist of history taking, range of motion testing of the low back, motor, sensory, and reflex testing, gait testing, palpation of the low back, hips, and sacroiliac joints, and measurement of calf circumference. (Notice, p. 2.) 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2032.310, subdivision (b), requires that a motion for an additional physical examination specify "the time, place, manner, conditions, scope, and nature of the examination, as well as the identity and the specialty, if any, of the person or persons who will perform the examination." Here, Defendant's motion identifies each of the required statutory particulars

Defendant contends good cause exists because Plaintiff's lumbar condition has materially changed since the initial orthopedic examination on November 20, 2024. Specifically, Defendant asserts that Plaintiff subsequently treated with two new spine surgeons, Drs. Kasra Rowshan and Wei Wu, who opined that the subject incident caused or aggravated Plaintiff's lumbar condition and recommended lumbar decompression surgery. Defendant further contends that Plaintiff underwent updated lumbar imaging, testified that lumbar surgery had been scheduled, and presented conflicting neurological findings in his subsequent treatment records. Defendant argues these post-examination developments materially expanded Plaintiff's lumbar surgical claim and warrant a limited neurosurgical examination. (Motion, pp. 2, 6-8; Reply, pp. 2, 4-7.)

Plaintiff opposes the motion, arguing Defendant has failed to establish good cause for a second physical examination. Plaintiff contends Defendant did not adequately meet and confer before filing the motion, as Defendant provided only four days to respond to its meet and confer letter before serving an examination demand and filing the instant motion. Plaintiff further argues the purportedly "new" developments are merely the progression of a lumbar and neurological injury that was disclosed before Defendant selected its orthopedic examiner for the first examination. According to Plaintiff, Defendant's preference for a different medical specialty does not constitute good cause for a second examination. Plaintiff also contends the requested examination is cumulative because Defendant already possesses Plaintiff's medical records, imaging, treating physician depositions, and the opinions of its retained orthopedic expert. (Opposition., pp. 2-6.)

As a preliminary matter, the Court exercises its discretion to consider Plaintiff's late-filed opposition. Plaintiff explains the delay resulted from an inadvertent calendaring error, and Defendant has not demonstrated any prejudice from the two-court-day delay, having filed a substantive reply addressing the merits of Plaintiff's opposition. Therefore, the Court considers the opposition.

The Court further finds Defendant has demonstrated good cause for a second physical examination under Code of Civil Procedure section 2032.320. Here, Defendant has shown that Plaintiff's post-examination treatment and expanded surgical claims raise issues that could not have been evaluated during the initial orthopedic examination, thereby establishing good cause for a limited additional examination. (See Shapira v. Superior Court (1990) 224 Cal.App.3d 1249, 1254-1256.)

Here, Defendant's initial orthopedic examination occurred on November 20, 2024. Since that examination, Plaintiff began treating with two additional spine surgeons who rendered new causation opinions and recommended materially broader lumbar surgical treatment, including decompression surgery with possible fusion.  (Reply, p. 2.) Plaintiff also underwent updated lumbar imaging and testified that lumbar surgery had been scheduled. (Id.) These developments did not exist at the time of the November 2024 examination and materially expanded Plaintiff's lumbar surgical claim. Under these circumstances, Defendant is entitled to have a qualified physician evaluate Plaintiff's current condition and respond to these post-examination developments.

The Court also finds a neurosurgical examination appropriate. Because Plaintiff's post-examination treatment reflects expanded neurological findings and recommendations for neurosurgical intervention, Defendant has established good cause for an examination by a board-certified neurosurgeon. 

Finally, Defendant has complied with the procedural requirements of Code of Civil Procedure section 2032.310, subdivision (b), by specifying the time, place, manner, conditions, scope, and nature of the proposed examination, as well as the identity and specialty of the examining physician. 

Based on the foregoing, the Court GRANTS the motion for leave.

IV.       CONCLUSION AND ORDER

            The Court GRANTS Defendants motion for leave to conduct a second physical examination of Plaintiff. Plaintiff shall appear for a neurosurgical examination to be performed by Paul E. Kaloostian, M.D., FAANS, FACS, a board-certified neurological surgeon. The examination shall take place on August 11, 2026, at 1:30 p.m., or on another mutually agreeable date before the discovery cutoff, at 960 E. Green Street, Suite 320, Pasadena, California 91106. The examination shall be limited to Plaintiff's lumbar spine and related lower-extremity neurological complaints and shall proceed in the manner, under the conditions, and within the scope set forth in Defendant's motion.

Defendant is ordered to give notice.

Case Number: 24NNCV03705    Hearing Date: August 7, 2026    Dept: P

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING IN PART AND DENYING IN PART PLAINTIFF’S MOTION FOR LEAVE TO CONDUCT FINANCIAL DISCOVERY

I.         INTRODUCTION

Plaintiffs Qing Ye Li, individually and as successor in interest to the Estate of Xingya Zhang, and Hui Hung Zhang (collectively, "Plaintiffs") filed this action on August 20, 2024, alleging causes of action for negligent security, negligence, battery, and elder abuse against Defendants Jade Court San Gabriel, LP ("Jade Court"), Starboard Seaview Manager, Inc. ("Starboard"), Jerry Uy ("Uy"), Mia Constance Williams, Jaumaul Rashaud Jackson, and Does 1 through 100. The action arises from the December 26, 2022, death of Xingya Zhang.

On July 13, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the instant motion for leave to conduct financial discovery pursuant to Civil Code section 3295, subdivision (c), as to Defendants Mashcole Property Management, Inc. ("Mashcole"), Uy, Jade Court, and Starboard. Jade Court and Starboard filed their opposition on July 20, 2026. Mashcole and Uy filed their opposition on July 27, 2026. Plaintiffs filed a consolidated reply on July 31, 2026.

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

Civil Code Section 3295 prevents pre-trial discovery of a party’s financial condition relevant to a Section 3294 claim for punitive damages. However, subsection (c) enables a plaintiff to move for a court order allowing the discovery “if the court finds, on the basis of the supporting and opposing affidavits presented, that the plaintiff has established that there is a substantial probability that the plaintiff will prevail on the claim pursuant to Section 3294.” (Civ. Code, § 3295(c).) “Such order shall not be considered to be a determination on the merits of the claim or any defense thereto and shall not be given in evidence or referred to at trial.” (Ibid.) “‘In this context, a “substantial probability” of prevailing on a claim for punitive damages means that it is “very likely” that the plaintiff will prevail on such a claim or there is a “‘strong likelihood’” that the plaintiff will prevail on such a claim.’ [Citation.]” (I-CA Enterprises, Inc. v. Palram Americas, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 257, 283.)

III.      ANALYSIS

Plaintiffs move for leave to conduct financial discovery pursuant to Civil Code section 3295, subdivision (c), as to Defendants Mashcole Property Management, Inc., Jerry Uy, Jade Court San Gabriel, LP, and Starboard Seaview Manager, Inc. Plaintiffs contend there is a substantial probability they will prevail on their punitive damages claims and therefore seek pretrial discovery of Defendants' financial condition. (Notice, p. 2.)

Specifically, Plaintiffs seek five years of financial information from Mashcole, Jade Court, and Starboard, including tax returns and related filings, business valuations, documents reflecting assets and liabilities, profit and loss statements, SEC filings, and the identities of the entities' accountant, tax manager, and wealth manager. As to Defendant Uy, Plaintiffs seek tax returns, W-2s, and 1099s. Plaintiffs state that any financial information produced would remain confidential pursuant to the parties' December 2025 protective order. (Id.)

Each Defendant opposes the motion. Because the parties raise distinct arguments as to the various defendants, the Court addresses those arguments separately below.

Maschole Property and Jerry Uy

Mashcole and Uy oppose the motion on several grounds. They argue: (1) the motion is premature because Plaintiffs will have an opportunity to obtain financial information if punitive damages are ultimately awarded; (2) this Court's prior ruling on summary judgment merely found a triable issue of fact regarding punitive damages and did not establish that Plaintiffs are substantially likely to prevail; (3) the evidence does not support an award of punitive damages as a matter of law; and (4) Plaintiffs have failed to satisfy the burden required under Civil Code section 3295, subdivision (c), to justify pretrial financial discovery.

Mashcole and Uy contend there is no prejudice in denying the motion because, should a jury ultimately find by clear and convincing evidence that punitive damages are warranted, Plaintiffs will have the opportunity to present evidence of Defendants' financial condition during the punitive damages phase of trial. They further argue that this Court's prior denial of summary adjudication merely established the existence of triable issues of fact regarding punitive damages, leaving that determination for the jury, and did not constitute a finding that Plaintiffs are substantially likely to prevail on their punitive damages claim. (Opposition, pp. 10-11.)

Finally, Mashcole and Uy argue Plaintiffs have not demonstrated the "substantial probability" required by Civil Code section 3295, subdivision (c). They contend the evidence, at most, supports claims of negligence, which is insufficient to warrant punitive damages, and fails to establish by the requisite showing that any corporate officer, director, or managing agent acted with malice, oppression, or fraud. Thus, they argue Plaintiffs have not met the statutory threshold for pretrial financial discovery. 

In reply, Plaintiffs argue Defendants apply the wrong legal standard. Plaintiffs contend Civil Code section 3295, subdivision (c), requires only a showing of a substantial probability of prevailing on their punitive damages claim, not proof by clear and convincing evidence. Plaintiffs further assert the motion is supported by counsel's declaration, certified deposition transcripts, and this Court's prior rulings denying summary adjudication on punitive damages, which they contend satisfy the statutory burden. (Reply, pp. 2-6.)

The Court finds Plaintiffs have met their burden under Civil Code section 3295, subdivision (c), as to Defendants Mashcole Property Management, Inc. and Jerry Uy.

Civil Code section 3295, subdivision (c), does not require Plaintiffs to establish their entitlement to punitive damages by clear and convincing evidence at this stage. Rather, the Court must determine, after weighing the parties' competing evidence, whether Plaintiffs have established a substantial probability they will prevail on their punitive damages claim. (See Jabro v. Superior Court (2002) 95 Cal.App.4th 754, 758; Kerner v. Superior Court (2012) 206 Cal.App.4th 84, 119.)

Here, Plaintiffs have submitted evidence, including deposition testimony and this Court's prior rulings, tending to show that Mashcole and Uy were aware of Jackson and Williams' threatening conduct before the homicide, yet allegedly failed to take adequate steps to remove them from the property or otherwise protect tenants. Plaintiffs further present evidence regarding Uy's qualifications, hiring, supervision, and the alleged ratification of his conduct following the incident. (Farrugia Decl., Exhs A-H; Oppositions to MSJ.) 

Specifically, Plaintiffs submit evidence that Jackson had a documented history of violent and disruptive incidents at the property dating back to at least June 2021, with additional reported incidents throughout 2021 and 2022, including shortly before the homicide. Plaintiffs also present evidence that these incidents were required to be reported under Mashcole's own policies and procedures (Farrugia Decl., ¶¶ 3-4, Exhs. A-B.) Plaintiffs also present evidence that Mashcole hired and retained Uy despite his lack of prior property management experience and that supervisory personnel, including Ayden Shabbir, remained employed following the incident. (Farrugia Decl., ¶ 5, Exhs. C-D.)

In addition, Plaintiffs present evidence that Mashcole's CEO, Craig Senia, and general manager Paul Belden testified that the homicide incident report would have been emailed to management, yet no incident report was retained in the produced property file. (Farrugia Decl., ¶¶ 10-12, Exhs. I-L.) 

While Defendants vigorously dispute these facts and contend the evidence demonstrates, at most, negligence, those arguments largely address the ultimate merits of Plaintiffs' punitive damages claim rather than the narrower inquiry presented by section 3295, subdivision (c).

After weighing the parties' submissions, the Court concludes Plaintiffs have demonstrated a substantial probability of prevailing on their punitive damages claim against Mashcole and Uy sufficient to permit limited pretrial financial discovery.

Jade Court San Gabriel, LP and Starboard Seaview Manager, Inc

Jade Court and Starboard oppose the motion, arguing Plaintiffs have failed to establish a substantial probability of prevailing on their punitive damages claims as to either entity. Starboard, in particular, contends it had no operational involvement with the subject property. According to Starboard, it did not own or manage the property, did not enter into the management agreement with Mashcole, did not employ, hire, or supervise Jerry Uy or other on-site personnel, and had no relationship with Jaumaul Jackson or Mia Williams. Rather, Starboard asserts its only connection to the property is its status as Jade Court's general partner. (Opposition., p. 5.)

Starboard further argues that its status as Jade Court's general partner does not, by itself, establish punitive liability under Civil Code section 3294. It contends Plaintiffs have presented no evidence that any Starboard officer, director, or managing agent had advance knowledge of Uy's alleged unfitness, authorized or ratified any wrongful conduct, or personally acted with oppression, fraud, or malice. Accordingly, Starboard argues Plaintiffs cannot demonstrate the substantial probability required under Civil Code section 3295, subdivision (c), to justify pretrial financial discovery. (Id. at pp. 5-7.)

Civil Code section 3295, subdivision (c), does not require Plaintiffs to establish their entitlement to punitive damages by clear and convincing evidence at this stage. Rather, the Court must determine, after weighing the parties' competing evidence, whether Plaintiffs have established a substantial probability they will prevail on their punitive damages claim. (See Jabro v. Superior Court (2002) 95 Cal.App.4th 754, 758; Kerner v. Superior Court (2012) 206 Cal.App.4th 84, 119.

As to Jade Court and Starboard, the Court reaches a different conclusion. The evidence indicates that Plaintiffs rely on an agency theory of liability as to these parties. Although Plaintiffs present evidence concerning the conduct of Mashcole and Uy, Civil Code section 3294, subdivision (b), requires more than a showing of vicarious liability to impose punitive damages on a corporate defendant. Rather, Plaintiffs must demonstrate a substantial probability of proving that an officer, director, or managing agent of the corporation had advance knowledge of the alleged unfitness, authorized or ratified the wrongful conduct, or was personally guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice. (College Hospital Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 723; Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (b).)

Here, Plaintiffs have not made that showing as to either Jade Court or Starboard. While Plaintiffs rely on evidence regarding the actions and knowledge of Mashcole personnel and Uy, they have not identified sufficient evidence demonstrating that an officer, director, or managing agent of Jade Court or Starboard possessed the requisite state of mind or otherwise satisfied the requirements of section 3294, subdivision (b). Nor is a corporate relationship with Mashcole, or Starboard's status as Jade Court's general partner, sufficient to establish a substantial probability of prevailing on punitive damages claim against those entities.

Given this, and after weighing the parties' submissions, the Court concludes Plaintiffs have not demonstrated a substantial probability of prevailing on their punitive damages claims against Jade Court or Starboard. The motion for leave to conduct financial discovery is therefore DENIED to those Defendants.

IV.       ORDER

            The motion is GRANTED as to Defendants Mashcole Property Management, Inc. and Jerry Uy. Plaintiffs may propound financial discovery limited to the following:

  1. Any and All tax filings, including returns in the last five years submitted to the State of California and Federal Internal Revenue Service and any responses or audits;
  2. Any and All business valuations performed in the last five years;
  3. Any and All documents evidencing the current assets of Defendant, including asset management documents indicating property holdings, asset valuation, and tax liabilities, outstanding liens, and mortgages or loans on property holdings or for the entity in the last five years;
  4. Any and All profit/loss statements in the last five years;
  5. Any and All documents filed with or received from the SEC in the last five years.
  6. From Jerry Uy: Any and All tax filings, W2s, 1099s submitted to the State of California and Federal Internal Revenue Service. 
  7.  And Defendants shall identify the accountant, tax manager and wealth manager related to any of the above companies. 

All documents produced pursuant to this Order shall remain subject to the parties' existing protective order.

The motion is DENIED as to Defendants Jade Court San Gabriel, LP and Starboard Seaview Manager, Inc.

Plaintiffs are to give notice.

Case Number: 25NNCV06136    Hearing Date: August 7, 2026    Dept: P

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING PLAINTIFF’S MOTIONS TO COMPEL REQUEST FOR PRODUCTION, SET ONE, DEEMING REQUESTS FOR ADMISSIONS ADMITTED and GRANTING MONETARY SANCTIONS

I.         INTRODUCTION

            On September 2, 2025, Plaintiff City of Pasadena (“Plaintiff” or “City”)  filed this action against Defendants Titan Disposal Corporation, Inc. and Bill Curren (collectively, “Defendants”) arising from a September 27, 2023, motor vehicle collision in which Defendant Bill Curren allegedly backed a skiploader into a City vehicle operated by Plaintiff's employee, William Crum. Plaintiff asserts causes of action for motor vehicle negligence and breach of contract, seeking to recover workers' compensation benefits paid on behalf of its employee pursuant to Labor Code section 3852 and damages allegedly recoverable under the parties' indemnity agreement.

On June 30, 2026, Plaintiff City of Pasadena filed four discovery motions: two motions to compel Defendants Titan Disposal Corporation, Inc. and Bill Curren to serve verified responses, without objections, to Requests for Production, Set One, and two motions to deem Requests for Admissions, Set One, admitted. No oppositions have been filed. Because the motions involve substantially identical facts, legal issues, and requested relief, the Court exercises its discretion to consolidate the motions for purposes of this ruling.

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

“Any party may obtain discovery . . . by a written request that any other party to the action admit the genuineness of specified documents, or the truth of specified matters of fact, opinion relating to fact, or application of law to fact. A request for admission may relate to a matter that is in controversy between the parties.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.010.) “Within 30 days after service of requests for admission, the party to whom the requests are directed shall serve the original of the response to them on the requesting party, and a copy of the response on all other parties who have appeared . . ..” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.250(a).) 

If a party to whom request for admissions are served fails to provide a timely response, the party to whom the request was directed waives any objections, including based on privilege or the work product doctrine. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280(a).) The requesting party can move for an order that the genuineness of any documents and the truth of any matters specified in the request be deemed admitted, as well as for monetary sanctions. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280(b).) The court shall issue this order unless the party to whom the request was made serves a response in substantial compliance prior to the hearing on the motion. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280(c).) 

A propounding party may move to compel responses to requests for production of documents where the responding party fails to provide responses. (CCP § 2031.300.) The responding party must provide responses within thirty days after the demand is served. (CCP § 2031.030(c)(2) & (3).) The responding party waives all objections, including privilege and work product, by failing to timely respond to requests for production of documents. (CCP § 2031.300.).

            III.      ANALYSIS

Request for Admission - Set One

Plaintiff moves to deem Requests for Admissions, Set One, admitted as to Defendants Titan Disposal Corporation, Inc. and Bill Curren on the ground that neither Defendant served timely responses.

The record reflects that Plaintiff served Requests for Admissions, Set One, on November 25, 2025. (Smith Decl., ¶ 2.) After Defendants failed to timely respond, Plaintiff sent a demand letter on January 9, 2026. Defense counsel represented that verified responses, without objections, would be served by January 23, 2026. (Smith Decl., ¶ 3.) When no responses were received, Plaintiff again contacted defense counsel on February 2, 2026, advising that motions would be filed if verified responses were not served by February 12, 2026. (Smith Decl., ¶ 4.) Despite these efforts, Defendants did not serve responses or request any additional extension of time before Plaintiff filed the instant motions. (Smith Decl., ¶ 5.) Defendants have not opposed the motions.

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.280, subdivision (c), the Court must deem the matters admitted unless, before the hearing, the responding party serves responses in substantial compliance with the Code. As no opposition has been filed and there is no indication that Defendants have served code-compliant responses before the hearing, the Court GRANTS Plaintiff's motions and deems admitted all matters contained in Requests for Admissions, Set One, as to Defendants Titan Disposal Corporation, Inc. and Bill Curren.

Request for Production - Set One (RFP)

Plaintiff moves to compel Defendants Titan Disposal Corporation, Inc. and Bill Curren to serve verified responses, without objections, to Request for Production, Set One.

Here, Plaintiff served RFP, Set One, on Defendants Titan Disposal Corporation, Inc. and Bill Curren on November 25, 2025. (Smith Decl., ¶ 2.) After neither Defendant timely responded, Plaintiff sent a demand letter on January 9, 2026. Defense counsel represented that verified responses, without objections, would be served by January 23, 2026. (Smith Decl., ¶ 3.) When no responses were received, Plaintiff again contacted defense counsel on February 2, 2026, advising that motions to compel would be filed if verified responses were not served by February 12, 2026. (Smith Decl., ¶ 4.) As of the filing of these motions, Defendants had not served any responses or requested any further extension of time. (Smith Decl., ¶ 5.) Defendants have not opposed the instant motions.

Based on the foregoing, the Court GRANTS the motions to compel responses to RFPs. Defendants Titan Disposal Corporation, Inc. and Bill Curren shall each serve verified responses, without objections, to Plaintiff's Request for Production, Set One, within 30 days of notice of the Court's order.

IV.       SANCTIONS

Monetary sanctions are mandatory against the party, the attorney, or both whose failure to serve timely responses necessitated the motions, unless the Court finds the party acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of sanctions unjust. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2031.300, subd. (c), 2033.280, subd. (c); Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1348(a).) Additionally, Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.030 authorizes the Court to impose monetary sanctions for misuse of the discovery process, including failing to respond to an authorized method of discovery. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2023.010, subd. (d), 2023.030.)

Plaintiff seeks monetary sanctions of $1,250 for each of the four motions, for a total of $5,000, against Defendants Titan Disposal Corporation, Inc. and Bill Curren. (Smith Decl., ¶ 6.) The motions are unopposed and involve nearly identical factual and legal issues. Therefore, the Court finds the requested amount excessive and reduces the reasonable time to 5 hours at counsel's hourly rate of $200, for a total award of $1000 for all four motions.

Thus, the Court GRANTS $1000 in monetary sanctions against Defendant Titan Disposal Corporation, Inc, and Bill Curren, jointly and severally. Monetary sanctions shall be payable within 30 days of notice of the Court's order. 

           V.       CONCLUSION AND ORDER

            The Court GRANTS Plaintiff’s motions to compel RFPs- Set One, and motions to deem RFA’s admitted. Defendants are ordered to respond to RFPs within 30 days of this order.

The Court further GRANTS monetary sanctions in the amount of $1000.00 against Defendant Titan Disposal Corporation, Inc, and Bill Curren, jointly and severally. Monetary sanctions shall be payable within 30 days’ notice of this order.

Defendants are to give notice.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING DEFENDANT’S MOTIONS TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES TO FORM INTERROGATORIES, SPECIAL INTERROGATORIES, REQUESTS FOR PRODUCTION, SET ONE and GRANTING MONETARY SANCTIONS

I.         INTRODUCTION

            Plaintiff Adolfo Galvez (“Plaintiff”) brings this employment action against Defendant Inline Translation Services, Inc. (“Defendant”), asserting causes of action for (1) Disability Discrimination, (2) Failure to Provide Reasonable Accommodation, (3) Failure to Engage in the Interactive Process, and (4) Wrongful Termination in Violation of Public Policy. Plaintiff alleges Defendant discriminated against him based on a disability and terminated his employment after failing to accommodate his condition.

 

On May 6, 2026, Defendant filed three motions to compel further responses, with requests for monetary sanctions, to Requests for Production (“RFP”), Special Interrogatories (“SPROGs”), and Form Interrogatories (“FROGs”), Set One. The Court addresses each motion below. Plaintiff filed oppositions on July 27, 2026, and Defendant filed replies on July 31, 2026.

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

A motion to compel a further response to interrogatories or production of documents must be noticed within 45 days of the service of the verified response, or any supplemental verified response, or on or before any specific later date to which the propounding party and the responding party have agreed in writing. (Cal. Code of Civ. Proc. §§ 2030.300(c), 2031.310(c); see also Sexton v. Superior Court (1997) 58 Cal.App.4th 1403, 1409; Vidal Sassoon, Inc. v. Superior Court (1983) 147 Cal.App.3d 681, 685).) Otherwise, the propounding party waives any right to compel further responses. (Id.) The 45-day time limit is mandatory and jurisdictional. (Sexton, supra,58 Cal. App. 4th at 1410.) 

C.C.P. §2033.290 provides, in pertinent part, as follows: 

(a) On receipt of a response to requests for admissions, the party requesting admissions may move for an order compelling a further response if that party deems that either or both of the following apply: 

(1) An answer to a particular request is evasive or incomplete. 

(2) An objection to a particular request is without merit or too general

III.      ANALYSIS

SPROGS, Set One

SPROGS Nos 1-12:

This set of requests seek information regarding Plaintiff's post-termination job search, prospective employers, offers of employment, subsequent employment, and efforts to mitigate damages.

The Court finds the requested information relevant to Plaintiff's claims for lost wages and Defendant's mitigation defense. The Court is not persuaded that Plaintiff's overbreadth and privacy objections preclude discovery. The interrogatories seek business contact information for prospective and subsequent employers, which does not implicate a sufficient privacy interest to outweigh Defendant's need for discovery. Plaintiff argues the interrogatories improperly seek personal contact information of non-parties. (Opposition., pp. 4-5.) However, Plaintiff cites no authority demonstrating that the business contact information sought is protected from discovery or otherwise not discoverable under the circumstances presented. Plaintiff also represents that he intends to serve supplemental responses. However, the promise of future supplementation does not render the motion moot.

Thus, Plaintiff shall serve verified, code-compliant further responses, without objection, within 30 days.

SPROGS Nos 13-16:

            This set of requests seek information regarding Plaintiff's medical and mental health providers and treatment for the depression and other conditions Plaintiff alleges constitute the basis for his disability discrimination and failure to accommodate claims.

However, the Court agrees with Plaintiff that, as drafted, the interrogatories are not reasonably limited to the relevant time period and instead seek Plaintiff's entire pre-termination treatment history without any temporal limitation tied to the claims at issue. (Opposition., p. 5.)

The Court GRANTS the motion in part. Plaintiff shall serve verified further responses identifying medical and mental health providers and treatment relating to the conditions placed at issue for the time period commencing one year prior to Plaintiff’s termination to the present..

FROGS Set one

This set of requests seek information regarding Plaintiff's medical and mental health conditions, treatment, and damages arising from the conditions Plaintiff alleges form the basis of his disability discrimination and failure to accommodate claims. To the extent Plaintiff's supplemental responses remain incomplete regarding treatment for conditions allegedly exacerbated by the conduct at issue or damages sought, further responses are required, limited to the time frame set forth above..

Defendant seeks monetary sanctions totaling $7,080, consisting of $1,560 for the RFP motion (Aroustamian Decl. (RFP), ¶ 5), $2,460 for the FROGs motion (Aroustamian Decl. (FROGs), ¶ 5), and $3,060 for the SPROGS motion (Aroustamian Decl. (SPROGs), ¶ 5).

[TENTATIVE] ORDER DENYING CROSS-COMPLAINANT ANGEL QUINONEZ’S MOTION TO VACATE ORDER COMPELLING ARBITRATION and GRANTING MONETARY SANCTIONS

I.         INTRODUCTION

            On April 28, 2025, Plaintiff Pablo Martinez, Trustee of the Patron Trust dated December 9, 2004 (“Pablo Martinez”) filed this unlawful detainer action against Defendants Angel Quinonez and Karla Martinez, husband and wife, based on a 3-day Notice to Pay Rent or Quit. On May 13, 2025, Defendants Angel Quinonez and Karla Martinez (“Defendants”) filed an answer to the UD complaint.

On June 2, 2025, Plaintiff represented to the Court that possession of the rental property was no longer at issue. Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure §1952.3, the Court reclassified the action as an ordinary civil limited jurisdiction case. On July 31, 2025, Defendants filed a cross-complaint (“Cross-Complainants” with respect to Cross-Complaint) against Plaintiff Pablo Martinez, an individual, and Equity Smart Home Loans, Inc. (“Equity”) (collectively “Cross-Defendants”) seeking damages in excess of $25,000. Cross-Complainants allege (1) breach of oral contract; (2) breach of implied contract; (3) breach of implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing; (4) promissory estoppel; (5) quantum meruit; (6) accounting; (7) breach of fiduciary duty; (8) constructive fraud; (9) violation of Business and Professions Code section 17200 (“Cross-Complaint filed on July 31, 2025”).

Cross-Complainants allege that Quinonez entered into a partnership or joint venture with Cross Defendant Equity whereby (1) Quinonez’s entity, Klovus, would be created as the Real Estate Division for Equity under the name Klovus Realty; (2) Equity would hire Quinonez as the Real Estate Division Manager for Klovus Realty; (3) Quinonez would be paid 30% of profits of Klovus Realty according to the prior month’s Profit and Loss Statement; (4) Equity would hire Karla Martinez to serve as Real Estate Operations Manager for Klovus Realty; (5) Karla Martinez would be entitled to continue to operate Klovus for her own third party transactions; and (6) Klovus Realty would operate its own bank account separate from Equity. Cross Complainants allege Cross-Defendants breached the parties’ partnership agreement by failing to pay Quinonez 30% of the profits. Cross-Complainants also allege Cross-Defendants committed multiple Labor Code violations against Karla Martinez during her employment as a Real Estate Operations Manager, including misclassifying her as an exempt employee and failing to provide her with meal and rest breaks. The action was reclassified as an unlimited action due to the Cross-Complaint filed on July 31, 2025. Notice of Reclassification was filed on August 1, 2025. On September 2, 2025, Cross-Defendants filed an answer to the Cross-Complaint filed on July 31, 2025. On September 2, 2025, Plaintiff Pablo Martinez filed a cross-complaint against Defendants alleging breach of contract, unfair business practices and intentional interference with prospective economic advantage. On January 5, 2026, the Court dismissed Plaintiff Pablo Martinez’s cross-complaint, inquiring as to why he filed a cross-complaint to add additional causes of action instead of amending the complaint.

On March 20, 2026, the Court granted in part Cross-Defendants' motion to compel arbitration, compelling Angel Quinonez's cross-claims to arbitration while denying the motion as to Karla Martinez's cross-claims and staying the action as to Quinonez.

On July 13, 2026, Quinonez filed the instant motion to vacate the order compelling arbitration and lift the stay pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.97. Cross-Defendants subsequently filed an untimely Opposition, which the Court has considered, and Quinonez filed a reply.

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure Section 1281.97(a)(1) states: 

             In an employment or consumer arbitration that requires, either expressly or through application of state or federal law or the rules of the arbitration administrator, the drafting party to pay certain fees and costs before the arbitration can proceed, if the fees or costs to initiate an arbitration proceeding are not paid within 30 days after the due date, the drafting party is in material breach of the arbitration agreement, is in default of the arbitration, and waives its right to compel arbitration under Section 1281.2. 

 

(Code Civ. Proc. § 1281.97, subd. (a)(1).) 

“If the drafting party materially breaches the arbitration agreement and is in default under subdivision (a), the employee or consumer may…withdraw the claim from arbitration and proceed in a court of appropriate jurisdiction.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 1281.97, subd. (b)(1).)

            III.      ANALYSIS

            Quinonez moves to vacate the Court’s March 20, 2026, order compelling arbitration, lift the stay of the action under Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.97, and award sanctions. (Motion, p. 2.) Quinonez argues that, after the Court compelled arbitration, he initiated the arbitration and paid his portion of the filing fee. JAMS then notified Cross-Defendants that their remaining $1,600 filing fee was due upon receipt. Despite the initial invoice and a final payment request, Cross-Defendants did not pay the fee, and JAMS closed the arbitration file on June 24, 2026, before an arbitrator was appointed. (Motion, pp. 2-5.)

Quinonez contends that section 1281.97 imposes mandatory consequences when the drafting party fails to pay the fees required to initiate arbitration within 30 days of the due date. Specifically, he argues that Cross-Defendants’ failure to timely pay constitutes a material breach of the arbitration agreement, places them in default of the arbitration, and waives their right to compel arbitration. He further argues that section 1281.97 gives him the right to withdraw from arbitration and proceed in court, and that sanctions are mandatory under section 1281.99. (Motion, pp. 5-6.)

Cross-Defendants filed an untimely opposition. Nevertheless, the Court has considered the Opposition because Quinonez had an opportunity to file, and did file, a reply addressing the arguments raised therein. Therefore, the Court finds Quinonez has suffered no prejudice, and the Opposition will be considered on its merits.

Cross-Defendants oppose the motion, arguing that their failure to timely pay the arbitration initiation fee resulted from excusable administrative error rather than an intentional effort to delay the arbitration. Specifically, they contend: (1) counsel timely forwarded the JAMS invoice to the client for payment and reasonably believed the payment was being processed through the client's normal administrative channels; (2) subsequent JAMS notices were inadvertently diverted by Google's email filtering system into an unmonitored administrative folder, preventing counsel from learning of the payment deadline until after JAMS had closed the file; and (3) once counsel discovered the issue, they acted promptly by directing the client to submit payment. According to Cross-Defendants, the client attempted payment within approximately 25 minutes of receiving notice, but the JAMS payment portal rejected the transaction because it indicated the invoice had already been paid. The client then immediately contacted the JAMS case manager seeking manual assistance to complete the payment. (Opposition., pp. 3-5.)

Cross-Defendants maintain they did not act in bad faith and argue the remaining delay was attributable to JAMS's response time rather than any lack of diligence on their part. They further contend Quinonez was aware of their payment efforts before filing the present motion. (Id, at p.7.)

Cross-Defendants argue that Hohenshelt v. Superior Court (2025) 18 Cal.5th 310 controls and makes clear that a technical payment delay does not automatically result in forfeiture of the right to arbitrate. (Id. at p.2.) They contend equitable principles, including Civil Code sections 1511 and 3275, excuse their delayed payment because the delay resulted from good-faith mistake, email routing issues, and a JAMS payment portal problem, rather than bad faith. They further argue Quinonez suffered no prejudice because the arbitration funds remain secure and the arbitration could be reopened. (Id. at pp. 8-13.)

In reply, Quinonez argues Cross-Defendants' explanations for both the late Opposition and the missed arbitration payment are inconsistent and unsupported by the record. Quinonez contends that the evidence demonstrates that Cross-Defendants failed to pay the initiation fee within the statutory deadline, that the first documented payment attempt occurred only after JAMS had already closed the file, and that Hohenshelt is distinguishable because it interpreted Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.98 rather than section 1281.97, which governs initiation fees. (Reply, pp. 2-7.)

The primary issue, as the Court sees it, is whether Cross-Defendants’ failure to timely pay the arbitration initiation fee constitutes a waiver of the right to compel arbitration under Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.97, or whether equitable relief is available,

As a preliminary matter, although Quinonez argues Cross-Defendants waived their right to arbitration by failing to timely pay the required initiation fees, the Court does not find the record demonstrates that Cross-Defendants acted intentionally, deliberately, or in bad faith. Rather, this appears to be an isolated administrative error. Once Cross-Defendants became aware that the arbitration file had been closed, they attempted to submit payment, and when that effort was unsuccessful, promptly contacted the JAMS case manager to resolve the issue. The Court has no basis to conclude that Cross-Defendants were attempting to evade their payment obligations or intentionally delay the arbitration proceedings. Moreover, the Court finds it unlikely that Cross-Defendants would intentionally forfeit the very forum they successfully sought through a contested motion to compel arbitration. That litigation history weighs against a finding of bad faith.

Next, the Court is not persuaded by Quinonez's argument that Hohenshelt is limited solely to Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.98 and has no application to section 1281.97. Although Hohenshelt v. Superior Court (2025) 18 Cal.5th 310 arose under section 1281.98, the Supreme Court emphasized that the arbitration payment statutes must be harmonized with California's longstanding equitable principles governing relief from forfeiture. (Hohenshelt, supra, 18 Cal.5th at p. 332.)  The Court finds those equitable principles are equally applicable here, where the record demonstrates the delayed payment resulted from good-faith mistake or excusable neglect rather than intentional or bad-faith conduct. 

Based on the foregoing, the Court finds that Cross-Defendants have established that the delayed payment resulted from excusable neglect rather than intentional refusal to fund the arbitration or a deliberate effort to delay the proceedings. Consistent with the equitable principles recognized in Hohenshelt, the Court concludes that Cross-Defendants have not forfeited their contractual right to arbitrate. 

Thus, the Court DENIES the motion to vacate. 

Sanctions

Although the Court finds Cross-Defendants are entitled to equitable relief from forfeiture under Hohenshelt v. Superior Court (2025) 18 Cal.5th 310, that does not necessarily preclude an award of compensatory monetary sanctions. Hohenshelt recognizes that equitable relief from forfeiture may coexist with the compensatory remedies available under Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.99. (Hohenshelt, supra, 18 Cal.5th at pp. 339-340.)

Here, Quinonez seeks $8,410.00 in attorney's fees and costs incurred as a result of Cross-Defendants' delayed payment. Based on the evidence submitted, the Court finds the requested fees excessive. The Court instead awards attorney's fees for 8 hours at the requested hourly rate of $450, plus the $400 JAMS filing fee, for a total award of $4,000.00. The Court finds this amount reasonable and directly attributable to Cross-Defendants' delayed payment of the arbitration initiation fee. (Kelly Decl., ¶¶ 12-14.) Accordingly, the Court awards monetary sanctions in the amount of $4,000.00, jointly and severally, against Cross-Defendants pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.99.

IV.       CONCLUSION AND ORDER

            The Court DENIES Quinonez’s motion to vacate order compelling arbitration and to lift stay.

The Court GRANTS monetary sanctions in the amount of $4,000.00, jointly and severally, payable within 30 days’ notice of this order.

Case Number: 26NNCV01286    Hearing Date: August 7, 2026    Dept: P

[TENTATIVE] ORDER SUSTAINING DEFENDANT’S DEMURRER WITH 30 DAYS’ LEAVE TO AMEND

I.         INTRODUCTION

            On February 20, 2026, Plaintiffs Laura Lasky, Ilana Mayes, Michael Cali, and Jeffrey James (collectively, "Plaintiffs") filed the instant action against Defendant 5312 Radford Ave 29, LLC ("Defendant") seeking declaratory relief concerning Plaintiffs' alleged rights under the Los Angeles Rent Stabilization Ordinance ("RSO") to reoccupy their former rental units following the property's renovation.

On May 13, 2026, Defendant filed the instant demurrer. On July 27, 2026, Plaintiffs filed an opposition. Defendant filed a reply on July 31, 2026.

Meet and confer

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, subdivision (a), the demurring party is required to meet and confer with the party who filed the pleading at least five days before the responsive pleading is due. This must occur in person, telephone or by video conference with the aim of resolving any objections raised in the demurrer. Additionally, the demurring party must file and serve a declaration detailing the meet and confer efforts. It should be noted, however, that the failure to meet and confer is not itself grounds for sustaining or overruling a demurrer or granting or denying a motion to strike. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 430.41, subd. (a)(4), 435.5, subd. (a)(4).)

Here, Defendant's counsel declares that, on April 8, 2026, counsel emailed Plaintiffs' counsel identifying the bases for the proposed demurrer and requesting to meet and confer. After receiving no response, counsel sent a follow-up email on April 13, 2026, but again received no response. (Goldstein Decl., ¶ 2.) 

The Court finds these efforts insufficient to satisfy the meet and confer requirements of Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41. Nevertheless, the Court elects to consider the merits of the demurrer.

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

“[A] demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the allegations in a complaint.” (Lewis v. Safeway, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 385, 388.) A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. (See Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994 [in ruling on a demurrer, a court may not consider declarations, matters not subject to judicial notice, or documents not accepted for the truth of their contents].) For purposes of ruling on a demurrer, all facts pleaded in a complaint are assumed to be true, but the reviewing court does not assume the truth of conclusions of law. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hosp. Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 967.) 

CCP section 430.10(f) provides that a pleading is uncertain if it is ambiguous and unintelligible. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10(f).) “A demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Khoury v. Maly’s of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.) “A demurrer for uncertainty will be sustained only where the complaint is so bad that defendant cannot reasonably respond—i.e., he or she cannot reasonably determine what issues must be admitted or denied, or what counts or claims are directed against him or her.” (Weil & Brown, Civil Procedure Before Trial (The Rutter Group) § 7:85 (emphasis in original).) “The objection of uncertainty does not go to the failure to allege sufficient facts.” (Brea v. McGlashan (1934) 3 Cal.App.2d 454, 459.) “It goes to the doubt as to what the pleader means by the facts alleged.” (Id.) “Such a demurrer should not be sustained where the allegations of the complaint are sufficiently clear to apprise the defendant of the issues which he is to meet.” (People v. Lim (1941) 18 Cal.2d 872, 882.)  

Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (See Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349 [court shall not “sustain a demurrer without leave to amend if there is any reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment”]; Kong v. City of Hawaiian Gardens Redevelopment Agency (2002) 108 Cal.App.4th 1028, 1037 [“A demurrer should not be sustained without leave to amend if the complaint, liberally construed, can state a cause of action under any theory or if there is a reasonable possibility the defect can be cured by amendment.”]; Vaccaro v. Kaiman (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 761, 768 [“When the defect which justifies striking a complaint is capable of cure, the court should allow leave to amend.”].) The burden is on the complainant to show the Court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

III.      ANALYSIS

            Defendant demurs to the Complaint pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivisions (e) and (f), on the grounds that the Complaint fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action for declaratory relief and is uncertain. (Notice, p. 1.) The Court address each below.

Failure to state a cause of action

Defendant contends the Complaint fails to allege an actual, present controversy because Plaintiffs seek a declaration based on speculative future events, namely, Defendant's potential future leasing decisions once renovations are complete. Defendant characterizes the Complaint as seeking declaratory relief based on hypothetical future conduct rather than an existing dispute concerning the parties' present legal rights and obligations. (Demurrer, pp. 4-7.)

“A complaint for declaratory relief must demonstrate: (1) a proper subject of declaratory relief, and (2) an actual controversy involving justiciable questions relating to the rights or obligations of a party.” (Brownfield v. Daniel Freeman Marina Hospital (1989) 208 Cal.App.3d 405, 410.) “The ‘proper subjects’ of declaratory relief are set forth in Code of Civil Procedure section 1060 and other statutes.” (Ibid.) “The ‘actual controversy’ requirement concerns the existence of present controversy relating to the legal rights and duties of the respective parties pursuant to contract (Code Civ. Proc., § 1060), statute or order.” (Ibid.) 

Whether an actual controversy exists depends, in part, on whether the dispute is ripe for judicial determination. A controversy is ripe when the facts have sufficiently congealed to permit an intelligent and useful decision, rather than an advisory opinion based upon hypothetical facts. (Pacific Legal Foundation v. California Coastal Com. (1982) 33 Cal.3d 158, 170.) In determining ripeness, courts consider "(1) whether the dispute is sufficiently concrete to make declaratory relief appropriate, and (2) whether withholding judicial consideration will impose a hardship on the parties." (Stonehouse Homes LLC v. City of Sierra Madre (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 531, 540.) A dispute is not sufficiently concrete where resolution would require the court to speculate regarding hypothetical future events. (Dominguez v. Bonta (2022) 87 Cal.App.5th 389, 410.)

Here, as currently pleaded, the Complaint does not allege a sufficiently concrete controversy to support declaratory relief. Plaintiffs allege that they have not been evicted from the subject property, have not surrendered possession of their units, and therefore continue to possess rights under the RSO. (Complaint, ¶ 20.) Plaintiffs further allege Defendant is repairing the subject property, the units are expected to become available within the coming weeks or months, and Defendant has failed to acknowledge Plaintiffs' claimed rights to possession and other rights under the RSO. (Complaint, ¶¶ 9, 29.) Based on these allegations, Plaintiffs seek a declaration that Defendant must offer them the first right to re-rent their respective apartments at LAHD-approved adjusted rental rates. (Complaint, ¶ 21.)

However, the Complaint does not allege that Defendant has actually denied Plaintiffs' claimed rights or otherwise indicated it does not intend to comply with the RSO. At this point, the dispute has not sufficiently crystallized because it remains unclear whether Defendant intends to recognize Plaintiffs' claimed rights or whether Defendant will take a position contrary to the RSO. Without such allegations, the Court is left to speculate about a future dispute that may never materialize. Plaintiff’s declaratory relief claim essentially seeks an order that Defendant follow the law and obey the RSO.[1]

That said, the Court cannot conclude that amendment would be futile. Plaintiffs may be able to allege additional facts showing Defendant has affirmatively denied their claimed rights under the RSO or otherwise indicated it will not honor those rights once the units become available. Such allegations would present a sufficiently concrete controversy for declaratory relief.  Therefore, the demurrer is SUSTAINED with 30 days’ leave to amend.

The Court orders the parties to meet and confer within 10 calendar days so that Defendant may explain its position on Plaintiff’s claimed rights under the RSO.

Uncertainty 

As to uncertainty, Defendant argues the Complaint fails to identify the specific legal rights and duties at issue or any present controversy between the parties. Instead, Defendant contends Plaintiffs merely allege that Defendant has "failed to acknowledge Plaintiffs' rights to possession and other rights pursuant to the RSO," without identifying any present violation of law or concrete dispute. (Demurrer, pp. 7-8.)

The Court finds that the Complaint is not uncertain. A demurrer based on uncertainty only applies where the complaint is so bad that a defendant cannot reasonably respond—i.e., cannot reasonably determine what issues must be admitted or denied, or what counts or claims are directed against him or her.  (Khoury v. Maly’s of Calif., Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.) Here, although the Complaint could be pleaded with greater specificity, it sufficiently apprises Defendant of the nature of the dispute. The Complaint identifies the parties' alleged dispute and seeks a declaration regarding those rights. Indeed, Defendant's demurrer demonstrates that it understands the nature of Plaintiffs' claim and is able to respond to it.

Thus, the Court OVERRULES the demurrer for uncertainty.

IV.       ORDER

            The Court SUSTAINS the demurrer with 30 days’ leave to amend. The Court orders the parties to meet and confer with 10 calendar days so Defendant can clarify its position on Plaintiff’s claimed rights under the RSO.

Defendant is to give notice.
