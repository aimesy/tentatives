I.            PLAINTIFFS’ MOTION FOR SUMMARY ADJUDICATION

Plaintiffs James Matyas (“Matyas”) and Eugene Robison Company, Inc. dba Oxford Risk Management & Insurance Services (“Oxford”) (collectively “Plaintiffs”) filed this motion for summary adjudication on “specific issues of liability.”

Defendants Weaver & Associates, Inc. (“Weaver & Associates”), Ralph Weaver, Matthew Weaver, Dana Dattola, and Denise Weaver (collectively, “Defendants”) filed an opposition brief on August 26, 2026, arguing that Plaintiffs’ motion improperly sought summary adjudication of “issues of liability” while reserving the issue of damages for trial. (Paramount Petroleum Corp. v. Superior Court (2014) 227 Cal.App.4th 226, 242-244.)

In a reply brief filed on September 4, 2026, Plaintiffs conceded that parts of their motion were improper because it would not completely dispose of a cause of action but maintained that the motion properly sought to summarily adjudicate Defendants’ affirmative defense of waiver and the existence of Weaver & Associates’ contractual duties.

Plaintiffs’ motion for summary adjudication is DENIED as procedurally defective in several respects. First, “[i] f summary adjudication is sought, … the specific … affirmative defense … or issues of duty must be stated specifically in the notice of motion and be repeated, verbatim, in the separate statement of undisputed material facts.” (California Rules of Court, rule 3.1350, subds. (b), (d).) Plaintiffs’ separate statement is 24 pages long and lists 12 facts without differentiating the numerous issues sought to be summarily adjudicated and the corresponding facts that support each issue. It also would be improper for Plaintiffs to claim that all 12 facts support each of the 8 issues identified in the notice of motion when some of them are clearly not material.

Second, the separate statement does not properly cite to the evidence because it does not include reference to the “exhibit, title, page, and line numbers” or follow the format for separate statements. (Id., subds. (d)(3), (h); see also Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (b)(1) [each of the material facts shall be followed by a reference to the supporting evidence].) These problems are compounded by the fact that Plaintiffs’ supporting evidence consists of the Declaration of Neva Cirkveni, a declaration filed in two volumes consisting of 750 and 795 pages without electronic bookmarks as required by CRC 3.1110, subd. (f)(4). “Judges are not like pigs, hunting for truffles buried in” the record. [Citation].” (Albrechtsen v. Bd. of Regents of Univ. of Wisconsin Sys. (2002) 309 F.3d 433, 436.)

In light of the foregoing, the motion for summary adjudication is DENIED in its entirety.

II.          MOTION FOR LEAVE TO WITHDRAW AND AMEND RESPONSES TO PLAINTIFF’S REQUESTS FOR ADMISSION, SET ONE

A.          Factual and Procedural Background

On July 23, Defendants filed this motion for leave to withdraw their responses to plaintiff James Matyas’ Requests for Admission, Set One, as follows:

1.   Denise Weaver’s responses to RFA Nos. 4 and 10

2.   Matthew Weaver’s response to RFA No. 1

3.   Ralph Weaver’s response to RFA No. 10

4.   Weaver & Associates’ response to RFA Nos. 2, 3, 10, and 14.

Plaintiffs filed an opposition brief on August 10, 2026.

Defendants filed a reply brief on August 17, 2026.

          On August 24, 2026, the Court continued the hearing for the parties to submit supplemental briefing and set an Order to Show Cause why Defendants and defense counsel should not be sanctioned for previously misrepresenting the nature of Defendants’ discovery responses.

B.          Legal Standard

“Any matter admitted in response to a request for admission is “conclusively established against the party making the admission in the pending action, unless the court has permitted withdrawal or amendment of that admission.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.410, subd. (a).) An admission may be withdrawn or amended upon noticed motion with leave of court. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.300, subd. (a).) The court may permit withdrawal or amendment of an admission only if it determines that the admission was the result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect, and that the party who obtained the admission will not be substantially prejudiced in maintaining that party’s action or defense on the merits. (Id., subd. (b).)

C.          Discussion

On July 15, 2026, the Court heard Plaintiff’s motion for evidentiary sanctions and struck 8 of Defendants’ supplemental responses to Plaintiff’s RFAs. These supplemental responses were served on March 5, 2026. The Court’s reasoning was that admissions could not be withdrawn or amended without leave of Court, which Defendants had not obtained.

The Court’s ruling was premised on Defendants’ representations that the initial responses served on January 14, 2026 (“Initial Responses”) were verified. However, recent briefing from both parties confirms that they, in fact, were not verified. Accordingly, the Initial Responses were tantamount to no responses at all and the motion is DENIED as moot. (Appleton v. Superior Court (1988) 206 Cal.App.3d 632, 636.) The Court further revisits its July 15, 2026, order and vacates the portion striking Defendants’ supplemental responses as being served without leave of Court.

III.        ORDER TO SHOW CAUSE RE: SANCTIONS FOR MISREPRESENTATIONS IN OPPOSING PLAINTIFF’S MOTION FOR EVIDENTIARY SANCTIONS

In opposing Plaintiff’s motion for evidentiary sanctions, Mr. Hayden declared under penalty of perjury that “[a]ll Defendants served verified responses to Plaintiff’s written discovery, Set One (Form Interrogatories, Special Interrogatories, Requests for Admission, and Requests for Production of Documents), on or about January 14, 2026. (6/17/2026 Declaration of Herbert J. Hayden, ¶ 3.) Defendants also argued against evidentiary sanctions claiming repeatedly that they obeyed the Court’s January 28, 2026, Order to serve verified responses by doing so on March 5, 2026. (6/17/2026 Opposition to Plaintiff’s Motion for Evidentiary Sanctions, pp. 3-4, 7.)

Both statements were misrepresentations because, as stated above, the Initial Responses were unverified and the Court’s ruling on January 28, 2026, ordered Defendants to serve verified, objection-free responses within 20 days, which Defendants did not do. Defendants concede that these statements were inaccurate, but defense counsel argues that they were the result of oversight and not intended to mislead the Court. Defendants also argue that the misrepresentations did not affect the outcome on Plaintiff’s motion for sanctions because the Court ultimately found Plaintiff’s arguments to be meritless.

Given the inadvertent nature of defense counsel’s error and the fact that the misrepresentations primarily served to avoid imposing sanctions on Plaintiff for unsuccessfully bringing his motion, the OSC is DISCHARGED.

IV.        CONCLUSION

Plaintiffs’ motion for summary adjudication is DENIED.

Defendants’ motion for leave to amend or withdraw their responses to requests for admission is DENIED as moot.

The OSC re: Sanctions is DISCHARGED.

Defendants are ordered to give notice.

I.      INTRODUCTION

         On January 27, 2026, plaintiffs Rhonda Rogers, Raven Rogers, Lawrence Rogers, and Pamela Rogers (collectively, “Plaintiffs”) filed this action as the surviving heirs of Ronald Rogers (“Rogers”). Plaintiffs bring claims for: (1) negligence wrongful death, (2) negligence survival, and (3) elder abuse – neglect. Plaintiffs allege that defendants Optivest Properties, LLC (“Optivest”) and Allen Avenue Self-Storage, LLC (“Allen Avenue”) (collectively, “Defendants”) own, operate, manage, and maintain a self-storage facility located at 234 Allen Avenue, Pasadena, California 91106 known as Allen Avenue Self-Storage (“Property”). Rogers executed a rental agreement with Defendants for a storage unit and rented the unit for an extended period of years. (Compl., ¶ 10.) On or about May 27, 2025, Rogers, who was 80 years old at the time, was discovered deceased, halfway inside and halfway outside of his storage unit. (Compl., ¶ 12.) Plaintiffs allege that Defendants were aware Rogers was living in the storage facility, that Defendants’ employees were aware of his possession of and even assisted him in moving “‘living items’” into the storage unit, that employees were aware of Rogers’s “attempt to mitigate the extreme heat,” despite cutting off air circulation and bathroom access in the facility each day, that Defendants knowingly and “actively participated in creating the danger by controlling essential utilities and profiting from the arrangement by continuing to collect rent,” (Compl., ¶ 64)

On April 13, 2026, Defendants filed a demurrer to all three causes of action in the Complaint and a motion to strike Plaintiffs’ requests for punitive damages and heightened remedies, including attorneys’ fees, allowed under Welfare and Institutions Code section 15657.

Plaintiffs filed opposition briefs on September 1, 2026.

Defendants filed reply briefs on September 8, 2026.

II.     LEGAL STANDARD

A.   Demurrer

A demurrer may be brought if insufficient facts are stated to support the cause of action asserted. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (e).) A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack; or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

A demurrer may also be brought on the grounds that it is uncertain, but such a demurrer will be sustained only where the complaint is so bad that the defendant cannot reasonably respond. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (f).) “A demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Khoury v. Maly’s of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.)

Where the complaint contains substantial factual allegations sufficiently apprising defendant of the issues it is being asked to meet, a demurrer for uncertainty will be overruled or plaintiff will be given leave to amend. (Williams v. Beechnut Nutrition Corp. (1986) 185 Cal.App.3d 135, 139, fn. 2.)

B.   Motion to Strike

          Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading may serve and file a notice of motion to strike the whole or any part thereof. (Code Civ. Proc., § 435, subd. (b)(1).) The court may, upon a motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a); Stafford v. Shultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782 [“Matter in a pleading which is not essential to the claim is surplusage; probative facts are surplusage and may be stricken out or disregarded”].) An immaterial or irrelevant allegation is one that is not essential to the statement of a claim or defense; is neither pertinent to nor supported by an otherwise sufficient claim or defense; or a demand for judgment requesting relief not supported by the allegations of the complaint. (Code Civ. Proc., § 431.10, subd. (b).) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437.)

III.    DISCUSSION

A.   Request for Judicial Notice

“Judicial notice may not be taken of any matter unless authorized or required by law.” (Evid.Code, § 450.) Matters that are subject to judicial notice are listed in Evidence Code sections 451 and 452.

Defendants request the Court take judicial notice of the Self-Storage Rental Agreement (“Rental Agreement”) entered into between Rogers and Optivest as manager for Allen Avenue. Defendants argue that the Rental Agreement is judicially noticeable because it was referenced by Plaintiffs in the Complaint and that its existence and terms are not reasonably subject to dispute, possibly implying that the request is brought subject to Evidence Code section 452(h), which allows for judicial notice to be taken of “[f]acts and propositions that are not reasonably subject to dispute and are capable of immediate and accurate determination by resort to sources of reasonably indisputable accuracy.” (RJN, pp. 2-3; Compl., ¶¶ 10-11.)

The cases cited by Defendants are inapposite because the cases involving contracts that were “referenced” in the complaint either attached the contract as an exhibit or cited portions of the contract. Defendants also rely on cases that involve recorded documents or government documents that are considered “[o]fficial acts of the legislative, executive, and judicial departments” of the federal and state government. Furthermore, case law holds that “the existence of a contract between private parties cannot be established by judicial notice under Evidence Code section 452, subdivision (h).” (The Travelers Indemnity Co. of Connecticut v. Navigators Specialty Ins. Co. (2021) 70 Cal.App.5th 341, 354, citing Gould v. Maryland Sound Industries, Inc. (1995) 31 Cal.App.4th 1137, 1145,

          Accordingly, the Rental Agreement cannot be judicially noticed and consists of extrinsic evidence that cannot be considered.

B.   Wrongful Death and Survival Claims for Negligence

The basic elements of a negligence claim are: a duty on the part of defendant toward plaintiff; defendant's breach of that duty; and harm to plaintiff caused by the breach. (Kesner v. Sup.Ct. (Pneumo Abex, LLC) (2016) 1 Cal.5th 1132, 1142.)

Defendants argue the Rental Agreement precludes Plaintiffs’ claims for negligence because the self-storage unit and the facility on a whole were not to be used as a residence, it was not maintained as a living space, it was accepted in “as is” condition; and any death or injury arising from such prohibited use was released. Defendants also argue that the Rental Agreement makes the Complaint ambiguous and unintelligible.

Since the Rental Agreement is not judicially noticeable, the demurrer on these grounds is OVERRULED.

C.   Elder Abuse Claims

A claim for elder abuse based on a theory of neglect requires the existence of “a robust caretaking or custodial relationship—that is, a relationship where a certain party has assumed a significant measure of responsibility for attending to one or more of an elder's basic needs that an able-bodied and fully competent adult would ordinarily be capable of managing without assistance.” (Winn v. Pioneer Medical Group, Inc. (2016) 63 Cal.4th 148, 158.) The plaintiff must also show that the defendant acted with recklessness, malice, oppression, or fraud in the commission of the abuse. (Welf. & Inst. Code, § 15657.) Recklessness, unlike negligence, involves more than “inadvertence, incompetence, unskillfulness, or a failure to take precautions” but rather rises to the level of a “conscious choice of a course of action ... with knowledge of the serious danger to others involved in it.” (Doe v. Kachru (2025) 115 Cal.App.5th 175, 214-215.) When an elder abuse claim is brought against a corporate defendant, the plaintiff must further allege that an officer, director or managing agent authorized or ratified the abuse or neglect. (Welf. & Inst. Code, § 15657, subd. (c); Civ. Code, § 3294.)

Here, Plaintiffs allege that Defendants assumed a custodial relationship with Rogers “[b]y knowingly permitting and facilitating the full-time residency of a vulnerable, 80-year-old man [Rogers] in a storage unit they controlled, and by controlling his access to essential services such as power, water, and building access.) (Compl., ¶ 60.) Plaintiffs allege Defendants acted with recklessness, oppression, and malice because they knew of Rogers’s living situation and unsafe conditions but consciously disregarded his rights and safety. (Compl., ¶ 64.) Evidence of Defendants’ knowledge include Defendants’ employees’ assistance with moving “living items” into the unit and an employee’s awareness that Rogers would habitually unplug a hallway fan in order to try and mitigate the backdraft that would cause high temperatures in the storage unit. (Compl., ¶¶ 20-21.) Plaintiffs contend Defendants not only failed to protect Rogers, but also “actively participated in creating the danger by controlling essential utilities and profiting from the arrangement by continuing to collect rent.” (Compl., ¶ 64.)

Here, Plaintiffs’ claim for elder abuse fails against Defendants, who are corporate entities. Plaintiffs do not allege that an officer, director or managing agent authorized or ratified the abuse or neglect. (Welf. & Inst. Code, § 15657.) Plaintiffs also do not allege facts showing that Defendants assumed a custodial relationship. “The distinctive relationship contemplated by the [Elder Abuse] Act entails more than casual or limited interactions.” (Winn, supra, 63 Cal.4th at p. 158.) The Complaint is devoid of allegations that Defendants explicitly assumed ongoing caretaking responsibilities, notwithstanding its continued collection of rent for the storage unit. (Kruthanooch v. Glendale Adventist Medical Center (2022) 83 Cal.App.5th 1109, 1130.)

          Therefore, the demurrer to the Third Cause of Action is SUSTAINED.

D.  Motion to Strike

As the Court sustains the demurrer to the Third Cause of Action, the motion to strike Paragraphs 63, 64, and 66 is moot. The motion to strike Plaintiffs’ prayer for punitive damages is GRANTED because Plaintiffs do not allege that any act of malice, fraud, or oppression was committed or ratified by an officer, managing agent, or director.

IV.    CONCLUSION

Defendants’ demurrer to the First and Second Causes of Action is OVERRULED.

Defendants’ demurrer to the Third Cause of Action is SUSTAINED with 20 days’ leave to amend.

Moving party to give notice.
