BACKGROUND

 

This is a consumer class action.  On January 28, 2022, Plaintiff Brinan Weeks, (“Plaintiff” or “Weeks”), individually and on behalf of all others similarly situated, commenced the instant action against Defendant Interactive Life Forms, LLC (“Interactive”) and Does 1 through 100 asserting claims for: (1) negligent misrepresentation (Civil Code §§ 1709, 1710, & 1572), (2) injunctive relief pursuant to the Consumer Legal Remedies Act (Civil Code § 1750, et seq.), (3) violation of the False Advertising Law (Business and Professions Code § 17500, et seq.), (4) breach of express warranty (Commercial Code § 2314), (5) breach of implied warranty (Commercial Code § 2314), and (6) violation of the Unfair Competition Law (Business and Professions Code § 17200, et seq.).  According to the Complaint, Interactive manufactures a variety of sexual products for men, called sleeves, under the “Fleshlight” brand.  (Complaint, ¶1.)  Plaintiff alleges that, in its advertising, Interactive emphasizes that its Fleshlight products, particularly the “Stamina Training Unit” (“STU”), are designed, in part, to improve and enhance the user’s sexual performance.  (Id. at ¶¶2-5.)  Plaintiff alleges that he relied on Defendant’s representations and purchased the STU, but after several months of use did not experience any improvement in his sexual performance.  (Id. at ¶¶6-7.)

On April 25, 2022, the Court granted Plaintiff leave to file an amended complaint pursuant to stipulation of the parties.  On April 28, 2022, Plaintiff filed a First Amended Class Action Complaint (“FAC”).

On July 22, 2022, the Court approved the parties’ Stipulation and Protective Order.

On July 26, 2022, the Court denied Defendant’s Motion for Order Compelling Arbitration and Staying Action.  Defendant filed an appeal of the Court’s July 26, 2022, ruling.  On November 8, 2022, the Court stayed the action pending the appeal, pursuant to the stipulation of the parties.  On March 25, 2024, the Court of Appeal affirmed the denial of Defendant’s Motion.  On April 17, 2024, Plaintiff’s Motion to Lift the Stay was granted.

On August 23, 2024, the Court denied Defendant’s Motion for Protective Order.

On October 7, 2024, Defendant filed an Answer to the FAC.

On July 23, 2025, the parties participated in a mediation presided over by the Honorable Carl J. West (Retired) and reached a settlement.  (Russell Decl., ¶4.)  A fully executed copy of the Class Action Settlement (“Settlement Agreement” or “Agreement”) is attached to counsel Russell’s declaration as Exhibit A and a redlined version of the LASC Model Agreement is attached as Exhibit B.  (Id. at ¶4, Exs. A-B.)

Now before the Court is the Motion for Preliminary Approval of Class Action Settlement (“MPA”), filed on October 3, 2025.

ANALYSIS OF SETTLEMENT AGREEMENT

 

1.     Does a presumption of fairness exist?

1.   Was the settlement reached through arm’s-length bargaining? Yes.  On July 23, 2025, the parties participated in a mediation presided over by the Honorable Carl J. West (Retired) and reached a settlement.  (Russell Decl., ¶4.)  A fully executed copy of the Class Action Settlement (“Settlement Agreement” or “Agreement”) is attached to counsel Russell’s declaration as Exhibit A and a redlined version of the LASC Model Agreement is attached as Exhibit B.  (Id. at ¶4, Exs. A-B.)

2.   Were investigation and discovery sufficient to allow counsel and the court to act intelligently?  Yes.  Plaintiff’s counsel represents that, prior to mediation, Plaintiff obtained, through formal discovery (i) over 115,000 pages of documents produced in response to 74 requests for production, (ii) responses and supplemental responses to 47 requests for admission and (iii) responses and supplemental responses to 50 interrogatories.  (Russell Decl., ¶2.) [Provide information about the specific documents reviewed prior to settlement.]

3.   Is counsel experienced in similar litigation?  Yes. Class Counsel are experienced in class action litigation, including consumer class actions.  (Russell Decl., ¶¶21-25.)

Provide a sworn declaration from counsel from Qureshi Law PC demonstrating that they are experienced in consumer class action litigation.

4.   What percentage of the class has objected?  This cannot be determined until the fairness hearing.  See Weil & Brown, Cal. Practice Guide: Civil Procedure Before Trial (The Rutter Group 2014) ¶ 14:139.18, (“Should the court receive objections to the proposed settlement, it will consider and either sustain or overrule them at the fairness hearing.”).

 

            CONCLUSION:  The settlement is entitled to a presumption of fairness.

 

2.     Is the settlement fair, adequate, and reasonable?

1.     Strength of Plaintiff’s case.  “The most important factor is the strength of the case for plaintiff on the merits, balanced against the amount offered in settlement.”  (Kullar v. Foot Locker Retail, Inc. (2008) 168 Cal.App.4th 116, 130.)

            According to Plaintiff’s counsel, the maximum possible recovery under a total refund theory would be a return of $69.95 to each class member who purchased the STU.  (Russell Decl., ¶10.)  Depending on the number of final class members (36,000-42,000), total recovery would range from $2,518,200 to $2,937,900.  (Ibid.)

What is the value of the injunctive relief obtained?

        2.   Risk, expense, complexity and likely duration of further litigation.  Given the nature of the class claims, the case is likely to be expensive and lengthy to try.  Procedural hurdles (e.g., motion practice and appeals) are also likely to prolong the litigation as well as any recovery by the class members.

3.   Risk of maintaining class action status through trial.  Even if a class is certified, there is always a risk of decertification.  (See Weinstat v. Dentsply Intern., Inc. (2010) 180 Cal.App.4th 1213, 1226 (“Our Supreme Court has recognized that trial courts should retain some flexibility in conducting class actions, which means, under suitable circumstances, entertaining successive motions on certification if the court subsequently discovers that the propriety of a class action is not appropriate.”).)

4.   Amount offered in settlement.

The non-reversionary settlement of $425,000, after reduction by the requested deductions, leaves $192,847.50 to be divided among approximately 36,000 to 42,000 Class Members.  The resulting payment will average $4.59 to $5.36.  This is approximately 6.6-7.7% of the price each Class Member paid for the STU.  Depending on the number of Valid Claims submitted, Class Members may receive up to $30, which is approximately 42.9% of the price paid for the STU.

Alternatively, the gross settlement amount of $425,000 represents 14.5-16.9% of the maximum possible recovery under a total refund theory ($2,518,200 to $2,937,900).

The Court finds that the monetary benefits obtained through the settlement are within the ballpark of reasonableness.

5.   Extent of discovery completed and stage of the proceedings.  As indicated above, the Court requests additional information about the discovery conducted prior to settlement.

6.   Experience and views of counsel.  The settlement was negotiated and endorsed by Class Counsel who, as indicated above, is experienced in class action litigation, including consumer class actions.

7.   Presence of a governmental participant.  This factor is not applicable here.

8.   Reaction of the class members to the proposed settlement. The class members’ reactions will not be known until they receive notice and are afforded an opportunity to object, opt-out and/or submit claim forms.  This factor becomes relevant during the fairness hearing.

 

      CONCLUSION:  The settlement can be preliminarily deemed “fair, adequate, and reasonable.”

4.     May conditional class certification be granted?

1.     Standards

A detailed analysis of the elements required for class certification is not required, but it is advisable to review each element when a class is being conditionally certified (Amchem Products, Inc. v. Winsor (1997) 521 U.S. 620, 622-627.)  The trial court can appropriately utilize a different standard to determine the propriety of a settlement class as opposed to a litigation class certification.  Specifically, a lesser standard of scrutiny is used for settlement cases.  (Dunk at 1807, fn 19.)  Finally, the Court is under no “ironclad requirement” to conduct an evidentiary hearing to consider whether the prerequisites for class certification have been satisfied. (Wershba at 240.)

2.     Analysis

a.     Numerosity.  The Parties have determined that the size of the Class is between 36,000 and 42,000 members.  (MPA at 17:11-12.)  This element is met.

b.     Ascertainability.  The proposed class is defined above.  The class definition is “precise, objective and presently ascertainable.”  (Sevidal v. Target Corp. (2010) 189 Cal.App.4th 905, 919.)  Here, the great majority of class members purchased the STU online from ILF for which ILF still maintains records. For the remaining class members, identification can be achieved through a standard claims process requiring a sworn affidavit or declaration.  (MPA at 17:20-22.)

c.      Community of interest.  “The community of interest requirement involves three factors: ‘(1) predominant common questions of law or fact; (2) class representatives with claims or defenses typical of the class; and (3) class representatives who can adequately represent the class.’”  (Linder v. Thrifty Oil Co. (2000) 23 Cal.4th 429, 435.)

Discuss specific facts showing that there are predominant common questions of law or fact in the instant action.

As to typicality, Plaintiff alleges that the factual and legal issues apply equally to Plaintiff and Class Members.  Just like Plaintiff, Class Members’ claims arise from the purchase of the STU and Plaintiff and all Class Members were exposed to the representations on the sales path of ILF’s and third-party reseller websites and on ILF’s packaging.  (MPA at 18:3-6.)

As to adequacy, Plaintiff is an adequate Class Representative because (1) he has no conflicts of interest with other Class Members, (2) understands his responsibilities as a Class Representatives, and (3) has actively participated in the litigation.  (Russell Decl., ¶26; see Declaration of Brinan Weeks.)

d.     Adequacy of class counsel.  As indicated above, Class Counsel has shown experience in class action litigation, including consumer class actions.

e.     Superiority.  Given the relatively small size of the individual claims, a class action appears to be superior to separate actions by the class members.

 

      CONCLUSION:  The class may be conditionally certified since the prerequisites of class certification have been satisfied.

Class Notice will be distributed in English.  (¶1.9)  “Because the ILF website and packaging is in English, the Parties agree that providing notice in another language is not necessary.”  (Russell Decl., ¶19.)

 

Address the following issues in the Class notice:

1)     Correct the Class Period dates on the first page of the Notice.

2)     The following sentences may confuse recipients of the Class Notice: (1) “You don’t have to do anything to participate in the proposed Settlement and be eligible for an Individual Class Payment;” and (2) “To receive an Individual Class Payment, you must timely submit a Claim Form, as described below.”  (Notice, p. 1.)  Ensure that the information is presented in a clear manner to notify recipients that they must submit a claim form to receive the Individual Class Payment.

Notice of changes to the date, time, and location of the Final Approval Hearing and the Final Judgment will be posted on the Settlement Administrator’s website.  (¶8.8.1)

3.     Cost of class notice.  As indicated above, settlement administration costs are estimated not to exceed $45,500. Prior to the time of the final fairness hearing, the administrator must submit a declaration attesting to the total costs incurred and anticipated to be incurred to finalize the settlement for approval by the Court.

 

6.     Attorney fees and costs

CRC rule 3.769(b) states: “Any agreement, express or implied, that has been entered into with respect to the payment of attorney fees or the submission of an application for the approval of attorney fees must be set forth in full in any application for approval of the dismissal or settlement of an action that has been certified as a class action.”

            Ultimately, the award of attorney fees is made by the court at the fairness hearing, using the lodestar method with a multiplier, if appropriate.  (PLCM Group, Inc. v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095-1096; Ramos v. Countrywide Home Loans, Inc. (2000) 82 Cal.App.4th 615, 625-626; Ketchum III v. Moses (2000) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132-1136.)  Despite any agreement by the parties to the contrary, “the court ha[s] an independent right and responsibility to review the attorney fee provision of the settlement agreement and award only so much as it determined reasonable.” (Garabedian v. Los Angeles Cellular Telephone Company (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 123, 128.)

            The question of whether Class Counsel is entitled to $141,652.50 (33.33% of GSA) in attorney fees will be addressed at the fairness hearing when class counsel brings a noticed motion for attorney fees. Class counsel must provide the court with billing information so that it can properly apply the lodestar method and must indicate what multiplier (if applicable) is being sought as to each counsel.

Class Counsel should also be prepared to justify the costs sought (capped at $35,000) by detailing how they were incurred.

 

7.     Incentive Awards

The Settlement Agreement provides for enhancement awards of up to $10,000 to the named Plaintiff. In connection with the final fairness hearing, the named Plaintiff must submit a declaration attesting to why he should be entitled to an enhancement award in the proposed amount.  The named Plaintiffs must explain why he “should be compensated for the expense or risk [he] has incurred in conferring a benefit on other members of the class.”  (Clark v. American Residential Services LLC (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 785, 806.)  Trial courts should not sanction enhancement awards of thousands of dollars with “nothing more than pro forma claims as to ‘countless’ hours expended, ‘potential stigma’ and ‘potential risk.’ Significantly more specificity, in the form of quantification of time and effort expended on the litigation, and in the form of reasoned explanation of financial or other risks incurred by the named plaintiff, is required in order for the trial court to conclude that an enhancement was ‘necessary to induce [the named plaintiff] to participate in the suit . . . .’”  (Id. at 806-807, italics and ellipsis in original.)  The Court will decide the issue of the enhancement awards at the time of final approval.
