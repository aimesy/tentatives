Case Number: 23SMCV05462    Hearing Date: July 9, 2026    Dept: P

Tentative Ruling

Meryle Moroney v. Binko Corp., et al., Case No. 23SMCV05462

Defendants’ Demurrer and Motion to Strike

Hearing Date: July 9, 2026

Background

Plaintiff Meryle Moroney sues Defendants Bink Corp., Serenity Care Health Corporation; Serenity Care Health Group; Specialized Community Healthcare Company; and Bioseh Ogbechie for wrongful death, elder abuse, and negligence. Moroney alleges that Defendants owned and operated the Bentley Manor assisted living facility, located at 3425 McLaughlin Ave., Los Angeles, CA 90066. (Compalint, ¶ 4.) Moroney alleges that her father, Carl Moroney, was a resident at Bently Manor and that Defendants’ negligence caused her father’s death on November 22, 2023. (Complaint, ¶ 23.)

On November 20, 2024, Moroney filed the complaint.

On October 11, 2024, Moroney entered default against Binko Corp., Serenity Care Health Corporation, Specialized Community Healthcare Company, Bioseh Ogbechie, and Serenity Care Health Group.

On March 19, 2025, the Court granted default judgment for Moroney for $10,001,503.08.

On August 4, 2025, Defendants Serenity Care Health Group and Bioseh Ogbechie (“Ogbechie Defendants”) moved to set aside default. On December 8, 2025, the Court set aside the default judgment against the Ogbechie Defendants.

On February 23, 2026, the Ogbechie Defendants filed a demurrer, arguing that Moroney failed to state a claim for wrongful death, elder abuse, and negligence because the Ogbechie Defendants do not own or operate Bently Manor, and that Moroney failed to state a claim for a “survival action” because it is not a recognized cause of action. On June 25, 2026, Moroney filed opposition, arguing that the complaint alleges sufficient facts to state claims for wrongful death, elder abuse, and negligence. On July 1, 2026, the Ogbechie Defendants replied. 

Arguments

The Ogbechie Defendants primarily contend that Moroney does not state a claim against them because they are not the licensed operators of Bentley Manor and therefore do not owe her or the decedent a duty of care. They also argue that Moroney does not have standing to sue for wrongful death and negligence on behalf of her deceased father because she did not file a declaration or affidavit under Code Civ. Proc. § 377.32 stating that she is her father’s successor in interest alongside the complaint. (Demurrer, p. 3.) Next, the Ogbechie Defendants argue that Moroney’s claims for wrongful death and negligence fail to state a claim and are uncertain because she does not allege specific facts showing that the Ogbechie Defendants caused her father’s death or owed her father a duty of care. (Demurrer, p. 5.) Then, the Ogbechie Defendants argue that Moroney’s claim for elder abuse fails to state a claim and is uncertain because it does not plead elder abuse with specificity. The Ogbechie Defendants argue that, as a statutory cause of action, elder abuse must be plead with specificity, citing Carter v. Prime Healthcare Paradise Valley (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 396, 410. (Demurrer, p. 7.) The Ogbechie Defendants argue that the complaint does not allege malice, oppression, or fraud, or involvement by a managing agent as required for an elder abuse claim. (Id. at pp. 8-9.) Finally, the Ogbechie Defendants argue that the complaint fails to state a claim for punitive damages and ask that the Court sustain their demurrer without leave to amend. (Id. at pp. 10-11.)

In opposition, Moroney argues that she inadvertently did not attach the Code Civ. Proc. § 377.32 declaration, and that she has filed a notice of errata correcting the issue. (Opposition, p. 4.) Moroney argues that the Ogbechie Defendants seek an unnecessary level of detail in their motion, and that the complaint alleges negligence and causation with sufficient specificity. (Id. at p. 5.) Next, Moroney argues that the complaint alleges neglect within the meaning of the Elder Abuse Act, relying on Delaney v. Baker (1999) 20 Cal.4th 23, Covenant Care Inc. v. Superior Court (2004) 32 Cal.4th 771, and Sababin v. Superior Court (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 81. (Id. at pp. 6-8.) Moroney argues that Carter v. Prime Healthcare Paradise Valley, supra, supports her claims and that she has alleged elder abuse with sufficient specificity. (Id. at pp. 8-9.) Moroney contends that the Ogbechie’s arguments that the complaint fails to state claims against them because they do not own Bentley Manor are arguments on the merits that should be rejected at the demurrer stage because Moroney brings these claims against defendants as alter egos of each other. (Id. at p. 10.) Finally, Moroney argues that her survival claim is properly pled and requests leave to amend if any portion of the Ogbechie Defendants’ demurrer is sustained. (Id. at pp. 12-13.)

In reply, the Ogbechie Defendants argue that Moroney does not address the core of their demurrer, which is that they are not the licensed operator of Bentley Manor. (Reply, p. 1.) The Ogbechie Defendants then argue that Moroney’s belated filing of the § 377.32 declaration does not moot the issue but admits that the complaint was deficient as filed. (Ibid.) The Ogbechie Defendants argue that Moroney’s causation allegations in support of her negligence claim are conclusory. (Id. at p. 2.) As to Moroney’s claim for elder abuse, the Obgechie Defendants distinguish Plaintiff’s caselaw on the grounds that the Ogbechie Defendants are not the operators of Bentley Manor. (Id. at p. 4.) The Ogbechie Defendants also argue that the complaint does not allege recklessness or corporate authorization/ratification of Bentley Manor’s alleged negligence. (Id. at p. 5.) Finally, the Ogbechie Defendants request that the demurrer be sustained without leave to amend. (Id. at p. 6-7.)

Request for Judicial Notice

The Ogbechie Defendants request notice of “Ex. A: California Department of Social Services Search Result for Bentley Manor showing Licensee as “Specialized Community Healthcare Company” (last visit February 23, 2026), available at < https://www.ccld.dss.ca.gov/carefacilitysearch/FacDetail/198320301>” (RJN, p. 1.)

Notice is GRANTED under Evid. Code § 452(h).

Demurrer

In a demurrer proceeding, the defects in the complaint must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) “A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Ibid.) The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Id.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.) However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245).

Before filing a demurrer, parties must meet and confer in person or by phone. (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41.)

Analysis

Procedure

a.      Meet and Confer

The parties satisfied the meet and confer requirement by discussing this demurrer by phone on February 5, 2026. (Jauregui Decl., ¶ 2.)

b.      Code Civ. Proc. § 377.32

A declaration or affidavit under Code Civ. Proc. § 377.32 must be filed alongside a complaint brought by or continued by a decedent’s successor in interest. (Code Civ. Proc. § 377.32(a).) Failure to do so could subject the action to a plea in abatement. (Aghaian v. Minassian (2021) 64 Cal. App. 5th 603, 614.) However, the Ogbechie Defendants cite no authority for the proposition that a demurrer to a survival action be sustained for failure to attach the required affidavit where the Plaintiff has later filed the declaration. (cf. Aghaian v. Minassian, supra, 64 Cal.App.5th  [“Section 377.32, however, ‘does not require that the affidavit be filed as a condition precedent to commencing or continuing the action’”].) Moroney has filed the affidavit, correcting the defect. Failure to file the affidavit earlier is not grounds for sustaining the Ogbechie Defendants’ demurrer. 

Moroney’s Complaint

a.      Moroney’s alter ego allegations

The Ogbechie Defendant’s primary argument rests on the fact that neither Serentiy Care Health Group nor Bioseh Ogbechie are the registered licensed operators of Bentley Manor. (RJN, Exhibit A.) Defaulted defendant Specialized Community Healthcare Company is the licensed operator. In opposition, Moroney argues that she brings this action against the Ogbechie Defendants as Specialized Community Healthcare Company’s alter egos. (Opposition, p. 10.)

To plead alter ego liability, a complaint must allege facts showing “that there is such a unity of interest and ownership that the individuality of the one corporation and the owner or owners of its stock has ceased, and further, that the observance of the fiction of separate existence would under the circumstances sanction a fraud or promote injustice.” (Hennessy’s Tavern, Inc. v. American Air Filter Co. (1988) 204, Cal.App.3d 1351, 1358.)

Moroney alleges facts showing a unity of interest between Specialized Community Healthcare Company and Serenity Care Health Group: she alleges that Defendants Specialized Community Healthcare Company and Serenity Care Health Group are staffed by the same employees, managers, and officers, and that Arlene Media served as secretary of both Specialized Community Healthcare Company and Serenity Care Health Group, and that both companies have the same registered agent. (Complaint, ¶ 6.) Moroney also alleges that Defendant Bioseh Ogbechie is the registered CEO of Serenity Care Health Group, which lists Bentley Manor as one of its facilities online. (Ibid.) Moroney alleges that there is no true separation between the companies, and that Defendant Bioseh Ogbechie is the head of all the corporate defendants, including Specialized Community Healthcare Company. (Id. at ¶ 9.) Taken together, the complaint alleges a unity of interest. Moroney also alleges that injustice would result if the Defendants are treated as separate entities because they are playing “a shell game” to preclude Moroney’s recovery. (Id. at ¶ 8.) Moroney properly alleges that Specialized Community Healthcare Company is an alter ego of the Ogbechie Defendants.

All Defendants alleged to be alter egos of each other are liable for claims stated against one alter ego entity. (See Hennesy’s Tavern Inc. v. American Air Filter Co., supra, 204 Cal.App.3d at 1358 [“An alter ego defendant has no separate primary liability to the plaintiff. Rather, plaintiff's claim against the alter ego defendant is identical with that claimed by plaintiff against the already-named defendant”].) The fact that the Ogbechie Defendants are not the registered operators of Bentley Manor is therefore not grounds for sustaining a demurrer.

b.      Wrongful Death and Negligence

The elements of a claim for wrongful death are (1) a wrongful act or neglect on the part of one or more persons; (2) causing the death of another. (Norgart v. Upjohn Co. (1999) 21 Cal.4th 383, 404 (citing Code Civ. Proc., § 377.60).) In any wrongful death action resulting from negligence, the complaint “must contain allegations as to all the elements of actionable negligence.” (Jacoves v. United Merchandising Corp. (1992) 9 Cal. App.4th 88, 105.) To state a claim for negligence, a plaintiff must allege that (1) the defendant owed the plaintiff a duty of care, (2) the defendant breached that duty, and (3) the breach proximately caused the plaintiff’s damages or injuries. (Peredia v. HR Mobile Services, Inc. (2018) 25 Cal.App.5th 680, 687.)

Moroney alleges that her father, Carl Moroney, was a resident at Bentley Manor between March, 2022, and his death on November 22, 2022. (Complaint, ¶ 15.) Moroney alleges that Defendants had care or custody of her father.  (Id. at ¶ 16.) Moroney alleges that she agreed to pay an additional fee so that Bentley Manor staff would stay in her father’s room at night to ensure his safety and provide any needed assistance. (Id. at ¶ 21.) Moroney alleges that despite Defendants’ obligations to her father, he developed a sore on his buttocks, a severe scabies infestation, developed a respiratory illness, and ultimately died because of his delayed care. (Complaint, ¶¶ 27, 32 [“Defendants’ breaches of care were the proximate cause of the DECEDENT’s injuries, death, and damages”].)

The Ogbechie Defendants argue that these allegations do not establish that the Ogbechie Defendants caused Moroney’s father’s death, nor do they establish that the Ogbechie Defendants owed the decedent a duty of care. (Demurrer, p. 5.) The Court’s finding that the complaint adequately alleges that the Ogbechie Defendants are Specialized Community Healthcare Company’s alter egos disposes of these arguments. Moroney’s allegations against the Ogbechie Defendants’ alter egos are made equally against the Ogbechie Defendants. Additionally, Moroney’s causation allegations are sufficient because there is no heightened pleading standard for negligence claims. (See Complaint, ¶¶ 27, 32 [“Defendants’ breaches of care were the proximate cause of the DECEDENT’s injuries, death, and damages”].)

The Complaint states a claim for wrongful death because it alleges duty, breach, causation, damages, and Mr. Moroney’s death. (See Complaint, ¶¶ 15, 21, 27, 30, 32.) The Ogbechie Defendants’ demurrer to Moroneys’ Claims for Wrongful Death and Negligence are OVERRULED.

c.       Elder Abuse

To state a claim for neglect under the elder abuse act, “the plaintiff must allege (and ultimately prove by clear and convincing evidence) acts establishing that the defendant: (1) had responsibility for meeting the basic needs of the elder or dependent adult, such as nutrition, hydration, hygiene or medical care [citation]; (2) knew of conditions that made the elder or dependent adult unable to provide for his or her own basic needs [citation]; and (3) denied or withheld goods or services necessary to meet the elder or dependent adult's basic needs, either with knowledge that injury was substantially certain to befall the elder or dependent adult (if the plaintiff alleges oppression, fraud or malice) or with conscious disregard of the high probability of such injury (if the plaintiff alleges recklessness) [citation]. The plaintiff must also allege (and ultimately prove by clear and convincing evidence) that the neglect caused the elder or dependent adult to suffer physical harm, pain or mental suffering. [citation] Finally, the facts constituting the neglect and establishing the causal link between the neglect and the injury “must be pleaded with particularity,” in accordance with the pleading rules governing statutory claims. [citation].” (Carter v. Prime Healthcare Paradise Valley, LLC (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 396,406-407.)

Professional negligence is not actionable under this provision of the elder abuse act. Neglect is distinguished from professional negligence by recklessness or malice: “if the neglect is ‘reckless,’ or done with ‘oppression, fraud or malice,’ then the action falls within the scope of section 15657 and as such cannot be considered simply ‘based on … professional negligence’ within the meaning of section 15656.2.” (Delaney v. Baker (1999) 20 Cal.4th 23, 35 [upholding a jury’s determination that a nursing home’s failure to prevent a patient’s death from complications arising from extremely severe pressure ulcers was actionable neglect and not just professional negligence because there was substantial evidence that the facility knew about her condition and failed to act].)

A plaintiff must allege specifically what the care custodian did to cause the elder individual harm. Allegations that a patient was admitted to the hospital three times, first for chest pain, then for pneumonia, sepsis, and pressure ulcers, and finally for complications related to the pneumonia did not state a claim for neglect under the elder abuse act where they did not allege that the hospital denied services or withheld necessary treatment. (Carter v. Prime Healthcare Paradise Valley, LLC, supra, 198 Cal.App.4th at 407-408.) This case suffers from the same defect.

Here, Moroney does not allege what treatment Bentley Manor withheld or what services it denied her father. With one exception, Moroney’s allegations of neglect are entirely conclusory. (See Complaint, ¶¶ 35-42.) Moroney does allege that the Ogbechie Defendants denied her father overnight supervision but does not allege how this caused Mr. Moroney’s death. (Complaint, ¶ 24.) Nor is causation obvious from the complaint’s description of Mr. Moroney’s ailments-- neither pressure ulcers, scabies, or respiratory illnesses is obviously attributable to the defendants’ failure to stay with Mr. Moroney overnight. (Complaint, ¶¶ 27-28.) The complaint does not meet the particularity standard for pleading causation on an action for neglect under the elder abuse act.

The Ogbechie Defendants also contend that this is a professional negligence action. Moroney alleges that the Ogbichie Defendants acted with recklessness, oppression, and/or malice. (Complaint, ¶ 41.) Though the allegation is conclusory, it would be premature to describe the alleged neglect as only professional negligence without further detail.

The Ogbichie Defendant’s demurrer to Moroney’s claim for elder abuse is SUSTAINED.

d.      Survival

Moroney does not contend that her survival claim is a separate cause of action. A plaintiff may bring a survival claim where the plaintiff alleges that they are the decedent’s successor-in-interest and where the plaintiff alleges that the decedent suffered injuries before his death as a result of the defendants’ conduct. (Quiroz v. Seventh Ave. Center (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 1256, fn. 28.). Moroney alleges those facts. (See Complaint, ¶ 27.)

The Ogbechie Defendants’ demurrer to Plaintiff’s survival claim is OVERRULED.

e.       Leave to Amend

Since the Court sustains the Ogbechie’s demurrer to Plaintiff’s elder abuse claim for lack of detail, leave to amend is appropriate.

Conclusion

The Ogbechie Defendants’ demurrer to Plaintiffs’ claims for wrongful death, elder abuse, and survival are OVERRULED.

The Ogbechie Defendants’ demurrer to Plaintiff’s claim for elder abuse is SUSTAINED with leave to amend.

The Ogbechie Defendants also move to strike treble damages arising from Plaintiff’s elder abuse claim. Since the Court sustains the Ogbechie Defendants’ demurrer to plaintiff’s claim for elder abuse with leave to amend, the motion to strike is moot.


Case Number: 24SMCV03946    Hearing Date: July 9, 2026    Dept: P

Tentative Ruling

SM 10000 Property LLC v. Michel Chakchay, et al., Case No. 24SMCV03946

Plaintiff’s Motion to Tax Costs

Hearing Date: July 9, 2026

Background

 Plaintiff SM 10000 Property, LLC. brought this unlawful detainer action against Defendants Michel Chakchay (Michel), Maya Haider (Ms. Haider), and Georgio Chakchay (Georgio) for nonpayment of rent for unit 2806 at 10000 Santa Monica Blvd. Los Angeles, CA 90067. This matter was resolved in favor of Defendants at a non-jury trial on March 25, 2025.

On September 29, 2025, the Court granted Defendants’ Motion for attorneys fees, awarding Defendants’ the maximum attorneys’ fees allowed under the lease, $50,000.

On October 22, 2025, Plaintiff filed this Motion to Tax Costs. On June 22, 2026, Defendants filed opposition. On July 1, 2026, Plaintiff replied.

Arguments

Plaintiff moves to tax Defendants’ costs for recording and indexing the abstract of judgment and filing the notice of judgment lien, contending that the costs described in Defendants’ Memorandum of Costs lack evidentiary support. (Motion to Tax Costs, p. 4.) Plaintiff contends that Defendants never served them with a copy of the Order for the Judgment Debtor’s examination and that Defendants’ request for fees and costs associated with that examination are unsupported. (Ibid.)

In opposition, Defendants attach support for the challenged costs, and argue that each challenged cost is authorized by statute and proper on its face, so the burden is on Plaintiff to prove that the costs are unreasonable. (Opposition, p. 4.)

In reply, Plaintiff concedes that Defendants have provided documentation for most of the requests Plaintiff challenged in their initial motion. Plaintiff nonetheless argues that defendants’ recording charge is $56.92 higher than it should be, and that the $5 notice of filing of a judgment lien should be taxed because it was a cost incurred through defective service and is therefore not reasonable or necessary. (Reply, p. 2.) Plaintiff also argues that the attorneys’ fees and filing fee related to the application and order for appearance and examination should be taxed because they were incurred on October 11, 2025, but included in the October 11, 2024 Memorandum of Costs as already-incurred costs. (Id. at p. 4.)

Motion to Tax Costs

Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5(a) sets forth the items allowable as costs while Section 1033.5(b) details the items that are not allowable as costs.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 1033.5, subd. (a), (b).)  “If the items appearing in a cost bill appear to be proper charges, the burden is on the party seeking to tax costs to show that they were not reasonable or necessary. On the other hand, if the items are properly objected to, they are put in issue and the burden of proof is on the party claiming them as costs.  Whether a cost item was reasonably necessary to the litigation presents a question of fact for the trial court and its decision is reviewed for abuse of discretion.”  (Acosta v. SI Corp. (2005) 129 Cal.App.4th 1370, 1380.)  Costs must be both “reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation rather than merely convenient or beneficial to its preparation,” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5(c)(1)), and reasonable in amount.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5(c)(2).) 

Generally, the burden on a motion to tax costs depends upon whether the costs appear proper. “If the items appear to be proper charges the verified memorandum is prima facie evidence that the costs, expenses and services therein listed were necessarily incurred by the defendant, and the burden of showing that an item is not properly chargeable or is unreasonable is upon the objecting party.”  (Nelson v. Anderson (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 111, 131.)  “The court's first determination, therefore, is whether the statute expressly allows the particular item, and whether it appears proper on its face.  If so, the burden is on the objecting party to show them to be unnecessary or unreasonable.”  (Ibid.) 

Analysis

At the end of the day and aside from attorneys’ fees, Plaintiff moves to tax $56.92 of credit card processing fees, a $5.00 lien cost, and a $60.00 filing fee. These costs are facially proper costs, so the burden is on Plaintiff to show that the costs are unreasonable. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 1033.5.)

Plaintiffs first contest $56.92 of Defendants’ $168.92 cost for recording judgment following Defendants’ attorneys’ fee motion. Defendants submit evidence that recording the judgment cost $168.92. (Hermansen Decl., Exhibit B, p. 5.) Plaintiff contends, with no support, that recording cost Defendants only $112.00. Plaintiff cites no authority holding that mandatory service and credit card processing fees associated with recording a judgment are not recoverable.

Next, Plaintiff contests the $5.00 fee for filing a notice of judgment lien on personal property on the grounds that it is a fee associated with improper service. Plaintiff submits no evidence that the service associated with the $5.00 lien cost was improper, and therefore does not meet its burden.[1]

Finally, Plaintiff’s argument that the $60 filing fee for the Application for Appearance and Examination is not recoverable because it was incurred on October 11, 2025, the same day that Defendant served the memorandum of costs, and therefore could not have been actually incurred on October 11, 2025, unreasonably splits hairs. It is possible for a cost to be incurred on the same day a short form is served.

Plaintiff did not raise the issue of attorneys’ fees related to the order for appearance of judgment debtor on its initial motion, which questioned only items 2, 3, and 6 in Defendants’ Memorandum of Costs. Additionally, Defendants cite no authority allowing the recovery of attorneys’ fees for opposing a motion to tax costs after the Court has awarded the maximum fees allowable under a lease agreement. The Court declines to tax fees that were not contested on the initial motion and also declines to award Defendants additional fees.

Plaintiff has not met its burden of demonstrating that Defendants’ costs should be taxed.

Conclusion

Plaintiff’s Motion to Tax Costs is DENIED.


[1] Plaintiff argues that service was improper because the lien mistakenly states that 2200 Biscayne Blvd. in Miami, FL is in Miami, CA in one place on the lien. There is no municipality called Miami in California, let alone one containing the famous Biscayne Blvd. The lien clearly refers to Miami, FL.


Case Number: 24SMCV06059    Hearing Date: July 9, 2026    Dept: P

Tentative Ruling

Shahid Sheikh, et al. v. Assil Gaur Eye Institute, et al., Case No. 24SMCV06059

Plaintiff’s Motion for Leave to Amend to File First Amended Complaint

Hearing Date: July 9, 2026

Background

Plaintiffs Shahid Sheikh (individually “Sheikh”) and his wife Gabriele Gasafy (collectively “Plaintiffs”) sue Defendants Assil Gaur Eye Institute and Dr. Kerry Assil for medical negligence, lack of informed consent, and loss of consortium arising from cataract surgery. The Sheikhs allege that Sheikh agreed to have Dr. Assil place a specific type of intraocular lens in his eye, but that Dr. Assil placed a different intraocular lens during surgery, leaving Sheikh nearsighted in his right eye and causing visual distortion. (Complaint, ¶¶ 17-19.)

On December 12, 2024, Plaintiffs filed the complaint.

On January 21, 2025, Defendants answered.

On May 7, 2026, Defendants filed a motion for summary judgment.

On May 22, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a Motion for Leave to amend, seeking to add four claims for false advertising, breach of fiduciary duty, intentional concealment/fraud, and violation of the UCL. On June 25, 2026, Defendants filed opposition. On June 30, 2026, Plaintiffs replied.

Trial in this matter is set for August 31, 2026.

Arguments

Plaintiffs argue that recently-produced surgical center records and Dr. Assil’s deposition revealed facts that would support their four new claims, specifically evidence that Dr. Assil billed both Sheikh and his insurance for the intraocular lens and misrepresented that the lens was not covered by insurance. (Motion for Leave to Amend, pp. 5-7.)

Defendants argue that they produced the evidence Plaintiffs cite a long time ago, and that leave to amend two months before trial will prejudice them because they will not be able to complete discovery in time. (Opposition, p. 4-8.)

In reply, Plaintiffs argue that the new evidence specifically revealed that the procedure originally referenced implantation of a monofocal lens, later indicated that the lens would be a multifocal lens, and finally implanted a monofocal lens. (Reply, p. 4.) Plaintiffs contend that this case is distinguishable from Defendants’ cited authority because they do not bring this motion on the eve of trial. (Id. at p. 7.)

Motion for Leave to Amend

The trial court has discretion to allow amendments to pleadings in the furtherance of justice. (Code Civ. Proc. § 473, subd. (a)(1).) “Any judge, at any time before or after commencement of trial, in the furtherance of justice, and upon such terms as may be proper, may allow the amendment of any pleading.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 576.) “There is a policy of great liberality in permitting amendments to the pleadings at any stage of the proceeding.” (Berman v. Bromberg (1997) 56 Cal.App.4th 936, 945 [citations omitted].) “An application to amend a pleading is addressed to the trial judge’s sound discretion.”  (Ibid. [citations omitted].) A judge may deny a motion for leave to amend where a moving party has been dilatory in seeking leave to amend and such delay has prejudiced the opposing party. (Hirsa v. Superior Court (1981) 118 Cal.App.3d 486, 490 (“Hirsa”).) “It is difficult to understand how [a named defendant] can be prejudiced by amendment to add an additional theory of liability against it.” (Ibid.) 

“[I]t is an abuse of discretion to deny leave to amend where the opposing party [is] not misled or prejudiced by the amendment.” (Kittredge Sports Co. v. Superior Court (1989) 213 Cal.App.3d 1045, 1048 (“Kittredge”).) Prejudice exists where the amendment would require delaying the trial, resulting in loss of critical evidence, or added costs of preparation. (Solit v. Tokai Bank, Ltd. New York Branch (1999) 68 Cal.App.4th 1435, 1448 (“Solit”).)  

Analysis

Plaintiffs contend that they did not delay moving for leave to amend because they had to wait until they had complete discovery to evaluate these new claims. In response, Defendants assert that they produced this evidence on April 25, 2025. (Hummasti Decl., ¶ 4.) Following a review of the evidence, the documents that Plaintiffs describe as newly-discovered are included in the records that Defendants produced on April 25, 2025, specifically documents describing insurance coverage and costs to be borne by the patient and describing the intraocular lens as both monofocal and multifocal. Plaintiff offers no evidence of more recently produced discovery that provides significantly more or significantly different information than what Defendants disclosed in April 2025.

This case is set for trial in less than two months. Plaintiff’s four proposed causes of action would require significant additional discovery because they concern Defendants’ business practices outside of the scope of Sheikh’s September 23, 2026, surgery. Widening the scope to include Defendants’ advertising and business records would prejudice Defendants because it would require continuing trial and would add significant additional discovery costs.

Conclusion

Plaintiff’s Motion for Leave to Amend is DENIED for failure to justify delay and because it would prejudice Defendants.


Case Number: 25SMCV06165    Hearing Date: July 9, 2026    Dept: P

Tentative Ruling

Taylor Megdal v. Doe 1, et al., Case No. 25SMCV06165

Genneva Maschler’s Motion to Set Aside Default Judgment

Hearing Date: July 9, 2026

Background

Plaintiff Taylor Megdal (“Megdal”), an attorney appearing in pro per, sues Defendant Genneva Maschler (“Maschler”) for fraud, breach of contract, violation of the UCL, and negligence, claiming that Maschler fraudulently failed to disclose a tenant when she sold Plaintiff a six-unit, tenant-occupied property located at 1241 21st Street, Santa Monica, CA.

On November 25, 2025, Megdal filed the complaint.

On January 21, 2026, Megdal filed proof of service by substituted service attesting that Plaintiff was served on December 21, 2025.

On February 9, 2026, Megdal entered default against Maschler.

On April 6, 2026, the Court granted default judgment against Maschler for $886,925.28.

On May 14, 2026, Maschler filed this motion to set aside default and a demurrer. On June 24, 2026, Megdal filed opposition. On July 1, 2026, Maschler replied.

Arguments

Maschler brings this motion to set aside default on the grounds that Megdal improperly served her. Maschler argues that despite the fact that Maschler’s sub-tenant received service and texted Maschler images of the complaint in December 2025. (Motion to Set Aside, p. 3.) Maschler contends that service was improper because Maschler was not permanently residing at the Santa Barbara address where she was served. (Ibid.) Maschler also argues that default should be set aside due to excusable neglect because Megdal’s service was improper and because Maschler received notice of the complaint during the height of Mammoth Lakes ski season, while she was preparing for a three-month trip to Japan from which she only recently returned. (Id. at pp. 6-7.) Maschler argues that despite her vacation, she was able to retain counsel in mid-March, 2026. (Ibid.) Maschler argues that the court lacks jurisdiction under Code Civ. Proc. § 473(d) because of Megdal’s improper service. (Id. at p. 9.)

In opposition, Megdal argues that Maschler’s motion should be denied because she had actual notice of the complaint, he properly served her by substituted service, and because Maschler does not identify any mistake or neglect that would justify setting aside default. (Opposition, pp. 3-5).  Megdal also argues that the record is not clear that service was improper, so such a finding would be inappropriate without an evidentiary hearing. (Id. at pp. 6-7.)

In reply, Maschler contends that Megdal does not prove that Maschler was properly served because he does not submit evidence that the summons and complaint were mailed following substitute service. (Reply, p. 4.) Next, Maschler argued that Megdal failed to serve Maschler at her “usual mailing address,” because he served her at her old Santa Barbara address and not at her current Mammoth Lakes address. (Id. at p. 5.) Finally, Maschler argues that service on her sub-tenant was improper because the sub-tenant was not a member of Maschler’s household. (Id. at pp. 6-7.)

Motion to Set Aside Default

CCP section 473(b) provides, in relevant part, that “the court shall, whenever an application for relief is made no more than six months after entry of judgment, is in proper form, and is accompanied by an attorney’s sworn affidavit attesting to his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect, vacate any (1) resulting default entered by the clerk against his or her client, and which will result in entry of a default judgment, or (2) resulting default judgment or dismissal entered against his or her client, unless the court finds that the default or dismissal was not in fact caused by the attorney’s mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect.”  (Id.)

Code of Civ. Proc. § 473.5(a) also provides a mechanism for the court to set aside default judgment if service did not result in actual notice. “The notice of motion shall be served and filed within a reasonable time, but in no event exceeding the earlier of: (i) two years after entry of a default judgment against him or her; or (ii) 180 days after service on him or her of a written notice that the default or default judgment has been entered.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 473.5(a).)

Default judgment may be set aside under Code Civ. Proc. § 473(d) if the judgment is void on its face. (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (d); Kremerman v. White (2021) 71 Cal.App.5th 358, 360-70.) “ ‘ “A judgment or order is said to be void on its face when the invalidity is apparent upon an inspection of the judgment-roll.” ’ [Citation.] This inquiry, however, ‘does not hinge on evidence: A void judgment’s invalidity appears on the face of the record.’ [Citation.]” (Kremerman v. White, supra, 71 Cal.App.5th at p. 370.)  “‘[C]ompliance with the statutory procedures for service of process is essential to establish personal jurisdiction. [Citation.] Thus, a default judgment entered against a defendant who was not served with a summons in the manner prescribed by statute is void. [Citation.]’” (Ellard v. Conway¿(2001) 94 Cal.App.4th 540, 544.) 

“The policy of the law favors a hearing on the merits.” (Purdum v. Holmes (2010) 187 Cal.App.4th 916, 922.) Therefore, when a party in default moves promptly to request relief, very slight evidence is required to justify a trial court's order setting aside a default. (Id.)¿

Analysis

a.      Code Civ. Proc. § 473(b)

Maschler’s motions under both the mandatory and permissive provisions of § 473(b) fail because Maschler fails to identify any mistake or neglect her or her attorney’s part or submit any evidence demonstrating that her or her attorney’s mistake caused the default.

b.      Code Civ. Proc. § 473.5

Maschler’s motion under Code Civ. Proc. § 473.5 also fails because Maschler admits she had actual notice of this suit when her sub-tenant, who received the substituted service, texted her with images of the complaint. 

c.       Code Civ. Proc. § 473(d)

Substituted service on an individual is proper “[i]f a copy of the summons and complaint cannot with reasonable diligence be personally delivered to the person to be served, as specified in Section 416.60, 416.70, 416.80, or 416.90, a summons may be served by leaving a copy of the summons and complaint at the person’s dwelling house, usual place of abode, usual place of business, or usual mailing address other than a United States Postal Service post office box, in the presence of a competent member of the household or a person apparently in charge of their office, place of business, or usual mailing address other than a United States Postal Service post office box, at least 18 years of age, who shall be informed of the contents thereof, and by thereafter mailing a copy of the summons and of the complaint by first-class mail, postage prepaid to the person to be served at the place where a copy of the summons and complaint were left. Service of a summons in this manner is deemed complete on the 10th day after the mailing.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 415.20(b).)

Here, it is disputed if the address Megdal served was Maschler’s “dwelling house, usual place of abode, usual place of business, or usual mailing address.” Maschler attests that she currently lives in “a custom home on Davison Road in Mammoth Lakes, California 93546,” and that she receives “mail at a Post Office Box at the 93546 US Postal office.” (Maschler-Fine Decl., ¶ 3.) Maschler does not provide a street address in Mammoth Lakes, and a plaintiff cannot make substituted service on a Post Office Box. (Code Civ. Proc. § 415.20(b).) Maschler admits that the address Megdal served, 464 Terrace Road, Santa Barbara, CA, is a dwelling house where she lived until May 2024 that she currently sub-leases. Maschler does not submit any evidence of any other “usual place of abode” that Megdal could have served. Therefore, the Santa Barbara address is the only usual mailing address that Megdal could have served.

Next, Maschler contends that Megdal did not mail a copy of the complaint and summons to Maschler after making substituted service. He did. (See Proof of Mailing (Substituted Service), filed January 23, 2026.)

Finally, Maschler contends that Megdal did not serve a proper member of her household. Megdal served Maschler’s sub-tenant Alison Garcia, who was over 18 and was informed of the content of the papers. (See Proof of Service, filed January 23, 2026, p. 3.)  “To be constitutionally sound the form of substituted service must be ‘reasonably calculated to give an interested party actual notice of the proceedings and an opportunity to be heard.” (Zirbes v. Statton (1986) 187 Cal.App.3d 1407, 1417-1418.) In contrast with cases where service was found to be defective, Megdal believed that Maschler lived at the Santa Barbara address when he served her at that address. (See Megdal Decl., ¶¶ 3-4.) Where the person to be served actually received the summons, substantial compliance with substituted service requirements is sufficient. (Ramos v. Homeward Residential, Inc. (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 1434, 1443 [“In general, substantial compliance with the code occurs when, although not properly identified in a proof of service, the person to be served in fact actually received the summons. ”].) Though Maschler did not actually receive the summons, she received notice that she had been sued from her sub-tenant. (Maschler-Fine Decl., ¶ 5.) She then chose not to do anything. (Id. at ¶ 6.) Megdal substantially complied with the requirements for substituted service.

Conclusion

The standard for granting a motion to set aside default is extremely low. Here, however, Maschler has failed to meet the extremely low standard under Code Civ. Proc. § 473(b) and has also failed to show that service was improper. Granting Maschler’s motion under § 473(d) would be tantamount to a determination that it is impossible to serve her by substituted service. That is not the case.

Maschler’s Motion to Set Aside Default is DENIED without prejudice.
