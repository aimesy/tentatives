TENTATIVE RULING

 

The Court’s tentative ruling is to GRANT preliminary approval, subject to the parties addressing the following:

 

1.      Provide a fully executed Settlement Agreement. The copy provided does not include counsels’ signatures.

2.      The escalator clause at Settlement Agreement ¶8 provides Defendant with the option to shorten the Class and PAGA Periods in lieu of increasing the Gross Settlement Amount. The time periods should be confirmed prior to preliminary approval being granted and notice being distributed. Based on current records, the parties and/or administrator should review and verify the workweek total and confirm the end dates of the Class and PAGA Periods. Provide specific end dates for the Class and PAGA Periods in the Settlement Agreement and Notice.

3.      The settlement of $300,000 is only approximately 0.38% of the maximum exposure according to counsel. Provide further briefing as to how this is fair and within the ballpark of reasonableness. It appears the settlement amount might be predicated on defendant’s financial situation. (Elihu Decl., ¶15.) Provide admissible evidence of Defendant’s financial situation, including, if appropriate, financial documents such as a balance sheet, statement of cash flows, profit and loss statement, and the like.

4.      The Class release at Paragraph 5.2 should not include references to the PAGA or be based on the facts alleged in the PAGA Notice. Class cases which include a PAGA claim should have a separate release for the PAGA claim tied to the facts alleged in the notice given to the LWDA. Any released class claims not presented directly in the operative complaint should be based on the facts alleged in the operative complaint. (See Amaro v. Anaheim Arena Mgmt. (2021) 69 Cal. App. 5th 521, 537 and FN. 5; Uribe v. Crown Building Maintenance Co. 70 Cal. App. 5th 986, 1005.)

5.      Adequacy of Class Counsel: Provide a summary of Class Counsels’ experience and a listing of all prior cases in which each named Class Counsel has been approved by a court to act as lead or co-counsel. (See Dunk v. Ford Motor Co. (1996) 48 Cal.App.4th 1794, 1802.)

6.      Adequacy of Class Representative(s): Provide evidence that each proposed class representative has agreed to act as same and understands his or her responsibilities.  (See Soderstedt v. CBIZ Southern California, LLC (2011) 197 Cal.App.4th 133, 155-156; Jones v. Farmers Ins. Exchange (2013) 221 Cal.App.4th 986, 998-999).

7.      Ensure the content of the notice complies with California Rules of Court, rule 3.766(d). Provide the following information:

a.      The specific amount the class member will receive and how that amount was calculated.

b.      A separate breakdown for PAGA payments.

c.       The terms of the release(s).

d.      The objection, dispute, and opt-out procedures. Do not make class members sign a form to opt-out.

e.      Ensure the judge’s name for the final approval hearing is correct.

 

The current notice reflects several errors and problems:  (1) it requires that absent class members seeking to opt out sign a specific opt out form, instead of directing that they may opt out by using the form or submitting a written notice clearly stating their intention not to participate in the class action; (2) it does not identify the correct judge who is currently presiding over this action; (3) the release information must be revised to reflect the revised language noted above; and (4) there must be information about the specific and separate amounts the class member will receive for the class claim and for the PAGA claim. 

 

8.      Provide proof of submission of the proposed settlement agreement to the LWDA. (Lab. Code, § 2699, subd. (l)(2).)

9.      The request for attorney’s fees of up to 35% appears excessive. Counsel should be prepared to justify this amount in connection with the motion for final approval.

10.  The request for an enhancement award of $10,000 to Plaintiff appears excessive. The Court will not award above $7,500 absent substantial justification. Plaintiff should be prepared to justify the higher amount at final approval.

BACKGROUND

 

This is a wage and hour class action. On March 27, 2020, Plaintiff filed a Class Action and PAGA Representative Action Complaint in Los Angeles County Superior Court on behalf of herself and other hourly, nonexempt workers against Defendant IGS Building Services, LLC (“IGS”), alleging the following: (1) failure to pay minimum, regular and overtime wages; (2) failure to compensate for all hours worked; (3) failure to provide legally compliant meal and rest periods; (4) failure to provide complete and accurate wage statements; (5) failure to timely pay wages upon separation of employment; (6) unlawful and unfair business practices; (7) failure to reimburse necessary business expenses; and (8) violations under the PAGA.

On August 26, 2020, Plaintiff amended her Complaint to add Defendant Ismael Garcia, the owner of IGS, as a named defendant.

Plaintiff worked as an hourly, non-exempt employee of IGS and Summit between May 2018 and February 2019. IGS no longer operates in California, but it was a contractor utilized by Summit to perform janitorial services in Summit-owned and managed buildings in California. Plaintiff alleged that as an employee for Defendants, she and the other employees were subject to the Labor Code violations alleged above. She alleged that Class Members were not paid all wages owed, were not provided legally compliant meal and rest breaks, were not reimbursed for necessary business expenses, and were not provided accurate itemized wage statements.

The Parties participated in a mediation with Daniel Turner on May 10, 2024. While the matter did not settle at this mediation, the Parties thereafter engaged in further discovery and, with Mr. Turner’s guidance, the Parties were eventually able to negotiate a complete settlement of Plaintiff’s claims as a result of a second mediation on November 11, 2025. A partially executed copy of the Settlement Agreement is attached to the Declaration of Kaveh S. Elihu (“Elihu Decl.”) as Exhibit A.  The parties are to submit a fully executed Settlement Agreement signed not only by the parties but also by counsel.

Now before the Court is the Motion for Preliminary Approval of the Settlement.

ANALYSIS OF SETTLEMENT AGREEMENT

 

·         Does a presumption of fairness exist? 

1.         Was the settlement reached through arm’s-length bargaining?  The Parties participated in a mediation with Daniel Turner on May 10, 2024. While the matter did not settle at this mediation, the Parties thereafter engaged in further discovery and, with Mr. Turner’s guidance, the Parties were eventually able to negotiate a complete settlement of Plaintiff’s claims as a result of a second mediation on November 11, 2025. (Elihu Decl., ¶¶11-14.)

2.         Were investigation and discovery sufficient to allow counsel and the court to act intelligently?  Class counsel represents that the Parties realized extreme difficulties in litigating this case through trial. For example, Defendants have maintained throughout the litigation of this case that they have no records relevant to the business or class in their possession, custody, or control. (Id. at ¶10.) Following Plaintiff’s subpoena of IGS’s payroll records from 2016 to 2021 from ADP, Inc. and Paychex, Inc.—the payroll processing services that IGS admitted to using when it was in business—Plaintiff attained the name, contact information, and dates of employment of putative class members who were part of the class prior to 2022. (Id. at ¶11.) During the second mediation, Defendant produced documents, subject to mediation privilege, showing limited means to satisfy any settlement or judgment in this matter, particularly for any settlement fund above $300,000. (Id. at ¶15.)  To the extent that the size of the settlement is justified by Defendant’s financial condition, the parties must submit admissible evidence to support this justification.

3.   Is counsel experienced in similar litigation?  Yes. Class Counsel is experienced in class action litigation, including wage and hour class action cases, but the Court requires addition substantiation of counsel’s asserted experience. (Id. at ¶2.)

4.      What percentage of the class has objected?  This cannot be determined until the fairness hearing.  See Weil & Brown, Cal. Practice Guide: Civil Procedure Before Trial (The Rutter Group 2014) ¶ 14:139.18, (“Should the court receive objections to the proposed settlement, it will consider and either sustain or overrule them at the fairness hearing.”)

 

            CONCLUSION:  The settlement is entitled to a presumption of fairness.

1.      Strength of Plaintiffs’ case.  “The most important factor is the strength of the case for plaintiffs on the merits, balanced against the amount offered in settlement.”  (Kullar v. Foot Locker Retail, Inc. (2008) 168 Cal.App.4th 116, 130.)

         2.  Risk, expense, complexity and likely duration of further litigation.  Given the nature of the class claims, the case is likely to be expensive and lengthy to try.  Procedural hurdles (e.g., motion practice and appeals) are also likely to prolong the litigation as well as any recovery by the class members.

3.   Risk of maintaining class action status through trial.  Even if a class is certified, there is always a risk of decertification. (See Weinstat v. Dentsply Intern., Inc. (2010) 180 Cal.App.4th 1213, 1226 (“Our Supreme Court has recognized that trial courts should retain some flexibility in conducting class actions, which means, under suitable circumstances, entertaining successive motions on certification if the court subsequently discovers that the propriety of a class action is not appropriate.”).)

4.   Amount offered in settlement.  Counsel negotiated a settlement of $300,000 that is approximately 0.38% of the potential exposure.  Counsel needs to justify this steep reduction in the calculated exposure Defendant faces in the case based on information about the weaknesses in Plaintiff’s case and/or present evidence of any financial constraints faced by Defendant in settling for real value.

The settlement amount, after reduction by the maximum requested deductions, leaves $137,500 to be divided among approximately 1,413 class members. Thus, the resulting payments will average $97.31.

5.   Extent of discovery completed and stage of the proceedings.  As indicated above, at the time of the settlement, Class Counsel had conducted sufficient discovery.

6.   Experience and views of counsel.  The settlement was negotiated and endorsed by Class Counsel who, as indicated above, is experienced in class action litigation, including wage and hour class actions. 

7.   Presence of a governmental participant.  This factor is not applicable here.

8.   Reaction of the class members to the proposed settlement. The class members’ reactions will not be known until they receive notice and are afforded an opportunity to object and or opt-out. This factor becomes relevant during the fairness hearing.

 

      CONCLUSION:  The settlement can be preliminarily deemed “fair, adequate, and reasonable.”

·         May conditional class certification be granted?

1.      Standards

A detailed analysis of the elements required for class certification is not required, but it is advisable to review each element when a class is being conditionally certified (Amchem Products, Inc. v. Winsor (1997) 521 U.S. 620, 622-627.)  The trial court can appropriately utilize a different standard to determine the propriety of a settlement class as opposed to a litigation class certification.  Specifically, a lesser standard of scrutiny is used for settlement cases.  (Dunk at 1807, fn. 19.)  Finally, the Court is under no “ironclad requirement” to conduct an evidentiary hearing to consider whether the prerequisites for class certification have been satisfied. (Wershba at 240.)

2.      Analysis

a.      Numerosity.  There are approximately 1,148 class members. (Elihu Decl., ¶11.) This element is met.

b.      Ascertainability.  The proposed class is defined above.  The class definition is “precise, objective and presently ascertainable.”  (Sevidal v. Target Corp. (2010) 189 Cal.App.4th 905, 919.) Class Members are identifiable through Defendants’ records. (MPA ISO Prelim, 21:4-5.)

c.       Community of interest.  “The community of interest requirement involves three factors: ‘(1) predominant common questions of law or fact; (2) class representatives with claims or defenses typical of the class; and (3) class representatives who can adequately represent the class.’”  (Linder v. Thrifty Oil Co. (2000) 23 Cal.4th 429, 435.)

As to commonality, Plaintiff contends that here, common issues of fact and law predominate because the California statutes relating to each of Plaintiff’s claims, and Defendant’s defenses thereto, apply with equal force and effect to all Class Members. Factually, Plaintiff contends that Defendant’s policies and practices apply class-wide and Defendant’s liability can be determined by facts common to all members of the class. The wage and hour issues are both numerous and substantial, and a class action is the most advantageous method of dealing with the claims of the Class Members. (MPA ISO Prelim, 21:6-11.)

As to typicality, Plaintiff contends that her wage and hour claims are typical of the proposed Class because they arise from the same factual bases and are based on the same legal theories applicable to the other Class Members. Likewise, Plaintiff’s interests are entirely coextensive with the interests of the Class. Plaintiff maintains that he was injured by the same company-wide practices to which the proposed Class was subject and seek the same relief. Plaintiff has already demonstrated his ability to advocate for the interests of the Class by initiating this litigation, undertaking representative discovery, and evaluating the proposed settlement to assure that it is fair. (Id. at 21:12-18.)

   As to adequacy, Plaintiff represents that she is aware of the duties and risks of serving as class representative and has participated in the litigation. (Declaration of Carolina Portillo.)

d.      Adequacy of class counsel.  As indicated above, Class Counsel has shown experience in class action litigation, including wage and hour class actions.

e.      Superiority.  Given the relatively small size of the individual claims, a class action appears to be superior to separate actions by the class members.

 

      CONCLUSION:  The class may be conditionally certified since the prerequisites of class certification have been satisfied.

Prior to the time of the final fairness hearing, the administrator must submit a declaration attesting to the total costs incurred and anticipated to be incurred to finalize the settlement for approval by the Court.

 

·         Attorney fees and costs

CRC rule 3.769(b) states: “Any agreement, express or implied, that has been entered into with respect to the payment of attorney fees or the submission of an application for the approval of attorney fees must be set forth in full in any application for approval of the dismissal or settlement of an action that has been certified as a class action.”

            Ultimately, the award of attorney fees is made by the court at the fairness hearing, using the lodestar method with a multiplier, if appropriate.  (PLCM Group, Inc. v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095-1096; Ramos v. Countrywide Home Loans, Inc. (2000) 82 Cal.App.4th 615, 625-626; Ketchum III v. Moses (2000) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132-1136.)  In common fund cases, the Court may utilize the percentage method, cross-checked by the lodestar. (Laffitte v. Robert Half Int’l, Inc. (2016) 1 Cal.5th 480, 503.)  Despite any agreement by the parties to the contrary, “the court ha[s] an independent right and responsibility to review the attorney fee provision of the settlement agreement and award only so much as it determined reasonable.” (Garabedian v. Los Angeles Cellular Telephone Company (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 123, 128.)

            The question of whether Class Counsel is entitled to $105,000 (35%) in attorney fees will be addressed at the fairness hearing when class counsel brings a noticed motion for attorney fees.  Class counsel must provide the court with billing information so that it can properly apply the lodestar method and must indicate what multiplier (if applicable) is being sought as to each counsel.    

Class Counsel should also be prepared to justify the costs (capped at $30,000) sought by detailing how they were incurred.

 

·         Incentive Award to Class Representatives

Class Counsel will seek a Service Payment of $10,000 to the named Plaintiff. In connection with the final fairness hearing, named Plaintiffs must submit declarations attesting to why they should be entitled to an enhancement award in the proposed amount.  The named Plaintiffs must explain why they “should be compensated for the expense or risk she has incurred in conferring a benefit on other members of the class.”  (Clark v. American Residential Services LLC (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 785, 806.)  Trial courts should not sanction enhancement awards of thousands of dollars with “nothing more than pro forma claims as to ‘countless’ hours expended, ‘potential stigma’ and ‘potential risk.’ Significantly more specificity, in the form of quantification of time and effort expended on the litigation, and in the form of reasoned explanation of financial or other risks incurred by the named plaintiffs, is required in order for the trial court to conclude that an enhancement was ‘necessary to induce [the named plaintiff] to participate in the suit . . . .’”  (Id. at 806-807, italics and ellipsis in original.)  The Court will decide the issue of the enhancement award at the time of final approval.

Moving Party:             Defendant Quixote Studios, LLC (DLA Piper, LLP)

Responding Party:      Plaintiff Eric Brown (Moon Law Group, PC)

Ruling:                        Motion to compel arbitration is DENIED.

 

This is a wage and hour putative class and Private Attorneys General Act (PAGA) action.  Plaintiff Eric Brown (Plaintiff) alleges that he and others were employed by defendant Quixote Studios, LLC (Defendant), during which employment Defendant committed various violations of labor law.  Defendant moves to compel Plaintiff to submit his individual claims pursuant to an arbitration agreement contained in a collective bargaining agreement (the CBA) Plaintiff is purportedly bound by.

 

LEGAL STANDARDS

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.2 states, in relevant part:¿ 

 

On petition of a party to an arbitration agreement alleging the existence of a written agreement to arbitrate a controversy and that a party thereto refuses to arbitrate such controversy, the court shall order the petitioner and the respondent to arbitrate the controversy if it determines that an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists….¿ 

 

“There is no public policy favoring arbitration of disputes which the parties have not agreed to arbitrate.” (Engineers & Architects Assn. v. Community Development Dept.¿(1994) 30 Cal.App.4th 644, 653.) On the other hand, once an enforceable arbitration agreement has been found, “[d]oubts as to whether an arbitration clause applies to a particular dispute are to be resolved in favor of arbitration[,]” and the law favors such agreements.  (Vianna v. Doctors’ Management Co.¿(1994) 27 Cal.App.4th 1186, 1189; see also People v. Maplebear Inc. (2022) 81 Cal.App.5th 923, 930-931.)¿ ¿¿ 

 

“The petitioner bears the burden of proving the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by the preponderance of the evidence, and a party opposing the petition bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence any fact necessary to its defense. In these summary proceedings, the trial court sits as a trier of fact, weighing all the affidavits, declarations, and other documentary evidence, as well as oral testimony received at the court's discretion, to reach a final determination.” (Giuliano v. Inland Empire Personnel, Inc.¿(2007) 149 Cal.App.4th 1276, 1284.)¿¿The movant may bear its initial burden “by attaching a copy of the arbitration agreement purportedly bearing the opposing party's signature.” (Iyere v. Wise Auto Group (2023) 87 Cal.App.5th 747, 755 (Iyere); Espejo v. Southern California Permanente Medical Group (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 1047, 1060.)  “At this step, a movant need not ‘follow the normal procedures of document authentication’ and need only ‘allege the existence of an agreement and support the allegation as provided in [Rules of Court, Rule 3.1330].’”  (Iyere, supra, 87 Cal.App.5th at 755; Condee v. Longwood Management Corp. (2001) 88 Cal.App.4th 215, 219 (Condee).) 

 

“The party opposing arbitration has the burden of demonstrating that an arbitration clause cannot be interpreted to require arbitration of the dispute. Nonetheless, this policy does not override ordinary principles of contract interpretation. The contractual terms themselves must be carefully examined before the parties to the contract can be ordered to arbitration: Although [t]he law favors contracts for arbitration of disputes between parties, there is no policy compelling persons to accept arbitration of controversies which they have not agreed to arbitrate.” (Rice v. Downs¿(2016) 247 Cal.App.4th 1213, 1223, citations and quotations omitted.)¿¿¿ 

 

“A party seeking to enforce an arbitration agreement has the burden of showing FAA preemption.”  (Lane v. Francis Capital Management, LLC (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 676, 687; see also Nixon v. AmeriHome Mortgage Company, LLC (2021) 67 Cal.App.5th 934, 946 [“The party seeking to enforce the arbitration agreement also bears the burden of establishing the FAA applies and preempts otherwise governing provisions of state law or the parties’ agreement.”].)

 

DISCUSSION

 

It is undisputed that at all pertinent times, Plaintiff was a member of the International Brotherhood of Teamsters, Local 399 (Local 399 or the Union).  Local 399 has several collective bargaining agreements with Defendant which were active during the class period: the 2021 Local 399 Drivers Agreement (which the parties call the Black Book), the Local 399 Black Book Memorandum of Agreement for 2024-2027 (the Black Book MOA), and the Commercial Drivers Local 399 Quixote Agreement 2024 (the Commercial Drivers/Quixote MOA).  The parties apparently agree that the Black Book MOA extended and modified the Black Book for the 2024-2027 period, and that the Commercial Drivers/Quixote MOA applied the requirements of the Black Book MOA to Defendant (who was not originally among the parties to the original Black Book). 

 

“A union representative may agree on an employee's behalf as part of the collective bargaining process to require the employee to arbitrate controversies relating to an interpretation or enforcement of a CBA.”  (Cortez v. Doty Bros. Equipment Co. (2017) 15 Cal.App.5th 1, 11 (Cortez), citing 14 Penn Plaza LLC v. Pyett (2009) 556 U.S. 247, 256-257.)  Although “contractual matters under a CBA are presumed arbitrable[,]…the presumption of arbitration in a CBA does not apply to statutory violations.”  (Cortez, supra, 15 Cal.App.5th at 11-12, citing Wright v. Universal Maritime Service Corp. (1998) 525 U.S. 70, 78-79.)  “Thus, although a union representative in negotiating a CBA in good faith may waive the employee's right to pursue in a judicial forum an action for a statutorily protected right [citation], the United States Supreme Court has made clear that waiver of the right to prosecute a statutory violation in a judicial forum is only effective if it is explicit, ‘clear and unmistakable.’”  (Cortez, supra, 15 Cal.App.5th at 12.)  “A waiver in a collective bargaining agreement is sufficiently clear if it is found in an explicit arbitration clause.”  (Vasquez v. Superior Court (2000) 80 Cal.App.4th 430, 435 (Vasquez).)  Alternatively,

 

[a] waiver in a collective bargaining agreement may…be sufficiently clear if broad, nonspecific language in the arbitration clause is coupled with “an ‘explicit incorporation of statutory antidiscrimination requirements' elsewhere in the contract. [Citation.] If another provision, like a nondiscrimination clause, makes it unmistakably clear that the discrimination statutes at issue are part of the agreement, employees will be bound to arbitrate their [state and federal statutory] claims.” [Citation.]  A simple agreement not to engage in acts violative of a particular statute will not suffice; the agreement must establish the intent of the parties to incorporate “in their entirety” the discrimination statutes. [Citation.] Compliance with a particular statute must be an express contractual commitment in the collective bargaining agreement. 

 

(Vasquez, supra, 80 Cal.App.4th at 435.)

 

For instance, in Cortez, the CBA was sufficiently “clear and unmistakable” where it read that “‘[a]ny dispute or grievance arising from this Wage Order 16[ ] shall be processed under and in accordance with’ the arbitration procedure” in the CBA, even though the plaintiff’s suit was asserted under the Labor Code, because wage orders could be enforced only through the Labor Code.  (Cortez, supra, 15 Cal.App.5th at 13-14.) 

 

Plaintiff contends that the pertinent agreements are not “clear and unmistakable” as applied to his claims.  Article 7 of the Black Book makes no mention of the Labor Code, Wage Orders, or any statutory rights at all, nor any waiver of a judicial forum for those rights.  It says only:

 

In the event of any dispute between the Union or any of the persons subject to this Agreement and the Producer with regard to wages, hours, or other conditions of employment under this Agreement, or with regard to the interpretation of this Agreement,[1] the procedure, unless otherwise specifically provided herein, shall be as follows:

 

[1] Any such disputes that give rise to an alleged violation of Sections 8(a)(1) and/or 8(a)(3) of the National Labor Relations Act, or in which the facts alleged would constitute such a violation, are also subject to the grievance and arbitration procedure in this Article 7.

 

(Nye Decl., Ex. A, p. 7.)  The Black Book then describes a four-step process of dispute resolution, culminating in a demand for either expedited or ordinary arbitration.  (See Id., pp. 7-12.) 

 

The Black Book MOA contains no dispute resolution provisions of its own but simply reincorporates the provisions of the prior Black Book with certain additions or revisions.  (See Nye Decl., Ex. B, p. 1.)  Finally, the Commercial Drivers/Quixote MOA incorporates the Black Book MOA.  (See Nye Decl., Ex. C, p. 1.)  On their faces, none of these agreements say anything about statutory claims or the waiver of a judicial forum for them. 

 

Defendant contends that the Black Book nonetheless clearly and unmistakably requires the arbitration of statutory claims because the Black Book and Commercial Drivers/Quixote MOA “define the entire wage, overtime, meal, and working-conditions framework” of the Labor Code as contractual requirements.  In other words, because the pertinent CBAs require the same things (or more) that the Labor Code requires, claims for violation of the Labor Code should be understood to be violations of the CBA and therefore subject to arbitration as “grievances.”

 

Vasserman v. Henry Mayo Newhall Memorial Hospital (2017) 8 Cal.App.5th 236 (Vasserman) is instructive.  There, the defendant contended that Labor Code provisions did not have to be contained in the arbitration provisions, relying partly on language in the CBA providing that the employer would pay “any and all penalties provided for under this Article, the Labor Code or the Wage Order no later than the pay period immediately following the day resulting in the penalties” and if they did not receive “any penalty called for by this article, the Labor Code, or the Wage Order the Nurse may file a Grievance” under the CBA.  (Id. at 248-249.)  The Court of Appeal rejected the defendant’s argument, reasoning that “[a]n employer’s agreement to pay a penalty required by law is not the same as a union’s agreement to waive members’ rights to a judicial forum for statutory violations.”  (Id. at 249.) 

 

Defendant’s argument is not meritorious.  A collective bargaining agreement which makes no mention of statutory requirements at all, let alone any waiver of a judicial forum for statutory violations, does not contain a “clear and unmistakable” intent to arbitrate statutory claims.  Defendant’s contention that the cases “do not require rote citation of every statute” is hyperbolic.  What the cases do require is that the CBA state, clearly and unmistakably, that the statutory claims the plaintiff is bringing will be submitted to arbitration, or contain an “express contractual commitment” to abide by a particular statute or otherwise make it “unmistakably clear that the…statutes at issue are part of the agreement[.]”  (Vasquez, supra, 80 Cal.App.4th at 435.)  They need not do that by citing every provision of the statutes one at a time, but may do so by reference or incorporation, as in Cortez where the CBA required arbitration of grievances arising from Wage Order 16 (which could necessarily only be enforced through the Labor Code, and so was a waiver of a judicial forum for those Labor Code claims).  (See Cortez, supra, 15 Cal.App.5th at 13-14.)  What they may not do is simply fail to mention them at all. It is telling that the closest language Defendant cites to an incorporation of statutory law is a promise to renegotiate the agreement if a provision is “affected by any legislation, decision by a court of competent jurisdiction or governmental regulation.”  (Reply, p. 3 fn. 1, quoting Nye Decl., Ex. A, article 8, pp. 13-14.)  This is not a commitment to abide by laws incorporated into the contract, it is a commitment to negotiate if, in the future, some unspecified law or court decision should affect the provisions of the agreement.  It does not come close to expressing a clear and unmistakable intent to submit statutory claims to arbitration. 

 

As discussed in Vasserman, the agreement to comply with legal requirements, there to pay meal and rest period premiums, “is not the same thing as a union's agreement to waive members’ rights to a judicial forum for statutory violations.”  (Vasserman, supra, 8 Cal.App.5th at 249.)  Where the CBA and the Labor Code happen to require the same things, a plaintiff may choose whether to sue on the CBA or under the Labor Code.  It is only if the CBA expressly waives a judicial forum for statutory claims, or explicitly incorporates statutory requirements in their entirety, such that “[c]ompliance with a particular statute [is] an express contractual commitment in the collective bargaining agreement” that the employee’s statutory claims may be compelled to arbitration.  (Vasquez, supra, 80 Cal.App.4th at 430.)

 

CONCLUSION

 

For the foregoing reasons, the motion is DENIED.  Plaintiff to give notice.
