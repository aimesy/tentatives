24VECV05185 GABRIELA OCHOA JASSO, AN INDIVIDUAL, et al. vs ABRAM MURADYAN, AN INDIVIDUAL, et al.

June, 2026, at 8:30 a.m.

Van Nuys Courthouse East, Dept. I

 Ruling – Minor’s Comp.

 

 

 

I.        Background

 

On October 22, 2024, Plaintiffs Gabriela Ochoa Jasso (“Petitioner”), Mason Montelongo, by and through his guardian ad litem Gabriela Ochoa Jasso (“Mason”), Matthew Montelongo, by and through his guardian ad litem Gabriela Ochoa Jasso (“Matthew”), and Maya Montelongo, by and through her guardian ad litem Gabriela Ochoa Jasso (“Maya”)(collectively “Plaintiffs”), filed a complaint against Defendants Abram Muradyan (“Muradyan”), Marine Glidzhyan (“Glidzhyan”), Helen Hee (“Hee”), and Does 1 to 50 alleging two causes of action: (1) negligence; and (2) premises liability.

 

On May 12, 2026, Petitioner filed the instant petitions for expedited approval of compromise of claim on behalf of Maya, Matthew, and Mason.

 

II.     Petition for Approval of Compromised Claim

 

Court approval is required for all settlements of a minor’s claim. (Cal. Prob. Code §§ 3500, 3600, et seq.; Cal. Code of Civ. Proc. § 372.) “‘[W]ithout trial court approval of the proposed compromise of the ward’s claim, the settlement cannot be valid. [Citation.] Nor is the settlement binding [on the minor] until it is endorsed by the trial court.” (Pearson v. Superior Court (2012) 202 Cal.App.4th 1333, 1338.) A minor, like Claimant, “shall appear either by a guardian or conservator of the estate or by a guardian ad litem appointed by the court in which the action or proceeding is pending, or by a judge thereof, in each case.” (CCP § 372(a)(1).) Alternatively, the petitioner may file a declaration demonstrating that he or she has a right to compromise the minor’s claim under Prob. Code § 3500.

 

Regarding the substance of the petition, to obtain court approval of the settlement of a minor’s claim, the petitioner must file a complete and “verified petition for approval of the settlement and must disclose ‘all information that has any bearing upon the reasonableness of the compromise.’ [Citations.]” (Barnes v. Western Heritage Ins. Co. (2013) 217 Cal.App.4th 249, 256; Cal. Rules of Court Rule 7.950.)

 

Petitioner seeks court approval for a settlement under which Maya, Matthew, and Mason (hereinafter “Claimants”) will receive $5,000.00 each. (See MC-350EX Item 11.) Of this amount, $100.00 will pay for medical expenses, $1,250.00 for attorney’s fees, and $844.60 for legal expenses, leaving Claimants with a net settlement amount of $2,805.40 each. (See MC-350EX Item 17.)

 

Medical records are provided as Attachment 9. (See MC-350EX Attachment 9.) Attorney’s fees are designated by retainer agreement and reasonably represent 25% of the gross settlement amounts. (See MC-350EX Attachment 14a [agreement].) The funds are to be deposited in a blocked account. (See Item 18.)

 

Notably, Petitioner is set to receive $70,000.00 in settlement. (See MC-350Ex Item 12.) Petitioner incurred greater medical expenses than Claimants. (See Attachment 11c.)

 

Based on the foregoing, the instant petitions are GRANTED.

 

Petitioner to give notice.


Case Number: 25STCV17113    Hearing Date: June 26, 2026    Dept: I

25STCV17113 ANNUNZIATA CRUPI vs EDWARD B MORETON, JR., et al.

June 26, 2026, at 8:30 a.m.

Van Nuys Courthouse East, Dept. I

Tentative Ruling - Motion for Attorney Fees

 

 

 

I.        Background

 

On June 12, 2025, Plaintiff Annunziata Crupi (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Defendants Edward B. Moreton Jr., Randolph M. Hammock, University of Southern California, Valter Longo, Sebastian Brandhorst, Wendy Snaer, Gregory Wong, Heather Cox, L-Nutra, Marta Alcumbrac, Howard Smith, Peter Walrod, Candice Zee, Mark Schaeffer, and Does 1 through 100 alleging twenty-three causes of action: (1) civil conspiracy; (2) civil conspiracy to persecute, isolate, and defame; (3) constructive eviction; (4) violation of civil rights via affirmative acts; (5) infliction of public humiliation; (6) retaliation; (7) discrimination; (8) defamation; (9) tortious interference with contract; (10) tortious interference with prospective economic advantage; (11) invasion of privacy; (12) civil harassment; (13) abuse of process; (14) fraud in arbitration; (15) economic coercion and duress; (16) declaratory and injunctive relief; (17) loss of consortium and familial association; (18) violation of constitutional right to travel; (19) violation of right to intimate association and family life; (20) violation of right to reproductive liberty and family formation; (21) unfair business practices; (22) intentional infliction of emotional distress; and (23) negligent infliction of emotional distress

 

On March 16, 2026, Defendant Mark Schaeffer (hereinafter “Schaeffer”) filed an anti-SLAPP motion to strike Plaintiff’s complaint.

 

On May 1, 2026, the Court granted the motion.

 

On May 15, 2026, Schaeffer filed the instant motion for attorney’s fees.

 

On June 3, 2026, Plaintiff filed her reply.

 

On June 8, 2026, Schaeffer filed his reply.

 

II.     Discussion

 

Cal. Code of Civ. Proc. § 425.16(c)(1) provides, in pertinent part, that in any action subject to a special motion to strike, “a prevailing defendant on a special motion to strike shall be entitled to recover that defendant’s attorney’s fees and costs.” (CCP § 425.16(c)(1).)

 

This fee-shifting provision is “broadly construed as to effectuate the legislative purpose of reimbursing the prevailing defendant for expenses incurred in extracting herself from a baseless lawsuit.” (Wanland v. Law Offices of Mastagni, Holstedt & Chiurazzi (2006) 141 Cal.App.4th 15, 45.) Fee-shifting is mandatory if the defendant prevails upon a special motion to strike. (Resolute Forest Products, Inc. v. Greenpeace International (N.D. Cal. (2017) 302 F.Supp.3d 1005, 1026.)

Plaintiff’s Opposition

 

As a preliminary matter, the Court addresses Plaintiff’s various declarations filed with her opposition, totaling over 600 pages of documents, including, inter alia, various mailing records, LinkedIn searches, correspondence with Plaintiff’s landlord, and pages-long narratives of Plaintiff’s personal history. (See generally Crupi Declarations.) Pursuant to Cal. Evid. Code § 350, no evidence is admissible except relevant evidence. (See Evid. Code § 350.) The court also retains discretion to exclude evidence if its probative value is substantially outweighed by the probability that its admission will necessitate undue consumption of time or create substantial danger of confusing the issues. (Id. at § 352.) Furthermore, California appellate courts have firmly established that it is not the function of the court to comb the record looking for the evidence or absence of evidence to support a party’s argument, of which the Court has put Plaintiff on notice. (See Becerra v. McClatchy Co. (2021) 69 Cal.App.5th 913.) Accordingly, the Court disregards these declarations.

 

Plaintiff further objects to “escalating litigation” on grounds of her “severe financial hardship,” “escalating debt and economic collapse,” and “inability to sustain abusive litigation.” (See generally Opposition.) However, an award of attorney’s fees on an anti-SLAPP is mandatory. (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122.) A trial court has no discretion to deny the award on the basis of financial hardship. (Paulus v. Bob Lynch Ford, Inc. (2006) 139 Cal.App.4th 659.) A court may only determine if the amount is reasonable. (Ibid.)

 

Analysis

 

Here, Plaintiff’s complaint arises from allegations of misconduct regarding Schaeffer’s official actions as counsel in Crupi v. USC et al. (See May 1, 2026, Order.) The Court granted Schaeffer’s anti-SLAPP motion on May 1, 2026. (Ibid.) Schaeffer is therefore entitled to attorney’s fees and costs pursuant to CCP § 425.16(c)(1).

 

Schaeffer seeks attorney’s fees in the total amount of $57,035. (See Motion at 3:3-11.) Notably, Plaintiff’s complaint was extensive, involving 23 causes of action. (See generally Complaint.) Anti-SLAPP motions are “complex and time consuming.” (Ketchhum, supra, at 1139.) Here, Plaintiff’s counsel states that her reasonably hourly rate is $850. (Stone Decl. ¶ 7.) Counsel has been practicing law since 1983. (Ibid.) Counsel also provides a table of billing entries as Exhibit 2 to her declaration. (Id. at Exh. 2.) These entries show 67.1 hours expended. (Ibid.)

 

Given Plaintiff’s extensive complaint, supplemental declarations, and objections, including hundreds of pages to review and analyze, the Court finds this amount reasonable.

The Court also finds the hourly rate reasonable.

 

III.  Conclusion

 

Based on the foregoing, the Court GRANTS Schaeffer’s motion for attorney’s fees. Attorney’s fees are awarded for Schaeffer and defense counsel and against Plaintiff in the total amount $57,035.

 

Schaefer  to give notice.

Tentative Ruling – Demurrer

 

 

 

On October 29, 2025, Plaintiff Zitser Family Law Group, APC (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Defendant Christopher J. O’Brien (“Defendant”) and Does 1 to 20 alleging two causes of action: (1) breach of contract; and (2) common counts.

 

On March 5, 2026, Defendant entered default.

 

On April 20, 2026, Defendant, without leave of court, and while in default, filed a cross-complaint against Plaintiff and Does 1 to 20 alleging four causes of action: (1) breach of fiduciary duty; (2) professional negligence; (3) breach of contract; and (4) equitable indemnity/contribution.

 

On April 29, 2026, the parties filed a joint stipulation to set aside default.

 

On May 7, 2026, the Clerk filed a notice of rejection of the electronic filing of the stipulation, stating that the stipulation incorrectly referenced a hearing in Department 47, despite this case being assigned to Department I.

 

Based on the foregoing, Defendant remains in default. Furthermore, the cross-complaint, filed by Defendant while in default, is void. (See Talbott v. Ghadimi (2025) 109 Cal.App.5th 967.) Accordingly, the Court strikes the cross-complaint pursuant to Cal. Code of Civ. Proc. § 436.

 

If the parties wish to set aside Defendant’s default, then the parties must file a stipulation in this department.

 

Based on the foregoing, and having struck Defendant’s cross-complaint, the Court finds the instant demurrer MOOT.

Case Number: 26VECV01496    Hearing Date: June 26, 2026    Dept: I

26VECV01496 JEROLD ADAMS vs CRESTVIEW LANDSCAPING, INC., et al.

June 26, 2026, at 8:30 a.m.

Van Nuys Courthouse East, Dept. I

Tentative Ruling – Motion to Compel Arbitration

 

 

 

I.        Background

 

On March 12, 2026, Plaintiff Jerold Adams (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Defendants Crestview Landscape, Inc. (“Crestview Landscape”), Pierre Landscape, Inc. (“Pierre Landscape”)(collectively “Defendants”), and Does 1 to 25 alleging sixteen causes of action: (1) disability discrimination; (2) failure to accommodate under FEHA; (3) failure to engage in the interactive process; (4) harassment under FEHA; (5) retaliation under FEHA; (6) failure to provide overtime wages; (8) failure to timely pay wages upon termination; (9) failure to furnish compliant wage statements; (10) failure to provide rest periods; (11) failure to provide meal breaks; (12) failure to reimburse necessary expenditures; (13) unfair business practices; (14) retaliation in violation of Cal. Lab. Code § 1102.5; (15) wrongful termination in violation of

public policy; and (16) breach of contract.

 

On May 11, 2026, Defendants filed the instant motion to compel arbitration.

 

On May 18, 2026, Plaintiff filed his “procedural objections” to the motion.

 

On June 3, 2026, Defendants filed their reply.

 

On June 10, 2026, the Court continued hearing on the motion to allow Plaintiff and Defendants opportunities to file substantive opposition and reply.

 

On June 12, 2026, Plaintiff filed his opposition.

 

On June 17, 2026, Defendants filed their supplemental reply.

 

II.     Legal Standard

 

In deciding a motion to compel arbitration, trial courts must first decide whether an enforceable arbitration agreement exists between the parties and then determine the second gateway issue of whether the claims are covered within the scope of the agreement. (See Omar v. Ralphs Grocery Co. (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 955, 961.)(See also Chiron Corp. v. Ortho Diagnostic Systems, Inc. (9th Cir. 2000) 207 F. 3d 1126, 1130 [“The court’s role under the [Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”)] is therefore limited to determining (1) whether a valid agreement to arbitrate exists and, if it does, (2) whether the agreement encompasses the dispute at issue.”]. “The petitioner bears the burden of proving the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by the preponderance of the evidence, and a party opposing the petition bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence any fact necessary to its defense.” (Engalla v. Permanente Medical Group, Inc. (1997) 64 Cal.Rptr.2d 903, 915-16.) “In these summary proceedings, the trial court sits as a trier of fact, weighing all the affidavits, declarations, and other documentary evidence, as well as oral testimony received at the court’s discretion, to reach a final determination.” (Id. at 916.) No jury trial is available for a motion to compel arbitration. (Ibid.)

Accordingly, under both the FAA and California Law, arbitration agreements are valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, except on such grounds that exist at law or equity for voiding a

contract. (Winter v. Window Fashions Professions, Inc. (2008) 166 Cal.App.4th 943, 947.)

 

III.  Discussion

 

Objection to Reply

 

As a preliminary matter, the Court addresses Plaintiff’s June 22, 2026, notice of non-service and objection. Plaintiff argues that “Defendants’ proof of service used an email addresses with a different domain, goldbergandloren.com, and Plaintiff did not receive [the reply] by June 17 filing through service.” (Objection at 3:1-5.) However, Plaintiff admits to downloading the reply from the Court’s website on June 19, 2026. (Id. at 3:18-21.) Additionally, Defendants re-served the reply to the correct email address on June 22, 2026. (See June 22, 2026, Herron Decl.)

 

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s objection is overruled.

 

Plaintiff also argues that Defendants’ reply exceeds the ten-page limit imposed by Cal. Rules of Court Rule 3.1113. (See CRC Rule 3.1113.) The Court agrees, and therefore disregards any argument made after page 10 of Defendants’ reply. (See generally Reply.)

 

Analysis

 

Defendants move for an order “compelling this matter back to arbitration in accordance with the arbitration agreement [Plaintiff] entered into on May, [sic] 10, 2020, and in accordance with the Court order dated September 5, 2025, which enforced the parties’ joint stipulation for arbitration . . .” (Motion at 1:22-2:2.) Defendants argue that “this case proceeded in arbitration for 8 months before Plaintiff sought to withdraw.” (Id. at 2:9-20.) “Throughout this time, [Defendants] diligently and fully paid for the arbitration, only to have Plaintiff seek to withdraw from the arbitration based on a purported minor delay in paying one arbitration invoice over the 2025 winter holidays.” (Ibid.) “Critically, multiple representatives from the [American Arbitration Association (“AAA”)] confirmed that the AAA has no records of untimely payments in the arbitration, and the AAA has not issued a notice of default related to the ongoing arbitration proceedings.” (Id. at 21-28.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that, under CCP § 1281.98, Defendants’ untimely payment of required arbitration fees permits Plaintiff to withdraw from arbitration and proceed in court. (See Opposition at p. 8.) “On December 3, 2025, AAA sent correspondence advising that the employer had been billed $1,491.00 for incurred arbitrator compensation . . . On December 20, 2025, AAA sent a courtesy reminder stating that the deposit remained due as of November 17, 2025, that [CCP] section 1281.98 applied, and that AAA could not extend the payment deadline unless all parties agreed . . . On January 8, 2026, AAA sent written notice stating that it had not received the $1,491.00 payment requested in the December 3 correspondence. That notice expressly cited section 1281.98, asked Claimant how he wished to proceed, and asked the parties to confirm any agreed extension of the payment deadline.” (Id. at 8:20-9:2.) “On January 16, 2026, AAA asked again how Claimant intended to proceed. On January 23, 2026, AAA followed up once more. Plaintiff then advised AAA that Claimant was awaiting the scheduled March 12 mediation before further engaging on the issue. AAA responded that the outstanding balance for incurred arbitrator compensation ‘should be paid immediately’ and that, if payment was not received, AAA would advise the arbitrator, who could suspend or close the matter for nonpayment.” (Id. at 9:9-16.)

 

Then, after the March 12, 2026, mediation did not resolve the action, Plaintiff gave notice pursuant to CCP § 1281.98. CCP § 1281.98 provides that “[i]f the drafting party materially breaches the arbitration agreement and is in default . . . the employee or consumer may unilaterally elect to do any of the following: (1) Withdraw the claim from arbitration and proceed in a court of appropriate jurisdiction.” (CCP § 1281.98(b).) “On March 17, 2026, Plaintiff advised AAA and the arbitrator that Claimant understood the Court would determine Claimant’s position under section 1281.98 . . .” (Opposition at 9:17-28.)

 

Notably, Defendants argue that the arbitration clause provides that “[e]xcept as otherwise specifically provided in this Agreement to Arbitrate (“Agreement”), the enforcement of this Agreement, and the conduct of the arbitration proceeding are governed by the Federal Arbitration Act.” (Motion at 12:3-7 [citing Herron Decl. Exh. A])(emphasis in original.) “By its terms, the parties agreed that both the enforceability of the Agreement and the procedures governing the arbitration would be controlled by federal law.” (Ibid.) “Under the FAA’s equal treatment principle, section 1291.98 cannot be applied in a manner that imposes arbitration-specific forfeiture or overrides the parties’ agreement absence conduct that would justify such a result under ordinary contract principles.” (Id. at 16-19.)

 

Defendants also rely on Hohenshelt v. Superior Court (2025) 18 Cal.5th 310, holding that CCP § 1291.98 does not act as a “strict forfeiture rule” and instead only applies where nonperformance is “willful, grossly negligent, or fraudulent.” (Hohenshelt, supra, at 323.) Notably, Hohenshelt also holds that CCP § 1291.98 is not preempted by the FAA. (Id. at 323.)

 

Hohenshelt Analysis

 

Pursuant to Hohenshelt, the governing framework is whether “the delay [in nonpayment] resulted in compensable harm.” (Hohenshelt, supra, at 349.)

 

 First, the Court finds that Defendants’ nonpayment was not “willful, grossly negligent, or fraudulent.” (Hohenshelt, supra, at 323.) Defendants missed a singular payment over the course of the holidays. Defendants also timely made all other payments in this matter. (Motion at 7:24-8:2.)

 

Plaintiff argues that he suffered “compensable harm” because he “was required to address the statutory default at issue, evaluate forum rights, respond to AAA’s payment correspondence, preserve the AAA file, make an election after mediation failed, and litigate Defendants’ renewed effort to compel arbitration.” (Opposition at 16:18-17:5.) Plaintiff also argues that he “continued to incur concrete arbitration-related costs while the payment issue remained unresolved. Plaintiff pursued arbitrator-issued third-party subpoenas and scheduled depositions during that period.” (Ibid.)

 

However, this “harm” described are costs ordinarily incurred in the course of arbitration or refusal to participate in arbitration. They are not “compensable harm” as described by Hohenshelt. Instead, Hohenshelt identifies “compensable harm” as consequences that are “significant to the employee, who may not be able to pay bills, rent or other expenses that could result in the loss of their residence, or damage to their credit rating, while the dispute remains unresolved.” (Hohenshelt, supra, at 339.) Here, Plaintiff does not identify any such extreme hardship; instead, Plaintiff identifies the ordinary costs and expenses of arbitration and litigation.

 

Based on the foregoing, the Court finds that arbitration remains proper pursuant to Hohenshelt.

 

IV.  Conclusion

 

Based on the foregoing, the instant motion is GRANTED. The parties are ordered back into arbitration and the action is stayed.

 

Moving party to give notice.
