Case Number: 19STCV05324    Hearing Date: July 8, 2026    Dept: 32

 

CENTRAVEL, INC.,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

FERDINAND LIPANA, et al.,

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  19STCV05324

  Hearing Date:  July 8, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

plaintiff’s motions to compel further responses (CRS# 1113, 0205)

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On February 15, 2019, Plaintiff Centravel, Inc. filed this action against Ferdinand Lipana, Bernadine Lipana, Lawrence Lipana, Lancelle Lipana, and Sandrine Lipana. Lawrence, Lancelle, and Sadrine Lipana were subsequently dismissed from the lawsuit.

            On August 15, 2022, the Court entered a stipulated judgment in favor of Plaintiff against Defendants Ferdinand Lipana and Bernadine Lipana.

            On June 3, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant two motions to compel Ferdinand’s and Bernadine’s further responses to post-judgment Requests for Production, Set Two. Defendants filed a combined opposition on June 16, 2026. Plaintiff filed a combined reply on June 30, 2026.

LEGAL STANDARD

Upon receiving responses to its discovery requests, the propounding party may move for an order compelling further responses if the responses are incomplete or evasive, or objections are without merit or too general. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300(a), 2031.310(a), 2033.290(a).)

 

DISCUSSION

            Plaintiff’s motion and exhibits demonstrate good cause for the requested documents. (See Ponce-Gomez Decl., Ex. 2, 3.) Plaintiff, as the judgment creditor, is entitled to conduct discovery into Defendants’ assets and financial condition. The scope of permissible discovery is broad. (See Li v. Yan (2016) 247 Cal.App.4th 56, 66; Yolanda's, Inc. v. Kahl & Goveia Commercial Real Estate (2017) 11 Cal.App.5th 509, 515.) Defendants argue that the Set Two RFPs are duplicative. However, Plaintiff is entitled to updated and current information. Thus, there is good cause for the discovery.

            Defendants served a joint response to the RFPs, consisting of the following four objections: (1) violation of 120-day “blackout period;” (2) lack of standing; (3) pending appeal; and (4) financial privacy. (Ponce-Gomez Decl., Ex. 5.) None of these objections has merit.

First, the Court has already rejected Defendants’ contentions regarding the 120-day period. Specifically, the Set Two RFPs were served in April 2026, more than 120 days after Defendants responded to the first set of discovery in July 2025, and more than 120 days after the judgment debtor examinations in October 2025. Defendants erroneously rely on their December 12, 2025 production of documents, which was not in response to any discovery request and is thus not relevant to the 120-day rule. In any case, the Set Two RFPs were served more than 120 days after December 12, 2025. Thus, Plaintiff has complied with Code of Civil Procedure section 708.030.

Second, the Court has stated multiple times that Plaintiff retains standing to pursue the action and enforce judgment despite being dissolved. The law is clear that “[a] corporation which is dissolved nevertheless continues to exist for the purpose of winding up its affairs, prosecuting and defending actions by or against it and enabling it to collect and discharge obligations, dispose of and convey its property and collect and divide its assets, but not for the purpose of continuing business except so far as necessary for the winding up thereof.” (Corp. Code, § 2010(a).)

Third, Defendants’ Petition for Writ of Supersedeas has been denied. (Ponce-Gomez Decl., Ex. 8.) Thus, there is no basis to stay discovery.

Fourth, privacy does not preclude the subject discovery. As stated above, the scope of judgment debtor discovery is broad. The need for disclosure outweighs the privacy interests. (See Williams v. Sup. Ct. (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 552.)

In sum, Defendants’ objections are meritless, and Defendants have failed to provide any valid justification for refusing to respond to the subject discovery. Thus, an order compelling responses, with sanctions, is warranted. Plaintiff requests $1,487.50 per motion. (Ponce-Gomez Decl. ¶ 12.) The Court finds that this is the reasonable amount for both motions combined.

CONCLUSION

            Plaintiff’s motions to compel further responses are GRANTED. Defendants Ferdinand Lipana and Bernadine Lipana shall provide further responses to the subject Requests for Production, Set Two within 30 days of today. The Court sanctions Defendants, jointly and severally, in the total amount of $1,487.50, to be paid within 30 days of today.

 

 


Case Number: 21STCV00366    Hearing Date: July 8, 2026    Dept: 32

 

ashley olsen, an individual

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

RANCHO PHYSICAL THERAPY, INC., a California corporation; and DOES 1-20, inclusive,

                       

                       Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  21STCV00366

  Hearing Date:  July 8, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

plaintiff’s motion to withdraw from arbitration

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On January 6, 2021, Plaintiff Ashley Olsen filed suit against Defendant Rancho Physical Therapy, Inc. (Rancho), alleging nine causes of action stemming from alleged discrimination, retaliation, and wrongful termination.

            On July 14, 2021, the Court granted Rancho’s motion to compel arbitration.

            On January 18, 2023, Plaintiff filed a motion to compel Rancho to pay arbitration fees.

            On January 30, 2023, the Court granted the parties’ stipulation to arbitrate the matter and for Rancho to pay Plaintiff’s case administrative fee of $1,500.

            On December 10, 2024, Plaintiff filed an amendment naming OptimisCorp as Doe 1.

            On May 19, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion to withdraw from arbitration. Defendants filed an opposition on June 25, 2026. Plaintiff filed a reply on June 30, 2026.

LEGAL STANDARD

“In an employment or consumer arbitration that requires . . . that the drafting party pay certain fees and costs during the pendency of an arbitration proceeding, if the fees or costs required to continue the arbitration proceeding are not paid within 30 days after the due date, the drafting party is in material breach of the arbitration agreement, is in default of the arbitration, and waives its right to compel the employee or consumer to proceed with that arbitration as a result of the material breach.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.98(a)(1).) “If the drafting party materially breaches the arbitration agreement and is in default under subdivision (a), the employee or consumer may unilaterally elect to . . . [w]ithdraw the claim from arbitration and proceed in a court of appropriate jurisdiction.” (Id., § 1281.98(b)(1).)

            However, the Supreme Court has rejected a “rigid construction” of the statute which inflexibly mandates waiver of arbitral rights in every instance of untimely payment. (Hohenshelt v. Superior Court (2025) 18 Cal.5th 310, 323.) Instead, the court reiterated “the long-standing principle, established by statute and common law, that one party’s nonperformance of an obligation automatically extinguishes the other party’s contractual duties only when nonperformance is willful, grossly negligent, or fraudulent.” (Ibid.) Section 1281.98 does not “strip companies and employers of their contractual right to arbitration where nonpayment of fees results from a good faith mistake, inadvertence, or other excusable neglect.” (Ibid.)

DISCUSSION

            Plaintiff moves to withdraw from arbitration based on Defendants’ failure to pay a $154,000 invoice issued on November 7, 2025. (Diamond Decl. ¶ 18, Ex. M.) On November 24, 2025, Defendants indicated to the arbitrator that they would not be able to pay by the deadline of December 7, 2025. (Id., ¶ 19.) Defendants did not pay by December 7, 2025. (Id., ¶ 21.) On January 27, 2026, the arbitrator terminated the arbitration. (Id., ¶ 25, Ex. R.)

            Defendants do not oppose reinstating the court action. However, Defendants request a reprieve from sanctions given the circumstances behind the nonpayment. Specifically, the Palisades Fire destroyed Defendants’ primary place of business and created severe economic strain.

            Sections 1281.98 and 1281.99 provide for mandatory monetary sanctions in the event of a breach. (See Code Civ. Proc., §§ 1281.98(c)(2) [“The court shall impose sanctions on the drafting party in accordance with Section 1281.99”]; 1281.99 [“The court shall impose a monetary sanction against a drafting party that materially breaches an arbitration agreement”].) “[S]ection 1281.99’s mandatory sanction provision . . . requires the drafting party to pay any reasonable expenses incurred by the employee or consumer as a result of its failure to timely pay arbitration fees . . . whether the nonpayment was willful or not.” (Hohenshelt, supra, 18 Cal.5th at p. 339.)   

However, Plaintiff presents no evidence of the reasonable expenses incurred as a result of the breach. Thus, the Court has no information by which to determine the amount of appropriate sanctions. Instead, Plaintiff requests permission to file a separate motion for attorney’s fees. (Mtn. 10:13-16.) However, such a motion is permissive. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.98(c)(1) [“The employee or consumer may bring a motion, or a separate action, to recover all attorney’s fees and all costs associated with the abandoned arbitration proceeding].) The Court finds that recovery of such attorney’s fees is unwarranted under the circumstances. The Court would have been inclined to grant sanctions under section 1281.99(a) had Plaintiff identified a reasonable amount—i.e., “the reasonable expenses, including attorney’s fees and costs, incurred by the employee or consumer as a result of the material breach.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.99(a).)

CONCLUSION

            Plaintiff’s motion to withdraw from arbitration is GRANTED.


Case Number: 24STCV18864    Hearing Date: July 8, 2026    Dept: 32

 

KARLA LEIVA,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

AIDS HEALTHCARE FOUNDATION,

                        Defendant.

 

  Case No.:  24STCV18864

  Hearing Date:  July 8, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

defendant’s motion to compel further responses

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On July 26, 2024, Plaintiff Karla Leiva filed this action against Defendant Aids Healthcare Foundation for employment discrimination and retaliation. Plaintiff alleges that she was terminated while on disability leave after protesting and reporting Defendant’s illegal activities, including unsafe or uninhabitable conditions.

            On June 10, 2026, Defendant filed the instant motion to compel further responses to Requests for Admission and the associated Form Interrogatory No. 17.1. Plaintiff filed an opposition on June 24, 2026. Defendant filed a reply on June 30, 2026.  

LEGAL STANDARD

Upon receiving responses to its discovery requests, the propounding party may move for an order compelling further responses if the responses are incomplete or evasive, or objections are without merit or too general. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300(a), 2031.310(a), 2033.290(a).)

 

 

DISCUSSION

            The RFAs at issue seek information about Plaintiff’s personal life and events dating back to childhood. These inquiries intrude on Plaintiff’s privacy, and Defendant has not demonstrated good cause or direct relevance. (See Williams v. Sup. Ct. (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 552; Davis v. Superior Court (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1008, 1017.) Plaintiff’s interest in privacy outweighs the need for disclosure. (Ibid.)

            Defendant argues that it is entitled to discover potential alternative causes of Plaintiff’s alleged emotional distress. However, Defendant has failed to show that the specific information requested in the RFAs is properly tailored to this purpose. The information is remote in time, and Defendant merely speculates that certain aspects of Plaintiff’s personal life or events in her personal life may have contributed to her emotional distress. Defendant fails to “demonstrate there is a nexus between damages from termination and those which may arise out of” the topics mentioned in the RFAs. (See Tylo v. Superior Court (1997) 55 Cal.App.4th 1379, 1388.) Defendant “merely assert[s] the conclusion that there are ‘other stressors that might have caused, or contributed to, [Plaintiff’s] alleged emotional injuries,’ a true fishing expedition.” (Ibid., emphasis in original.)

It is well-settled that filing a lawsuit “does not automatically open all of a plaintiff's past medical history to scrutiny.” (Britt v. Superior Court (1978) 20 Cal.3d 844, 849.) Questions about Plaintiff’s personal life, including her sexuality, drug usage, academics, and family relationships, go beyond the permissible scope of discovery in relation to the issues in litigation. Defendant argues that the information is not speculative because Plaintiff herself brought up the topics during mental health treatment. But Defendant has not established that the issues mentioned during therapy are related to the emotional distress damages alleged in this action. The mere fact that something is mentioned does not automatically establish the requisite nexus. Defendant is ultimately relying on speculation to draw the connection.  

CONCLUSION

            Defendant’s motion to compel further responses is DENIED.


Case Number: 24STCV24222    Hearing Date: July 8, 2026    Dept: 32

 

JOSE ARELLANO,

                        Plaintiff,

            v.

 

DIAMOND CONSTRUCTION, INC., et al.,

                        Defendants.

 

  Case No.:  24STCV24222

  Hearing Date:  July 8, 2026

 

     [TENTATIVE] order RE:

plaintiff’s motion to compel deposition

 

 

BACKGROUND

            On September 18, 2024, Plaintiff Jose Arellano filed this action against Defendants Diamond Construction, Inc. (DCI) and Thomas Anastasios Christopoulos (Anastasios Christopoulos) for wage violations and retaliation. On February 21, 2025, Tom Christopoulos (Tom Christopoulos) was added as Doe 1.

            On June 1, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion to compel the deposition of DCI’s person most knowledgeable. DCI filed an opposition on June 24, 2026. Plaintiff filed a reply on June 30, 2026.

LEGAL STANDARD

“If, after service of a deposition notice, a party to the action . . . without having served a valid objection under Section 2025.410, fails to appear for examination . . . or to produce for inspection any document, electronically stored information, or tangible thing described in the deposition notice, the party giving the notice may move for an order compelling the deponent’s attendance and testimony, and the production for inspection of any document … described in the deposition notice.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.450(a).)

DISCUSSION

            The instant motion concerns the deposition notice for DCI’s PMK, issued on March 27, 2026. (Chami Decl. ¶ 15, Ex. G.) The notice set the deposition for May 28, 2026. (Ibid.) On May 20, 2026, defense counsel mailed objections, which Plaintiff received on May 26, 2026. (Id., ¶ 20, Ex. I; Gemoets Decl. ¶ 8.) The objections indicated that the PMK would not appear on May 28, 2026 due to unavailability. (Ibid.) According to defense counsel, the PMK (Tom Christopoulos) suffered a work emergency, and counsel himself had a conflicting hearing in another matter. (Gemoets Decl. ¶ 7.) The PMK did not appear for deposition on May 28, 2026. (Chami Decl. ¶ 21.) The parties were subsequently unable to agree on alternative dates. (Id., ¶¶ 23-25; Gamoets Decl. ¶¶ 8-9.)

These facts indicate that an order compelling the deposition is warranted. However, sanctions are unwarranted as both parties acted with substantial justification.

CONCLUSION

            Plaintiff’s motion to compel deposition is GRANTED. Defendant Diamond Construction, Inc.’s person most knowledgeable shall appear for deposition on            , 2026. Sanctions are denied.
