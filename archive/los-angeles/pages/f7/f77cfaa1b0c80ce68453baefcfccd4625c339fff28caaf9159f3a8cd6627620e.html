III.         LEGAL STANDARD

The function of a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party cannot show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. (See Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) In analyzing such motions, courts must apply a three-step analysis: “(1) identify the issues framed by the pleadings; (2) determine whether the moving party has negated the opponent’s claims; and (3) determine whether the opposition has demonstrated the existence of a triable, material factual issue.” (Hinesley v. Oakshade Town Center (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 289, 294.) Thus, summary judgment is granted when, after the Court’s consideration of the evidence set forth in the papers and all reasonable inferences accordingly, no triable issues of fact exist, and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 437c(c);[1] Villa v. McFarren (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 733, 741.)¿ 

“On a motion for summary judgment, the initial burden is always on the moving party to make a prima facie showing that there are no triable issues of material fact.” (Scalf v. D.B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1519.) As to each claim as framed by the complaint, a defendant moving for summary judgment must satisfy the initial burden of proof by presenting facts to negate or establish an essential element. (See id., 128 Cal.App.4th at 1520.) Courts “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.” (Dore v. Arnold Worldwide, Inc.¿(2006) 39 Cal.4th 384, 389.) A motion for summary judgment must be denied where the moving party’s evidence does not prove all material facts, even in the absence of any opposition. (See Leyva v. Superior Court (1985) 164 Cal.App.3d 462, 475; Salesguevara v. Wyeth Labs., Inc. (1990) 222 Cal.App.3d 379, 384.)

Once the moving party has met its burden, the burden shifts to the opposing party to show via specific facts that a triable issue of material fact exists to a cause of action or a defense thereto. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 437c(o)(2).)

C.             Moving Defendants’ Motion for Summary Judgment is Granted

The Complaint alleges Plaintiff sought care and treatment with Dr. Sehgal on June 3, 2009. Plaintiff alleges Dr. Sehgal performed a guaiac test without consent and for no medical purpose, but instead for a sexual purpose. The Complaint alleges: (1) Sexual Rape; (2) Sexual Battery Pursuant to Civil Code § 1708.5; (3) Assault; (4) Battery; (5) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress; and (6) Professional Negligence. The sixth cause of action is pled only against SCPMG.

1.               Statute of Limitations – Code of Civil Procedure § 340.5

Code of Civil Procedure § 340.5 provides relevant part as follows.

“In an action for injury or death against a health care provider based upon such person’s alleged professional negligence, the time for the commencement of action shall be three years after the date of injury or one year after the plaintiff discovers, or through the use of reasonable diligence should have discovered, the injury, whichever occurs first. In no event shall the time for commencement of legal action exceed three years unless tolled for any of the following: ¿(1) upon proof of fraud, (2) intentional concealment, or (3) the presence of a foreign body, which has no therapeutic or diagnostic purpose or effect, in the person of the injured person.” (emphasis added.)

 

“The patient is charged with ‘presumptive’ knowledge of his negligent injury, and the statute commences to run, once he has “‘notice or information of circumstances to put a reasonable person on inquiry, or has the opportunity to obtain knowledge from sources open to his investigation...”’ (Gutierrez v. Mofid (1985) 39 Cal.3d 892, 896-97, quoting Sanchez v. South Hoover Hospital (1976) 18 Cal.3d 93, 101 [emphasis in original].)

Moving Defendants argue section 340.5 applies to Plaintiff’s claims as the gravamen of his claims are for professional negligence and Plaintiff is unable to establish he was sexually assaulted such that section 340.16[2] applies. (See Mot. at 6:12-19, 8:14-25, 10:9-11, citing Larson v. UHS of Rancho Springs, Inc. (2014) 230 Cal.App.4th 336.) In support of the latter, Moving Defendants cite to Plaintiff’s allegation that “without a written or verbal consent, without explaining anything and without Plaintiff’s permission…Dr. Ashish, Sehgal with gloves inserted his index finger into Plaintiff’s anus for a period of time.” (UMF No. 13.) Moving Defendants submit Plaintiff’s responses to interrogatories seeking all facts that the guaiac test had no professional purpose and was motivated by sexual gratification. Plaintiff responded the guaiac test had no professional purpose because his complaints had no relation to his genitalia and Dr. Sehgal was the only doctor who suggested and performed this exam. (See id., Nos. 14-15.)

Moving Defendants argue the alleged injury occurred on June 3, 2009, and thus Plaintiff’s claims were time-barred on June 3, 2012. (See UMF Nos. 1-2.) Moving Defendants additionally argue Plaintiff was on inquiry notice when the alleged conduct occurred because Plaintiff testified at his deposition he was aware of Dr. Sehgal’s inserting his finger into his rectum at the subject visit and believed it was wrong. (See id., Nos. 18-19.) Moving Defendants further argue, at best, the statue was tolled until 2018 because Plaintiff received a copy of his medical records on July 6, 2017, and that is when Plaintiff testified he “came to think that evaluation - - or the incident was not a medical evaluation properly done...” (See id., Nos. 9, 16.)

The Court finds Defendants do not show that Plaintiff’s first through fifth claims are at their core, professional negligence claims. “Section 340.5 is part of the Medical Injury Compensation Reform Act (MICRA)…when a plaintiff asserts a claim against a health care provider on a legal theory other than professional negligence, courts must determine whether the claim is nonetheless based on the health care provider's professional negligence, which would require application of MICRA. [Citation.] To make that determination, courts must examine not only the legal theory alleged, but also the nature of the health care provider's alleged conduct and the legislative history of the MICRA provision at issue. [Citations.] When, as here, the question presented concerns which limitations period applies, courts also must focus on the nature or gravamen of the claim, not the label or form of action the plaintiff selects.” (Larson v. UHS of Rancho Springs, Inc. (2014) 230 Cal.App.4th 336, 346-47.) Here, Plaintiff’s six causes of action are based on Dr. Sehgal’s performing a guaiac test “without a written or verbal consent…and without Plaintiff’s permission.” (See SAC ¶¶ 18, 26, 42, 46, 61, 74, 86.) Though Moving Defendants present evidence by way of interrogatories that Plaintiff lacks evidence to support his intentional tort claims, Moving Defendants do not submit an undisputed fact or evidence showing that Plaintiff consented to Dr. Sehgal’s performing the guaiac test such that the Court could treat these claims as ones based on professional negligence. (See Saxena v. Goffney (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 316, 324 [“An action ‘should be pleaded in negligence’ when the doctor performs an operation to which plaintiff consents, but without disclosing sufficient information about the risks inherent in the surgery.”].) Because Plaintiff’s intentional tort claims are based on the theory Dr. Sehgal performed a medical test without any consent and for an ulterior purpose, and Moving Defendants haven’t submitted evidence showing Plaintiff consented to the guaiac test, the Court finds Plaintiff’s first through fifth claims aren’t based on professional negligence. (See Cobbs v. Grant (1972) 8 Cal.3d 229, 240.)

As to Plaintiff’s sixth cause of action for professional negligence asserted against SCPMG, the Court cannot conclude section 340.5 applies to this claim. Section 340.5 applies to claims for professional negligence asserted against a health care provider. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 340.5.) A health care provider is defined as a licensed person, licensed clinic, licensed health dispensary, licensed health facility, or the legal representatives of a health care provider. (See id.) Moving Defendants haven’t presented any undisputed facts or evidence showing SCPMG is a health care provider within the meaning of the statute. Accordingly, the Court does not apply the statute of limitations imposed by § 340.5 on Plaintiff’s sixth cause of action.

Accordingly, the Court DENIES Defendants’ Motion on grounds Plaintiff’s claims are time barred by Code of Civil Procedure § 340.5.

2.               1st through 5th Causes of Action Against Dr. Seghal

a.               First and Second Causes of Action for Sexual Rape and Sexual Battery

Civil Code § 1708.5 provides, in relevant part,

(a) A person commits a sexual battery who does any of the following:

 

(1) Acts with the intent to cause a harmful or offensive contact with an intimate part of another, and a sexually offensive contact with that person directly or indirectly results.

(2) Acts with the intent to cause a harmful or offensive contact with another by use of the person's intimate part, and a sexually offensive contact with that person directly or indirectly results.

(3) Acts to cause an imminent apprehension of the conduct described in paragraph (1) or (2), and a sexually offensive contact with that person directly or indirectly results.

…

(d) … (2) “Offensive contact” means contact that offends a reasonable sense of personal dignity.

 

 (Civ. Code § 1708.5.)

“A cause of action for sexual battery under Civil Code section 1708.5 requires the batterer intend to cause a ‘harmful or offensive’ contact and the batteree suffer a ‘sexually offensive contact.’ Moreover, the section is interpreted to require that the batteree did not consent to the contact.” (Angie M. v. Superior Ct. (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 1217, 1225 [internal citation omitted].)

As an initial matter, as the Court isn’t aware of a cause of action for “sexual rape” per se, it will treat Plaintiff’s first cause of action for sexual rape as a sexual battery claim. This approach renders the first claim duplicative of the second. A duplicative cause of action “is the sort of defect that, if it justifies any judicial intervention at all, is ordinarily dealt with most economically at trial, or on a dispositive motion such as summary judgment.” (Blickman Turkus, LP v. MF Downtown Sunnyvale, LLC (2008) 162 Cal.App.4th 858, 890.)

Moving Defendants meet their prima facie burden in negating the elements of intent to cause harmful or offensive contact or apprehension of the same and a sexually offensive contact resulted. Moving Defendants submit the declaration of expert David Barcay, M.D. (See UMF No. 20.) Dr. Barcay is Board Certified in Emergency Medicine, Internal Medicine, and Critical Care Medicine and is familiar with the standard of care applicable to the relative timeframe in the emergency room setting. (See id.) Dr. Barcay opines a guaiac test was medically indicated because Plaintiff presented to the emergency department with lower abdominal pain, these complaints were noted by Dr. Sehgal and the emergency department nurse, and his lab work indicated a concern for abdominal pain. (See id.) Dr. Barcay opines “[Plaintiff’s] description, at deposition, of the manner in which the test was performed is consistent with the manner in which the test is to be performed.” (Id.) Dr. Barcay further opines “it is my opinion that the standard of care does not require a nurse to be present for a Guaiac test. A nurse would only be required as a chaperone, if the patient was of the opposite sex to the doctor.” (Id.) Dr. Barcay “conclude[s] to a reasonable degree of medical probability that the care provided to Mr. Flores by Dr. Seghal in the Emergency Department was within the standard of care.” (Id.)

Moving Defendants’ evidence Dr. Sehgal’s performance of a guaiac test was within the standard of care tends to show Dr. Sehgal did not intend to cause harmful or offensive contact, as Dr. Sehgal performed a guaiac exam because it was medically indicated. This evidence also tends to show a sexually offensive contact did not occur because Dr. Sehgal performed the exam in an appropriate manner. Thus, the burden shifts to Plaintiff to raise a triable issue.

Plaintiff fails to raise a triable issue. Plaintiff submits as evidence Dr. Sehgal’s responses to Plaintiff’s Requests for Admission, this Court’s January 7, 2025, Order, and a copy of a subpoena he propounded on Defendants’ expert. (See Sep. Stmt., Exs. A-C.) Plaintiff cites to Exhibit C with respect to Moving Defendant’s UMF No. 20. This evidence does not show Dr. Sehgal intended to cause harmful or offensive contact or apprehension of the same or Plaintiff suffered a sexually offensive contact. Thus, Plaintiff simply fails to raise a triable issue of material fact.

Accordingly, the Court GRANTS Moving Defendants’ Motion for Summary Adjudication as to the First and Second Causes of Action for Sexual Battery.   

b.              Third and Fourth Causes of Action for Assault and Battery

“The essential elements of a cause of action for assault are: (1) defendant acted with intent to cause harmful or offensive contact, or threatened to touch plaintiff in a harmful or offensive manner; (2) plaintiff reasonably believed [he or] she was about to be touched in a harmful or offensive manner or it reasonably appeared to plaintiff that defendant was about to carry out the threat; (3) plaintiff did not consent to defendant’s conduct; (4) plaintiff was harmed; and (5) defendant’s conduct was a substantial factor in causing plaintiff’s harm.” (So v. Shin (2013) 212 Cal.App.4th 652, 668-669.) 

In the medical context, the elements of battery are (1) “a doctor performs an operation to which the patient has not consented” or “a doctor obtains consent of the patient to perform one type of treatment and subsequently performs a substantially different treatment for which consent was not obtained....’”; (2) plaintiff was harmed; and (3) defendant’s conduct was a substantial factor in causing plaintiff’s harm. (Cobbs v. Grant (1972) 8 Cal.3d 229, 240; CACI 530A.) 

Moving Defendants argue they are entitled to summary adjudication on the claims for assault and battery because Dr. Barcay opined the guaiac test was medically indicated and performed within the standard of care. (See Mot., at 13:14-19; UMF No. 20.) Dr. Barcay opined, based on a review of Plaintiff’s medical charts, that the test was indicated based on Plaintiff’s abdominal pain complaints and “no act or omission on the part of Dr. Seghal with respect to the care and treatment he provided to [Plaintiff], caused or substantially contributed to plaintiffs alleged injuries.” (See UMF No. 20.) Moving Defendants further contend Plaintiff will be unable to put forth any evidence establishing Dr. Sehgal’s intent to harm Plaintiff. (See Mot. at 13:17-18.)

The Court finds Moving Defendants meet their burden in negating an element of Plaintiff’s assault and battery claims. As discussed above, the Court finds evidence Dr. Sehgal performed a guaiac test because it was medically indicated and complied with the standard of care carries Moving Defendants’ burden to likewise negate the intent or threat to cause harmful or offensive contact element of the assault claim.

Moving Defendants also negate the second and third element of the battery claim that Plaintiff was harmed and that their conduct was a substantial factor in causing Plaintiff’s harm. The evidence Moving Defendants submit tends to show Plaintiff was not harmed because Dr. Sehgal performed the Guaiac test within the standard of care. Defendant’s expert’s testimony also opined Dr. Sehgal’s conduct wasn’t a substantial factor in causing Plaintiff’s alleged harm.

Accordingly, the Court GRANTS Defendants’ Motion as to the Third and Fourth Causes of Action for Assault and Battery, respectively, as to Dr. Sehgal.

c.               Fifth Cause of Action for Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress

“The elements of a prima facie case for the tort of intentional infliction of emotional distress are: (1) extreme and outrageous conduct by the defendant with the intention of causing, or reckless disregard of the probability of causing, emotional distress; (2) the plaintiff’s suffering severe or extreme emotional distress; and (3) actual and proximate causation of the emotional distress by the defendant’s outrageous conduct. Conduct to be outrageous must be so extreme as to exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated in a civilized community.” (Wilson v. Hynek (2012) 207 Cal.App.4th 999, 1009, citation and ellipses omitted.) “Whether conduct qualifies as outrageous is usually a question of fact, but a court may determine in the first instance whether, as a matter of law, alleged conduct may reasonably be regarded as so extreme and outrageous as to allow the matter to proceed to a trier of fact.” (Sandoval v. Pali Institute, Inc. (2025) 113 Cal.App.5th 616, 635.)

Defendants argue they are entitled to summary adjudication on this claim because the true nature of Plaintiff’s claim is one of medical negligence based on lack of informed consent and such claim does not support a finding of extreme and outrageous conduct. (See Mot. at 15:22-25.)

The Court finds Moving Defendants meet their burden in negating the extreme and outrageous element. As discussed above, Moving Defendants submit the undisputed fact and evidence that Dr. Sehgal’s performance of a Guaiac test was within the standard of care. (See UMF No. 20.) The Court finds evidence showing Dr. Sehgal performed a medical test in a manner that met the standard of care supports a showing the conduct was not “so extreme as to exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated in a civilized community.” Thus, the burden shifts to Plaintiff to raise a triable issue of fact.

Plaintiff has failed to do so. Plaintiff argues this claim is valid and relies on this Court’s January 7, 2025, Order as evidence. (See Opp. at 8:3-6; UMF No. 26 [incorporating UMF Nos. 1-22].) This evidence is insufficient to raise a triable issue.

Accordingly, the Court GRANTS Moving Defendant’s Motion as to the Fifth Cause of Action for Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress against Dr. Sehgal.

3.               1st through 6th Causes of Action Against SCPMG

Plaintiff’s first through sixth claims against SCPMG are based on theories of SCPMG’s vicarious liability and its direct liability in negligently hiring, retaining, and supervising Dr. Sehgal. (See Mot. at 16:4-6; SAC, ¶¶ 30-31, 49-50, 64-65, 78, 89-90, 103-104.) “A motion for summary adjudication shall be granted only if it completely disposes of a cause of action, an affirmative defense, a claim for damages, or an issue of duty.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 437c(f)(1).) Accordingly, Moving Defendants must negate both theories to be entitled to adjudication on each claim.

///

a.               Negligent Hiring, Retention, Supervision

“[A]n employer can be liable to a third person for negligently hiring, supervising, or retaining an unfit employee.” (Doe v. Capital Cities (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 1038, 1054 (“Capital Cities”).) The elements of a cause of action for negligent hiring, retention, or supervision are (1) the employer’s hiring, retaining, or supervising an employee; (2) the employee was incompetent or unfit; (3) the employer knew or should have known the employee was unfit; and (4) harm occurs. (See Evan F. v. Hughson United Methodist Church (1992) 8 Cal.App.4th 828, 836-837.)

“To establish negligent supervision, a plaintiff must show that a person in a supervisorial position over the actor had prior knowledge of the actor's propensity to do the bad act.” (See Z.V. v. County of Riverside (2015) 238 Cal.App.4th 889, 902; see also Capital Cities, 50 Cal.App.4th at 1054.) “[T]he cornerstone of a negligent hiring theory is the risk that the employee will act in a certain way and the employee does act in that way.” (Capital Cities, 50 Cal.App.4th at 1038, 1054-1055.) 

Moving Defendants argue they cannot be held directly liable for Dr. Sehgal’s conduct based on a theory of negligent supervision. Moving Defendants submit Dr. Sehgal’s declaration in which he attests he has never been tried or convicted of any crime related to sexual assault. (See UMF No. 21.) Moving Defendants also submit the declaration of Debra Nelson, Director of Physician Relations for SCPMG, who attests SCPMG was “not aware of any complaints of a sexual nature being made against Dr. Sehgal prior to [Plaintiff’s] allegations.” (See id., No. 22.)

The Court finds Moving Defendants meet their burden in negating the element that they knew or should have known Dr. Sehgal was unfit because their evidence negates SCPMG knew or should have known Dr. Sehgal was unfit due to a propensity to engage in sexual misconduct as he had no history of such misconduct. Thus, the burden shifts to Plaintiff to raise a triable issue.

 Plaintiff fails to raise a triable issue. Plaintiff merely argues this claim is valid and relies on his Complaint and medical records as evidence. (See Opp. at 8:14-11:8; UMF No. 6-11.) This evidence fails to raise a triable issue as to this theory of liability.

The Court next considers whether Defendants negate the vicarious liability theory.

b.              Vicarious Liability

 “[A] principal who personally engages in no misconduct may be vicariously liable for the tortious act committed by an agent within the course and scope of the agency...” (Barenborg v. Sigma Alpha Epsilon Fraternity (2019) 33 Cal. App.5th 70, 85 (internal citations and quotations omitted).)

A principal may be vicariously liable for injury committed by an act of its agent where (1) the principal directly authorizes the act to be committed, (2) the agent commits the act in the scope of their agency and in performing service on behalf of the principal, or (3) the principal ratifies its agent’s conduct after the fact by electing to adopt the agent’s conduct. (See Doe v. Roman Catholic Archbishop of Los Angeles (2016) 247 Cal.App.4th 953, 969.)

          Moving Defendants argue SCPMG cannot be held vicariously liable for Dr. Sehgal's alleged misconduct. (See Mot. at 21:1-4.) Moving Defendants rely on Lisa M. v. Henry Mayo Newhall Memorial Hospital (1995) 12 Cal.4th 291 for this position.

          Because the Court granted Moving Defendants’ Motion as to Dr. Sehgal, there is no basis for vicarious liability against SCPMG. Accordingly, Moving Defendants successfully negate the theory of vicarious liability.  

          Moreover, the Court finds the instant facts are comparable to those in Lisa M. and thus the Court finds SCPMG cannot be held vicariously liable for Dr. Sehgal’s alleged misconduct as a matter of law. There, the California Supreme Court held “respondeat superior liability requires that the risk of the tort have been engendered by, ‘typical of or broadly incidental to,’ or, viewed from a somewhat different perspective, ‘a generally foreseeable consequence of,’ Hospital’s enterprise.., a sexual tort will not be considered engendered by the employment unless its motivating emotions were fairly attributable to work-related events or conditions.” (Lisa M., 12 Cal.4th at 298, 301.) In Lisa M., the Supreme Court held that because a technician’s personal motivations to commit a sexual tort during the performance of an ultrasound exam “were not generated by or an outgrowth of workplace responsibilities”, so as to render defendant hospital vicariously liable. (Id., 12 Cal. 4th at 301-02.)

          Here, the SAC’s theory of vicarious liability is based on Dr. Sehgal’s alleged performance of a Guaiac test for his sexual gratification. (See e.g. SAC, ¶¶ 21, 44.) Moving Defendants’ evidence shows Dr. Sehgal performed a Guaiac test during Plaintiff’s emergency department visit in compliance with the standard of care and because it was medically indicated. (See UMF Nos. 4, 6, 20.) This evidence negates that Dr. Sehgal performed a guaiac test because he was motivated by sexual gratification. Assuming arguendo the Motion as to Dr. Sehgal was not granted and Plaintiff met their burden of raising a triable issue as to whether Dr. Sehgal was motivated by sexual gratification, SCPMG would still not be vicariously liable. Like Lisa M. Court concluded, the Court finds Dr. Sehgal’s sexual gratification motivation would not be engendered by or an outgrowth of his responsibilities to provide emergency care to patients. Dr. Sehgal’s workplace responsibilities to evaluate Plaintiff during his emergency care visit would provide no trigger for Dr. Sehgal to exploit Plaintiff and any “conscious exploitation of the patient [would thus] not arise out of the performance of the examination, although the circumstances of the examination made it possible.” Accordingly, SCPMG could not be held vicariously liable for a sexual tort committed by Dr. Sehgal during the provision of his emergency care services to Plaintiff.

Because Moving Defendants have negated both theories of liability as to SCPMG asserted in all claims, the Court GRANTS Defendants’ Motion as to SCPMG.

V.             CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, the Court GRANTS Moving Defendants’ Motion for Summary Judgment as to Dr. Sehgal and SCPMG. Given there are no defendants remaining in this action, all future dates are vacated.

I.               BACKGROUND 

This is a lemon law action. Plaintiff Narek Bagirian (“Plaintiff”) alleges Defendant American Honda Motor Co., Inc. (“Defendant”) manufactured Plaintiff’s 2024 Honda Accord which was delivered with and developed serious defects and nonconformities to warranty. Plaintiff moves to compel further responses to his Form Interrogatories (“FROGs”) Set One.

II.            PROCEDURAL HISTORY

On August 15, 2025, Plaintiff filed a Complaint alleging (1) Violation of Song-Beverly Act - Breach of Express Warranty; (2) Violation of Song-Beverly Act - Breach of Implied Warranty; and (3) Violation of The Song-Beverly Act Section 1793.2(b).

On September 25, 2025, Defendant filed an Answer.

On June 8, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant Motion to Compel Further.

On July 8, 2026, Defendant filed an Opposition.

On July 14, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Reply.

III.         LEGAL STANDARD

A propounding party may move for an order compelling further responses to interrogatories if the responding party produced an evasive or incomplete answer or a meritless or overly general objection in response to an interrogatory. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.300(a).) A motion to compel further responses to interrogatories lies where the party to whom the interrogatories were directed gave responses deemed improper by the propounding party; e.g., objections, or evasive or incomplete answers. (See id. at § 2030.300.)¿The moving party must also include reasons why further answers should be ordered: legal or factual arguments why the answers given were incomplete or nonresponsive, or the objections invalid.¿(See Rules of Court, rule 3.1345(c).)  

However, where a timely motion to has been filed, “the burden is on the responding party to justify any objection or failure to fully answer the interrogatories.”¿(Weil & Brown, Cal. Prac. Guide: Civil Pro. Before Trial (The Rutter Group 2017) ¶ 8:1179.)¿“The ruling usually is based on consideration of the following factors: [¶] the relationship of the information sought to the issues framed in the pleadings; [¶] the likelihood that disclosure will be of practical benefit to the party seeking discovery; [¶] the burden or expense likely to be encountered by the responding party in furnishing the information sought.”¿(Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc. v. Superior Court (Rolfe) (1968) 263 Cal.App.2d 12, 19.) 

IV.          MEET AND CONFER

Before bringing a motion to compel further responses to any discovery request, the moving party must make efforts to meet and confer in good faith and submit a declaration showing a reasonable and good faith attempt, either in person, by telephone, or by videoconference, to informally resolve each issue presented by the motion. (See Code Civ. Proc. §§ 2016.040, 2031.310(b)(2), 2030.300(b), 2033.290(b).) Failure to make such attempt constitutes “misuse of discovery process”. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 2023.010(i) [amended eff. 1/1/26].)  

“A reasonable and good-faith attempt at informal resolution…requires that counsel attempt to talk the matter over, compare their views, consult, and deliberate.” (Townsend v. Superior Court (1998) 61 Cal.App.4th 1431, 1439.) In evaluating whether a meet and confer was reasonable and in good faith, “[a]n evaluation of whether, from the perspective of a reasonable person in the position of the discovering party, additional effort appeared likely to bear fruit, should also be considered.” (Clement v. Alegre (2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 1277, 1293-94.)  

The Court finds Plaintiff did not satisfy its statutory obligation to meet and confer in person or by telephone or videoconference. Here, Plaintiff’s counsel attests he sent a meet and confer letter in response to Defendant’s interrogatory responses. (See Declaration of Sepehr Daghighian filed June 8, 2026, ¶ 14.) Based on recent amendments to the discovery statutes at the beginning of this year, simply writing to opposing counsel is insufficient. The Court has decided to inform counsel about this recent change for the year since this change took effect. However, after it has been effect for a year, the Court will likely deny discovery motions outright if counsel fail to comply. As a result, the Court will consider the merits of Plaintiff’s Motion but reminds counsel to comply with its statutory meet and confer obligations in a substantively meaningful way or risk the denial of relief in the future.

V.             ANALYSIS

A.             No. 15.1

“Identify each denial of a material allegation and each special or affirmative defense in your pleadings and for each: (a) State all facts upon which you base the denial or special or affirmative defense; (b) State the names, ADDRESSES, and telephone numbers of all PERSONS who have knowledge of those facts; and (c) Identify all DOCUMENTS and other tangible things that support your denial or special or affirmative defense, and stat the name, ADDRESS, and telephone number of the PERSON who has each DOCUMENT.” (Sep. Stmt. No. 15.1.)

Defendant objects that this request is “vague, ambiguous, overly broad and asks for information protected by the attorney work-product doctrine or the attorney-client privilege. Subject to and without waiving these objections, AHM set forth its denials and affirmative defenses in its answer on file and in Plaintiff’s possession…AHM’s general denial and affirmative defenses were prepared by its attorneys and reflect their legal analysis.” (Id.)

Plaintiff contends further response is warranted because the objections are meritless and this information is pertinent to this lemon law action. (See id.)

Defendant argues its denials and affirmative defenses were drafted by counsel and therefore subject to the attorney work product privilege. (See Def. Sep. Stmt. No. 15.1.)

In reply, Plaintiff asserts Defendant’s response fails to individually address its affirmative defenses. (See Reply at 1:28-2:11.)

The Court finds further response to No. 15.1 is warranted. The Court overrules Defendant’s objections as they are inapplicable. This request seeks supporting facts, which should be pled in Defendant’s Answer regardless. (See California Trust Co. v. Gustason (1940) 15 Cal.2d 268, 273 [the answer must state facts on which the defense is founded]; Doe v. City of Los Angeles (2007) 42 Cal.4th 531, 550 [ultimate facts are generally sufficient, rather than evidentiary matters or legal conclusions].) This request does not seek any information that reflects an attorney’s impressions, opinions, or legal theories. It appropriately requests information concerning supporting facts and the identification of people or documents that possess knowledge of or contain those facts. (See e.g. DISC-001 Official Form Interrogatories No. 15 [interrogatory requesting defendant to explain the basis for affirmative defenses].) Accordingly, the Court finds further response is warranted and as to FROG No. 15.1.

B.             No. 17.1

“Is your response to each request for admission served with these interrogatories an unqualified admission? If not, for each response that is not an unqualified admission:

(a) state the number of the request;

(b) state all facts upon which you base your response;

(c) state the names, ADDRESSES, and telephone numbers of all PERSONS who have knowledge of those facts; and

(d) identify all DOCUMENTS and other tangible things that support your response and state the name, ADDRESS, and telephone number of the PERSON who has each DOCUMENT or thing.” (Sep. Stmt. No. 17.1.)

Defendant objects to each subpart on grounds that it is vague, overly broad, burdensome, seeks information that is protected by the attorney-client and work product privileges, prematurely seeks discovery of information to be provided by an expert witness, and seeks confidential information. (See id.) Defendant also responds with respect to Requests for Admission (“RFA”) Nos. 5, 9-14, 21, 30-32, 34, 39, 15-17, 22, 33, and 36-38. (See id.) As to Nos. 9-14, 21, 30-32, 34, and 39, Defendant responds “(b)-(d) See AHM’s objections to Request Nos. 9-14, 21, 30-32, 34, and 39, which are incorporated herein by reference.” (Id.)

Plaintiff incorporates its reasons for compelling further response from No. 15.1 here. (See id.)

          Defendant argues its response provides substantive, request specific information for each RFA not admitted unqualifiedly. (See Def.’s Sep. Stmt. No. 17.1.) Defendant argues as to Nos. 9-14, 21, 30-32, 34, and 39, it properly incorporated its objections which Plaintiff didn’t challenge in a separate motion to compel further. (See id.)

          In reply, Plaintiff argues Defendant failed to respond to No. 17.1(b) through (d) in connection with several of the RFAs identified. (See Reply at 3:23-24.) Plaintiff contends Defendant improperly incorporated objections to a valid interrogatory. Plaintiff also argues it is appropriate to challenge Defendant’s response to No. 17.1 without having moved to compel further responses to Defendant’s RFA responses. (See id., at 4:1-4.)

          The Court finds further response to No. 17.1 is not warranted. As Plaintiff’s separate statement does not identify deficiencies with respect to any RFA answered under No. 17.1, the Court turns to Plaintiff’s reply, which appears to only address Defendant’s response with respect to RFA Nos. 9-14, 21, 30-32, 34, and 39. Defendant served objection only responses to these RFA. (See Daghighian Decl., ¶ 13, Ex. E.) The Court agrees with Defendant that Plaintiff would have had to first challenge the RFA objection only responses in a separate motion to compel further. Otherwise, the Court cannot find Defendant’s response to No. 17.1 (b) through (d) as to RFA Nos. 9-14, 21, 30-32, 34, and 39 is deficient, as these subparts would not be applicable to objection only responses.

Thus, the Court DENIES the Motion FROGs as to No. 17.1.

I.               BACKGROUND 

This is an unlawful detainer action. Plaintiff Farhad Dorrani (“Plaintiff”) alleges Defendants Shawn L. Zanotti (“Zanotti”) and Exact Publicity, LLC (“Exact”) are in possession of the premises located at 22528 MacFarlane Drive, Woodland Hills, CA 91364, which Plaintiff owns. Plaintiff alleges at the time the 3-day notice to pay rent or quit was served, the amount of rent due was $79,200.00.

Plaintiff now moves to compel Special Interrogatories (Set One) from Zanotti and Exact. Plaintiff also moves to compel further responses to his Requests for Production (Set One) from Zanotti and Exact.

II.            PROCEDURAL HISTORY

On May 6, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Complaint for unlawful detainer.

On May 28, 2026, Exact filed an Answer.

On May 29, 2026, Zanotti filed an Answer.

On July 8, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant Motions.

On July 20, 2026, Defendants filed an omnibus Opposition.

As of July 20, 2026, no reply has been filed.

III.         LEGAL STANDARD

A.             Motion to Compel Further - Requests for Production

In responding to a request for production of documents, a party must provide one of the following: (1) a legally adequate statement of compliance; (2) a statement of inability to comply; or (3) an objection to the particular demand. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 2031.210(a).) [1] If any objections are made, they must: (1) “[i]dentify with particularity any document … falling within any category of item in the demand to which an objection is being made”; and (2) “[s]et forth clearly the extent of, and the specific ground for, the objection.”¿(Id. at § 2031.240(b).) A statement that the responding party is unable to comply with a particular demand “shall affirm that a diligent search and a reasonable inquiry has been made in an effort to comply with that demand”; “shall also specify whether the inability to comply is because the particular item or category has never existed, has been destroyed, has been lost, misplaced, or stolen, or has never been, or is no longer, in the possession, custody, or control of the responding party”;¿ and “shall set forth the name and address of any natural person or organization known or believed by that party to have possession, custody, or control of that item or category of item.” (Id. at § 2031.230.)¿ 

A party who believes a responding party has served (1) an incomplete statement of compliance, (2) an inadequate, incomplete, or evasive statement of compliance, or (3) an unmeritorious objection can move for an order compelling further responses but must “set forth specific acts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought by the demand.” (See id. at §§ 2031.310(a)-(b)(1).)  

B.             Motion to Compel - Interrogatories

If a party to whom interrogatories are directed fails to serve a timely response, the propounding party may move for an order compelling responses and for a monetary sanction. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.290(b).) The statute contains no time limit for a motion to compel where no responses have been served. All that need be shown in the moving papers is that a set of interrogatories was properly served on the opposing party, that the time to respond has expired, and that no response of any kind has been served. (See Leach v. Superior Court (1980) 111 Cal.App.3d 902, 905-906.

IV.          ANALYSIS

The Court will deny Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel and Motion to Compel Further Discovery presently before the Court. Plaintiff improperly combines two requests for relief as to two separate Defendants in each discovery motion. Plaintiff moves to compel Special Interrogatories (Set One) from Zanotti and Exact. Plaintiff also moves to compel further responses to his Requests for Production (Set One) from Zanotti and Exact. While there is no section formally requiring separate motions under the Code of Civil Procedure, discovery motions require separate filing fees; one party, one set of discovery, one motion. (See Govt. Code § 70617(a)(4) (setting forth the required filing fee for each motion, application, or any other paper or request requiring a hearing); see also Weil & Brown, Civil Procedure Before Trial (The Rutter Group 2024) [8:1140.1] (“Motions to compel compliance with separate discovery requests ordinarily should be filed separately.”)

Additionally, the Court notes another procedural defect. The separate statement in support of Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel Further does not state facts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought by the demand or reasons why the response is deficient. (See Code Civ. Proc. § 2031.310(b)(1); Rules of Court, rule 3.1345(c)(3); Sep. Stmt. Nos. 1-7.)

V.             CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, the Court DENIES Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel and Motion to Compel Further Discovery, including Plaintiff’s request for discovery sanctions.
