ROWLAND HEIGHTS MEDICAL PLAZA LLC vs SAMUEL CHUNG, et al.

July 13, 2026

8:30 AM

 

 

 

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: MOTION OF ARDENT LAW GROUP, PC TO BE RELIEVED AS COUNSEL-CIVIL.

 

RULING:

The Court grants the Motion.

The Court will complete, sign and file the Form Order received-stamped on June 5, 2026.

 

ANALYSIS:

On September 25, 2025, the Court filed the Judgment Confirming Arbitration Award.

On May 22, 2026, the appeal filed December 30, 2025, was dismissed.

By the Motion, filed June 5, 2026, counsel moves to be relieved as attorney of record for Respondent SAMUEL CHUNG, by declaring, in the form Declaration, at paragraph 2: “[C]lient Samuel Chung has ceased to paid his attorney's fees and communications with the Attorney. As a result, continuing to represent the client would cause undue hardship on attorney.”

Here, the form notice, declaration, proposed order, and proof of service sufficiently comply with the requirements for a motion to be relieved as counsel. (See Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362.)

Further, moving counsel’s declaration shows cognizable grounds for withdrawal:

  • The client by conduct renders it unreasonably difficult for the member to carry out the employment effectively (e.g., noncooperation or noncommunication). (Rules Prof. Conduct, rule 1.16(b)(4). Cf. Estate of Falco v. Decker (1987) 188 Cal.App.3d 1004, 1020 [“We find no abuse of discretion in the trial court's implicit finding rejecting the contention that respondents' lack of cooperation justified appellants' withdrawal.”].)
  • The client breaches an agreement or obligation to the member as to expenses or fees. (Rules Prof. Conduct, rule 1.16; Lempert v. Sup. Ct. (2003) 112 Cal.App.4th 1161, 1173.)

Finally, no opposing document is filed in order to show any prejudice caused by attorney withdrawal. (See Rules Prof. Conduct, rule 1.16(d); Vann v. Shilleh (1975) 54 Cal.App.3d 192, 197.)

Therefore, the Court grants the Motion and will sign the proposed Order counsel submitted.

24STCV05303

JACQUELYNN RUTTI, AN INDIVIDUAL vs HALLIER INVESTMENTS LLC, A NEVADA LIMITED LIABILITY COMPANY

July 13, 2026

8:30 AM

 

 

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: PLAINTIFF JACQUELYNN RUTTI’S MOTION TO COMPEL RESPONSES TO FORM INTERROGATORIES - GENERAL (SET ONE) TO DEFENDANT HALLIER INVESTMENTS LLC, AND REQUEST FOR MONETARY SANCTIONS AGAINST DEFENDANT HALLIER INVESTMENTS LLC AND DEFENDANT’S COUNSEL, NORTON & MELNIK, APC IN THE AMOUNT OF $2,961.65.  PLAINTIFF JACQUELYNN RUTTI’S MOTION TO COMPEL RESPONSES TO FORM INTERROGATORIES - EMPLOYMENT (SET ONE) TO DEFENDANT HALLIER INVESTMENTS LLC AND REQUEST FOR MONETARY SANCTIONS AGAINST DEFENDANT HALLIER INVESTMENTS LLC AND DEFENDANT’S COUNSEL, NORTON & MELNIK, APC, IN THE AMOUNT OF $2,961.65.  PLAINTIFF JACQUELYNN RUTTI’S MOTION TO COMPEL RESPONSES TO SPECIAL INTERROGATORIES (SET ONE) TO DEFENDANT HALLIER INVESTMENTS LLC; AND REQUEST FOR MONETARY SANCTIONS AGAINST DEFENDANT HALLIER INVESTMENTS LLC AND DEFENDANT’S COUNSEL, NORTON & MELNIK, APC IN THE AMOUNT OF $4,411.65.  PLAINTIFF JACQUELYNN RUTTI’S MOTION TO COMPEL ANSWERS TO REQUESTS FOR PRODUCTION, SET ONE, TO DEFENDANT HALLIER INVESTMENTS LLC AND REQUEST FOR MONETARY SANCTIONS AGAINST DEFENDANT HALLIER INVESTMENTS LLC AND DEFENDANT’S COUNSEL, NORTON & MELNIK, APC, IN THE AMOUNT OF $4,411.65.

 

RULING:

The Court orders the four motions off calendar, considering supplements served, and substantial justification for clerical errors.

Plaintiff to give notice.

 

I.                    BACKGROUND

On March 4, 2024, JACQUELYNN RUTTI (Plaintiff) filed a Complaint against HALLIER INVESTMENTS LLC (Defendant), listing Causes of Action for wage-and-hour violations and related claims.

Plaintiff alleges various wage-and-hour violations, including that, while employed with Defendant, Plaintiff was required to work off-the-clock and during rest and meal breaks.

On June 12, 2026, Plaintiff filed the Motions to Compel initial responses to form, special and employment interrogatories, and requests for production of documents, on grounds that Defendant failed to respond, and without justification. Plaintiff also requests monetary sanctions as to each Motion.

Defendant opposes, based upon counterarguments that responses have been served, counsel inadvertently missed the calendared deadline due to clerical error, and meeting and conferring could have avoided the motions and resolved any minor issues in the responses.

 

II.                 LEGAL STANDARD

A motion to compel initial discovery responses need only show that discovery was properly served on the opposing party, the time to respond expired, and no response was served. (Leach v. Superior Court (1980) 111 Cal.App.3d 902, 905-906; Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.290 [compelling initial interrogatory responses], 2031.300 [compelling initial document requests].) A motion to compel initial discovery responses need not show good cause, meeting and conferring, or timely filing, and need not be accompanied by a separate statement. (Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pac. Healthcare Consultants (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, 404 (Sinaiko).)

Monetary discovery sanctions are mandatory as to parties losing discovery motions, unless courts find substantial justification or other injustice. (Deck v. Devs. Inv. Co. (2023) 89 Cal.App.5th 808, 830.) “‘[T]he phrase “substantial justification” has been understood to mean that a justification is clearly reasonable because it is well grounded in both law and fact.’” (Kwan Software Engineering, Inc. v. Hennings (2020) 58 Cal.App.5th 57, 75.) “If the party seeking a monetary sanction meets its burden of proof, the burden shifts to the opposing party attempting to avoid a monetary sanction to show that it acted with ‘substantial justification.’” (Doe v. U.S. Swimming, Inc. (2011) 200 Cal.App.4th 1424, 1435.) Courts may determine substantial justification based on the “whole picture” of the discovery dispute, including where parties partially prevailed as to some discovery items in dispute, or where discovery concessions occurred only after improperly asserted objections. (In re Marriage of Moore (2024) 102 Cal.App.5th 1275, 1297.) Where satisfactory responses have been served after the filing of motions to compel, courts nonetheless may award sanctions. (Sinaiko, supra, at p. 409; County of San Benito v. Superior Court (2023) 96 Cal.App.5th 243, 256 [citing Sinaiko regarding court considering supplemental responses served after motion].)

 

III.              ANALYSIS

Plaintiff makes the Motions based upon Defendant’s failures to serve timely discovery responses and includes the sanctions requests.

Defendant responds that:

·         Full responses were served on June 25, 2026.

·         The calendared deadline was missed due to a clerical mistake causing counsel not to realize the response deadline.

·         Counsel met and conferred with opposing counsel to try, in good faith, to rectify any deficiencies with the responses.

·         Sanctions are inappropriate, because secretarial, clerical, or calendaring mistakes are considered excusable.

Where respondents served untimely discovery responses after parties have filed motions to compel initial responses, courts have broad discretion as to ruling, including: 1) denying the motion as moot, in whole or part, where valid responses without objections have resolved the motion; 2) awarding requested sanctions; 3) allowing moving party to take the motion off calendar; 4) considering the motion as voluntarily narrowed in scope; 5) compelling responses without objection, where no legally valid responses have been provided, as to some, or all, interrogatories; 6) treating the motion as one to compel further responses, and ruling accordingly, with, or without, a separate statement; 7) ordering the parties to meet and confer; 8) ordering moving party to file a separate statement; or, 9) ordering the motion off calendar while requiring the propounding party to file a motion to compel further responses. (Sinaiko, supra, at p. 409; County of San Benito v. Superior Court (2023) 96 Cal.App.5th 243, 256 [citing Sinaiko regarding court considering supplemental responses served after motion].) Under some circumstances, it may be an abuse of discretion for a court to treat a motion as being one to compel further responses without requiring a separate motion. (St. Mary v. Superior Court (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 762, 778.)

Analogously, to constitute grounds for relief under Code of Civil Procedure section 473, generally attorneys’ errors’ must be accompanied by a factor outside attorneys’ control in an unmanageable situation, such as mistakes of clerical staff. (Huh v. Wang (2007) 158 Cal.App.4th 1406, 1424.) Excusable neglect, including for purposes of Code of Civil Procedure section 473, includes the circumstances where an attorney relies upon employees who err in performing tasks. (Renteria v. Juvenile Justice, Dept. of Corrections and Rehabilitation (2006) 135 Cal.App.4th 903, 911-912 [attorney’s reliance on secretary's erroneous removal of the claim filing date from the calendar was excusable].) Generally, regarding miscalendaring, excusable neglect and a basis for relief have been found where an attorney relies on an employee or calendaring system to calendar deadlines, and the employee or system fails. (Renteria v. Juvenile Justice, Dept. of Corrections & Rehabilitation (2006) 135 Cal.App.4th 903, 911.) Attorneys’ or staff’s calendaring errors can support relief, if excusable under appropriate circumstances. (Comunidad en Accion v. Los Angeles City Council (2013) 219 Cal.App.4th 1116, 1133-1135 [“trial court abused its discretion in denying … relief from default. The one-week delay in requesting a hearing was an isolated mistake in an otherwise vigorous and thorough presentation of … claims…. [T]he lead attorney made a single calendaring error, not a series of errors resulting from disorganization.”].)

Here, Defendant reports that the requested responses now have been served, and expresses a willingness to meet and confer about any deficiencies. Additionally, defense counsel supports the finding of substantial justification in avoidance of sanctions, based upon clerical and attorney errors.

 

IV.              CONCLUSION

Accordingly, the Court orders the motions off calendar, without prejudice for Plaintiff to file any proper motions based upon the recent discovery responses.

PATRICIA BARTLEY vs LAWRENCE A. BROCKMAN, D.M.D., et al.

July 13, 2026

8:30 AM

 

 

 

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: DEFENDANT ARTHUR B. SCHLACHTMAN, DDS’ MOTION FOR PROTECTIVE ORDER AND MONETARY SANCTIONS.

 

RULING:

The Court denies the Motion for Protective Order and both sanctions requests.

Plaintiff to give notice.

 

I.                    BACKGROUND

On October 11, 2024, PATRICIA BARTLEY (Plaintiff), presently a self-represented litigant, filed a Complaint against LAWRENCE A. BROCKMAN, D.M.D.; ARTHUR B. SCHLACHTMAN DDS; and JOSEPH P. SABET, DDS, et al. (Defendants).

On June 25, 2025, Plaintiff filed the Second Amended Complaint (SAC), having claims for:

1. PROFESSIONAL NEGLIGENCE;

 2. FRAUD;

 3. MEDICAL BATTERY;

 4. COLLUSION AND CONCEALMENT;

 5. INTENTIONAL INFLICTION OF EMOTIONAL DISTRESS;

 6. FRAUDULENT BILLING;

 7. EXTORTION;

 8. PRIVACY BREACH;

 9. INTENTIONAL INTERFERENCE WITH CONTRACTUAL RELATIONS;

 10. BREACH OF CONTRACT (EXPRESS AND IMPLIED); and

 11. BREACH OF FIDUCIARY DUTY.

Plaintiff alleges that Defendants’ collective negligence and collusive fraudulent concealment, involving failed dental procedures and inappropriate medications, caused Plaintiff’s bone infections and loss, multiple tooth extractions, and the need for extensive corrective procedures, with estimated costs of up to $100,000. (E.g., SAC, ¶¶ 11-15.)

On January 28, 2026, the Court’s Minute Order relieved Plaintiff’s counsel as attorney of record.

On June 10, 2026, ARTHUR B. SCHLACHTMAN, D.D.S. (Defendant) filed the Motion for Protective Order against Plaintiff’s further communicating with the represented client and circumventing the Discovery Act, and requesting monetary sanctions.

Plaintiff filed an Opposition emphasizing statutory rights for patients to request their own medical information, and requesting monetary sanctions.

 

II.                 LEGAL STANDARD

Moving parties have the burden to show good cause for protective order. (Emerson Elec. Co. v Superior Court (1997) 16 Cal.4th 1101, 1110.) To prevail on motions for protective orders, moving parties have the burden “to demonstrate that the ‘discovery sought is unreasonably cumulative or duplicative, or is obtainable from some other source that is more convenient, less burdensome, or less expensive’…, or that the ‘selected method of discovery is unduly burdensome or expensive’….” (Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pacific Healthcare Consultants (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, 402.)

“While the discovery statutes give the trial court wide discretion in making such orders as may be necessary to protect parties and deponents from abuse or misuse of depositions ‘there can be no room for the exercise of such discretion if no ground exists upon which it might operate.’… If there is no legal justification for such exercise of discretion it must be held that an abuse occurred.” (Oak Grove School Dist. v. City Title Ins. Co. (1963) 217 Cal.App.2d 678, 712.)

Courts construe statutes with reference to the entire system of law, to harmonize, while avoiding anomalies. (Hohenshelt v. Superior Court (2025) 18 Cal.5th 310, 332.) “ ‘ “A court must, where reasonably possible, harmonize statutes, reconcile seeming inconsistencies in them, and construe them to give force and effect to all of their provisions.” ’ ” (Gamo v. Merrell (2025) 113 Cal.App.5th 656, 663.) “The legislature is … presumed to have enacted legislation with existing law in mind.” (Harris v. Verizon Communications (2006) 141 Cal.App.4th 573, 585, disapproved on other grounds by Azure Ltd. v. I-Flow Corp. (2009) 46 Cal.4th 1323, 1336.)

“‘The court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion for a protective order, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.’” (Safechuck v. MJJ Prods., Inc. (2023) 94 Cal.App.5th 675, 700 [quoting Code Civ. Proc., § 2025.420, subd. (h)].)

 

III.              ANALYSIS

Defendant essentially seeks a Protective Order prohibiting Plaintiff from further contacting the client and from requesting records unless via the Discovery Act preemptive of the other statutory provisions addressing patients obtaining records from health care providers.

Plaintiff’s Opposition emphasizes, as preempting the Discovery Act, various statutory rights for individual patients to obtain their own medical records, such as Health & Safety Code § 123110, HIPAA, HITECH, and the 21st Century Cures Act.

The parties are raising several first-impression issues, while citing nothing directly on point. The “A legal proposition asserted without apposite authority necessarily fails.” (People v. Taylor (2004) 119 Cal.App.4th 628, 643.) Court has done a lot of independent research that reveals a dearth of law on point.

For instance, the Motion cites no governing authority directly on point about California Rules of Professional Conduct, rule 4.2, applying to a self-represented litigant regarding contacting represented parties, or concerning Discovery Act circumvention. (See Motion, pp. 10-17.) For example, a cited case did not hold, “protective orders are an appropriate remedy where a party’s conduct demonstrates a pattern of attempting to circumvent the formal discovery process”. (Motion, 13:8-11.) (See Truitt v. Superior Court (1997) 59 Cal.App.4th 1183, 1186 [“filed a motion to quash the deposition subpoena … and for a protective order and evidentiary and monetary sanctions.”].)

Regarding attorney ethics and California Rules of Professional Conduct, rule 4.2: “This rule does not prohibit communications with a represented person* concerning matters outside the representation.” (CA ST RPC Rule 4.2.) Clients are permitted to communicate with each other, ex parte, without attorney involvement. Distinguishably, “ ‘[w]hen the content of the communication to be had with the opposing party originates with or is directed by the attorney, [the communication] is prohibited by rule 2–100 [now rule 4.2].’ ” (San Francisco Unified Sch. Dist. ex rel. Contreras v. First Student, Inc. (2013) 213 Cal.App.4th 1212, 1235.)

Additionally, requests for one’s own medical records relate to statutory rights, apart from the Discovery Act. For example: “In the interest of giving an individual free access to his or her own medical records, the covered entity is under an absolute obligation to furnish those records on request.” (Bugarin v. Chartone, Inc. (2006) 135 Cal.App.4th 1558, 1563.) (Underscoring added.) But the line drawn between requesting records as a patient, or in connection with litigation, is not always entirely clear, as it can be either. (See, e.g., Spencer S. Busby, APLC v. BACTES Imaging Sols. LLC (2022) 74 Cal.App.5th 71, 75 [“requests from patients seeking their own medical records, … requests from attorneys seeking their clients' medical records in anticipation of potential litigation.”].) Importantly, here Plaintiff is self-represented, and not a client of any attorney of record.

As for proof offered in support, Exhibit A to the Motion is expressly confined to requesting Plaintiff’s own records, such that it should not be preempted by the Discovery Act. However, Exhibit C arguably is misdescribed as a patient seeking records, and reads like a discovery request, as follows:

1. The current custodian of records for Mega Tech Dental Laboratory;

2. The current address, telephone number, email address, and contact information for the custodian of records;

3. The identity of any successor entity, purchaser, acquiring laboratory, parent entity, affiliated entity, records repository, storage vendor, archive vendor, or other person or entity maintaining Mega Tech Dental Laboratory records;

4. The current location of any laboratory prescriptions, work orders, invoices, models, impressions, scans, digital files, shade records, design specifications, photographs, communications, or other records relating to restorations fabricated for me;

5. The identity of any person or entity known to possess, maintain, store, archive, or control records relating to laboratory work performed for my treatment.

Additionally, a protective order can be premature before potential requests are made. Defendant may or may not be required by law to respond, outside of discovery procedure, depending upon the circumstances of the particular request. (Cf. O'Grady v. Superior Court (2006) 139 Cal.App.4th 1423, 1454 [“As a result, there is ordinarily no reliable indication that discovery will be sought until it is actually served. A request for a protective order will thus appear premature, because there is nothing to protect against.”].) Presently, the Court lacks sufficient information to rule soundly, as to whether any given request from Plaintiff involves one of several potentially applicable statutory rights, or a separate matter necessarily falling under the Discovery Act. As a matter of legislative interpretation, and harmonizing the provisions, no one statute appears to preempt the other as a matter of law, but factual information may affect the reasoning as to which statute actually applies.

Moreover, the possible burden upon Defendant does not appear to be substantial— Defendant theoretically could properly decline to provide records not statutorily authorized, or forward all communications to counsel to handle if part of cognizable procedure in relation to this case litigation, such as a discovery objection as follows: Parties cannot operate outside of the parameters of the Discovery Act that has subsumed the entire field pertaining to the gathering evidence in preparation for trial. (Pillsbury, Madison & Sutro v. Schectman (1997) 55 Cal.App.4th 1279, 1288.)

All things considered, the Court finds insufficient justification for the Proposed Protective Order and denies the Motion, in its discretion.

Finally, the Court denies the sanctions requests, given the debatable and unrefined law as to the several issues raised, premature state of the facts, and inherent discretion involved in protective orders. As such, there is substantial justification for positions taken by each side.

 

IV.              CONCLUSION

Accordingly, the Court denies the Motion, and both sanctions requests, for reasons set forth above.

25STCV32525

CARLA LOPEZ vs VAN NUYS-T, INC., DBA KEYES TOYOTA, et al.   

July 13, 2026

8:30 AM

 

 

 

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: PLAINTIFF’S MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES TO SPECIAL INTERROGATORIES TO DEFENDANT VAN NUYS-T, INC., SET ONE, AND SANCTIONS.

 

RULING:

The Motion is granted.

On or before August 7, 2026, Defendant VAN NUYS-T, INC., dba KEYES TOYOTA shall serve further responses, notwithstanding the discovery objections, and in full compliance with the California Discovery Act (Code Civ. Proc. § 2016.010 et seq.), as to Plaintiff’s Special Interrogatories, numbers 2 and 6.

On or before that same date, Defendant VAN NUYS-T, INC., dba KEYES TOYOTA, and Kolar & Associates, jointly and severally, shall pay discovery sanctions in the sum of $1,669.94 to Plaintiff CARLA LOPEZ, the Court finding the absence of substantial justification. (E.g., Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030.)

Plaintiff to give notice.

 

I.                    BACKGROUND

On November 6, 2025, CARLA LOPEZ (Plaintiff) filed a Complaint against VAN NUYS-T, INC., dba KEYES TOYOTA and TRAVELERS CASUALTY AND SURETY COMPANY OF AMERICA (Defendants)

Per Stipulation, on March 19, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Second Amended Complaint (SAC), listing Causes of Action for:

1. VIOLATION OF THE CONSUMERS LEGAL REMEDIES ACT, CIVIL CODE § 1750, ET SEQ.

2. FRAUD & DECEIT

3. NEGLIGENT MISREPRESENTATION

4. VIOLATION OF THE UNFAIR COMPETITION LAW, BUSINESS & PROFESSIONS CODE § 17200, ET SEQ.

5. VIOLATION OF VEHICLE CODE § 11711.

Plaintiff alleges that automobile dealer KEYES TOYOTA (Defendant) misrepresented and concealed, as to Plaintiff’s purchased 2020 Toyota Highlander’s accident and damage history, certification, and inspection.

On May 26, 2026, Plaintiff filed the Motion to Compel Further Responses to Special Interrogatories, numbers 2 and 6, on grounds that responses are evasive and incomplete, and discovery objections lack merit. Plaintiff additionally requests $1,669.94 in sanctions against Defendant and its counsel, Kolar & Associates.

Defendant opposes, based upon counterarguments that supplemental responses will be served, and discovery responses, and the privacy objection, are proper.

 

II.                 LEGAL STANDARD

If interrogatory responses lack specificity, then parties may move to compel further responses under Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.300, subdivision (a), providing for motions to compel, where parties deem that an answer is evasive, incomplete, or inadequate as to specification of documents. (Best Products, Inc. v. Superior Court (2004) 119 Cal.App.4th 1181, 1190.)

Even where satisfactory responses have been served after the filing of motions to compel, courts nonetheless may award sanctions. (Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pacific Healthcare Consultants (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, 409; County of San Benito v. Superior Court (2023) 96 Cal.App.5th 243, 256 [citing Sinaiko regarding court considering supplemental responses served after motion].)

Monetary discovery sanctions are mandatory as to parties losing discovery motions, unless courts find substantial justification or other injustice. (Deck v. Devs. Inv. Co. (2023) 89 Cal.App.5th 808, 830.) “‘[T]he phrase “substantial justification” has been understood to mean that a justification is clearly reasonable because it is well grounded in both law and fact.’” (Kwan Software Engineering, Inc. v. Hennings (2020) 58 Cal.App.5th 57, 75.) “If the party seeking a monetary sanction meets its burden of proof, the burden shifts to the opposing party attempting to avoid a monetary sanction to show that it acted with ‘substantial justification.’” (Doe v. U.S. Swimming, Inc. (2011) 200 Cal.App.4th 1424, 1435.)

 

III.              ANALYSIS

Plaintiff seeks further, code-compliant responses to Special Interrogatories 2 and 6, and monetary sanctions.

Defendant responds that the Motion is moot, because Defendant agreed to provide further substantive responses, and will serve them as soon as verifications are signed. Additionally, Defendant contends that the responses properly state an inability to answer number 2, and a privacy objection to providing the prior vehicle owner’s name, telephone number, and address. Finally, Defendant maintains that there is substantial justification for the above-referenced reasons.

“While the party propounding interrogatories may have the burden of filing a motion to compel if it finds the answers it receives unsatisfactory, the burden of justifying any objection and failure to respond remains at all times with the party resisting an interrogatory.” (Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 541.)

Here, the response to number 2 improperly failed to state a reasonable and good faith effort already made, rather than to be accomplished in the future. If a respondent does not have personal knowledge for a full response, the party shall state that and make a reasonable and good faith effort to obtain the information by inquiries. (Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pacific Healthcare Consultants (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, 406; Regency Health Services, Inc. v. Superior Court (1998) 64 Cal.App.4th 1496, 1504.) Where interrogatory responses reveal all information currently available to a party, but the respondent cannot furnish all requested information, then the party should set forth the efforts made to obtain that information. (Deyo v. Kilbourne (1978) 84 Cal.App.3d 771, 782, superseded by statute on another ground as stated in Guzman v. General Motors Corp. (1984) 154 Cal.App.3d 438, 444.)

And the response to number 6 unjustifiably stated a privacy objection to witness contact information. Disclosure of addresses and telephone numbers does not amount to a serious invasion of privacy rights. (Belaire-West Landscape, Inc. v. Superior Court (2007) 149 Cal.App.4th 544, 561.) Analogously, form interrogatories approved by the Judicial Council entail fundamentally routine discovery of witness contact information. (Puerto v. Superior Court (2008) 158 Cal.App.4th 1242, 1250.)

For the above reasons, the Court finds an absence of substantial justification, and awards sanctions.

 

IV.              CONCLUSION

The Court grants the Motion, as prayed.

JOSE HERRERA-ALEJO vs BARRET BUSINESS SERVICES, INC., A WASHINGTON CORPORATION, et al.

July 13, 2026

8:30 AM

 

 

 

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: DEFENDANT COOK EVENT SERVICES, INC. dba BAKER PARTY RENTALS’ MOTION TO COMPEL ARBITRATION AND TO STAY PROCEEDINGS.

 

RULING:

The Court denies the Motion.

The Court sustains all evidentiary objections of Plaintiff filed on June 26, 2026.

Plaintiff to give notice.

 

I.                    BACKGROUND

On April 1, 2026,  (Plaintiff) filed a Complaint against  (Defendant), listing Causes of Action for:

1. ASSAULT;

2. BATTERY;

3. VIOLATION OF CIVIL CODE §52.1;

4. RETALIATION AND/OR WRONGFUL TERMINATION IN VIOLATION OF LABOR CODE §1102.5;

5. RETALIATION AND/OR WRONGFUL TERMINATION IN VIOLATION OF LABOR CODE §6310;

6. WRONGFUL TERMINATION VIOLATING PUBLIC POLICY;

7. NEGLIGENT HIRING, SUPERVISION, AND/OR RETENTION; AND;

8. DEFAMATION.

Plaintiff alleges that wrongfully terminated Plaintiff’s employment for complaining about a violent coworker’s assault and battery and an unsafe work environment.

On June 4, 2026, COOK EVENT SERVICES, INC. dba BAKER PARTY RENTALS (Defendant) filed the Motion to Compel Arbitration, on grounds including that Plaintiff signed an Arbitration Agreement on paper, translated into Spanish, and no unconscionability exists as class action waivers are valid.

Plaintiff opposes, based upon counterarguments such as that he filed a Declaration denying signing or being presented with the Arbitration Agreement, and conveying that he cannot read Spanish or English. Alternatively, Plaintiff asserts unconscionability based upon missing documentation and a class action waiver.

 

II.                 LEGAL STANDARD

Parties petitioning to compel arbitration have the burden to prove the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by a preponderance of evidence, and parties opposing have the burden to prove by a preponderance of the evidence any fact necessary to a defense. (Gonzalez v. Nowhere Beverly Hills LLC (2024) 107 Cal.App.5th 111, 121; Doss v. Tesla, Inc. (2026) 121 Cal.App.5th 81, 89.)

Parties seeking to compel arbitration meet their initial burden simply by reciting the terms of the governing provision, or by attaching a copy of the provisions, unless there is a dispute over authenticity that is beyond merely contesting the preliminary showing. (Sprunk v. Prisma LLC (2017) 14 Cal.App.5th 785, 793. See also Iyere v. Wise Auto Grp. (2023) 87 Cal.App.5th 747, 758 [opposing party needs to create a factual dispute as to the signature's authenticity or that a contract was formed.].)

The failure to read an agreement, because of inability to read, or an inability to understand English, are not cognizable grounds to avoid contracts, because parties are expected to have others read or explain the contracts to them. (Randas v. YMCA of Metropolitan Los Angeles (1993) 17 Cal.App.4th 158, 163.) Limited English fluency and education, as to terms printed in English, adequately showed procedural unconscionability. (See Orcilla v. Big Sur, Inc. (2016) 244 Cal.App.4th 982, 997.) Employers failing to translate arbitration provisions for employees, who could not read English, constituted oppression, surprise and procedural unconscionability. (See Carmona v. Lincoln Millennium Car Wash, Inc. (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 74, 85 [“What elevates this case to a high degree of procedural unconscionability,… The … companies hid the enforceability clause and the entire confidentiality subagreement by failing to translate that portion of the agreement into Spanish.”].)

“[I]f substantial evidence supports the trial court's determination that a valid agreement to arbitrate exists, an appellate court will affirm that determination.” (Banner Ent., Inc. v. Superior Court (1998) 62 Cal.App.4th 348, 357. Accord, Zavala v. Scott Bros. Dairy, Inc. (2006) 143 Cal.App.4th 585, 589-590.) Findings of unconscionability as to arbitration provisions are reviewed de novo, where based on declarations raising no meaningful disputes, and otherwise are reviewed for substantial evidence. (Samaniego v. Empire Today LLC (2012) 205 Cal.App.4th 1138, 1144.)

If a court has ordered arbitration, then it shall stay the pending action until an arbitration is had in accordance with the order to arbitrate, or another earlier time, and the stay may be with respect to an issue that is severable. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.4; Mattson Tech., Inc. v. Applied Materials, Inc. (2023) 96 Cal.App.5th 1149, 1161; Twentieth Century Fox Film Corp. v. Superior Court (2000) 79 Cal.App.4th 188, 192; Heritage Provider Network, Inc. v. Superior Court (2008) 158 Cal.App.4th 1146, 1152, 1154, fn. 12.)

 

III.              ANALYSIS

According to Defendant, on April 26, 2023, Plaintiff executed an arbitration agreement. (Lee Previant Declaration, filed 6/4/26, ¶ 2, Exhibit A.) Further, Defendant asserts that the class action waiver is enforceable and not unconscionable, where the Complaint contains no PAGA claim.

Plaintiff maintains that evidence with the Motion is objectionable, Defendant did not provide the arbitration agreement or explain it, and Plaintiff cannot read English or Spanish. Further, Plaintiff asserts that unconscionability exists based upon Defendant’s not providing the entire purported agreement that contains an unduly harsh class action waiver.

Arbitration is a matter of contract, and a party cannot be required to arbitrate a dispute unless agreed. (Ajida Technologies, Inc. v. Roos Instruments, Inc. (2001) 87 Cal.App.4th 534, 541; Lopez v. Charles Schwab & Co., Inc. (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 1224, 1233.) An agreement’s signature can be shown by having a judge compare a genuine signature to that on an agreement, or by a declaration of a custodian of records. (Gamboa v. Ne. Cmty. Clinic (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 158, 170.)

The absence of “authentic informed choice,” due to the lack of straightforward, unbiased explanations of an agreement, may constitute procedural unconscionability. (Gentry v. Superior Court (2007) 42 Cal.4th 443, 470 [where employer indicated preference to arbitrate, it was likely that “nonexecutive”, lower echelon employees felt some pressure not to opt out of the agreement], overruling on other grounds recognized by Marenco v. DirecTV LLC (2015) 233 Cal.App.4th 1409, 1421.)

Cases that have held that the failure to provide a copy of incorporated documents support finding procedural unconscionability have involved an unconscionability claim that somehow depended upon the incorporated arbitration rules. (Baltazar v. Forever 21, Inc. (2016) 62 Cal.4th 1237, 1246.)

Here, Plaintiff filed valid evidentiary objections to the Declaration of Lee Previant and the signed arbitration agreement and translation, primarily based upon the declarant’s failure to lay foundation to show personal knowledge of the agreement.

And Plaintiff filed a declaration denying obtaining or signing the arbitration agreement, and explaining his inability to read English or Spanish.

Defendant has filed no Reply in rebuttal of the issue of failure to show assent to the arbitration agreement.

The Court need not reach the issue of unconscionability, since the lack of assent requires denying the request to compel arbitration. However, the Federal Arbitration Act (FAA) preempts California law holding that class action waivers are unenforceable, making them enforceable. (Vela v. Harbor Rail Services of California, Inc. (2026) 120 Cal.App.5th 353, 367; OTO, L.L.C. v. Kho (2019) 8 Cal.5th 111, 123 [class arbitration waivers are not unconscionable].)

 

IV.              CONCLUSION

Therefore, the Court denies the Motion, for reasons set forth above.
