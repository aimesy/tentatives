DEPARTMENT 514 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 23STCV02925    Hearing Date: September 23, 2026    Dept: 514

Anderson v. Williams
23STCV02925
Plaintiff’s Motion to File Amended Complaint

Tentative

The motion is granted.

Background

On February 10, 2023, Durand Anderson (“Plaintiff”) filed the complaint in this action against Thelma Williams and Does 1 through 10 for (1) breach of warranty of habitability, (2) negligent maintenance of premises, (3) nuisance, (4) breach of quiet enjoyment, and (5) intentional infliction of emotional distress, all arising out of alleged habitability issues with a residence located at 1555 West 80th Street in Los Angeles.

On June 28, 2023, Plaintiff’s counsel filed a declaration with the Court stating that Defendant Williams was deceased.

July 3, 2023, Plaintiff amended the complaint to name Vickie Buie, successor trustee of the Thelma E. Williams Revocable Trust (“Buie” or “Defendant”), as Doe 1.

On September 27, 2023, Defendant Buie filed an answer.

On November 17, 2023, Buie filed a cross-complaint against Plaintiff. Plaintiff filed an answer to the cross-complaint on December 5, 2023.

On August 11, 2026, the Court, on Defendant Buie’s motion, struck allegations in the complaint regarding remedies under Civil Code section 1942.5, on the ground that Plaintiff did not state, or attempt to state, a cause of action under section 1942.5 in the complaint.  Also on August 11: (1) the Court, at the request of Plaintiff, dismissed Defendant Williams without prejudice; and (2) the Court continued trial to December 7, 2026, with the Final Status Conference continued to November 20.

On August 31, 2026, Plaintiff filed this motion for leave to file a First Amended Complaint (the ”FAC”).  Defendant Buie filed an (untimely) opposition on September 15, and Plaintiff filed a reply on September 16.

Legal Standard

Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (a)(1), provides, in relevant part: 

“The court may, in furtherance of justice, and on any terms as may be proper, allow a party to amend any pleading or proceeding by adding or striking out the name of any party, or by correcting a mistake in the name of a party, or a mistake in any other respect ….  The court may likewise, in its discretion, after notice to the adverse party, allow, upon any terms as may be just, an amendment to any pleading or proceeding in other particulars; and may upon like terms allow an answer to be made after the time limited by this code.” 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (a)(1); see also Code Civ. Proc., § 576 [“Any judge, at any time before or after commencement of trial, in the furtherance of justice, and upon such terms as may be proper, may allow the amendment of any pleading ….”].)

“This discretion should be exercised liberally in favor of amendments, for judicial policy favors resolution of all disputed matters in the same lawsuit.”  (Kittredge Sports Co. v. Superior Court (1989) 213 Cal.App.3d 1045, 1047; accord Mabie v. Hyatt (1998) 61 Cal.App.4th 581, 596; 1 Weil & Brown, California Practice Guide: Civil Procedure Before Trial (2026), ¶ 6:638.) Ordinarily, the court will not consider the validity of the proposed amended pleading in ruling on a motion for leave to amend; after leave is granted, the opposing party will have the opportunity to challenge the pleading through, for example, a demurrer or motion to strike. (Atkinson v. Elk Corp. (2003) 109 Cal.App.4th 739, 760; Kittridge Sports Co., supra, 213 Cal.App.3d at p. 1048; see also 1 Weil & Brown, supra, ¶ 6:644.) The court does, however, have discretion to deny leave to amend where a proposed amendment fails to state a valid cause of action as a matter of law and the defect cannot be cured by further amendment. (E.g., Aroa Marketing v. Hartford Ins. Co. of Midwest (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 781, 789; Foxborough v. Van Atta (1994) 26 Cal.App.4th 217, 230.)

Leave to amend may be denied when there is a long, unexcused delay in seeking amendment that causes prejudice to the opposing party. (Hirsa v. Superior Court (1981) 118 Cal.App.3d 486, 490.) Prejudice may be shown, in certain circumstances, where the amendment would delay trial, result in the loss of critical evidence, add to the costs of preparation, or increase the burden of discovery. (Magpali v. Farmers Group, Inc. (1996) 48 Cal.App.4th 471, 486-488.) But delay alone, without unfair prejudice to other parties, is not a basis to deny leave to amend. (Atkinson, supra, 109 Cal.App.4th at p. 761; Higgins v. Del Faro (1981) 123 Cal.App.3d 558, 563-565.)

California Rules of Court, rule 3.1324, governs motions for leave to amend. Subdivision (a) requires that the motion must:

“(1) Include a copy of the proposed amendment or amended pleading, which must be serially numbered to differentiate it from previous pleadings or amendments;

(2) State what allegations in the previous pleading are proposed to be deleted, if any, and where, by page, paragraph and line number, the deleted allegations are located; and

(3) state what allegations are proposed to be added to the previous pleading, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the additional allegations are located.”

 

Subdivision (b) of rule 3.1324 requires that a declaration must accompany the motion and must state:

“(1) The effect of the amendment;

(2) Why the amendment is necessary and proper;

(3) When the facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered; and

(4) The reasons why the request for amendment was not made earlier.”

Request for Judicial Notice

Defendant Buie asks the Court to take judicial notice of the following: (1) a Stipulation and Judgment in Buie v. Anderson, an unlawful detainer matter, Case No. 24STUD11316; (2) this Court’s Minute Order dated August 11, 2026; and (3) the complaint in this action.

The request is granted.

Objections to Evidence

Defendant Buie asserts 8 objections to the declaration of Plaintiff’s counsel Christian Oronsaye.  The objections are overruled.

Discussion

The Court begins with two threshold matters.

First, the Court exercises its discretion to consider the late-filed opposition of Defendant Buie.

Second, the Court finds that Plaintiff has complied with, or at the least substantially complied with, the procedural requirements of California Rules of Court, rule 3.1324.

Turning now to the merits, Plaintiff moves for leave to file the FAC to add five new causes of action.  The new causes of action are (consecutively numbered): (6) unlawful conduct to influence a tenant to vacate a residential unit in violation of Civil Code section 1940.2; (7) unlawful demanding and collecting rent for an uninhabitable residential unit in violation of Civil Code section 1942.4; (8) unlawful retaliation in violation of Civil Code section 1942.5; (9) negligent hiring, training, retention, and supervision of agents; and (10) racial discrimination in violation of the Unruh Civil Rights Act, Civil Code section 51.

Plaintiff argues that amendment is proper as the new causes of action all arise out of the same basic landlord-tenant relationship as the causes of action in the original complaint.  Plaintiff also argues that the amendment is timely because counsel first learned of the facts supporting the new causes of action at the Plaintiff’s own deposition on April 30, 2026.  Plaintiff also suggests that the Court, on August 11, granted Plaintiff leave to amend.  This last point is simply incorrect; although the potential for amendment was discussed on August 11, the Court did not grant leave to amend or rule on any other matter that was not before the Court that day.

Defendant Buie raises a series of arguments in opposition to the motion.  Defendant argues, for example, that the new causes of action are time-barred, contradicted by Plaintiff’s own testimony, or otherwise subject to various defenses.  Here, however, the Court will not adjudicate the merits of the proposed new causes of action on a motion for leave to amend.  If the motion is granted, Defendant will have ample opportunity to present all available defenses to the amended complaint, whether through demurrer, motion for summary adjudication, or trial.

Defendant Buie also argues that Plaintiff has unreasonably delayed in bringing this motion.  The Court shares some of Defendant’s concerns about the late nature of the proposed amendment, particularly because the allegedly “new” facts were known to Plaintiff all along, although apparently not to Plaintiff’s counsel.  (See Oronsaye Decl., ¶¶ 5-10.)  On this record, however, Defendant has not shown that permitting the amendment would cause her undue or unfair prejudice.  To be sure, Defendant would rather defend against five causes of action rather than ten, but that is not the type of undue or unfair prejudice that the Court must consider in this context.  There are some new issues that Plaintiff seeks to inject into this action, and it would potentially raise concerns about undue prejudice if the Court were to grant leave to amend without continuing the trial date.  Indeed, if the Court grants leave to amend the Court will continue trial to permit Defendant (and Plaintiff) to have an adequate time to conduct discovery (and, potentially, motion practice) on these new claims.  A continuance, the Court finds, will address the legitimate concerns about unfair prejudice raised by Defendant.

In that regard, the Court notes that if this motion were denied on the grounds of undue delay, that ruling would not be an adjudication on the merits of these five new causes of action.  Nothing would stop Plaintiff from filing a new complaint against Defendant Buie with the new causes of action and then seeking to have the two cases related and ultimately consolidated.

Finally, Defendants ask for a number of substantive conditions that are unclear to the Court or are not adequately supported.  The Court will grant the motion.  The filing of an amended complaint reopens the pleadings: Defendant Buie will be able to respond to the amended complaint and may at that time file an amended cross-complaint.  All of Defendant’s defenses may be presented through her answer or other response to the amended complaint.

Conclusion

The Court GRANTS Plaintiff Durand Anderson’s motion for leave to file the First Amended Complaint.

The Court GRANTS LEAVE to Plaintiff to file and serve the First Amended Complaint by no later than September 30, 2026.  (To the extent that Plaintiff seeks an order that the First Amended Complaint is “deemed” filed, that request is denied.)

Defendant must file an answer or other responsive pleading within the time set by Code.

To protect Defendant against undue prejudice, the Court CONTINUES the trial date to May 3, 2027, at 9:30 a.m., in Department 514.  The Final Status Conference is CONTINUED to April 23, 2027, at 9:30 a.m., in Department 514.

All discovery deadlines are reset based on the new trial date.

Moving Party is ordered to give notice.

Case Number: 24STCV04371    Hearing Date: September 23, 2026    Dept: 514

Taghavi v. FAA Beverly Hills, Inc.
24STCV04371
Plaintiff’s Motion for Attorney’s Fees, Costs, and Expenses
Defendant’s Motion to Tax Costs

Tentative Ruling

The motion for attorney’s fees is granted in part.

The motion to tax costs is granted in part.

Background

This matter is brought under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act.

On February 21, 2024, Shane Taghavi (“Plaintiff”) filed the complaint in this action against FAA Beverly Hills, Inc dba Beverly Hills BMW (“FAA”); BMW of North America, LLC (“Defendant”); and Does 1 through 10.

On March 15, 2024, Defendant filed an answer.

On March 28, 2024, FAA filed an answer.

On June 5, 2026, Plaintiff filed a notice of settlement of the entire case.

Two motions are currently before the Court and set for hearing on September 23.

First, on August 3, 2026, Plaintiff filed this motion for attorney’s fees and expenses.  Defendant filed an opposition on August 26.  No reply has been filed.  The hearing was initially set for September 9 and was continued by the Court to September 23.

Second, also on August 3, 2026, Plaintiff filed a memorandum of costs.  On August 20, Defendant filed a motion to tax costs.  Plaintiff filed an opposition on September 10, and Defendant filed a reply on September 16.

Legal Standard

Attorney’s Fees and Expenses

Under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act, a consumer who is damaged by a failure to comply with the Act may bring an action for damages and other relief. (Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (a).)  A prevailing plaintiff may also recover attorneys’ fees and costs:

“If the buyer prevails in an action under this section, the buyer shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.”

(Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).) 

An attorneys’ fee award is generally to be based upon actual time expended rather than being tied to any percentage of the recovery. (Drouin v. Fleetwood Enterprises (1985) 163 Cal.App.3d 486, 493.) Because Plaintiff is suing under the Song-Beverly Act, the legislative policies favor recovery of all attorney fees reasonably expended without limiting the fees to a proportion of the actual recovery. (Graciano v. Robinson Ford Sales, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 140, 164.)

The fee setting inquiry in California ordinarily begins with the “lodestar” method, i.e., multiplying the number of hours reasonably expended by a reasonable hourly rate.  (PLCM Group v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095.)  Such an approach anchors the trial court’s analysis to an objective determination of the value of the attorney’s services, ensuring that the amount awarded is not arbitrary. (Serrano v. Priest (1977) 20 Cal.3d 25, 48 fn.23; Bernardi v. County of Monterey (2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 1379, 1393-1394.)  The Court may rely on its own experience and knowledge in determining the reasonable value of attorney fees.  (PLCM Group, supra, 22 Cal.4th at p. 1096; Taylor v. Nabors Drilling USA, LP (2014) 222 Cal.App.4th 1228, 1249.) 

The lodestar figure may be adjusted, based on consideration of factors specific to the case, in order to fix the fee at the fair market value for the legal services provided. (Serrano, supra, 20 Cal.3d at p. 49.) The factors considered in determining the modification of the lodestar include the nature and difficulty of the litigation, the amount of money involved, the skill required and employed to handle the case, the attention given, the success or failure, and other circumstances in the case. (Ibid.; Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132; EnPalm, LLC v. Teitler Family Trust (2008) 162 Cal.App.4th 770, 774.) 

A positive multiplier may be applied to compensate the attorney for the prevailing party at a rate reflecting the risk of nonpayment (and delayed payment) in contingency cases. (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1138.)  A negative modifier is appropriate when duplicative work has been performed. (Thayer v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2001) 92 Cal.App.4th 819.)

California courts have generally “rejected the notion that the fee award must be proportionate to the amount of damages recovered.” (Niederer v. Ferreira (1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 1485, 1508. 

Motion to Tax Costs

Code of Civil Procedure section 1032 provides that, in general, the “prevailing party” is entitled as a matter of right to recover costs for suit in any action or proceeding. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1032, subd. (b); see Santisas v. Goodin (1998) 17 Cal.4th 599, 606; Scott Co. Of Calif. v. Blount, Inc. (1999) 20 Cal.4th 1103, 1108.)  Assuming the “prevailing party” requirements are met, the trial court has no discretion to order each party to bear his or her own costs of suit.  (Michell v. Olick (1996) 49 Cal.App.4th 1194, 1198; Nelson v. Anderson (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 111, 129.)    

Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5, subdivision (a) enumerates items that are allowable as costs under section 1032.  These costs must be “reasonable in amount” and  “reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation, rather than merely convenient or beneficial to its preparation.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (c)(2) & (c)(3).)  Subdivision (b)of section 1033.5 identifies certain items that are generally not allowable as costs.  Subdivision (c) states that other items may be allowed or denied as costs in the discretion of the court.  (Id., subd. (c)(4).)

If the items appearing in a cost bill appear to be proper charges, the burden is on the party seeking to tax costs to show that they were not reasonable or necessary.  (Ladas v. California State Automotive Assoc. (1993) 19 Cal.App.4th 761, 773-774.)  But once there is a proper objection, the burden of proof is on the party claiming them as costs.  (Ibid.) Whether a cost item was reasonably necessary to the litigation presents a question of fact for the trial court.  (Ibid.)   

Request for Judicial Notice

Plaintiff asks the Court to take judicial notice of three Minute Orders issued in other cases in Los Angeles County Superior Court.  The request is granted, but the Court notes that trial court orders are not binding precedent or authority.

Discussion

Attorney’s Fees and Expenses

Plaintiff moves for fees and expenses as a prevailing party under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act.  

Plaintiff seeks to recover for 88.7 hours of time and for $3,842.34 in expenses.  As the expenses are also the subject of a memorandum of costs (and a motion to tax those costs), the expenses are discussed below.

There is no dispute that, in light of the settlement, Plaintiff is the prevailing party and is entitled to recover reasonable fees and costs. 

Defendant opposes the motion, arguing that many of the fees and costs requested are excessive, not reasonable, or otherwise not recoverable.

Time Expended

The Court begins with an analysis of the hours expended under a lodestar analysis.  The Court has reviewed the billing records of Plaintiff’s counsel on a line-by-line basis.  (See Yousef Decl., Exh. C.)  Based on the Court’s review of the time entries, the Court concludes that Plaintiff has not shown that some of the time expended was reasonable.  In some cases, the time entries were so heavily redacted that the Court could not conduct a reasonableness review; although of course the Court understands that some information in billing records may be privileged, the Court cannot determine that time spent is reasonable when it cannot make sense of the time entries.  In other cases, the descriptions were inadequate or the amount of time billed was not reasonable.

After conducting this review, the Court concludes as follows: Logan Hensley’s time is reduced to 26.4 hours; Laura Goolsby’s time is reduced to 4.6 hours; Virginia Walls’s time is reduced to 13.5 hours; Clarence Serrano’s time is reduced to 5.4 hours; Jennifer Romo’s time is reduced to 1.2 hours; Caleth Ibarra’s time is reduced to 2.6 hours; Jose Gonzalez’s time is reduced to 0.0 hours; Christian Abad’s time is reduced to 0.0 hours; and Walter Orellana’s time is reduced to 0.0 hours.  In all other respects, Plaintiff has shown that the time billed is reasonable, and Defendant’s challenges are rejected.

Billing Rates

The Court finds that the billing rates charged by Plaintiff’s counsel are reasonable and consistent with the rates reasonably charged by other attorneys, paralegals, and professional support staff for similar work in the community.

With the time adjustments noted above, the lodestar fee is reduced to $23,783.00

Multiplier

The Court declines to award a multiplier to Plaintiff.  Although the Court understands that this work is contingent in nature, and there is a risk of not recovering and delay in receiving payment for the work performed, the Court determines that the reasonable hourly fees requested by Plaintiff, and approved by this Court, takes into account and therefore fully compensates Plaintiff and the attorneys for those risks and delays.

The Court sets the amount of the award for reasonable attorney’s fees in this matter at $23,783.00.

Motion to Tax Costs

Plaintiff filed a Memorandum listing $3,842.34 in costs.  Defendant has filed this motion to tax costs.

The Court has considered the evidence and argument presented by both sides.  The Court finds that Plaintiff has not shown the expert witness fees of $2,721 and the downloading/postage fees of $91.99 are recoverable under Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5, subdivisions (a) and (b).  In all other respects, the motion is denied.

Accordingly, the Court taxes the memorandum of costs in the amount of $2,812.99, reducing the amount of costs that Plaintiff may recover to $1,029.35.

Conclusion

The Court GRANTS IN PART the motion of Plaintiff Shane Taghavi to recover reasonable attorney’s fees.

The Court DETERMINES that the amount of fees reasonably expended by Plaintiff’s counsel in this matter is $23,783.00.

The Court GRANTS IN PART the motion of Defendant BMW of North America, LLC to tax costs.

The Court taxes costs in the amount of $2,812.99, and accordingly reduces the total amount of recoverable costs to $1,029.35.

The Court ORDERS Defendant BMW of North America, LLC to pay fees and costs to Plaintiff Shane Taghavi in the amount of $24,812.35 pursuant to Civil Code section 1794 by no later than October 23, 2026.

Plaintiff is ORDERED to give notice.
