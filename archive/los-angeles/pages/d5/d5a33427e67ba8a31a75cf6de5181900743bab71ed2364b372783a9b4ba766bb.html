DEPARTMENT 515 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 19STCV17761    Hearing Date: September 10, 2026    Dept: 515

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: Hearing on Motion to Enforce Settlement

The Motion to Enforce Settlement is denied.

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiffs Vahe Menachkanian (Menachkanian) and Maryet Manaserian (Manaserian) (collectively, “Plaintiffs”) filed this action against Simon M. Keushkerian, M.D., Faisal A. Khan, M.D., and Glendale Adventist Medical Center (GAMC), alleging that a laparotomy sponge was left in Menachkanian’s body following colectomy surgery performed at GAMC.

The causes of action are: (1) Professional Negligence (Medical Malpractice); and (2) Loss of Consortium.

GAMC filed a Motion to Enforce Settlement, a Notice of No Opposition, and a supplemental declaration. No Opposition was filed.

On September 23, 2024, Plaintiffs and GAMC executed a Settlement Agreement and Release of All Claims (the Agreement), and on January 22, 2025, the Court entered Plaintiffs’ Request for Dismissal with prejudice as to the entire action. GAMC filed the Motion together with an Application to File Documents Under Seal, supporting both with a copy of the Agreement redacted at its payment provisions. On July 28, 2026, the Court continued the hearing and ordered GAMC to lodge a complete, unredacted copy of the Agreement conditionally under seal. On August 27, 2026, the Court denied the Application and continued the hearing again. GAMC then filed an unredacted copy of the Agreement in the public record.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6 authorizes the Court, on motion, to enter judgment on the terms of a written settlement signed by the parties to pending litigation. (Code Civ. Proc., § 664.6, subd. (a).) “ In ruling on a motion to enter judgment the trial court acts as a trier of fact. It must determine whether the parties entered into a valid and binding settlement.” (Kohn v. Jaymar-Ruby, Inc. (1994) 23 Cal.App.4th 1530, 1533.) The statute supplies a summary means of enforcing a settlement as written, and “nothing in section 664.6 authorizes a judge to create the material terms of a settlement, as opposed to deciding what terms the parties themselves have previously agreed upon.” (Weddington Productions, Inc. v. Flick (1998) 60 Cal.App.4th 793, 810.) The Agreement is a contract, and the Court ascertains its meaning from the writing, reading the whole together so that each part helps interpret the others. (Civ. Code, §§ 1639, 1641.)

 

ANALYSIS

I. Signed Writing

GAMC contends that the Agreement is a binding written settlement satisfying the statute because Plaintiffs and their counsel signed it.

Here, the parties reduced their settlement of this action to writing and executed it on September 23, 2024. Menachkanian and Manaserian signed the Agreement, Menachkanian initialed each of its nine pages, counsel of record for Plaintiffs approved it as to form and content, and counsel for GAMC signed it. (Kjar Sept. 4 Decl., Ex. A, at pp. 1-9.) No party contests its execution, its authenticity, or its terms. Thus, the Agreement is a valid and binding written settlement.

II. Medicare Holdback

GAMC contends that Plaintiffs materially breached the Agreement by failing to satisfy the Medicare lien and to provide proof of satisfaction, and that the Agreement entitles GAMC to send the withheld funds to the lienholders. GAMC describes the sum withheld as $36,276.41, stating that the settlement payment “was structured in two parts,” the second of which, “in the amount of $36,276.41[,] was to be held back until Plaintiffs presented proof of satisfaction of Medicare liens.” (Mot., at p. 3:16-19; Kjar Decl., ¶ 4.)

However, the plain terms of the Agreement permitted GAMC to withhold $26,000 and no more. Section 1.1 fixed the total settlement at $162,500, payable in two parts. Section 1.2 required GAMC to pay $136,500 to Plaintiffs and their counsel after execution and delivery of the signed Agreement, and permitted GAMC to “hold back $26,000.00 from the settlement proceeds until these liens have been satisfied by RELEASORS.” Section 1.3 directed that the sum withheld be forwarded to Plaintiffs’ counsel once Plaintiffs present lien closure letters or other evidence that the liens are satisfied. (Kjar Sept. 4 Decl., Ex. A, at pp. 1-2, §§ 1.1-1.3.) The two payments the Agreement describes sum to the total the Agreement states.

No provision adjusts that figure to the amount Medicare later demanded. The Agreement takes up the possibility of a larger demand and assigns it to Plaintiffs, who “voluntarily accepts the risk that Medicare may demand reimbursement in excess of any amount the parties allocate to satisfying any Conditional Payment.” (Kjar Sept. 4 Decl., Ex. A, at p. 6, § 6.6.) Plaintiffs and their counsel bear the obligation to secure a final demand letter and to verify, resolve, and satisfy any Medicare reimbursement obligation. (Kjar Sept. 4 Decl., Ex. A, at pp. 3-5, §§ 2.7, 4.1, 6.3.) The Agreement states that it “contains the ENTIRE AGREEMENT between the parties.” (Kjar Sept. 4 Decl., Ex. A, at p. 8, § 9.2.)

GAMC withheld $36,276.41, exceeding by $10,276.41 the sum the Agreement allowed, and the check GAMC sent Plaintiffs’ counsel on January 9, 2025 fell short of the $136,500 that section 1.2 required. (Kjar Decl., ¶ 7.) Accordingly, the Agreement capped the sum GAMC could withhold at $26,000, and it placed the risk that Medicare would demand more on Plaintiffs.

III. Requested Relief

GAMC asks the Court to authorize it to pay $36,276.41 directly to the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, and represents that the Agreement already permits that payment: “Under the express terms of the settlement agreement, Moving Defendant is authorized to pay the holdback funds directly to Medicare as the lienholder.” (Mot., at p. 6:11-13.) No Reply was filed.

Here, nothing in the Agreement conditions GAMC’S resort to the withheld funds on an order of this Court. Section 1.4 provides: “If RELEASORS do not present the lien closure letters to RELEASEE within 90 days from the time RELEASORS receive the settlement funds, then RELEASEE is entitled to send the holdback funds to the lienholders, jointly payable to RELEASORS and the lienholders.” (Kjar Sept. 4 Decl., Ex. A, at p. 2, § 1.4.) The section requires no application, no approval, and no order.

What section 1.4 permits and what GAMC requests are different things. Section 1.4 permits GAMC to send $26,000 to the lienholders on an instrument jointly payable to Plaintiffs and the lienholders. GAMC asks the Court to authorize payment of $36,276.41 to Medicare alone. (Mot., at pp. 6:14-21, 7:2-7.)

An order granting that request would not enforce the Agreement. It would enlarge by $10,276.41 the sum GAMC may disburse, and it would remove Plaintiffs from an instrument the Agreement makes payable to them. Each is a material term, and neither is a term the parties agreed upon. Nothing in section 664.6 authorizes the Court to create the material terms of a settlement rather than decide what terms the parties themselves agreed upon. (Weddington Productions, Inc. v. Flick, supra, 60 Cal.App.4th at p. 810.) GAMC identifies no obstacle to performing the Agreement as written, and the relief it seeks is available only by rewriting it.

Accordingly, the Motion to Enforce Settlement is denied.

CONCLUSION

The Motion to Enforce Settlement is denied.


Case Number: 24STCV26217    Hearing Date: September 10, 2026    Dept: 515

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: Hearing on Motion to Tax Costs

The Motion to Tax Costs is denied.

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiffs Fabian Ladera and Israel Torreblanca Benigno (Plaintiffs) filed this action against General Motors LLC (GM) and Felix Chevrolet (Dealer), alleging that they purchased a new 2021 Chevrolet 1500 that manifested defects GM failed to repair after a reasonable number of attempts.

The causes of action are: (1) Breach of Implied Warranty of Merchantability under the Song-Beverly Warranty Act; and (2) Breach of Express Warranty under the Song-Beverly Warranty Act.

GM filed a Motion to Strike or Tax Costs. Plaintiffs filed an Opposition.

The Court dismissed the action as to Dealer without prejudice on April 8, 2025. In October 2025, Plaintiffs accepted GM’s offer to compromise under Code of Civil Procedure section 998, which left attorney fees, costs, and expenses to determination by noticed motion. On December 18, 2025, the Court dismissed the action without prejudice and retained jurisdiction to enforce the settlement under Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6. On June 16, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a Memorandum of Costs and a Motion for Attorney Fees, Costs and Expenses. On August 17, 2026, the Court denied that motion as untimely and denied its request for costs and expenses without prejudice to their determination on the instant Motion.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

“Except as otherwise expressly provided by statute, a prevailing party is entitled as a matter of right to recover costs in any action or proceeding.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1032, subd. (b).) That entitlement operates as a default. “Nothing in this section shall prohibit parties from stipulating to alternative procedures for awarding costs in the litigation pursuant to rules adopted under Section 1034.” (Id., subd. (c).)

A prevailing party claims costs by serving and filing a memorandum of costs. The memorandum “must be verified by a statement of the party, attorney, or agent that to the best of his or her knowledge the items of cost are correct and were necessarily incurred in the case.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1700(a)(1).)

“If items on their face appear to be proper charges, the verified memorandum of costs is prima facie evidence of their propriety, and the burden is on the party seeking to tax costs to show they were not reasonable or necessary. ‘On the other hand, if items are properly objected to, they are put in issue and the burden of proof is on the party claiming them as costs.’ However, whether a cost item was reasonably necessary is still a question of fact to be decided by the trial court.” (Jones v. Dumrichob (1998) 63 Cal.App.4th 1258, 1266 (Jones), quoting Ladas v. California State Auto. Assn. (1993) 19 Cal.App.4th 761, 774.) A party’s “‘mere statements in the points and authorities accompanying its notice of motion to strike cost bill and the declaration of its counsel are insufficient to rebut the prima facie showing [that the costs were necessarily incurred].’” (Jones, supra, 63 Cal.App.4th at p. 1266, quoting Rappenecker v. Sea-Land Service, Inc. (1979) 93 Cal.App.3d 256, 266 [alteration made by the Jones court].)

 

ANALYSIS

I. Timeliness

“A prevailing party who claims costs must serve and file a memorandum of costs within 15 days after the date of service of the notice of entry of judgment or dismissal by the clerk under Code of Civil Procedure section 664.5 or the date of service of written notice of entry of judgment or dismissal, or within 180 days after entry of judgment, whichever is first.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1700(a)(1).) “The time provisions relating to the filing of a memorandum of costs, ‘while not jurisdictional, are mandatory.’” (Hydratec, Inc. v. Sun Valley 260 Orchard & Vineyard Co. (1990) 223 Cal.App.3d 924, 929.) “All dismissals ordered by the court shall be in the form of a written order signed by the court and filed in the action and those orders when so filed shall constitute judgments and be effective for all purposes.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 581d.)

A. Trigger Date

GM contends that the Memorandum of Costs is untimely in its entirety because the 15-day period of rule 3.1700(a)(1) began to run when Plaintiffs accepted GM’s section 998 offer in October 2025. Plaintiffs argue that the memorandum was filed within 180 days of the December 18, 2025 dismissal.

The Court rejects GM’s contention. GM relies on Madrigal v. Hyundai Motor America (2025) 17 Cal.5th 592, 605, for the proposition that “‘[a]s between the parties thereto and for purposes of enforcement of settlement agreements, a compromise agreement contemplating payment by defendant and dismissal of the action by plaintiff is the legal equivalent of a judgment in plaintiff’s favor.’” (Ibid., quoting DeSaulles v. Community Hospital of Monterey Peninsula (2016) 62 Cal.4th 1140, 1155.) That a settlement is the equivalent of a judgment for purposes of enforcing it does not make the acceptance of a section 998 offer the date on which judgment is entered. Here, judgment was entered when the Court dismissed the action under Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6 by written order filed December 18, 2025. (Code Civ. Proc., § 581d.) Timeliness is measured from that date.

B. Notice of Entry

GM replies that the Court’s August 17, 2026 ruling made findings that dispose of the Motion: the Court dismissed the action by written order signed and filed December 18, 2025, and the clerk electronically served a file-endorsed copy that day with the clerk’s certificate of service appended. GM adds that the same service was at least “written notice of entry of judgment or dismissal” under rule 3.1700(a)(1), that service satisfying rule 8.104’s stricter requirements cannot fail rule 3.1700’s, and that the 15-day period therefore ran from December 18, 2025 and expired in early January 2026.

GM’s argument reads the section 664.5 qualifier out of rule 3.1700(a)(1). The rule sets two 15-day triggers and one backstop. The first trigger is service by the clerk, and not any service by the clerk: service “under Code of Civil Procedure section 664.5.” The second is service of written notice of entry, which Code of Civil Procedure section 664.5, subdivision (a), assigns to “the party submitting an order or judgment for entry” in a contested action where the prevailing party is represented. The backstop is 180 days after entry of judgment.

The fee deadline is written differently. A motion for attorney fees must be served and filed “within the time for filing a notice of appeal under rules 8.104 and 8.108.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1702(b)(1).) Rule 8.104(a)(1)(A) runs 60 days from the date “the superior court clerk serves on the party filing the notice of appeal a document entitled ‘Notice of Entry’ of judgment or a filed-endorsed copy of the judgment, showing the date either was served.” The appeal rule asks only what document the clerk served and whether it shows the date it was served. That is the test the Supreme Court applied in Alan v. American Honda Motor Co., Inc. (2007) 40 Cal.4th 894 (Alan), which requires a single, self-sufficient document, holds that “the ‘file-stamped copy of the judgment’ ... must truly be file stamped” (id. at p. 903), and confirms that the clerk could satisfy the single-document requirement “by attaching a certificate of mailing to the file-stamped judgment or appealable order.” (Id. at p. 905.) Nothing in Alan conditions the appeal trigger on a court order directing the clerk to give notice.

The difference between the two rules is deliberate. In Van Beurden Ins. Services, Inc. v. Customized Worldwide Weather Ins. Agency, Inc. (1997) 15 Cal.4th 51 (Van Beurden), the Supreme Court construed the same “pursuant to Section 664.5” language in Code of Civil Procedure section 660. Before 1981, a clerk’s unrequired notice “was purely voluntary and ought to be given no more significance than if the same information had been conveyed by any disinterested person not a party to the action.” (Id. at p. 58.) The 1981 and 1982 amendments placed “the primary burden of providing formal ‘notice of entry’ of judgment on the party submitting the order or judgment for entry,” leaving the clerk to give notice in the situations the statute names or “[u]pon order of the court.” (Id. at pp. 59-60.) The new trial statutes and several rules of court, among them former rule 870(a) governing prejudgment costs, “continued to reference the date of mailing of notice by the clerk of the court ‘pursuant to Section 664.5,’” while the appeal rule alone had been revised to delete that reference. (Id. at p. 60 & fn. 3.) Former rule 870(a) is now rule 3.1700(a), and it still carries the reference.

As the Supreme Court explained, because a clerk who mails a file-stamped judgment “may be doing so either to fulfill his duty under California Rules of Court, rule 309 or because he was ordered to do so under Code of Civil Procedure section 664.5,” no presumption that the clerk acted under the statute is warranted. (Van Beurden, supra, 15 Cal.4th at p. 63.) Outside the situations in which section 664.5 makes clerk’s notice mandatory, “to qualify as a notice of entry of judgment under Code of Civil Procedure section 664.5, the clerk’s mailed notice must affirmatively state that it was given ‘upon order by the court’ or ‘under section 664.5,’ and a certificate of mailing the notice must be executed and placed in the file.” (Id. at p. 64.) Absent that statement, only the submitting party’s service and filing of notice of entry shortens the period. (Id. at p. 65.) Rule 3.1109, the successor to rule 309, states the same rule: the clerk’s notification of a ruling “constitutes service of notice only if the clerk is required to give notice under Code of Civil Procedure section 664.5.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1109(a).)

Thus, a clerk’s service of a file-endorsed judgment can start the time to seek attorney fees while leaving the time to claim costs governed by the 180-day period or by a party’s notice of entry. The Judicial Council removed the section 664.5 qualifier from the appeal rule and left it in the costs rule (Van Beurden, supra, 15 Cal.4th at p. 60, fn. 3), and the Court applies each rule as written.

Here, the clerk’s December 18, 2025 service of the order of dismissal was not made under section 664.5. No party served notice of entry. Therefore, the applicable period is 180 days from entry of judgment. Plaintiffs filed and served the Memorandum of Costs on June 16, 2026, which GM itself counts as the 180th day. (Reply, at p. 3:19.) Therefore the Memorandum of Costs is timely.

II. Costs Claimed

GM objects to four items in the Memorandum of Costs. Its objections put the items in issue, and Plaintiffs responded with the One Legal invoices and court receipts. (Nita Decl., ¶ 3, Ex. A.) Each item is a cost the statute expressly allows, and the Court finds that each was reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation, reasonable in amount, and reasonably incurred in the commencement and prosecution of this action. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subds. (a)(1), (a)(4), (a)(14), (c)(2), (c)(3); Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).) Because each item falls within section 1033.5, subdivision (a), the Court need not decide whether Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d), reaches expenses outside that section. The Court has considered GM’s itemized objections on reply to the components of the One Legal charges and finds that each component was incurred in prosecuting the action.

A. Filing Fee

GM contends that the $60 filing fee for Plaintiffs’ motion for attorney fees was optional, advanced nothing, and is overhead. Plaintiffs argue that the fee is recoverable. The fee was incurred and paid, and filing the motion was a step in prosecuting the action. (Nita Decl., Ex. A; Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (c)(1).) That the fee was paid the day after the Memorandum of Costs was served does not change that finding. The item is allowed.

B. Jury Fees

GM contends that the $150 jury fee is recoverable only when a trial occurs. Plaintiffs argue that they paid the fee to preserve their right to a jury trial. The nonrefundable jury fee was a reasonably incurred cost of a case set for jury trial. (Nita Decl., Ex. A: Code Civ. Proc., § 631, subd. (b).) The Court allows the item.

C. Service of Process

GM contends that the $113.58 charge for serving the summons on Dealer was unnecessary because the service did not contribute to the litigation and Dealer was later dismissed. Plaintiffs argue that Dealer, which sold and repaired the vehicle, was a proper defendant. Serving a named defendant with the summons is a necessary cost of commencing the action, and the charge is reasonable in amount. (Nita Decl., Ex. A; Complaint, ¶¶ 3, 7, 11.) The item is allowed.

D. Electronic Filing Fees

GM contends that the $641.19 in electronic filing fees is unsupported. Plaintiffs argue that the fees were paid to One Legal to file and serve documents in this action and are documented by the invoices. Electronic filing is required in this Court, and the invoices document the charges for each filing in this action. (Nita Decl., ¶ 3, Ex. A; Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (a)(14).) The Court allows the item.

Accordingly, the Motion to Tax Costs is denied.

III. Attorney Fees

Plaintiffs request $937 in attorney fees for 1.5 hours claimed for opposing this Motion, including 0.5 hours anticipated for the hearing, at $625 per hour, as prevailing parties under Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d), and argue that a party entitled to fees may recover fees for establishing the fee claim under Serrano v. Unruh (1982) 32 Cal.3d 621. (Nita Decl., ¶ 4.)

The request is denied. An Opposition is not an appropriate method of requesting attorney fees. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5 subd. (c)(5)(A) [“Attorney’s fees allowable as costs … may be fixed as follows: (i) upon a noticed motion, (ii) at the time a statement of decision is rendered, (iii) upon application supported by affidavit made concurrently with a claim for other costs, or (iv) upon entry of default judgment.”].)

 

CONCLUSION

The Motion to Tax Costs is denied.


Case Number: 26STCV00526    Hearing Date: September 10, 2026    Dept: 515

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: Hearing on Motion for Summary Adjudication

The Motion for Summary Adjudication is denied.

 

BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Karen Petrosov (Plaintiff) filed this action against Tanya Kurlyand (Kurlyand), Steven Kamenetsky (Steven), and Michael Kamenetsky (Michael) (collectively, “Defendants”), alleging that he holds record title to a residence at 4908 Hesperia Avenue in Encino (the Property), that Defendants displaced him from it, and that Defendants claim the Property as their own.

The causes of action in the Verified First Amended Complaint (FAC) are: (1) Quiet Title; (2) Ejectment; (3) Declaratory Relief; (4) Trespass; (5) Conversion; and (6) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress.

Plaintiff filed a Motion for Summary Adjudication of the first, second, and third causes of action. Defendants filed an Opposition. Plaintiff filed a Reply.

 

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

Plaintiff requests judicial notice of Exhibits 3 through 13 to the Declaration of John W. Martin, certified copies of 11 instruments recorded in the Official Records of Los Angeles County: five grant deeds, three deeds of trust, an abstract of judgment, and two notices of state tax lien. (RJN, at pp. 2-3.) Defendants’ Response to Plaintiff’s Separate Statement recites that an opposition to the request was filed concurrently with it. (Response to Separate Statement, at p. 1:19-20.) None was filed.

A recorded instrument is an official act of the executive branch, and the request is granted as to each exhibit. (Evid. Code, § 452, subds. (c), (h), § 453; Ragland v. U.S. Bank National Assn. (2012) 209 Cal.App.4th 182, 194.) Judicial notice of a recorded instrument extends to “‘the fact of a document’s recordation, the date the document was recorded and executed, the parties to the transaction reflected in a recorded document, and the document’s legally operative language,’” and the Court “‘may deduce and rely upon the legal effect of the recorded document, when that effect is clear from its face.’” (Linda Vista Village San Diego Homeowners Assn., Inc. v. Tecolote Investors, LLC (2015) 234 Cal.App.4th 166, 185.)

 

EVIDENTIARY OBJECTIONS

The Court rules on Defendants’ objections to the Declaration of Karen Petrosov as follows:

Objections 1 through 12. Overruled.

The Court rules on Plaintiff’s objections to the Declaration of Michael Kamenetsky as follows:

Objections 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8. Overruled.

Objection 3. Sustained. Lack of personal knowledge. (Evid. Code, § 702, subd. (a).)

 

LEGAL STANDARD

Summary judgment is proper only where no triable issue of material fact exists and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c).) A plaintiff moving for summary judgment “bears the burden of persuasion that ‘each element of’ the ‘cause of action’ in question has been ‘proved,’ and hence that ‘there is no defense’ thereto.” (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850 (Aguilar).) A plaintiff “has met that party’s burden of showing that there is no defense to a cause of action if that party has proved each element of the cause of action entitling the party to judgment on the cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(1).) Only then does the burden shift to the defendant to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense. (Ibid.)

“On a motion for summary judgment, the initial burden is always on the moving party to make a prima facie showing that there are no triable issues of material fact.” (Scalf v. D.B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1518.) The burden does not shift merely because no opposition was filed. A motion that fails to make that showing is denied whether or not it is opposed.

A party may also move for summary adjudication of one or more causes of action or affirmative defenses. The motion “shall be granted only if it completely disposes of a cause of action, an affirmative defense, a claim for damages, or an issue of duty.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (f)(1).) A motion for summary adjudication proceeds in all procedural respects as a motion for summary judgment. (Id., subd. (f)(2).)

 

ANALYSIS

I. Scope of Pleadings

Plaintiff argues on reply that Defendants’ resulting-trust theory, under which Leonid Kamenetsky (Leonid) retained beneficial ownership of the Property after conveying legal title to his corporation in 2012, is outside the issues framed by the FAC and the Answer, because the Answer pleads no such theory and Plaintiff had no notice of it when he filed the Motion.

“The pleadings delimit the scope of the issues on a summary judgment motion. [Citation.] A party may not oppose a summary judgment motion based on a claim, theory, or defense that is not alleged in the pleadings.” (Cal. Bank & Trust v. Lawlor (2013) 222 Cal.App.4th 625, 637, fn. 3 (Lawlor).) The rule reaches the new matter an answer must plead. “The phrase ‘new matter’ refers to something relied on by a defendant which is not put in issue by the plaintiff. . . . Where, however, the answer sets forth facts showing some essential allegation of the complaint is not true, such facts are not ‘new matter,’ but only a traverse.” (State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. v. Superior Court (1991) 228 Cal.App.3d 721, 725.) Facts that “‘directly tend to disprove’” an allegation of the complaint may be offered under a general denial; only facts that “‘establish a defense independently of them’” are new matter. (Walsh v. West Valley Mission Community College Dist. (1998) 66 Cal.App.4th 1532, 1546.)

A quiet title complaint must state “[t]he title of the plaintiff as to which a determination under this chapter is sought and the basis of the title.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 761.020, subd. (b).) The court “shall examine into and determine the plaintiff’s title against the claims of all the defendants.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 764.010.) The plaintiff’s title is therefore the element the plaintiff must prove, and the judgment sought is a determination that the defendant’s adverse claim is without right.

Here, Defendants’ theory is a traverse. Plaintiff alleges that he is the sole owner of the Property in fee simple, that his title is superior to any claim of Defendants, and that Defendants have no estate, right, title, or interest adverse to him. (FAC, ¶¶ 51-56.) Defendants denied those allegations. (Martin Decl., Ex. 14, ¶¶ 9-13, 51-57; Michael Kamenetsky Answer, ¶¶ 9-13, 51-57.) Evidence that Plaintiff holds bare legal title subject to a beneficial interest that Leonid retained, and that Defendants now claim through the Kamenetsky Revocable Living Trust (the Trust), directly tends to disprove the allegation of full ownership. It does not establish a defense independent of that allegation. Plaintiff himself relies on the Answer’s admission that Defendants claim to be the rightful owners of the Property to establish the adverse claim his cause of action requires. (Separate Statement, Fact 24; Martin Decl., Ex. 14, ¶ 53.) Michael’s Answer, moreover, pleads a claim of interest in the Property under Code of Civil Procedure section 761.030, subdivision (a)(1). (Michael Kamenetsky Answer, at p. 2:6-10.) Therefore, the general denials admit the evidence.

Plaintiff was not required to negate Defendants’ theory of a retained beneficial interest to carry his initial burden. However, Defendants may offer evidence of that interest to show a triable issue on the element of title itself. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(1).) Plaintiff’s contention that he lacked notice of the theory fails for the same reason. The general denials placed his title, legal and beneficial, in issue, and whether the Verified First Amended Cross-Complaint pleaded the same theory before or after the Motion was filed does not change what the Answer put at issue. The Court therefore considers Defendants’ evidence of a retained beneficial interest in deciding whether a triable issue exists.

II. Quiet Title

Plaintiff contends that the recorded chain of title establishes his sole legal title, that Evidence Code section 662 therefore presumes his full beneficial title, and that Defendants cannot produce clear and convincing evidence of a competing interest.

Defendants argue that section 662 does not apply where the validity of title is challenged, that their evidence in any event rebuts the presumption, and that a quiet title action adjudicates equitable as well as record interests.

Plaintiff replies that section 662 applies because Defendants accept the deeds and dispute only beneficial ownership, that a resulting trust arises from the circumstances of the transfer and not from later conduct, and that Defendants’ post-conveyance evidence is equivocal.

A. Legal Title

An action to quiet title may be brought “to establish title against adverse claims to real or personal property or any interest therein.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 760.020, subd. (a).) Evidence Code section 662 provides: “The owner of the legal title to property is presumed to be the owner of the full beneficial title. This presumption may be rebutted only by clear and convincing proof.” The section “has application, by its express terms, when there is no dispute as to where legal title resides but there is question as to where all or part of the beneficial title should rest.” (Murray v. Murray (1994) 26 Cal.App.4th 1062, 1067 (Murray).) It “does not apply, however, when title itself is challenged as not genuine.” (People v. Semaan (2007) 42 Cal.4th 79, 88.)

Here, Plaintiff established legal title. Leonid acquired the Property by grant deed in 2009 as his sole and separate property, and Kurlyand conveyed any community interest to him by interspousal transfer deed. (Martin Decl., ¶¶ 7-8, Exs. 3-4; Petrosov Decl., ¶ 12.) Leonid conveyed the Property to VIP Bail Bonds Corporation (VIP) by grant deed in 2012. (Martin Decl., ¶ 9, Ex. 5.) VIP conveyed it to Asset Managment LLC (the LLC) in July 2025, and the LLC conveyed it to Plaintiff by grant deed recorded November 17, 2025. (Martin Decl., ¶¶ 10-11, Exs. 6-7.) Counsel’s search of the Official Records disclosed no reconveyance from VIP to Leonid and no instrument altering that chain. (Martin Decl., ¶¶ 13-14.) Defendants do not dispute that each conveyance occurred. (Response to Separate Statement, at pp. 2-4.) Record legal title to the Property is therefore in Plaintiff.

Defendants contend that Leonid continued to hold the beneficial interest after conveying legal title to VIP, a corporation he owned. (Opp., at pp. 7:11-15, 8:18-25; Response to Separate Statement, at p. 3:4-13.) Defendants do not contend that the 2012 deed was forged, void, or ineffective to pass legal title. This is a dispute over where the beneficial title should rest; legal title is undisputed, and section 662’s presumption applies to it. (Murray, supra, 26 Cal.App.4th at p. 1067.)

Plaintiff’s initial burden was to establish each element of his cause of action, and it did not require him to negate Defendants’ theory of a retained beneficial interest. Plaintiff carried that burden. He established legal title through an unbroken recorded chain, and as to the 2012 conveyance the presumption of full beneficial title attaches to that showing. (Evid. Code, § 662.) The burden therefore shifted to Defendants to show a triable issue of material fact as to Plaintiff’s title. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(1).)

B. Triable Issue

A triable issue exists “if, and only if, the evidence would allow a reasonable trier of fact to find the underlying fact in favor of the party opposing the motion in accordance with the applicable standard of proof.” (Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at p. 850.) As to the 2012 conveyance, the question is whether Defendants’ evidence would permit a reasonable trier of fact to find by clear and convincing proof that Leonid retained beneficial ownership. Conduct consistent with ownership is competent evidence of beneficial ownership contrary to record title: a father who paid for property titled in his children, made no rent payments to them, and “at all times conducted himself in a manner consistent with belief in his ownership” held the beneficial interest, and the children “held bare legal title.” (Novak v. Novak (1967) 249 Cal.App.2d 438, 442.) Likewise, family members who contributed to the purchase price of property titled in two of them were found to hold equitable interests as beneficiaries of a resulting trust. (Lin v. Jeng (2012) 203 Cal.App.4th 1008, 1018.)

Here, Defendants’ evidence, although circumstantial, would permit a reasonable trier of fact to decide the question either way. First, the Property was the Kamenetsky family’s residence from 2009 and remained so after the 2012 conveyance to VIP, a corporation Plaintiff acknowledges Leonid wholly owned. (Kamenetsky Decl., ¶ 4; Petrosov Decl., ¶ 12.) Second, the mortgage statements and utility bills Michael attaches are addressed to Leonid at the Property through December 2025, and Michael states that his family paid the mortgage and the household expenses during the period in which Plaintiff claims to have owned the Property. (Kamenetsky Decl., ¶¶ 16-18, Exs. 2-3.) Third, the Property passed from Leonid’s corporation to Plaintiff through an entity Plaintiff controlled. The LLC was formed on July 25, 2025, with Plaintiff as its sole member and manager; VIP conveyed the Property to the LLC five days later; and the deed conveying it from the LLC to Plaintiff was recorded on November 17, 2025, while Leonid was hospitalized. (Petrosov Decl., ¶¶ 5, 7, 10; Kamenetsky Decl., ¶¶ 8, 11-12; Martin Decl., Ex. 7.) The LLC’s operating agreement provides that “[t]itle to all LLC property will be vested in the name of the LLC and not the name of any individual member.” (Petrosov Decl., Ex. B, art. VII(i).) During the same period Leonid’s health was declining and a commercial property connected with his businesses faced foreclosure. (Kamenetsky Decl., ¶ 6.) Finally, Plaintiff and his family moved into the Property while the Kamenetsky family was at the hospital with Leonid and with the family’s belongings still in the home. (Kamenetsky Decl., ¶ 13.)

Taken together, that evidence supports an inference that VIP and then the LLC held record title for Leonid’s benefit. A reasonable trier of fact could find by clear and convincing proof that Leonid retained the beneficial interest through the 2012 conveyance. The Court concludes that a triable issue of material fact exists as to Plaintiff’s title.

Plaintiff argues that a resulting trust arises from the circumstances existing when the transfer occurs and that Defendants identify no arrangement contemporaneous with the 2012 conveyance. Plaintiff concedes, however, that “[s]ubsequent conduct may have evidentiary significance in corroborating an ownership arrangement arising from the relevant transaction.” (Reply, at p. 7:13-15.) Defendants offer the family’s continued residence and payment of expenses for that purpose, as circumstantial evidence of the intent with which Leonid conveyed the Property to his corporation, and conduct of that kind is competent for that purpose. (Novak, supra, 249 Cal.App.2d at p. 442.) Nor does the clear and convincing standard change the result on this Motion. That standard governs the trier of fact’s ultimate finding; on summary adjudication it governs only whether a reasonable trier of fact could so find. (Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at p. 850.)

Accordingly, the Motion for Summary Adjudication of the First Cause of Action for Quiet Title is denied.

III. Ejectment

Plaintiff contends that his record title carries the exclusive right to possession and that Defendants possess the Property without any lease, license, or judicial authorization.

Defendants argue that the ejectment claim rises and falls with the quiet title claim, because whether their possession is wrongful depends on which party holds the superior right to possession.

Plaintiff replies that occupancy does not establish a legal right to possession superior to the owner’s.

“The essential elements of an ejectment action are (1) the plaintiff’s valid interest in the property and (2) the defendant’s wrongful possession and withholding thereof.” (2710 Sutter Ventures, LLC v. Millis (2022) 82 Cal.App.5th 842, 866.)

Here, Plaintiff’s right to possession derives from his ownership of the Property, and Defendants dispute ownership itself. Because a triable issue exists as to whether Plaintiff holds the full beneficial title, a triable issue exists as to whether his right to possession is superior to Defendants’ and whether their possession is wrongful. Plaintiff’s evidence that he demanded possession and that Defendants refused establishes the withholding but not its wrongfulness. (Petrosov Decl., ¶¶ 26-28.)

Accordingly, the Motion for Summary Adjudication of the Second Cause of Action for Ejectment is denied.

IV. Declaratory Relief

Plaintiff seeks a declaration that he is the sole owner of the Property in fee simple, that Defendants have no ownership or possessory interest in it and no right to occupy, control, or interfere with his possession, and that he is entitled to immediate and exclusive possession.

Defendants argue that the requested declaration cannot be separated from the disputed facts surrounding Plaintiff’s acquisition of title.

Here, the declaration Plaintiff seeks would resolve the same ownership dispute the quiet title cause of action presents. (FAC, ¶ 75.) For the same reasons articulated above, a triable issue of material fact precludes summary adjudication of the Third Cause of Action.

Accordingly, the Motion for Summary Adjudication of the Third Cause of Action for Declaratory Relief is denied.

 

CONCLUSION

The Motion for Summary Adjudication is denied.
