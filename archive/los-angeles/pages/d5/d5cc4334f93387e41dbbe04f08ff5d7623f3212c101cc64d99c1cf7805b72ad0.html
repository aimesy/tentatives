[Tentative] Order on Petition for an Order Relieving Petitioner from the Provisions of Government Code Section 945.4

 

 

INTRODUCTION

 

            On July 2, 2024, Petitioner Ilsia Maly Martinez Rodriguez (“Petitioner”) was allegedly injured due to an uneven sidewalk and a large pothole and filed her government claims after the deadline.  In this petition, Petitioner seeks an order relieving her from the provisions of Government Code section 945.4 for the purposes of suing the County of Los Angeles (“County”). The Court previously granted relief for the purposes of suing the City of Los Angeles (the “City”).

 

The court GRANTS the petition for an order relieving Petitioner from the provisions of Government Code section 945.4 with respect to the County. 

 

LEGAL STANDARD

 

Before a suit for damages may be filed against a public entity, a claimant must present a timely written claim to the public entity and the claim must have been acted upon by the board or deemed rejected.¿ (Gov. Code § 945.4;¿Munoz v. State of California¿(1995) 33 Cal.App.4th 1767, 1777.)¿¿A claim relating to a cause of action for injury to person or personal property must be presented to the public entity no later than six months after the accrual of the cause of action.¿ (Gov. Code § 911.2;¿Munoz,¿33 Cal. App. 4th at 1776.)¿ 

 

If a claimant fails to present a claim within the statutory period, the claimant may apply “within a reasonable time not to exceed one year after the accrual of the cause of action” to the public entity for leave¿to present a late claim.¿¿(Gov. Code § 911.4, subd. (b).)¿ If the public entity denies leave to present a late claim, a claimant may petition the Court under Government Code¿§ 946.6 for relief from the requirements of Government Code section 945.4.¿¿(Munoz, supra,¿33 Cal.App.4th at 1777.)¿ 

 

Government Code section 946.6(b) requires that the petition to the court must show each of the following: (1) that the late claim application made to the board was denied or deemed denied; (2) the reason for failure to present the claim within six months of the accrual of the cause of action; and (3) the contents of the claim as required by Government Code section 910.  The petition must be filed within six months after the application to present a late claim to the board was denied or deemed to be denied.  (Ibid.) 

 

The petitioner bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence that the late-claim application was made within a reasonable time and that one of the statutory requirements under Government Code section 946.6(c) was met.  (Drummond v. County of Fresno (1987) 193 Cal.App.3d 1406, 1410.)  Under section 946.4(e), the trial court must make its determination upon the petition, “relying upon any affidavits in support of, or in opposition to, the petition and any additional evidence received at hearing on the petition.”  (Ebersol v. Cowan (1983) 35 Cal.3d 427, 431.) 

 

Pursuant to Government Code section 946.6(c)(1), the court may grant relief from section 945.4 if “the failure to present the claim was through mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect unless the public entity establishes that it would be prejudiced in the defense of the claim if the court relieves the petitioner from the requirements of Section 945.4.” The showing required of a petitioner seeking relief because of mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect is the same as required by Code of Civil Procedure section 473(b). (Viles v. State (1967) 66 Cal.2d 24, 29.) “[I]t is uniformly held that for relief on any or all of the stated grounds it must be shown that one's misconception was reasonable, or that it might have been the conduct of a reasonably prudent person under similar circumstances." (Kaslavage v. West Kern County Water Dist. (1978) 84 Cal.App.3d 529, 539 fn.1.)

 

“Section 946.6 is a remedial statue intended to provide relief from technical rules which otherwise provide a trap for the unwary claimant.”  (Ebersol, supra, 35 Cal.3d at 435.)  “[D]oubts will be resolved in favor of the party attempting to get to trial to the end that wherever possible, cases may be heard on their merits.”  (Kaslavage, supra, 84 Cal.App.3d at 537.) 

 

DISCUSSION

 

Petitioner was injured on July 2, 2024, so she had until January 2, 2025, to present her claim. (See Jiminez v. Chavez (2023) 97 Cal.App.5th 50, 58 [six-month limitations period is the longer of six calendar months or 182 days].) She failed to do so. Petitioner submitted timely late claim applications to Respondents on or about January 27, 2025. (Kaur Decl., ¶ 8, Exh. 1.) The applications were rejected on or about February 24, 2025. (Id., Exh. 2-3.)

 

Petitioner retained Rafii & Associates, PC (the “firm”) to handle this case. However, the handling attorney, Alan Blanco, resigned from the firm in December 2024. (Kaur Decl., ¶¶ 6.) Blanco “failed to notify the calendaring department of the important deadlines, causing the firm to forgo the timely submission of government claim forms.” (Id., ¶ 6.) This is sufficient to constitute excusable neglect, especially because the time period of December 2024 to January 2, 2025, falls during the holidays when employees are out of the office.

 

The County argues that they will suffer prejudice because they will not have had an opportunity “to make an early investigation of the facts on which a claim is based” which would allow it “to correct the conditions or practices which gave rise to the claim.” (Arntz Builders v. City of Berkeley (2008) 166 Cal.App.4th 266, 279.) This Court is unaware of any other court that has denied leave to file a late claim based on this premise. A party can choose not to investigate a claim because they think their interpretation of the law is correct, but they run the risk of a court overriding their interpretation of the law. That is what has happened here.

 

CONCLUSION 

 

            Based on the foregoing, the court GRANTS the petition for an order relieving Petitioner from the provisions of Government Code section 945.4 with respect to the County.  

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

[Tentative] Order on Motion for Attorneys Fees and Motion for Leave to Amend the Complaint

 

 

Plaintiff Susan Raymond (“Plaintiff”) moves for an award of attorneys’ fees in the amount of $45,878.75 as a result of prevailing on the first cause of action for Inspection of Records. The Defendants in this case are Carmelita Investments Inc. and The Carmelita Group (“Defendants”).

 

             The Court DENIES without prejudice the Motion for Attorneys Fees.

 

The Court DENIES without prejudice the motion to amend the second through fifth claims, and will discuss with the parties whether it makes sense to grant the motion for leave to amend as to the first cause of action only. Once the first claim is complete, the Court will transfer this case to Department 534 (previously Department 1) for reassignments to an independent civil courtroom.

 

The motion for leave to intervene is permanently off calendar as a result of the July 21, 2026 stipulation.

BACKGROUND

 

           

On August 7, 2025, Plaintiff served on Defendants a written demand for inspection of partnership records under Corporations Code Section 15901.11 and 15903.04. (Shapiro Decl., ¶ 4.) Defendants produced the Partnership Agreement and three amendments but produced no tax returns, no financial statements, and none of the other records required by statute. (Shapiro Decl., ¶ 4.)

 

On March 20, 2026, after Plaintiff filed their Complaint, Defendants produced tax returns, K-1s, and financial statements responsive to the August 7, 2025 demand. (Shapiro Decl., ¶ 6.)

 

PROCEDURAL HISTORY

 

            Petitioner filed a Complaint on February 6, 2026 with the following causes of action: (1) Inspection of Records of The Carmelita Group, L.P., (2) Breach of Fiduciary Duty; (3) Accounting – The Carmelita Group, L.P.,; and (4) Breach of Partnership Agreement.

 

            On February 9, 2026, the Court stayed the second, third, and fourth causes of action pending resolution of the first cause of action.

 

            On June 1, 2026, Julie Klaunser filed a Motion to Intervene.

 

            On June 24, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Motion for Attorneys Fees.

 

            On July 1, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Motion for Leave to File a First Amended Verified Complaint.

 

            On July 13, 2026, Julie Klausner filed an Opposition to the Motion for Attorneys Fees.

 

            On July 16, 2026, Defendants filed an Opposition to Plaintiff’s Motion for Leave to File an Amended Complaint and Motion for Attorneys Fees.

 

            On July 21, 2026, the parties agreed to take the Motion for Leave to Intervene off calendar as moot.

 

MOTION FOR ATTORNEYS FEES DISCUSSION

 

A.     Legal Standard

 

Article 15.4 of the Partnership Agreement provides:

 

15.4 Attorneys' Fees. In the event of any litigation or other dispute resolution proceedings among any Partners arising out of or in connection with this Agreement, the prevailing party shall be reimbursed for all reasonable costs, including, but not limited to, reasonable accountants' fees and attorneys' fees, incurred in connection with such proceedings (including, but not limited to, any appellate proceedings relating thereto) or in connection with the enforcement of any judgment or award rendered in such proceedings (for which purpose this provision shall survive, and shall not merge in, any such judgment or award and may be the basis for a separate recovery action by the prevailing party). "Prevailing party" within the meaning of this Section shall include, without limitation, a party who dismisses an action for recovery hereunder in exchange for payment of the sums allegedly due, performance of covenants allegedly breached or consideration substantially equal to the relief sought in the action.

 

(Complaint, Exh. 2.)

 

            Civil Code Section 1717, subd. (a) provides:

 

(a) In any action on a contract, where the contract specifically provides that attorney's fees and costs, which are incurred to enforce that contract, shall be awarded either to one of the parties or to the prevailing party, then the party who is determined to be the party prevailing on the contract, whether he or she is the party specified in the contract or not, shall be entitled to reasonable attorney's fees in addition to other costs.

 

 

B.     The Court cannot make a determination of who was the “prevailing party” at this time

 

Plaintiff asks to be labeled the prevailing party on the first cause of action, based on Article 15.4 of the Partnership Agreement.

 

Defendant Carmelita Investments, Inc. is the general partner of The Carmelita Group, L.P., and a party to this action, and the dispute concerned Plaintiff’s rights as a limited partner under the Agreement. The litigation is therefore among partners within the meaning of Article 15.4.

 

The first cause of action enforced rights the Partnership Agreement and the limited partnership statutes secure to Plaintiff as a limited partner; the partnership agreement itself may not "unreasonably restrict the right to information under Section 15903.04." (Corporations Code, § 15901.10, subd. (b)(4).) Thus, this litigation arises out of the Partnership Agreement.

 

As part of this litigation, Plaintiff secured the tax returns, K-1s, and financial statements responsive to the August 7, 2025 demand that Defendants had previously not produced. (Shapiro Decl., ¶¶ 2, 6.)

 

However, Plaintiff is not the prevailing party, at least not yet. “The prevailing party determination is to be made only upon final resolution of the contract claims and only by ‘a comparison of the extent to which each party ha[s] succeeded and failed to succeed in its contentions.” (DisputeSuite.com, LLC v. Scoreinc.com (2017) 2 Cal.5th 968, 974.) There are claims for breach of fiduciary duty and breach of the partnership agreement – both claims that relate to the Partnership Agreement.

 

Plaintiff is also not entitled to attorneys fees on the first cause of action right now. In Chen v. Valstock Ventures, LLC (2022) 81 Cal.App.5th 957, 970, the Court of Appeal reviewed a case where the trial court had granted summary adjudication on one claim but not other claims. The Court of Appeal concluded that the language of Code of Civil Procedure Section 1717 means “the Legislature intended attorney’s fees…to be awarded only at the conclusion of all litigation in a case….” (Id., at p. 973.) There is no evidence that “the Legislature intended to allow for interim awards of attorney’s fees upon the resolution of the contract claims in a case even if further litigation remains on noncontract claims.” (Id., at p. 974.) “The statute creates no exceptions for cases in which contract claims are disposed of earlier than noncontract claims, such as through a demurrer or summary adjudication…” (Id., at p. 976.)

 

Indeed, interim attorney’s fees awards are anomalous in civil litigation. There is no indication that the Legislature intended “to depart from well-established principles that fees and costs are ordinarily not granted for interim success, and that the prevailing party is determined, and fees and costs awarded, at the conclusion of the litigation.” (Artus v. Gramercy Towers Condominium Association (2018) 19 Cal.App.5th 923, 927.)

 

The litigation is not concluded yet. Multiple other causes of action remain pending. Plaintiff can file for an award of attorneys fees at the conclusion of the litigation, if Petitioner is the “prevailing party” in the context of the entire litigation.

 

MOTION FOR LEAVE TO AMEND

 

A.     Legal Standard

 

Leave to amend at any time is liberally allowed in the interests of justice and in the absence of prejudice to another party, even up to the time of trial.  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 473(a)(1) & 576; Magpali v. Farmers Group, Inc. (1996) 48 Cal.App.4th 471, 487.)  “[T]he trial court has wide discretion in allowing the amendment of any pleading.” (Record v. Reason (1999) 73 Cal.App.4th 472, 486.)   

 

“[It] is a rare case in which ‘a court will be justified in refusing a party leave to amend his pleadings so that he may properly present his case.¿[Citations.]¿If the motion to amend is timely made and the granting of the motion will not prejudice the opposing party, it is error to refuse permission to amend and where the refusal also results in a party being deprived of the right to assert a meritorious cause of action or a meritorious defense.”  (Morgan v. Sup. Ct. (1959) 172 Cal.App.2d 527, 530.) 

 

“It is the general policy that courts should exercise liberality in permitting the filing of supplemental pleadings when the alleged ‘occurring-after’ facts are pertinent to the case.”  (Flood v. Simpson (1975) 45 Cal.App.3d 644, 647.) 

 

“If the proposed amendment fails to state a cause of action, it is proper to deny leave to amend.”  (Foxborough v. Van Atta (1994) 26 Cal.App.4th 217, 230.)  However, “even¿if the proposed legal theory is a novel one, ‘the preferable practice would be to permit the amendment and allow the parties to test its legal sufficiency by demurrer, motion for judgment on the pleadings or other appropriate proceedings.’”  (Kittredge Sports Co. v. Sup.Ct. (1989) 213 Cal.App.3d 1045, 1048.)  

 

B.     There is no need for Plaintiff to amend the first cause of action

 

Plaintiff seeks to add the following paragraphs to the first cause of action:

 

49. On or about March 20, 2026 -- after this action was filed, and only as a result of this action -- Defendants produced the documents that Plaintiff Raymond had originally demanded in the August 7, 2025 demand letter. Defendants would not have produced those records absent this action.

 

50. Defendants' production was limited to the documents originally demanded on

August 7, 2025; it did not include the additional documents and information that Plaintiff

Raymond subsequently requested, which are the subject of the Second Cause of Action.

 

51. Because Plaintiff Raymond obtained the production of the demanded records

through, and as a direct result of, this action, Plaintiff Raymond is the prevailing party with respect to this cause of action.

 

(Shapiro Decl, ¶ 2, Exh. B.)

 

            Technically speaking, Plaintiff has met all of the requirements to add these paragraphs to the first cause of action. Defendants have not identified any prejudice from these paragraphs being added to the complaint. Plaintiff has met all of the requirements of California Rules of Court, Rule 3.1324. From a technical perspective, the Court can grant leave to amend.

 

            From a practical perspective, Plaintiff’s amendments appear to be contrary to the goal of expeditiously prosecuting a civil case. None of the added paragraphs allow Plaintiff to “properly present his case” or argue a “meritorious cause of action.” The question of whether Plaintiff has prevailed is not an “allegation” to be included in a Complaint. It is a legal question for both parties to brief and for this Court to decide at the conclusion of the entire case.

 

            The existing complaint already allows Plaintiff to recover attorneys fees. (Complaint, ¶ 55.) Paragraph 50 is also not needed to support the second cause of action. Paragraphs 32, 58, and 64 cover the additional documents Plaintiff is requesting as part of the second cause of action.

 

If Plaintiff proceeds with amending the Complaint, the Court will have to delay transferring the case to Department 534 for expeditious resolution of the other causes of action. Instead, the Court will be required to let the parties conduct further briefing in Department 833 and/or discovery on these new “allegations.”

 

Therefore, the Court wishes to discuss with Plaintiff whether these allegations are truly necessary to the prosecution of this action.

The attempts to amend the second through fifth cause of action is not properly before the Court and is denied without prejudice.

  

CONCLUSION 

 

            The Court DENIES without prejudice the Motion for Attorneys Fees. The Court DENIES without prejudice the request to amend as to claims two through five, and will discuss with the parties whether leave to amend the first cause of action is truly necessary. Once the first claim is complete, the Court will transfer this case to Department 534 (previously Department 1) for reassignments to an independent civil courtroom. The motion for leave to intervene is permanently off calendar as a result of the July 21, 2026 stipulation.

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Order Regarding Petition for Writ of Mandate

           

INTRODUCTION

 

             Petitioner Green Dot Public Schools (“Petitioner”) seeks a writ of mandate compelling Respondents California Department of Education (“CDE”), Los Angeles Unified School District (“LAUSD”), and Andres E. Chait (“LAUSD Superintendent”), in his official capacity as Acting Superintendent of Los Angeles Unified School District (all collectively, “Respondents”) to comply with their respective ministerial duties by deeming and acknowledging that Petitioner’s charter renewal petition for Alain Leroy Locke College Preparatory Academy (“Locke”) was renewed by operation of law under California Code of Regulations, title 5, section 11966.4, subd. (c).

 

A preliminary question before the Court is whether the controversy is now moot because the LACBOE has renewed Petitioner’s charter. The Court does not issue a tentative ruling on this issue but asks the parties to address the following at the hearing:

 

            The general test for mootness is well established. Courts decide only justiciable issues. (Wilson & Wilson v. City Council of Redwood City (2011) 191 Cal.App.4th 1559, 1573.) A case becomes moot when a once ripe actual controversy no longer exists due to a change in circumstances. (Ibid.) A case becomes moot if a court cannot grant practical, effective relief. (Citizens for the Restoration of L Street v. City of Fresno (2014) 229 Cal.App.4th 340, 362.) “The legal test for effective relief is whether there is a ‘prospect of a remedy that can have a practical, tangible impact on the parties’ conduct or legal status.’” (Delta Stewardship Council Cases (2020) 48 Cal.App.5th 1014, 1053.) Courts do not issue writs of mandate to enforce an abstract right of no practical benefit to petitioner, or where to issue the writ would be useless, unenforceable, or unavailing. (Roger v. County of Riverside (2020) 44 Cal.App.5th 510, 529-530.)

 

            In light of this standard, the parties should address the following at hearing:

 

1.      Petitioner asserts that the renewal by the LACBOE, rather than by the LAUSD, subjects Petitioner to the following two negative practical effects: (1) Petitioner is required to maintain a 3% budgetary reserve under LACBOE rather than a .8% reserve under LAUSD; and (2) a renewal by LAUSD rather than LACBOE gives Petitioner two levels of administrative appellate review at a more favorable standard of review, rather than one level of administrative review solely under the abuse of discretion standard.  Petitioner further asserts that the Court can still grant it complete relief by issuing a writ of mandate validating the automatic renewal by LAUSD, thus restoring the .8% reserve and the more favorable procedure for review.  On what basis do Respondents contend that the matter is moot in light of these practical detriments to Petitioners?  Do Respondents have any case law or other authority that a case is moot even if certain practical detriments remain in effect so long as Petitioner’s main goal has been achieved?

 

2.      If the Court were to grant the petition for writ of mandamus, which entity would be overseeing Petitioner, and what rules would apply, pending any appeal of the Court’s ruling by Respondents?

v.                                                                     Location: Stanley Mosk Courthouse

                                                                                    Department: 833                                               Janna Holdings L.P. c/o San Pasqual                     Judge: Joseph Lipner                          

Fiduciary Trust Company              

 

[Tentative] Order on Application for Writ of Attachment 

 

 

INTRODUCTION

 

            Plaintiff Metropolitan Adjustment Bureau (“Plaintiff”) moves for a writ of attachment against Defendant Janna Holdings L.P. c/o San Pasqual Fiduciary Trust Company (“Defendant”) in the amount of $900,340.32.  

 

            The Court DENIES the writ of attachment.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

 

“Upon the filing of the complaint or at any time thereafter, the plaintiff may apply pursuant to this article for a right to attach order and a writ of attachment by filing an application for the order and writ with the court in which the action is brought.”  (Code Civ. Pro. § 484.010.)  “Except as otherwise provided by statute, an attachment may be issued only in an action on a claim or claims for money, each of which is based upon a contract, express or implied, where the total amount of the claim or claims is a fixed or readily ascertainable amount not less than five hundred dollars ($500) exclusive of costs, interest, and attorney's fees.”  (Code Civ. Pro. § 483.010.) 

 

The court shall issue a right to attach order if the court finds all of the following: 

 

(1)   The claim upon which the attachment is based is one upon which an attachment may be issued. 

(2)   The plaintiff has established the probable validity of the claim upon which the attachment is based. 

(3)   The attachment is not sought for a purpose other than the recovery on the claim upon which the attachment is based. 

(4)   The amount to be secured by the attachment is greater than zero.  

 

(Code Civ. Pro. § 484.090.) 

 

“A claim has ‘probable validity’ where it is more likely than not that the plaintiff will obtain a judgment against the defendant on that claim.” (Code Civ. Pro. § 481.190.)  “The application shall be supported by an affidavit showing that the plaintiff on the facts presented would be entitled to a judgment on the claim upon which the attachment is based.”¿(Code Civ. Pro. § 484.030.)¿“In contested applications, the court must consider the relative merits of the positions of the respective parties and make a determination of¿the probable outcome of the litigation.”¿  (Hobbs v. Weiss (1999) 73 Cal.App.4th 76, 80.)  “The Attachment Law statutes are subject to strict construction.” (Epstein v. Abrams (1997) 57 Cal.App.4th 1159, 1168.) 

 

DISCUSSION

 

A.    Probable Validity of Plaintiff’s Claim

 

The application is based on Plaintiff’s cause of action for breach of contract and breach of guaranty. To establish a claim for breach of contract, a plaintiff must prove: (1) the existence of a contract; (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance; (3) defendant’s breach of the contract; and (4) damages incurred by plaintiff as a result of the breach.  (Durell v. Sharp Healthcare (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 1350, 1367.)

 

Plaintiff’s application states that it entered into a written agreement to act as a public insurance adjuster with respect to Defendant’s insurance losses relating to the destruction of apartment buildings owed by Defendant in the Palisades Fire. Plaintiff says they performed all of its obligations under the Agreement, resulting in Defendant receiving insurance payments of $10,147,879. Plaintiff invoiced Defendant for $811,840.32 in fees based on the 8% fee due during the Agreement. After months of non-payment of the invoice, Defendant claims the Agreement is void. (Application, ¶ 7c.) Plaintiff states that copies of the agreement, related documents and invoices are attached as Exhibits 1 and 2. (Application, ¶ 13b.)

 

This evidence shows a contract and invoices that Defendant has not paid. The exhibits show that the invoices are for $400,000, $308,842.74, and $102,947.58. (Application, Exh. 2.) That adds up to $811,790.32, which is a different number than the amount Plaintiff stated they invoiced in the application.

 

“[A]n attachment may be issued only in an action on a claim or claims for money, each of which is based upon a contract, express or implied, where the total amount of the claim or claims is a fixed or readily ascertainable amount not less than five hundred dollars ($500) exclusive of costs, interest, and attorney's fees.”  (Code Civ. Pro. § 483.010, subd. (a).)

 

Here, the amount in the exhibits differs from the amount in the application, so the amount is not “fixed or readily ascertainable.”

 

Because writs of attachment are subject to strict construction, the Court does not believe Plaintiff has shown the probable validity of the claim for breach of contract.

 

Therefore, the Court DENIES the writ of attachment.

 

 

CONCLUSION

 

The Court DENIES the writ of attachment.

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

v.                                                                     Hearing: July 29, 2026

                                                                                    Department: 833                                            Los Angeles County Board of Supervisors                    Judge: Joseph Lipner

                                     

 

 

 

Order Denying Preliminary Injunction

 

 

            Plaintiff Adrienne Grzenda, M.D., Ph.D. (“Plaintiff”) requests a preliminary injunction ordering Defendant Los Angeles County Board of Supervisors (“Defendant”) to (1) reinstate Plaintiff’s staff privileges at Los Angeles County Olive View-UCLA Medical Center (“Olive View”); and (2) take no further action to prevent Dr. Grzenda’s exercise of their staff privileges unless carried out pursuant to and in compliance with the procedural requirements of the Bylaws of the Professional Staff Association of Olive View.

 

            The Court DENIES the Application for a Preliminary Injunction.

LEGAL STANDARD

 

The purpose of a preliminary injunction is to preserve the status quo pending a decision on the merits.  (Major v. Miraverde Homeowners Ass’n. (1992) 7 Cal. App. 4th 618, 623.)  In deciding whether or not to grant a preliminary injunction, the court looks to two factors, including “(1) the likelihood that the plaintiff will prevail on the merits, and (2) the relative balance of harms that is likely to result from the granting or denial of interim injunctive relief.”  (White v. Davis (2003) 30 Cal.4th 528, 553-54.) The factors are interrelated, with a greater showing on one permitting a lesser showing on the other.  (Dodge, Warren & Peters Ins. Services, Inc. v. Riley (2003) 105 Cal.App.4th 1414, 1420.)  However, the party seeking an injunction must demonstrate at least a reasonable probability of success on the merits.  (IT Corp. v. County of Imperial (1983) 35 Cal.3d 63, 73-74.)  The party seeking the injunction bears the burden of demonstrating both a likelihood of success on the merits and the occurrence of irreparable harm.  (Savage v. Trammell Crow Co. (1990) 223 Cal.App.3d 1562, 1571.) Irreparable harm may exist if the plaintiff can show an inadequate remedy at law.  (CCP § 526(a).)

 

“The granting or denying of a preliminary injunction does not constitute an adjudication of the ultimate rights in controversy.”  (Cohen v. Board of Supervisors (1985) 40 Cal.3d 277, 286; see also Yee v. American National Ins. Co. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 453, 457-458 [“a preliminary injunction is not a determination on the merits….”].) 

 

DISCUSSION

 

A.    Summary of Facts

 

The application is based on Plaintiff’s causes of action for writ of mandate and declaratory relief.

 

Since 2020, Plaintiff has been employed full-time by the LA County Department of Health Services (“DHS”) as a Physician Specialist, Non-Megaflex in Psychiatry. (Grzenda Decl., ¶ 6.) Since 2017, Plaintiff has held medical staff privileges at Olive View and since 2022 has most recently been assigned as an attending psychiatrist in the Office of Diversion and Re-Entry Psychiatric Inpatient Unit of Olive View (Unit 6C). (Grzenda Decl., ¶¶ 5-6, 8.)

 

The Professional Staff Association (“PSA”) Bylaws of Olive View acknowledge the County’s right to terminate a physician’s employment without PSA input or approval – or a hearing under the PSA Bylaws. (Grzenda Decl., Exh. A, PSA Bylaws, Art II, Sec. 1.I.)

 

Plaintiff’s employment with the County is governed by the Memorandum of Understanding between the County and the Union of American Physicians & Dentists, and the County’s Civil Service Rules. (Grzenda Decl, Exh. M, p. 8.) Neither of these entitles Plaintiff to a medical staff hearing to challenge an employment action taken by the County.

 

On July 8, 2025, Plaintiff engaged in a confrontation with a patient in Unit 6C at Olive View. (Waterman Decl., ¶ 5.) The patient became agitated, but the situation settled in less than two minutes. (Waterman Decl., ¶ 5.) Camera footage showed no patient behavior or staff reaction indicative of a credible threat. (Waterman Decl., ¶ 5.)

 

Afterwards, Plaintiff ordered an IM injection of antipsychotics. (Grzenda Decl., ¶ 16.) Involuntary IM administration is appropriate in exceptional circumstances where lesser restrictions are insufficient to address an imminent safety threat. (Waterman Decl., ¶ 6.) Nurses were reluctant to administer the IM shot, fearing the order may have been retaliation for the patient having called Plaintiff a “fat pig.” (Waterman Decl., ¶ 9.) Plaintiff contends the nurses’ observation of the patient’s behavior “has little informative bearing” on their order. (Grzenda Decl., ¶ 17.) Plaintiff believes “[m]y order [for involuntary IM] is effectively a Court order” that must be followed— even if the patient is calm and willing to take oral medication—and a nurse’s failure to do so violates the California Penal Code. (Grzenda Decl., ¶¶ 17, 19.)

 

Later that afternoon, Plaintiff contacted the Los Angeles County Sheriff’s Department, reporting that the patient had made a criminal threat to “shoot” Plaintiff. (Waterman Decl., ¶ 7.) Plaintiff asked that the patient be arrested. (Waterman Decl., ¶ 7.) During their conversation with the Deputy, Plaintiff said, at various times, that the patient had stabbed a nurse, that they had attempted to stab a nurse, and that they had thrown a pen. (Waterman Decl., ¶ 7.) The Sheriff’s Deputy concluded that no elements of a criminal threat existed, declined to make an arrest, and declined to prepare a report of the incident. (Waterman Decl., ¶ 7.)

 

Risk management personnel conducted a preliminary review of the matter, interviewing around ten witnesses who observed the incident, including the Sheriff’s Deputy who responded to Plaintiff’s request for arrest. (Waterman Decl., ¶ 9.) Multiple staff members reported that the patient had been rude and argumentative with Plaintiff, but then calmed down and was cooperative. (Waterman Decl., ¶ 9.) Staff members expressed discomfort with administering an IM shot because it did not seem warranted given the patient’s calm state. (Waterman Decl., ¶ 9.)  Staff expressed concern that Plaintiff’s order may have been retaliatory, tied to the patient having called them a “fat pig.” (Waterman Decl., ¶ 9.)  

 

On July 22, 2025, Dr. Waterman placed Plaintiff on Ordered Absence pending the outcome of a full investigation. (Waterman Decl., ¶ 10.) The decision to place Plaintiff on Ordered Absence was based on the need to protect the safety of patients and staff on Unit 6C from imminent danger, and to preserve the integrity of the ongoing administrative investigation into a serious matter. (Waterman Decl., ¶ 10.) Dr. Waterman noted that there had been a multi-year pattern of escalating disruptive conduct on Plaintiff’s part. (Waterman Decl., ¶ 10.)

 

After Plaintiff requested a hearing to challenge the summary suspension, they received notice on September 4, 2025, that reports were filed on September 3, 2025, with the Medical Board of California pursuant to Business and Professions Code section 805, subdivision (e), and the National Practitioner Data Bank, reporting that they had been summarily suspended. (Grzenda Decl., ¶¶ 44-46.) One week later, Plaintiff received a letter from the Medical Board, indicating that it had “opened an investigation of the complaint above regarding the Medical Board of California Health Facility/Peer Review Reporting Form.” (Grzenda Decl., ¶ 49.)

 

After being placed on administrative leave, Plaintiff then asked DHS to provide a hearing under the PSA Bylaws to challenge the Ordered Absence. (Grzenda Decl., Exh. G at pp. 1-5.) The Performance Management Unit clarified the separate processes, explaining to Plaintiff that Performance Management Unit investigations “are separate of the PSA and not governed by their bylaws.” (Ibid.)

 

After a union representative and the Performance Management Unit confirmed that Ordered Absence is not governed by the PSA Bylaws, Plaintiff then turned to the PSA. Plaintiff asked the PSA to lift the Ordered Absence immediately. (Grzenda Decl., Exh. O, pp. 3-15.) The PSA responded—accurately—that no action or recommendation had been taken against Plaintiff’s clinical privileges and that they were not entitled to a hearing under the PSA Bylaws. (Id., Exh. R, pp. 1-2.)

 

Plaintiff requested that their medical privileges be reinstated. (Grzenda Decl., ¶ 78.) Dr. Waterman did not respond. (Grzenda Decl., ¶ 78.)

 

Plaintiff claims that they have been unable to exercise clinical privileges, perform duties associated with their faculty appointment, supervise residents, conduct research, or participate fully in professional activities. (Grzenda Decl., ¶ 92.) Plaintiff explains that advancement as a physician-scientist is governed by fixed grant cycles, publication timelines, promotion reviews, and leadership opportunities and these opportunities occur once per year and require uninterrupted academic engagement. (Grzenda Decl., ¶ 93.) Delays measured in months can produce professional consequences lasting for years. (Grzenda Decl., ¶ 93.)

 

Plaintiff also claims that information that should have remained confidential was disclosed, and contributed to feelings of isolation, loss of trust, and continued concern regarding retaliation. (Grzenda Decl., ¶ 96.) Plaintiff has had to devote substantial time and resources to protecting their medical license and professional standing, which has created stress. (Grzenda Decl., ¶ 98.)

 

B.     Procedural Issues

 

“The general rule of motion practice…is that new evidence is not permitted with reply papers.” (Jay v. Mahaffey (2013) 218 Cal.App.4th 1522, 1537; see also Alliant Ins. Services, Inc. v. Gaddy (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 1292, 1308 [in a preliminary injunction proceeding, “the trial court has discretion whether to accept new evidence with the reply papers”].) Plaintiff filed four new declarations with the reply brief, and the Court declines to consider these declarations, with the exception of the portions of Plaintiff’s supplemental declaration responding directly to Dr. Waterman’s declaration.

 

C.     Plaintiffs’ Likelihood of Success on the Merits

 

The application is based on Plaintiff’s causes of action for writ of mandate and declaratory relief.

 

A cause of action for declaratory relief requires a plaintiff to show (1) a proper subject of declaratory relief, and (2) an actual controversy involving justiciable questions relating to the rights or obligations of a party. (Childhelp, Inc. v. City of Los Angeles (2023) 91 Cal.App.5th 224, 235.)

 

There are two essential requirements to the issuance of an ordinary writ of mandate under Code of Civil Procedure section 1085: (1) a clear, present, and ministerial duty on the part of the respondent, and (2) a clear, present, and beneficial right on the part of the petitioner to the performance of that duty. (California Ass’n for Health Services at Home v. Department of Health Services (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 696, 704.) “An action in ordinary mandamus is proper where … the claim is that an agency has failed to act as required by law.” (Id. at 705.) 

 

“Generally, mandamus is available to compel a public agency's performance or to correct an agency's abuse of discretion when the action being compelled or corrected is ministerial.”  (AIDS Healthcare Foundation v. Los Angeles County Dept. of Public Health (2011) 197 Cal.App.4th 693, 700.)  “‘While, of course, it is the general rule that mandamus will not lie to control the discretion of a court or officer, meaning by that that it will not lie to force the exercise of discretion in a particular manner ... [it] will lie to correct abuses of discretion, and will lie to force a particular action by the inferior tribunal or officer, when the law clearly establishes the petitioner's right to such action.’”  (Flores v. Dept. of Corrections & Rehabilitation (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 199, 208.)   

 

An agency is presumed to have regularly performed its official duties.  (Evid. Code § 664.) Petitioner “bears the burden of proof in a mandate proceeding brought under Code of Civil Procedure section 1085.”  (California Correctional Peace Officers Assn. v. State Personnel Bd. (1995) 10 Cal.4th 1133, 1154.)   

 

“‘On questions of law arising in mandate proceedings, [the court] exercise[s] independent judgment.’ ….¿Interpretation of a statute or regulation is a question of law subject to independent review.”  (Christensen v. Lightbourne (2017) 15 Cal.App.5th 1239, 1251.)

 

Plaintiff argues they are likely to prevail on the merits because the PSA—the Professional Staff Association of Olive View--never imposed or ratified any summary suspension. (See Bus. & Prof. Code § 809.5, subd. (a).) The only other group able to summarily suspend Plaintiff is the governing body of an acute care hospital, but this suspension can only last two days, unless ratified by the peer review body. (See Bus. & Prof. Code § 809.5, subd. (b).) Plaintiff argues that their current suspension was imposed by persons who are not designees of the Governing Body of Olive View and even if they were designees of the Governing Body of Olive View, the suspension automatically terminated after two working days since the peer review body never ratified any suspension. Therefore, Plaintiff alleges they have a ministerial duty to be reinstated.

 

However, Plaintiff’s briefing ignores a key underlying fact: the County and PSA have separate and independent authority and processes, and Plaintiff has affiliations with both. Those are two separate relationships, controlled by two separate authorities, governed by two separate procedures.

 

Numerous provisions in California’s peer review statute confirm that clinical privileges and employment as distinct aspects of a physician’s practice, governed by different entities. (See, e.g., Bus. & Prof. Code, § 805, sub. (e) [requiring reports for suspensions of “privileges, membership, or employment”] [emphasis added]; § 805, subd. (a)(1)(B)(i) & (iv) [creating separate classifications for hospital “medical or professional staff” and committees organized by entities “employing” physicians]; § 805, subd. (a)(4) [definition of “staff privileges” does not include employment].) As Defendant states, the PSA can terminate a physician’s clinical privileges without having to afford the physician civil service procedure. The County can terminate an employee without triggering a hearing under PSA bylaws.

 

In this case, the County acted on one side of that dual status—employment—by placing Plaintiff on Ordered Absence or paid administrative leave. (See Grzenda Decl., Exh. F [explaining that ordered absence is equivalent to administrative leave with pay].) Plaintiff’s employment status was governed by civil service rules – not the rules of the PSA bylaws. (See Grzenda Decl., Exh. G [explaining that Plaintiff’s status on ordered absence is governed by Civil Service Rules 16.01[1]].)  Thus, the PSA bylaws do not govern what must happen to Plaintiff after being placed on “Ordered Leave.”

 

Plaintiff insists that they were actually put on summary suspension, not ordered absence. This is despite the exhibits attached to Plaintiff’s own declaration. (See Grzenda Decl., ¶ 31 [union representative explaining that the action was temporary and non-disciplinary]; Grzenda Decl., Exh. F [notice from Risk Management Division of Health Services of Los Angeles County explaining Plaintiff is on Ordered Absence or administrative leave with pay]; Exh. G [email from Risk Management Division that Plaintiff is not suspended and explaining that paid admin leave is not a disciplinary action]; Exh. H [official letter from Risk Management Division that an 805 Report was submitted and Plaintiff remains on Ordered Absence or administrative leave with pay]; Exh. R [letter from PSA explaining that the Association has taken no action that bars Plaintiff from exercising her clinical privileges and Plaintiff is involved in a separate proceeding separate from the Association about the July 8, 2025 incident]; Exh. V [letter from PSA President explaining that they would not do anything while there was an active employment investigation].)

 

One of Plaintiff’s main arguments that they were on summary suspension is that they received a Section 805 report filed against them. Section 805, subd. (e) of the Business & Professions Code provides that, “An 805 report shall also be filed within 15 days following the imposition of summary suspension of staff privileges, membership, or employment, if the summary suspension remains in effect for a period in excess of 14 days.”

 

The 805 report proclaims, “Dr Grzenda was placed on an Ordered Absence (administrative leave with pay) effective July 18, 2025. This is a temporary assignment pending the outcome of an administrative investigation.” (Grzenda Decl., Exh. H, p. 5.) At the very bottom, Defendant’s expert, Dr. Waterman, checked “imposition of summary suspension on employment.” Dr. Waterman explained that he checked “imposition of summary suspension on employment” on the 805 report because that fit the description of the situation the best. (Waterman Decl., ¶ 11.)

However, Defendant made clear that an 805 report was necessary because even though “the ordered absence is not a disciplinary action, it requires reporting because it is based on a medical disciplinary cause or reason, as defined in Business & Professions Code section 805, subdivision (a)(6), and will be in place for a period in excess of 14 days.” (Grzenda Decl., Exh. H, p. 2.) The 805 report states that an employment action (and not clinical privileges) were taken by the employer. (Grzenda Decl., Exh. H, p. 4 [classifying action as suspension of employment by committee organized by an entity employing more than 25 licentiates that functions for the purpose of reviewing the quality of professional care provided by employees].)

 

Thus, the 805 report does not confirm that Plaintiff is experiencing a summary suspension. At the very best for Plaintiff, it creates a disputed issue of fact. When put in the context of the other exhibits discussed above, all indications point to Plaintiff being on ordered absence, not summary suspension.

 

The filing of an 805 Report also does not require a hearing under the PSA Bylaws. The 805 report means the County must afford Plaintiff due process, but that is not synonymous with a medical staff hearing under the PSA Bylaws or a statutory peer review hearing. (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 809.7 [exempting county hospitals from statutory peer review hearing procedure].) For non-disciplinary employment actions—like Plaintiff’s Ordered Absence—the civil service rules supply the proper due process requirements. (Shoemaker v. County of Los Angeles (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 618, 630.)

 

Simply put, paid leave from employment is not synonymous with a summary suspension of clinical privileges. Therefore, Plaintiff is unlikely to succeed on the merits.

 

D.    Balance of Harms

 

For the second factor, the court must consider “the interim harm that the plaintiff would be likely to sustain if the injunction were denied as compared to the harm the defendant would be likely to suffer if the preliminary injunction were issued.”  (Smith v. Adventist Health System/West (2010) 182 Cal.App.4th 729, 749.)  “Irreparable harm” generally means that the defendant’s act constitutes an actual or threatened injury to the personal or property rights of the plaintiff that cannot be compensated by a damages award.  (See Brownfield v. Daniel Freeman Marina Hospital (1989) 208 Cal.App.3d 405, 410.)  The showing of harm is even more demanding when a Plaintiff seeks injunctive relief against a public entity because “[t]here is a general rule against enjoining public officers or agencies from performing their duties.” (Tahoe Keys Property Owners’ Assn. v. State Water Resources Control Bd. (1994) 23 Cal.App.4th 1459, 1471.)

 

Plaintiff has not identified significant irreparable harm from a denial of an injunction. Instead, Plaintiff relies on generalized assertions of professional and reputational harm from not being able to work. (See, e.g., Grzenda Decl., ¶¶ 92-93.) The Court notes that Plaintiff is still being paid. The Court declines to make a finding of irreparable injury based on generalized assertions.

 

On the flip side, the Court does find that the County has a substantial interest in maintaining temporary workplace restrictions while it completes its review of allegations involving patient care and professional conduct. (Waterman Decl., ¶ 12.) The County has an interest in patient and staff safety, and must also prioritize the integrity of the investigation. (Waterman Decl., ¶ 12.)

 

Moreover, Plaintiff’s opening brief fails to even discuss one of the reasons they are on Ordered Leave – possibly false allegations made to law enforcement. (Grzenda Decl., Exh. H, p. 1.)

 

On July 8, 2025, Plaintiff reported a criminal threat to the Los Angeles County Sheriff’s Department on July 8, 2025. (Grzenda Decl., Exh. H.) After reporting the threat, a Sheriff’s Deputy showed up and determined that the patient was cooperative, non-aggressive, and calm. (Waterman Decl., ¶ 7.) Plaintiff’s story was inconsistent and changing, and included allegations that (1) the patient had stabbed a nurse; (2) that the patient had attempted to stab a nurse; and (3) that the patient had thrown a pen. (Waterman Decl., ¶ 7.) A later inpatient progress note stated that the patient had said, “I’m going to shoot you in the f**ing head.” (Waterman Decl., ¶ 8.) Plaintiff’s reports could not be corroborated. (Grzenda Decl, Exh. H.)

 

 These allegations need to be investigated. Without further investigation, Plaintiff would be allowed to return to work even though they have been accused of making false behavioral allegations and for allegations that were frequently changing and inconsistent. (Grzenda Decl., Exh. H, p. 1.) Moreover, Plaintiff’s comparison of a nurse’s reluctance to administering a retaliatory shot to a violation of the penal code has  the potential to create tension in the patient care environment if Plaintiff were to return.

 

The best way for the Court to prioritize the integrity of the investigation is to maintain the status quo – Plaintiff’s paid leave pending investigation. Any other relief would essentially give Plaintiff the relief they have sought before the Court has adjudicated whether Plaintiff has any right to that relief. 

 

 

CONCLUSION 

 

            The Court DENIES the application for a preliminary injunction.

Order Denying Preliminary Injunction

 

 

            Plaintiff Jacob Hartman (“Plaintiff”) moves for a preliminary injunction restraining Defendants Meta Platforms, Inc. (“Meta”) and Instagram, LLC (“Instagram”) (collectively, “Defendants”) from deleting, purging, altering, or otherwise destroying any data, photographs, videos, artwork, or content associated with Plaintiff’s Facebook account and Instagram accounts @jaikhartman and @2weekslefts. The application for a preliminary injunction is unopposed as to Instagram.

 

            The Court DENIES the application for a preliminary injunction.

LEGAL STANDARD

 

The purpose of a preliminary injunction is to preserve the status quo pending a decision on the merits.  (Major v. Miraverde Homeowners Ass’n. (1992) 7 Cal. App. 4th 618, 623.)  In deciding whether or not to grant a preliminary injunction, the court looks to two factors, including “(1) the likelihood that the plaintiff will prevail on the merits, and (2) the relative balance of harms that is likely to result from the granting or denial of interim injunctive relief.”  (White v. Davis (2003) 30 Cal.4th 528, 553-54.) The factors are interrelated, with a greater showing on one permitting a lesser showing on the other.  (Dodge, Warren & Peters Ins. Services, Inc. v. Riley (2003) 105 Cal.App.4th 1414, 1420.)  However, the party seeking an injunction must demonstrate at least a reasonable probability of success on the merits.  (IT Corp. v. County of Imperial (1983) 35 Cal.3d 63, 73-74.)  The party seeking the injunction bears the burden of demonstrating both a likelihood of success on the merits and the occurrence of irreparable harm.  (Savage v. Trammell Crow Co. (1990) 223 Cal.App.3d 1562, 1571.) Irreparable harm may exist if the plaintiff can show an inadequate remedy at law.  (CCP § 526(a).)

 

“The granting or denying of a preliminary injunction does not constitute an adjudication of the ultimate rights in controversy.”  (Cohen v. Board of Supervisors (1985) 40 Cal.3d 277, 286; see also Yee v. American National Ins. Co. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 453, 457-458 [“a preliminary injunction is not a determination on the merits….”].) 

 

DISCUSSION

 

A.    Summary of Facts

 

The application is based on Plaintiff’s causes of action for breach of contract, breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, conversion, and, unfair competition.

 

Plaintiff is a visual artist who uses Instagram accounts @jaikhartman and @2weeklefts and a connected Facebook profile (the “Accounts”) to create, publish, and sell his original artwork—including works from his project “Those hills are always on fire”—and to store the photographic and video record of his work. (Complaint, ¶¶ 11-12.) After the Eaton Fire destroyed his home and the devices on which his files had been kept, the works stored in the Accounts became, for many items, the only surviving copies. (Complaint, ¶¶ 15-16, 31.)

 

On or about June 8, 2026, Meta’s automated systems disabled Plaintiff’s Instagram and Facebook accounts. (Complaint, ¶ 17.) Meta provided no statement of any specific policy violation or factual basis for the disablement. (Complaint, ¶ 17.) Plaintiff’s Instagram account displayed a parallel notice stating that the account was disabled, that “[a]ll your information will be permanently deleted,” and that Plaintiff “cannot request another review of this decision.” (Complaint, ¶ 20.) The control needed to request review does not appear on Plaintiff’s interface. (Complaint, ¶¶ 22-25.)

 

On June 22, 2026, a Meta agent acknowledged on a recorded line that the enforcement technology “sometimes may be wrong and you may be given an option to submit an appeal,” and Plaintiff stated he had “never seen an option to submit an appeal.” (Complaint, ¶ 26.) A supervisor then closed the case, directing Plaintiff to the same application that lacks the appeal control. (Complaint, ¶ 27.) He asserted that Meta did not reopen the case, and the data is scheduled for permanent deletion on or about July 8, 2026. (Complaint, ¶¶ 29-30.)

 

On July 7, 2026, Plaintiff sought a temporary restraining order against Meta. The Court (Murillo, J.) granted the ex parte application, issuing a TRO restraining Meta from deleting any of Plaintiff’s materials pending the hearing on the OSC. The Court set the OSC for July 29, 2026.  

 

On July 8, 2026, the day after the TRO issued, Meta’s counsel informed Plaintiff’s counsel that Plaintiff’s account was active, and Plaintiff’s counsel affirmed that this was correct. (Viser Decl., ¶ 2, Exh. A.)

 

 

B.     Meta’s Initial Arguments

 

Meta argues that Plaintiff cannot get a preliminary injunction because his accounts have been reinstated. (Opposition, 2:20-21.) This is not the law. “[T]here is no hard-and-fast rule that a party’s discontinuance of illegal behavior makes injunctive relief against him or her unavailable. ‘While voluntary cessation of conduct may be a factor in a court’s exercise of its equitable jurisdiction to issue an injunction, it is not determinative….’” (Robinson v. U-Haul Co. of California (2016) 4 Cal.App.5th 304, 315.) There is the possibility that Defendants will disable the social media accounts again. Therefore, this is not a complete defense to the Court issuing a preliminary injunction.

 

Meta argues that “Plaintiff now has full access to his accounts” so “he can independently back up, download, or export any content he wishes to preserve.”(Opposition, 2:27-3:1.) However, downloading or backing up the content ignores what Plaintiff’s Complaint focuses on: the handles @jaikhartman and @2weekslefts, the audience and followers connected to the account, and the business identity Plaintiff has built surrounding those accounts. (Complaint, ¶ 24.)

 

Meta also argues that Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act bars any claim premised on the alleged removal of Plaintiff’s content. Section 230 provides that “[n]o provider or user of an interactive computer service shall be treated as the publisher or speaker of any information provided by another information content provider.” (47 U.S.C. § 230, subd. (c)(1).) Section 230 bars claims treating a provider as the “publisher” or “speaker” of information, not claims that arise from a breach of contract. This Complaint arises out of an allegation that Meta breached a contract , making Section 230 inapplicable. (Complaint, ¶¶ 32, 35; Barnes v. Yahoo!, Inc. (9th Cir. 2007) 570 F.3d 1096, 1107.) Barnes involved a promise made to one user; a promise in a contract Meta drafted, and that Plaintiff paid to accept, is no less a promise because Meta made it to many users.

 

Meta’s initial arguments are unpersuasive, though they have some obvious relationship to the merits discussed below. The Court next turns to the traditional preliminary injunction analysis.

 

C.     Plaintiff’s Likelihood of Success on the Merits

 

I. Conversion Claim

 

A cause of action for conversion requires a plaintiff to show (1) the plaintiff’s ownership or right to possession of the property, (2) the defendant’s conversion by a wrongful act or disposition of property rights, and (3) damages. (Welco Electronics, Inc. v. Mora (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 202, 208.)

 

According to Plaintiff’s Complaint, Meta’s own terms and conditions recognize that Plaintiff owns the photographs, videos, and digital artwork on Plaintiff’s accounts. (Complaint, ¶ 42.) The Complaint also alleges that “Meta has wrongfully exercised dominion over Plaintiff’s property by disabling his access to it, withholding it from him, and threatening to permanently destroy it, all inconsistent with Plaintiff’s ownership and right to possession.” (Complaint, ¶ 43.) As a result of this, Plaintiff “has been damaged in an amount to be proven at trial and the threatened destruction will cause harm that cannot be compensated in damages.” (Complaint, ¶ 45.)

 

            Meta argues that Plaintiff’s alleged damages consist of the mere possibility that his account content might be lost. (Complaint, ¶ 45.) Meta further states that because the account has been reinstated, Plaintiff has access to his accounts’ content, and Plaintiff will not be able to prove damages. (Opposition, 5:14-23.) The terms of service and terms of understanding also show that Meta and Instagram are not liable for lost profits, revenues, information, or data. (Allen Decl., Exh. A, § 4.3; Exh. B, § 4.3; Exh. C, § 7.3.) Thus, Meta and Instagram cannot be liable for damages, and Plaintiff does not challenge this argument on reply.

 

            Therefore, Plaintiff has not shown the likelihood of success of the conversion claim.

 

II. Breach of Contract Claim

 

To establish a claim for breach of contract, a plaintiff must prove: (1) the existence of a contract; (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance; (3) defendant’s breach of the contract; and (4) damages incurred by plaintiff as a result of the breach.  (Durell v. Sharp Healthcare (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 1350, 1367.)

 

The Complaint alleges a contract whereby Plaintiff was permitted to store and access his content on Meta and Instagram and was entitled to notice and to a process to request review of a disablement before any permanent deletion of his account or data. (Complaint, ¶ 32.) Plaintiff argues that he performed all obligations required under the Meta’s Terms of Service and the Instagram Terms of Use, such as paying his subscription and attempting to use the review process the Terms of Service directed him to use. (Complaint, ¶ 34.) Plaintiff argues that Meta breached the contract by disabling the Accounts with no valid determination that Plaintiff had breached the Terms; failing to let Plaintiff know and explain options he had to request review as Section 4.2 requires and providing an interface that made the promised review impossible; and threatening to permanently destroy Plaintiff’s stored content notwithstanding the review to which he was entitled. (Complaint, ¶ 35.) As a result of this, Plaintiff “has been damaged and faces the imminent, permanent loss of irreplaceable property, in an amount to be proven at trial, and is entitled to injunctive relief to prevent that loss.” (Complaint, ¶ 36.)

 

Meta argues that Plaintiff’s alleged damages consist of the mere possibility that his account content might be lost. (Complaint, ¶ 36.) Meta further states that because the account has been reinstated, Plaintiff has access to his accounts’ content, and Plaintiff will not be able to prove damages. (Opposition, 5:14-23.) The terms of service and terms of understanding also show that Meta and Instagram are not liable for lost profits, revenues, information, or data. (Allen Decl., Exh. A, § 4.3; Exh. B, § 4.3; Exh. C, § 7.3.) Thus, Meta and Instagram cannot be liable for damages, and Plaintiff does not challenge this argument on reply.

 

            Therefore, Plaintiff has not shown the likelihood of success of the breach of contract claim.

 

III. Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing Claim

 

“A breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing involves something beyond breach of the contractual duty itself and it has been held that bad faith implies unfair dealing rather than mistaken judgment.” (Careau & Co. v. Security Pacific Business Credit, Inc. (1990) 222 Cal.App.3d 1371, 1394.) “If the allegations do not go beyond the statement of a mere contract breach and, relying on the same alleged acts, simply seek the same damages or other relief already claimed in a companion contract cause of action, they may be disregarded as superfluous as no additional claim is actually stated … [T]he only justification for asserting a separate cause of action for breach of the implied covenant is to obtain a tort recovery.” (Id. at pp. 1394-1395.) To recover in tort for breach of the implied covenant, the defendant must “have acted unreasonably or without proper cause.” (Id. at p. 1395 [citations and italics omitted].)

 

Courts have raised the possibility that arbitrary or bad faith termination of user accounts, or even termination of user accounts with no explanation at all, could implicate the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing. (Young v. Facebook, Inc. (N.D. Cal. May 17, 2011) 790 F.Supp.2d 1110, 1118.)

 

Plaintiff has alleged that implied in the contract between Plaintiff and Meta is a covenant that neither party will do anything to deprive the other of the benefits of the agreement and the Meta will exercise any discretion it holds under the Terms in good faith and consistent with the parties’ reasonable expectations. (Complaint, ¶ 38.) Plaintiff has alleged that “Meta breached that covenant by exercising whatever discretion it possessed over the Accounts in an arbitrary and unreasonable manner, including by disabling the Accounts based on an automated miscategorization of protected artwork, removing or failing to provide the appeal control its own Terms and process require, closing Plaintiff’s support case on a stated rationale that was contradicted by the condition of his own interface, and allowing an automated process to proceed toward permanent destruction of his data, thereby depriving Plaintiff of the benefits of the agreement actually made.” (Complaint, ¶ 39.) Finally, Plaintiff has been damaged in an amount to be proven at trial and faces the imminent loss of irreplaceable property. (Complaint, ¶ 40.)

 

Meta argues that Plaintiff’s alleged damages consist of the mere possibility that his account content might be lost. (Complaint, ¶ 40.) Meta further states that because the account has been reinstated, Plaintiff has access to his accounts’ content, and Plaintiff will not be able to prove damages. (Opposition, 5:14-23.) The terms of service and terms of understanding also show that Meta and Instagram are not liable for lost profits, revenues, information, or data. (Allen Decl., Exh. A, § 4.3; Exh. B, § 4.3; Exh. C, § 7.3.) Thus, Meta and Instagram cannot be liable for damages, and Plaintiff does not challenge this argument on reply.

 

            Therefore, Plaintiff has not shown the likelihood of success on the breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing claim.

 

IV. Unfair Competition Claim

 

                Under Section 17204, a party must show that they have (1) established a loss or deprivation of money or property sufficient to qualify as injury in fact, i.e., economic injury, and (2) show that economic injury was the result of, i.e., caused by , the unfair business practice or false advertising that is the gravamen of the claim. (Kwikset Corp. v. Superior Court (2011) 51 Cal.4th 310, 322.)

 

            Plaintiff alleges that they have lost money or property as a result of Meta’s conduct and that Meta’s conduct causes harm to consumes such as Plaintiff because it represents that users may obtain review of a mistaken disablement, while its interface withholds the means to obtain that review and an automated process destroys the user’s data. (Complaint, ¶¶ 51-52.) Additionally, Plaintiff alleges that Meta’s conduct is also fraudulent in that its representations regarding the availability of a review or appeal process are likely to deceive reasonable consumers. (Complaint, ¶ 52.)

 

            Unfortunately for Plaintiff, the terms of service and terms of understanding also show that Meta and Instagram are not liable for lost profits, revenues, information, or data. (Allen Decl., Exh. A, § 4.3; Exh. B, § 4.3; Exh. C, § 7.3.) Thus, Plaintiff has not shown a substantial likelihood of success that Meta and Instagram can be liable for the alleged economic injury.

 

Plaintiff has not shown they are likely to succeed on the unfair competition claim.  

 

V. Injunctive Relief and Declaratory Relief

 

            Plaintiff’s Complaint listed two other causes of action: (6) declaratory relief; and (7) injunctive relief. Plaintiff’s opening brief did not request a preliminary injunction on the declaratory relief cause of action and so the Court declines to discuss that cause of action.

D.    Balance of Harms

 

For the second factor, the court must consider “the interim harm that the plaintiff would be likely to sustain if the injunction were denied as compared to the harm the defendant would be likely to suffer if the preliminary injunction were issued.”  (Smith v. Adventist Health System/West (2010) 182 Cal.App.4th 729, 749.)  “Irreparable harm” generally means that the defendant’s act constitutes an actual or threatened injury to the personal or property rights of the plaintiff that cannot be compensated by a damages award.  (See Brownfield v. Daniel Freeman Marina Hospital (1989) 208 Cal.App.3d 405, 410.)  

 

Plaintiff has alleged he will suffer irreparable harm because the photographs, videos, artwork in the accounts are unique and irreplaceable, exist nowhere else, and cannot be recreated once deleted. (Complaint, ¶ 30.) Additionally, Plaintiff explains that the “Download Your Information” option Meta presents is not an adequate substitute for the Accounts or a remedy for their loss. (Complaint, ¶ 24.) According to Plaintiff, the files produced through that option are compressed and do not preserve the full-resolution original photographs, videos, and artwork stored in the Accounts. (Complaint, ¶ 24.) However, the Court is mindful of the fact that the concern that Meta

 

Meta did not identify any harm if the Court issues a preliminary injunction. If Meta is already preserving the accounts, an order requiring Meta to continue doing so imposes no burden. Meta identifies no cost, no operational difficulty, and no prejudice of any kind.

 

The balance of harms analysis weighs slightly in favor of Plaintiff getting an injunction, though the lack of an immediate, practical concern does mitigate against any showing of risk of harm to Plaintiff.

 

CONCLUSION 

 

Plaintiff did not establish a likelihood of success on the merits. Accordingly, the Court DENIES the Application for a Preliminary Injunction.
