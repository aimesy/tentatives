DEPARTMENT 316 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 25STCV05589    Hearing Date: June 25, 2026    Dept: 316

BACKGROUND

This case is a lemon law action.

On February 27, 2025, Plaintiff Oswaldo Cabrera filed a complaint against Defendant General Motors, LLC and DOES 1-20. The complaint alleges two causes of action stemming from Plaintiff’s purchase of an allegedly defective vehicle.

On January 7, 2026, the parties filed a Notice of Settlement of Entire Case.

On May 8, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Motion for Attorney’s Fees (“Motion”).

On June 11, 2026, Defendant filed an opposition.

On June 17, 2026, Plaintiff filed a reply.

TENTATIVE RULING

Plaintiff’s Motion for Attorney’s Fees is GRANTED in part. However, the Court will award $20,407.25 in total fees and costs.

LEGAL STANDARD

Under the Song Beverley Consumer Warranty Act, if a buyer prevails in the action, the buyer is allowed to recover costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees based on actual time expended. (Civ. Code § 1794, subd. (d).) 

The determination of a reasonable amount of attorney fees is within the sound discretion of trial courts. (PLCM Group v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095.) When assessing the amount of any attorney’s fee award, courts typically determine what is reasonable through the application of the “lodestar” method. Under the lodestar method, a base amount is calculated from a compilation of time¿reasonably spent¿and the reasonable hourly compensation¿of each attorney. (See Serrano v. Priest (1977) 20 Cal.3d 25, 48); see also Meister v. Regents of University of California¿(1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 437, 448-449 [holding that the lodestar method applies to statutory attorney fees award unless the underlying statute provides for another method of calculation].)

A plaintiff’s verified billing invoices are prima facie evidence that the costs, expenses, and services listed were necessarily incurred. (See Hadley v. Krepel (1985) 167 Cal.App.3d 677, 682.) Counsel has the burden of providing the reasonable number of hours devoted to the litigation, through declarations, or redacted or unredacted timesheets or billing records. (See Concepcion v. Amscan Holdings, Inc. (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 1309, 1325.) “[T]he verified time statements of the attorneys, as officers of the court, are entitled to credence in the absence of a clear indication the records are erroneous.” (Horsford v. Board of Trustees of California State University (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 359, 396.)   

DISCUSSION

Here, Plaintiff seeks $33,652.99 for attorney’s fees and costs. (See Motion, p. 1:25.)

The Court will reduce this requested amount and award fees and costs in the amount of $20,407.25.

I.                Reasonable Hourly Rates

When considering each attorney’s history, experience, and expertise, the Court finds the listed hourly rates reasonable

There were four attorneys who worked on Plaintiff’s case – David N. Barry, Elizabth Quinn, Kristopher Storti, and Christopher Derhartounian. (See Motion, p. 14:4-12.) Barry claims an hourly rate of $695/hr., Quinn claims an hourly rate of $675/hr., Storti claims an hourly rate of $600/hr., and Derhartounian claims an hourly rate of $350/hr. (See ibid.)

Barry has been a licensed attorney for over 24 years. (See Barry Decl., ¶ 18.) 21 of those 24 years have been spent practicing lemon law. (See ibid.) Barry has handled over hundreds of lemon law cases. (See ibid.)

Quinn is a Supervising Attorney and Senior Associate at the Barry Law Firm. (See Quinn Decl., ¶ 1.) She has been a licensed attorney for over 26 years. (See ibid.) Eight of those 26 years have been spent practicing lemon law with the Barry Law Firm. (See id. at ¶ 11.)

Storti is an Associate at the Barry Law Firm. (See Storti Decl., ¶ 1.) He has been a licensed attorney for over 25 years. (See id. at ¶ 4.) Seven of those 25 years have been spent practicing lemon law with the Barry Law Firm. (See id. at ¶ 6.)

Derhartounian is an Associate at the Barry Law Firm. (See Barry Decl., § 10(d).) Derhartounian has been practicing law for over two years, which has been spent practicing lemon law at the Barry Law Firm. (See ibid.)

Given each attorney’s history, experience, and expertise, the Court finds the requested rates reasonable. (See PLCM Group v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095.)

II.             Reasonable Hours

The Court will award $18,859.50 for reasonable hours spent.

Here, Plaintiff’s counsel allegedly spent 47.2 hours on this case. This translates to $28,978 in attorney’s fees. (See Barry Decl., Exh. 2, p. 19.) Defendant requests the Court reduce or outright eliminate specific billing entries because they are unreasonable and excessive. (See Opposition, pp. 4:6-5:26.) The Court considers these specific entries below:

1.      Requested award of $607.50 for “prerepresentation” work done to evaluate Plaintiff’s claims and determine whether the firm should take him up as a client

o   UUNREASONABLE, reduced to $0 – Counsel shall not be awarded for any hours spent before Plaintiff was officially brought on as a client

2.      Requested award of $675 to draft Plaintiff’s initial disclosures

o   REASONABLE, no reduction – 1 hour to draft initial disclosures is reasonable, even if Counsel used a previous template

3.      Requested award of $3,112 for communications

o   REASONABLE, no reduction – Defendant makes no convincing argument or put forth evidence that said fees are unreasonable other than conclusory stating that they are excessive

4.      Requested award of $2,622.50 to review and respond to Defendant’s Motion for Confidential Protective Order

o   REASONABLE, no reduction – 2.9 hours to review a motion, draft and file an opposition, review reply brief, attend hearing, and review the Court’s ruling is more than reasonable. Further, the Court ultimately granting the motion has no bearing on whether Counsel should be compensated for its work.

5.      Requested award of $875 to attend vehicle surrender

o   REASONABLE, no reduction – The Court does not find a basis to reduce or eliminate time spent travelling to the vehicle surrender, which is part of the settlement of the case.

6.      Requested award of $1050 to prepare for and attend deposition of Defendant’s Person Most Knowledgeable

o   REASONABLE, no reduction – One hour to prepare for a deposition and two hours to conduct said deposition is reasonable, even if the scope of the deposition is limited to “routine” lemon law matters.

7.      Requested award of $543 for clerical and administrative tasks (i.e., reviewing and drafting court notices/filings)

o   UNREASONABLE, reduced to $0 – Counsel shall not be awarded for time spent in reviewing and drafting court notices/filings that appear to be administrative. These tasks could have been handled by support staff.

8.      Requested award of $1220 for Order to Show Cause Hearings

o   REASONABLE, no reduction – Counsel shall still be awarded for their work, even if these hearings only occurred because of their shortcomings. At the end of the day, these preparing for and attending these hearings were necessary for Plaintiff’s case to progress/continue.

9.      Requested award of $12,740.50 for this Motion and other related matters

o   UNREASONABLE, reduced to $3772.5 – The Court finds it should not take Counsel 3.7 hours to draft, review, prepare and finalize the present motion. This Motion is not new to Counsel nor is it relatively complex. Therefore, the Court will reduce the time spent working on this Motion to 2 hours at an hourly rate of $675/hr. Further, it should not reasonably take Counsel 2 hours to review Defendant’s opposition. Therefore, the Court will award Counsel for 0.5 hours of work at an hourly rate of $675/hr. The Court will also reduce the claimed travel and hearing appearance time claimed as excessive. Lastly, Counsel’s request for anticipated fees in relation to Defendant’s Motion to Tax Costs is entirely removed because Defendant never filed said Motion. In sum, the Court will reduce the award for fees claimed in preparing this motion and related matters to $3772.5 for 6.5 hours of work.

To summarize, there will be a $10,118.50 reduction in attorney’s fees. Therefore, Counsel will award attorney’s fees in the amount of $18,859.50

III.           Lodestar Multiplier

The Court will not apply a 0.1 lodestar multiplier.  

Once the court determines the total lodestar amount, said amount may be adjusted through the consideration of various factors, including “(1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, and (4) the contingent nature of the fee award.” (Ketchum v. Moses¿(2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132.) 

Here, Plaintiff requests a lodestar multiplier of 0.1 because of the contingent nature of this case. (See Motion, p. 6:14-21.) However, based on the non-complexity of this case and the routine nature of lemon law litigation, the Court does not find the multiplier reasonable or necessary. Therefore, the Court will not apply Plaintiff’s requested 0.1 lodestar multiplier.

IV.           Costs Incurred

The Court will award $1,547.75 in costs incurred.

Here, Defendant requests the Court reduce or outright eliminate Plaintiff’s incurred costs because they are unreasonable and excessive. (See Opposition, p. 6:1-21.) The Court considers these specific costs below:

1.      Jury Fees – Requested amount of $150

o   UNREASONBLE, reduced to $0 – Plaintiff can request the posted jury fees be returned given that no jury was empaneled.

2.      Deposition Costs – Requested amount of $699

o   REASONBLE, no reduction – Defendant’s argument is unsound; Plaintiff’s Memorandum of Costs is proof enough that Plaintiff actually incurred deposition costs.  

3.      Court Reporter Fees – Requested amount of $550

o   IRRELEVANT – Plaintiff is not requesting $550 for Court Reporter Fees, therefore Defendant’s request that the Court strike this requested amount is not relevant.

4.      Fees for Electronic Filing or Service – Requested amount of $112.25

o   REASONBLE, no reduction – Plaintiff’s Memorandum of Costs is evidence that these costs are legitimate.

5.      Other Costs – Requested amount of $279.44

o   UNREASONABLE, reduced to $200 – Parking and mediation fees were necessary to advance litigation However, tacking on costs for mileage is overly excessive. Therefore, mileage costs are stricken entirely.

To summarize, there will be a $229.44 reduction in awarded costs. Therefore, Counsel will award $1,547.75 for costs incurred.

CONCLUSION¿

For these reasons, Plaintiff’s Motion for Attorney’s Fees is GRANTED in part. The Court will award $20,407.25 in total fees and costs.

Case Number: 26STCV04358    Hearing Date: June 25, 2026    Dept: 316

BACKGROUND

This is a lemon law action.

On February 10, 2026, Plaintiff Sella Garoian filed a complaint against Defendants Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC (“MBUSA”), Mercedes-Benz of Beverly Hills, and DOES 1-50. The complaint alleges four causes of action stemming from Plaintiff’s purchase of an allegedly defective vehicle.

On April 1, 2026, MBUSA filed a Motion to Compel Arbitration and Stay Proceedings (“Motion”).

On April 21, 2026, Mercedes-Benz of Beverly Hills filed a Joinder to Motion.

On May 21, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition.

On May 28, 2026, Defendants filed a reply.

On June 4, 2026, the Court continued the hearing on the Motion to June 25, 2026. The Court also allowed Plaintiff to file a sur-reply and Defendants to respond.

On June 11, 2026, Plaintiff filed a sur-reply.

On June 18, 2026, Defendants filed a response to Plaintiff’s sur-reply.

TENTATIVE RULING

MBUSA’s Motion to Compel Arbitration is DENIED.

LEGAL STANDARD

A party to an arbitration agreement may summarily move or petition the court for an order compelling another non-compliant party to arbitrate their covered claims. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 1281.2, 1290.2; 9 U.SC. §§ 4, 6.)

California law, like Federal law, reflects a strong public policy favoring arbitration and the fundamental principle that arbitration is a matter of contract. (See AT&T Mobility LLC v. Concepcion (2011) 563 U.S. 333, 339; Moncharsh v. Heily & Blase (1992) 3 Cal.4th 1, 8-9.) “To further that policy, Code of Civil Procedure, section 1281.2 requires a trial court to enforce a written arbitration agreement unless one of three limited exceptions applies. Those statutory exceptions arise where (1) a party waives the right to arbitration; (2) grounds exist for revoking the arbitration agreement; and (3) pending litigation with a third party creates the possibility of conflicting rulings on common factual or legal issues.” (Acquire II, Ltd. v. Colton Real Estate Group (2013) 213 Cal.App.4th 959, 967 [internal citation omitted].) “[U]nder both the FAA and California law, ‘arbitration agreements are valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, save upon such grounds as exists at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract.’” (Higgins v. Superior Court (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 1238; Quach v. California Commerce Club, Inc. (2024) 16 Cal.5th 562, 569; Morgan v. Sundance (2024) 596 U.S. 411, 419.)¿

On petition of a party to an arbitration agreement alleging the existence of a written agreement to arbitrate a controversy and where a party thereto refuses to arbitrate such controversy, the court shall order the petitioner and respondent to arbitrate if it determines an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.2; Gorlach v. Sports Club Co. (2012) 209 Cal.App.4th 1497, 1505 [noting that “when presented with a petition to compel arbitration, the trial court’s first task is to determine whether the parties have in fact agreed to arbitrate the dispute.”].)¿

The petitioner bears the burden of proving the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by a preponderance of the evidence, and the opposing party bears the burden of proving any fact necessary to its defense by the evidentiary standard. (See Gatton v. T-Mobile USA, Inc. (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 571, 579.)¿

Finally, “[i]f a court of competent jurisdiction, whether in this State or not, has ordered arbitration of a controversy which is an issue involved in an action or proceeding pending before a court of this State, the court in which such action or proceeding is pending shall, upon motion of a party to such action or proceeding, stay the action or proceeding until an arbitration is had in accordance with the order to arbitrate or until such earlier time as the court specifies.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.4.) And similarly, under the FAA, a court is required to stay the proceedings for a case that has been referred to arbitration, upon application of one of the parties, during the pendency of such arbitration. (9 U.S.C. § 3.)

EVIDENTIARY OBJECTION

Plaintiff raises one evidentiary objection to the declaration of Macy Chan. The Court OVERRULES this objection.

DISCUSSION

I.               Existence of Arbitration Agreement

The Court finds that Defendant has failed to prove the existence of a valid arbitration agreement between the parties.

The party seeking arbitration bears the burden of proving the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by a preponderance of the evidence.¿(See Gamboa v. Northeast Community Clinic (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 158, 164-65 (Gamboa).) This can be done “by attaching to the [motion or] petition a copy of the arbitration agreement purporting to bear the [opposing party's] signature.” (Id. at 165 [citing Bannister v. Marinidence Opco, LLC (2021) 64 Cal.App.5th 541, 219].) Alternatively, this burden can be met by setting forth the agreement’s arbitration provisions in the motion. (See Gamboa, supra, 72 Cal.App.5th at 165.) 

Once the moving party meets their burden, the opposing party may produce evidence challenging the authenticity of the document. (See ibid.) The opposing party may do so by “[testifying] under oath or declare under penalty of perjury that the party never saw or does not remember seeing the agreement.” (Ibid.) If this burden is met, the burden shifts back to the moving party to prove the contract is authentic, including the authenticity of the signatures. (See Ruiz v. Moss Bros. Auto Group, Inc. (2014) 232 Cal.App.4th 836, 845-46.) Doubts are resolved in favor of arbitrability and ordinary rules of contract interpretation (e.g., resolve doubts against the drafter) apply. (See Greenspan v. LADT, LLC (2010) 185 Cal.App.4th 1413, 1437.)¿

Defendant submitted with the motion a purported lease agreement for the Subject Vehicle between Plaintiff and Keyes European, a dealership located in Van Nuys. (Chan Decl., Exh. 1, pp. 1, 6.) The lease agreement contained an arbitration provision (“Provision”). (See id. at p. 4.) The Provision states in relevant part:

Any claim or dispute, whether in contract, tort or otherwise (including any dispute over the interpretation, scope, or validity of this lease, arbitration section or the arbitrability of any issue), between you and us or any of our employees, agents, successors, assigns, or the vehicle distributor, including Mercedes-Benz USA LLC (each a “Third-Party Beneficiary”), which arises out of or relates to a credit application, this lease, or any resulting transaction or relationship arising out of this lease (including any such relationship with third parties who do not sign this contract) shall, at the election of either you, us, or a Third-Party Beneficiary, be resolved by a neutral, binding arbitration and not by a court action.

(Ibid.)

MBUSA provided a copy of the lease agreement with no signatures. (See generally Chan Decl., Exh. 1.) Then MBUSA provided a different copy of the lease agreement with its reply brief but as pointed out by Plaintiff, this version of the lease agreement is not signed by anyone representing the dealership. (See Chan Decl. submitted with Reply, Exh. 1.) Then MBUSA submitted a third version of the lease agreement with its response to Plaintiff’s sur-reply. (See Leung Decl., Exh. 1.) Submitting new documents with reply briefs is generally not proper but submitting a new document with a response to a sur-reply is well beyond that and in this case a step too far.

The Court finds that MBUSA has failed to meet its initial burden on this motion of showing that there is a valid arbitration agreement between the parties. (See Gamboa, supra, 72 Cal.App.5th at 164-65.)  

II.             MBUSA’s Cannot Compel Arbitration Because any Warranty Is Independent of the Lease Agreement

Assuming arguendo there was a valid arbitration agreement, the Court finds that MBUSA would still not be able to compel arbitration.

“Equitable estoppel allows a nonsignatory defendant to invoke an arbitration clause to compel a signatory plaintiff to arbitrate its claims when the causes of action against the nonsignatory are ‘intimately founded in and intertwined’ with the underlying contract obligations.” (Ford Motor Warranty Cases (2023) 89 Cal.App.5th 1324, 1332-33 (Ford).) To determine if Plaintiff’s claims are founded in or intertwined with the underlying lease agreement, the Court must examine the facts of the complaint. (See id. at 1333.) “[M]anufacturer vehicle warranties that accompany the sale of motor vehicles without regard to the terms of the sale contract between the purchaser and the dealer are independent of the sale contract.” (Ibid.)

Here, Plaintiff alleges that she received an express written warranty “by which MBUSA undertook to preserve or maintain the utility or performance of Plaintiff’s Vehicle or to provide compensation if there was a failure in such utility or performance.” (Complaint, ¶ 29.) Furthermore, Plaintiff claims that MBUSA was unable to service the subject vehicle to conform to the applicable express warranty. (See id. at ¶ ¶ 13, 30, 35, 37, 48, 57, 60.)

Based on the Complaint and the lease agreement submitted by MBUSA, the Court concludes that any express warranty did not arise out of the lease agreement itself. First, Plaintiff alleged that while the express warranty accompanied the lease agreement, it was not a part of it. (Complaint, ¶ 29.) Second, Plaintiff leased a pre-owned vehicle. (See Chan Decl., Exh. 1, p. 1; Complaint, ¶ 12.) The lease agreement states that pre-owned vehicles are not covered by a warranty unless indicated by one of the boxes checked below. (See id. at p. 3 [“If the vehicle is pre-owned, it is not covered by a warranty unless indicated by a check in the corresponding box below. . . .”].) None of the relevant boxes are checked. (See ibid.) Lastly, the lease agreement states:

We lease the vehicle to you "AS IS". EXCEPT AS EXPRESSLY PROVIDED UNDER THIS LEASE, AND UNLESS PROHIBITED BY LAW, WE MAKE NO WARRANTIES OR REPRESENTATIONS, EITHER EXPRESS OR IMPLIED, AS TO THE VEHICLE'S (OR ANY PART OR ACCESSORY THEREOF) CONDITION, MERCHANTABILITY, OR FITNESS FOR ANY PARTICULAR PURPOSE; AND WE MAKE NO OTHER REPRESENTATION OR WARRANTY WHATSOEVER.

(Id. at p. 4.)

Thus, the lease agreement did not provide the warranty, make any assurances regarding the quality of the subject vehicle, or make any promise of repairs or other remedies in the event problems arose.

These facts are very similar to those alleged in Ford. There, the sale contracts did not include any warranty, any assurances regarding the quality of the vehicles sold, or any promise of repairs or other remedies in the event problems arose. (See Ford, supra, at p. 1335.) In fact, the sale contracts disclaimed any warranty on the part of the dealers, just as the lease agreement presented by MBUSA here. (See ibid.) The lease agreement did not include any warranties, make any assurances as to quality or repair, and explicitly disclaimed any warranties. (See Chan Decl., Exh. 1, pp. 1, 3-4.) Therefore, Plaintiff’s reliance on Ford is well taken. The Court find that Plaintiff’s causes of action against MBUSA are not “intimately founded in and intertwined” with the underlying contract obligations, assuming Defendant had properly presented a lease agreement. (See Ford, supra, at pp. 1332-33.)

The Court finds that MBUSA cannot compel arbitration because any warranty is independent of the lease agreement.  

III.          MBUSA’s Ability to Compel Arbitration as a Third-Party Beneficiary

Even if MBUSA is a third-party beneficiary of the lease agreement, it still may not compel arbitration because Plaintiff’s claims do not arise out of the agreement itself.

“[T]he decision as to whether a contractual arbitration clause covers a particular dispute rests substantially on whether the clause in question is ‘broad’ or ‘narrow.’” (Bono v. David (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 1055, 1067.) An arbitration clause is broad when it contains language such as “any claim arising from or related to this agreement” or “arising in connection with the agreement.” (See Rice v. Downs (2016) 248 Cal.App.4th 175, 186.) A broadly worded arbitration clause may extend to tort claims only when said claims arise out of the underlying contract or “have their roots in the relationship between the parties which was created by the contract.” (See ibid. [citing Izzi v. Mesquite Country Club (1986) 186 Cal.App.3d 1309, 1315-16].) Put another way, “To require arbitration, [the] factual allegations need only ‘touch matters’ covered by the contract containing the arbitration clause and all doubts are to be resolved in favor of arbitrability.” (Rice v. Downs (2016) 248 Cal.App.4th 175, 186 [citing Simula, Inc. v. Autoliv, Inc. (9th Cir. 1999) 175 F.3d 716, 721].)

Here, the Provision states in relevant part:

Any claim or dispute, whether in contract, tort or otherwise (including any dispute over the interpretation, scope, or validity of this lease, arbitration section or the arbitrability of any issue), between you and us or any of our employees, agents, successors, assigns, or the vehicle distributor, including Mercedes-Benz USA LLC (each a “Third-Party Beneficiary”), which arises out of or relates to a credit application, this lease, or any resulting transaction or relationship arising out of this lease (including any such relationship with third parties who do not sign this contract) shall, at the election of either you, us, or a Third-Party Beneficiary, be resolved by a neutral, binding arbitration and not by a court action.

(Ibid. [emphasis added].)

The Court finds that the arbitration provision does not cover Plaintiff’s claims. As demonstrated in the prior section, Plaintiff’s claims do not arise out of the lease agreement. Therefore, even assuming MBUSA is a third-party beneficiary of the arbitration agreement in the lease agreement, it cannot compel Plaintiff to arbitrate her claims. (See Rice v. Downs (2016) 248 Cal.App.4th 175, 186.)  

IV.           Joinder of Mercedes-Benz of Beverly Hills

Mercedes-Benz of Beverly Hills filed a joinder to MBUSA’s Motion. Mercedes-Benz of Beverly Hills is not a party to the lease agreement bearing the arbitration provision. Nor is it named in the lease agreement as a third party beneficiary. Therefore, there is no basis on which Mercedes-Benz of Beverly Hills can compel arbitration. (See Ford Motor Warranty Cases (2023) 89 Cal.App.5th 1324.)

CONCLUSION¿

For the foregoing reasons, MBUSA’s Motion to Compel Arbitration is DENIED.
