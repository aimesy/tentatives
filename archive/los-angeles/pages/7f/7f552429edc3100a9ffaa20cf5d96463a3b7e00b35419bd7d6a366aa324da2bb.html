[Tentative] Order on Motion to Enforce Writ of Mandate and Related Court Orders

 

INTRODUCTION  

 

            Petitioner Oceans 11 RV Park LLC (‘‘Petitioner”) moves for an order enforcing the writ of mandate and related court orders issued against the City of Los Angeles (“Respondent” or the “City”) in this case.  Specifically, Petitioner seeks an order vacating the City’s revocation of a certificate of occupancy, ordering the certificate of occupancy reinstated, enjoining enforcement of an order to comply, and imposing a $1,000 fine on the City.  (Motion to Enforce (“Mot.”) 20.) 

 

BACKGROUND

 

Petitioner filed this petition for writ of mandate alleging that the City “unlawfully obstructed the completion and occupation of a fully approved and built out RV park in violation of the City’s own municipal code.” (Second Amended Petition (“SAP”) ¶ 1.) Petitioner sought to develop the park as a Public Benefit Project under Los Angeles Municipal Code (“LAMC”) section 14.00.A.7. Pursuant to that section, an RV park is permitted in the City’s A, R, and C zones if it meets 12 performance standards. (See Court’s Order dated September 11, 2025, at 1.) The Los Angeles Department of Building and Safety (“LADBS”), the Planning Director, and the City Planning Commission “found that the Project complies with all 12 performance standards set forth in section 14.00.A.7.” (Id. at 4.) However, the City refused to issue the permits following the actions of the City Councilmember for the respective district.

 

This case proceeded to trial on September 11, 2025, and the court (Mackenzie, J.) granted the petition and held that the City had a clear, ministerial duty under LAMC section 14.00.A.7 to approve the RV Park and issue the permits.  The City filed a notice of appeal from the judgment and a return to the writ stating that the City would not comply with the writ because it was stayed on appeal pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 916.  Petitioner moved for an order lifting the automatic stay of the City’s compliance with the writ pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1110b.  On March 3, 2026, the court (Goorvitch, J.) granted Petitioner’s motion and ordered the City to comply with Judge Mackenzie’s order of September 11, 2025, and the corresponding writ of mandate, issued on October 15, 2025.  (See Court’s Order dated March 3, 2026, at 8-9.)[1]  As relevant to the instant motion, Judge Goorvitch ruled as follows on the City’s contentions that the Project fails to comply with three performance standards:

 

The City argues that the public interest would be harmed if the court requires issuance of the permits because the Project allegedly fails to comply with three performance standards. Relatedly, the City asserts that the “injuries to the public interest” from lifting the stay outweigh the harms to Petitioner because the City would be “forced” to issue permits when the performance standards in LAMC section 14.00.A.7 are not met. Remarkably, the City makes these arguments despite never once having asserted in this litigation that the Project fails to comply with the 12 performance standards in section 14.00.A.7.  Judge Mackenzie has already determined—after full briefing, a complete administrative record, and a contested hearing—that the City acted unlawfully and has a ministerial duty to issue the permits.  At the hearing, the City argued that there is a public interest in enforcing the performance standard. There is also a public interest in the City following the Los Angeles Municipal Code and other applicable laws, and approving public benefit projects.

 

Nor does the City establish that there is a danger to public safety as a result of these issues. The performance standards at issue are: (1) The setback should be 20.5 feet, but the plan showed a setback of 20 feet, i.e., the setback is off by six inches; (2) The corresponding wall/fence is off by six inches; and (3) The property must have a ten-foot landscaped buffer along the periphery of the property, and there is encroachment by a portion of the RV spaces and a trash enclosure with a dumpster. (Oppo. at 8.) These are not the types of issues that give rise to a public safety concern. Regardless, lifting the stay does not eliminate public safeguards or waive final inspections. It does not authorize occupancy without compliance with the permits, or strip the City of enforcement authority…..[¶¶]

 

The City retains full authority to enforce its codes, conduct inspections, and withhold or revoke a certificate of occupancy if legally warranted.

 

(Court’s Order dated March 3, 2026, at 6-8, citations omitted and emphasis added.)

 

On or about March 18, 2026, the City filed a return to the writ stating that it had issued the necessary permits and complied with the writ.  (Hinks Decl. Exh. 16.)  However, on March 19, 2026, the City issued a “Correction Notice” stating that certain deficiencies needed to be corrected before the work authorized by the permits could be approved.  (Id. Exh. 17.)  Among other things, the City ordered that Petitioner must “[c]omply with the performance standards located in 14.00.A.7” and “[r]emove everything within the landscape buffer zone that does not belong there including white poles, galvanized steel posts, sheds, electric and plumbing connections, and transformers per page P1 of the approved plans.”  (Ibid.)  Petitioner moved ex parte to enforce the writ.  (Id. ¶ 18, Exh. 21-22.)  On March 27, 2026, after a hearing, the court (Chalfant, J.) granted the application and ordered the City to follow the approved plans. (Ibid.)  The court stated:

 

There’s been a determination made that the plans are consistent with the performance standards. So at this point, I don’t care about the performance standards. We’re done with those. Plans. If they build it according to the plans, that’s good enough. That’s all the City can check for….

 

(Id. Exh. 21 at 14.) 

 

            Following Judge Chalfant’s order, the City conducted a final inspection and, on April 20, 2026, issued the certificate of occupancy (“CofO). (Silver Decl., ¶¶ 26-32, Exh. C-D; Hinks Decl., ¶ 19, Exh. 23-24.) Petitioner’s RV park opened and tenants began moving into the park immediately after Petitioner received the CofO. (Silver Decl. ¶ 33.)  However, on April 23, 2026, the City issued a Notice of Intent to Revoke the CofO (the “Notice”).  (Hinks Decl. Exh. 25.)  The City stated the following basis for its action:

 

LADBS identified non-compliance with Performance Standard No. 5 of L.A.M.C. Section 14.00 A.7, it states that the property is improved with a ten foot landscaped buffer along the periphery of the property. A site inspection found customer installed recreation vehicle park pedestals (consisting of electrical, water and sewer connections) installed within the landscape buffer. This buffer shall only consist of landscaping and according to sheet P-1 of the plans approved by Department of City Planning and LADBS on supplemental BP No. 24026-10000-00086, there is no reference of equipment being located in that area on sheet P-1. [¶] Upon a careful examination of the approved plan and field conditions, LADBS determined that the Certificate of Occupancy associated with BP Nos. 23020-10000-02232 and 24026-10000- 00086 was issued in error and intends to revoke the associated Certificate of Occupancy.

 

(Ibid., italics in original.)  The Notice did not address sheets P-4 and P-7 of the approved plans, which, as discussed below, appear to show the utility infrastructure in the location where it was built, as confirmed by an LADBS approval stamp.  (See Id. ¶ 17, Exh. 19; see also Manalansan Decl. ¶ 9 [acknowledging that “plan sheets P4, P7 … show utility pedestals in the landscape buffer”]; Vidal Decl. ¶ 5 [same].) 

 

On May 7, 2026, the City revoked the CofO. (Hinks Decl. ¶ 20, Exh. 26.) The revocation letter was based on the same alleged violation of performance standard No. 5 of LAMC section 14.00 A.7 as stated in the Notice.  On May 28, 2026, the City issued an Order to Comply (“OTC”) and alleged other purported violations of the performance standards, as discussed below.  (Id. Exh. 27.)  The OTC directs Petitioner, by June 25, 2026, to remove the utility infrastructure from the landscape buffer area, remove trash enclosures and associated asphalt paving from the landscape buffer zone, relocate a fence, remove or reduce the height of the Concrete Masonry Unit walls in the front yard setback to 42 inches, close the Project, and obtain a new CofO.  (Ibid.)  This motion to enforce the writ followed.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

 

“The trial court that issues a writ of mandate retains continuing jurisdiction to make any orders necessary for complete enforcement of the writ.”  (Los Angeles Internat. Charter High School. v. Los Angeles Unified School Dist. (2012) 209 Cal.App.4th 1348, 1355.)  “If the petitioner or the court is dissatisfied with the return, the court may order the respondent to reconsider.”  (Ibid.)  Code of Civil Procedure section 1097 also “authorizes three methods by which a court may enforce a peremptory writ of mandate: (1) a court may impose a fine not exceeding $1,000; (2) a court may order the disobedient party to be imprisoned until the writ is obeyed; and (3) a court may make any order necessary and proper to enforce the writ.”  (King v. Woods (1983) 144 Cal.App.3d 571, 577-578.)[2] 

 

In addition, every court possesses the power to “compel obedience to its judgments, orders, and process.” (Code Civ. Pro. § 128(a)(4).) “The power to enforce their decrees is necessarily incident to the jurisdiction of courts.”  (Branson v. Sharp Healthcare, Inc. (2011) 193 Cal.App.4th 1467, 1476, fn. 4.)

 

EVIDENTIARY ISSUES

           

             The court grants, in part, the City’s request for judicial notice filed June 12, 2026.  The court does not judicially notice the truth of factual statements made in the subject records.  (Herrera v. Deutsche Bank National Trust Co. (2011) 196 Cal.App.4th 1366, 1375 [“While courts take judicial notice of public records, they do not take notice of the truth of matters stated therein.”].)

 

DISCUSSION

           

A.                Has the City Complied with the Writ?

 

Petitioner contends that the City’s “revocation of the CofO is nothing more than an impermissible attempt to relitigate performance standard issues that were conclusively resolved.”  (Mot. 13.)  In other words, Petitioner contends that “[i]ssuing a CofO only to revoke it on a previously adjudicated pretext is a circumvention of the writ.”  (Mot. 16.)  Judge Goorvitch granted relief from the automatic stay and ordered the City to comply with Judge Mackenzie’s order of September 11, 2025, and the corresponding writ of mandate, issued on October 15, 2025.  (See Court’s Order dated March 3, 2026, at 8-9.)  Although the City reinstated Permit No. 23020-10000-02232 and issued Supplemental Permit Application No. 24026-10000-00086, that does not resolve the motion.  (See Hinks Decl. Exh. 16.)  The writ also directed the City to “[a]pprove the Public Benefit Project pursuant to Los Angeles Municipal Code Section 14.00.A.7.”  (Id. Exh. 11.)  In granting the writ, Judge Mackenzie “determined that Petitioner has satisfied the 12 performance standards of LAMC section 14.00.A.7.”  (Court’s Order dated March 3, 2026, at 7.) This court agrees with Petitioner, as well as Judge Chalfant, that the judgment in this case necessarily includes a determination that the Project’s plans are consistent with the performance standards of LAMC section 14.00.A.7.  (See Hinks Decl. Exh. 21 at 13-14.)  If Petitioner built the Project in a manner that conflicts with the plans, the City has the power to revoke the CofO.  (See RJN Exh. 3 [LAMC § 91.109.6].)  However, if Petitioner built according to the plans, then the City’s revocation of the CofO for alleged noncompliance with the performance standards is reasonably viewed as an effort to circumvent the writ and may be enjoined pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1097.   Therefore, the court considers the City’s asserted reasons for revoking the CofO and issuing the OTC.

 

1.                  Utility Infrastructure in the Landscape Buffer

 

The City revoked the CofO on the grounds that the Project, as built, does not comply with performance standard No. 5 in LAMC section 14.00.A.7.  (Hinks Decl. Exh. 25-26.)  Section 14.00.A.7 states the following performance standard: “The property is improved with a ten foot landscaped buffer along the periphery of the property which is maintained and is equipped with an automatic irrigation system.”  The City found that “[a] site inspection found customer installed recreation vehicle park pedestals (consisting of electrical, water and sewer connections) installed within the landscape buffer.”  (Ibid.)  To support this finding, Allen Manalansan, the City’s Assistant Bureau Chief in the Permit and Engineering Bureau, declares as follows:

 

The reason for the [Notice of Intent to Revoke Certificate of Occupancy] is that a site inspection, conducted post-permit-issuance to verify compliance with Planning and DBS-approved plan sheets P1-P3, found concrete customer installed utility connection pedestals, consisting of electrical, water and sewer connections (“pedestals”), within the landscaped buffer of the RV Park…. My review of Project plan pages P1 and P3, that were reviewed and approved by Planning and DBS in relation to the Supplemental Building Permit, do not show any pedestals located in the landscaped buffer….

 

Based on my review of DBS records and resources, DBS Permit and Engineering Bureau did not review or approve Project plan sheets P4, P7, or other plan sheets that show utility pedestals in the landscape buffer, prior to the issuance of the DBS Permits. When a DBS plan check engineer reviews and approves a page of a project’s plan set, the specific plan page is stamped by the plan check engineer. Because the State of California Housing and Community Development Department (“HCD”) has jurisdiction over some portions of the RV Park, DBS would review and approve some but not all plan pages in a set of project plans for an RV Park…. To the extent a mark of DBS approval was somehow added to a complete RV Park plan set, including those plans sheets that show utility pedestals or other electrical components, it was added in error.

 

(Manalansan Decl. ¶¶ 8-9; see also Vidal Decl. ¶ 5 [“The Planning review and approval for the Project did not include review or approval of any other Project plan sheet, P4, P7, or otherwise, that show customer-installed utility connection pedestals in the landscaped buffer.”].)

 

            Adam Burgess, a Principal Inspector in the Inspection Bureau of LADBS, declares that the pedestals were not installed by the Los Angeles Department of Water and Power (“LADWP”).  (Burgess Decl. ¶ 7.)  He further declares:

 

Each pedestal was a raised concrete square about 4 feet on each side with electrical, water and sewer connections on it for recreational vehicles and trailers. There were about 16-17 pedestals in the area that is supposed to be the landscaped buffer. Attached as Exhibit 5 to the City’s Request for Judicial Notice is a true and correct copy of a photograph I took of the pedestals on March 19, 2026. Based on my observations, the site conditions violate Performance Standard No. 5 of LAMC § 14.00.A.7, which states that “the property is improved with a ten foot landscaped buffer along the periphery of the property.” Based on my observations, the site conditions also violate pages P1 through P3 of the Project plans approved by DBS which show landscaping, not utility pedestals located in the landscaped buffer.

 

(Id. ¶ 7.)  Exhibit 5, which this court reviewed, is a picture of the Project site showing the pedestals at issue.  (Id. Exh. 5.)

 

            The City’s evidence conflicts, in some respects, with the approved plans in the record and with Plaintiff’s declarations.  Specifically, the City’s declarants appear to acknowledge that sheets P-4 and P-7 of the approved plans show the utility infrastructure in the location where it was built.  (See Manalansan Decl. ¶ 9 [acknowledging that “plan sheets P4, P7 … show utility pedestals in the landscape buffer”]; Vidal Decl. ¶ 5 [same].)  Sheets P-4 and P-7 include a stamp that states “LADBS Approved” and is dated “3 22 24.”  (Hinks Decl. Exh. 19; see also Administrative Record (“AR”) 779-782.)  In addition, Stuart Silver, Petitioner’s owner, declares:

 

The location of the utility infrastructure has long been reflected in the approved plans, which are attached to the Declaration of Matthew D. Hinks (“Hinks Decl.”) as Exhibit 4 (AR0779). LADBS and City Planning reviewed, approved, and stamped those plans. The heads of those departments subsequently reaffirmed on multiple occasions, including through a formal Inter-Departmental Memorandum, that the plans comply with all applicable performance standards….

 

Moreover, the utilities equipment was not even constructed by Oceans 11. It was constructed entirely by the City’s Department of Water and Power (“LADWP”), who selected and approved the location of the equipment and charged Oceans 11 for its construction. I provided my counsel with a copy of the electrical plan created and approved by LADWP and other related documents….

 

In addition, LADBS has long been aware of the approved location of the utility equipment. Sheet P-4 of the City’s approved plan set for the Project is the electrical plan, which shows that LADBS approved and stamped the plan in the upper right hand corner. (Hinks Decl., Exh. 19 (LADBS B — LA 3-22-24 APPROVED).) The plans also show that the electrical infrastructure that the City tried to make us remove along the rear property line is exactly where the LADWP constructed it….

 

The plumbing plan is Sheet P-7. (Hinks Decl., Exh. 19.) The plumbing connections the City is requiring to be removed is also shown along the rear property line exactly where LADWP constructed it. Sheet P-7 contains the same LADBS perforated approval stamp.

 

(Silver Decl. ¶¶ 14-17.)  Silver also declares that the cost and time to remove the utility infrastructure from the buffer area would be prohibitive.  (Id. ¶ 13.) 

 

            On this record, the court concludes that the utility infrastructure at issue, including the “pedestals,” was constructed in compliance with the approved plans.  The City’s declarants appear to acknowledge that sheets P-4 and P-7 of the approved plans show the utility infrastructure in the location where it was built.  (See Manalansan Decl. ¶ 9; Vidal Decl. ¶ 5.)  Silver declares that the utility infrastructure is shown in the approved plans.  (Silver Decl. ¶¶ 14-17.)  Further, as Petitioner argues, there is an open question of whether the City even has jurisdiction to regulate the placement of utility infrastructure within the park itself.  (See Mot. 14-15; Health & Safety Code § 18865.)  Indeed, the City concedes that the California Housing and Community Development (“HCD”) “has jurisdiction over some portions of the RV Park,” and evidence shows that HDC approved the utility infrastructure on the site.  (Manalansan Decl. ¶ 9; Silver Decl. ¶ 20.)  Because Judge Mackenzie determined that the approved plans comply with the performance standards of LAMC section 14.00.A.7, the City’s revocation of the CofO on the grounds that the utility infrastructure allegedly does not comply with performance standard No. 5 appears to be an effort to circumvent the writ of mandate.  Therefore, the court is tentatively inclined to enjoin the OTC to the extent it is based on the City’s finding that “[t]he sewer connections, water connections, and electrical pedestals are within the 15 foot rear yard setback and within the 10 foot landscape buffer zone at the south end of the property.”  (See Hinks Decl. Exh. 27.) 

 

            The court has considered the parties’ remaining contentions related to the utility infrastructure, including the City’s citations to utility equipment exception in LAMC section 12.22.A.2.  (Oppo. 16; Reply 6-7.)  The court does not believe these arguments change the analysis above.  Of course, counsel may further make these arguments at the hearing. 

 

The Court will hear argument about whether the issue of the utility infrastructure should be part of the evidentiary hearing that the Court is setting on other issues. The Court also requests arguments on the effect of deciding the issue of the utility infrastructure in Petitioner’s favor. By its terms, the revocation of the CofO appears to be based solely on this asserted violation, rather than the other issues raised in the OTC discussed above. Accordingly, resolving this issue in Petitioner’s favor may fully resolve the issue of the CofO. If so, what is the practical effect of the OTC if it is limited only to the trash enclosures and paving and the decorative fence and masonry walls?

 

2.                  Trash Enclosures and Paving

 

The OTC states that “[t]he trash enclosures at the north and south ends of the property, and asphalt paving at the south end of the property, are within the ten foot landscaped buffer zone” in violation of LAMC section 14.00.A.7(a)(5).  (Hinks Decl. Exh. 27.) 

 

Joey Buchanan, Senior Building Inspector, declares: “The trash enclosures at the north and south ends of the property, and asphalt paving at the south end of the property, are within the 10-foot landscaped buffer zone, in violation of LAMC § 14.00.A.7(a)(5), or performance standard No. 5. The asphalt paving underlies part of the trash enclosure and parking. No asphalt paving at the south end of the property within the 10-foot landscaped buffer zone is shown on P1-P3.”  (Buchanan Decl. ¶ 11.)

 

            Silver declares that at an inspection on March 19 “Buchanan also noted on the plans that the trash enclosures were also within the landscape buffer” and “informed me that Oceans 11 had to obtain a new supplemental permit just to remove the trash enclosures from the plans.”  (Silver Decl. ¶ 9.)  “Otherwise, according to Mr. Buchanan, Oceans 11 would need to obtain a new or further approval from Planning to build the trash enclosures that were already approved on the plans.”  (Id. ¶ 9.)  After Judge Chalfant’s order that the parties followed the approved plans, Petitioner “constructed the dumpster enclosures in accordance with the approved plans.”  (Id. ¶ 27.)  Nonetheless, the OTC states that the trash enclosures violate LAMC section 14.00.A.7(a)(5). 

 

            The court finds this evidence to be in conflict.  Also, it appears neither side has provided pictures of the trash enclosures and paving, as constructed, or sufficient other evidence from which the court can determine if the trash enclosures and paving were built according to the approved plans.  The court will continue the hearing for additional evidence or an evidentiary hearing related to the trash enclosures and paving.

 

3.                  The Decorative Fence; and the Concrete Masonry Unit Walls

 

The OTC states that “[t]he decorative fence that encircles the periphery of the property extends into the required front yard setback at the Northwesterly and Southwesterly corners of the property” in violation of LAMC section 14.00 A.7(a)(3).  (Hinks Decl. Exh. 27.)  The OTC also states that “[t]he Concrete Masonry Unit walls at the Northwesterly and Southwesterly corners of the property are within the required front yard setback and exceed three and one-half feet in height” in violation of LAMC section 12.22.C.20(f)(2).  (Ibid.)

 

            The performance standard in LAMC section 14.00.A.7(a)(3) states that “[t]here is a solid, decorative, masonry or wrought iron wall or fence at least eight feet in height, or the maximum height permittee by the zone, whichever is less. The wall or fence encircles the periphery of the property and does not extend into the required front yard setback.”  (RJN Exh. 4.)  LAMC section 12.22.C.20(f)(2) states, in relevant part: “In the R Zones, fences, walls, and landscape architectural features of guard railing around depressed ramps, not more than three and one-half feet in height above the natural ground level adjacent to the feature, railing or ramp, may be located and maintained in any required front yard.”  (RJN Exh. 14.)

 

            Inspector Buchanan addresses these alleged violations in his declaration.  In relevant part, he declares as follows:   

 

The decorative fence that encircles the periphery of the property extends into the required front yard setback at the northwesterly and southwesterly corners of the property. This is in violation of LAMC § 14.00.A.7(a)(3), or performance standard number 3…. This is also not built according to the approved plan sheets P1-P3 …. For example, P1 shows, near the bottom of the sheet, near the center, an arrow that indicates “8’ wrought iron fence-new” pointing towards a dotted line that represents the fence as well as the labeled “Set back line”—so the plan sheet shows the fence lined up with the setback--it does not show the fence encroaching into the front yard setback at the northwesterly and southwesterly corners. However, the inspection of the actual site revealed that the fence is in fact encroaching into the front yard setback in the northwesterly and southwesterly corners, which is different than what is shown on P1.

 

…..[¶¶]

 

The concrete masonry unit walls at the northwesterly and southwesterly corners of the property are within the required front yard setback, and exceed three and one-half feet in height. The walls are not built according to the approved plan sheets P1-P3. For example, on P1, the lower right hand corner (the southwesterly corner) shows a bolded or thickened line that is the concrete masonry unit wall, as indicated by an arrow that says existing “8’ concrete block wall”, and the lower left hand corner (the northwesterly corner) also shows a bolded or thickened line that indicates a wall. There are two gates shown on P1, each one looking like two cat ears or a cursive “V”, one at the southwesterly corner and one on the northwesterly corner, in the driveway on each corner. On P1, the plan sheet shows the wall on the southwesterly corner as stopping at the gate, the same with the wall on the northwesterly corner. However, in reality, site inspection revealed that the wall on each corner extends past the respective gate, and extends parallel and adjacent to the portion of the driveway (in gray) that is outside of the gates, by at least more than 10 feet, almost to the sidewalk, and encroaches into the front yard setback. Although not stated in the OTC, as the citation was inadvertently left off, this is in violation of LAMC § 14.00.A.7(a)(3), or performance standard number 3….

 

(Buchanan Decl. ¶¶ 9, 12.) 

 

Buchanan also explains why he believes the concrete masonry unit walls violate LAMC section 12.22.C.20(f)(2) and present a safety hazard.  (Id. ¶ 12.)  He declares that “the portion of the tall wall extending parallel and adjacent to the driveway on each corner presents a safety hazard because, for example, if someone is driving out, the 8 feet height of that portion of the wall can obscure the driver’s view such that the driver cannot properly see a pedestrian approaching the driveway.”  (Id. ¶ 12.) 

 

            Petitioner disagrees with Buchanan’s assessment.  (Reply 10.)  Silver declares that the decorative fence “as constructed is precisely consistent with the approved plans.”  (Silver Decl. ¶ 43.)  He declares that the approved plans depict the concrete masonry unit walls “exactly where they currently exist.” (Id. ¶ 44.)  Petitioner’s evidence on this issue is fairly conclusory. 

 

The court finds this evidence to be in conflict.  Also, it appears neither side has provided pictures of the decorative fence or concrete masonry unit walls, as constructed, or sufficient other evidence from which the court can determine if these items were built according to the approved plans.  The court is concerned about Buchanan’s testimony that the concrete masonry unit walls violate LAMC section 12.22.C.20(f)(2) and present a safety hazard.  (Buchanan Decl. ¶¶ 11-12.)  Petitioner does not rebut that evidence in reply. The court will continue the hearing for additional evidence or an evidentiary hearing related to the decorative fence and concrete masonry unit walls.

 

4.                  The Court Orders an Evidentiary Hearing

 

With respect to the trash enclosures, the decorative fence, and the masonry wall, the court is inclined to hold an evidentiary hearing to resolve the factual disputes outlined above with respect to the City’s compliance with the writ and the sufficiency of the return.  (See Los Angeles Internat. Charter High School. v. Los Angeles Unified School Dist. (2012) 209 Cal.App.4th 1348, 1353 [trial court held hearing on the sufficiency of the agency’s return].)  To the extent possible, the parties should submit pictures, expert declarations, or other documentary evidence that would assist the court in deciding the issues presented by the motion.  If necessary, the court will hold a live evidentiary hearing in which cross-examination is permitted.  

 

B.                 Exhaustion and Estoppel

 

The City contends that “[a]ny issues that Oceans 11 has with the revocation of the C of O and the OTC should not be decided by this Court and is a different lawsuit.”  (Oppo. 18.)  Relatedly, the City contends that Petitioner may appeal the revocation of the CofO and the OTC to the Board of Building and Safety Commissioners (“BBSC”).  (RJN Exh. 7, 9; Los Globos Corp. v. City of Los Angeles (2017) 17 Cal.App.5th 627, 634-635.)  Presently, the issue before the court is the City’s compliance with the writ.  That issue must be decided in this lawsuit pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1097 and relevant case law.  (See Los Angeles Internat. Charter High School., supra, 209 Cal.App.4th at 1353-55.)  Petitioner does not have an administrative remedy before BBSC to challenge the City’s compliance with the writ.[3]

 

For similar reasons, the City’s equitable estoppel arguments are not persuasive.  (Oppo. 16.)  Petitioner is not asserting estoppel, but rather is seeking to enforce the writ.  (Reply 8-9.)

 

C.                 The Court Enjoins Enforcement of the OTC Pending a Final Ruling on this Motion  

 

Petitioner seeks an order vacating the City’s revocation of the CofO, ordering the CofO reinstated, enjoining enforcement of the OTC, and imposing a $1,000 fine on the City.  (Mot. 20.)  At this time, the court only considers the request for an injunction against enforcement of the OTC.  The OTC directs Petitioner, by June 25, 2026, to remove the utility infrastructure from the landscape buffer area, remove trash enclosures and associated asphalt paving from the landscape buffer zone, relocate a fence, remove or reduce the height of the Concrete Masonry Unit walls in the front yard setback to 42 inches, close the Project, and obtain a new CofO.  (Hinks Decl. Exh. 27.)  Therefore, if the court does not enjoin enforcement of the OTC, the issue of compliance with the writ may become moot before the court can consider all relevant evidence and issue a final ruling.

 

Concepts of injunctive relief apply in this context.  The purpose of a preliminary injunction is to preserve the status quo pending a decision on the merits.  (Major v. Miraverde Homeowners Ass’n. (1992) 7 Cal. App. 4th 618, 623.)  In deciding whether or not to grant a preliminary injunction, the court looks to two factors, including “(1) the likelihood that the plaintiff will prevail on the merits, and (2) the relative balance of harms that is likely to result from the granting or denial of interim injunctive relief.”  (White v. Davis (2003) 30 Cal.4th 528, 553-54.) The factors are interrelated, with a greater showing on one permitting a lesser showing on the other.  (Dodge, Warren & Peters Ins. Services, Inc. v. Riley (2003) 105 Cal.App.4th 1414, 1420.)  However, the party seeking an injunction must demonstrate at least a reasonable probability of success on the merits.  (IT Corp. v. County of Imperial (1983) 35 Cal.3d 63, 73-74.)  The party seeking the injunction bears the burden of demonstrating both a likelihood of success on the merits and the occurrence of irreparable harm.  (Savage v. Trammell Crow Co. (1990) 223 Cal.App.3d 1562, 1571.)

 

For the second factor, the court must consider “the interim harm that the plaintiff would be likely to sustain if the injunction were denied as compared to the harm the defendant would be likely to suffer if the preliminary injunction were issued.”  (Smith v. Adventist Health System/West (2010) 182 Cal.App.4th 729, 749.)  “Where, as here, the defendants are public agencies and the plaintiff seeks to restrain them in the performance of their duties, public policy considerations also come into play. There is a general rule against enjoining public officers or agencies from performing their duties…. This rule would not preclude a court from enjoining unconstitutional or void acts, but to support a request for such relief the plaintiff must make a significant showing of irreparable injury.” (Tahoe Keys Property Owners’ Assn. v. State Water Resources Control Bd. (1994) 23 Cal.App.4th 1459, 1471.) 

 

Here, for the reasons discussed above, Petitioner have shown some, reasonable probability of proving that the City’s revocation of the CofO and issuance of the OTC are efforts to circumvent the writ and violate the court’s orders in this case.  Therefore, the court considers the balance of harms.  For the reasons discussed in Judge Goorvitch’s order dated March 3, 2026, Petitioner has shown that it would suffer irreparable harm if the OTC is not enjoined until this court can make a final ruling on the motion to enforce.  (Court’s Order dated March 3, 2026, at 3-6.)  Indeed, this court finds that Petitioner has made a “significant showing of irreparable injury.” (Tahoe Keys, supra, 23 Cal.App.4th at 1471.)  The City has not shown that it, or the public, would suffer irreparable harm if the OTC is stayed for a short period of time so that this court can decide Petitioner’s motion to enforce.  For the most part, the court agrees with Judge Goorvitch that the alleged violations identified by the City, including the alleged encroachment on the buffer zone, “are not the types of issues that give rise to a public safety concern.”  (See Court’s Order dated March 3, 2026, at 7.)  Also, if there was a public safety concern, the City presumably would have set a more immediate compliance date in the OTC.

 

As noted above, the court is concerned about Buchanan’s testimony that the concrete masonry unit walls violate LAMC section 12.22.C.20(f)(2) and present a safety hazard.  (Buchanan Decl. ¶¶ 11-12.)  Petitioner does not rebut that evidence in reply.  Counsel should address this issue at the hearing and argue whether the court may stay the OTC except as to the concrete masonry unit walls.

 

The court defers a ruling on all remaining enforcement orders, including Petitioner’s request for a $1,000 fine, until after the evidentiary hearing specified above.   

 

D.                The Court Orders the City to Reconsider its Return

 

The City’s most recent return, dated March 1, 2026, states that the City “reinstated Permit No. 23020- 10000-02232” and “issued the Supplemental Permit.”  (Hinks Decl. Exh. 16.)  However, the return does not state that the City approved the Public Benefit Project pursuant to Los Angeles Municipal Code Section 14.00.A.7, as required by the writ.  (Id. Exh. 11.)  In addition, the return does not acknowledge the City’s revocation of the CofO and issuance of the OTC, and it does not explain why those actions are consistent with the writ.  Therefore, the court does not grant the City’s request to discharge the writ.  (See Hinks Decl. Exh. 16.)  Instead, for the reasons discussed above, the court orders the City to reconsider its return in light of the court’s views expressed herein and at the hearing on this motion.  (See Los Angeles Internat. Charter High School., supra, 209 Cal.App.4th at 1355 [“If the petitioner or the court is dissatisfied with the return, the court may order the respondent to reconsider.”].)

 

CONCLUSION 

 

            Based on the foregoing, the court orders as follows:

 

1.                  The court enjoins the OTC and the revocation of the CofO to the extent they based on the City’s finding that “[t]he sewer connections, water connections, and electrical pedestals are within the 15 foot rear yard setback and within the 10 foot landscape buffer zone at the south end of the property.”  (See Hinks Decl. Exh. 27.)  The OTC and the revocation of the CofO are not consistent with the preemptory writ in this respect.

The hearing will be for a date to be discussed with the parties.  The court authorizes the parties to submit additional evidence, including pictures of the Project as constructed.  The court also authorizes live witness testimony and cross-examination.

5.                  The court orders the City to reconsider its return in light of this court’s ruling and to file a new return within 10 days of this ruling.

INTRODUCTION

 

             Petitioner Oceans 11 RV Park LLC (“Petitioner”) filed this petition for writ of mandate alleging that the City of Los Angeles (“the “City”) violated its ministerial duties to approve the North Hills Park, an RV Park. The lawsuit also alleges that the City violated California’s Housing Accountability Act. Petitioner seeks a writ of mandate directing the City to exercise its ministerial duty to issue the requested permits for the RV park. Petitioner also seeks a writ under the Housing Accountability Act (the “HAA”) directing the City to approve the RV park within 60 days.

 

            The Court GRANTS the petition for writ of mandate as to the first two claims relating to LAMC §14.00.A(7).  The Court DENIES the petition for writ of mandate as to the third claim relating to the Housing Accountability Act and the fourth claim for declaratory relief.

BACKGROUND

 

A.     The Property and the Project

On September 25, 2024, the City approved the Project as a qualified “RV Park Mobile Home Park” Public Benefit Project, designating the code classification of “Mobilehome Park” as the Project’s “Proposed Use,” and issued the Permit on September 25, 2024. (AR 41-42; 1406; Silver Decl., ¶¶ 8, 18.)

 

On October 21, 2024, the City issued an electrical permit for temporary power during construction and internet. (Silver Decl., ¶ 20.)

 

On December 9, 2024, California Department of Housing and Community Development (“HCD”) issued a permit allowing the development of the Project. (AR1480; Silver Decl., ¶ 19.)

 

Construction began in January 2025. (Silver Decl., ¶ 23.)

 

On April 24, 2025, the City issued a plumbing permit to upgrade the water line, with a supplemental permit on May 15, 2025. (Silver Decl., ¶ 20.)

 

Between December 2024 and August 2025, HCD oversaw permitting and inspection of the work, conducting nine inspections. (Silver Decl., ¶ 24.) By early August 2025, the Project was fully built out. (Silver Decl. ¶¶ 23, 28.)

 

Oceans 11 expended over $1.3 million constructing the park in reliance on the permit. (Silver Decl., ¶ 22.) Oceans 11 requested the final DBS inspection on August 7, 2025. (AR1392; Silver Decl. ¶ 29.)

(AR149, 232-241, 499, 504-507; see also AR140, 147.) The stated reason for the ICO was that RV parks posed a current and immediate threat to public health, safety, and welfare. (AR 261.)

(Silver Decl., ¶ 33.)  At the time though, no ICO had been adopted and no draft ordinance was even pending. (Silver Decl., ¶ 33.)

The City then used that limited application to remove the approved “mobile home” park use from the project description without Petitioner’s consent. (Silver Decl., ¶¶ 47-48.) (Silver Decl., ¶¶ 50-52.)

 

DBS issued an extensive set of plan check comments as if the application was for a new project (and not just to document the removal of an inconsequential structure from the Project plans), and most of the comments did not even relate to the performance standards. LEGAL STANDARD

 

A.     Petition for Traditional Writ of Mandate

 

The first and second causes of action are for writ of ordinary mandate.  There are two essential requirements to the issuance of an ordinary writ of mandate under Code of Civil Procedure section 1085: (1) a clear, present, and ministerial duty on the part of the respondent, and (2) a clear, present, and beneficial right on the part of the petitioner to the performance of that duty. (California Ass’n for Health Services at Home v. Department of Health Services (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 696, 704.)   “An action in ordinary mandamus is proper where, as here, the claim is that an agency has failed to act as required by law.”  (Id. at 705.)  “Generally, mandamus is available to compel a public agency’s performance or to correct an agency’s abuse of discretion when the action being compelled or corrected is ministerial.”  (AIDS Healthcare Foundation v. Los Angeles County Dept. of Public Health (2011) 197 Cal.App.4th 693, 700.)    

 

Mandamus also “will lie to correct abuses of discretion. In determining whether a public agency has abused its discretion, the court may not substitute its judgment for that of the agency, and if reasonable minds may disagree as to the wisdom of the agency's action, its determination must be upheld. A court must ask whether the public agency's action was arbitrary, capricious, or entirely lacking in evidentiary support, or whether the agency failed to follow the procedure and give the notices the law requires.”  (County of Los Angeles v. City of Los Angeles (2013) 214 Cal.App.4th 643, 654.)   

 

An agency is presumed to have regularly performed its official duties.  (Evid. Code § 664.) Petitioner “bears the burden of proof in a mandate proceeding brought under Code of Civil Procedure section 1085.”  (California Correctional Peace Officers Assn. v. State Personnel Bd. (1995) 10 Cal.4th 1133, 1154.)  “‘On questions of law arising in mandate proceedings, [the court] exercise[s] independent judgment.’ ….¿Interpretation of a statute or regulation is a question of law subject to independent review.”  (Christensen v. Lightbourne (2017) 15 Cal.App.5th 1239, 1251.)   

 

B.     Housing Accountability Act

 

The third cause of action seeks to enforce the Housing Accountability Act (the “HAA”).  Government Code section 65589.5(m)(1) of the HAA requires that, “[a]ny action brought to enforce the provisions of this section shall be brought pursuant to Section 1094.5 of the Code of Civil Procedure....”  The court’s task “is therefore to determine whether the City ‘proceeded in the manner required by law,’ with a decision supported by the findings, and findings supported by the evidence; if not, the City abused its discretion.”  (Cal. Renters Legal Advocacy and Education Fund v. City of San Mateo (2021) 68 Cal.App.5th 820, 837.)  The City “bear[s] the burden of proof that its decision has conformed to all of the conditions specified in¿Section 65589.5.”  (§ 65589.6.)   

 

DISCUSSION

 

A.                 Judicial Notice

 

The Court GRANTS all requests for judicial notice.

 

B.                 The City Has a Ministerial Duty to Process and Approve Petitioner’s Applications

 

In the first and second causes of action, Petitioner alleges that “[t]he City has a ministerial duty to process and approve Petitioner’s applications under LAMC § 14.00.A(7),” and that “[t]he City has failed to perform its ministerial duties by issuing an intent to revoke Petitioner’s Use of Land Permit, and imposing conditions that violate State law and the LAMC.” (Petition, ¶¶ 136-137.)  In the first cause of action, “Petitioner seeks a Court order compelling the City to perform its ministerial duty to process and approve the applications, issue the corresponding permits, conduct the final inspections, and issue the certificate of occupancy.” (Petition, ¶ 142.)

 

Notably, other than its argument about exhaustion of remedies, the City does not make any substantive argument supporting its conduct or asserting that an ordinary writ of mandate should not issue. (See Opp. at 14-18.) While it has submitted declarations detailing the process and the issues raised by the City, the City fails to make any legal argument defending those actions.  (DuPont Merck Pharmaceutical Co. v. Superior Court (2000) 78 Cal.App.4th 562, 566 [“By failing to argue the contrary, plaintiffs concede this issue”].) The Court discusses the issue of exhaustion of remedies in the following section.

 

The background law concerning Petitioner’s right to a writ of mandate is well-established. The issuance of a building permit is generally a ministerial act.  (Friends of Juana Briones House v. City of Palo Alto (2010) 190 Cal. App. 4th 286, 302.)  The ministerial nature of the City’s duties is shown by the municipal code: “When the department determines that the information on the application and plans is in conformance with this Code and other relevant codes and ordinances, the department shall issue a permit upon receipt of the total fees.”  (LAMC § 91.106.4.1.)  As work progresses under the permit, DBS “shall approve that portion of the work inspected or notify the responsible person if the work fails to comply with the law.” (LAMC § 91.108.5.)  Upon completion of the final inspection, “the Superintendent of Building shall issue a [CofO], without charge, to the owner of the building.”  (LAMC § 91.109.3.)  Generally, courts construe the word “shall” as mandatory.  (Tarrant Bell Prop., LLC v. Sup. Ct. (2011) 51 Cal. 4th 538, 542.)   

 

Here, DBS, Planning, Fire, and Sanitation reviewed the Project and cleared it in September 2024. (AR 35-36.) The Planning Department confirmed that the Project complied with all twelve performance standards under LAMC §14.00.A.7 and signed off on the Project. (Silver Decl., ¶ 17.) The City issued the original permit on September 25, 2024. (AR41-42.)

 

This should have been the end of the story. But in August 2025, DBS decided to “expire the Permit because work had allegedly not commenced within six months of permit issuance.” (Silver Decl., ¶¶ 29-30.) DBS  made this decision even though LAMC § 98.0602 is clear – permits expire twelve months after issuance unless work commences under a permit, not six.

 

DBS also issued a Notice of Intent to Revoke the Permit, which included a list of claims that have no legal basis. DBS complained about “address approval” but address approval

is not a performance standard needed to obtain the permit in the first place, and Petitioner’s response showed that the address for the Project was already used in the City’s system, appeared on prior permits, and was reflected in the recorded covenant executed with DBS staff. (AR 65-67, 1481; Silver Decl. ¶ 16.) The demolition of the greenhouse is equally irrelevant because it does not concern any performance standard and HCD—not the City—has authority over construction and demolition work within the park under the Special Occupancy Parks Act. (AR 1481-1482, 1500-01504.) HCD verified that Petitioner complied with permitting requirements. (Ibid.) The side yard, reverse corner, and side yard projection issues also fail because they too are not part of the performance standards. (AR1421-1422 [LAMC § 14.00.A.7].) The RV spaces and unbuilt trash enclosure issues do not relate to any performance standard.  (AR 01482-1483, 1713.)

 

The City noted two issues that do relate to performance standards. The City claimed that the approved plans failed to demonstrate compliance with the front yard setback, which requires the front yard to be at least as deep as the setback required by the zone. (AR 1384-1385, 1421-422.) However, the City approved the plans before the permit, and the Project contains no structures within 40 feet of the front lot line. (AR35-36, 40-41, 1505-1509; Silver Decl., ¶¶ 17-18, 62, Ex. B.)

 

The existing single family house was built decades before the Project and is not part of the RV Park. In the RA zone where the Project is, the code requires a front yard of 20% of the depth of the lot not to exceed 25 feet, and where 40% of the lots have frontage that vary within 10 feet, the setback shall be the average of the setbacks of these lots, and no front yards are required to exceed 40 feet. (LAMC §12.07.C.1) In the RV Park, there are no structures within 40 feet of the front yard lot line; therefore, the RV Park complies with the front yard setback. (AR 1713.)

 

The landscape buffer objection is also meritless. The City approved the plans showing the buffer, the dumpster enclosure, and landscaping around the perimeter, including along the public street frontages. (AR 1484, 1776, Silver Decl., ¶¶ 17, 62, Ex. B.) If the City thought it was a true issue, the remedy would be for Petitioner to not build the trash enclosure in the area; not revoke the permit outright. (Silver Decl., ¶ 44.)

 

            In a declaration submitted with the opposition, Hayden Chan identified supposed noncompliance issues on the corrections sheet. (Chan Decl., ¶ 7.) None of the issues Chan identifies are violations of the performance standards: the side yard objection conflates a setback requirement with the landscape buffer of Performance Standard No. 5 (which the Project provides); the fence height issue was a request for “clarification” that was provided; the light pole objection invokes a general zoning provision that is not among the performance standards; the building height issue was resolved; and the park signage is compliant and has always been approved. (Ross Decl. ¶¶ 4-8.) Chan also demanded an equine keeping checklist for a use that does not exist; Petitioner provided it anyway, mooting the issue. (Ross Decl., ¶¶ 3, 9.) Chan also referenced the need for “clarification” of the project description—but this supposed ambiguity was the City’s own creation because the City revised the project description without Petitioner’s consent. (Ross Decl. ¶¶ 10-15.) Still, Petitioner responded to each item with detailed explanations. The City never replied. (Ross Decl., ¶ 26.)

 

The later supplemental permit was a result of another substantive concern: the removal of the trash enclosure from the approved plans. (Silver Decl., ¶ 45.) However, the revised plans removed the trash enclosure; there were no other changes to the approved permit. (AR114-115, 129, 1712-1713, 1922; Silver Decl., ¶¶ 45-46.) The City identified no law that allowed it to reopen the entire permit and reassess what it had already approved. Yet the City did exactly that. (Silver Decl., ¶¶ 46-49; AR113-117.)

 

DBS also relied on the ICO grounds for terminating the permit. This argument is meritless. The ICO did not become effective until October 31, 2025, more than a year after the City issued the permit. (AR 257-264; AR 1443-1244.) Under California law, “[o]nce a landowner has secured a vested right the government may not, by virtue of a change in the zoning laws, prohibit construction authorized by the permit upon which he relied.” (Avco Community Developers, Inc. v. South Coast Regional Com. (1976) 17 Cal.3d 785, 791 [enforcing new ordinance where property owner had expended $2,300 in reliance on building permits would result in the taking of that property without due process or just compensation].)

 

A right vests when (1) the developer performs substantial work, spends substantial funds, and/or incurs substantial liabilities; (2) the work is performed, and expenditures incurred pursuant to a valid grant or permit; and (3) the reliance on the grant or permit was in good faith. (Id., at p. 791.)

 

There is no question that Petitioner has a vested right. Petitioner performed substantial work and made substantial expenditures in good faith pursuant to the use of the land permit. (Silver Decl., ¶ 22) Petitioner applied for and obtained the use of land permit normally through City processes. (AR 33-36, 41-44, 1340-41, 1546-47; Silver Decl., ¶¶ 14-21.) The City issued the permits. (Id.) Petitioner spent over $1.3 million constructing the Project in reliance on the permit. (Silver Decl., ¶ 22.) Petitioner commenced construction in January 2025 and completed work by early August 2025. (Silver Decl., ¶¶ 23, 28.)

 

When a vested right is obtained, the scope of the vested right “is not governed by municipal ordinances, or policies,” but by “constitutional principles” protecting property rights. (Pardee Construction Co. v. California Coastal Com. (1979) 95 Cal.App.3d 471, 481.) The ICO itself confirms that it does not apply if the application would “result in a taking requiring compensation” or “would deprive any person of constitutional or statutory rights or privileges.” (AR 263.)

 

In sum, the City retracted the permit and refused to let the Project go forward. The City took actions contrary  to established law. As noted, the City fails to make any merits arguments defending this conduct. There is no discussion about why the project cannot move forward because of address approval, greenhouse demolition, or side yard, reverse corner, and side yard projection issues. There is no discussion of vesting or when work was performed on the project.

 

In effect, the City dedicated significant public resources to creating arguments outside of applicable legal principles for why the Project could not go forward. The City also revised the project description without the consent of Petitioners, and failed to engage when Petitioners requested information about the status of the Project. (Silver Decl., ¶¶ 47, 51, 56.) The Court has found the declarations from the City’s witness to be unpersuasive, especially on the question of what information was shared with Petitioner.

 

C.     Exhaustion of Administrative Remedies and Ripeness

 

The City contends that Petitioner failed to exhaust its administrative remedies to challenge DBS’s refusal to issue building permits for the Project, and therefore this failure bars the litigation.

 

Exhaustion of administrative remedies is “a jurisdictional prerequisite to judicial review.”  (Cal. Water Impact Network v. Newhall County Water Dist. (2008) 161 Cal.App.4th 1464, 1489.)  “The exhaustion requirement applies whether relief is sought by traditional (Code Civ. Proc., § 1085) or administrative (Code Civ. Proc., § 1094.5) mandamus.”  (Eight Unnamed Physicians v. Medical Executive Com. (2007) 150 Cal.App.4th 503, 511.)  “Before seeking judicial review a party must show that he has made a full presentation to the administrative agency upon all issues of the case and at all prescribed stages of the administrative proceedings.”  (Edgren v. Regents of University of California (1984) 158 Cal.App.3d 515, 520.) “The rule has important benefits: (1) it serves the salutary function of mitigating damages; (2) it recognizes the quasi-judicial tribunal's expertise; and (3) it promotes judicial economy by unearthing the relevant evidence and by providing a record should there be a review of the case.”  (Campbell v. Regents of University of California (2005) 35 Cal.4th 311, 322.) 

 

There are, however, exceptions. “The requirement of exhaustion of administrative remedies does not apply if the remedy is inadequate.”  (Glendale City Employees' Assn. v. City of Glendale (1975) 15 Cal.3d 328, 342.)  The exceptions to the doctrine also include futility: “when the aggrieved party can positively state what the administrative agency’s decision in his particular case would be.” (Ogo Associates v. City of Torrance (1974) 37 Cal.App. 830, 834.)

 

1.      Petitioner Does Not Have an Adequate Administrative Remedy

 

The City contends that Petitioner has a right to challenge LADBS’s “permitting action” in appeals to LADBS and then to the Board of Building and Safety Commissioners (“BBSC”) pursuant to LAMC sections 98.0403.1 and 98.0403.2.

 

Section 98.0403.1(b)2 states:

 

The Board shall have the power to hear and determine appeals from orders, interpretations, requirements, determinations, or actions of the Department pertaining to enforcement of specific ordinances, regulations, or laws in site-specific cases. These appeals shall state how the Department has erred or abused its discretion in the matter of the appeal.

 

Here, Petitioner does not contend that DBS erred or abused its discretion in enforcing any specific ordinance, regulation, or law.  To the contrary, Petitioner contends that DBS correctly applied LAMC section 14.00.A.7, and correctly determined that the Project complies fully with all applicable standards and is entitled to ministerial approval.   Specifically, DBS initially issued permits for the Project.  (AR 35-36; 41-42; 1340-1341; 1406; Silver Decl., ¶¶ 8, 18.)

 

Instead, Petitioner challenges DBS’s refusal to issue ministerial building permits at the direction of the City Council and the ICO. (AR149, 232-241, 499, 504-507; see also AR140, 147; Silver Decl., ¶ 33, 39, 51, 57, 59.) Sections 98.0403.1 and 98.0403.2 do not provide a remedy to challenge an order from the City Council to “halt” the issuance of ministerial permits, even if the City Council order is not applicable because the Project was vested. Nor do sections 98.0403.1 and 98.0403.2 authorize DBS or the BBSC to order the City Council to rescind its directive.  Because Petitioner does not have an adequate remedy in sections 98.0403.1 and 98.0403.2, the exhaustion doctrine does not apply. 

 

      The City’s argument that the Board can hear appeals from “actions” like this one is unconvincing. The City has not cited any evidence that City staff, specifically DBS, will disobey a directive of the City Council if ordered to do so by BBSC. Thus, even if Petitioner were to pursue an appeal to the BBSC and even if BBSC were to try and override the City Council’s orders (which as previous judges have found, seems unlikely), that process would not afford Petitioner the relief it seeks—namely, the issuance of the permits and other approvals.  

 

      Finally, the City’s approach makes little public policy sense. DBS raised new issues around the permit after the permit was already approved. If Petitioner finally got the permit approved after going through the administrative remedies process, would DBS then be able to raise new issues and require Petitioner to start the whole process all over again? The administrative remedies process is supposed to be about bringing decisions to a close, not allowing them to drag on indefinitely.

 

2.      Petitioner Has Proven the Futility Exception

 

The futility exception applies when a “petitioner can positively state that the [agency] has declared what its ruling will be in a particular case . . . . ” (Sea & Sage Audubon Society, Inc. v. Planning Comm. (1983) 34 Cal.3d 412, 418.)  Futility usually presents a question of fact “depending upon the precise nature of the communications from the administrative agency and their applicability to the circumstances of the particular case.” (People ex rel. State Pub. Wks Bd. V. Superior Court (1979) 91 Cal.App.3d 95, 102.) “The law does not require useless acts.” (Tilem v. City of Los Angeles (1983) 142 Cal.App.3d 694, 704.)

 

Petitioner has proven that any appeal to LADBS or BBSC would be futile. Here, the City Council adopted the ICO to stop RV parks from being built. DBS has told Petitioner that it will not act because of the ICO. (Silver Decl. ¶¶ 58-59; Ross Decl., ¶ 16.) Moreover, the City Council could override any action of BBSC under Charter Section 245, which it has done in the past to undo decisions that support Petitioner’s projects. (Hinks Decl. ¶ 11, Ex. 14 at 25.) In general, the City’s course of conduct from tprojectdent I litigation, and the parallel conduct here approving but then serially interfering with the current project, demonstrates that administrative appeals would be futile.

 

D.    Petitioner is entitled to relief under the Housing Accountability Act

 

In the third cause of action, Petitioner alleges that “the Project fully complies with all relevant objective zoning standards” and “the City Council’s decision to grant the Amended Motion and direct LADBS to close the permit constitutes a disapproval of a housing development project” in violation of the Housing Accountability Act (“HAA”).  (Petition, ¶ 159.) Petitioner seeks a writ of administrative mandate “directing the City to process Petitioner’s suspended applications, approve Petitioner’s housing development project and issue the corresponding permits.” (Petition, ¶ 162.)

 

Petitioner argues that the Project – an RV park – is a “housing development project” within the meaning of the HAA. The City argues that it is not. Neither party cites a case deciding this question of statutory interpretation.

 

The rules governing statutory construction are well settled. “We begin with the fundamental premise that the objective of statutory interpretation is to ascertain and effectuate legislative intent. To determine legislative intent, we turn first to the words of the statute, giving them their usual and ordinary meaning. When the language of a statute is clear, we need go no further. However, when the language is susceptible of more than one reasonable interpretation, we look to a variety of extrinsic aids, including the ostensible objects to be achieved, the evils to be remedied, the legislative history, public policy, contemporaneous administrative construction, and the statutory scheme of which the statute is a part.” (Nolan v. City of Anaheim (2004) 33 Cal.4th 335, 340, citations omitted.) “A statute must be construed in the context of the entire statutory system of which it is a part, in order to achieve harmony among the parts.” (People v. Woodhead (1987) 43 Cal.3d 1002, 1009.)

 

  The HAA prohibits a local government from disapproving a “housing development project” that complies with “applicable, objective general plan, zoning, and subdivision standards and criteria” unless it makes written findings, based upon a preponderance of the evidence, that the project would have a specific, adverse impact on public health or safety that cannot be feasibly mitigated in any way other than rejecting the project or reducing its size. (Gov. Code § 65589.5, subd. (j)(1).)  A “housing development project” is defined broadly to include “a use consisting of…[r]esidential units only.” (Gov. Code § 65589.5, subd. (h)(2)(A).) The definition of “housing development project” must be broad and must be “interpreted and implemented in a manner to afford the fullest possible weight to the interest of, and the approval and provision, of housing.” (Gov’t. Code § 65589.5, subd. (a)(2)(L).)

 

Petitioner argues that the Project qualifies as “residential units.”  The HAA does not define “residential units,” and the HAA does not mention “recreational vehicles” anywhere in its text.  The ordinary meaning of “residential” is “used as a residence or by residents” or “of or relating to residence or residences”; and a “residence” is “the place where one actually lives as distinguished from one’s domicile or a place of temporary sojourn” or “a building used as a home.”  (See merriam-webster.com.)  A “unit,” as relevant, is “a single thing, person, or group that is a constituent of a whole.”  (Ibid.)  Depending on its configuration and intended use, an RV park could plausibly be considered “residential units.”   However, since a recreational vehicle may be driven away from the RV park, it could be viewed as distinct from traditional residential units.  Thus, the phrase “residential units” in section 65589.5, subd. (h)(2) is ambiguous as applied to a RV park. 

 

As the City argues, the term “residential unit” appears not only in the HAA’s definition of “housing development project,” but also in the following definition of “unit type” in section 65589.5, subd. (h)(13): 

 

(13) “Unit type” means the form of ownership and the kind of residential unit, including, but not limited to, single-family detached, single-family attached, for-sale, rental, multifamily, townhouse, condominium, apartment, manufactured homes and mobilehomes, factory-built housing, and residential hotel.

 

(emphasis added.)  Because this definition of “unit type” expressly identifies “residential unit” to include structures like manufactured homes and mobilehomes, but omits recreational vehicles or RV parks, it supports the City’s position that the HAA was not intended to apply to RV parks.  (See Sullivan v Stroop (1990) 496 U.S. 478, 484 [“[I]dentical words used in different parts of the same act are intended to have the same meaning.].) 

 

Further, in context, the phrase “including, but not limited to” in section 65589.5(h)(13) also supports the City’s position.

 

[T]he doctrine of ejusdem generis ... states that where general words follow the enumeration of particular classes of persons or things, the general words will be construed as applicable only to persons or things of the same general nature or class as those enumerated. The rule is based on the obvious reason that if the Legislature had intended the general words to be used in their unrestricted sense, it would not have mentioned the particular things or classes of things which would in that event become mere surplusage.

 

(Peralta Community College Dist. v. Fair Employment & Housing Com. (1990) 52 Cal.3d 40, 50.)  Here, the enumerated list of “residential units” in section 65589.5, subd. (h)(13) are similar in that they are stationary structures for housing people. 

 

Petitioner argues that the definition of “unit type” in subsection (h)(13) was added to the HAA in 2024 and applies to “unrelated parts of the HAA.” These factors are not dispositive.  The definition of “unit type” expressly includes a list of “residential units,” which is the exact statutory term at issue in this case.  The Legislature was aware of the definition of “housing development units” (which includes “residential units”) when it added the definition of “unit type” to the statute.  Therefore, the Legislature’s decision to omit any reference to recreational vehicles or RV parks in subsection (h)(13), but to include manufactured homes and mobilehomes, supports the City’s interpretation of the HAA to not apply to RV parks. 

 

The City’s position also finds support in other statutes, some of which have existed for decades, in which the Legislature distinguishes between mobilehomes and recreational vehicles.  For example, the Mobilehome Parks Act’s definition of “mobilehome” expressly excludes recreational vehicles.  (Health & Safety Code §§ 18008(a), 18211.)  Similarly, Civil Code section 798.3 states that “‘Mobilehome’ is a structure designed for human habitation” and “does not include . . . a recreational vehicle located in a recreational vehicle park.” (Civil Code § 798.3(a); cf. Civ. Code § 799.29 [definition of “Recreational vehicle”].)[1]  In addition, RV parks are regulated separately from mobilehomes by the Special Occupancy Parks Act and also the Recreational Vehicle Park Occupancy Law.  (See generally, Health & Safety Code §§18860 et seq.; Civ. Code §§ 799.20 et seq.; and Griffith v. County of Santa Cruz (2000) 79 Cal.App.4th 1318, 1321-22.)  These statutory schemes show that the Legislature knows the difference between mobilehomes and recreational vehicles, and it could have easily included RV parks in the list of “residential units” in section 65589.5, subd. (h)(13) of the HAA.  “Where statutes involving similar issues contain language demonstrating the Legislature knows how to express its intent, the omission of such provision from a similar statute concerning a related subject is significant to show that a different legislative intent existed with reference to the different statutes.”  (County of San Diego v. San Diego NORML (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 798, 825 [internal quotation marks omitted].)  “When the legislature has carefully employed a term in one place and has excluded it in another, it should not be implied where excluded.”  (Wasatch Property Management v. Degrate (2005) 35 Cal.4th 1111.) 

 

Petitioner argues that the HAA should apply to this case because the Project includes bathrooms, showers, laundry, fencing, landscaping, and an administrative office. (Silver Decl., ¶¶ 10, 11, 15.) Relatedly, Petitioner argues that public policy suggests the Court should interpret and implement the HAA in a manner that leads to approval and provision of housing. (Gov’t. Code § 65589.5, subd. (a)(2)(L).) “Such rules of liberal construction do not, however, allow [courts] to ignore the statutory language or construe it to accomplish a purpose that does not appear on the face of the statute or from its legislative history.”  (Reznitskiy v. County of Marin (2022) 79 Cal.App.5th 1016, 1036 [internal quotation marks omitted].)   Furthermore, “statutes must be construed so as to give a reasonable and common-sense construction consistent with the apparent purpose and intention of the lawmakers—a construction that is practical rather than technical, and will lead to wise policy rather than mischief or absurdity.”  (Upland Police Officers Assn. v. City of Upland (2003) 111 Cal.App.4th 1294, 1303.)  

 

The Legislature did not intend for the HAA to cover every kind of land use project. It expressly excluded “hotel” and “transient lodging” from the coverage of the HAA.  (Gov. Code § 65589.5, subd. (h)(2)(B)(ii)(II) and (h)(2)(B)(iii)(IV).)  If “housing development project” is interpreted to include RV parks, then the HAA would also cover campgrounds, parking lots, hotel guest rooms, or similar projects.  (See Health & Safety Code § 18862.36 [defining “recreational vehicle park” as an area “to accommodate owners or users of recreational vehicles, camping cabins, or tents”].)  It appears unlikely that the Legislature intended this result.  In sum, the court finds insufficient evidence in the text of the HAA, its legislative history, and other extrinsic aids to conclude that the Legislature intended to include RV parks within the scope of the HAA.  To the extent Petitioner asserts policy reasons for including RV parks within the scope of the HAA, those are questions for the Legislature.  The court cannot expand the reach of the HAA beyond its text. 

 

 In light of this conclusion, the court does not reach the parties’ remaining contentions related to the HAA.

 

E.     Declaratory Relief

 

Petitioner’s FAP raised a fourth claim – declaratory relief. However, that claim is not discussed at all in the opening papers or the reply (or the City’s opposition). Accordingly, Petitioner cannot prevail on the fourth claim.

 

CONCLUSION

 

            The Court GRANTS the petition for writ of mandate as to the first two claims, but DENIES the petition for writ of mandate as to the third claim and the fourth claim.
