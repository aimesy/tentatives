DEPARTMENT 224 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 24STCV06415    Hearing Date: August 7, 2026    Dept: 224

Defendant’s demurrer is SUSTAINED in its entirety, WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND. Defendant’s motion to strike is DENIED as moot.

Plaintiff’s complaint is dismissed with prejudice.

Background

Plaintiff Pirus Group, Inc. (Plaintiff) filed its complaint in this action on March 14, 2024 against defendant Goldie Marshall (Defendant), asserting claims for specific performance and breach of contract. The complaint was subsequently amended to name Defendant both individually and as personal representative of the Estate of Henry Darshad. The parties’ dispute arises out of a Purchase Agreement for a property located at 8620-8624 Holloway Dr., West Hollywood 90069 (the Property), which Plaintiff alleges Defendant agreed to sell and Plaintiff to buy.

On April 4, 2025, the Court sustained Defendant’s demurrer to both causes of action, without leave to amend. That ruling was reiterated in a minute order dated January 27, 2026.

On February 6, 2026, Plaintiff filed a first amended complaint (FAC), asserting new claims for (1) fraud and (2) breach of agreement, the latter identified as for breach of “oral agreement” in the caption of the FAC but breach of “written contract/agreement” in the body of the FAC.

Defendant now demurs to the FAC, arguing Plaintiff has recast its same deficient allegations in different terms and remains unable to state a claim. Plaintiff opposes.

Legal Standard

Where pleadings are defective, a party may raise the defect by way of a demurrer. (Coyne v. Krempels (1950) 36 Cal.2d 257, 262.) A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint alleges facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. (Cal. Code Civ. Proc. § 430.10; Young v. Gannon (2002) 97 Cal.App.4th 209, 220.) When considering a demurrer, a court reads the allegations stated in the challenged pleading liberally and in context, and “treat[s] the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Serrano v. Priest (1971) 5 Cal.3d 584, 591.) Put differently: for purposes of demurrer, the court treats all facts alleged – but only the facts alleged – in the complaint as true. (Picton v. Anderson Union High School District (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 726, 732.) “The only issue involved in a demurrer hearing is whether the complaint, as it stands, unconnected with extraneous matters, states a cause of action.” (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)

Discussion

The demurrer is sustained for a second time without leave to amend.

Plaintiff’s first cause of action is barred by the economic loss rule on its face. It is an action for breach of the purported sale contract that Plaintiff depended on in the first iteration of its complaint.

“[T]he [economic loss rule] functions to bar claims in negligence for pure economic losses in deference to a contract between litigating parties.” (Sheen v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. (2022) 12 Cal.5th 905, 922.) The rule endeavors to “ ‘prevent[ ] the law of contract and the law of tort from dissolving one into the other.’ [Citation.]” (Robinson Helicopter Co., Inc. v. Dana Corp. (2004) 34 Cal.4th 979, 988.) “Not all tort claims for monetary losses between contractual parties are barred by the economic loss rule. But such claims are barred when they arise from — or are not independent of — the parties’ underlying contracts.” (Sheen, supra, 12 Cal.5th, at p. 923.)

Plaintiff’s first cause of action depends entirely on Defendant’s purported breach of a contract. It does not articulate damages falling outside the scope of Defendant’s purported breach. The claim does not sound separately in tort.

Plaintiff second cause of action for breach of agreement (oral or written) fails for the same reasons stated in the Court’s ruling regarding Plaintiff’s initial complaint. There are various other defects in the second cause of action – as, for instance, uncertainty, given that the FAC identifies the character of the agreement inconsistently and refers to an “Exhibit A” for a written agreement not attached to the FAC. (See FAC, ¶ 24.) The introduction of increased uncertainty into an amended pleading suggests that any further changes to this cause of action would be futile – and, in any case, leave to amend has already been denied.

Conclusion

Defendant’s demurrer is SUSTAINED in its entirety WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND. On this basis, Defendant’s motion to strike is DENIED as moot.

The Court dismisses the First Amended Complaint with prejudice this date.

Case Number: 25STCV19712    Hearing Date: August 7, 2026    Dept: 224

Akopyan v. County of Los Angeles

TENTATIVE RULING

The motion is GRANTED.

This action is stayed for six months from the date of this order. A status conference is set for March 2, 2027 at 8:30 a.m. in Department 224, at which time the parties shall be prepared to report on the status of the writ proceeding and to address whether the stay should be continued, modified, or lifted.

The parties shall file a joint status report no later than five court days before the conference.

ANALYSIS

Introduction

On July 2, 2025, Plaintiff filed the Complaint in this action, alleging a single cause of action for retaliation in violation of Labor Code section 1102.5.

On September 5, 2025, Plaintiff filed a Verified Petition for Writ of Mandate (Case No. 25STCP03487.

On February 13, 2026, Defendant County of Los Angeles filed the instant Motion to Stay Civil Proceedings Pending the Exhaustion of Administrative Remedies.

On July 28, 2026, Plaintiff filed an Opposition.

On July 30, 2026, Defendant filed a Reply.

Legal Standard

Any party may file a motion for an order under Code of Civil Procedure section 404.5 staying the proceedings in any action being considered for, or affecting an action being considered for, coordination, or the court may stay the proceedings on its own motion. The motion for a stay may be included with a petition for coordination or may be served and submitted to the Chair of the Judicial Council and the coordination motion judge by any party at any time prior to the determination of the petition. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.515, subd. (a).)

Code of Civil Procedure section 404.5 provides that, “[p]ending any determination of whether coordination is appropriate, the judge making that determination may stay any action being considered for, or affecting an action being considered for, coordination.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 404.5.)

“Trial courts generally have the inherent power to stay proceedings in the interests of justice and to promote judicial efficiency.” (Freiberg v. City of Mission Viejo (1995) 33 Cal.App.4th 1484, 1489.)

Discussion

As an initial matter, Defendant brings the motion under Code of Civil Procedure section 404.5. However, that statute does not apply here. No petition for coordination under section 404 has been submitted, no coordination motion judge has been assigned, and this action is not "being considered for" coordination within the meaning of the statute. Section 404.5 therefore furnishes no authority for the relief requested.

The exhaustion of remedies doctrine – for which the motion is expressly titled – likewise does not authorize the requested stay. Exhaustion is a jurisdictional prerequisite to suit, not a case-management device. Where the doctrine applies and administrative remedies have not been exhausted, the court is without jurisdiction to entertain the action. (Abelleira v. District Court of Appeal (1941) 17 Cal.2d 280, 293; accord Campbell v. Regents of University of California (2005) 35 Cal.4th 311, 321 [exhaustion is "a jurisdictional prerequisite to resort to the courts"].) The doctrine thus points to dismissal, not delay. Exhaustion does not support a temporary stay in this circumstance.

Because neither section 404.5 nor the exhaustion doctrine authorizes the requested stay, the sole basis on which the Court may grant it is the Court's inherent power to stay proceedings in the interests of justice and to promote judicial efficiency. (Freiberg v. City of Mission Viejo (1995) 33 Cal.App.4th 1484, 1489.) The Court considers the motion pursuant to its inherent authority.

First, the writ proceeding and this action arise from a single underlying event –Plaintiff's July 1, 2024 reassignment – and turn on substantially overlapping factual questions concerning the propriety of that reassignment. Allowing both matters to proceed in parallel would require duplicative discovery on the same facts and risk inconsistent determinations on the core question of whether the reassignment was a legitimate exercise of the County's authority. A stay conserves judicial and party resources and avoids that risk.

Second, the writ proceeding is procedurally further along. A trial setting conference is set there for March 2, 2026, and the related petitions are the subject of consolidation discussions before the court handling those matters. By contrast, this action has no trial date and no discovery has commenced. Resolution of the writ may narrow or, depending on its outcome, dispose of issues in this action, such that awaiting that resolution promotes orderly and efficient adjudication.

Third, the prejudice to Plaintiff from a temporary stay is minimal. The evidence and witnesses relevant to the reassignment are common to both proceedings, so the stay creates little risk of lost evidence or faded recollection. Because this action is in its earliest stages, a six-month stay imposes limited delay, and Plaintiff retains the ability to prosecute his retaliation claim after the stay is lifted. Plaintiff is himself actively litigating the related writ.

The Court notes that the Commission denied a hearing on timeliness rather than reaching the merits of the reassignment, so the extent to which the writ proceeding will produce a merits determination with preclusive effect on this action cannot be determined at this stage. That contingency does not defeat the stay, as the overlap in facts, the advanced position of the writ, and the conservation of resources independently support a temporary stay.

Conclusion

The motion is GRANTED.

This action is stayed for six months from the date of this order. A status conference is set for March 2, 2027 at 8:30 a.m. in Department 224, at which time the parties shall be prepared to report on the status of the writ proceeding and to address whether the stay should be continued, modified, or lifted.

The parties shall file a joint status report no later than five court days before the conference.

TENTATIVE RULING

The Court also sets an Order to Show Cause Re Sanctions Against Plaintiff’s Counsel in the Amount of $250 Pursuant to CRC 3.110(g)-(i) for Failure to Timely Obtain Entry of a Default Judgment CRC 3.110(g)-(i) for the same day and time. ANALYSIS

FACTS OF THE CASE: Plaintiff Flexi-Van Leasing, LLC seeks default judgment against Defendant Nine Trans, Inc., a California corporation, in the total amount of $154,127.13, consisting of $142,325.41 in principal, $10,021.27 in interest, $580.45 in costs, and $1,200.00 in attorney’s fees. The Complaint asserts common counts for an agreed balance due for goods and/or services, money due on an open book account, and money due on a written account stated.

PROOF OF SERVICE/DEFAULT: Default previously entered on 3/6/2026.

PRINCIPAL REQUESTED: $ 142,325.41

INTEREST: $ 10,021.27

RECOVERABLE COSTS: $ 580.45

ATTORNEY’S FEES: $ 1,200.00

TOTAL: $ 154,127.13

DISCUSSION

Entry of default is not void as the plaintiff did not file an amended complaint after default was entered, and the defendant did not appear before default was entered.

There is no pending motion to vacate default.

Plaintiff has dismissed DOES 1-10, all parties against whom a default judgment is not being sought.

The JC Form CIV-100 was used to request court judgment, item 2 is completed, and the declaration of nonmilitary status requirement is fulfilled because Defendant is a business entity.

A memorandum of costs is verified on the CIV-100 form.

Plaintiff submitted a proposed judgment on a JUD-100 form.

The principal amount requested in the JUD-100 does not exceed the amount demanded in the Complaint.

The 585 declaration of Steve Thomason was submitted in support of default judgment. The ledger matches the requested amount ($149,267.41 in charges minus a $6,942.00 credit equals the $142,325.41 claimed), and every line item on the ledger is backed by an attached invoice.

The Complaint appears to state valid common-count claims. Plaintiff alleges that Defendant agreed to pay $142,325.41 for goods and/or services rendered, that no part of the balance was paid after demand, and that the same amount is due on an open book account.

The Complaint requests interest at the rate of 10 percent per annum from and after June 22, 2025, and a reasonable attorney’s fee under Civil Code section 1717.5.

The attorney's fee request of $1,200.00 is correctly calculated under Civil Code section 1717.5 for a non-consumer open book account. Section 1717.5(a)(2) caps fees for a non-consumer book account at the lesser of $1,600 or 25% of the principal ($35,581.35.) The LASC Local Rule 3.214 schedule amount here would be $3,313.25 ($2,890 plus 1% of the excess over $100,000). Plaintiff's $1,200.00 request is below both statutory ceilings.

PROBLEMS:

There is a discrepancy in the interest calculation. The interest computation required by CRC 3.1800(a)(3) is provided and mathematically correct. 257 days from 6/22/2025 to 3/6/2026 at approximately $38.99 per diem (10% per annum on $142,325.41) equals $10,021.27. The 10% rate is proper under Civil Code section 3289(b). However, $3,420.81 of the $142,325.41 principal consists of Plaintiff's own monthly late-penalty "Interest Charge" invoices (“INT2502,” “INT2503,” and “INT2504,” totaling $3,420.81.) The supporting invoices describe these amounts as “Interest Charges” or “Late Penalty Charges” calculated as a percentage of overdue invoices. Therefore, the non-interest charges only, after application of the $6,942.00 credit, should total $138,904.60 rather than $142,325.41. Plaintiff calculated an additional 10 percent prejudgment interest on the entire $142,325.41 number, which includes the $3,420.81 in previously accrued interest charges. Awarding 10 percent prejudgment interest on the entire $142,325.41 compounds interest on interest, which the prove-up guidelines direct against.

Plaintiff’s declarations concerning the original documents are contradictory. The 585 declaration states that the originals “have become lost and/or unavailable.” However, the separately filed “Declaration Re: Lost Documents” states that “[t]he originals of these documents are in Plaintiff’s possession.” Plaintiff should clarify whether they have the originals.

The attorney's fee declaration’s date line is left blank. The interest declaration’s date line is also left blank. The e-filing of costs declaration’s date line is also left blank. LASC Local Rule 3.201 requires declarations supporting a default judgment to comply with Code of Civil Procedure section 2015.5, which requires a declaration to state its date of execution to be valid.

DISPOSITION:

The OSC is CONTINUED to December 8, 2026 at 9:00 a.m. for Plaintiff to remedy the deficiencies. Plaintiff shall submit its updated default judgment package 10 days before the hearing.

Case Number: 25STCV33112    Hearing Date: August 7, 2026    Dept: 224

Luis Valdez v. Los Angeles County MTA et al.,

TENTATIVE RULING

Defendant Los Angeles County Metropolitan Transportation Authority’s demurrer to Plaintiff’s fourth cause of action is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

Plaintiff is ordered to file a first amended complaint within 30 days or stand on his pleading subject to this Order.

The Case Management Conference is continued to December 8, 2026 at 8:30 a.m.

Defendant to give notice.

ANALYSIS

Background

Plaintiff filed this motor vehicle negligence action on November 12, 2025, arising from an alleged car accident caused by an employee of the Los Angeles Metropolitan Transportation Authority (LACMTA) on November 12, 2023. Plaintiff names the driver, Luis Eduardo Pereza Orduna, and LACMTA as defendants.

LACMTA demurs to Plaintiff’s fourth cause of action for negligent hiring, supervision, or retention on the ground that LACMTA, a governmental entity, may not be held directly liable for negligence. Plaintiff opposes.

Legal Standard

Where pleadings are defective, a party may raise the defect by way of a demurrer. (Coyne v. Krempels (1950) 36 Cal.2d 257, 262.) A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint alleges facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. (Cal. Code Civ. Proc. § 430.10; Young v. Gannon (2002) 97 Cal.App.4th 209, 220.) When considering a demurrer, a court reads the allegations stated in the challenged pleading liberally and in context, and “treat[s] the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Serrano v. Priest (1971) 5 Cal.3d 584, 591.) Put differently: for purposes of demurrer, the court treats all facts alleged – but only the facts alleged – in the complaint as true. (Picton v. Anderson Union High School District (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 726, 732.) “The only issue involved in a demurrer hearing is whether the complaint, as it stands, unconnected with extraneous matters, states a cause of action.” (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)

Discussion

As a general rule, “[a] public entity is not liable for an injury, whether such injury arises out of an act or omission of the public entity or a public employee or any other person.” (Gov. Code, § 815, subd. (a).) Notwithstanding section 815, Government Code section 815.2 permits imposition of vicarious liability for torts committed by public employees in the scope of their employment: “[a] public entity is liable for injury proximately caused by an act or omission of an employee of the public entity within the scope of his employment if the act or omission would, apart from this section, have given rise to a cause of action against that employee or his personal representative.” (Gov. Code, § 815.2, subd. (a); see also Peter W. v. San Francisco Unified Sch. Dist. (1976) 60 Cal.App.3d 814, 819.) To impose liability on a government entity for negligence, a plaintiff must identify either a statute permitting liability on particular facts, or a basis for vicarious liability through an employee. (See Susman v. City of Los Angeles (1969) 269 Cal.App.2d 803, 808-809 [“[i]n California all government tort liability is now based on statute”].)

LACMTA argues that unlike Plaintiff’s claim for simple negligence, which flows to LACMTA vicariously through its employee Orduna, Plaintiff’s claim for negligent hiring seeks to impose liability directly on LACMTA in violation of principles of sovereign immunity.

Plaintiff does not respond to LACMTA’s argument in his opposition. He argues that “employers can be liable to third parties” for negligent hiring and “Plaintiff has alleged the required elements” of such a claim. (Opp., 4:20-21, 5:3-4). Whether or not Plaintiff is correct in the abstract about the sufficiency of his complaint, he does not respond to LACMTA’s argument that it is a public entity, subject to sovereign immunity rules.

If Plaintiff intends to state a claim for negligence – including negligent hiring – directly against LACMTA, a public entity, he must identify some basis for doing so notwithstanding Government Code section 815. He has not done so.

The demurrer is sustained. Because Plaintiff has yet to amend his pleading in response to demurrer, the Court grants him an opportunity to amend.

Conclusion

Defendant Los Angeles County Metropolitan Transportation Authority’s demurrer to Plaintiff’s fourth cause of action is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

Plaintiff is ordered to file a first amended complaint within 30 days or stand on his pleading subject to this Order. The Case Management Conference is continued to December 8, 2026 at 8:30 a.m.

Case Number: 25STCV36854    Hearing Date: August 7, 2026    Dept: 224

Wenting Rohwer v. Labor Law PC

TENTATIVE RULING

Defendant Noel Harlow's motion to quash service of summons is DENIED. Plaintiff's request for sanctions under Code of Civil Procedure section 128.7 is DENIED. Defendant Harlow shall file a responsive pleading within the time provided by Code of Civil Procedure section 418.10, subdivision (b).

ANALYSIS

Introduction

On December 18, 2025, Plaintiff Wenting Rohwer filed this legal malpractice action against her former counsel, Defendants Labor Law PC and Noël Harlow, arising out of their prior representation of Plaintiff.

On March 25, 2026, Defendant Harlow filed the instant Motion to Quash Service of Summons.

On April 20, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition.

Legal Standard

“A defendant . . . may serve and file a notice of motion for one or more of the following purposes: (1) To quash service of summons on the ground of lack of jurisdiction of the court over him or her . . . .” (Code Civ. Proc., § 418.10, subd. (a).) “[Clompliance with the statutory procedures for service of process is essential to establish personal jurisdiction. [Citation.]” (Dill v. Berquist Construction Co. (1994) 24 Cal.App.4th 1426, 1444.) “[The filing of a proof of service creates a rebuttable presumption that the service was proper” but only if it “complies with the statutory requirements regarding such proofs.” (Id. at pp. 1441-1442.) When a defendant moves to quash service of the summons and complaint, the plaintiff has “the burden of proving the facts that did give the court jurisdiction, that is the facts requisite to an effective service.” (Coulston v. Cooper (1966) 245 Cal.App.2d 866, 868.)

Motion to Quash

Reasonable Diligence

Harlow frames her motion under Code of Civil Procedure section 415.20, subdivision (a). That subdivision, which authorizes office substituted service without a showing of diligence, applies only to the entities and persons specified in sections 416.10, 416.20, 416.30, 416.40, and 416.50. Because Harlow is a natural person sued as an individual defendant, she falls within section 416.90, and the operative provision is section 415.20, subdivision (b). That subdivision permits substituted service, where the summons cannot with reasonable diligence be personally delivered, by leaving copies at the person's "dwelling house, usual place of abode, usual place of business, or usual mailing address," in the presence of a competent member of the household or a person apparently in charge of the office or place of business, at least 18 years of age and informed of the contents, followed by mailing. The Court analyzes the service under subdivision (b).

"[T]wo or three attempts at personal service at a proper place should fully satisfy the requirement of reasonable diligence and allow substituted service to be made." (Espindola v. Nunez (1988) 199 Cal.App.3d 1389, 1392, quoted in Bein v. Brechtel-Jochim Group, Inc. (1992) 6 Cal.App.4th 1387, 1391–1392; see American Express Centurion Bank v. Zara (2011) 199 Cal.App.4th 383, 389; Board of Trustees of the Leland Stanford Junior University v. Ham (2013) 216

Cal.App.4th 330, 338–341 [five attempts on five different days constituted reasonable diligence under section 415.45].) Here, Plaintiff's server made five attempts, on five different days, at varying times. The contention that plaintiff was required also to attempt service at Harlow's residence is incorrect. Section 415.20, subdivision (b) states the permissible locations in the disjunctive, and a plaintiff who diligently attempts personal service at the defendant's usual place of business may effect substituted service there without also attempting the residence.

Harlow's reliance on the January 22, 2026 "subject moved" notation is likewise unpersuasive. That single, unattributed statement is contradicted by the three other substantive entries in plaintiff's own diligence declaration – January 21, February 2, and February 17 – each of which reflects that "subject resides" at the address. It is further contradicted by Harlow's own records, discussed below.

Finally, the assertion that the diligence declaration fails to distinguish between the two defendants by referring to "the subject" is immaterial. The Proof of Service of Summons expressly identifies the party served as "Noel Harlow" and checks the box for service "as an individual defendant."

Usual Place of Business

The premise that the Santa Monica office is not Harlow’s place of business is contradicted by her own evidence. Here, Harlow's active California State Bar membership record lists 100 Wilshire Boulevard, Suite 700 as her address. Her email signature lists the same address. In fact, the caption of this very motion identifies 100 Wilshire Boulevard, Suite 700 as the business address of "Attorneys for Defendant … Noel Harlow." (Opp., Exh. B.) Harlow's self-serving declaration that the address "is not my usual office location" is insufficient to rebut the evidence. (Harlow Decl., ¶ 2.) On this record, plaintiff has established by a preponderance of the evidence that 100 Wilshire Boulevard, Suite 700 is Harlow's "usual place of business" within the meaning of section 415.20, subdivision (b). Harlow's conclusory declaration, contradicted by her own public records and filings, does not rebut the presumption of proper service arising from the Proof of Service. (Evidence Code § 647.)

Person Apparently in Charge

Finally, Harlow's contention that the individual served was not "apparently in charge" misapprehends the governing standard. Substituted service does not require that the recipient be authorized to accept service, hold a supervisory position, or bear any particular relationship to the individual defendant. It requires only that the copies be left with a person apparently in charge of the office or place of business, who is informed of the general nature of the papers. (Code Civ. Proc., § 415.20, subd. (b); see Bein v. Brechtel-Jochim Group, Inc. (1992) 6 Cal.App.4th 1387, 1393 [gate guard was person "apparently in charge"].) The "apparently in charge" inquiry is assessed from the perspective of the process server. A front-desk or reception employee staffing the office where the defendant maintains her law practice ordinarily qualifies. The Proof of Service reflects that the documents were left with such an employee at Harlow's business address, that the individual's identity was confirmed, and that the individual was informed of the nature of the papers. (Opp., Exh. C.)

Sanctions

Plaintiff requests $1,000 in sanctions under Code of Civil Procedure section 128.7, and alternatively asks the Court to issue an order to show cause.

A party seeking sanctions under section 128.7 must proceed by a separate, noticed motion and must comply with the statute's 21-day safe-harbor requirement, serving the motion on the opposing party and affording 21 days to withdraw or correct the challenged paper before filing the motion with the Court. (Code Civ. Proc., § 128.7, subd. (c)(1).) Plaintiff's request was embedded in her opposition brief rather than presented as a separate motion satisfying the safe-harbor procedure, and it is therefore procedurally improper.

The request is denied.

Conclusion
