BENJAMIN MUESSIG,

Plaintiff,

vs.

GENERAL MOTORS, LLC,

Defendant.

Case No.: 

23STCV26600

Hearing Date: 

August 5, 2026

ORDER DENYING MOTION FOR ATTORNEY FEES

 

I.         BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Benjamin Muessig (“Muessig”) entered into a warranty contract with Defendant General Motors, LLC (“GM”) regarding a 2020 Chevrolet Bolt EV (“subject vehicle”). The subject vehicle allegedly has defects and nonconformities making the car effectively worthless. GM allegedly failed to reasonably repair or reimburse Muessig for the vehicle. Muessig sued GM for four causes of action, including breach of the implied warranty.

On October 10, 2025, the Court entered dismissal as to the entire action, pursuant to the parties’ settlement agreement.

On April 1, 2026, Muessig filed the instant motion for attorney fees. GM filed an opposition. Muessig filed a reply brief.

 

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

Attorneys’ fees are allowed as costs when authorized by contract, statute, or law. (Code of Civil Procedure § 1033.5, subd. (a)(10)(B).) Civil Code section 1717 states that “[i]n any action on a contract, where the contract specifically provides that attorney’s fees and costs, which are incurred to enforce that contract, shall be awarded either to one of the parties or to the prevailing party, then the party who is determined to be the party prevailing on the contract, whether he or she is the party specified in the contract or not, shall be entitled to reasonable attorney’s fees in addition to other costs.  (Civ. Code, § 1717, subd. (a).)  The term “on a contract” is liberally construed to include any action that “involves” a contact.  (Blickman Turkus, LP v. MF Downtown Sunnyvale, LLC (2008) 162 Cal.App.4th 858, 894.)  “To determine whether an action is on the contract, we look to the complaint and focus on the basis of the cause of action.”  (Brown Bark III, L.P. v. Haver (2013) 219 Cal.App.4th 809, 821.)  “[F]ees are properly awarded under section 1717 ‘to the extent that the action in fact is an action to enforce—or avoid enforcement of—the specific contract.’”  (Turner v. Schultz (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 974, 980.)  The prevailing party on the contract is “the party who recovered greater relief in the action on the contract.”  (Civ. Code, § 1717, subd. (b)(1).) 

 The attorney bears the burden of proof as to “reasonableness” of any fee claim. (§ 1033.5(c)(5).) This burden requires competent evidence as to the nature and value of the services rendered. (Martino v. Denevi (1986) 182 Cal.App.3d 553, 559.) “Testimony of an attorney as to the number of hours worked on a particular case is sufficient evidence to support an award of attorney fees, even in the absence of detailed time records.” (Ibid.)

A party's verified billing invoices are prima facie evidence that the costs, expenses, and services listed were necessarily incurred. (See Hadley v. Krepel (1985) 167 Cal.App.3d 677, 682.) “In challenging attorney fees as excessive because too many hours of work are claimed, it is the burden of the challenging party to point to the specific items challenged, with a sufficient argument and citations to the evidence. General arguments that fees claimed are excessive, duplicative, or unrelated to not suffice.” (Lunada Biomedical v. Nunez (2014) 230 Cal.App.4th 459, 488, quoting Premier Med. Mgmt. Sys., Inc. v. California Ins. Guarantee Ass’n (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 550, 564.) The Court has discretion to reduce fees that result from inefficient or duplicative use of time. (Horsford v. Bd. of Trustees of California State Univ. (2005) 132 Cal. App. 4th 359, 395.)

In determining a reasonable attorney fee, the trial court begins with the lodestar, i.e., the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate. (Warren v. Kia Motors America, Inc. (2018) 30 Cal.App.5th 24, 36.) The lodestar may then be adjusted based on factors specific to the case in order to fix the fee at the fair market value of the legal services provided. (Ibid.) These facts include (1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, (4) the contingent nature of the fee award. (Ibid.)

 

IV.       DISCUSSION

The Court denies Muessig’s motion for attorney fees, as it is untimely.

Pursuant to California Rules of Court rule 3.1702, subdivision (a),[1] a notice of motion for attorney’s fees must be served and filed within the time for filing a notice of appeal under rule 8.104. Rule 8.104 outlines notice must be filed on or before the earliest of the following: 1) 60 days after the superior court clerk serves on the party filing the notice of appeal a document entitled “Notice of Entry” of judgment or a filed-endorsed copy of the judgment, showing the date either was served; 2) 60 days after the party filing the notice of appeal serves or is served by a party with a document entitled "Notice of Entry" of judgment or a filed-endorsed copy of the judgment, accompanied by proof of service; or 3) 180 days after entry of judgment. (Rule 8.104, subd. (a)(1).)

For purposes of the California Rules of Court, an entry of judgment equates to a dismissal. (Sanabria v. Embrey (2001) 92 Cal.App.4th 424, 427.) Therefore, any notice of dismissal or filed-endorsed copy of the judgment, serve on or by Muessig, triggers the running of the time period to file.

The Court’s record reflects Muessig’s counsel was served with a filed-endorsed copy of the judgment by a superior court clerk on October 10, 2025. (See 10/10/2025 Order- Dismissal.) Therefore, the deadline to file this motion was, at the latest, 60 days after October 10, 2025, or December 9, 2025. As this motion was not filed until April 1, 2026, this motion is untimely. Therefore, the Court denies the motion.

V.        CONCLUSION

Plaintiff Benjamin Muessig’s motion for attorney fees is DENIED.

GM is ordered to give notice.

BNB WHOLESALE AND DISTRIBUTION, INC.,

Plaintiff,

vs.

OKRID PLAZA, LLC,

Defendant.

Case No.:

24STCV28160

Hearing Date:

August 5, 2026

ORDER DENYING MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT

 

I.         BACKGROUND

Defendant Orkid Plaza, LLC (“Orkid”) allegedly improperly relocated trash receptacles to an unauthorized area behind Orkid’s property (the “subject property”), causing a fire hazard. One or more third-party individuals allegedly started a fire near the area, which eventually spread to the portion of the subject property leased by Plaintiff BNB Wholesale and Distribution, Inc. (“BNB”), destroying BNB’s inventory. BNB sued Orkid for negligence, premises liability, and indemnification.

On April 14, 2026, Orkid filed the instant motion for summary judgment. BNB filed an opposition.

II.        OBJECTIONS

The Court rules on BNB’s objections as follows:

1.     Snyder Decl., ¶¶ 2 and 3 – OVERRULED.

2.     Separate Statement Fact No. 1 and Synder Decl. ¶ 4, Exhibit A – SUSTAINED as to lack of authentication and lack of personal knowledge.

3.     Separate Statement Fact Nos. 2-5 and Snyder Decl., ¶ 5, Exhibit B – SUSTAINED as to hearsay, lack of personal knowledge, and improper opinion

4.     Separate Statement Fact No. 6 and Synder Decl. ¶ 6, Exhibit C – SUSTAINED as to lack of authentication and lack of personal knowledge.

III.      LEGAL STANDARD

A party may move for summary judgment “if it is contended that the action has no merit or that there is no defense to the action or proceeding.”  (Code Civ. Proc. § 437c, subd. (a).[1]) “[I]f all the evidence submitted, and all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law,” the moving party will be entitled to summary judgment. (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.) 

The moving party bears an initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing of the nonexistence of any triable issue of material fact, and if he does so, the burden shifts to the opposing party to make a prima facie showing of the existence of a triable issue of material fact. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850, citing to § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence.  (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

Neither a moving nor responding party may rely on the mere allegations or denials of its pleadings. A moving party must submit specific admissible evidence showing that the responding party cannot establish at least one element of his, her or its cause of action or defense. The responding party, to defeat the motion, must submit specific admissible evidence showing that a triable issue of material fact does exist as to that element of the cause of action or defense. (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)

The “Golden Rule” on a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication is that “if [a fact] is not set forth in the separate statement, it does not exist.” (Zimmerman, Rosenfeld, Gersh & Leeds LLP v. Larson (2005) 131 Cal.App.4th 1466, 1477, citing United Community Church v. Garcin (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 327, 337.).

 

IV.       DISCUSSION

Orkid requests the Court enter summary judgment as BNB cannot establish evidence demonstrating a duty of care. “The elements of a negligence claim and a premises liability claim are the same: a legal duty of care, breach of that duty, and proximate cause resulting in injury.” (Kesner v. Superior Court (2016) 1 Cal.5th 1132, 1159.)

The Court finds Orkid has not provided sufficient evidence or analysis to support Orkid’s motion. First, the Court notes, all of Orkid’s separate statement and exhibits are subject to sustained objections on multiple grounds, including hearsay, lack of authentication, and lack of personal knowledge. Orkid has not presented any admissible evidence, as is required for the Court to grant summary judgment. (§ 437c, subd. (d).) Additionally, while Orkid cited to potentially relevant case law, Orkid provided no analysis of that case law to establish any connection to this case.

The party moving for summary judgment bears the burden of establishing there is no dispute of material fact, entitling the moving party to judgment as a matter of law. As Orkid has not provided any evidence or analysis that would establish Orkid cannot be found liable for causes of action for negligence or premises liability, the Court must deny summary judgment.

Additionally, the Court notes, even if Orkid did prove BNB cannot establish Orkid’s duty as to the negligence and premises liability claims, the Court would still be obligated to deny the motion. Orkid’s motion solely requests summary judgment; it does not request summary adjudication, in the alternative. Summary judgment may only be granted if it establishes there is no triable issue of material fact and moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law as to every cause of action. (§ 437c, subd. (c).) Orkid did not address BNB’s cause of action for indemnity in any manner. Therefore, the Court could not grant summary judgment as to the entire action.

The Court denies summary judgment.

 

V.        CONCLUSION

Defendant Orkid Plaza, LLC’s motion for summary judgment is DENIED.

BNB is to give notice.

Case Number: 25STCV07964    Hearing Date: August 5, 2026    Dept: 730

 

Superior Court of California

County of Los Angeles

Department 730

 

JOSUA CAMARENA CRUZ,

Plaintiff,

vs.

GENERAL MOTORS, LLC, et al.

Defendants

Case No.:

25STCV07964

Hearing Date:

August 5, 2026

ORDER GRANTING MOTION TO COMPEL COMPLIANCE WITH SECTION 871.26.

 

I.         BACKGROUND

Plaintiff Josua Camarena Cruz (“Cruz”) entered into a warranty contract with Defendant General Motors, LLC (“GM”) regarding a 2022 GM Sierra 1500 (“subject vehicle”). The subject vehicle allegedly has defects and nonconformities making the car effectively worthless. GM allegedly failed to replace the vehicle or reimburse Cruz. Cruz sued GM for five causes of action, including violation of the Song-Beverly act.

On June 30, 2026, GM filed the instant motion to compel compliance with Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26, subdivision (c)(1).[1] Cruz filed an opposition. GM filed a reply.

 

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

Effective January 1, 2025, in any action seeking restitution or replacement of a motor vehicle under § 871.20, the parties must follow these rules: within 120 days after an answer or other responsive pleading is filed, each party shall have the right to conduct initial depositions of the plaintiff. (§ 871.26, subdivision (c)(1).) The Court must impose sanctions for failure to comply with section 871.26, absent good cause. (§ 871.26, subd. (j).)

The California Legislature enacted section 871.20, et seq., which added requirements for certain lemon law actions. (Code Civ. Proc. § 871.20, subd. (a) [“Notwithstanding any other law, this chapter applies to an action, brought against a manufacturer who has elected under Section 871.29 to proceed under this chapter, seeking restitution or replacement of a motor vehicle pursuant to subdivision (b) or (d) of Section 1793.2, Section 1793.22, or Section 1794 of the Civil Code, or for civil penalties pursuant to subdivision (c) of Section 1794 of the Civil Code, where the request for restitution or replacement is based on noncompliance with the applicable express warranty”]; subd. (b) [“This chapter does not apply to service contract claims under Section 1794 of the Civil Code or any action seeking remedies that are not restitution or replacement of a motor vehicle”].) 

           

III.      DISCUSSION

A.        Compel Cruz's Deposition

GM requests the Court compel Cruz to appear for initial deposition, pursuant to requirements under section 871.26, subdivision (c)(1). Section 871.26, subdivision (c)(1), entitles GM to conduct an initial deposition of Cruz within 120 days after filing any responsive pleading. GM filed a demurrer on July 8, 2025. Therefore, GM was entitled to request and conduct Cruz’s initial deposition at any point prior to November 5, 2025.

On July 8, 2025, GM served a notice of deposition on Cruz for July 28, 2025. (Mot., Stark Decl., ¶ 5, Ex. B.) The notice was served alongside an e-mail requesting counsel contact GM’s counsel’s office if the noticed date did not work for Cruz or Cruz’s counsel. (Id. at ¶¶ 4-5, Ex. A.) On July 24, 2025, Cruz’s counsel objected to the notice of deposition due to unavailability and agreed to meet and confer regarding rescheduling the deposition. (Id. at ¶ 6, Ex. C.)

On October 15, 2025, counsel for GM contacted Cruz’s counsel requesting dates for Cruz’s deposition. (Id. at ¶ 7, Ex. D.) Cruz’s counsel did not respond. (Ibid.) On February 10, 2026, Cruz offered April 22, 2026, as an available date; GM confirmed this date and served an amended notice of deposition. (Id. at ¶¶ 8-9, Ex. E-F.) On April 17, 2026, Cruz objected on the basis of unavailability, stating Cruz was no longer available. (Id. at ¶ 11, Ex. H.) On April 21, 2026, Cruz’s counsel stated the objected was sent in error and agreed to attend to the deposition. (Id. at ¶ 12, Ex. I.) On April 22, 2026, 7 minutes before the deposition was set to occur, Cruz’s counsel e-mailed GM to inform them counsel was no longer available. (Ibid.) The deposition did not go forward and has yet to occur.

Cruz offers no explanation for the failure to proactively offer dates following the initial objection or to reply to the October e-mail requesting dates. Cruz also offers no explanation for the last-minute cancelation of the April deposition beyond stating the attorney assigned to take the deposition was no longer available. (Ibid.) After this, counsel lost contact with Cruz; they have yet to reestablish communication. (Opp., Smith Decl. ¶¶ 4-5.)

GM made diligent attempts to complete Cruz’s initial deposition during the 120 day period by serving notice of deposition and attempting to engage in meet and confer efforts. Cruz failed to make diligent efforts to comply with Section 871.26 requirements and produce Cruz for deposition. As the deposition has still yet to occur, despite GM’s efforts to exercise their discovery rights, the Court grants the motion to compel Cruz’s initial deposition.

B.        Sanctions

GM requests the Court impose sanctions of $1,500.00 against Cruz’s counsel, pursuant to section 871.26, subdivision (j)(2). Section 871.26, subdivision (j)(2), provides unless the party failing to comply shows good cause, the court shall impose $1,500.00 in sanctions upon plaintiff’s counsel for failure to comply with deposition requirements.

Cruz’s counsel argues Cruz’s counsel should not be punished with sanctions as counsel at all times acted diligently and in good faith. Counsel argues the initial objection was made pursuant to legitimate scheduling needs and “proactively offered an available date range.” (Opp., pg. 6 ¶¶ 6-7.) The Court finds no evidence to support Cruz’s counsel offered any dates beyond April 22, 2026, and notes that date did not occur due to counsel’s own failure to appear. No explanation is provided as to why Cruz’s counsel failed to provide any dates until months after the initial time period, despite GM’s attempts to meet and confer.

The Court does not find Cruz’s counsel shows good cause for failure to comply. Accordingly, the Court imposes sanctions of $1,500.00 on Cruz’s counsel.

 

IV.       CONCLUSION

Defendant General Motors, LLC’s motion to compel compliance with section 871.26 is GRANTED. Cruz is ordered to appear for initial deposition within 10 days of the hearing on this motion.

Defendant General Motors, LLC’s request for sanctions is GRANTED. Cruz's counsel is ordered to pay GM $1,500.00 in sanctions within 15 business days.

GM is ordered to give notice.

Case Number: 26STCV02831    Hearing Date: August 5, 2026    Dept: 730

 

Superior Court of California

County of Los Angeles

Department 730

 

RYAN FOLEY, et al.,

Plaintiff,

vs.

PABLO M. SOTO, et al.,

Defendants.

Case No.:

26STCV02831

Hearing Date:

August 5, 2026

ORDER SUSTAINING DEMURRER TO FIRST AMENDED CROSS-COMPLAINT

 

I.         BACKGROUND

Plaintiffs Ryan Foley (“Foley”), Thea Ceclia Martin (“Martin”), Katherine Joy Robinson (“Robinson”), Gillian Kay Rochkind (“Rochkind”), and Marissa Estela Solorzano (“Solorzano”) worked as hair stylists at Defendant Pablo Soto Hair LLC dba Rock Paper Salon (“RPS”), managed by Defendant Pablo M. Soto (“Soto”). Soto allegedly placed various limitations on Plaintiffs to restrict their financial autonomy and freedom to provide services as preferred, and also required Plaintiffs perform various duties beyond their styling roles. When Foley raised concerns about the working conditions, Soto allegedly terminated his contract. Plaintiffs sued Defendants for fourteen causes of action, including failure to pay wages and unlawful retaliation.

On May 14, 2026, Defendants filed the First Amended Cross-Complaint (“FACC”) against Plaintiffs and Cross-Defendants Ryan Foley LLC (“RF”) and Kat Hairs L.L.C. (“KH”) for seven causes of action, including failure to pay minimum wage. Defendants’ Cross-Complaint alleges Soto performed various duties at the direction and for the benefit of Cross-Defendants without proper compensation.

On June 24, 2026, Cross-Defendants filed the instant demurrer to the second through seventh causes of action to the FACC. Defendants filed an opposition. Cross-Defendants filed a reply.

 

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10[1] states: “The party against whom a complaint or cross-complaint has been filed may object, by demurrer or answer as provided in Section 430.30, to the pleading on any one or more of the following grounds: (a) The court has no jurisdiction of the subject of the cause of action alleged in the pleading; (b) The person who filed the pleading does not have the legal capacity to sue; (c) There is another action pending between the same parties on the same cause of action; (d) There is a defect or misjoinder of parties; (e) The pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action; (f) The pleading is uncertain. As used in this subdivision, “uncertain” includes ambiguous and unintelligible; and (g) In an action founded upon a contract, it cannot be ascertained from the pleading whether the contract is written, is oral, or is implied by conduct.”

As a general matter, in a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice.  (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)  “A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.”  (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.)  As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations.  (Ibid.)  The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action.  (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment.  (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.)  The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully.  (Ibid.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.)  However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.”  (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245).

           

III.      DISCUSSION

Cross-Defendants demur to the second through seventh causes of action for failure to state sufficient facts to establish the causes of action.

A.        Employment Status

Cross-Defendants first demurer to the second through fifth causes of action for failure to pay minimum wage, failure to provide waiting time penalties, and failure to provide accurate and itemized wage statements, as Defendants have not adequately pled facts to allege Cross-Defendants employed Defendants.

Three of the four relevant causes of action are pled under the Labor Code. (See Labor Code §§ 1194, 1197, 203, 218.6., 226, subd. (a).) These three causes of action only allow an employee to recover for alleged wrongdoing by their employer. Therefore, a requisite condition to pleading any causes of action under the relevant Labor Code sections is alleging facts in support of an employment relationship between Cross-Defendants and Defendants.

Pursuant to the Labor Code, an employer is a person or entity which employs any person or persons to perform services for a wage or salary. (Labor Code § 1132.2.) A person employs another through three alternative means: 1) by exerting control over the wages, hours or working conditions of another person; 2) to suffer or permit to work; or 3) to engage another person, creating a common law employment relationship (Martinez v. Combs (2010) 49 Cal.4th 35, 64.) To establish a party ‘suffered’ or ‘permitted’ work, said party must have the power to either cause or prevent another from working under some sort of employment structure. (Futrell v. Payday California, Inc. (2010) 190 Cal.App.4th 1419, 1429.) A common law employment relationship generally evaluates whether the alleged employer had ‘control of details,’ such as tools, place of business, length of time, method of payment, and supervision. (Ibid.)

Here, Defendants have not pled sufficient facts to establish any relationship in which Cross-Defendants employed Defendants. Defendants allege Soto was an employee of Cross-Defendants because he rendered various services to Cross-Defendants in support of Cross-Defendants' businesses, pursuant to Cross-Defendants' directions and requirements. (FACC ¶¶ 18, 27.) However, Defendants do not allege that Soto undertook these tasks for a wage or salary. Absent an agreement for Soto to perform these tasks in return for wage or salary, there are insufficient facts to establish any sort of employment relationship between Soto and Cross-Defendants. The mere allegation someone undertook tasks for another’s benefit or at another’s direction does not establish an employment relationship under any legal understanding.

Defendants’ cause of action for failure to pay minimum wage pursuant to Los Angeles Municipal Code (“LAMC”) similarly is subject to demurrer. As outlined in LAMC section 187.01, the code only applies to employees and employers, as defined in the Labor Code. As Defendants have insufficiently pled Defendants status as employees of Cross-Defendants as employers, Defendants have also failed to adequately plead this cause of action.

Finally, the seventh cause of action for violations of Business and Professions Code section 17200 also fails, as it is entirely based upon alleged unfair business practices stemming from the alleged employment relationship. “By the conduct alleged above, CROSS-DEFENDANTS have engaged and continue to engage in a business practice that violates California law, including but not limited to California Labor Code sections 203, 226, and 1197...[furthermore] CROSS-DEFENDANTS’ failure to produce wage statements deceives and defrauds employees regarding how and what they will be paid...[which] is, by definition, an unfair business practice.” (FACC ¶¶ 75-77.) The complaint contains no allegations of unfair business practices. As Defendants failed to plead sufficient facts to allege an employment relationship, Defendants have failed to plead sufficient facts to allege this cause of action.

Defendants have failed to plead sufficient facts to allege the second through fifth, as well as seventh, causes of action. Therefore, the Court sustains the demurrer to these causes of action.

B.        Quantum Merit

Cross-Defendants next demur to the sixth cause of action for quantum merit as insufficiently pled. “To recover on a claim for the reasonable value of services under a quantum meruit theory, a plaintiff must establish both that he or she was acting pursuant to either an express or implied request for services from the defendant and that the services rendered were intended to and did benefit the defendant.” (Ochs v. PacifiCare of California (2004) 115 Cal.App.4th 782, 794.) A party need not prove the existence of a contract but must show circumstances establishing an understanding or expectations of both parties that compensation was to be provided. (Huskinson & Brown v. Wolf (2004) 32 Cal.4th 453, 458.)

The FACC does not adequately plead facts establishing an understanding by Cross-Defendants that they were to provide compensation for Defendants’ services. Although Defendants plead “Soto’s services were not provided gratuitously,” and Cross-Defendants knowingly “accepted the benefit of Soto’s services,” no facts allege Cross-Defendants were aware and accepted Soto providing services in return for compensation. (FACC ¶¶ 68-69.) Absent allegations establishing potential understanding, the Court finds this action is insufficiently pled to present a viable cause of action.

The Court sustains the demurrer in full. The Court does not address the issues of the statute of limitations, as the Court sustained the demurrer to those causes of actions on other grounds.

C.        Leave to Amend

The burden is on the pleading party to show in what manner he can amend the complaint, and how that amendment will change the legal effect of the pleading. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349; Hendy v. Losse (1991) 54 Cal.3d 723, 742.) Defendants request leave to amend, in part, on the basis that he can provide additional facts regarding Cross-Defendants' control over Soto and Soto’s rate of pay. As further allegations may be sufficient to cure the defects warranting sustaining of this demurrer, the Court grants leave to amend.

 

IV.       CONCLUSION

Cross-Defendants’ demurrer to the FACC is SUSTAINED, with 30 days leave to amend.

Cross-Defendants are ordered to give notice.

•           If the parties neither submit nor appear at hearing, the Court may take the motion off calendar or adopt the tentative ruling as the order of the Court. After the Court has issued a tentative ruling, the Court may prohibit the withdrawal of the subject motion without leave.
