Case Number: 24STCV11166    Hearing Date: July 29, 2026    Dept: 407

Tentative Ruling

 

Judge Brock T. Hammond, Department 407

 

 

HEARING DATE:     July 29, 2026                          TRIAL DATE:  None

                                                          

CASE:                         Rashel Yadkar v. Kia America, Inc., et al.

 

CASE NO.:                 24STCV11166

 

 

MOTION FOR ATTORNEY’S FEES, COSTS, AND EXPENSES

 

MOVING PARTY:               Plaintiff Rashel Yadkar

 

RESPONDING PARTY:     Defendant Kia America, Inc.

 

I.         BACKGROUND

 

In this lemon law action, Plaintiff Rashel Yadkar (“Plaintiff”) alleges (among other things) that Defendant Kia America, Inc. (“Defendant”) failed to repair his new 2022 Kia Niro vehicle after the vehicle started manifesting defects and nonconformities during the warranty period.

 

On May 3, 2024, Plaintiff filed this action against Defendant and Does 1 through 100, inclusive, asserting causes of action for: (1) breach of implied warranty; (2) breach of express warranty; and (3) violation of the Song Beverly Consumer Warranty Act (“SBA”).

 

On December 23, 2025, Plaintiff filed Notice of Settlement of Entire Case.

 

On May 20, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion for attorney’s fees, costs, and expenses.

 

On July 16, 2026, Defendant filed its opposition.

 

On July 23, 2026, Plaintiff filed his reply.

 

No evidentiary objections or requests for judicial notice were filed in connection with the instant motion.

           

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

 

Under the SBA, “a buyer who ‘prevails in [their] action’ may recover ‘costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution’ of his action under the Act. ([Civ. Code] § 1794, subd. (d).)” (Duff v. Jaguar Land Rover North America, LLC (2022) 74 Cal.App.5th 491, 501.)

 

“Determining a reasonable attorney fee award in Song-Beverly Act cases ‘“ordinarily begins with the ‘lodestar,’ [which is] the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate.:”’ [Citation.]” (Tidrick v. FCA US LLC (2025) 112 Cal.App.5th 1147, 1157 (Tidrick).) “The burden is on the successful party to prove the appropriate market rate to be used in calculating the lodestar. [Citation.] … [T]he moving party may satisfy its burden through its own affidavits, without additional evidence. [Citation.]” (MBNA America Bank, N.A. v. Gorman (2006) 147 Cal.App.4th Supp. 1, 13 (MBNA).)

 

“‘The [lodestar] figure may then be augmented or diminished [through the use of a positive or negative multiplier, respectively] … taking various relevant factors into account, including (1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved and the skill displayed in presenting them; (2) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys; and (3) the contingent nature of the fee award, based on the uncertainty of prevailing on the merits and of establishing eligibility for the award.’ [Citation.]” (Doppes v. Bentley Motors, Inc. (2009) 174 Cal.App.4th 967, 998; Robertson v. Fleetwood Travel Trailers of California, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 785, 822 (Robertson) [“the use of a multiplier is authorized in awarding attorney’s fees under the Song–Beverly Act …”].) When determining the appropriate multiplier, the trial court should not consider the same factors (e.g., contingent nature of the attorney fees) it relied on to determine the lodestar amount. (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1138 (Ketchum).) “[T]he party seeking a fee enhancement bears the burden of proof.” (Ibid.)

 

III.      DISCUSSION

 

Plaintiff moves for an attorney fee award of $31,313.31, consisting of a lodestar of $25,470, costs of $749.31, and a 0.2 multiplier of $5,094. (Notice of Motion, p. 1:21-27.)

 

A.    Lodestar

 

To determine the appropriate lodestar, the SBA “‘“requires the trial court to [1] make an initial determination of the actual time expended; and then [2] …  ascertain whether under all the circumstances of the case the amount of actual time expended, and the monetary charge being made for the time expended are reasonable.” (Tidrick, supra, 112 Cal.App.5th at p. 1158.) “‘“These circumstances may include, but are not limited to, factors such as the complexity of the case and procedural demands, the skill exhibited, and the results achieved.”’” (Ibid.) “‘“If the time expended or the monetary charge being made for the time expended are not reasonable under all the circumstances, then the court must take this into account and award attorney fees in a lesser amount.”’” (Ibid.)

 

 

Here, Plaintiff was represented by The Law Office of Jacob K. Kashani, APC (the “Firm”).

 

Plaintiff’s counsel, Attorney Jacob K. Kashani (“Kashani”), testifies that the Firm spent 35.6 hours at the following billing rates: (1) Kashani spent 33.1 hours at $650 per hour (equals $21,515); (2) someone named “DC” spent 0.5 hours at $600 per hour (equals $300); (3) “RO” spent 1.5 hours at $400 per hour (equals $600); (4) “TT” spent 0.5 hours at $650 per hour (equals $325). (Motion, p. 9:22-24; Kashani Decl., Exh. B – a copy of the Firm’s billing records.)

 

Contrary to Defendant’s arguments, the Court finds Kashani’s hours and billing rates reasonable. ““In challenging attorney fees as excessive because too many hours of work are claimed, it is the burden of the challenging party to point to the specific items challenged, with a sufficient argument and citations to the evidence. General arguments that fees claimed are excessive, duplicative, or unrelated do not suffice.” (Premier Medical Management Systems, Inc. v. California Ins. Guarantee Assn. (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 550, 564.) Here, as Plaintiff argues, Defendant only makes general arguments that the hours are excessive instead of pointing out specific items. That is insufficient. The Court also finds Kashani’s billing rate of $650 per hour reasonable in light of the attorney’s experience. (See Kashani Decl., ¶ 11 [testifying that he is the principal proprietor of the Firm and has exclusively handled SBA cases for a decade]; Rey v. Madera Unified School Dist. (2012) 203 Cal.App.4th 1223, 1240 [“The determination of what constitutes reasonable attorney fees is committed to the discretion of the trial court. [Citation.] The experienced trial judge is the best judge of the value of professional services rendered in his or her court”].)

 

            However, Kashani has not explained who DC, RO, and TT are (e.g., attorneys or paralegals) or their experience so as to justify their billing rates. Therefore, the Court deducts the $1,225 that was charged by those three from the requested lodestar of $25,470.

 

For those reasons, the Court approves a lodestar of $24,245.

 

B.    Multiplier

 

The “lodestar figure may…be adjusted [according to a multiplier enhancement] based on consideration of factors specific to the case, in order to fix the fee at the fair market value for the legal services provided.” (PLCM Group v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095.) Relevant multiplier factors include “(1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, [and] (4) the contingent nature of the fee award.” (Ketchum, supra, 24 Cal.4th at p. 1132.)

 

The Court finds this was a straightforward case that does not justify a multiplier. Therefore, the request for a multiplier is denied.

 

C.    Costs

 

Plaintiff has submitted a Memorandum of Costs seeking to recover costs of $749.31, which consists of filing and motion fees of $703.18 and service of process fees of $41.20. (Motion, Kashani Decl., ¶ 16; Exh. E – a copy of the Memorandum of Costs.) Defendant has not challenged those costs.

 

The Court approves and grants the requested cost amount.  

 

IV.       CONCLUSION

 

The motion for attorney’s fees and costs is GRANTED IN PART and DENIED IN PART as follows. The request for attorney’s fees and costs is GRANTED. The Court awards Plaintiff attorney’s fees of $24,245 and costs of $749.31, for a total award of $24,994.31. The request for a multiplier is DENIED.

 

            Plaintiff to give notice.

CASE NO.:                 24STCV33937

 

 

MOTION TO BE RELIEVED AS COUNSEL

 

 

I.         INTRODUCTION

 

On April 30, 2026, Sharona Eslamboly Hakim, counsel for Plaintiff, Althea Doss, filed this Motion to be Relieved As Counsel. 

 

            The motion is unopposed. 

 

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

 

California Rules of Court, rule 3.1362 (Motion to Be Relieved as Counsel) requires (1) notice of motion and motion to be directed to the client (made on the Notice of Motion and Motion to be Relieved as Counsel—Civil form (MC-051)); (2) a declaration stating in general terms and without compromising the confidentiality of the attorney-client relationship why a motion under Code of Civil Procedure section 284(2) is brought instead of filing a consent under Code of Civil Procedure section 284(1) (made on the Declaration in Support of Attorney's Motion to Be Relieved as Counsel—Civil form (MC-052)); (3) service of the notice of motion and motion and declaration on all other parties who have appeared in the case; and (4) the proposed order relieving counsel (prepared on the Order Granting Attorney's Motion to Be Relieved as Counsel—Civil form (MC-053)).¿¿¿ 

¿¿ 

The court has discretion to allow an attorney to withdraw, and such a motion should be granted provided that there is no prejudice to the client, and it does not disrupt the orderly process of justice.¿ (See Ramirez v. Sturdevant (1994) 21 Cal.App.4th 904, 915.)¿¿¿¿ 

 

III.      DISCUSSION  

Plaintiff’s counsel seeks to be relieved as counsel of record for the following reasons: “There has been an irreparable breakdown of the attorney-client communications such that Plaintiffs [sic] counsel can no longer represent the plaintiff.”  (Form MC-052.) 

Absent a showing of resulting prejudice, an attorney’s request for withdrawal should be granted.¿ (People v. Prince (1968) 268 Cal.App.2d 398, 406.)¿¿¿ 

¿¿¿ 

Upon review, the court finds the Motion complies with California Rules of Court, rule 3.1362. 

 

IV.       CONCLUSION 

 

Accordingly, the unopposed Motion to Be Relieved as Counsel is GRANTED and effective upon the filing of the proof of service of this signed order upon Plaintiff. ¿¿

Case Number: 25STCV07903    Hearing Date: July 29, 2026    Dept: 407

Tentative Ruling

 

Judge Brock T. Hammond, Department 407

 

 

HEARING DATE:     July 29, 2026                          TRIAL DATE:  July19, 2027

                                                          

CASE:                         Stephen M. Villarreal v. American Honda Motor Co., Inc., et al.

 

CASE NO.:                 25STCV07903

 

 

(1)   MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES TO SPECIAL INTERROGATORIES FROM PLAINTIFF STEPHEN VILLARREAL, AND REQUEST FOR MONETARY SANCTIONS

 

(2)   MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES TO FORM INTERROGATORIES FROM PLAINTIFF STEPHEN VILLARREAL, AND REQUEST FOR MONETARY SANCTIONS

 

(3)   MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES TO REQUESTS FOR ADMISSION TO PLAINTIFF AND REQUEST FOR MONETARY SANCTIONS

 

(4)   MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES TO REQUESTS FOR PRODUCTION FROM PLAINTIFF STEPHEN VILLARREAL, AND REQUEST FOR MONETARY SANCTIONS

 

MOVING PARTY:               (1)-(4) Defendant American Honda Motor Co., Inc.

 

RESPONDING PARTY:     (1)-(3) Plaintiff Stephen M. Villarreal

                                                   (4) None

 

I.         BACKGROUND

 

In this lemon law action, Plaintiff Stephen M. Villarreal alleges (among other things) that Defendant American Honda Motor Co., Inc. (“Defendant”) failed to replace or repair his 2019 Honda Civic after the vehicle started manifesting defects and nonconformities during the express warranty period.

 

On March 18, 2025, Plaintiff filed this action against Defendant and Does 1 through 10, inclusive, asserting causes of action for: (1) violation of subdivision (d) of Civil Code section 1793.2 (“Section 1793.2”); (2) violation of subdivision (b) of Section 1793.2; (3) violation of subdivision (a)(3) of Section 1793.2; (4) breach of the implied warranty of merchantability; and (5) fraudulent inducement – concealment.

 

 

 On June 22 and June 23, 2026, Defendant filed the instant motions to compel Plaitniff to produce further responses to Requests for Admission (Set One) (“RFAs”), Request for Production of Documents (Set One) (“RFPs”), Form Interrogatories (Set One) (“FROGs”), and Special Interrogatories (Set One) (“SROGs”). The motions also seek sanctions against Plaintiffs and their attorney of record.

 

On July 13 and 16, 2026, Plaintiff filed oppositions to RFA, FROG, and SROG motions. Plaintiff has not opposed the RFP motion.  

 

On July 22, 2026, Defendant filed one reply to the oppositions.

           

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

 

On receipt of interrogatories, requests for production of documents, or requests for admissions, the requesting party may move for an order compelling a further response. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300, subd. (a) [interrogatories]; 2031.310, subd. (a) [requests for production]; 2033.290, subd. (a) [requests for admission].)

 

III.      DISCUSSION

 

A.    Timeliness

 

Motions to compel further responses to interrogatories, requests for production of documents, and requests for admission must be brought within 45 days of service of the verified response, supplemental verified response, or on a date to which the propounding and responding parties have agreed to in writing; otherwise, the propounding party waives the right to compel further responses. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300, subd. (c); 2031.310, subd. (c); 2033.290, subd. (c).)

 

The parties have not disputed that the motions to compel further responses are timely.

 

B.    Separate Statement

 

Defendant has filed separate statements as required. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1345(a)(1), (2), and (3).)

 

C.    Meet and Confer

 

The motions must be accompanied by meet and confer declarations under Code of Civil Procedure section 2016.040, which requires: “A meet and confer declaration in support of a motion shall state facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt, either in person, by telephone, or by videoconference, to informally resolve each issue presented by the motion” (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2016.040, subd. (a); 2030.300, subd. (b)(1); 2031.310, subd. (b)(2); 2033.290, subd. (b)(1).)

 

In his oppositions, Plaintiff argues that Defendant failed to meet and confer in good faith because it only sent a meet and confer letter demanding responses and failed to follow up on that request.

 

The Court finds that argument unpersuasive. According to the emails that Defendant has submitted, after defense counsel emailed the meet and confer letter, Plaintiff’s counsel promised to serve supplemental responses if granted an extension. (Motions, Declarations of Cassandra Tran (“Tran Decl.”), ¶¶ 6, 7; Exh. 4) Therefore, there was no reason for defense counsel to attempt to meet and confer with Plaintiff in person, by telephone, or by videoconference as required by Section 2016.040. Plaintiff, however, did not serve the responses by the promised date. Therefore, the Court will proceed on the merits.

 

D.    Request to Compel Further Responses and for Sanctions

 

Plaintiff argues that the motions are moot because he subsequently served supplemental responses with verifications to follow before the hearing date.

In its reply, Defendant argues that Plaintiff has not served verifications for supplemental responses to Defendant’s RFPs and RFAs, and the supplemental responses to all discovery requests (i.e., the RFPs, RFAs, SROGs, and FROGs) contain improper objections and fail to substantively respond to the discovery requests.

A trial court retains the authority to hear a motion to compel further responses even if the responding party serves supplemental responses after the motion has been filed and before the hearing. (See e.g., Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Pacific Healthcare Consultants (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, 408–409 [“If a party provides an untimely interrogatory response that does not contain objections and that sets forth legally valid responses to each interrogatory, the untime response might completely or substantially resolve the issues raised by a motion to compel responses under  section 2030.290. Even in such cases, however, the trial court retains the authority to hear the motion. [Citation.] Whether a particular response does satisfactorily resolve the issues raised by a motion is a matter best determined by the trial court in the exercise of its discretion, based on the circumstances of the case. In many cases involving untimely responses, the propounding party will take the motion off calendar or narrow its scope to the issue of sanctions. If the propounding party proceeds with the motion, however, the trial court has the discretion to rule on the motion. The trial court might compel responses without objection if it finds no legally valid responses have been provided to one or more interrogatories; it might deny the motion to compel responses as essentially unnecessary, in whole or in part, and just impose sanctions; it might treat the motion as one under section 2030.300 and either determine that further answers are required, or order the propounding party to ‘meet and confer’ (§ 2030.300, subd. (b)) and file a separate statement (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1020(a)(2), (c)); or it might take the motion off calendar, thereby requiring the propounding party to file a motion under section 2030.300”].)

            The Court grants Defendant’s request to compel Plaintiff to serve further responses to the RFAs and RFPs because there is no evidence that Plaintiff has served verifications to those discovery requests. (Appleton v. Superior Court (1988) 206 Cal. App. 3d 632, 635-636 [“‘Unsworn responses are tantamount to no responses at all’”].) 

            The Court denies as moot Defendant’s request to compel Plaintiff to serve further responses to the SROGs and FROGs motions. Although Defendant argues that the supplemental responses are deficient, Defendant has not submitted a separate statement or provided information from which the Court can determine the sufficiency of those responses. In so ruling, however, the Court is not foreclosing any right Defendant may have to file a motion to compel further responses to the SROGs and FROGs based on the supplemental responses.

            Defendant seeks sanctions of $1,310 for the RFA motion, $1,060 for the RFP motion, $1,185 for the FROG motion, and $1,060 for the SROG motion. Those amounts are based on defense counsel’s hourly rate of $250 per hour and include $60 filing fees. (Tran Decl., ¶ 9.)

The Court finds it proper to impose sanctions on Plaintiff for failing to serve timely supplemental responses, thereby requiring Defendant to file these motions. However, the requested sanctions are excessive as only one reply paper was filed, and all four motions will be heard on the same date. Instead, the Court finds sanctions of $560 per motion (i.e., 2 hours of defense counsel’s time at $250 per hour plus a $60 filing fee) to be reasonable.

Therefore, the request for sanctions is granted but in the reduced amount of $560 per motion for total sanctions of $2,240 against Plaintiff.

IV.       CONCLUSION

 

The motions to compel further responses are GRANTED IN PART and DENIED IN PART as follows.

 

The request to compel further, verified responses to Form Interrogatories (Set One) and Special Interrogatories (Set One) is GRANTED. Plaintiff is ordered to serve further, verified responses to those discovery requests within 30 days of this ruling.

 

The request to compel further responses to Requests for Admission (Set One) and Request for Production of Documents (Set One) is DENIED.

 

The request for sanctions against Plaintiff is GRANTED. Plaintiff is ordered to pay Defendant total sanctions of $2,240.

 

            Defendant to give notice.

Case Number: 25STCV30919    Hearing Date: July 29, 2026    Dept: 407

Tentative Ruling

 

Judge Brock T. Hammond, Department 407

 

 

HEARING DATE:     July 29, 2026                                      TRIAL DATE:  Not set

                                                          

CASE:                         Ruilian Huang, et al. v. Margaret Hsu, et al.

 

CASE NO.:                 25STCV30919

 

 

DEMURRER WITHOUT MOTION TO STRIKE

 

MOVING PARTY:               Defendants Fenping Wang and Zhigang Li

 

RESPONDING PARTY:     Plaintiffs Ruilian Huang and Rongkai Li

 

 

I.         BACKGROUND

 

            On October 22, 2026, plaintiffs Ruilian Huang (“Huang”) and Rongkai Li (“RLi”) commenced this action against defendants Margaret Hsu (“Hsu”); Alice Ma (“Ma”); Zhigang Li (“ZLi”); Jennifer Long (“Long”); IBIS Financial Corporation (“IBIS”); Fenping Wang, a/k/a Asha Wang (“Wang”); CC&N Investment LLC (“CC&N”); Michelle Chen (“Chen”); ABC Funding (“ABC”); GMCC Loan (“GMCC”); and DOES 1 to 250, inclusive; and all persons claiming by, through, or under such entities or persons; and all persons unknown, claiming any legal or equitable right, title, estate, lien, or interest in the real property described in the complaint adverse to Plaintiff’s title, or constituting any cloud on Plaintiff’s title thereto (collectively, “Defendants”).  

 

            The operative pleading is the First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) filed on May 1, 2026.  The FAC alleges causes of action against defendants for: (1) Rescission under TILA 15 U.S.C. § 1635; (2) Violation of 12 C.F.R. § 1026.43(c) and 15 U.S.C. § 1639c(a) (Failure to Conduct an Ability to Repay Analysis); (3) Violation of 12 C.F.R. §1026.34(a)(8) and 15 U.S.C.  1639(k) (Impermissible Late Fee); (4) Violation of 12 C.F.R. §1026.32(d)(4) and 15 U.S.C. § 1639(d) (Impermissible Default Interest); (5) Violation of 12 C.F.R. §1026.32(d)(1)(i) and 15 U.S.C. § 1639(e) (Impermissible Balloon Payment); (6) Violation of 12 C.F.R. §1026.32(d)(6) and 15 U.S.C. § 1639(c) (Impermissible Prepayment Penalty); (7) Violation of 12 C.F.R. §1026.34(a)(5)(Failure to Obtain Pre-Loan Counseling); (8) Breach of Contract; (9) Breach of Fiduciary Duty; (10) California Business and Professions Code § 17200; (11) Usury; (12) Wrongful Foreclosure; (13) Cancellation of Instrument; (14) Violation of Business and Professions Code §10232.45; and (15) Declaratory Relief.

 

 

 

            On June 16, 2026, Wang and ZLi (“Demurring Defendants”) filed separate Demurrers to the FAC. On July 16, 2026, Plaintiffs filed their Oppositions. On July 21, 2026, Wang filed a Reply. On July 22, 2026, ZLi filed a Reply.

 

As a preliminary matter, the Court notes that the demurrers are substantially similar as it pertains to the arguments raised by the Demurring Defendants. (See Wang Dem. at pp. 8-16; ZLi Dem. at pp. 3-9.) Accordingly, the Court addresses the demurrers jointly below.

           

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

 

“The primary function of a pleading is to give the other party notice so that it may prepare its case [citation], and a defect in a pleading that otherwise properly notifies a party cannot be said to affect substantial rights.” (Harris v. City of Santa Monica (2013) 56 Cal.4th 203, 240.)¿

 

“A¿demurrer¿tests the legal sufficiency of the factual allegations in a complaint.” (Ivanoff v. Bank of America, N.A.¿(2017) 9 Cal.App.5th 719, 725.) The Court looks to whether “the complaint alleges facts sufficient to state a cause of action or discloses a complete defense.” (Id.) The Court does not “read passages from a complaint in isolation; in reviewing a ruling on a demurrer, we read the complaint ‘as a whole and its parts in their context.’ [Citation.]” (West v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A. (2013) 214 Cal.App.4th 780, 804.) The Court “assume[s] the truth of the properly pleaded factual allegations, facts that reasonably can be inferred from those expressly pleaded and matters of which judicial notice has been taken.” (Harris, supra, 56 Cal.4th p. 240.) “The court does not, however, assume the truth of contentions, deductions, or conclusions of law. [Citation.]” (Durell v. Sharp Healthcare (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 1350, 1358.)

 

A general demurrer may be brought under Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (e) if insufficient facts are stated to support the cause of action asserted or under section 430.10, subdivision (a), where the court has no jurisdiction of the subject of the cause of action alleged in the pleading. All other grounds listed in Section 430.10, including uncertainty under subdivision (f), are special demurrers. Special demurrers are not allowed in limited jurisdiction courts. (Code Civ. Proc., § 92, subd. (c).)

 

A demurrer for uncertainty means the pleading is ambiguous or unintelligible. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (f).) “A demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Khoury v. Maly's of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.) “[D]emurrers for uncertainty are disfavored and are granted only if the pleading is so incomprehensible that a defendant cannot reasonably respond.” (A.J. Fistes Corp. v. GDL Best Contractors, Inc. (2019) 38 Cal.App.5th 677, 695 [internal quotations omitted].)

 

Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the complainant to show the Court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Id.)

 

 

III.      DISCUSSION

 

A.    Meet and Confer

 

Prior to filing a demurrer, the demurring party is required to meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by videoconference with the party who filed the pleading demurred to for the purposes of determining whether an agreement can be reached through a filing of an amended pleading that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41.)¿¿

 

Here, Wang’s counsel of record, Virginia Liu attests to the following meet and confer efforts: “On June 16, 2026, I met and conferred with Plaintiffs’ counsel by telephone regarding the grounds for demurrer set forth in the June 8, 2026 letter. The parties were unable to resolve the issues raised, and Defendant accordingly proceeded with the filing of this demurrer.” (Liu Decl., ¶¶2-3, Ex. B.) Likewise, ZLi’s counsel of record, Lisa Anne Coe, states that: “On May 29, 2026 I sent an email to Plaintiffs’ counsel Heidi Cheng, identifying the grounds for demurrer and inviting discussion. We then had a telephonic meet and confer on June 4, 2026 but were unable to resolve any of the issues discussed.” (Coe Decl., ¶2.)

 

Therefore, the Court finds that the Demurring Defendants fulfilled their meet and confer obligations prior to filing their instant demurrers.

 

B.    Analysis

 

            Demurring Defendants demur to all fifteen causes of action (“COA”) asserted in the FAC on the grounds that (1) Plaintiffs fail to plead sufficient facts to constitute a cause of action against them; (2) the FAC is uncertain; and (3) the FAC improperly joins multiple unrelated parties without alleging that the claims arise from the same transaction or series of related transactions.

 

1.     Truth in Lending Act (“TILA”) Claim (1st COA)

 

Under TILA, “Congress provided the consumer with the right to rescind a credit transaction under § 1635(a) solely by notifying the creditor within set time limits of his intent to rescind.” (Williams v. Homestake Mortg. Co. (11th Cir. 1992) 968 F.2d 1137, 1139.) “Under § 1635(b), however, all that the consumer need do is notify the creditor of his intent to rescind. The agreement is then automatically rescinded and the creditor must, ordinarily, tender first. Thus, rescission under § 1635 ‘place[s] the consumer in a much stronger bargaining position than he enjoys under the traditional rules of rescission.’ ” (Id. at 1140.) “[A]nother goal of § 1635(b) is to return the parties most nearly to the position they held prior to entering into the transaction.” (Id.) Furthermore, “plaintiffs can state a claim for rescission under TILA without pleading that they have tendered, or that they have the ability to tender, the value of their loan. Only at the summary judgment stage may a court order the statutory sequence altered and require tender before rescission—and then only on a “case-by-case basis,” once the creditor has established a potentially viable defense.” (Merritt v. Countrywide Financial Corp. (9th Cir. 2014) 759 F.3d 1023, 1033.)



Demurring Defendants argue this claim fails because: (1) Plaintiffs lack standing; (2) the claims for monetary damages under TILA are time-barred; and (3) TILA does not apply to non-consumer transactions.

 

a.      TILA Standing

 

“Except as otherwise provided in this section, in the case of any consumer credit transaction (including opening or increasing the credit limit for an open end credit plan) in which a security interest, including any such interest arising by operation of law, is or will be retained or acquired in any property which is used as the principal dwelling of the person to whom credit is extended, the obligor shall have the right to rescind the transaction until midnight of the third business day following the consummation of the transaction or the delivery of the information and rescission forms required under this section together with a statement containing the material disclosures required under this subchapter, whichever is later, by notifying the creditor, in accordance with regulations of the Bureau, of his intention to do so.” (15 U.S.C. § 1635, subd. (a) [emphasis added].) As such, TILA limits standing to the borrower, i.e., the consumer who is a party to the credit transaction and whose dwelling secures the loan. (See generally, Jesinoski v. Countrywide Home Loans, Inc. (2015) 574 U.S. 259, 260; Yamamoto v. Bank of New York (9th Cir. 2003) 329 F.3d 1167, 1171.)

 

            The FAC alleges, inter alia, that on or about July 30, 2024, Plaintiffs were fraudulently caused to purchase the real property located at 655 Alder Way, Montebello, CA 90640 (the “Subject Property”) pursuant to a Grant Deed recorded in on July 30, 2024. (FAC, ¶34.) The loans are fraudulent because Plaintiff have never engaged the lender, brokers, or Defendants including the DOES. (Id., ¶37.) At all relevant times, Plaintiffs were in China and never communicated with Defendants. (Id.) All documents signed and obtained by Defendants were signed on behalf of Plaintiffs via a fraudulently obtained power of attorney (“POA”). (Id., ¶¶37-38.) The loans were secured against the Subject Property by two Deeds of Trust dated July 26, 2024 and recorded on July 30, 2024. (Id., ¶¶39, 42, Exs. 1-2.)

 

Taking these allegations as true, Plaintiffs allege that they did not personally signed and obtain the loans at issue. The FAC further alleges that POA used to obtain the two loans in their names was fraudulently procured. Although there is no California or Ninth Circuit case, which addresses TILA standing where a plaintiff’s name was used to obtain a loan via a fraudulent power of attorney, in In re Crevier (9th Cir. 1987) 820 F.2d 1553, the Ninth Circuit held that “a Chapter 7 debtor who conveys an unauthorized lien on estate property fails to state a TILA claim for rescission of the lien.” (In re Crevier (9th Cir. 1987) 820 F.2d 1553, 1554.) The Ninth Circuit in In re Crevier further reasoned that “a debtor cannot convey a security interest in property owned by a third person.” (Id. at 1556.) To the extent the FAC alleges that the purchase of the Subject Property was fraudulently procured through the fraudulent POA, Plaintiffs effectively fail to plead facts showing that they are owners of the Subject Property and/or have a security interest in Subject Property.

 

By contrast, the court in Gonzalez v. Specialized Loan Servicing, LLC (C.D. Cal. 2023) 691 F.Supp.3d 1162, held that “Given the remedial nature of TILA, Congress’s intent to protect consumers, and the courts’ mandate that TILA be liberally construed, the term ‘obligor[s]’ must necessarily be construed to include those whom the creditor claims are obligors, as well as individuals who are in fact obligors in the contract law sense.” (Gonzalez v. Specialized Loan Servicing, LLC (C.D. Cal. 2023) 691 F.Supp.3d 1162, 1170.) Here, the FAC does allege that on September 24, 2025, ZLi and DOES 1 through 25 caused a Notice of Default and Election to Sell to be recorded against the Property, indicating a mortgage default of $598,888.50. (FAC, ¶56.) Thus, it can be reasonably inferred that Defendants considered Plaintiffs “obligors” for purposes of TILA.

 

b.     TILA Statute of Limitations

 

Under Section 1635, subdivision (f), “TILA rescission may be extended up to three years if the lender fails to comply with TILA disclosure requirements.” (Saldate v. Wilshire Credit Corp. (E.D. Cal. 2010) 268 F.R.D. 87, 95.) “The subsection says nothing in terms of bringing an action but instead provides that the ‘right of rescission [under the Act] shall expire’ at the end of the time period.” (Id.) Essentially, “[a]ny claim for rescission must be brought within three years of consummation of the transaction or upon the sale of the property, whichever occurs first ...” (Ortiz v. Accredited Home Lenders, Inc. (S.D. Cal. 2009) 639 F.Supp.2d 1159, 1163.)

 

Demurring Defendants do not challenge the timeliness of the Plaintiffs’ underlying TILA claim but rather their request for statutory damages set forth in TILA. (See FAC, Prayer for Relief, ¶¶C-E.) Where a plaintiff seek statutory damages under TILA, “Section 1640(e) provides that ‘[a]ny action under this section may be brought within one year from the date of the occurrance of the violation.’ ” (King v. State of Cal. (9th Cir. 1986) 784 F.2d 910, 913.) “[T]he limitations period in Section 1640(e) runs from the date of consummation of the transaction but that the doctrine of equitable tolling may, in the appropriate circumstances, suspend the limitations period until the borrower discovers or had reasonable opportunity to discover the fraud or nondisclosures that form the basis of the TILA action.” (Id. at 915.) In order to rely on the discovery rule for delayed accrual of a cause of action, ‘[a] plaintiff whose complaint shows on its face that his claim would be barred without the benefit of the discovery rule must specifically plead facts to show (1) the time and manner of discovery and (2) the inability to have made earlier discovery despite reasonable diligence.’ ” (Fox v. Ethicon Endo-Surgery, Inc. (2005) 35 Cal.4th 797, 808.)

 

The FAC alleges, inter alia, that the loans were secured against the Subject Property by two Deeds of Trust dated July 26, 2024 and recorded on July 30, 2024. (Id., ¶¶39, 42, Exs. 1-2.) These loans were taken without Plaintiffs’ knowledge, which Plaintiffs did not discover until sometime in mid-2025.

 

Taking these allegations as true, the FAC merely alleges in a conclusory manner that Plaintiffs’ did not discover these loans were taken without their knowledge. The FAC is otherwise devoid of any specific facts at to the time and manner of the discovery. The FAC also fails to allege why Plaintiffs’ were unable to discover this information earlier. Therefore, Plaintiffs’ request for damages under TILA are time-barred.

 

 

 

 

c.      TILA Does Not Apply to Non-Consumer Transactions

 

TILA protects “consumer credit transactions” entered into “primarily for personal, family, or household purposes.” (15 U.S.C. § 1602, subd. (i).) Contrarily, TILA does not apply to “Credit transactions involving extensions of credit primarily for business, commercial, or agricultural purposes, or to government or governmental agencies or instrumentalities, or to organizations.” (15 U.S.C. § 1603, subd. (1) [emphasis added].) However, the Court in Thorns v. Sundance Properties (9th Cir. 1984) 726 F.2d 1417, held that an investment made be entered into for “personal purposes” under TILA. (Thorns v. Sundance Properties (9th Cir. 1984) 726 F.2d 1417, 1418–1419.) “In determining whether credit to finance an acquisition—such as securities, antiques, or art—is primarily for business or commercial purposes (as opposed to a consumer purpose), the following factors should be considered: The relationship of the borrower’s primary occupation to the acquisition. The more closely related, the more likely it is to be business purpose. The degree to which the borrower will personally manage the acquisition. The more personal involvement there is, the more likely it is to be business purpose. The ratio of income from the acquisition to the total income of the borrower. The higher the ratio, the more likely it is to be business purpose. The size of the transaction. The larger the transaction, the more likely it is to be business purpose. The borrower’s statement of purpose for the loan.” (Id. at 1419.)

 

The FAC alleges, inter alia, that “the subject investment was for a consumer purpose as opposed to business or commercia purposes.” (FAC, ¶17.) “This action concerns a highly predatory and illegal home loan secured by a first and second mortgage on Plaintiffs’ real property which is used primarily for personal, family, or household purposes. The defendants are investors/lenders/brokers that funded the loan and the mortgage brokers who originated and then serviced the loan.” (Id., ¶19.) “[T]he subject loan term was 12 months, the loan amount was $1,630,000 and $570,000.00 the interest rate was 11.00%, resulting in a fully amortized monthly payment of $20,166.67.” (Id., ¶¶30, 38, 55, 126-127.)“Plaintiffs have limited to no experience with real property investments and related mortgages here in the United States.” (Id., ¶33; see also ¶147.) “Pursuant to Business and Professions Code §10232.45, a broker shall make reasonable efforts to ensure that for notes secured by a lien on real property, all person to whom notes are sold can be reasonably assumed to have the capacity to understand the fundamental aspects of the investment, by reason of their education, business, or financial experience, that they can bear the economic risk of the investment, and that the notes are suitable and appropriate for the purchaser.” (Id., ¶146.)

 

            Taking these allegations as true, the Plaintiffs’ entered into the loan transactions for business or commercial purposes, i.e., real property investment purposes. Furthermore, the FAC seeks to hold Defendants liable under Business and Professions Code Section 10232.45, which make further references to investment and economic risks associated with investments. Finally, the loan amounts are large, totaling over a million dollars, thus further supports the TILA claim is based on a transaction for business and/or commercial purposes as opposed to a personal purpose.

 

To the extent that Plaintiffs contend they can amend their claims to cure any deficiencies and the liberal policy for amendments, the demurrer to the first cause of action is SUSTAINED with leave to amend. Likewise, Regulation Z and HOEPA fall under TILA, therefore, the demurrers to the second through seventh causes of action are also SUSTAINED with leave to amend.

 

2.     Common Law Claims (8th – 9th, 11th – 12th COAs)

 

A party may plead inconsistent facts or theories in the alternative. (Crowley v. Katleman (1994) 8 Cal. 4th 666, 691; see also Adams v. Paul (1995) 11 Cal. 4th 583, 593; Third Eye Blind, Inc. v. Near North Entertainment Ins. Services, LLC (2005) 127 Cal.App.4th 1311, 1323.) “Inconsistencies between alternative counts cannot be attacked by demurrer, since each count stands on its own allegations, unaffected by those contained in other counts.” (Penziner v. West American Finance Co. (1933) 133 Cal.App. 578, 582.)

 

            Here, Plaintiffs plead the eighth cause of action breach of contract in alternative to their TILA claim. (See FAC, ¶102.) Establishing a breach of contract claim, “requires a showing of (1) the existence of the contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance, (3) defendant’s breach, and (4) the resulting damages to the plaintiff.” (D’Arrigo Bros. of California v. United Farmworkers of America (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 790, 800 (internal quotations omitted).) “Further, the complaint must indicate on its face whether the contract is written, oral, or implied by conduct.” (Otworth v. Southern Pac. Transportation Co. (1985) 166 Cal.App.3d 452, 458–459.) Generally, if the claim is based upon a written contract, the complaint must set out the contract’s terms verbatim or the plaintiff must attach a copy of the written contract to the complaint and incorporate it by reference. (Id.) Otherwise, “a plaintiff may plead the legal effect of the contract rather than its precise language.” (Construction Protective Services, Inc. v. TIG Specialty Ins. Co. (2002) 29 Cal.4th 189, 198–199.) The FAC alleges that Plaintiffs performed all their obligations under the loan agreements. (FAC, ¶105.) Defendants breached the loan agreements by seeking to collect the  Default Interest Rate charges, which is a liquidated damages penalty clause in a consumer contract. (Id., ¶¶103-104.) Plaintiffs were damaged and harmed as a result of Defendants’ breach. (Id., ¶106.) Reading the FAC as a whole, it fails to allege that the transactions were for consumer purposes as opposed to business and/or commercial purposes. Therefore, the FAC fails to sufficiently allege a claim for breach of a consumer contract.

 

With respect to the ninth cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty, plaintiff must plead: “(1) the existence of a fiduciary relationship, (2) its breach, and (3) damage proximately caused by that breach.” (O'Neal v. Stanislaus County Employees' Retirement Assn. (2017) 8 Cal.App.5th 1184, 1215.) The FAC alleges that Defendants were Plaintiffs’ mortgage brokers, lenders, and/or agents in the subject loan transactions, thus owed them a fiduciary duty to make full and accurate disclosure of the terms of the loans, act in good faith towards Plaintiffs, and disclose material facts concerning the transactions. (FAC, ¶¶108-109.) Defendants breached their fiduciary duties when they put Plaintiffs in a highly predatory and unlawful loan; imposed illegal charges; failing to advise Plaintiffs they were likely to default on the loans; and foreclosing on the Subject Property. (Id., ¶¶110-111.) Plaintiffs suffered damages as a result of these breaches. (Id., ¶113.) “However, as a general rule, a financial institution owes no duty of care to a borrower when the institution's involvement in the loan transaction does not exceed the scope of its conventional role as a mere lender of money.” (Nymark v. Heart Fed. Savings & Loan Assn. (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 1089, 1096.) “Thus, for example, a lender has no duty to disclose its knowledge that the borrower's intended use of the loan proceeds represents an unsafe investment.” (Id.) The FAC is devoid of any conduct by Defendants that exceeded their scope as lenders. Nevertheless, “[a] mortgage loan broker owes a fiduciary duty of the “highest good faith toward his principal” and “is ‘charged with the duty of fullest disclosure of all material facts concerning the transaction that might affect the principal's decision.’ ” (Barry v. Raskov (1991) 232 Cal.App.3d 447, 455.) “The broker owes this duty to the lender-investor as well as to the borrower.” (Id.) The FAC alleges that Defendants were not just lenders but brokers and/or agents of Plaintiffs’ as it pertains to the loan transactions.

 

As to the eleventh cause of action for usury, “[t]he essential elements of usury are: (1) The transaction must be a loan or forbearance; (2) the interest to be paid must exceed the statutory maximum; (3) the loan and interest must be absolutely repayable by the borrower; and (4) the lender must have a willful intent to enter into a usurious transaction.” (Creative Ventures, LLC v. Jim Ward & Associates (2011) 195 Cal.App.4th 1430, 1449.) “That willful intent ‘does not require a conscious attempt, with knowledge of the law, to evade it. The conscious and voluntary taking of more than the legal rate of interest constitutes usury and the only intent necessary on the part of the lender is to take the amount of interest which he receives; if that amount is more than the law allows, the offense is complete.’ ” (Id. at 1449–1450.) Here, the FAC alleges that “Defendants loaned to Plaintiffs the total sum of $1,630,000.00 and $570,000.00 at the usurious rate of 11.00%. These Loans fall within the usury statutes.” (FAC, ¶126.) “[T]he effective rate of interest on the Note exceeds the maximum rate allowed by the California Constitution Article XV, Section One,….” (Id., ¶127; see also ¶128-129.) On the other hand, “Section 1 of article XV of the California Constitution imposes certain limitations on the amount of interest that can be charged on a loan. That provision also contains an exemption for “any loans made or arranged by any person licensed as a real estate broker by the State of California and secured in whole or in part by liens on real property.” (Bock v. California Capital Loans, Inc. (2013) 216 Cal.App.4th 264, 268.) The FAC explicitly alleges that “Defendants HSU, MA, LI, LONG, IBIS, WANG, CC&N, CHEN, ABC, and GMCC are lenders, brokers, and/or agents of lenders and brokers, that conspired together and gave substantial assistance to each other to offer and provide illegal and predatory loans to consumers such as Plaintiffs.” (FAC, ¶11.) As such, it can be reasonably inferred that Defendants including Demurring Defendants were licensed real estate brokers in California, who made or arranged the loans alleged therein.

 

Regarding the twelfth cause of action for wrongful foreclosure, the gravamen of this claim Defendants alleged violation under TILA and Business and Professions Code Section 17200 (derivative remedy). Accordingly, the demurrers to the eighth, eleventh, and twelfth causes of action are SUSTAINED with leave to amend. The demurrers as to the ninth cause of action are OVERRULED.

 

3.     Violation of Business & Professions Code Section 10232.45 (14th COA)

 

Under Business & Professions Code Section 10232.45, subdivision (a): “Any broker subject to the provisions of Section 10232.3 or Article 6 (commencing with Section 10237) shall make reasonable efforts to ensure all of the following with respect to the offer or sale of notes or interest in notes to be secured by a lien on real property or a business opportunity: [¶](1) All persons to whom notes or interests are sold can be reasonably assumed to have the capacity to understand the fundamental aspects of the investment, by reason of their educational, business, or financial experience. [¶](2) All persons to whom notes or interests are sold can bear the economic risk of the investment. [¶](3) The investment in the notes or interests is suitable and appropriate for the purchaser, given the purchaser's investment objectives, portfolio structure, and financial situation.” (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 10232.45, subdivision (a).

 

The FAC alleges, inter alia, that Defendants knowingly or negligently put Plaintiffs in highly predatory and unlawful loans. (FAC, ¶148.) Defendants then failed to make any reasonable efforts to advise or notify Plaintiffs that they were likely to default on the loans. (Id., ¶149.) Defendants also failed to make any reasonable efforts to ensure Plaintiffs had the capacity to understand the nature of the Loans before the promissory notes were entered into. (Id., ¶150.) However, the FAC is devoid of any facts that Plaintiffs entered into a purchases notes or interest in notes to bring this action within the purview of the statute. Moreover, the FAC does not contain any allegations that Plaintiffs were offered the sale of notes or interests in notes by Defendants. Therefore, the FAC fails to show on its face that Business & Professions Code Section 10232.45 applies to this case.

 

4.     Derivative Remedies (10th, 13th, 15th COAs)

 

The tenth, thirteenth, and fifteenth causes of action are derivative remedies for the TILA, Business and Professions Code, Regulation Z, and HOEPA claims. Therefore, the demurrers to these causes of action are also SUSTAINED with leave to amend.

 

IV.       CONCLUSION

 

            Based on the foregoing, the demurrers to the first through eighth and tenth through fifteenth causes of action are SUSTAINED with 20 days leave to amend. The demurrers as to the ninth cause of action are OVERRULED.

(2) MOTION FOR LIMITED-EXPEDITED DISCOVERY

 

MOVING PARTY:               Plaintiff Margarita Arakelian

 

RESPONDING PARTY:     Meta Platforms, Inc.

 

 

I.         BACKGROUND

On November 7, 2025, Plaintiff Margarita Arakelian filed a Complaint against Does 1 through 20 (collectively, “Defendants”).  On December 15, 2025, Plaintiff filed the operative First Amended Complaint against Defendants for (1) Invasion of Privacy, (2) Unauthorized Access to Computers in Violation of California Penal Code § 502, et seq., (3) Conversion, (4) Identity Theft in Violation of California Penal Code § 530.5, and (5) Declaratory and Injunctive Relief.  No doe amendments have been filed and no defendants have appeared. 

             On April 15, 2026, Plaintiff filed a motion for limited-expedited discovery as to non-party Verizon Wireless (“Motion No. 1”).

On June 23, 2026, Plaintiff filed a motion for limited-expedited discovery as to non-parties Cox Communications, Inc., Instagram, LLC and Verizon Wireless (“Motion No. 2”). 

            As of July 24, 2026, no oppositions have been filed to either motion. 

II.        LEGAL STANDARD

 

“Subject to Sections 2025.270 and 2025.610, an oral deposition may be taken as follows:…(b) The plaintiff may serve a deposition notice without leave of court on any date that is 20 days after the service of the summons on, or appearance by, any defendant. On motion with or without notice, the court, for good cause shown, may grant to a plaintiff leave to serve a deposition notice on an earlier date.”  (Code of Civ. Proc., §2025.210, subd. (b).)  The 20-day “hold” on service of deposition notices by a plaintiff applies to all discovery by deposition, including business records subpoenas to nonparties.  (California Shellfish Inc. v. United Shellfish Co. (1997) 56 Cal.App.4th 16, 20 (deposition hold applies to deposition subpoena seeking business records and trial court properly quashed subpoenas served by plaintiff before any service of the complaint and summons on any defendant).) 

 

III.      DISCUSSION

 

A.  Motion No. 1

 

Motion No. 1 is only directed as to Non-Party Deponent Verizon Wireless (“Verizon”).  (Notice, 2:4-7.)  According to the Notice of Motion, Plaintiff requests “leave to engage in early discovery against” Verizon.  (Id. at 2:6-7).  However, based on the points and authorities and declaration of counsel, Plaintiff propounded a deposition subpoena on Verizon on January 12, 2026 seeking information identifying information for specified “users, customers, subscribers, or accounts” identified in Exhibit A to the subpoena (“1/12/26 Subpoena”).  (Atrizadeh Dec., Ex. A.)  Counsel states that non-party deponent failed or refused to comply with the subpoena absent a formal court order.  (Id. at ¶5.) 

 

Plaintiff served a deposition subpoena on Verizon before any defendant appeared and before service of summons on any defendant.  The 1/12/26 Subpoena was therefore served in violation of the “hold” on depositions under Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.210 subdivision (b).  Under section 2025.210 subdivision (b), a “plaintiff may serve a deposition notice without leave of court on any date that is 20 days after the service of summons on, or appearance by, any defendant.  On motion with or without notice, the court, for good cause shown, may grant to a plaintiff leave to serve a deposition notice on an earlier date.”  (Code of Civ. Proc, §2025.210, subd. (b).)  Plaintiff did not file a motion seeking leave before serving the 1/12/26 Subpoena.

 

For that reason, Plaintiff’s motion cannot seek compliance with the 1/12/26 subpoena.  Plaintiff’s motion can only seek leave to propound the same deposition subpoena on Verizon.  Plaintiff’s motion also does not entitle Plaintiff to any findings as to Verizon’s obligation to respond.  The available relief is limited solely to leave to serve discovery before expiration of the “hold” set forth under Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.210.  The proposed order submitted by Plaintiff is consistent with this limited relief.  (Proposed Order Submitted on 4/15/26.) 

 

The Court notes that Verizon raised Code of Civil Procedure section 1985.3 as grounds to refuse compliance with the deposition subpoena.  (Atrizadeh Dec., Ex. B.)  Section 1985.3 subdivision (b) requires that the individual consumer be served with the subpoena, the affidavit in support of the subpoena and the consumer notice described in subdivision (e).  Plaintiff attempted to comply with section 1985.3 by attaching a Notice to Consumer on mandatory Judicial Council Form SUBP-025 to the 1/12/26 Subpoena.  (Atrizadeh Dec., Ex. A, Notice to Consumer.)  Plaintiff’s Notice to Consumer is addressed to “Consumers.”  Such a notice would not comply with section 1985.3, nor is it clear that Plaintiff could comply with Section 1985.3 subdivision (b) and (e), given that the defendants’ identities are unknown.  Subdivision (h) provides, “[u]pon good cause shown and provided that the rights of witnesses and consumers are preserved, a subpoenaing party shall be entitled to obtain an order…waiving the requirements of subdivision (b) where due diligence by the subpoenaing party has been shown.”  (Code of Civ. Proc., §1985.3, subd. (h).)  Plaintiff has not made any request for waiver under subdivision (h).

 

Plaintiff is only seeking leave to serve discovery at this time.  However, Plaintiff does not address whether she can serve a valid deposition subpoena in light of section 1985.3 subdivision (b).  Plaintiff’s ability to serve a valid deposition subpoena on Verizon is a valid consideration in determining the existence of good cause.

 

More importantly, after reviewing the moving papers, the court finds Plaintiff has not demonstrated good cause for leave to serve discovery on Verizon before the expiration of the 20-day hold in subdivision (b) of section 2025.210 of the Code of Civil Procedure.  Plaintiff needs information to identify and locate the Doe Defendants in this action.  However, Plaintiff does not refer to Verizon in her FAC, nor is the relevance of Verizon explained in the supporting declarations or the memorandum of points and authorities.  Plaintiff’s motion for leave to serve expedited discovery is denied. 

 

B.  Motion No. 2

 

Motion No. 2 is directed at Cox Communications, Inc. (“Cox”), Instagram, LLC (“Instagram”) and Verizon Wireless (“Verizon”).  In the Notice of Motion for Motion No. 2, Plaintiff explicitly states that she only “seeks permission to serve or re-serve subpoenas and request limited identifying information subject to any applicable provider notice procedure, privacy objection, Stored Communications Act limitation, First Amendment safeguard, or motion to quash.”  (Notice, 2:14-17.)  Plaintiff “does not ask the Court to require immediate production from Non-party Deponents.”  (Id. at 2:12-13.)

 

The same concerns regarding Plaintiff’s ability to satisfy the requirements of Code of Civil Procedure section 1985.3 subdivisions (b) and (e) exist as to deposition subpoenas proposed by Motion No. 2.  Plaintiff does not address whether a valid deposition subpoena can be served on Cox, Verizon or Instagram given the requirements of Code of Civil Procedure section 1985.3’s requirements. 

 

In addition, Plaintiff’s Motion No. 2 still fails to explain the relevance of Verizon to identification of the DOE defendants.  Plaintiff’s Motion No. 2 also fails to explain the relevance of Cox to Plaintiff’s identification of DOE defendants.  The FAC also does not mention Cox.  Counsel’s declaration that these entities “should have identifiable information” is insufficient to establish good cause.  Counsel fails to explain why they should have identifiable information.

 

However, Plaintiff alleges that her Instagram account was accessed without her permission on multiple occasions by unknown and unauthorized users.  (FAC, ¶¶20-22.)  Plaintiff therefore establishes good cause for leave to propound expedited discovery on Instagram.  Plaintiff cannot reasonably ascertain the identity of the unauthorized users by other means.

 

Meta Platforms, Inc. filed a response on July 16, 2026 arguing that Motion No. 2 must be denied as redundant and moot based on the court’s April 8, 2026 order granting Plaintiff’s Motion for Limited-Expedited Discovery filed on January 7, 2026 (“1/7/26 Motion”).  However, the April 8, 2026 order only allows for limited expedited discovery as to Meta Platforms, Inc. and Google, Inc.  The Court’s April 8, 2026 order did not state that such discovery was allowed as to Instagram, even though 1/7/26 Motion only sought an order for limited-expedited discovery as to Instagram, LLC and Google, LLC.  Meta was not referenced in the motion, nor has Meta claimed that it and Instagram, LLC are the exact same entity.  Motion No. 2 is therefore not moot as a result of the April 8, 2026 order.  Plaintiff has not yet received an order allowing her to conduct limited expedited discovery before expiration of the “hold” on depositions under Code of Civil Procedure section 2025.210 subdivision (b). 

 

Plaintiff’s Motion No. 2 is denied as to Verizon and Cox and granted as to Instagram. 

 

IV.       CONCLUSION

 

            Plaintiff’s Motion for Limited-Expedited Discovery filed on April 15, 2026 is DENIED.

 

            Plaintiff’s Motion for Limited-Expedited Discovery filed on June 23, 2026 is DENIED as to Verizon Wireless and Cox Communications, Inc. and GRANTED as to Instagram, LLC. 

 

            Plaintiff is ordered to give notice.
