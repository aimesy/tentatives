NGA VO, et al. v. FORD MOTOR COMPANY, et al. [25STCV19354]

 

DEFENDANT HUNTINGTON BEACH FORD’S MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT AS TO THE SECOND AMENDED COMPLAINT

 

TIMELINE: motor vehicle Lemon Law action

 

1/8/2017: Plaintiffs Nga Vo and Alexander Lam (“Plaintiffs”) agree to lease a 2017 Ford 5-150 (the “Vehicle”) from Defendant Huntington Beach Ford (“HBF”). (SAC, ¶ 7.) With the lease, Plaintiffs receive a written warranty from Defendant Ford Motor Company (“Ford”), the manufacturer of the vehicle. (Id. ¶¶ 7-8, Exh. A.)

 

1/6/2020: Plaintiffs ultimately purchase the Vehicle from HBF. (Def. Exh. 1.)

 

1/24/2022: Plaintiffs present their Vehicle to HBF for general maintenance and a Vehicle Control Unit (“VCU”) inspection. (Def. Decl., ¶ 5, Exh. 4.)

 

7/23/2022: Plaintiffs present their Vehicle to HBF to replace lug nuts. (Def. Decl., ¶ 5, Exh. 5.)

 

12/16/2022: Plaintiffs present their Vehicle to HBF to complete asymptomatic Recalls 18N03 and 22S11. (Def. Decl., ¶ 5, Exhs. 3, 6.)

 

9/30/2023: Plaintiffs present their Vehicle to HBF for routine maintenance. (Def. Decl., ¶ 5, Exh. 7.)

 

6/1/2024: Plaintiffs present their Vehicle to HBF for routine maintenance and a VCU inspection. (Def. Decl., ¶ 5, Exhs. 3, 8.)

 

12/31/2024: Plaintiffs present their Vehicle to HBF for routine maintenance. (Def. Decl., ¶ 5, Exhs. 3, 9.)

 

6/30/2025: Plaintiffs file the Complaint. The operative Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”), filed 2/10/2026, alleges causes of action for: 

 

1.   Violation of Civ. Code § 1793.2, subd. (d)¿¿ 

2.   Violation of Civ. Code § 1793.2, subd. (b)¿¿ 

3.   Violation of Civ. Code § 1793.2, subd. (a)(3)¿¿ 

4.   Breach of the Implied Warranty of Merchantability

5.   Negligent Repair

6.   Fraudulent Inducement-Concealment¿¿ 

 

5/4/2026: The Court sustains without leave to amend the causes of action against Ford in the SAC. Thus, the sole remaining cause of action in the SAC is the fifth cause of action for negligent repair claim against HBF.

 

6/25/2026: HBF files this Motion for Summary Judgment. As of 9/21/2026, no opposition has been received.

 

TENTATIVE RULING: DEFENDANT HUNTINGTON BEACH FORD’S MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT is GRANTED.

 

MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT

 

HBF moves for summary judgment on the sole remaining cause of action for negligent repair in the SAC on the grounds that the claim is barred by the economic loss rule and because Plaintiff cannot present evidence to raise a triable issue of material fact on that cause of action. Plaintiffs have not opposed the motion.

 

The Court finds that no triable issues of material fact exist with respect to Plaintiffs’ negligent repair claim against HBF. “‘The elements of a cause of action for negligence are duty, breach, causation, and damages.’” (Woolard v. Regent Real Estate Services, Inc. (2024) 107 Cal.App.5th 783, 791.)

 

Here, Plaintiffs’ negligent repair claim in the SAC is entirely conclusory. Plaintiffs vaguely allege that they delivered the Vehicle to HBF for “substantial repair on at least one occasion.” (SAC, ¶ 99.) Plaintiffs allege that HBF breached its duty of care to Plaintiffs by “failing to properly store, prepare and repair” the Vehicle in accordance with industry standards. (Id. ¶ 101.) Plaintiffs also generally allege that HBF’s negligence caused Plaintiffs to suffer unspecified damages. (Id. ¶ 102.) Plaintiffs’ vague and conclusory allegations, which are devoid of any meaningful factual details identifying the negligent conduct by HBF or the harm suffered by Plaintiffs’, are inadequate to state a claim for negligent repair on the pleadings alone.

 

Even further, HBF argues that Plaintiffs’ negligent repair claim is barred by the economic loss rule. “In general, there is no recovery in tort for negligently inflicted ‘purely economic losses,’ meaning financial harm unaccompanied by physical or property damage.” (Sheen v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A.¿(2022) 12 Cal.5th 905, 922 (Sheen); Rattagan¿v. Uber Technologies, Inc.¿(2024) 17 Cal.5th 1, 38 [under the economic loss rule, there is¿generally no¿recovery in tort for pure economic losses arising from a contractual relationship].) 

 

In this case, HBF submits evidence that Plaintiffs presented the Vehicle to HBF for repairs and/or maintenance on at least six occasions between January 2022 and December 2024. (Def. Decl., ¶ 5, Exhs. 4-9.) It is undisputed that these repair services were performed by HBF pursuant to Plaintiffs’ Ford maintenance plan (a service contract) and/or Ford’s written warranty. (FAC, ¶¶ 7-8, 31, Exh. A; Def. Decl., ¶¶ 5-6, Exhs. 4-9.) Because Plaintiffs are seeking to recover in tort for alleged harms arising from their contractual relationship with HBF, the economic loss is squarely applicable to Plaintiffs’ claim here.

 

Additionally, Plaintiffs’ document production, which consists of the Retail Installment Contract, the Vehicle’s DMV registration card, and several repair orders, are devoid of any evidentiary support for the conclusion that Plaintiffs suffered any personal injury or physical damage to property other than the Vehicle itself. (Sloas Decl., ¶ 7, Exh. 3.) Under the “component exception” to the economic loss rule,¿tort recovery¿is not barred¿where economic losses are “accompanied by some form of personal injury or damage to property¿other than the defective product itself.” (KB Home v. Superior Court¿(2003) 112 Cal.App.4th 1076, 1079; Jimenez v. Superior Court¿(2002) 29 Cal.4th 473, 483-84.) Here, however, given Plaintiffs’ failure to oppose this motion, they have not identified or produced any documents confirming that they suffered harm beyond “purely economic losses.” (Sheen, supra, 12 Cal.5th at p. 922.)

 

As Plaintiffs fail to raise any triable factual dispute regarding: (1) the existence of a duty owed by HBF that is independent from the warranty and/or service contract, (2) whether Plaintiffs suffered any non-economic damages due to HBF’s alleged negligence, or (3) whether some exception to the economic loss rule applies here, the Court finds that the undisputed facts and the allegations on the face of the SAC together demonstrate that Plaintiffs’ negligent repair claim is barred by the economic loss rule. Accordingly, HBF is entitled to summary judgment on Plaintiffs’ sole remaining negligent repair claim as a matter of law.

 

Therefore, HBF’s motion for summary judgment is GRANTED.¿

ANITA HAYDEN v. CITY OF LOS ANGELES, et al. [26STCV05362]

 

DEFENDANT GREG MCMANUS’S DEMURRER and MOTION TO STIKE AS TO THE COMPLAINT

 

MEET AND CONFER: OK Counsel for moving party declares that the parties met and conferred by video conference in compliance with Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41 (Estes Decl., ¶ 3.)

 

TIMELINE: FEHA employment dispute

 

1/6/1992: Plaintiff Anita Hayden (“Hayden” or “Plaintiff”), who is an African American woman and a member of the Seventh Day Adventist Christian faith, begins her employment for Defendant City of Los Angeles (the “City”) as a Special Officer II with the Los Angeles Police Department of Airports. Hayden receives multiple promotions and is ultimately promoted to the position of Port Police Captain with the Harbor Department on 7/23/2017. In this position, Hayden is assigned to be the Commanding Officer of the Patrol Operations Division and serves as a Command Duty Officer.

 

Circa 2010-2025: Moving Defendant Greg McManus (“McManus”) is employed by the City as Deputy Chief and Operations Group Commander of the Los Angeles Port Police Division. McManus has direct supervisory authority over Hayden and other Port Police Captains. Hayden alleges that McManus participated in unspecified “harassing conduct” against her based on her race and gender. (Compl., ¶¶ 204, 218, 269.)

 

For brevity, selected allegations of harassment, discrimination, and retaliation are summarized in the Appendix below, because the specific allegations in the Complaint primarily pertain to Deputy Chief Randy Allen (“Allen”), as opposed to moving defendant McManus. (See infra, Section III.)

 

2/27/2024: After an internal affairs investigation is opened regarding an incident in July/August 2023 (see Appendix supra), Hayden participates in an administrative interview conducted by Port Police Professional Standards. The internal affairs investigation ultimately results in 45 allegations against Hayden (including falsifying time records, misuse of sick leave, unauthorized outside employment, dishonesty, and various policy violations). Hayden denies the allegations.

 

5/1/2024: Hayden receives a Notice of Intent to Discipline proposing her discharge by the City based on the internal affairs investigative results.

 

1/14/2025: Hayden receives a Notice of Discharge from the City’s Harbor Department, terminating her employment effective immediately. At the time of her termination, Hayden is 63 years old and months away from eligibility for full retirement benefits.

 

6/30/2025: Plaintiff files a government tort claim with the City, which is rejected on 8/18/2025. On 2/17/2026, Plaintiff receives a Right to Sue notice from the CRD.

 

2/17/2026: Plaintiff files the Complaint, alleging causes of action for:

 

1.   FEHA Discrimination on the Basis of Race

2.   FEHA Discrimination on the Basis of Gender

3.   FEHA Discrimination on the Basis of Age

4.   FEHA Discrimination on the Basis of Religious Creed

5.   FEHA Harassment on the Basis of Race

6.   FEHA Harassment on the Basis of Gender

7.   FEHA Retaliation

8.   FEHA Failure to Prevent Discrimination, Harassment, and Retaliation

9.   Wrongful Termination in Violation of Public Policy

10.        Violation of Labor Code section 132a (Workers’ Compensation Retaliation)

11.        Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress (“IIED”)

12.        Assault

13.        Battery

14.        Negligent Hiring Retention and Supervision

 

8/18/2026: McManus files this Demurrer with a Motion to Strike, which is followed by Plaintiff’s Opposition (9/9/2026) and McManus’s Reply (9/15/2026).

 

TENTATIVE RULING: DEFENDANT GREG MCMANUS’S DEMURRER is SUSTAINED with LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

DEFENDANT GREG MCMANUS’S MOTION TO STIKE is GRANTED.

 

I.        DEMURRER

 

Defendant demurs to the fifth, sixth, and eleventh causes of action in the Complaint pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subd. (e). This lawsuit arises out of an employment dispute between Hayden and her former employer, the City. Plaintiff also seeks to hold three individual defendants, including McManus, individually liable. Of the fourteen causes of action in the Complaint, only three are asserted against McManus: two causes of action for harassment (based on race and gender) and one cause of action for IIED. While the Complaint alleges extensive factual background alleging Hayden’s employment and disciplinary history at the City and hostility from other co-workers, the Complaint is entirely devoid of facts describing McManus’s specific role in the alleged harm to Hayden. Simply put, there is almost no mention of McManus in the lengthy Complaint beyond his identification as Hayden’s supervisor and conclusory allegations that he participated in the harassment of Hayden. For this reason, as expanded upon below, the Court finds that Hayden fails to state any viable cause of action against McManus in his individual capacity. (See e.g. Le Mere v. Los Angeles Unified School Dist. (2019) 35 Cal.App.5th 237, 243 [sustaining individual defendants’ demurrer to FEHA retaliation claim where the plaintiff did not allege facts to support the elements of a FEHA retaliation claim tailored to the individual defendants themselves, including whether they held retaliatory animus or engaged in retaliatory conduct].)

 

A.           5th and 6th Causes of Action: FEHA Harassment on the Basis of Race/Gender – SUSTAINED with LEAVE TO AMEND

 

Plaintiff fails to state a claim against McManus for harassment, either based on race or gender. To establish a claim for FEHA harassment, a plaintiff must demonstrate the following elements:¿(1) the employer harassed the employee, (2)¿on the basis of¿the employee’s membership in a protected class, and (3) the harassment was sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of employment. (Etter v.¿Veriflo¿Corp. (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 457, 465.)¿Under Government Code¿section¿12940, subd.¿(j), “‘harassment’ in the workplace may take the form of ‘discriminatory intimidation, ridicule and insult’ that is ‘sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of the victim’s employment and create an abusive working environment.’” (Serri¿v. Santa Clara University¿(2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 830, 869.) “‘[H]arassment focuses on situations in which the social environment of the workplace becomes intolerable because the harassment (whether verbal, physical, or visual) communicates an offensive message to the harassed employee.’” (Ibid.) Thus, “harassing¿conduct takes place ‘outside the scope of necessary job performance, [and is] conduct presumably engaged in for personal gratification,¿because of meanness or bigotry, or for other personal motives.’ …” (Ibid.) “Harassment¿is not conduct of a type necessary for management of the employer's business or performance of the supervisory employee's job.” (Reno v. Baird¿(1998) 18 Cal.4th 640, 646.) “A showing that harassment created a hostile work environment requires a showing¿‘that the defendant’s conduct would have interfered with a reasonable employee's work performance and would have seriously affected the psychological well-being of a reasonable employee.’¿…”¿(Martin v. Board of Trustees of California State University (2023) 97 Cal.App.5th¿149, 170.)

 

Here, Plaintiff alleges that she is an African American female, giving her protected status under FEHA on the basis of both her race and gender. (Compl., ¶¶ 11, 196, 210.) However, Plaintiff fails to allege any additional factual support for the remaining elements of her harassment claim against McManus. Plaintiff generally alleges that “all Defendants,” including Allen, McManus, and Defendant Rosa Reynosa, collectively subjected Plaintiff to “severe and pervasive harassment” which was substantially motivated by her race and gender. (Id. ¶¶ 196, 204, 210, 218.) However, the only specific factual support for this vague and conclusory allegation pertains to Defendant Allen. (Id. ¶¶ 197-202.) Plaintiff fails to describe any specific incidents of harassment, whether based on race or gender, which McManus allegedly participated in or knew about. Without any factual allegations detailing what McManus allegedly did or failed to do, it cannot be determined whether McManus, in fact, harassed Plaintiff under the meaning of FEHA, whether his harassing conduct, if any, was related to Plaintiff’s gender and race, or whether his harassing conduct, if any, was sufficiently severe and pervasive to constitute a claim for FEHA harassment.

 

A plaintiff is required to “set forth in his complaint the essential facts of his case with reasonable precision and with particularity sufficiently specific to acquaint the defendant of the nature, source, and extent of his cause of action.” (Elder v. Pac. Bell Tel. Co. (2012) 205 Cal.App.4th 841, 858, internal citation omitted.) Here, Plaintiff’s conclusory allegations that McManus participated in harassing conduct based on Plaintiff’s race and gender entirely fail to describe the “nature, source, and extent” of the alleged wrongdoing such that McManus can reasonably identify the basis for the harassment claims asserted against him. (Compl., ¶¶ 196, 204, 210, 218.) Notably, although Plaintiff alleges that McManus was her supervisor at the City, McManus’s mere supervisory position without any supporting factual allegations connecting McManus to any particular instance of harassment is insufficient to impute individual liability to McManus. (Id. ¶ 6.) In sum, Plaintiff is required to plead facts to support each element of her harassment claim, as it pertains to McManus, in order to maintain viable harassment causes of action against him.

 

Therefore, McManus’s demurrer to the fifth and sixth causes of action in the Complaint is SUSTAINED with LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

B.            11th Cause of Action: IIED – SUSTAINED with LEAVE TO AMEND

 

Plaintiff’s eleventh cause of action for IIED also fails. To establish a cause of action for IIED, a plaintiff must show: “(1) outrageous conduct by the defendant; (2) the defendant's intention of causing or reckless disregard of the probability of causing emotional distress; (3) the plaintiff's suffering severe or extreme emotional distress; and (4) actual and proximate causation of the emotional distress by the defendant's outrageous conduct.” (Trerice v. Blue Cross of California (1989) 209 Cal.App.3d 878, 883 (Trerice).)¿¿

 

Here, Plaintiff generally alleges that “Defendants Allen, Reynosa, and McManus acted in an extreme and outrageous manner when they subjected Plaintiff to years of racial and gender-based harassment, discrimination, and hostile treatment from 2010 through 2025.” (Compl., ¶ 269.) Plaintiff also generally alleges that “Defendants acted extremely and outrageously when they placed Plaintiff on administrative leave while she was on approved vacation in July 2023, contacted her husband to demand surrender of her duty weapon and department vehicle rather than contacting Plaintiff directly, and ultimately terminated her employment…” (Id. ¶ 272.) Like Plaintiff’s harassment claims against McManus, her IIED claim suffers from improper group pleading and fails to identify the nature and scope of McManus’s personal involvement in the above actions. The only specific factual support for Plaintiff’s IIED claim pertains to Defendants Allen and Reynosa, without ever referencing McManus beyond vague and conclusory allegations. (Id. ¶¶ 269-271.)

 

To the extent that Plaintiff’s IIED claim against McManus is based on harassing conduct, Plaintiff’s claim fails for the reasons outlined above. (See supra, Section I.A.) At minimum, the Complaint is entirely devoid of facts describing what “outrageous conduct” was committed by McManus himself or showing that McManus’s actions caused Plaintiff to suffer “severe or extreme” emotional distress. (Trerice, supra, 209 Cal.App.3d at p. 883.) To state a viable claim against McManus, Plaintiff is required to plead facts supporting each element of her IIED claim as it pertains to McManus. Vague group pleading and bare legal conclusions do not suffice.

 

Further, to the extent that Plaintiff’s IIED claim against McManus is based on managerial decisions, if at all, recovery for such conduct is likely barred by the workers’ compensation exclusivity doctrine. (Compl., ¶ 272.) Worker’s compensation is the exclusive remedy “if the injuries did arise out of and in the course of the employment… notwithstanding that the injury resulted from the intentional conduct of the employer, and even though the employer's conduct might be characterized as egregious.” (Shoemaker v. Myers (1990) 52 Cal.3d 1, 15.) That is, “[s]o long as the basic conditions of compensation are otherwise satisfied, and the employer's conduct neither contravenes fundamental public policy nor exceeds the risks inherent in the employment relationship, an employee's emotional distress injuries are subsumed under the exclusive remedy provisions of workers' compensation.” (Livitsanos v. Superior Court (1992) 2 Cal.4th 744, 754, internal citations omitted.) For example, “[e]motional distress caused by misconduct in employment relations involving […] promotions, demotions, criticism of work practices, negotiations as to grievances, is a normal part of the employment environment” and a claim for emotional distress based on such actions would be barred by workers’ compensation exclusivity. (Accardi v. Superior Court (1993) 17 Cal.App.4th 341, 352.)

 

Accordingly, as presently pled, Hayden fails to state a claim for IIED against McManus as a matter of law. Therefore, McManus’s demurrer to the eleventh cause of action in the Complaint is SUSTAINED with LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

II.       MOTION TO STRIKE – GRANTED

 

McManus moves to strike Plaintiff’s request for punitive damages from the Complaint. Punitive damages are recoverable in tort actions where¿“the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice.” (Cal. Civ. Code § 3294, subd. (a).) California law follows a heightened pleading standard for punitive damages— vague and conclusory allegations are insufficient. (G. D. Searle & Co. v. Superior Ct.¿(Ct. App. 1975) 49 Cal. App. 3d 22, 29.)

 

Here, Plaintiff is seeking punitive damages from McManus in connection with both of her harassment claims and her IIED claim. (Compl., ¶¶ 206, 220, 276.) Plaintiff alleges in a conclusory fashion that Defendants’ collective conduct was “willful, intentional, and malicious and done with intent to vex, injure, and annoy Plaintiff” and “in conscious disregard of plaintiff’s rights. (Ibid.) As described above, however, Plaintiff fails to identify what incidents or actions McManus personally participated in, as distinguished from the other defendants. Without any factual details identifying McManus’s actual conduct, Plaintiff’s boilerplate scienter-related allegations fail to overcome the heightened pleading requirement for punitive damages.

 

Therefore, McManus’s motion to strike is GRANTED.

DEFENDANT ANZHELA HONCHARENKO’S MOTION FOR RENEWED CONSIDERATION OF HER SPECIAL MOTION TO STRIKE THE COMPLAINT PURSUANT TO CODE OF CIVIL PROCEDURE SECTION 425.16

9/13/2023: Defendant Anzhela Honcharenko (“Defendant”) files a lawsuit for sexual battery, etc. against Plaintiff Alek Zeven (“Zeven”), who is a licensed California attorney and the principal and public face of Plaintiff Zeven Law Firm (collectively with Zeven, “Plaintiffs”) Anzhela Honcharenko v. Alek Zeven, et al. (Case No. 23VECV04042) (the “2023 Action”).[1]

 

Circa 8/2025: Defendant allegedly makes false statements concerning Zeven. Specifically, Defendant communicates to third parties that Zeven had engaged in sexual relations with his former client while acting as the client’s attorney. (Compl., ¶¶ 9-10.)

 

1/16/2026: The deposition of Yelena Zadoyen is taken in the 2023 Action. (Honcharenko Decl., ¶ 3.) Defendant attends and represents herself at the deposition, but without the assistance of a Ukranian interpreter. (Ibid.)

 

2/5/2026: In the 2023 Action, Plaintiffs file a motion for leave to file a Cross-Complaint, arising out of allegations that Defendant had published non-privileged, false statements to third parties involving Plaintiffs’ privacy interests and sensitive reputation material. (See Def.’s Req. for Judicial Notice in Support of Special Motion to Strike, 5/18/2026, Exh. B.) The court in the 2023 Action denies the motion, but notes that Plaintiffs could assert these claims in a separate lawsuit. (Id., Exh. F.)

 

5/5/2026: Plaintiff files the Complaint, alleging causes of action for:

1.   Defamation: Slander Per Se (Civ. Code, § 46)

2.   False Light

(See Minute Order, 6/12/2026 [adopting tentative ruling].)

TENTATIVE RULING: DEFENDANT ANZHELA HONCHARENKO’S MOTION FOR RENEWED CONSIDERATION OF HER SPECIAL MOTION TO STRIKE is DENIED.

 

MOTION FOR RENEWED CONSIDERATION

 

Defendant moves pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1008, subd. (b) for the renewed consideration of her previously denied special motion to strike on the grounds that new evidence was obtained on August 12, 2026, after the special motion to strike was ruled upon.

 

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 1008, subd. (b), “[a] party who originally made an application for an order which was refused in whole or part, or granted conditionally or on terms, may make a subsequent application for the same order upon new or different facts, circumstances, or law.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1008, subd. (b), italics added.) In effect, section 1008, subd. (b) concerns the “renewal” of a prior motion, seeking the same relief previously denied based on different factual or legal grounds. (See e.g. California Correctional Peace Officers Assn. v. Virga (2010) 181 Cal.App.4th 30, 45.) For this type of “renewed” motion, the moving party must show “by affidavit what application was made before, when and to what judge, what order or decisions were made, and what new or different facts, circumstances, or law are claimed to be shown.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1008, subd. (b).) The moving party is also required to “show diligence with a satisfactory explanation for not presenting the new or different information earlier.” (Even Zohar Construction & Remodeling, Inc. v. Bellaire Townhouses, LLC (2015) 61 Cal.4th 830, 833 (Even Zohar).)

 

Here, Defendant argues that the certified transcript of the January 16, 2026 deposition was unavailable to her at the time she filed her original special motion to strike because Defendant was “financially unable to purchase” the transcript and she only first obtained the transcript in August 12, 2026. (Mot., at p. 3.) Defendant contends that this deposition transcript provides evidence that Plaintiffs used formal discovery in the 2023 Action to inquire about Defendant’s statements to third parties related to Zeven’s “inappropriate relationships” with clients. (Id., at p. 4.) For example, in the deposition transcript, Zeven asks the witness whether Defendant had told the witness about Zeven’s inappropriate relationships with clients other than Defendant. (Honcharenko Decl., ¶ 7, Exh. C, at pp. 164:2-22, 336:2-22.)

 

The Court finds that Defendant fails to overcome her burden of demonstrating the existence of “new or different facts” based on the January 2026 deposition transcript. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1008, subd. (b).) The relevant deposition occurred in January 2026— four months prior to the filing of Defendant’s special motion to strike in May 2026. (Honcharenko Decl., ¶ 4.) Defendant admits that she was personally present at the deposition to represent herself in pro per (as is also reflected in the deposition transcript itself). (Id., ¶ 4, Exh. C.) Defendant argues that her imperfect English and lack of translation assistance impaired her ability to understand the scope of Zeven’s questioning as it occurred during the deposition. (Id., ¶ 5.) However, Defendant’s difficulty with English does not excuse her from diligently obtaining a copy of the deposition transcript, given that her presence at the deposition and her ability to understand the proceedings enough to offer basic objections at the time of questioning would have given her sufficient notice of the contents of the deposition such that she would have known she needed to promptly obtain a copy of the transcript. Even so, Defendant’s special motion to strike makes no reference to the contents of the January 2026 deposition as it pertains to her SLAPP theory and she did not request a leave to file a belated special motion to strike on the basis that she was still obtaining evidence that she knew or should have known might be relevant to the motion.

 

Additionally, Defendant declares that she could not obtain a certified transcript “because of financial hardship,” and only obtained the transcript in August 2026 (after the ruling on her special motion to strike) through the assistance of the California Court Reporters Board. (Honcharenko Decl., ¶¶ 6-7.) Even so, Defendant offers no legal authority for her contention that “financial hardship” excuses her from strict diligence in obtaining evidence that is otherwise available and known of. Notably, Defendant’s declaration is vague as to when she initially sought a copy of the deposition transcript or when she first sought assistance from the California Court Reporters Board. Defendant’s vague references to “financial hardship” fails to offer a “satisfactory explanation” as to why the deposition transcript could not have been obtained earlier. (Even Zohar, supra, 61 Cal.4th at p. 833.)

 

Even if Defendant had overcome her moving burden under Code of Civil Procedure section 1008, subd. (b), the underlying deposition testimony is insufficient to alter the Court’s original analysis on the First Prong of the special motion to strike. Plaintiffs’ slander per se and false light causes of action arise from allegations that Defendant published false statements to third parties about Zeven’s alleged sexual relations with an individual with whom he had an ongoing attorney-client relationship. (Compl., ¶¶ 9-10, 15-18, 21, 29.) While Plaintiffs allege that Defendant’s false statements were intended to advance her claims in litigation by “attempting to portray Zeven as engaging in a supposed pattern of sexual misconduct with clients,” Plaintiffs expressly allege that the statements underlying Defendant’s alleged liability were “made privately to third parties in a social setting and were not made in connection with any judicial proceeding, anticipated litigation activity, law-enforcement report, State Bar compliant, or other official proceeding.” (Id. ¶¶ 12, 14.)

 

While the deposition transcript reflects that Zeven questioned a witness about whether Defendant had made statements to her and other parties about his “inappropriate relationships with other clients,” the witness denied knowledge of any such communications. (Honcharenko Decl., Exh. C, at p. 164:2-23, 334:2-22.) The deposition involves a witness, Ms. Zadoyen, who seemingly was not involved with the alleged communications by Defendant in August 2025 which form the basis of the instant Complaint. According to the evidence submitted by Plaintiffs in support of their original opposition to the special motion to strike, Defendant’s false statements to the hairdresser of Oksana Popova, a former client of Zeven, form the basis of this Complaint. (Popova Decl., ¶¶ 2, 4-6.) Defendant’s motion for renewed consideration does not set forth “new or different” evidence to dispute that Defendant’s alleged comments to a hairdresser in August 2025 are protected petitioning activity. That the August 2025 statements were ultimately the subject of discovery in the 2023 Action does not mean that the statements themselves, which were made in a social setting outside the direct scope of the 2023 Action, are protected by the Anti-SLAPP statute. (Compl., ¶ 12.)

 

Finally, the Court notes that Defendant’s reply brief largely attempts to adjudicate the merits of Plaintiffs’ Complaint by attacking the credibility of the evidence submitted by Plaintiffs. However, these arguments are more relevant to Prong Two of Defendant’s special motion to strike, which the Court did not need to reach because Defendant’s motion failed on Prong One. Regardless, in evaluating an Anti-SLAPP motion, the court “neither ‘weigh[s] credibility [nor] compare[s] the weight of the evidence. Rather, [the court] accept[s] as true the evidence favorable to the plaintiff … and evaluate[s] the defendant's evidence only to determine if it has defeated that submitted by the plaintiff as a matter of law.’” (Soukup v. Law Offices of Herbert Hafif (2006) 39 Cal.4th 260, 269, fn. 11.) The sole evidentiary issue before the Court here is whether the January 2026 deposition transcript is sufficient to modify the Court’s original conclusion that Defendant’s Anti-SLAPP motion failed on Prong One. It is not.

 

Therefore, Defendant’s motion for renewed consideration of the Court’s June 12, 2026 ruling on Defendant’s special motion to strike is DENIED.
