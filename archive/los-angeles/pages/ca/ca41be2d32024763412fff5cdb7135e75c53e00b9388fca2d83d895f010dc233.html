Case Number: 25STCV33494    Hearing Date: September 21, 2026    Dept: 400

DEMURRER WITHOUT MOTION TO STRIKE

 

The Court tenders the following tentative decision in the matter Erika Kronk v. 8070 Laurelmont LLC, et al., Los Angeles County Superior Court case number 25STCV33494, set for hearing on September 21, 2026. 8070 Laurelmont LLC and Oliver Thornton (collectively, Defendants) demur to the first cause of action contained in Erika Kronk’s (Plaintiff) first amended complaint (FAC). Plaintiff opposes the demurrer. The demurrer is sustained. Plaintiff is granted 20 days’ leave to amend.

 

A.    Legal Standard

 

A demurrer is sustained where “[t]he pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (e).) “A demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the factual allegations in a complaint.” (Yalung v. State (2023) 98 Cal.App.5th 71, 80.) In reviewing a complaint’s legal sufficiency, a court will treat the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of law. (Esparza v. Kaweah Delta Dist. Hospital (2016) 3 Cal.App.5th 547, 552.) It is well settled that a “demurrer lies only for defects appearing on the face of the complaint[.]” (Stevens v. Superior Court (1999) 75 Cal.App.4th 594, 601.) “We not only treat the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but also give the complaint a reasonable interpretation, reading it as a whole and its parts in their context.” (Quelimane Co. v. Stewart Tit. Guaranty Co. (1998) 19 Cal.4th 26, 38.) For purposes of ruling on a demurrer, the complaint must be construed liberally by drawing reasonable inferences from the facts pleaded. (Wilner v. Sunset Life Ins. Co. (2000) 78 Cal.App.4th 952, 958.)

 

When ruling on a demurrer, a court may only consider the complaint’s allegations or matters judicially noticed. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) The Court may not consider any other extrinsic evidence or judge the credibility of the allegations pleaded or the difficulty a plaintiff may have in proving his allegations. (Ion Equipment Corporation v. Nelson (1980) 110 Cal.App.3d 868, 881.) A demurrer is properly sustained only when the complaint, liberally construed, fails to state facts sufficient to constitute any cause of action. (Kramer v. Intuit Inc. (2004) 121 Cal.App.4th 574, 578.)

 

When a demurrer is sustained the Court determines whether there is a reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment. (Blank, supra, 39 Cal.3d at p. 318.) When a plaintiff “has pleaded the general set of facts upon which his cause of action is based,” the court should give the plaintiff an opportunity to amend his complaint, since plaintiff should not “be deprived of his right to maintain his action on the ground that his pleadings were defective for lack of particulars.” (Reed v. Norman (1957) 152 Cal.App.2d 892, 900.)

 

B.     Discussion

 

Defendants demur to Plaintiff’s first cause of action for premises liability on the grounds that Plaintiff has not pled sufficient facts and the cause of action is uncertain. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subds. (e), (f).) The Court previously sustained Defendants’ demurrer to the original complaint on the same grounds.

 

Premises liability is a form of negligence. (Brooks v. Eugene Burger Management Corp. (1989) 215 Cal.App.3d 1611, 1619.) Thus, a plaintiff must prove the elements of negligence — duty, breach, causation, and damages. (Kesner v. Superior Court (Pneumo Abex, LLC) (2016) 1 Cal.5th 1132, 1158.) Plaintiff’s first theory regarding Defendants’ alleged duty is based on an alleged dangerous condition on their property. An owner of a property has a duty to warn of concealed hazards. (Rowland v. Christian (1968) 69 Cal.2d 108, 119.) However, a danger may be so obvious that any person may see it, making the condition itself a warning and placing no further duty on the defendant. (Kinsman v. Unocal Corp. (2005) 37 Cal.4th 659, 673.)

 

Here, Plaintiff alleges that Defendants owned and possessed the relevant property on which Plaintiff was injured. (FAC ¶ 10.) Plaintiff alleges that Defendants organized a party with food, alcohol, and other drugs. (Id. ¶ 11.) She alleges that she was seated on a pool bench beneath the jacuzzi when Defendant Evan Nigh fell or jumped from the ledge of the jacuzzi, landing on Plaintiff and injuring her. (Id. ¶ 15.) Plaintiff alleges that the design of the pool and jacuzzi created an unreasonable and foreseeable risk of harm that individuals would jump or fall from the jacuzzi and strike others below. (Id. ¶ 20.) The pool and jacuzzi lacked any barriers, signs, or warnings about this danger. (Id. ¶ 21.) However, Plaintiff does not allege that the relevant hazard was hidden from view. And Plaintiff emphasizes that the danger to her person arose from physical horseplay by Nigh along the jacuzzi ledge and Defendants’ failure to stop his reckless conduct. (Id. ¶¶ 17–18.) Thus, Plaintiff does not allege that the pool was dangerous due to its design, but rather because of the behavior of others at the party.

 

Second, Plaintiff argues that Defendants engaged in active negligent conduct as described in Hansen v. Richey (1965) 237 Cal.App.2d 475. There, the Court of Appeal held that the defendants could have engaged in negligent conduct by sponsoring a party on their property attended by over 100 youths who were unfamiliar with the premises. (Id. at p. 480.) It noted that the defendants could be expected to recognize that some of the youthful guests might display “flighty and immature inclinations.” (Ibid.) Defendants failed to prevent removal of barriers at the party which allowed individuals to access the pool area. (Ibid.) The pool was mostly empty, except for part of the deep end which had collected rainwater. (Id. at p. 476.) The plaintiff had fallen in the pool, sustained a skull fracture and drowned. (Ibid.)

 

Hansen does not support a finding that Defendants owed a duty in the present case. The hazard present in Hansen was much greater than that alleged here. Additionally, while the Court of Appeal noted that an expectation of “flighty and immature inclinations” by house guests might support a finding of a duty owed by the defendants, such an expectation arose from their youth. Here, the same expectation would need to be grounded in the potential use of intoxicants by guests. (FAC ¶ 11.) Yet “no social host who furnishes alcoholic beverages to any person may be held legally accountable for damages suffered by that person, or for injury to the person or property of, or death of, any third person, resulting from the consumption of those beverages.” (Id., § 1714, subd. (c).) Thus, section 1714 does not support a finding of duty in this case. Accordingly, Defendants’ demurrer is sustained.

 

C.     Conclusion

 

Defendants’ demurrer is sustained. Plaintiff is granted 20 days’ leave to amend.


Case Number: 26STCV15270    Hearing Date: September 21, 2026    Dept: 400

MOTION FOR ATTORNEY FEES

 

The Court tenders the following tentative decision in the matter Merrick Yzbick v. Studio City Universal Management LLC, et al., Los Angeles County Superior Court case number 26STCV15270, set for hearing on September 21, 2026. Vance Yzbick (Cross-Defendant) moves for an award of attorney’s fees in the amount of $37,531.25 against Studio City Universal Management LLC (Cross-Complainant). Cross-Complainant opposes the motion. The Court grants Cross-Defendant’s motion in the partial amount of $26,687.50.

 

A.    Legal Standard

 

“[A]s a general rule, attorney fees are not recoverable as costs unless they are authorized by statute or agreement.” (People ex rel. Dept. of Corporations v. Speedee Oil Change Systems, Inc. (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 424, 429.) “In any action on a contract, where the contract specifically provides that attorney’s fees and costs, which are incurred to enforce that contract, shall be awarded either to one of the parties or to the prevailing party, then the party who is determined to be the party prevailing on the contract, whether he or she is the party specified in the contract or not, shall be entitled to reasonable attorney’s fees in addition to other costs.” (Civ. Code, § 1717, subd. (a).) The court may determine fees pursuant to the section upon notice and motion by a party. The party prevailing on the contract is that “who recovered a greater relief in the action on the contract.” (Id., § 1717, subd. (b)(1).) The court may also determine that there is no prevailing party.

The attorney bears the burden of proof as to “reasonableness” of any fee claim. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (c)(5).) This burden requires competent evidence as to the nature and value of the services rendered. (Martino v. Denevi (1986) 182 Cal.App.3d 553, 559.) A plaintiff’s verified billing invoices are prima facie evidence that the costs, expenses, and services listed were necessarily incurred. (Hadley v. Krepel (1985) 167 Cal.App.3d 677, 682.) “In challenging attorney fees as excessive because too many hours of work are claimed, it is the burden of the challenging party to point to the specific items challenged, with a sufficient argument and citations to the evidence. General arguments that fees claimed are excessive, duplicative, or unrelated do not suffice.” (Lunada Biomedical v. Nunez (2014) 230 Cal.App.4th 459, 488.)

In determining whether the requested attorney’s fees are “reasonable,” the Court’s “first step involves the lodestar figure—a calculation based on the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the lawyer’s hourly rate. The lodestar figure may then be adjusted, based on consideration of facts specific to the case, in order to fix the fee at the fair market value for the legal services provided.” (Gorman v. Tassajara Development Corp. (2008) 178 Cal.App.4th 44, 92.) In determining whether to adjust the lodestar figure, the Court may consider the nature and difficulty of the litigation, the amount of money involved, the skill required and employed to handle the case, the attention given, the success or failure, and other circumstances in the case. (EnPalm LLC v. Teitler (2008) 162 Cal.App.4th 770, 774)

 

B.     Discussion

 

Cross-Defendant moves for an award of attorney’s fees based on his successful anti-SLAPP motion to strike pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16. A prevailing defendant on an anti-SLAPP motion “shall be entitled to recover [their] attorney’s fees and costs.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (c)(1).) The deadline to file and serve a separate motion for fees is the same deadline as filing a notice of appeal. (Rules of Court, rule 3.1702(b).) “Under rule 8.104(a) and (f), a notice of appeal must be filed on or before 60 days after service of a document entitled ‘Notice of Entry’ of the order granting the anti-SLAPP motion by the superior court clerk or a party; otherwise, the notice of appeal must be filed on or before 180 days after the entry of the order granting the anti-SLAPP motion.” (Mallard v. Progressive Choice Insurance Co. (2010) 188 Cal.App.4th 531, 545.)

 

Here, the Court entered an order granting Cross-Defendant’s special motion to strike on July 28, 2026. The notice of entry of order was filed on August 3. Thus, the deadline for filing a separate motion for fees is October 2, 60 days after the notice was filed. Cross-Defendant filed the present motion on August 3. Thus, the present motion is timely.

 

Counsel alleges an hourly rate of $625. (Teshale Decl. ¶ 8.) This is based on their seven years of experience practicing law and four years of specialized experience representing tenants. (Id. ¶¶ 4, 6.) The Court credits this hourly rate and will consider it when determining whether Counsel’s hourly estimates for work performed are reasonable.

 

Counsel claims to have spent 34.7 hours preparing, preserving, and prosecuting the anti-SLAPP motion, 0.6 hours preparing the notice of entry of order and proposed judgment, and 4 hours preparing the present fee motion, and anticipates spending 3.4 hours to review Cross-Complainant’s opposition and draft a reply brief. (Id. ¶¶ 33–36.) Cross-Complainant challenges these hourly totals as unreasonable; however, its characterization of Counsel’s claimed hours is inaccurate. Having reviewed Counsel’s submitted billing entries, the Court finds the amount of time claimed by Counsel to be reasonable. (Id. ¶ 38.) The anti-SLAPP motion raised complex and unique issues, warranting research and careful drafting even by an attorney with years of experience. The Court also credits Counsel for the time spent drafting the present fees motion, preparing the notice of order, and responding to Cross-Complainant’s opposition. Thus, the Court credits Counsel with 42.7 hours.

 

Counsel also requests a 1.5 lodestar multiplier. This is based on Counsel’s contingent fee agreement with Cross-Defendant. As Counsel properly identifies, their only way of obtaining fees in opposing the anti-SLAPP motion was through a fee award from the motion being granted. But this fact does not support the award of a multiplier. Counsel chose to bring the anti-SLAPP motion, and took on that extra work, knowing that attorney’s fees were available if the motion was granted. Counsel elected to bring the motion when it was not required. A multiplier is awarded in contingent cases because an attorney’s contingent fee award is highly variable, adding to the riskiness of the representation. Not only does the attorney receive no payment if the client loses; they receive a low payment if the client only wins a small amount. That same uncertainty is not present here, where counsel was guaranteed full compensation for the time spent on the anti-SLAPP motion if they prevailed. Thus, the Court does not award a lodestar multiplier. Accordingly, Cross-Defendant’s motion is granted in the partial amount of $26,687.50.

 

C.     Conclusion

           

The Court grants Cross-Defendant’s motion in the partial amount of $26,687.50.

Case Number: 26STCV17000    Hearing Date: September 21, 2026    Dept: 400

MOTION TO STRIKE WITHOUT DEMURRER

 

The Court tenders the following tentative decision in the matter Alfredo Salmeron v. Estela Alvarado, et al., Los Angeles County Superior Court case number 26STCV17000, set for hearing on September 21, 2026. Estela Alvarado (Defendant) moves to strike Alfredo Salmeron’s (Plaintiff) requests for punitive damages contained in his complaint. Plaintiff opposes the motion. The motion is denied.

 

A.    Legal Standard

 

The Court may, upon a motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a).) The Court may also strike all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the Court. (Id., § 436, subd. (b).) The grounds for a motion to strike are that the pleading has irrelevant, false or improper matter, or has not been drawn or filed in conformity with laws. (Id., § 436.) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (Id., § 437.)

 

When a motion to strike is granted, the Court determines whether there is a reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) When a plaintiff “has pleaded the general set of facts upon which his cause of action is based,” the court should give the plaintiff an opportunity to amend his complaint, since plaintiff should not “be deprived of his right to maintain his action on the ground that his pleadings were defective for lack of particulars.” (Reed v. Norman (1957) 152 Cal.App.2d 892, 900.)

 

B.     Discussion

 

Defendant moves to strike Plaintiff’s prayer for punitive damages and all paragraphs referencing punitive damages in the complaint. A plaintiff may recover punitive damages where it is proven by clear and convincing evidence that the defendant has been guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice. (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (a).) Malice is defined as conduct intended to injure the plaintiff or despicable conduct by a defendant acting with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights and safety of others. (Id., § 3294, subd. (c)(1).) Oppression means despicable conduct subjecting a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of their rights. (Id., § 3294, subd. (c)(2).) And fraud refers to intentional misrepresentations or concealment of a material fact known to the defendant. (Id., § 3294, subd. (c)(3).)

 

A plaintiff attempting to prove malice or oppression must offer clear and convincing evidence of despicable conduct. (Mock v. Michigan Millers Mutual Ins. Co. (1992) 4 Cal.App.4th 306, 328.) This refers to conduct “so vile, base, contemptible, miserable, wretched or loathsome that it would be looked down upon and despised by ordinary decent people.” (Id. at p. 331.) A claim for punitive damages is insufficient where it lacks a factual assertion supporting the conclusion that a party acted with oppression, malice, or fraud. (Smith v. Superior Court (1992) 10 Cal.App.4th 1033, 1042.)

 

Here, Plaintiff alleges that Defendant’s conduct has been willful, oppressive, and malicious. (Compl. ¶ 28.) He alleges that Defendant acted intentionally and with a willful disregard of his rights so that Plaintiff would vacate the subject property. (Ibid.) Specifically, Plaintiff alleges that Defendant allowed habitability defects to persist in their property despite being notified of their existence. These included “water intrusion, severe and ongoing rodent infestations, insect infestations, plumbing issues, defective walls, defective ceiling issues, defective flooring issues,” and “pervasive insect and pest issues.” (Id. ¶¶ 5–6.) Plaintiff notified Defendant of the uninhabitable conditions but Defendant failed to make necessary repairs and repeatedly cancelled maintenance requests. (Id. ¶ 9.) Plaintiff further alleges that the Los Angeles Housing Department (LAHD) conducted a site inspection on October 3, 2018, and issued a notice of order to comply. (Id. ¶ 16.) LAHD referred the case to its enforcement section and that case remained open and unresolved at the time of Plaintiff’s filing the complaint. (Ibid.) Defendant failed to make necessary repairs despite being notified by the LAHD. (Id. ¶ 17.)

 

Defendant argues that Plaintiff’s complaint does not include specific facts. That is not the case. As discussed above, Plaintiff identifies multiple habitability issues with Defendant’s property that went unabated despite repeated notification by Plaintiff and the LAHD. From these facts, it is possible for a jury to find that Defendant engaged in despicable conduct and showed a disregard for Plaintiff’s rights and safety. At the very least, it cannot be said that Plaintiff’s requests for punitive damages constitute irrelevant matters subject to a motion to strike. Indeed, Plaintiff alleges that Defendant knew deplorable conditions existed and were dangerous to Plaintiff and possessed the opportunity, time, and means to rectify them but deliberately and intentionally failed and refused to make necessary repairs. (Id. ¶ 12.) Thus, Plaintiff has pled sufficient facts to request punitive damages. Accordingly, Defendant’s motion is denied.

 

Conclusion:

 

The motion to strike is denied.
