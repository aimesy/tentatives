Case Number: 23STCV30161    Hearing Date: September 22, 2026    Dept: 511

Tentative Ruling

 

Judge Upinder S. Kalra, Department 511

 

HEARING DATE:   September 22, 2026                                       

 

CASE NAME:           Jazmin Copeland v. State of California Department of Public Health, et al.

 

CASE NO.:                23STCV30161

 

MOTION TO QUASH SECOND AMENDED COMPLAINT

 

MOVING PARTY:  Defendant California Department of Social Services; Defendant Emergency Medical Services Authority

 

RESPONDING PARTY(S): Plaintiff Jazmin Copeland

 

REQUESTED RELIEF:

 

1.      An order quashing the second amended complaint.

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

1.      Motion to quash second amended complaint are DENIED.

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

 

On December 11, 2023, Plaintiff Jazmin Copeland (Plaintiff) filed a complaint against Defendants State of California Department of Public Health (DPH), NRS Staffing LLC (NRS), and Evolve Healthcare, Inc. (Evolve) (sometimes collectively Defendants) with 10 causes of action for: (1) discrimination based on race; (2) harassment based on race; (3) discrimination based on sex; (4) sexual harassment; (5) failure to maintain a discrimination free environment; (6) retaliation; (7) violation of Lab. Code § 1102.5; (8) failure to pay hourly and overtime wages; (9) failure to provide meal periods of compensation in lieu thereof; and (10) failure to provide rest periods or compensation in lieu thereof. 

 

According to the complaint, Plaintiff alleges she was subjected to ongoing racial discrimination, wage-and-hour violations, and a hostile work environment by coworkers and supervisors at the TVRC facility, including being isolated, falsely accused, and denied legally required breaks and overtime pay. She further alleges that her direct supervisor engaged in repeated, severe sexual harassment and inappropriate conduct—including explicit comments, unwanted touching, and showing graphic photos—and that management failed to take corrective action after she reported it. After making internal complaints about discrimination, harassment, and labor violations, Plaintiff claims she was retaliated against and ultimately terminated under a pretextual justification. 

 

On February 1, 2024, Plaintiff filed a first amended complaint (FAC) against the same parties with the same causes of action.  

 

On March 15, 2024, DPH filed a motion for change of venue which the court DENIED. 

 

On March 18, 2024, DPH filed a demurrer to the FAC which the court SUSTAINED, in part, with leave to amend. 

 

On June 25, 2024, DPH filed an answer. 

 

On July 24, 2025, the parties filed a stipulated protective order which the court GRANTED. 

 

On November 20, 2025, Plaintiff filed a motion for leave to file a second amended complaint (SAC) which the court GRANTED. 

 

On January 7, 2026, Plaintiff filed the operative SAC. Plaintiff added Defendants California Department of Social Services (CDSS), California Department of General Services (CDGS), and California Emergency Medical Services Authority (EMSA). Plaintiff expanded allegations to include those obtained via discovery as well as an amended administrative exhaustion allegation. 

 

On March 13, 2026, CDSS filed a motion to quash the SAC. 

 

On March 19, 2026, EMSA filed a motion to quash the SAC. 

 

On April 10, 2026, CDGS filed a demurrer to the SAC which the court OVERRULED.

 

On May 20, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition to the CDSS’ motion to quash. On May 27, 2026, CDSS filed a reply.

 

On July 7, 2026, CDGS filed an answer.

 

On July 30, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition to EMSA’s motion to quash. On August 5, 2026, EMSA filed a reply.

 

LEGAL STANDARD:

 

“When the plaintiff is ignorant of the name of a defendant, he must state that fact in the complaint...and such defendant may be designated in any pleading or proceeding by any name, and when his true name is discovered, the pleading or proceeding must be amended accordingly.”  Code Civ. Proc., § 474.  “Improper service of a defendant under section 474 may be attacked by a motion to quash.”  McClatchy v. Coblentz, Patch, Duffy & Bass, LLP (2016) 247 Cal.App.4th 368, 375 (McClatchy). 

 

ANALYSIS:

 

CDSS and EMSA contend relief is proper because Plaintiff knew they were possible defendants in this matter prior to May 3, 2022 and so the statute of limitations had run before she amended her complaint to add them in. As to CDSS, Plaintiff’s testimony indicates she knew CDSS was on site and EMSA was on her timesheets. Finally, they contend they suffered prejudice due to Plaintiff’s delay because they could not conduct preserve evidence like Plaintiff could.

 

Plaintiff argues that her generalized awareness that outside staffing entities does not show that she knew CDSS or EMSA had contractual, supervisory, or operational structure involvement until the October 22, 2025 document production. She adds that she had no duty to investigate what the initials “EMSA” meant and that simply being aware of the organizations was not actual knowledge of her claims against them.

 

CDSS and EMSA reply that Plaintiff’s arguments miss the mark since the standard is actual ignorance of them as defendants and not ignorance of a cause of action. They add that she failed to address prejudice.

 

The only relevant inquiry is whether the plaintiff “had actual knowledge of the basic facts giving rise to her claim” against the defendant. (General Motors Corp. v. Superior Court (1996) 48 Cal.App.4th 580, 588 (General Motors); McClatchy, supra, 247 Cal.App.4th at pp. 371-372.) “In keeping with this liberal interpretation of section 474, it is now well established that even though the plaintiff knows of the existence of the defendant sued by a fictitious name, and even though the plaintiff knows the defendant’s actual identity (that is, his name), the plaintiff is ‘ignorant’ within the meaning of the statute if he lacks knowledge of that person’s connection with the case or with his injuries.” (General Motors, supra, 48 Cal.App.4th at p. 593-594; see also McClatchy, supra, 247 Cal.App.4th at p. 371-372 [noting this procedure (Section 474) is only available when the plaintiff is actually ignorant of the facts establishing a cause of action against the party to be substituted in as a doe defendant.]) “The fact that the plaintiff had the means to obtain knowledge is irrelevant.” (Id. at p. 594.) “Section 474 allows a plaintiff in good faith to delay suing particular persons as named defendants until he has sufficient knowledge of sufficient facts to cause a reasonable person to believe liability is probable.” (Id. [italics omitted].)

 

“[T]he plaintiff’s knowledge must be tested in the context of the theory of her claim against the new defendant.” (General Motors, supra, 48 Cal.App.4th at p. 597 [citation omitted.]) Then, courts consider what facts the plaintiff must show for that claim to be viable against the defendant. (Ibid.) Third, courts consider the means by which the plaintiff would prove that claim. (Ibid.)

 

Plaintiff’s claims are rooted in violations as an employer. (Compl. ¶ 7, FAC ¶ 7, SAC ¶ 6.) Thus, under General Motors and McClatchy, Plaintiff needed to be actually ignorant of facts that CDSS and EMSA could be joint employers subject to liability. After reviewing the evidence submitted by the parties, it appears Plaintiff did not have actual knowledge of facts that CDSS or EMSA could be joint employers until after the October 22, 2025 production.

 

As to CDSS, the evidence shows Plaintiff was aware of their presence at the TVRC. (Declaration of Nancy Droege ¶ 2, Exhibit A [deposition transcript excerpts.]) This evidence shows Plaintiff knew that two people at the site worked for CDSS. (Ibid. 181:18-22 [Adriana worked for CDSS.] 182:12-183:5 [David had a social services badge.]) Plaintiff previously alleged that Adriana/Adrianna was the alleged on-site manager for TVRA (Compl. ¶ 12) and David was allegedly taking over that role. (Compl. ¶ 20.) However, Plaintiff’s evidence shows she (and her counsel) did not know the extent of the contractual relationships between CDSS and the other defendants to impose liability on CDSS as an employer. (Declaration of C. Stephen Love ¶¶ 2, 3, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10.) Indeed, that evidence supports Plaintiff’s position that simply knowing CDSS was present at the TVRC did not mean Plaintiff actually knew CDSS could be liable as a joint employer. (Ibid.) Therefore, Plaintiff did not have actual knowledge of the facts that she had a viable claim against CDSS at the time she filed her complaint..

 

The analysis for EMSA is similar. To start, Plaintiff did not know what EMSA meant on her timesheets. (Copeland Decl. ¶ 4.) Her attorneys explained the relationship to her after October 22, 2025. (Id. at ¶¶ 8-9.) While she knew that various State agencies and private companies were involved at the TVRC, she did not know which one had authority of her. (Id. at ¶ 10.) This is corroborated by counsel’s declaration that he did not fully understand the joint employer relationship issue until after receiving the various contracts produced in discovery. (Declaration of C. Stephen Love ¶¶ 2, 3, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10.) Thus, Plaintiff did not have actual knowledge of the facts that she had a viable claim against EMSA at the time she filed her complaint.[1]

 

Accordingly, the court DENIES the motions to quash.

 

CONCLUSION:

 

            For the foregoing reasons, the Court decides the pending motion as follows:

 

1.      Motion to quash second amended complaint are DENIED.

Moving party is to give notice.

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Tentative Ruling

 

Judge Upinder S. Kalra, Department 511

 

HEARING DATE:   September 22, 2026                                       

 

CASE NAME:           All Inclusive Environmental Services, Inc. v. J-CM Services, Inc., et al.

 

CASE NO.:                25STCV37076

 

MOTION FOR RELIEF FROM DEFAULT

 

MOVING PARTY:  Defendants Amerco Real Estate Company and 8201 Santa Fe (CA), LLC

 

RESPONDING PARTY(S): None

 

REQUESTED RELIEF:

 

1.      An order granting relief from the clerk’s entry of default entered on April 27, 2026.

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

1.      Motions for relief from default are GRANTED;

2.      Amerco and 8201 to file their answer within 7 days of this ruling.

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

 

On December 18, 2025, Plaintiff All Inclusive Environmental Services, Inc. (Plaintiff) filed a complaint against Defendants J-CM Services, Inc. (J-CM), Amerco Real Estate Company (Amerco), and 8201 Santa Fe (CA), LLC (8201) with causes of action for: (1) breach of contract (J-CM); (2) foreclosure of mechanic’s lien; (3) stop notice; (4) account stated (J-CM); and (5) reasonable value of labor and material furnished (J-CM).

 

According to the complaint, this dispute concerns a remediation project at 8201 Santa Fe Avenue, Huntington Park, California 90255. Plaintiff alleges it was a licensed contractor and had a valid subcontract with J-CM. Plaintiff alleges it performed under the subcontract but J-CM breached. Plaintiff also alleges it has a valid mechanic’s lien for its services.

 

On February 11, 2026, J-CM filed an answer.

 

LEGAL STANDARD:

 

“The court may, upon any terms as may be just, relieve a party or his or her legal representative from a judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding taken against him or her through his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect.” (Code Civ. Proc., section 473, subd. (b).) “Application for this relief shall be accompanied by a copy of the answer or other pleading proposed to be filed therein, otherwise the application shall not be granted, and shall be made within a reasonable time, in no case exceeding six months, after the judgment, dismissal, order, or other proceeding was taken.” (Ibid.)¿¿ 

¿¿ 

ANALYSIS:

 

8201 and Amerco contend relief is mandatory because their failure to timely respond to the complaint was due to their general counsel’s error. The court has not received an opposition.

 

CCP section 473(b) provides that when an application for relief is made no more than six months after entry of dismissal and is accompanied by an attorney’s sworn affidavit attesting to his or her mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect, the court shall set aside a dismissal entered against the attorney’s client, unless the court finds that the default or dismissal was not in fact caused by the attorney’s mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect. (Code Civ. Proc., § 473(b).) “The court shall, whenever relief is granted based on an attorney's affidavit of fault, direct the attorney to pay reasonable compensatory legal fees and costs to opposing counsel or parties.”¿¿(Ibid.)

 

Where an “attorney¿affidavit of fault” is filed, there is no requirement that the¿attorney's¿mistake, inadvertence, or neglect be excusable. Relief¿must¿be granted even where the default or dismissal resulted from¿inexcusable¿neglect by defendant's¿attorney. (Robert E. Weil, et al., California Practice Guide: Civil Procedure Before Trial ¶ 5:495 (2018).)¿ 

 

Here, relief is warranted. First, the motion is timely. Default was entered April 27, 2026 and this motion was filed on June 10, 2026 which is within six months of entry of default. Second, Amerco and 8201 provided an attorney declaration from their general counsel indicating the mistaken belief that co-defendant J-CM would indemnify them without further action required. (Declaration of Randal Studer ¶ 6.) Counsel obtained litigation counsel after being informed of the defaults. (Id. at ¶ 7.) The mandatory provisions of CCP § 473(b) apply to in-house counsel. (Gutierrez v. G & M Oil Co., Inc. (2010) 184 Cal.App.4th 551, 561, 564.) Finally, 8201 and Amerco provided their proposed answer.

 

Accordingly, the court GRANTS 8201’s and Amerco’s motion for relief from default.

 

CONCLUSION:

 

            For the foregoing reasons, the Court decides the pending motion as follows:

 

1.      Motions for relief from default are GRANTED;

2.      Amerco and 8201 to file their answer within 7 days of this ruling.

Moving party is to give notice.

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Case Number: 26STCV02231    Hearing Date: September 22, 2026    Dept: 511

Tentative Ruling

 

Judge Upinder S. Kalra, Department 511

 

HEARING DATE:   September 22, 2026                                       

 

CASE NAME:           Gupreet “Pete” Sahani v. Brand Force One, LLC, et al.

 

CASE NO.:                26STCV02231

 

MOTION TO COMPEL ARBITRATION

 

MOVING PARTY:  Defendants Brand Force One, LLC; Juggernaut One LLC; SVJ One LLC; and Graftan Darnall

 

RESPONDING PARTY(S): Plaintiff Gurpreet “Pete” Sahani

 

REQUESTED RELIEF:

 

1.      An order compelling this matter o arbitration and staying proceedings.

TENTATIVE RULING:

 

1.      Motion to compel arbitration is GRANTED as to Brand Force One, LLC;

2.      This action is STAYED as to Juggernaut One LLC, SVJ One LLC, and Graftan Darnall.

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

 

On January 22, 2026, Plaintiff Gurpreet “Pete” Sahani (Plaintiff) filed a complaint against Defendants Brand Force One, LLC (Brand Force); Juggernaut One LLC (Juggernaut); SVJ One LLC (SVJ); and Graftan Darnall (Darnall) (sometimes collectively Defendants) with causes of action for: (1) failure to pay earned wages; (2) failure to pay all earned wages upon discharge; (3) breach of written employment contract; (4) retaliation [Lab. Code § 1102.5]; (5) retaliation [Lab. Code § 98.6]; and (6) wrongful termination in violation of public policy.

 

According to the complaint, Plaintiff worked as Defendants’ Senior Vice Present of Supply Chain and Expansion from late January 2025 through December 1, 2025. He alleges that after raising concern about his earned bonus, he was terminated.

 

On March 19, 2026, Defendants filed an answer.

 

On June 29, 2026, Defendants filed the instant motion to compel arbitration. On September 9, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition. On September 15, 2026, Defendants filed a reply. On September 16, 2026, Plaintiff filed a notice of supplemental authority in support of opposition.[1]

 

LEGAL STANDARD:

 

As a threshold matter,¿the parties do¿not dispute¿that the Federal Arbitration Act (FAA) applies to this matter. (Declaration of Bahare Mehrinfar (Mehrinfar Decl.) ¶ 15, Exhibit 1.) Thus, the FAA applies. 

 

The FAA, while a federal statute, applies in California courts and requires state courts to enforce arbitration agreements as required by the federal common law developed under the FAA. (See¿Southland Corp. v. Keating¿(1984) 465 U.S. 1, 15-16;¿Broughton v. Cigna¿Healthplans¿(1999) 21 Cal.4th 1066, 1074-78, superseded by statute on another ground as¿stated¿in¿Ferguson v. Corinthian Colleges, Inc.¿(9th Cir. 2013) 733 F.3d 928, 937.).) The FAA preempts and invalidates state law and state judicial decisions that disfavor arbitration or require arbitration provisions to pass higher scrutiny. (Southland Corp.,¿supra, at p. 12;¿Perry v. Thomas¿(1987) 482 U.S. 483, 490.) If the parties¿designate¿the FAA applies, then California arbitration law is preempted. (See, e.g.,¿Rodriguez v. American Techs., Inc.¿(2006) 136 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1121-1122.)¿However, courts have found that where the FAA is found not to apply, the California Arbitration Act (Code Civ. Proc. § 1280 et seq.) applies. (See¿Valencia v. Smyth¿(2010) 185 Cal.App.4th 153, 178 (Valencia).)¿¿¿¿¿¿ 

¿¿¿¿¿ 

¿A court’s inquiry is limited to a determination of (1) whether a valid arbitration agreement exists and (2) whether the arbitration agreement covers the dispute. (9 U.S.C. § 4;¿Chiron Corp. v. Ortho Diagnostics Systems, Inc.¿(9th Cir. 2000) 207 F.3d 1126, 1130;¿Howsam v. Dean Witter Reynolds, Inc.¿(2002) 537 U.S. 79, 84; see¿Simula, Inc. v. Autoliv, Inc.¿(9th Cir. 1999) 175 F.3d 716, 720 [if the finding is affirmative on both counts the FAA requires the Court to enforce the¿arbitration agreement in accordance with its terms]; see¿Omar v. Ralphs Grocery Co.¿(2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 955, 961 [In determining the enforceability of an arbitration agreement, the court first considers “two ‘gateway issues’ of arbitrability: (1) whether there was an agreement to arbitrate between the parties, and (2) whether the agreement covered the dispute at issue”]¿Lacayo v. Cataline Restaurant Group Inc.¿(2019) 38 Cal.App.5th 244, 257 (Lacayo) [Where moving party meets initial burden, “the party opposing arbitration must prove by a preponderance of the evidence any defense to the petition”].)¿¿¿¿¿¿ 

¿¿¿ 

The general rule is that the FAA governs all agreements to arbitrate in contracts “involving interstate commerce.” (Higgins v. Superior Court¿(2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 1238, 1247.) The term “involving” commerce “is broad and is indeed the functional equivalent of “affecting’ commerce.” (Allied-Bruce Terminix Companies, Inc. v. Dobson¿(1995) 513 U.S. 265, 273-274.) The U.S. Supreme Court has held that this broad interpretation includes employment contracts. (See¿Circuit City Stores v. Adams¿(2001) 532 U.S. 105, 106.) The defendant bears the burden of proving applicability of the FAA by showing that its activities¿constitute¿interstate commerce. (Hoover v. Am. Income Life Ins. Co.¿(2012) 206 Cal.App.4th 1193, 1207.) Failure to¿demonstrate¿that the employment agreement affects interstate commerce¿renders¿the FAA inapplicable. (See¿Lane v. Francis Capital Management LLC¿(2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 676, 687-688 (Lane);¿Woolls v. Superior Court¿(2005) 127 Cal.App.4th 197, 212.)¿¿¿¿ 

¿¿¿ 

Even where the FAA governs the interpretation of arbitration clauses, California law governs whether an arbitration agreement has been formed in the first instance. (Baker v. Osborne Development Corp.¿(2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 884, 893.)¿¿¿ 

 

ANALYSIS:

 

Defendants contend the court should compel arbitration of this matter based on an arbitration agreement (Agreement) that Plaintiff executed, there is no unconscionability preventing enforcement, and the Agreement covers all Defendants.

 

  1. Existence of Arbitration Agreement¿¿ 

 

¿“The party¿seeking¿to compel arbitration bears the burden of proving the existence of an arbitration agreement, while the party opposing the petition bears the burden of¿establishing¿a defense to the agreement’s enforcement.” ’”¿ (Beco v. Fast Auto Loans¿(2022) 86 Cal.App.5th 292, 302.)¿ To¿determine¿the existence of an arbitration agreement, the court uses “a three-step burden-shifting process.”¿ (Iyere¿v. Wise Auto Group¿(2023) 87 Cal.App.5th 747, 755.)¿ “The arbitration proponent must first recite verbatim, or provide a copy of, the alleged agreement.¿ [Citations.]¿ A movant can bear this¿initial¿burden ‘by attaching a copy of the arbitration agreement purportedly bearing the opposing party’s signature.’”¿ (Ibid.¿[internal citations omitted].)¿ “If the movant bears its initial burden, the burden shifts to the party opposing arbitration to identify a factual dispute as to the agreement’s existence¿. . . .”¿ (Ibid.)¿ If the opposing party meets its burden to “submit sufficient evidence to create a factual dispute” as to the existence of the agreement, the burden shifts back to the arbitration proponent, who¿retains¿the ultimate burden of proving its existence by a preponderance of the evidence.¿ (Ibid.) 

 

Here, there is no dispute an Agreement exists. Instead, there is a dispute as to who it covers.

 

Generally, “only a party to an arbitration agreement is bound by or may enforce the agreement.” (Thomas v. Westlake (2012) 204 Cal.App.4th 605, 613.) One exception to this general rule provides that “when a plaintiff alleges a defendant acted as an agent of a party to an arbitration agreement, the defendant may enforce the agreement even though the defendant is not a party thereto.” (Id. at p. 614; but see Barsegian v. Kessler & Kessler (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 446, 451-452 [requiring more than bare allegations of agency].)  

 

An intended third-party beneficiary can also enforce an arbitration agreement. (Bouton v. USAA Casualty Ins. Co.¿(2008) 167 Cal.App.4th 412, 424.)

 

Here, Defendants can compel arbitration under the Agreement, in part.

 

First, it is undisputed that Brand Force can compel arbitration as the signatory. (Mehrinfar Decl., Exhibit 1.) However, Darnall, SVJ, and Juggernaut cannot use the plead agency theory to compel arbitration because they explicitly deny this theory in their answer. (Barsegian, supra, 215 Cal.App.4th at p. 452-453.) They also advance their separateness in their own evidence. (Declaration of Nick Dickinson ¶ 2.)

 

Second, the remaining Defendants are not identified third party beneficiaries – Brand Force’s clients are. (Mehrinfar Decl., Exhibit 1, at item 1 [“This Agreement is intended to also inure to the benefit of Company’s clients for whom you performed work for to the extent an employment dispute arises between you and such client and involves any arbitrable claims under this Agreement.”]) The remaining Defendants are not Brand Force’s clients. The language Defendants rely on to show they are intended beneficiaries concern covered claims, not third-party beneficiary status. (Id. at item 2(a).)

 

Finally, equitable estoppel does not apply because Plaintiff’s claims are not inextricably bound up with the contract containing the arbitration agreement – i.e., the Agreement. (Goldman v. KPMG, LLP (2009) 173 Cal.App.4th 209, 220-221.)

 

Accordingly, there is an enforceable agreement to arbitrate as to Brand Force but not as to Juggernaut, SJV, or Darnall.

 

      ii.            Application to Current Dispute

There is no dispute that the Agreement covers Plaintiff’s employment claims in this case. (Mehrinfar Decl., Exhibit 1, item 2(a).)

 

Accordingly, the Agreement covers this dispute.

 

    iii.            Defenses to Enforceability

In¿Armendariz,¿the California Supreme Court¿stated¿that when¿determining¿whether an arbitration agreement was unconscionable, there is both a procedural and a substantive element. (Armendariz v. Foundation Health¿Psychcare¿Service, Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 82, 114 (Armendariz)).¿

 

a.      Procedural Unconscionability

Plaintiff argues the Agreement is procedurally unconscionable because Brand Force drafted it, controlled Plaintiff’s access to sign it, provided it post-hire, and there was no opt-out provision.

 

Defendants reply there is no evidence of surprise or oppression since the Agreement is a standalone document with a conspicuous title and the terms are written in plain language. They add that the Agreement was not a condition of employment, explicitly said so, and that Plaintiff’s mistaken belief it was a condition of employment does not make it so.

 

Courts¿determine¿whether an agreement is procedurally unconscionable by looking at surprise and oppression. Oppression is an “inequality of bargaining power, when one party has no real power to negotiate or a meaningful choice. Surprise occurs when the allegedly unconscionable provision is¿hidden.”¿(Carmona v. Lincoln Millennium Car Wash, Inc.¿(2014) 226 Cal.App.4th¿74, 84 (Carmona).) Examples of contracts that are procedurally unconscionable are contracts of adhesion, which is a “standardized contract, which, imposed and drafted by the party of superior bargaining strength, relegates to the subscribing party only the opportunity to adhere to the¿contract or reject it.” (Armendariz, supra, at p. 113). “The circumstances relevant to establishing oppression include, but are not limited to (1) the amount of time the party is given to consider the proposed contract; (2) the amount and type of pressure exerted on the party to sign the proposed contract; (3) the length of the proposed contract and the length and complexity of the challenged provision; (4) the education and experience of the party; and (5) whether the party’s review of the proposed contract was aided by an attorney. (OTO, L.L.C. v. Kho¿(2019) 8 Cal.5th 111, 126-27 (OTO).)¿

 

Here, there is minimal procedural unconscionability. Critically, the Agreement was voluntary. (Mehrinfar Decl., Exhibit 1.) It states in bold at the end of the first paragraph: “I understand that my decision to enter into this Agreement is wholly voluntary and that my entering into this Agreement is not required by Company as a condition of employment.” (Ibid.) It follows that Plaintiff was not obligated to execute the Agreement. So, there was no need for an opt-out provision. However, Defendants’ own evidence demonstrates reasonable confusion since the Agreement is part of an onboarding task list that states the document “needs signing” and provides only one option to sign. (Mehrinfar Decl., ¶¶ 11, 12.)

 

Thus, there is minimal procedural unconscionability.

 

b.      Substantive Unconscionability

Plaintiff argues the Agreement is substantively unconscionable because there’s an uncertain decisionmaker conflict concerning challenging enforceability (i.e., delegation clause), overbroad non-signatory scope of Agreement, overbroad PAGA waivers, and lack of mutuality when considered with the concurrently-signed confidentiality agreement that allows only Brand Force to seek injunctive relief in court. Plaintiff argues these provisions permeate the Agreement so the court cannot sever them.

 

Defendants reply that the confidentiality agreement cannot render the Agreement substantively unconscionable since they do not refer to each other or require each other to resolve ambiguous terms. Additionally, the waiver provisions are severable and follow the law. Finally, the non-signatory scope is not overly broad, Plaintiffs allegations demonstrate the Agreement covers all Defendants, and Plaintiff is equitably estopped from asserting otherwise.

 

“Substantive unconscionability pertains to the fairness of an agreement's actual terms and to assessments of whether they are overly harsh or one-sided.” (Carmona, supra, at p. 85). There are five minimum substantive requirements to an enforceable arbitration agreement: (1) neutral arbitrators, (2) more than minimal discovery, (3) written award sufficient for judicial review, (4) all types of relief otherwise available in court, and (5) no unreasonable costs or fees as a condition of access. (Armendariz, supra, at p.102.) When there is little procedural unconscionability, a party opposing arbitration must show substantial substantive unconscionability. (Id. at 114.)¿¿

 

Here, the Agreement is not unconscionable.

 

First, as discussed above, the Agreement is not overbroad in scope of non-signatories because it clearly only applies to disputes between Plaintiff and Brand Force. (Agreement, item 2(a).)

 

Second, the purported delegation clause issue does not render the Agreement unconscionable because the court can determine whether or not there is a valid delegation clause.[2]

 

Third, the Class Action Waiver is severable by its terms so it does not permeate the Agreement. (Agreement, item 4(a)(i).) So, too, is the Collective Action Waiver. (Id. at item 4(a)(ii).) What is more, the Agreement does not cover “disputes that cannot be arbitrated or subject to a pre-dispute arbitration agreement in accordance with applicable law.” (Id. at item 3(a).) Therefore, the purported PAGA Waiver is also severable.

 

Fourth, the court rejects Plaintiff’s argument on lack of mutuality. The Agreement explicitly allows both parties to bring claims in court for injunctive relief that are then finally determined in arbitration. (Agreement, item 3(c).) Additionally, the court is not persuaded that the confidentiality agreement renders this Agreement unconscionable.

 

The remaining Armendariz factors are undisputed.

 

Thus, the Agreement is not substantively unconscionable.

 

Accordingly, the court GRANTS the motion to compel arbitration, in part.

 

CONCLUSION:

 

            For the foregoing reasons, the Court decides the pending motion as follows:

 

1.      Motion to compel arbitration is GRANTED as to Brand Force One, LLC;

2.      This action is STAYED as to Juggernaut One LLC, SVJ One LLC, and Graftan Darnall.

Moving party is to give notice.

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Case Number: 26STCV09604    Hearing Date: September 22, 2026    Dept: 511

Tentative Ruling

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

 

On March 25, 2026, Plaintiff Tahliah Barnett (p/k/a FKA twigs) (Plaintiff) filed a complaint against Defendant Shia LaBeouf (Defendant) for declaratory relief.

 

According to the complaint, Plaintiff and Defendant entered into a settlement agreement of a prior action. Plaintiff alleges the agreement contains an unlawful NDA (or gag order) purporting to prohibit her ability to discuss sexual abuse.

 

On July 2, 2026, Defendant filed a motion to compel arbitration.

 

On July 2, 2026, Defendant also filed a motion to seal the unredacted confidential settlement agreement attached as Exhibit 1 to the motion to compel arbitration.

 

On July 7, 2026, Plaintiff filed the operative first amended complaint (FAC).

 

On July 24, 2026, Defendant filed another motion to compel arbitration and another motion to seal.

 

Oppositions to the motion to seal were due on or before September 9, 2026. As of September 16, 2026, the court has not received any opposition.

 

LEGAL STANDARD:

 

“Unless confidentiality is required by law, court records are presumed to be open.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.550(c); see also Savaglio v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. (2007) 149 Cal.App.4th 588, 597 (Savaglio); Overstock.com, Inc. v. Goldman Sachs Group, Inc. (2014) 231 Cal.App.4th 471, 483 (Overstock).)

 

The court may order that a record be filed under seal only if it finds that:

(1) There exists an overriding interest that overcomes the right of public access to the record;

(2) The overriding interest supports sealing the record;

(3) A substantial probability exists that the overriding interest will be prejudiced if the record is not sealed;

(4) The proposed sealing is narrowly tailored; and

(5) No less restrictive means exist to achieve the overriding interest.

(Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.550(d).)¿¿

 

An order sealing the records must:

(A)¿Specifically state the facts that support the findings; and

(B)¿Direct the sealing of only those documents and pages, or, if reasonably practicable, portions of those documents and pages, that contain the material that needs to be placed under seal. All other portions of each document or page must be included in the public file.

(Cal Rules of Court, rule 2550(e)(1).)

 

A motion seeking an order sealing records must be accompanied by a declaration containing facts sufficient to justify the sealing.¿¿(Cal. Rules of Court, rule 2.551(b)(1).)¿“The findings themselves, however, may be sort forth in fairly cursory terms.” (Overstock, supra 231 Cal.App.4th at p. 487.)

 

ANALYSIS:

 

Defendant contends relief is proper because the confidential settlement agreement contains sensitive financial information and private dealings between the parties which they agreed would remain confidential. Additionally, Defendant contends the contractual obligation not to disclose supports finding an overriding interest to keep the records sealed and that he narrowly tailored this request by redacting portions of the agreement rather than the whole thing.

 

The court has not received any opposition.

 

A binding contractual agreement not to disclose can be a potential overriding interest. (Universal City Studios, Inc. v. Superior Court (2003) 110 Cal.App.4th 1273, 1283.) “[O]nce it is established there is a potential overriding interest, the party seeking closure or sealing must prove prejudice to that interest is substantially probable.” (Ibid.)

 

Here, relief is not warranted at this time. Notably, while Defendant has identified a potential overriding interest via the confidential settlement agreement, they failed to provide evidence of prejudice to that interest. Indeed, the public-facing arbitration agreement already contains redactions. (Declaration of Shawn Holley In Support of Motion to Compel Arbitration, filed July 24, 2026.) Thus, relief is not warranted.

 

Accordingly, the court DENIES Defendant’s motion to seal without prejudice.

 

CONCLUSION:

 

            For the foregoing reasons, the Court decides the pending motion as follows:

 

1.      Motion to seal is DENIED without prejudice.

Moving party is to give notice.
