BACKGROUND

This is a medical data breach class action.  On January 30, 2023, Plaintiff Michael Merle (“Plaintiff” or “Merle”), individually and on behalf of all others similarly situated, initiated this action against USC Arcadia Hospital (“Defendant”) asserting the following claims: (1) violation of the California Invasion of Privacy Act (“CIPA”), Cal. Penal Code §631(a) and (2) violation of the Confidentiality of Medical Information Act (“CMIA”), Cal. Civil Code §56.10(d).  Plaintiff alleges that USC Arcadia Hospital is a healthcare provider that owns and/or operates the website uscarcadiahospital.org, along with other websites and patient portals.  Users can utilize these websites to search for medical information, services and physicians; schedule appointments; pay for medical services; and sign up for emails/event updates.  Plaintiff further alleges that Defendant used web analytics technologies, including Meta Pixel and other tracking tools on its websites, which resulted in the transmission of users’ personal and medical information to third parties without the users’ knowledge or consent.

On October 30, 2023, the Court sustained Defendant’s demurrer as to the second cause of action for violation of the CMIA with leave to amend.  Plaintiff did not file an amended complaint and the litigation proceeded on the CIPA claim only.

On March 5, 2024, Defendant filed an Answer.

On January 6, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Notice of Class Action Settlement and on January 23, 2026, he filed an Amended Notice of Class Action Settlement.  A copy of the fully executed Class Action Settlement Agreement (“Settlement Agreement” or “Agreement”) is attached to counsel Coulson’s declaration as Exhibit 1.

Now before the Court is the Motion for Preliminary Approval of Class Action Settlement, filed on March 2, 2026.

ANALYSIS OF SETTLEMENT AGREEMENT

·         Does a presumption of fairness exist?

1)      Was the settlement reached through arm’s-length bargaining?  According to Class Counsel, the parties litigated the case for nearly three years, from the time of filing in January 2023 to the time of reaching a resolution in November 2025.  (Coulson Decl., ¶2.)  The proposed settlement was reached as a result of arms-length negotiations following significant investigation of the facts and law and the negotiations were, at all times, adversarial and non-collusive in nature.  (Id. at ¶3.)  Good faith but prolonged negotiations were required to ultimately reach an agreement, after multiple offers were rejected on both sides.  (Ibid.)  After multiple starts and stops to the litigation, rejection of multiple offers on both sides, serious consideration of the risks of litigation, and review of the legal basis for both parties’ positions, the parties reached agreement in principle in November 2025, and a full set of terms in February of 2026.  (Id. at ¶4.)  The proposed Settlement takes into account the strengths and weaknesses of each side’s position and the uncertainty of how the case might have concluded at certification or trial.  (Id. at ¶5.)

2)      Were investigation and discovery sufficient to allow counsel and the court to act intelligently?  According to Class Counsel, the parties conducted extensive factual investigation, litigated a demurrer, served extensive written discovery, met-and-conferred about Defendant’s discovery responses on multiple occasions, subpoenaed third party discovery, and extensively negotiated numerous avenues of settlement before arriving at resolution of the claims.  (Coulson Decl., ¶2.)  The parties first began discussing settlement after the parties exchanged written discovery requests and responses in June 2024, but they did not reach a settlement. Thereafter, Plaintiff served certain third-party discovery, advancing the litigation while continuing to discuss the possibility of settlement. After multiple starts and stops to the litigation, rejection of multiple offers on both sides, serious consideration of the risks of litigation, and review of the legal basis for both parties’ positions, the parties reached agreement in principle in November 2025, and a full set of terms in February of 2026.  (Id. at ¶4.)

3)      Is counsel experienced in similar litigation?  Class Counsel are experienced in class action litigation, including data privacy class actions.  (Coulson Decl., ¶¶10-19, Exs. 2-3.)

According to the Settlement Agreement, Plaintiff is represented by attorneys from Coulson P.C., and Arias Sanguinetti Wang & Team LLP.  (¶1.6)  Provide a sworn declaration summarizing the relevant experience of counsel from Arias Sanguinetti Wang & Team LLP and include a list of all prior class actions in which counsel have been approved to act as lead or co-counsel.

1. Strength of Plaintiff’s case. “The most important factor is the strength of the case for plaintiff on the merits, balanced against the amount offered in settlement.”  (Kullar v. Foot Locker Retail, Inc. (2008) 168 Cal.App.4th 116, 130.)

According to Class Counsel, “The maximum recovery for the class members’ CIPA claim is $5,000 per infraction.”  (MPA at 9:17-18.)

Procedural hurdles (e.g., motion practice and appeals) are also likely to prolong the litigation as well as any recovery by the class members.

3.   Risk of maintaining class action status through trial. Even if a class is certified, there is always a risk of decertification. (See Weinstat v. Dentsply Intern., Inc. 4.   Amount offered in settlement.

5.   Extent of discovery completed and stage of the proceedings. This case was actively litigated for several years, including that the parties conducted extensive discovery prior to settlement.

6.   Experience and views of counsel. The settlement was negotiated and endorsed by Class Counsel who, as indicated above, are experienced in class action litigation, including data privacy class actions.  [See above for request for additional information.]

7.   Presence of a governmental participant. This factor is not applicable here.

8.   Reaction of the class members to the proposed settlement. The class members’ reactions will not be known until they receive notice and are afforded an opportunity to object, opt-out and/or submit claim forms. This factor becomes relevant during the fairness hearing.

May conditional class certification be granted?

1.         Standards

A detailed analysis of the elements required for class certification is not required, but it is advisable to review each element when a class is being conditionally certified.  (Amchem Products, Inc. v. Winsor (1997) 521 U.S. 620, 622-627.)  The trial court can appropriately utilize a different standard to determine the propriety of a settlement class as opposed to a litigation class certification.  Specifically, a lesser standard of scrutiny is used for settlement cases.  (Dunk at 1807, fn. 19.)  Finally, the Court is under no “ironclad requirement” to conduct an evidentiary hearing to consider whether the prerequisites for class certification have been satisfied.  (Wershba at 240.)

 

2.      Analysis

a.      Numerosity.  There are approximately 582 putative class members.  (MPA at 10:22.)  This element is met.

b.      Ascertainability.  The proposed class is defined above.  The class definition is “precise, objective and presently ascertainable.”  (Sevidal v. Target Corp. (2010) 189 Cal.App.4th 905, 919.)  Settlement Class Members are easily identifiable through common and objective characteristics.  Each Settlement Class Member signed up for email/event updates on Defendant’s website, and Defendant therefore has a list of all Settlement Class Members and their email addresses.  (MPA at 10:15-17.)

c.       Community of interest.  “The community of interest requirement involves three factors: ‘(1) predominant common questions of law or fact; (2) class representatives with claims or defenses typical of the class; and (3) class representatives who can adequately represent the class.’”  (Linder v. Thrifty Oil Co. (2000) 23 Cal.4th 429, 435.)

As to commonality, Plaintiff’s claim is based on Defendant’s alleged use of website tracking technologies, which can be established through common proof.  (MPA at 11:3-5.)

As to typicality, Plaintiff’s claim is typical of the proposed class’s because they are based on the same alleged conduct and harm.  (MPA at 11:10-11.)

As to adequacy, Plaintiff does not have any interest that conflicts with, or is antagonistic to, the interests of the proposed class, and his attorneys are experienced in consumer litigation and class actions.  (MPA at 11:14-17.)

Provide Plaintiff’s sworn declaration explaining why he is an adequate Class Representative.

d.      Adequacy of class counsel.  As indicated above, Class Counsel have shown experience in class action litigation, including wage and hour class actions.  [See above for request for additional information.]

e.                  Superiority.  Given the relatively small size of the individual claims, a class action appears to be superior to separate actions by the class members.

 

CONCLUSION:  The class may not be conditionally certified since the prerequisites of class certification have been satisfied, based on the current record.

·         Attorney fees and costs

CRC rule 3.769(b) states: “Any agreement, express or implied, that has been entered into with respect to the payment of attorney fees or the submission of an application for the approval of attorney fees must be set forth in full in any application for approval of the dismissal or settlement of an action that has been certified as a class action.”

Ultimately, the award of attorney fees is made by the court at the fairness hearing, using the lodestar method with a multiplier, if appropriate. (PLCM Group, Inc. v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095-1096; Ramos v. Countrywide Home Loans, Inc. (2000) 82 Cal.App.4th 615, 625-626; Ketchum III v. Moses (2000) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132-1136.) In common fund cases, the Court may utilize the percentage method, cross-checked by the lodestar. (Laffitte v. Robert Half Int’l, Inc. (2016) 1 Cal.5th 480, 503.) Despite any agreement by the parties to the contrary, “the court ha[s] an independent right and responsibility to review the attorney fee provision of the settlement agreement and award only so much as it determined reasonable.” (Garabedian v. Los Angeles Cellular Telephone Company (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 123, 128.)

The question of whether Class Counsel is entitled to $86,600 in attorney fees and costs will be addressed at the fairness hearing when class counsel brings a noticed motion for attorney fees. Class counsel must provide the court with billing information so that it can properly apply the lodestar method and must indicate what multiplier (if applicable) is being sought as to each counsel.

·         Incentive Award to Class Representatives

The Settlement Agreement provides for an enhancement award of $1,500 to the named Plaintiff. In connection with the final fairness hearing, named Plaintiff must submit a declaration attesting to why he or she should be entitled to an enhancement award in the proposed amount. The named Plaintiff must explain why he or she “should be compensated for the expense or risk she has incurred in conferring a benefit on other members of the class.”  (Clark v. American Residential Services LLC (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 785, 806.) Trial courts should not sanction enhancement awards of thousands of dollars with “nothing more than pro forma claims as to ‘countless’ hours expended, ‘potential stigma’ and ‘potential risk.’ Significantly more specificity, in the form of quantification of time and effort expended on the litigation, and in the form of reasoned explanation of financial or other risks incurred by the named plaintiff, is required in order for the trial court to conclude that an enhancement was ‘necessary to induce [the named plaintiff] to participate in the suit . . . .’” (Id. at 806-807, italics and ellipsis in original.) The Court will decide the issue of the enhancement award at the time of final approval.
