ANTONIO GARCIA, ET AL. V. MG SERVICE ENTERPRISES, INC., ET AL.

CASE NO.:  26NWCV00525

HEARING:  06/29/2026 @ 9:30 a.m.

 

#12

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Defendants MG Service Enterprises, Inc., dba Cremation Society of Los Angeles, dba Allen-English & Estrada Funeral Service, and Christian Mendez's demurrer to the entire First Amended Complaint, as to Defendant Christian Mendez, is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

Defendants’ demurrer to the First, Second, and Fourth Causes of Action is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

Defendants’ motion to strike is MOOT.

 

Plaintiffs Antonio Garcia, Nayeli Garcia, and Brenda Brito shall file and serve a Second Amended Complaint within 30 days of notice of this ruling.

 

Moving parties to give notice.

 

Defendants MG Service Enterprises, Inc., dba Cremation Society of Los Angeles, and dba Allen-English & Estrada Funeral Service and Christian Mendez (collectively, Defendants) demur to the first, second and fourth causes of action of the First Amended Complaint (FAC) filed by Plaintiffs Antonio Garcia, Nayeli Garcia, and Brenda Brito (collectively, Plaintiffs).

 

Defendants also move to strike portions of the FAC on the grounds that they constitute irrelevant and improper matter and seek non-recoverable damages.

 

Background

 

On May 1, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the operative FAC against Defendants and Does 1 though 50, inclusive, asserting four causes of action: (1) Breach of Contract; (2) Breach of Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing; (3) Negligence; and (4) Unfair Business Practices.

 

Plaintiffs allege that Defendants mishandled and failed to properly preserve their mother’s remains and that Defendants breached their contract, acted negligently, and engaged in unfair business practices by failing to provide required disclosures and by misrepresenting that the body would be adequately preserved.

 

Legal Standard

 

As a general matter, in a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. ¿ (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)  “A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.”  (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.)  As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Id.)  The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action.  (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)

 

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment.  (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.)  The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully.  (Id.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.)  However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.”  (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245). 

 

Plaintiff is only required to allege ultimate facts, not evidentiary facts.  (See Committee on Children's Television, Inc. v. General Foods Corp. (1983) 35 Cal.3d 197, 212 [“the complaint should set forth the ultimate facts constituting the cause of action, not the evidence by which plaintiff proposes to prove those facts”); 1 Cal. Affirmative Def. § 10:2 (2d ed.) [allegations of agency, course and scope of employment, etc. qualify as ultimate facts].)  Plaintiff’s allegations must be accepted as true on demurrer.  (See Yvanova v. New Century Mortgage Corp. (2016) 62 Cal.4th 919, 924 [“For purposes of reviewing a demurrer, we accept the truth of material facts properly pleaded in the operative complaint”].)

 

Request for Judicial Notice

 

Defendants request judicial notice of the following documents:

 

1.    Complaint filed on February 13, 2026, in the Los Angeles Superior Court as case no. 26NWCV00525 (Ex. 1.)

2.    First Amended Complaint filed on May 1, 2026, in the Los Angeles Superior Court as case no. 26NWCV00525 (Ex. 2.)

3.    Funeral Service Purchase Agreement between Cremation Society of Los Angeles and Antonio Garcia (Ex. 3.)

 

Judicial notice as to RJN Nos. 1-3 is GRANTED.  (Evid. Code § 452(d), (h); See¿Kalnoki v. First American Trustee Servicing Solutions, LLC (2017) 8 Cal.App.5th 23,37.)  However, the court only takes judicial notice of the foregoing documents only as to “the existence, content and authenticity of public records and other specified documents”; it does not take judicial notice of the truth of the factual matters asserted in those documents.  (Dominguez v. Bonta (2022) 87 Cal. App. 5th 389, 400.)¿¿

 

Meet and Confer

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, subdivision (a) requires the moving party to meet and confer “in person, by video conference, or by telephone” in an attempt to resolve a demurrer’s deficiencies five days before the responsive pleading is due.  (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41(a)(2).)

 

The Court finds that Defendants have fulfilled the meet and confer requirement.  (Shirley Decl., ¶¶ 4-7.)

 

Analysis

 

          Whether the FAC States a Claim Against Defendant Christian Mendez

 

Defendants first argue that the entire FAC fails as to Defendant Christian Mendez (Mendez) because the FAC contains no factual allegations regarding any conduct by Mendez and therefore fails to state facts sufficient to constitute any cause of action against him.

 

A complaint must allege facts sufficient to state a cause of action against each named defendant.  Even where a complaint contains boilerplate allegations that each defendant was the agent or employee of the others, or generally refers to "Defendants" collectively, such allegations do not state a cause of action against a defendant who is not identified in the charging allegations.  (See Falahati v. Kondo (2005) 127 Cal.App.4th 823, 829.)

 

Here, Mendez is named as a defendant in the caption of the FAC and is identified only in paragraph 4 as an individual domiciled in the State of California.  (FAC, ¶ 4.)  As Defendants point out, these are the only instances in which Mendez is mentioned, and the FAC contains no other factual allegations concerning him.  (Motion, p. 5.)

 

The court has held that alleging conduct of defendants and each of them was sufficient to charge each with liability.  (Lee v. Escrow Consultants, Inc. (1989) 210 Cal. App. 3d 915, 925.)  But that is not the case here.  The FAC broadly alleges that "DEFENDANTS" entered into the contract, mishandled the decedent's remains, and committed the acts giving rise to each cause of action, but it contains no factual allegations identifying Mendez's role in those events or explaining how he personally participated in the alleged wrongdoing.

 

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue they were not required to identify Mendez separately under each cause of action because the claims arise from general allegations against all Defendants.  (Opp., p. 5.)  Plaintiffs contend they sufficiently alleged Mendez's involvement by alleging he was an agent and employee of MG Service Enterprises, Inc., and that all Defendants acted through their agents and employees.  (Ibid., citing FAC, ¶ 10.)

 

The Court is not persuaded.  Paragraph 10 merely alleges that all defendants acted as agents and employees of one another and that each defendant authorized and ratified the conduct of the others.  (FAC, ¶ 10.)  It does not identify any act or omission by Mendez or otherwise allege facts connecting him to the conduct underlying Plaintiffs' claims.  As the California Supreme Court has observed, the blanket use of the term "defendants," without referring to a particular defendant, creates confusion regarding the factual basis for each defendant's alleged liability.  (Moore v. Regents of University of California (1990) 51 Cal.3d 120, 125 fn. 1, 134 fn. 12.)  Accordingly, Plaintiffs have failed to allege facts sufficient to state any cause of action against Mendez.

 

Accordingly, the demurrer to the FAC as to Defendant Christian Mendez is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

          First Cause of Action: Breach of Contract

 

Defendants argue that the first cause of action for breach of contract fails because Plaintiffs neither attached a copy of the contract nor set forth its terms verbatim in the FAC.  (Motion, p. 3.)

 

The elements of a breach of contract claim are: (1) the existence of a contract; (2) plaintiff's performance or excuse for nonperformance; (3) defendant's breach; and (4) resulting damages.  (Oasis West Realty, LLC v. Goldman (2011) 51 Cal.4th 811, 821.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue they were not required to attach the written contract or plead its terms verbatim because they adequately alleged the legal effect of the contract.  (Opp., pp. 3-4.)  Plaintiffs contend the FAC sufficiently alleges the material terms of the agreement, Defendants' breach, and the resulting damages.  (Ibid.)

 

Although older authority states that "[i]f the action is based on an alleged breach of a written contract, the terms must be set out verbatim in the body of the complaint or a copy of the written instrument must be attached and incorporated by reference", the modern rule permits a plaintiff to plead the legal effect of the contract rather than its precise language.  (Otworth v. Southern Pac. Transportation Co. (1985) 166 Cal.App.3d 452, 458-459.)  As the court explained in Miles v. Deutsche Bank National Trust Co. (2015) 236 Cal.App.4th 394, 402 (Miles), relying on Construction Protective Services, Inc. v. TIG Specialty Ins. Co. (2002) 29 Cal.4th 189, 199, a plaintiff's failure to attach the written contract or plead its terms verbatim is not fatal so long as the complaint adequately alleges the legal effect of the agreement.

 

Here, although the FAC neither attaches the written agreement nor recites its terms verbatim, it sufficiently alleges the substance of the parties' agreement.  Plaintiffs allege that, on or about February 9, 2024, they entered into a contract with Defendants whereby Defendants agreed to provide cremation-related services, including a private viewing of Decedent prior to cremation, in exchange for $1,595.  (FAC, ¶¶ 16-17, 22, 29.)  Plaintiffs further allege that Defendants breached the agreement by failing to properly preserve Decedent's remains, thereby preventing the viewing from occurring as agreed.  (Id., ¶ 30.)  Plaintiffs also allege that they performed all obligations required under the contract and that they sustained monetary and emotional distress damages as a result of the alleged breach.  (Id., ¶¶ 31-32.)

 

These allegations adequately plead the legal effect of the contract because they identify the essential terms of the agreement, Plaintiffs' alleged performance, Defendants' alleged breach, and the resulting damages.  At the pleading stage, Plaintiffs are not required to attach the written contract or plead each contractual provision verbatim.  (Miles, supra, 236 Cal.App.4th at p. 402.)

 

Defendants, however, argue that the FAC still fails to identify the contracting parties.  (Motion p. 6.)  The Court agrees.  Although the FAC alleges that "PLAINTIFFS entered into a contract with DEFENDANTS" for cremation-related services, it does not identify which Plaintiff or Plaintiffs entered into the agreement, who executed the contract, or otherwise allege facts demonstrating that each Plaintiff is entitled to enforce the contract.  (FAC, ¶ 16.)  Likewise, paragraph 29 alleges only that the "family of DECEDENT entered into an Agreement with DEFENDANTS," which similarly fails to identify the contracting parties with specificity.

 

Relying on Christensen v. Superior Court (1991) 54 Cal.3d 868, 906 (Christensen), Plaintiffs argue that relatives who are aware of funeral or crematory services and for whose benefit the services are rendered may recover for emotional distress caused by negligent mishandling of remains even if they were not parties to the contract.  (Opp., p. 4.) 

 

Plaintiffs' reliance on Christensen is misplaced.  Christensen addressed the class of persons who may recover emotional distress damages arising from the negligent mishandling of a decedent's remains and did not address the pleading requirements for a breach of contract claim or hold that every family member may enforce a funeral services contract.  To the contrary, in describing the proposed plaintiff class, the Supreme Court explained that the individual plaintiffs consisted of persons who possessed the statutory right under Health and Safety Code section 7100 to control disposition of the decedent's remains, and/or who contracted for Defendants' services, paid for those services, or represented the estates of persons who did so.  (Christensen, supra, 54 Cal.3d at 876-877.)  Thus, Christensen does not relieve Plaintiffs of pleading facts establishing the basis upon which each Plaintiff is entitled to enforce the alleged contract.

 

Accordingly, the FAC fails to allege facts sufficient to establish which Plaintiffs are parties to the alleged contract or the basis upon which each Plaintiff seeks to enforce it.

 

The demurrer to the first cause of action is therefore SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

          Second Cause of Action: Breach of Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing

 

Defendants argue that Plaintiffs’ claims for breach of implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing is premised on the same allegations as that of Plaintiff’s first cause of action for breach of contract.  Thus, Plaintiffs’ second cause of action is duplicative and should be discarded at the pleading stage.

 

In every contract there is an implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing.  (Sutherland v. Barclays American/Mortgage Corp. (1997) 53 Cal.App.4th 299, 314.)  The covenant of good faith and fair dealing imposes a general duty upon each contracting party “to perform faithfully and not to deprive the other party of the benefits of the contract.”  (Floystrup v. City of Berkeley Rent Stabilization Bd. (1990) 219 Cal.App.3d 1309, 1318.) 

 

A breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing requires something more than breach of the contractual duty itself.  (Careau & Co v. Security Pacific Business Credit, Inc. (1990) 222 Cal.App.3d 1371, 1394.)  “Thus, allegations which assert such a claim must show that the conduct of the defendant, whether or not it also constitutes a breach of a consensual contract term, demonstrates a failure or refusal to discharge contractual responsibilities, prompted not by an honest mistake, bad judgment or negligence but rather by a conscious and deliberate act, which unfairly frustrates the agreed common purposes and disappoints the reasonable expectations of the other party thereby depriving that party of the benefits of the agreement.”  (Id. at 1395.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiffs cite Wilson v. Houston Funeral Home (1996) 42 Cal.App.4th 1124, 1132-1133 (Wilson) to argue that contracts for funeral services are unique in that they inherently involved emotional considerations and courts have recognized that such contracts imply a duty to provide services with the dignity and respect as bereaved families can reasonably expect.  (Opp., p. 6.)  Plaintiffs argue that similarly Plaintiffs contracted and expressly informed Defendants that they expected a dignified viewing service to pay their respects to their mother.  (Ibid., citing FAC ¶ 22.) 

 

In Wilson, a decedent’s wife entered into a contract with a company to provide her deceased spouse with funeral and burial services.  (Id. at 1129.)  The decedent’s wife brought an action against the funeral service provider, alleging breach of contract and breach of the implied covenant of good faith based on the funeral service provider’s conduct in demanding payment before driving the family to the burial service and in driving recklessly to the burial service.  (Id. at 1130-1131.)  In holding that the complaint properly alleged a cause of action for breach of the implied covenant of good faith, the Wilson court found every contract for funeral services contains an implied covenant that the services will be conducted with “dignity and respect toward the family members for whose benefit the services are performed.”  (Id. at 1133.)  In reaching this conclusion, the Wilson court rejected the funeral service provider’s argument that the contract only required that the decedent’s family be transported to the burial service and did not require a specific manner of transport.  (Id. at 1132-1133.)

 

Here, the FAC incorporates Plaintiffs' earlier allegations regarding the breach of contract cause of action.  (FAC, ¶ 33.)  The FAC further alleges that Defendants breached the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing by "failing to properly handle and preserve the DECEDENT'S remains in a manner that would have sufficiently slowed the rate of decomposition to allow for the dignified viewing service that was contracted for."  (Id., ¶ 39.)

 

The Court disagrees that the second cause of action is duplicative.  Wilson is instructive insofar as it recognizes that contracts for funeral services carry an implied covenant that the services will be performed with dignity and respect toward the decedent's family.  However, unlike in Wilson, the FAC here does not sufficiently allege the contractual relationship giving rise to the implied covenant.  As discussed above, the FAC alleges only that "PLAINTIFFS" entered into a contract with "DEFENDANTS" but fails to identify which Plaintiff or Plaintiffs were parties to the agreement or otherwise allege the basis upon which each Plaintiff may enforce the contract.  Because the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing is implied only in the existence of a valid contract and cannot exist independently of the contractual relationship, Plaintiffs have failed to allege facts sufficient to state a claim for breach of the implied covenant.

 

Accordingly, the demurrer to the second cause of action is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

          Fourth Cause of Action: Violation of Business and Professions Code section 17200

 

Defendants argue that Plaintiffs' fourth cause of action under Business and Professions Code section 17200 (UCL) fails because Plaintiffs have not alleged facts demonstrating that the alleged failure to provide a price list caused them to suffer any economic loss.

 

The UCL "focuses solely on conduct and prohibits anything that can properly be called a business practice and that at the same time is forbidden by law."  (Albillo v. Intermodal Container Services, Inc. (2003) 114 Cal.App.4th 190, 206.)  To state a claim under the UCL, a plaintiff need not plead the elements of a tort, but instead must establish that the challenged practice is "unlawful," "unfair," or "fraudulent." (Ibid.; see Adhav v. Midway Rent A Car, Inc. (2019) 37 Cal.App.5th 954, 970.)  Moreover, allegations of actual deception, reasonable reliance, and damages are not required to establish a violation of the UCL.  (Searle v. Wyndham Internat., Inc. (2002) 102 Cal.App.4th 1327, 1333.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that the FAC sufficiently states a UCL claim because it alleges that Defendants failed to provide the disclosures required by Business and Professions Code section 7685.2, thereby leaving Plaintiffs unaware of what they were paying for and inducing them to pay for services based on representations that Decedent's remains would be properly preserved for a private viewing.  (Opp., p. 7.)  Plaintiffs further contend that they suffered an economic loss in the amount of $1,595, in addition to emotional distress damages.  (Ibid.)

 

In reply, Defendants argue that Plaintiffs fail to allege facts demonstrating that the alleged failure to provide a general price list caused them to suffer any economic loss.  (Reply pp. 5-6.)  Defendants contend that Plaintiffs do not allege they would have declined to enter into the contract had the price list been provided, that the prices charged differed from those that should have been disclosed, or that they failed to receive the cremation services for which they paid.  (Ibid.)  Defendants further argue that Plaintiffs' remaining allegations concerning the mishandling of Decedent's remains merely duplicate their negligence allegations and do not state a claim under the UCL.  (Id. at p. 6.)

 

Here, Plaintiffs allege that Defendants engaged in unlawful and unfair business practices by: (1) failing to provide the itemized price information required by Business and Professions Code section 7685.2 before entering into the contract; (2) taking advantage of Plaintiffs' grief by inducing them to purchase cremation-related services without knowing what they would receive; (3) failing to disclose accurate pricing information as required by the federal Funeral Rule (16 C.F.R. Part 453); and (4) inducing Plaintiffs to pay for services by representing that Decedent's remains would be properly preserved for a private viewing when they allegedly were not.  (FAC, ¶ 60.)

 

The Court finds that Plaintiffs have sufficiently alleged conduct that could constitute unlawful, unfair, or fraudulent business practices.  However, Plaintiffs have not adequately alleged that they lost money or property as a result of those alleged practices.  (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 17204.)

 

Although Plaintiffs allege they paid $1,595 for Defendants' services, the FAC does not allege facts demonstrating that this economic loss resulted from Defendants' alleged failure to provide the disclosures required by Business and Professions Code section 7685.2 or the federal Funeral Rule.  The FAC does not allege, for example, that Plaintiffs would have declined to enter into the agreement had the required disclosures been provided, that Defendants charged prices inconsistent with those required to be disclosed, or that Plaintiffs otherwise suffered an economic injury caused by the alleged statutory violations.  Rather, the FAC alleges that Plaintiffs' damages stemmed from Defendants' subsequent mishandling of Decedent's remains.  

 

Accordingly, the demurrer to the fourth cause of action is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

Motion to Strike

 

The court may, upon a motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a).)  The court may also strike all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court.  (Id. at § 436, subd.(b).)  The grounds for a motion to strike are that the pleading has irrelevant, false or improper matter, or has not been drawn or filed in conformity with laws.  (Id. at § 436.)  The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice.  (Id. at § 437.)

 

In light of the Court's ruling sustaining the demurrer with leave to amend, the Court declines to rule on the motion to strike.  Because Plaintiffs will have an opportunity to amend the pleading, Defendants may renew any appropriate challenges to the amended complaint, if necessary.

 

Accordingly, the motion to strike is MOOT.

 

Conclusion

 

Based on the foregoing, Defendants’ demurrer to the entire First Amended Complaint, as to Defendant Christian Mendez, is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

Defendants’ demurrer to the First, Second, and Fourth Causes of Action is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

Defendants’ motion to strike is MOOT.

 

Plaintiffs Antonio Garcia, Nayeli Garcia, and Brenda Brito shall file and serve a Second Amended Complaint within 30 days of notice of this ruling.


Case Number: 26NWCV01274    Hearing Date: June 29, 2026    Dept: SED

LATASHA MYLES V. RAISING CANE’S USA, LLC, ET AL.

CASE NO.:  26NWCV01274

HEARING:  06/29/2026 @ 9:30 a.m.

 

#13

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Defendant Glenn Curro’s unopposed demurrer is SUSTAINED AND OVERRULED IN PART.

 

Defendant Glenn Curro shall file and serve an Answer within 30 days of this order.

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

Defendant Glenn Curro (Curro) demurs to the second and third causes of action of the Complaint filed by Plaintiff Latasha Myles. (Plaintiff).

 

Background

 

On April 10, 2026, Plaintiff filed the operative Complaint against Defendants Raising Cane’s USA, LLC, Curro, and Does 1 through 10, inclusive (collectively, Defendants) asserting three causes of action: (1) Negligence; (2) Premises Liability; and (3) Negligent Hiring, Supervision, or Retention of Employee.

 

Plaintiff alleges she slipped and fell on a damaged and/or slippery entry rug at Raising Cane’s due to Defendants’ negligent maintenance, failure to warn, and unsafe premises conditions.  She further alleges Defendants failed to properly hire, train, and supervise employees responsible for safety, leading to her injuries.

 

No opposition has been filed.

 

Legal Standard

 

As a general matter, in a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. ¿ (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.)  “A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.”  (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.)  As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Id.)  The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action.  (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)

 

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment.  (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.)  The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully.  (Id.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 226.)  However, “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.”  (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245). 

 

Plaintiff is only required to allege ultimate facts, not evidentiary facts.  (See Committee on Children's Television, Inc. v. General Foods Corp. (1983) 35 Cal.3d 197, 212 [“the complaint should set forth the ultimate facts constituting the cause of action, not the evidence by which plaintiff proposes to prove those facts”); 1 Cal. Affirmative Def. § 10:2 (2d ed.) [allegations of agency, course and scope of employment, etc. qualify as ultimate facts].)  Plaintiff’s allegations must be accepted as true on demurrer.  (See Yvanova v. New Century Mortgage Corp. (2016) 62 Cal.4th 919, 924 [“For purposes of reviewing a demurrer, we accept the truth of material facts properly pleaded in the operative complaint”].)

 

Meet and Confer

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, subdivision (a) requires the moving party to meet and confer “in person, by video conference, or by telephone” in an attempt to resolve a demurrer’s deficiencies five days before the responsive pleading is due.  (Code Civ. Proc. § 430.41(a)(2).)

 

The Court finds that Curro has fulfilled the meet and confer requirement.

 

Analysis

 

          Second Cause of Action

 

Curro argues that Plaintiff’s cause of action for premises liability with respect to Curro fails because, as general manager of the Raising Cane’s location where the incident took place (Subject Location), he does not own the Subject Location or have any control over the premises.  (Motion p. 3.)  Additionally, Plaintiff’s Complaint does not establish facts that show how Curro had knowledge of the alleged dangerous condition.  (Id. at p. 4.)

 

The elements of a cause of action for premises liability are the same as those for negligence:¿duty, breach, causation, and damages.  (Castellon v. U.S. Bancorp (2013) 220 Cal.App.4th 994, 998.)  Those who own, possess, or control property generally have a duty to exercise ordinary care in managing the property to avoid exposing others to an unreasonable risk of harm.  (Annocki v. Peterson Enterprises, LLC (2014) 232 Cal.App.4th 32, 37 (Annocki).)  If a dangerous condition exists, the property owner is “under a duty to exercise ordinary care either to make the condition reasonably safe for [customers’] their use or to give a warning adequate to enable them to avoid the harm.”  (Bridgman v. Safeway Stores, Inc. (1960) 53 Cal.2d 443, 446.)  The existence and scope of a property owner’s duty are legal questions for the court.  (Annocki, supra, 232 Cal.App.4th¿ at 36.)¿

 

Because a duty of care extends to those who own, possess, or control property, ownership is not the sole basis for liability.  Here, the Complaint alleges that Curro was the manager of the Subject Location and exercised authority over its operation and safety.  (Complaint ¶¶ 5, 8.)  These allegations are sufficient to plead that Curro possessed or controlled the premises and therefore owed Plaintiff a duty of ordinary care. 

 

The Complaint also sufficiently alleges breach.  Specifically, Plaintiff alleges that Defendants, including Curro, knew or should have known that the damaged and/or slippery entry rug posed a substantial risk of harm and failed to repair the condition or provide adequate warning.  (Complaint ¶¶ 9, 40-42.)  At the pleading stage, these allegations are sufficient to plead actual or constructive notice. 

 

Finally, the Complaint sufficiently alleges causation because it alleges Plaintiff tripped and fell to Defendants' failure to repair or warn of the damaged and/or slippery entry rug.  (Complaint ¶ 41.) 

 

Accordingly, the Complaint sufficiently states a cause of action for premises liability.  

 

The demurrer to Plaintiff’s second cause of action is OVERRULED.

 

          Third Cause of Action: Negligent Hiring, Supervision, or Retention

 

Curro argues that Plaintiff improperly alleges a cause of action for negligent hiring, training, and supervision against Curro because Curro, in his capacity as a general manager and therefore an employee of Defendant Raising Cane’s, cannot possibly be liable under such a theory of liability as the theory is reserved for employers, not employees.  (Motion p. 4.)

 

The necessary elements for negligent hiring are: (1) that the employer hired the employee; (2) who was incompetent or unfit; (3) that the employer had reason to believe undue risk of harm would exist because of the employment; and (4) that harm occurred.  (Federico v. Superior Court (1997) 59 Cal.App.4th 1207, 1213-1214.)

 

Here, although the Complaint alleges in a conclusory fashion that "Defendants" employed, retained, supervised, trained, and controlled employees responsible for customer safety, it does not allege facts showing that Curro, individually, hired or retained any employee, or that he possessed the authority to do so.  (Complaint ¶¶ 46-50.)  Nor does the Complaint allege that any employee was incompetent or unfit, or that Curro knew or had reason to know that employing or retaining a particular employee created an undue risk of harm.  Instead, the allegations consist of generalized conclusions asserted collectively against all Defendants.  

 

These allegations are insufficient to state a cause of action for negligent hiring, supervision, or retention against Curro.

 

Plaintiff did not file a timely opposition to the demurrer.  Accordingly, the Court finds that Plaintiff has not met her burden of demonstrating a reasonable possibility that the Complaint can be amended to state a viable cause of action.  (See Shaeffer v. Califia Farms, LLC (2020) 44 Cal.App.5th 1125, 1145.)

 

The demurrer to Plaintiff’s third cause of action against Curro is SUSTAINED WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

Conclusion

 

Based on the foregoing, Defendant Glenn Curro’s unopposed demurrer is SUSTAINED IN PART AND OVERRULED IN PART.

 

The Court OVERRULES Defendant Glenn Curro’s demurrer to Plaintiff Latasha Myles’s second cause of action.

 

The Court SUSTAINS WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND Defendant Glenn Curro’s demurrer to Plaintiff Latasha Myles’s third cause of action.  

 

Defendant Glenn Curro shall file and serve an Answer within 30 days of this order.
