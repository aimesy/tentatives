[THE FOLLOWING IS A TENTATIVE RULING IN THE ABOVE CASE]:

 

(1) Grant attorneys’ fees in the reduced sum of $13,622.50; (2) Deny request for costs.

 

I.                BACKGROUND

On June 8, 2023, Plaintiffs Myra Preciado Garcia and Maria Castaneda (“Plaintiffs”) leased a new 2023 Cadillac CT5 (the “Vehicle”). (Complaint ¶ 8.) The Vehicle was defective. (Complaint ¶ 9.)

On February 21, 2025, Plaintiffs filed a complaint against Defendants General Motors LLC (“Defendant”) and Does 1 through 10, alleging causes of action for: (1) violation of Song-Beverly Act - breach of express warranty; (2) violation of Song-Beverly Act - breach of implied warranty; and (3) violation of the Song-Beverly Act section 1793.2, subdivision (b).

On October 13, 2025, Plaintiffs filed a notice of settlement of entire case.

On December 12, 2025, the court dismissed the complaint without prejudice pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6. (12/12/25 Minute Order.)

On April 9, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a motion for attorneys’ fees (the “First Motion”). On May 22, 2026, Plaintiffs withdrew the First Motion. That same day, Plaintiffs filed another motion for attorneys’ fees (the “Motion”). As of June 23, 2026, no opposition has been filed.

II.             LEGAL STANDARD

California Civil Code section 1794, subdivision (d) states:

 

If the buyer prevails in an action under this section, the buyer shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney's fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.

 

“The plain wording of the statute requires the trial court to base the fee award upon actual time expended on the case, as long as such fees are reasonably incurred – both from the standpoint of time spent and the amount charged.” (Robertson v. Fleetwood Travel Trailers of California, Inc. (“Robertson”) (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 785, 817, original italics.) The “prevailing party has the burden of showing that the fees incurred were reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation, and were reasonable in amount.” (Id. at pp. 817-818.) “The amount of an attorney fee to be awarded is a matter within the sound discretion of the trial court.” (Id. at p. 822.)

 

“[T]he lodestar adjustment method is the prevailing rule for statutory attorney fee awards to be applied in the absence of clear legislative intent to the contrary[.]” (Robertson, supra, 144 Cal.App.4th at p. 818.) The lodestar adjustment method “requires the trial court to first determine a touchstone or lodestar figure based on a careful compilation of the actual time spent and reasonable hourly compensation for each attorney. [Citation] The touchstone figure may then be augmented or diminished by taking various relevant factors into account, including (1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved and the skill displayed in presenting them; (2) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys; and (3) the contingent nature of the fee award, based on the uncertainty of prevailing on the merits and of establishing eligibility for the award.” (Id. at p. 819.) However, a trial court is “not required to apply a multiplier.” (Id. at p. 822, original italics.)

 

III.           DISCUSSION

Plaintiffs move for an order awarding Plaintiffs attorneys’ fees and costs in the sum of $28,658.82. (Motion p. 2.) This sum reflects $17,685.00 in attorneys’ fees, a 0.5 multiplier enhancement increasing the fees by $8,842.50, and $2,131.32 in costs and expenses. (Motion p. 2.)

Hourly Rates:

Counsel Sepehr Daghighian billed 4.90 hours of work on this matter and expects to bill another 7.50 hours of work on this matter, at $625.00 per hour. (Daghighian Declaration ¶ 7.) Counsel Michael H. Rosenstein billed 1.90 hours of work on this matter, at $700.00 per hour. ((Daghighian Declaration ¶ 8.) Counsel Brian T. Shippen-Murray billed 14.50 hours of work on this matter, at $550.00 per hour. (Daghighian Declaration ¶ 9.) Counsel Miguel A. Ortiz billed 1.20 hours of work on this matter, at $525.00 per hour. (Daghighian Declaration ¶ 10.)

Defendant filed no opposition challenging these hourly rates. Daghighian submits a declaration discussing counsels relevant background and experience. (Daghighian Declaration ¶¶ 4-10.) Daghighian states that in establishing the firm’s rates, they considered the United States Consumer Law Attorney Fee Survey published in 2019 that reflected rates from 2017 to 2018, and “determined that the rates charged by the attorneys were commensurate with the hourly rates charged by other attorneys with comparable experience in consumer rights law.” (Daghighian Declaration ¶ 12.) Daghighian also considered the 2021 Wolters Kluwer Real Rate Report in setting the firm’s rates. (Daghighian Declaration ¶ 13.)

The court finds the hourly rates billed in this matter are reasonable. (See Goglin v. BMW of North America, LLC (2016) 4 Cal.App.5th 462, 464, 473-474 [concluding a trial court did not abuse its discretion in finding a $575 hourly rate was appropriate in a Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act action.])

Time Billed:

Plaintiffs’ counsels billed 22.50 hours on this matter and expect to bill another 7.50 hours on reviewing the opposition, preparing a reply, and preparing for and appearing at the hearing on the Motion. (Daghighian Declaration ¶¶ 7-10; Ex. A pdf p. 17.)

Defendant filed no opposition and Plaintiffs filed no reply. Thus, fees for reviewing the opposition and preparing a reply are not warranted.

Further, the Motion is unopposed, so court finds that the requested 2.50 hours of anticipated fees for preparing for and appearing at the hearing is excessive. (Daghighian Declaration; Ex. A pdf p. 17.) The court will reduce the requested hours to 1.0 hour for preparing for and appearing at the hearing.

These two reductions reduce the requested fees by $4,062.50.  (Daghighian Declaration Ex. A pdf p. 17.)

This matter was pending for about eight months before Plaintiffs filed the notice of settlement of entire case. The court has reviewed Plaintiffs’ counsels’ billing records and finds that the remaining 22.50 hours of billed fees are reasonable for this matter. (Daghighian Declaration Ex. A pdf pp. 12-17.)

Lodestar Multiplier:

Plaintiffs request a 0.5 lodestar multiplier. (Motion p. 2)

The court understands that litigating Song-Beverly actions requires technical expertise.  But “a trial court should award a multiplier for exceptional representation only when the quality of representation far exceeds the quality of representation that would have been provided by an attorney of comparable skill and experience billing at the hourly rate used in the lodestar calculation. Otherwise, the fee award will result in unfair double counting and be unreasonable.” (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1139.) Here, Plaintiffs’ counsels displayed the skill, proficiency, and technical expertise commensurate with counsels’ experience and specialty. Nothing before the court indicates Plaintiffs’ counsels’ representation exceeded that which would have been provided by other attorneys of comparable skill and experience billing at counsels’ rates.

 

Accordingly, the court denies Plaintiffs’ request for an enhancement.

 

Costs:

 

Plaintiffs request costs and expenses in the sum of $2,131.32. (Motion p. 2.) California Rules of Court, rule 3.1700(a)(1) states:

 

A prevailing party who claims costs must serve and file a memorandum of costs within 15 days after the date of service of the notice of entry of judgment or dismissal by the clerk under Code of Civil Procedure section 664.5 or the date of service of written notice of entry of judgment or dismissal, or within 180 days after entry of judgment, whichever is first. The memorandum of costs must be verified by a statement of the party, attorney, or agent that to the best of his or her knowledge the items of cost are correct and were necessarily incurred in the case.

 

Plaintiffs filed no memorandum of costs with the court. Thus, the request for costs is denied.

 

IV.           CONCLUSION

 

For the foregoing reasons, the Motion is granted in the reduced sum of $13,622.50, reflecting 22.50 hours of billed fees and 1.0 hour of anticipated fees for preparing for and appearing at the hearing on the Motion. Defendant is ordered to pay Plaintiffs’ counsel $13,622.50 within thirty (30) days of this order. Plaintiffs’ request for costs and expenses in the sum of $2,131.32 is denied.

COURT’S [TENTATIVE] ORDER RE:

Motion for Protective Order

[THE FOLLOWING IS A TENTATIVE RULING IN THE ABOVE CASE]:

 

Grant, in part, and deny, in part.

 

I.                BACKGROUND 

 

On April 8, 2023, Plaintiff Michael Scruton (“Plaintiff”) acquired a certified pre-owned 2021 Ford Ecosport (the “Vehicle”). (Complaint ¶ 6.) The Vehicle was defective. (Complaint ¶ 11.)

 

On February 17, 2026, Plaintiff filed a complaint against Defendants Ford Motor Company (“Defendant”) and Does 1 through 20, alleging causes of action for: (1) breach of express warranty under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act; (2) breach of implied warranty under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act; and (3) violation of the Song-Beverly Act section 1793.2, subdivision (b).

 

On May 15, 2026, Defendant filed a motion for entry of protective order to govern production of confidential materials (the “Motion”). On June 12, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition. On June 18, 2026, Defendant filed a reply.

 

II.             MEET AND CONFER 

 

A motion for a protective order must be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.060, subd. (a).) “A meet and confer declaration in support of a motion shall state facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt, either in person, by telephone, or by videoconference, to informally resolve each issue presented by the motion.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2016.040, subd. (a).

 

Defendant’s counsel submits a declaration stating they emailed Plaintiff’s counsel. (Villanueva Declaration ¶¶ 3-7.) Defendant was required to meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by videoconference. Meeting and conferring by email is insufficient under Code of Civil Procedure section 2016.040. Defendant did not satisfy the meet and confer requirement.

 

III.           LEGAL STANDARD 

 

“The court, for good cause shown, may make any order that justice requires to protect any party or other person from unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression, or undue burden and expense. This protective order may include, but is not limited to . . . [T]hat a trade secret or other confidential research, development, or commercial information not be disclosed, or be disclosed only to specified persons or only in a specified way.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.060, subd. (b).)

 

“[T]he burden is on the party seeking the protective order to show good cause for whatever order is sought.” (Fairmont Ins. Co. v. Superior Court (“Fairmont”) (2000) 22 Cal.4th 245, 255.)

 

IV.           DISCUSSION

 

Defendant seeks the entry of a protective order applicable to Defendant’s initial disclosures of materials pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26 to protect Defendant’s confidential, proprietary, and trade secret information and materials. (Motion p. 2.)

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues Defendant has not shown good cause because Amanda Pannell’s declaration is conclusory and fails to show these documents contain trade secrets. (Opposition p. 4.) Plaintiff also notes Defendant responded indicating the Written Statement of Lemon Law Policies and Procedures will only be produced subject to a protective order, but Defendant’s Motion does not reference this document. (Opposition pp. 5-6.) Finally, Plaintiff argues the information Defendant is seeking to protect is within the disclosure requirements of Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26, subdivision (h), and Defendant voluntarily opted into this statutory framework. (Opposition p. 7.) Plaintiff argues that “[a]llowing [Defendant] to withhold these documents acts as a unilateral stay of discovery, violating the express text and intent of the statute.” (Opposition p. 7.)

 

In reply, Defendant argues Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26 does not eliminate the court’s authority to impose a protective order. (Reply p. 2.) Defendant also argues it has shown good cause for a protective order because the Warranty Policy & Procedure Manuals and Workshop Manual are proprietary. (Reply p. 3.) Finally, Defendant argues Plaintiff has shown no prejudice they will suffer if the court enters a protective order. (Reply p. 4.)

 

Defendant submits a declaration by Amanda Pannell, Defendant’s California RAV Escalation Manager operations. (Pannell Declaration ¶ 2.) Pannell discusses two documents: (1) Defendant’s Warranty Policy & Procedure Manuals; and (2) Defendant’s Workshop Manual. (Pannell Declaration ¶ 6.) Pannell does not discuss the document referenced by Plaintiff, Defendant’s Written Statement of Lemon Law Policies and Procedures. (Opposition pp. 5-6.) The court here considers only if a protective order should be entered in reference to the Warranty Policy & Procedure Manuals and Workshop Manual. (Pannell Declaration ¶ 6.)

 

 

“’Gaps left unaddressed by statutes’ – that is, legislative silence – are still gaps, and may be filled by courts through the exercise of their inherent authority in the absence of a clear legislative intent to the contrary that goes beyond the silence itself.” (Chavez v. Superior Court (2024) 99 Cal.App.5th 165, 180.)

 

The court acknowledges Plaintiff’s concern here that permitting Defendant to move for the entry of a protective order acts as a stay of the initial disclosures under Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26. But Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26 does not clearly indicate the Legislature intended to remove the court’s ability to enter a protective order covering materials responsive to the documents set forth in Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26. Thus, the court is not persuaded it lacks authority to enter a protective order here.

 

The court finds good cause exists to enter a protective order relating to the Warranty Policy and Procedure Manual. Pannell sets forth that the Warranty Policy and Procedure Manual contains confidential proprietary information relating to Defendant’s warranty and recall processes and procedures. (Pannell Declaration ¶ 9.) Pannell states that “[t]hese practices and procedures were developed over time and with significant cost and investment by [Defendant]. These documents are unique to [Defendant] and would have value to [Defendant’s] competitors if made public, because they would allow [Defendant’s] competitors to replicate [Defendant’s] warranty policies and procedures.” (Pannell Declaration ¶ 9.)

 

The court finds no good cause exists to enter a protective order relating to the Workshop Manual. Pannell states the Workshop Manual is offered for a fee. (Pannell Declaration ¶ 10.) Defendant’s concern here appears to be that producing the Workshop Manual may result in lost revenue. (Pannell Declaration ¶ 10.) The court disagrees that Defendant’s concern for loss of revenue transforms the Workshop Manual into a confidential document.

 

Finally, Defendant requests the court enter a “[m]odified” protective order. (Motion p. 4.) Defendant bears the burden of showing good cause for the proposed protective order. (Fairmont, supra, 22 Cal.4th at p. 255.) The court finds no good cause exists to enter the requested modified protective order and directs entry only of the Los Angeles Superior Court model protective order.

 

V.              CONCLUSION 

 

For the foregoing reasons, the Motion is granted, in part, and denied, in part. The court grants the Motion as it relates to the Warranty Policy and Procedure Manual but denies it as it relates to the Workshop Manual. The court denies Defendant’s request for a modified protective order but will enter the Los Angeles Superior Court model protective order.
