      I.          Background

 

This is a Song-Beverly Action. Plaintiff Alfonso Cardenas (Plaintiff) moves to compel further discovery responses from Defendant Ford Motor Company (Ford).

 

On April 21, 2025, Plaintiff filed a complaint against Defendants Ford and Dalia Motor Group dba Diamond Ford (Diamond), asserting four causes of action for (1) Violation of the Song-Beverly Act – Breach of Express Warranty, (2) Violation of the Song-Beverly Act – Section 1793.2, (3) Fraudulent Inducement – Concealment, and (4) Negligent Repair.

 

On May 20, 2025, Plaintiff filed the operative first amended complaint (FAC) asserting four causes of action for (1) Violation of the Song-Beverly Act – Breach of Express Warranty, (20 Violation of the Song-Beverly Act – Breach of Implied Warranty, (3) Violation of the Song-Beverly Act – Section 1793.2, and (4) Negligent Repair against Ford and Diamond.

 

On June 10, 2026, Plaintiff filed a motion to compel further responses to special interrogatories and a motion to compel further responses to requests for production from Ford.

 

On June 22, 2026, the Court granted the parties’ stipulation and protective order regarding discovery.

 

On September 2, 2026, Ford filed its oppositions.

 

On September 8, 2026, Plaintiff filed his replies.

 

-----

 

   II.          Preliminary Procedural Issues

 

Meet and Confer – A motion to compel further responses must be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration showing “a reasonable and good faith attempt at an informal resolution of each issue presented by the motion.” (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2016.040, 2030.300, subd. (b)(1), 2031.310, subd. (b)(2).)

 

The Liete Declaration provided by Plaintiff establishes that counsel for both parties met and conferred regarding these motions and attended an IDC prior to filing. The Court finds this requirement satisfied.

 

Timeliness of Ford’s Opposition  – All opposition papers are due nine court days before the hearing on the motion. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1005, subd. (b).) Due to the Court holiday on Monday, September 7, 2026, Ford’s opposition was due on or before August 1, 2026. Therefore, the opposition is untimely.

 

However, in the absence of any prejudice to Plaintiff, and in the interests of adjudicating the issues raised on the merits, the Court will exercise its discretion and consider the opposition on the merits.

 

-----

 

 III.          Legal Standard

Standard for Motions to Compel Further Discovery Responses - Interrogatories – In responding to a set of interrogatories, a party must provide one of the following: (1) an answer “containing the information sought to be discovered”; (2) “an exercise of the party’s option to produce writings”; or (3) an objection to the particular interrogatory.¿(Code Civ. Proc.,  § 2030.210, subd. (a).) Each answer to an interrogatory “shall be as complete and straightforward as the information reasonably available to the responding party permits.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 2030.220, subd. (a).) If any objections are made, “the specific ground for the objection shall be set forth clearly in the response.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.240, subd. (b).) 

Upon receipt of responses to interrogatories, the propounding party may move for an order compelling further responses if they deem the responses to be “evasive or incomplete” or if “[a]n objection to an interrogatory is without merit or too general.” (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300(a)(1), (a)(3).) A motion to compel further responses to interrogatories must be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration showing “a reasonable and good faith attempt at an informal resolution of each issue presented by the motion.” (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2016.040, 2030.300, subd. (b)(1).) The motion must also be accompanied by a separate statement containing the text of the interrogatories at issue, the text of each response at issue, and a statement of the reasons why a further response is required, though the court may permit a party to submit “a concise outline of the discovery request and each response in dispute” in lieu of a separate statement. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300, subd. (b)(2); Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1345.)  

A motion to compel further response lies where the party to whom the interrogatories were directed gave responses deemed improper by the propounding party; e.g., objections, or evasive or incomplete answers. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.300.) Unlike a motion to compel further responses to a request for production, there is no “good cause” requirement applicable to a motion to compel further responses to interrogatories. “The ruling usually is based on consideration of the following factors:  the relationship of the information sought to the issues framed in the pleadings; the likelihood that disclosure will be of practical benefit to the party seeking discovery; the burden or expense likely to be encountered by the responding party in furnishing the information sought.” (Weil, et al., Cal. Prac. Guide, Civil Procedure Before Trial, Ch. 8F-7, ¶ 8:1181; see also Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc v. Superior Court (1968) 263 Cal.App.2d 12, 18–19 [“[I]n deciding a motion under [former] section 2030 the trial court must, of necessity, consider not only the stated objections to the interrogatories, but also the interrogatories themselves, as well as the pleadings, and the contentions of the interrogating party as to the purpose and validity of the interrogatories. If the interrogatories stray too far and seek information which cannot reasonably serve the acknowledged purposes of pretrial discovery, the motion should be denied.”].) If a timely motion to compel has been filed, the burden is on the responding party to justify any objection or failure to fully answer. (Coy v. Superior Court of Contra Costa County (1962) 58 Cal.2d 210, 220–221.) 

If a party fails to serve a timely response to interrogatories, the party waives the right to exercise the option to produce writings under Section 2030.230 and waives any objection to the demand, including objections based on privilege or work product. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (a).) The Court may relieve a party of this waiver only if the party has subsequently served a substantially code-compliant response and the party’s failure to serve a timely response was the result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2030.290, subd. (a)(1)-(2).)

Standard for Motions to Compel Further Discovery Responses – Requests for Production of Documents – Motions to compel further responses to requests for production must set forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought by the request. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(1).)¿To establish good cause, a discovery proponent must identify a disputed fact that is of consequence in the action and explain how the discovery sought will tend in reason to prove or disprove that fact or lead to other evidence that will tend to prove or disprove the fact.¿(Digital Music News LLC v. Superior Court (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 216, 224 (disapproved on other grounds by Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531).)

A demanding party may move for an order compelling further response to RPDs if (1) a statement of compliance with the demand is incomplete, (2) a representation of inability to comply is inadequate, incomplete, or evasive, or (3) an objection in the response is without merit or too general. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (a).) “A representation of inability to comply with the particular demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling shall affirm that a diligent search and a reasonable inquiry has been made in an effort to comply with that demand. This statement shall also specify whether the inability to comply is because the particular item or category has never existed, has been destroyed, has been lost, misplaced, or stolen, or has never been, or is no longer, in the possession, custody, or control of the responding party. The statement shall set forth the name and address of any natural person or organization known or believed by that party to have possession, custody, or control of that item or category of item.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.230.)

If a party fails to serve a timely response to a request for production, the party waives any objection to the demand, including objections based on privilege or work product. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300, subd. (a).) The Court may relieve a party of this waiver only if the party has subsequently served a substantially code-compliant response and the party’s failure to serve a timely response was the result of mistake, inadvertence, or excusable neglect. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.300, subd. (a)(1)-(2).)

 IV.          Discussion

 

Application – Plaintiff moves to compel Ford’s further responses to Special Interrogatories, Nos. 10, 26, 47, and 49-58, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 2030.300, subdivision (e) and 2023.030, subdivision (g) on the grounds that Ford has failed to provide code-compliant answers responsive to the request and has asserted meritless objections. (Notice of Motion, p. 1:8-11.) Plaintiff also moves to compel further responses to Request for Production, Nos. 16-18, 20-30, 47, 49, 50-54 and 55-60 pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 2031.310 on the same grounds. (Notice of Motion, p. 1:8-10.)

 

The relevant factual background is set forth by Plaintiff as follows:

 

Plaintiff served the discovery requests on November 19, 2025. (Leite Decl., ¶ 3, Ex. A.) Ford served unverified responses on December 23, 2025, later serving verifications on January 23, 2026. (Leite Decl., ¶ 4, Ex. B.) Plaintiff initiated a meet and confer process on January 13, 2026 with a letter to Ford’s counsel identifying deficiencies in the responses. (Liete Decl., ¶ 5, Ex. C.) On January 30, 2026, counsel for both parties agreed to extend the deadline to file a motion to compel to June 10, 2026 to allow time to attend an IDC and continue meet and confer efforts. (Liete Decl., ¶ 6, Ex. D.) The parties attended an IDC on May 27, 2026 and judicial guidance was provided as to which responses should be supplemented by Ford, but Plaintiff had not received any supplemental responses as of the date of filing. (Liete Decl., ¶¶ 7-8.)

 

a.     Special Interrogatories

 

At issue are Special Interrogatory Nos. 10, 26, 47, and 49-58.

 

No. 10 asks Ford to state all facts supporting its contention that the vehicle does not qualify for repurchase under the Song-Beverly Act. Ford responded that the facts in support of its contention are or may be found in a number of documents produced with Ford’s initial disclosure, listed the relevant documents, and objected on the grounds that the interrogatory was overly broad, unduly burdensome, seeks irrelevant information because it is not limited to a reasonable or relevant timeframe, to the components or systems at issue, or to the allegations in Plaintiff’s Complaint, seeks expert opinions and/or premature disclosure of information developed by Ford’s experts and/or consultants, and seeks information protected from disclosure by the attorney-client privilege and/or the work product doctrine.

 

Plaintiff argues Ford’s reliance on reference to documents provided in response is insufficient, as there is no way for Plaintiff to know what facts within the documents, if any, are responsive to the request.

 

The documents referenced by Ford relate to their general records regarding the vehicle, communications regarding the vehicle, repair and service information, reimbursements to Ford dealers, service contracts, and other generally applicable documents like TSBs and an Owner’s Manual and Warranty Guide. The Court finds Ford’s response generic and non-compliant. Ford argues that it provided documents containing the responsive facts, and they are therefore equally available to Plaintiff. (Opp., p. 7:2-3.) While the general facts themselves may be contained within those documents, what is not available to Plaintiff are what facts Ford is relying on when contending that  Plaintiff’s vehicle does not qualify for repurchase. Ford needs to state why it has determined the vehicle does not qualify.  

 

Interrogatory No. 26 asks Ford to describe all training provided from 2022 to present regarding how to evaluate a pre-litigation repurchase request under the Song-Beverly Act. Ford responded by referring Plaintiff to its Warranty Policy and Procedure Manuals for 2022 to present subject to a protective order, objected on the same grounds as stated above, on relevance grounds as Plaintiff did not request a repurchase or replacement of the subject vehicle before filing this lawsuit, and asserts that the facts and circumstances surrounding vehicle repurchases or replacements vary widely depending on any number of factors, so the training materials made available may vary or differ depending on the circumstances of a repurchase request.

 

Plaintiff again argues Ford’s reliance on reference to documents provided in response is insufficient, as there is no way for Plaintiff to know what facts within the documents, if any, are responsive to the request. Here, the Court disagrees.

 

In response to this request, Ford offered to produce certain documents which outline its training policies and procedures from 2022 through 2025, when Plaintiff filed suit. Ford’s counsel Adam Trott asserts that upon the stipulation and protective order being filed on June 18, 2026, Ford served a Workshop Manual for the vehicle and Ford’s warranty policy and procedure applicable from 2022 through 2025. (Trott Decl., ¶ 10, Ex. A.) Trott states Ford made a second confidential production on August 31, 2026, producing Ford’s Customer Relationship Center policies and procedures applicable from 2021 through 2025 and the RAV policy and procedure manual applicable from 2022 to present. (Trott Decl., Ex. B.) Here, unlike Interrogatory No. 10, the information regarding training provided for evaluation of a pre-litigation repurchase request is available to Plaintiff through these documents. There is no basis provided justifying an order requiring Ford to separately describe this information where it has been made available to Plaintiff pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 2030.230.

 

Interrogatory No. 47 asks Ford to describe its efforts to preserve relevant and discoverable information in this case, including preservation letters, the custodians to whom such preservation letters were sent, and the efforts taken to prevent against deletion or destruction of information. Ford objected on the same grounds as stated above and on the ground that the request is irrelevant as Plaintiff had not identified any documents that Ford has failed to produce and that Ford’s records management program is confidential and proprietary. Interrogatory Nos. 49-54 ask Ford to identify each person responsible for the operations, maintenance, expansion, archiving and upkeep of the network where its customer contact call logs (No. 49), documents regarding any pre-litigation repurchase analysis it performed for the vehicle (No. 50), policies and procedures for evaluating a repurchase or replacement under the Song-Beverly Act (No. 51), technical assistance cases between Ford and its authorized repair facilities (No. 52), documents evidencing any consumer complaints for defects in materials and workmanship made to Ford (No. 53), and warranty claim records (No. 54) are electronically stored from 2022 to present. Interrogatory Nos. 55-58 ask Ford to state its document/data retention or destruction policies used by those who evaluate California consumer requests for repurchase or replacement (No. 55), and that govern customer call log records (No. 56), technical assistance cases between Ford and its authorized repair facilities (No. 57), and warranty claim records (No. 58) from 2022 to the present.

 

Ford objected on the same grounds asserted above, that the requests are overly broad, unduly burdensome, seek irrelevant information, seek expert opinions and/or premature disclosure of information developed by Ford’s experts and/or consultants, seek information protected from disclosure by the attorney-client privilege and/or the work product doctrine, and seek confidential and proprietary information.

 

Plaintiff cites to federal authority holding that a defendant is required to disclose certain information about electronically stored information sources it has preserved in support of compelling further responses to these requests, and asserts that Ford’s record retention and policies directly relate to whether Ford has withheld or destroyed responsive evidence and relate to willfulness. In opposition, Ford cites to a number of federal decisions holding that discovery about discovery is, by definition, not information reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence. (Opp., p. 12:15-13:3.)

 

While the Court finds that some of Ford’s objections are without merit, including objections on the basis of expert opinion, premature disclosure of information developed by Ford’s experts, and attorney-client privilege, the Court agrees that these requests are largely irrelevant, overbroad in scope, and the Court likewise fails to see how Ford’s data retention and destruction policies dating back to 2022 are associated with Ford’s repair analysis of Plaintiff’s vehicle or Plaintiff’s ability to prove his case. Plaintiff argues that this information is necessary to confirm Ford’s compliance with their discovery requests, but the Court is not aware of any law permitting discovery to the extent sought here to obtain the names of every person who has been responsible for archival and maintenance of records for three years. Furthermore, as discussed below, it does not appear that Ford failed to produce responsive documents on the grounds that the documents do not exist or have been overwritten or deleted in the normal course of implementation its records retention policies. In short, the information sought is irrelevant to Plaintiff’s claims.

 

Furthermore, Plaintiff’s argument on reply, if nothing else, establishes that these requests are not necessarily specific to the case at hand. Plaintiff argues that his counsel routinely litigates Song-Beverly actions against Ford’s counsel and has observed varying and inconsistent productions by Ford across numerous other cases, raising questions as to whether Ford’s general search, retention, and preservation practices are being consistently applied in every case. (Reply, p. 4:14-18.) Plaintiff does not point to any specific deficiencies in the production in this case to overcome the validity of Ford’s relevance objections, and discovery produced by Ford in unrelated, separate cases has no bearing on the relevancy of the documents sought here.

 

As a final note, Plaintiff relies, in part, on Kwan v. Mercedes-Benz of North America, Inc. (1994) 23 Cal.App.4th 174, 186 to support its argument as to the relevance of retention policies. The passage cited by Plaintiff from Kwan related to a “written policy on implementation of the Act's provisions,” where the plaintiff argued that the defendant’s “lack [of a Song-Beverly implementation policy] is circumstantial evidence of a corporate state of mind that intended to violate the Act.” (Kwan, supra, 23 Cal.App.4th at 186.) The Kwan Court made no mention of relevancy of retention policies.

 

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s request is GRANTED as to Special Interrogatory No. 10. The remainder of Plaintiff’s requests are DENIED.

 

b.     Requests for Production

 

At issue are Request Nos. 16-18, 20-30, 47, and 49-59.

 

Request No. 16 seeks each written warranty provided by Ford for the subject vehicle. Ford produced the New Vehicle Limited Warranty Guide for the 2022 Ford Mustang Mach-E and objected on the grounds that the request is overly broad, unduly burdensome, and seeks irrelevant documents because it is not limited to any specific written warranty, to the concerns related to the subject vehicle contained in Plaintiff’s repair or service records and to the allegations in Plaintiff’s Complaint. The Court finds that Ford’s objections are without merit and that the response provided is not code-compliant. “A representation of inability to comply with the particular demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling shall affirm that a diligent search and a reasonable inquiry has been made in an effort to comply with that demand. This statement shall also specify whether the inability to comply is because the particular item or category has never existed, has been destroyed, has been lost, misplaced, or stolen, or has never been, or is no longer, in the possession, custody, or control of the responding party. The statement shall set forth the name and address of any natural person or organization known or believed by that party to have possession, custody, or control of that item or category of item.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.230.) This request is reasonably tailored in scope to the time period from sale of the vehicle to date, and Plaintiff has shown good cause to compel production of the documents sought. It may very well be that Ford has produced all the documents responsive to the request. But its current response is qualified and ambiguous. If no other responsive documents are available, Ford must provide a code-compliant response so asserting.

 

Request No. 17 seeks all documents constituting Ford’s warranty claims policy and procedures from 2022 to the present. Ford produced its Warranty Policy and Procedure Manual applicable to 2022 to the present, subject to a protective order. Ford is correct that “all documents constituting” renders the request unreasonably open-ended and subject to interpretation: “Plaintiff’s assertion that Ford’s responses are not Code compliant because Ford does not identify with particularity any document to which an objection is made is baseless, because it ignores the fact that Plaintiff’s Requests seek the production of ‘all documents.’” (Opposition, at 10.) Given the nature of the request, the Court does not know how Ford is to “identify with particularity” every document that is not produced that could conceivably all within the scope of the request. Ford’s response is adequate.  

 

 

Request No. 18 seeks Ford’s workshop manuals for the subject vehicle. Ford referred Plaintiff to the Workshop Manual for the 2022 Ford Mustang Mach-E and otherwise objected on the grounds stated above. For the reasons stated as to Request No. 16, Ford’s objections are without merit and its response is not code-compliant.

 

Request Nos. 20-22 seek Ford’s policies and procedures for evaluating consumer complaints, requests for a refund of money paid toward a vehicle, and determination of whether a vehicle is eligible for repurchase for vehicles manufactured or distributed by Ford from 2022 to present. Request Nos. 23-24 seek training materials regarding the handling of consumer requests for a vehicle repurchase and determination of whether a vehicle is eligible for repurchase. Request No. 25 seeks all scripts and flow charts used by Ford in handling consumer requests for repurchase or replacement. Request Nos. 26-30 seek Ford’s policies and procedures for defining certain terms and establishing time frames in which to respond to repurchase requests in connection with vehicle repurchase. Ford agreed to comply in part and produce all responsive documents not subject to objection, and otherwise objected on the grounds stated above and because complaints, requests, and determinations regarding repurchase vary widely from customer to customer. For the reasons stated as to Request No. 17, and considering the scope of the requests, Ford’s responses, with its description of its supplemental productions, are adequate...

 

Request No. 47 seeks documents regarding Ford’s efforts to preserve relevant and discoverable information, including preservation letters, the custodians to whom such preservation letters were sent and efforts taken to prevent against deletion or destruction of information. Request Nos. 50-59 seek all documents regarding Ford’s policies and procedures related to disaster recovery backups of Ford’s databases and regarding Ford’s document retention policies and procedures for: (1) evaluating requests for repurchase; (2) customer call logs; (3) customer complaints; (4) technical assistance cases; and (5) customer warranty claim records, from 2022 to the present. Ford produced no documents and objected to these requests.

 

For the reasons stated above regarding Plaintiff’s motion to compel further responses to his Special Interrogatories, the Court finds that no good cause exists to compel these documents and that Ford’s relevancy objections have merit. Again, Plaintiff has failed to establish why disaster recovery backups of Ford’s databases are relevant to the issues raised in this action, and the discovery sought does not seek to address any specific deficiency present in Ford’s discovery production in this case.

 

Request No. 49 is not included on Plaintiff’s separate statement and the Court takes no action as to this request.

 

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s request is GRANTED in part as to Request Nos. 16 and 18.

 

Plaintiff’s request is DENIED as to the remaining requests.

 

c.      Sanctions

 

Plaintiff requests monetary sanctions in the amount of $1,980 for his special interrogatories motion and $2,240.00 for his document production motion. (Liete Decl., ¶ 5.)

 

“The court shall impose monetary sanctions … against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel a further response …, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (h).) 

 

The Court finds that Ford’s objections were warranted for a majority of the requests addressed in the motion to compel further responses to Plaintiff’s Special Interrogatories. The Court also finds that Ford acted with substantial justification in opposing the motions, and therefore declines to award monetary sanctions.

 

 

Accordingly, the Court DENIES Plaintiff’s request for sanctions.

 

-----

 

    V.          Conclusion

 

Plaintiff Alfonso Cardenas’ Motion to Compel Further Responses to Special Interrogatories from Defendant Ford Motor Company is GRANTED in part as to Special Interrogatory No. 10. The remainder of Plaintiff’s requests are DENIED.

 

Plaintiff Alfonso Cardenas’ Motion to Compel Further Responses to Requests for Production from Defendant Ford Motor Company is GRANTED in part as to Requests Nos. 16 and-18, and otherwise DENIED.  

 

Plaintiff’s request for monetary sanctions is DENIED..

 

Defendant Ford is to serve amended, code-compliant responses in accordance with this Order within 15 days.


Case Number: 25AVCV01119    Hearing Date: September 15, 2026    Dept: A14

25AVCV01119 EVELYN NIEBLA, et al. vs 38665 11TH STREET EAST, LLC, A CALIFORNIA LIMITED LIABILITY COMPANY, et al

 

Before the Court is Defendants’ Demurrer to Plaintiffs’ Complaint. On September 2, 205, Plaintiffs filed their initial Complaint. On March 10, 2026, Plaintiffs filed their First Amended Complaint (“FAC”). On June 3, 2026, Defendants filed their demurrer to the “Complaint.”

 

The Demurrer on file appears directed at the Complaint of September 2, 2025. For instance:

 

 

  1. The Demurrer reads: “the Complaint alleges that emotional distress arose from the duty to maintain the Property in a habitable condition. (Complaint, ¶ 42).” (Demurrer, p. 8:7-9.) This correlates to ¶ 42 in the initial Complaint, but ¶ 42 of the FAC reads: “Subject to the terms of Plaintiffs’ leases and/or applicable law, Plaintiffs are entitled to an award of attorney's fees and costs.”
  2. The Demurrer reads: “the Complaint alleges that the duty in question is ‘the obligation to provided Plaintiffs with safe and habitable housing…’ (Complaint, ¶ 54.)” (Demurrer, p. 7:27-8:1.) The quoted language is present in ¶ 54 of the Complaint, but ¶ 54 of the FAC reads: “Plaintiffs re-allege and incorporate by reference each and every allegation described above as though set forth in full hereat.”
  3. The Demurrer reads: “the Complaint alleges that the private nuisance resulted from ‘vermin infestation, mold, water leaks, and other physical defects impacting the Property…’ (Complaint, ¶ 65).” (Demurrer, p. 8:26-27.) Again, this is consistent with ¶ 65 of the Complaint, while ¶ 65 of the FAC reads: “Subject to the terms of Plaintiffs’ leases and/or applicable law, Plaintiffs are entitled to an award of attorney's fees and costs.”

 

To further confuse matters, Plaintiffs opposition appears to assume that the Demurrer is directed towards the FAC.

 

The Court notes that, from the record before it, it is not entirely clear that the FAC was serve on Defendants. The three Proofs of Service filed by Plaintiffs (two filed on 4/10, one filed on 6/1) state the Complaint and other papers were served on Defendants but make no mention of service of the FAC. It could be that the “Complaint” referred to in the proofs of service is the FAC, but that is not apparent.

 

Given this confusion, the Court has no tentative ruling at this time. The Court will discuss with the parties the state of the pleadings at the hearing.

  Judge William H. Forman 

 

 

      I.          Background

 

This is a breach of contract action regarding probate administration and involving the real property located at 41788 156th Street East, Lancaster, CA 93535 (the Property). Cross-Defendants Mario Menjivar (Menjivar) and Kevin Hernandez (Hernandez) (together Cross-Defendants) each independently demurs to the First Amended Cross-Complaint (FACC) filed by Defendant and Cross-Complainant Tina M. Headley (“Headley” or “Cross-Complainant”).

 

On January 28, 2026, Plaintiff Menjivar filed a complaint against Defendants Headley, as an individual and as administrator of the Estate of Linda A. Garrett (Estate), and the Estate, asserting three causes of action for (1) Breach of Contract, (2) Quiet Title, and (3) Fraud.

 

Menjivar asserts that Linda A. Garrett (Garrett) is the biological mother of Headley, who serves as the Administrator of the Estate, that Garrett passed away on February 22, 2024 without a will or trust, and on July 15, 2025, Menjivar and Headley agreed that Headley would file probate in the Superior Court, County of Los Angeles and sell the Property as soon as she was appointed Administrator. (Compl., ¶¶ 3-4, 8-10, Ex. 1, 2.) Menjivar asserts that Headley filed for Probate Administration on July 23, 2025, was appointed Administrator on September 25, 2025, and Menjivar and Buyer Homes Built AR, LLC (Buyer) subsequently deposited $25,000 in earnest money to purchase the Property, which is the Estate’s only asset. (Compl., ¶¶ 11, 13-14, Ex. 1.) As of July 15, 2025, Menjivar alleges the Property carried a loan balance of $70,913.86 and fees of $22,984.38, went into default, and on September 18, 2025, Menjivar, Buyer, and Headley agreed Menjivar would contribute $27,498 to prevent the foreclosure of the Property, then scheduled for September 19, 2025. (Compl., ¶¶ 16-17, Ex. 4.) Menjivar alleges that Headley refused to obtain a Court-ordered surety bond for issuance of the letters, refused to approve a publication notice so the Property could be sold and Probate concluded, refused to sign off on the Notice of Sale draft publication, that the Property again went into foreclosure, forcing Menjivar and Buyer to pay $48,490.43 to prevent the foreclosure of the Property and protect their investment, and the mortgage is now paid in full with the Property remaining as Headley’s personal residence despite her agreement to sell the Property and never having made any escrow payments. (Compl., ¶¶ 20-32.)

 

On April 30, 2026, Headley filed an answer to the complaint and filed a Cross-Complaint against Cross-Defendants Menjivar, Hernandez, and Does 11 through 25, asserting six causes of action for (1) Breach of Fiduciary Duty, (2) Fraud and Intentional Misrepresentation, (3) Constructive Fraud, (4) Negligent Misrepresentation, (5) Unfair Business Practices (Bus. & Prof. Code § 17200), and (6) Unjust Enrichment.

   II.          Preliminary Procedural Issues

 

Cross-Complaint’s Noncompliance with Pleading Requirements – The FACC is not in compliance with the pleading requirements of the California Rules of Court, which require for all documents filed with the Court specific line spacing, consecutive line numbering and detailed placement and alignment, consecutive page numbering, a footer with the requisite specifications and containing the title of the paper, and compliance with various other formatting rules. (See Cal. Rules of Court rules 2.108 – 2.111.)

 

Meet and Confer Requirement – Before filing a demurrer or motion to strike, the demurring or moving party is required to meet and confer at least five days before the date the responsive pleading is due, in person, by telephone, or by video conference, with the party who filed the pleading for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer or motion to strike. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 430.41, subd. (a), 435.5, subd. (a).).) The demurring or moving party shall file and serve with the demurrer or motion to strike a declaration stating the means by which the parties met and conferred and that the parties did not reach an agreement to resolve the issues raised, or that the party who filed the pleading demurred or objected to failed to respond or otherwise failed to meet and confer in good faith. (Ibid.) 

 

Cross-Defendants each filed a declaration of demurring or moving party regarding meet and confer efforts, and state that Cross-Complainant failed to respond to their requests to meet and confer or otherwise failed to meet and confer in good faith. The Court finds this requirement satisfied.

 III.          Legal Standard

Standard for Demurrer – A party against whom a complaint has been filed may object by demurrer to the pleading on the ground that the pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action, the pleading is uncertain, or that it cannot be ascertained from the pleading whether the contract is written, oral, or implied by conduct in an action founded upon a contract. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (e) – (g).) A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations reasonably, liberally, and in context. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

As a general matter, in a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) “A demurrer tests the pleading alone, and not the evidence or facts alleged.” (E-Fab, Inc. v. Accountants, Inc. Servs. (2007) 153 Cal.App.4th 1308, 1315.) As such, the court assumes the truth of the complaint’s properly pleaded or implied factual allegations. (Ibid.) Courts must “treat the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Blank, supra, 39 Cal.3d at 318.) The only issue a demurrer is concerned with is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action. (Hahn, supra, 147 Cal.App.4th at 747.)

Where a demurrer is sustained, leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 348.) The burden is on the plaintiff to show the court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Id.; Lewis v. YouTube, LLC (2015) 244 Cal.App.4th 118, 126.) The threshold for meeting this burden is low, and “[i]f there is any reasonable possibility that the plaintiff can state a good cause of action, it is error to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend.” (Youngman v. Nevada Irrigation Dist. (1969) 70 Cal.2d 240, 245).

 

Standard for Motion to Strike – A motion to strike lies where either (1) there is “irrelevant, false or improper matter inserted in any pleading,” or (2) where a pleading or part thereof is “not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule or order of court.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) The Court has the authority to strike out any part of a pleading not filed in conformity with an order of the court. (See Ibid; Ferraro v. Camarlinghi (2008) 161 Cal.App.4th 509, 528.) 

When the pleadings are defective, “the defect may be raised by demurrer or motion to strike, or by motion for judgment on the pleadings.” (Coyne v. Krempels (1950) 36 Cal.2d 257, 262.) The grounds for a motion to strike shall appear on the face of the challenged pleading or form any matter of which the court is required to take judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437, subd. (a).) A matter to be judicially noticed shall be specified in the notice of motion. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437, subd. (b).) The court then may strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading and strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) When the defect which justifies striking a complaint is capable of cure, the court should allow leave to amend. (Perlman v. Municipal Court (1979) 99 Cal.App.3d 568, 575.)

 IV.          Discussion

 

Application – Cross-Defendants demur to the FACC pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.010, subdivisions (e) – (g) on the grounds that the FAC fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action because there is no causal connection between Cross-Defendants’ alleged conduct and damages, is unintelligible, ambiguous, and uncertain as it cannot be ascertained whether an alleged contract is written, oral, or implied in fact, the FACC does not sufficiently plead the elements necessary for each cause of action, and Cross-Complainant references a probate case to which Cross-Defendants are not parties without reference to a real estate sales agreement or agreement with Cross-Defendants. (Demurrers, p. 3:5-4:24.)

 

a.     Cross-Defendants’ Demurrers to the FACC

 

                                                  i.     Uncertain, Ambiguous, and Unintelligible

 

As a preliminary matter, Defendants demur to the FACC pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (f) on the basis that the FACC and allegations within are uncertain, ambiguous, and unintelligible.

 

“A defendant may demur to a complaint… on the ground that the ‘pleading is uncertain.’ (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (f).) The term ‘uncertain’ includes ‘ambiguous” and ‘unintelligible.’ (Ibid.).” (Van Horn v. Dep't of Toxic Substances Control (2014) 231 Cal. App. 4th 1287, 1295.) A “[d]emurrer for uncertainty is directed at the allegations actually made. [Citation.] Demurrer for uncertainty does not lie as to immaterial matter. [Citation.] Nor will a demurrer for uncertainty lie where the facts are presumptively within the knowledge of the demurring parties. [Citation.]” (Dumm v. Pac. Valves (1956) 146 Cal. App. 2d 792, 799.) “A special demurrer on the ground that an answer is (a) ambiguous, (b) unintelligible, or (c) uncertain is insufficient unless the demurrer points out specifically wherein the pleading is ambiguous, uncertain or unintelligible. [Citation.].” (Coons v. Thompson (1946) 75 Cal. App. 2d 687, 690 (citation omitted).)

 

Cross-Defendants specifically argue the FACC is uncertain because it does not allege required facts, contains conclusory allegations, and fails to comply with the California Rules of Court’s pleading standards as it has no page or line numbers. Here, the Court does not find that the FACC is so uncertain, ambiguous, or unintelligible that Cross-Defendants are not on notice of what is being alleged. Here, there are clear legal causes of action and related facts asserted throughout the pleading. Furthermore, Cross-Defendants’ arguments are, in effect, that the FACC fails to plead facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action, which is an issue raised appropriately pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.010, subdivision (e), not subdivision (f).

 

Cross-Defendants’ demurrers are OVERRULED to the extent the demurrers are brought pursuant to Section 430.10(f) for ambiguity. The Court will consider Cross-Defendants’ arguments as to failure to state facts sufficient to support a cause of action pursuant to subdivision (e) and, where applicable, (g).

 

                                                ii.     Action Founded Upon Contract

 

Cross-Defendants argue that it cannot be ascertained from the FACC whether the contract referenced throughout the FACC is written, oral, or implied by conduct, and therefore the FACC is subject to demurrer. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.010, subd. (g).) However, the FACC makes clear that the Agreement at issue is a written contract, as it alleges that Hernandez obtained Cross-Complainant’s signature on the Agreement and related addenda, that the Agreement was C.A.R. Form RLA, that an addendum was also signed by Menjivar as co-agent, cites to specific paragraphs within the Agreement, and establishes that the subsequent purchase agreement presented was written. (FACC, ¶¶ 9, 11-12, 13-14.)

 

Cross-Defendants’ demurrers are OVERRULED to the extent they are brought pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.010, subdivision (g).

 

                                              iii.     First Cause of Action – Breach of Fiduciary Duty

 

“The elements of a cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty are the existence of a fiduciary relationship, breach of fiduciary duty, and damages.” (Oasis West Realty, LLC v. Goldman (2011) 51 Cal.4th 811, 820.) Facts giving rise to a confidential, fiduciary or trustee relationship must be pled, and a “bare allegation that defendants assumed a fiduciary relationship” is a conclusion. (Zumbrun v. Univ. of So. Cal. (1972) 25 Cal.App.3d 1, 13.) “A fiduciary relationship is created where a person reposes trust and confidence in another and the person in whom such confidence is reposed obtains control over the other person’s affairs.” (Lynch v. Cruttenden & Co. (1993) 18 Cal.App.4th 802, 809.) But “[t]he mere placing of a trust in another person does not create a fiduciary relationship...[and] an agreement to communicate one’s knowledge, exercising his special knowledge and skill in the area of learning concerned, does not create a trust but only a contractual obligation.” (Zumbrun, supra, 25 Cal.App.3d at 13.)

 

Cross-Defendants argue the FACC does not plead damages connected to the allegations and does not allege what duty is owed to Cross-Complainant. (Demurrers, p. 5:19-22.)

 

The FACC alleges facts which, taken as true, establish the following: (1) Cross-Defendants entered into the Agreement with Cross-Complainant, establishing that Hernandez was listing agent of record and Menjivar was co-agent (FACC, ¶ 9); (2) by undertaking to act as Cross-Complainant’s listing agents, Cross-Defendants assumed a fiduciary relationship with Cross-Complainant and owed her the affirmative duties of utmost care, integrity, honesty, and loyalty under Civil Code section 2079.16 and Business and Professions Code section 10176 (FACC, ¶ 25); (3) Cross-Defendants engaged in numerous breaches of these duties supported by specific factual allegations, including, but not limited to, obtaining Cross-Complainant’s signature on a defective Agreement before she had any legal authority to bind the estate in knowing violation of Probate Code section 840, failing to submit the Agreement to his broker within 72 hours of execution thereby eliminating all oversight, utilizing the improper form Agreement as required under Probate Code section 10160, failed to disclose the relationship between Menjivar and Buyer and denying the relationship when confronted, blocking the return of Buyer’s deposit against Cross-Complainant’s direction, filing an incorrectly prepared bond waiver petition and falsely representing that refiling was impossible, keeping Cross-Complainant legally incapacitated, and filing litigation to enforce contracts they know are void, non-compliant, and unsupported (FACC, ¶ 26); and (4) that as a direct and proximate result of these breaches, Cross-Complainant has suffered and continues to suffer damages, including the costs and burden of defending this action, loss of the ability to administer and protect her late mother's estate for nearly a year, emotional distress arising from the coordinated exploitation of her grief and financial vulnerability, and other damages in amounts to be proven at trial (FACC, ¶¶ 23, 27.)

 

Thus, the FACC states specific factual allegations that Cross-Defendants owed Cross-Complainant specific fiduciary duties under specific statutes as listing agents, that they breached those duties, and Cross-Defendant suffered damages as a result. (See Branch v. Homefed Bank (1992) 6 Cal.App.4th 793, 800 [recovery of emotional distress damages is permitted where the defendant’s conduct constitutes a breach of fiduciary duties].) Moreover, “‘[t]he law imposes on a real estate agent ‘the same obligation of undivided service and loyalty that it imposes on a trustee in favor of his beneficiary.’ [Citations.] This relationship not only imposes upon him the duty of acting in the highest good faith towards his principal but precludes the agent from obtaining any advantage over the principal in any transaction had by virtue of his agency. [Citation.] ‘Such an agent is charged with the duty of fullest disclosure of all material facts concerning the transaction that might affect the principal’s decision.’ [Citations.]” (Roberts v. Lomanto (2003) 112 Cal. App. 4th 1553, 1562-1563.)

 

Finally, “‘A written contract may be pleaded either by its terms—set out verbatim in the complaint or a copy of the contract attached to the complaint and incorporated therein by reference—or by its legal effect. [Citation.] In order to plead a contract by its legal effect, plaintiff must ‘allege the substance of its relevant terms. This is more difficult, for it requires a careful analysis of the instrument, comprehensiveness in statement, and avoidance of legal conclusions.’” (Heritage Pacific Financial, LLC v. Monroy (2013) 215 Cal. App. 4th 972, 993.) Here, the legal effect of the Agreement between Cross-Complainant is sufficiently pleaded, as Cross-Complainant sets forth the exact standardized form used, certain terms under the Agreement, and the legal effect in that Cross-Defendants assumed responsibility as acting agents for the Property.

 

The FACC pleads facts sufficient to state a cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty. Cross-Defendants’ demurrers are OVERRULED.

 

Cross-Defendants did not demur to individual causes of action asserted within the Cross-Complaint and demurred only to the Cross-Complaint as a whole on the grounds that all causes of action fail to state facts sufficient to constitute causes of action. Accordingly, that the demurrer is not sustained as to one cause of action necessarily means that Cross-Defendants’ demurrer will be overruled in full. Cross-Defendant may, consistent with the Code of Civil Procedure, bring a motion for judgment on the pleadings as to such individual claims.

 

b.     Cross-Defendants’ Motions to Strike Portions of the FACC

 

Cross-Defendants also move to strike the requests for punitive damages in paragraphs 28, 34, and the Prayer for Relief in the FACC. (Mtns, p. 3:1-3.)

 

Punitive damages may be recovered upon a proper showing of malice, fraud, or oppression. (Civ. Code, § 3294, subd. (a).) “Malice” is defined as conduct intended to cause injury to a person or despicable conduct carried on with a willful and conscious disregard for the rights or safety of others. (Turman v. Turning Point of Cent. Cal., Inc. (2010) 191 Cal.App.4th 53, 63.) “Oppression” means despicable conduct subjecting a person to cruel and unjust hardship, in conscious disregard of the person’s rights. (Ibid.) “Fraud” is an intentional misrepresentation, deceit, or concealment of a material fact known by defendant, with intent to deprive a person of property, rights or otherwise cause injury. (Ibid.) Conclusory allegations, devoid of any factual assertions, are insufficient to support a conclusion that parties acted with oppression, fraud or malice. (Smith v. Superior Court (1992) 10 Cal.App.4th 1033, 1042.)

 

To succeed on a motion to strike punitive damages allegations, it must be said as a matter of law that the alleged behavior was not so vile, base, or contemptible that it would not be looked down upon and despised by ordinary decent people. (Angie M. v. Superior Court (1995) 37 Cal. App. 4th 1217, 1228-1229.) “Under the statute, malice does not require actual intent to harm.  Conscious disregard for the safety of another may be sufficient where the defendant is aware of the probable dangerous consequences of his or her conduct and he or she willfully fails to avoid such consequences.  Malice may be proved either expressly through direct evidence or by implication through indirect evidence from which the jury draws inferences.”  (Pfeifer v. John Crane, Inc. (2013) 220 Cal. App. 4th 1270, 1299 (internal quotations omitted).) 

 

“In order to state a prima facie claim for punitive damages, a complaint must set forth the elements as stated in the general punitive damage statute, Civil Code section 3294.” (Turman v. Turning Point of Central California, Inc. (2010) 191 Cal.App.4th 53, 63.) “In addition to the requirement that the operative complaint set forth the elements as stated in section 3294, it must include specific factual allegations showing that defendant's conduct was oppressive, fraudulent, or malicious to support a claim for punitive damages. [Citation.] Punitive damages may not be pleaded generally.” (Today's IV v. Los Angeles County Metropolitan Transportation Authority (2022) 83 Cal. App. 5th 1137, 1193.) “Not only must there be circumstances of oppression, fraud or malice, but facts must be alleged in the pleading to support such a claim.” (Grieves v. Superior Court (1984) 157 Cal. App. 3d 159, 166.)

 

As to Cross-Complainant’s request for punitive damages for Cross-Defendants’ alleged breach of fiduciary duty, the FACC sufficiently alleges fraud. Indeed, breach of fiduciary duty in a confidential relationship alone is “a species of fraud,” that “fraud itself implies malice,” and the maintenance of breach of fiduciary duty claim based on fraud and intentional misrepresentations, wrongful conduct, and concealment in violation of these duties, where Cross-Complainant was approached by Cross-Defendants shortly after the passing of her mother so they may control Probate proceedings and engage in a conflicted sale of the Property to a listed co-agent and deprive Cross-Complainant of the Estate Property is sufficient to maintain a punitive damages request. (Nicholson v. Rose (1980) 106 Cal.App.3d 457, 462.)

 

For the same reasons, and because the FACC sufficiently alleges intentional fraudulent conduct on behalf of Cross-Defendants intended to deprive Cross-Complainant of her rights to the Property in the context of a fiduciary relationship, the request for punitive damages for fraud is sufficiently pled to survive Cross-Defendants’ motions to strike.

 

Accordingly, Cross-Defendants’ Motions to Strike are DENIED.
