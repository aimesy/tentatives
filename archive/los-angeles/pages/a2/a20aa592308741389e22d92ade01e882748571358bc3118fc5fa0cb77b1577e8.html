Case Number: 24STCV18329    Hearing Date: June 26, 2026    Dept: 529

IMAD AKIKI VS COSIC SECURITY SERVICES, INC., ET AL

 

MOTION TO SET ASIDE DEFAULT JUDGMENT

 

Date of Hearing:        June 26, 2026                                     Trial Date:       N/A

Department:              529                                                     Case No.:        24STCV18329

 

Moving Party:            Defendants Drazen Cosic and Cosic Security Services, Inc.

Responding Party:     Late opposition by Plaintiff Akiki.

 

BACKGROUND

 

On July 24, 2024, Plaintiff Imad Akiki filed a complaint against Defendant Cosic Security Services, Inc., Drazen Cosic, and Albert Cosic for various violations of the Labor Code and Government Code along with wrongful termination in violation of public policy, intentional infliction of emotional distress, civil assault, civil battery, declaratory relief, and injunctive relief.

 

Default was entered against Defendants on January 21, 2025.

 

[TENTATIVE] RULING

 

Defendants Drazen Cosic and Cosic Security Services, Inc.’s Motion to Set Aside Default Judgment is DENIED.

 

DISCUSSION

 

Defendants Drazen Cosic and Cosic Security Services, Inc. move this court for an order setting aside the default judgment entered against Defendant. Defendants makes the motion on the grounds that the default judgment was entered as a result of Defendant's mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect.

 

It is well established that Code of Civil Procedure section 473 is remedial, and its provisions to be liberally construed so as to dispose of cases upon their merits. (See, e.g., Laguna Village, Inc. v. Laborers' Internat. Union of North America (1983) 35 Cal.3d 174, 182; Taliaferro v. Taliaferro (1963) 217 Cal.App.2d 216, 220.) Code of Civil Procedure section 473(b) consists of two distinct parts: “a discretionary provision, which applies permissively, and a mandatory provision, which applies as of right.” (Minick v. City of Petaluma (2016) 3 Cal.App.5th 15, 25-26; Bailey v. Citibank, N.A. (2021) 66 Cal.App.5th 335, 348.) 

 

Defendants argue they did not timely respond to the complaint because Defendant Cosic never received proper service of the summons and complaint. (Cosic Decl. ¶3.)

 

Initially, the court notes that Defendant Cosic Security Services, Inc. is a corporation and thus must be represented by counsel. The court cannot consider the motion for relief filed on behalf of this corporation because it is not represented by counsel. Accordingly, the motion for relief from default by Cosic Security Services, Inc. is denied.

 

As for Drazen Cosic’s motion, plaintiff contends that it is untimely because not filed within 6 months of the entry of default.  However, Drazen Cosic challenges the default on grounds that he was not properly served, rendering the default void and not voidable (and not subject to the six-month limitation for bringing a motion to set aside.)   Drazen Cosic’s declaration merely states that he was not personally handed the documents.  But service can be accomplished in many ways, including by substitute service, which is what occurred here.  Drazen Cosic does not dispute that the papers were delivered to Vinko Cosic, that the Palmetto drive address is his residence, or that the document was mailed to the same address thereafter.   Thus, Drazen Cosic does not satisfactorily establish that service was not accomplished or that the judgment is void.  

 

Accordingly, the court denies the motion for relief.

 

 


Case Number: 24STCV28848    Hearing Date: June 26, 2026    Dept: 529

LINDA FOK VS CHICKEN NOW MONTEBELLO, LLC, ET AL.

 

DEMURRER WITH MOTION TO STRIKE

 

Date of Hearing:        June 26, 2026                                     Trial Date:       None set

Department:              529                                                      Case No.:        24STCV28848

 

Moving Party:            Defendant Professional Security Consultants

Responding Party:     No opposition

Meet and Confer:      Yes. Jensen Decl.

 

BACKGROUND

 

On November 4, 2024, Plaintiff Linda Fok filed a complaint against Defendants Chicken Now Montebello, LLC dba Chicken Now, Tammy Lee, and Spinoso Real Estate Group DLS, LLC for intentional infliction of emotional distress, negligence, violation of Unruh Civil Rights Act (Civ. Code §§51, 52), negligent violation of statutory duty, premises liability and false imprisonment.

 

On October 15, 2025, Plaintiff dismissed Defendant Tammy Lee. Plaintiff also dismissed Defendant Spinoso Real Estate Group DLS, LLC on December 22, 2025.

 

Plaintiff named Bridge Group Investments II LLC as Doe 4, Professional Security Consultants as Doe 3, and Centennial Real Estate Management, LLC as Doe 2.

 

[TENTATIVE] RULING

 

Defendant Professional Security Consultants’ Demurrer to the Complaint is SUSTAINED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND; Defendant Professional Security Consultants’ Motion to Strike is GRANTED.

 

DISCUSSION

 

Defendant Professional Security Consultants (“PSC”) demur to the complaint on the grounds the complaint fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action and are fatally uncertain.

 

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) When considering demurrers, courts read the allegations liberally and in context. In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) “A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters. Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed. (CCP §§ 430.30, 430.70.) At the pleading stage, a plaintiff need only allege ultimate facts sufficient to apprise the defendant of the factual basis for the claim against him. (Semole v. Sansoucie (1972) 28 Cal. App. 3d 714, 721.) A “demurrer does not, however, admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law alleged in the pleading, or the construction of instruments pleaded, or facts impossible in law.” (S. Shore Land Co. v. Petersen (1964) 226 Cal.App.2d 725, 732 (internal citations omitted).) 

 

Defendant argues the pleading fails to state facts sufficient to constitute any cause of action against Defendant because the complaint alleges only conclusory detention-related assertions without identifying any PSC policy, act, omission, or employee conduct tying PSC to the alleged discrimination, negligence, premises conditions, or intentional misconduct. Moreover, the causes of action are fatally uncertain as to PSC because the Complaint lumps “All Defendants” together, does not distinguish between the restaurant, the mall operator, and security contractors, and fails to specify who did what, when, or under what authority with respect to PSC. Moreover, the sixth cause of action for false imprisonment is time-barred.

 

The court agrees Plaintiff has failed to allege facts sufficient to constitute a claim for intentional infliction of emotional distress, negligence, violation of Unruh Civil Rights Act, negligent violation of statutory duty, premises liability and false imprisonment against PCS. The complaint generally alleges the mall security personnel, acting within the course and scope of their employment then took Plaintiff to the mall security office where they detained her and subjected her to approximately three hours of custodial interrogation and during this detention, forced Plaintiff to write a statement prolonging her ordeal. (Compl. ¶¶25-26.) The complaint also alleges Defendants failed to properly screen security personnel, train security personnel and supervise security personnel. (Compl. ¶37.) These allegations do not connect PSC to the alleged misconduct; show how PSC breached any duty owed or caused harm to Plaintiff; show how PSC acted with discriminatory animus, adopted the restaurant’s alleged animus, or applied a discriminatory policy; and does not identify any physical condition causing injury. As for the false imprisonment claim, the court agrees the statute of limitations has run. The statute of limitations for false imprisonment is one year, which accrues upon release from confinement. (See CCP § 340(c); Scannell v. County of Riverside (1984) 152 Cal.App.3d 596, 606.) Plaintiff alleges that mall security personnel escorted her to the mall security office, and detained her, on April 23, 2023. (Compl., ¶ 15) The complaint in this case was filed November 4, 2024, more than one year later.

 

Based on the foregoing, the demurrer is SUSTAINED as to the first – fifth causes of action with leave to amend and without leave to amend as to the sixth cause of action.

 

Motion to Strike

 

Defendant PSC moves to strike Paragraph 34 and Paragraph 3 of the Prayer for punitive damages on the grounds that the punitive damages allegations against PSC are conclusory, lack specific ultimate facts showing oppression, fraud, or malice by PSC or any officer, director or managing agent involvement, authorization or ratification by PSC.

 

The court may, upon a motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (CCP § 436(a).) The court may also strike all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of this state, a court rule, or an order of the court. (CCP § 436(b).) The grounds for a motion to strike are that the pleading has irrelevant, false or improper matter, or has not been drawn or filed in conformity with laws. (CCP § 436.) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (CCP § 437.) 

 

Defendant PSC argues the complaint pleads only conclusory punitive buzzwords with no supporting ultimate facts against PSC and does not allege conscious disregard, authorization, or ratification on the part of an officer, director, or managing agent of the corporation. The court agrees the complaint has not alleged facts sufficient to support a claim for punitive damages against PSC.

 

Based on the foregoing, the motion to strike is GRANTED WITH LEAVE TO AMEND.

 


Case Number: 26STCV04503    Hearing Date: June 26, 2026    Dept: 529

26STCV04503

 

Moving Party:            Defendants Dave & Buster’s Management, LLC, Dave & Buster’s of California, Inc. and Dave & Buster’s, Inc.

Responding Party:     Plaintiff Christian Singer

 

BACKGROUND

 

On February 10, 2026, Plaintiff Christian Singer, individually and on behalf of other aggrieved employees pursuant to the California Private Attorneys General Act filed a complaint against Dave & Buster’s Management, LLC, Dave & Buster’s of California, Inc., and Dave & Buster’s Inc. for enforcement under the Private Attorney General Act, California Labor Code §2698, et seq.

 

[TENTATIVE] RULING

 

Defendant Dave & Buster’s Management, LLC, Dave & Buster’s of California, Inc., and Dave & Buster’s Inc.’s Motion to Compel Arbitration and Stay Proceedings is GRANTED.

 

EVIDENTIARY OBJECTIONS

 

Defendant Dave & Buster’s Management, LLC, Dave & Buster’s of California, Inc., and Dave & Buster’s Inc. submits objections to the declaration of Christian Singer.

 

The court OVERRULES objections nos. 1 – 7.

 

DISCUSSION

 

Defendant Dave & Buster’s Management, LLC, Dave & Buster’s of California, Inc., and Dave & Buster’s Inc. move for an order compelling Plaintiff Christian Singer to arbitrate all individual claims on the grounds that all employment-related disputes involving Plaintiff against Defendants are subject to a valid and enforceable arbitration agreement that requires Plaintiff to resolve all individual claims, including his individual PAGA claims, through arbitration and not in civil court.

 

Parties may be compelled to arbitrate a dispute upon the court finding that: (1) there was a valid agreement to arbitrate between the parties; and (2) said agreement covers the controversy or controversies in the parties’ dispute.¿ (CCP §1281.2; Omar v. Ralphs Grocery Co. (2004)¿118 Cal.App.4th 955, 961.) Because the right to arbitration depends upon contract, the party seeking arbitration bears the initial burden of proving that the parties actually agreed to arbitrate the instant dispute.¿ (Hotels Nevada v. L.A. Pacific Center, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 754, 761.)¿ If the moving party does so, the burden shifts to the opposing party to show that the subject agreement is unenforceable.¿ (Id. at 761.)¿ The court “sits as a trier of fact, weighing all the affidavits, declarations, and other documentary evidence, as well as oral testimony received at the court’s discretion, to reach a final determination.”¿ (Engalla v. Permanente Med. Grp., Inc. (1997) 15 Cal.4th 951, 972.)¿¿¿ 

 

Defendants argue Plaintiff brings this representative action for penalties under the PAGA despite entering into a binding arbitration agreement with Dave & Buster’s Management, LLC (“D&B”) agreeing to submit all claims, including any individual PAGA claims, to arbitration on an individual basis. As part of the application process, there was an arbitration agreement for Plaintiff to review and complete. Plaintiff electronically signed his arbitration agreement through the ATP system on March 8, 2023. (Walters Decl., Exh. C.) The Agreement provides:

 

All disputes covered by this Agreement between me and the Company shall be decided by a single arbitrator through arbitration and not by way of court or jury trial. The Company and I expressly agree that this Agreement shall be construed, interpreted and its validity and enforceability determined, in accordance with the Federal Arbitration Act (9 U.S.C. § 1, et seq.).

 

*** 1. COVERED DISPUTES. . . . the Company and I mutually understand, contract and agree to the resolution by arbitration of . . . . claims arising out of or related to my application for employment, employment, and/or the termination of my employment, that the Company may have against me or that I may have against the Company, and/or any of its . . . officers, directors, employees, or agents . . . and/or . . . successors and assigns of any of them (all of whom may enforce this Agreement as a direct or third-party beneficiary).

 

Except as it otherwise provides, this Agreement is intended to apply to the resolution of disputes that otherwise would be resolved in a court of law or before a forum other than arbitration. This Agreement requires all such disputes to be resolved only by a single arbitrator through final and binding arbitration. . . .

 

Except for claims excluded in the section of this Agreement entitled “Disputes Not Covered by this Agreement” below, this Agreement also applies, without limitation, to disputes arising out of or in connection with the employment relationship . . . .

 

The Agreement also expressly requires Plaintiff to submit his individual PAGA claims to arbitration, as follows:

 

PRIVATE ATTORNEYS GENERAL ACT (“PAGA”) INDIVIDUAL ACTION REQUIREMENT. YOU AND THE COMPANY AGREE TO ARBITRATE PAGA CLAIMS ON AN INDIVIDUAL BASES ONLY. THEREFORE, ANY CLAIM BY YOU UNDER PAGA TO RECOVER YOUR UNPAID WAGES OR OTHER INDIVIDUAL RELIEF MUST BE ARBITRATED UNDER THIS AGREEMENT. (Exh. C, p. 3 §5.)

 

The Agreement also includes a “delegation clause”. (See Exh. C, p. 1 -2 §1.) Based on all these provisions, Defendants the Agreement broadly provides that it applies to all claims between Plaintiff, D&B, and any related entities and the arbitrator has the exclusive authority to “resolve any dispute relating to the interpretation, applicability, enforceability, formation, or waiver of this Agreement including, but not limited to any claim that all or any part of this Agreement is void or voidable.”

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues there is no valid arbitration agreement as the electronic signature cannot be authenticated and Plaintiff disputes that the arbitration agreement was presented to him during the onboarding process. Moreover, plaintiff contends the agreement is a contract of adhesion and is unconscionable. Specifically, the arbitration agreement must be accepted on a take-it-or-leave-it basis; the arbitration agreement attempts to limit discovery in this fact-intensive PAGA action; and contains an unlawful fee-shifting provision that allows the Arbitrator to award attorney’s fees and costs to the employer in a PAGA action.

 

Here, the court finds Defendant has established the existence of an arbitration agreement. A proponent of an arbitration agreement that is electronically signed must “present sufficient evidence to support a finding that the electronic signature on the .¿.¿. agreement was the act of” the other party. (Ruiz v. Moss Bros. Auto Group, Inc. (2014) 232¿Cal.¿App.¿4th¿836, 842.) “An electronic record or electronic signature is attributable to a person if it was the act of the person. The act of the person may be shown in any manner, including the showing of the efficacy of any security procedure applied to determine the person to which the electronic record or electronic signature was attributable.” (Civ. Code §¿1633.9(a).)

 

To verify an electronic signature, there must be “evidence about the process used to verify [the signer’s] electronic signature .¿.¿. including who sent [the signer] the Contract, how the Contract was sent to [the signer], how [the signer’s] electronic signature was placed on the Contract, who received the signed [] Contract, how the signed Contract was returned to [the moving party], and how [the signer’s] identification was verified as the person who actually signed the Contract.” (Fabian v. Renovate America, Inc. (2019) 42¿Cal.¿App.¿5th¿1062, 1069.) Details about transmission and use of unique login credentials and the steps an electronic signer must take to affix his or her electronic signature suffice to properly authenticate a digitally signed document. (See Espejo v. Southern California Permanente Medical Group (2016) 246¿Cal.¿App.¿4th¿1047, 1062.)

 

Here, Defendants have satisfied by affidavit testimony the requirements of Ruiz, Fabian, and Espejo. Ms. Walters, the Senior Director of Total Rewards, testifies from during the relevant time period, D&B used an Administrative Transformation Program (“ATP”) on IBM Kenexa (“Kenexa”), a third-party application and onboarding management platform. (Walters Decl. ¶8.) Candidates applying for a job at D&B were required to visit the Kenexa Brassring portal online and either: (a) click the option to create a personal account by providing their name, address, phone number, and an email address, and setting up a password; (b) log in with a personal account they previously created by providing the same information and entering their password; or (c) apply without creating an account by providing certain personal information including their name, phone number, and email address. (Walters Decl. ¶9.) To extend an employment offer to a candidate, D&B created an offer in the ATP system, which generated an offer letter that was sent to the email address provided by the candidate on the Brassring portal. (Walters Decl. ¶10.) If a candidate stated an intent to accept an offer letter, D&B moved them along in the ATP system, which generated an email to the address the candidate provided when applying for the position. (Walters Decl. ¶11.) The email included an invitation to complete onboarding paperwork on the Kenexa Onboard portal. (Walters Decl. ¶11.) To complete onboarding paperwork necessary before reporting to work, the candidate was required to create a personal profile, including creating a password. (Walters Decl. ¶11.) The candidate could create a personal signature by moving their computer mouse, and after creating a signature, click to approve it. (Walters Decl. ¶11.) A candidate who created a profile on the Onboard portal was then provided with onboarding paperwork directly on the Onboard portal, including the Arbitration Agreement. (Walters Decl. ¶12.) The documents were presented sequentially, so that a candidate must complete one before going to the next, and they could not be moved along to the HRIS system, including payroll, unless all onboarding documents were completed. (Walters Decl. ¶12.) There was no restriction limiting the time a candidate could review the Arbitration Agreement. (Walters Decl. ¶13.) Once the candidate reviewed the Arbitration Agreement, the candidate was directed to click on a box to upload their signature to be applied to the Arbitration Agreement if they had already created one, or to draw their signature using their mouse if they had not created one. (Walters Decl. ¶14.) Once the onboarding documents, including the Arbitration Agreement, were completed by a candidate, they were stored within Kenexa. (Walters Decl. ¶16.) An email communication to complete D&B’s onboarding paperwork was sent to Plaintiff through ATP on March 7, 2023, to [email protected], which was the personal email address that Plaintiff provided to D&B. (Walters Decl. ¶18.) Ms. Walters attaches a screenshot on the ATP System confirming that, as part of the onboarding paperwork process, Plaintiff completed the initial task of providing D&B with certain personal information and confirming that Plaintiff completed all onboarding paperwork as of March 8, 2023. (Walters Decl. ¶¶19-20, Exhs. A, B.)

 

This testimony overcomes Plaintiff’s contention he does not recall seeing any arbitration agreement during the onboarding process and the signature does not look like his. (See Craig v. Brown & Root, Inc. (2000) 84 Cal.App.4th 416.) As for Plaintiff’s other contentions, the court agrees with Defendant that there is no requirement for anyone to be physically present to verify Plaintiff’s electronic signature and D&B had already set forth in detail the security procedure applied. In Defendant’s supplemental declaration, Ms. Walters’ also attaches the missing screenshot of the ATP system showing the acknowledgment of the Agreement.

 

Next, regarding Plaintiff’s contention that the Arbitration Agreement is a contract of adhesion, the mere fact that an employment agreement was presented on a take-it-or-leave-it basis, thus making it a contract of adhesion, indicates the presence of only some procedural unconscionability. (See Baltazar v. Forever 21, Inc. (2016) 62¿Cal.¿4th¿1237, 1245.) Moreover, the court finds Plaintiff’s contention that the Arbitration Agreement is unconscionable because it provides for limited discovery does not make the Arbitration Agreement here unconscionable. The discovery provision here provides: “Each party shall have the right to take the deposition of two individual fact witnesses and any expert witness designated by another party. Each party also shall have the right to propound one (1) set of written interrogatories (not to exceed ten (10)) and one (1) set of requests for production of documents (not to exceed twenty-five (25)) to any party. Additional discovery may be had by mutual agreement of the parties or where the Arbitrator selected so orders pursuant to a request by either party. Each party shall have the right to subpoena witnesses and documents for the arbitration, as well as documents relevant to the case from third parties, in accordance with any applicable state or federal law.” The court in Dotson v. Amgen, Inc. (2010) 181 Cal.App.4th 975, held a provision limiting parties to one deposition as of right with additional depositions available upon request to the arbitrator was not unconscionable. Like Dotson, the language here permits the arbitrator to expand discovery upon request by the parties and allows robust third party discovery, as well as trial subpoena powers.

 

Lastly, the court agrees Section 14 is not an unlawful fee-shifting provision. Section 14 provides: “To the extent allowed by applicable law and if the claim(s) or counterclaim(s) brought by either party in arbitration allow for imposition of sanctions, the Arbitrator may award either party its reasonable attorneys” fees and costs, including reasonable expenses associated with production of witnesses or proof, upon a finding that the claim or counterclaim was frivolous or brought solely to harass the Employee or the Company.” This provision is limited to claims that are "frivolous or brought solely to harass,” and is bound by applicable state law.   As such, it does not contain an unconscionable term. 

 

Based on the foregoing, the motion to compel arbitration is GRANTED. The representative PAGA claims are stayed pending conclusion of the individual arbitration claim.
