[TENTATIVE] RULING ON PETITION FOR WRIT OF MANDATE

 

Petitioner William Molinari (“Petitioner”) petitions for a writ of mandate against respondents City of Montebello (“City”), Montebello City Council (“Council”), and Montebello Planning Commission (“Commission”) (collectively, “Respondent”), directing it to set aside the decision to deny his application to build 15 single-family residences at 301 Jacmar Drive, Montebello, California (“Project”) and compelling Respondent to approve the Project.

The petition for writ of mandate is granted.

I.                Factual Background

The following facts are undisputed.  

The Project site is a 63,745 square foot lot improved with a one-story single-family residence. (AR 212, 240.)

On January 22, 2024, Petitioner applied to demolish the existing improvement, subdivide the lot into 16 lots, and build a single-family residence on each of 15 of those lots. (AR 1528-33.) The same day, Respondent deemed the application complete. (AR 1528.)

There is no notice of inconsistency in the administrative record.[1]

The Commission held hearings on the Project on June 4, 2024, June 18, 2024, and July 16, 2024. (AR 986-89, 1234-37, 1438-42.) For each hearing, staff issued staff reports recommending approval. (AR 990-1001, 1238-48, 1443-53.) At the end of the July 16, 2024 hearing, the Commission voted not to approve the Project. (AR 984-85.)

Petitioner appealed the Commission’s decision to the Council. (AR 881-82.)

On March 26, 2025, the Council held a public hearing. (AR 196-203.) City staff again recommended approving the Project. (AR 204-214.) The Council denied Petitioner’s appeal, letting stand the Commission’s disapproval of the Project. (AR 133-34.)

II.             Procedural History

On June 23, 2025, Petitioner filed his verified petition. Proofs of service filed June 26, 2025 indicate Petitioner served the summons and petition on Respondent by personal service on June 26, 2025.

On February 26, 2026, Petitioner filed his opening brief. On April 23, 2026, Respondent filed its opposition.[2] On May 19, 2026, Petitioner filed his reply.

On May 20, 2026, Petitioner lodged the certified administrative record with the Court.

On May 29, 2026, the Court continued the hearing on the petition (originally calendared for June 4, 2026) and directed the parties to submit limited supplemental briefing addressing a single issue not covered in the original filings, namely, whether Richman v. Santa Monica Rent Control Board (1992) (“Richman”) governs the exhaustion analysis presented in this case. The Court ordered that the supplemental briefs address only that discrete question of law.

On June 18, 2026, Petitioner filed a supplemental brief. On July 7, 2026, Respondents likewise filed a supplemental brief. 

III.           Standard of Review

1.               Administrative Mandamus

Code of Civil Procedure section 1094.5 is the administrative mandamus provision that structures the procedure for judicial review of adjudicatory decisions rendered by administrative agencies. (Topanga Ass’n for a Scenic Community v. County of Los Angeles (1974) 11 Cal.3d 506, 514-15 [“Topanga”]).) Section 1094.5 does not specify which cases are subject to independent review, leaving that issue to the courts. (Fukuda v. City of Angels (1999) 20 Cal.4th 805, 811). In cases reviewing decisions that affect a vested, fundamental right, the trial court exercises independent judgment on the evidence. (Bixby v. Pierno (1971) 4 Cal.3d 130, 143; see Code Civ. Proc. § 1094.5(c).)

An agency is presumed to have regularly performed its official duties (Evid. Code § 664), and the petitioner therefore has the burden of proof on mandamus. (Steele v. Los Angeles County Civil Service Commission (1958) 166 Cal.App.2d 129, 137.) “[T]he burden of proof falls upon the party attacking the administrative decision to demonstrate wherein the proceedings were unfair, in excess of jurisdiction or showed prejudicial abuse of discretion.” (Alford v. Pierno (1972) 27 Cal.App.3d 682, 691.)

Where the issue is whether a fair administrative hearing was conducted, a petitioner is entitled to an independent judicial determination of the issue. (Sinaiko v. Superior Court (2004) 122 Cal.App.4th 1133, 1141; Pomona Valley Hospital Medical Center v. Superior Court (1997) 55 Cal.App.4th 93, 101.) Therefore, the court must independently review the fairness of the administrative proceedings as a legal issue. (Rosenblit v. Superior Court, (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 1434, 1438.)

2.               Housing Accountability Act

Actions to enforce the Housing Accountability Act (“HAA”) are brought in administrative mandate. (Gov. Code § 65589.5(m); Honchariw v. County of Stanislaus (2011) 200 Cal.App.4th 1066, 1072.) Although a court would normally defer to an agency’s interpretation of its own ordinances, “land use decisions under the HAA are different,” and “it is inappropriate for [courts] to defer to the City’s interpretation.” (California Renters Legal Advocacy & Education Fund v. City of San Mateo (2021) 68 Cal.App.5th 820, 844.) Thus, courts must engage in a “more rigorous independent review . . . in order to prevent the City from circumventing what was intended to be a strict limitation on its authority.” (Ibid.; Ruegg & Ellsworth v. City of Berkeley (2021) 63 Cal.App.5th 277, 299.)

HAA cases are governed by a “reasonable person” standard that favors project approval, under which a court must determine whether “substantial evidence . . . would allow a reasonable person to conclude that the housing development project” complies with applicable standards. (Gov. Code § 65589.5(f)(4).) Additionally, the local agency bears the burden of proof to demonstrate that its decision conformed to the HAA. (Gov. Code § 65589.6.) Finally, the HAA must “be interpreted and implemented in a manner to afford the fullest possible weight to the interest of, and the approval and provision of, housing.” (Gov. Code § 65589.5(a)(2)(L).)

V.              Analysis

Petitioner argues Respondent denied the Project application without complying with the HAA.

1.               Respondent Has Not Met Its Burden to Show Compliance with the HAA

Petitioner argues that Respondent was required, under the HAA, to approve the Project because (1) it did not give timely notice that the Project violated any objective zoning or land use regulations, including the applicable General Plan; and (2) did not make the required findings under the HAA to disapprove the Project.

As noted above, the standard of review for HAA is different from ordinary administrative mandamus. HAA cases are governed by a “reasonable person” standard that favors project approval, under which a court must determine whether “substantial evidence . . . would allow a reasonable person to conclude that the housing development project” complies with applicable standards. (Gov. Code § 65589.5(f)(4).) Critically, the local agency bears the burden of proof to demonstrate that its decision conformed to the HAA. (Gov. Code § 65589.6.)

Petitioner has established, and Respondent has not disputed, that the HAA applies to Respondent’s consideration of the Project application. The record shows the Project qualifies as a housing development project because it consists of only residential units. (See, e.g., AR 262; Gov. Code § 65589.5(h)(2)(A).)

In opposition, Respondent has neither established nor argued that its decision complied with the HAA. (See Opp. at 8-12.) Indeed, nothing in the record shows that Respondent provided the appropriate notice under the HAA, which mandates written notice of any claimed inconsistency with zoning or land use regulations within 30 days, failing which the application is automatically deemed consistent. (Gov. Code § 65589.5(j)(2).) The record further indicates that both the Commission and the Council disapproved the Project without making any written factual findings of a specific, adverse impact upon public health or safety as the statute requires. (Gov. Code § 65589.5(j)(1); AR 132-34, 953-56.)

Respondent has therefore not met its burden to show that its disapproval of the Project complied with the HAA.

2.               Respondent’s Defenses

Respondent raises defenses to Petitioner’s HAA claim: (1) Petitioner failed to exhaust its administrative remedies; (2) Respondent never evaluated the Project application under the relevant General Plan; and (3) the Project application does not specify whether the included road will be private or public. [3]

a.                Petitioner Did Not Fail to Exhaust His Administrative Remedies

Respondent argues that Petitioner failed to exhaust administrative remedies because he did not invoke the HAA before either the Planning Commission or the City Council. In substance, Respondent argues an issue exhaustion doctrine, asserting that Petitioner needed to present the HAA-specific objections (the 30-day notice requirement of Government Code section 65589.5(j)(2) and the mandatory findings requirement of section 65589.5(j)(1)) at the hearings before seeking judicial review. (Opp. at 8-9.)

The Court agrees that Petitioner did not cite the HAA during the Commission or Council proceedings. (See AR 911-983, 1132-1231, 423-617; Opp. at 8.) Petitioner does not dispute this. Instead, Petitioner argues he raised “the same issues that underlie the HAA,” such as project consistency with applicable zoning and land use standards, and that this is sufficient to satisfy issue exhaustion. (Reply at 3.)

Petitioner relies on McPherson v. City of Manhattan Beach (2000) 78 Cal.App.4th 1252 (“McPherson”), Save Our Residential Environment v. City of West Hollywood (1992) 9 Cal.App.4th 1745 (“SORE”), and Center for Biological Diversity v. County of San Bernardino (2010) 185 Cal.App.4th 866 (“CBD”), to support the premise that a litigant need not cite the precise statute later relied upon in litigation, so long as the substance of the factual or environmental concern was presented to the agency. However, each of these cases depended on a condition that is not present here: the specific factual issue later litigated was clearly before the agency in the underlying proceeding. In McPherson, the timeliness and expiration of the vesting map was raised “at every administrative proceeding,” even if the petitioner only later put a statutory label on it. (McPherson, supra, 78 Cal.App.4th at p. 1264.) In SORE, the objections explicitly addressed the environmental impacts of the proposed site, which directly “fairly apprised” the City of the issue later litigated. (SORE, supra, 9 Cal.App.4th at p. 1750.) In CBD, the central issue of the adequacy of water supply to support the contested project was “squarely before the County.” (CBD, supra, 185 Cal.App.4th at p. 890.)

By contrast, nothing in the administrative record here establishes that Petitioner or any other participant ever raised (1) the City’s statutory duty to issue a written notice of inconsistency within 30 days (Gov. Code § 65589.5(j)(2)(A)), or (2) the City’s duty to make written health and safety findings supported by a preponderance of evidence before denying a housing project (Gov. Code § 65589.5(j)(1)). (See AR 938-39, 952-56, 127-134.) Thus, Petitioner’s authorities only partially support him. While a statute need not be named, the underlying issue must be put before the agency. The Court finds that that did not occur here.

Neither are Respondent’s authorities directly on point. Respondent relies on strict CEQA exhaustion cases that do not assist the Court’s analysis. For example, Coalition for Student Action v. City of Fullerton (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 1194, involved challengers who failed to raise factual environmental objections that the agency had jurisdiction to address. Similarly, City of Walnut Creek v. County of Contra Costa (1980) 101 Cal.App.3d 1012 (“Walnut Creek”) is distinguishable. There, petitioners sought to raise a new theory about the meaning of the county’s own ordinance, a matter directly within that agency’s jurisdiction to interpret, and one it could have addressed had it been presented at the administrative level. The concern in Walnut Creek was that the challengers had deprived the county of the opportunity to construe and apply its own law in the first instance. (Id. at pp. 1020-1022.) These cases reflect the concern that agencies first be given an opportunity to evaluate and resolve factual issues that are within their purview.

Here, Petitioner’s HAA theory does not require further factual development. Whether Respondent issued written notice under subdivision (j)(2) is answered by the administrative record, which contains no such notice. (See AR 1522-1527, 1528; Opp. at pp. 5-6.) Whether Respondent made the findings required by subdivision (j)(1) is likewise answered by the record, which contains no written findings at either the Commission or Council stages. (See AR 953-56, 133-34.) These are not matters within the agency’s discretion or fact-finding authority; they are matters of statutory compliance triggered automatically by the City’s decision to disapprove a housing project.

This is the type of question addressed in Richman, supra, 7 Cal.App.4th 1457. As in this case, the petitioner in Richman raised a statutory argument, the applicability of the Petris Act, for the first time in a writ proceeding. (Id. at p. 1463.) The administrative agency argued the issue was unexhausted. (Ibid.) The appellate court rejected that position, holding that when the issue concerns the applicability or construction of a statute, and when the facts relevant to that statutory question are undisputed, issue exhaustion does not apply because the agency lacks jurisdiction to finally determine such legal questions. (Id. at pp. 1463-1464.) Pure questions of statutory construction “lie[] outside the agency’s jurisdiction,” and petitioners may raise them for the first time in court. (Ibid.)

The reasoning of Richman applies equally to this case. Respondent does not dispute the facts relevant to HAA applicability: that the Project is a “housing development project” under section 65589.5(h)(2); that Respondent issued no written notice under section 65589.5(j)(2); and that Respondent made no written findings under section 65589.5(j)(1). (See AR 262, 1528, 133-34, 953-56; Opp. at 8-12.)

The Court is not persuaded by Respondent’s argument, advanced in its supplemental brief, that Richman does not apply because the City here “had authority to apply the HAA.” (Respondent’s Supp. Brief, pp. 2-3.). Richman did not turn on whether the agency had general authority to apply the statute at issue. It held that issue exhaustion does not bar a petitioner from raising, for the first time in mandamus proceedings, a statutory limitation on the agency’s discretion when the question is purely one of statutory applicability and the facts relevant to that statutory question are undisputed. Richman explains that “the construction of a statute and its applicability is solely a question of law,” the “ultimate resolution” of which “rests with the courts,” and therefore “lies outside the agency’s jurisdiction.” (Richman, supra, 7 Cal.App.4th at pp. 1463-1464.)

Respondent’s supplemental brief does not engage with that reasoning. Whether the HAA constrained the City’s discretion to deny the Project is not a matter the City had authority to determine for itself. Respondent does not have jurisdiction to determine whether its own decision complied with mandatory state law requirements governing notice and written findings. As in Richman, these compliance questions are committed to judicial review. Similarly, Respondent’s characterization of the HAA issues as fact-intensive does not change that the statutory consequences of those undisputed facts present a pure question of law.

With the factual grounds undisputed, the legal question before the Court is whether the HAA applies and what consequences follow from Respondent’s failure to satisfy its statutory duties. Respondent had no authority to interpret, modify, or excuse compliance with statutory notice and findings requirements. Because Petitioner’s HAA claims turn on statutory duties that were not dependent on Petitioner raising them, and because the dispositive questions lie outside Respondent’s adjudicatory jurisdiction, issue exhaustion does not bar judicial review. (Richman, supra, 7 Cal.App.4th at 1463-1464.) Accordingly, Respondent’s first defense fails.[4]

b.               Respondent Cannot Rely on Its Own Failure to Review the Project Application under the Proper General Plan

Respondent contends that the applicable General Plan was the 1973 General Plan, but the Project application was reviewed under the 2040 General Plan. On this basis, Respondent argues, “[i]t would be most injudicious to grant the sweeping relief sought by Petitioner without the City’s having had the opportunity to evaluate the proposed project under the legally correct standard.” (Op. at 11.)

Respondent has presented no legal authority to explain why its own failure to evaluate the Project application under the correct General Plan either demonstrates or excuses HAA compliance. Moreover, as Petitioner argues in reply, Respondent had a duty to give written notice of any inconsistency with the applicable General Plan within 30 days. (Gov. Code § 65589.5(j)(2)(A)(i).) Whether Respondent (or its staff) was aware of which of its own General Plans applied, the record does not show Respondent issued any written notice of inconsistency whatsoever, timely or untimely.

Therefore, Respondent’s General Plan argument cannot form a basis for denying Petitioner’s requested writ relief.

c.                Ambiguity Regarding the Road Does Not Bar Relief

Respondent argues the application is ambiguous about whether the road on the property will be public or private, which has implications under Streets and Highways Code section 1805 and Fire Code section 503.2.[5]

In reply, Petitioner notes that Respondent did not disapprove the project on these grounds, and that it does not alleviate Respondent’s duties under the HAA.

The Court agrees with Petitioner that this defense is unavailing.

Nothing in either of these statutes has any bearing on whether the Project should be approved. Streets and Highways Code section 1805 permits the City to pass a resolution to allow for a street smaller than 40 feet wide. (Sts. & Hy. Code § 1805.) Fire Code section 503.2 allows for private access roads 20 or more feet wide. (24 Cal. Code Regs. § 503.2.) The proposed road, at 26 feet wide (AR 243), can therefore be compliant with the Streets and Highways Code if public or the Fire Code if private. Also, as Petitioner argues, neither of these provisions alleviate Respondent of its burdens under the HAA.

3.               Petitioner Has Not Met His Burden to Show Respondent Acted in Bad Faith

Petitioner argues Respondent denied his application in bad faith, arguing that the Commission and Council each denied his application based primarily on community opposition.

If a court finds that an agency acted in bad faith in disapproving a project in violation of the HAA, the appropriate remedy is an “order or judgment directing the local agency to approve the housing development project or emergency shelter.” (Gov. Code § 65589.5(k)(1)(A)(ii).) “Bad faith,” for the purposes of Government Code section 65589.5, “includes, but is not limited to, an action or inaction that is frivolous, pretextual, intended to cause unnecessary delay, or entirely without merit.” (Gov. Code § 65589.5(l).)

Community opposition is not a basis for denial under the HAA. (See Gov. Code § 65589.5(j)(1).) The HAA allows for denial only if “the housing development project would have a specific, adverse impact upon the public health or safety unless the project is disapproved” where “a ‘specific, adverse impact’ means a significant, quantifiable, direct, and unavoidable impact, based on objective, identified written public health or safety standards, policies, or conditions as they existed on the date the application was deemed complete.” (Gov. Code § 65589.5(j)(1)(A).) Further, there must be “no feasible method to satisfactorily mitigate or avoid the adverse impact” except denial. (Gov. Code § 65589.5(j)(1)(B).) This decision must be based on “written findings supported by a preponderance of the evidence on the record” showing the adverse impact. (Gov. Code § 65589.5(j)(1).)

Respondent’s opposition does not address Petitioner’s “bad faith” argument. However, having reviewed the record, the Court is not persuaded that Respondent acted in bad faith.

At the Commission hearing, to the extent Commission members expressed their thoughts before denying the Project application, it appears they denied based on public opposition. (AR 952-53, 956.) Commissioner Valdez gave preliminary comments, stating:

“They said this is the most effective outreach, right, door-to-door knocking. Now, we have a petition here with over 500 signatures. So, you mean to tell me that 500 people answered their door and had an opinion enough to sign this document, whether it's qualified or not because this outreach is not qualified, either. The ten people that they spoke to out of 112, so that is a huge concern.

. . .

And instead, we are being presented a letter saying that if we do not grant this variance, it is potentially a breach in constitutional right to the applicant. That is my concern, Commissioners. I'll just be blunt. I do not appreciate feeling as though we’re being bullied instead of working with the community, working with the staff, and working with the commission to grant this variance.” (AR 938-39.)

Commissioner Lomeli stated in final comments, “I believe that I am here to hear what our community wants. And based on what our community wants, it is very clear that this is not what they want.” (AR 952.) Commission Chair Morales stated:

“And definitely we want to – well, we do have a General Plan that the city is working with as well, right? We have development happening all around the City of Montebello. And definitely we want each zone in our city to also continue to develop and grow in this development. But, of course, we have to take into consideration the voice of the city, of the residents and on how we can make this work and how we can, you know, work together as a community as well. Well, after hearing my colleagues as well and coming into the conclusion, yes, this is a conflict. But coming in to the conclusion, I would like to motion to deny the project.” (AR 953.)

Commissioners Valdez and Chair Morales voted to deny, Commissioners Lomeli and Cuevas abstained, and Commissioner Medina voted against denial. (AR 956.) The motion included no findings. (AR 956.)

The Council expressed their view even less clearly. Councilmember Peralta offered no comments. (AR 130.) Councilmember Alonzo commented that he has never used his garage for parking (AR 127), asked about nondiscretionary SB 9 lot division (AR 127-28), and asked if Petitioner had been providing the community inaccurate information (AR 128-29). Councilmember Romero asked about potential ADU conversions. (AR 129-30.) However, Mayor Melendez stated:

“There is opposition from our residents. So I do have my concerns about the potential – potential integrity of the project itself.

. . .

So there is definitely some concerns here I know some of the residents have spoken out on. So I do have some concerns as well, and some of the concerns that my colleagues have raised as well. So I do have some concerns about the integrity of the community in allowing a project like this.” (AR 132-33.)

Mayor Melendez proceeded to close the public hearing, and the Council unanimously voted to sustain the Commission’s denial. (AR 133-34.) The motion included no findings. (AR 133.)

While neither the Commission’s nor Council’s decisions were supported by any findings, written or otherwise, the record at most reflects disagreement with the Project and deference to community sentiment. The Court finds no evidence of conduct that was frivolous, pretextual, or intentionally obstructionist. Petitioner has therefore not met his burden to show that Respondent acted in bad faith.

 

VI.           Conclusion

Respondent has not shown its decision was made in compliance with the HAA, and its defenses do not overcome this failing. Petitioner has not established, however, that Respondent disapproved the Project in bad faith. In accordance with Government Code section 65589.5(k)(1)(A), Respondent must approve the Project. The petition for administrative mandamus is therefore GRANTED.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: OSC RE: TURNOVER

 

Receiver Michael Fiorina (“Receiver”) requests an order compelling defendant BR SFS, LLC (“Borrower”) to turn over all revenue generated by Borrower’s property located at 10025 Bloomfield Avenue, Santa Fe Springs, CA 90670 (“Property”) from oil leases in April and May, 2026 (“Revenues”). Plaintiff F4 Santa Fe Springs Lender, LLC (“Lender”) joins.

The request is granted.

I.                Background

On December 26, 2025, Lender filed its complaint against Borrower for judicial foreclosure and specific performance of deed of trusts including assignment of rents, appointment of receiver, and injunctive relief.

On January 21, 2026, the Court heard Lender’s ex parte application for appointment of receiver, temporary restraining order (“TRO”), and order to show cause (“OSC”) re: confirmation of appointment of receiver and for preliminary injunction. After briefing and oral argument, the Court continued the hearing to January 23, 2026 to allow the parties to address certain evidentiary issues.

On January 23, 2026, the court heard and granted Lender’s continued ex parte application The Court set an OSC re: confirmation of appointment of receiver for February 17, 2026. The Court issued the TRO and Order Appointing Receiver appointing Michael Fiorina as rents and profits receiver the same day. On January 26, 2026, Receiver filed his oath of receiver and undertaking. On February 17, 2026, the Court heard the OSC re: confirmation of appointment of receiver and confirmed Michael Fiorina as Receiver.

On March 3, 2026, Borrower filed its answer to the complaint.

On July 1, 2026, the Court heard Receiver’s ex parte application for an order compelling turnover of all funds derived from the Property in compliance with the Order Appointing Receiver, authorizing Receiver to hire a forensic accountant, authorizing Receiver to hire counsel, and issuing an OSC re: contempt. The Court granted the application in part, granting Receiver authority to engage SandDollar Financial, LLC (“SandDollar”) as a forensic accountant and granting the Receiver authority to engage Buchalter LLP as counsel. The Court denied Receiver’s request for an OSC re: contempt. The Court denied Receiver’s request for an immediate turnover order as to the Revenues without prejudice and set an OSC re: turnover for July 16, 2026.

II.             Discussion

Receiver seeks an order compelling Borrower to turn over the Revenues in compliance with the Order Appointing Receiver, whereby Borrower was ordered to cooperate with Receiver and turn over all income received from the Property to the Receiver, including all revenue generated by the oil leases attached to the Order Appointing Receiver.

Every court shall have the power to compel obedience to its judgments, orders, and process, and to the orders of a judge out of court, in an action or proceeding pending therein. (CCP § 128(a)(4).) This enforcement power is also an inherent power of the court. (See Security Trust & Sav. Bank v. Southern Pac. R. Co. (1935) 6 Cal.App.2d 585, 588 [“It is a well-established principle of law that a court possesses power to enforce its judgments.”]; Machado v. Myers (2019) 39 Cal.App.5th 779, 796 n.13, 252 [“Trial courts have the inherent authority to enforce their rulings.”].)

In opposing the ex parte application which preceded the instant OSC, Borrower argued that, due to an accounting error, it had prepaid all revenue for the relevant period. It further argued that the matter was inappropriate for ex parte hearing because the accounting involved is too complicated.

As noted above, the Court denied the ex parte application and set the instant OSC. At the ex parte hearing, the court ordered Borrower:

Borrower’s Opposition must be filed and served no later than July 12, 2026[[1]]. Borrower’s opposition should specifically address why no revenues were turned over for April or May 2026, as well as how Borrower’s position for not doing so is supportable under the Order Appointing Receiver entered by this Court on February 17, 2026.

In opposition to the instant OSC, Borrower makes only one argument. Borrower contends that because the Receiver did not retain SandDollar as its forensic accountant until July 6, 2026, SandDollar has not yet issued a report. Consequently, Borrower argues, there is nothing new for the Court to examine, and so Borrower requests the Court to “consider that the state of the evidence is not sufficient to determine the totality of facts and to set further hearings as necessary.” (Opp. at 3.)

The Court needs no further evidence.

In its Order Appointing Receiver, the Court charged Receiver with taking possession of and managing the Property, collecting the income from the Property, and caring for the Property, including incurring expenses necessary for that care. (Order Appointing Receiver, ¶ 9.) The Court ordered Borrower to immediately turn over possession of the Property to the Receiver, including all rental or lease payments. (Order Appointing Receiver, ¶ 31(a).) The Court restrained Borrower from demanding, collecting, or in any other way diverting or using any of the rents from the Property, and from interfering in any manner with the discharge of Receiver’s duties. (Order Appointing Receiver, ¶ 31(d)(2)-(3).)

Despite the Court’s explicit direction, Borrower’s opposition does not address why no revenues were turned over for April or May 2026, nor how Borrower’s position for failing to turn over those Revenues was supportable under the Order Appointing Receiver. (See generally Opp.)

The Court finds Borrower is in violation of the Order Appointing Receiver.

III.           Conclusion

Borrower is in violation of the Order Appointing Receiver. The turnover request is therefore GRANTED. Borrower is ordered to turn over all gross proceeds from the Property for April and May 2026.

III.           Legal Standard

A demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the pleading alone and will be sustained where the pleading is defective on its face.

A demurrer may be asserted on any one or more of the following grounds: (a) the court has no jurisdiction of the subject of the cause of action alleged in the pleading; (b) the person who filed the pleading does not have legal capacity to sue; (c) there is another action pending between the same parties on the same cause of action; (d) there is a defect or misjoinder of parties; (e) the pleading does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action; (f) the pleading is uncertain (“uncertain” includes ambiguous and unintelligible); (g) in an action founded upon a contract, it cannot be ascertained from the pleading whether the contract is written, oral, or implied by conduct; (h) no certificate was filed as required by Code of Civil Procedure (“CCP”) section 411.35 or (i) by CCP section 411.36. (CCP § 430.10.)

Accordingly, a demurrer tests the sufficiency of a pleading, and the grounds for a demurrer must appear on the face of the pleading or from judicially noticeable matters; a demurrer does not test the evidence or other extrinsic matters. (CCP § 430.30(a); Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) The face of the pleading includes attachments and incorporations by reference (Frantz v. Blackwell (1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 91, 94); it does not include inadmissible hearsay. (Day v. Sharp, (1975) 50 Cal.App.3d 904, 914.)

IV.           Evidentiary Issues

Respondent seeks judicial notice of the following exhibits: (1) the Order after Hearing filed March 6, 2025 in the Potentially Dangerous Dog Case (RJN Ex. 1); (2) the Order Dismissing Appeal – Failure to Procure Record and Remittitur in the appeal of the Potentially Dangerous Dog Case (RJN Ex. 2); (3) the Order after Hearing filed on July 15, 2025 in 25STCP01783 (“Vicious Dog Case”) (RJN Ex. 3), (4) the Minute Order dated July 16, 2025 in the Vicious Dog Case (RJN Ex. 4); (5) the Minute Order dated February 2, 2026 in the Vicious Dog Case (RJN Ex. 5); (6) the Minute order dated March 25, 2026 in the Vicious Dog Case (RJN Ex. 6); (7) the Minute Order Dated May 8, 2026 and Vexation Litigant Orders in case no. 25PSCV02281 (“Civil Case”) (RJN Ex. 7); (8) the Minute Order After Non-Jury Trial and Judgment in case no. 25WCSC01247 (“Small Claims Case”) (RJN Ex. 8); (9) the Minute Order dated March 2, 2026 and Dismissal Without Prejudice in case no. 26PSCV00685 (RJN Ex. 9); and (10) the pleadings, declarations by Mayra Langarica (“Langarica”), and ex parte application filed April 23, 2026 in this case.

The Court need not take judicial notice of documents already filed with the Court in this matter. Request 10 is therefore denied. The remaining requests are for court records subject to judicial notice and are therefore granted. (Evid. Code § 452(d).)

It is generally improper to submit evidence that is not judicially noticeable in opposition to a demurrer. Respondent’s objections to the City of Pomona Council Report, Resolution No. 2021-51, and the Animal Shelter and Control Service Agreement are nevertheless overruled. However, while admitted, the Court assigns the evidence little weight given the standards on demurrer.

V.              Analysis

Respondent demurs to the petition on the grounds that the petition fails to state facts showing entitlement to relief because (1) Condon lacks standing; (2) Condon is barred from relief by the unclean hands doctrine; (3) the petition is untimely; (4) the petition fails to show a ministerial duty; (5) assuming Condon has standing, he had an adequate remedy at law; and (6) assuming Condon has standing, the petition is barred by res judicata.

1.               Condon Lacks Standing to Bring the Petition

Respondent argues that Condon is not the owner of the dogs and therefore has no standing to seek mandamus.

Ordinarily, legal standing for mandamus requires that the petition have a beneficial interest in issuance of the writ. (Rialto Citizens for Responsible Growth v. City of Rialto (2012) 208 Cal.App.4th 899, 913.) “A petitioner is beneficially interested if he or she has ‘some special interest to be served or some particular right to be preserved or protected over and above the interest held in common with the public at large.” (Ibid.) The beneficial interest must be direct and substantial. (Ibid.) This interest must be an interest greater than that of the general public. (Flora Crane Service, Inc. v. Ross (1964) 61 Cal.2d 199, 203-04.)

Given the uncertainty regarding which of Petitioner’s July 9 and July 10 submissions corresponds to the opposition served on Respondent, the Court has reviewed each filing. Within them, Petitioner makes a limited reference to public interest standing, relying on Board of Social Welfare v. County of Los Angeles (1945) 27 Cal.2d 98. The Court finds the argument insufficient to demonstrate standing.

The Supreme Court of California has recognized “a ‘public interest’ exception to the requirement that a petitioner for writ of mandate have a personal beneficial interest in the proceedings” when the issue is one of public right and the object of the writ action is to obtain enforcement of a public duty. (Common Cause v. Board of Supervisors, (1989) 49 Cal.3d 432, 440, citing Green v. Obledo (1981) 29 Cal.3d 126, 144.) Public interest standing may apply “when the public duty is sharp and the public need weighty.” (SJJC Aviation Services, LLC v. City of San Jose (2017) 12 Cal.App.5th 1043, 1058 [“SJJC Aviation Services”].) Conversely, “where the claim of ‘citizen’ or ‘public interest’ standing is driven by personal objectives rather than ‘broader public concerns,’ a court may find the litigant to lack such standing.” (Ibid., citing Save the Plastic Bag Coalition v. City of Manhattan Beach (2011) 52 Cal.4th 155, 165.)

It is undisputed that Langarica, not Condon, owns the dogs. (Pet., Exs. A-B.) Nothing in the petition or other provided documents shows any connection between Condon, the dogs, or the underlying proceedings. (See generally Pet.) Similarly, the petition does not identify any public interest in issuance of the writ. (See generally Pet.) The facts, harm, and remedies in the petition are limited to Respondent, the City, and the Superior Court’s handling of and conclusions regarding Langarica’s dogs. (See Pet. at 5-9.) Even if the pleadings did show a sharp public duty and a weighty public need, Langarica’s direct involvement in the action—to the point of intermittently naming herself as a petitioner (e.g. Pet. at 4, 9; see generally Langarica Decl.)—shows Condon’s claim for public interest standing is driven by personal objectives rather than broader public concerns. (See SJJC Aviation Services at 1058.)  Based on the foregoing, the pleadings on their face do not show Condon has any beneficial interest in issuance of the writ, nor do they show Condon can claim public interest standing.

Accordingly, Condon lacks standing to pursue the petition.

2.               This Court Cannot Grant Relief

Respondent also argues the petition is barred by the doctrines of res judicata or collateral estoppel, and that Condon had an adequate remedy at law in appeal. Neither of these arguments are availing for the same reason Condon lacks standing: Condon has no substantive right or interest in issuance of the writ. However, both arguments point to a fundamental issue in Condon’s action. This Court cannot grant Condon the relief he seeks without essentially overruling orders issued by other departments of the Los Angeles Superior Court.

“One department of the superior court cannot enjoin, restrain, or otherwise interfere with the judicial act of another department of the superior court. … A judgment rendered in one department of the superior court is binding on that matter upon all other departments until such time as the judgment is overturned.” (Glade v. Glade (1995) 38 Cal.App.4th 1441, 1450; Silverman v. Superior Court (1988) 203 Cal.App.3d 145, 151.)

Condon seeks return of the dogs, various corrections of allegedly improper actions by Respondent, a declaration that Respondent’s actions were unlawful, and compensation. (Pet. at 8-9.) However, the Superior Court for the County of Los Angeles has already adjudicated the relevant issues in the Potentially Dangerous Dog Case and Vicious Dog Case, heard by Departments 25 and 26, respectively, of the Spring Street Courthouse. Those departments issued orders in those cases finding Boy and Rocky potentially dangerous and vicious, respectively. (E.g. RJN Exs. 1, 3.) Most critically, the court in the Vicious Dog Case ordered that Langarica turn Rocky and Boy over to Respondent and prohibited Langarica from owning any dog for three years. (RJN Ex. 3.) Therefore, the Court cannot grant the petition without directly contradicting the orders of the other departments.[2]

As this Department has no authority to review the actions of another department, it cannot grant Condon relief even if Condon had standing to bring the instant petition.

The Court has reviewed each of Petitioner’s July 9 and July 10, 2026, filings. Even assuming the “supplemental declaration” is properly advanced to oppose a demurrer, none of the submissions address the core defects identified in the demurrer: lack of standing, lack of a mandatory duty, the existence of adequate legal remedies, untimeliness under the statutory scheme, and the preclusive effect of prior judgments. The filings therefore do not alter the Court’s analysis.

 

VI.           Conclusion

Condon lacks standing to pursue the petition, and the Court cannot grant the requested relief. The demurrer to the petition is SUSTAINED without leave to amend.
