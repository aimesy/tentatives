Case Name:

William Tatham, et al. v. Scott Kelley, et al.

Case No.:

21VECV00843

MOTIONS

Motion to Set Aside Dismissal

Moving Party:

Plaintiffs William Tatham and Grand Prix Entertainment Holdings, LLC

Responding Party:

Defendants Scott Kelley, and Kelley & Kelley, LLP

Notice:

Proof of Service on April 1, 2026.

Documents:

Plaintiffs’ Motion to Set Aside/Vacate Dismissal – 4/1/26

Declaration of David Schneider in Support – 4/1/26

Request for Judicial Notice – 4/1/26

Defendants’ Opposition – 6/29/26

Plaintiffs’ Reply – 7/6/26

 

Ruling:

 

The Court DENIES Plaintiffs’ Motion to Set Aside the dismissal issued in their JAMS arbitration proceeding.  

 

Relevant Background:

 

This is a legal malpractice case. Plaintiffs are William Tatham and Grand Prix Entertainment Holdings, LLC. Plaintiffs were working to develop a professional indoor volleyball league. 

 

In 2012, Plaintiffs retained Defendants to negotiate a licensing deal with non-party USA Volleyball. By 2017, Plaintiffs were falling behind on payments to USA Volleyball. On August 24, 2018, USA Volleyball demanded arbitration for nearly $506,205.00 in unpaid licensing fees. Plaintiff William Tatham alleges that he was held personally liable as part of this suit due to Defendants’ poor contract drafting. 

 

Plaintiffs further allege that Defendant Kelley improperly made short-term loans to Plaintiffs. These loans were allegedly issued in order to cover Plaintiffs’ licensing fee shortfalls, but they included incredibly high interest rates – in some cases upwards of 50%. 
 
On July 1, 2021, Plaintiffs brought suit against Defendants alleging four causes of action: 1) legal malpractice; 2) breach of fiduciary duty; 3) violation of BPC § 17200; and 4) request for declaratory relief. 

 

On January 7, 2022, this case was sent to binding arbitration. 

 

On October 1, 2025, Arbitrator Rex Heeseman heard a dismissal motion from Defendants. This motion was unopposed and Plaintiffs did not appear. Arbitrator Heeseman granted that motion, and, one week later, dismissed the case with prejudice. Neither party was awarded any money. 

 

On November 18, 2025, Defendants submitted a petition to confirm this dismissal.  

 

On January 21, 2026, this Court heard the petition to confirm arbitration award. The Court continued the motion so that Defendants could cure a single procedural defect with their petition. Specifically, the Court found that Defendants did not provide proof that the neutral arbitrator served Plaintiffs with a signed copy of this award, or that this service was not required by the arbitration agreement.

 

On April 14, 2026, this Court once again heard Defendants’ Petition to Confirm Arbitration Award. The Court granted the petition.

 

However, on April 1, 2026, before the Court ruled on the petition, Plaintiffs filed the instant motion to set aside the dismissal ordered by JAMS arbitrator.

 

The Court now hears this motion to set aside.

 

Analysis:

 

Plaintiffs move this Court to set aside the JAMS dismissal of their case. (Mot. to Set Aside, p. 2:10-14.) Specifically, Plaintiffs request this Court set aside the dismissal under CCP § 473(b). (Ibid.)

 

CCP § 473(b) states that, “the court shall . . . vacate any (1) resulting default entered by the clerk against the attorney's client, and which will result in entry of a default judgment, or (2) resulting default judgment or dismissal entered against the attorney's client” so long as the application for relief is made “no more than six months after entry of judgment, is in proper form, and is accompanied by an attorney's sworn affidavit attesting to the attorney's mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect . . .unless the court finds that the default or dismissal was not in fact caused by the attorney's mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect.

 

Plaintiffs’ case was dismissed On October 1, 2025, in binding arbitration, when Plaintiffs failed to oppose or appear for a dismissal motion brought by Defendants. (Schneider Decl. Ex. 1.) Arbitrator Heeseman granted the motion, and, one week later, dismissed the case with prejudice. Neither party was awarded any money. (Ibid.)

 

Here, Plaintiffs provide a declaration from their previous attorney, Michael Shulte, stating that he failed to attend the October 2025 dismissal hearing due to multiple tragedies in his personal life, namely his wife’s cancer diagnosis, and the sudden death of his father – both of which occurred in April of 2025. (Shulte Decl. ¶¶ 2, 3, 6.) Plaintiffs assert that, through this declaration, they have satisfied the requirements of CCP § 473(b), and thus this Court is obligated to set aside the dismissal order entered by the arbitrator.

 

The problem with Plaintiffs’ argument is that they fail to explain how CCP § 473(b) can apply to a private arbitrator’s ruling. As far as the Court is aware, CCP § 1286.2 is the statute that outlines this Court’s power to overturn an arbitration award, not CCP § 473(b).

 

Under CCP § 1286.2, the Court’s power to vacate an arbitration award is limited to a small number of exceptions: 

 

“(1) The award was procured by corruption, fraud or other undue means. 

 

(2) There was corruption in any of the arbitrators. 

 

(3) The rights of the party were substantially prejudiced by misconduct of a neutral arbitrator. 

 

(4) The arbitrators exceeded their powers and the award cannot be corrected without affecting the merits of the decision upon the controversy submitted. 

 

(5) The rights of the party were substantially prejudiced by the refusal of the arbitrators to postpone the hearing upon sufficient cause being shown therefor or by the refusal of the arbitrators to hear evidence material to the controversy or by other conduct of the arbitrators contrary to the provisions of this title. 

 

(6) An arbitrator making the award either: (A) failed to disclose within the time required for disclosure a ground for disqualification of which the arbitrator was then aware; or (B) was subject to disqualification upon grounds specified in Section 1281.91but failed upon receipt of timely demand to disqualify himself or herself as required by that provision.”

 

(CCP § 1286.2(a)(1-6).) 

 

None of these exceptions apply to the circumstances outlined in Plaintiff’s motion – attorney mistake.

 

Plaintiffs cite a significant amount of case law in their motion. However, none of it addresses the question of whether a trial court can set aside a dismissal ordered by a private arbitrator. For instance, Plaintiffs cite to Bernasconi Commercial Real Estate v. St. Joseph's Regional Healthcare System for the proposition that mandatory relief under CCP § 473(b) applies to dismissals that are the "procedural equivalent of a default". (Mot. to Set Aside, p. 4:25-27 [citing Bernasconi Commercial Real Estate v. St. Joseph's Regional Healthcare System (1997) 57 Cal.App.4th 1078, 1082].) Plaintiffs point out that this includes dismissals caused by counsel's failure to oppose a dismissal motion. (Mot. to Set Aside, p. 5:2-3 [citing J.A.T Entm’t v. Reed (1998) 62 Cal.App.4th 1485.].)

 

While the Court agrees that these cases establish that CCP § 473(b) would apply if a case was dismissed due to a failure to attend a dismissal hearing, these cases have nothing to say about the applicability of the statute to dismissals entered in private arbitration. (See generally, Bernasconi, supra; see also, J.A.T Entm’t, supra.) Neither case even uses the word “arbitration”. (Ibid.)

 

Additionally, in response to Defendants’ argument about jurisdiction, Plaintiffs attempt to shift the focus of this analysis. They argue that, “the Court should construe the motion as seeking relief from the April 14, 2026, confirmation order and the April 20, 2026, judgment, both of which flowed directly from the attorney-caused dismissal challenged in Plaintiffs’ moving papers.” (Reply, p. 3:3-5.) The Court takes issue with this argument, as it directly contradicts Plaintiffs’ original motion, which stated that this was a, “Motion to Set Aside Dismissal of JAMS Proceeding Pursuant to Section 473(b).” (Mot. to Set Aside, p. 1:24 [emphasis added].) Plaintiff cannot now, in Reply, ask this Court to shift the focus of this analysis to a petition to confirm dismissal entered two weeks after the instant motion was filed.

 

Given that Plaintiffs have provided no case law to suggest that CCP § 473(b) empowers a trial court to set aside a dismissal entered in binding arbitration, the Court defaults to CCP § 1286.2, which limits this Court’s power to vacate an arbitration award is limited to a small number of exceptions – none of which are met here.

 

For these reasons, the Court DENIES Plaintiffs’ Motion to Set Aside the dismissal issued in their JAMS arbitration proceeding. 

 

Conclusion:

 

The Court DENIES Plaintiffs’ Motion to Set Aside the dismissal issued in their JAMS arbitration proceeding.

Ramin Yousezadeh, et al. v. Archstone Oak Creek I, LLC, et al.

25VECV01132

Motion to be Relieved as Counsel

Moving Party:

Plaintiff’s Attorney Ilya Signayevsky

Responding Party:

None.

Notice:

Proof of Service on June 2, 2026.

Documents:

Motion to be Relieved as Counsel – 6/2/26

Proof of Service 6/2/26

Declaration of Ilya Signayevsky in Support – 6/2/26

 

Ruling:

 

The Court GRANTS Ilya Signayevsky’s Motion to be Relieved as Counsel.

 

Relevant Background:

 

This is a habitability case. Plaintiffs Ramin Yousefzadeh and Katrin Yousefzadeh allege that on March 8, 2020, they entered into a rental agreement with Defendants. Defendants in this case are the corporate owners of the rental property, Archstone Oak Creek I, LLC, Archstone Oak Creek II, LLC, the individual owner, Tishman Archstone-Smith, and the corporate leasing entity Avalonbay Communities, Inc. (Moving Defendants are all Defendants except Tishman Archstone-Smith.)

 

Plaintiffs allege that the apartment they rented from Defendants was uninhabitable from the start. Allegedly, it suffered from severe mold infestations, water damage, inadequate heating, had no working air conditioner, and was infested with rodents. Plaintiffs allege they submitted multiple service requests, but Defendants refused to make repairs.

 

On February 28, 2025, Plaintiffs brought suit alleging six causes of action: 1) failure to provide habitable dwelling, 2) breach of the covenant of quiet enjoyment, 3) private nuisance, 4) unfair business competition, 5) negligence, and 6) failure to return security deposit.

 

Now, Plaintiff Ramin Yousezadeh’s counsel, Ilya Signayevsky, moves to be relieved as counsel.

 

Analysis:

 

Counsel Ilya Signayevsky moves to be relieved as Plaintiff’s counsel.

 

The requirements for a Motion to be Relieved as Counsel are outlined in CRC 3.1362. Moving counsel must: (1) submit form MC-051 - notice of the motion to the client, (2) submit form MC-052 - a declaration explaining why they could not acquire client’s consent to withdraw, (3) submit form MC-053 – a copy of the proposed order, and (4) serve these documents and notice of the motion to both the client and all other parties in the case. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362(a)-(e).) Additionally, relief cannot be granted if it is reasonably foreseeable that the client would suffer prejudice as a result of the withdrawal. (Mossanen v. Monfared (2000) 77 Cal.App.4th 1402, 1409.)

 

First, Ms. Signayevsky has submitted Form MC-051 and has served Plaintiff with notice of this motion. (Motion to be Relieved, MC-051.) Second, Ms. Signayevsky has submitted Form MC-052, stating that:

 

“My representation of Ramin Yousezadeh in this matter was undertaken in conjunction with the representation provided by Binh Bui, Esq. During that representation, Mr. Yousezadeh was advised on multiple occasions, both orally and in writing, that responses to Defendant's written discovery were required and overdue. Despite repeated requests for Mr. Yousezadeh's cooperation and participation in preparing responses, Mr. Yousezadeh failed and refused to provide the necessary information and/or to verify responses. Mr. Yousezadeh's refusal to cooperate in this regard rendered it unreasonably difficult for counsel to effectively carry out the representation and to fulfill counsel's obligations to the Court and to opposing counsel.”

 

(Declaration in Support, ¶ 2.)

 

Third, Ms. Signayevsky submitted a copy of the proposed order via Form MC-053. (Proposed Order, MC-053.) Fourth, Ms. Signayevsky served these documents onto all parties in this case. (Mot. to be Relieved, Proof of Service, p. 2.) Thus, Ms. Signayevsky has met all the procedural requirements under CRC 3.1362.

 

Finally, the Court must determine whether it is reasonably foreseeable that the client will suffer prejudice as a result of this withdrawal. Here, there are no motions scheduled in this case. Additionally, trial is scheduled for February 8, 2027. Thus, there is no foreseeable way that Plaintiff could be prejudiced by this withdrawal.

 

For these reasons, the Court GRANTS Ilya Signayevsky’s Motion to be Relieved as Counsel.

 

Conclusion:

 

The Court GRANTS Ilya Signayevsky’s Motion to be Relieved as Counsel.

Todd Maidy v. FCA US, LLC, et al.

25VECV01319

MOTIONS

Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel Compliance

Moving Party:

Plaintiff Todd Maidy

Responding Party:

Defendant FCA US, LLC

Notice:

Proof of Service on March 20, 2026.

Documents:

Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel Initial Disclosures – 3/20/26

Defendant’s Opposition – 6/29/26

Declaration of Adam Rivera in Support – 6/29/26

Plaintiff’s Reply – 7/2/26

 

Ruling:

 

The Court tentative DENIES Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel Initial Disclosures as MOOT.

 

The Court GRANTS Plaintiff’s request for $2,500 in statutory sanctions.

 

Relevant Background:

 

This is a lemon law case. Plaintiff Todd Maidy alleges that on August 19, 2022, Plaintiff purchased a vehicle manufactured by Defendant FCA US, LLC. Plaintiff alleges that the vehicle exhibited numerous defects that could not be cured after a reasonable number of repair attempts.

 

On March 10, 2025, Plaintiff brought suit against Defendants FCA US, LLC, and Van Nuys Chrysler Dodge Jeep Ram, alleging multiple violations of the Song Beverly Act, as well as fraudulent inducement.

 

On July 21, 2025, Defendant FCA US, LLC, filed their Answer.

 

Now, almost a year later, Plaintiff moves to compel initial disclosures from Defendant FCA, US, LLC.

 

 

Analysis:

 

Discovery Analysis

 

Plaintiff moves to compel the production of multiple documents required under CCP § 871.26(b) from Defendant FCA US, LLC. (Mot. for Compliance, p. 2:8-10.) Specifically, Plaintiff requests production of documents listed under CCP § 871.26, subdivisions (h)(6), (h)(7), (h)(8), (h)(9), (h)(10), (h)(12), and (h)(13). (Ibid.)

 

In 2024, the California Legislature passed AB 1755, which created multiple new procedural and timing rules for lemon law cases. As part of this bill, the legislature enacted CCP § 871.26, which outlines mandatory discovery requirements for all Song-Beverly cases. This includes the rule that, “[w]ithin 60 days after the filing of the answer or other responsive pleading, all parties shall, without awaiting a discovery reques, provide to all other parties an initial disclosure and documents pursuance to [871.26] subdivisions (f), (g), and (h).” (CCP § 871.26(b).)

 

Here, Defendant filed their Answer on July 1, 2025. (See, Answer.) This put Defendant’s 60-day deadline for automatic discovery disclosures under CCP § 871.26(b) on August 30, 2025. To Defendant’s credit, they produced a significant number of documents before they had even filed their answer. (Riviera Decl. ¶ 9.) However, according to Plaintiff, this production was incomplete, as it did not include multiple required disclosures, such as the Warranty Policies and Procedure Manual, PDI Documentation for the vehicle, and various TBS and ISB report. (Mot. for Compliance, Rucker Decl. ¶ 11.) As of March 20, 2026, Defendant had not produced those documents. This means that Defendant missed their 60-day deadline to disclose these required documents.

 

For these reasons, the Court finds that Defendant failed to comply with the mandatory disclosure requirements under CCP § 871.26.

 

However, Defendant argues in their Opposition that this motion is MOOT. Specifically, Defendant highlights that, on June 10, 2026, this Court granted a stipulated protective order, which enabled Defendant to produce all of the remaining documents that are the subject of this motion. (Opp. p. 2:6-15; see also, Minute Order 6/10/26.) The Court is inclined to believe Defendant. However, Defendant merely asserts that this Protective Order brings them into full compliance, without providing any supporting declaration or evidence. (See Rivera Decl.) Thus, the Court will need to resolve this factual dispute at the hearing on this motion. There, the Court will ask the parties to confirm Defendant’s compliance.

 

For these reasons, the Court TENTATIVELY DENIES this motion as MOOT, pending discussing at the hearings.

 

Sanctions

 

Plaintiff also requests $2,500 in statutory sanctions in conjunction with this motion. (Mot. for Compliance, p. 15:16-22.)

 

 

CCP § 871.26(j)(1) states that, “[u]nless the party failing to comply with this section shows good cause, notwithstanding any other law and in addition to any other sanctions imposed pursuant to this chapter, a court shall impose sanctions as follows: … [a] two-thousand-five-hundred-dollar ($2,500) sanction against the defense attorney …, paid within 15 business days for failure to comply with the document production requirements as prescribed in subdivision (b).” (CCP § 871.26(j)(1).) Notably, the language of this statute mandates the imposition of sanctions, with the only exception being good cause shown. Thus, even though this motion is potentially moot, sanctions are still appropriate.

 

The Court finds that Defendant has not shown good cause for their lack of compliance with this statute.

 

First, as discussed above, Defendant did not produce all of the required documents under CCP § 871.26 by the statutory deadline. (Rucker Decl. ¶ 11.) In their Opposition, Defendant argues that, because their initial disclosure was “substantially compliant” with the statute, sanctions are inappropriate. (Opp. p. 3:22-28.) However, Defendant cites no case law to suggest that substantial compliance is sufficient under this statute. The statute itself states that, “[t]he defendant or manufacturer shall provide the following documents to all other parties.” (CCP § 871.26(h).) This language clearly mandates full disclosure of the listed documents. Defendant did not produce all of the documents. Thus, they failed to comply with the statute.

 

Defendant also argues that their non-compliance is justified, because the documents they withheld included confidential and proprietary information, and thus Defendant needed a protective order in order to produce them. (Opp. p. 4:2-11.) However, Defendant once again cites no case law that suggests that documents that require a protective order are except from CCP § 871.26. (Ibid.) Notably, the statute itself makes no mention of protective orders. (See generally, CCP § 871.26.) Thus, the Court does not find that Defendant’s proclaimed need for a protective order constitutes good cause.

 

Third, Defendant points out that Plaintiff filed this motion long after the initial disclosure deadline, and failed to meet and confer before filing this motion. (Opp. p. 4:19-28.) The Court agrees with Defendant that it would be better for the parties to resolve these discovery disputes outside of Court. However, the Court does not agree that Plaintiff’s actions here create good cause for Defendant’s failure to produce the statutorily required initial disclosures.

 

The purpose of CCP § 871.26 is to establish a set of statutorily required discovery disclosures for both parties in lemon law cases. This statute puts the parties on notice of all the documents they need to produce, meaning they can produce them proactively. If Defendant had proactively complied with CCP § 871.26 from the beginning, Plaintiff’s delayed motion, or failure to meet and confer would not matter, as Defendant would be in compliance with the statute. But, as explained above, Defendant did not produce all of the documents required under CCP § 871.26. Therefore, sanctions are appropriate.

 

For these reasons, the Court GRANTS Plaintiff’s request for $2,500 in statutory sanctions against Defendant FCA US, LLC.

 

Conclusion:

 

The Court tentative DENIES Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel Initial Disclosures as MOOT.

 

The Court GRANTS Plaintiff’s request for $2,500 in statutory sanctions.

Case Name:

Jay Stein v. Lionel Paul Levin, et al.

Case No.:

25VECV01763

MOTIONS

Motion to be Relieved as Counsel

Moving Party:

Plaintiff’s Attorney Jacob Zadeh

Responding Party:

Plaintiff Jay Stein

Notice:

Proof of Service on June 8, 2026.

Documents:

Motion to be Relieved as Counsel – 6/8/26

Declaration of Jacob Zadeh in Support – 6/8/26

Proposed Order – 6/8/26

Plaintiff’s Opposition – 6/29/26

Declaration of Jay Stein in Opposition – 6/29/26

Jacob Zadeh’s Reply – 6/30/26

 

Ruling:

 

The Court TENTATIVELY GRANTS Mr. Zadeh’s Motion to be Relieved as Counsel, pending discussion at the hearing.

 

The Court CONTINUES the Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings currently scheduled for August 26, 2026, to September 23, 2026.

 

Relevant Background:

 

This is a legal malpractice case. On September 21, 2021, Plaintiff Jay Stein hired Defendants Lionel Paul Levin, Elyse Margolin, and the firm Levin & Margolin to represent him in a marriage dissolution. Over time, Plaintiff and Defendants began to clash over the total anticipated costs of the litigation, as well as Defendants’ strategy, billing practices, and their handling of client funds.

 

After two years of litigation, Defendant Lionel Levin informed Plaintiff that he would be leaving the firm of Levin & Margolin. At this point, both Defendant Lionel Levin and Defendant Elyse Margolin moved to be relieved as Plaintiff’s counsel. Both motions were granted, leaving Plaintiff without counsel just over two months before his dissolution trial.

 

On March 28, 2025, Plaintiff filed this suit against Defendants for legal malpractice. Plaintiff alleges five causes of action: 1) fraud; 2) breach of fiduciary duty; 3) professional negligence; 4) accounting; and 5) indemnity.

 

Now, Plaintiff’s new attorney Jacob Zadeh, moves to be relieved as counsel, citing an irreparable breakdown in the attorney/client relationship.

 

Analysis:

 

Counsel Jacob Zadeh moves to be relieved as Plaintiff’s counsel.

 

The requirements for a Motion to be Relieved as Counsel are outlined in CRC 3.1362. Moving counsel must: (1) submit form MC-051 - notice of the motion to the client, (2) submit form MC-052 - a declaration explaining why they could not acquire client’s consent to withdraw, (3) submit form MC-053 – a copy of the proposed order, and (4) serve these documents and notice of the motion to both the client and all other parties in the case. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1362(a)-(e).) Additionally, relief cannot be granted if it is reasonably foreseeable that the client would suffer prejudice as a result of the withdrawal. (Mossanen v. Monfared (2000) 77 Cal.App.4th 1402, 1409.)

 

First, Mr. Zadeh has submitted Form MC-051, and has served Plaintiff with notice of this motion. (Motion to be Relieved, MC-051.) Second, Mr. Zadeh has submitted Form MC-052, stating that, “despite repeated efforts, there has been a breakdown in communication between counsel and client that has made effective representation unreasonably difficult.” (Declaration in Support, ¶ 2.) Third, Mr. Zadeh submitted a copy of the proposed order via Form MC-053. (Proposed Order, MC-053.) Fourth, Mr. Zadeh served these documents onto all parties in this case. (Mot. to be Relieved, Proof of Service, p. 2.) Thus, Mr. Zadeh has met all the procedural requirements under CRC 3.1362.

 

Finally, the Court must determine whether it is reasonably foreseeable that the client will suffer prejudice as a result of this withdrawal.

 

First, the Court notes that there are no imminent motions in this case. This motion is scheduled to be heard on July 13, 2026. The next substantive motion in this case is a motion for judgment on the pleadings, scheduled on August 26, 2026. Moreover, the trial in scheduled for April 19, 2027. Thus, Plaintiff, who is himself a lawyer, will have more than a month to acquire new counsel for the impending MJOP, and months to prepare for trial. These facts suggest that Plaintiff is unlikely to be prejudiced as a result of this withdrawal. However, in order to ensure that Plaintiff suffers no prejudice, the Court will CONTINUE the Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings currently scheduled for August 26, 2026, to September 23, 2026.

 

However, the Court notes that this motion is opposed by Plaintiff himself. In his Opposition, Plaintiff asserts that Mr. Zadeh has not spoken to Plaintiff in nearly eight months and has failed to return any of Plaintiff’s phone calls. (Opp. p. 2:16-23.) Plaintiff also provides an unsworn declaration, where he states that the actual reason for Mr. Zadeh’s withdrawal is that he, “just wants not to handle the lawsuit any longer.” (Stein Decl. ¶ 2.) He also states that Mr. Zadeh gave him an ultimatum to either sign a substitution of attorney or he would withdraw. (Id. ¶ 3.)

 

In response to these claims, Mr. Zadeh submits a supplemental sworn declaration in reply. There, he states that, “Plaintiff’s opposition asks the Court to deny the motion on the asserted basis that

my stated reason is false, and it invites me to respond with a detailed account of my communications, or asserted lack of communications, with Plaintiff.” (Reply, Zadeh Decl. ¶ 3.) However, Mr. Zadeh explains that he cannot produce this detailed account without violating his duty of confidentiality. (Ibid.) Thus, Mr. Zadeh offers to provide the Court with further information, in an in-camera hearing. (Id. ¶ 4.)

 

Thus, the Court is faced with two competing declarations. However, only Mr. Zadeh’s declaration is sworn. Thus, the Court’s tentative inclination is to grant this motion, and continue next month’s Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings. However, the Court will reserve it’s ruling until after the hearing, as it intends to hear from both Plaintiff and from Mr. Zadeh in-camera.

 

For these reasons, the Court TENTATIVELY GRANTS Mr. Zadeh’s Motion to be Relieved as Counsel, pending discussion at the hearing.

 

The Court also CONTINUES the Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings currently scheduled for August 26, 2026, to September 23, 2026.

 

Conclusion:

 

The Court TENTATIVELY GRANTS Mr. Zadeh’s Motion to be Relieved as Counsel, pending discussion at the hearing.

 

The Court CONTINUES the Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings currently scheduled for August 26, 2026, to September 23, 2026.
