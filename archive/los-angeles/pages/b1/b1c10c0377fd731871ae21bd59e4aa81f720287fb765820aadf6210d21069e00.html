Case Number: 25PSCV00258    Hearing Date: September 15, 2026    Dept: G

Plaintiff Zuojun Zhuang’s Motion for Leave to File First Amended Complaint

Respondent: Defendant JCN Investment Group LLC

TENTATIVE RULING

Plaintiff Zuojun Zhuang’s Motion for Leave to File First Amended Complaint is DENIED WITHOUT PREJUDICE.

BACKGROUND

This is a personal injury action.  On January 24, 2023, plaintiff Juojun Zhuang (Zhuang) allegedly fell through the roof of defendant JCN Investment Group LLC (JCN) and sustained injuries.

On January 23, 2025, Zhuang filed the Complaint, alleging causes of action for (1) general negligence and (2) premises liability.

On May 21, 2026, Zhuang filed this motion for leave to file amended complaint.  On September 1, 2026, JCN filed the opposition, and on September 3, 2026, Zhuang filed the reply.

This case is set for a status conference and a hearing on the motion on September 15, 2026.

ANALYSIS

Zhuang moves to file a proposed Amended Complaint.  For the following reasons, the motion is DENIED.

Legal Standard

“A court may, in furtherance of justice, and on any terms as may be proper, allow a party to amend any pleading.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (a)(1).)  The court’s discretion will usually be exercised liberally to permit amendments of the pleadings.  (Nestle v. City of Santa Monica (1972) 6 Cal.3d 920, 939.) “A motion to amend a pleading before trial must . . . [i]nclude a copy of the proposed . . . amended pleading, which must be serially numbered to differentiate it; [s]tate what allegations in the previous pleading are proposed to be deleted, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the deleted allegations are located; and [s]tate what allegations are proposed to be added to the previous pleading, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the additional allegations are located.”  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1324(a).)  The motion must include a declaration that specifies the amendment’s effect, why it is necessary and proper, when the facts supporting the amended allegations were discovered, and why the request was not made earlier.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1324(b).)

Nevertheless, even where a moving party complies with the requirements of Rule 3.1324 and such proposed amendment is proper, leave to amend is not an absolute right.  (See In re Marriage of Liss (1992) 10 Cal.App.4th 1426, 1429, noting, “Of course, a trial court also has discretion to deny amendments to pleadings if amendment would not be in furtherance of justice.”)  A court may properly deny amendment based on several factors, including the bad faith conduct of the moving party, the belated presentation of the amendment, or the futility of the amendment.  (See Leader v. Health Industries of America, Inc. (2001) 89 Cal.App.4th 603, 613; see also Jenkins v. JP Morgan Chase Bank, Nat. Assn. (2013) 216 Cal.App.4th 497, 535, suggesting that the court deny leave to amend based on futility where the facts are not in dispute and no liability exists under substantive law; see, e.g., Hirsa v. Super. Ct. (1981) 118 Cal.App.3d 486, 490, holding that the court may deny leave to amend if the moving party needlessly delayed, which prejudiced the opposing party.)  Further, leave to amend must be denied where prejudice is shown to the adverse party.  (Miles v. City of Los Angeles (2020) 56 Cal.App.5th 728, 739.)  Prejudice exists where the amendment would require delaying the trial, resulting in loss of critical evidence, or added costs of preparation, including the completion of additional discovery.¿ (Solit v. Tokai Bank, Ltd. N.Y. Branch (1999) 68 Cal.App.4th 1435, 1448; see also Miles, supra, at 739.)

Discussion

As a preliminary matter, the court must determine whether the motion complies with the requirements of Rule 3.1324.  As stated above, the California Rules of Court require the moving plaintiff to identify the proposed changes to the pleading, explain why such changes are necessary and proper, and further explain why the changes were not made sooner.  (See Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1324, subds. (a)–(b).)

Zhuang provides a copy of the proposed Amended Complaint.  (See Mot., Exh. A.)  Even though Zhuang does not include a redlined copy of the Amended Complaint to identify the proposed changes, the court finds the motion sufficiently identifies the proposed changes.  (See Mot., pp. 12–13.)  Zhuang explains that the proposed changes “add a Cause of Action Negligence Pursuant to Labor Code section 3706 (failure to secure workers’ compensation insurance); and [Section] 6403 (failure to provide a safe place of employment).”  (Mot., p. 7.) 

According to counsel, Zhuang did not seek amendment sooner because he learned the new facts that serve as the basis for amendment only after litigation started and the parties engaged in discovery.  (See Mot., Bourgi Decl., ¶¶ 3–6, 8–9.)  However, as JCN highlights, counsel does not identify which facts Zhuang learned from specific discovery responses.  (See Opp., pp. 4–5.)  Furthermore, the purported newly discovered facts were known to Zhang when he filed the original Complaint.  (See Opp., pp. 5–6.)  Thus, the court finds the motion is procedurally deficient under Rule 3.1324.

Accordingly, the motion for leave to file an amended complaint is DENIED WITHOUT PREJUDICE.  Since the court decides this motion based on the failure to comply with Rule 3.1324, the parties’ arguments regarding prejudice are HELD IN ABEYANCE.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the motion for leave to file first amended complaint is DENIED WITHOUT PREJUDICE.

Case Number: 25PSCV03352    Hearing Date: September 15, 2026    Dept: G

Defendants ProVest California LLC and David Mealancon’s Motion for Attorney’s Fees and Costs

Respondent: NO OPPOSITION

Defendants Lendmark Financial Services, LLC, The Dunning Law Firm APC, and James MacLeod’s Motion for Attorney’s Fees and Costs

Respondent: NO OPPOSITION

TENTATIVE RULING

Defendants ProVest California LLC and David Mealancon’s Motion for Attorney’s Fees and Costs is GRANTED, in a reduced amount.  Defendants ProVest California LLC and David Mealancon are AWARDED a total of $18,904.00.

Defendants Lendmark Financial Services, LLC, The Dunning Law Firm APC, and James MacLeod’s Motion for Attorney’s Fees and Costs is GRANTED in a reduced amount.  Defendants Lendmark Financial Services, LLC, The Dunning Law Firm APC, and James MacLeod are AWARDED a total of $10,141.56.

BACKGROUND

This is an action under the Rosenthal Act and for abuse of process.  On August 29, 2024, defendant James MacLeod (MacLeod) of defendant The Dunning Law Firm APC (Dunning) allegedly filed a Complaint on behalf of their client defendant Lendmark Financial Services, LLC (Lendmark) in a debt collection action (case no. 24NWLC36833) against plaintiff Stewart Nolan Hall (Hall).  Lendmark, MacLeod, and Dunning (collectively, the Lendmark Defendants) allegedly hired defendant ProVest California LLC (ProVest) to serve Hall in the debt collection action, and ProVest allegedly assigned process server defendant David Mealancon (Mealancon) to execute service.  Mealancon allegedly failed to serve Hall but nevertheless completed a proof of service form stating that he did.  On October 15, 2024, MacLeod and Dunning, on behalf of Lendmark, filed Mealancon’s proof of service with the court in the debt collection action.  On November 25, 2024, the court allegedly issued a default judgment against Hall in the debt collection action.  (On December 10, 2024, the court vacated the default judgment in the debt collection action.) 

In December 2024, Hall allegedly entered into an agreement with Lendmark to pay off his original debt, the subject of the debt collection action, in monthly installments.  On August 1, 2025, MacLeod and Dunning, on behalf of Lendmark, allegedly served Hall with a renewed request for entry of default in the debt collection action.  Hall allegedly continues to make monthly payments to Lendmark according to their alleged agreement.

On September 16, 2025, Hall filed the Complaint in this case.  On November 12, 2025, Hall filed the operative Amended Complaint, alleging causes of action for (1) debt collection without service of process, (2) debt collection by false means, (3) debt collection without assignment, and (4) abuse of process.

On October 6, 2025, the court consolidated the debt collection action with this case.

On April 7, 2026, the court issued a Judgment in favor of ProVest and Mealancon (collectively, the Process Servers) and against Hall.  On May 15, 2026, the court issued a Judgment in favor of the Lendmark Defendants and against Hall.

On June 22, 2026, the Process Servers filed a motion for attorney’s fees, which is unopposed.

On July 14, 2026, the Lendmark Defendants filed a motion for attorney’s fees, which is unopposed.

This case is set for an order to show cause re: default judgment (setting date only in non-lead case) and a hearing on the motions on September 15, 2026.

ANALYSIS

All defendants request attorney’s fees because they were prevailing parties on their special motions to strike.  For the following reasons, the motions are GRANTED, in a reduced amount.

Legal Standard

In trial court litigation, attorney’s fees are recoverable if authorized by contract, statute, or law.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (a)(10).)

“It is well established that the determination of what constitutes reasonable attorney fees is committed to the discretion of the trial court, whose decision cannot be reversed in the absence of an abuse of discretion.”  (Melnyk v. Robledo (1976) 64 Cal.App.3d 618, 623.)  The fee setting inquiry in California ordinarily “begins with the ‘lodestar’ [method], i.e., the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate.”  (Graciano v. Robinson Ford Sales, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 140, 154.)  “The reasonable hourly rate is that prevailing in the community for similar work.”  (Margolin v. Regional Planning Com. (1982) 134 Cal.App.3d 999, 1004.)  “Under the lodestar method, a party who qualifies for a fee should recover for all hours reasonably spent unless special circumstances would render an award unjust.”  (Vo v. Las Virgenes Municipal Water Dist. (2000) 79 Cal.App.4th 440, 446.)

“[A] computation of time spent on a case and the reasonable value of that time is fundamental to a determination of an appropriate [attorney’s] fee award.”  (Margolin v. Regional Planning Com., supra, 134 Cal.App.3d at 1004.)  The lodestar figure may then be adjusted, based on consideration of factors specific to the case, in order to fix the fee at the fair market value for the legal services provided.  (See Serrano v. Priest (1977) 20 Cal.3d 25, 49, discussing factors relevant to proper attorney’s fees award.)  Such an approach anchors the trial court’s analysis to an objective determination of the value of the attorney’s services, ensuring that the amount awarded is not arbitrary.  (See id. at 48, fn. 23.)  The factors considered in determining the modification of the lodestar include “(1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, [and] (4) the contingent nature of the fee award.”  (Mountjoy v. Bank of America (2016) 245 Cal.App.4th 266, 271.)  “[T]he burden is on the party seeking attorney fees to prove that the fees it seeks are reasonable.”  (Gorman v. Tassajara Development Corp. (2009) 178 Cal.App.4th 44, 98.)  “[A]n award of attorney fees may be based on counsel’s declarations, without production of detailed time records.”  (Raining Data Corp. v. Barrenechea (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 1363, 1375.)  “[P]aralegal fees may be awarded as attorney’s fees if the trial court deems it appropriate. . . .”  (Roe v. Halbig (2018) 29 Cal.App.5th 286, 312.)

Where a party is challenging the reasonableness of attorney’s fees as excessive that party must “attack the itemized billing[] with evidence that the fees claimed were not appropriate, or obtain the declaration of an attorney with expertise in the procedural and substantive law to demonstrate that the fees claimed were unreasonable.”  (Premier Medical Management Systems, Inc. v. Cal. Ins. Guarantee Assn. (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 550, 563–564.)  “[I]t is the burden of the challenging party to point to the specific items challenged, with a sufficient argument and citations to the evidence and arguments that fees claimed are excessive, duplicative, or unrelated do not suffice.”  (Id. at 564.)  “General arguments that fees claimed are excessive, duplicative, or unrelated do not suffice.”  (Ibid.)

A “court should defer to the winning lawyer’s professional judgment as to the tasks completed in an action because he won, and might not have, had he been more of a slacker.”  (Moreno v. City of Sacramento (9th Cir. 2008) 534 F.3d 1106, 1111.)  A losing party cannot litigate tenaciously then be heard to complain about the time spent or tasks performed by the prevailing party in response.  (City of Riverside v. Rivera (1986) 477 U.S. 561, 580, fn. 11.)  Where a defendant does not produce evidence contradicting the reasonableness of counsel’s hourly rates, the court will deem an attorney’s hourly rate as reasonable.  (Goglin v. BMW of North America, LLC (2016) 4 Cal.App.5th 462, 473.)

Discussion

To decide this motion, the court must determine (1) whether the moving defendants are entitled to attorney’s fees as authorized by contract, statute, or law, and if so, then the court determines the attorney’s fee award by considering (2) the reasonableness of counsel’s rates, (3) the reasonableness of the claimed hours, and (3) the reasonableness of the litigation costs and expenses.

Authorization of Attorney’s Fees

The court finds moving defendants are entitled to their attorney’s fees and costs.  As stated above, attorney’s fees are recoverable if authorized by contract, statute, or law.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subd. (a)(10).)  The California Anti-SLAPP Statute provides that “a prevailing defendant on a special motion to strike shall be entitled to recover that defendant’s attorney’s fees and costs.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (c)(1).)

The Process Servers prevailed on their special motion to strike on March 30, 2026.  (See 3/30/2026 Order Re: Court’s Final Order, pp. 1–2, 5.)  The Lendmark Defendants prevailed on their special motion to strike on May 11, 2026.  (See 5/11/2026 Order Re: Court’s Final Ruling, pp. 1–2, 5.)

Therefore, the court finds the moving defendants are entitled to their attorney’s fees and costs.

Reasonableness of Counsel’s Rates

The trial court is the ultimate arbiter of what rates are reasonable.  (See Warren v. Kia Motors America, Inc. (2018) 30 Cal.App.5th 24, 36, stating, “experienced trial judges are the best judges of the value of professional services rendered in their courts.”)  To determine if an hourly rate is reasonable, courts consider the rates of similar attorneys in the community as well as “the experience, skill, and reputation of the attorney requesting fees.”  (Heritage Pacific Financial, LLC v. Monroy (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 972, 1009.)

Counsel for the Process Servers request attorney rates between $275 and $378 and paralegal rates between $145 and $158.  (See Ready Decl., ¶ 3.)  Counsel for the Lendmark Defendants request an attorney rate of $350 and a paralegal rate of $125.  (See MacLeod Decl., ¶ 7.)  Counsels’ declarations provide an overview of their experience and credentials.  (See Ready Decl., ¶¶ 2, 4, 6; see also MacLeod Decl., ¶¶ 5–6.)  The court considered counsels’ declarations, and based on a review of this case compared to other Los Angeles-area counsel in similar cases, the court finds that the charged rates are reasonable.  (See Goglin, supra, 4 Cal.App5th at 473, finding it appropriate to deem counsel’s hourly rates reasonable absent contradicting evidence of reasonableness.)

Therefore, the court does not adjust the rates of the moving defendants’ counsel for reasonableness.

Reasonableness of the Claimed Hours

A verified fee bill is “prima facie evidence the costs, expenses and services listed were necessarily incurred.”  (Hadley v. Krepel (1985) 167 Cal.App.3d 677, 682.)  However, counsel bears the burden to demonstrate the reasonableness of charges.  (See Mikhaeilpoor v. BMW of North America, LLC (2020) 48 Cal.App.5th 240, 247.)  Fees should be denied where the court finds the tasks unreasonable or unnecessary since “padding in the form of inefficient or duplicative efforts is not subject to compensation.”  (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132.)

The Process Servers’ counsel claims 72.7 hours of legal work and provides their itemized fee bill.  (See Ready Decl., ¶¶ 16–17; see also Ready Decl., Exh. 1.)  The Lendmark Defendants’ counsel claims 31.2 hours of legal work and provides their itemized fee bill.  (See MacLeod Decl., ¶ 10; see also MacLeod Decl., Exh. A.)  Upon review of the invoices, the court finds the hours spent on the special motions to strike appear reasonable.  (See Hadley, supra, 167 Cal.App.3d at 682, stating, “An award of attorney fees is a matter within the sound discretion of the trial court and absent a manifest abuse of discretion the determination of the trial court will not be disturbed”; see also Moreno, supra, 534 F.3d at 1111, instructing court to “defer to the winning lawyer’s professional judgment as to the tasks completed in an action because he won, and might not have, had he been more of a slacker.”)

For his part, Hall did not attack the moving defendants’ itemized billing with evidence that the fees claimed are not appropriate.  (See Premier Medical Management Systems, Inc., supra, 163 Cal.App.4th at 563–564; see also Moulton Niguel Water Dist. v. Colombo (2003) 111 Cal.App.4th 1210, 1215, stating that “[c]ontentions are waived when a party fails to support them with reasoned argument and citations to authority.”)  As a result, Hall does not overcome the prima facie evidence of the moving defendants’ fee bills.

However, the Process Servers’ counsel and the Lendmark Defendants’ counsel both claim anticipated time.  The court must base its award of attorney’s fees on “actual time expended.”  (Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).)  The plain language of Section 1794, subdivision (d) necessarily precludes an award based on speculative fees, including reviewing an opposition that was never filed or drafting a reply that was not necessary.

The Process Servers’ counsel anticipates 17.1 hours of future time in connection with their motion.  (See Ready Decl., ¶ 17.)  The Lendmark Defendants’ counsel anticipates 9.5 hours of future time in connection with their motion.  (See MacLeod Decl., ¶ 9.)  The parties do not need to review any opposition or draft a reply, so the court limits the parties to 1.0 hour of future time to prepare for and attend the hearing on these motions.  For the Process Servers, the court reduces the award of attorney’s fees by 6.0 hours for Marty B. Ready, 9.3 hours for Chelsea J. Gaudet, and 0.8 hours for Callie Jaggard.  For the Lendmark Defendants, the court reduces the award of attorney’s fees by 7.0 hours for Christina C. MacLeod and 1.5 hours for Nancy Kennealy.

Thus, for Marty B. Ready, who claimed and substantiated 20.3 hours at a rate of $350 and who claimed 21.5 hours and substantiated 14.5 hours at a rate of $378, the court reduces the Process Servers’ award by $2,268.00.  For Chelsea J. Gaudet, who claimed and substantiated 17.0 hours at a rate of $275 and who claimed 9.3 hours and substantiated 0.0 hours at a rate of $315, the court reduces the Process Servers’ award by $2,929.50.  For Callie Jaggard, who claimed 0.8 hours and substantiated 0.0 hours at a rate of $157.50, the court reduces the Process Servers’ award by $126.00.  For Christina C. MacLeod, who claimed 16.9 hours and substantiated 9.9 hours at a rate of $350, the court reduces the Lendmark Defendants’ award by $2,450.00.  For the Nancy Kennealy, who claimed 3.8 hours and substantiated 2.3 hours at a rate of $125, the court reduces the Lendmark Defendants’ award by $187.50.

Therefore, adjusting counsel’s hours for reasonableness, the appropriate award of attorney’s fees is $18,904.00 for the Process Servers and $8,792.50 before adding litigation costs and expenses.

Costs and Expenses

A prevailing plaintiff has a right to recover all costs and expenses that were reasonably incurred in prosecuting the case.  (See Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).)  Allowable costs must be reasonably necessary to conduct the litigation and reasonable in amount.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subds. (c)(2)–(c)(3).)

Apart from the attorney’s fee award, the Process Servers do not request litigation costs or expenses.  (See generally ProVest Mot. P&A.)  The Lendmark Defendants requests $1,349.06 in costs and expenses in the moving brief and the memorandum of costs.  (See Lendmark Mot. P&A, pp. 2–3; Memo. of Costs, p. 1.)  Based on the memorandum of costs and the expenses described therein, the court finds that all claimed costs were reasonably incurred.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5)

Therefore, the Process Servers are not entitled to additional costs and expenses, and the Lendmark Defendants are entitled to $1,349.06 in costs and expenses.

Accordingly, the Process Servers’ motion for attorney’s fees and costs is GRANTED, in a reduced amount, and the Lendmark Defendants’ motion for attorney’s fees and costs is GRANTED in a reduced amount.  The Process Servers are AWARDED a total of $18,904.00, and the Lendmark Defendants are AWARDED a total of $10,141.56.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, defendants ProVest California LLC and David Mealancon’s motion for attorney’s fees and costs is GRANTED in a reduced amount.  Defendants ProVest California LLC and David Mealancon are AWARDED a total of $18,904.00.

Defendants Lendmark Financial Services, LLC, The Dunning Law Firm APC, and James MacLeod’s motion for attorney’s fees and costs is GRANTED, in a reduced amount.  Defendants Lendmark Financial Services, LLC, The Dunning Law Firm APC, and James MacLeod are AWARDED a total of $10,141.56.
