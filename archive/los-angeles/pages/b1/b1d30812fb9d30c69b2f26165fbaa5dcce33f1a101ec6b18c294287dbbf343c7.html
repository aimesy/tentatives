Case Number: 23PSCV03268    Hearing Date: September 21, 2026    Dept: G

Plaintiff/Cross-Defendant Carlos Olivetti’s Motion for Summary Judgment, or in the Alternative, Summary Adjudication

Respondent: Defendant/Cross-Complainant Mike Nijjar and Cross-Complainants Group I El Monte Properties Ltd. and Simple Capital Inc.

TENTATIVE RULING

Plaintiff/Cross-Defendant Carlos Olivetti’s Motion for Summary Judgment, or in the Alternative, Summary Adjudication is DENIED.

BACKGROUND

This is an action for breach of contract.  On March 23, 1999, defendant/cross-defendant Mike Nijjar (Nijjar) and plaintiff/cross-defendant Carlos Olivetti (Olivetti) allegedly entered into a partnership/joint venture, memorialized through a handwritten document, though which Nijjar would purchase residential real property (the Bryant Property) and take title in the name of defendant/cross-complainant Group I El Monte Properties Ltd. (Group I); Nijjar would pay the purchase price, closing costs, and repair costs on the Bryant Property; Olivetti and his mother would reside in the Bryant Property; Olivetti would receive the rental income from the other residential units on the Bryant Property; Olivetti would reimburse Nijjar for the purchase price, closing costs, and repair costs from the rental income; and if the Bryant Property were ever sold or refinanced, Nijjar and Olivetti would split evenly any gain above the purchase price or the market value at the time of refinancing.  On April 23, 1999, Nijjar allegedly purchased the Bryant Property and held title in the name of Group I.

On July 24, 2013, Olivetti allegedly acquired the Bryant Property and signed a promissory note secured by deed of trust in favor of Nijjar using the Bryant Property as collateral.  In late 2020, Olivetti allegedly requested that Nijjar transfer title in the Bryant Property from Group I to Olivetti, and Nijjar allegedly agreed on the condition that the 2013 promissory note be amended to add that it was secured by deed of trust and to reaffirm that Nijjar and Olivetti would split evenly any gain above the purchase price if the Bryant Property were ever sold or refinanced.  On January 22, 2021, Nijjar and Olivetti allegedly signed the 2021 amendment to the 2021 promissory note, and Nijjar allegedly transferred title in the Byrant Property from Group I to Olivetti.  On May 19, 2023, Nijjar allegedly assigned the 2013 promissory note to cross-complainant Simple Capital Inc. (Simple Capital).  On July 21, 2023, Olivetti allegedly sent Nijjar a demand letter for an accurate payoff amount so that Olivetti could refinance the Bryant Property.

On October 23, 2023, Olivetti filed the Complaint.  On October 3, 2025, Olivetti filed the operative Second Amended Complaint, alleging causes of action for (1) breach of contract, (2) fraud, (3) unfair business practices, (4) cancellation of instrument, (5) declaratory relief, (6) usury, and (7) omission of joint venture terms in security instrument.

On January 5, 2024, Nijjar, Group I, and Simple Capital (collectively, Cross-Complainants) filed the Cross-Complaint.  On February 21, 2025, Cross-Complainants filed the operative Amended Cross-Complaint, alleging causes of action for (1) breach of contract, (2) fraud, (3) constructive trust, (4) cancellation of deed, (5) declaratory relief, and (6) breach of fiduciary duty.

On June 22, 2026, Olivetti filed this motion for summary judgment, or in the alternative, summary adjudication.  On September 1, 2026, Cross-Complainants filed the opposition, and on September 10, 2026, Olivetti filed the reply.

The motion is set for hearing on September 21, 2026.

REQUESTS FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

Cross-Complainants request that the court take judicial notice of two grant deeds, a deed of trust, and an assignment of deed, which are all recorded with the Los Angeles County Recorder’s Office.  The court may take judicial notice of facts and propositions that are not reasonably subject to dispute and are capable of immediate and accurate determination.  (See Evid. Code, § 452, subd. (h).) 

Thus, the court takes judicial notice of the requested records.

EVIDENTIARY OBJECTIONS

Cross-Complainants object to the exhibits attached to the motion and portions of Olivetti’s declaration.  The following objections are SUSTAINED: 1–3 (lack foundation and authentication).  The following objections are OVERRULED: 4–16.

ANALYSIS

Olivetti moves for judgment on the first, third, fifth, and sixth causes of action (erroneously referred to as the first, second, third, and fourth causes of action, respectively).  For the following reasons, the motion is DENIED.

Legal Standard

A motion for summary judgment or adjudication provides “courts with a mechanism to cut through the parties’ pleadings in order to determine whether, despite their allegations, trial is in fact necessary to resolve their dispute.”  (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.)  In a motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication, “the initial burden is always on the moving party to make a prima facia showing that there are no triable issues of material fact.”  (Scalf v. D. B. Log Homes, Inc. (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 1510, 1519.)  It must be granted “if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.”  (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119, quoting Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c).)  A defendant moving for summary judgment or summary adjudication “has met [their] burden of showing that a cause of action has no merit if the party has shown that one or more elements of the cause of action . . . cannot be established, or that there is a complete defense to the cause of action.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).)

To establish a triable issue of material fact, the opposing party must produce substantial responsive evidence.  (See Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.)  Courts “liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.”  (Dore v. Arnold Worldwide, Inc. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 384, 389.)¿ “A motion for summary adjudication shall be granted only if it completely disposes of a cause of action, an affirmative defense, a claim for damages, or an issue of duty.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (f)(1).)

Discussion

First Cause of Action: Breach of Contract

Olivetti argues that he is entitled to judgment on the cause of action for breach of contract.  The court disagrees.

To state a cause of action for breach of contract, the plaintiff must be able to establish “(1) the existence of the contract, (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance, (3) defendant’s breach, and (4) the resulting damages to the plaintiff.”  (Oasis West Realty, LLC v. Goldman (2011) 51 Cal.4th 811, 821.)

Moving Party’s Burden

Olivetti meets his initial burden of demonstrating that Nijjar breached their contract.  Here, for the first element, Olivetti presents testimonial evidence of the 2013 promissory note.  (See UMF No. 1; but see Nijjar Obj. No. 1.)  For the second element, Olivetti also presents testimonial evidence that he paid monthly installments pursuant to the agreement for eight (8) years.  (See UMF No. 3.)  For the third and fourth elements, Olivetti’s declaration states that Nijjar refused to allow him to pay off the loan according to their original agreement, and as a result, Olivetti could not complete the refinancing process.  (See UMF Nos. 10–11.)  Thus, Olivetti’s evidence demonstrates that Nijjar breached their contract.

Since Olivetti establishes that Nijjar breached their contract, the burden shifts to Nijjar to show a triable issue of material fact that he did not breach the contract.  (See Sangster, supra, 68 Cal.App.4th at 166.)

Opposing Party’s Burden

Nijjar meets his burden of establishing a triable issue of fact whether he breached the contract.  As a preliminary matter, the court notes that it sustained Nijjar’s objection to Olivetti’s Exhibit A, the purported 2013 promissory note, based on a lack of foundation and authentication.  (See Nijjar Obj. No. 1.) 

For the first element, Nijjar presents testimonial and documentary evidence demonstrating that he and Olivetti entered into a joint venture in 1999 and that the 2013 promissory note was produced consistent with the original 1999 agreement.  (See UMF No. 1.)  Nijjar does not dispute that he refused Olivetti’s request to pay off the loan under the terms of the 2013 promissory note.  (See UMF No. 10.)  However, the court finds there is still a triable issue concerning whether Nijjar’s refusal was a breach of the 2013 promissory note in light of the original 1999 agreement and 2021 amendment.  (See UMF Nos. 11–13.)  Thus, there is a triable issue of material fact whether Nijjar breached the contract.  As such, the court determines  Olivetti is not entitled to judgment on the cause of action for breach of contract as a matter of law.

Therefore, the motion for summary adjudication is DENIED as to the first cause of action for breach of contract.

Third Cause of Action: Unfair Business Practices

Olivetti argues that he is entitled to judgment on the cause of action for unfair business practices.  The court disagrees.

A claim for unfair business practices requires a plaintiff to establish that the defendant engaged in an “unlawful, unfair[,] or fraudulent business act or practice and unfair, deceptive, untrue[,] or misleading advertising.”  (Bus. & Prof. Code, § 17200.)

Moving Party’s Burden

Olivetti meets his burden of demonstrating that Nijjar engaged in a fraudulent business act.  Here, Olivetti presents testimonial evidence that he requested a payoff statement from Nijjar but was told he still owed money from a shared equity provision to which he did not agree.  (See UMF Nos. 5, 10; see also UMF Nos. 6–7; but see Nijjar Objs. Nos. 1–2.)  Thus, Olivetti’s evidence demonstrates that Nijjar engaged in a fraudulent business act.

Since Olivetti establishes that he did not agree to the shared equity provision, the burden shifts to Nijjar to show a triable issue of material fact that he did not engage in a fraudulent business act.  (See Sangster, supra, 68 Cal.App.4th at 166.)

Opposing Party’s Burden

Nijjar meets his burden of establishing a triable issue of material fact that Olivetti agreed to the shared equity provision.  Nijjar presents testimonial and documentary evidence demonstrating that Olivetti agreed to and signed the 2021 amendment to the 2013 promissory note, which provides that Nijjar and Olivetti will split evenly any gain above the purchase price if the Bryant Property is refinanced.  (See UMF Nos. 5–7.)  Thus, the court finds there is a triable issue of material fact whether Nijjar engaged in a fraudulent business act.  As such, the court determines  Olivetti is not entitled to judgment on the cause of action for unfair business practices as a matter of law.

Therefore, the motion for summary adjudication is DENIED as to the third cause of action for unfair business practices.

Fifth Cause of Action: Declaratory Relief

Olivetti argues that he is entitled to judgment on the cause of action for declaratory relief.  The court disagrees.

“To qualify for declaratory relief, a party would have to demonstrate its action presented two essential elements: (1) a proper subject of declaratory relief, and (2) an actual controversy involving justiciable questions relating to the party’s rights or obligations.”  (Jolley v. Chase Home Finance, LLC (2013) 213 Cal.App.4th 872, 909, cleaned up.)

Olivetti fails to meet his initial burden of demonstrating that he is entitled to declaratory relief.  As a preliminary matter, the court finds this action presents a proper subject of declaratory relief and that an actual controversy exists relating to the parties’ rights and obligations.  (See Jolley, supra, 213 Cal.App.4th at 909; see also Code Civ. Proc., § 1060, providing that “[a]ny person interested under a written instrument . . . or under a contract . . . in respect to, in, over or upon property . . . may . . . bring an original action . . . for a declaration of [their] rights and duties in the premises, including a determination of any question of construction or validity arising under the instrument or contract.”)  However, Olivetti does not present evidence in support of his contentions relating to the exhibits attached to the motion.  (See UMF Nos. 7–9; see also Nijjar Objs. Nos. 1–3.)

Since Olivetti fails to establish that he is entitled to declaratory relief, the burden does not shift to Nijjar to demonstrate a triable issue of material fact.  (See Sangster, supra, 68 Cal.App.4th at 166.)

Therefore, the motion for summary adjudication is DENIED as to the fifth cause of action for declaratory relief.

Sixth Cause of Action: Usury

Olivetti argues that he is entitled to judgment on the cause of action for usury.  The court disagrees.

By initiative in 1918, the people of the State enacted a comprehensive usury law that is set forth in an uncodified statute; the usury prohibition was later incorporated into the California Constitution, but the 1918 statute remains in full force today to the extent it does not conflict with the Constitution.  (See Creative Ventures, LLC v. Jim Ward & Associates (2011) 195 Cal.App.4th 1430, 1441, citing the uncodified statutes as West’s Civ. Code, § 1916-1 et seq.)  There are two remedies available to borrowers who have paid interest under a usurious loan agreement.  A borrower who pays interest at a usurious rate may recover treble the amount paid, “providing such action shall be brought within one year after such payment or delivery.”  (Ibid., citing West’s Civ. Code, § 1916-3, subd. (a).)  “The borrower also retains the common law right as a party to an illegal contract to bring an action for money had and received to recover usurious interest paid within two years of the suit.”  (Ibid., internal citation omitted.)

For purposes of the usury law, the interest charged is computed by adding the stated interest rate to any bonus the borrower must pay in addition; if the interest rate plus the bonus exceeds the statutory limit, the loan is usurious.  (See Creative Ventures, LLC, supra, 195 Cal.App.4th at 1441, internal citation omitted.)  Absent an exception, the maximum allowable interest rate for consumer loans is 10% per year.  (See Cal. Const, art. XV, § 1, subd. (2).)  For non-consumer loans, the interest rate can bear the maximum of whichever is greater between either: (a) 10% per annum; or (b) the federal discount rate plus 5%.  (See Cal. Const., art. XV, § 1, subd. (2).)  In the absence of an agreement between the parties as to what is the rate of interest, the law imposes a rate of 7%.  (See Cal. Const., art. XV § 1.)

Hare, Olivetti fails to meet his initial burden of demonstrating that Nijjar engaged in usury.  “This is the Golden Rule of Summary Adjudication: if it is not set forth in the separate statement, it does not exist.”  (United Community Church v. Garcin (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 327, 337.)  The Court of Appeal clarified, however, that the “it” in the Golden Rule refers to the material fact and not the evidence in support of that fact.  (Parkview Villas Assn., Inc. v. State Farm Fire and Casualty Co. (2005) 133 Cal.App.4th 1197, 1214, stating that “the undisputed material fact . . . must appear in the separate statement or be disregarded, not the underlying evidence supporting the fact”; see also San Diego Watercrafts, Inc. v. Wells Fargo Bank (2002) 102 Cal.App.4th 308, 315, instructing the trial court to “consider all the evidence submitted, except the court may ignore evidence not disclosed in moving party’s separate statement of undisputed facts” but rejecting “the absolute prohibition against consideration of nonreferenced evidence.”)  Since Olivetti’s separate statement does not contain any undisputed material facts about the payment of interest, Olivetti fails to meet his burden.  (See UMF Nos. 1–13.)

Since Olivetti fails to establish that Nijjar charged usurious interest, the burden does not shift to Nijjar to demonstrate that he did engage in usury.  (See Sangster, supra, 68 Cal.App.4th at 166.)

Therefore, the motion for summary adjudication is DENIED as to the sixth cause of action for usury.

Accordingly, the motion for summary judgment is DENIED.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the motion for summary judgment, or in the alternative, summary adjudication, is DENIED.

Case Number: 25PSCV03488    Hearing Date: September 21, 2026    Dept: G

Defendant/Cross-Defendant Kevin Jagielo’s Motion to Set Aside Default and for Leave to Defend Cross-Action

Respondent: Defendant/Cross-Complainant Green Coast Building & Development, Inc.

TENTATIVE RULING

Defendant/Cross-Defendant Kevin Jagielo’s Motion to Set Aside Default and for Leave to Defend Cross-Action is GRANTED.

Defendant and Cross-Defendant Jagielo is ordered to FILE SEPARATELY THE PROPOSED ANSWER, attached as Exhibit 1 to the moving paper, in TEN (10) DAYS.

BACKGROUND

This is an action for breach of contract and fraud.  Defendant/cross-defendant Kevin Jagielo (Jagielo) allegedly represented himself as an officer of defendant/cross-complainant Green Coast Building & Development, Inc. (Green Coast).  On March 27, 2022, Jagielo allegedly entered into a written contract on Green Coast’s behalf for goods and services from plaintiff/cross-defendant SRS Distribution, Inc. (SRS) and allegedly served as a personal guarantor on the contract.  On August 20, 2025, Green Coast and Jagielo allegedly defaulted on the contract.

On September 29, 2025, SRS filed the Complaint, alleging a sole cause of action for breach of contract (and of personal guaranty).

On December 15, 2025, Green Coast filed the Cross-Complaint, alleging causes of action for (1) negligence, (2) declaratory relief, (3) fraud, (4) constructive fraud, (5) breach of fiduciary duty, and (6) indemnity.

On February 19, 2026, the court entered default against Jagielo (on the Cross-Complaint).

On August 18, 2026, Jagielo filed this motion to set aside entry of default. On September 8, 2026, Defendant/Cross-Complainant Green Coast Building & Development, Inc. filed an opposition.  On September 14, 2026, Jagielo filed a reply.

The motion is set for hearing on September 21, 2026.

ANALYSIS

Jagielo moves to set aside the entry of default based on a party mistake, or in the alternative, attorney mistake.  For the following reasons, the motion is GRANTED.

Legal Standard

Whenever an application for relief from default is made no more than six months after entry of default, is in proper form, and is accompanied by the moving party’s sworn affidavit attesting to their mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect, the court may vacate any (1) resulting default entered by the clerk against the moving party or (2) resulting default judgment or dismissal entered against the moving party, unless the court finds that the default or dismissal was not in fact caused by the moving party’s mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b).)  When the motion is based on a party’s mistake, the “six-month period runs from entry of default, not entry of judgment.”  (Manson, Iver & York v. Black (2009) 176 Cal.App.4th 36, 42.)  “The six-month time limit for granting statutory relief is jurisdictional and the court may not consider a motion for relief [under Section 473] made after that period has elapsed.”  (Ibid., emphasis added.)

If based on the mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect of the party’s attorney, then the court must set aside the entry of default and vacate any resulting default judgment against that party.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b).)  “[R]elief may be denied if the court finds the default was not in fact the attorney’s fault, for example when the attorney is simply covering up for the client’s neglect.”  (Bailey v. Citibank, Nat. Assn. (2021) 66 Cal.App.5th 335, 349.)  The provision based on attorney mistake “does not afford relief to a client who, after inexcusably allowing his default to be entered, hires an attorney whose neglect results in a default judgment”; in other words, the entry of default must be the attorney’s fault.  (Cisneros v. Vueve (1995) 37 Cal.App.4th 906, 908.)  To obtain relief, the attorney must include a sworn affidavit attesting to their mistake, and the court must direct the attorney to pay reasonable compensatory legal fees and costs to opposing counsel and parties.  (See ibid.)  In contrast to the discretionary provision based on a party’s mistake, the six-month period for a motion to set aside entry of default based on an attorney’s mistake runs from the entry of judgment, not the entry of default.  (See Jimenez v. Chavez (2023) 97 Cal.App.5th 50, 60.)

Courts liberally grant motions to vacate default judgments when relief is promptly sought and the opposing party is not prejudiced as the law strongly favors resolution on the merits.  (See Kramer v. Traditional Escrow, Inc. (2020) 56 Cal.App.5th 13, 28.)  Courts only require “slight evidence” to support vacating a default and resolve all doubts in favor of the party seeking relief.  (Ibid.)  However, “[t]he only occasion for the application of [S]ection 473 is where a party is unexpectedly placed in a situation to [their own] injury without fault or negligence of [their] own and against which ordinary prudence could not have guarded.”  (Hearn v. Howard (2009) 177 Cal.App.4th 1193, 1206; see Hodge Sheet Metal Products v. Palm Springs Riviera Hotel (1961) 189 Cal.App.2d 653, 657, defining “excusable neglect” within the meaning of Section 473 as “neglect which might have been the act of a reasonably prudent person under the same circumstances.”)

Discussion

As a preliminary matter, the court must determine whether this motion is timely.  As stated above, a party must seek relief from default under Section 473 within six months.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 473, subd. (b).)  For discretionary relief based on party mistake, the time runs from the entry of default.  (See Manson, Iver & York, supra, 176 Cal.App.4th at 42.)  For mandatory relief based on attorney mistake, the time runs from the entry of judgment.  (See Jimenez, supra, 97 Cal.App.5th at 60.)

Here, the court entered default against Jagielo on February 19, 2026 and did not issue a judgment.  Jagielo moved for relief on August 18, 2026, within six months of the entry of default.  Thus, this motion is timely under both the discretionary and the mandatory provisions of Section 473.

Jagielo argues that the entry of default was due to his excusable neglect.  The court disagrees.

“[A] summons may be served by leaving a copy of the summons and complaint at the person’s dwelling house, usual place of abode, usual place of business, or usual mailing address other than a United States Postal Service post office box, in the presence of a competent member of the household or a person apparently in charge . . . and by thereafter mailing a copy of the summons and of the complaint . . . to the person to be served at the place where a copy of the summons and complaint were left.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 415.20, subd. (b).)  “The return of a [registered process server] . . . upon process . . . establishes a presumption, affecting the burden of producing evidence, of the facts stated in the return.”  (Evid. Code, § 647.)

The proof of service reflects that a process server served Jagielo with the Cross-Complaint by substituted service on December 23, 2025.  (See 12/29/2025 POS, ¶¶ 2–5.)  As stated above, process server’s proof of service creates a presumption that he completed service according to the facts stated in the return.  (See Evid. Code, § 647.)  Jagielo’s declaration states that he “was not aware that [he] had been served with the summons or Cross-Complaint by Green Coast until on or about March 19, 2026.”  (Mot., Jagielo Decl., ¶ 3.)  However, Jagielo does not attest to any facts for the court to conclude that the failure to timely respond to the Cross-Complaint was the result of his own excusable neglect.  Thus, the court finds that Jagielo fails to meet his burden for discretionary relief under Section 473, subdivision (b).

Alternatively, Jagielo argues that the entry of default was due to his counsel’s neglect.  The court agrees.

As stated above, to obtain relief under the mandatory provision of Section 473, subdivision (b), the attorney must include a sworn affidavit attesting to their mistake.  (See Cisneros, supra, 37 Cal.App.4th at 908.)

Here, Jagielo’s counsel states that they were retained on February 17, 2026, before the court entered default against Jagielo.  (See Mot., Felton Decl., ¶ 6.)  However, counsel did not check the docket in this case, so counsel did not discover that Green Coast served a Cross-Complaint on Jagielo or that Green Coast had recently sought default against Jagielo.  (See Mot., Felton Decl., ¶ 6.)  In other words, counsel could have avoided the entry of default had they been attentive to the docket, which the court finds is a sufficient basis to grant the motion under Section 473, subdivision (b).  (See Graham v. Beers (1994) 30 Cal. App. 4th 1656, 1660, stating, “No reason need be given for the existence of [mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or neglect].  Attestation that one of these reasons existed is sufficient to obtain relief. . . .”)  Thus, the court finds that Jagielo meets his burden for discretionary relief under Section 473, subdivision (b).

Therefore, the motion to set aside entry of default is GRANTED.  Defendant and Cross-Defendant Jagielo is ordered to FILE SEPARATELY THE PROPOSED ANSWER, attached as Exhibit 1 to the moving paper, in TEN (10) DAYS.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the motion to set aside default and for leave to defend cross-action is GRANTED.

Defendant Jagielo is ordered to FILE SEPARATELY THE PROPOSED ANSWER, attached as Exhibit 1 to the moving paper, in TEN (10) DAYS.

Case Number: 25PSCV03816    Hearing Date: September 21, 2026    Dept: G

Plaintiff Darleen Vorobyov’s Motion to Compel Nonparty Google, LLC’s Compliance with Subpoena for Production of Business Records and Request for Monetary Sanctions

Respondent: Nonparty Google, LLC

TENTATIVE RULING

Plaintiff Darleen Vorobyov’s Motion to Compel Nonparty Google, LLC’s Compliance with Subpoena for Production of Business Records is GRANTED IN PART and deemed MOOT IN PART. 

Plaintiff Darleen Vorobyov’s Request for Monetary Sanctions is DENIED WITHOUT PREJUDICE.

BACKGROUND

This is a negligence action against public entities.  On July 15, 2025, nonparty David Steven Vorobyov allegedly operated his motorcycle on Interstate 210, which is allegedly controlled and maintained by defendants State of California (the State), the California Department of Transportation (Caltrans), and the California Highway Patrol (CHP), when he allegedly struck a metal pole, lost control of his motorcycle, and sustained fatal injuries.  David Steven Vorobyov is survived by his widow, plaintiff Darleen Vorobyov, and his parents, plaintiffs Oleg Vorobyov and Inna Vorobyov.

On October 23, 2025, Darleen Vorobyov filed the Complaint.  On July 29, 2026, Darleen Vorobyov, Oleg Vorobyov, and Inna Vorobyov (collectively, the Vorobyov Family) filed the operative Amended Complaint, alleging causes of action for (1) dangerous condition of public property, (2) vicarious liability, and (3) negligence.

On July 14, 2026, Darleen Vorobyov filed this motion to compel production of business records.  On September 8, 2026, nonparty Google, LLC (Google) filed the opposition, and on September 14, 2026, Darleen Vorobyov filed the reply.

The motion is set for hearing on September 21, 2026.

ANALYSIS

Darleen Vorobyov moves to compel Google’s compliance with a subpoena for business records.  For the following reasons, the motion is GRANTED IN PART and deemed MOOT IN PART.

Legal Standard

A party seeking discovery from a person who is not a party to the action may obtain discovery by oral deposition, written deposition, or deposition subpoena for production of business records.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2020.010.)¿ A deposition subpoena may command: (1) only the attendance and testimony of the deponent, (2) only the production of business records for copying, or (3) the attendance and testimony of the deponent, as well as the production of business records, other documents, electronically stored information, and tangible things.¿ (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2020.020.)  Service of a deposition subpoena shall be done with sufficient time in advance of the deposition to provide the deponent a reasonable opportunity to locate and produce any designated documents and, where personal attendance is commanded, a reasonable time to travel to the place of deposition.¿ (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2020.220, subd. (a).)¿ Personal service of any deposition subpoena is effective to require a deponent who is a resident of California to: personally appear and testify, if the subpoena so specifies; to produce any specified documents; and to appear at a court session if the subpoena so specifies.¿ (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2020.220, subd. (c).)

If a deponent fails to appear or produce requested documents for a deposition, the deposing party may file a noticed motion to compel deponent’s compliance.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.1, subd. (a).)  A deponent who disobeys a deposition subpoena may also be punished for contempt without the necessity of a prior court order court directing compliance.¿ (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2020.240.)¿ A noncompliant deponent may also be ordered to pay $500 to the deposing party in addition to any damages the deposing party incurred as a result of the deponent’s failure to attend the deposition.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2020.240, citing Code Civ. Proc., § 1992.)

Discussion

A.    Motion to Compel Compliance

Darleen Vorobyov obtained an order permitting early discovery on May 5, 2026.  (See Mot., King Decl., ¶ 9; see also 5/5/2026 Order, p. 1.)  Darleen Vorobyov then served Google with a deposition subpoena for business records on May 7, 2026.  (See Mot., King Decl., ¶ 10; see also Mot., Exh. 4.)  The deposition subpoena sought (1) records of reports about debris, objects, and hazards on the stretch of highway where David Steven Vorobyov lost control and passed away and (2) records reflecting the transmission of those reports to government entities.  (See Opp., pp. 4, 6; see also Leach Decl., Exh. C.)

Google produced the records of reports about debris, objects, and hazards, the first category of documents sought, on July 17, 2026.  (See Leach Decl., ¶¶ 11–12.)  Google obtained the records reflecting the transmission of reports to government entities, the second category of documents sought, but communicated that it would not produce these records until after the entry of a stipulated protective order.  (See Leach Decl., ¶¶ 17, 21.)  The court signed the stipulated protective order on September 16, 2026.  The stipulated protective order provides for the “immediate production of [c]onfidential [m]aterials” under its own terms.  (9/8/2026 Stip. P.O., ¶ 23.)  Thus, the court finds there are no further grounds upon which Google may justify the withholding of the documents subject to the deposition subpoena.  However, since the court just signed the protective order two (2) court days prior to this hearing, Google is ordered to provide the remaining documents responsive to Vorobyov’s subpoena on or before September 28, 2026.

Therefore, the motion to compel compliance with subpoena is GRANTED IN PART as to the second category of documents and deemed MOOT IN PART as to the first category of documents.

B.    Monetary Sanctions

Monetary sanctions are mandatory as against any party who fails “to respond or to submit to an authorized method of discovery,” “who unsuccessfully . . . opposes a motion to compel [discovery],” or whose failure to serve a timely response necessitated a motion to deem request for admissions as admitted, unless the court finds “the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.”  (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2023.010, subd. (d); 2023.030, subd. (a); 2025.450, subd. (g)(1); 2025.480, subd. (j); 2030.290, subd. (c); 2031.300, subd. (c); 2033.280, subd. (c).) 

Justification is “substantial” if it is “clearly reasonable because it is well-grounded in both law and fact.”  (Doe v. U.S. Swimming, Inc. (2011) 200 Cal.App.4th 1424, 1434.)  The court may award sanctions in favor of a party seeking to compel discovery even though no opposition was filed, the opposition was withdrawn, or the requested discovery was provided to the moving party after the motion was filed.  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1348(a).)  While the imposition of sanctions is mandatory, the court “has discretion to reduce the amount of fees and costs requested as a discovery sanction in order to reach a reasonable award.”  (Cornerstone Realty Advisors, LLC v. Summit Healthcare Reit, Inc. (2020) 56 Cal.App.5th 771, 791.)

Since the court signed the protective order two (2) court days before this hearing, on September 16, 2026, the court finds Google acted with substantial justification by waiting for the court to sign the stipulated protective order before producing the remaining documents, even though the protective order provides by its own terms for “immediate production.”

Therefore, Vorobyov's request for sanctions pursuant to the Code of Civil Procedure is DENIED WITHOUT PREJUDICE.  If Google fails to provide the remaining responsive documents by the September 28, 2026 deadline set by the court, Vorobyov may renew the request for sanctions. 

Therefore, the request for monetary sanctions is DENIED WITHOUT PREJUDICE.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the motion to compel compliance with subpoena for production of business records is GRANTED IN PART as to the second category of records and deemed MOOT IN PART as to the first category of records. 

The request for monetary sanctions is DENIED WITHOUT PREJUDICE.
