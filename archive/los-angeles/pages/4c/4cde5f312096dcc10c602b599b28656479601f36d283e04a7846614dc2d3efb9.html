DEMURRER

 

MOVING PARTY: Defendants, Sky Vision Insurance Agency and Tiffany Xu

RESPONDING PARTY: Plaintiff, Yu Geng

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Demurrer to First Amended Complaint

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

On February 6, 2026, Plaintiff Yu Geng filed the instant action against Defendants Pacific Life Insurance Company (Pacific), Tiffany Xu, and Sky Vision Insurance Agency (Sky Vision) alleging she was fraudulently induced to purchase a life insurance policy issued by Pacific. She claims that Xu, her life insurance agent and broker, represented that the policy offered “perpetual retirement income of $190,000 or more per year, all tax-free,” after 5 years and “guaranteed that the policy would yield an annual return of at least six percent (6%).” Under the policy, Plaintiff was required to make annual premium payments of $500,000 for 5 consecutive years, totaling $2.5 million in total premiums to be paid. Plaintiff alleges that Xu intentionally omitted “extremely excessive insurance policy costs and charges,” “significant financial risks involved in the issuance” of the policy, and “failed to explain the significant risk of policy lapse in the event that the accumulated value of the policy decreased to zero.”

 

During the sixth year of the policy, and after Plaintiff fully paid the total $2.5 million in premiums, Xu informed her that if she were to withdraw $190,000 per year, the policy would lapse by year 9 or 10 due to “insufficient accumulated value to sustain the policy in force.” Fearing the loss of her total investment, Plaintiff terminated the policy and was only able to recover and receive back approximately $1.4 million of the total $2.5 million paid. Plaintiff asserts causes of action for: (1) Breach of Contract; (2) Violation of CA Business & Professions Code §17200, et seq.; (3) Fraud and Intentional Misrepresentation; (4) Negligent Misrepresentation; (5) Negligence; (6) Breach of Fiduciary Duty; and (7) Breach of Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing.  

 

RULING: Overruled.

Sky Vision and Xu demur to Plaintiff’s First Amended Complaint arguing that Plaintiff fails to join her husband who is an indispensable party and that her first through fourth, sixth, and seventh causes of action are time-barred. Plaintiff opposes the demurrer, denying that her husband is an indispensable party and maintaining that her claims are timely. Defendants’ demurrer is overruled.

 

A demurrer is an objection to a pleading, the grounds for which are apparent from either the face of the complaint or a matter of which the court may take judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.30, subd. (a); see also Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) The purpose of a demurrer is to challenge the sufficiency of a pleading “by raising questions of law.” (Postley v. Harvey (1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 280, 286.) “In the construction of a pleading, for the purpose of determining its effect, its allegations must be liberally construed, with a view to substantial justice between the parties.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 452.) The court “treat[s] the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law . . . .” (Berkley v. Dowds (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 518, 525.) In applying these standards, the court liberally construes the complaint to determine whether a cause of action has been stated. (Picton v. Anderson Union High School Dist. (1996) 50 Cal.App.4th 726, 733.) “If the complaint states a cause of action under any theory, regardless of the title under which the factual basis for relief is stated, that aspect of the complaint is good against a demurrer.” (Quelimane Co. v. Stewart Title Guaranty Co. (1998) 19 Cal.4th 26, 38.)

 

The Court first rejects Defendants’ argument regarding a defect of parties due to Plaintiff’s failure to join her husband in the action. Defendants argue that because Plaintiff alleges the premiums were paid using the couple’s life savings, proceeding in her husband’s absence exposes them to multiple or inconsistent obligations. But if the premiums constitute community property, then their recovery would be community property. (Vick v. DaCorsi (2003) 110 Cal.App.4th 206, 212.) Management and control of the community personal property is vested in either spouse, allowing either Plaintiff or her husband to act on behalf of the community, including accepting a settlement offer. (Id. at p. 212.) Thus, there is no risk of double recovery by Plaintiff’s husband and Defendants’ demurrer on grounds of defect of parties is overruled.

 

The Court next addresses Defendants’ statute of limitations argument. Defendants contend that Plaintiff’s causes of action for breach of contract, violation of the Business and Professions code, fraud and intentional misrepresentation, negligent misrepresentation, breach of fiduciary duty, and breach of implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing are time-barred. Their reasoning is that the disclosures and illustrations Plaintiff received prior to purchasing the policy in January 2018 were sufficient to put her on notice of the fees and costs to be charged by Pacific and the potential underperformance of the policy. Plaintiff responds that whether the disclosures or illustrations were sufficiently clear to place her on notice is a question of fact and that she was entitled to rely on the representations of Defendants as her agents. She identifies February 2024 as the time she discovered Defendants’ alleged fraud after months of communications with Defendants regarding the policy and her own investigation into the policy. The Court finds that Plaintiff has the better of the two arguments.

 

Plaintiff’s claims sound in fraud and are thus subject to a 3-year statute of limitations period, with the exception of the UCL claim. (Code Civ. Proc., § 338, subd. (d).) The causes of action do not accrue “until the discovery, by the aggrieved party, of the facts constituting the fraud or mistake.” (Ibid.) The UCL claim is governed by a 4-year statute of limitations, and the discovery rule applies. (Aryeh v. Canon Business Solutions, Inc. (2013) 55 Cal.4th 1185, 1192-97.)

 

On the face of the complaint, Plaintiff’s claims are not time-barred. The complaint places Plaintiff’s discovery of the alleged fraud in August 2023, when Plaintiff discovered that “Xu had only provided her with a partial set of the policy materials and had intentionally concealed the final portion of the documents, which contained critical and material disclosures regarding the policy risks and charges in the subject Policy issued to her.” [FAC ¶ 13.] Plaintiff filed her complaint in November 2025 and therefore the claims are timely. The policy and any attendant disclosures or illustrations are not before the Court and thus it cannot be assessed whether they were “so clear and so obvious” as to defeat Plaintiff’s claims of delayed discovery. (Broberg v. The Guardian Life Ins. Co. of America (2009) 171 Cal.App.4th 912, 921.) Plaintiff, of course, was entitled to rely on the representations of Xu regarding the policy. (See Clement v. Smith (1993) 16 Cal.App.4th 39, 45 [“Absent some notice or warning, an insured should be able to rely on an agent’s representations of coverage without independently verifying the accuracy of those representations by examining the relevant policy provisions”].) The demurrer on statute of limitations grounds is therefore overruled.

 

Sky Vision and Xu to answer the operative complaint within 10 days.

 

Sky Vision and Xu to give notice.

PRO HAC VICE

 

MOVING PARTY: Defendant, Pacific Life Insurance Company

RESPONDING PARTY: Unopposed/Plaintiff, Yu Geng

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Motion for Pro Hac Vice Admission

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

On February 6, 2026, Plaintiff Yu Geng filed the instant action against Defendants Pacific Life Insurance Company (Pacific), Tiffany Xu, and Sky Vision Insurance Agency (Sky Vision) alleging she was fraudulently induced to purchase a life insurance policy issued by Pacific. She claims that Xu, her life insurance agent and broker, represented that the policy offered “perpetual retirement income of $190,000 or more per year, all tax-free,” after 5 years and “guaranteed that the policy would yield an annual return of at least six percent (6%).” Under the policy, Plaintiff was required to make annual premium payments of $500,000 for 5 consecutive years, totaling $2.5 million in total premiums to be paid. Plaintiff alleges that Xu intentionally omitted “extremely excessive insurance policy costs and charges,” “significant financial risks involved in the issuance” of the policy, and “failed to explain the significant risk of policy lapse in the event that the accumulated value of the policy decreased to zero.”

Plaintiff asserts causes of action for: (1) Breach of Contract; (2) Violation of CA Business & Professions Code §17200, et seq.; (3) Fraud and Intentional Misrepresentation; (4) Negligent Misrepresentation; (5) Negligence; (6) Breach of Fiduciary Duty; and (7) Breach of Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing.  

 

RULING: Granted.

Defendant Pacific moves for pro hac vice admission of attorney Irma T. Solares.

 

Pro hac vice admission in California is governed by California Rules of Court, rule 9.40. To be admitted pro hac vice, one must be “an attorney in good standing of and eligible to practice before the bar of any United States court or the highest court in any state, territory, or insular possession of the United States.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(a).) However, in no case shall an attorney appear pro hac vice if the attorney is a resident of California, regularly employed in California, or “regularly engaged in substantial business, professional, or other activities in the State of California.” (Ibid.)

 

An attorney seeking pro hac vice admission must file a verified application in court establishing:

 

(1) [t]he applicant’s residence and office address; (2)[t]he courts to which the applicant has been admitted to practice and the dates of admission; (3)[t]hat the applicant is a licensee in good standing in those courts; (4)[t]hat the applicant is not currently suspended or disbarred in any court; (5)[t]he title of each court and cause in which the applicant has filed an application to appear as counsel pro hac vice in this state in the preceding two years, the date of each application, and whether or not it was granted; and (6)[t]he name, address, and telephone number of the active licensee of the State Bar who is attorney of record.

 

(Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(d).)

 

The application complies with California Rules of Court, rule 9.40. Solares lives and practices in Florida. She has applied for pro hac vice admission in California state court once within the last two years. While the proof of service indicates that the State Bar was served by mail and Plaintiff via email, service must be made by mail on all parties who have appeared in the case. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(c)(1).) The application for pro hac vice admission is granted, with counsel to file the requisite proof of service.

 

Pacific to give notice.

QUASH SERVICE

 

MOVING PARTY: Specially Appearing Defendants, Sae Hyun Yang and Brooke Yang

RESPONDING PARTY: Plaintiff, Kae-soon Choe

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Motion to Quash Service of Summons

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

Plaintiff Kae-soon Choe sued her pastor, Defendant Jun Ho Yang, alleging that Yang used her confidence in him to his own advantage. Plaintiff claims that in helping her sell her apartment, Yang diverted $6.4 million of the net proceeds to his non-profit, Defendant The Christian Herald, Inc. Plaintiff alleges that she signed numerous documents presented by Yang based on his representation that they were related to the sale of her property. The documents were only in English, a language Plaintiff does not read or speak well. Plaintiff also claims that she was not provided with copies of the documents. Plaintiff brings causes of action for conversion, breach of fiduciary duty, civil damages for theft, and financial elder abuse.

 

On June 25, 2026, Plaintiff substituted in Jun Ho’s son, Sae Hyun Yang, and his daughter in law, Brooke Yang, for Does 4 and 5.      

 

RULING: Granted.

Request for Judicial Notice: Granted.

The Court takes judicial notice of the existence of documents filed with the California and North Carolina secretary of state, the Certificate of Limited Partnership produced in discovery, the deeds and property records, and other documents filed in this action. (Evid. Code, § 452, subds. (c), (d), (h).)  

 

Evidentiary Objections: Sustained in part.

 

Plaintiff’s objections to Sae’s declaration are overruled, except as to Objection No. 2, which is sustained on grounds of improper legal conclusion.

 

Plaintiff’s objections to Brooke’s declaration are overruled, except as to Objection No. 2, which is sustained on grounds of improper legal conclusion.

 

Specially Appearing Defendants’ objections to the declaration of J. Douglas Kirk are overruled.

 

The Court does not rule on Specially Appearing Defendants’ objections to the declaration of Grace Choe because the declaration was not filed.

 

Specially Appearing Defendants Sae and Brooke move to quash service of summons arguing that the Court lacks personal jurisdiction over them because they are North Carolina residents and were not involved in the underlying real estate transaction. On the same basis, they also argue that the exercise of jurisdiction would be unreasonable. Plaintiff opposes the motion, maintaining that Sae purposefully availed himself of California through solicitation of funds from his father and Brooke through her incorporation of a North Carolina corporation, Yang Legacy Holdings, Inc., that received such funds and her individual receipt of Zelle transfers. Plaintiff further contends that the exercise of jurisdiction here is fair and reasonable. Alternatively, Plaintiff requests a continuance for the purpose of conducting jurisdictional discovery as to Brooke. Because Plaintiff fails to establish that Sae and Brooke purposefully availed themselves of California, Specially Appearing Defendants’ motion is granted.   

 

“When a defendant moves to quash service of process on jurisdictional grounds, the plaintiff has the initial burden of demonstrating facts justifying the exercise of jurisdiction. (Citation.) Once facts showing minimum contacts with the forum state are established, however, it becomes the defendant’s burden to demonstrate that the exercise of jurisdiction would be unreasonable. (Citation.) When there is conflicting evidence, the trial court’s factual determinations are not disturbed on appeal if supported by substantial evidence. (Citation.) When no conflict in the evidence exists, however, the question of jurisdiction is purely one of law and the reviewing court engages in an independent review of the record.” (Vons Companies, Inc. v. Seabest Foods, Inc. (1996) 14 Cal.4th 434, 449; Shisler v. Sanfer Sports Cars, Inc. (2006) 146 Cal.App.4th 1254, 1258-59.)

 

Personal jurisdiction is either general or specific. (Pavlovich v. Superior Court (2002) 29 Cal.4th 262, 268-69.) Where, as here, specific jurisdiction is at issue, courts consider the “relationship among the defendant, the forum, and the litigation.” (Id. at p. 269.) Specific jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant may be exercised only if: (1) “the defendant has purposefully availed himself or herself of forum benefits”; (2) “the ‘controversy is related to or “arises out of” [the] defendant’s contacts with the forum’”; and (3) “the assertion of personal jurisdiction would comport with ‘fair play and substantial justice.’” (Ibid.)

 

For tort claims, purposeful availment is analyzed using the “effects test,” which “requires intentional conduct expressly aimed at or targeting the forum state in addition to the defendant’s knowledge that his intentional conduct would cause harm in the forum.” (Pavlovich, supra, 29 Cal.4th at p. 271.) “[T]he plaintiff cannot be the only link between the defendant and the forum. Rather, it is the defendant’s conduct that must form the necessary connection with the forum State that is the basis for its jurisdiction over him.” (Walden v. Fiore (2014) 571 U.S. 277, 285.)

 

Plaintiff has not established that Sae and Brooke purposefully availed themselves of California. Sae and Brooke both deny any involvement in the underlying real estate sale and state that they were unaware that the funds received from Jun Ho were the subject of any dispute or were wrongfully obtained. [Sae Decl. ¶¶ 8, 10; Brooke Decl. ¶¶ 8, 10.] While Jun Ho testified in his deposition that he sent money to his son based on his financial difficulties and marriage and that Sae instructed a certain payment be made to Yang Legacy Holdings, this does not show Sae’s knowledge that any request for money would cause harm in California. [Kirk Decl. ¶ 2, Ex. 2: Jun Ho Depo. Vol. 2, pp. 159:10-162:20, 182:21-22, 196:12-15, 197:1-10.] Sae’s mere position as CFO for The Christian Herald is insufficient to infer such knowledge on Sae’s behalf, especially considering that Jun Ho testified that Sae was given that position as a “blessing” and “did not carry out as a Chief Financial Officer.” [Id. ¶ 1, Ex. 1: Jun Ho Depo. Vol. 1, pp. 86:8-15, 88:14-16.] Jun Ho similarly listed his two other children, one of whom was only 13 years old at the time, as Officers and Vice Presidents as a blessing. [Gans Decl. ¶ 11, Ex. E, pp. 90:8-91:13.] Plaintiff likewise presents no evidence of knowledge on Brooke’s part and the mere receipt of Zelle transfers and the direction of $50,000 to Yang Legacy Holdings are insufficient to infer such knowledge. Specially Appearing Defendants’ motion is therefore granted.

 

The Court declines Plaintiff’s request for a continuance to conduct jurisdictional discovery as to Brooke. Plaintiff does not explain how “discovery is likely to lead to the production of evidence of facts establishing jurisdiction.” (In re Automobile Antitrust Cases I & II (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 100, 127.) Plaintiff has had the ability to obtain from Jun Ho communications between himself and Sae and Brooke.

 

Service of summons upon Specially Appearing Defendants is quashed.

 

The action is dismissed without prejudice as to Specially Appearing Defendants. (Code Civ. Proc., § 581, subd. (h).)

 

Specially Appearing Defendants to give notice.

MOTION FOR OSC RE: CONTEMPT

 

MOVING PARTY: Plaintiff, Kae-soon Choe

RESPONDING PARTY: Defendant, Jun Ho Yang

 

RELIEF REQUESTED

Motion for OSC re: Contempt

 

SUMMARY OF ACTION

Plaintiff Kae-soon Choe sued her pastor, Defendant Jun Ho Yang, alleging that Yang used her confidence in him to his own advantage. Plaintiff claims that in helping her sell her apartment, Yang diverted $6.4 million of the net proceeds to his non-profit, Defendant The Christian Herald, Inc. Plaintiff alleges that she signed numerous documents presented by Yang based on his representation that they were related to the sale of her property. The documents were only in English, a language Plaintiff does not read or speak well. Plaintiff also claims that she was not provided with copies of the documents. Plaintiff brings causes of action for conversion, breach of fiduciary duty, civil damages for theft, and financial elder abuse.

 

On June 25, 2026, Plaintiff substituted in Jun Ho’s son, Sae Hyun Yang, and his daughter in law, Brooke Yang, for Does 4 and 5.

 

RULING: Granted.

Evidentiary Objections: Overruled.

The Court does not rule on Plaintiff’s objections to Jun Ho’s evidence as the evidence is irrelevant to disposition of the motion.

 

Plaintiff moves for an issuance of an order to show cause re: contempt as to Defendant Jun Ho, alleging 29 violations of the Court’s TPO prohibiting him from transferring or disposing of his personal or real property. Jun Ho opposes the motion, contending that the refinancing of the Coastline Property cannot constitute a violation of the TPO because he does not own the property and that Plaintiff cannot prove his willful disobedience of the TPO. Jun Ho further challenges certain alleged violations as falling within the TPO’s exemption for “necessities of life.” Because Plaintiff sufficiently alleges each element of her contempt charge, Plaintiff’s motion is granted and an OSC re: Contempt is set for a date and time to be determined at the hearing.    

 

A court has “inherent power to punish contempts of court.” (In re M.R. (2013) 220 Cal.App.4th 49, 57.) Where, as here, an alleged act of contempt is committed outside “the immediate view and presence of the court” (i.e., indirect contempt), “an affidavit must be presented to the court setting forth the facts constituting the contempt.” (Id. at p. 57.) The affidavit must state facts showing: “(1) the making of the order; (2) knowledge of the order; (3) ability of the respondent to render compliance; (4) willful disobedience of the order.” (Bd. of Supervisors v. Superior Court (1995) 33 Cal.App.4th 1724, 1736.) “After notice to the opposing party’s lawyer, the court (if satisfied with the sufficiency of the affidavit) must sign an order to show cause re contempt in which the date and time for a hearing are set forth.” (Cedars-Sinai Imaging Medical Group v. Superior Court (2000) 83 Cal.App.4th 1281, 1286.) “Unless the citee has concealed himself from the court, he must be personally served with the affidavit and the order to show cause; otherwise, the court lacks jurisdiction to proceed.” (Id. at pp. 1286-87.)

 

Plaintiff’s affidavit makes the requisite showing. The alleged contempt is Jun Ho’s 29 violations of the Court’s TPO, which prohibited him from “hiding, secreting, concealing, hypothecating, transferring, selling, disposing of, spending, or otherwise encumbering any of his personal or real property, except for necessities of life.” [Kirk Decl. ¶ 3, Ex. 4, p. 2.] Plaintiff itemizes the violations, which consist of transfers from Jun Ho’s alleged personal JPMorgan Chase Bank account and the refinancing of the Coastline Property. [Id. ¶¶ 13-15, Ex. 1.] The Coastline Property is subject to the TPO. [Id. ¶ 3, Ex. 4, p. 3.] Jun Ho had knowledge of the order because it was personally served on him at his home and he acknowledged in his deposition that he received it. [Id. ¶¶ 5, 7.] Jun Ho had the ability to comply because the order merely required him to not take certain action as to his property and he confirmed each of the transactions in his deposition. [Id. ¶¶ 13-15.] Plaintiff alleges the violations were willful because Jun Ho “ignored” the TPO despite acknowledging that he understood it and confirmed the transactions. [Id. ¶¶ 7, 11, 13-15.] Plaintiff thus sufficiently alleges the elements of her contempt charge. Whether Plaintiff can prove certain violations or Jun Ho’s willful disobedience of the order is not proper for consideration on the motion to set the OSC. (See Mery v. Superior Court (1937) 9 Cal.2d 379, 380 [“The settled rule is that an affidavit charging contempt is like a complaint in a criminal case and must charge facts which show that a contempt has been committed”].)

 

Jun Ho is ordered to show cause why he should not be held in contempt for the alleged 29 violations of the TPO, at a date and time to be determined at the hearing.

 

Plaintiff to personally serve her affidavit and the order to show cause on Jun Ho.
