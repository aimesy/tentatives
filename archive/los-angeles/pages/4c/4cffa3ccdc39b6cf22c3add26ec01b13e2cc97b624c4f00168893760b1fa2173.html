Case Number: 23PSCV00321    Hearing Date: September 22, 2026    Dept: G

Plaintiff Elizabeth Turriaga’s Motion for Attorney’s Fees

Respondent: Defendant General Motors LLC

TENTATIVE RULING

Plaintiff Elizabeth Turriaga’s Motion for Attorney’s Fees is GRANTED in the reduced amount $10,392.00.   

Plaintiff’s request for costs is DENIED WITHOUT PREJUDICE.

BACKGROUND

This is a Song-Beverly action arising out of the purchase of an allegedly defective motor vehicle.  On March 9, 2018, plaintiff Elizabeth Turriaga (Turriaga) bought a car manufactured by defendant General Motors LLC (GM).

On February 2, 2023, Turriaga filed the Complaint.  On June 11, 2024, Turriaga filed the operative Second Amended Complaint, alleging causes of action for (1) violation of Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (d); (2) violation of Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (b); (3) violation of Civil Code section 1793.2, subdivision (a)(3); (4) breach of the implied warranty of merchantability; and (5) fraudulent inducement by concealment.

On February 20, 2026, the parties informed the court that they settled the case.

On August 18, 2026, Turriaga filed this motion for attorney’s fees.  On September 4, 2026, GM filed the opposition, and there is no reply.

The motion is set for hearing on September 22, 2026.

REQUESTS FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

Turiaga requests that the court take judicial notice of documents of the United States District Court and the Superior Court of California.  The court may take judicial notice of the records of any federal or California court.  (See Evid. Code, § 452, subd. (d).)

Thus, the court takes judicial notice of the requested records.

EVIDENTIARY OBJECTIONS

Turriaga objects to portions of the declaration of GM’s counsel.  The following objections are OVERRULED: 1–12.

ANALYSIS

Plaintiff Turriaga moves for an award of attorney’s fees and costs in the amount of $22,005.47.  For the following reasons, the motion for attorney’s fees is GRANTED in the reduced amount of $10,392.00.  Plaintiff’s request for costs is DENIED WITHOUT PREJUDICE.

Legal Standard

“If the buyer prevails in an action under this section, the buyer shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.”  (Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).)  “[T]he negotiation of a possible settlement does not excuse the failure to proceed with due diligence in the filing of pleadings, absent some understanding that pleadings need not be filed while negotiations are in process.”  (Turley v. Turley (1967) 254 Cal.App.2d 169, 173.)  In the context of an award of attorneys’ fees “the time expended by attorneys in obtaining a reasonable fee is justifiably included in the attorneys’ fee application, and in the court’s fee award.”  (Serrano v. Unruh (1982) 32 Cal.3d 621, 631.)  “[I]t is inappropriate and an abuse of a trial court’s discretion to tie an attorney fee award to the amount of the prevailing buyer/plaintiff’s damages or recovery in a Song-Beverly Act action.”  (Hanna v. Mercedes-Benz USA, LLC (2019) 36 Cal.App.5th 493, 510.)

In assessing attorney’s fees under the Song-Beverly Act, a trial court must make an “initial determination of the actual time expended; and then to ascertain whether under all the circumstances of the case the amount of actual time expended and the monetary charge being made for the time expended are reasonable.  These circumstances may include, but are not limited to, factors such as the complexity of the case and procedural demands, the skill exhibited and the results achieved.  If the time expended or the monetary charge being made for the time expended is not reasonable under all the circumstances, then the court must take this into account and award attorney fees in a lesser amount.”  (Robertson v. Fleetwood Travel Trailers of Cal., Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 785, 817.)

“It is well established that the determination of what constitutes reasonable attorney fees is committed to the discretion of the trial court, whose decision cannot be reversed in the absence of an abuse of discretion.”  (Melnyk v. Robledo (1976) 64 Cal.App.3d 618, 623.)  The fee setting inquiry in California ordinarily “begins with the ‘lodestar’ [method], i.e., the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate.”  (Graciano v. Robinson Ford Sales, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 140, 154.)  “The reasonable hourly rate is that prevailing in the community for similar work.”  (Margolin v. Regional Planning Com. (1982) 134 Cal.App.3d 999, 1004.)  “Under the lodestar method, a party who qualifies for a fee should recover for all hours reasonably spent unless special circumstances would render an award unjust.”  (Vo v. Las Virgenes Municipal Water Dist. (2000) 79 Cal.App.4th 440, 446.)

“[A] computation of time spent on a case and the reasonable value of that time is fundamental to a determination of an appropriate attorneys’ fee award.”  (Margolin v. Regional Planning Com., supra, 134 Cal.App.3d 999, 1004.)  The lodestar figure may then be adjusted, based on consideration of factors specific to the case, in order to fix the fee at the fair market value for the legal services provided.  (See Serrano v. Priest (1977) 20 Cal.3d 25, 49, discussing factors relevant to proper attorneys’ fees award.)  Such an approach anchors the trial court’s analysis to an objective determination of the value of the attorney’s services, ensuring that the amount awarded is not arbitrary.  (Id. at 48, fn. 23.)  The factors considered in determining the modification of the lodestar include “(1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, [and] (4) the contingent nature of the fee award.”  (Mountjoy v. Bank of America (2016) 245 Cal.App.4th 266, 271.)  “[T]he burden is on the party seeking attorney fees to prove that the fees it seeks are reasonable.”  (Gorman v. Tassajara Development Corp. (2009) 178 Cal.App.4th 44, 98.)  “[A]n award of attorney fees may be based on counsel’s declarations, without production of detailed time records.”  (Raining Data Corp. v. Barrenechea (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 1363, 1375.)  “[P]aralegal fees may be awarded as attorney’s fees if the trial court deems it appropriate. . . .”  (Roe v. Halbig (2018) 29 Cal.App.5th 286, 312.)

Where a party is challenging the reasonableness of attorney’s fees as excessive that party must “attack the itemized billing[] with evidence that the fees claimed were not appropriate, or obtain the declaration of an attorney with expertise in the procedural and substantive law to demonstrate that the fees claimed were unreasonable.”  (Premier Medical Management Systems, Inc. v. Cal. Ins. Guarantee Assn. (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 550, 563–564.)  “[I]t is the burden of the challenging party to point to the specific items challenged, with a sufficient argument and citations to the evidence and arguments that fees claimed are excessive, duplicative, or unrelated do not suffice.”  (Id. at 564.)  “General arguments that fees claimed are excessive, duplicative, or unrelated do not suffice.”  (Ibid.)

A “court should defer to the winning lawyer’s professional judgment as to the tasks completed in an action because he won, and might not have, had he been more of a slacker.”  (Moreno v. City of Sacramento (9th Cir. 2008) 534 F.3d 1106, 1111.)  A losing party cannot litigate tenaciously then be heard to complain about the time spent or tasks performed by the prevailing party in response.  (City of Riverside v. Rivera (1986) 477 U.S. 561, 580, fn. 11.)  Where a defendant does not produce evidence contradicting the reasonableness of counsel’s hourly rates, the court will deem an attorney’s hourly rate as reasonable.  (Goglin v. BMW of North America, LLC (2016) 4 Cal.App.5th 462, 473.)

Discussion

As the prevailing party, the court determines that Turriaga is entitled to attorney’s fees and costs.  To decide the award, the court considers the reasonableness of the claimed hours, the reasonableness of the rates charged, whether to apply a lodestar multiplier, and the amount of costs and expenses.

Reasonableness of the Claimed Hours

The Song-Beverly Act provides for the recovery of “costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer.”  (Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).)  A verified fee bill is “prima facie evidence the costs, expenses and services listed were necessarily incurred.”  (Hadley v. Krepel (1985) 167 Cal.App.3d 677, 682.)  However, counsel bears the burden to demonstrate the reasonableness of charges.  (Mikhaeilpoor v. BMW of North America, LLC (2020) 48 Cal.App.5th 240, 247.)  Fees should be denied where the court finds the tasks unreasonable or unnecessary since “padding in the form of inefficient or duplicative efforts is not subject to compensation.”  (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132.)

Turriaga’s counsel claims 29.7 hours of legal work.  (See Mot., pp. 2, 5, 7; see also Shahian Decl., Exh. 1, p. 2.)  The verified fee bill is prima facie evidence of the legal services necessarily incurred.  (See Shahian Decl., Exh. 1; see also Hadley, supra, 167 Cal.App.3d at 682.)  To determine the reasonableness of the claimed hours, the court considers the arguments from GM’s opposition. 

The court categorizes the challenged billing entries with which GM takes issue as follows: (1) excessive time for work using templates, (2) overstaffing and inefficient litigation, and (3) anticipated time.  (See Opp., pp. 7–9, 11–12.)

Excessive Time for Work Using Templates

GM argues a reduction is required in the attorney’s fee award for the “unreasonable” time used on counsel’s “litigation-by template strategy.”  (Opp., p. 8.)  The court agrees.

The court does not award attorney’s fees if the time billed is excessive for the type of work.  (See Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d); see also Premier Medical Management Systems, Inc., supra, 163 Cal.App.4th at 564.)  The court may reduce an attorney’s fee award if “plaintiff’s counsel failed to act efficiently.”  (Mikhaeilpoor, supra, 48 Cal.App.5th at 256.)

The court reviewed the specific entries GM challenged as unreasonable and found that Turriaga’s counsel spent excessive time drafting the Complaint, the meet and confer letter, the discovery requests, the discovery responses, and this motion.  Given that the pleadings were based on templates, and this case did not present novel or complex issues, the court finds the time billed to be excessive.  (See Opp., pp. 7–9, referencing Shahian Decl., Exh. 1; see also Quezada Decl., Exhs. A–J.) 

Counsel billed 1.3 hours to draft the Complaint, but the court finds counsel should have drafted the Complaint in 1.0 hour.  Counsel billed 1.2 hours to draft the meet and confer letter, but the court finds counsel should have drafted the letter in 0.5 hours.  Counsel billed 2.0 hours to draft the discovery requests, but the court finds counsel should have drafted the requests in 0.5 hours.  Counsel billed 2.0 hours to draft the discovery responses, but the court finds counsel should have drafted the responses in 0.5 hours.  Counsel billed 1.8 hours to draft this motion, but the court finds counsel should have drafted the motion in 1.0 hour.

Thus, the court reduces the award of attorney’s fees for excessive time by 4.8 hours.  (0.3 hours from Mark Gibson, 0.7 hours from Nino Sanaia, 1.5 hours from Shelby Clark, 1.5 hours from James Carroll, and 0.8 hours from Angel Baker.)

Overstaffing and Inefficient Litigation

GM argues a reduction is required in the attorney’s fee award for “non-compensable inefficiencies [which] resulted from the excessive number of billing attorneys.”  (Opp., p. 11, capitalization changed.)  The court disagrees.

The court may reduce an attorney’s fee award if “plaintiff’s counsel failed to act efficiently.”  (Mikhaeilpoor, supra, 48 Cal.App.5th at 256.)  “In evaluating whether the attorney fee request is reasonable, the trial court should consider whether the case was overstaffed, how much time the attorneys spent on particular claims, and whether the hours were reasonably expended.”  (Morris v. Hyundai Motor America (2019) 41 Cal.App.5th 24, 38, cleaned up.)  Additionally, the court does not award attorney’s fees for work that is unnecessarily duplicative.  (See Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d); see also Premier Medical Management Systems, Inc., supra, 163 Cal.App.4th at 564.)

The court reviewed the specific entry GM challenged as unreasonable but found that the preparation for and attendance at the final status conference was necessary and not excessive.  Moreover, while fifteen (15) timekeepers billed in connection with this action, there are no demonstrated inefficiencies caused by purported overstaffing, except for the excessive billing previously discussed.

Thus, the court does not reduce the award of attorney’s fees for overstaffing.

Anticipated Time

GM argues a reduction is required in the attorney’s fee award for the fee motion because the “anticipated” fees are excessive.  (Opp., pp. 8–9.)  The court agrees, but notes that Plaintiff failed to specify the number of “anticipated” hours.

The court must base its award of attorney’s fees on “actual time expended.”  (Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).)  The plain language of Section 1794, subdivision (d) necessarily precludes an award based on speculative fees.

The motion anticipates an unspecified number of hours in connection with reviewing the opposition, drafting the reply, and attending the hearing.  (See Mot., Ibrahim Decl., Exh. B.)  Anticipated fees are speculative and not recoverable as “actual time expended.”  (Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).)

Thus, the court reduces the award of attorney’s fees for anticipated time since Plaintiff did not specify a number of anticipated hours.  Counsel claimed $1,500.00 for anticipated time, which is not compensable.

Therefore, Plaintiff substantiated 24.9 of the 29.7 hours claimed by counsel in this litigation.

Reasonableness of Counsel’s Rates

The court finds the rates charged by some of Turriaga’s counsel are unreasonable.  The trial court is the ultimate arbiter of what rates are reasonable.  (Warren v. Kia Motors America, Inc. (2018) 30 Cal.App.5th 24, 36, stating, “experienced trial judges are the best judges of the value of professional services rendered in their courts.”)  To determine if an hourly rate is reasonable, courts consider the rates of similar attorneys in the community as well as “the experience, skill, and reputation of the attorney requesting fees.”  (Heritage Pacific Financial, LLC v. Monroy (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 972, 1009.)

Therefore, adjusting counsel’s rates for reasonableness, the appropriate award of attorney’s fees is $10,392.00 before applying any multiplier.

Lodestar Multiplier

The factors that courts look at to determine if a multiplier is reasonable are: “(1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, [and] (4) the contingent nature of the fee award.”  (Ketchum, supra, 24 Cal.4th at 1132.)  “[T]he purpose of a fee enhancement is primarily to compensate the attorney for the prevailing party at a rate reflecting the risk of nonpayment in contingency cases.”  (Id. at 1138.)  When a case is not complicated, “the request for a multiplier [is] specious.”  (Mikhaeilpoor, supra, 48 Cal.App.5th at 255.)

Here, Plaintiff requests a lodestar multiplier of 1.35.  (See Mot., pp. 8–9i.)  To justify the multiplier, Plaintiff cites the “excellent outcome,” “substantial risks posed by this litigation,” and “delay of payment.”  (Mot., pp. 8–9, paraphrased.)  However, the other Ketchum factors do not support applying a multiplier.  The court determines that the case was a relatively straightforward Song-Beverly action, counsel provided adequate and appropriate representation, and counsel were not precluded from other employment.

Therefore, the court declines to apply a lodestar multiplier to the award of reasonable attorney’s fees of $10,392.00.

Costs and Expenses

A prevailing plaintiff has a right to recover all costs and expenses that were reasonably incurred in prosecuting the case.  (Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).)  Allowable costs must be reasonably necessary to conduct the litigation and reasonable in amount.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1033.5, subds. (c)(2)–(c)(3).)

Plaintiff’s counsel claims $1,010.80 in costs and expenses.  (See Mot., pp. 2, 10; see also Shahian Decl., Exh. 1, p. 3.)  As a preliminary matter, the court notes that Turriaga did not provide a memorandum of costs.  A memorandum of costs is not due under California Rules of Court, rule 3.1700(a)(1) until after a notice of entry of judgment or dismissal is served.  (See Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1700(a)(1).)  Although not required before notice of entry of judgment or dismissal, a memorandum of costs is useful to the court to determine whether the costs requested are categorically allowed by Code of Civil Procedure section 1033.5.  Additionally, Turriaga’s counsel does not provide invoices that allow the court to determine that the costs claimed were actually incurred.

Therefore, the court cannot yet determine whether Turriaga is entitled to the costs claimed.

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s request for costs is DENIED WITHOUT PREJUDICE since Plaintiff failed to submit (1) a form MC-010 memorandum of costs and (2) the invoices for her claimed costs.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the motion for attorney’s fees is GRANTED.  Plaintiff is awarded attorney’s fees in the reduced amount of $10,392.00.   

Plaintiff’s request for costs is DENIED WITHOUT PREJUDICE.

Case Number: 25PSCV03241    Hearing Date: September 22, 2026    Dept: G

Plaintiff/Cross-Defendant Jihong Guo and Cross-Defendants Shaoxiong Zheng and Caixing Ma’s Special Motion to Strike Defendant/Cross-Complainant Yuhang Li’s Cross-Complaint Pursuant to the Anti-SLAPP Statute

Respondent: Defendant/Cross-Complainant Yuhang Li

TENTATIVE RULING

Plaintiff/Cross-Defendant Jihong Guo and Cross-Defendants Shaoxiong Zheng and Caixing Ma’s Special Motion to Strike Defendant/Cross-Complainant Yuhang Li’s Cross-Complaint Pursuant to the Anti-SLAPP Statute is GRANTED IN PART and DENIED IN PART.

BACKGROUND

This is an action for quiet title.  Plaintiff/cross-defendant Jihong Guo (Guo) and defendant/cross-complainant Yuhang Li (Li) allegedly both reside in China.  In October 2022, Guo allegedly purchased residential real property in La Puente, California from Li.  Guo allegedly requested Li to hold legal title in the real property in trust for her until she can move to the United States, and Li allegedly orally agreed.  Guo allegedly used the property for living accommodations for her company’s employees.  In August 2025, Li allegedly contacted the residents of the property representing himself as the owner and allegedly requested that they vacate the property to allow him to rent it out to a tenant.

On September 8, 2025, Guo filed the Complaint in this action, alleging causes of action for (1) quiet title, (2) constructive trust, and (3) declaratory relief.

On May 19, 2026, Li filed a Complaint in an unlawful detainer action (case no. 26WCUD00839).

On November 25, 2025, the court entered default against Li.  On May 13, 2026, the court issued a default judgment against Li.  On June 9, 2026, the court appointed cross-defendant Caixing Ma (Ma) as elisor to enforce the default judgment (Ma was not a party in this action at the time of appointment; Ma was solely Guo’s counsel).  On July 23, 2026, the court set aside the entry of default and vacated the default judgment.

On July 24, 2026, the court consolidated this action and the unlawful detainer action.

On August 3, 2026, Li filed a Cross-Complaint, alleging causes of action for (1) fraud (upon the court), (2) fraud (by intentional misrepresentation), (3) fraudulent concealment, (4) promissory fraud, (5) abuse of process, (6) intentional infliction of emotional distress, (7) fraud (by misuse of personal information), (8) breach of contract, (9) common counts, (10) cancellation of written instruments, (11) quiet title, (12) declaratory relief, (13) constructive trust, (14) permanent injunction, and (15) abuse of authority.

On August 28, 2026, Guo and Ma (collectively, the Movants) filed this special motion to strike pursuant to the anti-SLAPP statute.  On September 9, 2026, Li filed the opposition, and on September 15, 2026, the Movants filed the reply.

The motion is set for hearing on September 22, 2026.

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

Li requests that the court take judicial notice of documents filed in the United States District Court and the Superior Court of California.  The court may take judicial notice of the records of any federal or California court.  (See Evid. Code, § 452, subd. (d).) 

Thus, the court takes judicial notice of the requested records.

EVIDENTIARY OBJECTIONS

Li objects to portions of the declaration of the Movants’ counsel.  The following objections are OVERRULED: 1–6.

The Movants object to Li’s requests for judicial notice.  The following objections are OVERRULED: 1–10.

The Movants also object to portions of Li’s declaration.  The following objection is SUSTAINED: 4 (lacks foundation and authentication).  The following objections are OVERRULED: 1–3, 5–39.

The Movants finally object to portions of the declaration of Li’s counsel.  The following objections are OVERRULED: 1–9.

ANALYSIS

The Movants seek to strike the first, fifth, sixth, and fifteenth causes of action of the Cross-Complaint pursuant to the anti-SLAPP statute.  For the following reasons, the motion is GRANTED IN PART and DENIED IN PART.

Legal Standard

California’s anti-SLAPP statute “is designed to protect defendants from meritless lawsuits that might chill the exercise of their rights to speak and petition on matters of public concern.  To that end, the statute authorizes a special motion to strike claims arising from any act of that person in furtherance of the person’s right of petition or free speech under the United States Constitution or the California Constitution in connection with a public issue.”  (Wilson v. Cable News Network, Inc. (2019) 7 Cal.5th 871, 883–884, quoting Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (b), cleaned up.)  “The special motion may be filed within 60 days of the service of the complaint or, in the court’s discretion, at any later time upon terms it deems proper.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (f).)  “Anti-SLAPP motions are evaluated through a two-step process.  Initially, the moving defendant bears the burden of establishing that the challenged allegations or claims arise from protected activity in which the defendant has engaged.  If the defendant carries its burden, the plaintiff must then demonstrate its claims have at least minimal merit.”  (Park v. Bd. of Trustees of Cal. State Univ. (2017) 2 Cal.5th 1057, 1061, cleaned up.)

Discussion

Moving Party’s Burden: Allegations or Claims Arise from Protected Activity

The Movants argue that the Cross-Complaint’s first, fifth, sixth, and fifteenth causes of action arise from statements made before a judicial proceeding and in connection with an issue under consideration by a judicial body.  The court agrees.

The moving defendant bears the initial burden of establishing that the challenged claims arise from protected activity in which the defendant has engaged.  (See Park, supra, 2 Cal.5th at 1061.)  The Anti-SLAPP Statute defines this protected activity as “(1) any written or oral statement or writing made before a legislative, executive, or judicial proceeding, or any other official proceeding authorized by law, (2) any written or oral statement or writing made in connection with an issue under consideration or review by a legislative, executive, or judicial body, or any other official proceeding authorized by law, (3) any written or oral statement or writing made in a place open to the public or a public forum in connection with an issue of public interest, or (4) any other conduct in furtherance of the exercise of the constitutional right of petition or the constitutional right of free speech in connection with a public issue or an issue of public interest.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 425.16, subd. (e).)

The first cause of action in the Cross-Complaint alleges that the cross-defendants “made, authorized, adopted, or caused to be made false representations and concealed material facts from the Superior Court. . . .”  (XC, ¶ 86.)  The fifth cause of action alleges that the cross-defendants “misused judicial process for improper purposes . . . through false representations, concealment, and other wrongful conduct. . . .”  (XC, ¶¶ 113–114.)  The sixth cause of action alleges that the cross-defendants “misus[ed] judicial proceedings to obtain title in the [s]ubject [p]roperty.”  (XC, ¶ 118.)  The fifteenth cause of action alleges that the court appointed Ma as elisor and that Ma, acting as the elisor, “contact[ed] real estate agents, . . . marketing the [s]ubject [p]roperty for sale on the open market, negotiating a proposed sales price and terms, and otherwise acting beyond the authority conferred by the [c]ourt. . . .”  (XC, ¶ 157; see XC, ¶ 156.)  Thus, the court determines that the allegations asserted as the grounds for the first, fifth, sixth, and fifteenth causes of action arise from statements made before a judicial proceeding or in connection with an issue under consideration by a judicial body.

Since the Movants demonstrate that the claims arise from protected activity, the burden shifts to Li to establish a probability of prevailing.  (See Park, supra, 2 Cal.5th at 1061.)

Opposing Party’s Burden: Probability of Prevailing

Li argues that he has a probability of prevailing on the Cross-Complaint’s first, fifth, sixth, and fifteenth causes of action.  The court agrees in part and disagrees in part.

A probability of prevailing means “a reasonable probability of prevailing, not prevailing by a preponderance of the evidence.”  (Gerbosi v. Gaims, Weil, West & Epstein, LLP (2011) 193 Cal.App.4th 435, 444.)  To establish a probability of prevailing, “the plaintiff must demonstrate that the [complaint] is legally sufficient and supported by a prima facie showing of facts to support a favorable judgment if the evidence submitted by the plaintiff is accepted.”  (Kenne v. Stennis (2014) 230 Cal.App.4th 953, 962.)  The plaintiff’s burden of establishing a probability of prevailing is not high, only requiring a showing of “minimal merit.”  (Park, supra, 2 Cal.5th at 1061; see Overstock.com, Inc. v. Gradient Analytics, Inc. (2007) 151 Cal.App.4th 688, 700.)  The court does not weigh credibility, nor does it evaluate the weight of the evidence; instead, it accepts as true all evidence favorable to the plaintiff and assesses the defendant’s evidence only to determine if it defeats the plaintiff’s submission as a matter of law.  (See Overstock.com, Inc., supra, at 699–700; see also Gerbosi, supra, at 444.)  A defendant’s evidence that a plaintiff’s claims are barred may defeat the plaintiff’s submission as a matter of law.  (See Gerbosi, supra, at 447, finding that a defendant’s evidence that the plaintiff’s claims were time-barred meant that the plaintiff could not prevail on those causes of action.)

Regarding the first, fifth, and sixth causes of action, according to Li’s declaration, the Movants’ representations to the court were knowingly untruthful or made with disregard for the truth when they obtained Li’s default and a default judgment.  Even though the Movants knew, or had reason to know, that Li was in China and possessed his personal contact information, the Movants misrepresented to the court that they personally served Li in the United States and unsuccessfully attempted to contact him concerning the litigation.  (See Li Decl., ¶¶ 7–14, 24–26.) 

Additionally, Li asserts in his declaration that Ma represented him in previous legal matters and learned confidential information though the representation.  (See Li Decl., ¶¶ 7–14, 24–26.)  These assertions raise issues regarding whether Ma breached the duties he owes and owed to current and former clients under the California Rules of Professional Conduct.  (See Rules Prof. Conduct, rules 1.7(a), 1.9(a), 1.9(c).)  Thus, the first, fifth, and sixth causes of action have at least minimal merit.

Regarding the fifteenth cause of action, according to Li’s testimonial and documentary evidence, Ma listed the subject property for sale through his wife.  (See Li Decl., ¶ 37; see also Li Decl., Attach. I, pp. 82–83.)  Thus, the fifteenth cause of action has at least minimal merit.

Therefore, the court finds that Li has a probability of prevailing.  However, the court must also consider whether the defendant’s submission nevertheless defeats the plaintiff’s submission as a matter of law.  (See Gerbosi, supra, 193 Cal.App.4th at 444.)

The Movants argue that the claims in the Cross-Complaint’s first, fifth, and sixth causes of action are subject to the litigation privilege.  The court agrees.

The litigation privilege applies to a communication made in a judicial proceeding or with some relation to a judicial proceeding.  (See Civ. Code, § 47, subd. (b)(2); see also Rubin v. Green (1993) 4 Cal.4th 1187, 1193, holding that “communications with ‘some relation’ to judicial proceedings” are “absolutely immune from tort liability.”)  “[T]he key in determining whether the privilege applies is whether the injury allegedly resulted from an act that was communicative in its essential nature.”  (Rubin, supra, at 1196.)  The privilege immunizes liability from “all torts except malicious prosecution” and “has been applied specifically in the context of abuse of process claims alleging the filing of false or perjurious testimony or declarations.”  (Rusheen v. Cohen (2006) 37 Cal.4th 1048, 1057–1058.)

Here, even though Li argues that the first, fifth, and sixth causes of action are not solely based on communicative conduct, the allegations in the Cross-Complaint and the statements made in Li’s declaration demonstrate that the claims are based on the Movants’ representations to the court in obtaining Li’s default and a default judgment.  (See XC, ¶¶ 86, 113–114, 118, 156–157; see also Li Decl., ¶¶ 14, 26.)  Thus, the litigation privilege immunizes the Movants from tort liability for their representations to the court.

Therefore, Li demonstrates a probability of prevailing on the fifteenth cause of action but fails to establish a probability of prevailing on the first, fifth, and sixth causes of action.

Accordingly, the special motion to strike is GRANTED IN PART as to the first, fifth, and sixth causes of action as to Guo and Ma and DENIED IN PART as to the fifteenth cause of action.  Furthermore, since Li discussed the possibility of counsel’s disqualification, the court will SET an ORDER TO SHOW CAUSE RE: DISQUALIFICATION OF COUNSEL.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the special motion to strike pursuant to the anti-SLAPP statute is GRANTED IN PART as to the first, fifth, and sixth causes of action of the Cross-Complaint against plaintiff/cross-defendant Jihong Guo and cross-defendant Caixing Ma and DENIED IN PART as to the fifteenth cause of action.

An ORDER TO SHOW CAUSE RE: DISQUALIFICATION OF COUNSEL is SET for a date to be determined at the hearing in Department G (Pomona).
