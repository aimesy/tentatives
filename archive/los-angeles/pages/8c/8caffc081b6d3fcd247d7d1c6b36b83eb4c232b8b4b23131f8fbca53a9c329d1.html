[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: MOTION FOR JUDGMENT ON THE PLEADINGS

 

Respondent County of Los Angeles Department of Public Social Services (“Respondent”) moves for judgment on the pleadings against petitioner David George Karkour (“Petitioner”).

The motion for judgment on the pleadings is granted.

I.                Background

1.               Factual Allegations

The petition alleges in pertinent part as follows:

Petitioner applied for General Relief cash aid from Respondent. (Pet. at 4.) [1] On December 23, 2024, Respondent notified Petitioner it was denying his application for General Relief because Petitioner failed to provide copies of bank or credit union statements, because his Social Security benefits exceeded the General Relief income limit, and because the total value of his personal property exceeded the General Relief personal property limit. (Pet. at 4; see also Pet. at 2-3.) Petitioner alleges he in danger due to exceptional circumstances and requires General Relief cash aid. (Pet. at 1.)

2.               Procedural History

On January 15, 2025, Petitioner filed his petition in the South District (Long Beach Courthouse). A proof of service filed January 22, 2025 shows Petitioner served the summons and petition on Respondent by personal service on January 21, 2025. On March 12, 2025, Respondent filed its answer generally denying the petition. On March 21, 2025, Petitioner filed a reply to Respondent’s answer.

On May 15, 2025, Department S25 granted Respondent’s motion to transfer and ordered the case transferred to Department 1 of Stanley Mosk Courthouse. On July 1, 2025, the same Department denied Petitioner’s motion to reconsider that order.

On December 17, 2025, Department 1 reassigned the case to Judge Stephen Goorvitch in Department 82 of Stanley Mosk Courthouse.

On February 26, 2026, Respondent filed the instant Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings (“MJOP”), originally noticed to be heard in Department 82 (now Department 833) on April 17, 2026.[2] On March 16, 2026, Petitioner filed an opposition. On March 24, 2026, Respondent filed a reply.

On April 21, 2026, the case was reassigned to Judge Joseph Lipner in Department 82 (833), and on May 6, 2026, that Department granted Petitioner’s Code of Civil Procedure (“CCP”) section 170.6 challenge and reassigned the case to Department 834.

As the matter is fully briefed, this Court re-calendared the MJOP hearing for July 2, 2026 at 9:30 a.m.

II.             Legal Standard

An MJOP has the same function as a general demurrer, but it is made after the time for demurrer has expired. (See, e.g., Lance Camper Mfg. Corp. v. Republic Indemnity Co. of America (1996) 44 Cal.App.4th 194, 198 [“Lance”]; Weil & Brown, Civil Proceedings Before Trial (1998) § 7:275.)

The rules governing demurrers apply to statutory MJOPs except as provided by CCP section 438. (Cloud v. Northrop Grumman Corp. (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 995, 999 [“Cloud”]; Lance at 198.) An MJOP by a defendant can be made on the ground that (1) the court lacks jurisdiction of the subject of one or more of the causes of action alleged or (2) the complaint (or any cause of action) does not state facts sufficient to state a cause of action. (CCP § 438(c).)

An MJOP performs the same function as a general demurrer, and thus attacks only defects disclosed on the face of the pleadings or by matters that can be judicially noticed. (See, e.g., Weil & Brown, Civil Procedure Before Trial (1998) §§ 7:275, 7:322; Lance, supra, 44 Cal.App.4th at 198.) Presentation of extrinsic evidence is therefore not proper on an MJOP. (Cloud, supra, 67 Cal.App.4th at 999.) However, on an MJOP, a court may take judicial notice of something that cannot reasonably be controverted, even if it negates an express allegation of the pleading. (See Columbia Casualty Co. v. Northwestern Nat. Ins. Co. (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 457; Evans v. California Trailer Court, Inc. (1994) 28 Cal.App.4th 540, 549.)

Both a demurrer and MJOP accept as true all material factual allegations of the challenged pleading, unless contrary to law or to facts of which a court may take judicial notice. The sole issue is whether the complaint, as it stands, states a cause of action as a matter of law. (Mechanical Contractors Assn. v. Greater Bay Area Assn. (1998) 66 Cal.App.4th 672, 677; Edwards v. Centex Real Estate Corp. (1997) 53 Cal.App.4th 15, 27.)

In addition to statutory grounds, an MJOP may be made under the common law at any time either prior to or at the trial itself. (See Stoops v. Abbassi (2002) 100 Cal.App.4th 644, 650; Weil & Brown, Civil Proceedings Before Trial (2015) §7:277.)

III.           Request for Judicial Notice

Respondent seeks judicial notice of the following exhibits (1) Los Angeles County Code of Ordinances (“LACC”) Chapter 2.102 – Department of Public Social Services (RJN Ex. 2); and (2) Department of Public Social Services Policy (“Policy”) 44-301 0 Aid Payments (RJN Ex. 3). Petitioner objects on the grounds that the local policies should not control and are offered to show a “disputed legal and factual conclusion.” This argument applies to the merits of the motion, not whether the material is judicially noticeable. The requests are for legislative and regulatory enactments subject to judicial notice and, therefore, are granted. (Evid. Code § 452(b).)

IV.           Analysis

Respondent moves for judgment on the pleadings on the grounds that the petition fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. Respondent asserts that, accepting the allegations as true, Petitioner cannot establish eligibility for General Relief as a matter of law.

As Respondent argues, under LACC Chapter 2.102, the amount Respondent may grant in General Relief is set by the General Relief Basic Budget Table (“Basic Budget Table”) as adopted by the Los Angeles County (“County”) Board of Supervisors. (LACC § 2.102.180.) The amount established in the Basic Budget Table must be reduced by the amount of a recipient’s income, expressly including Social Security benefits. (LACC § 2.102.100(A); Policy 44-101.) According to the Basic Budget Table, the maximum General Relief grant for a household of one individual is $221. (Policy 44-301.) Thus, based on the facts alleged, Petitioners undisputed $1,589 monthly Social Security benefit (Pet. at 2-3) fully offsets any potential General Relief grant and renders him ineligible. (LACC §§ 2.102.100(A), 2.102.180; Policies 44-101, 44-301.)

In opposition, Petitioner makes three arguments.

First, Petitioner argues that Welfare and Institutions Code (“WIC”) section 17000 overrides local policies and provides an absolute requirement to “relieve and support all incompetent, poor, indigent persons.” (WIC § 17000.) Petitioner cites Mooney v. Pickett (1971) 4 Cal.3d 669 (“Mooney”) to assert that “a county cannot use rigid regulations to arbitrarily exclude people from relief.” (Opp. at 3.)

In Mooney, the California Supreme Court struck down a San Mateo County General Assistance eligibility requirement categorically excluding “employable single men.” (Mooney at 671.) The Court acknowledged that WIC section 17001 authorizes a county’s board of supervisors to adopt standards for eligibility, but held that standards must be consistent with and not in conflict with WIC section 17000. (Id. at 678-79.) An “employable single man” who is nevertheless unable to find work, the Mooney court reasoned, “does not differ in economic resources from the man whose unemployment stems from more personal disabilities.” (Id. at 680.) Therefore, the Court determined the employable single man requirement was in conflict with WIC section 17000’s purpose of providing support and relief to the indigent. (Id. at 681.)

Here, the income eligibility requirement directly measures the potential recipient’s economic resources. It is not an arbitrary categorial exclusion of the type invalidated in Mooney. Therefore, it does not conflict with WIC section 17000, and falls squarely within the County’s authority under WIC section 17001 to establish standards for support.

Second, Petitioner argues that, under Cooper v. Swoap (1974) 11 Cal.3d 856 (“Cooper”), Petitioner’s income is not “actually available” because it is exhausted by his medical and safety expenses. (Opp. at 3.) This contention misapplies Cooper. In Cooper, the California Supreme Court evaluated whether and by what measure a “noncash economic benefit” may be treated as income for the purposes of calculating benefit payments. (Cooper at 858-860.) No such imputed income is at issue here. Petitioner receives actual Social Security cash benefits (Pet. at 2-3), and Cooper does not permit exclusion of such income based on how a recipient needs or chooses to spend it.  Accordingly, Cooper is inapplicable.

Third, Petitioner argues that the provision in article I, section 1 of the California Constitution providing “inalienable rights” including “pursuing and obtaining safety” requires Respondent to grant him General Relief cash aid. (Opp. at 3.) Petitioner further asserts that the Equal Protection provisions in article I, section 7 of the California Constitution bar a flat income threshold for the General Relief program. (Opp. at 3.) Petitioner cites Goldberg v. Kelly (1970) 397 U.S. 254 (“Goldberg”) for the proposition that “public assistance is a property interest vital to a recipient’s very survival, and its denial must meet constitutional standards of fairness that account for the individual’s actual circumstances.” (Opp. at 3.)

As an initial matter, Goldberg does not address the standards a public entity may set for benefit eligibility. Rather, Goldberg concerns procedural due process; namely, the requirement that a welfare recipient be afforded a meaningful opportunity to be heard before termination of benefits. (Goldberg at 268-70.) It does not limit a public entity’s authority to define eligibility criteria in the first instance.

More generally, Petitioner provides no legal authority or explanation showing how the cited portions of the California Constitution compel Respondent to provide General Relief cash aid notwithstanding statutory and regulatory income limits. Unsupported references to constitutional concepts such as “safety” and “equal protection,” without a developed legal theory or authority, are insufficient to state a cause of action.

Accordingly, the petition does not allege facts establishing legal entitlement to General Relief benefits and therefore fails to state a cause of action.

V.              Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, the motion for judgment on the pleadings is GRANTED without leave to amend.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: APPLICATION FOR WRIT OF ATTACHMENT

 

Defendant and cross-complainant David Shield Security, Inc. (“Defendant”) seeks a writ of attachment against plaintiff and cross-defendant Socially Oriented United Living, Inc. (“Plaintiff”).

The application is granted.

I.                Background

1.     Factual Background

Defendant is registered and licensed by the California Bureau of Security and Investigative Services to provide security services and provides trained security officers at facilities around the Los Angeles area. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 3.)

In September 2024, Plaintiff and Defendant executed a Security Services Agreement (“Agreement”). (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 6, Ex. A.) The Agreement provided for Defendant to provide security officers at Plaintiff’s Hyland facility. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 6.) The Agreement further provided that the Hyland facility would be the first facility for which Defendant would provide services and staffing, with the “mutual intention” that Defendant would later provide security services at other Plaintiff facilities. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 6.) Under the Agreement, the parties agreed to a three-year service period to terminate September 22, 2027, after which the Agreement would automatically renew for one-year terms. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 7.)

Any party could terminate the Agreement with a 30-day notice. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 7.) For Plaintiff to terminate with less than the required 30-day notice, Plaintiff must provide a written notice of claimed performance deficiencies. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 7.) Defendant would then have a 15-day opportunity to cure the deficiencies. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 7.) If Defendant were to cure the deficiencies, the Agreement would remain in force. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 7.) Otherwise, Plaintiff could then immediately terminate the Agreement. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 7.)

Plaintiff agreed to pay $55 hourly for Armed Shift Supervisors, $50 hourly for Armed officers, and $38 hourly for Unarmed Officers. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 8.) All security officers would be billed for a minimum of eight hours, and would receive meal and rest breaks. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 8.) Defendant agreed to invoice Plaintiff every 14 days, and Plaintiff agreed to pay invoices within 15 days. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 8.) Defendant was responsible under the Agreement for hiring, training, and licensing obligations for guards at Plaintiff’s facilities, including determining staffing levels. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 9.)

Starting September 23, 2024, Defendant provided security officers and services to Plaintiff. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 5.) Eitan Bazaz (“Bazaz”), President of Defendant, was Defendant’s primary contact for Plaintiff’s upper management, consisting of President Yigal Keren (“Keren”), Chief Executive Officer Eric Schames (“Schames”), and Chief Operations Officer Casey Reinholtz (“Reinholtz”). (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 5.) Bazaz primarily spoke with Reinholtz when discussing facility operations, staffing levels, officer assignments, guard performances, Defendant’s practices, policies, and procedures, and issues related to facility staff and tenants, including complaints and investigations. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 5.)

Defendant continued to provide security services until March 2025. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 10.) Within 3 months, Plaintiff increased the number of facilities for which Defendant provided services from three to 16. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 10.) Ultimately, Defendant provided security services 24 hours daily, seven days weekly, through over 130 trained guards logging a total of 4,600 hours weekly. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 10.)

Defendant received complaints about guards sleeping during the service term. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 15.) Defendant characterizes those complaints as isolated, and most of the complaints as unsubstantiated. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 15.) Defendant investigated complaints that guards were sleeping on shift and determined in most cases that the guards were on break, not while on duty. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 16.) Defendant argues that all but one of Plaintiff’s facilities lacked any designated break location, and therefore Plaintiff staff and residents were unaware that sleeping guards were on break. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 17.)

Defendant also received complaints about guards using cell phones while on duty. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 18.) Defendant contends the guards were using their cell phones for work-related purposes, including documenting violations and other situations affecting the safety of tenants, staff, and guards, as well as internal communications. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 18.)

Defendant investigated any complaints Plaintiff reported and communicated its findings to Plaintiff. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 19.) Where Defendant found the complaints substantiated, it administered discipline. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 19.) Plaintiff expressed no dissatisfaction with Defendant’s complaint resolution and did not notify Defendant that it considered Defendant’s performance was deficient. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 20.)

Plaintiff contends the complaints were not isolated, and that in at least some instances the complaints were substantiated. (Reinholtz Decl., ¶¶ 12, 16, Ex. 1.) The Gardena guards in particular were uncooperative and Reinholz was frustrated by Defendant’s failure to correct the issues. (Reinholtz Decl., ¶ 13, Ex. 2.) Plaintiff conducted a review of video footage and discovered guards engaging in unauthorized cell phone use, sleeping while on duty, arriving late or failing to arrive for shifts, and otherwise failing to perform duties for a combined 379.5 hours. (Reinholtz Decl., ¶ 22.)

In January 2025, Plaintiff sent Defendant a 30-day Performance Review letter. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 21, Ex. B.) Bazaz then spoke with Reinholtz, who stated the letter pertained only to the Gardena facility. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 22.) Reinholtz stated performance at all other facilities except Hyland and 801 Hoover was satisfactory, and did not specify what was unsatisfactory at Hyland and 801 Hoover. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 22.) Defendant replaced the Gardena facility guard team, provided specific training to the new team relating to the issues Plaintiff raised, reviewed onsite protocols and operations, and submitted written recommendations to enhance security. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 22.) However, Plaintiff asserts that Defendant did not submit an action plan as requested, and performance did not improve. (Reinholtz Decl., ¶ 18.)

Around that time, Defendant contends Plaintiff informed Defendant that it would contract with a new security services provider unless Defendant agreed to lower its hourly rate and take on additional tasks relating to tenant care, including administering medication. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 23.)

Plaintiff contends it never asked Defendant guards to administer medication to tenants and were rather directed never to administer medication. (Reinholtz Decl., ¶¶ 14-15, Ex. 3.)

On February 5, 2025, Plaintiff removed Defendant from the Gardena facility on the basis that a guard used unjustified excessing force against a tenant. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 24.) Defendant characterizes the event as a struggle where a guard fell while attempting to escort an intoxicated, belligerent tenant of the facility. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 24.) Plaintiff contends Defendant fell while dragging the tenant out of the facility, and then while in full control of the tenant, slammed the tenant to the ground. (Reinholtz Decl., ¶ 19.)

On February 14, Plaintiff issued a notice terminating Defendant’s services at Gardena and phasing out its services at two other facilities. (Reinholtz Decl., ¶ 21.) Plaintiff contends Defendant agreed to amend to the Agreement to that effect, though Plaintiff cannot locate a fully-executed amendment. (Reinholtz Decl., ¶ 21, Ex. 4.) Thereafter, Plaintiff asserts Defendant overstaffed the remaining facilities and allowed unauthorized overtime shifts. (Reinholtz Decl., ¶ 23.)

On March 19, 2025, Defendant received a Notice of Termination from Plaintiff, effective 4pm that day. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 28; Safyan Decl., Ex. 5.) The notice stated Defendant had not cured deficiencies in guard performance. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 28, Ex. G.)

Defendant contends Plaintiff failed to pay six invoices, nos. 9958, 9860, 9966, 9983, and 10157, for which it owes Defendant $1,326,228.34. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 36, Exs. C-E, H.)

2.     Procedural History

On May 23, 2025, Plaintiff filed its complaint against Defendant for breach of contract, negligence, and declaratory relief. A proof of service filed September 22, 2025, shows Plaintiff served the summons and complaint on Defendant by substituted service on July 21, 2026. On September 19, 2025, Defendant filed its answer to the complaint.

On September 19, 2025, Defendant filed its cross-complaint against Plaintiff for breach of contract, fraud in the inducement, and declaratory relief. On October 21, 2025, Plaintiff filed its answer to the cross-complaint.

On May 18, 2026, Defendant filed the instant application. On June 25, 2026, Plaintiff filed its opposition. On June 30, 2026 Defendant filed its reply.

II.             Applicable Law

“Upon the filing of the complaint or at any time thereafter, the [applicant] may apply pursuant to this article for a right to attach order and a writ of attachment by filing an application for the order and writ with the court in which the action is brought.” (CCP § 484.010.)

The application shall be executed under oath and must include: (1) a statement showing that the attachment is sought to secure the recovery on a claim upon which an attachment may be issued; (2) a statement of the amount to be secured by the attachment; (3) a statement that the attachment is not sought for a purpose other than the recovery on the claim upon which the attachment is based; (4) a statement that the applicant has no information or belief that the claim is discharged or that the prosecution of the action is stayed in a proceeding under the Bankruptcy Act (11 U.S.C. section 101 et seq.); and (5) a description of the property to be attached under the writ of attachment and a statement that the [applicant] is informed and believes that such property is subject to attachment. (CCP § 484.020.)

The application for a writ of attachment must be supported “by an affidavit showing that the [applicant] on the facts presented would be entitled to a judgment on the claim upon which the attachment is based.” (CCP § 484.030.)

The court shall consider the showing made by the parties, as well as the pleadings and other papers in the record. (CCP § 484.090(a), (d).) The Court shall issue a right to attach order if it finds all of the following:

“(1) The claim upon which the attachment is based is one upon which an attachment may be issued.

(2) The [applicant] has established the probable validity of the claim upon which the attachment is based.

(3) The attachment is not sought for a purpose other than the recovery on the claim upon which the attachment is based.

(4) The amount to be secured by the attachment is greater than zero.”

(CCP § 484.090(a)(1-4).)

At the times prescribed by CCP section 1005, subdivision (b), the defendant must be served with a copy of the summons and complaint, notice of application and hearing, and a copy of the application and supporting affidavits. (CCP § 484.040.)

“The Attachment Law statutes are subject to strict construction….” (Epstein v. Abrams (1997) 57 Cal.App.4th 1159, 1168.)

III.           Evidentiary Objections

Plaintiff objects to portions of the Bazaz Declaration. The objections to page 2, line 28 through page 3, line 2 are sustained for hearsay and lack of personal knowledge. While Plaintiff is correct that Exhibits H.1 and H.3 are improperly authenticated as stated, this appears to be a defect arising solely from misnumbering the exhibits, rather than any substantive defect. The Court directs Defendant to file a corrected declaration properly numbering the exhibits. Upon correction, the documents satisfy the standards for admissibility.

The remaining objections are overruled.

Defendant objects to a portion of the Reinholz Declaration. The objection is overruled.

IV.           Analysis

1.     Claim on Which Attachment Can Be Based

“[A]n attachment may be issued only in an action on a claim or claims for money, each of which is based upon a contract, express or implied, where the total amount of the claim or claims is a fixed or readily ascertainable amount not less than five hundred dollars ($500) exclusive of costs, interest, and attorney’s fees.” (CCP § 483.010(a).) “An attachment may not be issued on a claim which is secured by any interest in real property arising from agreement ….” (CCP § 483.010(b).)

Here, Defendant’s claim against Plaintiff is premised on its cause of action for breach of the Agreement. (Mem. at 12.) Plaintiff does not oppose on this ground. Defendant may seek attachment based on its breach of contract claim.

2.     Fixed or Readily Ascertainable Amount

A claim is “readily ascertainable” where the damages may be readily ascertained by reference to the contract and the basis of the calculation appears to be reasonable and definite. (CIT Group/Equipment Financing, Inc. v. Super DVD, Inc. (2004) 115 Cal.App.4th 537, 540-41.) The fact that the damages are unliquidated is not determinative. (Ibid.) However, the contract must furnish a standard by which the amount may be ascertained and there must be a basis by which the damages can be determined by proof. (Ibid.)

Here, Defendant’s evidence shows Plaintiff owes $1,303,273.92 for invoiced services rendered under the Agreement. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 32.)

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that the amount is not measurable by reference to the Agreement, and that the basis is not reasonable and certain because the invoices include services and charges not agreed to in the Agreement and are accompanied by registers which do not support the amount charged. (Op. at 14.)

Ultimately, Plaintiff objects to a limited subset of the items billed in the invoices, including the 3.5% merchant processing fees appearing on several invoices and the patrol vehicle services, or at least to the service occurring after contract termination. The Court agrees that nothing in the Agreement allows Defendant to apply a 3.5% merchant processing fees charge, and the patrol vehicle service includes a charge period after the contract termination.[1] (See Bazaz Decl., Exs. A, H.3.)

However, as Defendant explains in reply, Defendant did not include the merchant processing fees or the late charges in the amount sought in its application. (Reply at 3-4.) As a result, the pre-interest amount need only be reduced by the amount of the post-termination patrol vehicle services charge, which is $33,600. (Bazaz Decl., Ex. H.3.) Therefore, the relevant amount from invoice 10157 is $309,323 less $33,600, or $275,723. Consequently, the daily interest accrual on invoice no. 10157 is reduced from $15.25 (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 31) to $13.60, which at 340 days (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 31) amounts to $4,624, or a $561 reduction. Finally, the total appropriate deduction to the readily ascertainable amount is $561 plus $33,600, or $34,161, making the total readily ascertainable amount $1,292,067.34.

Accordingly, the readily ascertainable amount is $1,292,067.34. This finding is consistent with CCP section 483.010(a), which requires that the amount be measurable by reference to the contract and supported by admissible evidence. The Agreement furnishes clear hourly rates, minimum shift requirements, and weekly patrol fees from which the Court can determine a definite calculation. With the deductions identified above, the resulting figure is sufficiently “fixed or readily ascertainable” for purposes of prejudgment attachment.

3.     Probable Validity of Defendant’s Claim

“A claim has ‘probable validity’ where it is more likely than not that the [applicant] will obtain a judgment against the defendant on that claim.” (CCP § 481.190.) “If the defendant opposes the application, ‘the court must then consider the relative merits of the positions of the respective parties and make a determination of the probable outcome of the litigation.’ [Citations.]” (Pech v. Morgan (2021) 61 Cal.App.5th 841, 855.)

In evaluating probable validity, the Court does not adjudicate the ultimate merits of either party’s breach of contract theories. The Court instead weighs the parties’ competing factual showings to determine, on balance, whether the applicant is more likely than not to obtain judgment. (See CCP §§ 481.190, 484.090.) The Court therefore assesses the credibility, internal consistency, and relative weight of the evidence, while avoiding any determination that would amount to a final merits ruling. The analysis below is made solely for the limited purpose of determining the “probable validity” of the applicant’s claim at this preliminary stage.

Here, the application is based on claims for breach of contract. To establish a claim for breach of contract, a plaintiff must prove: (1) existence of a contract; (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance; (3) defendant’s breach of the contract; and (4) damages incurred by plaintiff as a result of the breach. (Durell v. Sharp Healthcare (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 1350, 1367.)

In support of attachment, Defendant’s evidence shows that Plaintiff and Defendant entered into the Agreement in September 2024. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 6, Ex. A.) Defendant performed services under the Agreement and invoiced Plaintiff for those services. (Bazaz Decl., ¶¶ 26-27, 31, 34, Exs. C-E, H.) In total, Plaintiff has not paid $1,326,228.34, inclusive of interest. (Bazaz Decl., ¶ 36.)

Defendant has established a prima facie case for breach of contract.

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that Defendant materially failed to perform its contractual obligations. While Plaintiff presents evidence of performance concerns, the Court notes that Plaintiff continued to request, accept, and rely upon Defendant’s services for nearly two months after the earliest-documented incidents and did not exercise its contractual right to terminate until March 19, 2025. (Bazaz Decl., Ex. A; Reinholtz Decl., ¶¶ 11-12, 17, 24.) Plaintiff’s continued use of Defendant’s services, together with its own evidence acknowledging that many facilities were operating satisfactorily, substantially undermines the weight of its argument that Defendant’s alleged breaches were material and excused Plaintiff’s nonpayment. At this stage, the Court does not find that Plaintiff has established a material breach or failure of performance that is likely to defeat Defendant’s claim at trial. Rather, the Court finds that Plaintiff’s evidence, even taken at face value, is less persuasive considering its decision to retain Defendant’s services despite its stated concerns. On balance, Defendant’s showing is more likely than not to prevail on the merits for purposes of “probable validity” under CCP section 484.090(a)(2).

Accordingly, Defendant has shown the probable validity of its claims for breach of the Agreement. All ultimate factual and legal determinations remain reserved for trial.

4.     Purpose of Attachment

The other required finding under CCP section 484.090 is that the “attachment is not sought for a purpose other than the recovery on the claim upon which the attachment is based.” (CCP § 484.090(a)(3).)

Defendant declares that “[a]ttachment is not sought for a purpose other than the recovery on a claim upon which the attachment is based.” (App. ¶ 4.) Plaintiff does not oppose on this ground. Attachment is sought for proper purpose.

5.     Bankruptcy

CCP section 484.020(d) requires a “statement that the applicant has no information or belief that the claim is discharged in a proceeding under Title 11 of the United States Code (Bankruptcy) or that the prosecution of the action is stayed in a proceeding under Title 11 of the United States Code (Bankruptcy).”

Defendant provides this statement. (App. ¶ 5.) Plaintiff does not oppose on this ground. This requirement has been satisfied.

6.     Claims of Exemption

No claims of exemption are sought.

7.     Undertaking

CCP section 489.210 requires the applicant to file an undertaking before issuance of a writ of attachment. CCP section 489.220 provides, with exceptions, for an undertaking in the amount of $10,000. Plaintiff does not object to the amount of the undertaking. Accordingly, the Court will order an undertaking in the amount of $10,000.

V.              Conclusion

Defendant’s application for attachment against Plaintiff is GRANTED in the amount of $1,292,067.34. Defendant is ordered to submit an updated proposed order to reflect the orders and findings herein. The updated proposed order must be filed and served within 3 court days.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: APPLICATIONS FOR WRIT OF ATTACHMENT

 

Plaintiff Nature’s Produce (“Plaintiff”) has filed two applications for writ of attachment, one against defendant Simi Town Restaurant, LLC (“Simi Town”) and the other against defendant Freddy Braidi (“Braidi”) (collectively, “Defendants”).

The unopposed applications are granted.

I.                Background

1.     Factual Background

On or about September 24, 2025, Plaintiff and Simi Town entered a partially written, partially oral agreement (“Agreement”) by which Plaintiff agreed to periodically sell Simi Town produce and related products at Plaintiff’s standard rates. (Polisky Decl., ¶ 2, Ex. A.) The agreement provides that Plaintiff may collect attorney fees if it must incur collection costs or file suit to collect any amount due under the Agreement. (Polisky Decl., ¶ 5.)

Also on or about September 24, 2025, Braidi executed a written guarantee (“Guarantee”) for Simi Town’s obligations under the Agreement. (Polisky Decl., ¶ 6.) The Guarantee provides that the prevailing party in any litigation involving the Guarantee may recover attorney fees. (Polisky Decl., ¶ 10, Ex. A.) The Guarantee is incorporated into the Agreement. (Polisky Decl., ¶ 6, Ex. A.)

From November 21, 2025 through January 15, 2026, Plaintiff sold and delivered goods to Simi Town under the Agreement, for which Simi Town has an unpaid balance in the amount of $10,840.10. (Polisky Decl., ¶ 3.) Simi Town made no complaint about the goods, and any issues relating to returned or unreceived goods were resolved with credits or replacements to the satisfaction of the parties. (Polisky Decl., ¶ 3, Ex. A.)

On March 17, 2026, Plaintiff sent Simi Town a statement of the overdue balance of $10,840.10 for goods sold and delivered. (Polisky Decl., ¶ 4, Ex. B.) Simi Town has made no payments since. (Polisky Decl., ¶ 4.) Likewise, Braidi made no payments on the balance under the Guarantee. (Polisky Decl., ¶ 8.)

 

2.     Procedural History

On April 1, 2026, Plaintiff filed this action against Defendants. Proofs of service filed April 28, 2026 show Plaintiff served the summons and complaint on Defendants by substituted service on April 9, 2026. On May 6, 2026, Defendants filed their answer.

On April 6, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant two applications. To date, Defendants have not filed any opposition. Any opposition was due five court days before the hearing date, or on June 25, 2026.

II.             Applicable Law

“Upon the filing of the complaint or at any time thereafter, the plaintiff may apply pursuant to this article for a right to attach order and a writ of attachment by filing an application for the order and writ with the court in which the action is brought.” (CCP § 484.010.)

The application shall be executed under oath and must include: (1) a statement showing that the attachment is sought to secure the recovery on a claim upon which an attachment may be issued; (2) a statement of the amount to be secured by the attachment; (3) a statement that the attachment is not sought for a purpose other than the recovery on the claim upon which the attachment is based; (4) a statement that the applicant has no information or belief that the claim is discharged or that the prosecution of the action is stayed in a proceeding under the Bankruptcy Act (11 U.S.C. section 101 et seq.); and (5) a description of the property to be attached under the writ of attachment and a statement that the plaintiff is informed and believes that such property is subject to attachment. (CCP § 484.020.)

The application for a writ of attachment must be supported “by an affidavit showing that the plaintiff on the facts presented would be entitled to a judgment on the claim upon which the attachment is based.” (CCP § 484.030.)

The court shall consider the showing made by the parties, as well as the pleadings and other papers in the record. (CCP § 484.090(a), (d).) The Court shall issue a right to attach order if it finds all of the following:

“(1) The claim upon which the attachment is based is one upon which an attachment may be issued.

(2) The plaintiff has established the probable validity of the claim upon which the attachment is based.

(3) The attachment is not sought for a purpose other than the recovery on the claim upon which the attachment is based.

(4) The amount to be secured by the attachment is greater than zero.”

(CCP § 484.090(a)(1-4).)

At the times prescribed by CCP section 1005, subdivision (b), the defendant must be served with a copy of the summons and complaint, notice of application and hearing, and a copy of the application and supporting affidavits. (CCP § 484.040.)

“The Attachment Law statutes are subject to strict construction….” (Epstein v. Abrams (1997) 57 Cal.App.4th 1159, 1168.)

III.           Analysis

1.     Claim on Which Attachment Can Be Based

“[A]n attachment may be issued only in an action on a claim or claims for money, each of which is based upon a contract, express or implied, where the total amount of the claim or claims is a fixed or readily ascertainable amount not less than five hundred dollars ($500) exclusive of costs, interest, and attorney’s fees.” (CCP § 483.010(a).) “An attachment may not be issued on a claim which is secured by any interest in real property arising from agreement ….” (CCP § 483.010(b).)

Here, Plaintiff’s claims against Defendants are premised on its causes of action for breach of the Agreement and breach of the Guarantee. (Mem. at 2-4.) Plaintiff may seek attachment based on its breach claims.

2.     Contract Based on a Commercial Claim

If the action is against a defendant who is a natural person, an attachment may be issued only on a commercial claim which arises out of the defendant’s conduct of a trade, business, or profession. (CCP §483.010(c).) Consumer transactions cannot form a basis for attachment. (CCP §483.010(c); Kadison, Pfaelzer, Woodard, Quinn & Rossi v. Wilson (“Kadison”) (1987) 197 Cal.App.3d 1, 4 [action involving trust property was a commercial, not a consumer, transaction].)

Braidi is the principal of Simi Town, and the Guarantee was signed to induce the Agreement between Plaintiff and Simi Town. (Polisky Decl., ¶ 6, Ex. A.) Accordingly, the claim against Braidi for breach of the Guarantee is a commercial claim arising out of Braidi’s business.

3.     Fixed or Readily Ascertainable Amount

A claim is “readily ascertainable” where the damages may be readily ascertained by reference to the contract and the basis of the calculation appears to be reasonable and definite. (CIT Group/Equipment Financing, Inc. v. Super DVD, Inc. (2004) 115 Cal.App.4th 537, 540-41.) The fact that the damages are unliquidated is not determinative. (Ibid.) But the contract must furnish a standard by which the amount may be ascertained and there must be a basis by which the damages can be determined by proof. (Ibid.)

Here, Plaintiff’s evidence shows Defendants owe $10,840.10 for goods sold and delivered under the Agreement. (Polisky Decl., ¶¶ 5, 9, Ex. B.)

The readily ascertainable amount is $10,840.10.

4.     Probable Validity of Plaintiff’s Claims

“A claim has ‘probable validity’ where it is more likely than not that the plaintiff will obtain a judgment against the defendant on that claim.” (CCP § 481.190.) “If the defendant opposes the application, ‘the court must then consider the relative merits of the positions of the respective parties and make a determination of the probable outcome of the litigation.’ [Citations.]” (Pech v. Morgan (2021) 61 Cal.App.5th 841, 855.)

Here, the applications are based on claims for breach of contract. To establish a claim for breach of contract, a plaintiff must prove: (1) existence of a contract; (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance; (3) defendant’s breach of the contract; and (4) damages incurred by plaintiff as a result of the breach. (Durell v. Sharp Healthcare (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 1350, 1367.)

In support of attachment, Plaintiff’s evidence shows that Plaintiff and Defendant entered into the partially written, partially oral Agreement on or about September 24, 2025. (Polisky Decl., ¶ 4, Ex. A.) On or about the same day, Braidi executed the Guarantee for Simi Town’s obligations under the Agreement. (Polisky Decl., ¶ 6, Ex. A.) Under the Agreement, Plaintiff would periodically sell Simi Town produce and related goods at its standard rates. (Polisky Decl., ¶ 2, Ex. A.) Defendants currently owe Plaintiff $10,840.10 for goods sold and delivered. (Polisky Decl., ¶¶ 3-4, Ex. B.) Plaintiff has made demands for payment, but Defendants have not paid. (Polisky Decl., ¶¶ 5, 8, Ex. B.)

Plaintiff has established a prima facie case for breach of contract. Defendants have not opposed. Accordingly, Plaintiff has shown the probable validity of its claims for breach of the Agreement and Guarantee.

5.     Purpose of Attachment

The other required finding under CCP section 484.090 is that the “attachment is not sought for a purpose other than the recovery on the claim upon which the attachment is based.” (CCP § 484.090(a)(3).)

Plaintiff declares that “[a]ttachment is not sought for a purpose other than the recovery on a claim upon which the attachment is based.” (App. ¶ 4.) Attachment is sought for proper purpose.

6.     Bankruptcy

CCP section 484.020(d) requires a “statement that the applicant has no information or belief that the claim is discharged in a proceeding under Title 11 of the United States Code (Bankruptcy) or that the prosecution of the action is stayed in a proceeding under Title 11 of the United States Code (Bankruptcy).”

Plaintiff provides this statement. (App. ¶ 5.) This requirement has been satisfied.

7.     Property Subject to Attachment

CCP section 487.010(a) states that, where the defendant is a corporation, all corporate property for which a method of levy is provided in CCP section 488.300 et seq. is subject to attachment.

Where the defendant is a natural person, the property to which attachment can apply are limited per CCP section 487.010(c).

As to Braidi, Plaintiff seeks property described as follows:

“(1) interests in real property; (2) deposit accounts” (Apps. ¶ 9.)

This description is sufficient.

8.     Claims of Exemption

No claims of exemption are sought.

9.     Undertaking

CCP section 489.210 requires the plaintiff to file an undertaking before issuance of a writ of attachment. CCP section 489.220 provides, with exceptions, for an undertaking in the amount of $10,000. Accordingly, the Court will order an undertaking in the amount of $10,000.

IV.           Conclusion

Plaintiff’s applications for attachment against Defendants are GRANTED. Plaintiff must submit two proposed orders for a writ of attachment (one for each application), with the contents filled out consistent with the terms of this order (including the proposed findings). Plaintiff must file and serve the proposed orders within three court days.
