I.            INTRODUCTION

This action was filed on September 13, 2024 by plaintiffs Serena Chua (“Chua”), Irene Matsubara (“Matsubara”), and Hoi King Tsui (“Tsui”) (collectively, “Plaintiffs”) against defendants Continental Townhomes at Monterey Park Owners Association, Inc. (“HOA”) and Partners Community Management, Inc. (“Management”) (collectively, “Defendants”). Plaintiffs assert causes of action for negligence, trespass, nuisance, and violation of Health and Safety Code § 13007. The action arises from a fire that took place on November 29, 2023, at a condominium building located at 178 Casuda Canyon Drive, Monterey Park, California 91754 (“Property”). Chua owned and occupied Unit E, Matsubara owned and occupied Unit F, and Tsui owned and occupied Unit D. (Compl., ¶ 1.) Plaintiffs allege that the common areas were negligently inspected, maintained, repaired, and managed by Defendants. Plaintiffs additionally allege that Defendants committed a trespass, created a nuisance, and violated Health and Safety Code § 13007 when they allowed the spread of a negligently-caused fire.

On May 18, 2026, Defendants filed this motion for summary judgment or, in the alternative, summary adjudication on each cause of action.

Plaintiffs filed an opposition brief on July 22, 2026.

Defendants filed a reply brief on July 31, 2026.

II.          LEGAL STANDARD

In reviewing a motion for summary judgment, courts must apply a three-step analysis: “(1) identify the issues framed by the pleadings; (2) determine whether the moving party has negated the opponent’s claims; and (3) determine whether the opposition has demonstrated the existence of a triable, material factual issue.” (Hinesley v. Oakshade Town Center (2005) 135 Cal.App.4th 289, 294.) A defendant moving for summary judgment or summary adjudication “has met his or her burden of showing that a cause of action has no merit if the party has shown that one or more elements of the cause of action . . . cannot be established, or that there is a complete defense to the cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) To meet this burden of showing a cause of action cannot be established, a defendant must show not only “that the plaintiff does not possess needed evidence” but also that “the plaintiff cannot reasonably obtain needed evidence.” (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 854.) A moving defendant need not conclusively negate an element of plaintiff’s cause of action, but it is insufficient for the defendant to merely point out the absence of evidence. (Id. at p. 854; Gaggero v. Yura (2003) 108 Cal.App.4th 884, 891.) The supporting evidence can be in the form of affidavits, declarations, admissions, depositions, answers to interrogatories, and matters of which judicial notice may be taken. (Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at p. 855.) “Once the defendant . . . has met that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff . . . to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to the cause of action or a defense thereto.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (p)(2).) The plaintiff may not merely rely on allegations or denials of its pleadings to show that a triable issue of material fact exists, but instead, “shall set forth the specific facts showing that a triable issue of material fact exists as to the cause of action.” (Ibid.) “If the plaintiff cannot do so, summary judgment should be granted.” (Avivi v. Centro Medico Urgente Medical Center (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 463, 467.)

III.        EVIDENTIARY OBJECTIONS AND REQUESTS FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

A.   Request for Judicial Notice

Defendants’ request for judicial notice of the complaint in this action is GRANTED. (Evid Code, § 452(d).)

B.   Plaintiffs’ Evidentiary Objections

Plaintiffs object to three paragraphs of the Declaration of William Kang on the grounds that he lacks personal knowledge to attest to the ownership of Unit C, any notices or complaints received by the HOA about electrical wires, and whether there had been other fires on the Property. The objections are SUSTAINED. While Mr. Kang declares that he is the president of the HOA, there are no facts specifying the duration of his tenure and how he knows about the ownership of Unit 3 or whether the HOA received any notice, complaints, or comments about the electrical wires.

C.   Defendants’ Evidentiary Objections

Since the Court concludes that Defendants failed to meet their moving burden to show an absence of a triable issue of fact, the Court need not consider Plaintiffs’ evidence and Defendants’ objections thereto.

IV.        DISCUSSION

Defendants argue that summary judgment is appropriate because the primary issue depends on the interpretation of the Declaration of Covenants, Conditions, and Restrictions for Contintental Townhomes at Monterey Park, recorded November 20, 1973 (“CC&Rs”). Plaintiffs each admit that they are members of the HOA and bound by the CC&Rs and there is no dispute that the CC&Rs require the HOA to maintain the “Common Area”, which is defined as “[t]he entire project excepting all units therein granted or reserved.” (Defendants’ Undisputed Material Fact (“UMF”) Nos. 2-3, 6-10, 19.) The term “Unit” is defined as “the elements of a condominium which are not owned in common with the other owners of the condominiums in the project” and “Condominium” is defined as “[a] fee simple estate in real property consisting of an undivided fractional interest in the common area, together with an interest in a unit as hereinabove defined.” (UMF Nos. 20-21.) The CC&Rs require the HOA to maintain the common areas in a first class condition and in a good state of repair, while “[e]ach owner should provide insurance on his personal property, and any necessary repairs or maintenance to such unit shall be the responsibility of the unit thereof.” (UMF Nos. 24, 26.)

Defendants argue that Plaintiffs’ causes of action for negligence, trespass, nuisance, and violation of Health and Safety Code § 13007 fail because Plaintiffs cannot prove that the electrical wires that caused the fire were owned or the responsibility of Defendants since the wiring was not owned in common and not a “Common Area” for which the HOA was responsible. Alternatively, Defendants argue that even if the electrical wiring was a common area, the fire was unforseeable and the HOA had no affirmative duty to open interior walls to inspect the wires where there was no indication that there were any hazards.

A.   Whether the Electrical Wiring is Owned In Common

First, Defendants contend that the wiring that caused the fire was an element of Unit C, and not a Common Area. (Motion, pp. 9-10.) Each individual Unit contains electrical wiring, an electric panel, and an electric meter; these elements are not connected to any other Unit at the Property. (UMF Nos. 22-23.) Defendants claim it is undisputed that the fire originated in the void space between walls above Unit C’s entryway door and was caused by an “adverse electrical event associated with the copper-clad wiring having been compressed in between the structural members and 2x4 backing.” (UMF Nos. 15-17.) Defendants argue these wires connect to and service only Unit C and are not owned in common with any other Unit owner.  

Defendants argue that no duty was owed because the electrical wiring that caused the fire was an element of Unit C, and not a Common Area. Defendants rely on their “cause and origin investigator”, Michale Koster of Reliant Investigations, Inc., who conducted an investigation on December 13, 2023 and concluded that the fire originated “within the void space directly above the front entryway man [sic] door to unit C at the Property.” (UMF No. 33; Ex. H, ¶ 3.) Defendants argue that the HOA did not owe a duty to maintain or repair the wiring connecting to and servicing Unit C because a unit’s electrical wiring is not owned in common with the other owners because it is not shared, controlled, used by, and/or paid for by more than one owner. (Motion, pp. 9-10.)

In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that Defendants fail to show that no triable issues exist with regard to whether the electrical wiring in the “void space” above Unit C is a common area. Civil Code section 4145(b) states that unless the governing documents provide otherwise, the interior surfaces of perimeter walls, floors, ceilings, windows, doors, and outlets are part of the separate interest if they are designated as the interest’s boundaries; any other portions of the walls, floors or ceiling are part of the common area. Defendants do not introduce any evidence about Unit C’s recorded boundaries and, given the statutory default limiting a separate interest to the interior space of a unit, a triable issue of material fact exists as to whether the electrical wiring “compressed in between the structural members and 2x4 backing” is a common area.

Since Defendants fail to meet their moving burden to show no triable issues of material fact exist with respect to the characterization of the electrical wiring as a common area, the motion for summary judgment/adjudication on this basis is DENIED.

Defendants argue that in the alternative, even if the electrical wiring were considered a Common Area, there is no duty to open the walls of every Unit when there are no known or foreseeable dangerous conditions. Defendants list the factors identified in Rowland v. Christian (1968) 69 Cal.2d 108, 112, which weigh against the imposition of an affirmative duty to protect and state that the HOA received no notice, complaints, or comments prior to the fire about the electrical wires and that there had never been a fire at this Property. Defendants’ sole evidence regarding any lack of prior notice is the declaration of William Kang, its president. As stated above, the objections to Kang’s statements that the HOA received no notice, complaints or comments before the fire about the electrical wires or that no other fire had ever occurred are sustained. Therefore, Defendants’ claim is unsubstantiated by any evidence.

Also, homeowners’ associations generally owe a duty to exercise due care for the residents' safety in those areas under their control. (Frances T. v. Village Green Owners Assn. (1986) 42 Cal.3d 490, 499.) Here, the CC&Rs provide the HOA with the exclusive right to repair and maintain common area installations and improvements and precludes individual owners from doing so. (Defs.’ Ex. B, Art. IX, subd. (b) [“No structural alterations to the interior of any unit shall be made, and no plumbing or electrical work within any bearing or party walls shall be made by an individual owner without prior written consent of the Board of Governors.”]) The fact that no prior fires had occurred or that complaints about the electrical wiring in C is not determinative because Defendants did not have to forsee “the precise injury” so long as “the possibility of this type of harm was foreseeable.” (Frances T., supra, 42 Cal.3d at p. 503.) Here, the risk of fire due to improperly maintained electrical wiring is foreseeable when the HOA retains control over maintainance and repair.

VI.    CONCLUSION

          In light of the foregoing, Defendants’ motion for summary judgment is DENIED.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: DEMURRER TO CROSS-COMPLAINT FILED BY VARTAN TER MINASYAN AND MOTION TO STRIKE

 

Dept. 3

8:30 a.m.

August 11, 2026

 

I.      INTRODUCTION

         On January 24, 2026, plaintiff Arsen Koseyan (“Arsen”) filed this action against defendant Vartan Ter Minasyan (“Minasyan”), among others, arising from a real estate transaction. Arsen filed the operative First Amended Complaint on May 28, 2025. Due to the similarity of the parties’ names in the action, the Court refers to them by their first names and intends no disrespect by doing so.

          On December 29, 2025, Minasyan filed a cross-complaint against Arsen, Arutyun Koseyan (“Arutyun”), and Gardena Truck Stop Incorporated (“Gardena Truck Stop”) (collectively, “Defendants”). The Cross-Complaint alleges causes of action for: (1) breach of oral partnership agreement, (2) breach of fiduciary duty, (3) Uniform Voidable Transfer Act, (4) conspiracy, and (5) accounting.

Defendants filed a demurrer and motion to strike on March 19, 2026.

Minasyan filed opposition briefs on July 29, 2026.

Defendants have not filed any reply briefs.

II.     LEGAL STANDARDS

A.   Demurrer

A demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the pleadings and will be sustained only where the pleading is defective on its face. (City of Atascadero v. Merrill Lynch, Pierce, Fenner & Smith, Inc. (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 445, 459.) “We treat the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law. We accept the factual allegations of the complaint as true and also consider matters which may be judicially noticed. [Citation.]” (Mitchell v. California Department of Public Health (2016) 1 Cal.App.5th 1000, 1007; Del E. Webb Corp. v. Structural Materials Co. (1981) 123 Cal.App.3d 593, 604 [“the facts alleged in the pleading are deemed to be true, however improbable they may be”].)

A demurrer may be brought on the grounds that it is uncertain. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (f).) “A demurrer for uncertainty is strictly construed, even where a complaint is in some respects uncertain, because ambiguities can be clarified under modern discovery procedures.” (Khoury v. Maly’s of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.) A demurrer for uncertainty will be sustained only where the complaint is so bad that the defendant cannot reasonably respond. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.10, subd. (f).) Where the complaint contains substantial factual allegations sufficiently apprising defendant of the issues it is being asked to meet, a demurrer for uncertainty will be overruled or plaintiff will be given leave to amend. (Williams v. Beechnut Nutrition Corp. (1986) 185 Cal.App.3d 135, 139, fn. 2.)

B.   Motion to Strike

          Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading may serve and file a notice of motion to strike the whole or any part thereof. (Code Civ. Proc., § 435, subd. (b)(1).) The court may, upon a motion, or at any time in its discretion, and upon terms it deems proper, strike any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a); Stafford v. Shultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782 [“Matter in a pleading which is not essential to the claim is surplusage; probative facts are surplusage and may be stricken out or disregarded”].) The court may also strike all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with California law, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (b).) An immaterial or irrelevant allegation is one that is not essential to the statement of a claim or defense; is neither pertinent to nor supported by an otherwise sufficient claim or defense; or a demand for judgment requesting relief not supported by the allegations of the complaint. (Code Civ. Proc., § 431.10, subd. (b).) The grounds for moving to strike must appear on the face of the pleading or by way of judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437.)

III.    DISCUSSION

A.   Demurrer

Minasyan alleges that Gardena Truck Stop is the legal title holder of the property commonly known as 333 East Rosecrans Avenue, Gardena, CA 90248 (“Property”). Minasyan claims that he was engaged in a sole proprietorship at the Property, pursuant to a lease, selling to the public diesel fuel, propane, tires, foods, drinks, and other items, and performing vehicular services, when he entered into an oral partnership agreement with Arsen to co-own and operate the business as equal partners. (Cross-Compl., ¶¶ 11-13.)

Minasyan claims Arsen breached this agreement by converting Minasyan’s portion of the business’s net profits. Minasyan also alleges that Arsen breached his fiduciary duty by misappropriating an opportunity for the partnership to acquire an interest in the Property and acquired that interest through Arsen’s alter ego, Gardena Truck Stop. (Cross-Compl., ¶¶ 21-28, 35-38.) The other cross-defendant, Arutyun, is Arsen’s brother and allegedly participated in a conspiracy with Arsen and Gardena Truck Stop to usurp the partnership opportunity and breach the fiduciary duties owed by Arsen to Minasyan. (Cross-Compl., ¶¶ 45-46.)

Cross-Defendants argue that the First and Second Causes of Action for breach of contract and breach of fiduciary duty are uncertain and fail to state sufficient facts because Minasyan fails to specify the precise date when any breach of contract or breach of fiduciary duties occurred or what contractual or fiduciary duty was breached. Cross-Defendants claim that Minasyan is required to allege the dates of breach in order to determine whether the statute of limitations has lapsed. Cross-Defendants also contend that Minasyan failed to allege the date the partnership was formed, its terms, the parties’ capital contributions, the respective ownership interests, along with other details. Cross-Defendants further argue that Minasyan’s claim for breach of fiduciary duty is barred by the economic loss rule.

These arguments are meritless because any uncertainty of the timing of specific breaches can be determined through discovery and “[w]here a complaint does not reveal on its face that it is barred by the statute of limitations, a plaintiff has no obligation to plead around the defense.” (JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A. v. Ward (2019) 33 Cal. App. 5th 678, 689.)

Also, there is no uncertainty about any terms of the partnership agreement that was breached because Minasyan alleges that Arsen converted his portion of the business’s net profits. Likewise, Minasyan sufficiently pleads that Arsen breached the duty of loyalty by usurping a partnership opportunity to acquire the Property, since control of the Property “determines whether the partnership keeps its location, its rent, and ultimately its going-concern value.” (Opp., p. 6.)

Furthermore, additional detail regarding the partnership agreement is unnecessary because Minasyan alleges that he and Arsen orally agreed to “co-own and operate the BUSINESS as co-equal, co-partners, thereby creating a general partnership” and partners are entitled to an equal share of partnership profits, pursuant to Corporations Code § 16401(b). Moreover, Cross-Defendants argue that Minasyan’s claim for breach of fiduciary duty is barred by the economic loss rule. (Demurrer, pp. 7-8.) However, the economic loss rule does not apply because fiduciary duties are imposed by statute and independent of contractual duties. (Rattagan v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (2024) 17 Cal.5th 1, 42-43.) Accordingly, the demurrer to the First and Second Causes of Action is OVERRULED.

Third, Cross-Defendants argue that Minasyan fails to allege a UVTA claim because no debtor-creditor relationship is alleged. (Demurrer, pp. 10-11.) Minasyan argues that he is the creditor and Arsen is the debtor because Arsen converted Minasyan’s share of the business’s profits and usurped the partnership’s business opportunity. (Opp., p. 7.) However, even if Minasyan alleges a debtor-creditor relationship, no transfer between Koseyan and Gardena Truck Stop is alleged because title was taken by Gardena Truck Stop and there are no allegations that the original owner of the Property failed to receive its reasonably equivalent value in exchange.  

Accordingly, the demurrer to the Third Cause of Action is SUSTAINED.

Fourth, Cross-Defendants argue that Minasyan cannot allege a cause of action for “conspiracy” because it is merely a legal doctrine that imposes liability on conspirators. (Demurrer, p. 11.) Even if “conspiracy” is not technically a cause of action, it serves to allege joint liability between Arsen, Arutyan, and Gardena Truck Stop for Arsen’s breach of fiduciary duty. To support a conspiracy claim, a plaintiff must allege the following elements: “(1) the formation and operation of the conspiracy, (2) wrongful conduct in furtherance of the conspiracy, and (3) damages arising from the wrongful conduct.” (AREI II Cases (2013) 216 Cal.App.4th 1004, 1022.) Here, Minasyan sufficiently alleges that Arsen and Arutyun formed Gardena Truck Stop for the purpose of usurping the partnership’s opportunity to purchase the Property. (Cross-Compl., ¶ 7.) Therefore, the demurrer to the Fourth Cause of Action is OVERRULED.

Last, Cross-Defendants argue that Minasyan cannot assert a claim for an accounting against Arutyun or Gardena and that an accounting is inappropriate because damages can be determined through ordinary discovery and legal remedies since there are identifiable profits and proceeds. (Demurrer, pp. 11-12.) In opposition, Minasyan argues that an accounting, not a fixed damages calculation, is the appropriate remedy due to the information asymmetry between the parties. The Court agrees; Cross-Defendants apparently “seized exclusive control of the partnership business and property, and have operated them for years” without providing any records and Minasyan cannot state a sum certain without them. (Opp., p. 10.) Where it is necessary to have an accounting to determine the plaintiff’s rights, “whether the facts would have given jurisdiction to a court of equity is of no consequence.” (Remme v. Herzog (1963) 222 Cal.App.2d 863, 865.) Therefore, the demurrer to the Fifth Cause of Action is OVERRULED.

B.   Motion to Strike

Cross-Defendants move to strike Minasyan’s allegations of alter ego and punitive damages, as well as Minasyan’s prayer for reasonable attorney fees.

Cross-Defendants argue that Minasyan fails to allege sufficient facts to establish an alter ego relationship. Alter ego allegations may be pleaded by ultimate fact and here, Minasyan pleads a unity of interest between Arsen and Gardena Truck Stop, that Gardena Truck Stop is a mere shell, instrumentality, and conduit through which Arsen does business, and that adherence to the fiction of separateness would sanction fraud and promote injustice. (Cross-Compl., ¶¶ 5-7.) These allegations are sufficient according to the holding of Rutherford Holdings, LLC v. Plaza Del Rey (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 221, 235-236.) Therefore, the motion to strike Minasyan’s alter ego allegations is DENIED.

Next, Cross-Defendants argue that Minasyan’s request for punitive damages is unsupported by any allegation of fraud, malice or oppression as required by Civil Code § 3294. In opposition, Minasyan argues that he sufficiently alleges that Cross-Defendants acted fraudulently by concealing the opportunity to purchase the Property, using Gardena Truck Stop to purchase the Property on February 2, 2023, and diverting profits that were owed to Minasyan. Accordingly, the motion to strike Minasyan’s prayer for punitive damages is DENIED.

Last, Cross-Defendants move to strike Minasyan’s request for attorney’s fees because there is no statute or contract that provide a basis for recovering them. (Motion, pp. 9-10.) While Minasyan claims that his Third Cause of Action under the UVTA entitles him to fees under the “tort of another” doctrine, the Court sustained the demurrer to his Third Cause of Action; therefore, the motion to strike the request for attorneys’ fees is GRANTED with leave to amend.

IV.    CONCLUSION

The demurrer is OVERRULED as to the First, Second, Fourth, and Fifth Causes of Action and SUSTAINED as to the Third Cause of Action with 20 days’ leave to amend.

The motion to strike is DENIED as to Minasyan’s allegations of alter ego and prayer for punitive damages. The motion is GRANTED with respect to Minasyan’s request for attorneys’ fees with 20 days’ leave to amend.

On March 12, 2025, plaintiff Juan Pablo Ochoa (“Plaintiff”), in pro per, filed this defamation action against defendant Young Men’s Christian Association of Metropolitan Los Angeles (“Defendant”).

Defendant filed this motion to compel Plaintiff’s further responses to Request for Production, Set One on July 7, 2026. Defendant also requests monetary sanctions against Plaintiff in the amount of $1,000.

The motion is unopposed and Defendant established good cause for the requested documents because they are documents supporting Plaintiff’s contentions of liability and damages. Furthermore, Plaintiff’s responses are not Code-compliant because Plaintiff failed to specify whether his inability to comply is because the particular item or category has never existed, has been destroyed, has been lost, misplaced, or stolen, or has never been, or is no longer, in his possession, custody, or control. (C.C.P. § 2031.230.) Plaintiff also failed to set forth the name and address of any natural person or organization known or believed by him to have possession, custody, or control of that item or category of item. (Ibid.)

Accordingly, the motion to compel further responses is GRANTED in its entirety and Plaintiff is ordered to provide complete, code-compliant, and verified further responses to Request for Production (Set One), Nos. 1-3, and 6-23, without objections, within 20 days. Sanctions are imposed on Plaintiff in the reduced amount of $600, consisting of 3 hours at defense counsel’s hourly rate of $200, payable within 20 days.
