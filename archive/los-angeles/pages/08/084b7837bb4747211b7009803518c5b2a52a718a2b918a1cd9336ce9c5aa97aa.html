DEPARTMENT P LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 23TRCV03261    Hearing Date: July 2, 2026    Dept: P

Motion for Summary Judgment or Summary Adjudication 

 

Moving Party: Defendants Westin Operator LLC (DOE 1) and Wesco Specialist Inc. (DOE 2)  

Responding Party: Plaintiff Sammy Kiflu  

 

RULING 

 

The court considered the moving papers and opposition. Moving Defendants’ Motion for Summary Judgment is GRANTED.  

  

 

PROCEDURAL ISSUES 

 

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure Section 437c(b)(2), an opposition to a motion for summary judgment must be served and filed “not less than 20 days preceding the noticed or continued date of hearing, unless the court for good cause orders otherwise.”  

 

Here, the hearing on the instant motion is scheduled for July 2, 2026, and Plaintiff filed his opposition on June 23, 2026. Therefore, Plaintiff’s opposition is untimely.  

 

Further, Code of Civil Procedure Section 437c(b)(3) provides: 

 

“The opposition papers shall include a separate statement that responds to each of the material facts contended by the moving party to be undisputed, indicating if the opposing party agrees or disagrees that those facts are undisputed. The statement also shall set forth plainly and concisely any other material facts the opposing party contends are disputed. Each material fact contended by the opposing party to be disputed shall be followed by a reference to the supporting evidence. Failure to comply with this requirement of a separate statement may constitute a sufficient ground, in the court’s discretion, for granting the motion.” 

 

(Code Civ. Proc., Section 437c(b)(3).)  

 

Here, Plaintiff has not filed a separate statement, instead attempting to include one in his opposition brief. While Plaintiff has pointed to multiple material facts as being disputed, Plaintiff has not provided any supporting evidence. 

 

Therefore, the court finds that Plaintiff’s opposition is procedurally defective pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure Section 437c(b). The court will not address Plaintiff’s request for sanctions.  

 

PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND 

 

On October 4, 2023, Plaintiff Sammy Kiflu (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Defendants The Westin Los Angeles Airport, Marriott International, Inc., Wesco Transportation LLC, John Doe, and DOES 1 through 100, inclusive, alleging five causes of action for: (1) Assault; (2) Battery; (3) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress; (4) Negligence; and (5) Negligent Hiring, Supervision, and Retention. Plaintiff’s first three causes of action are alleged only against Defendant John Doe.  

 

On May 30, 2024, Plaintiff filed an amendment identifying Defendant Westin Operator LLC (“Westin Operator”) as DOE 1. On June 7, 2024, Plaintiff filed an amendment identifying Defendant Wesco Specialist Inc. (“Wesco Specialist”) as DOE 2. 

 

On April 9, 2026, Defendants Westin Operator and Wesco Specialist (collectively, “Moving Defendants”) filed the instant motion for summary judgment or summary adjudication. On June 18, 2026, Moving Defendants filed a Notice of Non-Receipt of Opposition to Moving Defendants’ motion. On June 23, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition.  

 

On April 9, 2026, Moving Defendants also filed a motion to seal.  

 

FACTUAL BACKGROUND 

 

On April 5, 2022, Plaintiff chased after a moving shuttle at Los Angeles International Airport (“LAX”) driven by Defendant John Doe and subsequently boarded the shuttle. (UMF No. 4.) After Defendant John Doe informed Plaintiff that he could not be transported by the shuttle, Defendant John Doe exited the shuttle and stood nearby on the sidewalk. (UMF No. 5.) Plaintiff remained on the shuttle, then exited and confronted Defendant John Doe. (UMF No. 6.) After Defendant John Doe reiterated to Plaintiff that he would not be transported by the shuttle, Plaintiff attempted to re-enter the shuttle. (UMF Nos. 7-8.) Plaintiff attempted to board the shuttle as Defendant John Doe attempted to close the shuttle’s doors to prevent Plaintiff’s entry, allegedly injuring Plaintiff. (UMF No. 9.)  

 

Defendant John Doe was hired, trained, supervised, and managed by Wesco Specialist. (UMF No. 3.) As a condition of his employment with Wesco Specialist, Defendant John Doe agreed to having no license suspensions or disqualifications, no felony charges, no reckless, careless, or exhibition driving charges within the past five years, and no at-fault motor vehicle accidents within the previous three years. (UMF No. 10.) Wesco Specialist conducted a background screening of Defendant John Doe’s criminal history before hiring him, which did not reveal any concerns. (UMF No. 11.) Defendant John Doe received Wesco Specialist’s Policy Against Harassment and Personal Appearance and Behavior and completed required trainings, including two titled “Sexual Harassment and Abusive Conduct Prevention” and “Practice Safe Driving, Injury & Safety Reporting.” (UMF No. 12.) During the duration of Defendant John Doe’s employment with Wesco Specialist, Defendant John Doe was not the subject of any complaints, disciplinary actions, or reports of misconduct. (UMF No. 13.)  

 

LEGAL STANDARD  

 

The function of a motion for summary judgment or adjudication is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party cannot show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) “[T]he party moving for summary judgment bears the burden of persuasion that there is no triable issue of material fact and that he is entitled to judgment as a matter of law[.] There is a triable issue of material fact if, and only if, the evidence would allow a reasonable trier of fact to find the underlying fact in favor of the party opposing the motion in accordance with the applicable standard of proof.”  (Id. at 850.) “[T]he party moving for summary judgment bears an initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing of the nonexistence of any triable issue of material fact; if he carries his burden of production, he causes a shift, and the opposing party is then subjected to a burden of production of his own to make a prima facie showing of the existence of a triable issue of material fact.” (Ibid.) 

 

“On a summary judgment motion, the court must therefore consider what inferences favoring the opposing party a factfinder could reasonably draw from the evidence. While viewing the evidence in this manner, the court must bear in mind that its primary function is to identify issues rather than to determine issues. Only when the inferences are indisputable may the court decide the issues as a matter of law. If the evidence is in conflict, the factual issues must be resolved by trial.” (Binder v. Aetna Life Ins. Co. (1999) 75 Cal.App.4th 832, 839 [cleaned up].) Further, “the trial court may not weigh the evidence in the manner of a factfinder to determine whose version is more likely true. Nor may the trial court grant summary judgment based on the court's evaluation of credibility.” (Id. at 840 [cleaned up]; see also Weiss v. People ex rel. Department of Transportation (2020) 9 Cal.5th 840, 864 [“Courts deciding motions for summary judgment or summary adjudication may not weigh the evidence but must instead view it in the light most favorable to the opposing party and draw all reasonable inferences in favor of that party”].) 

 

A party may move for summary adjudication as to one or more causes of action, affirmative defenses, claims for damages, or issues of duty if that party contends that there is no merit to the cause of action, defense, or claim for damages, or if the party contends that there is no duty owed. (See Code Civ. Proc. Section 437c(f)(1).) “A motion for summary adjudication shall be granted only if it completely disposes of a cause of action, an affirmative defense, a claim for damages, or an issue of duty.” (Ibid.) A cause of action has no merit if: (1) one or more elements of the cause of action cannot be separately established, even if that element is separately pleaded, or (2) a defendant establishes an affirmative defense to that cause of action. (See Code Civ. Proc. Section 437c(n); Union Bank v. Superior Court (1995) 31 Cal.App.4th 573, 583.) Once the defendant has shown that a cause of action has no merit, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to show that a triable issue of material fact exists as to that cause of action. (See Code Civ. Proc. Section 437c(o)(2); Union Bank v. Superior Court, supra, 31 Cal.App.4th at 583.) 

 

DISCUSSION   

 

Moving Party’s Argument 

 

Moving Defendants argue that Plaintiff’s fourth and fifth causes of action fail as alleged against Westin Operator because the evidence establishes that Westin Operator had no employment relationship with and exercised no control over Defendant John Doe, the shuttle driver. Moving Defendants argue that Plaintiff’s fourth cause of action also fails as alleged against Wesco Specialist because the evidence does not establish that Defendant John Doe acted negligently, and thus Wesco Specialist cannot be held liable via respondeat superior. Here, Moving Defendants argue that Plaintiff was a trespasser attempting unauthorized entry onto the shuttle and thus only an ordinary duty of care applied. Moving Defendants argue that the evidence establishes that Defendant John Doe acted reasonably in response to Plaintiff’s attempt to force his way onto the shuttle, and thus Plaintiff cannot establish the breach element of his negligence cause of action. Moving Defendants argue that Plaintiff’s fifth cause of action fails as alleged against Wesco Specialist because the evidence shows that Wesco Specialist had no knowledge or reason to know that Defendant John Doe carried any risk of causing harm to Plaintiff.  

 

Opposing Party’s Argument 

 

Plaintiff argues that Moving Defendants’ motion fails because they have not addressed Plaintiff’s intentional tort causes of action. Plaintiff argues that triable issues of material fact exist as to whether Defendant John Doe was an employee of Westin Operator because a shuttle to and from an airport from a hotel is a core amenity of the hotel and because Westin Operator retained control over the shuttle service. Plaintiff argues that he was not a trespasser but rather a hotel guest who had already boarded its shuttle. Plaintiff argues that sanctions are warranted for Moving Defendant’s motion because it has not addressed three of Plaintiff’s causes of action.  

 

Evidentiary Objections 

 

Plaintiff’s objections to the Declarations of Iliana Thomas and Todd Hartley are OVERRULED.  

 

Merits of the Motion 

 

Fourth Cause of Action: Negligence 

 

In order to state a claim for negligence, Plaintiff must allege the elements of (1) “the existence of a legal duty of care,” (2) “breach of that duty,” and (3) “proximate cause resulting in an injury.” (McIntyre v. Colonies-Pacific, LLC (2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 664, 671.) 

 

Here, Moving Defendants first argue that Plaintiff’s fourth cause of action fails as alleged against Westin Operator because the evidence shows that Westin Operator had no employment relationship with and exercised no control over Defendant John Doe at the time of the incident, and thus Westin Operator cannot be held vicariously liable.   

 

When determining whether a worker qualifies as an employee or independent contractor for purposes of determining whether vicarious liability applies, the court applies the “ABC Test.” “The ABC test presumptively considers all workers to be employees, and permits workers to be classified as independent contractors only if the hiring business demonstrates that the worker in question satisfies all of the following three conditions: (a) that the worker is free from the control and direction of the hirer in connection with the performance of the work, both under the contract for the performance of the work and in fact; and (b) that the worker performs work that is outside the usual course of the hiring entity's business; and (c) that the worker is customarily engaged in an independently established trade, occupation, or business of the same nature as that involved in the work performed.” Dynamex Operations W. v. Superior Court (2018) 4 Cal.5th 903, 955-956 (Dynamex). 

 

In support, Moving Defendants offer the declaration of Iliana Thomas (“Thomas”), the former Director of Wesco Specialist, who declares that Wesco Specialist “operated as an independent contractor providing shuttle transportation services pursuant to a written Shuttle Services Agreement with Westin.” (Thomas Decl., ¶ 2.) Thomas declares that Wesco Specialist formerly employed Defendant John Doe. (Thomas Decl., ¶ 3.) Thomas further declares that Defendant John Doe “signed an acknowledgment confirming, among other things, that he was employed by Wesco---a third-party contractor—and not by the Westin hotel.” (Thomas Decl., ¶ 5.) Thomas declares that Wesco Specialist was “solely responsible” for the hiring, training, supervision, compensation, benefits, and personnel records of Defendant John Doe. (Thomas Decl., ¶ 5.) Moving Defendants have also provided copies of the Shuttle Services Agreement between Westin Operator and Wesco Specialist and Wesco Specialist’s Employment Acknowledgment, signed by Defendant John Doe, attached as Exhibits A and D to the Thomas Declaration, respectively. Thomas Decl., Exhs. A, D.)  

 

The court has reviewed Exhibit A attached to the Thomas Declaration and finds that the Shuttle Services Agreement expressly provides that individuals providing shuttle transportation services to the Westin hotel from LAX are employees of Wesco Specialist and not of Westin Operator and that Wesco Specialist exercises control over payroll records, payroll deductions, benefits, liability insurance, and workers’ compensation insurance. (Thomas Decl., Exh. A., p. 4.)  

The court has also reviewed Exhibit D attached to the Thomas Declaration and finds that the Employment Acknowledgement provides that signatories, including Defendant John Doe, acknowledge that they are employees of Wesco Specialist and that Wesco Specialist will determine the signatory’s pay rate and benefits. (Thomas Decl., Exh. D.)  

 

Moving Defendants also offer the declaration of Todd Hartley (“Hartley”), an employee of Westin Operator, who declares that Defendant John Doe “is not employed by Westin. Westin did not participate in or have authority over the hiring of John Doe, did not provide him with training, and did not supervise or direct the manner in which he performed his work.” (Hartley Decl., ¶ 3.)  

 

The court finds that Moving Defendants’ evidence is sufficient to establish that no triable issue of material fact exists regarding the ABC test, being that Defendant John Doe was free from the control and direction of Westin Operator, operation of shuttle buses is outside the usual course of hotel operations, and bus operations were the normal part of John Doe’s occupation.  

 

Next, Moving Defendants argue that Plaintiff’s fourth cause of action fails because the evidence does not establish that Defendant John Doe acted negligently, such that there can be no respondeat superior liability. Here, Moving Defendants argue that Plaintiff was a trespasser who was attempting to enter the shuttle without authorization, and thus Moving Defendants owed only an ordinary duty of care. 

 

Civil Code Section 2100 establishes a heightened duty of care owed by common carriers, providing that “[a] carrier of persons for reward must use the utmost care and diligence for their safe carriage, must provide everything necessary for that purpose, and must exercise that end to a reasonable degree of skill.”  

 

The common carrier-passenger relationship which creates a heightened duty begins when, “’one, intending in good faith to become a passenger, goes to the place designated as the site of departure at the appropriate time and the carrier takes some action indicating acceptance of the passenger as a traveler’” and ends when the passenger has safely exited the carrier’s vehicle. (Orr v. Pacific Southwest Airlines (1989) 208 Cal.App.3d 1467, 1473, internal citation omitted; Simon v. Walt Disney World Co. (2004) 114 Cal.App.4th 1162, 1170.) Before the relationship is created between common carrier and passenger, the common carrier owes prospective passengers only a general duty of care rather than Section 2100’s utmost standard of care. (Orr, supra, 208 Cal.App.3d at 1472.) 

 

Civil Code Section 1714(a) establishes a general duty of ordinary care, and provides that “[e]veryone is responsible, not only for the result of his or her willful acts, but also for an injury occasioned to another by his or her want of ordinary care or skill in the management of his or her property or person, except so far as the latter has, willfully or by want of ordinary care, brought the injury upon himself or herself.”  

 

Here, Moving Defendants offer excerpts from Plaintiff’s deposition transcript, attached as Exhibit A to the declaration of Derek M. Mayor (“Mayor”). Plaintiff testified that he waited at terminal 5 of LAX for the shuttle to the Westin hotel, but the shuttle passed Plaintiff. (Mayor Decl., Exh. A, 33:4-6.) Plaintiff testified that he sprinted after the shuttle, caught up to it, and “tapped into the shuttle.” (Mayor Decl., Exh. A, 33:7-15.) Next, Plaintiff testified that Defendant John Doe told Plaintiff that he refused to drive him to the hotel, after which Defendant John Doe “got off the shuttle.” (Mayor Decl., Exh. A, 34:12-20.) Plaintiff subsequently clarified that Defendant John Doe asked Plaintiff why he hit the shuttle before Defendant John Doe told Plaintiff that he would not drive him. (Mayor Decl., Exh. A, 36:22-37:6.) Plaintiff testified that he believed that Defendant John Doe was upset because Plaintiff “hit” or “tapped the end of the shuttle” to get Defendant John Doe’s attention. (Mayor Decl., Exh. A, 39:17-19.) Plaintiff testified that he believed that his striking the shuttle “frustrated [Defendant John Doe] enough to – to stop driving the shuttle or to stop driving [Plaintiff].” (Mayor Decl., Exh. A, 39:17-21.)  

 

Next, Plaintiff testified that after Defendant John Doe exited the shuttle, Plaintiff remained inside the shuttle alone for a few minutes before exiting. (Mayor Decl., Exh. A, 36:10-12; 43:1-2.) Plaintiff testified that after Defendant John Doe reboarded the shuttle, Plaintiff attempted to reboard when Defendant John Doe closed the shuttle doors. (Mayor Decl., Exh. A, 36:13-15.) Plaintiff testified that when Plaintiff stepped onto the shuttle stairs, Defendant John Doe closed the shuttle door, after which Plaintiff opened the door, and Defendant John Doe kicked Plaintiff’s right knee. (Mayor Decl., Exh. A, 43:7-24; 45:16-17.)  

 

Moving Defendants also offer a video produced by Plaintiff of the incident, attached as Exhibit B to the Mayor Declaration. The court has reviewed the video and notes that it depicts the shuttle doors closing entirely before Plaintiff apparently forcibly opens the shuttle doors. (Mayor Decl., Exh. B.) It is not apparent from the video itself that Defendant John Doe made contact with Plaintiff’s leg.  

 

The court finds that Moving Defendants’ evidence is sufficient to establish that the common carrier-passenger relationship had not yet begun as Defendant John Doe had not taken any action indicating his acceptance of Plaintiff as a passenger. Indeed, Moving Defendants’ evidence indicates that Defendant John Doe had expressly refused to transport Plaintiff.  

 

Therefore, applying the ordinary standard of care, the court finds that Moving Defendants have met their burden of establishing that no triable issue of material fact exists as to whether Moving Defendants may be held liable for Defendant John Doe’s conduct under respondeat superior. The court finds that the evidence indicates that Plaintiff’s injuries were brought upon by himself after he attempted to make an unauthorized entry onto the shuttle. Further, regarding the evidence of Defendant John Doe kicking Plaintiff, an employer will not be held liable for an intentional tort that “did not have a causal nexus to the employee’s work.” (Lisa M. v. Henry Mayo Newhall Memorial (1995) 12 Cal.4th 291, 297.)  

 

An intentional tort is only foreseeable for the purposes of the respondeat superior doctrine if “in the context of the particular enterprise an employee’s conduct is not so unusual or startling that it would seem unfair to include the loss resulting from it among other costs of the employer’s business.” (Rodgers v. Kemper Constr. Co., (1975), 50 Cal.App.3d 608, 619.) For the harm to be foreseeable, the “employment must be such as predictably to create the risk employees will commit intentional torts of the type for which liability is sought.” (Lisa M., supra, 12 Cal.4th at 302.) 

 

The court finds that Moving Defendants’ evidence does not indicate that the shuttle driver role carries an inherent risk of tortious injury. Therefore, the court finds that Moving Defendants have met their burden of establishing that no triable issue of material fact exists as to whether Moving Defendants may be held liable for the conduct of Defendant John Doe. Next, the burden shifts to Plaintiff. The court has previously found that Plaintiff’s opposition is procedurally defective in that no evidence was presented, such that Plaintiff does not meet his burden.  

 

Additionally, and in the alternative, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure Section 437c(b)(3), the court exercises its discretion to grant Moving Defendants’ motion for Plaintiff’s failure to file a code-compliant separate statement.  

 

Accordingly, Moving Defendants’ Motion for Summary Judgment is GRANTED.  

 

Fifth Cause of Action: Negligent Hiring, Supervision, and Retention 

 

The necessarily elements for negligent hiring are: (1) that the employer hired the employee; (2) who was incompetent or unfit; (3) that the employer had reason to believe undue risk of harm would exist because of the employment; and (4) that harm occurred. (Federico v. Superior Court (1997) 59 Cal.App.4th 1207, 1213-1214.) 

 

First, Moving Defendants reiterate their argument that Plaintiff’s fifth cause of action fails against Westin Operator because Defendant John Doe was not an employee of Westin Operator. The court refers to its previous discussion regarding Moving Defendants’ evidence of Defendant John Doe’s employment status. 

 

Next, Moving Defendants argue that Plaintiff’s fifth cause of action fails because there is no evidence that Defendant John Doe was unfit for his position or that Moving Defendants had reason to believe that undue risk of harm would exist because of Defendant John Doe’s employment.  

 

In support, Moving Defendants offer the Thomas Declaration, in which Thomas declares that Defendant John Doe received a copy of Wesco Specialist’s Policy Against Harassment and Personal Appearance and Behavior, completed “Sexual Harassment and Abusive Conduct Prevention” training, and completed “Practice Safe Driving, Injury & Safety Reporting” training. (Thomas Decl., ¶ 6.) Copies of Defendant John Doe’s acknowledgement of his receipt of Wesco Specialist’s policy, a certificate confirming that Defendant John Doe completed the Sexual Harassment and Abusive Conduct Prevention training, and Wesco Specialist’s employee training roster for 2021 are attached to the Thomas Declaration as Exhibits E, F, and G, respectively. (Thomas Decl., Exhs. E-G.)  

 

Thomas also declares that to her knowledge, throughout the duration of Defendant John Doe’s tenure at Wesco Specialist, he “did not engage in any prohibited behavior, and there are no disciplinary reports reflecting any prior complaints, misconduct, or similar incidents.” (Thomas Decl., ¶ 6.)  

 

Thomas further declares that prior to hiring Defendant John Doe, Wesco Specialist “conducted a background screening report on [Defendant John Doe’s] criminal history, which found no issues.” (Thomas Decl., ¶ 4.) A copy of Defendant John Doe’s background screening report is attached to the Thomas Declaration as Exhibit C. (Thomas Decl., Exh. C.) 

 

Finally, Thomas declares that Defendant John Doe “agreed to a series of driver qualifications as conditions for employment with Wesco, including that he could not have any license disqualification or suspensions, no felony charges, reckless driving charges, careless driving charges, or exhibition driving charges in the past five years, and no at-fault motor vehicle accidents within the past three years, among other things.” (Thomas Decl., ¶ 3.) A copy of Wesco Specialist’s driver qualifications, signed by Defendant John Doe, is attached to the Thomas Declaration as Exhibit B. (Thomas Decl., Exh. B.)  

 

The court has reviewed the evidence and concludes that Moving Defendants have met their burden of establishing that no triable issue of material fact exists regarding whether Defendant John Doe was unfit and whether Moving Defendants had reason to believe that undue risk of harm would exist because of Defendant John Doe’s employment as a shuttle driver. 

 

The burden next shifts to Plaintiff to establish the existence of a triable issue of material fact. The Plaintiff has furnished no evidence in opposition. Therefore, Plaintiff has not met his burden.  

 

In the alternative, the court exercises its discretion pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure Section 437c(b)(3) to grant Moving Defendants’ motion for Plaintiff’s failure to file a code-compliant separate statement. 

 

Therefore, as only Plaintiff’s fourth and fifth causes of action are alleged against Moving Defendants, Moving Defendants’ Motion for Summary Judgment is GRANTED.  

 

CONCLUSION 

 

Based on the foregoing, Moving Defendants’ Motion for Summary Judgment is GRANTED.  

 

Wesco Services, Inc. and Westin Operator, LLC are ordered to file a proposed judgment consistent with this ruling and to give notice to Plaintiff. 

Case Number: 26TRCV01089    Hearing Date: July 2, 2026    Dept: P

Motion to Compel Arbitration 

Moving Party: Defendant American Honda Motor Co., Inc.
Responding Party: Plaintiff No Name Given Sagar 

RULING

The court considered the moving papers, opposition, and reply. Honda’s Motion to Compel Arbitration is DENIED. 
 

PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

On March 25, 2026, Plaintiff No Name Given Sagar (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Defendants American Honda Motor Co., Inc. (“Honda”) and DOES 1 through 20, inclusive, alleging three causes of action for: (1) Breach of Express Warranty Under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act; (2) Breach of Implied Warranty Under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act; and (3) Violation of the Song-Beverly Act Section 1793.2(b).

On May 11, 2026, Honda filed the instant motion. On June 18, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition. On June 25, 2026, Honda filed a reply. 

FACTUAL BACKGROUND

Plaintiff alleges that on June 8, 2025, Plaintiff purchased a new 2025 Honda CR-V (the “Subject Vehicle”). (Complaint, ¶ 6.) Plaintiff alleges that in connection with his purchase of the Subject Vehicle, he received written warranties from Honda. (Complaint, ¶ 7.) Plaintiff alleges that the Subject Vehicle was delivered to him with serious defects and nonconformities to warranty and developed additional defects and nonconformities to warranty. (Complaint, ¶ 9.) Plaintiff alleges that he has delivered the Subject Vehicle to Honda’s authorized repair facilities on numerous occasions, but Honda has failed to conform the Subject Vehicle to the applicable warranties. (Complaint, ¶¶ 17-18.) 
LEGAL STANDARD

Parties may be compelled to arbitrate a dispute upon the court finding that: (1) there was a valid agreement to arbitrate between the parties; and (2) said agreement covers the controversy or controversies in the parties’ dispute. (Code Civ. Proc. Section 1281.2.) California law favors enforcement of valid arbitration agreements. (Armendariz v. Foundation Health Psychcare Services, Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 83, 97.) A party petitioning to compel arbitration has the burden of establishing the existence of a valid agreement to arbitrate and the party opposing the petition has the burden of proving, by a preponderance of the evidence, any fact necessary to its defense. (Banner Entertainment, Inc. v. Superior Court (1998) 62 Cal.App.4th 348, 356-357.)

The Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”) applies to any contract evidencing a transaction involving interstate commerce which contains an arbitration clause. (Wolls v. Superior Court (2005) 127 Cal.App.4th 197, 211.) Section 2 of the FAA provides that arbitration provisions shall be enforced, save upon grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract. (Ibid.) A state court may refuse to enforce an arbitration clause on the basis of generally applicable contract defenses, such as fraud, duress, or unconscionability. (Ibid.) A state court, however, may not defeat an arbitration clause by applying state laws applicable only to arbitration provisions. (Ibid.)

For the FAA to apply, a contract must involve interstate commerce. (Ibid.) When it applies, the FAA preempts any state law rule that stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment of the FAA’s objectives. (Carbajal v. CWPSC, Inc. (2016) 245 Cal.App.4th 227, 238.) A party asserting FAA preemption bears the burden to present evidence establishing a contract with the arbitration provision affects interstate commerce, and the failure to do so renders the FAA inapplicable. (Ibid.) Evidence must be presented, in the form of declarations or other evidence, that establishes that the contract affects interstate commerce. (Ibid.) “[S]ince arbitration is a matter of contract, the FAA also applies if it is so stated in the agreement.” (Davis v. Shiekh Shoes, LLC (2022) 84 Cal.App.5th 956, 963.)

“On petition of a party to an arbitration agreement alleging the existence of a written agreement to arbitrate a controversy and that a party thereto refuses to arbitrate such controversy, the court shall order the petitioner and the respondent to arbitrate the controversy if it determines that an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists, unless it determines that: (a) the right to compel arbitration has been waived by the petitioner; or (b) grounds exist for the revocation of the agreement.” (Code Civ. Proc. Section 1281.2(a-b).) Thus, in assessing a motion to compel arbitration, the moving party “bears the burden of proving the existence of a valid arbitration agreement by a preponderance of the evidence, and a party opposing the [motion] bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence any fact necessary to its defense.” (Giuliano v. Inland Empire Personnel, Inc. (2007) 149 Cal.App.4th 1276, 1284, citation omitted.)

“If a court of competent jurisdiction . . . has ordered arbitration of a controversy which is an issue involved in an action or proceeding pending before a court of this State, the court in which such action or proceeding is pending shall, upon motion of a party to such action or proceeding, stay the action or proceeding until an arbitration is had in accordance with the order to arbitrate or until such earlier time as the court specifies.” (Code Civ. Proc. Section 1281.4.)

DISCUSSION  

Moving Party’s Argument

Honda argues that Plaintiff agreed to arbitrate all claims against Honda because the limited warranty provided to Plaintiff by Honda in connection with his purchase of the Subject Vehicle (the “Warranty Booklet”) contains an arbitration provision. Honda also argues that Plaintiff entered into a Retail Installment Sale Contract (the “RISC”) with a vehicle dealer which also contained an arbitration provision. Honda argues that equitable estoppel applies because the parties’ arbitration agreement is part of the same warranty contract upon which Plaintiff’s claims are based. Honda argues that the parties’ arbitration agreement in the Warranty Booklet is not procedurally or substantively unconscionable because Plaintiff was given an opportunity to opt-out, the arbitration agreement is mutual, Honda has agreed to pay virtually all costs of arbitration, and the AAA rules are fair.

Opposing Party’s Argument

Plaintiff argues that Honda cannot compel arbitration based on the RISC because Honda did not address the RISC in its notice of the instant motion, and that regardless, the California Supreme Court’s decision in the Ford Motor Warranty Cases prevents a non-signatory vehicle manufacturer from compelling arbitration of Song-Beverly Act claims based on an arbitration provision in the vehicle dealer’s sales contract. Plaintiff also argues that he is not fluent in English and thus there was no mutual assent to enter into an arbitration agreement. Plaintiff argues that equitable estoppel does not apply here because Plaintiff’s claims do not arise solely out of the RISC or the Warranty Booklet.

Reply Argument

Honda argues that its motion is based on the Warranty Booklet, and thus Plaintiff improperly focuses his opposition on the arbitration provision contained in the RISC. Honda argues that Plaintiff has not denied that he received the warranty Booklet and has not claimed to have exercised his right to opt out of arbitration. As a result, Honda argues that Plaintiff has failed to meet his burden of challenging the existence of the parties’ arbitration agreement. Honda argues that the fact that Plaintiff is not fluent in English does not relieve him from the obligations of the parties’ arbitration agreement because Plaintiff has not argued that the contents of the Warranty Booklet were misrepresented or that he was prevented from reviewing it. Honda again clarifies that it seeks enforcement of the arbitration provision contained in the Warranty Booklet and does not seek to compel arbitration based on the RISC. 

Merits of the Motion

A. Existence of a Valid Arbitration Agreement

The party moving to compel arbitration must establish the existence of a written arbitration agreement between the parties. (Code of Civ. Proc. Section 1281.2.) This is usually done by presenting a copy of the signed, written agreement to the court. “A petition to compel arbitration or to stay proceedings pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 1281.2 and 1281.4 must state, in addition to other required allegations, the provisions of the written agreement and the paragraph that provides for arbitration. The provisions must be stated verbatim or a copy must be physically or electronically attached to the petition and incorporated by reference.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1330.)

The Warranty

Here, Honda moves to compel arbitration on the grounds that Plaintiff assented to an arbitration provision found within Honda’s Warranty Booklet for 2025 model year vehicles (the “Warranty Booklet”). In support of its motion, Honda offers the declaration of Honda’s counsel, Michael J. Hurvitz. Attached to the Hurvitz Declaration as Exhibit B is a copy of the Warranty Booklet. 

The Warranty Booklet contains an arbitration provision stating the following:

“You and we agree that any dispute arising out of or relating to any aspect of the relationship between you and Honda will not be decided by a judge or jury but instead by a single arbitration administered by the American Arbitration Association (AAA) under its Consumer Arbitration Rules in effect on the date of delivery of your vehicle to you. This Agreement to Arbitrate includes all claims, whether based in contract, tort, statute, fraud, misrepresentation or any other legal theory; claims arising out of your warranty; claims arising before or after this Agreement, such as claims related to statements about our products; claims about the performance, design of our products, or manufacturing of our products; and claims that are currently the subject of purported class action litigation in which you are not a member of a certified class.” (Hurvitz Decl., Exh. B, p. 7.) 

However, the Warranty Booklet also contains an opt-out provision which states the following:

“You may opt out of arbitration within 30 days after the date of delivery of your vehicle to you by sending a letter to: Honda’s Office of the General Counsel, 1919 Torrance Blvd., Mail Stop CHI-5, Torrance, CA 90501, stating your name, Vehicle Identification Number, and intent to opt out of the arbitration provision. If you do not opt out, then this agreement to arbitrate is binding.” (Hurvitz Decl., Exh. B, p. 8.)

The Hurvitz Declaration is silent as to whether this opt-out provision was invoked by Plaintiff and whether there was any notice to Plaintiff of the existence of the Warranty Booklet or the arbitration provision therein. Without such evidence, Honda fails to meet its burden of showing that there is a valid arbitration agreement to which Plaintiff assented. Therefore, the court cannot compel arbitration under the terms of the Warranty Booklet.

Accordingly, Honda’s Motion to Compel Arbitration is DENIED.

CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, Honda’s Motion to Compel Arbitration is DENIED.

Moving party is ordered to give notice of the court’s ruling.
