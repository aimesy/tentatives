23STCV24476 Jose Hernandez v. Koos Manufacturing, Inc.

Tuesday, August 11, 2026

 

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING PLAINTIFF, JOSE L. HERNANDEZ’S, MOTION FOR EVIDENTIARY SANCTIONS TO EXCLUDE DR. CRANDALL’S REPORT AND, TESTIMONY, AND/OR FOR MONETARY SANCTIONS (7052)

 

I.         BACKGROUND

      The first amended complaint alleges that Plaintiff was subjected to discrimination based on his disabilities while employed by Defendant. Plaintiff alleges seven causes of action for violations of the Fair Employment and Housing Act and for unfair business practices.

II.        ARGUMENTS

      Plaintiff requests an order to exclude from evidence at trial all testimony, reports, opinions, findings, conclusions, and materials relating to the defense mental examination of Plaintiff conducted by Dr. Rebecca Crandall on February 19, 2026; and for an order prohibiting Dr. Crandall from testifying at trial, whether as a percipient witness or expert witness, regarding any observations, findings, opinions, or conclusions derived from that defense mental examination.

      Plaintiff argues that Dr. Crandall violated the parties’ stipulation limiting the exam to certain issues and excluding inquiry into Plaintiff’s immigration status and other highly personal background matters. Dr. Crandall based her opinion on matters that the parties’ agreed would be excluded. Plaintiff also asks for imposition of monetary sanctions.

      In opposition, Defendant contends that Dr. Crandall complied with the terms and requirements of the stipulation. The mental examination is directly relevant to Plaintiff’s claim for emotional distress. Plaintiff opened the door into the specific line of inquiry about his immigration status. Plaintiff contradicts his privacy claims by filing the transcript and expert report. The Court should impose monetary sanctions against Plaintiff.

      In reply, Plaintiff argues that Defendant’s opposition confirms the fact that Dr. Crandall received the parties’ stipulation before the examination and then conducted the exam as if the stipulation did not exist. Dr. Crandall did not articulate any good faith basis to justify inquiry into prohibited areas.

III.       DISCUSSION

A.     The Court’s authority to impose sanctions.  

      The Court has the inherent authority to impose sanctions including monetary, issue, and evidentiary sanctions for a party’s abuse of the discovery process. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030.) Conduct subject to such sanctions include, in relevant part, "employing a discovery method in a manner or to an extent that causes unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression, or undue burden and expense." (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.010.)

      Defendant does not disagree with this proposition but contends that in the absence of unusual circumstances, such as repeated and egregious discovery abuses, there must be two factors present to justify imposition of nonmonetary sanctions: that there was a failure to comply with a Court order, and such failure was willful.

      As Plaintiff observes in reply, this is contrary to the California Supreme Court’s holding that a trial court may rely on Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.010 and section 2023.030 as an independent source of sanctions “only when confronted with an unusual form of discovery abuse, or a pattern of abuse, not already addressed by a relevant sanctions provision.” (City of Los Angeles v. PricewaterhouseCoopers, LLP (2024) 17 Cal.5th 46, 74.) Imposition of sanctions under these sections are not limited to “extreme misconduct” or a pattern of discovery abuse. (Agnone v. Agnone (2025) 111 Cal.App.5th 758, 765.

      Defendant’s argument that Plaintiff waived privacy claims by filing this motion with the transcript of the examination and the expert report is immaterial to the issue Plaintiff raises which is that the parties’ stipulated to limiting Dr. Crandall’s inquiries, and Dr. Crandall exceeded those limitations although she was given the stipulation with the relevant parameters. (Crandall decl., ¶ 5).

B.     The stipulation

      The stipulation sets forth the parameters of the examination: permissible inquiry was limited to “(i) Plaintiff’s symptoms, complaints, diagnoses, treatment, medications, and functional limitations; and (ii) Plaintiff’s work history with Defendant and the alleged workplace

events at issue.” (Hynes decl., Ex. 1, 2:20-23.) In relevant part, Dr. Crandall could not inquire about the following topics:

“Inquiry into developmental/childhood history, family history,  marital/ relationship history, financial history, immigration status, criminal/legal history, and other highly personal background matters is prohibited unless (a) Plaintiff affirmatively places the specific subject matter in issue during the interview, or (b) the examiner articulates a good-faith basis that the specific line of questioning is reasonably necessary to assess causation of the emotional distress

damages claimed in this lawsuit.

 

      Thus, by the terms of the stipulation the limitations applied unless Plaintiff “affirmatively places the specific subject matter in issue during the interview, or (b) the examiner articulates a good-faith basis that the specific line of questioning is reasonably necessary to assess causation of the emotional distress.” (Id.)

      Plaintiff argues that “articulate[]” means “an express articulation on the record  before the agreed-upon boundaries could be crossed.” (Mot.  11:13-14.) Plaintiff argues that “the transcript is completely silent on any such attempts. The absence of any good-faith articulation is not a technical deficiency—it is the mechanism the parties negotiated specifically to address this outcome.” (Id.) 11:15-16.

      Defendant argues that the stipulation did not require Dr. Randall to state her good-faith basis on the record for the line of questioning prior to inquiry. Dr. Crandall was not prevented from following up on underlying facts when Plaintiff opens the door. (Opp.  9:16-19.) Defendant contends because Plaintiff referred to “the safety of immigrants, ‘people with a Hispanic face’, and feeling ‘persecuted’ in the current environment of immigration enforcement, Plaintiff affirmatively place his immigration status at issue.

      However, those concerns were preceded by Dr. Crandall’s inquiry into his marital status and why he needed to get married now, a topic about which she could not inquire. This was preceded by questions concerning Plaintiff’s family history and marital/relationship history, which was precluded from inquiry, which later morphed into questions about his childhood, a topic also precluded by stipulation.

      Dr. Crandall started with an inquiry into his family history. (Tr. 4:25-5:1) This prohibited inquiry continued through pages 5 through 16 and covered whether his sister financially supported him, whether he moved from his brother’s house because he had enough money to support himself, followed by “Do you remember the year you got married.” (Tr. 16: 23-24.) Dr. Crandall inquired about his girlfriend, marital status, who he had children with, why he separated from his girlfriend, where his child lived, and whether he got another partner.

      Dr. Crandall asked for the reasons Plaintiff wanted to get married, which Plaintiff gave. When pressed, Plaintiff said “I already told you some. I don’t know what else to tell you.” Dr. Crandall said, “You didn’t tell me, actually.” Plaintiff mentioned “changes in this country.” (Tr. 21:19-22.) Plaintiff said “I don’t know. I feel like I’m being persecuted, or like, I don’t understand. Maybe I don’t feel very confident, like stuff like that.” (21:25-2:2.)

      This colloquy followed:

      Dr: Crandall: “Do you feel like you could be arrested because there's a lot of people going toward like someone who may be considered illegal immigrant?”

      Plaintiff: “Well, that's what the news are saying right now recently, only because they see a face that's Hispanic, they want to arrest you.”

      Dr. Crandall: Are you a legal citizen?· Are you a citizen of the United States?

      Plaintiff: I would prefer not to answer that ·question.

      Dr. Crandall: Okay. Okay. Is your potential is the woman that's your partner, is she a citizen of the United States?

      Plaintiff: The same. I would prefer not to answer.

      Dr. Crandall: Okay. Okay.· I can see why that is unsettling, the current situation in the country. So I'm going to ask you some questions that are just standard that I ask in all of my evaluations. Okay?” (Tr. 22:4.)

      To say that the word “persecution” opened the door is to ignore that the sixth question posed after preliminary questions about process was “where were you born” and “who was in your family.” 4:25-5:1.  

      Dr. Crandall did not articulate why the interview started with family history, which was outside the scope of inquiry. Defendant contends that on-the-record articulation was not required. It is not clear how anyone could evaluate Dr. Crandall’s reasoning for a good-faith basis for a particular inquiry if she did not express it out loud. The dictionary definition requires an outward expression.

      Articulate: “expressing oneself readily, clearly, and effectively.” (Merriam-Webster.com)

      Express: “a: to represent in words: STATE; b: to make known the opinions or feelings of (oneself).” (Id.)

      Dr. Crandall continued inquiring about lawsuits, legal history, or whether Plaintiff had ever been arrested. (Tr. 35:8-13.) This prohibited inquiry was not preceded by an articulation of a good faith basis to justify it. Rather, Dr. Crandall asked about spots on Plaintiff’s skin. (Id.)

      Dr. Crandall went on to discuss financial issues, who Plaintiff supported, and whether that was stressful. (Tr. 41:7-42:2.)

      These topics were outside the scope of inquiry and Dr. Crandall did not articulate a good-faith basis for her inquiry. 

 

C.     Imposition of sanctions is warranted.

      Dr. Crandall declares she received a draft and final version of the stipulation before conducting the exam. (Crandall decl., ¶ 5.)  However, as the transcript shows, she did not confine inquiry according to the parties’ stipulation.

      Plaintiff’s counsel incurred $5,460 to prepare the motion, review the opposition, prepare the reply and to appear which the Court finds reasonable. Costs of $60 were incurred for the filing fee.

      In addition, the Court imposes evidentiary sanctions as articulated below.

       IV.       CONCLUSION

      Accordingly, Plaintiff’s motion is GRANTED. The Court imposes monetary sanctions against Koos Manufacturing, Inc. and its counsel of record, Buchalter, LLP, jointly and severally, in the amount of $5,460 payable to Plaintiff within 10 days.

      The Court imposes evidentiary sanctions:

1)     The Court excludes from evidence at trial all testimony, reports, opinions, findings, conclusions, and materials arising from or related to the defense mental examination of Plaintiff conducted by Dr. Rebecca Crandall on February 19, 2026; and

2)     Dr. Rebecca Crandall is prohibited from testifying at trial, whether as a percipient

witness or expert witness, regarding any observations, findings, opinions, or conclusions

derived from the February 19, 2026, defense mental examination.


Case Number: 25STCV15725    Hearing Date: August 11, 2026    Dept: 414

25STCV15725 Larios v. American Honda Motor, Co., Inc.

Tuesday, August 11, 2026

 

[TENTATIVE] ORDER GRANTING DEFENDANT, AMERICAN HONDA MOTOR CO., INC.’S, MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT OR IN THE ALTERNATIVE, MOTION FOR SUMMARY ADJUDICATION (8732)

 

I.       BACKGROUND

      The complaint alleges violations of the Song Beverly Consumer Warranty Act in connection with a warranty contract issued by Defendant for a 2018 Honda Civic, purchased by Plaintiff on July 2, 2020. Defendant now moves for summary judgment or alternatively for summary adjudication of all claims on grounds the vehicle does not qualify as a new motor vehicle under the SBA, and Defendant did not issue a new vehicle limited warranty to Plaintiff. The SBA does not apply.

II.     LEGAL STANDARDS

      Summary judgment is proper “if all the papers submitted show that the material facts are undisputed and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c subd. (c).) The moving party defendant’s burden is to show that based on the undisputed facts “one or more elements of the cause of action, even if not separately pleaded, cannot be established, or that there is a complete defense to the cause of action." (.) If that threshold burden is met, the burden shifts to the opposing party to show a triable issue of one or more material facts. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c (p)(2).

      A party may move for summary adjudication “as to one or more causes of action within an action, one or more affirmative defenses, one or more claims for damages, or one or more issues of duty, if the party contends that the cause of action has no merit, that there is no affirmative defense to the cause of action, that there is no merit to an affirmative defense as to any cause of action, [or] that there is no merit to a claim for [punitive] damages. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c (f)(1).) A motion for summary adjudication shall be granted only if it completely disposes of a cause of action, an affirmative defense, a claim for damages, or an issue of duty. (Id.)

      The court applies the three-step analysis to motions for summary judgment or adjudication: (1) identify the issues framed by the pleading, (2) determine whether the moving party established facts which negate the opponents’ claim, (3) if the moving party meets its threshold burden of persuasion and the burden shifts, determine whether the opposing party has controverted those facts with admissible evidence. (Torres v. Reardon (1992) 3 Cal.App.4th 831, 836.)

III.    DISCUSSION

A.     Defendant’s separate statement

      Plaintiff argues that Defendant’s separate statement does not comply with the California Rules of Court because the separate statement does not identify the particular issue to be adjudicated with references to the material facts under each issue and each cause of action. Defendant did not identify what element of a claim is being challenged and the supporting material facts relevant to that issue.

      The separate statement is not defective. The issue asserted is not complicated; Plaintiffs do not have a remedy under the SBA because they bought the vehicle used. This is the issue asserted to defeat each of four causes of action alleged: (1) Violation of Civil Code § 1793.2 (d); (2) Violation of Civil Code § 1793.2 (b); (3) Violation of Civil Code § 1793.2 (a) (3); breach of the implied warranty of merchantability under Civil Code § 1791.1; 1794.

      Requiring Defendant to assert the same nine facts separately under each separately identified cause of action would be redundant and would serve form over substance.     

B.     Applicability of the Act to used vehicles

      There is no dispute that Galpin Honda sold Plaintiffs the 2018 Honda Civic with a Certified Pre-Owned Warranty (“CPO”). (UF 1, 4.) Plaintiff does not dispute that because they purchased the vehicle used, they did not receive the full New Vehicle Limited Warranty coverage. (UF 7.)

      Subpart (b) of Civil Code section 1793.2 requires the manufacturer to commence service and repair within a reasonable time where the goods do not conform with the applicable express warranties. Subpart (d)(2) requires replacement of the vehicle or reimbursement to the buyer where the manufacturer cannot service or repair after a reasonable number of attempts. (Civ. Code, § 1793.2)

      Defendant contends all the remedies under 1793.2 do not apply to used vehicles. Section 1793.2 (d)(2) is the only subpart referencing “new motor vehicle” which includes in pertinent part, “a dealer-owned vehicle and a ‘demonstrator’ or other motor vehicle sold with a manufacturer's new car warranty …” (Rodriguez v. FCA US LLC (2024) 17 Cal.5th 189, 206.)

      The California Supreme Court stated that "just as with new products, the Act's remedies for failure to repair a used product in conformity with an express warranty have applicability where the express warranty accompanies the sale of the product. For new products, liability extends to the manufacturer; for used products, liability extends to the distributor or retail seller and not to the manufacturer, at least where the manufacturer has not issued a new warranty or played a substantial role in the sale of a used good." (Rodriguez v. FCA US LLC (2024) 17 Cal.5th 189, 202.)

      Plaintiff argues that Rodriguez decided one issue; namely the interpretation of “new vehicle” under § 1793.22 as it relates to a claim for violation of 1793.2(d)(2) requiring replacement if the vehicle does not conform to the warranty after a reasonable number of attempts. Only 1793.2(d)(2) refers to a “new vehicle.”

      Plaintiff argues that the other subsections at issue refer to “goods” or availability service and repair facilities and literature. Plaintiffs contend Defendant reads Rodriguez too broadly.  Plaintiff argues that Rodriguez did not address remedies under the remaining subparts which refer to “goods.” (Discussed in more detail below.)

      The only statutes examined by Rodriguez was limited: “Civil Code Section 1793.2, subdivision (d)(2) gives new car buyers what is known as a refund-or-replace remedy: It requires manufacturers to “promptly replace” a defective new motor vehicle or “promptly make restitution” to the buyer when the manufacturer is “unable to service or repair a new motor vehicle, as that term is defined in paragraph (2) of subdivision (e) of Section 1793.22, to conform to the applicable express warranties after a reasonable number of attempts. These enhanced remedies under the Act for breach of express warranty are ‘distinct from’ and ‘in addition to’ remedies otherwise available in contract under the California Uniform Commercial Code.

      The first cause of action seeks remedies under 1793.2(d) generally, without reference to subparts, alleging Defendant failed to promptly repair and/or replace or make restitution. (Complaint, ¶ 14-16). This remedy falls under subpart (d)(2) which requires a “new vehicle” under Rodriguez.

      Plaintiffs argue that Defendant issued a Certified Pre-owned vehicle upon purchase. (See Plaintiffs’ arguments opposing (UF 5.) A CPO vehicle is not a “new vehicle." (Crescencio v. Ford Motor Company (C.D. Cal. 2025) 811 F.Supp.3d 1132, 1137 ["Does a manufacturer's “Certified Pre-Owned” warranty, issued at the time of the sale of a used car, qualify as a manufacturer's “new car warranty” under Civil Code section 1793.22(e)(2) as interpreted in Rodriguez II? The Court concludes it does not.”]

      Accordingly, adjudication of the first cause of action is granted as it seeks the repair and replace remedy under subpart(d)(2) is GRANTED.

C.     Adjudication of the second cause of action under 1793.2(b) (time limit for service and repairs) and the third cause of action 1793.2 (a)(3) failing to provide literature.

      Section 1793.2 generally applies to “every manufacturer of consumer goods” or its agent for which the manufacturer made an express warranty. (Civ. Code § 1793.2(a), (d)(1).) Plaintiffs argue that the subparts at issue are still viable because they refer to “goods” and not “new vehicles.”

      Even if the other subparts of 1793.2 refers to goods, the statutory definition of “consumer goods” is "any new product or part thereof that is used, bought, or leased for use primarily for personal, family, or household purposes, except for clothing and consumables.” (Civ. Code, § 1791(a). Additionally, the SBA expressly provides remedies for used goods under a separate section (1795.5), a claim that Plaintiff did not allege. The complaint measures the materiality of the facts tendered in a defendant's challenge to the plaintiff's cause of action.’ [Citation.]” (Eriksson v. Nunnink (2011) 191 Cal.App.4th 826, 848.) Section 1795.5 is not material to Plaintiffs’ complaint.

      A second reason these causes of action fail is that 1793.2 imposes liability on the manufacturer. Defendant argues that Galpin Honda, the seller is a wholly independent entity from Defendant and operates as an independently owned and operated dealer. (UF 6.) David Gillum is the General Manager for Galpin Honda (Gillum decl., ¶ 1.)  Based on his personal knowledge, it is separate from Defendant and is independently owned. (Id. ¶ 2). Plaintiffs’ objections to the declarations are overruled. Who owns the dealership is not a legal conclusion.

      To dispute this material fact, Plaintiff offers the fact that Honda issues CPO warranties with Honda’s sale of their vehicles. Plaintiffs’ request for judicial notice of Honda’s certified pre-owned vehicle program downloaded from the internet is denied. First, judicial notice is not permitted as the information is not beyond dispute. (Jolley v. Chase Home Finance, LLC (2013) 213 Cal.App.4th 872, 889 [“We know of no ‘official Web site’ provision for judicial notice in California. … Simply because information is on the Internet does not mean that it is not reasonably subject to dispute.”].) Secondly, it is irrelevant as there is no evidence that this CPO was the one issued to Plaintiff or that the manufacturer issued the CPO to Plaintiff.

      Plaintiff’s evidence does not controvert the contention that Galpin is independent from the manufacturer. Plaintiff does not otherwise show that Galpin is a representative of the manufacturer. (UF 6.) Plaintiff signed the purchase agreement issued by Galpin Honda. (Gillum decl., Ex. A.)

      Accordingly, adjudication of the second and third causes of action is GRANTED.

D.     Fourth cause of action for breach of the implied warranty under Civ Code §1791.1 and §1794.

      Section 1791.1 states that there is an implied warranty of merchantability that applies to goods. (Civ. Code, § 1791.1.) As previously noted, “goods” is defined as “new goods.” Section 1794 governs buyers’ measures of damages and recovery of penalties, costs and expenses. (Civ. Code, § 1794.) There is no dispute that Plaintiffs bought the car used from Galpin who is independently owned. Therefore, whatever warranty was issued at the time of sale was not “new” and was not made by Defendant. This precludes the claim as alleged against Defendant.

      Defendant anticipates that Plaintiffs will argue in opposition that they have a remedy under Civil Code § 1795.5 which imposes obligations on the distributor or retail seller with respect to used consumer goods. (Civ. Code, § 1795.5.) Plaintiffs’ opposition cites that section. (Opp. P. 11.) Plaintiff did not assert a claim under that section. Plaintiff did not sue Galpin Ford to the extent it is the “distributor or retail seller” for purposes of the statute.

E.     Plaintiffs’ request for continuance.

      If the opposing party shows by declaration that essential evidence “may exist but cannot, for reasons stated, be presented, the court shall deny the motion” or continue it for a reasonable period, or “make any other order as may be just.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c (h).) The declaration supporting a continuance must show "(1) the facts to be obtained are essential to opposing the motion; (2) there is reason to believe such facts may exist; and (3) the reasons why additional time is needed to obtain these facts.” (Cooksey v. Alexakis (2004) 123 Cal.App.4th 246, 254.)

      Plaintiffs argue that they need to take the deposition of the person most knowledgeable to controvert Defendant’s contention that the CPO warranty is not a “new vehicle” warranty. Plaintiffs contend this will establish the existence, identity, and terms of the manufacturer-issued warranty that accompanied the sale of the Subject Vehicle.

      As noted previously a CPO vehicle is not a “new vehicle." (Crescencio v. Ford Motor Company (C.D. Cal. 2025) 811 F.Supp.3d 1132, 1137. This is a legal issue not a factual one. Moreover, "[a] good faith showing that further discovery is needed to oppose summary judgment requires some justification for why such discovery could not have been completed sooner.” (Cooksey v. Alexakis (2004) 123 Cal.App.4th 246, 257.

      Plaintiffs requested continuance via ex parte application which the Court denied. (M.O. 7.16.26.) After reviewing Plaintiffs’ efforts to complete discovery, the Court determined that Plaintiffs offered no explanation for why discovery could not have been propounded earlier. While Plaintiffs largely attribute the delay to Defendant’s continued objections, Plaintiffs did not explain at that time or now why Plaintiffs did not seek court intervention at the time.

IV.   CONCLUSION

      Based on the foregoing, Defendant’s motion for summary judgment is GRANTED.

 


Case Number: 25STCV19721    Hearing Date: August 11, 2026    Dept: 414

25STCV19721 Afzali v. Los Angeles-M, Inc., et al.

Tuesday, August 11, 2026

 

[TENTATIVE] ORDER DENYING DEFENDANTS, LOS ANGELES-M, INC. AND LITHIA MOTORS, INC.’S, MOTION TO STRIKE PUNITIVE DAMAGES FROM PLAINTIFF’S COMPLAINT (1316)

 

I.       BACKGROUND

      The complaint alleges Defendants discriminated and retaliated against Plaintiff during Plaintiff’s employment in violation of the Fair Employment and Housing Act and the Labor Code. Defendants move to strike the claim for punitive damages.

II.     LEGAL STANDARDS

      A motion to strike is limited to matters that appear on the face of the pleading or on any matter of which the court can take judicial notice. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437.) The court may strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or strike all or any part of the pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of California, a court rule, or an order of the court. (Code Civ. Proc. §436 subd. (a)-(b).)

      To support recovery of punitive damages, Plaintiff can allege ultimate facts. (Clauson v. Superior Court (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1253, 1255 ["In order to survive a motion to strike an allegation of punitive damages, the ultimate facts showing an entitlement to such relief must be pled by a plaintiff. A plaintiff may recover on a claim for exemplary damages wPlaintiff’se the defendant is guilty of oppression, fraud, or malice. (Civ. Code, § 3294 subd. (a).)

      The predicate acts to support the claim must be intended to cause injury or must constitute “malicious” or “oppressive” conduct. “Malice” is defined as “conduct which is intended by the defendant to cause injury to the plaintiff or despicable conduct which is carried on by the defendant with a willful and conscious disregard of the rights or safety of otPlaintiff’ss.” (Civ. Code, § 3294 subd. (c)(1); College Hospital Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 725 ["malice involves awareness of dangerous consequences and a willful and deliberate failure to avoid them"].) "Oppression" is defined as “despicable conduct that subjects a person subjects a person to cruel and unjust hardship in conscious disregard of that person's rights.”].) In otPlaintiff’s words, absent an intent to injure the plaintiff, the conduct must be “despicable” defined as “base, vile, or contemptible.” (College Hospital Inc. v. Superior Court (1994) 8 Cal.4th 704, 724.)

A.     Untimely filing of the motion to strike

      As Plaintiff observes, a party may file a motion to strike within the time allowed to respond to the pleading. (Code Civ. Proc., § 435.) However, the Court has discretion to strike a pleading or any part of a pleading at any time. (Code Civ. Proc., § 436.) Defendant cites Jackson v. Doe (2011) 192 Cal.App.4th 742 for the proposition that the Court may consider an untimely motion to strike "so long as its action does ‘not affect the substantial rights of the parties.” (Jackson at 750.)

      Plaintiff has timely filed an opposition, which the Court has considered. Plaintiff has not identified any substantial right that has been affected by the late filing.

B.     Judicial notice

      The Court grants Defendants’ request for judicial notice of the complaint but not of the corporate filings with the Secretary of State. Defendants’ status as corporations in fact is not necessary to consider the merits of Defendants’ motion. Plaintiff alleges that the Defendants, both corporations, are authorized to do business in California, which the Court accepts as true. (Complaint, ¶ 2.)

      The Court denies Defendants’ request to take judicial notice of another trial court’s determination on a motion to strike. The ruling did not consider Kiseskey v. Carpenters' Trust for So. California (1983) 144 Cal.App.3d 222, 235 (discussed below.)

C.     Plaintiff has adequately alleged corporate ratification.

      Liability for punitive damages will not be imposed against a corporate employer absent proof of advance knowledge of an employee’s unfitness or ratification of that employee’s conduct by an officer, director or managing agent. (Civ. Code, § 3294 (b).) The statute governs the necessary proof to impose such liability; it does not govern the standard of pleading.

      Defendants have not cited any authority that requires Plaintiff to specifically identify the person who ratified the conduct on behalf of Defendants. In Scannell v. County of Riverside (1984) 152 Cal.App.3d 596, 614, the Court determined that a motion to strike should have been granted because "plaintiff has not pleaded that acts of employees of defendant corporation were done with the knowledge or under the express direction or ratification of an officer, director or managing agent of the corporation so as to justify exemplary damages against the corporation.” (Id.)  These are ultimate facts which are sufficient to support a claim for punitive damages showing entitlement to such relief. (Clauson v. Superior Court (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 1253, 1255.)

      As Plaintiff observes in opposition, the Court, in evaluating whether the punitive damage allegations are sufficient, reads the complaint as a whole, “all parts in their context, and assume their truth;” allegations are not read in isolation. (Id.)

      Both parties cite White v. Ultramar, Inc. (1999) 21 Cal.4th 563, which appealed from a verdict after a jury trial on grounds the plaintiff did not "show that the employee exercised substantial discretionary authority over significant aspects of a corporation's business” in order to be regarded as a “managing agent.” (White at 577.) It is not instructive on the adequacy of pleading.

      Whether a principal authorized the doing and manner of the act can be alleged using “conclusional words.” (Kiseskey v. Carpenters' Trust for So. California (1983) 144 Cal.App.3d 222, 235; O'Hara v. Western Seven Trees Corp. (1977) 75 Cal.App.3d 798, 806 ["The corporate respondents contend that no facts are alleged to support punitive damages against them. But it was alleged that the misrepresentations were made by persons who acted ‘with the permission and consent’ of all the defendants. For the purpose of meeting a general demurrer, this was a sufficient allegation that the corporations had authorized their agent's acts; a corporation is liable for punitive damages when it authorizes the wrongful act."].)

      While neither party cited Kiseskey, it is instructive. There, the complaint did not identify a managing agent either by name or facts to support a finding that a managing agent ratified the conduct. Rather, the court "conclude[d] that the conclusional words ‘permission and consent’ used in the FAC, coupled with the factual allegations therein, constitute an allegation, sufficient if proven, upon which punitive damages may be assessed against defendant for the conduct of its agents." (Id. at 235.)

      Here, Plaintiff has similarly alleged that each Defendant was acting “with the permission and consent of the co-Defendants.” (Complaint, ¶ 3.) The pleading also asserts that “[a]ll actions of Defendants were taken by employees, supervisors, executives, officers, and directors during employment with Defendants, were taken on behalf of Defendants, and were engaged in, authorized, ratified, and approved of by all Defendants.” (Complaint, ¶ 4.) Corporate ratification is supported by the allegations.

D.     Plaintiff has alleged specific facts showing malice or oppression.

      In a civil action under FEHA, "all relief generally available in noncontractual actions, including punitive damages, may be obtained." (Commodore Home Systems, Inc. v. Superior Court (1982) 32 Cal.3d 211, 221, 220 ["California law long has recognized that discharges in violation of public policy may be actionable torts for which punitive damages can be recovered under Civil Code section 3294."].)" An employee’s right to be free from discrimination is a civil right, and "plaintiff's status as an employee should entitle him to a greater degree of protection from insult and outrage than if he were a stranger to defendants....”(Commodore at 220.) The right to seek and hold employment free of prejudice is fundamental and such discrimination “foments domestic strife and unrest, deprives the state of the fullest utilization of its capacities for development and advance, and substantially and adversely affects the interest of employees, employers, and the public in general. (§ 12920.) The statute's aim is to provide effective remedies against the evil." (Id.)

      The complaint alleges that Plaintiff suffered disabling injuries in a non-work-related accident. (Complaint, ¶ 50.)  Plaintiff sent Defendants regular progress notes from Plaintiff’s doctor supporting each request for additional time off. (Complaint, ¶ 51-52.) Four months later, Plaintiff informed Defendants that Plaintiff was ready to return to work and asked what Plaintiff  needed to do in order to return. (Complaint, ¶ 53.) The complaint alleges that “Plaintiff was informed that Defendants are not doing any rehires and that Plaintiff has been fired. In response by another supervisor, Plaintiff was informed that Plaintiff should have returned to work a while ago and that Plaintiff cannot return to work anymore.” (Complaint, ¶ 53.) Plaintiff alleges the misconduct violated FEHA which precludes discrimination and retaliation on the basis of disability.

      The complaint alleges that Plaintiff’s medical condition, medical leave, protected leave, need for accommodation, disability (actual and/or perceived), and applying for state disability leave, substantially motivated Defendants’ termination of Plaintiff’s employment. (Complaint, ¶ 63.)         

      These facts, if true, are sufficient to support a jury’s finding that Defendants’ conduct was “despicable” and constituted malice and oppression. Defendants allegedly refused to accommodate Plaintiff as required by statute, did not engage in a good faith interactive process, and then immediately terminated Plaintiff, all in disregard of Plaintiff’s rights.

III.    CONCLUSION

      Based on the foregoing, Defendants’ motion to strike is DENIED.

Case Number: 25STCV25966    Hearing Date: August 11, 2026    Dept: 414

25STCV25966 Ferguson v. JPMorgan Chase Bank, et al.

Tuesday, August 11, 2026

 

[TENTATIVE] ORDER SUSTAINING DEMURRER OF DEFENDANT, CAPITAL ONE, N.A., TO PLAINTIFF’S FIRST AMENDED COMPLAINT (6929)

 

I.       BACKGROUND

      The first amended complaint alleges that Defendant, JPMorgan Chase Bank (“Chase”), approved a wire transfer from Plaintiff’s account to a Capital One, N.A. (“Capital One”) account owned by an individual who presented multiple high fraud risks that were readily discoverable by both institutions. (FAC ¶ 16.) Plaintiff alleges claims for negligence and violation of Commercial Code UCC Article 4A against both Defendants, and breach of contract and breach of fiduciary duty against Chase only.

      Capital One demurs to the claims for (1) negligence and (2) Violation of California Commercial Code UCC Article 4a. Capital One timely served the demurrer on Plaintiff who did not file an opposition.

II.     LEGAL STANDARDS

      A demurrer tests the sufficiency of a complaint as a matter of law and raises only questions of law. (Schmidt v. Foundation Health (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 1702, 1706.) In testing the complaint’s sufficiency, the court must assume the truth of the properly pleaded factual allegations as well as facts that can be reasonably inferred from those expressly pleaded facts. The court may also consider matters properly subject to judicial notice. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.)

III.    DISCUSSION

A.     Negligence

      Defendant argues the negligence claim is displaced by Division 11 of the California Commercial Code which provides exclusive remedies under the alleged circumstances. This is supported by Zengen, Inc. v. Comerica Bank (2007) 41 Cal.4th 239, 253 which held that “division 11 provides that common law causes of action based on allegedly unauthorized funds transfers are preempted in two specific areas: (1) where the common law claims would create rights, duties, or liabilities inconsistent with division 11; and (2) where the circumstances giving rise to the common law claims are specifically covered by the provisions of division 11." (Id.) The intent of the drafters was to provide a "comprehensive body of law to govern the rights and obligations resulting from wire transfers. [Citations.]" (Zengen, at 253 [italics in original.].) Capital One also relies on

      In Zengen, the plaintiff’s claims were based on the allegation that the bank should not have accepted and executed the fraudulent payment order. (Id. at 254-255.) The statute sets forth the respective rights and liabilities of the parties upon the acceptance of a payment order. (Id. at 255.)

      The Court considers the gravamen of the complaint in deciding whether the negligence claim is displaced by the UCC. (Sliders Trading Co. L.L.C. v. Wells Fargo Bank NA (N.D. Cal., Dec. 21, 2017, No. 17-CV-04930-LB) 2017 WL 6539843, at *5 [“Here, the gravamen of the claim is that Wells Fargo should not have accepted and executed the fraudulent wires. The California Supreme Court has held that because the U.C.C. provides a remedy for a bank’s processing of fraudulent payment orders, it displaces common-law claims like the negligence claim here."
       Here, Plaintiff’s claims arise from conduct surrounding the sending of the wire transfer by Chase and Capital One’s receipt of the transfer. Plaintiff alleges that Capital One was negligent in accepting the transfer, because there were indicia of fraud. (FAC ¶¶ 15-17, 62-64.) Plaintiff alleges that Capital One should have detected “the suspicious nature of its account holder who initiated multiple suspicious and fraudulent high-value transfer requests” (Id. ¶ 76); and that “the transferee . . . presented multiple high fraud risk indicators that were readily

discoverable through basic due diligence” and “should have triggered heightened

fraud review and interdiction procedures by . . . Capital One[.]” Id. ¶¶ 16-17.

      The SAC alleges Capital One negligently failed to hold the funds after the transfer from Chase. (id. ¶¶ 55-57) and failed to reasonably investigate what happened to the funds (id. ¶¶ 37, 47). As this involves a wire transfer the negligence claim is displaced by UCC Article 4A.

      The negligence claim fails for a second reason: Capital One does not owe a duty to Plaintiff, a non-customer. (Software Design & Application, Ltd. v. Hoefer & Arnett, Inc. (1996) 49 Cal.App.4th 472, 479 ["Recent cases have held that absent extraordinary and specific facts, a bank does not owe a duty of care to a noncustomer.].)

B.     Violation Of California Commercial Code UCC Article 4A

      Applying the definitions under the statute, Chase is the receiving bank, as it received the wire instructions. (Cal. U. Com. Code, § 11103 (a)(4). Capital One is the beneficiary bank who is identified in a payment order which directs which account is to be credited. (Cal. U. Com. Code, § 11103 (a)(2)(3).

      Plaintiff alleges that the wire transfer at issue to be sent from Chase to Capital One was “fraudulent and unauthorized.” (SAC ¶ 23.) If payment is authorized, the receiving bank “shall refund any payment of the payment order received from the customer to the extent the bank is not entitled to enforce payment and shall pay interest on the refundable amount calculated from the date the bank received payment to the date of the refund." (Cal. U. Com. Code, § 11204 (a).)

      If Capital One, as the beneficiary bank, accepts a payment order, "the bank is obliged to pay the amount of the order to the beneficiary of the order." (Cal. U. Com. Code, § 11404 (a) [emphasis added].) Accordingly, Chase, the bank who received wire instructions, had the obligation to refund an unauthorized payment.

      Accordingly, Plaintiff has not alleged facts to support a claim against Capital One under the UCC.

IV.  CONCLUSION

      Plaintiff did not file an opposition to address the viability of the negligence and UCC claims as alleged against Capital One. Accordingly, demurrer is SUSTAINED as to the first and second causes of action without leave to amend. (Lawrence v. Bank of America (1985) 163 Cal.App.3d 431, 436 [“… where the facts are not in dispute and the nature of the claim is clear, but no liability exists under substantive law," leave to amend should be denied.].)
