Discussion

 

            The Court has reviewed the moving, opposing and reply briefs filed by the parties, but only addresses the points which the Court deems to be material to the disposition of this motion.

 

Defendants Patrick Gogerchin, individually and as Trustee of The Patrick Gogerchin Family Trust, and Bianca Gogerchin Kaveh move for an order trying first the issue of whether Plaintiff and Defendant agreed to a contract or whether no contract was agreed to. Contrary to Plaintiff’s argument, this is not a disguised motion for summary judgment, as Defendants are not asking at this time for a ruling on the merits as to whether a contract was in fact formed.

 

Civ. Proc. Code, § 598 provides:


The court may, when the convenience of witnesses, the ends of justice, or the economy and efficiency of handling the litigation would be promoted thereby, on motion of a party, after notice and hearing, make an order, no later than the close of pretrial conference in cases in which such pretrial conference is to be held, or, in other cases, no later than 30 days before the trial date, that the trial of any issue or any part thereof shall precede the trial of any other issue or any part thereof in the case, except for special defenses which may be tried first pursuant to Sections 597 and 597.5. The court, on its own motion, may make such an order at any time. . . .

(Civ. Proc. Code, § 598.)       

 

Civ. Proc. Code, § 1048 provides:

 

(a) When actions involving a common question of law or fact are pending before the court, it may order a joint hearing or trial of any or all the matters in issue in the actions; it may order all the actions consolidated and it may make such orders concerning proceedings therein as may tend to avoid unnecessary costs or delay.

(b) The court, in furtherance of convenience or to avoid prejudice, or when separate trials will be conducive to expedition and economy, may order a separate trial of any cause of action, including a cause of action asserted in a cross-complaint, or of any separate issue or of any number of causes of action or issues, preserving the right of trial by jury required by the Constitution or a statute of this state or of the United States.

 

(Civ. Proc. Code, § 1048.)

 

The trial court had ample authority to make this discretionary decision. Aside from the language in Code of Civil Procedure section 598, which concerns pretrial motions, Evidence Code section 320 provides that “[e]xcept as otherwise provided by law, the court in its discretion shall regulate the order of proof.” Similarly, Code of Civil Procedure section 1048, subdivision (b) states that a trial court, “in furtherance of convenience or to avoid prejudice, or when separate trials will be conducive to expedition and economy, may order a separate trial of any cause of action . . . or of any separate issue or of any number of causes of action or issues . . . .” Under these provisions, trial courts have broad discretion to determine the order of proof in      the interests of judicial economy. ( Buran Equipment Co. v. H & C Investment Co. (1983) 142 Cal. App. 3d 338, 343-344 [190 Cal. Rptr. 878].)

(Grappo v. Coventry Fin. Corp. (1991) 235 Cal.App.3d 496, 503.)

 

            This will be a non-jury trial, so jury considerations are not relevant here. There are five theories asserted in the Complaint, three of them revolve around contract formation—whether it be written, oral or implied-in-fact. However, in essence this motion only seeks to have formation of the breach of written contract claim tried first, and assumes that a finding that no written contract was ever formed will necessarily dispose of the other causes of action.

 

            However, theories of recovery based on oral contracts and implied-in-fact contracts are separate from written contracts, and even if the Court finds that the parties did not enter into a written contract, the Court will still have to proceed to address Plaintiff’s alternative theories of breach of oral contract and breach of implied-in-fact contract. “Pleading of alternative theories of relief on the same set of facts is, of course, quite proper and is often done (for example) where there is a legally recognized basis for recovery in both contract and tort.” (Gebert v. Yank (1985) 172 Cal.App.3d 544, 554.)

 

“An oral contract may be pleaded generally as to its effect, because it is rarely possible to allege the exact words. (Citation omitted.)” (Khoury v. Maly’s of California, Inc. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 612, 616.)

 

The existence and terms of an implied contract are manifested by conduct. (Civ. Code, § 1621.) The distinction reflects no difference in legal effect but merely in the mode of manifesting assent. (Citation omitted) Accordingly, a contract implied in fact “consists of obligations arising from a mutual agreement and intent to promise where the agreement and promise have not been expressed in words.” (Citation omitted.)

(Retired Employees Assn. of Orange County, Inc. v. County of Orange (2011) 52 Cal.4th 1171, 1178 [bold emphasis and underlining added].)

 

Moreover, a theory of false promise does not require formation of a contract.

 

Since plaintiff’s complaint sounds in tort rather than contract, its sufficiency must be tested by the general rules of tort law. So tested, it adequately states a cause of action for fraud and deceit. A plaintiff may recover for fraud when he shows that by reason of a defendant’s misrepresentations he has sustained some pecuniary damage or injury by reason of having been put in a position worse than he would have occupied had there been no fraud. (Citation omitted.)

(R. D. Reeder Lathing Co. v. Cypress Ins. Co. (1970) 3 Cal.App.3d 995, 999.)

 

Cases involving fraud where property was not acquired have limited damages to out-of-pocket losses. . . . [A] defrauded party may recoup his out-of-pocket losses and expenditures in reliance on the fraud, but he may not recover benefit-of-the-bargain damages (i.e., damages placing him in the economic position he would have occupied had the representation been true), at least where the recovery is not premised on a specific property actually acquired by the defrauded party.

 

(Kenly v. Ukegawa (1993) 16 Cal.App.4th 49, 54.)

 

That is, the measure of tort damages would be Plaintiff’s out-of-pocket loss, not benefit of the alleged contractual bargain.

 

Indeed, a finding that no express contract exists between the parties would not foreclose the possibility that an implied contract exists because they are not simply duplicative theories of recovery:

 

[T]here simply cannot exist a valid express contract on one hand and an implied contract on the other, each embracing the identical subject but requiring different results and treatment. (Citations omitted.)

(Tollefson v. Roman Catholic Bishop (1990) 219 Cal.App.3d 843, 855-856.)

 

Further, a declaratory relief claim is proper even if the judgment is in favor of Defendant. A cause of action is stated whether Plaintiff is entitled to declaratory relief in [his] favor or not. (Columbia Pictures Corp. v. DeToth (1945) 26 Cal.2d 753, 760 [“It is not essential, to entitle a plaintiff to seek declaratory relief, that he should establish his right to a favorable declaration.”])

 

In this regard, the Court finds that trying the issue of contract formation first will not promote the convenience of witnesses, the ends of justice, or the economy and efficiency of handling the litigation. (Civ. Proc. Code, § 598.) Nor would it further convenience or avoid prejudice. (Civ. Proc. Code, § 1048.) The Court would end up trying all theories at once, which is contrary to bifurcating the issue of formation.

 

            As such, the motion for an order that the issue of contract formation be tried first is DENIED.

Discussion

 

The Court has reviewed the moving, opposing and reply briefs filed by the parties, but only addresses the points which the Court deems to be material to the disposition of this motion. The Court also notes that Defendants filed a reply separate statement, which is a violation of Code Civ. Proc. § 437c(b)(4).

 

Defendants Patrick Gogerchin, individually and as Trustee of The Patrick Gogerchin Family Trust, and Bianca Gogerchin Kaveh moves for summary judgment as to the operative First Amended Complaint. The Court notes that this motion was not brought as a motion for summary adjudication in the alternative. As such, the Court cannot grant summary adjudication if Defendants are not entitled to summary judgment:

 

The language in Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (f) makes it clear that a motion for summary adjudication cannot be considered by the court unless the party bringing the motion duly gives notice that summary adjudication is being sought. (Citation omitted.) “If a party desires adjudication of particular issues or subissues, that party must make its intentions clear in the motion. . . .” (Citation omitted.) There is a sound reason for this rule: “. . . the opposing party may have decided to raise only one triable issue of fact in order to defeat the motion, without intending to concede the other issues. It would be unfair to grant a summary adjudication order unless the opposing party was on notice that an issue-by-issue adjudication might be ordered if summary judgment was denied.” (Citation omitted.)

(Gonzales v. Superior Court (1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 1542, 1545-46.)

 

The burden of proof imposed upon a party who seeks summary judgment under section 437c is substantial. Where, as here, a defendant is the moving party, its evidence submitted in support of the motion must either disprove at least one essential element of every cause of action of the plaintiff’s complaint or prove an affirmative defense that would bar every cause of action of the complaint. ( Frazier, Dame, Doherty, Parrish & Hanawalt v. Bocardo, Blum, Lull, Niland, Teerlink & Bell (1977) 70 Cal.App.3d 331, 338 [138 Cal.Rptr. 670]; Weil & Brown, Cal. Practice Guide: Civil Procedure Before Trial (Rutter 1989) § 10:125 rev. #1, 1989.)

Accordingly, if a properly noticed motion for summary adjudication (§ 437c, subd. (f)) is not also before the court and the defendant’s proof leaves at least one plausible theory or basis of recovery unchallenged, the burden is not met and plaintiff’s failure to oppose the motion is immaterial. ( Conn v. National Can Corp. (1981) 124 Cal.App.3d 630, 639 [177 Cal.Rptr. 445]; Nazar v. Rodeffer (1986) 184 Cal.App.3d 546 [229 Cal.Rptr. 209]; Weil & Brown, supra, § 10:126 rev. #1, 1989.) That the plaintiff will have the affirmative burden of proof at trial is of no consequence. ( Security Pac. Nat. Bank v. Associated Motor Sales (1980) 106 Cal.App.3d 171, 179 [165 Cal.Rptr. 38]; Weil & Brown, supra, § 10:133 rev. #1, 1989.)

(Twain Harte Assocs. v. County of Tuolumne (1990) 217 Cal.App.3d 71, 79-80.)

 

            As noted above, Defendant only noticed a motion for summary judgment, not a motion for summary adjudication in the alternative. In this regard, the separate statement does not set forth any issues to be summarily adjudicated.  As such, the Court cannot parse and summarily adjudicate individual causes of action:  summary judgment must be denied if Defendant does not meet his initial burden of demonstrating the absence of any triable issue of material fact.

 

[10:28] Single material fact issue defeats motion: The requirement that there be “no triable issue of material fact” means that summary judgment can be granted only where the essential facts are either conceded or beyond dispute. If there is one, single material fact in dispute, the motion must be denied. (A factual issue is material whenever its determination could make a difference to the disposition of the motion. See CRC 3.1350(a)(2); ¶ 10:270 ff.)

 

(Weil & Brown, et al., California Practice Guide: Civil Procedure Before Trial [¶ 10:28.][The Rutter Group June 2026 Update].)

            The 1AC appears to allege that the sale of Defendants’ interest in the Entities was separate from the sale of the apartment portfolio:

 

Additionally, Abbie and Patrick agreed that the Powerco and APB Broadway redemption of Patrick and Bianca’s interests would not be contingent on a contemporaneous buyout of the family’s apartment portfolio (See Exhibit 2).

 

(1AC, ¶ 22.)

 

            As such, the Court does not consider the terms pertaining to “Apartments” in the October 26, 2020 e-mail attached as Exh. 1 to the 1AC. The Court only addressed the terms pertaining to “Company” which states as follows:

 

1. Each party selects an appraiser that the other party approves. An average of the two is the price. If after 2 appraisals either side is unhappy then we do 2 more appraisers (that the other party approves) and we do an average of the 4.

 

2. Abbie may elect to include 14000 Broadway if he’d like in which case 2 broker opinions would be needed (same approval process as above). If either party is unhappy we can get 4 broker opinions and average them.

 

3. Once price is concluded the payment terms are the following

 

4. 10% down, 25-35k per month with a balloon at year 10 (interest free). A sale triggers full payment.

 

5. Form of payment (distribution vs consulting payments etc) to be determined later, price to be adjusted by appraiser to compensate for tax rate (cap gains v. ordinary income)

 

6. We need some sort of guarantees since we are stringing this thing out 10 years. Personal guarantee is preferred but if you have other ideas we can certainly talk about them.

 

            Nothing in the e-mail indicates that the agreement shall not be binding until a written agreement is executed.

 

            The appraisal procedure is sufficiently defined such that the price would be objectively determined by the average of up to two rounds of appraisals yielding four appraisals. “The law in California is clear that so long as the price may be objectively determined, a contract in which the price is not expressed may nonetheless be enforced.” (Carver v. Teitsworth (1991) 1 Cal.App.4th 845, 853.)

 

            Notably, the 1AC alleges at ¶¶ 29 – 35 that both Plaintiff and Defendant Patrick retained appraisers to value Defendants interests. The trier of fact (in this case, the Court) could find at trial that this demonstrated that both parties considered the oral agreement binding with regards to ascertaining the purchase price.

 

The parties’ conduct subsequent to the formation of a contract, “including the dispute which arises and the remedy sought,” may be relevant in determining which terms they considered essential. (Citations omitted.) However,  few contracts would be enforceable if the existence of subsequent disputes were taken as evidence that an agreement was never reached.

(Patel v. Liebermensch (2008) 45 Cal.4th 344, 351-52 [bold emphasis added].)

 

            The other side of the coin is that the appraisal are not conclusive regarding contract formation:

 

[T}he failure to reach a meeting of the minds on all material points prevents the formation of a contract even though the parties have orally agreed upon some of the terms, or have taken some action related to the contract. (Citations omitted.)

(Banner Entertainment, Inc. v. Superior Court (1998) 62 Cal.App.4th 348, 359 [italics in original].)

 

The e-mail reflects that the parties agreed to the following payment terms: “10% down, 25-35k per month with a balloon at year 10 (interest free). A sale triggers full payment.”

 

If no time is specified for the performance of an act required to be performed, a reasonable time is allowed. If the act is in its nature capable of being done instantly—as, for example, if it consists in the payment of money only—it must be performed immediately upon the thing to be done being exactly ascertained.

 

(Civ. Code § 1657.)

            While the e-mail reserves for future agreement matters including form of payment, tax issues and guarantees, it is a question of fact as to whether the parties considered these terms to be so essential that the parties did not intend to enter into a binding agreement without agreeing upon such terms, and to enforce the agreement without such terms would be unfair. Likewise, as to the other terms which Defendant claims the parties did not agree upon—timing of transfer of interests, minority discount, security interest, attorney’s fees and arbitration provisions, and financial covenants.

 

The general rule is that if an “essential element” of a promise is reserved for the future agreement of both parties, the promise gives rise to no legal obligation until such future agreement is made. (Citations omitted.) “The enforceability of a contract containing a promise to agree depends upon the relative importance and the severability of the matter left to the future; it is a question of degree and may be settled by determining whether the indefinite promise is so essential to the bargain that inability to enforce that promise strictly according to its terms would make unfair the enforcement of the remainder of the agreement. [Citations.] Where the matters left for future agreement are unessential, each party will be forced to accept a reasonable determination of the unsettled point or if possible the unsettled point may be left unperformed and the remainder of the contract be enforced. [Citations.]” (Citations omitted.)

(Coleman Engineering Co. v. North American Aviation, Inc. (1966) 65 Cal.2d 396, 405 [bold emphasis and underlining added].)

 

            As for additional terms not mentioned in the October 26, 2020 e-mail, these cannot be utilized to avoid a meeting of the minds:

 

Accordingly, the decision is one of numerous others to the effect that when the parties orally agree upon all the terms and conditions of an agreement with the mutual intention that it shall thereupon become binding, the mere fact that a formal written agreement to the same effect is to be prepared and signed does not alter the binding validity of the oral agreement. (Citation omitted.) The rule of such cases has been stated thus by Witkin: “Parties may engage in preliminary negotiations, oral or written, in order to reach an agreement. These negotiations ordinarily result in a binding contract when all of the terms are definitely understood, even though the parties intend that a formal writing embodying these terms shall be executed later.” (Citation omitted.) Too, whether it was the intention of the parties that such oral agreement should become binding eo instante is to be determined by the trier of fact from the surrounding circumstances. (Citation omitted.)  Finally, justice and fair dealing fully support the rationale of the above holdings as set forth in Clarke v. Fiedler, supra, quoting from a New York case: “‘Any other rule would always permit a party who has entered into a contract like this, through letters and telegraphic messages, to violate it, whenever the understanding was that it should be  [*849]  reduced to another written form, by simply suggesting other and additional terms and conditions. If this were the rule the contract would never be completed in cases where, by changes in the market, or other events occurring subsequent to the written negotiations, it became the interest of either party to adopt that course in order to escape or evade obligations incurred in the ordinary course of commercial business. . . . If the parties did not become bound in this case they cannot be bound in any case.’” (Citation omitted.) The above statement may be considered to have been prophetically made to fit the instant proceeding.

(Stephan v. Maloof (1969) 274 Cal.App.2d 843, 847-49 [bold emphasis and underlining added].)

 

            Moreover, some terms may be implied based upon the prior course of dealings between the parties—a principle particularly apt in this case involving relatives who had been doing business together for a while.

 

An actual negotiation regarding every term has never been required for the formation of a contract. The existence of mutual assent is determined by objective criteria, not by one party’s subjective intent. The test is whether a reasonable person would, from the conduct of the parties, conclude that there was a mutual agreement. (Citations omitted.) There is no dispute that an oral contract was formed when Benco hired Reliable in August 1996, obligating Benco to pay for the hours worked at the stated price. Uncontroverted evidence demonstrated that pursuant to the parties’ past practices Benco knew that the crane operator would present the Work Authorization and Contract to its on-site supervisor, who would approve the number of hours worked that day. This course of dealing, conducted over many years and numerous hirings, establishes that the oral agreement to hire included the terms contained in the Work Authorization and Contract. The parties’ prior course of dealings may determine the meaning of a contract term or may add an agreed but unstated term. (Citations omitted.)

. . .

The trial court’s conclusion in the instant case that it would be inappropriate to bind Benco to an indemnity agreement entered into after the accident occurred misses the mark. This is not a situation where the parties were contracting for the first time or where Reliable was inserting a new indemnity provision when none had existed before. The parties’ consistent history of past dealings informs our interpretation of their agreement. The indemnity provision was implicit in the parties’ oral agreement to hire, which predated the accident.

(Marin Storage & Trucking, Inc. v. Benco Contracting & Engineering, Inc. (2001) 89 Cal.App.4th 1042, 1050-51 [bold emphasis added].)

 

            Defendants’ UF Nos. 13 and 14 are as follows:

 

13. Plaintiff’s verified discovery responses definitively establish that there were no other agreements, either written or oral, other than the October 26th Email. [Plaintiff’s Responses to Form Interrogatories attached as Exhibit “A” to the Declaration of James M. Kilkowski (“JMKDec”) attached hereto, Response 50.1(b)]

 

14. Plaintiff’s verified discovery responses clearly state the terms of the October 26th Email have not been modified. [Plaintiff’s Responses to Form Interrogatories attached as Exhibit “A” to the Declaration of James M. Kilkowski (“JMKDec”) attached Hereto, Response 50.1(d)]

 

            However, responses to interrogatories do not have the same effect as deemed admissions:

Although requests for admissions are included in the Code of Civil Procedure among discovery procedures (Code Civ. Proc., § 2019.010, subd. (e)), they “differ fundamentally from other forms of discovery. Rather than seeking to uncover information, they seek to eliminate the need for proof. [Citation.]” (Citations  [*736] omitted.)

As a general rule an admission is conclusive in the action as to the party making it. (Citations omitted.) Absent leave of court to amend or withdraw the admission, no contradictory evidence may be introduced. (Citations omitted) This is not always true for answers to interrogatories or deposition testimony. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2025.620, 2030.410; see, e.g., Scalf v. D.B. Log Homes, Inc., supra, 128 Cal.App.4th at p. 1522 [“For summary judgment purposes, deposition answers are simply evidence … considered and weighed in conjunction with other evidence. They do not constitute incontrovertible judicial admissions as do … answers to requests for admissions” (citations omitted)]; McLaughlin v. Sikorsky Aircraft (1983) 148 Cal. App. 3d 203, 210 [195 Cal. Rptr. 764] [when expert testified at trial contrary to deposition testimony, opposing party allowed to bring in new expert].)

(Murillo v. Superior Court (2006) 143 Cal.App.4th 730, 735-736 [bold emphasis added].)

 

            Moreover, responses to interrogatories may be explained in opposing summary judgment:

 

As they did in the trial court, the Hansens argue that since Mason’s initial response to the interrogatory indicates she suffered damage in 1977, even under Bispo her claim is time barred as a matter of law. Relying on D’Amico v. Board of Medical Examiners (1974) 11 Cal.3d 1, 22 [112 Cal.Rptr. 786, 520 P.2d 10], and Leasman v. Beech Aircraft Corp. (1975) 48 Cal.App.3d 376, 382 [121 Cal.Rptr. 768], the Hansens contend no consideration should be given to Mason’s explanation of her response. We do not believe we are free to ignore Mason’s explanation.

 

. . .  [*546]  . . .

 

In this case Mason’s explanation of her initial response to the interrogatory, viewed in light of the whole record, is credible. As we have seen, Mason claims she was injured by a sexual relationship which allegedly began in 1983. Plainly the injuries caused by those relations could not have occurred in 1977, as she indicated in her initial interrogatory response. Thus if a trier of fact believes the relations began in 1983, the trier of fact could reasonably conclude that the initial interrogatory response was, as Mason explained in her declaration, a simple mistake.

 

In sum then, the summary judgment must be reversed because there is no evidence which establishes the date of Mason’s injury within the meaning of section 340.5.

 

(Mason v. Marriage & Family Ctr. (1991) 228 Cal.App.3d 537, 545-46 [bold emphasis added].)

 

            As such, Plaintiff’s responses to interrogatories do not constitute binding admissions against Plaintiff.

5. After October 26, 2020, counsel for Defendants and counsel for Plaintiff exchanged a lengthy email and three lengthy letters, one of which is dated November 3, 2020, a copy of which is attached as Exhibit “1” to the Declaration of Martin Jannol (“MJDec”) filed concurrently herewith. [MJDec, ¶4]

 

6. After October 26, 2020, counsel for Defendants and counsel for Plaintiff exchanged a lengthy email and three lengthy letters, one of which is dated November 10, 2020, a copy of which is attached as Exhibit “2” to the MJDec. [MJDec, ¶5]

9. The letter from Plaintiff’s counsel to Defendants’ counsel dated December 15, 2020, SSUF8, states explicitly that the transaction is contingent on the execution of definitive agreement(s): “14. Purchase Agreement. This transaction is contingent upon the execution of definitive agreement(s) consistent with this Letter of Intent and otherwise in a form mutually acceptable to the parties.” [Exhibit “4” to the MJDec, ¶14]

 

10. No definitive agreement, or even a letter of intent, was ever executed by Plaintiff and Defendants for the purchase and sale of Defendants’ stock interests in Powerco or Defendants’ interests in APB Broadway. [Declaration of Patrick Gogerchin (“PGDec”) filed concurrently herewith, ¶3]

 

            While the statements of counsel following the parties’ negotiations is relevant to the parties’ intent, it is only a factual circumstance for the trier of fact to consider in determining whether the parties reached an agreement as to all material terms:

 

When it is clear, both from a provision that the proposed written contract would become operative only when signed by the parties as well as from any other evidence presented by the parties that both parties contemplated that acceptance of the contract’s terms would be signified by signing it, the failure to sign the agreement means no binding contract was created. (Citations omitted.) This is so even though the party later sought to be bound by the agreement indicated a willingness to sign the agreement. (Citation omitted.) On the other hand, if the respective parties orally agreed upon all of the terms and conditions of a proposed written agreement with the mutual intention that the oral agreement should thereupon become binding, the mere fact that a formal written agreement to the same effect has not yet been signed does not alter the binding validity of the oral agreement. (Citation omitted.)

Whether it was the parties’ mutual intention that their oral agreement to the terms contained in a proposed written agreement should be binding immediately is to be determined from the surrounding facts and circumstances of a particular case and is a question of fact for the trial court. (Citations omitted.) Evidence as to the parties’ understanding and intent in taking what actions they did take is admissible to ascertain when or whether a binding agreement was ever reached. (Citations omitted.)

(Banner Entertainment, Inc., supra, 62 Cal.App.4th at 358- [bold emphasis added].)

 

            Just as one example, Defendants’ counsel admits that he modified terms which Plaintiff and Defendant Patrick discussed, i.e., Defendant’s counsel may have been modifying an already binding contract by introducing new terms:

 

b. The value of the Corporation will be determined by an appraisal process. Each side will pick an appraiser who shall have at least 5 years active appraisal experience. The appraisers will evaluate the Corporation on the basis of a third party, arms-length sale of the entire Corporation (and all of its business however structured). The appraisers shall have access to the books and records of the Corporation as agreed upon by the parties on a confidential basis. If the two appraisers are less than 10% apart, then the value will be the average of the two appraisals. If their appraisals are more than l0% apart, then the two appraisers will pick a third appraiser (with comparable experience). The third appraiser will review the two appraisals and decide which is closest to the correct value. The one chosen (i.e. most correct) will determine the value. The decision of the third appraiser will be binding and final. Please note that this process was modified by me based on my experience with these sorts of transactions. I felt that the method the two gentlemen had discussed was not optimum.

 

(Martin Jannol Decl., Exh. 1, November 3, 2020 e-mail [bold emphasis and underlining added].)_

 

            On the other hand, the boilerplate statement in ¶ 14 of the proposed Letter of Intent dated December 15, 2020 sent by Plaintiff’s counsel Elliot H. Stone to Defendants’ counsel Martin B. Jannol, may be evidence of the parties intent that their October 25, 2025 oral negotiations at the Jonathan Club did not result in a binding oral agreement:

 

14. Purchase Agreement. This transaction is contingent upon the execution of definitive agreement(s) consistent with this Letter of Intent and otherwise in a form mutually acceptable to the parties.

 

            However, this is only a factor for the trier of fact to consider as to contract formation at trial.

 

            As for Defendant’s argument that the alleged contract is too indefinite to be enforced, this is part of the foregoing inquiries, and thus Defendant has not negated the existence of a triable issue of material fact in this regard:

 

Where a contract is so uncertain and indefinite that the intention of the parties in material particulars cannot be ascertained, the contract is void and unenforceable. [Citations.] “Although the terms of a contract need not be stated in the minutest detail, it … must evidence a meeting of the minds upon the essential features of the agreement.”‘“ (Citation omitted.) Whether a term is material or essential “‘depends on its relative importance to the parties and whether its absence would make enforcing the remainder of the contract unfair to either party.’” (Citations omitted.)

In determining whether there is mutual consent, the court will often consider extrinsic evidence and resolve a question of fact. (Citations omitted.) But where, as here, the facts are not in dispute, whether there is mutual consent is a question of law, and we review the court’s ruling de novo. (Citations omitted.)

(Santana v. Studebaker Health Care Center, LLC (2026) 120 Cal.App.5th 1, 12.)

 

            In light of the foregoing, the Court finds that Defendants have not met their initial burden of demonstrating that no triable issue of material fact exists as to the first cause of action for breach of written contract. This precludes summary judgment.

The burden of proof imposed upon a party who seeks summary judgment under section 437c is substantial. Where, as here, a defendant is the moving party, its evidence submitted in support of the motion must  [*80]  either disprove at least one essential element of every cause of action of the plaintiff’s complaint or prove an affirmative defense that would bar every cause of action of the complaint. (Citations omitted.)

Accordingly, if a properly noticed motion for summary adjudication (§ 437c, subd. (f)) is not also before the court and the defendant’s proof leaves at least one plausible theory or basis of recovery unchallenged, the burden is not met and plaintiff’s failure to oppose the motion is immaterial. (Citations omitted.) That the plaintiff will have the affirmative burden of proof at trial is of no consequence. (Citations omitted.)

(Twain Harte Assocs. v. County of Tuolumne (1990) 217 Cal.App.3d 71, 79-80 [bold emphasis and underlining].)

            Further, even if the Court were to find that no triable issue of material fact exists as to the breach of written contract claim, as noted above re: the motion to set order of proof at trial, theories of recovery based oral contracts and implied-in-fact contracts are separate from written contracts. Thus, even if the Court were to find at trial that the parties did not enter into a written contract, the Court will still have to proceed to address Plaintiff’s alternative theories of breach of oral contract and breach of implied-in-fact contract. “Pleading of alternative theories of relief on the same set of facts is, of course, quite proper and is often done (for example) where there is a legally recognized basis for recovery in both contract and tort.” (Gebert v. Yank (1985) 172 Cal.App.3d 544, 554.) Moreover, a theory of false promise does not require formation of a contract. Further, a declaratory relief claim is proper even if the judgment is in favor of Defendant. 

Defendant does not adequately address these theories in the moving papers, nor even in the Reply, other than to attack the way these theories are pled.

            As such, the motion for summary judgment is DENIED.

A well-established exception or departure from the hearsay rule applies to cases in which the very fact in controversy is whether certain things were said and not whether these things were true or false, and in these cases the words are admissible not as hearsay, but as original evidence. (Citation omitted.) Thus, written or oral utterances, which are acts in themselves constituting legal results in issue in the case, do not come under the hearsay rule. (Citation omitted.)

 

(Zuckerman v. Pac. Sav. Bank (1986) 187 Cal.App.3d 1394, 1404.)
