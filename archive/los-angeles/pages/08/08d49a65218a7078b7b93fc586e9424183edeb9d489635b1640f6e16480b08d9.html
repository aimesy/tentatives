[TENTATIVE] RULING ON PETITION FOR WRIT OF MANDATE

 

Petitioner John Doe (“Petitioner”) petitions for a writ of administrative mandate against Respondent Board of Trustees of the California State University (“Respondent”) directing it to set aside its decision to expel him.

The petition for writ of mandate is denied.

I.                Factual Background

Petitioner’s verified petition alleges that Petitioner was an undergraduate student at California Polytechnic State University, San Luis Obispo (“University”) on track to earn a bachelor’s degree in June 2023. (Pet., ¶ 19.)

On May 15, 2023, the University’s Civil Rights & Compliance Office (“CRCO”) received a complaint (“Complaint”) that Petitioner sexually assaulted another student (“Complainant”) on or about March 18, 2023. (Pet., ¶ 20.) On June 6, 2023, Petitioner and Complainant received a notice of Referral of Formal Complaint to CSU Nondiscrimination Policy – Track 2 pursuant to the University’s Procedures for Complaints of Discrimination, Harassment, Sexual Misconduct, Sexual Exploitation, Dating Violence, Domestic Violence, Stalking, and Retaliation Made Against a Student (“Procedures”). (Pet., ¶ 23.)

By the end of the term on June 16, 2023, Petitioner successfully completed all remaining criteria to earn his degree, but the University deferred conferring his degree pending resolution of the investigation and any disciplinary proceedings. (Pet., ¶ 24.) Complainant completed her education and received her degree in June 2023. (Pet., ¶ 32.)

From June 2023 through November 2023, CRCO Associate Director Kaitlyn Blakey (“Director Blakey”) investigated Complainant’s allegations. (Pet., ¶ 25.) On August 21, 2023, Petitioner submitted a written statement disputing the allegations. (Pet., ¶ 26.) On November 9, 2023, Director Blakey completed her CSU Nondiscrimination Policy, Track 2 – Final Investigation Report (“Final Report”) which she submitted to CRCO Assistant Vice President Maren Hufton (“VP Hufton”). (Pet., ¶ 27.)

On January 30 and 31, 2024, Hearing Officer Adrienne Publicover (“Hearing Officer”) presided over the hearing on the Complaint. (Pet., ¶ 29.) Petitioner denied all allegations of sexual misconduct.

On February 21, 2024, the Hearing Officer issued her Hearing Decision Report accepting all Complainant’s allegations and concluding Petitioner committed sexual misconduct. (Pet., ¶ 30.) On March 11, 2024, the Hearing Officer recommended expulsion (together with the Hearing Decision Report, “Decision”). (Pet., ¶ 33.)

On March 22, 2024, Petitioner appealed the findings and sanction to the Office of the Chancellor. (Pet., ¶ 34.) On June 1, 2024,[1] the Office of the Chancellor denied the appeal. (Pet., ¶ 35.)

II.             Procedural History

On November 26, 2024, Petitioner filed his verified petition. Proof of service on file shows Petitioner served the summons and petition on Respondent by personal service on December 2, 2024. On January 17, 2025, Respondent filed its answer.

On April 22, 2025, the Court set the hearing on the petition for October 7, 2025. The Court later continued that hearing to November 6, 2025. Per joint stipulation of the parties, the Court again continued the writ hearing to May 7, 2026. Meanwhile, on August 14, 2025, Petitioner filed his opening brief. On October 14, 2025, Respondent filed its opposition. On April 22, 2026, Petitioner filed his reply.

On May 6, 2026, the Court heard Respondent’s ex parte application to correct the administrative record, styled as a motion to augment. The Court granted the application and, on its own motion, continued the hearing on the petition to September 10, 2026. The Court ordered Respondent to file and serve a five-page supplemental brief no later than June 26, 2026. The Court ordered Petitioner to file and serve a five-page supplemental brief by July 27, 2026. On June 26, 2026, Respondent filed its supplemental brief. On July 27, 2026, Petitioner filed his supplemental brief.

III.           Standard of Review

Code of Civil Procedure (“CCP”) section 1094.5 is the administrative mandamus provision which structures the procedure for judicial review of adjudicatory decisions rendered by administrative agencies. (Topanga Ass’n for a Scenic Community v. County of Los Angeles (1974) 11 Cal.3d 506, 514-15.). Section 1094.5 does not specify which cases are subject to independent review, leaving that issue to the courts. (Fukuda v. City of Angels (1999) 20 Cal.4th 805, 811.) In cases reviewing decisions that affect a vested, fundamental right, the trial court exercises independent judgment on the evidence. (Bixby v. Pierno (1971) 4 Cal.3d 130, 143; see CCP §1094.5(c).)

The right at issue is Petitioner’s interest in continuing his education at the University and obtaining his degree. Although higher education is an important interest, it is not a fundamental one. Courts have recognized that the value of higher education is “an interest of almost incalculable value” to enrolled students, and that attendance at a public university is “a benefit somewhat analogous to that of public employment.” (Goldberg v. Regents of University of California (1967) 248 Cal.App.2d 867, 876.) However, unlike food, shelter, or clothing, higher education is not considered a fundamental right. (Gurfinkel v. Los Angeles Community College Dist. (1981) 121 Cal.App.3d 1, 7 [addressing claimed fundamental rights in the community college context].)

Accordingly, a university’s factual findings in student disciplinary matters are reviewed under the substantial evidence standard. (Doe v. University of Southern California (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 221, 239, 248–249; Doe v. Regents of University of California (2016) 5 Cal.App.5th 1055, 1073 [“UCSD”].) That standard applies equally to Respondent and the University in this case.

Under the substantial evidence standard, a “court’s scrutiny of the agency’s factual findings is limited to a determination [of] whether those findings are supported by substantial evidence in light of the whole record.” (Doe v. Regents of the University of California (2013) 216 Cal.App.4th 1474, 1485.) A decision is deemed to be supported by substantial evidence unless “no reasonable person could reach the conclusion reached by the [University], based on the entire record before it.” (UCSD at 1073.) In making that determination, the court accepts all evidence which supports the successful party, disregards the contrary evidence, and draws all reasonable inferences in favor of the findings. (Id. at 1073-74.) The “testimony of a single witness, even that of a party, is sufficient to provide substantial evidence to support a finding of fact.” (Id. at 1074 [citation omitted]; see also Kearl v. Board of Medical Quality Assurance (1986) 189 Cal.App.3d 1040, 1052.)

IV.           Analysis

Petitioner seeks a writ of mandate compelling Respondent to set aside its decision to expel him on the grounds that (1) withholding his degree pending the outcome of the investigation and any disciplinary proceedings was an abuse of discretion; (2) the penalty of expulsion was excessive; and (3) he did not receive a fair hearing.

1.               The University’s Pre-Adjudication Withholding of Petitioner’s Degree

Petitioner argues that the University abused its discretion by withholding his degree despite his completion of all required coursework. In support, he relies solely on a letter from the U.S. Department of Education Office for Civil Rights (“OCR”) advising the University of Virginia that withholding a degree based only on pending allegations does not comply with Title IX. This argument is unpersuasive. As Respondent points out, Petitioner’s disciplinary proceedings were not conducted under Title IX, and the OCR letter specifically addresses the University of Virginia’s Title IX grievance procedures.

In reply, Petitioner shifts focus and argues that the University violated California Code of Regulations (“CCR”), Title 5, section 41301(c), which states that “[t]he Chancellor shall adopt procedures to ensure students are afforded appropriate notice and an opportunity to be heard before the University imposes any sanction for a violation of the Student Conduct Code.” (CCR, tit. 5, § 41301(c).) This argument also fails. Petitioner raised section 41301(c) for the first time in reply, depriving Respondent of the opportunity to respond.

More importantly, Petitioner’s degree had not vested when the investigation began. By the University’s own policy, “[t]here are eight general requirements which all students must meet in order to earn the bachelor’s degree from Cal Poly and participate in commencement.” (AR 946.) The eighth general requirement states:

“When an allegation has been made that a student has violated the Interim CSU Policy Prohibiting Discrimination, Harassment, Sexual Misconduct, Sexual Exploitation, Dating Violence, Domestic Violence, Stalking, and Retaliation, Revised December 24, 2021, or any of the Standards for Student Conduct (Title 5, section 41301 of the California Code of Regulations), and the student is under inquiry and/or investigation or a sanction has been applied for a violation, degree conferral may be impacted. If a student is expelled from the University, regardless of academic progress, including when a student has completed all academic requirements at the time of the expulsion, the student will not receive a degree. Expulsion means permanent separation from the University and no degree will be conferred.” (AR 946.)

Because the investigation was pending, Petitioner had not satisfied all prerequisites for degree conferral. Although Petitioner characterizes the withholding of his degree as a “sanction,” the record shows instead that Petitioner had not yet met the University’s requirements to receive a degree. He therefore was not automatically entitled to degree conferral while the investigation and adjudication were unresolved.

As previously noted, the Court ordered the record corrected and allowed limited supplemental briefing on the newly included material. The added document is a procedure entitled “Student Conduct Process,” which states:

This Procedure is established pursuant to California Code of Regulations, Title 5, Section 41301 et seq., and governs all Student disciplinary matters systemwide, except that as noted, specific Procedures apply for allegations involving Discrimination, Harassment, Retaliation, Sexual Misconduct, Sexual Exploitation, Domestic, Dating Violence, and Stalking, as set forth in Article IV of this Policy and in the CSU Policy Prohibiting Discrimination, Harassment, Sexual Misconduct, Sexual Exploitation, Dating Violence, Domestic Violence, Stalking, and Retaliation (Nondiscrimination Policy).

(AR 983, footnote omitted.)

Respondent argues only that the policy “is significant because it comprises some of the applicable policies and procedures in this matter.” (Resp. Suppl. Br. at 2.) Petitioner argues that the policy is “largely irrelevant,” does not overcome Petitioner’s prior arguments, and in fact supports those arguments (Pet. Suppl. Br. at 3-7).

As with Petitioner’s arguments in opening and reply, Petitioner’s supplemental position rests on the assumption that he had already earned and was entitled to his degree. As explained above, this assumption is incorrect. Petitioner had not met the eighth general requirement, which requires resolution of qualifying allegations and the absence of expulsion before a degree may be conferred. Because Petitioner had not earned his degree, withholding it was not a sanction.

Accordingly, Petitioner has not shown that the University abused its discretion by withholding his degree while the proceeding was pending.

2.               Petitioner Received a Fair Hearing

Petitioner next contends that he did not receive a fair hearing because the Hearing Officer was biased, impermissibly shifted the burden to Petitioner to prove his own innocence, and penalized Petitioner for exercising his procedural rights.

The court reviews fairness of the administrative proceeding de novo. (Nasha v. City of Los Angeles (2004) 125 Cal.App.4th 470, 482, 22 Cal.Rptr.3d 772.) “The statute's requirement of a ‘¿“fair”¿’ trial means that there must have been ‘a fair administrative hearing.’¿” (Gonzalez v. Santa Clara County Dept. of Social Services (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 72, 96, 167 Cal.Rptr.3d 148.) Where student discipline is at issue, the university must comply with its own policies and procedures. (Berman v. Regents of University of California (2014) 229 Cal.App.4th 1265, 1271, 178 Cal.Rptr.3d 62.) The question whether the trial was “fair” encompasses pre-hearing and post-hearing events or procedures affecting the due process requirements of adequate notice and a meaningful opportunity to be heard. (See Vollstedt v. City of Stockton (1990) 220 Cal.App.3d 265, 273.) Under Government Code section 11513(b), each party has the right to call and examine witnesses, cross-examine opposing witnesses, and impeach any witness regardless of which party called him or her.

The trial court’s independent evaluation of the fair trial issue is not a trial de novo; the court renders its independent judgment on the basis of the record. (City of Fairfield v. Superior Court (1975) 14 Cal.3d 768, 776.) Here, the burden is on Petitioner to show his hearing was unfair.

First, Petitioner asserts that the Hearing Officer was biased. To invalidate the University’s disciplinary decision on this ground, Petitioner must demonstrate actual bias, not just “an inference or appearance of bias.” (Doe v. Occidental College (2019) 40 Cal.App.5th 208, 226.) Petitioner does not identify any actions or statements by the Hearing Officer demonstrating bias. Rather, he asks the Court to infer bias solely from the fact that the Hearing Officer reached an allegedly unreasonable conclusion. (Op. Br. at 15-16.) Petitioner has therefore not met his burden of establishing actual bias on the part of the Hearing Officer.

Petitioner next contends that the Hearing Officer impermissibly shifted the burden to Petitioner to prove his own innocence. Petitioner cites two portions of the administrative record to support this claim, each referencing Respondent’s Nondiscrimination Policy. The Nondiscrimination Policy correctly states that the accused in a Title IX proceeding is presumed not responsible (AR 121), and that the University has the burden of gathering evidence sufficient to reach a determination (AR 81). These provisions do not establish that the Hearing Officer shifted the burden of proof to Petitioner.

If Petitioner’s argument is that the Hearing Officer shifted the burden through her credibility analysis, the Court finds that characterization unsupported by the record. Credibility determinations in administrative proceedings necessarily involve evaluating a party’s consistency and willingness to provide information. The Hearing Officer’s discussion of Petitioner’s omissions does not demonstrate that she required Petitioner to disprove the allegations. Rather, these points appear in the Hearing Officer’s explanation of why she found the Complainant’s account more credible. The Nondiscrimination Policy explicitly assigns the University the burden of proof, and the Hearing Officer’s written findings reflect that she credited the Complainant’s testimony and corroborating witness statements as affirmative evidence of responsibility. Nothing in the decision suggests that she treated Petitioner’s silence, strategy, or lack of detail as a substitute for proof by Respondent.[2]

Finally, Petitioner argues that the Hearing Officer penalized him for exercising his procedural rights, pointing to the following comment in the Hearing Officer’s penalty recommendation:

“Moreover, Respondent declined to participate in the process until after he had the opportunity to review the Preliminary Investigation Report, and then he would only respond in writing. Respondent declined to meet and be interviewed by the investigator. People who have nothing to hide, hide nothing.”

(AR 932.)

Standing alone, the Hearing Officer’s statement may appear to penalize Petitioner for choosing not to participate in certain parts of the process. Read in context, however, it conveys something different. The comment appears in the portion of the Hearing Officer’s analysis responding to Petitioner’s post-hearing written submission challenging the sanction recommendation. (AR 931-32.) The Hearing Officer noted that, although she had already issued her decision finding Complainant credible and Petitioner not credible, Petitioner continued to attack Complainant’s credibility and to insist he was innocent, even though responsibility had already been adjudicated. (AR 932.) After briefly summarizing the basis for her credibility determinations, the Hearing Officer included the challenged comment. (AR 932.)

In this context, the Court understands the comment as an observation that Petitioner, through his own strategic choices, declined several opportunities to bolster his credibility, such as giving earlier statements, participating in an interview with the investigator, or maintaining a consistent account throughout the process. The Hearing Officer is not prohibited from noting how those choices affected her assessment of credibility.

In any event, Petitioner identifies only this single comment. There is no evidence of repeated remarks or a pattern of similar statements by the Hearing Officer. Because this one statement is susceptible to a reasonable, permissible interpretation, the Court is not persuaded that it reflects unfairness in the hearing. Accordingly, Petitioner has not met his burden.

3.               Imposing Expulsion Was Not a Manifest Abuse of Discretion

Finally, Petitioner contends that the penalty of expulsion was an abuse of discretion because it did not serve the goals of disciplinary sanctions as stated in CCR, Title 5, section 41301, and that the Hearing Officer improperly considered Petitioner’s exercise of his procedural rights as an aggravating factor.[3]

In arguing that the Hearing Officer abused her discretion in recommending expulsion, citing CCR, title 5, section 41301, Petitioner asserts that “disciplinary sanctions serve the dual purposes of maintaining a safe and orderly campus environment and furthering the University’s educational mission by addressing student behavior that threatens the safety, well-being, or rights of the University community.” (Pet. Op. Br. at 13.) This appears to reference subsection (d) of CCR, Title 5, section 41301, which identifies the categories of individuals who may be sanctioned and the types of conduct subject to sanction.[4] (CCR, tit. 5, §41301(d).) However, this subdivision does not establish the standard for choosing what sanction to impose.

The propriety of a penalty imposed by an administrative agency is a matter in the discretion of the agency, and its decision may not be disturbed unless there has been a manifest abuse of discretion. (Lake v. Civil Service Commission (1975) 47 Cal.App.3d 224, 228.) Neither an appellate court nor a trial court is free to substitute its discretion for that of the administrative agency concerning the degree of punishment imposed. (Nightingale v. State Personnel Board (1972) 7 Cal.3d 507, 515; UCSD, supra, 5 Cal.App.5th at 1106.) “Moreover, ‘[i]t is only in the exceptional case, when it is shown that reasonable minds cannot differ on the propriety of the penalty, that an abuse of discretion is shown.’¿” (UCSD at 1106, quoting Deegan v. City of Mountain View (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 37, 47.)

The Hearing Officer enumerated the factors she considered in making her recommendation, stating under each factor the relevant findings that influenced her conclusion. (AR 933-34.)

First, the Hearing Officer considered the severity of the offense, and the harm, or potential harm to the campus community or member of the campus community. (AR 932.) She found that Petitioner forcibly penetrated Complainant despite her clear rejections, a severe offense. (AR 932.) This finding was supported primarily by Complainant’s testimony to the same. (AR 584.)

Second, the Hearing Officer discussed the actual or potential consequences of the behavior in question. (AR 933.) The Hearing Officer noted that Complainant had to have an STD test, requested a shift change to avoid Petitioner, experienced a change in her voice, experienced emotional distress including anxiety and panic attacks, and lost her friendship with Petitioner.¿ (AR 933.) This finding was supported by Complainant’s testimony and statements in interview, by witness testimony, and by Petitioner’s statements. (E.g. AR 163 [Director Blakey’s summary of her interview with Complainant reporting Complainant experienced anxiety, fear, and panic], 597 [Complainant’s testimony that she got an STD test], 705-06 [Nicholas White discussing Complainant’s schedule change request], 725 [Witness 6 noting a substantial change in Complainant’s voice], 823 [Petitioner: “it ruined our friendship”].)

Third, the Hearing Officer considered Petitioner’s past disciplinary record. (AR 933.) She was unaware of any prior discipline. (AR 933.)

Fourth, the Hearing Officer discussed Petitioner’s grade level, as the expectation of awareness of the inappropriateness and consequences of his behavior may be impacted by his maturity or experience in the campus community. (AR 933.) Based on the incident occurring near the end of Petitioner’s fourth year, the Hearing Officer concluded Petitioner must have attended the University’s mandatory training regarding sexual misconduct at least four times. (AR 933.)

Fifth, the Hearing Officer weighed the use of force, weapons, foreign objects, coercion, intimidation, threats, or humiliation. (AR 933.) She reiterated her conclusion that Petitioner forcibly penetrated Complainant in spite of her clear rejections. (AR 933; see AR 584.) Consistent with the Complainant’s testimony, the Hearing Officer found Petitioner held Complainant down by her neck, flipped her over, and then continued to penetrate her until he saw she was crying heavily. (AR 933; AR 584.)

Sixth, the Hearing Officer considered serious physical injury and/or intent to cause physical injury. (AR 933.) Here, she reiterated her conclusions that Complainant had to get an STD test and experienced a change in her voice. (AR 933; see AR 597, 725.)

Seventh, the Hearing Officer discussed premeditated behavior, planning, and/or predatory behavior. (AR 933-34.) She concluded that Petitioner forcibly penetrated Complainant in multiple instances, showing “repetitious predatory behavior.” (AR 933-34; see AR 583-84, 594-98.)

Eighth and finally, the Hearing Officer weighed violation of disciplinary sanctions, including disciplinary probation, suspension, no contact directives, and/or interference with interim measures. (AR 934.) She noted that there were no such violations. (AR 934.)

Petitioner argues that the penalty of expulsion was excessive. However, based on the Hearing Officer’s findings that he forcibly penetrated Complainant’s vagina with his penis multiple times in the face of her explicit refusal to have sex, and her further findings on the impact to Complainant, the Court cannot conclude that “no reasonable person could reach the conclusion” that Petitioner should be expelled.

As Petitioner argues, the Hearing Officer did mention Petitioner’s procedural choices in her sanction recommendation. (AR 931-32.) Although this comment is somewhat concerning, the Court, consistent with its fair hearing analysis, understands this comment as a poorly phrased note on Petitioner’s credibility rather than an improper consideration. Regardless, the record demonstrates that the Hearing Officer’s evaluation of the relevant factors relied solely on permissible matters and was sufficient to justify her conclusion.

Petitioner has not shown that the University manifestly abused its discretion in deciding to expel Petitioner.

V.              Conclusion

Petitioner has not met his burden to show Respondent abused its discretion either in withholding his degree or expelling him. Nor has Petitioner met his burden to show the proceedings were unfair. The petition for administrative mandamus is DENIED. Pursuant to Local Rule 3.231(n), respondent Board of Trustees of the California State University shall prepare, serve, and ultimately file a proposed judgment.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: MOTION FOR BOND UNDER CCP § 529.2

 

Real party in interest Hollywood Community Housing Corporation (“RPI”) seeks to require petitioner/plaintiff La Brea Washington Co., LLC (“Petitioner”) to post a $500,000 bond under Code of Civil Procedure (“CCP”)[1] section 529.2.

The motion is granted.

I.                Background

This matter involves two related actions filed by Petitioner La Brea Washington Co., LLC: (1) “Petition for Writ of Administrative Mandamus (CEQA)” (“Petition 154”) in case no. 25STCP00154 (“Case 154”) challenging the City of Santa Monica’s (“City”) CEQA determinations for an affordable-housing project proposed at 1217 Euclid Street; and (2) “Petition for Writ of Mandamus for Violations of Due Process and the Government Code and Complaint for Quiet Title” (“Petition 961”) in case no. 25STCP00961 (“Case 961”), challenging the City’s approval process for the same project and asserting additional property-related claims.

Respondent City and its City Council (collectively, “Respondent”) are defendants and respondents in both matters, and Hollywood Community Housing Corporation (“RPI”) is the real party in interest in both matters.

Petition 154 centers on Petitioner’s allegation that the City failed to properly evaluate environmental impacts of the affordable-housing project, including potential effects on the historic Wilshire Theater and nearby parking, when issuing a Notice of Exemption under CEQA Guidelines sections 15182 and 15061(b)(3). Petitioner also challenges the City’s reliance on the previously certified Housing Element EIR and Addendum, which addressed program-level impacts of the Housing Element and acknowledged that site-specific impacts would be reviewed at the project level.

Petition 961 asserts that the City’s approval of the project violated a 1954 deed restriction requiring the Property to be used for public purposes, that the City failed to provide adequate notice regarding elimination of public parking, and that the project would adversely affect Petitioner’s adjacent historic Wilshire Theater. Petitioner also challenged aspects of the City’s RFP process, replacement parking considerations, and the City’s December 17, 2024 approval of development agreements and ground leases for the affordable housing project, which includes demolition of approximately 100 public parking spaces and construction of 130 affordable units.

Both petitions arise from the same affordable housing project and assert that the City’s CEQA compliance, procedural conduct, and decisions regarding public parking were improper.

1.               Procedural History

Petitioner filed Petition 154 on January 16, 2025, and Petition 961 on March 14, 2025. Both matters proceeded before various judicial officers due to reassignment and a peremptory challenge, and were ultimately related and assigned to Judge James C. Chalfant.

Respondent and RPI filed special motions to strike each petition under section 425.19. The Court granted Special Motion 154 on June 9, 2025, and granted Special Motion 961 on July 29, 2025. The Court then consolidated both cases, designated Petition 154 as the lead case, and directed preparation of judgment.

Judgment dismissing both petitions was entered on August 15, 2025, and an Amended Judgment was entered on August 25, 2025. Petitioner filed a notice of appeal from the judgment on October 14, 2025.

On November 18, 2025, the Court granted Respondent and RPI’s motion for attorney fees, awarding a total of $548,911.30. Petitioner filed a notice of appeal from that order on January 21, 2026.

RPI filed the present motion for a bond under CCP section 529.2 on June 26, 2026. Petitioner filed opposition on August 25, 2026, and RPI filed its reply on August 31, 2026.

II.             Evidentiary Matters

1.               Request for Judicial Notice

RPI requests judicial notice of Petitioner’s[2] opening brief filed in appeal from this case, Appellate Case No. B350347 (“Appeal”) (RPI RJN Ex. A). The request is granted. (Evid. Code § 452(d).)

Petitioner requests judicial notice of the following exhibits: (1) Petitioner’s opening brief in the Appeal (Pet. RJN Ex. 1); (2) the Court’s denial of RPI’s[3] request to rule on its motion to dismiss (Pet. RJN Ex. 2); and (3) the Amended Judgment in this case entered August 25, 2025 (Pet. RJN Ex. 3.)

As the Court ruled for RPI’s request for judicial notice, Request 1 is granted. (Evid. Code § 452(d).) Request 2 is likewise granted. (Evid. Code § 452(d).) The Court need not take judicial notice of its Amended Judgment entered in this case. Request 3 is therefore denied.

2.               Evidentiary Objections

Petitioner lodges 44 objections to the Senna Declaration, objecting to every paragraph except the first two, despite Senna’s statement that the matters declared are within her personal knowledge and her role as CEO of RPI. As courts have recognized, “objecting to every single thing with no display of professional judgment or restraint is an abusive practice.” (Cohen v. Kabbalah Centre Internat., Inc. (2019) 35 Cal.App.5th 13.) Many of Petitioner’s objections challenge facts Petitioner itself alleges. (See Senna Decl., ¶ 3; Pet. 961, ¶ 78.)

The objections are largely boilerplate recitations of evidentiary grounds without explanation. Forty-three of the 44 objections invoke the best evidence rule (Evid. Code, §§ 1520, 1521, 1523(a)), even though most challenged statements do not reference any document. Thirty-eight objections claim lack of foundation, personal knowledge, or speculation (Evid. Code, §§ 403, 405, 702(a), 800, 802), notwithstanding Senna’s express foundation. Petitioner also asserts authentication objections (Evid. Code, § 1400) to non-documentary facts, and hearsay objections (Evid. Code, § 1200) where no out-of-court statements are offered. Scattered objections for argument, legal conclusion, and vagueness are similarly unsupported. Petitioner further objects to the balance of the declaration on relevance, despite its direct relation to the Project’s low-income housing status and alleged harm related to delay.

All objections are overruled. Any legal conclusions in the Senna Declaration will be disregarded.

RPI objects to portions of the Netty Declaration. Objection No. 3 to paragraph 4 is sustained as to the statement that the bond “would cause extreme hardship,” which is a legal conclusion. Objection No. 4 is sustained only as to the word “significant” in characterizing the appeal. The remaining objections concern weight rather than admissibility and are overruled.

RPI also objects to portions of the Fong Declaration, including assertions that certain statements are “incorrect,” which is not a proper evidentiary objection. Objection No. 3 to paragraph 6 is sustained only as to the word “timely,” a legal conclusion. All other objections are overruled.

Petitioner objects to portions of the Paster Declaration, repeating the same boilerplate approach described above. Petitioner also objects on the ground that certain statements reflect settlement discussions. Settlement communications outside mediation are inadmissible only when offered to prove liability or the validity of a claim. (HMS Capital, Inc. v. Lawyers Title Co. (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 204.) RPI offers the statements for a different purpose, and the objections are therefore overruled.

All evidence is admitted subject to appropriate weight.

III.           Discussion

1.               This Court has Jurisdiction

Proceedings that seek to enforce, vacate, or modify an appealed judgment, or that substantially interfere with an appellate court’s ability to conduct an appeal, are stayed. (Varian Medical Systems, Inc. v. Delfino (2005) 35 Cal.4th 180, 189-90 [“Varian”].) RPI asserts that a motion under section 529.2 is a collateral proceeding over which the Court retains jurisdiction; Petitioner asserts the bond requires findings “intertwined with the merits of the pending appeal” such that ruling now would “overlap with, and potentially conflict with,” the Court of Appeal’s review. (Mot. at 10; Opp. at 4.)

Petitioner’s argument does not frame the governing standard. The Court finds no authority specifically discussing its jurisdiction to hear a motion under section 529.2 pending appeal, so a more thorough discussion of the basic rule is warranted. Section 916 provides that perfecting an appeal stays proceedings “upon the judgment or order appealed from or upon the matters embraced therein or affected thereby,” including enforcement of the judgment. (§ 916(a).) The statute specifically provides that the trial court “may proceed upon any other matter embraced in the action and not affected by the judgment order.” (Ibid.)

The Supreme Court in Varian explained that section 916’s purpose is “to protect the appellate court's jurisdiction by preserving the status quo” and preventing trial court proceedings that may “affect” the appealed judgment. (Varian, supra, 35 Cal.4th at 189, quoting Elsea v. Saberi (1992) 4 Cal.App.4th 625, 629 (“Elsea”).) A matter is “embraced in” or “affected by” the judgment if post judgment proceedings on that matter would have any effect on the effectiveness of the appeal. (Varian at 189, citing In re Marriage of Horowitz (1984) 159 Cal.App.3d 377, 381 (“Horowitz”).) Proceedings that do not affect the appeal’s effectiveness are not stayed. (Varian at 190, quoting Betz v. Pankow (1993) 16 Cal.App.4th 931, 938 (“Betz”).

The Supreme Court further clarified that the fact that a post judgment order may moot an appeal is not, by itself, enough to establish that such proceeding “affects the effectiveness of the appeal and should be stayed under section 916. Rather, something more is needed.” (Varian at 189.) A proceeding warrants a stay only where (a) it directly or indirectly seeks to enforce, vacate, or modify the appealed judgment; (b) substantially interferes with the appellate court’s ability to conduct the appeal; (c) the possible outcomes of the appeal and proceeding are irreconcilable; or, (d) the purpose of the appeal is to avoid the proceeding. (Varian at 189-90; Elsea, supra, 4 Cal.App.4th at 629; Hollaway v. Scripps Memorial Hosp. (1980) 111 Cal.App.3d 719, 723-724; In re Marriage of Varner (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 932, 937; Prudential-Bache Securities, Inc. v. Superior Court (1988) 201 Cal.App.3d 924, 925.)

Thus, ancillary or collateral matters are not stayed. (Varian at 191, citing Betz, supra, 16 Cal.App.4th at 938.) Classic examples include motions for new trial and motions to expunge a lis pendens, even though the latter requires assessing the claimant’s likelihood of prevailing on the merits. (Varian at 191, citing In re Estate of Waters (1919) 181 Cal. 584, 587; Neff v. Ernst (1957) 48 Cal.2d 628, 634; United Professional Planning, Inc. v. Superior Court (1970) 9 Cal.App.3d 377, 383-386 (“United Professional Planning”).) A proceeding is collateral if it could occur regardless of the outcome on appeal. (Varian at 191, citing Horowitz, supra, 159 Cal.App.3d at 383.)

Attorney fee motions are ancillary or collateral. (E.g. In re Marriage of Sherman (1984) 162 Cal.App.3d 1132, 1140 [“Sherman”].) Prohibitory injunctions also generally proceed notwithstanding the appeal because they preserve, rather than alter, the status quo. (Daly v. San Bernardino County Bd. of Supervisors (2021) 11 Cal.5th 1030, 1035 [“Daly”]; Dewey v. Superior Court (1889) 81 Cal. 64, 68; Heinlen v. Cross (1883) 63 Cal. 44, 46; Merced Mining Co. v. Fremont (1857) 7 Cal. 130, 132 [”Merced Mining”].) Section 916 also contains statutory exceptions for matters the Legislature chose not to stay, including enforcement of monetary judgments and provisional remedies conditioned on undertakings. (§§ 917.1, 917.5, 917.65.)

The matters deemed ancillary or collateral or otherwise exempted by statute suggest a few general principles. The legislature did not create section 916 to stay monetary awards. (See Sherman, §§ 917.1.) The legislature also favors undertakings as security for judgments when enforcement is stayed. (See §§ 917.1-917.9.) The legislature prefers not to disturb provisional remedies. (See §§ 917.5, 917.65; Merced Mining, supra, 7 Cal. 130, 132 [“The language of [the predecessor statute] is general, and would at first seem to include the appeal from an order granting an injunction; but, upon an examination of the provisions of [the predecessor statute], it will be seen, that in all those cases the party is required by the judgment or order to do some affirmative act, not to refrain from doing a thing.”]; Daly, supra, 11 Cal.5th 1030, 1040 [approving Merced Mining].)

Section 529.2 provides a provisional remedy, namely, an undertaking designed to protect a developer from costs arising from a meritless action challenging a low- or moderate-income housing project. (§ 529.2.) The most analogous proceeding is a motion to expunge a lis pendens, which also requires evaluation of the probable validity of the underlying claim yet is treated as collateral to the appeal. (United Professional Planning, supra, 9 Cal.App.3d at 383-386.)

Petitioner’s sole jurisdictional argument is that the bond motion “turns on findings that are intertwined with the merits of the pending appeal.” (Opp. at 4.) Therefore, Petitioner argues, “[d]eciding [those issues] now would necessarily overlap with, and potentially conflict with, the issues now before the appellate court.” (Ibid.) But this argument would apply equally to lis pendens proceedings, and yet those are collateral. A section 529.2 motion does not enforce, vacate, or modify the judgment; does not interfere with the appellate court’s ability to resolve the appeal; does not produce an outcome irreconcilable with the appeal; and is not the type of proceeding the appeal seeks to avoid. Indeed, but for the appeal, the bond motion would be unnecessary.

For these reasons, the Court concludes that a motion under CCP section 529.2 is an ancillary or collateral matter. The Court therefore retains jurisdiction to decide it.

2.               Merits

a.                Petitioner Has Shown Grounds for the Motion

CCP section 529.2 permits a defendant to seek a bond where five conditions are met: (1) the plaintiff brings a civil action to challenge a housing project that qualifies as a “development project” under Government Code section 65928; (2) the project meets or exceeds the requirements for low- or moderate-income housing under Government Code section 65915; (3) the plaintiff seeks relief, including but not limited to injunctive relief, which has the effect of preventing or delaying the project; (4) the action is without merit; and (5) the action was brought in bad faith, vexatiously, for the purpose of delay, or to thwart the low- or moderate-income nature of the housing development project. (§ 529.2(a).)

Petitioner does not dispute the first three statutory elements, namely, that the Project qualifies under Government Code sections 65928 and 65915, and that the relief it seeks would delay the Project. The issues, therefore, are the fourth and fifth elements, i.e., whether the action is without merit, and whether it was brought for an improper purpose. (§ 529.2(a).)

This Court previously issued detailed rulings granting Special Motion 154 and Special Motino 961. Here is a summary of the pertinent findings:

For Petition 961, the quiet title cause of action failed because Petitioner did not file a lis pendens, did not include a legal description of the Property or adverse claimants, and did not seek quiet title relief. Petitioner asserted no ownership interest and later disclaimed the claim. The second cause of action for “failures to comply” is not a legally recognized cause of action and was waived when Petitioner declined to defend it. The third cause of action was substantively a due process claim, but Petitioner disclaimed any property interest and thus could not state either procedural or substantive due process. The challenged decision was quasi-legislative, eliminating any procedural notice requirement. In short, Petition 961 was facially defective: two causes of action depended on a property interest Petitioner disclaimed, and one was not a cognizable claim.

For Petition 154, Petitioner’s first and third CEQA causes of action were time-barred because the statute of limitations to challenge the Housing Element EIR expired years earlier. Even assuming review could proceed under a tiered-EIR or “new information” framework, the Housing Element EIR expressly analyzed the environmental effects Petitioner claims were overlooked, including impacts from redevelopment of parking lots adjacent to historic resources. It found such impacts significant and unavoidable. The EIR contemplated affordable development on the Property and identified the Wilshire Theater as historic. Petitioner offered no evidence that the Project fell outside the scope of the Housing Element EIR. Respondent lacked discretion to require replacement parking, making parking impacts not subject to CEQA mitigation review. Petitioner’s asserted economic impacts are non-environmental as a matter of law. The second CEQA cause of action failed because Respondent did not rely on the “common sense” exemption Petitioner challenged.

While the Court’s extensive analysis may suggest the first and third causes of action had some merit but were ultimately unlikely to succeed, that is not the case. The record demonstrates these claims were independently unsupported and legally deficient. The gravamen of Petitioner’s CEQA challenge was that Respondent failed to analyze impacts to the Wilshire Theater from reduced parking. That claim is contradicted by the Housing Element EIR’s existing program level analysis of parking-related impacts to historic resources, and the City’s lack of discretion regarding parking requirements. Thus, all CEQA claims lacked merit.

The record also supports the conclusion that the petitions were filed for the purpose of delay.

A lack of objective merit may support an inference of improper purpose. (Save Livermore Downtown v. City of Livermore (2024) 87 Cal.App.5th 1116, 1135, 1138 [“Save Livermore”].) Petition 154 presented a facial CEQA challenge, but Petition 961, filed two months later, consisted exclusively of claims that were facially defective. Petitioner repeatedly delayed proceedings, including filing Petition 154 near the end of the limitations period, seeking an extension for preparation of the record, exercising a peremptory challenge on the last permissible day (vacating the scheduled hearing on Special Motion 154), and waiting roughly 50 of 60 days to file its appeal. Petitioner then failed to timely pay required appellate transcript fees, resulting in a default notice.

RPI identifies the foregoing acts, and Petitioner offers no rebuttal. Petitioner also does not affirmatively state, even in conclusory form, that it is not litigating for delay.[4]

In reply,[5] RPI submits evidence that Petitioner’s principal, Netty, stated he intended to prolong proceedings to prevent the project from moving forward absent settlement on his preferred terms. (Paster Decl., ¶¶ 4-5.) Petitioner objected to the evidence but did not dispute its substance.

Considering the weakness of the claims, Petitioner’s litigation conduct, and the unrebutted evidence of intent, the Court finds that Petitioner initiated and maintained this action for the purpose of delay. Accordingly, RPI has shown the grounds for a bond motion under CCP section 529.2.

b.               Amount of the Undertaking

If the defendant establishes the grounds for imposing a bond, the court shall order the plaintiff to file the undertaking in an amount specified as security for the defendant’s costs and damages. (§ 529.2(b).) The plaintiff may seek to limit the amount of the bond by showing the bond would cause it to suffer undue economic hardship. (§ 529.2(b).) The court must take into consideration any admitted evidence of the plaintiff’s economic hardship, and must avoid causing the plaintiff undue economic hardship, even if this would require that the court impose no bond. (§ 529.2(b).) The bond shall in no case exceed $500,000. (§ 529.2(b).)

RPI seeks a $500,000 bond. RPI submits evidence that delay caused by this proceeding is likely to result in forfeiture of $33,598,519. (Senna Decl., ¶¶ 7-10.) RPI has also shown it has already incurred a loan of $28,500,000 for construction, which would ordinarily be repaid through the obtained tax credits. (Senna Decl., ¶ 9.) RPI has shown it has incurred over $1,000,000 in predevelopment debt and spent $967,000 to pay predevelopment costs. (Senna Decl., ¶ 10.) Even presuming the entire Project is halted and there are no expenditures beyond those attested in the Senna Declaration, RPI will not recover its expenditures and will be obligated to repay the predevelopment debt for costs around $2,000,000.

Based on this showing, a $500,000 undertaking at the statutory maximum of $500,000 is appropriate.

Petitioner argues such a bond would cause undue hardship because it cannot afford a $500,000 bond. Specifically, Petitioner argues that RPI’s evidence does not establish Petitioner can pay a $500,000 bond, that Petitioner has no revenue and has been paying for mortgage, insurance, maintenance, upkeep, and taxes out of pocket, and that Petitioner would be chilled from pursuing appellate review if compelled to post a $500,000 bond.

This argument improperly shifts the burden. Section 529.2, subdivision (b) provides that “the plaintiff may seek to limit the amount of the undertaking by presenting admissible evidence that filing an undertaking will cause it…undue economic hardship.” (§ 529.2(b), emphasis added.) The only relevant admitted evidence Petitioner offers is its assertion that it operates at a deficit and lacks sufficient funds to “accommodate” a $500,000 bond. (Netty Decl., ¶¶ 3-4.) This showing provides the Court no basis to determine what amount, if any, would avoid undue hardship. Where Petitioner bears the burden to make that showing, the Court will not eliminate the bond based on a failure to do so.

Moreover, Petitioner’s evidence is insufficient on its face. The assertion that the entity is operating at a deficit does not establish inability to post a bond; businesses may operate at a deficit while still being able to absorb discrete expenses. Petitioner also acknowledges it is paying litigation costs in this matter and on appeal. (Netty Decl., ¶ 3.) The Court has discretion to evaluate hardship, and Petitioner’s conclusory statements of inability to pay are unpersuasive. (See Save Livermore Downtown v. City of Livermore, supra, 87 Cal.App.5th at 1135, 1138.)

Accordingly, the Court will set the bond at $500,000.

IV.           Conclusion

The Court has jurisdiction to require Petitioner to post a bond under CCP section 529.2, and RPI has shown the action was brought to delay the project. The motion is granted. Petitioner is ordered to post a bond in the amount of $500,000 within fifteen (15) days.

I.                Background

On December 26, 2025, F4 Santa Fe Springs Lender, LLC (“Lender”) filed its complaint against Borrower for judicial foreclosure and specific performance of deed of trusts including assignment of rents, appointment of receiver, and injunctive relief.

On January 21, 2026, the Court heard Lender’s ex parte application for appointment of receiver, temporary restraining order (“TRO”), and OSC re: confirmation of appointment of receiver and for preliminary injunction. After briefing and oral argument, the Court continued the hearing to January 23, 2026 to allow the parties to address certain evidentiary issues.

On January 23, 2026, the court heard and granted Lender’s continued ex parte application. The Court set an OSC re: confirmation of appointment of receiver for February 17, 2026. The Court issued the TRO and Order Appointing Receiver appointing Michael Fiorina as rents and profits receiver the same day. On January 26, 2026, Receiver filed his oath of receiver and undertaking. On February 17, 2026, the Court heard the OSC re: confirmation of appointment of receiver and confirmed Michael Fiorina as Receiver.

On March 3, 2026, Borrower filed its answer to the complaint.

On July 1, 2026, the Court heard Receiver’s ex parte application for an order compelling turnover of all funds derived from the Property in compliance with the Order Appointing Receiver, authorizing Receiver to hire a forensic accountant, authorizing Receiver to hire counsel, and issuing an OSC re: contempt. The Court granted the application in part, granting Receiver authority to engage SandDollar Financial, LLC (“SandDollar”) as a forensic accountant and granting Receiver authority to engage Buchalter LLP as counsel. The Court denied Receiver’s request for an OSC re: contempt. The Court denied Receiver’s request for an immediate turnover order as to the April and May 2026 revenues without prejudice and set an OSC re: turnover for July 16, 2026. The Court again ordered Borrower and its property operator Bridgeland Resources, LLC (“Operator”) to fully cooperate with the Receiver.

On July 16, 2026, the Court heard the OSC re: turnover. The Court determined Borrower was in violation of the Order Appointing Receiver, and ordered Borrower to turn over all gross proceeds for April and May 2026 by July 24, 2026.

On August 21, 2026, the Court heard Receiver’s ex parte application for instructions and enforcement of the receivership order. The Court denied the ex parte application, but issued the instant OSC re: OSC re: contempt (that is, an order to show cause why this Court should not set an OSC re: contempt). The Court directed Borrower “to file a meaningful written response, supported by competent declarations, addressing the allegations and requests for relief raised in the Receiver’s August 19, 2026 ex parte application,” no later than 2:00 p.m. on September 2, 2026. The Court also ordered that Receiver may file a reply and must file a status update, each by 2:00 p.m. on September 8, 2026. On September 2, 2026, at 6:24 p.m., Borrower untimely filed its written response and supporting declarations. On September 8, 2026, Receiver timely filed its reply and status update.

II.             Analysis

The receiver is an agent and officer of the appointing court.[1] (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1179(a); City of Santa Monica v. Gonzalez (2008) 43 Cal.4th 905, 930 [“Gonzalez”]; People v. Stark (2005) 131 Cal.App.4th 184, 204 [“Stark”].) “The Receiver is but the hand of the court to aid it in preserving and managing the property involved in the suit for the benefit of those to whom it may ultimately be determined to belong.” (Free Gold Min. Co. v. Spiers (1901) 135 Cal.130, 132.) Property in receivership remains under the court's control and continuous supervision. (Gonzalez at 930; Stark at 204.) The functions and powers of a receiver are controlled by statute, by the order of appointment, and by the court's subsequent orders. (Gonzalez at 930; Cal-American Income Property Fund VII v. Brown Development Corp. (1982) 139 Cal.App.3d 268, 273.)

In its ex parte Order Appointing Receiver, later confirmed after an OSC re: confirmation of receiver, the Court ordered, inter alia, that the Receiver shall operate the property and take possession of all accounts relating to the property. In the included temporary restraining order, the Court ordered Borrower, inter alia, to “[i]mmediately turn over to the receiver all keys, books, documents, and records relating to the property and advise the receiver of federal taxpayer identification numbers relating to the property.” The Court renewed this order in the preliminary injunction included in the Order Confirming Appointment of Receiver.

This Court also ordered in the Order Appointing Receiver: “The receiver and the parties may at any time apply to this court for further instructions and orders and for additional powers necessary to enable the receiver to perform the receiver’s duties properly.”

This Court, on multiple prior occasions, has found Borrower in defiance of this Court’s orders, to the point where this Court on July 1, 2026 “again ordered [Borrower and Operator] to fully cooperate with the Receiver.”

Borrower has shown no justification for its noncompliance in response to this OSC. Borrower styles its untimely response as its “Further Opposition to Ex Parte Application by Receiver Michael Fiorina Continued to September 10, 2026,” but makes no mention of contempt or any OSC. Borrower cites no court case, statute, or other legal authority. Most critically, Borrower has not argued that it had legal justification excusing it from complying with the Court’s orders. Borrower instead uses its briefing to allege a series of misconduct and incompetence by Receiver and presupposes that such misconduct and incompetence entitle it to refuse demands by Receiver and defy Court orders. Borrower has not, to date, sought affirmative relief to correct any purported improper conduct by the Receiver. Borrower is not free to disregard the Court’s orders and attempt to justify its noncompliance only after Receiver seeks relief.

In addition, the Court finds that Borrower’s filings do not constitute the “meaningful written response, supported by competent declarations” that this Court ordered on August 21, 2026. Borrower neither addresses nor substantiates multiple categories of information identified in the Receiver’s August 19, 2026 application. This includes an accounting for the April and May 2026 revenues; shut-in dates or operational basis for the shut-in; restoration plan or identification of any actual impediment to restoring pipeline service or production; documentation regarding the April 19 spill, pipeline repair, or insurance; CalGEM compliance materials or abandonment-related records; current permit lists, regulatory correspondence, or other compliance documentation; utility records; or operational, technical, engineering, reservoir, or budgetary materials necessary for the forensic accounting or for the Receiver’s preservation duties. Borrower did not provide verified responses addressing these subjects and did not identify when any missing information would be produced.

In his declaration, Will DePuy (“DePuy”) asserts Borrower fully complied, cooperated, and timely responded. (DePuy Decl., ¶ 10.) DePuy also asserted, “Per the above Timeline Summary and attached emails, Bridgeland has timely produced all documents requested.” (DePuy Decl., ¶ 38.) Yet despite DePuy’s conclusory statements, the DePuy Declaration affirmatively shows noncompliance with Receiver’s required weekly reports. (See e.g. DePuy Decl., ¶¶ 6, 23, 26.) Similarly, Borrower’s supplemental brief implies Borrower fully complied by repeatedly asserting Borrower provided responses. Yet the responses Borrower provides, as found in exhibits to the DePuy Declaration, do not reflect turnover to and complete cooperation with Receiver.[2] (See e.g. DePuy Decl., Ex. D at pp. 1-2,  Ex M at p. 2, Ex. O. at pp. 1-4.) These responses include failures to produce documents on several bases including that they are not digitized, have been previously produced to Lender, and/or that the information is public. (DePuy Decl., Ex. O at pp. 1-4.) In some cases, Borrower has conditioned its compliance on certain actions by Receiver. (DePuy Decl., Ex. O at p. 2.)

In sum, Borrower has neither complied with the Court’s orders, nor provided any legal justification for noncompliance.
