DEPARTMENT I LAW AND MOTION RULINGS

If the parties wish to submit on the tentative ruling and avoid a court appearance on the matter, the moving party must contact the opposing party and all other parties who have appeared in the action and confirm that each will submit on the tentative ruling. Please call the court no later than 4:30 p.m. on the court day before the hearing, leave a message with the court clerk at (310) 260-3629 advising her that all parties will submit on the tentative ruling and waive hearing, and finally, serve notice of the Court's ruling on all parties entitled to receive service. If any party declines to submit on the tentative ruling, then no telephone call is necessary, and all parties should appear at the hearing.

Case Number: 24SMCV00593    Hearing Date: August 10, 2026    Dept: I

The court is disappointed.  This hearing is the latest in a series of hearings arising from a startling lapse of ethics by defense counsel.  The court has already stated that sanctions will be awarded to plaintiff for the extra work that was required by virtue of defendants’ misconduct.  Plaintiff’s bill was, it is fair to say, huge.  Multiple times greater than a discovery sanction in even atypical cases.  The reason is that plaintiff’s counsel included fees relating to the motion to disqualify defense counsel based on defense counsel’s false statement as to who their client was and also for plaintiff’s work to prepare FSC materials because defense counsel would not participate.  The court asked the parties to discuss the numbers and try to reach an agreement.  But it appears that the parties have made no effort to discuss this.  Defendants, while saying that they take the issues seriously, do not, at least in the court’s view.  The DQ motion arose directly from defendants’ misconduct in purporting to represent a client that they did not represent—something that they still claim was just an “oops.” 

 

As the court has previously stated, that conduct—and other misconduct detailed in prior orders—is sanctionable under both the Discovery Act and CCP sections 128.5 and 128.7.  There is absolutely no justification for purporting to represent a client that one does not represent.  And the objections made on behalf of the non-client were in patent bad faith—which warrants not only a discovery sanction, but also a 128.7 sanction that is designed to punish.  For example, defendants objected to terms as vague and ambiguous that were not even used in the discovery request at issue.  Defendants objected based on a lack of consumer record notice although the production did not call for such records.  (That is important because, once made, it subjects the producing party to jeopardy if documents are produced anyway.)  Defendants seem to not understand that the court takes this seriously.  They claim that they are sorry, but other than put into place calendaring procedures that have been proven to be ineffective, they have done absolutely nothing to convince the court that they understand the gravity of the situation or have done anything to ensure that this does not happen again.  For example, they have taken no ethics courses, they have made no offers to compensate plaintiff for the extra time and expense to which they put plaintiff due to their bad behavior, and other than the ineffective calendaring system the court has seen nothing put into place to ensure that this sort of problem does not happen again.  As to payment of sanctions, even if the parties cannot agree on a number that is acceptable to both sides, it appears that defendant’s counsel’s position is that the number should be zero, because that is all that counsel has offered to pay unconditionally.

 

Plaintiff, for its part, has lumped in the entire trial preparation costs.  That is to some extent not surprising.  Defendants blew off the FSC, forcing plaintiff to go it alone.  Defendants state that this work would have had to be done anyway.  The court agrees, and the court is inclined to take this as binding on both parties.  That means that plaintiff cannot recover for the FSC work, and it means that defendants have conclusively joined in plaintiff’s FSC papers and stipulate to those documents—including witness lists, exhibit lists (and the admissibility of the exhibits), jury instructions, and everything else.  If the court is mistaken about defendant’s position, then defendant’s conclusion makes no sense.  (Although even then, the court cannot say that 100% of the FSC work was useless.)  Plaintiff, for its part, has made no effort to segregate these fees or try and figure out how much of the work is truly bound for the circular file if defendants are allowed to engage in the FSC process.   

 

At this point, no one seems to care about the case or the merits.  Defendants want to sweep this under the rug and hope to get away with minimal monetary sanctions.  For example, as stated above, the court notes that defendants have made no effort to consider what an appropriate sanction would be and simply pay it.  It would be a concession that sanctions are appropriate—but even the “oops” defense concedes that much.  Instead, defendants continue to act as if this is just not a big deal.  Plaintiffs seem to try and use this as a profit center rather than what it was designed to be.

 

Under the circumstances, the court might need to report defense counsel to the bar.  The court does not do this lightly.  In fact, the court believes that this might be the first time it has done so.  But counsels’ past conduct is inexcusable, and the apparent failure of counsel to understand the seriousness of the problem and take responsibility for it in a meaningful way means that the court cannot be confident that defendants’ behavior will not repeat in this or another case.  The court is surprised that it has come to this pass; frankly, the court assumed that defendants would take action that demonstrates that it is serious when a named partner and managing partner sign documents in bad faith and without so much as reading the document.  But actions speak louder than words, and defense counsel has taken no action to demonstrate that they understand the serious nature of the problem.  Therefore, as to the discovery sanctions, the court will ask plaintiff’s counsel to separate out those sanctions that relate to discovery alone.  The court will review the new numbers and determine if the court agrees that the number is correct.  As to the motion to disqualify, the court is inclined to grant sanctions as to that amount under CCP sections 128.5 and 128.7.  If defendants want an opportunity to oppose that, the court will discuss it, but the court thinks defendants have had plenty of opportunity.  The fact is that plaintiff believed that it had grounds to seek disqualification because defense counsel falsely stated that it represented a client that plaintiff believed created a conflict.  The fact that defense counsel in fact did not represent that client notwithstanding the repeated statements to the contrary in the document (including right below the signature line) does not mean that plaintiff’s motion was unjustified when made.  As to sanctions relating to the FSC, the court is inclined to DENY them in light of defendant’s apparent agreement that plaintiff’s FSC materials will govern. 


Case Number: 24SMCV03128    Hearing Date: August 10, 2026    Dept: I

The case is here on an FSC, but it is in an odd posture.  Defense counsel withdrew, so that the non-human entity cannot go forward unless new counsel has been retained.  The court has received no FSC documents, but the court has a suspicion as to why that might be.  Plaintiff had filed an MSJ earlier based on what plaintiff assumed would be an order deeming certain requests for admissions to be deemed admitted.  But the motion was filed before the order, so the MSJ was denied.  The court notes that the MSJ was unopposed.  Since then, the order deeming the RFAs to be admitted was made.  So there really is not likely to be a trial.

 

If both parties are here, the court will discuss what the right procedure will be in light of the deemed admitted order.  The court is of the view that those admissions in fact do dispose of the case.  As such, it might be that the parties (or plaintiff if defendant is not here) will want to waive jury, admit the admissions and exhibits, and then rest, or rest after a very short bit of testimony from the assignor.  The court’s current thinking is that the judgment plaintiff proposes would then be entered with two differences.

 

The case, according to plaintiff, involves two financial transactions.  One is a loan made in 2017.  It was for $150,000.  It was supposed to be repaid quickly but was not.  Defendant allegedly then agreed to repay that loan from a business partnership that the parties entered into later.  And that is the subject of the second transaction.  Plaintiff invested $300,000 in a partnership with defendant involving masks.  Defendant personally guaranteed that the investment would be repaid within a couple of months.  It was not repaid and the business seems to have gone nowhere.  Plaintiff sues for those sums, plus interest.  That much seems established given the admissions.  What is not established is plaintiff’s attempt to treble the $300,000 pursuant to Penal Code section 496, which so allows when a defendant receives stolen property.  The problem the court has here is that it does not see any stolen property.  The $300,000 was not stolen—it was invested.  Plaintiff claims that defendant wrongly used the money to buy masks.  The court is not sure how that is wrongful—that was, after all, the purpose of the partnership.  True, there is an RFA where defendant has been deemed to admit that this statute applies, but one cannot use an RFA that way—at least not bindingly.  RFAs are used to admit facts, not legal conclusions.  And the court does not see it here.  As such, the court is not inclined to award treble damages.  Nor does the court see a basis for attorneys fees.  Those fees are sought based only on the same statute, and they are therefore not recoverable for the same reason.

 

Thus, on this record, the court would be inclined to award judgment the principal amount of $450,000, prejudgment interest per plaintiff’s calculation, and costs. 

 

If plaintiff wants to proceed with an actual trial, or if a defendant is here and ready, we might have to wait.  That will depend on whether it is a jury trial or a bench trial and the length.  Right now, the court is otherwise engaged in a trial, but could hear the matter today if it is expedited.


Case Number: 24SMCV04259    Hearing Date: August 10, 2026    Dept: I

This is an application to shorten time for plaintiff’s motion to deem RFAs to be admitted.  This case was filed in September 2024.  The FSC was held on February 28, 2025, and the trial date was set then.  Plaintiff served RFAs on defendant on July 1, 2026, making responses due on August 4, 2026.  No responses were served.  In fact, according to plaintiff, the defendant to whom the RFAs were served has done nothing in the entire case other than filing an answer and a cross complaint.  On August 6, 2026, plaintiff called defense counsel to inquire about this and to ask if there would be responses coming.  No one at counsel’s office was available, so plaintiff left a message, which was apparently ignored.  Because of that, plaintiff reserved a date for a hearing on the motion to deem admitted.  Sadly, due to the court’s calendar and a somewhat lengthy period that the court will be dark, the next available date for that hearing is November 20, which is after the November 9, 2026, trial date.  Were the court able to hold the hearing on statutory notice, a motion filed on August 6, 2026, would be timely.

 

The court’s inclination would be to GRANT the application to shorten time and set a hearing date that it before the discovery cut off, and to have briefing per Code.  The court assumes that the mere filing of the motion and the setting of the date will wind up mooting the substance, as the failure to serve verified responses without objection prior to the hearing would, assuming that plaintiff’s representations are true, lead to the court’s granting the motion.  Because that is a harsh penalty, albeit one required by the Code of Civil Procedure, the court ventures to guess that well before the hearing, plaintiff will serve responses.  But if not, the court would expect the opposition and optional reply to be filed per Code.
