DEPARTMENT S27 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS



Case Number: 23LBCV02237    Hearing Date: August 6, 2026    Dept: S27

 

1.     Background Facts

Plaintiff, Hector Jalomo filed this action against Defendants, Muldoon’s Saloon, Cocktails, LLC, Glenn Vaughn, and Joseph Annigoni for damages arising out of an altercation that occurred in the entity defendant’s bar. 

 

2.     Motion for Summary Judgment

a.     Allegations of the Complaint

The altercation occurred at Muldoon’s Saloon, which is entirely owned by Cocktails, LLC.  Cocktails is an LLC with a single member – Joseph Annigoni.  Plaintiff sues Annigoni for premises liability, alleging the premises were in a dangerous condition due to Defendants’ negligent failure to have security guards and/or to ensure their employees intervened when it was apparent the altercation was about to occur.

 

b.     Burdens on Summary Judgment

Summary judgment is proper “if all the papers submitted show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.”  (Code Civ. Proc. §437c(c).)  Where a defendant seeks summary judgment or adjudication, he must show that either “one or more elements of the cause of action, even if not separately pleaded, cannot be established, or that there is a complete defense to that cause of action.”  (Id. at §437c(o)(2).)  A defendant may satisfy this burden by showing that the claim “cannot be established” because of the lack of evidence on some essential element of the claim.  (Union Bank v. Superior Court (1995) 31 Cal.App.4th 574, 590.)  Once the defendant meets this burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to show that a “triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to that cause of action or defense thereto.”  (Ibid.) 

 

The moving party bears the initial burden of production to make a prima facie showing that there are no triable issues of material fact.  (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850.)  A defendant moving for summary judgment must show either (1) that one or more elements of the cause of action cannot be established or (2) that there is a complete defense to that cause of action.  (Id. at §437c(p).)  A defendant may discharge this burden by furnishing either (1) affirmative evidence of the required facts or (2) discovery responses conceding that the plaintiff lacks evidence to establish an essential element of the plaintiff's case. If a defendant chooses the latter option he or she must present evidence “and not simply point out that plaintiff does not possess and cannot reasonably obtain needed evidence….” Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at 865-66,

[A] defendant may simply show the plaintiff cannot establish an essential element of the cause of action “by showing that the plaintiff does not possess, and cannot reasonably obtain, needed evidence.” (Id. at p. 854.)  Thus, rather than affirmatively disproving or negating an element (e.g., causation), a defendant moving for summary judgment has the option of presenting evidence reflecting the plaintiff does not possess evidence to prove that element. “The defendant may, but need not, present evidence that conclusively negates an element of the plaintiff's cause of action. The defendant may also present evidence that the plaintiff does not possess, and cannot reasonably obtain, needed evidence—as through admissions by the plaintiff following extensive discovery to the effect that he has discovered nothing” to support an essential element of his case. (Aguilar, supra, at p. 855.) Under the latter approach, a defendant's initial evidentiary showing may “consist of the deposition testimony of the plaintiff's witnesses, the plaintiff's factually devoid discovery responses, or admissions by the plaintiff in deposition or in response to requests for admission that he or she has not discovered anything that supports an essential element of the cause of action.” (Lona v. Citibank, N.A., supra, 202 Cal.App.4th at p. 110.)  In other words, a defendant may show the plaintiff does not possess evidence to support an element of the cause of action by means of presenting the plaintiff's factually devoid discovery responses from which an absence of evidence may be reasonably inferred. (Scheiding v. Dinwiddie Construction Co. (1999) 69 Cal.App.4th 64, 83.)

 

Thus, a moving defendant has two means by which to shift the burden of proof under the summary judgment statute: “The defendant may rely upon factually insufficient discovery responses by the plaintiff to show that the plaintiff cannot establish an essential element of the cause of action sued upon. [Citation.] [Or a]lternatively, the defendant may utilize the tried and true technique of negating (‘disproving’) an essential element of the plaintiff's cause of action.” (Brantly v. Pisaro (1996) 42 Cal.App.4th 1591, 1598.)  Leyva v. Garcia (2018) 20 Cal.App.5th 1095, 1103.

 

Until the moving defendant has discharged its burden of proof, the opposing plaintiff has no burden to come forward with any evidence. Once the moving defendant has discharged its burden as to a particular cause of action, however, the plaintiff may defeat the motion by producing evidence showing that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to that cause of action.  (Id. at §437c(p)(2).)  On a motion for summary judgment, the moving party's supporting documents are strictly construed and those of his opponent liberally construed, and doubts as to the propriety of summary judgment should be resolved against granting the motion.  (D’Amico v. Board of Medical Examiners (1974) 11 Cal.3d 1, 21.)

 

c.     Issues Presented on Motion

The parties agree that Annigoni can be held liable for Plaintiff’s injuries if one of two circumstances is present.  First, he is liable if he personally participated in the tortious activity that gives rise to the complaint.  Second, he is liable if he is Cocktails, LLC’s alter ego.

 

d.     Evidentiary Objections

Plaintiff filed an objection to the entirety of the Annigoni Declaration.  The objection is overruled.

 

e.     Personal Participation

Plaintiff contends Annigoni personally participated in the tortious conduct because he was the sole person responsible for making the decisions that led to the existence of the dangerous condition – choosing not to hire security guards, choosing not to adequately train his employees, and choosing not to have an insurance policy in place that would allow Cocktails to pay for Plaintiff’s injuries.

 

Annigoni, in his moving papers, merely concludes that he did not participate in the tortious conduct and is therefore not liable under this theory.  The Court notes that Annigoni’s position is supported by the allegations of the complaint itself, and therefore Annigoni meets his moving burden in this regard.

 

In opposition and reply, the parties address the seminal case of Frances T. v. Village Green Owners Assn (1986) 42 Cal.3d 490.  Plaintiff's reliance on Frances T., however, is misplaced. While Frances T. recognized that corporate directors may be liable for nonfeasance under limited circumstances, such liability requires (1) an independent duty of care owed directly by the individual to the plaintiff, and (2) specific knowledge and direct participation in creating or increasing the specific hazard. Id. at 506.  General managerial omissions—such as corporate staffing or policy decisions regarding security—constitute nonfeasance owed solely to the entity, not a personal breach of duty to individual business invitees.  Plaintiff therefore failed to raise triable issues of material fact in this regard. 

 

f.      Alter Ego

i.      Law Governing Alter Ego Liability

Under California law, the corporate/LLC form will be disregarded and an individual member held liable for the entity's obligations only under narrowly defined circumstances. To invoke the alter ego doctrine, two conditions must be met:

  1. Unity of Interest and Ownership: There must be such unity of interest and ownership that the separate personalities of the corporation/LLC and the individual no longer exist.
  2. Inequitable Result: It must be demonstrated that an inequitable result will follow—that adherence to the fiction of separate corporate existence would sanction a fraud or promote injustice.

Sonora Diamond Corp. v. Superior Court (2000) 83 Cal.App.4th 523, 538–539; Associated Vendors, Inc. v. Oakland Meat Co. (1962) 210 Cal.App.2d 825, 837–838.

 

ii.          Moving Burden

Defendant supports his motion with his own declaration, wherein he avers that Cocktails was formed in 2018, and it retains all business records that it creates contemporaneously with decisions and acts taken.  He declares he is only on the lease as a guarantor of Cocktails, but is not personally a lessor.  He declares he does not operate Muldoon’s, which is operated by Cocktails.  He declares Cocktails has all relevant licenses, and has its own bank accounts, which are separate from his personal accounts.  He declares he keeps separate book and records from those of the LLC, and there is no comingling of funds. 

 

Defendant’s declaration is sufficient to meet the moving burden on summary judgment.

 

ii.          Shifted Burden

Plaintiff opposes summary judgment on the alter ego theory by relying on three facts:

  1. The individual Defendant is the sole member/owner of the LLC.
  2. The LLC holds minimal assets (approximately $5,000 in bank balance).
  3. The individual Defendant failed to obtain liability insurance for the business (and breached a lease covenant to do so).
  4. Cocktails was formed through Legal Zoom without an attorney, and its sole purpose was to operate Muldoon’s; Cocktails is not mentioned on receipts or menus, it has no telephone number, email address, or home address separate from Annigoni’s, and Annigoni sometimes signed corporate documents in his own name. 

 

Taking these facts as true, they fail as a matter of law to create a triable issue of material fact under either prong of the alter ego doctrine.

 

First, sole ownership of an LLC is explicitly permitted by statute and does not, standing alone, establish a unity of interest. Cal. Corp. Code, § 17701.01 et seq.  Under California Corporations Code section 17703.04(b), the failure to hold meetings or observe organizational formalities is explicitly excluded as a factor in establishing alter ego liability for a limited liability company. Thus, the mere fact that the individual Defendant operated as the single member and sole manager of the LLC does not satisfy the "unity of interest" prong.

 

Plaintiff argues that operating an uninsured bar with $5,000 in assets creates an "inequitable result" because it leaves Plaintiff with an uncollectible claim. However, California courts have consistently held that a plaintiff's difficulty or inability to collect on an unsatisfied judgment against an insolvent entity is insufficient as a matter of law to satisfy the second prong of the alter ego test.  In Sonora Diamond, supra, at 539, the Court held:

The alter ego doctrine does not guard every dissatisfied creditor against an insolvent debtor... The difficulty in enforcing a judgment or collecting a debt does not satisfy this standard. The alter ego doctrine is applied only where to do so will prevent an inequitable result, not to satisfy an unsatisfied judgment.

 

The "inequity" required to pierce the corporate veil must flow from the individual's bad-faith abuse or manipulation of the corporate form—such as commingling personal and business funds, siphoning entity assets into personal accounts to avoid potential creditors, or using the corporate shell as a scheme to commit fraud.  While undercapitalization is a factor considered under Associated Vendors, supra, undercapitalization or lack of insurance must be coupled with bad-faith financial conduct or commingling. Operating a business without insurance or with thin cash reserves constitutes poor management or insolvency, but it does not, standing alone, demonstrate that the individual defendant used the LLC structure as a bad-faith shield to perpetrate a fraud or injustice.

 

Finally, the individual defendant's failure to maintain insurance in breach of a lease covenant is a contractual breach owed to the lessor, not to Plaintiff. It does not convert an ordinary corporate failure into an independent equity-piercing event for a third-party tort claimant.

 

Plaintiff further contends that alter ego is established because the LLC was formed via LegalZoom without counsel, lacked a separate telephone or email address, failed to display the entity name on certain internal documents, and because the individual Defendant signed contracts without noting his representative capacity.

 

These facts, individually and collectively, fail to raise a triable issue of material fact.

 

First, the use of an online formation service rather than legal counsel is legally irrelevant to entity validity under Corporations Code section 17702.01. Second, under Corporations Code section 17703.04(b), the failure to observe internal organizational formalities is explicitly excluded as an alter ego factor for limited liability companies. Third, while signing a contract in an individual capacity may expose an agent to personal contractual liability on that specific instrument, it does not pierce the corporate shell to impose personal liability for unrelated third-party torts.

 

Finally, factors such as shared contact information or failure to hold out the corporate name carry weight primarily in contract actions where a plaintiff was fraudulently induced into dealing with an undisclosed shell. In a premises liability action, Plaintiff cannot show that these informalities caused any inequity or abuse of the corporate form relative to Plaintiff's injury. Without evidence of commingling, bad-faith asset draining, or fraud, Plaintiff's showing consists merely of typical small-business informalities, which are insufficient to defeat summary judgment on alter ego.

 

Because Plaintiff offers no evidence of commingling, asset-stripping, bad faith, or treatment of LLC accounts as personal funds, Plaintiff has failed to raise a triable issue of material fact as to either prong of the alter ego doctrine.

 

 

g.     Conclusion

Defendant met his moving burden to establish he did not personally participate in the acts that give rise to Plaintiff’s claims, and that he was not the alter ego of Cocktails.  Plaintiff failed to raise a triable issue of material fact in either regard, and the motion for summary judgment is granted.

 

Defendant is ordered to give notice.

Case Number: 23LBCV02241    Hearing Date: August 6, 2026    Dept: S27

 

1.    Background Facts

Plaintiff, Michael Bornn filed this action against Defendants, Community Loan Servicing, LLC and Nationstar Mortgage, LLC (erroneously sued and served as Community Loan Services Right Path Servicing) on November 22, 2023. The complaint includes a cause of action for constructive fraud and related claims. The complaint arises out of Defendants’ servicing of Plaintiff’s loan and attempts at foreclosure on the loan.

 

On June 27, 2024, the Court sustained a demurrer to Plaintiff’s First Amended Complaint. On October 16, 2024, Plaintiff filed his Second Amended Complaint. The SAC includes causes of action for title fraud, promissory estoppel, negligent misrepresentation, fraudulent misrepresentation, abuse of process, breach of contract, violation of BPC §17200, California Homeowners Bills of Rights Civil Code §§2923.7 and 2924.17, Violation of §1026.41, periodic statements, and elder abuse. The Court heard Defendants’ demurrer to the SAC and sustained it without leave to amend as to the title fraud, promissory estoppel, negligent misrepresentation, abuse of process, §2923.74, §2923.17, and §1026.41 causes of action without leave to amend as to Moving Defendants. Thus, the remaining claims pending against Moving Defendants are for fraudulent misrepresentation, breach of contract, UCL, and elder abuse. 

 

On May 1, 2025, the Court heard and granted Defendant’s summary judgment motion, finding it met its moving burden to show it was entitled to judgment as a matter of law, and in the absence of opposition, Plaintiff failed to raise a triable issue of material fact. On May 9, 2025, Plaintiff filed a motion for reconsideration asking the Court to allow Plaintiff to file an opposition to the motion for summary judgment. On July 15, 2025, the Court granted Plaintiff’s motion for reconsideration and allowed Plaintiff to file an opposition that complied with the Rules of Court and the Code of Civil Procedure.

 

On September 16, 2025, Plaintiff filed an opposition. On October 3, 2025, Defendant filed a reply and objections to the Plaintiff’s separate statement.  On 11/20/25, the Court granted the motion for a second time.

 

2.     Motion for Attorneys’ Fees

  1. Request for Judicial Notice

Defendants seek judicial notice of the subject promissory note, deed of trust, loan modification agreement, and various minute orders and other documents filed in this case.  The RJN is granted.

 

  1. Payoff Agreement

While this litigation was pending, the parties agreed that Plaintiff could sell the property and used the proceeds to pay off all debts related to the property.  The balance of the proceeds from the sale are being held by Klinedinst PC pending a ruling on how the proceeds should be distributed.

 

  1. Law Governing Attorneys’ Fees Awards in Breach of Contract Actions

California Civil Code §1717 establishes that in any action on a contract where the contract provides for attorney's fees, the prevailing party is entitled to reasonable attorney's fees as an element of costs.  The statute defines the prevailing party as the party who recovered greater relief in the action on the contract.  Id.  See also DisputeSuite.com, LLC v. Scoreinc.com (2017) 2 Cal.5th 968. This determination is made upon final resolution of the contract claims, comparing the relief awarded with the parties' demands and litigation objectives.  Sears v. Baccaglio (1998) 60 Cal.App.4th 1136.

 

  1. Subject Contract Provisions

Defendants contend the attorneys’ fees provisions in the payoff agreement and deed of trust require Plaintiff to pay their attorneys’ fees incurred in this proceeding. 

 

The only cited provision in the payoff agreement states, “The parties agree that any other debt or liability, if any shall be handled pursuant to Civil Code Section 2943 et seq.  The parties agree to reserve all rights to any fees and costs stemming from litigation.”  This provision does not create a right to fees, but instead merely reserves the right that may already exist.

 

Plaintiff’s relationship with Defendants is grounded in part on a deed of trust.  Defendants include a copy of the deed of trust with the request for judicial notice.  ¶9 of the deed of trust clearly creates an attorneys’ fees obligation in the event of a breach of the agreement.  Specifically, ¶9 includes language allowing Defendants to appear in court and pay attorneys’ fees in order to protect their rights under the instrument, and any such fees “shall become the additional debt of Borrower security by this Security Instrument.”  The Court therefore finds Defendants met their moving burden to show there is a contract that requires an attorneys’ fees award in this action. 

 

e.     Prevailing Party

Defendants prevailed on summary judgment, and are therefore the undisputable prevailing parties in this action.

 

  1. Amount of Fees

Defendants seek to recover $121,657.00 in attorneys’ fees incurred in this action.  The amount is fully supported by the Declaration of Kayleigh E. Thomas.  The Court has also reviewed the history of the action, both through the moving papers and also through a review of the court file.  The Court notes that the litigation was protracted and Plaintiff’s self-represented status often complicated the litigation.  The Court finds the amount reasonable, and in light of the lack of opposition to the motion, the amount is granted in full. 

 

  1. Costs

California Code of Civil Procedure Section 1032(b) states that, except as otherwise expressly provided by statute, a prevailing party is entitled as a matter of right to recover costs in any action or proceeding. Additionally, California Rules of Court, Rule 3.1700(a)(1) specifies that a prevailing party who claims costs must serve and file a memorandum of costs within 15 days after the date of service of written notice of entry of judgment or dismissal.  Kahn v. The Dewey Group (2015) 240 Cal.App.4th 227.

 

The Court will not award costs by way of noticed motion and will only do so by way of any timely and properly filed memorandum of costs. 

 

  1. Conclusion

The motion for attorneys’ fees is granted in full.  To the extent the motion seeks a costs order, the request is denied; the ruling is without prejudice to Defendants’ right to file a timely and proper memorandum of costs if they have not already done so. 

 

Defendants are ordered to give notice.

Case Number: 25LBCV01542    Hearing Date: August 6, 2026    Dept: S27

 

1.     Background Facts

Plaintiffs, Alfredo and Maria Fernandez filed this action against Defendants, City of Long Beach and Long Beach Public Transportation Company for damages arising out of an attack that occurred on a public bus in view of the bus driver.

 

2.     Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings

a.     Pre-Filing Requirements

LBTC moves for judgment on the pleadings, contending Plaintiffs did not file timely government tort claims.  Plaintiffs, in opposition, contend they served timely claims and, in any event, LBTC has waived any right to assert lack of compliance at this time.

 

LBTC seeks judicial notice of the plaintiffs’ tort claims and of the notice of rejection of claim.  Pursuant to Gong v. City of Rosemead (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 363, 376, the RJN is granted.

 

The parties agree that claims were due on or before six months after 5/22/24, which fell on or around 11/22/24.  The tort claims and related evidence are confusing.  The claims include a cover letter from Plaintiffs’ attorney dated 11/15/24.  The claims themselves include Plaintiffs’ signatures on the date 12/06/24.  LBTC’s responsible person declares he received the claims on 12/06/24. 

 

Plaintiffs’ attorney, with the opposition papers, submits a declaration explaining that he signed the claims on 11/20/24 and deposited them in certified mail that same day; the receipt shows arrival on 11/22/24, the last day for timely service of the claims.  Plaintiffs’ attorney explains that, after LBTC received the claims, its claims administrator asked Plaintiffs’ attorney to have the individual plaintiffs sign the claims, which he then prepared for signature and re-served. 

 

Cal Gov Code §910.2 provides that the claim “shall be signed by the claimant or some person on his behalf.”  The Court finds it cannot determine, from the face of the complaint and judicially noticeable documents, that the claims were not timely submitted.  The motion for judgment on the pleadings directed at the entire complaint on the ground that Plaintiffs failed to submit timely government tort claims is therefore denied.

 

The Court declines to rule on Plaintiffs’ remaining arguments re: waiver and/or Plaintiffs’ request for judicial notice, as doing so is not necessary to a resolution of the merits of the motion. 

 

b.     Negligence, NIED, and Negligence Per Se

LBTC moves for judgment on the pleadings on the negligence per se and NIED causes of action, contending they are duplicative of the negligence cause of action.  Negligence per se is technically just an evidentiary doctrine and does not state an independent tort.  See Quiroz v. Seventh Ave. Center (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 1256, 1285-1286. 

 

In Potter v. Firestone Tire & Rubber Co. (1993) 6 Cal.4th 965, 984-985, the Court held that there is no separate tort for NIED; it is merely the tort of negligence. 

 

It is, however, common to plead negligence per se and NIED separately, and is similar to pleading three “counts” of negligence.  At trial, Plaintiffs will, of course, be limited to a single recovery.  At the pleading stage, however, the Court finds the duplicative nature of the claims is not problematic, and the motion on this ground is denied.

 

c.     Conclusion

The motion for judgment on the pleadings is denied.

 

LBCT is ordered to give notice.

Case Number: 25LBCV01828    Hearing Date: August 6, 2026    Dept: S27

Defendant propounded RPDs, FROGs, and SROGs on Plaintiff on 1/06/26.  To date, Plaintiff has not served responses.  Defendant therefore moves to compel responses to the RPDs, FROGs, and SROGs. 

 

Plaintiff opposes the motion.  Plaintiff’s attorney declares there have been staffing issues at the firm, the client is not tech-savvy, which has delayed responding to discovery, and responses will be served prior to the hearing. 

 

To the extent responses are not served prior to the hearing, the motions to compel will be granted.  If responses are served, the motions will be deemed substantively moot.  If the parties submit on the tentative, the motions will be deemed substantively moot. 

 

Defendant’s notice of motion indicates Defendant seeks imposition of sanctions against Plaintiff and her law firm in the amount of its fees and costs incurred in connection with the motion.  Defense Counsel declares fees in excess of $1000 have been or will be incurred in connection with the motion.  Plaintiff argues sanctions should not be imposed because Plaintiff acted with good cause or substantial justification.  CCP §§2030.290(c), 2031.300(c).  Plaintiff relies on Foothill Properties v. Lyon/Copley Corona Associates (1996) 46 Cal.App.4th 1542, 1557-58, Doe v. U.S. Swimming, Inc. (2011) 200 Cal.App.4th 1424, 1434, and Do v. Superior Court (2003) 109 Cal.App.4th 1210, 1213 to support her position. 

 

In Lyon, the court of appeals reversed a sanctions order, finding that a party’s refusal to turn over discovery documents until after a hearing on a motion for a protective order was substantially justified and made in good faith.  In Doe, the court of appeals affirmed a trial court’s imposition of sanctions where the defendant persisted in insisting the trial court must review the documents in camera before they would be produced, despite the trial court’s admonishment that it would not do so.  In Do, the issue was whether an indigent client represented by a pro bono attorney was entitled to fees or not.

 

Plaintiff’s interpretation of the sanctions statute would turn the statute on its head.  Defendant propounded discovery, and Plaintiff failed to timely respond due to problems in Plaintiff’s (and her attorneys’) control and outside Defendant’s control.  The Court therefore finds imposition of sanctions appropriate.

 

The request for sanctions is granted.  Sanctions in the requested amount of $1000 are imposed against Plaintiff and her attorney of record, jointly and severally; the sanctions must be paid to Defendant, by and through its attorney of record, within twenty days.

 

The Court notes that Defendant has a motion to deem RFAs admitted scheduled for 9/03/26.  Plaintiff’s opposition addressed this motion also, though it is not before the Court at this time.  The Court asks the parties to work together to resolve the issues relating to the motion without the need for a hearing.  Plaintiff is admonished that, per §2033.280(c), sanctions are ABSOLUTELY mandatory in connection with a motion to deem RFAs admitted, and there is no good cause or substantial justification carve-out in the statute.

 

Defendant is ordered to give notice.

Case Number: 25LBCV03018    Hearing Date: August 6, 2026    Dept: S27

 

  1. Background Facts

Plaintiffs, Kevin Mann and Michael Wroten, Jr. filed this action against Defendants, Ackerfield 2 LLC and Golden Bee Management LLC for tenant harassment, violation of BPC §17200, violation of Penal Code §496, and declaratory relief.  The crux of the complaint is that Defendants own and manage the apartment buildings in which Plaintiffs reside, and they have unlawfully imposed charges on tenants and interfered with tenants’ rights to organize.   

 

  1. Demurrer

a.     Legal Standard on Demurrer

A demurrer is a pleading used to test the legal sufficiency of other pleadings. It raises issues of law, not fact, regarding the form or content of the opposing party’s pleading.  It is not the function of the demurrer to challenge the truthfulness of the complaint; and for purpose of the ruling on the demurrer, all facts pleaded in the complaint are assumed to be true, however improbable they may be.

 

A demurrer can be used only to challenge defects that appear on the face of the pleading under attack; or from matters outside the pleading that are judicially noticeable. Blank v. Kirwan 39 Cal.3d 311 (1985). No other extrinsic evidence can be considered (i.e., no “speaking demurrers”). A demurrer is brought under CCP § 430.10 [grounds], § 430.30 [as to any matter on its face or from which judicial notice may be taken], and § 430.50(a) [can be taken to the entire complaint or any cause of action within].  Specifically, a demurrer may be brought per CCP § 430.10(e) if insufficient facts are stated to support the cause of action asserted.  Per CCP §430.10(a) a demurrer may be brought where the court has no jurisdiction of the subject of the cause of action alleged in the pleading.  Furthermore, demurrer for uncertainty will be sustained only where the complaint is so bad that the defendant cannot reasonably respond.  CCP § 430.10(f). 

 

However, in construing the allegations, the court is to give effect to specific factual allegations that may modify or limit inconsistent general or conclusory allegations. Financial Corporation of America v. Wilburn, 189 Cal.App.3rd 764, 769 (1987). And, if the facts pled in the complaint are inconsistent with facts which are incorporated by reference from exhibits attached to the complaint, the facts in the incorporated exhibits control. Further, irrespective of the name or label given to a cause of action by the plaintiff, a general demurrer must be overruled if the facts as pled in the body of the complaint state some valid claim for relief. Special demurrers are not allowed in limited jurisdiction courts. (CCP § 92(c).)

 

Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment. Goodman v. Kennedy, 18 Cal.3d 335, 348 (1976). The burden is on the complainant to show the Court that a pleading can be amended successfully. (Id.)

 

Finally, CCP section 430.41 requires that “[b]efore filing a demurrer pursuant to this chapter, the demurring party shall meet and confer in person or by telephone with the party who filed the pleading that is subject to demurrer for the purpose of determining whether an agreement can be reached that would resolve the objections to be raised in the demurrer.” (CCP §430.41(a).) The parties are to meet and confer at least five days before the date the responsive pleading is due. (CCP §430.41(a)(2).) Thereafter, the demurring party shall file and serve a declaration detailing their meet and confer efforts. (CCP §430.41(a)(3).)

 

b.     Meet and Confer

Defense Counsel declares she sent a meet and confer letter and invited Plaintiff’s attorney to meet and confer, and Plaintiff’s attorney emailed back disagreeing with the letter and further inviting Defense Counsel to meet and confer.  The Code requires meet and confer “in person or over the telephone.”  The Court will rule on the demurrer despite this requirement, but Defense Counsel must make active efforts to do so in the future in this and other actions.    

 

c.     Tenant Harassment

Long Beach Municipal Code §8.101.030 defines tenant harassment.  §8.103.030(L) provides, in pertinent part:

No owner shall… [i]nterfere with the right of tenants to organize as tenants and engage in concerted activities with other tenants for the purpose of mutual aid and protection; deny property access to tenant organizers, advocates, or representatives working with or on behalf of tenants living at a property; prevent tenant or tenant organization meetings in an appropriate space accessible to tenants under the terms of their rental agreement(s).  

 

Plaintiffs allege the following concerning their attempts to organize and Defendant’s interference with those rights:

39. During his tenancy, Mr. Wroten was permitted to reserve the clubhouse located at the Ackerfield Buildings for tenant use. On or about October 2024, he reserved the clubhouse for a tenant “Know Your Rights” presentation scheduled for November 6, 2024, to be conducted by Long Beach Residents Empowered (LIBRE), and paid the required $100 reservation fee.

40. The event was intended to educate tenants at the Ackerfield Buildings about their legal protections under California law.  

41. On or around November 6, 2024, Defendants canceled Mr. Wroten’s reservation, stating that the clubhouse could not be used for a tenant-rights meeting. 

 

Defendant raises various factual issues outside the scope of demurrer in its demurrer, such as whether Plaintiff really had the right to reserve the clubhouse, whether the clubhouse was appropriate for this type of meeting, whether the meeting could have been held elsewhere, etc.  The Court finds the allegations sufficient to state a claim for tenant harassment under the provision, and the demurrer is overruled.

 

The Court declines to rule on the other arguments concerning the tenant harassment cause of action, as doing so is not necessary to a resolution of the merits of the demurrer. 

 

d.     Unfair Business Practices

Defendant argues the §17200 cause of action fails because the tenant harassment cause of action fails.  The demurrer is overruled because the demurrer to the tenant harassment cause of action is overruled.

 

e.     Penal Code §496

Penal Code §496 permits a civil action when a defendant has bought or received stolen property.  Defendant argues this section does not apply to rent payments.  Plaintiffs, in opposition, cite Johnson v. Connie, LLC (2025) 113 Cal.App.5th 850 to support their position that it applies under the circumstances. 

 

In Johnson, the plaintiffs sued the defendants for illegal rent increases.  The complaint included a cause of action for violation of §496, and the trial court granted the defendant’s motion for a directed verdict on the cause of action.  The plaintiff appealed, and the court of appeals reversed.  The court of appeals concluded that knowingly accepting illegally increased rent constitutes theft such that a violation of §496 is properly alleged.  Plaintiffs herein allege Defendant knowingly accepted rent that included improper mandatory Spectrum charges and late fees, which is substantially the same allegation that was made in Johnson.  The demurrer is overruled.

 

f.      Declaratory Relief

Defendant’s demurrer to the declaratory relief cause of action rises and falls with its demurrer to the other causes of action, and is therefore overruled.

 

g.     Uncertainty

Defendant argues the complaint is uncertain because it is not clear which tenants were charged improper fees and for how long those fees were charged.  These details are the proper scope of discovery; the allegations are sufficient to allege wrongful conduct, and therefore the demurrer is overruled.

 

3.         Motion to Strike

a.         UCL Relief

Defendant moves to strike Plaintiffs’ prayer for relief in connection with the UCL cause of action, contending the remedies for a violation of the UCL consist solely of injunctive relief and restitution of money or property obtained through the unfair competition. 

 

Defendant argues Plaintiffs’ request for restitution is improper because they do not clarify how long they were charged unlawful Spectrum fees or in what amount.  As noted above, this is the proper subject of discovery, not of a pleading challenge. 

 

Defendant argues the prayer for disgorgement is not proper because “nonrestitutionary disgorgement” is not available in a UCL action.  Plaintiffs, in opposition, agree with the law, but contend they are seeking restitutionary disgorgement.  The claims for restitution and disgorgement appear to be redundant, but there is no harm in allowing both words to appear in the complaint; Plaintiffs will clearly only be permitted a single recovery at the time of the trial.

 

Defendant argues the prayer for injunctive relief is not proper because there is no threat of continuing or future misconduct.  This is a factual issue not appropriate for resolution at the pleading stage.

 

Finally, Defendant argues attorneys’ fees are not recoverable under the UCL.  Plaintiffs concede this, but contend they are seeking fees per CCP §1021.5, the so-called “private attorney general doctrine.”  The Court finds, at the pleading stage, that Plaintiffs have stated a claim for fees under §1021.5, such that the prayer for relief is proper.

 

The motion to strike the requests for relief in connection with the §17200 cause of action is denied.

 

b.         Penal Code and Declaratory Relief

The remainder of Defendant’s motion to strike is redundant of their demurrer and is denied for the reasons detailed above. 

 

4.     Conclusion

Defendant’s demurrer is overruled.  Its motion to strike is denied.  Defendant is ordered to file an answer to the complaint within ten days.

 

The parties are reminded that there is a Case Management Conference on calendar concurrently with the hearing on the demurrer and motion to strike.  The Court asks the parties to make arrangements to appear remotely at the CMC and hearing on the pleading challenge.
