DEFENDANT MYUNG FAMILY TRUST’S DEMURRER TO PLAINTIFF’S FAC

 

Background

On July 23, 2021, Plaintiff Grand View Real Property, LLC (“Grand View”) filed a complaint in this action for “Temporary Restraining Order, Preliminary and Permanent Injunctions and Damages” against Defendants Benjamin Joon Tae Kim and Jullie Myung, Trustees of the Myung Family Trust, dated November 9, 2019.

On September 21, 2021, Kim and Myung, Trustees of the Myung Kim Family Trust, dated November 9, 2019 (the “Kim Trust”)[1] filed a cross-complaint against Grand View and Wells Fargo Bank, N.A. The cross-complaint alleges causes of action for (1) private nuisance, (2) quiet title, (3) trespass, (4) declaratory relief, and (5) negligence.

On June 13, 2025, Grand View filed a first amended complaint (“FAC”) against Defendants the Kim Trust and Benjamin Joon Tae Kim (jointly, “Defendants”), alleging fourteen causes of action for (1) declaratory relief, enforcement of recorded easements and for damages resulting from interference with the rights thereof, (2) for establishment of a prescriptive easement, (3) for establishment/recognition of an implied easement, (4) for establishment/ recognition of easement by necessity, (5) for establishment/recognition of an equitable easement, (6) quiet title, (7) declaratory relief, or (8) breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, (9) negligence, (10) trespass, (11) private nuisance, (12) intentional interference with contract, (13) intentional interference with prospective economic advantage, (14) negligent interference with prospective economic advantage.

On August 11, 2025, the Kim Trust demurred to the first eight causes of action in Grand View’s FAC. Grand View opposed. The Kim Trust replied.

On January 9, 2026, the Court continued the hearing to March 6, 2026 and ordered the Kim Trust “to meet and confer with Grand View within 10 days.” (Order 1/9/26, 3:5.) The Court further ordered that “[i]f the parties are unable to resolve the pleading issues or if the parties are otherwise unable to meet and confer in good faith, Kim Trust is to thereafter file and serve a declaration setting forth the efforts to meet and confer … within 15 days.” (Id. at 3:6-10.)

At the March 6, 2026 hearing, the Court took the demurrer off calendar because no declaration was filed, and pursuant to the Court’s January 9, 2026 Order, this meant the parties had resolved their issues. However, the Kim Trust filed an untimely declaration at 10:32 p.m. the night before the hearing, which the Court could not see in the docket before it took the demurrer off calendar. Additionally, it appears that at the hearing, the Kim Trust did not inform the Court that the issues in the demurrer had not been resolved, nor did the Kim Trust alert the Court to the filing.

The Kim Trust’s counsel’s untimely declaration attests that “[o]n March 4, 2026, I had a 10-minute telephone call with counsel for the plaintiff Grand View regarding the merits of the first amended complaint. Counsel informed me that he was going to file an errata notice with a quiet title verification form attached. Importantly, the Kim Trust maintains that the quiet title action remains deficient because the City of Los Angeles is an indispensable party under both CCP Sections 760.010(a) and 389 as set forth in the demurrer.” (Graham Decl., ¶ 2.) Counsel further stated that “[t]he parties otherwise remained firm in their respective legal positions.” (Graham Decl., ¶ 3.)

Also at the March 6, 2026 hearing, the Court ordered that “Plaintiff is to give notice.” (Minute Order 3/6/26, p. 2.) In Grand View’s notice, it stated that “the Court ruled as follows: … In light of Defendants failure to comply with the Court’s prior order of January 9, 2026 concerning the Demurrer to the First Amended Complaint, the Court declines to hear it on its merits. The Court confirmed that Defendants had ten (10) days to file an Answer to the First Amended Complaint.” (Notice of Ruling 3/9/26, 2:2-7.)

The Kim Trust thereafter filed an objection to Plaintiff’s notice of ruling, asserting that the statement in the notice that the “Kim Trust had ten (10) days to file an answer … is not true.” (Obj. to Notice of Ruling, 2:2-3.) The Kim Trust contends that “[t]he Court took the demurrer off calendar because a timely meet and confer declaration had not been filed per the Court’s minute order from the initial hearing on the demurrer. The Court did not have the authority to overrule the demurer [sic], as CCP Section 430.41(A)(4) reads, ‘A determination by the court that the meet and confer process was insufficient shall not be grounds to overrule or sustain a demurrer.’ The Court took the matter off calendar because it realized that it could not overrule the demurrer. As the demurrer had not been overruled, the Court could not order or ‘confirm’ that the Kim Trust had 10 days to file an answer.” (Obj. to Notice of Ruling, 2:3-11.) The Kim Trust further asserts that “[a]s with any motion, when the court takes a demurrer off calendar, there is no decision on the merits and therefore no bar to refiling. Accordingly, the Kim Trust has submitted for electronic filing a renewed demurrer to the first amended complaint.” (Obj. to Notice of Ruling, 2:12-14.)

Subsequently, the Kim Trust filed a renewed demurrer to the FAC on March 9, 2026. Grand View filed an objection to the demurrer, as well as a separate request to strike the Kim Trust’s renewed demurrer as “unauthorized and untimely.” The Kim Trust replied to both.

 

 

Objection and Request to Strike the Kim Trust’s Second Demurrer

As a preliminary matter, Grand View objects to the Kim Trust’s renewed demurrer. Grand View provides key background information, contending that “Plaintiff filed its First Amended Complaint on June 13, 2025, over a year ago. After extensions of time to respond, Defendants response was due in August of 2025. Defendants did not timely respond. Instead, they waited till March of 2026 to file their Demurrer to the First Amended Complaint. As such, the Demurrer to the First Amended Complaint is untimely, ought not to be heard, and properly subject to a request to strike, here asserted.” (Obj., 2:3-8.) Grand View further explains that “[a]t the March 2026 hearing, and in light of the failures to comply with its earlier order, the Court refused to consider the continued Demurer. During the discussions at the hearing, Defendants were told to file an Answer to the First Amended Complaint and to do so within ten (10) days. At the hearing, Defendants’ counsel expressly agreed to do so. Instead, Defendants filed a second Demurrer to the Fist [sic] Amended Complaint, apparently upon the grounds that they had reconsidered their agreement (and discovered that the subsequent minute order did not fully memorialize what had been said).” (Obj., 2:18-24.)

Grand View asserts the following: “First, the current demurrer was filed seven (7) months after the response was due, and is grossly untimely, and subject to being stricken on that ground alone. Second, one cannot file a demurrer, have the Court work it up, receive an order to comply with certain procedural requirements, ignore the order, have the Court refuse to consider the demurrer for violation of its order, and then ignore all that and simply file a new demurrer to the same First Amended Complaint. Third, the Court ordered Defendants to file an answer within ten (10) days of the March 6, 2026 hearing, and Defendants’ counsel expressly agreed to do so – and has acknowledged in writing doing so. Defendants cannot simply ignore the Court’s order – and their own agreement before the Court – and perpetuate these challenges. The demurrer ought to be stricken and not considered, and Defendants ordered to either answer the First Amended Complaint, or have their default entered.” (Obj., 2:25-3:8.)

The Kim Trust takes issue with Grand View’s assertion, contending that “none of the court’s minute orders indicates that the demurrer was taken off calendar. … The minute order dated January 9, 2026 does not indicate that the demurrer would be taken off calendar if a timely meet and confer declaration were not filed. Neither does the March 6, 2026 minute indicate that the demurrer was actually taken off calendar. The court merely informed the parties from the bench at the March 6, 2026 hearing that it had taken the demurrer off calendar.” (Reply to Obj., p. 2, ¶ 2.) The Kim Trust also quotes Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41, subdivision (a)(4), which states that “[a] determination by the court that the meet and confer process was insufficient shall not be grounds to overrule or sustain a demurrer.” The Kim Trust uses this statute to assert that “[b]ecause the court did not (and could not) overrule the demurrer, the Kim Trust was not precluded from re-filing the same.” (Reply to Obj., pp. 2-3, ¶ 4.)

The Kim Trust contends that the Court’s removal of its demurrer from the calendar is an invitation to file a new demurrer. However, the Court’s lack of a ruling on the demurrer’s merits does not invite the Kim Trust to file a subsequent demurrer. The Court removed the demurrer from the calendar because the Kim Trust’s failure to file a timely declaration demonstrated to the Court that the parties resolved the issues during the meet and confer process. The Court’s January 9, 2026 Order explicitly stated that “[i]f the parties are unable to resolve the pleading issues or if the parties are otherwise unable to meet and confer in good faith, Kim Trust is to thereafter file and serve a declaration.” (Order 1/9/26, 3:6-8 [emphasis added].) Accordingly, the Kim Trust’s failure to file a timely declaration and failure to alert the Court that issues persisted meant the Court believed the issues were resolved. Now, the Kim Trust seeks to have the Court hear its renewed demurrer without explaining why it failed to follow the Court’s orders, nor does the Kim Trust provide a legal basis for its assertion that a renewed demurrer is allowed. The Kim Trust’s moving papers merely state that this “is a renewed demurrer because on March 6, 2026 the court took the Kim Trust’s first demurrer off calendar because a supplemental meet and confer declaration had not been timely filed.” (Notice of Mot., 2:16-19.)

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.40, subdivision (a), prescribes that “[a] person against whom a complaint or cross-complaint has been filed may, within 30 days after service of the complaint or cross-complaint, demur to the complaint or cross-complaint.” Accordingly, demurrers must be filed within thirty days of filing the pleading. When a party seeks to attack the pleadings more than thirty days after its filing date, the proper avenue is filing a motion for judgment on the pleadings, not a demurrer, because “[a] motion for judgment on the pleadings may be made at any time either prior to the trial….” (Stoops v. Abbassi (2002) 100 Cal.App.4th 644, 650.) “A motion for judgment on the pleadings performs the same function as a general demurrer.” (Cloud v. Northrop Grumman Corp. (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 995, 999, citations omitted.)

Accordingly, the Court declines to hear the Kim Trust’s renewed demurrer for its failure to follow proper procedure.

Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, the Court declines to hear the Kim Trust’s demurrer for being untimely and improper. The Kim Trust is ordered to file its answer to the first amended complaint within ten days of the date of this Order.

The Kim Trust is ordered to give notice of this Order.

PLAINTIFFS’ MOTION FOR DISCOVERY OF PEACE OFFICER PERSONNEL AND OTHER RECORDS

 

Background

On July 23, 2024, Plaintiffs Derrick Boykins and Jonathan Pacheco (jointly, “Plaintiffs”) filed this action against Defendant the City of Los Angeles (the “City”), alleging two causes of action. On August 12, 2024, Plaintiffs filed a first amended complaint.

On December 3, 2025, the Court held a hearing on Plaintiffs’ Pitchess motion. “At the hearing, the parties agreed that … Plaintiff will file and serve an amended complaint to include allegations that Captain Mota took additional retaliatory actions against Plaintiffs after the filing of the complaint in this case.” (Order 12/3/25, p. 2.) The motion was “withdrawn without prejudice.” (Ibid.)

On January 5, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the operative second amended complaint (“SAC”) against the City, alleging three causes of action for (1) retaliation in violation of the California Fair Employment and Housing Act (Gov. Code § 12940, et seq.), (2) whistleblower retaliation (Labor Code § 1102.5), and (3) failure to take corrective measures to prevent retaliation in violation of the California Fair Employment and Housing Act (Gov Code § 12940(k)).

On May 27, 2026, the Court sustained the City’s demurrer to the third cause of action of Plaintiffs’ SAC.

On June 12, 2025, the Court found “that the following cases, 24STCV18337 and 25STCV14105, are related within the meaning of California Rules of Court, rule 3.300(a). 24STCV18337 is the lead case.” (Minute Order 6/12/25.)

Plaintiffs again move for an order that the City and the Los Angeles Police Department (“LAPD”) produce records. The City opposes. Plaintiffs replied.

Legal Standard

There is a special two-step procedure for securing disclosure of peace officer personnel records. ((Warrick v. Superior Court (2005) 35 Cal.4th 1011, 1019.) First, the party seeking disclosure must file a motion that identifies the peace officer, the agency in possession of the records, a description of the records, who is seeking the records, as well as the time and place of the hearing. ((Evid. Code, § 1043, subd. (b)(1).) The motion must be accompanied by a declaration showing “good cause” for disclosure of the records: setting forth the materiality of the records, and stating upon reasonable belief that the governmental agency has the requested documents. ((Id., § 1043, subd. (b)(3).) This is a so-called “Pitchess” motion, derived from Pitchess v. Superior Court (1974) 11 Cal.3d 531.

The Evidence Code section 1043, subdivision (b) “good cause” declaration must be sufficiently specific “to preclude the possibility of [the movant] simply casting about for any helpful information.” ((People v. Mooc (2001) 26 Cal.4th 1216, 1226.) However, a declaration based upon information and belief and containing hearsay may be used to evidence good cause in support of a Pitchess motion. ((Haggerty v. Superior Court (2004) 117 Cal.App.4th 1079, 1086.) The moving party need show only a “plausible factual foundation” for discovery—i.e., a scenario of officer misconduct that might occur or could have occurred. (Warrick v. Superior Court, supra, at p. 1025.) All that is required is the presentation of a scenario that might have or could have occurred, i.e., a “relatively low threshold.” ((Uybungco v. Superior Court (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 1043, 1048); see also Blumberg v. Superior Court (2011) 197 Cal.App.4th 1245, 1248 [the good cause requirement of section 1043(b) “embodies a ‘relatively low threshold’ for discovery’ [citation], under which a defendant need demonstrate only ‘a logical link between the defense proposed and the pending charge’ and describe with some specificity ‘how the discovery being sought would support such a defense’”]); Becerrada v. Superior Court (2005) 131 Cal.App.4th 409, 413 [“A showing of good cause is measured by ‘relatively relaxed standards’ that serve to ‘insure the production’ for trial court review of ‘all potentially relevant documents.’”].)

Second, if the court finds good cause, then an in camera hearing must be held. ((Slayton v. Superior Court (2006) 146 Cal.App.4th 55, 61.) After personally examining the records in camera, the trial court shall order disclosure of peace officer personnel records that are “relevant to the subject matter involved in the pending litigation.” ((Evid. Code, § 1045, subd. (a)); (People v. Mooc, supra, 26 Cal.4th at p. 1226.) If disclosure is ordered, the court must also order that the disclosed information may not be used “for any purpose other than a court proceeding pursuant to applicable law.” ((Evid. Code, § 1045, subd. (e).)

In terms of procedure, notice of the motion must be served in compliance with Code of Civil Procedure section 1005, subdivision (b), on the parties and on the governmental agency that has the records. ((Evid. Code, § 1043, subd. (a).) The agency is required to notify the individual officer whose records are sought. (Evid. Code, § 1043, subd. (c); see also Evid. Code, § 1043, subd. (d) [“No hearing upon a motion for discovery or disclosure shall be held without full compliance with the notice provisions of this section except upon a showing by the moving party of good cause for noncompliance, or upon a waiver of the hearing by the governmental agency identified as having the records.”].)

Discussion

A.    Allegations of the SAC

Plaintiffs’ SAC alleges that “Plaintiff Derrick Boykins and Plaintiff Jonathan Pacheco are both employed by the City of Los Angeles as sworn peace officers in the Los Angeles Police Department (the ‘Department’ or ‘LAPD’).” (SAC, ¶ 11.) “From in or around November of 2021 until mid-2023, Plaintiff Pacheco was loaned continuously from Metro Division to the U.S. Marshals Task Force out of Gang and Narcotics Division (‘GND’),” and “[f]rom in or around June 2022 until mid-2023, Plaintiff Boykins was also loaned almost continuously from Metro Division to the U.S. Marshals Task Force.” (SAC, ¶¶ 14-15.)

Plaintiffs allege that the Department began singling out Black officers to work as Mayor Karen Bass’ bodyguard detail, since the mayor is Black, and “Lt. Morgan asked Plaintiff Boykins if he wanted to be assigned to the Mayor’s Detail.” (SAC, ¶¶ 18-19, 24.) “Plaintiff Boykins said he did not, and he also told Lt. Morgan that he did not appreciate that because he is a certain ethnicity (Black), he was getting a phone call asking him to work the Detail.” (SAC, ¶ 25.) Plaintiff Pacheco, in the vehicle with Plaintiff Boykins at the time, heard the phone call. (SAC, ¶ 27.) “[O]n or about February 4, 2023, Plaintiff Boykins filed a personnel complaint complaining that the Department’s singling out of Black officers for recruitment to the Mayor’s Detail constituted race discrimination,” because many Black officers believed that if they rejected the offer, “they would suffer retaliation in the future.” (SAC, ¶¶ 30-31.) “On or about February 9, 2023, Plaintiff Boykins was interviewed by Internal Affairs regarding his personnel complaint.” (SAC, ¶ 32.)

Plaintiffs then allege that “[a]fter Plaintiffs engaged in the above protected activities, the Department retaliated against them.” (SAC, ¶ 38.) “First, on or about July 2, 2023, Plaintiff Pacheco’s long-term loan to the U.S. Marshals Task Force was ended and he was transferred back to Metro Division B Platoon.” (SAC, ¶ 39.) “Then, on or about August 2, 2023, Plaintiff Boykins’s long-term loan to the U.S. Marshals Task Force was ended and he was transferred back to Metro Division B Platoon.” (SAC, ¶ 40.) “Since Plaintiffs’ transfer back to Metro Division, the Department has subjected each of them to additional actions that have materially impacted the terms and conditions of their employment, and that are designed to harm their promotability, isolate them, and damage their reputation. These actions include, but are not limited to, assigning Plaintiff Pacheco to work the desk during DP 10 in 2023, and assigning Plaintiff Boykins to a position in DP 10 in 2023 to isolate him from the rest of B Platoon.” (SAC, ¶ 45.) “In addition, on or about May 5, 2024, Captain Mota caused both Plaintiffs to be ‘benched’ (or removed from the field) and subjected them to a retaliatory audit.” (SAC, ¶ 47.)

Then, “[i]n August 2024, the Department commenced an internal investigation to determine whether Captain Mota had retaliated against Plaintiffs. In or about April 2025, the Department unfounded Plaintiff’s allegations that Captain Mota had retaliated against them.” (SAC, ¶ 50.) “In May 2025, the Department placed Captain Mota on the promotional list and in June or July 2025, the Department promoted Captain Mota to Commander.” (SAC, ¶ 51.) “As a result of the Department’s actions, Plaintiffs, and each of them, have lost and will continue to lose overtime, as well as other privileges and benefits.” (SAC, ¶ 52.)

B.    Documents Sought

            Plaintiffs seek production of “the following records pursuant to Evidence Code §§ 1043 and 1045:

1. The complaint face sheet and the complete witness interviews in audio or transcript form, or both, for LAPD Internal Affairs Complaint Form (‘CF’) No. 23-000352; and

2. The complaint face sheet and the complete witness interviews in audio or transcript form, or both, for LAPD Internal Affairs investigation conducted in response to the instant lawsuit, Case No. 24STCV18337.”

(Notice of Mot., 2:8-13.)

C.    Good Cause

Plaintiffs explain that “[i]n this Motion, Plaintiffs seek discovery of the LAPD’s investigation into Plaintiff Boykins’ race discrimination complaint (CF No. 23-000352), specifically the complaint face sheet and the complete witness interviews in audio or transcript form, or both. Plaintiffs also seek discovery of the complaint face sheet and the complete witness interviews in audio or transcript form that LAPD’s Internal Affairs conducted in response to the instant lawsuit, Case No. 24STCV18337.” (Mot., 5:8-12.) Plaintiffs assert that “there is good cause for the discovery sought because it is relevant to prove the elements of Plaintiffs’ retaliation claims.” (Mot., 12:23-25.)

In support of the motion, Plaintiffs’ counsel provides a declaration, stating inter alia, that “Plaintiffs Derrick Boykins and Jonathan Pacheco are both employed by the City of Los Angeles as sworn peace officers in the Department.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 4.) “A loan in the LAPD is a provisional assignment that can be long-term or short-term,” and “[b]oth Plaintiffs were loaned almost continuously from their assignment at Metropolitan (‘Metro’) Division to the U.S. Marshals Task Force.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶¶ 5-6.) “Plaintiffs’ loans to the U.S. Marshals Task Force constituted a coveted assignment because the Task Force uses a high level of investigative skill to locate fugitives and dangerous felony suspects; conducts complex surveillance operations; and apprehends a large number of fugitives and Part 1 crime suspects.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 7.) “The experience Plaintiffs gained through working in the Task Force significantly contributed to their ability to advance and/or obtain other coveted assignments in the Department.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 8.) 

Plaintiffs’ counsel explains that “[i]n December of 2022, Karen Bass was sworn in as Mayor of the City of Los Angeles,” and “Chief of Police Michel Moore directed supervisors in Metro to attempt to recruit Black officers specifically for the Mayor’s Detail, which is the bodyguard detail for the Mayor, and falls under Metropolitan Division. Mayor Karen Bass is Black.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶¶ 9-10.) “In or around January of 2023, Plaintiff Boykins heard other officers talking about how Black officers in Metro were being solicited to work the Mayor’s Detail. Plaintiff Boykins is Black.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 12.) “Shortly thereafter, on or about February 2, 2023, Plaintiff Boykins was driving while on duty when he received a phone call from Lt. Gregory Morgan. Plaintiff Boykins accepted the call using the speakerphone,” and “Lt. Morgan asked Plaintiff Boykins if he wanted to be assigned to the Mayor’s Detail. Plaintiff Boykins said he did not, and he also told Lt. Morgan that he did not appreciate that because he is a certain ethnicity (Black), he was getting a phone call asking him to work the Detail.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶¶ 13-14.) Boykins said “words to the effect of, ‘If I was a white officer, and this was a white mayor, the City would go crazy. Why is this any different?’ Lt. Morgan responded by stating words to the effect of, ‘We all have to follow orders.’” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 15.) “Plaintiff Pacheco was in the vehicle and heard Lt. Morgan’s conversation with Plaintiff Boykins.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 16.)

Thereafter, “Boykins spoke with multiple other Black officers in Metro who told him that they had also been asked to go to the Mayor’s Detail; they did not want to work the Detail; and yet they were concerned the Department would punish them if they turned down the offer,” so “Boykins filed a personnel complaint complaining that the Department’s singling out of Black officers for recruitment to the Mayor’s Detail constituted race discrimination.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶¶ 17-18.) “Plaintiffs were both interviewed by the LAPD’s Internal Affairs in the investigation generated by Boykins’s complaint,” and “[d]uring Plaintiff Pacheco’s IA interview, he told the truth about what he heard during Lt. Gregory Morgan’s February 2, 2023 phone call with Plaintiff Boykins; however, “[a]fter Plaintiffs engaged in the above protected activities, the Department retaliated against them.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶¶ 19-21.)

Plaintiff’s counsel attests that on “July 2, 2023, Captain Mario Mota ended Plaintiff Pacheco’s long-term loan to the U.S. Marshals Task Force and transferred Pacheco back to Metro Division B Platoon. Then, on or about August 2, 2023, Captain Mota ended Plaintiff Boykins’s long-term loan to the U.S. Marshals Task Force and transferred him back to Metro Division B Platoon as well.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 22.) “Thereafter, because Plaintiffs had been removed from the Marshals Task Force, but because the Task Force needed personnel to replace them, Metro Division was forced to attempt to recruit officers from B Platoon to volunteer to go on loan to the Task Force.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 23.) “Plaintiffs were the only two officers who volunteered to go on loan to the U.S. Marshals Task Force, yet, they were ignored and denied the opportunity to return to the assignment that they had been removed from out of retaliation.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 24.) “Since Plaintiffs’ transfer back to Metro Division, the Department also subjected each of them to additional adverse actions, including assigning Plaintiff Pacheco to work the desk during DP 10 in 2023, and assigning Plaintiff Boykins to a position in DP 10 in 2023 to isolate him from the rest of B Platoon.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 25.) Then, on “May 5, 2024, Captain Mota caused both Plaintiffs to be ‘benched’ (or removed from the field), subjected them to a retaliatory audit, and sought to transfer them out of Metro Division.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 26.)

Plaintiffs first assert that they demonstrate good cause because “Plaintiff’s counsel’s declaration has articulated a plausible factual scenario that Plaintiffs were subjected to retaliation by the Department for complaining of race discrimination.” (Mot., 12:26-27.) The Court finds that Plaintiffs’ counsel’s declaration demonstrates a plausible factual scenario that retaliation may have occurred.

Plaintiffs next contend that the documents sought are discoverable because the documents sought are material to their case. Plaintiffs’ counsel explains that “Request No. 1 seeks copies of the complaint face sheet and the full witness interviews related to Internal Affairs Investigation File for CF No. 23-000352, which is the investigation Plaintiff Boykins initiated when he filed a personnel complaint alleging race discrimination.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 27.) “A ‘complaint face sheet’ in the LAPD is a three-page form that is the first document created whenever a personnel complaint is initiated against an LAPD employee.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 28.) “Request No. 2 seeks the complaint face sheet and the full witness interviews related to the investigation LAPD conducted in response to Plaintiffs’ allegations contained in this lawsuit. This second investigation was done in response to Plaintiffs’ lawsuit to determine whether Captain Mota retaliated against Boykins after he filed the personnel complaint alleging race discrimination, and whether Mota retaliated against Pacheco after he testified in support of Boykins’ personnel complaint.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 29.) “The requested investigations concern the very incidents that are the subject of this civil claim.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 30.)

Moreover, Plaintiffs’ counsel attests that “Plaintiffs will have the burden of proving the elements of their retaliation claims at trial. Specifically, Plaintiffs will first have the burden of proving at trial that they engaged in a protected activity under Labor Code § 1102.5 and the FEHA by complaining about what they reasonably believed to be race discrimination” and “[t]he complaint face sheet and witness interviews from the first investigation that Plaintiffs request through this motion plausibly contain admissible evidence that will help Plaintiffs meet this burden.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶¶ 31-32.) “Plaintiffs will also have the burden of proving at trial that their complaints and/or testimony about race discrimination motivated the adverse employment actions against them.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 33.) “In particular, Plaintiffs allege that after engaging in the protected activity above, Mota took retaliatory actions against them, which led to this lawsuit.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 34.) “The LAPD then conducted an internal investigation to determine whether Mota took any retaliatory actions against Plaintiffs. In other words, this internal investigation examined the incidents that are the basis for this lawsuit.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶ 35.) “The relevancy of the complaint face sheet and witness interviews from this second investigation is self-evident,” and “the witness statements in the investigation file are important for cross-examination and impeachment purposes.” (O’Beirne Decl., ¶¶ 36-37.) Plaintiffs’ counsel attests that “[u]pon information and belief, LAPD interviewed Captain Mario Mota, Lieutenant Gregory Morgan (who had called Plaintiff Boykins to recruit him to the Mayor’s Detail), and other employees as part of these investigations[,] [t]hus, Plaintiffs are also entitled to this investigation file to impeach the testimony and/or refresh the recollections of witnesses.”  (O’Beirne Decl., ¶¶ 38-39.)

Plaintiffs discuss Riske v. Superior Court (2018) 6 Cal.App.5th 647, stating that “[t]he Riske decision provides a model for Pitchess discovery in civil employment cases.” (Mot., 12:13.) In Riske, the plaintiff “sued the City of Los Angeles (City) alleging the Los Angeles Police Department (Department) had retaliated against him for protected whistleblower activity by failing to assign or promote him to several positions, selecting instead less qualified candidates. Riske filed a discovery motion pursuant to Evidence Code sections 1043 and 1045.” (Id. 22 Cal.App.5th at p. 651.) The City asserted that the plaintiff “failed to show good cause because he provided no specific evidence in his supporting affidavit” and argued the plaintiff was engaged in a “fishing expedition.” (Id. at p. 663.) Pertinently, the Court of Appeal reasoned that “[g]ood cause in the initial phase of a discovery request for officer personnel records requires a plausible factual showing of materiality. Riske made that showing by articulating his whistleblower activity, a history of being maligned by other officers for that activity and his substantial qualifications for each of the 14 positions for which he applied… Further particularity is not required.” (Ibid.) The Court “requir[ed] the City to produce the reports sought by Riske for an in camera inspection pursuant to Evidence Code section 1045 and to thereafter order production of all discoverable information.” (Id. at p. 652.) Plaintiffs assert that Riske demonstrates that “as long as Plaintiffs here set forth a plausible factual foundation for retaliation relating to the requested documents, this Court should grant Plaintiff’s Motion and proceed to an in camera review.” (Mot., 12:19-21.)

Plaintiffs also cite Haggerty v. Superior Court (2004) 117 Cal.App.4th 1079 in support of their request. In Haggerty, the court found that the trial “court did not abuse its discretion in refusing to limit the discovery to the names, addresses and telephone numbers of the witnesses identified in the Internal Affairs report.” (Id. at p. 1091.) The court stated that “the requested discovery was important, not only for determining the events that occurred during the incident, but also for [plaintiff’s] counsel to prepare effective cross-examination of defense witnesses, including to impeach witnesses whose testimony at trial differs from statements made to the investigating officers and/or to refresh the recollections of these witnesses.” (Id. at p. 1089.) The Court of Appeal expressed that there is “a reasonable basis to conclude the internal investigation file of the incident at issue in the lawsuit could contain information that was material to the lawsuit. (See Robinson v. Superior Court (1978) 76 Cal.App.3d 968, 977, 143 Cal.Rptr. 328 [noting that the relevancy of an investigation of the incident that is the basis for the lawsuit is ‘self-evident’].).” (Id. at p. 1087.)

Plaintiffs contend that “[h]ere, as in Haggerty, the requested investigations concern the very incidents that are the subject of this civil claim. (O’Beirne Decl. ¶ 30.) As in Haggerty, Plaintiffs will have the burden of proving the elements of their retaliation claims at trial. Specifically, Plaintiffs will first have the burden of proving at trial that they engaged in a protected activity under Labor Code § 1102.5 and the FEHA by complaining about what they reasonably believed to be race discrimination. (O’Beirne Decl. ¶ 31.) The complaint face sheet and witness interviews from the first investigation that Plaintiffs request through this motion plausibly contain admissible evidence that will help Plaintiffs meet this burden.” (Mot., 14:9-15 [emphasis omitted].) Plaintiffs further contend that they “will also have the burden of proving at trial that their complaints and/or testimony about race discrimination motivated the adverse employment actions against them. (O’Beirne Decl. ¶ 33.) In particular, Plaintiffs allege that after engaging in the protected activity above, Mota took retaliatory actions against them, which led to this lawsuit. (O’Beirne Decl. ¶ 34.) The LAPD then conducted an internal investigation to determine whether Mota took any retaliatory actions against Plaintiffs. (O’Beirne Decl. ¶ 35.) In other words, this internal investigation examined the incidents that are the basis for this lawsuit. (Id.) According to Haggerty, the relevancy of the complaint face sheet and witness interviews from this second investigation is self-evident.” (Mot., 14:16-22 [emphasis omitted].)

In opposition, the City contends that “Plaintiffs do not articulate good cause for the production of the Internal Affairs (‘IA’) investigation concerning Plaintiffs’ lawsuit—an investigation concluded a year after the events at issue in this case. As made clear in Plaintiffs’ Motion and FAC and SAC, the events of this lawsuit occurred between July 2023 and May 2024. The IA investigation, by contrast, would have been conducted sometime after August 2024, upon receipt of Plaintiffs’ lawsuit. Plaintiffs’ Motion alleges that Captain Mario Mota was the ‘bad actor’ who engaged in the alleged unlawful conduct toward them, and neither their pleading nor their Motion indicate that Mota played any role in any IA investigation. Moreover, of paramount importance, Plaintiffs, after being given ample opportunity, cannot show that they suffered any adverse employment action as a result of the investigation into his lawsuit.” (Opp., 3:7-15.) The City contends, “[a]s a result of the sustained demurrer to the third cause of action in the SAC and Plaintiffs failure to amend, they do not have a cause of action for failure to prevent retaliation under Govt. Code section 12940 (k) such that the adequacy or inadequacy of the investigation IA conducted after it received his lawsuit is simply not at issue here.” (Opp., 4:1-4.) The City contends that “[a]s discussed further below, Plaintiffs do not allege that they suffered any adverse employment action as a result of the IA investigation into their lawsuit. The IA Investigation—its scope, analysis, conclusions, and methodology—is not relevant to Plaintiffs’ remaining causes of action here, and Plaintiffs do not offer any theory under which these documents would be admissible.” (Opp., 4:7-10.)

The City also “opposes wholesale production of third-party sworn officers’ personnel information and work history, pursuant to their privacy rights.” (Opp., 4:28-5:1.) The City contends that “the information disclosed during the [Complaint File] investigations at issue were likely provided under compulsion, with the potential penalty of administrative discipline that could result in discharge, or removal from employment with LAPD. Where third party peace and/or custodial officers provide statements under such potential disciplinary consequences, it is of the upmost importance that their privacy rights are protected. Therefore, to the extent that the Court finds that Plaintiffs have not demonstrated any “plausible factual foundation” for how third-party personnel records (particularly as it relates to their work history and disciplinary history that may be contained within the CFs) are material to the subject matter of the pending litigation, Defendant requests an order of non-disclosure.” (Opp., 5:10-17.) Further, the City “opposes wholesale production of the investigations in question absent redaction of third-party birthdates, personal telephone numbers, social security numbers, and the like, pursuant to Art. I § 1 of the California Constitution, which entails the right to privacy. Defendant requests that if the Court is inclined to grant the Pitchess Motion, that the order of the Court explicitly permit the City to redact the foregoing private information.” (Opp., 5:18-22.)

Next, the City contends that Plaintiffs are not entitled to information contained in the requested complaint files or to portions of witness interviews relating to the identities of other accused officers. The City cites Penal Code section 832.7, subdivisions (b)(1)(E)(5)-(7), which governs the disclosure of law enforcement personnel records. The City asserts that “information relating to the identity, personal data and information, allegations of misconduct, findings, and adjudication of allegations against other officers accused of misconduct in the requested complaint files should be redacted.” (Opp., 6:18-20.) The City also contends that “information contained in witness interviews conducted in the requested complaint files pertaining to the identity, personal data and information, allegations of misconduct, findings, and adjudication of allegations against other officers accused of misconduct in these investigations should also be redacted. Witness interviews for such investigations are kept in audio format only. In order to redact information from such witness interviews, the audio interviews must first be transcribed.” (Opp., 6:23-28.)

In response, Plaintiffs contend that the information they seek is material. Plaintiffs contend the City “diverts the Court’s attention away from what Plaintiffs seek, and focuses on the ‘scope, analysis, conclusions, and methodology’ of the second IA investigation. (Opp. 4: 8-9.) This may have been germane to what Plaintiffs sought in the original Pitchess Motion but has no bearing on what Plaintiffs request in this Motion. Plaintiffs need not make a good cause showing for the ‘scope, analysis, conclusions, and methodology’ of the second internal investigation because they are not seeking such documents. Plaintiffs have shown good cause for seeking the statements made by Mota and others during their interviews; Defendant has not made any counter argument as to why these interviews should not be produced.” (Reply, 3:6-12.)

Plaintiffs further assert that the City’s “assertion that the IA investigation has ‘nothing to do with what Plaintiffs allege in their lawsuit’ is simply false. (Opp. 4:11-12.) The first internal investigation examined whether race discrimination occurred—the cause of action alleged in the civil Complaint. (O’Beirne Decl. ¶10.) Defendant seems to concede this internal investigation is relevant to what was alleged in the lawsuit: other than a conclusory statement that it is not relevant, the Opposition makes no showing in support of its argument.” (Reply, 3:25-4:2.)

Moreover, Plaintiffs contend that “Defendant argues that ‘the events of this lawsuit occurred between July 2023 and May 2024’ and the second IA investigation took place after August 2024. (Opp. 3:9-11.) Again, this may have been relevant to the first Pitchess Motion but has no relevance to this current Motion. When the IA investigation took place is immaterial. What the IA investigation examined is relevant. Here, what the IA investigations examined—whether retaliation occurred—is the substance of Plaintiffs’ second cause of action. The relevancy is, again, self-evident. Plaintiffs are not trying to show that they suffered any adverse employment actions as a result of the investigation into what they alleged in their lawsuit. (Opp. 3: 14-15.) Rather, they are trying to show that they suffered adverse employment actions that led to the investigation into what their lawsuit alleged.” (Reply, 4:12-20.)

Plaintiffs contend that “Defendant’s assertion that privacy rights must be protected because police officers were required to testify honestly is specious. The public should demand officers testify truthfully, and the Department is not entitled to some special privilege when they do.” (Reply, 6:9-11.) Plaintiffs lastly assert that “Defendant’s concern regarding ‘third-party birthdates, personal telephone numbers, social security numbers, and the like’ is yet another red herring. Plaintiffs do not seek, nor does this Motion request, such information. If, for some inexplicable reason, an interview subject provided their birthdate, phone number, or social security number, Plaintiffs are not opposed to a redaction of this information.” (Reply, 6:11-15.)

The Court finds that Plaintiffs demonstrate good cause for the documents requested in this motion. Plaintiffs demonstrate that the documents sought are material to their case because the two investigations arose from the allegations within the complaint or in response to the lawsuit. Plaintiffs also demonstrate that they significantly limited the information requested in this motion from their previous Pitchess motion. Plaintiffs also do not appear to seek confidential information pertaining to the third-parties, such as their social security numbers. Regardless, Plaintiffs’ reply explicitly represents that they are not opposed to redactions due to privacy concerns. Further, the Court notes that a finding of good cause is just the first step; so, to the extent that information should be redacted or deemed confidential, the Court will make these determinations after the in camera hearing.

As set forth above, if the court finds good cause, then an in camera hearing must be held. ((Slayton v. Superior Court, supra, 146 Cal.App.4th at p. 61.) After personally examining the records in camera, the trial court shall order disclosure of peace officer personnel records that are “relevant to the subject matter involved in the pending litigation.” (Evid. Code, § 1045, subd. (a); (People v. Mooc, supra, 26 Cal.4th at p. 1226.) If disclosure is ordered, the court must also order that the disclosed information may not be used “for any purpose other than a court proceeding pursuant to applicable law.” (Evid. Code, § 1045, subd. (e); see Alford v. Superior Court (2003) 29 Cal.4th 1033, 1039-1040 [overruled on other grounds].)

Legal Standard

“Disobeying a court order to provide discovery,” “[f]ailing to respond or to submit to an authorized method of discovery,” “[m]aking, without substantial justification, an unmeritorious objection to discovery,” and “[m]aking an evasive response to discovery” are all misuses of the discovery process. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.010, subds. (d)-(g)¿.) There is a broad range of sanctions available against anyone engaging in conduct that is a misuse of the discovery process, including the issuance of monetary, evidentiary, contempt, and terminating sanctions. (¿Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030¿.) “¿The court may impose a terminating sanction by one of the following orders: (1)¿An order striking out the pleadings or parts of the pleadings of any party engaging in the misuse of the discovery process. (2)¿An order staying further proceedings by that party until an order for discovery is obeyed. (3)¿An order dismissing the action, or any part of the action, of that party. (4)¿An order rendering a judgment by default against that party.”¿ (¿Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030, subd. (d)¿.)

“The discovery statutes evince an incremental approach to discovery sanctions, starting with monetary sanctions and ending with the ultimate sanction of termination.”¿(Doppes v. Bentley Motors, Inc. (2009) 174 Cal.App.4th 967, 992.)¿“Discovery sanctions ‘should be appropriate to the dereliction, and should not exceed that which is required to protect the interests of the party entitled to but denied discovery.’” (Ibid.)¿A terminating sanction is a “drastic measure which should be employed with caution.”¿(Deyo v. Kilbourne (1978) 84 Cal.App.3d 771, 793.)¿“A decision to order terminating sanctions should not be made lightly. But where a violation is willful, preceded by a history of abuse, and the evidence shows that less severe sanctions would not produce compliance with the discovery rules, the trial court is justified in imposing the ultimate sanction.”¿(Mileikowsky v. Tenet Healthsystem (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 262, 279-280.)

B.    Terminating Sanctions

As a preliminary matter, the Court notes that terminating sanctions are a drastic remedy, often imposed in instances where “¿the record is replete with instances of delay and failure to comply with a court order.¿” (¿Laguna Auto Body v. Farmers Ins. Exchange (1991) 231 Cal.App.3d 481, 489 [disapproved on other grounds]¿.) “[T]he sanctioned party’s history as a repeat offender is not only relevant, but also significant, in deciding whether to impose terminating sanctions.¿” (¿Liberty Mutual Fire Ins. Co. v. LcL Administrators, Inc. (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 1093, 1106¿.) “¿A decision to order terminating sanctions should not be made lightly. But where a violation is willful, preceded by a history of abuse, and the evidence shows that less severe sanctions would not produce compliance with the discovery rules, the trial court is justified in imposing the ultimate sanction.”¿ (¿Mileikowsky v. Tenet Healthsystem (2005) 128 Cal.App.4th 262, 279-280¿.) 

Defendants assert that “[a]s noted in the accompanying declaration, Defendants have been attempting to obtain Plaintiff’s deposition since December 2024; almost a year and a half ago. Plaintiff’s failure to comply with the court’s valid discovery order warrants terminating sanctions. Further, Defendants contend that “Plaintiff has refused to comply with or participate in the discovery process as statutorily required and pursuant to specific court orders.             Background

On July 11, 2025, Plaintiffs Margaret Molloy and Peter Demian (jointly, “Plaintiffs”) filed a complaint against MDI Homes, LLC (“MDI”), alleging two causes of action for (1) violation of Los Angeles Municipal Code §§ 151.04, 151.10, and (2) declaratory relief.

On February 3, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a first amended complaint (“FAC”) alleging three causes of action for (1) violation of Los Angeles Municipal Code §§ 151.04, 151.10, (2) declaratory relief as to current legal rent, and (3) declaratory relief as to the applicability of the Los Angeles Rent Stabilization Ordinance to the Rental Unit Under L.A.M.C. §§ 151.01, et seq.

            MDI demurs to Plaintiffs’ FAC. Plaintiffs oppose. MDI replied.

Request for Judicial Notice

The Court grants MDI’s request for judicial notice of Exhibit A.

For Exhibit B, MDI seeks judicial notice under Evidence Code sections 452, subdivisions (b) and (c), which allows “[r]egulations and legislative enactments issued by or under the authority of the United States or any public entity in the United States” and “[o]fficial acts of the legislative, executive, and judicial departments of the United States and of any state of the United States” to be judicially noticed. However, Exhibit B appears to be an email from the Los Angeles Housing Department, which is not an official act or a legislative enactment. Further, an email is “not reasonably subject to dispute and [] capable of immediate and accurate determination,” so it cannot fall within the catch-all provision of Evidence Code section 452, subdivision (h). Accordingly, the Court denies MDI’s request for judicial notice of Exhibit B.

Legal Standard

“A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda¿(2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) In testing the sufficiency of the complaint, the court assumes the truth of properly pleaded factual allegations, facts reasonably inferred from those expressly pleaded, and judicially noticed matters. (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) “A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the¿evidence or other extrinsic matters.¿Therefore, it lies only where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed.” (SKF Farms v. Superior Court¿(1984) 153 Cal.App.3d 902, 905.) Accordingly, “[w]hether the plaintiff will be able to prove the pleaded facts is irrelevant to ruling upon the demurrer.” (Stevens v. Superior Court (1986) 180 Cal.App.3d 605, 609-10.)

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10 subdivisions (e) and (f), a demurrer may be filed if the pleading is uncertain or does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action. For purposes of ruling on a demurrer, all facts pleaded in a complaint are assumed to be true, but the reviewing court does not assume the truth of conclusions of law. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hosp. Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 967.)

Leave to amend must be allowed where there is a reasonable possibility of successful amendment, otherwise, it is abuse of discretion. (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349.)

            Discussion

MDI demurs to Plaintiffs’ FAC for being defective in two ways: first, because the property is not subject to LARSO, Plaintiffs’ causes of action are invalid; second, Plaintiffs improperly brought this action in court, when “California law is clear that Plaintiffs must challenge that decision through the proper administrative and judicial channels.” (Dem., 4:12-13.)

A.    Allegations of the FAC

Plaintiffs allege the following: “Plaintiffs are tenants residing in a rental unit in the City of Los Angeles that is not otherwise exempt from the City of Los Angeles Rent Stabilization Ordinance (Los Angeles Municipal Code §§ 151.00, et seq., hereinafter ‘LARSO’), and the address is 3841 South Beethoven Street, Los Angeles, California 90066 (the ‘Property’).” (FAC, ¶ 11.) “During Plaintiffs’ tenancy, the Defendants, or an agent thereof, intentionally demanded, accepted, or retained payments of rent in excess of the maximum rent or maximum adjusted rent permitted at that time.” (FAC, ¶ 20.) “Defendants demanded, accepted, or retained excess rent in at least the following ways:

a. From July 15, 2000 to the present, Defendant and / or their predecessors in interest, have continuously demanded, accepted, and retained rent from the Plaintiffs without having procured or served valid rent registration or rent registration renewal statements from the Los Angeles Housing Department. Monthly rent has been illegally demanded, collected, and retained without proper LAHD registration for at least twenty years of Plaintiffs nearly twenty-six year tenancy – amounting to approximately 240 rental payments demanded, accepted, and retained, in violation of LAMC § 151.05.

b. Rent increases by more than the maximum allowable percentage increase permitted under LARSO, on at least the following occasions: (1) On or about June 1, 2017, Plaintiffs’ monthly rent was increased from $1875.00 to $1,9680.00 – an increase of 4.96% – in excess of the maximum 3% rent increase permitted by the LARSO for the period of July 1, 2016 to June 30, 2017; (2) On or about April 1, 2024, Plaintiffs’ rent was increased from $1968.00 to $2,086.00 a month – an increase of approximately 5.9% – in excess of the maximum 4% rent increase permitted by the LARSO for period of February 1, 2024 through June 30, 2024.

c. Imposition of rent increases without properly serving the proper written notice of rent increase;

d. A reduction in housing service(s) without a proper corresponding reduction in rent equal to the value of the reduced housing service(s);

e. An unpermitted late fee;

f. An increase in rent for additional occupants more than 60 days after the Landlord’s actual or constructive knowledge of the alleged additional occupants; and/or

g. Utility charges (e.g., water, electric, natural gas, trash, etc.) without a contractual [sic] without a written agreement related to the division of utility charges on a property with a single meter.”

(Compl., ¶ 21 [emphasis in original].) Plaintiffs further allege that “[a]s a result of the aforementioned demands and collections of excess rent in violation of the law, among others, Defendants have stolen money that rightfully belongs to Plaintiff thereby damaging Plaintiff.” (Compl., ¶ 22 [emphasis in original].)

B.    Property Subject to the Los Angeles Municipal Code

MDI asserts that the FAC alleges “three causes of action: (1) alleged LARSO violations based on rent increases, notice, and fees, and (2) declaratory relief claims expressly seeking a judicial determination of LARSO’s applicability and the consequences that would follow. Each claim turns entirely on that single threshold issue.” (Dem., 4:6-10.) Specifically, “[t]he alleged rent overcharges, notice violations, and fee claims exist only if the Property is subject to LARSO. Likewise, each request for declaratory relief is expressly directed at establishing LARSO coverage and the consequences that would follow from such a determination.” (Dem., 6:2-5.) MDI asserts that “that issue has already been resolved by the agency charged with administering the Ordinance. The Los Angeles Housing Department has determined that the Property is not subject to LARSO. Public records for the Property identify its Rent Stabilization Ordinance status as ‘No.’” (Dem., 6:6-9 [emphasis in original].)

Defendants’ request for judicial notice demonstrates that the City of Los Angeles’ Department of City Planning parcel profile report lists the property as not subject to the Rent Stabilization Ordinance. (RJN, Ex. A.) However, the Court notes that the report is from March 24, 2026. In the FAC, Plaintiffs allege they have occupied the property “for at least twenty-six years.” (FAC, ¶ 17.) MDI does not unequivocally demonstrate that the property has been exempt from the Rent Stabilization Ordinance for the past twenty-six years. Moreover, as Plaintiffs assert in opposition, “[c]rucially, the documents that Defendant seeks judicial notice, even if accepted by the Court, do not stand for the propositions that Defendant claims – that an official, administrative review process took place resulting in a final, binding administrative determination that Plaintiffs’ rental unit is not subject to the LARSO.” (Opp., 5:17-20.)

The Court agrees that the document provided does not affirmatively demonstrate the property is not currently or has not previously been subject to LARSO. Accordingly, the Court does not sustain the demurrer on the basis that the property is not subject to LARSO.

C.    Proceeding in an Administrative Forum

MDI asserts that “[h]ere, the Los Angeles Housing Department has already determined that the Property is not subject to LARSO, as evident from the City’s public records and documents submitted with MDI’s Request for Judicial Notice. Plaintiffs never challenged that determination through administrative mandamus or any other authorized procedure. That failure is fatal. Because every claim in the FAC depends on the opposite premise, Plaintiffs’ action is nothing more than an impermissible collateral attack on a binding administrative determination.” (Dem., 7:18-23.) However, as the Court noted above, MDI’s request for judicial notice of Exhibit B was not granted. Because a demurrer may only lie “where the defects appear on the face of the pleading or are judicially noticed” (SKF Farms v. Superior Court, supra, 153 Cal.App.3d at p. 905), there is no indication that a binding administrative determination occurred.

Moreover, MDI contends that “[t]he statutory framework underscores why this dispute belongs in the administrative forum. The Los Angeles Municipal Code § 151.00 et seq. establishes a comprehensive and accessible process for challenging Housing Department determinations. Section 151.14, for example, provides a streamlined mechanism for review before the General Appeals Board, including flexible filing options, fee waivers for qualifying applicants, and procedures to ensure complete factual development. It also authorizes hearing officers to adjudicate allegations that a party ‘willfully or knowingly’ made false statements or failed to disclose material facts—the very misconduct Plaintiffs allege against MDI (FAC ¶ 19)—with further review available to the Rent Adjustment Commission.” (Dem., 8:12-20.)

Plaintiffs’ opposition first asserts that “[a]ll rental units in the city of Los Angeles are subject to the provisions of the LARSO, unless the unit qualifies for one of the articulated exceptions to the definition of ‘Rental Unit.’” (Opp., 5:23-24.) Plaintiff contends that “[t]he LARSO contains no section delineating the Los Angeles Housing Department or any other commission as responsible for making administrative determinations as to the applicability of the ordinance. While Defendant’s demurrer, misleadingly citing LAMC § 151.14, claims that the LARSO ‘…expressly allows tenants to seek review through the General Appeals Board…’, LAMC §151.14 does no such thing. Rather, LAMC § 151.14, to be read in conjunction with LAMC § 151.07, is related to the application process for landlords who seek rent adjustments beyond the automatic adjustments as permitted by LAMC § 151.06.” (Opp., 6:4-10.)

Plaintiffs bring their causes of action under the Los Angeles Municipal Code sections 151.04 and 151.10. Section 151.04, subdivision (A), provides that “[i]t shall be unlawful for any landlord to demand, accept or retain more than the maximum adjusted rent permitted pursuant to this chapter or regulation or orders adopted pursuant to this chapter. Whereas section 151.10 provides remedies. Specifically, “[a]ny person who demands, accepts or retains any payment of rent in excess of the maximum rent or maximum adjusted rent in violation of the provisions of this chapter, or any regulations or orders promulgated hereunder, shall be liable in a civil action to the person from whom such payment is demanded, accepted or retained for damages of three times the amount by which the payment or payments demanded, accepted or retained exceed the maximum rent or maximum adjusted rent which could be lawfully demanded, accepted or retained together with reasonable attorneys’ fees and costs as determined by the court.” (L.A. Mun. Code, § 151.10, subd. (A).)

MDI contends that Plaintiffs are subject to an administrative forum, due to Los Angeles Municipal Code section 151.14. However, that section provides that “[w]henever the provisions of this chapter provide that applications or requests for hearing or appeals be filed in the office of the Department, such applications or requests for hearing or appeals may be filed in any of the branch offices of the Department.” (L.A. Mun. Code, § 151.14, subd. (B).) Additionally, as Plaintiffs contend, Los Angeles Municipal Code sections 151.14 and 151.07 should be read in conjunction. Section 151.14 pertains to filing an application for rent adjustments, requests for hearing, and appeals, while section 151.07 provides for the authority of the department and the commission to grant individual rent adjustments. Los Angeles Municipal Code section 151.14 applies to landlords, providing, inter alia, that “[t]he Department, in accordance with such regulations and guidelines as the Commission may establish, shall have the authority to grant adjustments in the rent for a rental unit or units located in the same housing complex upon receipt of an application for an adjustment filed by the landlord.” (L.A. Mun. Code, § 151.07, subd. (A)(1).) If a tenant contests this, they may request a hearing. Under the same code section, “[t]he determination of the Department shall be final unless a request for hearing is filed by or on behalf of the applicant or an affected tenant, and such request is received by the Department within 15 days after the mailing of the findings and determination. A request for hearing shall be in writing and filed in the office of the Department upon a form and with the number of copies required by the Department.” (L.A. Mun. Code, § 151.10, subd. (A)(3)(a).)

Additionally, Plaintiffs contend that “[t]here is no clear California state law that ‘Plaintiffs must challenge’ the ‘decision’ of LAHD through administrative or judicial channels. (See Defendant’s Demurrer, p. 4).” (Opp., 6:20-21.) Further, “[d]espite Defendant’s broad claims that the LARSO provides a ‘…comprehensible and accessible administrative framework to challenge department determinations,’ as articulated in Section IV(B) supra, a reading of the plain language of the LARSO itself reveals no such ‘comprehensive and accessible’ framework. The LARSO provides absolutely no administrative remedy for a tenant seeking a declaration of their rights as to the statute’s applicability to their unit, and damages for rent overpayments.” (Opp., 6:25-7:2.)

MDI’s demurrer also contends that “[e]ven if the prior determination did not already resolve the issue, this Court should decline to adjudicate Plaintiffs’ claims under the doctrine of primary jurisdiction” or the “related doctrine of abstention.” (Dem., 7:24-28.) “[T]he primary jurisdiction doctrine allows courts to take advantage of administrative expertise,” and “the appropriate procedure for a court that applies the primary jurisdiction doctrine is to stay the lawsuit pending a decision by the administrative agency.” (Bradley v. CVS Pharmacy, Inc. (2021) 64 Cal.App.5th 902, 913 [internal quotations omitted].) “Case law states that a court may abstain from adjudicating a suit that seeks equitable remedies if ‘granting the requested relief would require a trial court to assume the functions of an administrative agency, or to interfere with the functions of an administrative agency.’” (Blue Cross of California, Inc. v. Superior Court (2009) 180 Cal.App.4th 1237, 1258.)

MDI asserts that “[t]his case falls squarely within those principles. Whether a property is subject to LARSO is a determination entrusted in the first instance to the Los Angeles Housing Department, which possesses specialized expertise in interpreting and applying the Ordinance. Plaintiffs’ claims would require this Court to re-decide that issue, second-guess the agency’s determination, and effectively substitute its judgment for that of the body charged with administering the regulatory scheme.” (Dem., 8:6-11.) As noted above, there is no indication that an administrative decision was made.

Plaintiffs assert that “Defendant’s conclusory assertions that both the doctrines of primary jurisdiction and abstention are applicable such that this Court is required to give deference to ‘administrative expertise,’ are without merit. Again, there exists no regulatory scheme under which the Los Angeles Housing Department has been given any ‘special competence’ to determine legal questions of the LARSO’s applicability to a rental unit.” (Opp., 7:17-21.) Plaintiffs cite Bradley v. CVS Pharmacy, Inc., supra, 64 Cal.App.5th at p. 919 where the court stated that under primary jurisdiction, the “issue is not whether the courts are capable of adjudicating [a plaintiff’s] claims, but whether those claims require the resolution of issues which, under a regulatory scheme, have been placed within the special competence of an administrative body.” (Internal quotations omitted.) Plaintiffs contend that “[h]ere, aside from the LARSO’s total silence on granting LAHD such special competence, the Court only need to read the plain language of the LARSO’s definition of ‘rental unit’ to see that interpretation of such an ordinance requires absolutely no special competency by an administrative agency.” (Opp., 7:25-28.) Plaintiff contends “[t]he LARSO’s protections apply to all ‘Rental Units’ in the city of Los Angeles,” unless a unit fits within an exception, and “[t]here is nothing inherently specialized in determining whether Plaintiff’s rental unit can fit under one of these exemptions. Plaintiffs alleges in their FAC that their unit is not exempted, an allegation which this Court must accept as true for purposes of this demurrer, and a determination of which Plaintiffs property seek from this Court in these judicial proceedings.” (Opp., 8:1-16.)

Based on the foregoing, MDI does not demonstrate that Plaintiffs are required to exhaust administrative remedies before pursuing violations of Los Angeles Municipal Code sections 151.04 and 151.10. Additionally, MDI does not demonstrate that the causes of action are better determined in an administrative forum. Plaintiffs properly brought their causes of action in court, so the Court declines to stay proceedings and order an administrative hearing. Moreover, MDI’s reply contends that “[t]he factual determination of whether a specific property qualifies for an exemption — such as whether 3841 South Beethoven Street was actually constructed, used, or registered as a single-family home — involves property records, construction history, and zoning classifications that LAHD routinely evaluates and that fall squarely within the agency’s institutional expertise.” (Reply, 8:10-13.) However, the Court does not believe that the documents and knowledge necessary to pursue or defend against Plaintiffs’ causes of action could not be obtained through discovery. Accordingly, the demurrer to Plaintiffs’ FAC is overruled is in its entirety.

Conclusion

Based on the foregoing, MDI’s demurrer to the FAC is OVERRULED in its entirety. MDI is ordered to file its Answer to the FAC within 20 days of this Order.
