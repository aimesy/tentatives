[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: PLAINTIFF MICHAEL BRIAN SMITH’S MOTION TO SET ASIDE/VACATE JUDGMENT [CRS #8855] Dept. 731 8:30 a.m. July 21, 2026

Moving Party: Plaintiff, Michael Brian Smith

Opposing Party: Defendant, Michael Kors Inc.

Notice: OK

Plaintiff Michael B. Smith brought this suit against his former employer, Defendant Michael Kors Inc., alleging race discrimination in his employment and a subsequent unlawful layoff.

On March 26, 2026, the Court granted a motion for judgment on the pleadings filed by Defendant, which disposed of the then-operative March 19, 2024, Second Amended Complaint (“SAC”). Plaintiff filed a motion for reconsideration on March 30, 2026, which was opposed by Defendant, and was ultimately denied by the Court on May 20, 2026. The Court entered judgment in Defendant’s favor that same day.

Now before the Court is Plaintiff’s motion to set aside/vacate the May 20, 2026, judgment on the grounds that another action affecting the outcome of the proceedings before this Court is purportedly presently pending before the Compton, California branch of the Los Angeles Superior Court as 22CMCV00753, Smith v. Michael Kors Inc. (the “Santa Monica action”). (See Motion, p. 2, ¶ 4.)

The motion is DENIED, with prejudice.

Plaintiff’s motion is noticed as being premised on Code of Civil Procedure sections 473, subdivision (d), and 1008, subdivisions (a), (b), and (e). (Motion, pp. 1-2.) Both premises fail.

As argued in Defendant’s opposition, there is no applicable provision of Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (d), here. (See Opposition pp. 11-12.) Plaintiff is asking that the Court vacate a final judgment based on the purported pendency of the Santa Monica action. This is not a valid ground for relief under section 473, subdivision (d), which is limited to clerical errors and void judgments and orders, including those voided by extrinsic fraud or mistake, neither of which Plaintiff’s paper show. (See Kramer v. Traditional Escrow, Inc. (2020) 56 Cal.App.5th 13, 29 [discussing § 473, subd. (d)].)

Similarly, none of the grounds for party-requested reconsideration—new or different facts, circumstances, or law (Code Civ. Proc., § 1008, subd. (a))—appear here, as the requested relief is based on the purported pendency of the Santa Monica action rather than some fact, circumstance, or new law relating to this action.

Last, the Court takes judicial notice of Exhibit A attached to Defendant’s opposition, which shows a June 18, 2026, minute order for the Santa Monica action, in which the court in that case denied a similar motion by Plaintiff in this case to vacate or set aside the judgment in that case, which was entered on November 6, 2025. (Opposition, Request for Judicial Notice, Ex. A, pp. 6-7.)

Plaintiff’s motion is accordingly DENIED. No further motions seeking identical relief shall be entertained absent a proper statutory basis.

Plaintiff Michael B. Smith is ordered to give notice.

Case Number: 21STLC01780    Hearing Date: July 28, 2026    Dept: 731

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE:

Moving Party:               Cross-Complainant, Michellei Cephus

Opposing Party:           Cross-Defendant, Robert Bare dba BARE LAW

Notice:                         OK

 

  1. Background Facts

Plaintiff/Cross-Defendant Robert Bare d/b/a BARE LAW (“Mr. Bare”) sues Defendant/Cross-Complainant Michellei Cephus (“Ms. Cephus”) pursuant to a February 4, 2021, Complaint alleging a single cause of action for breach of contract arising from allegations that Ms. Cephus failed to pay for legal services rendered by Mr. Bare in a separate legal action (the “underlying action”) and owes Mr. Bare $3,475 in fees related thereto.

In turn, Ms. Cephus sues Mr. Bare pursuant to a November 3, 2023, First Amended Cross-Complaint (“FACC”) alleging claims of legal malpractice, breach of contract, and breach of fiduciary duty arising from allegations that Mr. Bare engaged in conduct amounting to these violations in his representation of Ms. Cephus in the underlying action.

Now before the Court is Ms. Cephus’s opposed motion for leave to file a Second Amended Cross-Complaint (“SACC”).

 

  1. Motion for Leave to Amend

a.     Legal Standard

California Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (a)(1) provides, in relevant part: “The court may, in furtherance of justice, and on any terms as may be proper, allow a party to amend any pleading or proceeding by adding or striking out the name of any party, or by correcting a mistake in the name of a party, or a mistake in any other respect; and may, upon like terms, enlarge the time for answer or demurrer. The court may likewise, in its discretion, after notice to the adverse party, allow, upon any terms as may be just, an amendment to any pleading or proceeding in other particulars; and may upon like terms allow an answer to be made after the time limited by this code.”

Under California Rules of Court Rule, rule 3.1324, subdivision (a), a motion to amend a pleading shall:

(1) Include a copy of the proposed amendment or amended pleading, which must be serially numbered to differentiate it from previous pleadings or amendments;

(2) State what allegations in the previous pleading are proposed to be deleted, if any, and where, by page, paragraph and line number, the deleted allegations are located; and

(3) State what allegations are proposed to be added to the previous pleading, if any, and where, by page, paragraph, and line number, the additional allegations are located.

Under California Rules of Court, rule 3.1324, subdivision (b), a separate declaration must accompany the motion and must specify:

(1) The effect of the amendment;

(2) Why the amendment is necessary and proper;

(3) When the facts giving rise to the amended allegations were discovered; and

(4) The reasons why the request for amendment was not made earlier.

“This discretion should be exercised liberally in favor of amendments, for judicial policy favors resolution of all disputed matters in the same lawsuit.” (Kittredge Sports Co. v. Superior Court (1989) 213 Cal.App.3d 1045, 1047 (Kittredge Sports).)

California courts will not ordinarily consider the validity of a proposed amended pleading in ruling on a motion for leave to amend given that any grounds for a demurrer or a motion to strike will be premature at that time. (See California Casualty General Ins. Co. v. Superior Court (1985) 173 Cal.App.3d 274, 280-281 (California Casualty), citations omitted, overruled on other grounds by Kransco v. American Empire Surplus Lines Ins. Co. (2000) 23 Cal.4th 390, 403-406; see also Kittredge Sports, supra, 213 Cal.App.3d at p. 1048 [Where “the proposed legal theory [at issue] is a novel one, ‘the preferable practice would be to permit the amendment and allow the parties to test its legal sufficiency by demurrer, motion for judgment on the pleadings or other appropriate proceedings’”], quoting California Casualty, supra, at p. 280.)

Even if a good amendment is proposed in proper form, a long, unwarranted and unexcused delay in presenting it may be a good reason for denial. (Roemer v. Retail Credit Co. (1975) 44 Cal.App.3d 926, 939-940 (Roemer).) In most cases, the factors for timeliness are: (1) lack of diligence in discovering the facts or in offering the amendment after knowledge of them; and (2) the effect of the delay on the adverse party. (Id. at p. 940.) If the party seeking the amendment has been dilatory, and the delay has prejudiced the opposing party, the judge has discretion to deny leave to amend. (Hirsa v. Superior Court (1981) 118 Cal.App.3d 486, 490.) Prejudice exists where the amendment would require delaying the trial, resulting in loss of critical evidence, or added costs of preparation such as an increased burden of discovery. (Magpali v. Farmers Group, Inc. (1996) 48 Cal.App.4th 471, 486-488.)

b.     Discussion

Cross-Complainant Michelle Cephus brings the present motion for leave to file a SACC adding three new causes of action to her pleading: fraud and intentional misrepresentation; fraudulent concealment; and intentional infliction of emotional distress. The new claims are premised on the idea that in April 2026, Ms. Cephus first obtained evidence showing that Mr. Bare failed to raise a jurisdictional challenge in the underlying action inconsistent with his litigation plan as shared with Ms. Cephus, an omission that was known to Ms. Cephus from the onset but for which she did not have evidence until April 2026. (See Motion, p. 3 [final paragraph].)

In opposition Mr. Bare argues that the motion is belated, coming nearly three years after the FACC was filed, with various trial dates having come and gone, where the three new causes of action are not based on new information, but rather, are based on a mischaracterization of the underlying record, where amendment would prejudice Mr. Bare.

No reply appears in the record.

After review, the Court finds in favor of Mr. Bare.

First, the Court is not satisfied that Ms. Cephus’s motion satisfies the requirement to provide “reasons why the request for amendment was not made earlier.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1324(b)(4).) The fact that Ms. Cephus did not have, at the time she first filed her Cross-Complaint, evidence to show that Mr. Bare acted inconsistently with representations he made during the pendency of the underlying action does not change the fact that she appears to have had reasonable grounds, at the time she filed her initial Cross-Complaint, to believe that Mr. Bare’s conduct had injured her in the manner described in her present motion. This unexplained and unjustified delay constitutes an independent basis for denying leave to amend. (Roemer, supra, 44 Cal.App.3d at pp. 939-940.)

 

Moreover, the proposed amendment would substantially expand the scope of this litigation by adding three new intentional tort causes of action after this action has been pending for several years. Allowing amendment at this stage would likely require additional written discovery, depositions, expert analysis, and motion practice, thereby increasing litigation costs and further delaying resolution of the case. Such prejudice weighs against granting leave to amend. (Magpali, supra, 48 Cal.App.4th at pp. 486-488.)

c.     Conclusion

Accordingly, Cross-Complainant, Michellei Cephus’s motion for leave to amend is DENIED.

Cross-Complainant, Michellei Cephus, is ordered to give notice.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: MOTION TO BE RELIEVED AS COUNSEL—CIVIL

Moving Party:               Counsel, Moses O. Onyejekwe, Esq.

Opposing Party:           None

Notice:                         OK

 

Counsel for Defendants Jamar Harten and First Class Tax & Consulting, Moses O. Onyejekwe, Esq., moves for court orders relieving him of representation of these Defendants.

After review, the Court finds relief is not merited here.

Trial is scheduled in this action for September 21, 2026, and there is absolutely no clarity in the record regarding whether new counsel is immediately assuming representation or how Defendants, including a corporate entity that cannot represent itself at trial, will be able to move forward on September 21, 2026. California courts should consider whether the attorney’s “withdrawal can be accomplished without undue prejudice to the client’s interests.” (Ramirez v. Sturdevant (1994) 21 Cal.App.4th 904, 915.) Without more information regarding who will represent Defendants moving forward, such prejudice is too great to grant relief here.

Moreover, the motion should also be denied if it will cause undue delay in the proceeding or cause injustice. (Mandell v. Superior Court (1977) 67 Cal.App.3d 1, 4.) If the motions are here granted, the trial in this action may be continued, causing undue delay.

Counsel Moses O. Onyejekwe’s motions are thus DENIED, without prejudice.

Defendants Jamar Harten and First Class Tax & Consulting are ordered to give notice.

Case Number: 24STCV23097    Hearing Date: July 28, 2026    Dept: 731

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: PLAINTIFF/CROSS-DEFENDANT JESUS RODRIGUEZ’S MOTION TO STRIKE FIRST AMENDED CROSS-COMPLAINT PERSUANT TO CCP §425.16

Moving Party:               Plaintiff/Cross-Defendant, Jesus Rodriguez

Joinder Parties:           Cross-Defendants Rene González and Julia Molina

Opposing Party:           Defendants and Cross/Complainants Maria Guadalupe Diaz, an individual, and Maria Guadalupe Diaz, Trustee of the Diaz Family Revocable Living Trust Dated September 7th, 2024

Notice:                         OK

 

  1. Background Facts

This action arises from a dispute in real property.

a.     First Amended Complaint

Plaintiff/Cross-Defendant Jesus Rodriguez (“Rodriguez”) sues Defendant/Cross-Complainant Maria Guadalupe Diaz (“Diaz”), both as an individual and as Trustee of The Diaz Family Revocable Living Trust, Dated September 7th, 2024, pursuant to a March 12, 2025, First Amended Complaint (“FACC”) alleging claims of (1) quiet title (verified), (2) cancellation of instrument, (3) accounting, (4) unfair business practices, (5) injunctive relief, and (6) declaratory relief.

The FACC’s claims chiefly arise from the following allegations, among others. Rodriguez purchased Diaz’s interest in 1451 E. Vernon Avenue, Los Angeles, CA 90011 (the “subject property”), a multi-unit residential property, in February 2007 pursuant to a sale agreement giving Diaz certain rights, with the parties’ agreement including the right to tenancy for several months and right to collect rents and pay mortgage on the subject property. Later, Diaz let the property fall into bankruptcy by collecting rent but failing to pay the mortgage, which Rodriguez had to remedy. Later still, after Rodriguez had acquired a full interest in the subject property, Diaz recorded a forged deed conveying the subject property to Diaz, as an individual, then transferring the property to herself as Trustee of the Diaz Family Revocable Living Trust, thus damaging Rodriguez.

b.     First Amended Cross-Complaint

In turn, Diaz, as an individual and as Trustee, sues Rodriguez, as well as Rene Gonzalez (“Gonzalez”), Julia Molina (“Julia”), and Roes 1 through 10 pursuant to an April 24, 2026, First Amended Cross-Complaint alleging claims of (1) quiet title, (2) slander of title, (3) fraud, (4) removal of lien, (5) breach of contract, (6) tortious interference with contractual relationship, (7) violation of covenant of good faith and fair dealing, (8) intentional infliction of emotional distress, and (9) negligent infliction of distress.

The claims chiefly arise from the following allegations, among others. Diaz first acquired full interest in the subject property in 2003, specifically through deed from Diaz and her then-husband, Roberto Diaz, to Diaz as her own separate property. Sometime in late 2006, Angie Villareal (“Villareal”), a real estate agent, introduced Diaz to Israel Luna (“Luna”), who offered to lend Diaz $200,000 as secured by the subject property. Diaz went forward with the transaction. However, Diaz, a person with a third-grade education who does not read English, was presented with a deed conveying title of the property to Luna and to Rodriguez, a person Diaz had never met, as tenants in common, which, without knowledge or any reason to believe that she was conveying the subject property, and for no consideration, she executed. The deed was then recorded. Luna moreover executed a Deed of Trust with Assignment of Rents, designating himself as trustor and Diaz as lender. In turn, Rodriguez executed a deed of trust to secure nearly $200,000.00 in his benefit, which Rodriguez continues to assert as a lien on the subject property. Later, Diaz took an extended trip to Mexico and assigned Villareal and Luna to collect rents, hired Rodriguez as counsel in evicting a certain tenant at the subject property, and in October 2012 rented a unit to Gonzalez, with Molina living in the unit as an unnamed tenant. Then, in October 2024, Diaz attempted to evict Gonzalez and Molina, serving a three-day notice to quit and, after Gonzalez and Molina did not comply, instituted unlawful detainer proceedings. In that litigation, Gonzalez and Molina alleged that Diaz was not the property’s real owner, producing a backdated 2012 rental agreement listing Rodriguez as owner, with rents being paid to Rodriguez. Rodriguez then visited Diaz stating he did not want problems and turning over a deed conveying Rodriguez’s interest in the property to Diaz, which was subsequently recorded. Rodriguez nevertheless went back to Gonzalez and Molina and executed a new agreement to pay rent monies to Rodriguez as owner.

c.     Motion Before the Court

Before the Court is Rodriguez’s opposed anti-SLAPP motion to strike various portions of the FACC as protected activity, on which there is no probability of prevailing on the merits as actionable conduct. Gonzalez and Molina joined Rodriguez’s anti-SLAPP motion by way of a June 30, 2026, filing.

 

  1. Anti-SLAPP Motion

a.     Legal Standard

Anti-SLAPP analysis under Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16 proceeds in two familiar steps. In the first step, the defendant or moving party must make “a threshold showing that the challenged cause of action is one ‘arising from’ protected activity.” (Barry v. State Bar of California (2017) 2 Cal.5th 318, 321, quotations omitted (Barry).) In this context, the term “protected activity” refers to speech or petitioning activities. (Barry, 2 Cal.5th at p. 321.) A claim arises from protected activity when that activity underlies or forms the basis for the claim; otherwise stated, “the defendant’s act underlying the plaintiff’s cause of action [must] itself [be] … an act in furtherance of the right of petition or free speech.” (Park v. Board of Trustees of California State University (2017) 2 Cal.5th 1057, 1063, quotations omitted (Park).) “[T]he focus is on determining what ‘the defendant’s activity [is] that gives rise to his or her asserted liability—and whether that activity constitutes protected speech or petitioning.’” (Ibid.) In teasing out whether protected conduct exists, courts should consider the elements of the challenged claim and what actions by the defendant supply those elements and consequently form the basis for liability. (Ibid.)

If the court finds the defendant or moving party succeeds at the first step, then the burden shifts to the plaintiff to “demonstrate[] a probability of prevailing on the claim.” (Ibid. [quotations omitted].) At the second step, courts “evaluate the defendants’ evidence only to determine if it defeats that submitted by the plaintiff as a matter of law.’ [Citation.] ‘[I]n order to establish the requisite probability of prevailing [citation], the plaintiff need only have “‘stated and substantiated a legally sufficient claim.’” [Citation.] “Put another way, the plaintiff ‘must demonstrate that the complaint is both legally sufficient and supported by a sufficient prima facie showing of facts to sustain a favorable judgment if the evidence submitted by the plaintiff is credited.’”‘ [Citation.] … That burden [is] not particularly high.” (Area 51 Productions, Inc. v. City of Alameda (2018) 20 Cal.App.5th 581, 602 (Area 51 Productions, Inc.).) “Claims with the requisite minimal merit may proceed.” (Navellier v. Sletten (2002) 29 Cal.4th 82, 94 (Navallier).) If the cause of action satisfies both prongs of the anti-SLAPP statute, then it is subject to being struck. (Barry, supra, 2 Cal.5th at p. 321.)

As stated by our State’s highest Court: “This is a ‘summary-judgment-like procedure at an early stage of the litigation.’” (Varian, supra, 35 Cal.4th at p. 192.)

b.     Discussion

                              i.        First Prong – Protected Activity

Rodriguez’s motion challenges three sets of acts as protected activity that should be stricken from the FACC pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16:

(1)           Rodriguez's recordation of a Notice of Lis Pendens on the Vernon Property in connection with the pending litigation (FACC, ¶ 23);

(2)           Rodriguez’s assertion of a lien in the amount of $198,536.00 against the Vernon Property, recorded in the Official Records of Los Angeles County (Recording No. 20130398612) (FACC, ¶ 23); and

(3)           Rodriguez's oral and written statements asserting an ownership interest in the Vernon Property, including allegedly communicating to tenant Gonzalez that Diaz was not the owner of the property, made after the filing of the underlying complaint and initiation of this litigation (FACC ¶¶ 21-22, 24, 31).

 

Although the filing of a lis pendens, litigation communications, and statements

made in connection with judicial proceedings generally constitute protected petitioning

or speech within the meaning of section 425.16, subdivision (e), that does not end the

 inquiry. The Court must determine whether the challenged conduct itself supplies the

 elements of the challenged claims or instead merely constitutes evidence supporting

 liability based on nonprotected conduct. (Park v. Board of Trustees of California State

 University (2017) 2 Cal.5th 1057, 1063; Wilson v. Cable News Network, Inc. (2019) 7

 Cal.5th 871, 884.)

Moreover, because Rodriguez seeks to strike discrete allegations within causes

of action rather than the entire pleading, the Court must evaluate each challenged

allegation separately as required by Baral v. Schnitt (2016) 1 Cal.5th 376 and Bonni v.

St. Joseph Health System (2021) 11 Cal.5th 995. Bonni explicitly rejected judicial

reliance upon a generalized "gravamen" approach when distinct acts are targeted.

However, Bonni did not eliminate or alter the threshold requirement articulated in Park:

the challenged act itself must supply the basis for liability. (Bonni, supra, 11 Cal.5th at

pp. 1009-1011.) If the targeted allegations merely provide evidentiary support for liability

arising from other, unprotected conduct, section 425.16 does not apply.

Here, the Court concludes Rodriguez has not met his threshold burden because, viewed individually, none of the challenged allegations independently supplies the elements of liability for the asserted cross-claims.

First, although the recording of a lis pendens constitutes protected petitioning activity, the FACC does not seek to impose liability because Rodriguez recorded a lis pendens. Rather, the lis pendens is alleged merely as one of several downstream means by which Rodriguez continued to assert ownership rights that were allegedly acquired through an underlying fraud. Because the cross-claims do not seek damages or quiet title predicated on the act of filing the document itself, the lis pendens serves only as evidence of Rodriguez’s ongoing assertion of ownership; it is not the actionable wrong upon which liability is based.

Second, Rodriguez's recorded lien likewise does not independently form the basis for liability. The FACC does not allege that the mere mechanical or communicative act of recording the lien constitutes the actionable tort. Instead, the lien is pleaded as a consequence and ongoing manifestation of Rodriguez's purportedly fraudulent acquisition of title, illustrating the continuous cloud upon Diaz's property rights. Accordingly, the recording of the lien functions as evidentiary support for a broader, unprotected fraudulent scheme rather than actionable conduct itself.

Third, Rodriguez's oral and written statements to third parties—including communications to tenant Gonzalez—are similarly pleaded only as evidence of Rodriguez's continued assertion of ownership. The challenged communications do not independently establish the required elements of quiet title, fraud, breach of contract, or removal of lien. Rather, they serve an evidentiary function, illustrating Rodriguez's ongoing efforts to enforce and preserve ownership rights that Diaz contends were wrongfully obtained.

This case is highly analogous to Department of Fair Employment & Housing v. 1105 Alta Loma Road Apartments, LLC (2007) 154 Cal.App.4th 1273. There, although a landlord's unlawful detainer actions and related notices constituted protected petitioning activity, the Court of Appeal held that the tenant's discrimination claims arose from the landlord's underlying failure to accommodate a disability, while the litigation activities merely served as evidence supporting those claims. (Id. at pp. 1284-1285.) Likewise, the Court finds guidance in Copenbarger v. Morris Cerullo World Evangelism (2013) 215 Cal.App.4th 1237, which concluded that allegations concerning a protected three-day notice actually targeted the defendants' alleged misuse of that notice in furtherance of an underlying, unprotected scheme. (Id. at pp. 1248-1249.) The same reasoning applies here: the targeted allegations concern Rodriguez’s use of filings and statements in furtherance of the underlying fraudulent acquisition and retention of title, not independent wrongdoings arising from protected speech or petitioning.

The moving papers focus heavily upon whether certain claims, such as slander of title, could factually survive if the challenged allegations were excised. That, however, is not the dispositive first-prong inquiry under Park. The relevant question is whether the protected acts themselves constitute the conduct giving rise to liability. (Park, supra, 2 Cal.5th at p. 1063.) Because the core wrong alleged is the fraudulent deprivation of title, the subsequent publications merely evidence the falsity and cloud created by that underlying fraud.

Finally, the Court notes that the motion fails to identify with precision the exact sentences or phrases within the challenged paragraphs that supposedly constitute protected activity. Instead, Rodriguez moves to strike entire paragraphs wholesale, many of which contain blended allegations of both protected and unprotected conduct. While Baral authorizes striking discrete allegations, the moving party retains the burden to precisely identify the specific allegations subject to section 425.16. Rodriguez’s generalized references to entire paragraphs further undermine his threshold showing.

Because the challenged allegations are evidentiary and incidental to the broader unprotected fraudulent scheme underlying Cross-Complainant's claims, Bonni does not alter the analysis. Rodriguez has failed to demonstrate that the challenged claims arise from protected activity within the meaning of Code of Civil Procedure section 425.16, and he has failed to satisfy the first prong of the anti-SLAPP framework. Cross-defendants Gonzalez and Molina, having joined in the motion, obtain no greater relief.

Accordingly, Rodriguez fails to meet his burden on anti-SLAPP, as to Gonzalez and Molina, who joined in Rodriguez’s motion.

                             ii.        Second Prong – Probability of Prevailing on the Merits

Having failed to meet his burden on the first prong, the Court does not proceed to further discussion of the second prong.

c.     Conclusion

Plaintiff/Cross-Defendant Jesus Rodriguez’s anti-SLAPP motion is DENIED.

Plaintiff/Cross-Defendant Jesus Rodriguez is ordered to give notice.

Case Number: 24STCV32554    Hearing Date: July 28, 2026    Dept: 731

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: PLAINTIFFS’ ATTORNEY FEES, COURT COSTS, AND LITIGATION EXPENSES


Moving Party:               Plaintiffs, Samuel Sufrin and Century Quality Management, Inc.

Opposing Party:           Defendant, Genesis Motor America LLC

Notice:                         OK

 

  1. Background Facts

Plaintiffs brought this Song-Beverly Warranty Act action against Defendant Genesis Motor America LLC and Does 1 to 50, in December of 2024 and settled with Defendant in May 2026. (See May 6, 2026, Notice of Settlement) Plaintiffs seek attorney's fees and costs as prevailing buyers pursuant to Civil Code section 1794(d). Plaintiffs also contend they are entitled to recover post-offer costs pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 998.

 

  1. Motion for Attorneys’ Fees and Costs

a.     Legal Standard on Motion

 

Plaintiffs obtained a settlement providing relief on their Song-Beverly claims. A plaintiff who obtains such relief through settlement is the prevailing buyer within the meaning of Civil Code section 1794(d). (Duff v. Jaguar Land Rover North America, LLC (2022) 74 Cal.App.5th 491, 502.) Plaintiffs are therefore entitled to recover reasonable attorney's fees and costs.

The Court begins this inquiry “with the ‘lodestar,’ i.e., the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate.” (PLCM Group v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095 (PLCM Group).) From there, the “lodestar figure may then be adjusted [according to a multiplier enhancement] based on consideration of factors specific to the case, in order to fix the fee at the fair market value for the legal services provided.” (Ibid.) Relevant multiplier factors include “(1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, [and] (4) the contingent nature of the fee award.” (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132.)

b.     Discussion

The parties before the Court dispute whether Plaintiffs are the prevailing parties for purposes of this motion and whether the $27,359.95 sought in fees and costs by Plainitffs—$23,767.50 in fees, a 0.1 multiplier of $2,376.75, and costs of $1,215.70—are reasonable.

After review, the Court finds it in favor of Plaintiffs, but only in part.

The Court first determines that Plaintiffs are the prevailing party under Code of Civil Procedure section 1032 and consistent with Duff v. Jaguar Land Rover North America, LLC (2022) 74 Cal.App.5th 491, 502.

Although counsel requests hourly rates of $575 and $375, the Court finds those rates exceed the reasonable market rates for attorneys of comparable experience litigating routine Song-Beverly actions in Los Angeles County. Based on the Court's familiarity with prevailing market rates and the nature of this litigation, reasonable hourly rates are $500 for Mr. Kohen and $300 for Ms. Artolozaga. (See Motion, Kohen Decl., Ex. 1 [chart of hours and fee rates claimed].)

The Court declines to apply a positive multiplier. Although counsel accepted the matter on a contingent basis, the litigation did not present novel or particularly difficult legal issues, did not require exceptional skill beyond that expected in Song-Beverly litigation, and does not appear to have significantly precluded counsel from accepting other employment. The lodestar adequately compensates counsel for the work performed.

Costs of $1,215.70 are reasonable and properly supported.

Relief is thus resolved in the amount of $21,765.70.

c.     Conclusion

Plaintiffs Samuel Sufrin and Century Quality Management, Inc.’s motion for attorneys’ fees and costs is GRANTED, in part, in the amount of $21,765.70.

Plaintiffs Samuel Sufrin and Century Quality Management, Inc. are ordered to give notice.

Case Number: 25STCV08940    Hearing Date: July 28, 2026    Dept: 731

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: DEFENDANT FCA US, LLC’S MOTION FOR JUDGMENT ON THE PLEADINGS

Moving Party:               Defendant, FCA US, LLC

Opposing Party:           Plaintiff, Charles Kitchel

Notice:                         OK

 

  1. Background Facts

Plaintiff Charles Kitchel sues Defendant FCA US, LLC (“FCA”), Eureka Chrysler Dodge Jeep Ram (“Eureka”), and Does 1 through 10 pursuant to a March 26, 2026, Complaint alleging four claims arising from the California Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act (“SBA”) as against FCA, a negligent repair claim against Eureka, and a concealment claim against FCA.

Now before the Court is FCA’s opposed motion for judgment on the pleadings challenging the Complaint’s SBA and concealment claims alleged against it.

 

  1. Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings

a.     Request for Judicial Notice

The Court takes judicial notice of FCA’s webpage printout of its Code of Civil Procedure section 871.30 opt in into the procedures of Code of Civil Procedure section 871.20, et seq., with the Department of Consumer Affairs. (Evid. Code, §§ 452, subd. (h), 453.)

b.     Meet and Confer

Before filing a statutory motion for judgment on the pleadings, the moving party must contact the party whose pleading is subject to the motion and determine whether the claims to be raised in the motion can be resolved by agreement. (Code Civ. Proc., § 439, subd. (a).)

Here, the moving papers satisfy the statutory requirements by showing meet and confer efforts that failed to resolve in a resolution of the disputes now before the Court. (Motion, Downs Decl., ¶¶ 3-4, Exs. A-B; see Code Civ. Proc., § 439, subd. (a)(3)(B).)

c.     Legal Standard

Either prior to trial or at the trial—and barring statutory provisions otherwise—the plaintiff or the defendants may move for judgment on the pleadings where the appropriate ground for such a motion is the same as that arguable by general demurrer, namely, the failure to state a cause of action or defense. (Dobbins v. Hardister (1966) 242 Cal.App.2d 787, 791 A motion for JOP is used to challenge a pleading in the same manner as a general demurrer—that is, the challenged pleading (1) establishes that the court does not have subject-matter jurisdiction or (2) does not allege facts sufficient to support a cause of action or defense. (Code Civ. Proc., § 438, subd. (c)(1); see Marzec v. Public Empls. Ret. Sys. (2015) 236 Cal.App.4th 889, 900; International Assn of Firefighters v. City of San Jose (2011) 195 Cal.App.4th 1179, 1196.)

To sufficiently allege a cause of action, a complaint must allege all the ultimate facts—that is, the facts needed to establish each element of the cause of action pleaded. (Committee on Children’s Television, Inc. v. General Foods Corp. (1983) 35 Cal.3d 197, 212, superseded by statute as stated in Branick v. Downey Savings & Loan Assn. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 235, 242.) In testing the sufficiency of the cause of action, the demurrer admits the truth of all material facts properly pleaded. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 966-967.) Courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (Taylor v. City of Los Angeles Dept. of Water and Power (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 1216, 1228, disapproved on other grounds, Jones v. Lodge at Torrey Pines Partnership (2008) 42 Cal.4th 1158, 1162.) However, this analysis “does not admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Daar v. Yellow Cab Co. (1967) 67 Cal.2d 695, 713.) The face of the complaint includes exhibits attached to the complaint. (Frantz v. Blackwell (1987) 189 Cal.App.3d 91, 94.) If facts appearing in the exhibits contradict those alleged, the facts in the exhibits take precedence. (Holland v. Morse Diesel Intern., Inc. (2001) 86 Cal.App.4th 1443, 1447, superseded by statute on other grounds as stated in White v. Cridlebaugh (2009) 178 Cal.App.4th 506, 521.)

As with a demurrer, the grounds for a motion for judgment on the pleadings must appear on the face of the pleading or be based on facts capable of judicial notice. (Bufil v. Dollar Fin. Grp. (2008) 162 Cal.App.4th 1193, 1202, disapproved on other grounds in Noel v. Thrifty Payless, Inc. (2019) 7 Cal.5th 955, 985-986, fn. 15.) The only significant difference between the two motions is that a motion for judgment on the pleadings is brought after the time to file a general demurrer has expired. (Code Civ. Proc., § 438, subd. (f); Caldera Pharms. v. Regents of the Univ. of Cal. (2012) 205 Cal.App.4th 338, 350; International Assn of Firefighters v. City of San Jose, supra, at p. 1196.

d. Discussion

I. Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings – First Through Fourth Causes of Action (Song-Beverly Act)

A. Code of Civil Procedure Section 871.21

Defendant first contends that Plaintiff's Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act ("SBA") causes of action are barred by the statute of limitations established in Code of Civil Procedure section 871.21.

The Court agrees.

Code of Civil Procedure section 871.20 et seq. establishes a comprehensive statutory framework governing restitution and replacement actions involving motor vehicles. Section 871.21 provides that an action subject to the chapter must be commenced within one year after expiration of the applicable express warranty and, regardless of that period, may not be brought later than six years after the date of the vehicle's original delivery, subject only to the tolling provisions expressly provided in the statute.

Plaintiff argues that Chapter 12 cannot apply because the subject vehicle was purchased before the statute's enactment. The Court disagrees.

California law presumes that statutes operate prospectively unless the Legislature clearly expresses a contrary intent. However, the presumption against retroactivity applies only where application of the statute would attach new legal consequences to completed conduct or impair vested substantive rights. A statute that regulates the maintenance of actions filed after its operative date or modifies procedural remedies ordinarily applies to pending or future litigation absent constitutional concerns.

Here, the Legislature expressly provided that Chapter 12 applies to manufacturers that timely elected to participate in the statutory arbitration program. Defendant has established, through judicially noticeable evidence, that FCA timely exercised that election. Accordingly, Chapter 12 governs restitution and replacement actions filed against FCA after the statute became operative.

The Court recognizes that the subject vehicle was purchased in 2016, before enactment of Chapter 12. Nevertheless, Plaintiff did not commence this action until after the statutory scheme became operative. Under these circumstances, application of section 871.21 does not impose new obligations concerning the original vehicle sale; rather, it governs the maintenance of an action filed after the effective date of the statute against a manufacturer that elected into the statutory program.

The Complaint alleges that Plaintiff purchased the subject vehicle on February 1, 2016, and filed this action on March 26, 2026. More than six years elapsed between the vehicle's original delivery and commencement of this action. Plaintiff has not alleged facts demonstrating that any of the statutory tolling provisions contained in section 871.21 apply, nor has Plaintiff identified any other statutory basis avoiding the six-year limitations period.

 

Accordingly, Plaintiff's first through fourth causes of action are barred by Code of Civil Procedure section 871.21.

Because this conclusion is dispositive, the Court need not reach Defendant's alternative statute of limitations arguments under Commercial Code section 2725 or those relating specifically to the implied warranty claim.

B. Leave to Amend

Leave to amend is denied.

The statute of limitations issue turns upon undisputed historical facts appearing on the face of the Complaint—the vehicle's purchase date and the filing date of this action. Plaintiff has not identified, either in the pleadings or opposition, any additional facts that could bring the action within the statutory limitations period or within one of the statutory tolling provisions. Under these circumstances, amendment would be futile.

Accordingly, Defendant's motion for judgment on the pleadings is GRANTED WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND as to the first through fourth causes of action.

II. Sixth Cause of Action – Concealment

Defendant also seeks judgment on the pleadings as to Plaintiff's concealment claim, contending, among other things, that the economic loss rule bars recovery.

The Court agrees.

The California Supreme Court recently clarified the scope of the economic loss rule in Rattagan v. Uber Technologies, Inc. There, the Court held that a plaintiff may pursue a tort claim arising in connection with a contractual relationship only where the alleged tortious conduct is independent of the parties' contractual rights and obligations and exposes the plaintiff to a risk of harm beyond that reasonably contemplated by the contract itself.

 

Here, Plaintiff alleges that Defendant concealed an engine defect before the sale of the vehicle and seeks recovery of the economic consequences flowing from the alleged failure of the vehicle to conform to warranty. The Complaint, however, contains only conclusory allegations that Defendant possessed pre-sale knowledge of the alleged defect. It does not plead with the requisite specificity facts showing when Defendant acquired such knowledge, how Defendant obtained that knowledge, what information was allegedly concealed from Plaintiff, or facts establishing a duty to disclose independent of Defendant's warranty obligations.

Nor does the Complaint allege personal injury, damage to property other than the vehicle itself, or other harm independent of the alleged contractual expectations arising from the purchase transaction. As presently pleaded, Plaintiff seeks only the economic benefit of the bargain allegedly lost because the vehicle did not perform as warranted.

Accordingly, the Court concludes that the Complaint does not sufficiently allege an independent fraudulent inducement claim of the type recognized in Dhital, but instead alleges a warranty dispute for which the claimed damages are purely economic. Under these circumstances, the concealment claim is barred by the economic loss rule as explained in Rattagan.

Leave to amend is denied. In opposition, Plaintiff has not identified any additional facts that could be alleged establishing Defendant's pre-sale knowledge of the alleged defect, a duty to disclose independent of the contractual relationship, or damages independent of the alleged warranty breach. Because Plaintiff has not demonstrated a reasonable possibility that these deficiencies can be cured by amendment, further leave to amend would be futile.

Defendant's motion for judgment on the pleadings is therefore GRANTED WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND as to the sixth cause of action.

 

d.     Conclusion

Defendant FCA US, LLC’s motion for judgment on the pleadings is GRANTED, as to the first to fourth and sixth causes of action alleged in the Complaint, without leave to amend.

Case Number: 25STCV27828    Hearing Date: July 28, 2026    Dept: 731

 

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: GENERAL MOTORS LLC’S MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT, OR IN THE ALTERNATIVE, SUMMARY ADJUDICATION

Moving Party:               Defendant, General Motors LLC

Opposing Party:           Plaintiff, Jose Salazar

Notice:                         OK

 

  1. Background Facts

Plaintiff Jose Salazar (“Plaintiff”) sues Defendant General Motors LLC (“Defendant”) and Does 1 through 20, inclusive, pursuant to a September 23, 2025, Complaint alleging claims of breach of implied warranty of merchantability under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act (“Song-Beverly Act”) and breach of express warranty under the Song-Beverly Act.

Now before the Court is Defendant’s opposed motion for summary judgment or, in the alternative, summary adjudication of the Complaint’s two causes of action.

 

  1. Motion for Summary Judgment or, in the Alternative, Summary Adjudication

a.     Legal Standard

A motion for summary judgment shall be granted if all the papers submitted show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact for trial or that the moving party is entitled to a judgment as a matter of law. (Code of Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c).) A party may also seek summary adjudication of select causes of action, affirmative defenses, claims for damages, or issues of duty, which may be made by a standalone motion or as an alternative to a motion for summary judgment and proceeds in all procedural respects like a motion for summary judgment, but which must completely dispose of the challenged cause of action, affirmative defense, claim for damages, or issue of duty. (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subds. (f)(1)-(2), (t).)

The moving party bears the initial burden of production to make prima facie showing no triable material fact issues. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 850.) This burden on summary judgment or adjudication “is more properly one of persuasion rather than proof, since he must persuade the court that there is no material fact for a reasonable trier of fact to find, and not to prove any such fact to the satisfaction of the court itself as though it were sitting as the trier of fact.” (Id. at p. 850, fn. 11.) If the moving party meets this burden, the burden shifts to the opposing party to make a rebuttal prima facie showing that a triable issue of material fact exists. (Id. at p. 849.)

Where a defendant seeks summary judgment or adjudication, the defendant must show that either “one or more elements of the cause of action, even if not separately pleaded, cannot be established, or that there is a complete defense to that cause of action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (o)(2).) A defendant may satisfy this burden by showing that the claim “cannot be established” because of the lack of evidence on some essential element of the claim. (Union Bank v. Superior Court (1995) 31 Cal.App.4th 574, 590.) Once the defendant meets this burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to show that a “triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to that cause of action or defense thereto.” (Ibid.)  “[I]n ruling on motions for summary judgment courts are to ‘“liberally construe the evidence in support of the party opposing summary judgment and resolve doubts concerning the evidence in favor of that party.”’ [Citations].” (Cheal v. El Camino Hospital (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 736, 760.)

b.     Discussion

Defendant’s moving papers make a single argument for summary judgment or adjudication of the claims alleged in the Complaint: Plaintiff presented the Subject Vehicle to an authorized GM facility for only one warranty-related repair, which, under well established law, cannot support a breach of warranty claim under the Song-Beverly Act, because such warranty claims require that the plaintiff provide the manufacturer with a reasonable number of opportunities to repair the vehicle under warranty, and here, Defendant presents evidence to show Plaintiff only presented the vehicle for a single repair visit. (Motion, pp. 5-6.)

In legal support of this position, Defendant points to Krieger v. Nick Alexander Imports (1991) 234 Cal.App.3d 205, 213, and other authorities to support the position that California authorities hold that more than one repair visit is necessary to support breach of warranty claims under the Song-Beverly Act. (Motion, pp. 5-6.)

In factual support of this position, Defendant points to Undisputed Material Fact (“UMF”) No. 6, which stands for the proposition that Plaintiff presented the subject vehicle for only one repair under warranty: an engine replacement on July 7, 2025. In turn, UMF No. 6 is supported by documents showing a single repair order at Casa De Cadillac Buick GMC Sherman Oaks, and a passage of Plaintiff’s deposition. (Motion, Separate Statement, UMF No. 6, citing Motion, Jensen Decl., Exs. C, D [repair order] & Motion, Roybal Decl., Ex. E at pp. 28-33 [deposition transcript excerpt].)

The record fails to reflect that Plaintiff objected to this evidence or any evidence advanced by Defendant in support of summary judgment or summary adjudication.

For ease of discussion, the Court discusses Defendant’s arguments in relation to each respective cause of action, mixing its summary judgment and adjudication discussions together.

                              i.        First Cause of Action, Breach of Implied Warranty

Defendant’s argument fails in relation to the first cause of action, both on summary judgment and on summary adjudication.

Defendant’s sole argument on summary judgment and adjudication against the first cause of action is that an implied breach of warranty of merchantability claim under the Song-Beverly Act requires presentation of the subject vehicle for repair. (Motion, pp. 5-6, 6-7.)

However, California law is clear. As directly quoted from Carver v. Volkswagen Grp. of Am., Inc. (Dec. 26, 2024) 107 Cal.App.5th 864, 878,

A fundamental difference between an express warranty claim and an implied warranty of merchantability claim is that before bringing an express warranty claim under the Act, the plaintiff must present the defective vehicle to an authorized representative of the manufacturer for repair and give the manufacturer a reasonable opportunity to fix the vehicle. (Donlen v. Ford Motor Co. (2013) 217 Cal.App.4th 138, 152, 158 Cal.Rptr.3d 180 [(Donlen)]; Mocek [v. Alfa Leisure, Inc. (2003) 114 Cal.App.4th 402,] 407, 7 Cal.Rptr.3d 546 [(Mocek)] [if “an express warranty is breached, the Act sets out an extensive scheme requiring manufacturers to repair (Civ. Code, § 1793.2), and the buyer has a concomitant duty to allow a reasonable number of opportunities for repair before it can demand a replacement of the goods or reimbursement”].) If the vehicle cannot be repaired, the manufacturer must offer to replace or repurchase the vehicle from the consumer. (§ 1793.2, subd. (d).) However, this aspect of the Act “does not apply to the breach of an implied warranty of merchantability.” (Mocek, at p. 407, 7 Cal.Rptr.3d 546.) Because the defects involved in an implied warranty of merchantability are so fundamental, “the buyer is not required to await a seller's attempt to make repairs.” (Brand v. Hyundai Motor America (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 1538, 1548, 173 Cal.Rptr.3d 454 (Brand); § 1794, subd. (a).)

Based on the clear authority in Carver, which is drawn from a line of authority going back to Mocek and confirmed again in cases such as Mexia v. Rinker Boat Co. (2009) 174 Cal.App.4th 1297, 1307 (cited in Plaintiff’s opposition), Defendant’s argument fails as to the first cause of action.

Summary judgment is accordingly DENIED because not all theories for liability have been negated here, i.e., the implied breach of warranty claim. (Lopez v. Superior Court (1996) 45 Cal.App.4th 705, 714; see Johnson v. Chui (2011) 199 Cal.App.4th 775, 778 [“[A] defendant is entitled to summary judgment only when all theories of liability have been negated”].)

Summary adjudication of the first cause of action is likewise DENIED given that Defendant’s argument in support of summary adjudication of the first cause of action is identical to the argument in support of summary judgment of the same. (Motion, pp. 6-7.)

                             ii.        Second Cause of Action, Breach of Express Warranty

The Court thus only proceeds with a discussion of summary adjudication of the second cause of action for breach of express warranty under the Song-Beverly Act.

A buyer must prove four elements to succeed on a claim for breach of an express warranty for a vehicle under the Song-Beverly Act: (1) the vehicle had a defect or nonconformity covered by a written warranty that substantially impaired the vehicle's use, value, or safety to a reasonable person (the nonconformity element); (2) the vehicle was presented to an authorized representative of the manufacturer for repair (the presentation element); (3) the manufacturer or its authorized repair facility did not repair the defect after a reasonable number of repair attempts (the failure-to-repair element); and (4) the manufacturer did not promptly replace or repurchase the vehicle (the failure-to-replace-or-repurchase element). (Carver, supra, 107 Cal.App.5th at p. 879.)

Here, for purposes of summary adjudication, Defendant is correct in arguing that for breach of express warranty claims, the Song-Beverly Act “give[s] recourse to the buyer of a new automobile that suffers from the same defect repeatedly, or is out of service for cumulative repairs for an extended period.” (Krotin v. Porsche Cars North America, Inc. (1995) 38 Cal.App.4th 294, 301.) “[A]t a minimum, there must be more than one opportunity to fix the nonconformity.” (Robertson v. Fleetwood Travel Trailers of California, Inc. (2006)¿144 Cal.App.4th 785, 799, citing Silvio v. Ford Motor Co.¿(2003) 109 Cal.App.4th 1205, 1208.) “Nonconformity” connotes one, or the same nonconformity. (See Civ. Code § 1793.22, subd. (e)(1) [“nonconformity” is “nonconformity which substantially impairs the use, value, or safety of the new motor vehicle to the buyer or lessee”]; see, e.g., Oregel v. American Isuzu Motors, Inc.¿(2001) 90 Cal.App.4th 1094, 1103-1104 [six attempts to fix same oil leak] (Oregel); Robertson, supra, at p. 800 [several attempts to fix same water leak].)

A review of Defendant’s evidence shows testimony from Plaintiff to the effect that before the July 2025 repair visit related to the subject vehicle, he had no issues with the subject vehicle, and that since the visit, he has driven it without any issues, even if Plaintiff testified that who knows what may happen in the future. (Motion, Jensen Decl., Exs. C, D [repair order] & Motion, Roybal Decl., Ex. E at pp. 28-33 [deposition transcript excerpt].)

Defendant's evidence establishes that Plaintiff afforded Defendant only one opportunity to repair the alleged nonconformity. Because proof that the manufacturer was afforded a reasonable number of opportunities to repair is an essential element of a Song-Beverly express warranty claim, Defendant has negated an essential element of that claim and therefore satisfied its initial burden under Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (p)(2). The burden accordingly shifts to Plaintiff to demonstrate the existence of a triable issue of material fact.

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that even though Plaintiff only presented the subject vehicle for one repair visit, there is still an express breach of contract because evidence exists to show that in May 2025, Defendant circulated internal memoranda showing the engine in Plaintiff’s vehicle was defective and that the defect was not then repairable and no repair attempts should be made, and that in July 2025, Plaintiff’s vehicle stalled on the freeway, with the vehicle towed to a Defendant-affiliated repair facility, where the engine was replaced. (Opposition, p. 7, citing Opposition, Separate Statement, Additional Material Facts (“AMF”) Nos. 2-3.)

In reply, Defendant argues that Plaintiff has not addressed the presentation requirement’s need to have more than one attempt at repair and that Plaintiff’s arguments merely attempt to create triable issues of fact under circumstances where the presentation requirement is just not met. (Reply, pp. 6-7.)

The Court agrees with Defendant and determines that Plaintiff has failed to meet his burden on summary adjudication vis-à-vis the second cause of action. Plaintiff has not presented evidence to show more than the single repair visit in July 2025, and “at a minimum, there must be more than one opportunity to fix the nonconformity.” (Robertson v. Fleetwood Travel Trailers of California, Inc. (2006)¿144 Cal.App.4th 785, 799, citing Silvio v. Ford Motor Co.¿(2003) 109 Cal.App.4th 1205, 1208.) The single visit here does meet that standard here on summary adjudication. (Cf. Oregel, supra, 90 Cal.App.4th at pp. 1103-1104 [six attempts to fix same oil leak]; Robertson, supra, 144 Cal.App.4th 785 at p. 800 [several attempts to fix same water leak].)

Summary adjudication of the second cause of action is thus GRANTED.

c.     Conclusion

Defendant General Motors LLC’s motion for summary judgment or, in the alternative, summary adjudication, is:

(1)   DENIED as to summary judgment of the Complaint’s causes of action;

(2)   DENIED as to summary adjudication of the Complaint’s first cause of action; and

(3)   GRANTED as to summary adjudication of the Complaint’s second cause of action.

Defendant General Motors LLC is ordered to give notice.

[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: PETITIONER’S MOTION TO COMPEL ARBITRATION PURSUANT TO INSURANCE CODE SECTION 11580.2(f)

Moving Party:               Petitioner, Aram Djavadian

Opposing Party:           Respondent, Mobilitas Insurance Company of Arizona

Notice:                         OK

 

  1. Background Facts

On April 23, 2026, Petitioner Aram Djavadian (“Petitioner”) initiated this action pursuant to a petition seeking assignment of a case number and a hearing on a petition to compel arbitration between the parties in this uninsured motorist dispute pursuant to Insurance Code section 11580.2, subdivision (f).

Now before the Court is Petitioner’s petition to compel arbitration and appoint arbitrator, to which Mobilitas has filed a partial non-opposition, and Petition has filed a reply.

 

  1. Petition to Compel Arbitration and Appoint Arbitrator

a.     Legal Standard

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure §1281.2, generally, on a petition to compel arbitration, the court must grant the petition unless it finds that (1) no written agreement to arbitrate exists, (2)¿the right to compel arbitration has been waived, (3) grounds exist for revocation of the agreement, or (4) litigation is pending that may render the arbitration unnecessary or create conflicting¿rulings on common issues.

“If [an] arbitration agreement provides a method of appointing an arbitrator, that method shall be followed. If the arbitration agreement does not provide a method for appointing an arbitrator, the parties to the agreement who seek arbitration and against whom arbitration is sought may agree on a method of appointing an arbitrator and that method shall be followed. In the absence of an agreed method, or if the agreed method fails or for any reason cannot be followed, or when an arbitrator appointed fails to act and his or her successor has not been appointed, the court, on petition of a party to the arbitration agreement, shall appoint the arbitrator.

When a petition is made to the court to appoint a neutral arbitrator, the court shall nominate five persons from lists of persons supplied jointly by the parties to the arbitration or obtained from a governmental agency concerned with arbitration or private disinterested association concerned with arbitration. The parties to the agreement who seek arbitration and against whom arbitration is sought may within five days of receipt of notice of the nominees from the court jointly select the arbitrator whether or not the arbitrator is among the nominees. If the parties fail to select an arbitrator within the five-day period, the court shall appoint the arbitrator from the nominees.”

(Code Civ. Proc., § 1281.6.)

b.     Parties’ Parties

Here, Plaintiff petitions for an order compelling arbitration of the parties’ insurance coverage dispute and appointing an arbitrator. As to compelling arbitration, Plaintiff argues that: (1) the controversy is ripe for adjudication in arbitration; and (2) Mobilitas’ argument against arbitration to date—i.e., that “Petitioner’s claim for occupational accident benefits with Blue Star pursuant to Business & Professions Code section 7455 constitutes a workers’ compensation claim preclusive of arbitration under Insurance Code section 11580.2(f))” (Petition, p. 7)—is incorrect for four distinct reasons.

In its opposition, Mobilitas clarifies that it has since changed its position on arbitration given that, as raised in Petitioner’s moving papers, Petitioner’s occupational insurance claim has been resolved. Mobilitas, however, argues that this Court should confine its order to compelling the action into arbitration and appointing Jake Courtney (the parties’ chosen arbitrator) as arbitrator, and not rule on the question of whether Petitioner’s proceeds from his occupational insurance claim offset the underinsured motorist claim against Mobilitas, with Mobilitas claiming an offset here.

In reply, Petitioner argues that this Court should not only grant this motion as unopposed and confirm Jake Courtney as arbitrator, but also make a legal determination that occupational accident insurance does not result in proceeds arising from “any worker’s compensation laws,” and that the arbitrator is not confined to only awarding general damages in arbitration—two points that Petitioner claims remain outstanding by the parties and can only be resolved by this Court.

c.     Discussion

The parties no longer dispute that this matter should proceed to arbitration pursuant to Insurance Code section 11580.2. Mobilitas expressly does not oppose an order compelling arbitration and agrees that Jake Courtney should be appointed as arbitrator.

Because there is no dispute regarding the existence of the arbitration agreement, waiver, revocation, or any other statutory basis for denying arbitration, the petition is properly granted.

Petitioner further requests that the Court determine, as a matter of law, that:

 

  1. occupational accident insurance benefits are not benefits arising under a workers' compensation

 law and therefore do not offset any recovery under Insurance Code section 11580.2; and

  1. The arbitrator is authorized to determine all legally recoverable damages and is not limited to

 general damages.

The Court declines to decide these issues in the present proceeding. The additional questions

presented by Petitioner concern substantive issues that may arise during or following the arbitration.

Resolution of those issues is unnecessary to the disposition of the petition presently before the

Court. Courts generally do not issue advisory opinions or decide legal questions unnecessary to the

determination of the pending proceeding.

Accordingly, the Court expresses no opinion concerning the merits of the parties' respective

 positions regarding offsets, the interpretation of Insurance Code section 11580.2, or the categories of

damages recoverable in arbitration. Those issues may be presented in an appropriate proceeding if and

when they become ripe for judicial determination.

d.     Conclusion

The Petition to Compel Arbitration is GRANTED.

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure sections 1281.2 and 1281.4, the parties are ordered to

proceed to arbitration, and this action is STAYED pending completion of the arbitration.

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.6, Jake Courtney is appointed as the

 arbitrator, consistent with the parties' agreement.

The Court declines to make the additional legal determinations requested by Petitioner regarding

the effect of occupational accident insurance benefits or the scope of damages recoverable in

arbitration, without prejudice to the parties raising those issues in an appropriate proceeding

should they become ripe.
