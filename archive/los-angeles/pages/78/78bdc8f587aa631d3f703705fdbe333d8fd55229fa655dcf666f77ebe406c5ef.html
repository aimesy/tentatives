Case Name: Harnage v. Applebee’s Restaurants, LLC, et al.

MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT OR SUMMARY ADJUDICATION OF ISSUES
[CCP § 437c]

Moving Party: Defendant, Apple Mid Cal II LLC
Responding Party: Plaintiff, Jamie Harnage

RELIEF REQUESTED:
Grant summary judgment, or in the alternative, summary adjudication of issues, on Plaintiff’s Complaint in favor of Defendant.

CAUSES OF ACTION: from Form Complaint
1) General Negligence
2) Premises Liability

SUMMARY OF FACTS:
The Complaint alleges that on July 31, 2020, Jamie Harnage (“Plaintiff”) was an invited guest/paying patron at an Applebee’s restaurant when an unsecured umbrella fell and struck Plaintiff causing severe physical, emotional and mental injuries. 

ANALYSIS:
The function of a motion for summary judgment or adjudication is to allow a determination as to whether an opposing party can show evidentiary support for a pleading or claim and, if not, to enable an order of summary dismissal without the need for trial. (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 843.) CCP section 437c, subdivision (c) “requires the trial judge to grant summary judgment if all the evidence submitted, and ‘all inferences reasonably deducible from the evidence’ and uncontradicted by other inferences or evidence, show that there is no triable issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” (Adler v. Manor Healthcare Corp. (1992) 7 Cal.App.4th 1110, 1119.) “The function of the pleadings in a motion for summary judgment is to delimit the scope of the issues; the function of the affidavits or declarations is to disclose whether there is any triable issue of fact within the issues delimited by the pleadings.” (Juge v. County of Sacramento (1993) 12 Cal.App.4th 59, 67, citing FPI Development, Inc. v. Nakashima (1991) 231 Cal. App. 3d 367, 381-82.) 
 
As to each claim as framed by the complaint, the defendant or cross-defendant must satisfy the initial burden of proof by showing that one or more elements of the cause of action, even if not separately pleaded, cannot be established, or that there is a complete defense to the cause of action. (CCP, § 437c subd. (p)(2).) Once the defendant has met that burden, the burden shifts to the plaintiff to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists as to that cause of action or a defense thereto. (CCP, § 437c subd. (p)(2).) To establish a triable issue of material fact, the party opposing the motion must produce substantial responsive evidence. (Sangster v. Paetkau (1998) 68 Cal.App.4th 151, 166.) 

“On a summary judgment motion, the court must therefore consider what inferences favoring the opposing party a factfinder could reasonably draw from the evidence.¿While viewing the evidence in this manner, the court must bear in mind that its primary function is to identify issues rather than to determine issues.¿[Citation.]¿Only when the inferences are indisputable may the court decide the issues as a matter of law.¿ If the evidence is in conflict, the factual issues must be resolved by trial.”¿(Binder v. Aetna Life Ins. Co. (1999) 75 Cal.App.4th 832, 839.)¿Further, “the trial court may not weigh the evidence in the manner of a factfinder to determine whose version is more likely true.¿[Citation.]¿Nor may the trial court grant summary judgment based on the court’s evaluation of credibility. [Citation.]” (Id. at p. 840; see also Weiss v. People ex rel. Department of Transportation (2020) 9 Cal.5th 840, 864 [“Courts deciding motions for summary judgment or summary adjudication may not weigh the evidence but must instead view it in the light most favorable to the opposing party and draw all reasonable inferences in favor of that party”].)

First and Second Causes of Action, Premises Liability and Negligence 
The elements of a negligence claim and a premises liability claim are the same: a legal duty of care, breach of that duty, and proximate cause resulting in injury. (Kesner v. Superior Court (2016) 1 Cal.5th 1132, 1158.) California law requires landowners to maintain land in their possession and control in a reasonably safe condition.¿(Ann M. v. Pacific Plaza Shopping Center (1993) 6 Cal.4th 666, 674 [disapproved on other grounds in Reid v. Google, Inc. (2010) 50 Cal.4th 512, 527, fn. 5].)¿ 

“To impose liability for injuries suffered by an invitee due to [a] defective condition of the premises, the owner or occupier must have either actual or constructive knowledge of the dangerous condition or have been able by the exercise of ordinary care to discover the condition, which if known to him, he should realize as involving an unreasonable risk to invitees on his premises ...”. (Ortega v. K-Mart Corp. (2001) 26 Cal.4th 1200, 1206.)  Plaintiff has the burden of showing that the owner had notice of the defect in sufficient time to correct it. (Id. at 1205.)

Defendant Apple Mid Cal II (“Defendant”) argues that Plaintiff cannot show that Defendant had actual or constructive notice of the allegedly dangerous condition, i.e. the unstable patio umbrella. Defendant asserts that there is no evidence of any prior or subsequent incidents involving the umbrellas on the outdoor patio falling or coming out of their bases. (Antonio Depo., pp. 86:4-6, 128:14-22, 132:21-25.) Defendant’s General Manager Ramon Antonio (“Antonio”) testified that he set up the umbrellas on the day of the incident. (Antonio Depo., p. 56:5-11.) He testified that the procedure for checking the umbrellas is to ensure they are in working order, that the base is in good condition, that each base has a weight on top of it, such as sandbags, and that each base has a screw that goes into the base of the umbrella. (Antonio Depo., pp. 60:16-64:10.) Antonia further testified that throughout the day he conducted multiple inspections of the outdoor patio area. (Antonio Depo., pp. 56:5-58:7.)

However, Defendant presents no evidence that the procedures for securing the umbrellas were adequate, or that Antonio, or any other employee, followed these procedures on the day of the incident. One could reasonably infer that the procedures were either inadequate or not properly executed, given that the umbrella ultimately became dislodged. The existence of a procedure for securing the outdoor umbrellas does not establish that the procedure was actually followed on the day of the incident or that the umbrellas were, in fact, properly secured. Moreover, it is not sufficient to merely argue that Plaintiff has no evidence of prior incidents. Defendant’s burden on summary judgment is to present evidence, not simply to point out that Plaintiff does not possess needed evidence. (See Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at 854-855.) Accordingly, the Court does not find that Defendant has met its burden to satisfy the initial burden of proof. 

Even if Defendant had met its initial burden, Plaintiff meets her burden to show that a triable issue of one or more material facts exists. Plaintiff presents evidence that the umbrellas in question were residential-grade umbrellas purchased from a discount store, not commercial-grade systems designed for outdoor dining. (Colburn Decl., ¶¶ 8-9.) Plaintiff presents evidence that the umbrellas were not installed with bolted bases or engineered anchoring systems. (Colburn Decl., ¶ 9.) Plaintiff’s restaurant safety expert opines that by purchasing residential umbrellas and deploying them without proper securing hardware, Defendant acted below accepted industry standards of care. (Colburn Decl., ¶ 9.) Plaintiff further presents evidence that the forecast for the day of the incident predicted windy conditions and that wind gusts that afternoon ranged from 16 mph to 26 mph (Colburn Decl., ¶ 14; Burns Decl., ¶¶ 8-9.) The manufacturer’s instruction manual for the subject umbrella warns users to close and put the umbrellas away on windy days. (Burns Decl., ¶ 11, Ex. 9.) Defendant’s Person Most Knowledgeable and General Market Leader is unaware of any policy instructing restaurant staff to remove umbrellas on windy days or monitor weather conditions to prevent accidents from windblown umbrellas. (Lamaina Depo., pp. 84:7-18, 92:5-24, 93:9-14.) 

This evidence is sufficient to create a triable issue of fact as to whether Defendant knew of, or should have known of, the dangerous condition and, through the exercise of ordinary care, could have discovered and corrected it. A reasonable trier of fact could determine that Defendant should have known that using umbrellas not intended for commercial use could create a dangerous condition. A reasonable trier of fact could also determine that Defendant had actual or constructive notice of the dangerous condition in light of the warnings in the instruction manual, the windy conditions on the day of the incident, and the foreseeable risk that an open outdoor umbrella could become dislodged from its base by a strong gust of wind. 

In the reply, Defendant reasserts that Plaintiff cannot establish that Defendant had actual or constructive notice of a dangerous condition. Defendant argues that the General Manager conducted regular inspections of the outdoor patio area, thus demonstrating ordinary care and negating any inference of constructive notice. (Reply, pp. 5:18-6:27.) This argument is unavailing. “Where the dangerous or defective condition of the property which causes the injury has been created by reason of the negligence of the owner of the property or his employee acting within the scope of the employment, the owner of the property cannot be permitted to assert that he had no notice or knowledge of the defective or dangerous condition in an action by an invitee for injuries suffered by reason of the dangerous condition. Under such circumstances knowledge thereof is imputed to him.” (Hatfield v. Levy Bros. (1941) 18 Cal.2d 798, 806.) A factfinder could reasonably infer that Defendant created a dangerous condition by using outdoor umbrellas not intended for commercial use and failing to use them in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. It is foreseeable that wind could cause an open umbrella to dislodge from its base. 

Thus, a triable issue of fact exists regarding whether Defendants maintained their property in a safe condition. 

The motion is DENIED. 

RULING:
Defendant Apple Mid Cal II LLC’s Motion for Summary Judgment is DENIED.

Case No: 24GDCV00214 Trial Date:  n/a
Case Name: Manoukian v. FCA US LLC, et al.

MOTION FOR ATTORNEYS’ FEES
[CCP § 1794 subd. (d)]

Moving Party: Plaintiff, Valod Manoukian   
Responding Party: Defendant, FCA US LLC 

RELIEF REQUESTED:
Order awarding attorneys’ fees and costs in the amount of $102,318.00 comprised of attorneys’ fees of $67,122.00, a 1.5 lodestar multiplier of $33,561.00, assistant fees of $1,635.00 and costs of $7,182.02. 

CAUSES OF ACTION: from Complaint 
1) Fraud and Deceit 
2) Breach of Written Warranty [Magnuson-Moss]
3) Breach of Implied Warranty [Magnuson-Moss]
4) Breach of Written Warranty [Song-Beverly]
5) Breach of Implied Warranty [Song-Beverly]
6) Violation of Business & Professions Code § 17200, et seq.
7) Violation of Business & Professions Code § 17500, et seq.
8) Negligence 
9) Strict Liability 

SUMMARY OF FACTS:
This case is a lemon-law action. Valod Manoukian (“Plaintiff”) allege that on November 10, 2023, Plaintiff leased a 2024 Jeep Wrangler Rubicon, for which FCA US LLC (“Defendant”) issued express warranties. Plaintiff alleges that the vehicle was delivered with defects and nonconformities to warranties. Plaintiff alleges that the defects and nonconformities to warranty manifested themselves within the express warranty period. The Complaint alleges that notwithstanding Plaintiff’s entitlement, Defendant has failed to either promptly replace the new motor vehicle or to promptly make restitution in accordance with the Song-Beverly Act. 

ANALYSIS:
“Except as otherwise expressly provided by statute, a prevailing party is entitled as a matter of right to recover costs in any action or proceeding.” (CCP § 1032 subd. (b).) Attorney fees are allowed as costs when authorized by contract, statute or law. (CCP § 1033.5, subd. (a)(10)(B).)

In a lemon law action, costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees, may be recovered by a prevailing buyer under the Song-Beverly Act. Civil Code section 1794 states: 

“If the buyer prevails in an action under this section, the buyer shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney’s fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action.” 

(Civil Code § 1794, subd. (d).) 

Entitlement to Attorneys’ Fees
The parties here do not dispute that Plaintiff is the prevailing parties pursuant to a May 15, 2025, Release Agreement which provides for an award of attorneys’ fees, costs, and expenses to be determined by noticed motion. (Mot., p.5:8-9.) Thus, Plaintiff is the prevailing party in this action and is entitled to a reasonable amount of attorneys’ fees. 

Reasonableness of Fees
Plaintiff seeks an award of attorneys’ fees and expenses in the total amount of $102,318.00 consisting of: (1) $ 67,122.00 in attorneys’ fees; (2) a 1.5 multiplier enhancement on the attorneys’ fees and costs [or $33,561.00 ]; (3) assistant fees equal to $1,635.00; and (4) $7,182.02 in costs. The motion seeks approximately 113.60 hours of time spent by attorneys at The Margarian Law Firm at billing rates ranging from $570.00 to $750.00 per hour. 

Hourly Rate
In determining a reasonable attorneys’ fee, the trial court begins with the lodestar, i.e., the number of hours reasonably expended multiplied by the reasonable hourly rate. (PLCM Group, Inc. v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084. 1095.) The lodestar may then be adjusted based on factors specific to the case in order to fix the fee at the fair market value of the legal services provided. (Warren v. Kia Motors America, Inc. (2018) 30 Cal.App.5th 24, 36.) These facts include (1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, (2) the skill displayed in presenting them, (3) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys, (4) the contingent nature of the fee award. (Ibid.) 

“The reasonable hourly rate is that prevailing in the community for similar work.” (PLCM Group, supra, 22 Cal.4th at p. 1095 [“The experienced trial judge is the best judge of the value of professional services rendered in his court.”].)

In connection with attorneys’ fee awards under the Song-Beverly Act, “The statute “requires the trial court to make an initial determination of the actual time expended; and then to ascertain whether under all the circumstances of the case the amount of actual time expended and the monetary charge being made for the time expended are reasonable. These circumstances may include, but are not limited to, factors such as the complexity of the case and procedural demands, the skill exhibited and the results achieved. If the time expended or the monetary charge being made for the time expended are not reasonable under all the circumstances, then the court must take this into account and award attorney fees in a lesser amount. A prevailing buyer has the burden of ‘showing that the fees incurred were “allowable,” were “reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation,” and were “reasonable in amount.” ’ ” (Goglin v. BMW of North America, LLC (2016) 4 Cal.App.5th 462, 470, quoting Nightingale v. Hundai Motor America (1994) 31 Cal.App.4th 99, 104.) 

In the opposition, Defendant argues that the Court should reduce the hourly rates requested and apply the rates from the 2023 Real Rate Report. (Opp., p. 3:5-20.)

The moving papers submit evidence justifying the billing rates claimed, describing the experience and expertise of each attorney who billed on this matter (H. Margarian Decl., ¶¶ 12-15; S. Margarian Decl., ¶¶ 2-11; Kimball Decl., ¶¶ 2-11; Ter-Saakyan Decl., ¶¶ 2-8.) The rates requested conform with what this Court would expect to be charged in matters of this nature. The Court has reviewed all evidence submitted and is familiar with the customary billing rates in this county and in cases of this nature. Under the circumstances, the Court finds the billing rates reasonable, and the Court will not reduce the billing rates charged. The Court will however, take into account the high hourly rates charged and counsels’ attested levels of expertise when assessing whether the total number of hours expended was reasonable.

Number of Hours
Plaintiff’s fee recovery is based on 113.60 hours of time spent litigating this case through the instant motion for which Plaintiff has submitted billing records. (H. Margarian Decl., Ex. A.) Defendant objects to Plaintiff’s requested hours on the grounds that several of the billing entries are unreasonable. (Opp., pp. 3:23-7:23.)

“In challenging attorney fees as excessive because too many hours of work are claimed, it is the burden of the challenging party to point to the specific items challenged, with a sufficient argument and citations to the evidence. General arguments that fees claimed are excessive, duplicative, or unrelated do not suffice.” (Premier Medical Management Systems, Inc. v. California Ins. Guarantee Ass’n (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 550, 564.)

First, Defendant objects to $1,720.00 hours billed and associated with reviewing senior attorneys’ work and the subsequent finalized work completed by those senior attorneys. Upon review of the time entries, the Court does not find the time expended to be unreasonable or excessive. The time spent on each task was minimal and it is not unreasonable for counsel to review another attorney’s product to ensure accuracy. 

Accordingly, the Court declines to reduce the fees on this ground. 

Second, Defendant objects to $14,035.00 [21.70 hours] billed to draft, review, and finalize Plaintiff’s pleadings and discovery. The Court agrees that the hours claimed are excessive. Modifying template based discovery for the instant case and reviewing the discovery responses from the car manufacturer and dealership should take minimal time for counsel with the level of experience and high hourly rate claimed here. 

The Court therefore reduces the fee by 6.5 hours, or $4,550.00. 

Third, Defendant objects to $4,215.00 for time spent calculating attorneys’ fees/costs and preparing a breakdown of the hours expended. Upon review of the time entries, the Court finds that the time billed by “AJT” on March 23, 2026 is duplicative of the time billed by the same timekeeper on May 4, 2025. 

The Court therefore reduces the fee by 2.8 hours, or $1,960.00. 

Fourth, Defendant objects to $2,390.00 for time billed for clerical tasks, including calendaring, reviewing documents, proofreading, and updating the file. Upon review of the disputed time entries, the Court finds that several entries are for non-compensable administrative tasks. 

The Court therefore reduces the fee by 1.6 hours, or $860.00. 

Fifth, Defendant objects to $2,695.00 for time associated with Plaintiff’s second vehicle inspection on grounds that the inspection was duplicative and cumulative of the first vehicle inspection. Upon review of the time entries, the Court does not find the time expended to be unreasonable or excessive. The first vehicle inspection appears to have been conducted by counsel while the second vehicle inspection was conducted by a retained expert. Thus, the inspections are not duplicative. 

Accordingly, the Court declines to reduce the fees on this ground. 

Sixth, Defendant objects to $2,992.00 for time billed for Plaintiff’s motions to compel initial responses. The Court agrees that the hours claimed are excessive. Motions to compel initial discovery responses are routine, non-complex motions and should take minimal time for counsel with the level of experience and high hourly rate claimed here. In addition, the four motions prepared were largely duplicative of each other. 

The Court therefore reduces the fee by 5.0 hours, or $3,500.00.  

Seventh, Defendant objects to $3,135.00 for routine preparation work. These hours relate to assessing and researching the case and preparing the Complaint. Upon review of the time entries, the Court does not find the time expended to be unreasonable or excessive.

Accordingly, the Court declines to reduce the fees on this ground. 


Lastly, Defendant objects to $15,267.00 for the time billed and associated with the instant fee motion. While the billing and supporting evidence may have taken some time to gather, this appears to be substantially a template motion regularly used by Plaintiff’s counsel. In addition, the hours anticipated to review the opposition, prepare a reply and appear at the hearing are excessive. The Court finds a reasonable time spent to be no more than 1.0 hour to review the opposition, 3.0 hours to prepare the reply, and 1.0 hour to attend the hearing by video appearance. 

The Court therefore reduces the fee by 5.0 hours, or $3,525.00. 

Having analyzed the motion and pleadings filed, and having reviewed the billing statements provided, the Court determines that a reasonable lodestar in this case is $52,727.00 representing 92.70 hours.

Multiplier
While the lodestar reflects the basic fee for comparable legal services in the community, it may be adjusted based on various factors, including “(1) the novelty and difficulty of the questions involved, and the skill displayed in presenting them; (2) the extent to which the nature of the litigation precluded other employment by the attorneys; (3) the contingent nature of the fee award” and (4) the success achieved. (Serrano v. Priest (1977) 20 Cal.3d 25, 49.) 

Nonetheless, the court must not consider extraordinary skill and the other Serrano factors to the extent these are already included with the lodestar. (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal. 4th 1122, 1138-1139.) “[A] trial court should award a multiplier for exceptional representation only when the quality of representation far exceeds the quality of representation that would have been provided by an attorney of comparable skill and experience billing at the hourly rate used in the lodestar calculation. Otherwise, the fee award will result in unfair double counting and be unreasonable.” (Id. at 1139.) 

Plaintiff requests a lodestar multiplier enhancement of 1.5 based on securing a favorable result and to account for the risk in taking on matters on a contingency basis. (Mot., pp. 16:23-19:28.)

The Court finds that under the circumstances of this case, a lodestar multiplier is not appropriate. There is no indication that counsel was prevented from taking on other clients by the representation. While counsel accepted the case on a contingency basis, and Plaintiff’s attorneys advanced all litigation costs and expenses, the Court finds that such consideration and risk is already included within the lodestar amount. That is because the substantial hourly rates allowed for by the Court are hourly rates for lemon law cases done on a contingency basis. 

Based on the foregoing, the Court declines to award a lodestar multiplier.

Entitlement and Reasonableness of Costs 
Allowable costs “shall be reasonably necessary to the conduct of the litigation rather than merely convenient or beneficial to its preparation.” (CCP § 1033.5, subd. (c)(2).) Any items not specifically mentioned by statute “may be allowed or denied in the court's discretion.” (CCP, § 1033.5 subd. (c)(4).)

Song-Beverly allows a successful plaintiff to recover both “costs” and “expenses.” (Civ. Code, § 1794, subd. (d).) Courts have held that “it is clear the Legislature intended the word
‘expenses' to cover items not included in the detailed statutory definition of ‘costs.”’ (Jensen v.
BMW of North America, Inc. (1995) 35 Cal.App.4th 112, 137.) The court in Jensen
held that “[t]he legislative history indicates the Legislature exercised its power to permit the
recovery of expert witness fees by prevailing buyers under the Act … ,” noting that the
legislature included “expenses” in the lemon law act because '“[t]he addition of awards of “costs
and expenses” by the court to the consumer to cover such out-of-pocket expenses as filing fees,
expert witness fees, marshall’s fees, etc., should open the litigation process to everyone.’
[Citation.]” (Ibid.)

Plaintiffs request a total of $7,182.02 in litigation costs and expenses. (Memo of Costs.) The items claimed are proper and necessary on their face, shifting the burden to Defendant to show that they were not reasonable or necessary. Defendant in opposition does not contest the costs. Thus, the costs and expenses are awarded in full as requested in the sum of $7,182.02. 

RULING:
Plaintiff’s Motion for Attorney Fees, Costs, and Expenses is GRANTED, IN PART.

The Court finds that reasonable attorney’s fees, costs and expenses are: 
Lodestar Adjusted = $52,727.00 [$67,122.00 sought] 
Expenses and Costs = $7,182.02
Total Award= $59,909.02 

The total fee award of $59,909.02 [$102,318.00 sought] is to be awarded to Plaintiff Valod Manoukian against Defendant FCA US LLC and added to the judgment.

Case Name: T’s and Sons, LLC v. Krajeski, et al.

MOTION TO BE RELIEVED AS COUNSEL
[CCP § 284]

Moving Party: David M. Syme, Esq., Counsel for Plaintiff, T’s and Sons, LLC  
Responding Party: Plaintiff, T’s and Sons, LLC [Unopposed]

RELIEF REQUESTED:
Leave for counsel to be relieved as Plaintiff’s attorney of record in this action.

CAUSES OF ACTION: from Complaint
1) Breach of Contract
2) Specific Performance
3) Intentional Interference with Contractual Relations
4) Intent to Hinder, Delay, or Defraud
5) Constructive Fraudulent Transfer

SUMMARY OF FACTS:
On July 25, 2023, T’s and Sons, LLC (“Plaintiff”) and defendant Jan Krajeski (“Krajeski”) entered into a Purchase and Sale Agreement for real property located at 309 Oaklawn Avenue, South Pasadena, CA. Plaintiff alleges that defendant Oregon Limitless, Inc. (“Oregon Limitless”) is the current titleholder of the subject property. The Complaint alleges that Krajeski breached the agreement by failing to perform its obligations to provide a clear and insurable title to the Property, failing to perform its obligations to provide disclosures, and failing to close the escrow and complete the purchase and sale. Plaintiff alleges that Krajeski transferred the property to Oregon Limitless on February 8, 2024, by way of a quitclaim deed while the property was still under contract with Plaintiff. 

ANALYSIS:
“[T]he attorney in an action…may be changed at any time before or after judgment or final determination, as follows: (1) upon the consent of both client and attorney…; (2) upon the order of the court, upon the application of either client or attorney, after notice from one to the other.”¿ (CCP § 284; California Rules of Court, rule 3.1362.)¿ The withdrawal request may be denied if it would cause an injustice or undue delay in proceeding; but the court's discretion in this area is one to be exercised reasonably. (Mandell v. Superior (1977) 67 Cal.App.3d 1, 4; Lempert¿v. Superior Court (2003) 112 Cal.App.4th 1161, 1173.)¿

In making a motion to be relieved as counsel, the attorney must comply with procedures set forth in CRC, rule 3.1362.¿ The motion must be made using mandatory forms:¿ 
¿ 
1. Notice of Motion and Motion to be Relieved as Counsel directed to the client (MC-051);¿ 
2. Declaration “stating in general terms and without compromising the confidentiality of the attorney-client relationship” the reasons that the motion was brought (MC-052);¿and 
3. Proposed Order (MC-053).¿ 
¿ 
The forms must be timely filed and served on all parties who have appeared in the case.¿ (CRC rule 3.1362.)¿ If these documents are served on the client by mail, there must be a declaration stating either that the address where the client was served is their “current residence or business address” or “the last known residence or business address of the client and the attorney has been unable to locate a more current address after making reasonable efforts to do so within 30 days before the filing of the motion to be relieved.”¿ (CRC rule 3.1362 subd. (d)(1).)¿ 

The court has discretion on whether to allow an attorney to withdraw, and a motion to withdraw will not be granted where withdrawal would prejudice the client. (Ramirez v. Sturdevant (1994) 21 Cal.App.4th 904, 915.) Withdrawal is generally permitted unless there is a compelling reason to continue the representation. (Heple v. Kluge (1951) 104 Cal.App.2d 461, 462.)   

Counsel filed a Notice of Motion and Motion (MC-051), Declaration in Support of Motion (MC-052) and Proposed Order (MC-053). As reason for the Motion, Counsel states: “We cannot continue to represent Clients. We have served Client via Email a Voluntary Substitution of Attorney form, but they have not responded.” (MC-052, ¶ 2.) The forms were served via mail on the client and electronically served on all other parties who have appeared in this matter on June 22, 2026. (POS.) Counsel confirmed via email in the last 30 days that the client’s address is current. (MC-052, ¶ 3(b)(1)(d).) 

Finally, there is no showing that withdrawal would cause injustice or undue delay in the proceedings as trial in this action is not yet set. Although Plaintiff is a corporation and thus cannot represent itself in this case, there is sufficient time before trial for Plaintiff to obtain new counsel. Thus, the Court finds that the motion sets forth an adequate basis for withdrawal. 

The motion is GRANTED.

RULING:
Counsel David M. Syme, Esq.’s Motion to be Relieved as Counsel is GRANTED. 

Counsel will be relieved effective upon the efiling of the proof of service of the signed order upon the client.

Case Name: Martinez v. Los Angeles Community College District, et al.

MOTION FOR LEAVE TO CONDUCT IME
[CCP § 2032.310]

Moving Party: Defendant, Los Angeles Community College District
Responding Party: Plaintiff, Noah Martinez 

RELIEF REQUESTED:
An order granting leave to conduct a neuropsychological examination on Plaintiff. 

CAUSES OF ACTION: from Complaint 
1) Dangerous Condition of Public Property 

SUMMARY OF FACTS:
The Complaint alleges that on October 3, 2023, Noah Martinez (“Plaintiff”) was leaving a classroom at East Los Angeles Community College, located at 1301 Avenida Cesar Chavez, Monterey Park, California, when he slipped over a slippery and wet walking surface. The Complaint alleges that Los Angeles Community College District (“Defendant”) owned, maintained and controlled the premises. 

ANALYSIS:
“Any party may obtain discovery, subject to the restrictions set forth in Chapter 5 (commencing with Section 2019.010), by means of a physical or mental examination of (1) a party to the action, (2) an agent of any party, or (3) a natural person in the custody or under the legal control of a party, in any action in which the mental or physical condition (including the blood group) of that party or other person is in controversy in the action.” (CCP § 2032.020 subd. (a).) “A mental examination conducted under this chapter shall be performed only by a licensed physician, or by a licensed clinical psychologist who holds a doctoral degree in psychology and has had at least five years of postgraduate experience in the diagnosis of emotional and mental disorders.” (CCP § 2032.020 subd. (c)(1).)

Where any party seeks to obtain discovery by a physical or mental examination, the party must obtain leave of the court. (CCP § 2032.310, subd. (a).) The motion must “specify the time, place, manner, conditions, scope, and nature of the examination, as well as the identity and the specialty, if any, of the person or persons who will perform the examination”. (CCP § 2032.310, subd. (b).) The court shall grant the motion only for good cause shown. (CCP § 2032.320, subd. (a).)  A showing of good cause generally requires “that the party produce specific facts justifying discovery and that the inquiry be relevant to the subject matter of the action or reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence.” (Vinson v. Superior Court (1987) 43 Cal.3d 833, 840.)¿

Defendant moves for leave to conduct a neuropsychological examination on Plaintiff. Defendant argues that there is good cause for the examination because Plaintiff’s discovery responses state that Plaintiff suffers from “[m]emory loss/difficulty remembering; trouble concentrating/focusing; confusion/disorientation; reduced attention to details; difficulty with language or finding words; changes in mood or behavior.” (Kohrs Decl., Ex. 1.) In addition, during his deposition, Plaintiff testified that he has had a hard time focusing and has had memory issues since the incident. (Kohrs Decl., Ex. 2.) Defendant argues that, thus Plaintiff has placed his memory, attention, concentration, executive functioning, and emotional condition in controversy. 

The motion specifies the place, manner, conditions, scope and nature of the examination, as well as the identity and specialty of the person who will perform the examination. (Kohrs Decl., Ex. 4.) Thus, the motion satisfies the requirements of CCP section 2032.310, subdivision b.

In the opposition, Plaintiff asserts that Defendant has not demonstrated good cause for the examination. Plaintiff argues that Defendant has already obtained a neurological examination directed to Plaintiff’s alleged neurological injuries, which “overlap substantially with the claimed head injury and post-concussive complaints.” (Opp., p. 4:9-10.) Plaintiff argues that Defendant has not explained why the prior neurological examination is insufficient to evaluate Plaintiff’s neurological and head-injury claims. (Opp., p. 6:8-9.) Lastly, Plaintiff argues that the proposed examination is overbroad and not narrowly tailored. (Opp., pp. 7:25-9:14.) 

The Court finds that there is good cause for the examination. “[B]y asserting a causal link between [his] mental distress and [Defendant’s] conduct, plaintiff implicitly claims it was not caused by a preexisting mental condition, thereby raising the question of alternative sources for the distress.” (Vinson, supra, 43 Cal.3d at p. 840.) Plaintiff’s neuropsychological condition has been put at issue in this case. To be sure, placing the issue in controversy is only a threshold issue; the Court must also find good cause to order the mental examination requested. (See Vinson supra, 43 Cal.3d at pp. 841-842.) While a neurological examination is highly complementary to a neuropsychological examination, they assess different brain functions. In addition, the neurologist conducting the neurological exam specifically recommended follow-up neuropsychological testing. Accordingly, there is good cause for the examination and no cumulative burden for the additional testing. 

Thus, the motion is GRANTED.

RULING:
Defendant Los Angeles Community College District’s Motion for Leave to Conduct IME is GRANTED.

Case Name: Pickett, et al. v. Tesla Inc., et al.

MOTION TO BE RELIEVED AS COUNSEL
[CCP § 284]

Moving Party: Kevin Jacobson, counsel for Plaintiffs 
Responding Party: Plaintiffs, Taunya Renee Pickett and Four Sisters Naturally, LLC [Unopposed]

RELIEF REQUESTED:
Leave for counsel to be relieved as Plaintiffs’ attorney of record in this action.

CAUSES OF ACTION: from Complaint 
1) Violation of Song-Beverly Act [Breach of Express Warranty]
2) Violation of Song-Beverly Act [Breach of Implied Warranty]
3) Violation of the Song-Beverly Act Section 1793.2

SUMMARY OF FACTS:
This is a lemon-law action. Taunya Renee Pickett and Four Sisters Naturally, LLC (“Plaintiffs”) allege that on January 16, 2023, Plaintiffs purchased a 2023 Tesla Model X for which Tesla, Inc., a Texas Corporation d/b/a Tesla Motors, Inc. (“Defendant”) issued written warranties. Plaintiff alleges that the vehicle was delivered with defects and nonconformities to warranties. Plaintiff alleges that the defects and nonconformities to warranty manifested themselves within the express warranty period. The Complaint alleges that notwithstanding Plaintiffs’ entitlement, Defendant has failed to either promptly replace the new motor vehicle or to promptly make restitution in accordance with the Song-Beverly Act. 

ANALYSIS:
“[T]he attorney in an action…may be changed at any time before or after judgment or final determination, as follows: (1) upon the consent of both client and attorney…; (2) upon the order of the court, upon the application of either client or attorney, after notice from one to the other.”¿ (CCP § 284; California Rules of Court, rule 3.1362.)¿ The withdrawal request may be denied if it would cause an injustice or undue delay in proceeding; but the court's discretion in this area is one to be exercised reasonably. (Mandell v. Superior (1977) 67 Cal.App.3d 1, 4; Lempert¿v. Superior Court (2003) 112 Cal.App.4th 1161, 1173.)¿

In making a motion to be relieved as counsel, the attorney must comply with procedures set forth in CRC, rule 3.1362.¿ The motion must be made using mandatory forms:¿ 
¿ 
1. Notice of Motion and Motion to be Relieved as Counsel directed to the client (MC-051);¿ 
2. Declaration “stating in general terms and without compromising the confidentiality of the attorney-client relationship” the reasons that the motion was brought (MC-052);¿and 
3. Proposed Order (MC-053).¿ 
¿ 
The forms must be timely filed and served on all parties who have appeared in the case.¿ (CRC rule 3.1362.)¿ If these documents are served on the client by mail, there must be a declaration stating either that the address where the client was served is their “current residence or business address” or “the last known residence or business address of the client and the attorney has been unable to locate a more current address after making reasonable efforts to do so within 30 days before the filing of the motion to be relieved.”¿ (CRC rule 3.1362 subd. (d)(1).)¿ 

The court has discretion on whether to allow an attorney to withdraw, and a motion to withdraw will not be granted where withdrawal would prejudice the client. (Ramirez v. Sturdevant (1994) 21 Cal.App.4th 904, 915.) Withdrawal is generally permitted unless there is a compelling reason to continue the representation. (Heple v. Kluge (1951) 104 Cal.App.2d 461, 462.)   

Counsel filed a Notice of Motion and Motion (MC-051), Declaration in Support of Motion (MC-052) and Proposed Order (MC-053). As reason for the Motion, Counsel states: “The attorney-client relationship has irreparably broken down. Despite counsel’s multiple attempts at limiting prejudice by making repeated attempts at contacting the client through various means, such as a third-party vendor via a SkipTrace, Plaintiffs and counsel have reached irreconcilable differences wherein withdrawal is required and/or permitted by CRPC 1.16(b).” (MC-052, ¶ 2.) The forms were served via mail on the client and electronically served on all other parties who have appeared in this matter on June 25, 2026. (POS.) Counsel confirmed via Skiptrace in the last 30 days that the client’s address is current. (MC-052, ¶ 3(b)(1)(d).) 

However, trial in this action is currently set for July 27, 2026. In addition, Four Sisters Naturally, LLC is a corporation and thus cannot represent itself in this case. Accordingly, to avoid prejudice to Plaintiffs, the Court will continue the trial date. The Court will discuss new trial dates with the parties at the hearing. The Court otherwise finds that the motion sets forth an adequate basis for withdrawal. 

The motion is GRANTED.

RULING:
Counsel Kevin Jacobson’s Motion to be Relieved as Counsel is GRANTED. 

Counsel will be relieved effective upon the efiling of the proof of service of the signed order upon the client.

TENTATIVE RULING

Calendar: 3
Date: July 17, 2026
Case No: 25NNCV01500 Trial Date:  Not yet set
Case Name: Xiao v. Ma, et al.

DEMURRER
[CCP § 430.10 subd. (e)]

Moving Party: Defendants, Xuetang Ma, CTOUR Investment Holding Inc., and CTOUR Holiday LLC
Responding Party: Plaintiffs, Anna Xiao and Roy Ruosong Fan 

Deficiencies to Note:
Defendants assert that neither the SAC nor the opposition were properly served on them. 

RELIEF REQUESTED:
Sustain demurrer to the Second Amended Complaint.

CAUSES OF ACTION: from Second Amended Complaint
1) Breach of Contract
2) Breach of Implied Covenant of Good Faith and Fair Dealing
3) Negligence
4) Fraud/Misrepresentation
5) Breach of Express Warranty
6) CLRA Violations
7) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress 

SUMMARY OF FACTS:
Anna Xiao (“Xiao”) and Roy Ruosong Fan (“Fan”) (collectively, “Plaintiffs”) allege that in August of 2024, they entered into a contract with MEBO Travel Group Inc. (“MEBO”) for an 11-day, 9-night tour of Singapore, Malaysia, and Thailand. Plaintiff alleges that MEBO is the authorized retail sales agent of defendant CTOUR Holiday, LLC (“CTOUR Holiday”) which created and manufactured the tour package. The Second Amended Complaint (the “SAC”) alleges that CTOUR Holiday is a wholly owned subsidiary of defendant CTOUR Investment Holding Inc. (“CTOUR Holding”) and that CTOUR Holding and CTOUR Holiday operate under the combined brand name CTOUR/Universal Vision together with Universal Vision Holdings Corporation (“Universal Vision”). The SAC further alleges that defendant Xuetang Ma, also known as Mark Ma, (“Ma”) is the CEO of CTOUR Holding and CTOUR Holiday. 

 Plaintiffs allege that the MEBO travel itinerary directly incorporates CTOUR Holiday’s terms and conditions throughout the document, including terms that state that “CTOUR/Universal Vision reserves the right to appropriately adjust the itinerary order to facilitate tour group operations and ensure smooth travel, but will not reduce items included in the itinerary.” Plaintiffs allege that the tour was marketed and sold as a “Platinum Exclusive” premium experience that would include five-star hotel accommodations, authentic fine dining experiences including a Michelin-starred restaurant meal, no mandatory shopping activities, and would provide a premium, high-quality travel experience justifying the premium price Plaintiffs paid. Instead, Plaintiffs allege that they received a substandard tour that bore no resemblance to what was advertised and sold. 

Plaintiffs allege that they were provided with two-star to three-star hotel accommodations, and substandard and unsanitary meals. Plaintiffs allege that in Thailand, they were merged with a low-budget domestic shopping tour group from China, receiving the same treatment and food as participants in this budget tour, despite having paid a premium price for a “Platinum Exclusive” experience with no shopping. Plaintiff Xiao alleges that as a result of consuming unsanitary food provided during the tour, she contracted food poisoning leaving her unable to fully participate in tour activities. On the last evening, although the contract promised a Michelin-starred dinner, no such meal was provided. The SAC further alleges that on several occasions Plaintiffs were taken on mandatory shopping activities. 

Following the tour, Plaintiffs submitted a written complaint to MEBO detailing the substandard conditions and requesting compensation, which MEBO transmitted to CTOUR Holiday. CTOUR Holiday provided a written response to Plaintiffs’ complaint apologizing for their experience and offering compensation of $100 per person. In total, Plaintiffs allege that they have incurred approximately $20,694.97 in trip-related expenses in reasonable reliance on Defendants’ representations regarding the “Platinum Exclusive” tour package.

ANALYSIS:
A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747; CCP, § 430.10, subd. (e).) 

To sufficiently allege a cause of action, a complaint must allege all the ultimate facts—that is, the facts needed to establish each element of the cause of action pleaded. (Committee on Children’s Television, Inc. v. General Foods Corp. (1983) 35 Cal.3d 197, 212, superseded by statute as stated in Branick v. Downey Savings & Loan Assn. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 235, 242.) “[E]ach evidentiary fact that might eventually form part of the plaintiff’s proof need not be alleged.” (C.A. v. William S. Hart Union High School Dist. (2012) 53 Cal.4th 861, 872.) 

In testing the sufficiency of the cause of action, the demurrer admits the truth of all material facts properly pleaded. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 966-67.) Courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (Taylor v. City of Los Angeles Dept. of Water and Power (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 1216, 1228, disapproved on other grounds, Jones v. Lodge at Torrey Pines Partnership (2008) 42 Cal.4th 1158, 1162.) A demurrer, however, “does not admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Daar v. Yellow Cab Co. (1967) 67 Cal.2d 695, 713.)

Alter-Ego/Agency Allegations 
Defendants demur to the SAC on the ground that Plaintiffs’ allegations are directed at MEBO’s conduct, and that Plaintiff fails to allege facts sufficient to support liability against CTOUR Investment, CTOUR Holiday or Ma under either an alter ego or agency theory. 

Under the alter-ego doctrine, “where a corporation is used by an individual or individuals, or by another corporation, to perpetrate fraud, circumvent a statute, or accomplish some other wrongful or inequitable purpose, a court may disregard the corporate entity and treat the corporation's acts as if they were done by the persons actually controlling the corporation.” (Toho-Towa Co., Ltd. v. Morgan Creek Productions, Inc. (2013) 217 Cal.App.4th 1096, 1106.) 

“A court may also disregard the corporate form in order to hold one corporation liable for the debts of another affiliated corporation when the latter is so organized and controlled, and its affairs are so conducted, as to make it merely an instrumentality, agency, conduit, or adjunct of another corporation. Thus, where there is such domination of finances, policies and practices that the controlled corporation has, so to speak, no separate mind, will or existence of its own and is but a business conduit for its principal, the affiliated corporations may be deemed to be a single business enterprise, and the corporate veil pierced. Under the ‘single business enterprise’ doctrine, separate corporations may operate with integrated resources in pursuit of a single business purpose. The ‘single-business-enterprise’ theory is an equitable doctrine applied to reflect partnership-type liability principles when corporations integrate their resources and operations to achieve a common business purpose.” (Id. at pp. 1107-1108, internal citations and quotations omitted.) 

The SAC alleges that “CTOUR Holiday is a wholly owned subsidiary of CTOUR Holding, which is listed as its sole member on its Statement of Information filed with the California Secretary of State.” (SAC, ¶ 3.) Plaintiff alleges that CTOUR Holding created CTOUR Holiday to conduct its travel business. (SAC, ¶ 13.) CTOUR Holding and CTOUR Holiday share the same principal place of business. (SAC, ¶ 13.) CTOUR Holding, CTOUR Holiday and Universal Vision share common corporate officers. (SAC, ¶ 5, Exs. G-I.) Defendant Ma is the CEO of both CTOUR Investment and CTOUR Holiday. (SAC, Exs. G-I.) The SAC alleges that defendant Ma “personally participated in, directed, authorized, ratified, and controlled the creation, marketing, and sale of the tour package at issue in this action, including the false "Platinum Exclusive" representations contained in CTOUR Holiday's promotional materials.” (SAC, ¶¶ 12, 14.) The SAC further alleges that CTOUR Holding, CTOUR Holiday, and Universal Vision operate under the combined brand name CTOUR/Universal Vision. (SAC, ¶ 4.) CTOUR/Universal Vision is referenced in the contractual terms incorporated into the MEBO travel itinerary and the contract identifies CTOUR Holiday as the party imposing the contractual obligations. (SAC, ¶ 8.) The SAC alleges that “CTOUR Holiday is the operating entity that created, manufactured, marketed, and sold the "Platinum Exclusive" tour package at issue, assigned tour code SINBKK11, produced the promotional materials containing the false representations, and controlled the delivery of tour services through its agent MEBO.” (SAC, ¶ 10.) The SAC alleges that “CTOUR Holding exercises complete control over CTOUR Holiday's finances, policies, and business practices” and that treating “CTOUR Holding and CTOUR Holiday as separate entities would sanction fraud and promote injustice.” (SAC, ¶ 13.) The SAC further alleges that when Plaintiffs’ complained about their trip to MEBO, MEBO transmitted the complaint to CTOUR Holiday who subsequently addressed the issues with Plaintiffs directly. (SAC, ¶¶ 37-41, Exs. C-E.)

These alter-ego allegations are sufficient to avoid demurrer. Detailed pleading is not required to allege an alter ego theory of liability, as Plaintiffs are required to allege only “ultimate rather than evidentiary facts.” (Rutherford Holdings, LLC v. Plaza Del Rey (2014) 223 Cal.App 4th, 221, 236.) Moreover, Plaintiffs’ allegations here are more specific than alter-ego allegations courts have held to pass muster in other instances. (See, e.g., Rutherford Holdings, LLC, supra, 223 Cal.App.4th at p. 235, First Western Bank & Trust Co. v. Bookasta (1968) 267 Cal.App.2d 910, 915-916.) Assuming Plaintiff’s allegations can be proved, Ma and the corporate entities may be held liable. 

Defendants also demur to each of the individual causes of action on the same ground, i.e. that Plaintiffs have failed to allege facts establishing a relationship between Plaintiffs and Defendants sufficient to impose liability. As discussed above, however, Plaintiffs have adequately alleged an alter-ego/agency relationship. 

Thus, the demurrer is OVERRULED on this ground. 

Fifth Cause of Action, Breach of Express Warranties
The California Commercial Code applies only to the sale of “goods.” (Commercial Code, § 2106.) “Any affirmation of fact or promise made by the seller to the buyer which relates to the goods and becomes part of the basis of the bargain creates an express warranty that the goods shall conform to the affirmation or promise.” (Commercial Code, § 2313 subd. (1)(a).) In addition, “[a]ny description of the good which is made part of the basis of the bargain creates an express warranty that the goods shall conform to the description.” (Commercial Code, § 2313 subd. (1)(b).)

Defendants further argue that Plaintiffs cannot assert a cause of action for breach of express warranty because the contract at issue is a contract for services, not goods. (Demurrer, pp. 10:27-1:23.) In the opposition, Plaintiffs assert that “the SAC plausibly alleges that the transaction included a sale of goods” and that meals are within the ambit of the UCC. (Opp., pp. 8:22-9:5.) 

 In “determining whether or not a contract is one of sale or to provide services, we must look to the ‘essence’ of the agreement. When service predominates, the incidental sale of items of personal property [ ] does not alter the basic transaction.” (Filmservice Laboratories, Inc. v. Harvey Bernhard Enterprises, Inc.¿(1989) 208 Cal.App.3d 1297, 1305.) 

Here, the contract is a comprehensive travel package including a pre-planned itinerary, scheduled tours, a tour guide, meals, and accommodation. (SAC, ¶¶ 20-25, Ex. A.) The essence of the travel package is the rendition of services—namely guiding, hosting, transporting and facilitating experiences. (SAC, Ex. A.) The meals included in the package are merely incidental to those services. California courts have reinforced this conclusion. For example, in Pena v. Sita World Travel, Inc. (1978) 88 Cal.App.3d 642, 644-645, the Court of Appeal determined that a defendant travel agency arranging a tour package was providing a service, not a good or product. (See also, Ferrari v. Grand Canyon Dories (1995) 32 Cal.App.4th 248, 259 [finding that a defendant commercial raft trip operator was providing a service for recreational raft transportation, and that use of the raft was merely an incident to this service.].) 

Plaintiffs request leave to amend, but only in passing. Although leave to amend should be freely granted, “[t]he burden is on the plaintiff to demonstrate the manner in which the complaint can be amended. (Ross v. Creel Printing & Publishing Company (2002) 100 Cal.App.4th 736, 748.) “To carry its burden, plaintiff must show the manner it can amend its complaint and demonstrate how that amendment will change the legal effect of the pleading.” (Community Assisting Recovery, Inc. v. Aegis Security Insurance Co. (2001) 92 Cal. App. 4th 886, 895, citation omitted.) 

Plaintiffs have not demonstrated how they might cure the fifth cause of action. As discussed above, the facts otherwise alleged in the SAC do not appear proper for a breach of express warranty claim. 

Thus, the demurrer to the fifth cause of action is SUSTAINED, without leave to amend.  

Seventh Cause of Action, Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress 
“The elements of a prima facie case for the tort of intentional infliction of emotional distress are: (1) extreme and outrageous conduct by the defendant with the intention of causing, or reckless disregard of the probability of causing, emotional distress; (2) the plaintiff’s suffering severe or extreme emotional distress; and (3) actual and proximate causation of the emotional distress by the defendant’s outrageous conduct. Conduct to be outrageous must be so extreme as to exceed all bounds of that usually tolerated in a civilized community.” (Wilson v. Hynek (2012) 207 Cal.App.4th 999, 1009, citation and ellipses omitted.)

Defendants also argue that Plaintiffs have failed to allege facts demonstrating conduct that is sufficiently extreme or outrageous to support a claim for intentional infliction of emotional distress. (Demurrer, pp. 12:10-13:2.) The SAC alleges that Plaintiffs prepaid a premium price for their trip, traveled to a foreign country and were dependent on Defendants to provide accommodations, transportation, meals, and the daily itinerary. (SAC, ¶¶ 20-26.) Once abroad and reliant on Defendants for these services, Plaintiffs allegedly were served unsanitary food, merged with a low-budget domestic shopping tour group, and subjected to a substandard travel experience that materially differed from the premium experience Plaintiffs had paid for and were promised. (SAC, ¶¶ 26-36.) At the pleading stage, these allegations are sufficient to state a claim for intentional infliction of emotional distress. A trier of fact could conclude that Defendants’ alleged conduct was extreme and outrageous in light of Plaintiff’s particular vulnerability after having paid a substantial sum of money for the trip, traveling thousands of miles away to a foreign country, and being dependent on Defendants for their food, transportation, accommodation, and daily itinerary.

Thus, the demurrer to the seventh cause of action is OVERRULED.   

RULING:
Defendants Xuetang Ma, CTOUR Investment Holding Inc., and CTOUR Holiday LLC’s Demurrer is SUSTAINED, without leave to amend, as to the fifth cause of action and OVERRULED as to the remaining causes of action. 

Answer to be filed within 10 days.

Case Name: Hernandez v. Compass Group USA, Inc., et al.

MOTION TO COMPEL ARBITRATION
[9 U.S.C. § 1, et seq and CCP § 1281.2]

Moving Party: Defendant, Compass Group USA, Inc. 
Responding Party: Plaintiff, Julio Cesar Hernandez 

RELIEF REQUESTED:
An order compelling Plaintiff to arbitrate this matter and to stay the proceedings pending completion of arbitration. 

CAUSES OF ACTION: from Complaint
1) Harassment in Violation of Gov. Code § 12940 et seq.
2) Disability Discrimination in Violation of Gov. Code § 12940 et seq.
3) Failure to Accommodate in Violation of Gov. Code § 12940(m)
4) Failure to Engage in Interactive Process in Violation of Gov. Code § 12940(n)
5) Age Discrimination in Violation of Gov. Code § 12940 et seq.
6) Failure to Prevent Discrimination in Violation of Gov. Code § 12940(k)
7) Retaliation in Violation § 12940(h)
8) Wrongful Termination
9) Meal and Rest Break Violations of Labor Code § 226.7
10) Failure to Pay Overtime & Wages
11) Failure to Pay All Compensation at Termination; Waiting Time Penalties
12) Violation of Business & Professions Code § 17200 et seq

SUMMARY OF FACTS:
  This action arises out of an employment relationship. The Complaint alleges that on July 3, 2023, Compass Group USA, Inc. (“Defendant”) hired Julio Cesar Hernandez (“Plaintiff”) as a head cook at the JPL Café located in Pasadena, California. Plaintiff alleges that in March 2024, he expressed concerns about food temperature not being maintained at safe levels to his direct supervisor “Sarah” and the director “Christian.” Plaintiff alleges that Christian instructed him to continue with these practices but to ensure compliance when inspectors were present. Plaintiff alleges that due to the repetitive and physical nature of his job duties, he began to suffer severe pain in his knees, which he reported to his supervisor. Plaintiff further alleges that Defendant and its agents made derogatory age-related comments about Plaintiff and other elderly employees and that Defendant had a systematic policy of pushing elderly employees to leave the company. Plaintiff also alleges various wage and hour claims. Defendant terminated Plaintiff on May 8, 2024, citing a reduction in hours and slowdown in work. 

ANALYSIS:
Under both the Federal Arbitration Act, 9 U.S.C. § 1, et seq. (the “FAA”), and Title 9 of Part III of the California Code of Civil Procedure commencing at section 1281 (known as the California Arbitration Act), arbitration agreements are valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, except on such grounds that exist at law or equity for voiding a contract. (Winter v. Window Fashions Professions, Inc. (2008) 166 Cal.App.4th 943, 947.)¿¿¿

Section 2 of the FAA provides, in pertinent part that “[a] written provision . . . to settle by arbitration a controversy thereafter arising out of such contract . . .  shall be valid, irrevocable, and enforceable.”  (9 U.S.C. § 2.)  Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.2 permits a party to file a motion to request that the Court order the parties to arbitrate a controversy. The Court may grant the motion if the Court determines that an agreement to arbitrate the controversy exists, “unless it determines that: (a) The right to compel arbitration has been waived by the petitioner; or (b) Grounds exist for rescission of the agreement.” (CCP § 1281.2.) 

A court’s inquiry is limited to a determination of (1) whether a valid arbitration agreement exists and (2) whether the arbitration agreement covers the dispute. (9 U.S.C. § 4; Chiron Corp. v. Ortho Diagnostics Systems, Inc. (9th Cir. 2000) 207 F.3d 1126, 1130; Howsam v. Dean Witter Reynolds, Inc. (2002) 537 U.S. 79, 84; Omar v. Ralphs Grocery Co. (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 955, 961; Lacayo v. Cataline Restaurant Group Inc. (2019) 38 Cal.App.5th 244, 257 [Where moving party meets initial burden, “the party opposing arbitration must prove by a preponderance of the evidence any defense to the petition”].) 

Existence of Valid Arbitration Agreement
The party moving to compel arbitration must establish the existence of a written arbitration agreement between the parties. (CCP, § 1281.2.) The moving party can meet this initial burden by attaching to the petition a copy of the arbitration agreement purporting to bear the respondent’s signature. (Bannister v. Marinidence Opco, LLC (2021) 64 Cal.App.5th 541, 541-543.) Alternatively, the moving party can meet its initial burden by setting forth the agreement’s provisions in the motion. (See Condee v. Longwood Management Corp. (2001) 88 Cal.App.4th 215, 219; see also Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1330.) 

In support of the motion, Defendant attaches a copy of the Arbitration Agreement executed by Plaintiff on May 23, 2023. (Bailey Decl., Ex. A.) 

Plaintiff argues that Defendant has not established the validity of Plaintiff’s electronic signature because Defendants fails to show the efficacy of the security procedures used in the signing process. (Opp., pp. 1:9-3:11.) This argument is unavailing. 

An opposing party can meet its burden to dispute an arbitration agreement by “testify[ing] under oath or declar[ing] under penalty of perjury that the party never saw or does not remember seeing the agreement, or that the party never signed or does not remember signing the agreement.”¿(Gamboa v. Northeast Community Clinic¿(2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 158, 165; see also Ruiz v. Moss Bros. Auto Group, Inc. (2014) 232 Cal.App.4th 836, 846 [finding that although Ruiz did not deny the electronic signature was his, Moss Bros. had the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence that the electronic signature was authentic once Ruiz claimed he did not recall signing the agreement to arbitrate].)¿¿ 

Plaintiff does not dispute that the signature on the Arbitration Agreement is his, nor does he assert that he does not recall signing the Agreement. Challenging a signature’s authenticity generally requires a declaration by the plaintiff stating he or she did not sign the agreement or does not recall signing it. (Espejo v. Southern California Permanente Medical Group (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 1047, 1054; Ruiz, supra 232 Cal.App.4th at p. 846.) 

In addition, Defendant presents ample indicia that the electronic signature and date on the Arbitration Agreement is attributable to Plaintiff. “[T]he burden of authenticating an electronic signature is not great.” (Ruiz, 232 Cal.App.4th at p. 844.)¿ “An ... electronic signature is attributable to a person if it was the act of the person. The act of the person may be shown in any manner, including a showing of the efficacy of any security procedure applied to determine the person to which the electronic record or electronic signature was attributable.” (Civil Code, § 1633.9, subd. (a).) “For example, a party may establish that the electronic signature was ‘the act of the person’ by presenting evidence that a unique login and password known only to that person was required to affix the electronic signature, along with evidence detailing the procedures the person had to follow to electronically sign the document and the accompanying security precautions.” (Bannister v. Marinidence Opco, LLC (2021) 64 Cal.App.5th 541, 545, citing Espejo, supra, 246 Cal.App.4th at p. 1062.)¿

Here, Defendant presents an affidavit from its HRIS Operations Manager which details the electronic onboarding process by which employees sign the Arbitration Agreement. (Bailey Decl.) Applicants create and generate a username and unique password using PeopleHub, a human resources software system. (Bailey Decl., ¶¶ 3-4.) PeopleHub generates a password protected, unique identifying number and log-in for each employee. (Bailey Decl., ¶ 4.) “When the employee is ready to sign the document, the employee is prompted to enter his name and password to generate his electronic signature on the onboarding documents.” (Bailey Decl., ¶ 5.) Defendant attaches a copy of the audit trail for the activity associated with Plaintiff’s specific employee number. (Bailey Decl., Ex. B.) The audit trail shows that Plaintiff signed the Arbitration Agreement on May 23, 2023, at 16:28:17 UTC. (Bailey Decl., Ex. B.) Once the documents are signed, the manager cannot alter or change the employee’s documents. (Bailey Decl., ¶ 13.) This information is sufficient evidence to authenticate Plaintiff’s electronic signature on the Arbitration Agreement.

Thus, the Court finds that Defendant has met its burden to establish the existence of a written arbitration agreement between the parties.

Arbitrability of Dispute 
“[I]n determining whether a matter is subject to arbitration, courts apply the presumption in favor of arbitration [citation].... ‘ “Doubts as to whether an arbitration clause applies to a particular dispute are to be resolved in favor of sending the parties to arbitration. The court should order them to arbitrate unless it is clear that the arbitration clause cannot be interpreted to cover the dispute.” (California Correctional Peace Officers Assn. v. State of California (2006) 142 Cal.App.4th 198, 205.) 

In relevant portion, the Arbitration Agreement states:

“I and Compass Group USA, Inc. and its subsidiaries, sectors, affiliates, and divisions (collectively, "Compass Entities") mutually agree to use binding individual arbitration as the sole and exclusive means to resolve all legal claims between us, including without limitation those that may arise out of or be related to my employment, compensation, or termination of my employment, except as provided below. For all claims covered by this Agreement, I and the Compass Entities waive our rights to bring a claim against the other in a court of law and in doing so specifically waive our rights to a jury trial or other judicial proceeding. Except as provided below, any claim, dispute, and/or controversy that I may have against the Compass Entities (or their directors, officers, employees, or agents, whether current, past, or future), or that the Compass Entities may have against me, shall be submitted to and determined exclusively by binding arbitration under the Federal Arbitration Act ("FAA") (in conformity with the procedures of the California Arbitration Act -Cal. Code Civ. Proc. Sec 1280 et seq. including section 1283.05 and all of the Act's other mandatory and permissive rights to discovery, unless such provisions are preempted by the FAA). Except as carved out below, "claim, dispute, and/or controversy" includes, but is not limited to, any claims of alleged discrimination, harassment, or retaliation; claims for pay, commissions, bonuses, or benefits; claims regarding any background check or form or process regarding the same; and any other federal, state, or local statute, ordinance, executive order, regulation or any amendments thereto or any other legal theory. The FAA applies to this Agreement because my employer's business involves interstate commerce.” (Bailey Decl., Ex. A.)

Accordingly, the Arbitration Agreement applies to all legal claims arising out of or relating to Plaintiff’s employment, compensation, or termination, including “any claims of alleged discrimination, harassment, or retaliation,” as well as claims relating to wages and pay. (Bailey Decl., Ex. A.) Here, Plaintiff asserts claims for harassment and discrimination, retaliation, wrongful termination, and various wage and hour violations. (Compl.) Each of these causes of action arises out of, or relates to, Plaintiff’s employment, compensation, or termination and therefore falls within the scope of the Arbitration Agreement. 

Thus, the Court finds that the Arbitration Agreement covers the instant dispute. The burden thus shifts to Plaintiff to establish that a defense exists to enforcement of the agreement.   

Defenses 
Under the FAA, a party may challenge the validity or applicability of the arbitration provision by raising the same defenses available to a party seeking to avoid the enforcement of any contract. (Cox v. Ocean View Hotel Corp. (2008) 553 F.3d 1114, 1121.) 

“Both procedural unconscionability and substantive unconscionability must be shown [for a finding of unconscionability to exist], but ‘they need not be present in the same degree’ and are evaluated on a ‘sliding scale.’ [Citation.] ‘[T]he more substantively oppressive the contract term, the less evidence of procedural unconscionability is required to come to the conclusion that the term is unenforceable, and vice versa.” (Pinnacle Museum Tower Assn. v. Pinnacle Market Dev. (US), LLC (2017) 55 Cal.4th 223, 247.) 

A party claiming that one or more provisions of an arbitration agreement is unconscionable, must not only prove unconscionability in the abstract, but also show how such unconscionability specifically affects the party’s arbitration claim. (Htay Htay Chin v. Advanced Fresh Concepts Franchise Corp. (2011) 194 Cal.App.4th 704, 714 [partial limitation on award of fees and costs for “all claims” limiting amount to one-third of any compensatory damages awarded may be unconscionable, but is not a defense in the absence of a showing that the party would in fact be entitled to recover attorney’s fees].) 

Procedural Unconscionability 
Procedural unconscionability “addresses the circumstances of contract negotiation and formation, focusing on oppression or surprise due to unequal bargaining power.” (Pinnacle Museum Tower Assn., supra, 55 Cal.4th at p. 246.) Established case law explains that “‘[o]ppression’ arises from an inequality of bargaining power which results in no real negotiation and ‘an absence of meaningful choice’ [and] ‘[s]urprise’ involves the extent to which the supposedly agreed-upon terms of the bargain are hidden [in the agreement] by the party seeking to enforce the disputed terms.” (Zullo v. Superior Court (2011) 197 Cal.App.4th 477, 484.) 

Plaintiff argues that the Arbitration Agreement is procedurally unconscionable because it is a mandatory employment-based contract of adhesion. (Opp., pp. 4:6-5:23.) “When arbitration is a condition of employment, there is inherently economic pressure on the employee to accept arbitration. This alone is a fairly low level of procedural unconscionability.” (Cisneros Alvarez v. Altamed Health Services Corporation (2021) 60 Cal.App.5th 572, 591) Thus, the Agreement is minimally procedurally unconscionable. Accordingly, Plaintiff must show a strong degree of substantive unconscionability for the Arbitration Agreement to be found unenforceable.

Substantive Unconscionability 
Substantive unconscionability focuses on the terms of the agreement and whether those terms are so one-sided as to shock the conscience.” (Kinney v. United HealthCare Servs., Inc. (1999) 70 Cal.App.4th 1322, 1330.) 

Plaintiff argues that the Arbitration Agreement is substantively unconscionable because it is non-mutual. (Opp., pp. 5:27-8:17.) Upon review, the Agreement is not one-sided. The Arbitration Agreement mutually applies to all disputes between Plaintiff and Defendant. (Bailey Decl., Ex. A [“I and Compass Group USA, Inc. […] mutually agree to use binding individual arbitration as the sole and exclusive means to resolve all legal claims between us.”].) Plaintiff argues that the Agreement identifies 9 categories of claims covered by the Agreement which are claims that only an employee would bring. (Opp., p. 7:2-4.) However, the Agreement plainly identifies these categories as examples of covered claims, not as an exclusive or exhaustive list. (Bailey Decl., Ex. A.) Thus, Plaintiff fails to show a strong degree of substantive unconscionability. 

Accordingly, the Court finds that a valid arbitration agreement exists between the parties which covers the dispute at issue. 

The motion is GRANTED. 

RULING:
Defendant Compass Group USA, Inc.’s Motion to Compel Arbitration is GRANTED. 

The case is STAYED pending completion of arbitration.

Case No: 25NNCV07452 Trial Date:  Not yet set
Case Name: M.A., et al., v. Pasadena Unified School District, et al.

DEMURRER
[CCP § 430.10 subd. (e)]

Moving Party: Defendant, Pasadena Unified School District
Responding Party: Plaintiffs, M.A., a minor, by and through his Guardian ad Litem, Areg A., and A.A., a minor, by and through his Guardian ad Litem, Areg A.

RELIEF REQUESTED:
Sustain demurrer to First Amended Complaint. 

CAUSES OF ACTION: from First Amended Complaint
1) Negligence
2) Negligent Hiring, Supervision, or Retention of Employee
3) Failure to Perform Mandatory Duty 

SUMMARY OF FACTS:
The Complaint alleges that in October 2024, during school hours and on campus, M.A. was assaulted and battered by another student, J.H., in the presence of multiple students, causing visible bruising to his face, neck, and chest. The Complaint alleges that school staff notified Areg A., but downplayed the incident and failed to mention the severity of M.A.’s injuries. The Complaint alleges that when Areg A. arrived to pick M.A. up, he observed severe bruising across M.A.’s face, neck, and chest and immediately took M.A. to the emergency room. The school claims it implemented a safety plan; however, Plaintiffs allege that the plan was not implemented or enforced.

The Complaint alleges that in the weeks following the assault, the bullying escalated. On March 12, 2025, a student warned M.A. that J.H. intended to attack him that day. M.A. reported the warning to school staff, but Plaintiffs allege no efforts were made to investigate the threat or implement any meaningful safety measures. The Complaint alleges that later that same day, J.H. and several of his friends surrounded M.A. and his brother A.A. Fearing for their safety, the brothers attempted to flee the area. While trying to escape, M.A. fell and sustained a severe left distal femur fracture, while A.A. suffered head bruising and swelling. M.A. underwent emergency surgery to repair his leg. Since then, M.A. has suffered from persistent pain, nausea, dizziness, visual disturbances, and difficulty breathing, and is expected to require a second surgery for hardware removal required to repair the fracture. Both M.A. and A.A. have suffered severe emotional distress—including anxiety, hypervigilance, nightmares, and fear of returning to school—and have required trauma focused therapy.

ANALYSIS:
Procedural
Unless the Court has granted permission to file a longer memorandum, any reply or closing memorandum may not exceed 10 pages. (California Rules of Court, rule 3.113, subds. (d)-(e).) “A memorandum that exceeds the page limits of these rules must be filed and considered in the same manner as a late-filed paper.” (CRC, rule 3.113, subd. (g.).) Here, Defendant’s reply memorandum is 17 pages long. Because this is the first occurrence, the Court has exercised its discretion to consider the reply in its entirety. However, any future filings that exceed the applicable page limits will be treated as untimely filed. 

Substantive
A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747; CCP, § 430.10, subd. (e).) 

To sufficiently allege a cause of action, a complaint must allege all the ultimate facts—that is, the facts needed to establish each element of the cause of action pleaded. (Committee on Children’s Television, Inc. v. General Foods Corp. (1983) 35 Cal.3d 197, 212, superseded by statute as stated in Branick v. Downey Savings & Loan Assn. (2006) 39 Cal.4th 235, 242.) “[E]ach evidentiary fact that might eventually form part of the plaintiff’s proof need not be alleged.” (C.A. v. William S. Hart Union High School Dist. (2012) 53 Cal.4th 861, 872.) 

In testing the sufficiency of the cause of action, the demurrer admits the truth of all material facts properly pleaded. (Aubry v. Tri-City Hospital Dist. (1992) 2 Cal.4th 962, 966-67.) Courts read the allegations liberally and in context. (Taylor v. City of Los Angeles Dept. of Water and Power (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 1216, 1228, disapproved on other grounds, Jones v. Lodge at Torrey Pines Partnership (2008) 42 Cal.4th 1158, 1162.) A demurrer, however, “does not admit contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.” (Daar v. Yellow Cab Co. (1967) 67 Cal.2d 695, 713.)

Third Cause of Action, Failure to Perform Mandatory Duty 
Government Code section 815.6 provides, “Where a public entity is under a mandatory duty imposed by an enactment that is designed to protect against the risk of a particular kind of injury, the public entity is liable for an injury of that kind proximately caused by its failure to discharge the duty unless the public entity establishes that it exercised reasonable diligence to discharge the duty.”

Defendant demurs to the third cause of action on grounds that Plaintiffs have not identified a statutory basis for direct public entity liability or a specific mandatory, ministerial duty under Government Code section 815.6. The FAC alleges that Defendant is subject to mandatory statutory duties imposed by Education Code sections 44807 and 234.1. (FAC, ¶¶ 52-53.) 

Under the Education Code, “[e]very teacher in the public schools shall hold pupils to a strict account for their conduct on the way to and from school, on the playgrounds, or during recess.” (Education Code, § 44807.) Education Code section 234.1 requires the State Department of Education to assess whether local education agencies have adopted anti-discrimination policies and compliant procedures for investigating discrimination complaints. (Education Code, § 234.1.)

Defendant argues that Section 44807 imposes only a general duty to supervise students, which does not create Section 815.6 liability because statutes which require judgment or discretion do not constitute mandatory duties under Section 815.6. (Demurrer, pp. 4:23-5:11.) The Court is not persuaded by Defendant’s argument. First, Defendant’s interpretation is contrary to the plain language of the statute. The statute states that teachers “shall hold pupils to a strict account for their conduct.” (Education Code, § 44807.) The mandate to supervise is not discretionary. Moreover, there is ample case law confirming that Education Section 44807 constitutes a mandatory duty under Government Code section 815.6. (E.I. v. El Segundo Unified School District (2025) 111 Cal.App.5th 1267, 1281; C.A. v. William S. Hart Union High School Dist. (2012) 53 Cal.4th 861, 869; Lucas v. Fresno Unified School Dist. (1993) 14 Cal.App.4th 866, 872; Rodriguez v. Inglewood Unified School Dist. (1986) 186 Cal.App.3d 707, 720-721.) 

“While school districts and their employees have never been considered insurers of the physical safety of students, California law has long imposed on school authorities a duty to ‘supervise at all times the conduct of the children on the school grounds and to enforce those rules and regulations necessary to their protection. [Citations.];’ Education Code, § 13557 [now § 44807] ... The standard of care imposed upon school personnel in carrying out this duty to supervise is identical to that required in the performance of their other duties. This uniform standard to which they are held is that degree of care ‘which a person of ordinary prudence, charged with [comparable] duties, would exercise under the same circumstances.’ [Citations.] Either a total lack of supervision [citation] or ineffective supervision [citation] may constitute a lack of ordinary care on the part of those responsible for student supervision. Under section 815.2, subdivision (a) of the Government Code, a school district is vicariously liable for injuries proximately caused by such negligence.” (Lucas, supra,14 Cal.App.4th at p. 872.) 

In the reply, Defendant cites to Thompson v. Sacramento City Unified Dist. (2003) 107 Cal.App.4th 1352 for the proposition that “courts have repeatedly declined to construe Education Code section 44807 as creating a mandatory ministerial duty enforceable through Government Code section 815.6.” (Reply, p.12:15-17.) Thompson, however, does not support Defendant’s position. The Court in Thompson explicitly recognized that school districts owe a duty to exercise reasonable care in supervising students and that breach of this duty may support liability under the Government Code. (Thompson, supra, 107 Cal.App 4th at pp. 761-764.) 

Defendant next argues that it is immune from liability under Government Code section 820.2. (Demurrer, p. 5:12-19.) This argument is unavailing. Government Code section 820.2 provides: “Except as otherwise provided by statute, a public employee is not liable for an injury resulting from his act or omission where the act or omission was the result of the exercise of the discretion vested in him, whether or not such discretion be abused.” Immunity under Section 820.2 is reserved for basic policy decisions and does not extend to “lower level decisions that merely implement a basic policy already formulated.” (E.I., supra, 111 Cal.App.5th at pp. 1282-1283.) Here, the FAC alleges that Defendant failed to follow or enforce its bullying and misconduct policy, failed to intervene in the altercation, and failed to adequately supervise the school grounds. (FAC, ¶¶ 19, 22-24, 27, 54-57.) Such conduct does not qualify as “quasi-legislative policy-making” making entitled to immunity under Government Code section 820.2. (E.I., supra, 111 Cal.App.5th at p. 1285.)

Lastly, Defendant argues that the FAC fails to adequately plead causation. (Demurrer, p. 5:20-26.) Upon review of the allegations in the FAC, Plaintiffs sufficiently allege that their injuries were caused by Defendant’s failure to protect Plaintiffs from J.H.’s conduct. (FAC, ¶¶ 27, 51-58.) 

Thus, the Court finds that Plaintiffs have sufficiently alleged the cause of action for failure to perform mandatory duty. 

The demurrer is OVERRULED. 

RULING:
Defendant Pasadena Unified School District’s Demurrer is OVERRULED. 

Answer to be filed within 10 days.

MOTION TO DEEM RFAS ADMITTED
[CCP § 2033.280]

Moving Party: Plaintiff, Britecap Financial, LLC
Responding Party: Defendant, Jose Guel, an individual dba JRG Transportation

RELIEF REQUESTED:
Deem the requests for admissions propounded by Plaintiff on Defendant Jose Guel to be admitted. 

CAUSES OF ACTION: from Form Complaint 
1) Common Count
2) Common Count 
3) Common Count 
4) Breach of Contract

SUMMARY OF FACTS:
Britecap Financial, LLC (“Plaintiff”) alleges that on June 18, 2025, Plaintiff and Jose Guel, an individual dba JRG Transportation, (“Defendant”) entered into a business loan agreement. Plaintiff alleges that Defendant breached the agreement and is indebted to Plaintiff in the amount of $46,561,98. 

ANALYSIS:
A discovering party may make a motion to deem as admitted any unanswered requests for admission or any requests answered in a late or unverified response. (CCP § 2033.280, subd. (b); CCP, § 2033.240, subd. (a) [RFA responses must be signed by responding party under oath]; Appleton v. Superior Court (1988) 206 Cal.App.3d 632, 636 [unsworn response to RFAs is treated like no response].) These requests are not automatically deemed admitted; the discovering party must make the motion. (CCP, § 2033.280, subd. (b).)

To establish this ground, a movant must show:
(1) Proper service (CCP, § 2033.070 [requests for admission]);
(2) Expiration of the deadline for the initial response 30 days after service or on date agreed to by parties (CCP, § 2033.250, subds. (a), (b) [requests for admission]); and
(3) No timely response. (CCP, § 2033.280, subd. (b) [requests for admission])

A court must deny a motion to compel initial discovery where the discovery sought is outside the scope of discovery. (CBS, Inc. v. Superior Court (1968) 263 Cal.App.2d 12, 19; CCP § 2017.010 [scope of discovery].)

The RFAs were served via mail on February 20, 2026. (Blanda Decl. ¶ 2, Ex. 1) The deadline to respond was 30 days after service, plus five days for service via mail. (CCP § 1013 subd. (a).) Thus, responses were due on March 25, 2026. As of the date of the hearing on this motion, there is no evidence before the Court that Defendant has responded to the discovery requests. 

Thus, the Motion is GRANTED.

RULING:
Plaintiff’s Motion to Deem Requests for Admissions Admitted is GRANTED. The admissions are hereby deemed ADMITTED as truth.
