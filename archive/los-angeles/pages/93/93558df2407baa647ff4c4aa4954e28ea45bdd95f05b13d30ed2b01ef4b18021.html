[TENTATIVE] ORDER RE: ATTORNEYS ELAN DARVISH AND STEVEN ZELIG’S MOTIONS TO BE RELIEVED AS COUNSEL

I.                   INTRODUCTION

This action arises from alleged inappropriate sexual misconduct in administering a

COVID-19 vaccine.

On September 8, 2022, Plaintiff Maisy Kendrick (“Plaintiff”) initiated this action.

On January 26, 2023, Plaintiff filed a first amended complaint (“FAC”) against Defendants USMD Direct, Inc. (“USMD”), Fawad Zafar-Khan (“Zafar-Khan”), Daniel Garcia (“Garcia”) and Cynthia Meza (“Meza”) alleging causes of action for (1) Deceit, (2) Negligent Misrepresentation, (3) Deceit, (4) Negligent Misrepresentation, (5) Civil Rights Violation, (6) Civil Rights Violation, (7) Sexual Battery, (8) Sexual Battery, (9) Battery, (10) Battery, (11) Breach of Fiduciary Duty, (12) Privacy Violations, (13) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress, (14) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress, (15) Negligence, and (16) Professional Negligence.

            On May 18, 2026, Attorney Elan Darvish filed the instant motion to be relieved as counsel for Plaintiff. On May 22, 2026, Attorney Steven Zelig also filed the instant motion to be relieved as co-counsel for Plaintiff.

            No opposition was filed to either motion. 

II.                LEGAL STANDARD

An attorney in an action may be changed at any time before or after judgment or final determination upon the order of the court, upon the application of either client or attorney, after notice from one to the other. (Code Civ. Proc., § 284(2)). Rules of Court, rule 3.1362 requires:

1.      Notice of motion and motion to be directed to the client (made on the Notice of Motion and Motion to be Relieved as Counsel Civil form (MC-051)); 

2.      A declaration stating in general terms, and without compromising the confidentiality of the attorney client relationship, why a motion under Code of Civil Procedure section 284(2), is brought instead of filing a consent under CCP §284(1) (made on the Declaration in Support of Attorney's Motion to Be Relieved as Counsel Civil form (MC-052)); 

3.      Service of the notice of motion and motion and declaration on all other parties who have appeared in the case; and 

4.      The proposed order relieving counsel (prepared on the Order Granting Attorney's Motion to Be Relieved as Counsel Civil form (MC-053)). 

 The motion should be denied if withdrawal would prejudice the client.  (Rules Prof. Conduct, Rule 3-700(A)(2); Weil & Brown, Cal. Prac. Guide: Civ. Pro. Before Trial, 9:385.2 (2008); 1 Witkin Cal. Pro., Attorneys §72 (2008).) The motion should be denied if it will cause undue delay in the proceeding or cause injustice.  (Mandell v. Superior Court, (1977) 67 Cal.App.3d 1, 4.) The determination whether to grant or deny an attorney’s motion to withdraw as counsel of record lies within the sound discretion of the trial court, having in mind whether such withdrawal might work an injustice in the handling of the case. (Lempert v. Superior Court (2003) 112 Cal.App.4th 1161, 1173.)  A breakdown in the attorney-client relationship (or personality clash) is grounds for withdrawal.  (Estate of Falco (1987) 188 Cal.App.3d 1004, 1014.) An attorney is not limited to withdrawing from a case for cause and may withdraw when withdrawal can be accomplished without undue prejudice to the client. (Ramirez v. Sturdevant (1994) 26 Cal.Rptr.2d 554, 559.)¿¿¿ 

III.             DISCUSSION

Here, Attorney Elan Darvish on behalf of The Darvish Firm and Attorney Steven Zelig on behalf of Bay Cities Law Group each have provided the Court with all the required documents. They each have filed Judicial Council Form MC-051 (Notice of Motion and Motion), MC-052 (Declaration), and MC-053 (Proposed Order). They have also served the required documents upon all parties in the action. They seek to be relieved as counsel on the grounds that there has been a breakdown in the attorney-client relationship, making continued representation to be unreasonably difficult. This is proper ground for withdrawal. (See Estate of Falco (1987) 188 Cal.App.3d 1004, 1014 [a breakdown in the attorney-client relationship is grounds for allowing the attorney to withdraw].)

IV.             CONCLUSION

Thus, the motions to be relieved as counsel are GRANTED.

I.                   INTRODUCTION

This is a Song-Beverly action.

On November 27, 2023, Plaintiff Kevin R. Carter (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against Defendant General Motors LLC (“Defendant” or “GM”), alleging causes of action for (1) Violation of Subdivision (d) of Civil Code Section 1793.2, (2) Violation of Subdivision (b) of Civil Code Section 1793.2, (3) Violation of Subdivision (a)(3) of Civil Code Section 1793.2, and (4) Breach of the Implied Warranty of Merchantability.

On October 15, 2025, Plaintiff filed a memorandum of costs.

On January 15, 2026, the Court denied Defendant’s motion to tax costs.

On January 30, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion for attorney fees.

On June 11, 2026, Defendant filed an opposition.

On June 17, 2026, Plaintiff filed a reply.

II.                LEGAL STANDARD

If the buyer prevails in an action under [the Song-Beverly Act], the buyer shall be allowed by the court to recover as part of the judgment a sum equal to the aggregate amount of costs and expenses, including attorney's fees based on actual time expended, determined by the court to have been reasonably incurred by the buyer in connection with the commencement and prosecution of such action. 

 

(Civ. Code, §1794, subd. (d).) 

The prevailing party has the burden of showing that the requested attorney fees are reasonable. (Robertson v. Fleetwood Travel Trailers of California, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 785, 817-818 (Robertson).) The party seeking attorney fees “is not necessarily entitled to compensation for the value of attorney services according to [his] own notion or to the full extent claimed by [him].” (Levy v. Toyota Motor Sales, USA, Inc. (1992) 4 Cal.App.4th 807, 816, brackets in original and internal citations omitted.) “’A trial court may not rubber stamp a request for attorney fees, but must determine the number of hours reasonably expended.’ [Citation.]” (Morris v. Hyundai Motor America (2019) 41 Cal.App.5th 24, 38.) 

The lodestar approach is the prevailing method for calculating attorney fees under Song-Beverly Act claims. (Robertson, supra, 144 Cal.App.4th at pp. 818-819.) “The reasonableness of attorney fees is within the discretion of the trial court, to be determined from a consideration of such factors as the nature of the litigation, the complexity of the issues, the experience and expertise of counsel and the amount of time involved. [Citation.] The court may also consider whether the amount requested is based upon unnecessary or duplicative work. [Citation.]” (Wilkerson v. Sullivan (2002) 99 Cal.App.4th 443, 448.) “The courts repeatedly have stated that the trial court is in the best position to value the services rendered by the attorneys in his or her courtroom [citation], and this includes the determination of the hourly rate that will be used in the lodestar calculus. [Citation.]” (569 E. Cnty. Boulevard LLC v. Backcountry Against the Dump, Inc. (2016) 6 Cal.App.5th 426, 436–437.) ¿

 

III.             EVIDENTIARY OBJECTIONS

Plaintiff objects to certain portions of the Declaration of Kyle Roybal submitted in

opposition to the instant motion. The Court overrules each of the objections.

IV.             DISCUSSION

Plaintiff is seeking an attorney’s fees award of $33,397.90, which includes (1) $14,605.50 in fees to Prestige Legal Solutions, (2) $2,789.89 in costs to Prestige Legal Services, and (3) $16,002.50 in fees to the Castruita Law Firm.

A.    Timeliness

Defendant first contends that the Court should deny the instant motion on the ground that

it is untimely.

California Rules of Court, Rule 3.1702(b)(1) states that “[a] notice of motion to claim attorney's fees for services up to and including the rendition of judgment in the trial court-including attorney's fees on an appeal before the rendition of judgment in the trial court-must be served and filed within the time for filing a notice of appeal under rules 8.104 and 8.108 in an unlimited civil case or under rules 8.822 and 8.823 in a limited civil case.”

Rule 8.104 provides that a notice of appeal must be filed on or before the earliest of: (A) 60 days after the superior court clerk serves on the party filing the notice of appeal a document entitled “Notice of Entry” of judgment or a filed-endorsed copy of the judgment, showing the date either was served; (B) 60 days after the party filing the notice of appeal serves or is served by a party with a document entitled “Notice of Entry” of judgment or a filed-endorsed copy of the judgment, accompanied by proof of service; or (C) 180 days after entry of judgment.

            Here, on June 13, 2025, Plaintiff filed the Notice of Settlement. On September 30, 2025, the Court ordered the case dismissed without prejudice pursuant to the parties’ oral stipulation. (See Sept. 30, 2025 Minute Order.) The Court then served the parties with the order of dismissal on this same date. Defendant maintains that the order of dismissal is considered a “Notice of Entry,” which would trigger the 60-day clock, making the instant motion untimely. The Court agrees.

The newly decided Hatlevig v. Gen. Motors LLC (2026) 118 Cal.App.5th 644 (Hatlevig) is instructive. There, the Court dealt with the same issue of what exactly “started the clock running” for purposes of filing an attorney fee motion in a Song-Beverly action. (Id. at p. 649.) In Hatlevig, the parties reached a settlement whereby the plaintiff would surrender the vehicle and dismiss the action, and GM would pay the plaintiff $100,00.00. (Id. at p. 646.) The plaintiff’s counsel notified the trial court of the settlement at a trial readiness conference on June 2, 2023, when the Court ordered a dismissal to be filed within 45 days. (Ibid.) On July 11, 2023, the court clerk generated a “NOTICE OF DISMISSAL BY COURT,” which stated the “CASE [WOULD BE] DEEMED DISMISSED WITHOUT PREJUDICE ON 08/15/2023” unless a judgment or dismissal was filed or a party appeared ex parte and showed good cause why the case should not be dismissed. The clerk mailed the notice to the parties on July 12, 2023. Neither event specified in the notice occurred by August 15, 2023, and the plaintiff filed a motion for attorney fees on August 31, 2023. (Id. at pp. 646-647.) The trial court denied the motion on the ground that it was untimely. (Id. at p. 647.) The Hatlevig Court affirmed.  (Id. at p. 651.)

First, the Hatlevig Court stated that “[a] voluntary dismissal, though not appealable, starts the clock running on the time to move for attorney fees when the dismissal concludes the litigation and triggers a right to fees.” (Id. at p. 649.) “A voluntary dismissal is ‘effectively a “judgment”’ within the meaning of the rule prescribing the time to appeal. (Citations.) Thus, ‘the clock starts to run [on the time to move for attorney fees] from either the service of notice of entry of judgment or dismissal (starting a 60-day clock), or if no such notice is given, the entry of judgment or dismissal (starting a 180-day clock).’ (Citation.)” (Ibid.) While no formal order of dismissal was filed until June 17, 2024, the Hatlevig Court found that the August 15, 2023 notice of would-be dismissal is considered a dismissal for purposes of triggering the time the plaintiff had to move for attorney’s fees because the 45-day period had come and gone, and “[w]hen a case settles, ‘a dismissal of the action generally follows as a matter of law.’ (Citations.)” (Id. at pp. 649-650.) Having had most 180 days following the August 15, 2023 date, the plaintiff failed to timely file his motion for attorney fees. (Id. at p. 650.)

The case here is more straightforward. Here, on September 30, 2025, the Court ordered the case dismissed following Plaintiff’s filing of a Notice of Settlement. That same day, the court clerk then gave notice of the dismissal to all parties. There is no denying that the September 30, 2025 date began the clock for Plaintiff to file his motion for attorney fees. (See above.) However, the Court finds that the 60-day clock, rather than the 180-day clock, was triggered. The “60–day time limit commences to run at notice of entry of judgment or dismissal.” (Sanabria v. Embrey (2001) 92 Cal.App.4th 422, 429 [finding a motion for attorney fees filed more than 60 days after service of written notice of entry of dismissal to be untimely].) The court clerk here unequivocally provided notice of dismissal on September 30, 2025. Sixty days after September 30, 2025 was December 1, 2025. Thus, Plaintiff filing the instant motion on January 30, 2026 is considered untimely.

Lastly, the Court does not find that good cause exists to extend Plaintiff’s deadline to file the instant motion. (Cal. Rules of Court, Rule 3.1702(d) [“For good cause, the trial judge may extend the time for filing a motion for attorney's fees in the absence of a stipulation or for a longer period than allowed by stipulation.”].) Plaintiff has provided no justifiable explanation as to why he did not file the motion for attorney fees in a timely fashion. That Defendant entered into a settlement wherein it agreed that Plaintiff is the prevailing party entitled to fees is not a relevant consideration to the Court’s analysis, as GM in Hetlevig also entered into a settlement where it agreed that Plaintiff is the prevailing party entitled to fees. The Hetlevig Court still found the plaintiff’s motion untimely.

Plaintiff’s motion is denied. However, Plaintiff is entitled to his costs as the Court held in its ruling on January 15, 2026.

V.                CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, Plaintiff’s motion for attorney’s fees is DENIED. However,

Plaintiff is entitled to recover the entirety of his claimed costs, which is $2,789.89. Costs are payable within sixty (60) days of this order.

Dept. 733

I.                   INTRODUCTION

On December 6, 2023, Plaintiff Kerry Iaconelli (“Plaintiff”) filed a complaint against LA Grand Hotel (“LA Grand”), New World New Age I LLC (“New World”), City of Los Angeles (“City”), County of Los Angeles (“County”), California Department of Housing and Community Development (“DHCD”), State of California (“State”), Los Angeles Homeless Services Authority (“LAHSA”), Housing Authority of the City of Los Angeles (“LA Housing Authority), and Desirae Doe (“Desirae”), alleging causes of action for (1) Assault, (2) Battery, (3) Intentional Infliction of Emotional Distress, (4) Negligence, and (5) Negligent Hiring, Supervision and Retention.

The complaint alleges the following. On or about December 21, 2022 Plaintiff was a guest and/or resident at LA Grand Hotel through the Project Room Key program, a program created during the Covid-19 Pandemic which assisted homeless persons with acquiring housing in hotels and motels to curb the spread of Covid-19. As Plaintiff was in a restroom within the subject premises, Defendants’ employees and/or security guards entered the restroom and forced her to remove her clothing to be searched for a rash and/or similar condition. As Plaintiff was being searched, Defendant Doe, suddenly and without warning, physically attacked and assaulted Plaintiff with such force that Plaintiff suffered injuries and damages. (Compl., ¶ 18.) Project Roomkey was created and operated through collaboration between the City of Los Angeles, the State of California, the County of Los Angeles, and moving party, Los Angeles Homeless Services Authority.

On February 6, 2026, Plaintiff filed an amendment to add The Salvation Army, Inc. (“Salvation Army”) as DOE 2. On February 13, 2026, Plaintiff filed for dismissal of Salvation Army from the action, which was granted on February 18, 2026.

On May 22, 2026, LAHSA filed the instant motion for leave to file a cross-complaint.

No opposition was filed.

II.                LEGAL STANDARD

The court may grant leave to file a cross-complaint if the failure to plead a cause of action was the result of oversight, inadvertence, mistake, neglect, or other cause. (Code Civ. Proc., § 426.50.) Where the proposed cross-complaint arises out of the same transaction as plaintiff’s claim, the court must grant leave to file the cross-complaint as long as defendant is acting in good faith. (Ibid.; see Silver Organizations Ltd. v. Frank (1990) 217 Cal.App.3d 94, 99 [the Court may not “exercise discretion” in the denial of a motion to file a compulsory cross-complaint].) Judicial policy favors resolution of all disputed matters between the parties in the same lawsuit and discretion will usually be exercised liberally to permit amendment of the pleadings. (See Nestle v. Santa Monica (1972) 6 Cal.3d 920, 929.) 

“‘Bad faith,’ is defined as ‘[t]he opposite of “good faith,” generally implying or involving actual or constructive fraud, or a design to mislead or deceive another, or a neglect or refusal to fulfill some duty or some contractual obligation, not prompted by an honest mistake ..., but by some interested or sinister motive[,] ... not simply bad judgment or negligence, but rather ... the conscious doing of a wrong because of dishonest purpose or moral obliquity; ... it contemplates a state of mind affirmatively operating with furtive design or ill will. [Citation.]’ [Citations.]” (Silver Organizations, supra, 217 Cal.App.3d at p. 100.)

A party may obtain leave of court to file a cross-complaint at any time during the course of a lawsuit, and leave may be granted in the interest of justice. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 426.50, 428.50; see also Sidney v. Superior Court (1988) 198 Cal.App.3d 710, 718.) “To the extent practicable, all supporting memorandums and declarations must be attached to the notice of motion.” (Cal. Rules of Court Rule 3.1113(j).)

III.             DISCUSSION

LAHSA moves the Court for leave to file a cross-complaint against Salvation Army and Desirae for 1) equitable indemnity; 2) breach of contract; 3) express indemnity; 4) declaratory relief; 5) apportionment; and 5) contribution.

Here, the proposed cross-complaint is premised on compulsory counterclaims, given that the claims in the proposed cross-complaint are based on the same Incident at the Subject Property, specifically regarding LAHSA’s pursuant to a proposed indemnity clause contained in the Project Roomkey contract between The Salvation Army and LAHSA. (Barkinskaya Decl., Ex, A; see Code Civ. Proc., § 428.10(b); Sattinger v. Newbauer (1954) 123 Cal.App.2d 365, 369 [“The fundamental reason for allowing cross-complaints is to have a complete determination of a controversy among the parties in the one action, thus avoiding circuity of action”].)

The Court also finds that LAHSA acted in good faith. Counsel provides LAHSA seeks only to protect itself and to secure its right to attribute liability to all appropriate potentially responsible parties, pursuant to a claimed contract with Salvation Army. (Barkinskaya Decl., ¶ 5.) LAHSA has also requested that counsel for The Salvation Army and Plaintiff stipulate to allow LAHSA to file the proposed cross-complaint, to no avail. (Id. ¶ 7.)

Lastly, the Court notes that the motion is unopposed.

IV.             CONCLUSION

Based on the foregoing, LAHSA’s motion for leave to file a cross-complaint is GRANTED.

LAHSA is ordered to file the proposed Cross-Complaint within five (5) days of this order.
