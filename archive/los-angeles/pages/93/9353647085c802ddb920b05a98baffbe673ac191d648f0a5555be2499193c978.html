     PLAINTIFFS’ MOTION FOR TRIAL PREFERENCE

 

 

I.                Background

 

On December 10, 2025, Plaintiffs Lorenzo and Anita Torres filed their complaint for personal injury alleging Mr. Torres developed mesothelioma from direct exposure to asbestos from his time working as a longshoreman at docks and shipyards in Southern California between 1969 and 2018. Plaintiffs also allege that Mr. Torres was exposed to asbestos performing automotive repair work throughout his lifetime. Plaintiffs now move for trial preference, arguing Mr. Torres is over the age of 70, has a substantial interest in the action as a whole, and that his health is such that preference is necessary to prevent prejudicing Plaintiffs’ interest in the litigation. Alternatively, Plaintiffs contend they present clear and convincing evidence that Mr. Torres’s illness raises a substantial medical doubt of his survival beyond six months.

On June 9, 2026, Defendant Metalclad Insulation, LLC (“Metalclad”) opposed, arguing trial preference will impede its due process rights. On the same date, Defendant Mechanical Drives & Belting fka Los Angeles Rubber Company, Inc. (“Mechanical Drives”) opposed, requesting that the court make trial setting orders and continue the hearing date of this motion to a date after Mr. Torres’s deposition. On June 10, 2026, Defendants American President Lines, LLC and Matson Navigation Company, Inc. filed a conditional non-opposition. On the same date, Defendant Lykes Bros., Inc. (“Lykes Bros”) opposed, arguing that Plaintiffs failed to demonstrate preference was necessary to prevent prejudicing Plaintiffs’ interests in litigation.

On June 16, 2026, Plaintiffs replied.

 

The court finds Plaintiffs satisfied their burden to present sufficient evidence that trial preference is necessary to prevent prejudicing Plaintiffs’ interest in the litigation. The motion for trial preference is granted.

 

II.             Discussion

 

A.    Legal Standard

 

A party who is over 70 years old may petition the court for a preference for trial date, which the court shall grant if it makes both of the following findings: (1) the party has a substantial interest in the action as a whole; and (2) the health of the party is such that a preference is necessary to prevent prejudicing the party’s interest in the litigation. (Code Civ. Proc., § 36, subd. (a).) An affidavit submitted in support of a motion for preference under this subdivision may be signed by the attorney for the party seeking preference based upon information and belief as to the medical diagnosis and prognosis of any party. (Id., § 36.5.)

 

In addition, the court may grant a motion for trial preference accompanied by clear and convincing medical documentation concluding that one of the parties suffers from an illness or condition raising substantial medical doubt of survival of that party beyond six months and satisfying the court that the interests of justice will be served by granting the preference.  (Code Civ. Proc. § 36, subd. (d).)

 

“Upon the granting of such a motion for preference, the court shall set the matter for trial not more than 120 days from that date and there shall be no continuance beyond 120 days from the granting of the motion for preference except for physical disability of a party or a party’s attorney, or upon a showing of good cause stated in the record.”  (Id., § 36, subd. (f).)  “Any continuance shall be for no more than 15 days and no more than one continuance for physical disability may be granted to any party.” (Ibid.)

 

B.    Analysis  

 

Plaintiff Lorenzo Torres is 80 years old and was diagnosed with mesothelioma in September 2025. (Plaintiffs’ Index, Exh. A at ¶6.) Mr. Torres presented himself to his pulmonologist, Dr. Ricard Huynh, reporting exertional shortness of breath and chronic inflammatory changes in his right lung. (Id. at ¶5.) Mr. Torres had previously undergone surgery in 2020 for hydropneumothorax management and again in 2022 to place a catheter to drain fluid from his chest. (Id. at ¶4.) By 2025, a CT scan of Mr. Torres’s chest revealed new pleural masses in his right lung which a biopsy revealed to be malignant mesothelioma. (Id. at ¶6.) Dr. Huynh diagnosed Mr. Torres with stage IV pleural mesothelioma of the epithelioid subtype and recommended that Mr. Torres undergo chemotherapy and immunotherapy to slow disease progression. (Id. at ¶8.) On January 1, 2026, Mr. Torres underwent another CT scan, which revealed masses and lesions in his right chest that were increasing in size. (Id. at ¶9.) Mr. Torres began immunotherapy in February 2026 and tolerated it well with mild abdominal discomfort and abdominal ascites. (Id. at ¶10.)

Dr. Huynh opines that “Mr. Torres suffers from advanced malignant mesothelioma of the pleura, an incurable disease. Mr. Torres’s treatment of malignant mesothelioma is ongoing, and he continues to follow with my clinic at regular intervals for palliative care. His symptoms since diagnosis have included but are not limited to shortness of breath, cough with minimal sputum production, and hemoptysis. He has followed his doctors’ recommendations and has made every effort to alleviate his symptoms and enhance his quality of life. His symptoms will continue to progress as his disease worsens, eventually resulting in his death…It is my opinion, based on my years of experience working with patients diagnosed with mesothelioma, and having personally reviewed Mr. Torres’s pathology reports and medical records as stated above, that to a reasonable degree of medical certainty, there is substantial doubt that he will survive beyond six months from the date of this declaration. This opinion is based on my training as a physician; my considerable experience treating cancer patients; my experience treating patients suffering from Mr. Torres’s specific disease of mesothelioma; my specialized knowledge regarding the typical course of this cancer in patients like Mr. Torres; my knowledge, through review of Mr. Torres’s reports, of the significance of his signs and symptoms. I hold this opinion to a reasonable degree of medical certainty.” (Plaintiffs’ Index, Exh. A at ¶¶11, 13.)

Mr. Torres’s medical records from the time of his diagnosis show that CT scans taken in August 2025 revealed right pleural thickening, small pleural calcification, new nodules and masses in his right chest, a mass in his liver, lesions in his right chest, and small calcified nodules in his left chest. (Plaintiffs’ Index, Exh. A, TMP 9.) Specifically, the scans revealed a “ 6.6 x 5.2 cm bilobed mass protruding into the right lower lobe posteriorly, adjacent to contiguous slightly more cephalad 5.7 x 3.6 cm mass eroding into the right posterior eighth rib, and a 4.7 x 4.0 cm medial right basilar mass at the level of the diaphragmatic hiatus…a 4.4 x 4.0 cm mass along the right anterior hemidiaphragm and liver dome protruding into the right middle lobe…hepatic cysts…[and] pleural calcifications.” (Id.) By October 2025, Mr. Torres’s physician planned to refer him to Torrance Memorial’s palliative care group and recommended that he look into his affairs and think about his end-of-life wishes. (Id., TMCC 9.) Mr. Torres could theoretically have been a candidate for surgery, but only if he had a very good response to treatment. (Id.) The records also show that Mr. Torres’s mesothelioma was metastatic by November 2025. (Id., TMCC 2.) A CT scan from January 2026 showed multiple large masses in Mr. Torres’s right chest and liver and lesions along his ribs. (Id., TMCC 49.) Specifically, the CT scan revealed a “large mass 89 x 64 mm” which previously measured “69 x 67 mm” and a liver mass measuring “60 x 56 mm” that was previously “59 x 57 mm.” (Id.) By January 2026, Mr. Torres’s physician referred him back to palliative care. (Id., TMCC 43.) Mr. Torres’s most recent records from March 2026 show that he continues to undergo palliative care and that he is tolerating treatment well. (Id., TMCC 28-TMCC 32.)

In opposition, Lykes Bros argues that Plaintiffs have not demonstrated that preference is necessary to prevent prejudicing their interests in litigation: “While Defendant does not dispute that Plaintiff is 80 years old and has a substantial interest in the action, Plaintiffs have not demonstrated that the current pace of litigation is such that Plaintiff’s interests are being prejudiced. Notably section 36(a) requires more than a showing of age and illness; it requires a showing that preference is “necessary to prevent prejudicing the party’s interest in the litigation.” (Code Civ. Proc. § 36(a), emphasis added.)…Plaintiffs have provided no evidence that the current pace of discovery or the litigation schedule is prejudicing Plaintiff’s interests.” (Lykes Bros. Opp. at p.4.) Lykes Bros further asserts that the Mr. Torres testified in May 2026 that he is doing better, exercising, helping with household chores, and that his tumor has not grown. (Id.) Lykes Bros argues that this testimony “demonstrates that Plaintiff’s current condition does not create the kind of urgent prejudice to his litigation interests that would mandate preference under section 36(a).” (Id.) The remaining Defendants make no substantive arguments in their opposition about Plaintiffs’ burden under Code of Civil Procedure, section 36, subdivisions (a) and (d).

In reply, Plaintiffs argue that Defendants failed to advance medical evidence to dispute his condition. Plaintiffs also argue that Defendants’ arguments that they have not had an opportunity to conduct expert discovery or that preference will impair their due process rights have no merit.

Code of Civil Procedure, section 36, subdivision (d) requires Plaintiffs to show by clear and convincing medical documentation that Mr. Torres suffers from an illness or condition raising substantial medical doubt of his survival beyond six months. Additionally, the court must be satisfied that the interests of justice will be served by granting the preference. The record here supports Plaintiffs’ assertion that there is substantial medical doubt of Mr. Torres’s survival beyond six months. Mr. Torres was initially diagnosed with stage IV mesothelioma. (Plaintiffs’ Index, Exh. A at ¶8.) Between August 2025, Mr. Torres’s CT scans showed large tumors, nodules, and lesions in his chest and liver. (Id., TMP 9.) By January 2026, the large tumors had grown by one or two centimeters compared to August 2025. (TMCC 49.) These changes caused Mr. Torres’s physicians to recommend palliative care. (Id., TMCC 43.) Although Mr. Torres’s most recent medical records show that he is tolerating his current treatments well and that his symptoms are well-managed, his mesothelioma has already advanced to the point where he is receiving palliative, end-of-life care. (Id., TMCC 28-TMCC 32.) Even if Mr. Torres testified to feeling better and seeing no additional tumor growth in May 2026, Mr. Torres’s disease is still advanced according to his physicians. Lykes Bros fails to advance expert testimony refuting Dr. Huynh’s opinion that there is substantial medical doubt that he will survive beyond six months. Mr. Torres’s advanced age coupled with the severity of his disease supports a finding that there is substantial medical doubt that he will survive beyond six months. Accordingly, the court finds that Plaintiffs meet the requirements of Code of Civil Procedure, section 36, subdivision (d).

Defendants request accommodations for discovery and motion deadlines, status conferences, and the trial setting conference. The parties appear amenable to further discussion of the appropriate deadlines for discovery and law and motion matters prior to the submission of a proposed trial setting order. If necessary, the court will review any jointly submitted trial setting order and make rulings on any disputed deadlines at the hearing on the proposed trial setting order.  

 

III.           Conclusion

Plaintiffs’ motion for trial setting preference is granted. The parties are to meet and confer regarding appropriate conditions for a trial setting order. The court sets trial for October 19, 2026 at 9 a.m. The final status conference is set for October 5, 2026 at 9 a.m. The court sets a status conference re: trial setting order for July 15, 2026 at 9 a.m. Plaintiffs are ordered to give notice.

     ALLIED FLUID PRODUCTS CORP.’S MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT OR, IN THE ALTERNATIVE, SUMMARY ADJUDICATION

 

 

I.                Background

On December 30, 2025, Plaintiffs Robert and Mary Campbell filed their complaint for personal injury alleging Mr. Campbell developed mesothelioma from occupational exposure to asbestos-containing construction products, electrical products, and pumps from the 1950s to the 1990s.

On April 30, 2026, Defendant Allied Fluid Packing & Supply, Inc. (“Allied”) moved for summary judgment or adjudication on the grounds that Plaintiffs lack and cannot reasonably obtain evidence that Mr. Campbell was exposed to asbestos-containing products for which it was responsible.

On June 4, 2026, Plaintiffs opposed, arguing that Allied failed to shift its burden on summary judgment to Plaintiffs. Plaintiffs also argue that their responses to written discovery contained facts establishing Mr. Campbell’s exposure to Allied’s asbestos-containing materials.

On June 12, 2026, Allied replied, arguing that its moving papers shifted the burden to Plaintiffs and that Plaintiffs’ discovery responses were devoid of facts establishing Mr. Campbell was exposed to asbestos-containing products for which Allied was responsible.

Allied satisfied its initial burden to show Plaintiffs lack and cannot reasonably obtain needed evidence to support their causes of action against it. Plaintiffs’ discovery responses were factually devoid as to whether any asbestos-containing products for which Allied was responsible exposed Mr. Campbell to asbestos. Plaintiffs fail to present affirmative evidence to support their claims. Therefore, the court grants Allied’s motion for summary judgment. Allied’s motion for summary adjudication is moot.

 

II.             Discussion

 

A.    Legal Standard

 

A defendant seeking summary judgment must “conclusively negate[] a necessary element of the plaintiff’s case, or . . . demonstrate[] that under no hypothesis is there a material issue of fact that requires the process of trial.”  (Guz v. Bechtel Nat. Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 317, 334.)  To show that a plaintiff cannot establish an element of a cause of action, a defendant must make the initial showing “that the plaintiff does not possess, and cannot reasonably obtain, needed evidence.”  (Aguilar v. Atlantic Richfield Co. (2001) 25 Cal.4th 826, 854.)  “The defendant may, but need not, present evidence that conclusively negates an element of the plaintiff’s cause of action.  The defendant may also present evidence that the plaintiff does not possess, and cannot reasonably obtain, needed evidence – as through admissions by the plaintiff following extensive discovery to the effect that he has discovered nothing.”  (Id. at p. 855.) “ ‘If plaintiffs respond to comprehensive interrogatories seeking all known facts with boilerplate answers that restate their allegations, or simply provide laundry lists of people and/or documents, the burden of production will almost certainly be shifted to them once defendants move for summary judgment and properly present plaintiff’s factually devoid discovery responses.’ ”  (Weber v. John Crane, Inc. (2006) 143 Cal.App.4th 1433, 1440.)

B.    Causation

In asbestos litigation, a plaintiff is required to show causation. A plaintiff must show “some threshold exposure to the defendant's defective asbestos-containing products” and must further establish in “reasonable medical probability” that a particular exposure or series of exposures was a “legal cause” of his injury, i.e., a substantial factor in bringing about the injury. (Rutherford v. Owens-Illinois, Inc. (1997) 16 Cal.4th 953, 982.)  “[T]he proper analysis is to ask whether the plaintiff has proven exposure to a defendant's product, of whatever duration, so that exposure is a possible factor in causing the disease and then to evaluate whether the exposure was a substantial factor.” (Lineaweaver v. Plant Insulation Co. (1995) 31 Cal.App.4th 1409, 1416.)  

Where a plaintiff’s allegations pertain to asbestos as an intended ingredient of a product the plaintiff was exposed to occupationally, the plaintiff is required to present evidence “defendant's product was definitely at his work site and that it was sufficiently prevalent to warrant an inference that plaintiff was exposed to it…” (Turley v. Familian Corp. (2017) 18 Cal.App.5th 969, 980 [Citing Lineaweaver, supra, 31 Cal.App.4th at p. 1420.].) ¿ 

1.      Defendant’s Burden: Factually Devoid

Allied moves for summary judgment on the grounds that Plaintiffs lack and cannot reasonably obtain evidence that Mr. Campbell was exposed to an asbestos-containing product for which it is responsible. Specifically, Allied argues that Plaintiffs’ discovery responses failed to include specific facts identifying any product supplied by Allied which could have exposed Mr. Campbell to asbestos: “Plaintiffs’ discovery responses generally contend that Plaintiff was exposed to asbestos by several different methods, but never has any factual information been provided that discusses how Plaintiff was allegedly exposed to an Allied supplied product. Plaintiffs provide no evidence, factual information, or documents that show an Allied supplied product ever exposed Plaintiff. Plaintiffs’ responses provide no specific responsive information as to location of the exposure, the dates, the type of work performed that would have exposed the Plaintiff or even the brand name or manufacturer of the asbestos containing product. Plaintiffs provide only generalized non-specific statements that Plaintiff was exposed Allied. Plaintiffs failed to identify a witness to support their claims that Plaintiff was exposed by Allied, nor did they identify a specific document that establishes Plaintiff was exposed by Allied. Plaintiffs provided a laundry list of unnamed persons and documents without any specific reference on how these persons or documents actually establish that Allied supplied an asbestos containing product that exposed the Plaintiff…Importantly, Plaintiff is the only named witness against Allied and Plaintiff admitted he had no personal information regarding any alleged exposure by Allied. Plaintiffs, therefore, have factually devoid written discovery responses and the only witness identified against Allied has no personal knowledge regarding Allied.” (Motion at pp.9-10.)

               In opposition, Plaintiffs argue that Allied failed to shift its burden because it failed to show that it served comprehensive discovery on Mr. Campbell’s asbestos exposure. Specifically, Plaintiffs argue that Allied failed to provide evidence that it served requests for production on Plaintiffs regarding Mr. Campbell’s asbestos exposure. (Motion at p.9.) Plaintiffs also argue that they identified specific witnesses and documents which establish that Mr. Campbell was exposed to asbestos-containing products that Allied supplied. (Id. at p.10.) Plaintiffs further argue that Allied failed to ask Plaintiffs about Mr. Campbell’s work with asbestos-containing gaskets or packing supplied by Allied. (Id. at p.12.)

            In reply, Allied argues it was not required to provide Plaintiffs’ responses to requests for production. (Reply at p.5.) Allied also reiterates its arguments that Plaintiffs’ discovery responses failed to establish that Mr. Campbell was exposed to an asbestos-containing product for which Allied was responsible.

            Allied relies on Plaintiffs’ responses to standard interrogatories, Plaintiffs’ responses to special interrogatories, and Mr. Campbell’s deposition testimony to support its motion.

            When Allied asked Plaintiffs to “state all facts that support” their contention that Mr. Campbell was “exposed to an ASBESTOS-CONTAINING PRODUCT supplied, sold, distributed, or marketed by” Allied, Plaintiffs responded as follows:

            “Plaintiffs refer Defendant to the deposition of Robert Campbell taken in this matter. Robert Campbell was a career electrician from 1961 until his retirement in 2004. Additionally, he was an Electrician’s Mate in the United States Navy from 1957-1960. He was diagnosed with mesothelioma on October 31, 2025.

The Plaintiff worked at Sherman Electric and assisted in the construction of California State University – north hall and East Wing and auditorium for approximately 1.5 years. He also worked on new construction of the County Art Museum on Wilshire Blvd for approximately two years. He next worked on the new construction at Ernest Lawrence Middle school on 10100 Variel Ave, Chatsworth, CA for about one year. At Shugart Electric, the plaintiff worked at Los Angeles Times newspaper building remodeling an existing building at First and Spring in Downtown LA. At Coronet Electric, he worked building a new mental hospital for about 8-9 months. He also worked building United Silver in City of Commerce for approximately 2-3 months. At Electroc Construction he worked on the CBS building in Studio City. In 1969, Plaintiff begins working for the City of Los Angeles and is assigned to work at LAX. He works on many buildings at LAX as an electrician. In the mid 1970s Plaintiff was assigned to the public works/general services in Van Nuys. He participated in the maintenance of city owned buildings. He worked in office buildings and libraries owned by the city. In the late 1970s and early 1980s, the Plaintiff worked in the LA Civic Center in the maintenance division.

He worked in LA City Hall, City Hall East, City Hall South buildings and Parker Center. In the mid 1980s, the plaintiff worked in supervision in the General Services construction division. Plaintiff does not personally know which products contained asbestos or asbestiform fibers. He worked with and around many construction products, including fire rated ceiling tiles, drywall, joint compound, floor tiles, electrical products. He recalls Lo-Tone and Simpson fire rated ceiling tiles, Kaiser Gypsum, Georgia Pacific and Hamilton joint compounds and texturing, electrical products made by Square D, Cutler Hammer, Eaton, GE, Westinghouse, Allen Bradley, Crouse Hinds, Chico X and other electrical products. Plaintiff recalls working with Crouse Hinds electrical equipment and installing and removing Chico X from electrical conduits

Based upon information and belief from his attorneys’ preliminary investigation, it is believed that many of the products Plaintiff worked with and around would have contained asbestos. The exposure period at issue in this case is approximately 1957 to 2004.

Since the early 1950s, Defendant Allied Packing & Supply, Inc. was a supply house in San Francisco and Wilmington. Defendant marketed, sold, supplied and distributed various types of asbestos-containing gasket and packing material manufactured by various manufacturers to various industrial and commercial users in California. Per the May 8, 1995 deposition of Ulrick Motzfeldt, former warehouseman then “partner” in the company since 1963, Allied sold material including from Johns-Manville, Sacomo, Raybestos Manhattan, U.S. Rubber (Uniroyal), and Garlock.In interview, Howard Day, co-founder of Allied Packing, recalled that at the company’s inception (circa 1956), Allied carried Sacomo, U.S. Rubber and Raybestos. Mr. Day also recalled that Allied sold gaskets and packing private shipyards, refineries and other users at least from 1956 to 1957 when Day sold his interest in Allied to his partner, George Heiner (deceased). Plaintiff was unaware of the dangers of defendant’s asbestos-containing products…

(Romeo Decl., Exh. E at pp.1-4.)

            Plaintiffs also identified Mr. Campbell’s responses to standard interrogatories, employment records, medical records, and Allied’s responses to discovery in other matters as documents supporting their contention that Mr. Campbell was exposed to an asbestos-containing product supplied, sold, distributed, or marketed by Allied. (Id. at pp.5-6.)

            Mr. Campbell testified at his deposition that he did not recall the names Allied Fluid Products Corporation, Allied Packing & Rubber Incorporated, Allied Packing & Supply Incorporated, or Allied Supply Company. (Romeo Decl., Exh. F at 163:7-20.) Mr. Campbell did not associate any products or materials with Allied and had no information about whether Allied acted with a conscious disregard for his safety or intended to harm him. (Id. at 164:1-25.)

            Allied meets its burden to show that Plaintiffs’ discovery responses are devoid of facts supporting their contention that Mr. Campbell was exposed to an asbestos-containing product for which it was responsible. Plaintiffs’ discovery responses were required to provide a factual basis to show products for which Allied was responsible were present at Mr. Campbell’s worksite and that these products contained asbestos. (Turley, supra, 18 Cal.App.5th at p. 980; Collin, supra, 228 Cal.App.4th at p. 593.) 

            Plaintiffs’ interrogatory responses fail to state the factual basis for their contention that Allied supplied asbestos-containing products to Mr. Campbell’s worksite. Plaintiffs’ interrogatory responses set forth where Mr. Campbell worked, which products he worked with, and that Allied sold and supplied various asbestos-containing gaskets and packing materials to various commercial users in California. (Romeo Decl., Exh. E at pp.1-4.) The responses at most establish that Mr. Campbell worked in Los Angeles City buildings between 1957 and 2004 and that Allied supplied asbestos-containing products in California in the same time period. Mr. Campbell could not recall the names of the allied entities and could not associate any products with Allied.

Plaintiffs’ discovery responses fail to establish that Allied supplied asbestos-containing products to Mr. Campbell’s worksite. McGonnell v. Kaiser Gypsum Co., Inc. (2002) 98 Cal.App.4th 1098 (“McGonnell”) is instructive. There, a decedent alleged he developed mesothelioma after being exposed to Kaiser Gypsum’s joint compound. (McGonnell, supra, 98 Cal.App.4th at p. 1101.) The decedent testified at his deposition that he had never heard of Kaiser Gypsum, worked with its products, or worked near others using its products. (Id.) The evidence also showed that the Kaiser Gypsum cement product at issue did not contain asbestos after 1973 in Northern California and 1976 in Southern California. (Id. at p.1102.) The plaintiffs advanced evidence that the cement product could have been used at the decedent’s worksite in the 1970s. (Id.) The plaintiffs appealed an order granting Kaiser Gypsum’s motion for summary judgment, arguing that it was within the realm of possibility that the decedent might have cut into a wall with Kaiser joint compound during his 24 years of employment. (Id. at p.1105.) The plaintiffs presented evidence that invoices existed showing sale of the joint compound to a contractor for a project at the decedent’s worksite a few years before decedent began his employment. (Id.) The Court of Appeal rejected the plaintiff’s arguments and, citing¿Lineaweaver,¿supra,¿31 Cal.App.4th¿at p. 1421, explained that speculation that the decedent might have cut into a wall that might have contained Kaiser Gypsum’s joint compound “creates only a ‘dwindling stream of probabilities that narrow into conjecture.’” (McGonnell, supra, 98 Cal.App.4th at p. 1106.)

            Here, the connection between Allied and Mr. Campbell’s worksite is supported by even less evidence than the scenario presented in McGonnell. In McGonnell, there was at minimum evidence that Kaiser Gypsum had delivered products to the plaintiff’s worksite at some point before he began his employment. Here, there is no evidence that Allied supplied Mr. Campbell’s worksites with any products, let alone asbestos-containing products. Plaintiffs’ discovery responses showing that Allied sold asbestos-containing in California at the same time Mr. Campbell worked in Los Angeles City buildings are not sufficient to establish that Allied supplied products to Mr. Campbell’s worksites. The argument it was possible for Allied to have supplied products to Mr. Campbell’s worksites amounts to speculation. Even if Mr. Campbell worked with the same brands that Allied sold and distributed, there is no evidence that Allied sold any products to Mr. Campbell’s employer or that Allied was the sole distributor of these brands. The discovery record therefore contains no evidence that products supplied by Allied were present at Mr. Campbell’s worksite.

Accordingly, the discovery responses give rise to an inference that Plaintiffs lack and cannot reasonably obtain evidence to support their assertion that Mr. Campbell was exposed to an asbestos-containing product for which Allied was responsible. The burden shifts to Plaintiffs to present sufficient evidence to create a triable issue of material fact as to Mr. Campbell’s exposure to an asbestos-containing product for which Allied is responsible.

2.      Plaintiffs’ Burden

Plaintiffs fail to present sufficient evidence to create a triable issue of material fact as to whether Mr. Campbell was exposed to asbestos by a product for which Allied was responsible. Plaintiffs first argue that there is no evidence that Allied served comprehensive discovery on whether Mr. Campbell was exposed to asbestos by products supplied by Allied because Allied provided no evidence that it served requests for production on Plaintiffs. However, summary judgment is proper where a defendant presents evidence that a plaintiff does not possess and cannot reasonably obtain evidence to support their claims against it. (Aguilar, supra, 25 Cal.4th at p.855.) It is reasonable to infer that a plaintiff lacks and cannot reasonably obtain such evidence where the plaintiff’s response to comprehensive interrogatories seeking all known facts is factually devoid. (Weber, supra, 143 Cal.App.4th at p.1440.) Here, Allied propounded comprehensive special interrogatories on Plaintiffs asking them to “state all facts that support” their contention that Mr. Campbell was “exposed to an ASBESTOS-CONTAINING PRODUCT supplied, sold, distributed, or marketed by” Allied. (Romeo Decl., Exh. E at pp.1-4.) As discussed, Plaintiffs’ responses were devoid of facts establishing that Allied supplied products to Mr. Campbell’s worksite. It is reasonable to infer that Plaintiffs lack and cannot reasonably obtain evidence to support their claims against Allied even without evidence that Allied propounded requests for production on Plaintiffs.

Plaintiffs next argue that Allied failed to shift its burden because the responses identified witnesses and documents which Allied failed to address in its moving papers. Plaintiffs point to their responses to Allied’s special interrogatories numbers 3 and 4, which identify Allied employees as witnesses and an extensive list of documents, including Allied’s responses to discovery in other matters. However, Plaintiffs fail to explain how these witnesses or documents support their claims against Allied. Plaintiffs make no argument that the witnesses or documents  identified in their responses have or include evidence that Allied supplied products to Mr. Campbell’s worksite. As it stands, the evidence in the discovery record fails to establish that asbestos-containing products supplied by Allied were present at Mr. Campbell’s worksite.

Plaintiffs further argue that Allied never asked Mr. Campbell about his work with gaskets or packing during his deposition. Specifically, Plaintiffs argue: “Allied did not ask Plaintiff Robert Campbell at his deposition if he ever worked with any gaskets or packing at any time, nor did any other defendant…This matters because Allied admittedly did not ever supply asbestos-containing Allied-branded products, but rather supplied asbestos-containing gasket and packing material manufactured by U.S. Rubber, Raybestos-Manhattan, Johns-Manville, Sacomo, and Garlock, and sold these products in packaging bearing these manufacturers’ logos…In other words, Mr. Campbell certainly would know if he ever worked with gaskets and packing (if he had been asked), and might have been able to identify the manufacturers of same (had he been asked), but he wasn’t.” (Opp. at p.12.) Even if Allied had asked about Mr. Campbell’s work with asbestos-containing gaskets and packing material, Mr. Campbell’s responses would have at most established that he worked with those products at his worksite, not that Allied supplied these products. Mr. Campbell testified that he could not recall the names of the Allied entities and did not associate Allied with any products. (Romeo Decl., Exh. F at 163:7-20, 164:1-25.) Thus, even if he had information about the products he worked with, Mr. Campbell had no information about whether Allied supplied these products. Plaintiffs’ argument that Allied failed to shift its burden is thus without merit.

Plaintiffs rely on additional excerpts from Mr. Campbell’s deposition to argue that Mr. Campbell identified former co-workers William Older, Art Smith, and Art Prost as witnesses who may have information regarding Mr. Campbell’s exposure to products supplied by Allied. (Opp. at p.13.) Mr. Campbell testified at his deposition that these witnesses had information about whether he worked with Square D equipment. (Stewart Decl., Exh. 1 at 154:18-157:20 [“Q: Okay. Are you aware of any witnesses with information about the work you did with or around Square D components or equipment? A: I’m sure my coworkers could identify me working on their equipment…A: William Older…Q: And where did Mr. Older work with you? A: We worked for the City of Los Angeles with the Building Maintenance Division, and also the City of Los Angeles General Services Construction Division…A:…Art Smith…Q: Okay. And where did you work with Mr. Smith? A: I worked with Mr. Smith in Los Angeles Civic Center in the Maintenance Division and also in the Los Angeles General Services Construction Division…Q: Okay. Anyone else that you can recall besides Mr. Older and Mr. Smith who might have information about your work with or around Square D products? A: Another name, Art Prost…Q: And where did you work with Mr. Prost? A: I worked with Mr. Prost in the General City of Los Angeles General Service Construction Division.”].)

Mr. Campbell testified that Mr. Older, Mr. Smith, and Mr. Prost could testify about his work with Square D products, not that they could testify as to whether Allied supplied products to his worksite. Mr. Campbell’s testimony at most establishes where these witnesses worked and that they have knowledge of Mr. Campbell’s work with Square D products. There is no evidence that these witnesses have information about Allied and whether it supplied products to Mr. Campbell’s worksites. As discussed, Allied successfully shifted its burden on summary judgment. It is now Plaintiffs’ burden to raise a triable issue of material fact over whether Allied supplied asbestos-containing products to Mr. Campbell’s worksite. Plaintiffs had ample time since Mr. Campbell’s deposition in April 2026 to investigate these witnesses, determine whether they had information about Allied, and depose them or request a continuance to depose them. Plaintiffs failed to present any other affirmative evidence that Allied supplied asbestos-containing products to Mr. Campbell’s worksite.

Accordingly, Plaintiffs fail to meet their burden to raise a triable issue of material fact over whether Allied supplied asbestos-containing products to Mr. Campbell’s worksite. Therefore, Allied’s motion for summary judgment is granted.

III.           Conclusion

Allied satisfied its initial burden to show Plaintiffs lack and cannot reasonably obtain needed evidence to support their causes of action against it. Plaintiffs’ discovery responses were factually devoid as to whether any asbestos-containing products for which Allied was responsible exposed Mr. Campbell to asbestos. Plaintiffs fail to present affirmative evidence to support their claims. Therefore, the court grants Allied’s motion for summary judgment. Allied’s motion for summary adjudication is moot.
