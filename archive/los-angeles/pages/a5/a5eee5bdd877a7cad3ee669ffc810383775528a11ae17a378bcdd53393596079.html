TENTATIVE RULING

 

DEPARTMENT

207

HEARING DATE

September 10, 2026

CASE NUMBER

21SMCV00498

MOTION

Motion for Reconsideration

MOVING PARTY

Defendant Jake Seal

OPPOSING PARTY

Plaintiff Myriad Pictures, Inc.

 

BACKGROUND

 

This case arises from a dispute concerning the production and distribution of the fourth movie in the “Jeepers Creepers” franchise. 

 

On June 18, 2024, Plaintiff Myriad Pictures, Inc. (“Plaintiff”) filed the operative Third Amended Complaint (“TAC”) against Defendants Infinity Films Holdings, LLC (“Infinity”); Michael Ohoven (“Ohoven”); Brandon Farm, LLC (“Brandon Farm”); Jake Seal (“Seal”); Screen Media Ventures, LLC (“SMV”); Chicken Soup for the Soul Entertainment, Inc. (“CSS”)[1]; Independent Frame, LLC (“Independent”); Orwo Film Distribution, LLC; PVS Studios, LLC (“PVS”); David Fannon (“Fannon”); Seth Needle (“Needle”); and the Estate Mark Damon (“Damon”), alleging five causes of action for (1) breach of contract; (2) breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing; (3) intentional interference with contractual relations; (4) fraudulent concealment; and (5) declaratory relief.

 

On February 24, 2022, Cross-Complainants Orwo Film Studios, LLC; Orwo Film Distribution, LLC (the “Orwo” Defendant above); PVS Studios, LLC; JCP4 IP, LLC; and Jake Seal (“Cross-Complainants”) filed the operative Second Amended Cross-Complaint against Plaintiff; Kirk D’Amico; Infinity; CSS; Ohoven; Brandon Farm; and SMV; alleging six causes of action as follows:

 

1.     Intentional Interference with Contract

2.     Intentional Interference with Prospective Economic Advantage

3.     Negligent Interference with Prospective Economic Advantage

4.     Equitable Indemnity

5.     Contribution

6.     Declaratory Relief

 

Essentially, Plaintiff and Cross-Complainants each allege they have contracts purporting to confer the exclusive rights to finance and distribute the same films, and they argue the opposing side interfered with their own contract to finance, produce, and distribute the films.

 

On May 28, 2025, the Court granted issue sanctions against Seal, the two Orwo entities, and PVS (the “Seal Parties”), holding:

 

The Court hereby deems the allegations alleged in the Third Amended Complaint to be established with respect to the third cause of action for intentional interference with contractual relations against Defendants Seal, Orwo, and PVS and the fourth cause of action for fraudulent concealment against Defendant Seal. Defendants Seal, Orwo, and PVS shall be prohibited from opposing these claims against them, from raising defenses to these claims, or from introducing evidence or testimony in support of their own defenses to these claims.

 

(Minute Order, May 28, 2025.)

 

On February 24, 2026, the Court granted Myriad’s motion for summary adjudication of the first, second, and third causes of action alleged in the Second Amended Cross-Complaint, finding, “both sides cannot have valid contracts for the same exclusive rights to finance and distribute the same films.  As such, if one party’s contract is deemed valid, the other party’s contract cannot be valid.”  (Minute Order, Feb. 24, 2026 at p. 7.)  Further, the Court held, “by virtue of the issue sanctions order, Myriad’s contract was deemed valid, and the Seal Parties’ knowing offer to finance and distribute Jeepers Creepers Reborn improperly undercut Myriad’s rights thereunder, causing Myriad to incur damages.  As such, Seal’s agreement was wrongful, invalid, and/or unlikely to produce an economic advantage, and Myriad’s interference thereof was not wrongful, but rather a lawful exercise of Myriad’s rights.”  (Id. at p.89.)  Further, the Court noted that the Seal Parties did not timely oppose the motion to create any triable issues of material fact.  (Ibid.)

 

At the April 29, 2026 bench trial, the Court further awarded Myriad economic damages in the amount of $2,117.905.

 

On July 27, 2026, the Court granted in part Plaintiff’s unopposed motion for punitive damages, awarded punitive damages in the amount of $3,388,648, and vacated Phase II of the non-jury trial.

 

Seal now moves for reconsideration of the Court’s July 27, 2026 order pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 1008, subdivision (a).  Plaintiff opposes the motion. 

 

LEGAL STANDARD - RECONSIDERATION

 

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 1008, subdivision (a), “[w]hen an application for an order has been made to a judge, or to a court, and refused in whole or in part, or granted, or granted conditionally, or on terms, any party affected by the order may, within 10 days after service upon the party of written notice of entry of the order and based upon new or different facts, circumstances, or law, make an application to the same judge or court that made the order, to reconsider the matter and modify, amend, or revoke the prior order.  The party making the application shall state by affidavit what application was made before, when and to what judge, what order or decisions were made, and what new or different facts, circumstances, or law are claimed to be shown.”  (Code Civ. Proc., § 1008, subd. (a).)  Where the statutory requirements are met, reconsideration should be granted; upon reconsideration, however, the court may simply reaffirm its original order.  (Corns v. Miller (1986) 181 Cal.App.3d 195, 202.) 

 

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

 

            Seal requests judicial notice of the following:

 

1. United Kingdom Income Tax Act 2007, section 157, governing the requirement that an individual seeking Enterprise Investment Scheme relief subscribe for qualifying shares in the issuing company. A true and correct copy is attached as Exhibit 1.

 

2. United Kingdom Income Tax Act 2007, section 181, governing the trading requirement applicable to the issuing company. A true and correct copy is attached as Exhibit 2.

 

3. United Kingdom Income Tax Act 2007, section 183, governing the requirement that the qualifying business activity be carried on by the issuing company or a qualifying subsidiary. A true and correct copy is attached as Exhibit 3.

 

4. HM Revenue & Customs Venture Capital Schemes Manual, VCM8560, Example 3, constituting official guidance issued by HM Revenue & Customs concerning application of the Enterprise Investment Scheme requirements to film production businesses operating through a parent company and wholly owned production subsidiary. A true and correct copy is attached as Exhibit 4.

 

5. United Kingdom Data Protection Act 2018, section 170, governing the knowing or reckless obtaining, disclosure, procurement, or retention of personal data without the consent of the data controller, subject to the statutory defenses and exceptions stated therein. A true and correct copy is attached as Exhibit 5.

 

            Courts may take judicial notice of “the law of an organization of nations and of foreign nations and public entities in foreign nations.”  (Evid. Code, § 452, subd. (f).)  Therefore, the Court takes judicial notice of the above exhibits.

 

ANALYSIS

 

            Seal first challenges the statements in the July 27 order indicating the motion was unopposed.  Specifically, Seal argues he did file an opposition, but it was late.

 

            Upon review of the record, the Court notes that Seal apparently filed an opposition the afternoon of Friday, July 24.  As it takes 3 to 5 court days for electronically filed documents to appear on the Court’s docket, and tentative rulings are generally posted at least 24 hours in advance of the hearing, the opposition was not actually received by the Court before the posting of the tentative ruling, which the Court ultimately adopted as the final order of the Court after noting “Defendant’s opposition filed on 07/24/2026 is untimely.”  As such, the Court’s statement was accurate when written, and remained accurate, as clarified by the notation that the July 24, 2026 opposition was untimely.

 

            The substance of Seal’s opposition argues that a UK tax law newly discovered by Seal’s counsel demonstrates that Seal could not have owned or financed Jeepers Creepers 4 or 5 in his individual capacity.  Seal’s argument essentially rests on the same premise the Court rejected on credibility grounds at trial – namely, Seal sought to exculpate himself from any and all liability due to creative accounting practices, while refusing to provide straightforward answers about who did finance the franchise or how he personally pays for his own daily living expenses.  Nor does Seal adequately explain why he failed to raise this UK tax law defense at trial or at any other point prior to the Friday afternoon before the hearing on Plaintiff’s motion for punitive damages.

 

            Instead, Seal argues that he “learned of Plaintiff’s motion only three days before the hearing because counsel had not transmitted the motion or May 4 order,” implying that Seal’s counsel did not receive adequate notice of the motion for punitive damages.  (Motion at p. 3.)  However, the Siegelman declaration clarifies it was actually Seal’s counsel who failed to transmit the order and motion to his own client.  (Siegelman Decl. ¶ 2.)  Thus, there is no deficiency of notice warranting reconsideration of the July 27 order or consideration of the belated opposition.

 

            Ultimately, neither counsel’s belated discovery of the UK tax law, nor counsel’s belated forwarding of case related documents or motions to his client constitute new facts, circumstances, or law warranting reconsideration.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

 

            For all the reasons stated above, the Court denies Seal’s Motion for Reconsideration.   

 

            Seal shall provide notice of the Court’s ruling and file the notice with a proof of service forthwith.

Case Number: 24SMCV01214    Hearing Date: September 10, 2026    Dept: 207

TENTATIVE RULING

 

DEPARTMEMT

207

HEARING DATE

September 10, 2026

CASE NUMBER

24SMCV01214

MATTER

Request for Default Judgment

 

This case arises from a motor vehicle collision. 

 

On March 14, 2024, Plaintiff Michelle M. May filed suit against Defendants Fieldworks, LLC, Dakota Taylor, and Does 1 to 10.  Although the Complaint purports to allege three causes of action for (1) motor vehicle negligence, (2) general negligence, and (3) strict liability.  No cause of action pages are attached to the Complaint. 

 

Taylor and Does 1 to 10 were voluntarily dismissed on August 27, 2025, leaving only Defendant Fieldworks, LLC (“Fieldworks” or “Defendant”).

 

The proof of service indicates Defendant was personally served with a copy of the summons and complaint on December 2, 2024 and personally served with a copy of the statement of damages on August 25, 2025.  Default was entered against Defendant on September 22, 2025. 

 

Plaintiff now requests default judgment against Defendant in the amount of $43,269.98, representing special damages in the amount of $43,269.98.

 

Defendant has submitted a declaration, indicating that Plaintiff has refused to file an Amended Complaint in conformance with the Court’s prior order denying Plaintiff’s request for default judgment on the grounds that Plaintiff’s operative complaint does not specify the damages sought and the Court cannot award damages in excess of those demanded in the Complaint.  Defendant requests the Court sanction Plaintiff pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure Section 128.5 for unnecessarily delaying the proceedings and dismiss the action pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 583.410, subdivision (a) for delay in prosecution.

 

A.    Default Judgment

 

            As the Court previously explained, Plaintiff’s Complaint does not specify the amount of damages sought.  (See Compl.)  In general, the Court cannot award damages that are in excess of what is pled in the Complaint. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 580, subd. (a) [“The relief granted to the plaintiff, if there is no answer, cannot exceed that demanded in the complaint”]; Levine v. Smith (2006) 145 Cal.App.4th 1131, 1136-1137 [“when recovering damages in a default judgment, the plaintiff is limited to the damages specified in the complaint”].)   

 

            Plaintiff also served a statement of damages on August 25, 2025.  However, the Court finds that service of a Statement of Damages in lieu of alleging specific damages in the operative complaint is not apt.  Generally, “A statement of damages cannot be relied on to establish a plaintiff's monetary damages on the defendant's default, except in a personal injury or wrongful death case.”  (Cal. Judges Benchbook, Civ. Proc. Before Trial § 16.16, p. 1924, citations omitted.)   In Sporn v. Home Depot USA, Inc., the Court of Appeal determined:

 

Defendant also attacks the judgment under section 425.11, contending that plaintiff was required to serve it with a statement of damages and failed to do so. Section 425.11 applies to an action for personal injuries or wrongful death and was passed concurrently with the amendment to section 425.10 that prohibits stating the amount demanded in the complaint filed in such an action. Section 425.11 was enacted to satisfy the due process requirement that defendants be apprised of their exposure before a default may be taken.  But here the complaint, which was not limited to personal injuries and did not claim wrongful death, expressly apprised defendant of the amount demanded. A statement of damages would have been superfluous and was not required under these circumstances.

 

(Sporn v. Home Depot USA, Inc. (2005) 126 Cal.App.4th 1294, 1302 [Plaintiff’s claims of negligence, gross negligence, fraudulent misrepresentation and fraud centered on the theft of his identity] [cleaned up].)  Here, Plaintiff alleges only property damage, not personal injuries or wrongful death.

 

            Therefore, because the operative Complaint does not specify the amount of damages sought and this deficiency cannot be cured by serving a statement of damages, the Court cannot enter default judgment for any amount of damages on the current operative Complaint.

 

            As such, the Court cannot enter default judgment as requested on the current Complaint.

 

B.    Defendant’s Requests

 

            “The entry of a default terminates a defendant's rights to take any further affirmative steps in the  litigation until either its default is set aside or a default judgment is entered.”  (Devlin v. Kearny Mesa AMC/Jeep/Renault, Inc. (1984) 155 Cal.App.3d 381, 385.  Further, the entry of default “[c]uts off the defendant's right to file pleadings and motions (other than a motion to set aside default under § 473), and it also cuts off the defendant's right to notices and the service of pleadings or papers.”  (Bailey v. Citibank, N.A. (2021) 66 Cal.App.5th 335, 347, emphasis added.) 

 

            Here, Defendant is currently in default and thus, it lacks standing to request sanctions.  Further, Defendant has not demonstrated compliance with the safe harbor provision to request sanctions under Code of Civil Procedure section 128.5.  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 128.5, subd. (f).)  Even further, courts may not dismiss actions for delay in prosecution unless (1) service was not made within two years after the action commenced against the defendant [not applicable here]; (2) the action is not brought to trial within three years [not yet applicable, as the Complaint was filed March 14, 2024]; or (3) the action is not timely brought to trial after a new trial is granted [not applicable here].  (Code Civ. Proc., § 583.420, subd. (a).)

 

            Accordingly, the Court denies Defendant’s requests for sanctions.

 

CONCLUSION

 

            For the foregoing reasons, Plaintiff’s request for default judgment is denied without prejudice.   

 

            Further, the Court sets an Order to Show Cause on April 7, 2027 at 8:30 AM in Department 207 regarding the dismissal of the action due to Plaintiff’s delay in prosecution of the action and/or in bringing the action to trial within three years of commencing the action under Code of Civil Procedure sections 583.410 and 583.420, subdivision (a)(2)(A), and failure to request entry of default judgment under California Rules of Court, rule 3.110(h) [Failure to obtain default judgment]].  Any opposition to the Order to Show Cause shall be filed no later than 9 court days before the scheduled hearing.

 

            The Court may dismiss the action at the time of the Order to Show cause hearing if counsel or the parties fail to appear, or fail to provide good cause for the delay in prosecution of the action; in bringing the action to trial, and/or in obtaining a default judgment.

 

            The Clerk shall provide notice of the Court’s order.

MOTION

Motion for Order Authorizing Substituted or Alternative Service of Deposition Subpoena for Production of Business Records to Nonparty Jose Flores

MOVING PARTY

Defendant Deividas Strole

OPPOSING PARTY

none

 

MOTION

 

This case arises from a dispute between employer and employees in which the employees are alleged to have engaged in a false work order scheme to defraud their employer. 

 

On February 18, 2025, Plaintiff Roque & Mark Co., Inc. (“Plaintiff” or “Roque”) brought suit against Defendants Deividas Strole (“Strole”); Kestutis Dvarvydis (aka Kestas Dvarvydis) (“Dvarvydis”); and Tomas Chevykinas (“Chevykinas”) (together, “Defendants”) alleging seven causes of action for (1) fraud; (2) conversion; (3) breach of fiduciary duty; (4) aiding and abetting; (5) breach of fiduciary duty; (6) open book account; and (7) declaratory relief.

 

Defendant Strole moves for an order authorizing substituted or alternative service of a deposition subpoena for production of business records to nonparty Jose Flores.  The motion is unopposed.

 

ANALYSIS

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 2020.220 requires personal service of a deposition subpoena on a nonparty.  Further, as Defendant acknowledges, “a deposition subpoena that has not been personally served in compliance with section 2020.220 imposes no obligations on a nonparty deponent.”  (In re Marriage of Moore (2024) 102 Cal.App.5th 1275, 1288.)

 

            Yet, Defendant provides no legal authority for the Court to deviate from the applicable service provision.  “In general, the moving party must carry the initial burden of informing its opponent and the court of the specific basis for its motion.  If the rule were otherwise, then the party opposing the motion would have to try to guess, and then refute, every possible basis for the motion, which would always be inefficient and would often produce arbitrary results.”  (People v. Williams (1999) 20 Cal.4th 119, 129, citation omitted.)  To wit, the California Rules of Court provides in relevant part:  “The memorandum must contain a statement of facts, a concise statement of the law, evidence and arguments relied on, and a discussion of the statutes, cases, and textbooks cited in support of the position advanced.”  (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1113(b), emphasis added; see also Chavez v. Netflix, Inc. (2008) 162 Cal.App.4th 43, 52 [trial court was justified in denying the motion on procedural grounds due to  deficient memorandum in support of motion].) 

 

            Additionally, the Second District Court of Appeal in affirming the trial court’s denial of post-trial motions on procedural grounds, observed:  “Rule 3.1113 rests on a policy-based allocation of resources, preventing the trial court from being cast as a tacit advocate for the moving party's theories by freeing it from any obligation to comb the record and the law for factual and legal support that a party has failed to identify or provide.”  (Quantum Cooking Concepts, Inc. v. LV Associates, Inc. (2011) 197 Cal.App.4th 927, 934.) 

 

Instead of providing the Court any legal authority for the relief Defendant seeks, Defendant merely argues that Defendant has been unsuccessful at personally serving Mr. Flores at an address confirmed to be his home, despite multiple attempts.  That is insufficient.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDERS

 

Finding Defendant has not provided legal authority for the relief sought, the Court denies Defendant’s motion without prejudice.

 

Defendant shall provide notice of the Court’s ruling and file the notice with a proof of service forthwith.

Case Number: 26SMCV03642    Hearing Date: September 10, 2026    Dept: 207

TENTATIVE RULING

 

DEPARTMENT

207

HEARING DATE

September 10, 2026

CASE NUMBER

26SMCV03642

MOTIONS

Demurrer and Motion to Strike Portions of Complaint

MOVING PARTY

Defendant Discovery Talent Services, LLC

OPPOSING PARTY

Plaintiff Lab Rat Productions, Inc.

 

MOTION

 

This case arises from an alleged breach of a pay-or-play agreement to narrate spinoffs of the television series Deadliest Catch.

 

On July 1, 2026, Plaintiff Lab Rat Productions, Inc. f/s/o Mike Rowe (“Plaintiff”) filed suit against Defendant Discovery Talent Services, LLC (“Defendant” or “Discovery”) alleging a single cause of action for breach of contract.

 

Discovery now demurs to the Complaint on the grounds that it fails to state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action, pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 430.10, subdivision (e).  In the alternative, Defendant moves to strike any and all requests for relief concerning episodes of Deadliest Catch: Bloodline as time barred and any “materially different” internationally aired episodes of the original Deadliest Catch series as inadequately pleaded.

 

Plaintiff opposes the motion and Discovery replies.

 

REQUEST FOR JUDICIAL NOTICE

 

            Discovery requests judicial notice of the following:

 

Exhibit A: A true and correct copy of Amendment 4D to the Deal Memo for Lab Rat Productions, Inc. f/s/o Mike Rowe for Overall Personal Services / “Bering Sea Gold,” “Deadliest Catch” & Additional Voiceover Services Outside the Term, dated July 31, 2020 (“Amendment 4D”)

 

Exhibit B: A true and correct copy of the following web address, as captured on August 10, 2026, showing the air dates of Deadliest Catch: Discovery GO, Deadliest Catch series page, https://go.discovery.com/show/deadliest-catch-discovery

 

Exhibit C: A true and correct copy of the following web address, as captured on August 10, 2026, showing the first episode air date of Deadliest Catch: Bloodline: TVmaze, Deadliest Catch: Bloodline Season 1 – Episode List, https://www.tvmaze.com/seasons/107300/deadliest-catch-bloodline-season-1/episodes

 

Exhibit D: A true and correct copy of the following web address, as captured on August 10, 2026, showing the last episode air date of Deadliest Catch: Bloodline: TVmaze, Deadliest Catch: Bloodline Season 3 – Episode List, https://www.tvmaze.com/seasons/137135/deadliest-catch-bloodline-season-3/episodes

 

Exhibit E: A true and correct copy of the following web address, as captured on August 10, 2026, showing the episode air dates of Deadliest Catch: The Viking Returns: Discovery GO, Deadliest Catch: The Viking Returns series page, https://go.discovery.com/show/deadliest-catch-the-viking-returns-discovery-atve-us

 

Exhibit F: A true and correct copy of the following web address, as captured on August 10, 2026, announcing the planned premiere of Deadliest Catch: Northern Edge: Warner Bros. Discovery, Warner Bros. Discovery’s Cable Portfolio Primes Programming Slate With Over 3,000 Premiere Hours Including 225 New and Returning Series (May 13, 2026), https://press.wbd.com/us/media-release/warner-bros-discoverys-cable-portfolioprimes-programming-slate-over-3000-premiere-0?language_content_entity=en&

 

            Discovery seeks judicial notice pursuant to Evidence Code section 452, subdivision (h) which provides that courts may take judicial notice of “[f]acts and propositions that are not reasonably subject to dispute and are capable of immediate and accurate determination by resort to sources of reasonably indisputable accuracy.”

 

            The Court is unconvinced that a private party’s contract and/or website is a “source of reasonably indisputable accuracy” warranting judicial notice undersection 452, subdivision (h).

 

            In support of its request that the Court take judicial notice of the parties’ contract, Discovery cites to JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A. (2013) 214 Cal.App.4th 743, 754.  But there, the agreement at issue was between JP Morgan Chase and the FDIC, a governmental agency, and the agreement was posted to the FDIC’s website – a governmental website of reasonably indisputable accuracy.  JPMorgan does not support the contention that courts may properly take judicial notice of agreements between private parties.

 

            In support of Discovery’s request that the Court take judicial notice of various private websites purportedly demonstrating the air dates for the episodes in question, Discovery similarly cites to various cases either involving a governmental entity, or press publications of which the court took judicial notice that the information had been published, but not the truth of the matters asserted.

 

            While Discovery argues it does not ask the Court to take judicial notice of the truth of the matter asserted, that is precisely what Discovery seeks.  Even if the Court were to take judicial notice of the fact that the private websites in question indicated (as captured in recent months) that certain episodes premiered on certain dates, it would be improper for the Court to take judicial notice of the fact that those episodes indeed aired on certain dates, as that would be the truth of the matter asserted.

 

            Therefore, the Court denies Discovery’s request for judicial notice in its entirety.

 

ANALYSIS

 

1.     DEMURRER

 

“It is black letter law that a demurrer tests the legal sufficiency of the allegations in a complaint.”  (Lewis v. Safeway, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th 385, 388.)  In testing the sufficiency of a cause of action, a court accepts “[a]s true all material facts properly pled and matters which may be judicially noticed but disregard contentions, deductions or conclusions of fact or law.  [A court also gives] the complaint a reasonable interpretation, reading it as a whole and its parts in their context.”  (290 Division (EAT), LLC v. City & County of San Francisco (2022) 86 Cal.App.5th 439, 450 [cleaned up]; Hacker v. Homeward Residential, Inc. (2018) 26 Cal.App.5th 270, 280 [“in considering the merits of a demurrer, however, “the facts alleged in the pleading are deemed to be true, however improbable they may be”].)

 

Further, in ruling on a demurrer, a court must “liberally construe” the allegations of the complaint “with a view to substantial justice between the parties.”  (See Code Civ. Proc., § 452.)  “This rule of liberal construction means that the reviewing court draws inferences favorable to the plaintiff, not the defendant.”  (Perez v. Golden Empire Transit Dist. (2012) 209 Cal.App.4th 1228, 1238.)  

 

In summary, “[d]etermining whether the complaint is sufficient as against the demurrer on the ground that it does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action, the rule is that if on consideration of all the facts stated it appears the plaintiff is entitled to any relief at the hands of the court against the defendants the complaint will be held good although the facts may not be clearly stated, or may be intermingled with a statement of other facts irrelevant to the cause of action shown, or although the plaintiff may demand relief to which he is not entitled under the facts alleged.”  (Gressley v. Williams (1961) 193 Cal.App.2d 636, 639.)

 

A.    FAILURE TO STATE A CAUSE OF ACTION

 

                                                         i.          First Cause of Action – Breach of Contract

 

“To prevail on a cause of action for breach of contract, the plaintiff must prove (1) the contract, (2) the plaintiff's performance of the contract or excuse for nonperformance, (3) the defendant's breach, and (4) the resulting damage to the plaintiff.”  (Richman v. Hartley (2014) 224 Cal.App.4th 1182, 1186.)  Here, Plaintiff alleges:

 

1. Lab Rat Productions, Inc. (“Lab Rat”) is the loan out company for Mike Rowe (“Rowe”), a well-known television host, writer, narrator, producer, actor, and commercial spokesperson. Rowe has had a close association with the Discovery network for more than two decades, since his breakthrough show Dirty Jobs first aired in 2003. Over the years, Rowe’s role has expanded to include, among other things, narrating several Discovery network shows. Rowe has played a significant role in shaping the network’s brand identity and drawing millions of viewers with authentic, relatable storytelling that reinforces the network’s reputation for compelling, real-world programming.

 

2. At issue in this action is Discovery Talent Services, LLC’s (“DTS”) refusal to honor an agreement pursuant to which DTS “locked for life” Rowe’s services as Narrator for the Discovery show Deadliest Catch. Pursuant to the “pay-or-play” agreement, for each new episode, DTS must either use Rowe’s services to narrate the episode (play) or compensate Rowe his hourly rate for any new episode DTS does not want him to narrate (pay).

 

3. DTS made clear in writing that the pay-or-play agreement applied not just to the original Deadliest Catch series, but to all spinoffs of Deadliest Catch. In fact, prior to 2020, DTS expressly “played” Rowe as Narrator pursuant to the pay-or-play agreement in all Deadliest Catch spinoffs, including Deadliest Catch: The Bait and Deadliest Catch: Dungeon Cove. In connection with the former work, DTS specifically said no paperwork was needed when Rowe first started to narrate because Deadliest Catch: The Bait was part of the original Deadliest Catch series and thus covered by the existing Deal Memo. In connection with the later work, DTS sent an email “acknowledging that Dungeon Cove would be treated just like Deadliest Catch, since it is a spinoff.” It was clear from correspondence that DTS considered spinoffs to be part of the Deadliest Catch series, at least where the spinoffs used the Deadliest Catch name in the title.

 

4. However, unbeknownst to Rowe, beginning in 2020, several other spinoffs of the original Deadliest Catch series were created and Rowe was not cast as Narrator in any of the episodes of any of these spinoffs (the “Spinoffs”). In fact, DTS never even told Rowe about the Spinoffs. These episodes were branded as Deadliest Catch spinoffs rather than entirely new series in order to take advantage of the popularity of Deadliest Catch—a popularity due in part to the popularity of Rowe. By the time the Spinoffs started airing, the original Deadliest Catch series had aired for more than 15 seasons and was one of the Discovery Channel’s flagship franchises.

 

5. Because DTS chose not to use Rowe’s services as Narrator in the Spinoffs, Rowe is entitled to compensation for DTS’s choice not to “play” him pursuant to the terms of the pay-or-play agreement. By this action, Lab Rat seeks to recover the compensation to which Rowe is due.

 

A.        The Pay-or-Play Agreement.

 

10. On November 14, 2011, Lab Rat and DTS executed a Deal Memo for Overall Personal Services. (This deal memo, along with its amendments, are collectively referred to as “the Deal Memo”). The Deal Memo has been amended various times over the years. The Fourth Amendment to the Deal Memo was executed as of June 26, 2013. It contains the following pay-or-play agreement: “[DTS] hereby engages [Lab Rat] on a pay-or-play basis (subject to Artist’s professional availability), and [Lab Rat] hereby accepts such engagement (subject to Artist’s professional availability), to furnish the services of Artist as a narrator for and in connection with episodes produced (if any) of any season of Deadliest Catch produced during the Narration Period.”

 

11. Under the terms of the original Deal Memo, Rowe was only locked on a pay-or-play basis for a specified period of time (called the “Narration Period”). But in 2020, the parties agreed that Rowe would be “locked for life.” This is reflected in Amendment 4D, the latest iteration of the Deal Memo. Under the heading “Deadliest Catch & Bering Sea Gold / Lock for Life,” Amendment 4D provides as follows: “Subject to the ‘Lock Conditions’ (defined below), Artist shall be locked as Narrator on a pay-or-play basis to all originally produced episodes of Deadliest Catch and BSG.”

 

12. The “Lock Conditions” in the Deal Memo (all of which relate to Rowe’s ability to perform) have at all times been met. As such, under the plain language of the Deal Memo, for all originally produced episodes of Deadliest Catch and BSG, Rowe shall either (a) narrate the episode and be paid (play) or (b) be paid just the same even if Rowe is not the Narrator or if there is no is no narrator (pay).

 

13. The pay-or-play agreement in the Deal Memo applies with equal force to spinoffs of the original Deadliest Catch series. Pursuant to the pay-or-play agreement, Rowe was cast as Narrator in two separate Deadliest Catch spinoffs, Deadliest Catch: The Bait and Deadliest Catch: Dungeon Cove. In connection with Deadliest Catch: The Bait, DTS specifically said no paperwork was needed because it was part of the original Deadliest Catch series and thus covered by the existing Deal Memo. In connection with Deadliest Catch: Dungeon Cove, DTS expressly acknowledged that “Dungeon Cove would be treated just like Deadliest Catch, since it is a spinoff.” For these spinoffs, Lab Rat was paid the Deadliest Catch voiceover rates set forth in the Deal Memo. These rates are different than the rates for other voiceover work Rowe did on other Discovery shows. Specifically, prior to the pay-or-play agreement that attached Rowe for life (rather than just during a specified period), Rowe’s voiceover rates for Deadliest Catch would increase over time. Typically, increases were based on a specified time periods or as part of renegotiating the Deal Memo. In exchange for the benefits of the pay-or-play agreement attaching him for life to all produced episodes, Rowe agreed to cap his voiceover rates. 14. Not only has DTS admitted that the pay-or-play agreement applies to spinoffs and actually cast Rowe as Narrator in spinoffs pursuant to the pay-or-play agreement, it makes complete sense that the Deal Memo would apply to spinoffs. For the most part, these spinoffs use the original Deadliest Catch series name in the title. This is done specifically to take advantage of the popularity of the original series and the goodwill that has developed in the name; goodwill that Rowe had an integral part in generating over his almost 25 seasons as the Narrator of the series.

 

B.        The Breaches.

 

15. Lab Rat and Rowe have learned that there are numerous other Deadliest Catch spinoff series for which DTS chose not to cast a Narrator. These include Deadliest Catch: Bloodline (33 episodes), Deadliest Catch: The Viking Returns (10 episodes), and Deadliest Catch: Northern Edge (8 episodes). Lab Rat is informed and believes that these Spinoffs were produced by the same production company that produced Deadliest Catch.

 

16. Because DTS exercised its choice not to play Rowe in these 51 episodes by crafting episodes that did not use a Narrator, DTS must “pay” Lab Rat for each of these episodes pursuant to the express terms of the pay-or-play agreement in the Deal Memo. DTS has refused to do so. Lab Rat and Rowe have also learned that the episodes of the original Deadliest Catch series that are aired internationally are materially different than the episodes aired in the United States. To the extent any of these international episodes are determined to be “originally produced episodes,” the pay-or-play agreement would apply to those as well.

 

17. In prelitigation discussions, DTS stated that its understanding of the payor-play provision in the Deal Memo is that the provision only applies when DTS chooses to have a Narrator in the episode. This “understanding” is contrary to (a) the clear and unambiguous language of the Deal Memo and (b) the very definition of a pay-or-play agreement. See Locke v. Warner Bros. (1997) 57 Cal.App.4th 354, 358 (“the provision is called ‘pay or play’ BECAUSE IT GIVES THE STUDIO A CHOICE: It can either ‘play’ the director by using the director’s services, or pay the director his or her fee”). DTS contractually “locked” Rowe as Narrator to “all originally produced episodes.” None of the Lock Conditions give DTS the ability to avoid the pay-or-play obligation simply by not including a Narrator in a new episode. Stated differently, for DTS’s “understanding” of the agreement to be correct, the Deal Memo would need to provide that Rowe was locked to “all originally produced episodes that contain a Narrator.” It does not. No such language is contained in the specifically enumerated “Lock Conditions” or anywhere else in the Deal Memo.

 

18. Furthermore, DTS’s argument is barred as a matter of law. “The language of a contract is to govern its interpretation, if the language is clear and explicit, and does not involve an absurdity.” Cal. Civ. Code, § 1638. As stated by the California Supreme Court: “If contractual language is clear and explicit, it governs.” Powerline Oil Co., Inc. v. Super. Ct. (2005) 37 Cal.4th 377, 390. Here, the language of the Deal Memo is clear and unambiguous and, on its face, is not susceptible to any other interpretation. As such, and as a matter of law, its meaning cannot be altered based on DTS’s amorphous and undisclosed “understandings.” See, e.g., Winet v. Price (1992) 4 Cal.App.4th 1159, 1167 (“parol evidence is admissible only to explain a meaning to which the language is ‘reasonably susceptible,’ not to flatly contradict the express terms of the agreement”). This is particularly true given that DTS has a history of paying Lab Rat for episodes of Deadliest Catch in which Rowe was not cast as Narrator, thus debunking DTS’s entire argument.

 

19. The Deal Memo provides that Lab Rat is entitled to payment of $40,000 for each one hour episode of Deadliest Catch. Because DTS exercised its choice not to use Rowe as a Narrator in at least 51 episodes of Deadliest Catch spinoffs, Lab Rat is entitled to payment of at least $2.04 million (plus potentially additional payments for longer episodes).

 

20. Lab Rat and Rowe only recently learned about the Spinoffs and international episodes. Specifically, in 2025, a dispute arose between Lab Rat and DTS concerning five episodes during Season 21 of the original Deadliest Catch series in which DTS did not cast a Narrator. DTS refused to pay Rowe for these episodes even though it had previously paid Rowe for episodes he did not narrate, as required by the pay-or-play agreement. (This is the subject of a separate lawsuit pending in this Court as Case No. 26SMCV02139.)

 

21. Prior to these disputes with DTS, Lab Rat and Rowe had no reason to question whether DTS was acting in good faith and in compliance with the pay-or-play agreement. Furthermore, Lab Rat and Rowe were not even told about the Spinoffs and the changes to international episodes. As such, Lab Rat and Rowe had no basis to suspect any violations of the pay-or-play agreement. It was only after the incident with Season 21 of Deadliest Catch, and the resulting general loss of trust in DTS, that Plaintiff first undertook its investigation.

 

23. As described above, DTS and Lab Rat entered into the Deal Memo.

 

24. Lab Rat and Rowe performed all of the obligations required of them under the Deal Memo.

 

25. DTS breached the Deal Memo, and the covenant of good faith and fair dealing implied into the Deal Memo, by failing to pay Lab Rat for the Spinoffs and the international episodes of the Deadliest Catch that constitute originally produced episodes in which Rowe was not cast as Narrator.

 

26. As a direct and proximate result of DCL’s breaches, Lab Rat has been damaged in the amount of at least $2.04 million, plus interest.

 

(Complaint ¶¶ 1-5, 10-21, 23-26.)

 

            As set forth in the complaint, the Court finds that Plaintiff has alleged:  (1) the pay-or-play agreement (Complaint ¶¶ 2-3, 10-14, 23); (2) Plaintiff’s performance thereunder (Complaint ¶ 24); (3) Discovery’s breaches (Complaint ¶¶ 4-5, 15-21, 25); and (4) resulting damages (Complaint ¶ 26).

 

            Defendant demurs on the grounds that (1) the Deal Memo does not mention or pertain to spinoffs at all; (2) to the extent Plaintiff alleges breaches based on spin-offs, the claim is time barred; and (3) to the extent Plaintiff alleges breaches based off of internationally aired episodes, they are inadequately pled.

 

            Defendant’s argument that the contract at issue does not in fact reference spinoffs is premised upon the contract itself, which, as discussed above, the Court declines to take judicial notice of. 

 

            Similarly, Defendant’s argument that Plaintiff’s claims are time barred is premised upon the air dates of which the Court similarly declines to take judicial notice.

 

            Finally, Defendant argues that to the extent Plaintiff’s claims are premised on categorizing internationally-aired episodes of Deadliest Catch that are materially different from the episodes that aired in the United States as “originally produced episodes” for which Plaintiff is entitled additional compensation, it is inadequately pled.  The Court disagrees. 

 

            Plaintiff clearly alleges that the contract entitles Plaintiff to be the narrator on a pay-or-play basis “to all originally produced episodes” (Complaint ¶ 11) and that Defendant breached the agreement with respect to such international episodes (Complaint ¶ 25).  The allegations are sufficient at this juncture of the litigation. There is no heightened pleading requirement to allege a breach of contract. [1]

 

            Whether the parties actually agreed that Defendant would compensate Plaintiff as alleged, and whether Defendant actually breached the contract at issue are factual questions to be resolved at later stages of the litigation.

 

2.     MOTION TO STRIKE

 

Any party, within the time allowed to respond to a pleading, may serve and file a motion to strike the whole pleading or any part thereof.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 435, subd. (b)(1); Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.1322, subd. (b).)  On a motion to strike, the court may: (1) strike out any irrelevant, false, or improper matter inserted in any pleading; or (2) strike out all or any part of any pleading not drawn or filed in conformity with the laws of California, a court rule, or an order of the court.  (Code Civ. Proc., § 436, subd. (a)-(b); Stafford v. Shultz (1954) 42 Cal.2d 767, 782.) 

 

Because Defendant’s arguments in connection with the alternative motion to strike are premised upon the same documents of which the Court declines to take judicial notice, the Court denies Defendant’s motion to strike.

 

CONCLUSION AND ORDER

 

For the reasons stated, the Court overrules Defendant’s Demurrer and denies Defendant’s Motion to Strike.
