Dept. F49

Date: 7/8/26

Case Name: Nathan Ivan Ewing-Herrick vs. Los Angeles Unified School Dist.; Dyer Street Elementary School; and Does 1-25

Case No. 23CHCV01268

 

LOS ANGELES SUPERIOR COURT

NORTH VALLEY DISTRICT

DEPARTMENT F49

 

JULY 8, 2026

 

MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES TO REQUEST FOR PRODUCTION

Los Angeles Superior Court Case No. 23CHCV01268

 

Motion filed: 3/27/26

 

MOVING PARTY: Plaintiff Nathan Ivan Ewing-Herrick

RESPONDING PARTY: Defendant Los Angeles Unified School District

NOTICE: OK

 

RELIEF REQUESTED: An order from this Court: (1) compelling Defendant Los Angeles Unified School District to provide further verified responses to Plaintiff’s Requests for Production of Documents, Set Two, Nos. 79, 81-83, and 94 and to produce all documents responsive to the requests; and (2) awarding sanctions in the amount of $3,937.50.

 

TENTATIVE RULING: The motion is GRANTED. The request for sanctions is DENIED.

 

BACKGROUND

 

This action arises out of a schoolyard accident in which Plaintiff Nathan Ivan Ewing-Herrick  (“Plaintiff”) severed a portion of his left index finger. On April 27, 2023, Plaintiff, a minor represented by and through his guardian ad litem, Tanya Renee Herrick-Ewing, initiated this action against Defendants Los Angeles Unified School District (“LAUSD”), Dyer Elementary School (“Dyer Street”), and Does 1 through 25 (collectively, “Defendants”), alleging causes of action for general negligence and premises liability. Subsequently, on September 7, 2023, Plaintiff filed the operative First Amended Complaint (“FAC”).

 

On October 16, 2023, LAUSD and Dyer jointly filed an Answer. On June 24, 2024, at the request of Plaintiff, the Court dismissed Dyer Street from the action.

 

On March 27, 2026, Plaintiff filed the instant motion to compel LAUSD to further respond to Plaintiff’s Requests for Production of Documents, Set Two, Nos. 79, 81-83, and 94 (the “Motion”).

 

On June 24, 2026, LAUSD filed an Opposition.

 

On June 30, 2026, Plaintiff filed a Reply.

 

ANALYSIS

 

            “On receipt of a response to a demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling, the demanding party may move for an order compelling further response to the demand if the demanding party deems that any of the following apply: (1) A statement of compliance with the demand is incomplete. (2) A representation of inability to comply is inadequate, incomplete, or evasive. (3) An objection in the response is without merit or too general.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (a).)

 

A.    Procedural Requirements

 

(1)   Timeliness of Motion

 

A party must bring a motion to compel further responses to requests for production within 45 days after the responding party serves verified responses or supplemental verified responses. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (c).) Parties may also agree in writing to a specific later date by which to file a motion to compel further responses. (Ibid.) If a motion to compel further responses is not timely made, the propounding party waives any right to compel a further response. (Ibid.)

 

Although not “‘jurisdictional’ in the fundamental sense, [the time limit] is … ‘jurisdictional’ in the sense that it renders the court without authority to rule on motions to compel other than to deny them.” (Sexton v. Superior Court (1997) 58 Cal.App.4th 1403, 1410.)

 

LAUSD served verified responses to Plaintiff’s Requests for Production for Documents, Set Two on January 27, 2026 via email, and further responses on March 13, 2026. (Mot., Merzel Decl., Exhs. 2, 6.) The deadline to bring a motion to compel further responses was thus April 29, 2026. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 1010.6, 2031.310.) The motion, filed on March 27, 2026, was accordingly timely.

 

(2)   Meet and Confer

 

A motion to compel further responses to a demand for production must be accompanied by a meet and confer declaration under Section 2016.040. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(2).) “A meet and confer declaration in support of a motion shall state facts showing a reasonable and good faith attempt, either in person, by telephone, or by videoconference, to informally resolve each issue presented by the motion.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2016.040.)

 

Plaintiff’s counsel describes exchanging several meet and confer letters with LAUSD’s counsel concerning the responses at issue. (Merzel Decl., ¶ 4; Mot. at p. 4.) Written communication does not generally satisfy the in-person or telephonic meet and confer requirement of Code of Civil Procedure section 2016.040. However, the Court finds that the breadth of communications between counsel demonstrates an earnest effort to resolve the issues, fulfilling the purpose of the meet and confer requirement.

 

(3)   Separate Statement

 

A motion to compel further responses to a demand for production must be accompanied by a separate statement. (Cal. Rules of Court, rules 3.1345, subd. (a)(3).) “A separate statement is a separate document filed and served with the discovery motion that provides all the information necessary to understand each discovery request and all the responses to it that are at issue.” (Id., rule 3.1345, subd. (c).)

 

Plaintiff has filed a Separate Statement (“Pl. SS”) concurrently with the Motion, stating both the text of the requests at issue and LAUSD’s responses, indicating why the responses are believed to be inadequate, and providing reasons why further response should be compelled. (See generally, Pl. SS.) The Court thus finds that Plaintiff has complied with the requirements of California Rules of Court, Rule 3.1345.

 

B.     Motion to Compel Further Responses to Requests for Production, Set One

 

The party to whom a demand for production has been made must respond separately to each demand by any of the following: (1) a statement that the party will comply with the demand by the date set; (2) “[a] representation that the party lacks the ability to comply with the particular demand;” or (3) an objection to the demand. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.210, subd. (a).)

 

A statement of compliance with a particular demand must state whether compliance will be made in whole or in part, and “that all documents or things in the demanded category that are in the possession, custody, or control of that party and to which no objection is being made will be included in the production.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.220.)

 

“A representation of inability to comply with [a] particular demand … shall affirm that a diligent search and a reasonable inquiry has been made in an effort to comply with that demand. This statement shall also specify whether the inability to comply is because the particular item or category has never existed, has been destroyed, has been lost, misplaced, or stolen, or has never been, or is no longer, in the possession, custody, or control of the responding party.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.230.) The statement must also set forth the name and address of any person or organization known or believed to have possession, custody, or control of that item or category of item. (Ibid.)

 

If the responding party objects to the demand, the response must: (1) identify with particularity any document or item falling within the category of the demand to which an objection is being made; and (2) clearly set forth the extent of and specific ground for the objection. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.240, subd. (b).) “If an objection is based on a claim of privilege, the particular privilege invoked shall be stated.” (Id., § 2031.240, subd. (b)(2).)

 

(1)   The Responses at Issue

 

Plaintiff contends that LAUSD’s responses to Requests for Production of Documents, Set Two, Nos. 79, 81-83, and 94 are incomplete and accordingly noncompliant with the Discovery Act.

 

(a)   Request Nos. 79 and 94

 

Request No. 79 seeks “All DOCUMENTS RELATING TO or CONCERNING teacher workload, staffing shortages, overwork, or insufficient supervision coverage at Dyer Street Elementary, including UTLA communications, grievances, or staff complaints.” (Pl. SS, at p. 1.)

 

Request No. 94 seeks “All DOCUMENTS RELATING TO or CONCERNING school overcrowding, insufficient staff, insufficient budget, insufficient training of teachers or staff, or concentration of students with behavioral concerns.” (Pl. SS, at p. 21.)

 

LAUSD’s initial and further responses to both requests assert several objections, and the further response to both adds:

 

Without waiving and subject to the aforementioned objections, and in the spirit of discovery, Responding Party responds as follows: This request is overly broad as it is not limited to a reasonable period of time. Responding Party has conducted a diligent search and reasonable inquiry in an effort to locate the items demanded for the five years prior to the incident. Responding Party is unable to comply with this request as Responding Party is not in the possession, custody or control of any documents responsive to this request. Responding Party was unable to locate any responsive documents and no such documents are believed to exist. Discovery is ongoing and continuing and Responding Party reserves the right to supplement this response.

 

(Pl. SS, at pp. 2-3; 21-23.)

 

Plaintiff contends that LAUSD’s representation that no responsive documents exist is “implausible” because it is inconsistent with public media coverage of staffing shortages within LAUSD and because schools “necessarily maintain teacher assignments and staffing supervision schedules and duty assignments; administrative communications regarding staffing and coverage; and records relating to workload, complaints, or staffing issues.” (Pl. SS, at pp. 3-4.) Plaintiff further contends that LAUSD’s language making its representation of inability to comply “subject to” its objections renders it unclear whether a reasonable search for responsive documents was actually conducted or whether the search included only documents deemed relevant and non-privileged. (Reply, at p. 4.)

 

LAUSD maintains that hearsay statements referring to LAUSD schools generally as understaffed are irrelevant to the staffing reality at Dyer Street. (Defendant’s Separate Statement (“Def. SS”), at pp. 12, 35-36.) LAUSD avers that its representation of inability to comply is a complete response because there are no responsive documents to compel. (Opp’n, at pp. 4-5.)

 

The Court agrees with Plaintiff that LAUSD’s response is incomplete and does not comply with the requirements of the Discovery Act. Making the response subject to LAUSD’s objections, without setting forth the extent of any of the objections, (see Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.240, subd. (b)), obscures whether specific objections are being asserted, whether documents exist to which an objection applies, and whether the search conducted and inquiry made were reasonable. The response to Request Nos. 79 and 94 is accordingly insufficient.

 

(b)   Request Nos. 81-83

 

Request Nos. 81-83 seek any and all documents related to behavioral issues among students at Dyer Street Elementary School. (Pl. SS, at pp. 12, 15, 18.) Specifically, Plaintiff requests documents concerning: “behavioral, social emotional, or disciplinary issues documented in Mr. Legaspi’s classroom during the 2021–2022 school year, including records of referrals to a Psychiatric Social Worker (PSW)” (No. 81); “DOCUMENTS RELATING TO or CONCERNING all communications with parents, including Nathan’s parents, regarding behavioral concerns, classroom conduct, or playground behavior, whether written, electronic, or via school platforms” (No. 82); and “DOCUMENTS RELATING TO or CONCERNING behavioral interventions, SST meetings, counseling referrals, or administrative interventions concerning PLAINTIFF or any other students in Mr. Legaspi’s class” (No. 83). (Ibid.)

 

LAUSD’s response to each of these requests consists largely of objections on the following grounds: vagueness, ambiguity, and overbreadth; burden; attorney-client privilege, work-product protection, deliberative process privilege, and official information privilege; violation of privacy and confidentiality; and violation of the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (“FERPA”) and the Health Insurance Portability Accountability Act (“HIPAA”). (Pl. SS, at pp. 12-13, 15-16, 18-19.) In addition to these objections, LAUSD’s response to these requests indicates that it agrees to comply in part, with regard to Plaintiff only, referring Plaintiff to documents already produced. (Ibid.)

 

Plaintiff contends that the production of documents relating solely to Plaintiff is an incomplete response to the inquiry. LAUSD does not dispute that its response limits production only to certain documents and does not deny that other responsive documents exist. The Court accordingly finds the foregoing response to be incomplete.

 

(2)   Good Cause for Further Response

 

In addition to identifying the insufficiency of responses to a demand for production, a motion to compel further response “shall set forth specific facts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought by the demand.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (b)(1).) Good cause may be shown by a fact-specific showing of relevance. (Kirkland v. Superior Court (2002) 95 Cal.App.4th 92, 98.) Information is relevant to the subject matter if it “might reasonably assist a party in evaluating the case, preparing for trial, or facilitating settlement.” (Lipton v. Superior Court (1996) 48 Cal.App.4th 1599, 1611, emphasis omitted.)

 

Plaintiff contends that the documents sought by Request Nos. 79 and 94 relating to staffing shortages are directly relevant to Plaintiff’s theories of liability. Plaintiff alleges that LAUSD employees failed to adequately supervise students and failed properly to inspect and maintain school equipment and to enforce safety rules, resulting in Plaintiff’s injury. (FAC, ¶¶ 9, 11-18.) The Court agrees that evidence of staffing shortages, teacher workload, and coverage bears on whether there were adequate personnel in place to supervise the conduct of students under the circumstances leading to Plaintiff’s injury. Moreover, LAUSD does not dispute the relevance of these documents. Instead, it contends that there is nothing further to compel because no responsive documents exist. (Opp’n, at p. 4-5; Def. SS, at pp. 11-13, 35-36.) The Court accordingly finds that Plaintiff has established good cause justifying further response to Request Nos. 79 and 94.

 

As to Request Nos. 81-83, which request documents concerning behavioral issues among students in teacher Moises Legaspi’s class, Plaintiff argues that this information relates to the supervisory capabilities of staff at the school. (Pl. SS, at pp. 14-15, 20-21, 25-26.) Plaintiff contends that the presence of behaviorally problematic students, to which Mr. Legaspi testified at deposition, is relevant to whether the demands placed on teachers affected their ability to supervise and manage the needs of a large classroom. (Ibid.)

 

LAUSD argues that documents relating to the social-emotional and behavioral issues of other students are irrelevant to the incident at issue because there are no allegations that another student caused or contributed to the incident. (Opp’n, at pp.6-7.)

 

While Plaintiff does not allege that any other student contributed to his injuries, information concerning the demands placed on teachers is relevant to whether there was sufficient staff in place to supervise students. The Court agrees with Plaintiff’s premise that individuals have a limited to capacity to manage others. The requirement that teachers routinely address significant behavioral issues would thus tend to show that the burden to teachers in maintaining their classrooms exceeded manageable limits. The Court finds that good cause exists to compel responses to Request Nos. 81-83.

 

(3)   LAUSD’s Objections to the Requests

 

Once the moving party establishes good cause, the burden shifts to the responding party to justify any objections or failure to provide a complete response. (Coy v. Superior Court (1962) 58 Cal.2d 210, 220-221; Standon Co. v. Superior Court (1990) 225 Cal.App.3d 898, 903.) Objections must be specific and substantiated, and a claim of inability to comply requires evidence of a diligent search and reasonable inquiry (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2031.230, 2031.240; Korea Data Systems Co. v. Superior Court (1997) 51 Cal.App.4th 1513, 1518.) 

 

As to Request Nos. 79 and 94, LAUSD contends that it did not provide a statement of compliance because Plaintiff is seeking to compel documents that do not exist. (Opp’n, at p. 5.) While a verified representation that no documents ever existed is generally entitled to credence, given that the Court has found LAUSD’s representation to be unclear in light of the conditional language of the response, further response is warranted to clarify the extent of LAUSD’s objections and whether the nature of LAUSD’s search and inquiry was limited to material it deemed non-objectionable.

 

As to Request Nos. 81-83, LAUSD contends that blanket production of unredacted student records would compromise the privacy interests of its students. (Opp’n, at p. 6.) LAUSD argues that the names and contact information of students are protected by the right to privacy and by FERPA, and that they may not be disclosed without consent or a court order. (Id., at pp. 6-7.) In Reply, Plaintiff asserts that he does not seek personal information and is amenable to the redaction of student names and other identifying information. (Reply, at pp. 2-3.)

 

The California Constitution recognizes that individuals have an inalienable right to privacy. (Cal. Const., Art. 1, § 1.) That right, however, must be balanced against a litigant’s broad right to discovery of non-privileged matters that are relevant to the subject matter of the litigation. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 2017.010.)

 

“A party asserting a privacy interest must establish a legally protected privacy interest, an objectively reasonable expectation of privacy in the given circumstances, and a threatened intrusion that is serious. [Citation.]” (Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 552.) Once the party asserting privacy meets these threshold requirements, the Court must balance “the legitimate and countervailing interests” served by disclosure against feasible alternatives or protective measures that would serve the same interests. (Ibid.)

 

FERPA prohibits the disbursement of public educational funds to institutions that “permit[] the release of education records (or personally identifiable information contained therein other than directory information, … ) of students without the written consent of their parents,” except under limited circumstances. (20 U.S.C. § 1232g, subd. (b)(1).) Schools may release education records without the consent of parents if the disclosure is made pursuant to a court order. (34 C.F.R. § 99.31, subd. (a)(9)(1).)

 

Plaintiff does not dispute that Request Nos. 81-83 implicate the privacy interests of LAUSD’s students. Instead, Plaintiff argues that the privacy interests at stake are adequately protected by the redaction of students’ identifying information or a protective order. (Reply, at p. 3.) LAUSD does not argue against the disclosure of redacted records, apparently taking issue only with the production of unredacted records and with any disclosure without either consent or a court order. (Opp’n, at pp. 6-7.) Given that the information sought is relevant to the issues of notice and adequate supervision and the parties do not disagree that redaction of identifying information is both sufficient and appropriate, the Court finds that, as to Request Nos. 81-83, the interests balance in favor of discovery.

 

The Motion is accordingly GRANTED, with LAUSD to redact any personal identifiable information from documents produced in response to Request Nos. 81-83.

 

C.    Monetary Sanctions

 

“[T]he court shall impose a monetary sanction under Chapter 7 (commencing with Section 2023.010) against any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel further response to a demand, unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2031.310, subd. (h).)

 

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 2023.030, the Court may impose a monetary sanction on any person, party, or attorney who engages in misuse of the discovery process. Misuses of the discovery process include: making, without substantial justification, an unmeritorious objection to discovery; and making an evasive response to discovery. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.010, subd. (f).)

 

The phrase “substantial justification” is generally understood to mean justification that is “well-grounded in both law and fact.” (Doe v. United States Swimming, Inc. (2011) 200 Cal.App.4th 1424, 1434.)

 

LAUSD contends that sanctions are unwarranted because it provided complete responses to Request Nos. 79 and 94 and was substantially justified in its objections to Request Nos. 81-83. While the Court has found that the requests seek relevant and discoverable information, LAUSD’s objections to Request Nos. 81-83 are well-grounded in law and fact. FERPA does not permit LAUSD to disclose student records absent consent or a court order. Its objection to the production of student records, redacted or otherwise, was thus made in compliance with its obligations under FERPA. The Court accordingly finds sanctions to be unwarranted.

 

The request for sanctions is therefore DENIED.

 

CONCLUSION

 

The Court GRANTS Plaintiff Nathan Ivan Ewing-Herrick’s motion to compel Defendant Los Angeles Unified School District’s further response to Plaintiff’s Request for Production of Documents, Set Two, Nos. 79, 81-83, and 94, consistent with the foregoing ruling. Defendant is ordered to serve further response to Plaintiff’s Request for Production of Documents, Set Two, Nos. 79, 81-83, and 94 within 30 days.

 

Plaintiff’s request for sanctions is DENIED.

Case Name: A.R., et al. vs. Charisma Academy I Inc. dba Granada Hills Montessori, et al.

Case No. 25CHCV01812

 

LOS ANGELES SUPERIOR COURT

NORTH VALLEY DISTRICT

DEPARTMENT F49

 

JULY 8, 2026

 

MOTION TO SEAL DECLARATION

Los Angeles Superior Court Case No. 25CHCV01812

 

Motion filed: 2/6/26

 

MOVING PARTY: Plaintiffs A.R., et al.

RESPONDING PARTY: None

NOTICE: Incomplete

 

RELIEF REQUESTED: An order from this Court granting Plaintiffs leave to redact the full names of their guardians ad litem in declarations filed in support of Plaintiffs’ motion for trial preference.

 

TENTATIVE RULING: The Motion is CONTINUED.

 

I.                   BACKGROUND

 

This action arises from alleged personal injuries sustained by a group of minor Plaintiffs while attending Defendants’ pre-schools. 

 

On May 23, 2025, Plaintiffs A.R., by and through Guardian Ad Litem (“GAL”) A.U.; A.E., by and through GAL J.E.; B.L., by and through GAL B.L.; D.V., by and through GAL A.V.; H.N. and M.N., by and through GAL A.K.; L.G., by and through L.G.; M.O., by and through GAL M.J.O.; N.M., by and through GAL V.M.; S.S., by and through GAL R.S.; and Z.W., by and through GAL J.W. (each, a “Plaintiff,” and collectively, “Plaintiffs”) initiated this action.

 

On January 12, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the operative First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) against Defendants Charisma Academy I Inc. dba Granada Hills Montessori Preschool (“GHMP”); Charisma Academy Inc. (“Charisma Corp.”); Charisma Academy II Inc. (“Charisma II”); Charisma Academy III Inc. (“Charisma III”); Charisma Academy IV Inc. (“Charisma IV”); Charisma Academy V Inc. (“Charisma V”); Charisma Academy VI Inc. (“Charisma VI”) (collectively, “Charisma Defendants”); Noresha Jayasundara (“Jayasundara”); Noesh Hordagoda (“Noesh”); Gladys Villalobos (“Villalobos”); Roshini Hordagoda (“Roshini”), and Does 1 through 100. The FAC alleges the following six causes of action: (1) assault, (2) battery, (3) intentional infliction of emotional distress, (4) negligence and negligence per se for breach of mandatory duty—failure to report, (5) negligent hiring, supervision and retention of employee, and (6) premises liability.

 

On February 11, 2026, Charisma Defendants, Noesh, Villalobos, and Roshini filed their joint Answer to the FAC. On February 13, 2026, Jayasundara filed an Answer to the FAC.

 

On February 6, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the instant motion to seal (the “Motion”).

 

No opposition papers have been filed.

 

II.                ANALYSIS

 

A.    Legal Standard

 

“A party requesting that a record be filed under seal must file a motion or an application for an order sealing the record.” (California Rules of Court, rule 2.551, subd. (b)(1).) The motion must be served on all parties that have appeared in the case. (Id., rule 2.551, subd. (b)(2).)

 

“The court may order that a record be filed under seal only if it expressly finds facts that establish: (1) [t]here exists an overriding interest that overcomes the right of public access to the record; (2) [t]he overriding interest supports sealing the record; (3) [a] substantial probability exists that the overriding interest will be prejudiced if the record is not sealed; (4) [t]he proposed sealing is narrowly tailored; and [n]o less restrictive means exist to achieve the overriding interest.” (California Rules of Court, rule 2.550, subd. (d).)

 

B.     Procedural Considerations

 

As a preliminary consideration, the Court notes that the proof of service attached to the Motion indicates that the papers served were Plaintiffs’ Notice of Motion and Motion for Trial Preference and declarations supporting Plaintiff’s Motion for Trial Preference. (Mot., Proof of Service.) This is insufficient to establish that the instant motion to seal was served on Defendants. The requirements of California Rules of Court, rule 2.551, subdivision (b)(2) have therefore not been satisfied and service is incomplete.

 

Based on the foregoing, the Court continues the Motion to allow Plaintiffs the opportunity to comply with service requirements.

 

CONCLUSION

 

The Court CONTINUES Plaintiffs’ Motion to Seal.

Case Name: Vladimir Loganov vs. Walmart Inc.; Wal-Mart Real Estate Business Trust; PRTC, L.P.; Athena Property Management, LLC; Enterprise Rent-A-Car of Los Angeles, LLC; Ferdinand Padilla; and Does 1-100

Case No. 25CHCV04612

 

LOS ANGELES SUPERIOR COURT

NORTH VALLEY DISTRICT

DEPARTMENT F49

 

JULY 8, 2026

 

APPLICATION FOR ADMISSION PRO HAC VICE

Los Angeles Superior Court Case No. 25CHCV04612

 

Motion filed: 6/3/26

 

MOVING PARTY: Defendants Walmart, Inc. and Walmart Real Estate Business Trust

RESPONDING PARTY: None

NOTICE: Incomplete

 

RELIEF REQUESTED: An order from this Court admitting Daniel Simon of Susman Godfrey LLP to appear as counsel pro hac vice in this matter.

 

TENTATIVE RULING: The motion is CONTINUED.

 

BACKGROUND

 

This action arises from personal injuries allegedly sustained by Plaintiff Vladimir Loganov (“Plaintiff”) when he was struck by a motor vehicle while crossing an access road on property controlled and operated by Walmart, Inc. (“Walmart”), Walmart Real Estate Business Trust (“Walmart Trust”), and Athena Property Management, LLC (“Athena”). On December 23, 2025, Plaintiff filed the Complaint against Defendants Walmart, Walmart Trust, PRTC, L.P. (“PRTC”), Athena, Enterprise Rent-A-Car of Los Angeles, LLC, Ferdinand Padilla, and Does 1 through 100, alleging the following causes of action: (1) motor vehicle negligence, (2) general negligence, and (3) premises liability.

 

On February 17, 2026, Walmart and Walmart Trust filed their joint Answer.

 

On February 18, 2026, PRTC concurrently filed its Answer and a Cross-Complaint against Roes 1 through 25 for equitable/implied indemnity, apportionment and contribution, and declaratory relief.

 

On June 3, 2026, Walmart and Walmart Trust filed the instant Application to Admit Daniel Simon Pro Hac Vice (the “Application”).

 

No objection or opposition to the Application has been filed.

 

ANALYSIS

 

“A person who is not a licensee of the State Bar but who is an attorney in good standing and eligible to practice before the bar of any United States court or the highest court in any state … and who has been retained to appear in a particular cause pending in a court of this state, may in the discretion of such court be permitted upon written application to appear as counsel pro hac vice, provided that an active licensee of the State Bar is associated as attorney of record.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(a).)

 

A person who is a resident of the State of California or is regularly employed or regularly engaged in substantial business, professional, or other activities in the State of California is ineligible for admission pro hac vice. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(a).) “Absent special circumstances, repeated appearances by any person under this rule is a cause for denial of an application.” (Id., rule 9.40(b).)

 

A.    Application for Admission Pro Hac Vice

 

Walmart and Walmart Trust seek admission of their counsel, Daniel Simon (“Simon”), pro hac vice in this matter.

 

(1)   Contents of Application

 

An application to appear as counsel pro hac vice must include:

 

(1) The applicant's residence and office address;

(2) The courts to which the applicant has been admitted to practice and the dates of admission;

(3) That the applicant is a licensee in good standing in those courts;

(4) That the applicant is not currently suspended or disbarred in any court;

(5) The title of each court and cause in which the applicant has filed an application to appear as counsel pro hac vice in this state in the preceding two years, the date of each application, and whether or not it was granted; and

(6) The name, address, and telephone number of the active licensee of the State Bar who is attorney of record.

 

(Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(d).)

 

Here, Simon’s application does not meet the foregoing content requirements. While Simon states that he has not been admitted pro hac vice in California state courts within the past two years and indicates that there is one application pending, he does not indicate whether this application represents all applications filed for pro hac vice admission within the past two years. (Simon Decl., ¶ 7.) The declaration thus fails to comply with rule 9.40, subdivision, (d)(5).

 

(2)   Eligibility

 

Simon is a member in good standing of the following courts: (1) the State Bar of Texas, (2) the State Bar of New York, (3) the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit, (4) the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit, (5) the United States District Court for the Southern District of Texas, and (6) the United States District Court for the Northern District of Texas, (7) the United States District Court for the Eastern District of New York, and (8) the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York. (Simon Decl., ¶¶ 3-4.) He attests that is not currently suspended or disbarred by any court. (Id., ¶ 5.)

 

Simon further attests that he is a New York resident and does not regularly engage in the practice of law or any other business in the State of California. (Simon Decl., ¶¶ 1, 6.) Simon indicates that, within the past two years, he has not been admitted pro hac vice in the state of California, but that he does have one application pending in Alameda County. (Id., ¶ 7.) However, Simon does not indicate whether the pending application in Alameda County represents all applications for pro hac vice admissions filed within the past two years. The Court accordingly has insufficient information to evaluate whether Simon meets the eligibility requirements of rule 9.40.

 

(3)   Verification and Service of Application

 

A verified application to the Superior Court to appear as counsel pro hac vice must be filed “together with proof of service by mail in accordance with Code of Civil Procedure section 1013a of a copy of the application and of the notice of hearing of the application on all parties who have appeared in the cause and on the State Bar at its San Francisco office.” (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 9.40(c)(1).) “[N]otice of [the] hearing must be given at the time prescribed in Code of Civil Procedure section 1005 unless the court has prescribed a shorter period.” (Ibid.)

 

The proof of service attached to the Application indicates that it was served by email on Plaintiff’s counsel and counsel for PRTC on June 3, 2026. However, rule 9.40(c) requires service by mail. Electronic service is accordingly insufficient to satisfy the requirements of the rule, and service is therefore incomplete. Moreover, there is no proof of service on the State Bar of California. While associated counsel Heather N. Seigler attests that she served the application on the San Francisco offices of the State Bar, (Seigler Decl., ¶ 5), there is no indication of when or how service was effectuated. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1013a, subd. (1) [requiring proof of service to show the date and place of deposit in the mail, the exact title of the document served, and the name and address of the recipient].)

 

Based on the foregoing, the Court CONTINUES the Application to allow counsel to address the noted deficiencies.

 

CONCLUSION

 

The Court CONTINUES Defendant’s Walmart, Inc. and Walmart Real Estate Business Trust’s Application to Admit Daniel Simon Pro Hac Vice.
