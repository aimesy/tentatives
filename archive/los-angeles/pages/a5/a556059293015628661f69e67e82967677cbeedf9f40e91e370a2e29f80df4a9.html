CREDITORS ADJUSTMENT BUREAU, INC. v. CHAMPION POWER EQUIPMENT, INC.

CASE NO.: 23NWCV00682

HEARING: 06/30/2026 @ 10:30 AM

 

#20

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

I.             Plaintiff Creditors Adjustment Bureau, Inc.’s motion for monetary sanctions is DENIED.

 

II.            Defendant Champion Power Equipment, Inc.’s motion for discovery sanctions is DENIED.

 

Plaintiff to give notice.

 

There are two motions on calendar: (1) Plaintiff Creditors Adjustment Bureau, Inc. (Plaintiff) moves for monetary sanctions pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 128.7 against Defendant Champion Power Equipment, Inc. (Defendant) and its counsel of record and (2) Defendant moves for monetary discovery sanctions against Plaintiff and its counsel of record.

 

Background

 

Plaintiff filed the operative second amended complaint (SAC) against Champion Power Equipment, Inc., a terminated California Corporation, Champion Power Equipment, Inc., a Nevada Corporation, and Does 1 through 10 on September 5, 2024. The SAC alleges that Plaintiff is a collection agency and was assigned this claim from China Export & Credit Insurance Corporation (CECIC) for collection. (SAC, ¶ 6.) Plaintiff seeks to collect a debt based on eight unpaid invoices totaling $401,760 (Debt) for goods that were sold to Defendant. (SAC, ¶ 7.) The SAC alleges three assignments took place prior to the commencement of this action: (1) Suzhou Warrior Machinery Co., Ltd. (Warrior), an agent of the manufacturer of the subject goods Suzhou Aavix Garden Equipment Co. Ltd. (Aavix), assigned its rights to Aavix, (2) Aavix assigned its rights to CECIC, and (3) CECIC assigned its rights to Plaintiff. (SAC, ¶¶ 8-11, Exhs. 1-3.) The complaint asserts five causes of action: (1) breach of contract, (2) goods sold and delivered, (3) open book account, (4) account stated, and (5) reasonable value.

 

On August 5, 2025, Defendant’s Motion for Issuance of an OSC re Dismissal came on for hearing, and the Court continued the matter to August 27, 2025 to be heard concurrently with Defendant’s demurrer.

 

On August 13, 2025, Plaintiff filed a Request for Dismissal as to the entire action without prejudice. On August 28, 2025, Defendant filed a Memorandum of Costs.

 

On January 20, 2026, Plaintiff filed its motion for sanctions.

 

On January 22, 2026, the Court denied Defendant’s motion for orders vacating the Request for Dismissal without prejudice filed by Plaintiff; (2) awarding monetary sanctions against Plaintiff’s counsel discovery abuses; and (3) dismissing the SAC with prejudice.

 

On February 23, 2026, Defendant filed its motion for sanctions.

 

On May 28, 2026, the Court, due to its congested calendar, continued the two instant motions for sanctions to today’s date.

 

Plaintiff’s Motion for Sanctions (Code of Civil Procedure section 128.7)

 

Legal Standard

 

“By presenting to the court, whether by signing, filing, submitting, or later advocating, a pleading, petition, written notice of motion, or other similar paper, an attorney or unrepresented party is certifying that to the best of the person’s knowledge, information, and belief, formed after an inquiry reasonable under the circumstances, all of the following conditions are met:

 

(1) It is not being presented primarily for an improper purpose, such as to harass or to cause unnecessary delay or needless increase in the cost of litigation.

(2) The claims, defenses, and other legal contentions therein are warranted by existing law or by a nonfrivolous argument for the extension, modification, or reversal of existing law or the establishment of new law.

(3) The allegations and other factual contentions have evidentiary support or, if specifically so identified, are likely to have evidentiary support after a reasonable opportunity for further investigation or discovery.

(4) The denials of factual contentions are warranted on the evidence or, if specifically so identified, are reasonably based on a lack of information or belief.

 

(Code Civ. Proc., § 128.7, subd. (b).) “If…the court determines that subdivision (b) has been violated, the court may…impose an appropriate sanction upon the attorneys, law firms, or parties that have violated subdivision (b) or are responsible for the violation.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 128.7, subd. (c).) “A motion for sanctions under this section shall be made separately from other motions or requests and shall describe the specific conduct alleged to violate subdivision (b). Notice of motion shall be served as provided in Section 1010, but shall not be filed with or presented to the court unless, within 21 days after service of the motion, or any other period as the court may prescribe, the challenged paper, claim, defense, contention, allegation, or denial is not withdrawn or appropriately corrected.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 128.7, subd. (c)(1).)

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 128.7 should only be utilized in “the rare and exceptional case where the action is clearly frivolous, legally unreasonable or without legal foundation, or brought for an improper purpose.” (Kumar v. Ramsey (2021) 71 Cal.App.5th 1110, 1121, citing Operating Engineers Pension Trust v. A-C Co. (9th Cir. 1988) 859 F.2d 1336, 1344.) “Because our adversary system requires that attorneys and litigants be provided substantial breathing room to develop and assert factual and legal arguments, [section 128.7] sanctions should not be routinely or easily awarded even for a claim that is arguably frivolous [citations], and instead should be made with restraint.” (Kumar, supra, 71 Cal.App.5th at p. 1344, citing Peake v. Underwood (2014) 227 Cal.App.4th 428, 448.) “A trial court is to apply an objective standard in making its inquiry concerning the attorney's or party's allegedly sanctionable behavior in connection with a motion for sanctions brought under section 128.7.” (Optimal Markets, Inc. v. Salant (2013) 221 Cal.App.4th 912, 921.)

 

Request for Judicial Notice

 

Defendant requests the Court take judicial notice of a transcript of proceedings on May 11, 2026 in Creditors Adjustment Bureau, Inc. v. Champion Power Equipment, Inc., LASC Case No. 25NWCV03878.

 

Defendant’s request for judicial notice is GRANTED pursuant to Evidence Code section 452, subd. (d). (See Hart v. Darwish (2017) 12 Cal.App.5th 218, 224-25 [“transcripts are ‘[r]ecords’ of a ‘court of this state.’”].)

 

Discussion

 

Plaintiff moves for sanctions pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 128.7 against Defendant and Defendant’s counsel, David P. King and Sheng Jin in the amount of $8,700.

 

Plaintiff moves on the grounds that Defendant’s filing of its Notice of Motion and Motion for Orders: (1) Vacating Plaintiff’s Request for Dismissal Without Prejudice; (2) Awarding Monetary Sanctions Against Plaintiff’s Counsel for Discovery Abuses; and (3) Dismissing the Second Amended Complaint With Prejudice (Motion for Orders) was filed for an improper purpose and is not warranted by existing law or the evidentiary record.

 

As an initial matter, Plaintiff complies with the 21-day safe harbor provision. Plaintiff first served this motion on Defendant on December 23, 2025, and then filed this motion on January 20, 2026. (Brown Decl., ¶ 8.) The Court notes that it retains jurisdiction to consider Plaintiff’s motion for sanctions. “Even after a party is dismissed from the action he may still have collateral statutory rights which the court must determine and enforce. These include the right to statutory costs and attorneys fees and the right to notice and hearing on a motion to set aside the dismissal.” (Frank Annino & Sons Construction, Inc. v. McArthur Restaurants, Inc. (1989) 215 Cal.App.3d 353, 357; Day v. Collingwood (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 1116, 1124-26 [finding trial court has jurisdiction to consider a postjudgment motion for sanctions].) The Court therefore considers the merits of this motion.

 

“A claim is factually frivolous if it is ‘not well grounded in fact’ and is legally frivolous if it is ‘not warranted by existing law or a good faith argument for the extension, modification, or reversal of existing law.’ In either case, to obtain sanctions, the moving party must show the party's conduct in asserting the claim was objectively unreasonable. A claim is objectively unreasonable if any reasonable attorney would agree that it is totally and completely without merit.” (Bucur v. Ahmad (2016) 244 Cal.App.4th 175, 189.)

 

Plaintiff argues that Defendant’s Motion for Orders was an attempt “to leverage collateral sanctions and dismissal with prejudice relief to obtain a litigation advantage and fee-shifting outcome, rather than litigate the underlying commercial debt on the merits.” (Mot., 4:27-28, 5:1.) Plaintiff argues that Defendant consistently shifted the litigation of this case away from the merits and into issues of standing by arguing that Plaintiff lacked standing based on prior errors regarding the chain of assignments. Plaintiff contends that the improper purpose is clear as Defendant was aware about the voluntary dismissal and that “that CAB had repeatedly disclosed and explained the assignment chain and its limited collection role, and that CAB’s new action attaches the relevant contract and assignment documentation.” (Mot., 5:23-25.)

 

In opposition, Defendant first describes an alleged connection between Plaintiff and a Chinese entity. This argument is outside of the scope of the pleadings, not relevant to this motion, and relies on evidence that is not properly before this Court. The Court does not consider this argument.

 

Defendant further argues that Plaintiff made sham pleadings regarding the standing issue. Defendant cites to a declaration by Lu Xiaochu, a purported sales manager of Warrior and Aavix, admitting to backdating written assignments. Defendant argues that this evidence, as well as the changes in the amended pleadings, constitute evidence that shows that the Motion for Orders was not frivolous. Defendant argues that it was reasonable for it to seek to litigate the issue of standing before reaching the merits of this action. “A litigant's standing to sue is a threshold issue to be resolved before the matter can be reached on the merits.” (Blumhorst v. Jewish Family Services of Los Angeles (2005) 126 Cal.App.4th 993, 1000.)

 

Defendant additionally contends that the Court has authority to dismiss an action with prejudice and that Defendant properly raise this argument based on a good faith argument.

 

A plaintiff may voluntarily dismiss an action “at any time before the actual commencement of trial, upon payment of the costs, if any.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 581, subd. (b)(1).) “But ‘[t]he right of a plaintiff to voluntarily dismiss an action before commencement of trial is not absolute.’” (Cole v. Hammond (2019) 37 Cal.App.5th 912, 921.) “The meaning of the term ‘trial’ is not restricted to jury or court trials on the merits, but includes other procedures that ‘effectively dispose of the case.’” (Id. at p. 921.) Defendant argues that its contentions in the Motion for Orders had merit it introduced evidence and the introduction of evidence can be considered the commencement of trial. (Code Civ. Proc., § 581, subd. (a)(6).)

 

Here, Plaintiff does not show that “any reasonably attorney” would find Defendant’s Motion for Orders “totally and completely without merit.” (Bucur, supra, 244 Cal.App.4th at p. 189.) While Plaintiff takes issue with the fact that Defendant’s Motion for Orders sought to vacate the dismissal without prejudice and dismiss the action with prejudice, this same issue has been the subject of proper litigation before. (Cole, supra, 37 Cal.App.5th at p. 920-21.) Here, the procedural history, with the continuance of the demurrer, the voluntary dismissal, and then the Motion for Issuance of an OSC raised legitimate questions as to whether a trial could have been found to have commenced, thereby cutting off Plaintiff’s statutory right to dismiss. These questions then formed the basis of the Court’s analysis in ruling on the Motion for Orders.

 

Moreover, as raised by Defendant, it had reason to suspect that Plaintiff lacked standing based on changes in the pleadings and statements made an associate of Warrior and Aavix. Defendant argues that its Motion for Orders was an attempt to argue that Plaintiff lacked standing. Defendant refers to Lyons v. Wickhorst (1986) 42 Cal.3d 911, 915 to show that the Court has power to dismiss a claim with prejudice if “the complaint has been shown to be “fictitious or sham” such that the plaintiff has no valid cause of action.” The Court finds that Defendant sufficiently shows that the contentions made in its Motion for Orders were reasonable. “[T]he evidentiary burden to escape sanctions under section 128.7 is light. [Defendant] must make a sufficient evidentiary showing to demonstrate that [it] made a reasonable inquiry into the facts and entertained a good faith belief in the merits of the claim. [Defendant] need not amass even enough evidence to create a triable issue of fact.” (Kumar, supra, 71 Cal.App.5th at p. 1126.) The Court finds that Defendant meets this low standard and thus escapes sanctions under Code of Civil Procedure section 128.7.

 

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s motion for sanctions is DENIED.

 

Defendant’s Motion for Discovery Sanctions

 

Legal Standard

 

“The court may impose a monetary sanction ordering that one engaging in the misuse of the discovery process, or any attorney advising that conduct, or both pay the reasonable expenses, including attorney’s fees, incurred by anyone as a result of that conduct. The court may also impose this sanction on one unsuccessfully asserting that another has engaged in the misuse of the discovery process, or on any attorney who advised that assertion, or on both. If a monetary sanction is authorized by any provision of this title, the court shall impose that sanction unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 2023.030, subd. (a).)

 

Under Code of Civil Procedure sections 2031.310, subdivision (h) and 2030.300, subdivision (d), “[t]he court shall impose a monetary sanction…any party, person, or attorney who unsuccessfully makes or opposes a motion to compel a further response to interrogatories [or demand], unless it finds that the one subject to the sanction acted with substantial justification or that other circumstances make the imposition of the sanction unjust.”

 

Discussion

 

Defendant moves for monetary discovery sanctions against Plaintiff and its counsel of record. Specifically, Defendant seeks monetary sanctions in connection with (1) Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel Further Discovery to the First Set of Requests for Production of Documents (RPDs), (2) Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel Further Discovery to the First Set of Special Interrogatories (SROGs), and (3) ) Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel Further Discovery to the First Set of Form Interrogatories (FROGs) (collectively, Motions to Compel Further Discovery), which were each filed on January 19, 2024.

 

Defendant moves for monetary sanctions on the basis that Plaintiff’s Motions to Compel Further Discovery were frivolous as Plaintiff withdrew each motion after Defendant opposed the Motions to Compel Further Discovery on the grounds that Plaintiff lacked standing and brought the motions in bad faith.

 

          Jurisdiction

 

Defendant argues the Court retains jurisdiction to impose discovery sanctions against Plaintiff despite the voluntary dismissal without prejudice.

 

As noted in Plaintiff’s motion for sanctions, despite the general rule that a voluntary dismissal deprives the Court of both subject matter jurisdiction and personal jurisdiction, “ ‘[c]ourts have ... held jurisdiction is retained postdismissal and postjudgment to decide motions for sanctions.’” (Manhan v. Gallagher [Manhan] (2021) 62 Cal.App.5th 504, 509, citations omitted.)

 

Defendant cites to Manhan as an example of the Court’s jurisdiction. In Manhan, following a trial court’s order granting motions to compel and awarding a plaintiff monetary sanctions, the plaintiff dismissed the entire action without prejudice. (Manhan, supra, 62 Cal.App.5th at p. 506-07.) On the same date of the dismissal, the defendant subject to the sanctions order filed a motion for reconsideration or to set aside the sanctions order on the grounds that the plaintiffs did not incur attorney’s fees because they were being represented pro bono. (Id., at p. 507.) The Court of Appeal concluded that “a trial court's authority to reconsider sanctions survives even after a plaintiff voluntarily dismisses an action.” (Id., at p. 510.) The Court of Appeal specifically noted that this was “consistent with critical functions that reconsideration performs, such as allowing courts to correct errors and thereby prevent miscarriages of justice, and pressuring litigants to use great care in presenting motions and ascertaining the facts.” (Ibid.) Unlike in Manhan, here, the issue is not a reconsideration of an order imposing monetary sanctions.

 

In another case cited by Defendant, City of Los Angeles v. PricewaterhouseCoopers, LLP [PricewaterhouseCoopers] (2024) 17 Cal.5th 46, 58-59 the Supreme Court of California upheld an order granting monetary sanctions for discovery abuses after a voluntary dismissal. The Court does not address the jurisdiction issue but rather notes that the Court of Appeal rejected the argument based on lack of jurisdiction. (Id., at p. 58.) However, upon review, unlike in this action, in PricewaterhouseCoopers, the trial court had ruled on many discovery motions and “[t]hroughout the pretrial proceedings, PwC had raised the possibility of discovery sanctions, but the trial court had instructed PwC to wait until the close of discovery to move for sanctions so the court could address the motions on a complete record.” (Id., at p. 56.) Here, this Court has not ruled on any discovery motions.

 

Even if the Court retains jurisdiction as a collateral issue, the Court finds that monetary sanctions are not warranted. None of the cases cited by Defendant are on point, either involving different types of motions or sanctions associated with discovery motions that were heard prior to the sanctions motions. Here, Defendant seeks monetary sanctions for discovery motions that were filed over two years ago and withdrawn on September 30, 2024. To the extent that Defendant argues that the motions are frivolous, they were taken off calendar prior to any hearings. To the extent that Defendant argues that sanctions were mandatory under Code of Civil Procedure sections 2031.310, subdivision (h) and 2030.300, subdivision (d), the Court does not agree. Sanctions are mandatory when a party is “unsuccessful,” absent substantial justification. (Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2031.310, subd. (h), 2030.300, subd. (d).) Here, neither party was “unsuccessful,” as the Motions to Compel Further were never heard.

 

The Court therefore finds that the imposition of monetary sanctions arising out of the Motions to Compel Further, requested after the Motions to Compel Further were withdrawn and again following Plaintiff’s voluntary dismissal of this action, is not warranted and unnecessarily punitive. “The sanctions the court may impose are such as are suitable and necessary to enable the party seeking discovery to obtain the objects of the discovery he seeks but the court may not impose sanctions which are designed not to accomplish the objects of discovery but to impose punishment.” (Fairfield v. Superior Court for Los Angeles County (1966) 246 Cal.App.2d 113, 120.)

 

Accordingly, Defendant’s motion for discovery sanctions is DENIED.

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Plaintiff Susana Gallardo’s motions to compel initial responses to requests for production, set three, and special interrogatories, set three are MOOT.

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

Plaintiff Susana Gallardo (Plaintiff) moves to compel Defendant Ford Motor Company (Defendant) to serve responses to (1) requests for production of documents (RPDs), set three and (2) special interrogatories (SROGs), set three.

 

Background

 

This is a lemon law action. On January 18, 2024, Plaintiff filed this action against Ford Motor Company, Cerritos Ford, Inc. dba Norm Reeves Ford Lincoln/Norm Reeves Ford Superstore, and Does 1 through 10, inclusive. On February 14, 2025, Plaintiff filed the operative FAC. The FAC alleges that Plaintiff and Ford entered into a written warranty contract regarding Plaintiff’s vehicle (Subject Vehicle). (FAC, ¶ 9.) The FAC alleges the Subject Vehicle contains a defect regarding a ten-speed automatic transmission (10R80 Transmission Defect). (FAC, ¶ 16.) The FAC asserts three causes of action: (1) violation of Song-Beverly Act – breach of express warranty, (2) fraudulent inducement-concealment, and (3) negligent repair.

 

On January 27, 2026, these motions first came on for hearing. The Court issued a tentative ruling granting the motions and denying Plaintiff’s request for monetary sanctions. (Order, 1/27/26.) During the hearing, the Court granted Defendant’s oral request for a stay of this order based on Defendant’s intentions to move for relief from waivers. (Order, 1/27/26.)

 

On May 21, 2026, the Court heard and granted Defendant’s motions for relief from waiver of objections as to RPDs, set three, and SROGs, set three.

 

Discussion

 

Based on the Court’s ruling granting Defendant’s motions for relief from waiver of objections as to RPDs, set three, and SROGs, set three, the instant motions are moot.

 

Accordingly, Plaintiff’s motions to compel responses to RPDs, set three, and SROGs, set three, are MOOT.

JOEL CESSNA, ET AL. v. EDWARD YOUNG, ET AL.

CASE NO.: 24NWCV04924

HEARING: 06/30/2026 @ 9:30 AM

 

#16

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

I.             Defendant Edward Young’s demurrer is CONTINUED to Tuesday, August 11, 2026 in Dept. P at 9:30 AM.

 

II.            The parties are ORDERED to meet and confer. Defendant Young’s meet and confer declaration must be filed on or before July 27, 2026. Any supplemental opposition is due by July 31, 2026 and any supplemental reply is due by August 6, 2026. See below for a detailed order.

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

Defendant Edward Young (Young) demurs to the first amended complaint (FAC).

 

Background

 

On December 18, 2024, Plaintiffs Joel R. Cessna (Cessna) and The Young and Cessna Partnership (the Partnership), a general partnership (Plaintiffs), filed this breach of contract action against Defendants Edward L Young (Young), Sunny Rose Corporation (SRC), Junk King Enterprises, Inc. (JKE), Junk King Franchise Systems, Inc. (JKFS), and Does 1 through 10. On April 16, 2026, following the Court’s ruling on Young’s demurrer to the complaint sustaining the demurrer with leave to amend, Plaintiffs filed the operative FAC.

 

The FAC alleges the following: Young and Cessna have known each other for over thirty years. (FAC, ¶ 13.) In 2011, Young joined JKE and/or JFKS as a franchise owner and encouraged Cessna to invest in the Junk-King franchise (Franchise). (FAC, ¶¶ 14-15.) On June 17, 2011, Young and Cessna executed a partnership agreement (Partnership Agreement) under which the two would be legal partners in the Franchise and by which “Young agreed to sell a 49% working interest in the Franchise to Mr. Cessna for the sum of $250,000.00. Mr. Cessna’s 49% working interest was to be a fully participating partnership interest in every aspect of the Franchise with no exclusions or exceptions of any kind whatsoever.” (FAC, ¶¶ 16-19.) The FAC alleges that since then, Young breached the Partnership Agreement by paying Cessna less than the amount owed, underperforming under the Partnership Agreement, and misrepresenting the nature of Cessna’s investment. (FAC, ¶¶ 26-46.) Prior to the formation of the Partnership Agreement and without Cessna’s knowledge, Young incorporated SRC. (FAC, ¶ 22, 25.) The FAC alleges an alter ego relationship between Young and SRC. (FAC, ¶¶ 22-25.)

 

The FAC asserts nine causes of action: (1) breach of contract, (2) breach of implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing, (3) violation of Corporations Code section 25401, (4) breach of fiduciary duty, (5) violation of Penal Code section 496, (6) accounting, (7) declaratory relief re Cessna’s share of the franchise, (8) declaratory relief re JKE, and (9) declaratory relief re JFKS.

 

Meet and Confer

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41 requires that the demurring party meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference at least five days before the responsive pleading is due. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a).) The demurring party shall file and serve a declaration stating either: (a) “The means by which the demurring party met and conferred…and that the parties did not reach an agreement resolving the objections raised in the demurrer” or (b) “[t]hat the party who filed the pleading subject to demurrer failed to respond to the meet and confer request…or otherwise failed to meet and confer in good faith.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(3).)

 

There is no meet and confer declaration filed with the demurrer. The Court notes that there was no meet and confer declaration filed with Young’s first demurrer. As to the first demurrer, the Court stated the following:

 

There is no meet and confer declaration filed with the demurrer. As Plaintiffs do not raise this defect in opposition, and in the interest of judicial economy, the Court proceeds to rule on the merits of this demurrer. The Court admonishes Young for his failure to meet and confer as required under the Code.

 

(Order, 3/24/26.)

 

Herre, in opposition, counsel for Plaintiffs attests that “[n]either Young nor his counsel of record made attempts to meet and confer prior to filing the Demurrer: Young’s counsel neither made attempts to reach out by telephone nor by email to meet and discuss, no less discuss the [FAC].” (Sir Decl., ¶ 3.) Plaintiffs argue that based on Young’s failure to meet and confer as required under the Code, the parties were deprived of the opportunity to determine whether Young’s objections could be resolved without motion practice and Plaintiffs were not apprised of the claims that Young believed to be objectionable until they were served with the demurrer.

 

Despite receiving admonishment in the Court’s prior Order, Young fails to attempt to meet and confer or file a declaration to extend the time to meet and confer, again. (See Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a).) While the Court does not sustain or overrule a demurrer on the basis of an insufficient meet and confer process, the Court finds a continuance warranted to permit the parties to meet and confer to informally resolve this dispute. (Dumas v. Los Angeles County Bd. of Supervisors (2020) 45 Cal.App.5th 348, 355, n. 3 [the Court “retains discretion to order counsel to meaningfully discuss the pleadings with an eye toward reducing the number of issues or eliminating the need for a demurrer.”].)

 

Therefore, Young is ordered to meet and confer with Plaintiffs. If, after exhausting those efforts, court intervention is needed, the parties may appear and argue the merits on the continued hearing date. If, after meeting and conferring, Young seeks to argue the demurrer, counsel for Young is instructed to file a meet and confer declaration which outlines the issues discussed and the remaining disputed issues for which a ruling is required.

 

Accordingly, Young’s demurrer to the FAC is CONTINUED to Tuesday, August 11, 2026 in Dept. P at 9:30 AM. The parties are ORDERED to meet and confer. Young’s meet and confer declaration must be filed on or before July 27, 2026. The meet and confer declaration shall update the Court on the remaining issues. Any supplemental opposition is due by July 31, 2026 and any supplemental reply is due by August 6, 2026. If there is no meet and confer declaration filed by July 27, 2026, the Court may presume the issues in this demurrer are moot.

CHRON MAYWOOD, LLC v. CYPRESS MANUFACTURING COMPANY

CASE NO.: 25NWCV02684  

HEARING: 06/30/2026 @ 9:30 AM

 

#17

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

I.             Defendant Cypress Manufacturing Company’s motion to set aside default is GRANTED.

 

II.            Defendant’s motion to compel arbitration is taken OFF-CALENDAR.

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

There are two motions on calendar: (1) Defendant Cypress Manufacturing Company (Defendant) moves to set aside default and (2) Defendant moves to compel arbitration.

 

Background

 

On July 24, 2025, Plaintiff Chron Maywood, LLC (Plaintiff) filed this breach of contract action against Defendant and Does 1 through 10. This action involves independent purchase orders in which Plaintiff sold licensed cannabis products to Defendant and Defendant agreed to pay for the products within 90 days of the deliveries. (Complaint, ¶ 1.) The complaint alleges that Defendant failed to make full payments for its orders when payment because due. (Complaint, ¶ 9.) The complaint asserts four causes of action: (1) breach of contract, (2) common counts, (3) fraudulent misrepresentation, and (4) conversion.

 

On October 8, 2025, Plaintiff filed a Request for Entry of Default, and on the same date, Default was entered as to Defendant (Default).

 

On March 12, 2026, the Court granted the parties stipulation to continue the Default Prove-Up Hearing so that the parties can engage in settlement negotiations.

 

Legal Standard

 

Pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 473, subdivision (d): “The court may, upon motion of the injured party, or its own motion, correct clerical mistakes in its judgment or orders as entered, so as to conform to the judgment or order directed, and may, on motion of either party after notice to the other party, set aside any void judgment or order.”

 

“When service of a summons has not resulted in actual notice to a party in time to defend the action and a default or default judgment has been entered against the party in the action, the party may serve and file a notice of motion to set aside the default or default judgment and for leave to defend the action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 473.5, subd. (a).) The motions shall be filed and served no later than the earlier of “(i) two years after entry of a default judgment against him or her; or (ii) 180 days after service on him or her of a written notice that the default or default judgment has been entered.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 473.5, subd. (a).)

 

The notice of motion “shall be accompanied by an affidavit showing under oath that the party's lack of actual notice in time to defend the action was not caused by his or her avoidance of service or inexcusable neglect.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 473.5, subd. (b).) “The party shall serve and file with the notice a copy of the answer, motion, or other pleading proposed to be filed in the action.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 473.5, subd. (b).)

 

Request for Judicial Notice

 

Plaintiff requests the Court take judicial notice of the Statements of Information for Cypress Manufacturing Company filed on October 24, 2024 and on January 27, 2026.

 

Plaintiff’s request for judicial notice is GRANTED pursuant to Evidence Code section 452, subdivision (c), (h).

 

Discussion

 

Defendant moves to set aside the Default entered against it on three grounds (1) invalid service of process, (2) Defendant did not have actual notice of this action, and (3) due to excusable mistake/neglect.

 

          Invalid Service of Process

 

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 416.10, subdivision (b), “[a] summons may be served on a corporation by delivering a copy of the summons and the complaint…[t]o the president, chief executive officer, or other head of the corporation, a vice president, a secretary or assistant secretary, a treasurer or assistant treasurer, a controller or chief financial officer, a general manager, or a person authorized by the corporation to receive service of process.” “[S]ection 415.20, subdivision (a), provides that in lieu of personal service on a corporate officer or authorized agent listed in section 416.10, “a summons may be served by leaving a copy of the summons and complaint during usual office hours in his or her office ... with the person who is apparently in charge thereof, and by thereafter mailing a copy of the summons and complaint by first-class mail, postage prepaid to the person to be served at the place where a copy of the summons and complaint were left.” (Chinese Theater, LLC v. Starline Tours USA, Inc. [Chinese Theater] (2025) 115 Cal.App.5th 1048, 1057.)

 

Here, the Proof of Service indicates that Plaintiff served Defendant via substitute service on August 22, 2025 at 20 Quail Run Circle, Salinas, CA 93907 to Danny Garcia, a person in charge. (Proof of Service, 8/28/25.)

 

Defendant argues that Mr. Garcia has no recollection of receiving service of the summons and complaint. Defendant puts forth Mr. Garcia’s declaration stating that he manages portion of Defendant’s facility and does not recall being served. (Garcia Decl., ¶¶ 2, 3.) Mr. Garcia states that it is his standard practice to send any documents to Ann Lawrence for handling. (Garcia Decl., ¶ 6.) Mr. Garcia states that he received the Entry of Default and Motion for Entry of Default Judgment on December 30, 2025 which he sent to Ann Lawrence. (Garcia Decl., ¶ 5.) Defendant argues that Mr. Garcia was not a qualified person to be served under Code of Civil Procedure section 416.10 because he was not an executive officer, secretary, treasurer, or general manager. Defendant additionally shows that Plaintiff did not attempt to serve the registered agent for service of process or the person whom the parties’ agreements assigned to give notice. (Lawrence Decl., ¶ 21.)

 

Defendant also argues that Plaintiff attempted service at the wrong location. The Proof of Service indicates that service was effectuated at 20 Quail Run Circle, Salinas, CA 93907, however, the registered agent was listed to be located at 19 Quail Run Circle, Salinas, CA. (Lawrence Decl., ¶ 20.)

 

Defendant further argues that the process server only made one attempt to personally serve Defendant before substitute service. This argument is not persuasive. Code of Civil Procedure section 415.20, subdivision (a) does not require prior attempts of personal service and specifies that it is an option “[i]n lieu of personal delivery.”

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that Defendant refers to Mr. Garcia as a manager of the facility. Plaintiff also relies on the fact that on January 27, 2026, Defendant filed a Statement of Information with the California Secretary of State amending its Agent for Service of Process to Daniel Garcia and Agent Adress to 20 Quail Run Circle, Salinas, California. (RJN, Exhs. 1, 2.)

 

Here, the Court agrees with Defendant that service was improper. Evidence that Mr. Garcia later became a registered agent for Defendant does not change the fact that at the time of service, Mr. Garcia was not a registered agent and the service was effectuated at a neighboring address.

 

Moreover, the evidence does not show that Mr. Garcia was a general manager and therefore authorized to receive service. CEO of Defendant, Ann Lawrence, specifically attests that “Danny Garcia is not the ‘person in charge’ of the warehouse at 20 Quail Run Circle. He manages the manufacturing area but not the whole facility.” (Lawrence Decl., ¶ 25.) Mr. Garcia himself states that he manages “the manufacturing portion of the facility.” (Garcia Decl., ¶ 2.) “General Manager,” “which has not been specifically defined by the Legislature, includes any agent of the corporation ‘of sufficient character and rank to make it reasonably certain that the defendant will be apprised of the service made.’” (Gibble v. Car-Lene Research, Inc. (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 295, 313.) Here, Plaintiff’s only evidence that Mr. Garcia was a general manager was the fact that the word “manager,” was used in Defendant’s moving papers and that he states that his responsibilities included sending legal papers to Defendant’s management. Plaintiff neglects to show that Defendant states that Mr. Garcia was a manager of a portion of the facility only. Additionally, Plaintiff does not show how it was aware that Mr. Garcia would apprise Defendant of the service when designating Defendant as a general manager to be served.

 

Based on the foregoing, the Court finds the Default is void based on the lack of sufficient service of process.

 

Accordingly, Defendant’s motion to set aside the default is GRANTED.

 

“‘[C]ompliance with the statutory procedures for service of process is essential to establish personal jurisdiction. [Citation.] Thus, a default judgment entered against a defendant who was not served with a summons in the manner prescribed by statute is void. [Citation.]’” (Ellard v. Conway (2001) 94 Cal.App.4th 540, 544.) Because the Court finds the service of process void, the Court does not have jurisdiction to rule on the motion to compel arbitration.

 

Accordingly, Defendant’s motion to compel arbitration is OFF-CALENDAR.


Case Number: 25NWCV03975    Hearing Date: June 30, 2026    Dept: P

EVA ROJAS FLORES v. LOS ANGELES UNIFIED SCHOOL DISTRICT, ET AL.

CASE NO.: 25NWCV03975 

HEARING: 06/30/2026 @ 9:30 AM

 

#18

TENTATIVE ORDER

 

Defendant Los Angeles Unified School District’s demurrer to the first cause of action in the first amended complaint is SUSTAINED with 30 days LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

Moving party to give notice.

 

Defendant Los Angeles Unified School District (Defendant or LAUSD) demurs to the first amended complaint (FAC).

 

Background

 

On November 7, 2025, Plaintiff Eva Rojas Flores, a minor by and through her Guardian Ad Litem, Laura Flores (Plaintiff) filed this action against Defendants LAUSD, Chester W. Nimitz Middle School, Angelica Carpio, and Does 1 through 100. On January 28, 2026, Plaintiff filed the operative FAC against LAUSD, Angelica Carpio, and Does 1 through 100.

 

The FAC alleges the following: on September 22, 2025, while Plaintiff was a sixth-grade middle school student enrolled at Nimitz Middle School, Plaintiff was walking toward the lunch pavilion when a group of students lead by Carpio approached Plaintiff from behind. (FAC, ¶ 16.) A verbal discussion began at which time a nearby school aide instructed the group to disburse. (FAC, ¶ 17.) The group grew, with other students recording the ordeal and instructing Carpio to batter Plaintiff. (FAC, ¶ 17.) The FAC alleges that once Plaintiff tried to walk away, “CARPIO pulled EVA’s hair, slammed her to the ground, continuously punched her, repeatedly banged EVA’s head on the concrete ground, and threw EVA’s hearing aids to the ground, severely damaging them.” (FAC, ¶¶ 18-19.) The FAC asserts four causes of action: (1) premises liability pursuant to Government Code sections 835 and 840 against LAUSD and Does 1 to 100; (2) failure of a school to provide adequate supervision of students as to LAUSD and Does 1 to 100; (3) negligence against Carpio and Does 1 to 100; and (4) battery as to Carpio and Does 1 to 100.

 

On March 26, 2026, Plaintiff dismissed this action as to Carpio without prejudice.

 

Legal Standard

 

A demurrer for sufficiency tests whether the complaint states a cause of action. (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.) In a demurrer proceeding, the defects must be apparent on the face of the pleading or via proper judicial notice. (Donabedian v. Mercury Ins. Co. (2004) 116 Cal.App.4th 968, 994.) In assessing a demurrer, the court “treat[s] the demurrer as admitting all material facts properly pleaded, but not contentions, deductions or conclusions of law.” (Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) “The only issue involved in a demurrer hearing is whether the complaint, as it stands, unconnected with extraneous matters, states a cause of action.” (Hahn, supra, 147 Cal.App.4th at p. 747.) A complaint will be upheld against a demurrer if it pleads facts sufficient to place the defendant on notice of the issues sufficient to enable the defendant to prepare a defense. (Doe v. City of Los Angeles (2007) 42 Cal.4th 531, 549-50.) 

 

“Generally it is an abuse of discretion to sustain a demurrer without leave to amend if there is any reasonable possibility that the defect can be cured by amendment.” (Goodman v. Kennedy (1976) 18 Cal.3d 335, 349.) A “[p]laintiff must show in what manner he can amend his complaint and how that amendment will change the legal effect of his pleading.” (Ibid.) 

 

Meet and Confer

 

Code of Civil Procedure section 430.41 requires that the demurring party meet and confer in person, by telephone, or by video conference at least five days before the responsive pleading is due. (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a).) The demurring party shall file and serve a declaration stating either: (a) “The means by which the demurring party met and conferred…and that the parties did not reach an agreement resolving the objections raised in the demurrer” or (b) “[t]hat the party who filed the pleading subject to demurrer failed to respond to the meet and confer request of the demurring party or otherwise failed to meet and confer in good faith.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 430.41, subd. (a)(3).)

 

The parties adequately met and conferred. (Estrada Decl., ¶ 4.)

 

Discussion

 

Defendant demurs to the first cause of action for premises liability.

 

“A dangerous condition of public property ‘means a condition of property that creates a substantial (as distinguished from a minor, trivial or insignificant) risk of injury when such property or adjacent property is used with due care in a manner in which it is reasonably foreseeable that it will be used.’ (§ 830, subd. (a).) The elements for that cause of action are: ‘(1) a dangerous condition of public property; (2) a foreseeable risk, arising from the dangerous condition, of the kind of injury the plaintiff suffered; (3) actionable conduct in connection with the condition, i.e., either negligence on the part of a public employee in creating it, or failure by the entity to correct it after notice of its existence and dangerousness; (4) a causal relationship between the dangerous condition and the plaintiff's injuries; and (5) compensable damage sustained by the plaintiff.’” (Toeppe v. City of San Diego (2017) 13 Cal.App.5th 921, 925-26.) “The existence of a dangerous condition ordinarily is a question of fact, but the issue may be resolved as a matter of law if reasonable minds can come to only one conclusion.” (Zelig v. County of Los Angeles (2002) 27 Cal.4th 1112, 1133.)

 

The FAC alleges that “the physical configuration of NIMITZ’s lunch area—including the fixed placement of lunch tables and surrounding fixtures, the layout of the lunch pavilion area, and the configuration of adjacent student walkways—created a funnel-like flow pattern and a recurring congestion chokepoint during lunch transition periods.” (FAC, ¶ 24.) Further, “this funnel/chokepoint condition of NIMITZ’s lunch area and walkways materially increased the danger faced by students on campus because it (1) forced students into close proximity in a congested travel path, (2) allowed groups of students to gather and move together rapidly through a narrowed corridor, and (3) increased the likelihood that confrontations would escalate into physical violence before staff could intervene.” (FAC, ¶ 25.) The FAC alleges that “the lunch-area configuration and table placement created a substantial risk of injury when the property was used with due care in a manner that was reasonably foreseeable—specifically, use by students moving in large numbers during scheduled lunch transitions.” (FAC, ¶ 28.)

 

Defendant first argues that the physical configuration of the lunch area does not create a substantial risk of injury when used with due care by students because it was at most congested and congestion is inherent in school transitions.

 

Taking the allegations as true, as required on demurrer, the Court finds that the FAC adequately pleads a dangerous condition. Defendant’s argument requires the Court to assume that the layout and resulting congestion cannot constitute a dangerous condition, which is not permitted on demurrer.

 

Next, the parties dispute the applicability of Zelig, supra, 27 Cal.4th at p. 1134. In Zelig, the Supreme Court of California notes the general rule “that ‘third party conduct by itself, unrelated to the condition of the property, does not constitute a “dangerous condition” for which a public entity may be held liable.’” (Ibid.) Moreover, “ ‘[i]f the risk of injury from third parties is in no way increased or intensified by any condition of the public property ... courts ordinarily decline to ascribe the resulting injury to a dangerous condition of the property. In other words, there is no liability for injuries caused solely by acts of third parties. [Citations.] Such liability can arise only when third party conduct is coupled with a defective condition of property.’” (Id., at p. 1137, citations omitted.)

 

Defendant argues that the FAC fails to plead that the lunch pavilion area constituted a dangerous condition in the absence of third-party conduct by the alleged student perpetrators. Defendant argues that the FAC alleges that Plaintiff’s injuries arose from the student pulling Plaintiff’s hair, not from being trapped or unable to move due to the chokepoint condition.

 

In opposition, Plaintiff argues that the FAC does not allege that the assault constituted the dangerous condition, rather, it alleges that the physical configuration of the lunch area increased the likelihood that students would be surrounded and assaulted and that the staff would be limited in its ability to intervene. Plaintiff argues that Zelig is distinguishable. In Zelig, plaintiffs alleged a dangerous condition of public property after their mother was shot by her former husband at a courthouse. (Zelig, supra, 27 Cal.4th at p. 1118-19.) The Supreme Court of California concluded that the complaint failed to allege causation, reasoning that “the risk of injury was not increased or intensified by the condition of the property, and the necessary causal connection between the condition of the property and [the] crime was not present.” (Id., at p. 1138.) Further, the High Court noted that the risk of injuries was as great outside the courthouse or while picking up her children from a public or private school. (Ibid.) Lastly, the High Court reasoned that despite alleging “long corridors, hidden escalators, and maze-like design of the building,” the pleading failed to allege that these conditions had a causal relationship to the shooting. (Ibid.) Plaintiff argues that the facts here are governed by Bonanno v. Central Contra Costa Transit Authority (2003) 30 Cal.4th 139, 152, which recognizes that a dangerous condition of public property exists when “some characteristic of the property that exposed its users to increased danger from third party negligence or criminality.”

 

Here, the Court agrees with Plaintiff that the FAC sufficiently alleges that the physical configuration of the lunch area increased the risk of injuries to Plaintiff. The FAC alleges that “[a]s a result of the funnel/chokepoint configuration of the lunch area and walkways, Plaintiff was effectively forced into and through a congested location where the crowd was able to follow, surround, and assault Plaintiff before staff could timely intervene.” (FAC, ¶ 27.) Unlike Zelig, the FAC alleges that the configuration was a cause of Plaintiff’s injuries by increasing the likelihood of danger from third parties. Therefore, the FAC alleges that the third party conduct together with the physical configuration of the lunch area caused Plaintiff’s injuries, which is sufficient under both Zelig and Bonanno.

 

Lastly, Defendant argues that the FAC fails to allege a foreseeable risk of injury or notice of a dangerous condition. Defendant shows that the FAC at most alleges knowledge or congestion, but not of fights, violence, or assaults by other students.

 

“A public entity had constructive notice of a dangerous condition…only if the plaintiff establishes that the condition had existed for such a period of time and was of such an obvious nature that the public entity, in the exercise of due care, should have discovered the condition and its dangerous character.” (Gov. Code, § 835.2, subd. (b).)

 

Here, the FAC alleges that because the configuration was inherent to the lunch area, it existed in a recurring basis and “due to the daily recurrence and predictability,” Defendant should have known of the risks. (FAC, ¶ 29.) The FAC alleges that Defendant failed to take measures such as modifying the layout, providing access routes, or reducing congestion points. (FAC, ¶ 30.) The Court agrees that the FAC alleges Defendant’s notice as to the existence of the physical configuration and possibly of congestion, not of any assaults by students. Plaintiff attempts to raise additional facts in opposition to support notice, however, the Court cannot consider extrinsic evidence. (See Hahn, supra, 147 Cal.App.4th at p. 747.) Therefore, the FAC fails to allege sufficient facts to support actual or constructive notice of the alleged dangerous condition.

 

Accordingly, Defendant’s demurrer to the first cause of action in the first amended complaint is SUSTAINED with 30 days LEAVE TO AMEND.
