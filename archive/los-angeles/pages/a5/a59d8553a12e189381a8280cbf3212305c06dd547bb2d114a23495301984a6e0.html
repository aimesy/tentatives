DEPARTMENT 833 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 25STCP04451    Hearing Date: August 14, 2026    Dept: 833

 

El Rovia Mobile Home Park, LLC, v. City of El Monte (Case No. 25STCP04451)

 

August 14, 2026

Department 833

                                   

 

Order Denying Petition for Writ of Mandate

           

INTRODUCTION

 

             Petitioner El Rovia Mobile Home Park, LLC (“Petitioner”)  moves for a writ of mandate directing the Court to overturn the City of El Monte’s (“Respondent”) denial of Petitioner’s application for a fair return rent increase for its mobilehome park in 2021.

 

The Court concludes as follows:

-        The Hearing Officer did not err in allowing the City to apply a new phase-in calculation to the fair return for unrealized rent increases as applied by the City

 

-        The Hearing Officer did not err in allowing the City to pro-rate the inflation-adjusted Base Year Net Operating Income (“NOI”) as well as the comparison year NOI for only non-exempt spaces or rent control spaces as applied by the City

 

Therefore, the Court DENIES the petition for writ of mandate.

  

 

BACKGROUND

 

A.    Rent Control in El Monte

 

The City of El Monte’s Mobilehome Park Rent Stabilization Ordinance No. 2860 (“Ordinance”) has several purposes:

 

1.     Prevent excessive and unreasonable increases in mobilehome park space rents.

2.     Preserve the availability of available mobilehome park spaces in the city.

3.     Enable mobilehome owners to preserve their equity in their mobilehomes.

4.      Permit mobilehome park owners to receive a fair return.

5.      Help preserve the affordability of space rents within the city.

 

(AR 4-5.)

 

The Ordinance authorizes a mobilehome park owner to petition the City for a fair return rent adjustment (“Fair Return”) under El Monte Municipal Code (“EMMC”) section 8.70.090. (AR 18-22.) A Fair Return increase is appropriate if the annual Consumer Price Index (“CPI”) increase does not provide the park owner with a “fair and reasonable return.” (AR 12.) It is presumed that the net operating income, as received by a park owner in the base year, provided the park owner with a fair and reasonable return. (AR 12.) Under EMMC section 8.70.090, subd. (C)(3)(e), the Fair Return must be supported by “[e]vidence documenting the income and expenses claimed by the park owner.” (AR 18.)

 

EMMC section 8.70.080, subd. (E)(5)(a)(viii) limits eligible legal expenses to those “incurred in connection with successful good faith attempts to recover rents owing” and “necessarily incurred in dealings with respect to the normal operation of the park.” (AR 15.)

 

According to EMMC section 8.70.090, subd. (D)(2), a City Manager decision on a Fair Return petition may be appealed within twenty calendar days, and the appealing party is responsible for paying the costs of the appeal. (AR 20.) Administrative hearings, such as those conducted by the Office of Administrative Hearings (“OAH”) are conducted in accordance with such rules and regulations as promulgated by the El Monte City Council or the hearing officer who presides over the hearing. (AR 21.) EMMC section 8.70.090, subd. (D)(15) provides that any party alleging the Administrative Law Judge (“ALJ”)’s decision contains mathematical or clerical inaccuracies in the decision may so notify the hearing officer and the other parties within fifteen calendar days of the mailing of the decision, and the ALJ may issue a revised decision with ten business days. (AR 23.)

 

The City utilizes a third-party consultant, RSG Solutions, Inc. (“RSG”), to process fair return petitions and provide recommendations to the City Manager. (AR 100.) EMMC section 8.70.090, subd. (C)(7) provides for “employment of experts.” (AR 19.)

 

B.    The 2021 Fair Return

 

On October 17, 2022, Petitioner submitted a Fair Return for year 2021. (AR 36.) On October 18, 2023, the City Manager issued a decision that Petitioner was not entitled to a permanent rent increase. (AR 100.) The decision did, however, award Petitioner a temporary rent increase of $6.30 per space for eligible legal and capital improvement expenses for a period of five years. (AR 100.)

 

On November 3, 2023, Petitioner appealed the City Manager’s decision. (AR 632.) The ALJ issued his decision on August 26, 2025. (AR 770-816.)The ALJ affirmed the City’s denial of Petitioner’s requested permanent rent increase. (AR 816.) The ALJ found Petitioner failed to meet its burden of showing that the City’s decisions to offset unrealized rent increases and to prorate operating expenses (but not income) to rent-controlled spaces were unreasonable or erroneous. (AR 815.)

 

The petition followed.

LEGAL STANDARD

 

Under Code of Civil Procedure section 1094.5, subd. (b), the pertinent issues are whether the respondent has proceeded without jurisdiction, whether there was a fair trial, and whether there was a prejudicial abuse of discretion.  An abuse of discretion is established if the agency has not proceeded in the manner required by law, the decision is not supported by the findings, or the findings are not supported by the evidence.  (Code Civ. Pro. § 1094.5, subd. (b).)

 

The ALJ’s actions are presumed to be supported by substantial evidence and the petitioner has the burden of proving otherwise. (Colony Cove Properties, LLC v. City of Carson (2013) 220 Cl.App.4th 840, 866.) Substantial evidence is evidence of “ponderable legal significance…reasonable in nature, credible, and of solid value” that a “reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” (H.N. & Frances C. Berger Foundation v. City of Escondido (2005) 127 Cal.App.4th 1, 7.)

 

In applying the substantial evidence standard, the Court does not reweigh the evidence, and must resolve all conflicts and reasonable doubts in favor of the ALJ’s findings and decision. (See Center for Biological Diversity v. County of San Bernardino (2010) 185 Cal.App.4th 866, 881-882.)

 

“On questions of law arising in mandate proceedings, [the court] exercise[s] independent judgment.’”  (Christensen v. Lightbourne (2017) 15 Cal.App.5th 1239, 1251.)  The interpretation of statute or regulation is a question of law.  (See State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. v. Quackenbush (1999) 77 Cal.App.4th 65, 77.)   In addition, “[a] challenge to the procedural fairness of the administrative hearing is reviewed de novo on appeal because the ultimate determination of procedural fairness amounts to a question of law.”  (Nasha L.L.C. v. City of Los Angeles (2004) 125 Cal.App.4th 470, 482.) 

 

DISCUSSION

 

A.         Petitioner’s argument that Respondent’s process lacked a legal basis is unpersuasive

 

Petitioner first argues that Respondent’s expert, RSG, Inc. “made…major changes in 2021 that were inconsistent with both the Ordinance and how the four prior applications were processed with respect to providing a new additional calculation for unrealized phase-in rent increases…” (Opening Brief (“OB”), 10:16-18.) In essence, this major change involved the City of El Monte’s decision to offset unrealized rent increases to prevent the duplication of these increases. According to Petitioner, this change was “a clear violation of Petitioner’s due process rights” and “was done solely in order to prevent the Park any permanent rent increase.” (OB, 10:10-13.)

 

 Petitioner’s argument is unpersuasive. First, Petitioner cites no law explaining why the change in process was “a clear violation of Petitioner’s due process rights.” Petitioner cites no evidence indicating that the change in process was done solely to prevent any permanent rent increase. Petitioner’s inability to connect the dots in his papers is fatal to the Court granting the petition.

 

As the City of El Monte explains, the methodology Petitioner used in 2021 has previously been upheld by California courts. In Carson Harbor, a rental board allocated a mobilehome park’s $190,333 expense over a three-year period based on a 28-month loan after the owner sought to deduct it in full in a single year. (Carson Harvor Village, Ltd. v. City of Carson Mobilehome Park Rental Review Bd. (1999) 70 Cal.App.4th 281, 292 (Carson Harbor).) The Court of Appeal reasoned that expensing the entire cost in one year would overstate the park’s operating expenses, understate its gross profit, and “artificially inflate [owner’s] need for a monthly rental increase.” (Id., at p. 292.) Failing to account for it would lead to a “permanent, artificially high monthly rent increase” that “would permit [owner] to realize unwarranted profits from increased revenues,” a result “inconsistent with the overarching goal of protecting residents through rent control laws.” (Ibid.)

 

Here, as the ALJ found, a deflated income artificially inflates the Park’s apparent need for a rent increase, producing a “duplicative” increase that compounds income the Park “was scheduled to receive in the near future when the unrealized rent increases were implemented.” (AR 786.) The City of El Monte corrected that understatement exactly as the board in Carson Harbor did. (AR 0786-787.) Although the specific application of the offsetting differs between Carson Harbor and this case, the effect is the same.

 

The absence of an express offsetting provision in the Ordinance does not render the City of El Monte’s approach improper. As the Court of Appeal explained in Carson Harbor, when an “ordinance does not address the Board’s authority to distinguish between extraordinary non-recurring expenses and normal operating expenses, it is within the Board’s authority to make such a distinction if it relates to the impact of a rent increase on mobilehome park residents.” (Carson Harbor, supra, 70 Cal.App.4th at p. 293.)

 

Petitioner argues that because the City of El Monte had never done an analysis before, it was not appropriate to do so here. This argument is unsupported. No law states that the City of El Monte must conduct its fair return analysis in any specific manner, or must do its analysis every year in the same way.

 

Exercising its independent judgment, the Court concludes that ALJ had a legal basis for allowing offsetting.

 

B.    Petitioner’s argument that Respondent’s process lacked an evidentiary basis is equally unpersuasive

 

Petitioner argues that Respondent has never before engaged in an offsetting analysis, and so Respondent’s conclusions are not entitled to deference. This is not true. Substantial evidence indicates Respondent had a basis for deciding to engage in offsetting. For example, Respondent’s expert, RSG, applied a comparable adjustment in Oceanside, California. (AR 748-762.)

 

The ALJ found that RSG’s staff, Ms. Dominique Clark, had “unique and broad experience in her role analyzing rent increase petitions for diverse mobilehome parks throughout Southern California, an intimate working knowledge of the Ordinance, as well as experience analyzing similar rent control provisions in other jurisdictions.” (AR 796.) In contrast, after assessing the experts “based on the facts supporting them and their logical nexus to the record[,]” the ALJ found Petitioner’s expert [Mr. Eid] unpersuasive on every point he raised on the offsetting issue. (AR 796.)

 

The City of El Monte and RSG recognized that the combination of annual Fair Returns and phasing created a risk of “bigger rent increases in the future,” and offset the unrealized phases to avoid that unintended consequence. (AR 988.) RSG recommended this approach because “in the absence of specific instruction on how to handle something” the goal is to “follow the spirit of the ordinance which is to prevent excessive rent increases while also providing the park ownership their Constitutional right to a fair return.” (AR 989.)

 

Thus, substantial evidence supports the ALJ’s determination that the City’s decision “to offset the unrealized rent increases was within the broad zone of reasonableness afforded to the City in reviewing the 2021 [Fair Return]”—i.e., a reasonable response to changing circumstances consistent with the Ordinance. (AR 0812.)

 

Meanwhile, substantial evidence indicates that Petitioner’s argument that the “phase-in approach of the prior rent increases has caused permanent losses in the form of uncollected rents” is not true. (OB, 11:16-11:19.) Petitioner failed to implement rent increases on its exempt spaces in 2021, even though it had the authority to do so, and despite those long-term lease spaces being subject to automatic annual increases. (AR 67-68; 915 at 99:10-17; 953.) The ALJ relied on this evidence, finding that because Petitioner “failed in 2021 to implement annual rent increases for exempt spaces with the same rigor as it has sought increases for the rent-controlled spaces,” that “dynamic tends to show park owner was not concerned in 2021 with its return on investment for El Rovia.” (AR 811.)

 

Thus, substantial evidence supports what the City of El Monte did, and there are no grounds for the Court granting this petition.

 

C.    Petitioner’s argument that the City did not appropriately pro-rate the inflation-adjusted Base Year NOI as well as the comparison year NOI is unpersuasive

 

Petitioner argues that the “City, for the first time, began pro-rating the inflation-adjusted Base Year NOI as well as the comparison year NOI for only non-exempt spaces or rent control spaces. While this may result in a similar outcome as a calculation with no pro-rating, (1) the effect is uncertain without performing the actual calculation and (2) it is inconsistent with the prior four years of analyses performed by the same expert. It is also not discussed in the Ordinance.” (OB, 14:18-23.)

            Petitioner’s argument is unconvincing. First, Petitioner can show prejudice if the City of El Monte’s calculations “may result in a similar outcome as a calculation with no pro-rating.” (OB, 14:20-21.) Petitioner again offers no logical argument for why the City is compelled to conduct its analysis in the same way every year. (OB, 14:21-22.)

 

            The rest of Petitioner’s argument is not very clear. Petitioner appears to critique RSG’s decision to allocate rent from 21 non-exempt spaces using a ratio of non-exempt spaces to total spaces to allocate expenses. (OB, 15:12-13.) Petitioner argues that the same ratio should have been used for both revenues and expenses in the Comparison Year. (OB, 15:21-22.)

 

            In a petition for writ of mandate, the fact that Petitioner wishes an alternative method was used for calculations is irrelevant. Instead, the real question is whether the hearing officer’s adoption of the City of El Monte’s data and analysis was so outside the bounds of reason so as to constitute an abuse of discretion. (TG Oceanside, L.P. v. City of Oceanside (2007) 156 Cal.App.4th 1355, 1384.)

 

            Petitioner does not meet that mark. California law has confirmed that what RSG did for the City of El Monte is perfectly acceptable: “Attempting to segregate out both income and related expenses for the unregulated spaces would create an accounting nightmare. Apportioning expenses would be highly subjective and difficult to verify.” (Id., at p. 1385.)

 

The AJJ’s made clear that the ALJ’s determination was based on substantial evidence. (AR 805-810.)

 

In sum, substantial evidence supports what the City of El Monte did, and there are no legal grounds for the Court granting this petition.

 

D.    Petitioner’s other arguments are unpersuasive

 

Petitioner argues that a conflict arose when RSG consulted with the City Attorney, which represented City at the ALJ hearing and in this writ proceeding. (OB, 13:13-17.) Petitioner also argues that the City of El Monte told RSG how to calculate the fair return and provide a net zero result. (OB, 13:15-17.)

 

These arguments lack merit. Both RSG and the City Attorney are advisors to the City. The City Attorney is statutorily charged with advising City officials and staff on legal matters concerning City business. (Gov. Code, § 41801.) The Court identifies no issues when RSG consulted with the City Attorney.

 

Petitioner points to no evidence that the City of El Monte instructed RSG to engineer a particular outcome, either.

 

E.     CONCLUSION

 

The Court DENIES the petition for writ of mandate.

 

Pursuant to Local Rule 3.231, subd. (n), the City of El Monte shall prepare, serve, and ultimately file a proposed judgment.

 

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

[Tentative] Order Sustaining Demurrer to Petition for Writ of Mandate

 

 

Respondents City of Los Angeles (“City”), Los Angeles Animal Services (“LAAS”), Los Angeles Police Department (“LAPD”), Karen Bass, and Annette Ramirez (“Respondents”) demur to the entirety of the Petition (“Petition”) filed by Petitioner Stand up for Pits Foundation (“Petitioner”). Respondents also move to strike various portions of the Petition.

 

The Court SUSTAINS the Demurrer to the first three claims with NO LEAVE TO AMEND. The Court SUSTAINS the Demurrer to the fourth claim with LEAVE TO AMEND. Since the mandamus actions have been dismissed, the Court will transfer the remainder of the case to Department 534 (formerly Department 1) so that a third amended petition raising only negligence can proceed in an unlimited civil court.

 

            The motion to strike is MOOT as a result of the Court’s ruling on the demurrer.

 

BACKGROUND

 

            According to the Petition, Respondents bear statutory duties to care for impounded animals humanely, to enforce California’s animal anti-cruelty laws, and to comply with the California Public Records Act (“CPRA”) and other state transparency laws.

 

            The petition alleges that Respondents have repeatedly failed to provide veterinary care to animals in city custody, causing unnecessary suffering and imposing financial burdens on rescue organizations subsequently assuming responsibility. The petition also alleges that Respondents have failed to provide appropriate shelter to animals in the city’s care, including causing unnecessary suffering and health risks. Examples of those conditions include a failure to clean kennels, a failure to provide clean water, animals who suffer after spaying and neutering surgeries, and animals who are left tethered and starving. 

 

            Petitioner also alleges that the City has exhibited gross negligence and animal cruelty within shelters. Petitioner has been forced to expend substantial resources rescuing and rehabilitating animals the City failed to move, and has incurred at least $50,319 in veterinary and care expenses for dogs saved from Skid Row.

 

            After seeking to and failing to resolve the issues directly with Respondents, Petitioner now seeks judicial intervention compelling Respondents to comply with state law, fulfill their statutory duties, and implement remedial measures to protect public welfare and prevent ongoing animal cruelty.

 

            Petitioner has brought four claims, all under the asserted rubric of a petition for writ of mandate: (1) Violations of the Public Records Act; (2) Violations of Civil Code Section 1834; (3) Violations of Civil Code Section 1846; and (4) Negligence – Failure to Enforce Penal Code Section 597.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

 

A demurrer tests the sufficiency of a pleading, and the grounds for a demurrer must appear on the face of the pleading or from judicially noticeable matters. (Code Civil Proc. § 430.30, subd. (a); Blank v. Kirwan (1985) 39 Cal.3d 311, 318.) “We assume the truth of the allegations in the complaint, but do not assume the truth of contentions, deductions, or conclusions of law.”  (California Logistics, Inc. v. State (2008) 161 Cal.App.4th 242, 247.)  “A demurrer tests the pleadings alone and not the evidence or other extrinsic matters.”  (Hahn v. Mirda (2007) 147 Cal.App.4th 740, 747.)  The allegations in the petition must be liberally construed in favor of Petitioner on demurrer.  (See Mobil Oil Corp. v Exxon Corp. (1986) 177 Cal.App.3d 942, 947.)  “A demurrer must dispose of an entire cause of action to be sustained.”  (Poizner v. Fremont General Corp. (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 97, 119.)

 

JUDICIAL NOTICE

 

The Court grants Respondents’ request for judicial notice (“RJN”).

DISCUSSION

 

First Claim: Violation of the California Public Records Act

 

            Government Code Section 7922.535 provides:

Each agency, upon a request for a copy of records, shall, within 10 days from receipt of the request, determine whether the request, in whole or in part, seeks copies of disclosable public records in the possession of the agency and shall promptly notify the person making the request of the determination and the reasons therefor. If the agency determines that the request seeks disclosable public records, the agency shall also state the estimated date and time when the records will be made available.

            To bring a CPRA violation claim, a party must show that the files (1) qualify as public records and (2) were in possession by the agency that the party has requested the records from. (Board of Pilot Commissioners v. Superior Court (2013) 218 Cal.App.4th 577, 598.)

            Here, the Petition alleges that Petitioner submitted a public records request to LAAS on September 15, 2025, and LAAS has continued to roll its documents production, providing some documents at a time every few weeks to few months. (Petition, ¶¶ 47, 52.) Petitioner alleges that the LAAS is violating the law by not producing documents fast enough. (Petition, ¶ 56; County of San Benito v. Superior Court (2023) 96 Cal.App.5th 243, 254 [explaining documents under the PRA must be produced “promptly”].)

 

            This allegation fails as a matter of law. In Voice of San Diego v. San Diego Unified School Dist. (2026) 120 Cal.App.5th 923, 942, the Court of Appeal rejected an argument that “promptly available” means production of records must be made within “a few days or weeks, not months or years.” The Court of Appeal explained that statutes are to be interpreted by the language in which they are written, and the way the PRA is written, there is no requirement that documents are produced in a certain period of time. Therefore, Petitioner cannot bring a claim based on the premise that documents are not being produced fast enough.

 

            Petitioner also argues that they are bringing a CPRA claim against LAPD because LAPD did not state what information was being withheld as a response to a different public records request. (Petition, ¶ 64.) Finally, Petitioner argues that LAPD did not produce any relevant documents and instead directed Petitioner to an open data portal. (Petition, ¶ 65.)

 

            This allegation also fails as a matter of law. Government Code Section 7922.545, subd. (a) allows a public agency to post public records on its website and direct members of the public to the location on the internet website where the public record is posted. LAPD provided detailed information about how to use the public website. (RJN, Exh. A.) LAPD accurately described what records were being withheld: investigatory records compiled by law enforcement. (RJN, Exh. A.)

 

            Petitioner does not even seek to respond to the demurrer’s arguments about this claim in the Opposition. Therefore, Petitioner has not met its burden to show that the claim could be amended successfully.

 

            Accordingly, the Court SUSTAINS the demurrer to the first claim WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

Second and Third Claim: Violation of Civil Code Section 1834 and 1846

 

A party seeking writ relief under section 1085 must show three things: (1) There is no other plain, speedy, and adequate remedy in the ordinary course of law; (2) The respondent has a clear, present, and ministerial duty to act in a particular way; and (3) The petitioner has a clear, present, and beneficial right to the performance of that duty. (Code Civ. Proc. §§ 1085, subd. (a); 1086.)

 When a statute or ordinance defines the specific duties or course of conduct that a governing body must take, the course of conduct becomes mandatory and eliminates any element of discretion. (Senior & Disability Action v. Weber (2021) 62 Cal.App.5th 357, 364.) A statute imposes a mandatory duty on a public official only if the statute affirmatively imposes the duty and provides implementing guidelines. (Physicians Committee for Responsible Medicine v. Los Angeles Unified School Dist. (2019) 43 Cal.App.5th 175, 185 (Physicians Committee).)  Mandamus does not lie to compel a public agency to exercise discretionary powers in a particular manner, only to compel it to exercise its discretion in some manner. (Ibid.) Mandamus may also lie to require an official to exercise his discretion under a proper interpretation of the applicable law. (Common Cause v. Board of Supervisors (1989) 49 Cal.3d 432, 442.) Whether a particular statute imposes a mandatory duty is a question of statutory interpretation for the courts. (Physicians Committee, supra, 43 Cal.App.5th at p. 185.)

 

Civil Code Section 1834 provides:

 

A depositary of living animals shall provide the animals with necessary and prompt veterinary care, nutrition, and shelter, and treat them kindly. Any depositary that fails to perform these duties may be liable for civil damages as provided by law.

 

(Civ. Code § 1834.)

 

Civil Code Section 1846 provides:

 

(a)   A gratuitous depositary must use, at least, slight care for the preservation of the thing deposited.

 

(b)   A gratuitous depositary of a living animal shall provide the animal with necessary and prompt veterinary care, adequate nutrition and water, and shelter, and shall treat it humanely and, if the animal has any identification, make reasonable attempts to notify the owner of the animal’s location. Any gratuitous depositary that does not have sufficient resources or desire to provide that care shall promptly turn the animal over to an appropriate care facility.

 

(c)   If the gratuitous depositary of a living animal is a public animal shelter, shelter operated by a society for the prevention of cruelty to animals, or humane shelter, the depositary shall comply with all other requirements of the Food and Agricultural Code regarding the impounding of live animals.

 

(Civ. Code § 1846.)

            Respondents argue that both Civil Code Section 1834 and Section 1846 provides the City expansive discretion as to what is “necessary” veterinary care for any particular animal’s circumstances and what amounts to “kind” and “humane” treatment.

Petitioner argues that Civil Code Section 1834 and Section 1846 include ministerial duties and that Petitioner requests that Respondents comply with the statutory floor established by the Legislature. Petitioner further argues that Respondents have violated the obligations imposed by Civil Code section 1834 and 1846. (Petition, ¶¶ 73-80, 93-100.)

A writ of mandate will not issue if a duty is mixed with discretionary power. (Childhelp, Inc. v. City of Los Angeles (2023) 91 Cal.App.5th 224, 239 (Childhelp).) Here, Petitioner is right that there is a duty to provide necessary veterinary care and to treat animals kindly. However, Respondents are correct that City officials have discretion to determine how to carry out those duties. When a duty is mixed with discretionary power, like it is here, a writ of mandate will not issue. (California High-Speed Rail Authority v. Superior Court (2014) 228 Cal.App.4th 676, 715.)

 

Petitioner does propose additional amendments that can be added to another amended petition, such as the City’s failure to provide veterinary treatment for treatable medical conditions and the City’s decisions to euthanize animals rather than provide necessary medical care. (Opposition, 9:2-6.)

 

Neither amendment would fix the underlying issue with the petition: when a duty is mixed with discretionary power, a writ of mandate cannot issue.

 

Petitioner has not shown a likelihood that either two claims can be amended to state good causes of action.

 

Therefore, the Court SUSTAINS the demurrer to the second and third causes of action with NO LEAVE TO AMEND.

 

Fourth Claim: Negligence

 

            A public entity in California is not liable for a tortious injury caused by an act or omission of a public entity or public employee or any other person, except as otherwise provided by statute. (Gov. Code, § 815, subd. (a).) All government tort liability must be based on statute. (Hoff v. Vacaville Unified School Dist. (1998) 19 Cal.4th 925, 932.)

 

            Petitioner alleges that Respondents have negligently ignored dangerous conditions that plague City shelters and the streets of the Skid Row. (Petition, ¶ 111.) Petitioner argues that liability for this exists under Government Code Section 835, Section 815.6, and Section 820.

 

Government Code Section 835 states that:

 

a public entity is liable for injury caused by a dangerous condition of its property if the plaintiff establishes that the property was in a dangerous condition at the time of the injury, that the injury was proximately caused by the dangerous condition, that the dangerous condition created a reasonably foreseeable risk of the kind of injury which was incurred, and that either: A negligent or wrongful act or omission of an employee of the public entity within the scope of his employment created the dangerous condition; or the public entity had actual or constructive notice of the dangerous condition under Section 835.2 a sufficient time prior to the injury to have taken measures to protect against the dangerous condition.

 

Additionally, Government Code section 815.6 provides that “[w]here a public entity is under a mandatory duty imposed by an enactment that is designed to protect against the risk of a particular kind of injury, the public entity is liable for an injury of that kind proximately caused by its failure to discharge the duty unless the public entity establishes that it exercised reasonable diligence to discharge the duty.”

 

Government Code Section 820 provides that, “Except as otherwise provided by statute... a public employee is liable for injury caused by his act or omission to the same extent as a private person.”

 

            Respondents argue that this cause of action is problematic because it relies on Penal Code Section 597. There is no private right of action to enforce an alleged violation of Penal Code Section 597. (Animal Legal Defense Fund v. California Exposition & State Fairs (2015) 239 Cal.App.4th 1286, 1298.) However, the Court interprets the fourth claim as relying on Government Code Section 835, which provides a carveout from the general rule that public entities are not liable for negligence.

            Respondents argue that that the cause of action is also problematic because it does not plead every element of the negligence claim with specificity. To state a cause of action against a public entity, every act material to the existence of its statutory liability must be pleaded with particularity. (Peter W. v. San Francisco Unified School District (1976) 60 Cal.App.3d 814, 819.) It is a little unclear from the pleading what the “dangerous condition” is and Petitioner has not pled that it was reasonably foreseeable that the dangerous condition would lead to the injuries described in Paragraphs 119 and 120.  

 

            Finally, Respondents argue that Petitioner’s damages claims are defective because Petitioner has not filed leave to file a late claim against the government, and claims for personal injury must be presented no later than six months after the accrual of the cause of action. (Gov. Code § 911.2, subd. (a).) An application for leave to file a late claim must be made no more than one year after the accrual of a cause of action. (Gov. Code § 911.4, subd. (b).) There are no allegations that Petitioner obtained leave to file a late claim. Additionally, only one of the allegations mentioned in Paragraph 125 occurred under a year ago. Therefore, Petitioner cannot proceed with the negligence claim as currently pled.

 

            The Court SUSTAINS the demurrer to the fourth claim. There seems to be a reasonable probability that the fourth claim can be amended to solve the defects identified by the Court, so there with be LEAVE TO AMEND as to this claim only.

 

Inclusion of Karen Bass and Annette Ramirez as Respondents

 

            Respondents argue that Karen Bass and Annette Ramirez must be dismissed as parties. The Court makes no ruling on this matter because the Court has sustained the demurrer to all claims.

CONCLUSION 

 

             The Court SUSTAINS the Demurrer to the first three claims WITHOUT LEAVE TO AMEND. The Court SUSTAINS the Demurrer to the fourth claim with LEAVE TO AMEND. Since the mandamus actions have been dismissed, the Court transfer the case to Department 534 (formerly Department 1) for reassignment to an independent calendar court so that a third amended petition raising only negligence can proceed in an unlimited civil court.

 

            The motion to strike is MOOT as a result of the Court’s ruling on the demurrer.

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

[Tentative] Order on Application for Writ of Attachment  

 

 

INTRODUCTION

 

            Plaintiffs Randall Avazian and Irving Adlen (“Plaintiffs”) move for a writ of attachment against Defendant Graphic Visions, Inc. (“Graphic Visions”) in the amount of $402,697.36, which includes attorneys fees in the amount of $250,000.

 

The Court DENIES the Writ of Attachment.

 

LEGAL STANDARD

 

“Upon the filing of the complaint or at any time thereafter, the plaintiff may apply pursuant to this article for a right to attach order and a writ of attachment by filing an application for the order and writ with the court in which the action is brought.”  (Code Civ. Pro. § 484.010.)  “Except as otherwise provided by statute, an attachment may be issued only in an action on a claim or claims for money, each of which is based upon a contract, express or implied, where the total amount of the claim or claims is a fixed or readily ascertainable amount not less than five hundred dollars ($500) exclusive of costs, interest, and attorney's fees.”  (Code Civ. Pro. § 483.010.) 

 

The court shall issue a right to attach order if the court finds all of the following: 

 

(1)   The claim upon which the attachment is based is one upon which an attachment may be issued. 

(2)   The plaintiff has established the probable validity of the claim upon which the attachment is based. 

(3)   The attachment is not sought for a purpose other than the recovery on the claim upon which the attachment is based. 

(4)   The amount to be secured by the attachment is greater than zero.  

 

(Code Civ. Pro. § 484.090.) 

 

“A claim has ‘probable validity’ where it is more likely than not that the plaintiff will obtain a judgment against the defendant on that claim.” (Code Civ. Pro. § 481.190.)  “The application shall be supported by an affidavit showing that the plaintiff on the facts presented would be entitled to a judgment on the claim upon which the attachment is based.”¿(Code Civ. Pro. § 484.030.)¿“In contested applications, the court must consider the relative merits of the positions of the respective parties and make a determination of¿the probable outcome of the litigation.”¿  (Hobbs v. Weiss (1999) 73 Cal.App.4th 76, 80.)  “The Attachment Law statutes are subject to strict construction.” (Epstein v. Abrams (1997) 57 Cal.App.4th 1159, 1168.) 

 

DISCUSSION

 

A.    Probable Validity of Plaintiff’s Claim

 

The application is based on Plaintiff’s cause of action for breach of contract. To establish a claim for breach of contract, a plaintiff must prove: (1) the existence of a contract; (2) plaintiff’s performance or excuse for nonperformance; (3) defendant’s breach of the contract; and (4) damages incurred by plaintiff as a result of the breach.  (Durell v. Sharp Healthcare (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 1350, 1367.) 

 

Plaintiffs were previously record and beneficial owners of all of the outstanding shares of stock of Graphic Visions. (Adlen Decl., ¶ 2.) On or about June 30, 2025, Plaintiffs entered a Securities Purchase Agreement pursuant to which Defendant RG USA acquired the shares of both Plaintiffs. (Adlen Decl., ¶ 3.)

 

On or about December 31, 2024, allegedly “in conjunction” with the SPA, both Plaintiffs entered into separate long-term promissory notes with Graphic Visions, whereby Graphic Visions borrowed an amount of $277,500, at an interest rate of 2% per year, with interest due and payable monthly. (Adlen Decl., ¶ 4; Avazian Decl., ¶ 4.)

 

On or about October 15, 2025, allegedly in conjunction with the SPA, Plaintiff Adlen entered into a short-term promissory note with Graphic Visions, whereby Graphic Visions borrowed an amount of $55,000, at an interest rate of 6% and payable on or before February 15, 2026. (Adlen Decl., ¶ 5.) On or about October 15, 2025, allegedly in conjunction with the SPA, Plaintiff Avazian entered into a short-term promissory note with Graphic Visions, whereby Graphic Visions borrowed an amount of $77,000, at an interest rate of 6% and payable on or before February 15, 2026. (Avazian Decl., ¶ 5.)

 

Both Plaintiffs contend that they performed all of their obligations under both promissory notes and Graphic Visions has failed to pay any amount. (Adlen Decl., ¶ 6; Avazian Decl., ¶ 6.) According to Plaintiff Adlen, Graphic Visions owes $7,861.23 on the Adlen Long-Term Promissory Note and $57,072.05 on the Adlen Short-Term Promissory Note. (Adlen Decl., ¶ 6.) According to Plaintiff Avazian, Graphic Visions owes $7,861.23 on the long-term promissory note and $79,902.85 on the short-term promissory note. (Avazian Decl., ¶ 6.)

 

This leads a total of $152,697.40.

 

Graphic Visions argues that there are no specifics as to (1) what consideration each Plaintiff provided to Graphic Visions; (2) when and how the consideration was delivered; (3) the account or recipient that received it; (4) whether the consideration was a loan, a purchase price component, or another obligation; or (5) the corporate act authorizing Graphic Visions to incur the alleged debt.

 

However, none of these specifics are required to establish a claim for breach of contract. Plaintiffs have adequately introduced evidence of a breach of contract by introducing the promissory notes and explaining that Graphic Visions has not repaid the amount due on the notes.

 

What is more concerning is that there are no monthly interest calculations, which prevents a reasonable and definite standard by which the amount due can be ascertained.

 

For example, Adlen claims to have entered into a short-term promissory note on October 15, 2025 whereby Graphic Visions borrowed an amount of $55,000, at an interest rate of 6%, and payable on or before February 15, 2026. (Adlen Decl., ¶ 6.) Additionally, the note claims that Graphic Visions must pay Adlen $5,500 a month beginning on the first day of every month, starting in November 2025, and continuing until the loan is paid off. (Adlen Decl., ¶ 6.)

 

Adlen claims as of June 1, when the latest declaration was filed, there is $57,072.05 due on the short-term promissory note. (Adlen Decl., ¶ 6.)

 

How is there only $2,072.05 in interest that has accumulated between October 15, 2025 and June 1, 2026, when the declaration was submitted to the Court? The promissory note is unclear how frequently interest is calculated, preventing the Court from determining the amount to be attached. (Adlen Decl., Exh. 3.) Plaintiffs must produce competent evidence to show how interest is being calculated, and as of now, the evidence is unclear whether interest is being calculated monthly or yearly or by some other metric. Plaintiffs do not address this issue in their papers.

 

The long-term promissory note has the same issues. Plaintiffs allege that $7,861.23 is due on two long-term promissory notes. It is unclear how the interest is calculated, preventing the Court from verifying the amount to be attached.

 

A writ of attachment is a harsh remedy and requires the Court to strictly construe the statute. In this case, there are too many unanswered questions to warrant the Court granting the writ.

 

CONCLUSION

 

The Court DENIES the writ of attachment.

 

IT IS SO ORDERED.
