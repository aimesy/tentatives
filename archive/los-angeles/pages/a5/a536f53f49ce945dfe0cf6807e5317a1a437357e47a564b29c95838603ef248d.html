NEXGEN AIR CONDITIONING AND HEATING, LLC, A CALIFORNIA LIMITED LIABILITY COMPANY, vs ZAKI S. MANSOUR

August 5, 2026

8:30 AM

 

 

 

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: PLAINTIFF/CROSS-DEFENDANT NEXGEN AIR CONDITIONING AND HEATING, LLC’S MOTION FOR ORDER DEEMING REQUESTS FOR ADMISSION ADMITTED AND FOR SANCTIONS.

 

RULING:

The Court grants the unopposed Motion.

The truth of each matter and the genuineness of each document specified in the Requests for Admission, Set Two (Nos. 76-114), served on April 24, 2026 upon Defendant/Cross-Complainant ZAKI S. MANSOUR, are deemed admitted and conclusively established for all purposes in this action.

Defendant/Cross-Complainant ZAKI S. MANSOUR is ordered to pay to Plaintiff/Cross-Defendant NEXGEN AIR CONDITIONING AND HEATING, LLC the sum of $3,840.00 as discovery sanctions.

Plaintiff to give notice.

 

ANALYSIS:

By the Motion filed June 17, 2026, Plaintiff/Cross-Defendant moves for an Order that (1) the truth of each matter specified and the genuineness of each document specified in the Requests for Admission, Set Two served on Mr. Mansour on April 24, 2026 be deemed admitted and conclusively established for all purposes in this action; and (2) Mr. Mansour pay to NexGen the sum of $3,840.

On March 2, 2026, the Court filed the Order granting the Motion to be Relieved as Counsel of Record for ZAKI S. MANSOUR.

A motion to deem admitted requests for admissions lies based upon a showing of failure to respond timely. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (b); Demyer v. Costa Mesa Mobile Home Estates (1995) 36 Cal.App.4th 393, 395, disapproved on other grounds by Wilcox v. Birtwhistle (1999) 21 Cal.4th 973, 983.) Requests for admissions must be deemed admitted where no responses in substantial compliance were served before the hearing. (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (c).) As to motions to deem matters admitted, no meet and confer is required. (Demyer v. Costa Mesa Mobile Home Estates (1995) 36 Cal.App.4th 393, 395, overruled on other grounds by Wilcox v. Birtwhistle (1999) 21 Cal.4th 973, 983.)

Where a party never responded to requests for admissions, and never filed a motion for relief based on mistake, inadvertence or excusable neglect (Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.280, subd. (a)(2)), an order deeming admitted the requests is conclusive, and may not be contested with evidence in dispute. (People v. $2,709 United States Currency (2014) 231 Cal.App.4th 1278, 1285–1286.)

“Under Code of Civil Procedure section 2033.280, it is mandatory that the court impose a monetary sanction on the party or attorney whose failure to serve a timely response to a request for admission necessitates a motion to compel responses….” (Stover v. Bruntz (2017) 12 Cal.App.5th 19, 31–32. Accord, Appleton v. Superior Court (1988) 206 Cal.App.3d 632, 635.)

There is no opposition filed as to this matter to show substantial justification for failing to serve discovery responses.

Therefore, the Court grants the Motion, as prayed.

25STCV15517

PATRICIA JAMGOTCHIAN, et al. vs DONE! VENTURES, LLC, et al.

August 5, 2026

8:30 AM

 

 

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: DEFENDANT DONE! VENTURES, LLC’S MOTION FOR AN ASSIGNMENT ORDER PURSUANT TO CCP §708.510 AGAINST PLAINTIFF PATRICIA JAMGOTCHIAN.

 

RULING:

The Motion is granted, as prayed.

The Court will sign and file the proposed Order received on July 29, 2026.

Defendant Done! Ventures, LLC to give notice.

 

I.                    BACKGROUND

The Court incorporates by reference the Minutes entered on September 10, 2025, granting Defendants’ Anti-SLAPP Motions to Strike, as to Plaintiffs’ allegations that Defendants maliciously prosecuted collection proceedings against sole and separate property, while knowing that it could not be collected on to satisfy the underlying judgment.

By the Motion, filed July 8, 2026, DONE! VENTURES, LLC (Defendant) moves for an Order assigning the right to payments due to PATRICIA JAMGOTCHIAN (Plaintiff) from her interests in the following entities: (a) Olympic Avenue Ventures, Inc.; (b) Olympic Avenue Ventures Property, Inc.; (c) Rialto Pockets, Incorporated; and (d) Rialto Pockets Property, Inc., until such time that the March 27, 2025 order for the payment of attorneys' fees is fully paid, including any additions to the judgment and post-judgment interest.

The Opposition advocates denying, including because the Motion relies upon stale evidence, and Plaintiff’s Third-Party Claims to the interests have been denied.

 

II.                 LEGAL STANDARD

“The EJL ‘is a comprehensive scheme governing the enforcement of all civil judgments in California.’…. Under this scheme, a lien is created on all nonexempt personal property when the debtor is served with notice to appear for examination…. ‘Except as otherwise provided by law, all property of the judgment debtor is subject to enforcement of a money judgment.’ (§ 695.010, subd. (a).) However, ‘property of the judgment debtor that is not assignable or transferable is not subject to enforcement of a money judgment.’ (§ 695.030, subd. (a).).” (Juarez v. Ward (2023) 88 Cal.App.5th 730, 735.) “For purposes of the Enforcement of Judgments Law, a judgment creditor is someone ‘in whose favor a judgment is rendered.’” (See § 680.240.)” (Luxury Asset Lending, LLC v. Philadelphia Television Network, Inc. (2020) 56 Cal.App.5th 894, 914.)

California's Enforcement of Judgments Law (EJL) applies to a judgment, and includes orders. (Lucky United Properties Inv., Inc. v. Lee (2010) 185 Cal.App.4th 125, 143–144; Newland v. Superior Court (1995) 40 Cal.App.4th 608, 615; Constellation-F, LLC v. World Trading 23, Inc. (2020) 45 Cal.App.5th 22, 31.)

 

III.              ANALYSIS

Defendant filed the Motion, as a creditor, for an Order assigning rights to payments of Plaintiff, as a debtor, as to awarded attorneys’ fees. Specifically, Defendant describes the payments as amounts that Plaintiff receives from stock in four corporations running Spearmint Rhino clubs in Los Angeles and Rialto, California.

Plaintiff responds by emphasizing these points:

1.      On July 10, 2026, Judge Kevin C. Brazile issued a Judgment denying Plaintiff’s Third-Party Claims as to the payment rights, inferentially denying the claims.

2.      The Court has discretion and is to consider “all relevant factors” per Code of Civil Procedure section 708.510.

3.      The Motion relies upon payments made for 11 months ending in February 2025, without evidencing current payment rights, but instead relying upon speculation.

Code of Civil Procedure section 708.510 is a procedure within the Enforcement of Judgments Law (§ 680.010 et seq.) (EJL), allowing judgment creditors to receive involuntary assignments of judgment debtors’ interests in payments. (Wanke, Indus., Com., Residential, Inc. v. AV Builder Corp. (2020) 45 Cal.App.5th 466, 475.) The “court may order the judgment debtor to assign to the judgment creditor….” (Code of Civil Procedure §708.510.)

“An assignment order is a court order assigning to the judgment creditor or a receiver appointed pursuant to CCP § 708.610 et seq…. the debtor's right to payments due from a third person. It is authorized by CCP § 708.510 et seq….” (Cal. Prac. Guide Enf. J. & Debt § 6:1422.5.)

The treatise summarizes some applicable law, as follows:

[6:1440] Court's discretion to issue order: The court has broad discretion in determining whether to order an assignment, and in fixing the amount to be assigned (subject to the limitations discussed above, ¶ 6:1437 ff.).

The court may consider all relevant factors, including but not limited to:

·         The reasonable economic needs of a natural person judgment debtor and those supported partly or wholly by the debtor;

·         Payments the judgment debtor is required to make or that are deducted in satisfaction of other judgments and wage assignments;

·         The amount remaining due on the money judgment; and

·         The amount remaining to be received on the right to payment. [CCP § 708.510(c)]

(Id., §§ 6:1422.5-6:1448.)

 

Having read the Motion, the Court finds that Plaintiff has shown compliance with requirements of Code of Civil Procedure, sections 708.510 et seq. and 708.610 et seq.

The Court finds that the proof is sufficiently recent to reasonably infer there are still currently assignable rights to the payments that properly may be used to satisfy the creditor. In the underlying case, Plaintiff stated under penalty of perjury that the stock right was her “sole and separate property”. (See Request for Judicial Notice, filed July 8, 2026, Exhibit A). Even more broadly, Defendant references years of like payments. (See Reply, 4:16-28.) Normally, appellate courts defer to trial judges’ reasonable inferences from the evidence viewed in light of the whole record, which are not based on suspicion, speculation, surmise, conjecture, guesswork, or imagination. (E.g., In re O.M. (2026) 120 Cal.App.5th 489, 509.) Moreover, Plaintiff has not filed any contrary documentation to disprove rights to the payments.

Additionally, there is no inconsistent ruling against the payment rights. “The same rules apply in ascertaining the meaning of a court order or judgment as in ascertaining the meaning of any other writing.” (Mendly v. County of L. A. (1994) 23 Cal.App.4th 1193, 1205.) The July 10, 2026 Judgment, in DONE! Ventures, LLC vs. Jamgotchian, case number BC643275, expressly reasoned that the issues were moot, after levied sums had been applied towards Judgment satisfaction. “Mootness is sometimes defined in terms of the court's loss of ability to grant effective relief.” (Breaux v. Agricultural Labor Relations Bd. (1990) 217 Cal.App.3d 730, 743.) It is not any determination on the merits.

In summary, the Court having considered the discretionary and relevant factors pursuant to section 708.510, decides to order the assignments, as Defendant requests.

 

IV.              CONCLUSION

Accordingly, the Court grants the Motion, as prayed.

25STCV27067

BENNY BERN, et al. vs AMERICAN HONDA MOTOR CO., INC.   

 

August 5, 2026

8:30 AM

 

 

 

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: DEFENDANT AMERICAN HONDA MOTOR CO., INC.’S MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES TO SPECIAL INTERROGATORIES, SET ONE, TO PLAINTIFFS AND REQUEST FOR MONETARY SANCTIONS.  DEFENDANT AMERICAN HONDA MOTOR CO., INC.’S MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES TO REQUESTS FOR PRODUCTION, SET ONE, TO PLAINTIFFS AND REQUEST FOR MONETARY SANCTIONS.  DEFENDANT AMERICAN HONDA MOTOR CO., INC.’S MOTION TO COMPEL FURTHER RESPONSES TO REQUESTS FOR ADMISSION, SET ONE, TO PLAINTIFFS AND REQUEST FOR MONETARY SANCTIONS.

 

RULING:

The Court orders the three Motions off calendar as moot and denies sanctions.

Defendant to give notice.

 

 

I.                    BACKGROUND

On September 15, 2025, BENNY BERN and ROSIE BERN (Plaintiffs) filed a Lemon Law Complaint against AMERICAN HONDA MOTOR CO., INC. (Defendant), alleging four Causes of Action under the Song-Beverly Consumer Warranty Act (Civ. Code §§ 1790-1795.8), along with a Fifth Cause of Action for Fraudulent Inducement - Concealment.

 

Plaintiffs allege purchasing, with warranty provisions, a 2025 Honda Accord that Defendant manufactured, which had defects, including infotainment and engine. Plaintiffs further allege that Defendant failed to repair the vehicle within a reasonable number of attempts and did not promptly replace it or make restitution.

 

On July 2, 2026, Defendant filed the Motions to Compel Further Responses to Special Interrogatories, Document and Admission Requests, and for sanctions, on grounds including that objection-only responses to specified item numbers are not code-compliant.

Plaintiffs oppose, based on arguments that the Motions are moot after supplemental responses, and Defendant only met and conferred by one letter on May 20, 2026, before filing the Motions.

On August 4, 2026, the Court heard a similar Discovery Motion.

 

II.                 LEGAL STANDARD

Where respondents object or respond inadequately to discovery requests, a motion lies to compel further responses, as to which respondent has the burden to justify the objections. (Fairmont Ins. Co. v. Superior Court (2000) 22 Cal.4th 245, 255; Code Civ. Proc., §§ 2030.300, subd. (a) [motion to compel further responses lies “[o]n receipt of a response to interrogatories….”]; and 2031.310, subd. (a) [motion to compel further responses lies “[o]n receipt of a response to an inspection demand….”].) A propounding party's remedy when it deems objections in discovery responses to be without merit is to move for an order compelling further responses. (Catalina Island Yacht Club v. Superior Court (2015) 242 Cal.App.4th 1116, 1127.) “While the party propounding interrogatories may have the burden of filing a motion to compel if it finds the answers it receives unsatisfactory, the burden of justifying any objection and failure to respond remains at all times with the party resisting an interrogatory.” (Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 541.) Where respondents timely responded to requests for admissions, requesting parties may move to compel further answers based upon a showing of some deficiency. (Wimberly v. Derby Cycle Corp. (1997) 56 Cal.App.4th 618, 636; Code Civ. Proc., § 2033.290.)

Monetary discovery sanctions are mandatory as to parties losing discovery motions, unless courts find substantial justification or other injustice. (Deck v. Devs. Inv. Co. (2023) 89 Cal.App.5th 808, 830.) “‘[T]he phrase “substantial justification” has been understood to mean that a justification is clearly reasonable because it is well grounded in both law and fact.’” (Kwan Software Engineering, Inc. v. Hennings (2020) 58 Cal.App.5th 57, 75.) “If the party seeking a monetary sanction meets its burden of proof, the burden shifts to the opposing party attempting to avoid a monetary sanction to show that it acted with ‘substantial justification.’” (Doe v. U.S. Swimming, Inc. (2011) 200 Cal.App.4th 1424, 1435.) Courts may determine substantial justification based on the “whole picture” of the discovery dispute, including where parties partially prevailed as to some discovery items in dispute, or where discovery concessions occurred only after improperly asserted objections. (In re Marriage of Moore (2024) 102 Cal.App.5th 1275, 1297.)

 

III.              ANALYSIS

In one combined Reply, Defendant advocates $1,500.00 in monetary sanctions against Plaintiff’s counsel.

In the Oppositions, Plaintiffs advocate denying the Motions as moot, after supplemental responses served July 23, 2026. They add that Defendant served a meet and confer letter but failed to do anything thereafter.

Where respondents served untimely discovery responses after parties have filed motions to compel responses, courts have broad discretion as to ruling. (Sinaiko Healthcare Consulting, Inc. v. Klugman (2007) 148 Cal.App.4th 390, 409; County of San Benito v. Superior Court (2023) 96 Cal.App.5th 243, 256 [citing Sinaiko regarding court considering supplemental responses served after motion].) Under some circumstances, it may be an abuse of discretion for a court to treat a motion as being one to compel further responses, without requiring a separate motion. (St. Mary v. Superior Court (2014) 223 Cal.App.4th 762, 778.)

Here, the Court determines that the requests to compel further responses are moot after supplemental responses.

As for sanctions, the Court finds that while limited meeting and conferring occurred, it was insufficient without at least one follow-up under the circumstances where Plaintiffs had not responded to the meet and confer letter.

 

IV.              CONCLUSION

The Court orders the Motions off calendar, and denies sanctions, for reasons set forth above.

JEFF MOSES VS ROK DRINKS LTD ET AL

August 5, 2026

8:30 AM

 

 

 

NATURE OF PROCEEDINGS: PLAINTIFF’S RENEWED MOTION FOR ATTORNEY FEES AND COSTS ON REMAND FROM THE COURT OF APPEAL.

 

RULING:

The Court grants the Motion, as prayed.

Plaintiff JEFF MOSES is awarded attorneys’ fees in the total amount of $1,278,893.75, and costs in the sum of $3,140.59, against Defendants ROK DRINKS, LTD and ROK STARS PLC.

Plaintiff to give notice.

 

I.                    BACKGROUND

JEFF MOSES (Plaintiff) filed this lawsuit against ROK DRINKS, LTD., ROK STARS PLC, and JOHNATHAN KENDRICK (Defendants), alleging the following causes of action in the Second Amended Complaint (SAC):

 1. BREACH OF WRITTEN CONTRACT [AGAINST ROK DRINKS AND ROK STARS];

 2. BREACH OF IMPLIED-IN-FACT CONTRACT [AGAINST ROK DRINKS AND ROK STARS];

 3. BREACH OF THE IMPLIED COVENANT OF GOOD FAITH AND FAIR DEALING [AGAINST ROK DRINKS AND ROK STARS];

 4. QUANTUM VALEBANT/QUASI-CONTRACT [AGAINST ROK DRINKS AND ROK STARS];

 5. PROMISSORY ESTOPPEL [AGAINST ALL DEFENDANTS];

 6. FRAUD [AGAINST ALL DEFENDANTS];

 7. NEGLIGENT MISREPRESENTATION [AGAINST ALL DEFENDANTS];

 8. VIOLATION OF CAL. BUS. & PROF. C. § 17200 ET SEQ. [AGAINST ALL DEFENDANTS];

 9. INTENTIONAL INTERFERENCE WITH PROSPECTIVE ECONOMIC RELATIONS [AGAINST ALL DEFENDANTS]; and

 10. NEGLIGENT INTERFERENCE WITH PROSPECTIVE ECONOMIC ADVANTAGE [AGAINST ALL DEFENDANTS].

Plaintiff alleges that Defendants refused to comply with their contractual obligations to compensate Plaintiff for a trademark and other related assets.

After a jury trial, on June 17, 2024, the Court filed a Further Amended Judgment as to Plaintiff and Defendants ROK DRINKS LTD. and ROK STARS PLC.

On May 11, 2026, Los Angeles Superior Court filed a Remittitur with an unpublished Opinion reversing Orders, based upon some aspects of the previously assigned Judge’s attorneys’ fees rulings, and remanding for the calculation of Plaintiff’s attorneys’ fees amounts. Specifically, per page 16 of the opinion, the matter was “remanded for the trial court to calculate Moses’s attorney fees.” There are no other directions given to follow. After an appeal and remittitur, trial courts are authorized to act solely in accordance with the directions of the reviewing court, and any other ruling is void. (MHC Financing Ltd. Partnership Two v. City of Santee (2010) 182 Cal.App.4th 1169, 1175, fn.4; Karlsen v. Superior Court (2006) 139 Cal.App.4th 1526, 1530.)

On June 3, 2026, Plaintiff filed the Motion for Attorneys’ Fees, requesting these amounts: 1) $1,239,762.50 in attorney’s fees incurred at the trial court level, 2) $39,131.25 in attorney’s fees incurred on appeal, and 3) $3,140.59 in costs incurred on appeal.

Defendants ROK DRINKS, LTD and ROK STARS PLC oppose, advocating denial or reduced amounts, as not being reasonably incurred.

 

II.                 LEGAL STANDARD

In supporting attorneys’ fees motions, “‘there is no required level of detail that counsel must achieve.’” (Syers Properties III, Inc. v. Rankin (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 691, 699.) “Block billing occurs when ‘a block of time [is assigned] to multiple tasks rather than itemizing the time spent on each task.’ ” (Mountjoy v. Bank of Am., N.A. (2016) 245 Cal.App.4th 266, 279.) Records amounting to vague block billing are not objectionable per se, but are a risky choice, because moving parties have the burden to support fees requests, and the strategy may lead to trial courts’ discretionary curtailing of the number of compensable hours. (Christian Research Inst. v. Alnor (2008) 165 Cal.App.4th 1315, 1325.)

“[V]erified time statements of the attorneys, as officers of the court, are entitled to credence in the absence of a clear indication the records are erroneous.” (Horsford v. Board Of Trustees Of California State Etc. (2005) 132 Cal.App.4th 359, 396 [deciding it was an abuse of discretion to completely disregard counsel’s time records based upon finding numerous instances of overlapping work].)

“In determining what constitutes a reasonable attorney fee when a contract or statute provides for such an award, courts should consider the nature of the litigation, its difficulty, the amount involved, and the skill required and success of the attorney's efforts, his or her learning, age and experience in the particular type of work demanded, the intricacies and importance of the litigation, the labor and necessity for skilled legal training and ability in trying the cause, and the time consumed.” (Contractors Labor Pool, Inc. v. Westway Contractors, Inc. (1997) 53 Cal.App.4th 152, 168.)

“An across-the-board reduction in hours claimed based on the percentage of total time entries that were flawed, without respect to the number of hours that were actually included in the flawed entries, is not a legitimate basis for determining a reasonable attorney fee award.” (Mountjoy v. Bank of America, N.A. (2016) 245 Cal.App.4th 266, 282.) An across-the-board percentage reduction in attorney fees may not be a legitimate basis for determining attorney fee awards, except where the reduction reflects a determination of fees reasonably incurred. (Morris v. Hyundai Motor Am. (2019) 41 Cal.App.5th 24, 39-40.)

Attorneys’ collaboration and division in creating work product, does not necessarily amount to noncompensable duplication, unless otherwise evidenced. (Premier Med. Mgmt. Sys., Inc. v. California Ins. Guarantee Assn. (2008) 163 Cal.App.4th 550, 562. Cf. McKenzie v. Ford Motor Co. (2015) 238 Cal.App.4th 695, 698, 703 [reversing a judge’s denial of fees based on instances of duplicative work].)

As for overstaffing, a court “‘indicated a fee reduction was warranted because it was unreasonable to have so many lawyers staffing a … case that did not present complex or unique issues, did not involve discovery motions, and did not go to trial.’” (Mikhaeilpoor v. BMW of North America, LLC (2020) 48 Cal.App.5th 240, 253.) “[I]t is appropriate for a trial court to reduce a fee award based on its reasonable determination that a routine, non-complex case was overstaffed to a degree that significant inefficiencies and inflated fees resulted.” (Morris v. Hyundai Motor Am. (2019) 41 Cal.App.5th 24, 39.)

In calculating a reasonable value of attorney services and hourly rates, courts may consider their knowledge and familiarity with the legal market, the experience, skill, and reputation of the attorney requesting fees, the difficulty or complexity of the litigation, affidavits from attorneys regarding prevailing fees in the community, and rate determinations of other cases. (569 East County Boulevard LLC v. Backcountry Against the Dump, Inc. (2016) 6 Cal.App.5th 426, 437.) In setting the hourly rate for an attorney fees award, courts are entitled to consider the rate of “‘fees customarily charged by that attorney and others in the community for similar work.’" (Bihun v. AT&T Information Systems, Inc. (1993) 13 Cal.App.4th 976, 997 [affirming rate of $450 per hour), overruled on other grounds by Lakin v. Watkins Associated Indus. (1993) 6 Cal.4th 644, 664.) Judges are not required to award the lower market rate of attorneys’ fees, and may have discretion to determine that the actual rate charged was reasonable. (Syers Properties III, Inc. v. Rankin (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 691, 702.) Based on an undisputed record, a trial court's award of $550 per hour was lower than the comparable, market rate for similarly experienced attorneys in the area, and thus the order was reversible. (See Caldera v. Dept. of Corrections & Rehab. (2020) 48 Cal.App.5th 601, 611.) A “trial court properly determined the market rate for experienced appellate lawyers in Los Angeles County …” and reduced the hourly rate for one of the partners. (Pasternack v. McCullough (2021) 65 Cal.App.5th 1050, 1058-1059.) For purposes of determining a reasonable hourly rate for attorneys’ fees, the “relevant ‘community’ is generally based on where the services are rendered, i.e., where the court is located.” (Tidrick v. FCA US LLC (2025) 112 Cal.App.5th 1147, 1157.)

Support services, including expenses related to secretaries and paralegals, are includable in an award for attorneys’ fees. (City of Oakland v. McCullough (1996) 46 Cal.App.4th 1, 7.) As to parties prevailing in an action on a contract with terms for recovery of attorney fees and costs, counsel's disbursement costs, such as secretarial, copying, telephone, expert fees and travel, are recoverable as a part of attorney fees, “if they represent expenses ordinarily billed to a client and are not included in the overhead component of counsel's hourly rate.” (Bussey v. Affleck (1st Dist. 1990) 225 Cal.App.3d 1162, 1166-1167 (“Bussey”), abrogated by Hsu v. Semiconductor Sys., Inc. (2005) 126 Cal.App.4th 1330, 1342 [noting split of authority]. But see, e.g., Benson v. Kwikset Corp. (4th Dist. 2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 1254, 1283 [recognizing split of authority]; Fairchild v. Park (2d Dist. 2001) 90 Cal.App.4th 919, 930 [disagreeing with Bussey opinion, and following view that attorney fees and expenses are distinctly different by statute]; Robert L. Cloud & Assocs., Inc. v. Mikesell (1st Dist. 1999) 69 Cal.App.4th 1141, 1154 [“attorney fees do not include expenses….”].)

The determination of a reasonable amount of attorney fees is within the sound discretion of trial courts. (PLCM Group v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095; Akins v. Enterprise Rent-A-Car Co. (2000) 79 Cal.App.4th 1127, 1134.)

 

III.              ANALYSIS

In the Reply, Plaintiff represents that:

1.      The Court of Appeal remanded the matter for a required determination of the amount of fees.

2.      The hours worked, tasks performed, and hourly rates, are established by exhibits with the Motion and declarations in the Court’s record since 2023.

3.      The Opposition is accompanied by no evidence to meet the Defendants’ burden to challenge specific items beyond just making generalized arguments and assertions.

4.      The fees correlate with this case pending since 2018 until Plaintiff’s successful Judgment.

5.      Two attorneys handled the case over eight years, through demurrer, extensive discovery matters, a jury trial, post-trial motions, and appellate proceedings.

6.      The attorneys did not duplicate tasks but collaborated and did complementary work.

7.      The hourly billing rates have been consistently the same Los Angeles market rate-- $875 and $575.

8.      An across-the-board reduction percentage is unauthorized by law.

9.      Even where there is a contingency fee agreement, the prevailing party is entitled to reasonable attorney fees (Nightingale v. Hyundai Motor Am. (1994) 31 Cal.App.4th 99, 105, fn.6).

10.  References to attorneys doing clerical work were incidental, minimal, and efficient.

11.  The Opposition says nothing about Plaintiff’s attorney’s fees on appeal.

 

Defendants ROK DRINKS, LTD and ROK STARS PLC advance the following main points:

1.      The billing records demonstrate excessive duplication and overstaffing.

2.      The fee request includes excessive attorney conferencing and internal communications.

3.      The records reflect clerical and administrative tasks improperly billed as attorneys’ fees.

4.      Extensive block billing prevents meaningful review of the claimed hours.

5.      The records reflect a lack of billing judgment.

6.      An across-the-board reduction is appropriate.

 

 

“ ‘In challenging attorney fees as excessive because too many hours of work are claimed, it is the burden of the challenging party to point to the specific items challenged, with a sufficient argument and citations to the evidence. General arguments that fees claimed are excessive, duplicative, or unrelated do not suffice.’ ” (Lunada Biomedical v. Nunez (2014) 230 Cal.App.4th 459, 488. Accord, Etcheson v. FCA US LLC (2018) 30 Cal.App.5th 831, 848.) Parties opposing motions for attorneys' fees fail to show any abuse of discretion where they merely contend that amounts of attorneys' fees are excessive without providing a specific analysis or factual support. (Mallard v. Progressive Choice Ins. Co. (2010) 188 Cal.App.4th 531, 545; Raining Data Corp. v. Barrenechea (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 1363, 1376 [an “‘assertion [that] is unaccompanied by any citation to the record or any explanation of which fees were unreasonable or duplicative’ is insufficient to disturb the trial court's discretionary award of attorney fees.”]; Maughan v. Google Technology, Inc. (2006) 143 Cal.App.4th 1242, 1250; Avikian v. Wtc Fin. Corp. (2002) 98 Cal.App.4th 1108, 1119; Premier, supra, 163 Cal.App.4th at p. 560 [emphasizing that opposing parties “submitted no evidence that the hours claimed by counsel were excessive,” and declining to “declare as a matter of law that the hours were unreasonable”]; Villanueva v. City of Colton (2008) 160 Cal.App.4th 1188, 1204 [opposing party “offered no evidence of any kind which might have warranted a reduced fee award.”].)

Here, the Opposition is accompanied by no evidence to meet the burden to disprove any specified fee items. Primarily, it contains unsupported, broad-sweeping assertions, as illustrated by this excerpt: “Plaintiff seeks to recover a whopping $1.2 million in attorneys’ fees based on inflated, improperly block billed time entries that are riddled with charges for clerical tasks, wholly unreasonable duplicative work (arising in part from blatant overstaffing), and excessive hourly rates for purported time that, due to the contingency agreement in place, was never actually billed to Plaintiff.” (Opposition, 1:3-7.)   

As for the unsupported and generalized assertions in the Opposition referencing Plaintiff’s own evidence, the Court has reviewed Plaintiff’s billing documentation with the two Declarations filed on June 3, 2026, and observes that the proof is sufficiently detailed in descriptions to ascertain that all claimed hours are reasonable. And any instances of block billing are relatively few amongst the many other entries in the billing records, and they mostly involve logically interrelated subject matters. (See Opposition, 7:13-28.)

That only two attorneys worked on the same subject matters or work product, does not necessarily equate with overstaffing or attorneys’ duplicating the specific contributions. (See, Opposition, 4:20-5:19.) The issue of overstaffing typically has involved debates over five-to-seven attorneys billing.

The Court lacks sufficient information of the substance of internal conferences to soundly decide that they were excessive. (See Opposition, 5:20-6:10.) Moreover, the cited federal trial court rulings do not govern in California courts. California appellate courts are not bound to follow decisions of lower federal courts. (People v. Superior Court (2002) 103 Cal.App.4th 409, 431.)

Further, the Court finds that the billing rates are reasonable and commonly charged in this locality.

Next, courts do not routinely apply an across-the-board percentage, but restrict that to determining the hours reasonably incurred, whereas Defendants suggested random percentages of 40-to-60 percent. (See, Opposition, 8:25-9:12.)

In summary, while the amounts involved are high compared to most cases, this action is uniquely aged, it having been filed in 2018. Further, it has been hotly litigated by both sides, often in great detail, and has involved days of trial after some extensive rulings requiring increased attorney and judicial time investments into this case. Indeed, the official Court docket has 58 computer screens for this case, far exceeding the number of docket pages in almost all other cases.

 

IV.              CONCLUSION

Therefore, the Court grants the Motion, as prayed.
