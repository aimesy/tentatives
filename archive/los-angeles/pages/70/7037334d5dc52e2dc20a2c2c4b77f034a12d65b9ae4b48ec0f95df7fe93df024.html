[THE FOLLOWING IS A TENTATIVE RULING IN THE ABOVE CASE]:

 

Deny.

 

I.                BACKGROUND

 

This action arises from an alleged bicycle versus automobile collision, which plaintiff Zeferino Juarez Vazquez (“Plaintiff”) alleges occurred on or about June 2, 2021, in Panorama City, California. (Complaint, ¶ 19.)

On June 1, 2023, Plaintiff filed a complaint against defendants Uber Technologies, Inc., Raiser, LLC, Raiser-CA, LLC, Raiser-DC, LLC, Raiser-PA, LLC, Caeros O. Durias (“Defendant”), PV Holding Corp., and DOES 1 to 70, alleging the following causes of action: (1) Negligence/Negligence Per Se; (2) Negligence (Hiring, Training, Supervision and Retention); and (3) Negligent Entrustment.

 

On July 29, 2026, Defendant Caeros O. Durias filed a Motion for an OSC re: Contempt (CCP 1209).  As of September 10, 2026, no opposition or reply has been filed. 

 

II.             LEGAL STANDARD¿

 

A contempt proceeding is commenced by the filing of an affidavit showing (1) the rendition of a valid order; (2) respondent's actual knowledge of the order; (3) respondent's ability to comply with the order; and (4) respondent's willful disobedience of the order.  (CCP § 1211, 2nd para.)  The affidavit frames the issues to be tried.  (Reliable Enterprises, Inc. v. Sup.Ct. (People) (1984) 158 Cal.App.3d 604, 616.  Each allegation (valid order, knowledge, ability to comply and willful disobedience) must be pleaded by factual statements; however the affidavit may be amended to correct technical insufficiencies if the respondent would not be prejudiced thereby.  (Code of Civ. Proc., § 1211.5, subd. (b).)  To establish willful disobedience, the affidavit should show that the alleged contemnor had personal notice of the contents of the order. 

If the court is satisfied that the affidavit alleges sufficient grounds for contempt, it signs an “OSC re Contempt,” setting the date and time for a hearing.  (Code of Civ. Proc., § 1211.)  The court will then issue an order to show cause (OSC) re contempt. The OSC and affidavit must be served personally on the witness.  (Cedars-Sinai Imaging Med. Group v. Sup.Ct. (Moore) (2000) 83 Cal.App.4th 1281, 1286-1287.)  Full compliance with these procedures is mandatory, given that contempt proceedings are quasi-criminal.  (People v. Gonzalez (1996) 12 Cal.4th 804, 816.)

“A contempt proceeding is punitive and separate from the cause out of which it arises…”  (Van v. Language Line Services, Inc. (2017) 8 Cal.App.5th 73, 81.)  “Although prosecuted under the title of the main action or proceeding, contempt is nevertheless a separate proceeding.  And, although the proceedings are located in the Code of Civil Procedure with other “special proceedings of a civil nature” (Code of Civ. Proc., §1209, et seq.), they have long been held to be quasi-criminal in character.”  (3 Witkin, Cal. Proc. (5th 3d. 2008), Actions, §69.)

 

III.           DISCUSSION

 

Defendant Durias moves for an order to show cause regarding contempt as to Plaintiff Vazquez.

 

Defendant Durias identifies two valid court orders:  (1) the March 12, 2025 discovery order compelling Plaintiff’s attendance at deposition and payment of $420 in sanctions and (2) the January 5, 2025 discovery order ordering Plaintiff to appear for deposition within 10 days and payment of $1,260 in sanctions.  (J. Ren Dec., ¶¶4-6.)

 

Defendant Durias establishes Plaintiff’s actual knowledge of the orders.  Notice of the March 12, 2025 ruling was served on Plaintiff’s counsel on March 12, 2025.  (Id. at ¶4, Ex. A.)  Plaintiff’s counsel appeared at the January 5, 2026 hearing and waived notice of the January 5, 2026 order.  (Id. at ¶6,  Ex. B.)  Defendant Durias also emailed Plaintiff’s counsel on February 4, 2026 notifying him that the deadline to pay for the $1,680 in total sanctions was that day.  (Id. at ¶8, Ex. C.)  Defendant emailed again on July 24, 2026 informing Plaintiff’s counsel again that Plaintiff had not yet paid the court-ordered sanctions.  (Id. at ¶9, Ex. D.)  Plaintiff ultimately appeared for deposition but the sanctions were not paid.  (Id. at ¶10.)

 

Defendant Durias establishes Plaintiff’s failure to comply with the orders to pay monetary sanctions.  (Id. at ¶¶7-10.)  Willfulness is reasonably inferred from Plaintiff’s failure to pay despite Defendant’s repeated emails notifying Plaintiff of the outstanding sanctions.  

 

However, Defendant’s affidavit does not attest to Plaintiff’s ability to pay the monetary sanctions.  Plaintiff’s ability to pay for the sanctions is an essential element of a contempt proceeding, not an affirmative defense that Plaintiff must raise.  (Koehler v. Superior Court. (2010) 181 Cal.App.4th 1153, 1170.)  Ability to comply with the order must be pled and supported by Defendant’s affidavit.

 

Defendant Durias’s Motion for an OSC re: Contempt is denied.  Defendant’s charging affidavit is insufficient. 

 

IV.           CONCLUSION 

¿ 

Defendant Durias’s Motion for an OSC re: Contempt is denied.

COURT’S [TENTATIVE] ORDER RE:

Granted in part and Denied in part

 

I.                BACKGROUND

 

Action for violations of Song Beverly Warranty Act.  Plaintiffs filed a complaint on January 24, 2025 against FCA US, LLC and Rydell Chrysler Dodge Jeep Ram.  The operative complaint is the First Amended Complaint filed on January 27, 2026 alleging (1) violation of Civil Code section 1793.2(d); (2) violation of Civil Code section 1793.2(b); (3) violation of Civil Code section 1793.2(a)(3); (4) breach of the implied warranty of merchantability; and (5) fraudulent inducement. 

 

On June 15, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the instant Motion to Compel Initial Disclosures pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26 and Request for Monetary Sanctions.  On September 8, 2026, Plaintiffs filed a Notice of Non-Opposition.

 

II.             LEGAL STANDARD¿

“Within 60 days after the filing of the answer or other responsive pleading, all parties shall, without awaiting a discovery request, provide to all other parties an initial disclosure and documents pursuant to subdivisions (f), (g), and (h).”  (CCP §871.26(b).)

“The defendant or manufacturer shall provide the following documents to all other parties pursuant to the timelines prescribed in subdivision (b): (1) Copy of or access to a version of the owner's manual for a motor vehicle of the same make, model, and year. (2) Any warranties issued in conjunction with the sale of the motor vehicle. (3) Sample brochures published for the motor vehicle. (4) The motor vehicle's original invoice, if any, to the selling dealer. (5) Sales or lease agreement, if the manufacturer is in possession. (6) Motor vehicle information reports, including build documentation, component information, and delivery details. (7) Entire warranty transaction history for the motor vehicle. (8) Listing of required field actions applicable to the motor vehicle. (9) Published technical service bulletins (”TSBs”)1 for the same make, model, and year reasonably related to the nonconformities pertaining to the motor vehicle. (10) Published information service bulletins (“ISBs”) for the same make, model, and year reasonably related to the nonconformities pertaining to the motor vehicle. (11) Records relating to communications between the manufacturer or dealership and the owner or lessee of the motor vehicle, including those related to repair orders or claims involving the motor vehicle. (12) Warranty policies and procedure manuals. (13) Service manuals reasonably related to the nonconformities pertaining to the motor vehicle. (14) If a pre-suit restitution or replacement request is made, all call recordings of pre-suit communications with the consumer available at the time of service of the complaint. (15) If a pre-suit restitution or replacement request is made, the manufacturer's written statement of policies and procedures used to evaluate customer requests for restitution or replacement pursuant to “Lemon Law” claims. (16) If a pre-suit restitution or replacement request is made, any nonprivileged, prelitigation evaluation. (17) Any warranty extensions or modifications issued by the manufacturer on the motor vehicle.”  (CCP §871.26(h).) 

“Unless the party failing to comply with this section shows good cause, notwithstanding any other law and in addition to any other sanctions imposed pursuant to this chapter, a court shall impose sanctions as follows: (1) A one-thousand-five-hundred-dollar ($1,500) sanction against the plaintiff's attorney or two-thousand-five-hundred-dollar ($2,500) sanction against the defense attorney respectively, paid within 15 business days for failure to comply with the document production requirements as prescribed in subdivision (b).”  (CCP §871.26(j).) 

 

III.           DISCUSSION

 

Plaintiffs ask that the Court order Defendant FCA US LLC to comply with its mandatory initial disclosure obligations under CCP §871.26.  Plaintiffs ask that Defendant produce all documents required under CCP §871.26(h)(6)-(10), (12) and (13).  Plaintiffs also request sanctions in the amount of $2,500 against Defendant FCA US LLC and its counsel.

 

Defendant filed a demurrer to the original complaint on April 14, 2025, a demurrer to the FAC on February 26, 2026 and an answer on April 22, 2026.  Although Defendant has produced some documents, Defendant has not yet served initial disclosures with verifications required by section 871.26(h)(6)-(10), (12) and (13).  (Rucker Dec., ¶4.) 

 

Plaintiff fails to cite any authority authorizing the Court to compel initial disclosures under section 871.26.  Section 871.26 requires imposition of mandatory sanctions but there is no language authorizing the Court to compel initial disclosures.  Plaintiff’s motion to compel initial disclosures is therefore denied. 

 

However, Plaintiff also requests sanctions in the amount of $2,500.  Section 871.26 subdivision (j) specifically authorizes mandatory sanctions for failure to provide the required disclosures, unless the non-complying party shows good cause.  Defendant did not oppose or respond.  Pursuant to subdivision (j)(1), a defense attorney who fails to comply with section 871.26 must be sanctioned in the amount of $2,500.  Plaintiff establishes non-compliance and is entitled to an order imposing sanctions against defense counsel.

 

Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel Initial Disclosures pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26 and Request for Monetary Sanctions is granted as to the request for sanctions and denied as to all other requested orders, including an order compelling initial disclosure. 

 

IV.           CONCLUSION 

¿ 

Plaintiff’s Motion to Compel Initial Disclosures pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 871.26 and Request for Monetary Sanctions is granted as to the request for sanctions and denied as to all other requested orders, including an order compelling initial disclosure.

   v.

COURT’S [TENTATIVE] ORDER RE:       

Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings

 

[THE FOLLOWING IS A TENTATIVE RULING IN THE ABOVE CASE]:

 

Grant without leave to amend as to the first cause of action. 

Deny as to the second through fourth causes of action.

 

I.                BACKGROUND

 

On May 21, 2024, Plaintiff obtained a judgment (the “Judgment”) against Defendants Cashbloom, LLC (“Cashbloom”) and Ramin Aminian (“Aminian”) in related case LASC No. 20STCV42954 (“Prior Action”).  Plaintiff alleges Cashbloom became insolvent and was unable to pay the full settlement owed in the amount of $3,259,578.42.  Plaintiff alleges that Cashbloom made several overpayments to Defendant Aminian, a member of Cashbloom, around the time it became insolvent and was unable to pay the settlement.  Plaintiff alleges the payments were improper and not for reasonable equivalent value.  Plaintiff alleges Aminian has not paid the entirety of the amount owed under the Judgment. 

 

On June 27, 2025, Plaintiff filed a complaint against Defendants Cashbloom and Aminian alleging (1) suite on judgment per Code of Civil Procedure section 683.050; (2) creditors’ suit per Code of Civil Procedure section 708.210, et seq.; (3) voidable transfer per Civil Code section 3439.05, et seq.; and (4) voidable transfer per Civil Code section 3439.05 subdivision (a). 

 

On July 30, 2026, Defendant Aminian filed the instant Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings.  On September 1, 2026, Plaintiff filed an opposition.  On September 8, 2026, Defendant filed a reply.

 

II.             LEGAL STANDARD¿

 

“Judgment on the pleadings is akin to a demurrer and is properly granted only if the complaint does not state facts sufficient to state a cause of action against that defendant.  The grounds for the motion must appear on the face of the complaint, and in any matters subject to judicial notice. The court accepts as true all material factual allegations, giving them a liberal construction, but it does not consider conclusions of fact or law, opinions, speculation, or allegations contrary to law or judicially noticed facts.  Appellate courts review the record de novo to determine whether the complaint states a cause of action as a matter of law.”  (Shea Homes Limited Partnership v. County of Alameda (2003) 110 Cal.App.4th 1246, 1254.)

            A statutory motion for judgment on the pleadings may be brought pursuant to CCP §438.  CCP §438(c)(1) states, “A motion provided for in this section may only be made on one of the following grounds:…(B) If the moving party is a defendant, that either of the following conditions exist:  (i) The court has no jurisdiction of the subject of the cause of action alleged in the complaint; (ii) The complaint does not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action against that defendant.”  A motion for judgment on the pleadings may be brought on the same grounds raised in a previously overruled demurrer “provided that there has been a material change in applicable case law or statute since the ruling on the demurrer.”  (Code of Civ. Proc., §438, subd. (g)(1).)

 

III.           DISCUSSION

 

A.  Request for Judicial Notice

 

            Defendant requests judicial notice of the Satisfaction of Judgment and 12/2/24 Minute Order in the Prior Action.  Defendant’s request is granted pursuant to Evidence Code section 452 subdivision (d)(1).

 

            B.  Meet and Confer

           

            Defendant met and conferred with Plaintiff telephonically on July 12, 2026.  The meet and confer required under Code of Civil Procedure section 439 is satisfied. 

 

C.  Res judicata does not bar the first, third and fourth causes of action

 

Defendant moves for judgment on the pleadings of the first, third and fourth causes of action for (1) suit on judgment per CCP §683.050; (3) voidable transfer per Civil Code section 3439.04 subdivision (a)(1); and (4) voidable transfer per Civil Code section 3439.05.  Defendant argues these claims are an impermissible attempt to relitigate the Prior Action and to obtain additional monies under the Judgment therein. 

 

Defendant argues Plaintiff’s complaint omits several material facts that require judgment on the pleadings of these causes of action.  Defendant argues that after the Judgment, Defendant Aminian and Plaintiff entered into a settlement agreement whereby he would pay $185,000 in full satisfaction of his liability under the Judgment.  Defendant argues he paid the settlement amount and successfully moved to enforce the settlement agreement when Plaintiff claimed he did not pay the full settlement amount.  Defendant submits for judicial notice the Minute Order in the Prior Action granting his motion for enforcement of settlement, finding that he paid his $185,000 settlement obligation in full.  Defendant also submits for judicial notice the Satisfaction of Judgment entered in the Prior Action in his favor.  Defendant argues these judicially noticeable facts establish that the first, third and fourth cause of action are barred by res judicate and/or collateral estoppel.

As noted in DKN Holdings LLC v. Faerber (2015) 61 Cal.4th 813, 824, the use of the terms “res judicata” and “collateral estoppel” historically in case law has resulted in enormous confusion.  “To avoid future confusion, we will follow the example of other courts and use the terms ‘claim preclusion’ to describe the primary aspect of the res judicata doctrine and ‘issue preclusion’ to encompass the notion of collateral estoppel.”  (DKN Holdings LLC, supra, 61 Cal.4th at 824.)

“Claim and issue preclusion have different requirements and effects. Claim preclusion prevents relitigation of entire causes of action. Claim preclusion applies only when a second suit involves (1) the same cause of action (2) between the same parties or their privies (3) after a final judgment on the merits in the first suit. Issue preclusion, by contrast, prevents relitigation of previously decided issues, rather than causes of action as a whole. It applies only (1) after final adjudication (2) of an identical issue (3) actually litigated and necessarily decided in the first suit and (4) asserted against one who was a party in the first suit or one in privity with that party.” (Samara v. Matar (2018) 5 Cal.5th 322, 326).

“Issue preclusion differs from claim preclusion in two ways. First, issue preclusion does not bar entire causes of action. Instead, it prevents relitigation of previously decided issues. Second, unlike claim preclusion, issue preclusion can be raised by one who was not a party or privy in the first suit. Only the party against whom the doctrine is invoked must be bound by the prior proceeding. In summary, issue preclusion applies: (1) after final adjudication (2) of an identical issue (3) actually litigated and necessarily decided in the first suit and (4) asserted against one who was a party in the first suit or one in privity with that party.” (DKN Holdings LLC, supra, 61 Cal.4th at 825.)

“California adheres to a ‘primary rights’ theory in determining whether the claims or causes of action are the same.  The significant factor is whether the claim or cause of action is for invasion of a single primary right.  Whether the same facts are involved in both suits is not conclusive…Moreover, more than one act may constitute a single cause of action. Under Pomeroy's primary rights theory,  a cause of action consists of 1) a primary right possessed by the plaintiff, 2) a corresponding primary duty devolving upon the defendant, and 3) a derelict or wrong done by the defendant which consists in a breach of such primary right and duty.  Thus, two actions constitute a single cause of action if they both affect the same primary right.”  (Burdette v. Carrier Corp. (2008) 158 Cal.App.4th 1668, 1684.) 

“If two actions involve the same injury to the plaintiff and the same wrong by the defendant then the same primary right is at stake even if in the second suit the plaintiff pleads different theories of recovery, seeks different forms of relief and/or adds new facts supporting recovery.”  (Le Parc Community Ass'n v. Workers' Compensation Appeals Bd. (2003) 110 Cal.App.4th 1161, 1170.)

 

Defendant fails to establish that claim preclusion, or res judicata, applies.  Parties did not previously litigate the causes of action alleged in the first, third and fourth causes of action.  The first cause of action is a suit on the Judgment pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 683.050, which provides, “nothing in this chapter limits any right the judgment creditor may have to bring an action on a judgment….”  (Code of Civil Procedure section 683.050.)  The third and fourth causes of action are statutory causes of action for fraudulent transfers that allegedly took place after entry of the Judgment.  Plaintiff seeks to enforce its rights under the Judgment, to recover damages for injury resulting from Defendants’ alleged fraudulent attempts to avoid the Judgment and to collect thereon.  These causes of action therefore seek to enforce a different primary right from that raised in the Prior Action, which sought to enforce Plaintiff’s right to repayment of its loan.  (RJN, Ex. B, p. 1; Mycogen Corp. v. Monsanto Co. (2002) 28 Cal.4th 888, 904.) 

 

Defendant also fails to establish that issue preclusion applies.  The relevant inquiry to determine identity of issues is “whether the operative facts and law relating to this action are different from the prior action.”  (Daar v. Newman v. VRL International (2005) 129 Cal.App.4th 482, 486; MIB, Inc. v. Supr. Ct. (1980) 106 Cal.App.3d 228, 229-230, .)  Defendant fails to clearly identify the specific issue he believes was already finally adjudicated in the Prior Action that would the first, third and fourth causes of action as a matter of law.  As discussed above, Plaintiff is not attempting to relitigate liability under the parties’ loan agreement.  Plaintiff is attempting to enforce the Judgment and undo fraudulent transfers allegedly undertaken to avoid the Judgment.  The operative facts and law at issue in this case are different from those in the Prior Action.

 

D.  Plaintiff’s first cause of action for creditor’s suit pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 683.050 fails to state a cause of action against Aminian

 

            Although Plaintiff fails to establish that the first cause of action is barred by issue preclusion or res judicata, Plaintiff’s judicially noticeable evidence establish that Defendant cannot state a claim for creditor’s suit pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 683.050.  A creditor’s claim pursuant to section 683.050 allows a judgment creditor to bring an action on a judgment.  (Code of Civ. Proc., §683.050, subd. (a).)  Based on Plaintiff’s RJN Exhibits A and B, Plaintiff is no longer a judgment creditor on any judgment against Defendant Aminian.  A Full Satisfaction of Judgment was filed and is attached as Exhibit A. 

 

Plaintiff admits that its creditor’s suit seeks to enforce the Judgment against Cashbloom, not Aminian.  (Opposition, 6:25.)  Plaintiff admits that the Settlement Agreement precludes any action against Aminian for recovery of the Judgment.  Plaintiff cites to Code of Civil Procedure section 708.210, but that is the basis for Plaintiff’s second cause of action, not Plaintiff’s first cause of action.

 

However, the first cause of action for creditor’s suit seeks to enforce the Judgment against Aminian.  It does not seek recovery of assets fraudulently transferred to Aminian, which section 708.201 contemplates.  Instead, Plaintiff alleges that Aminian “has failed to pay the entirety of the amount owed under the Judgment,” the first cause of action alleges the Judgment entered against Aminian in the amount of $251,623.87 and the prayer for relief requests judgment for the principal sum remaining on the Judgment.  Plaintiff’s allegations are directly contradicted by the judicially noticeable evidence. 

 

Plaintiff cannot allege that it is a judgment creditor Aminian’s or that it has an outstanding judgment against Aminian.  Defendant Aminian’s motion for judgment on the pleadings is granted without leave to amend as to the first cause of action for creditor’s suit pursuant to Code of Civil Procedure section 683.050.

 

E.  Plaintiff’s second cause of action for creditor’s suit under Code of Civil Procedure section 708.210 states a cause of action against Aminian

 

            Code of Civil Procedure section 708.210 provides, “If a third person has possession or control of property in which the judgment debtor has an interest or is indebted to the judgment debtor, the judgment creditor may bring an action against the third person to have the interest or debt applied to the satisfaction of the money judgment.” 

 

            Defendant argues Plaintiff’s second cause of action fails to identify the specific property or debt in Aminian’s possession that rightfully belong to Cashbloom, when Aminian came into possession of it or how it relates to the conduct already resolved in the Prior Action.  Defendant fails to cite any authority holding that a cause of action under section 708.210 must be pled with heightened specificity akin to that applicable to common law fraud claims.  Section 708.210’s statutory elements do not include fraud.  Plaintiff’s allegation that Defendant Aminian is currently in possession of “cash, assets or business income rightfully belonging to Cashbloom” is sufficient to survive motion for judgment on the pleadings. 

 

            Defendant Aminian’s Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings is denied as to the second cause of action for creditor’s suit under Code of Civil Procedure section 708.210.

 

              F.  Plaintiff’s third and fourth causes of action for statutory fraudulent transfer under Civil Code sections 3439.04 subdivision (a)(1) and 3439.05 subdivision (a) state causes of action against Aminian

 

            The elements of a fraudulent conveyance claim under the Uniform Fraudulent Transfers Act (“UFTA”) are set forth at Civil Code section 3439.04 subdivision (a):  “A transfer made or obligation incurred by a debtor is voidable as to a creditor, whether the creditor's claim arose before or after the transfer was made or the obligation was incurred, if the debtor made the transfer or incurred the obligation as follows:  (1) With actual intent to hinder, delay, or defraud any creditor of the debtor….”  (Civ. Code, §3439.04, subd. (a).)  A transfer made or obligation incurred by a debtor is voidable as to a creditor whose claim arose before the transfer was made or the obligation was incurred if the debtor made the transfer or incurred the obligation without receiving a reasonably equivalent value in exchange for the transfer or obligation and the debtor was insolvent at that time or the debtor became insolvent as a result of the transfer or obligation.

 

Defendant argues the third and fourth causes of action for statutory fraudulent transfer fail to state a claim against him, because they are not pled with sufficient specificity.  Defendant argues Plaintiff alleges fraudulent transfers totaling $340,000 but Plaintiff fails to allege when those transfers occurred relative to Aminian’s payment of the $185,000 settlement, Cashbloom’s alleged insolvency.  Defendant argues Plaintiff also lacks standing to sue Aminian for fraudulent transfer, because it is no longer Aminian’s judgment creditor. 

 

            Plaintiff alleges it is still Cashbloom’s judgment creditor and it has standing to sue for fraudulent transfers by Cashbloom under the UVTA.  Plaintiff need not be a judgment creditor of Aminian. 

 

            Defendant Aminian also fails to cite any authority requiring that a statutory claim for fraudulent transfer be plead with heightened specificity ordinarily applied to common law fraud.  Plaintiff alleges sufficient facts to state a claim for fraudulent transfer under the UFTA against Aminian as the transferee of the fraudulent transfer.  Plaintiff alleges it is a creditor of Cashbloom, that Cashbloom made several overpayments to Aminian at or around the same time it became insolvent, that these payments were not for reasonable value and amounted to $340,000, that property was transferred to Aminian at this time without receipt of reasonable value and that the transfers were made without any legitimate business purpose or corresponding value exchange, that the transfers rendered Cashbloom insolvent and the transfers were made with the actual intent to hinder, delay or defraud Plaintiff.  (Complaint, ¶¶11-13, 24-31.) 

 

            Defendant Aminian’s Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings is denied as to the third and fourth causes of action for fraudulent transfer. 

 

IV.           CONCLUSION 

¿ 

            Defendant Aminian’s Motion for Judgment on the Pleadings is granted without leave to amend as to the first cause of action for creditor’s suit under Code of Civil Procedure section 683.050 and denied as to the second cause of action for Code of Civil Procedure section 708.210, the third cause of action for fraudulent transfer under Civil Code section 3439.04 and fourth cause of action for fraudulent transfer under Civil Code section 3439.05.
