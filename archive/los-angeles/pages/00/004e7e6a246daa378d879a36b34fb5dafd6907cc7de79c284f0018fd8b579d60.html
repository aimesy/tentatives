BACKGROUND

 

This is a wage and hour class action. On May 26, 2023, Plaintiff Pereira filed a class action Complaint alleging that Defendants Bulldog Auction House, LLC (“BAH”) and Bulldog-Simi Valley, Inc. (“BSV”) (1) failed to pay all wages; (2) failed to provide meal periods; (3) failed to provide rest periods; (4) failed to reimburse business expenses; (5) failed to provide accurate itemized wage statements; (6) failed to timely pay all wages due upon separation of employment; and (7) violated Business and Professions Code §§ 17200, et seq.

On August 1, 2023, Plaintiff Pereira filed his First Amended Complaint (“FAC”) which added a cause of action under PAGA.

On April 15, 2024, Plaintiff Pereira and Defendants attended mediation with Steve Pearl, Esq. regarding the class and PAGA claims alleged in this action. Plaintiff Pereira and Defendants did not settle but continued to meet and confer regarding a second mediation and a potential resolution. Pereira and Defendants BAH and BSV thereafter engaged in substantial written discovery and law and motion work.

On March 28, 2024, Bryan Bernabel filed a class action against Defendant Atomic Enterprises, Inc. (“Atomic”) alleging similar claims as the Pereira Action. On June 20, 2024, Plaintiff Bernabel amended his complaint to add a cause of action for PAGA penalties.

Defendants substituted in new counsel and Plaintiffs and Defendants (collectively, the “Parties”) attended a second global mediation on December 31, 2024 which did not result in a settlement.

On May 1, 2025, Plaintiff Pereira filed a Second Amended Complaint and added Defendants Bulldog-Chatsworth, Inc. (“BC”), Bulldog-Valencia, Inc. (“BV”), Bulldog-Camarillo, Inc. (“BCI”), Atomic, and Jonathan Manhan as defendants as well as additional allegations.

Defendants substituted in new counsel and the Parties engaged in months of settlement negotiations and additional informal discovery with the assistance of mediator, Michael D. Young, Esq. and reached a global settlement to resolve the Pereira and Bernabel Actions. A fully executed copy of the Settlement Agreement is attached to the Declaration of Daniel J. Hyun (“Hyun Decl.”) as Exhibit 1.

On April 9, 2026, the Parties submitted a stipulation to consolidate the Actions and for leave to file a Third Amended Consolidated Complaint which was granted.

On April 27, 2026, Plaintiffs filed the Third Amended Complaint.

Now before the Court is the Motion for Preliminary Approval of the Settlement Agreement.

ANALYSIS OF SETTLEMENT AGREEMENT

 

1.     Does a presumption of fairness exist? 

1.   Was the settlement reached through arm’s-length bargaining?  Yes. On April 15, 2024, Plaintiff Pereira and Defendants attended mediation with Steve Pearl, Esq. regarding the class and PAGA claims alleged in this action. Plaintiff Pereira and Defendants did not settle but continued to meet and confer regarding a second mediation and a potential resolution. Pereira and Defendants BAH and BSV thereafter engaged in substantial written discovery and law and motion work. (Hyun Decl., ¶4.) Defendants substituted in new counsel and Plaintiffs and Defendants (collectively, the “Parties”) attended a second global mediation on December 31, 2024 which did not result in a settlement. (Id. at ¶6.) Defendants substituted in new counsel and the Parties engaged in months of settlement negotiations and additional informal discovery with the assistance of mediator, Michael D. Young, Esq. and reached a global settlement to resolve the Pereira and Bernabel Actions. (Id. at ¶8.)

2.   Were investigation and discovery sufficient to allow counsel and the court to act intelligently?  Class Counsel represents that prior to mediation, Plaintiff’s Counsel engaged in numerous communications with opposing counsel regarding Plaintiff’s claims and obtained data and documents produced by Defendants, including all available Class Members’ time keeping and payroll records, and other information. Thereafter, Plaintiff’s Counsel engaged an expert to conduct a thorough analysis and calculation of the damages. Pereira and Defendants BAH and BSV thereafter engaged in substantial written discovery and law and motion work. (Id. at ¶4.) Following the filing of the initial complaint, the Parties exchanged documents and information before mediating this action. Defendant produced a sample of time and pay records for class members. Defendant also provided documents of its wage and hour policies and practices during the class period, and information regarding the total number of current and former employees in its informal discovery responses. After reviewing documents regarding Defendant’s wage and hour policies and practices and analyzing Defendant’s timekeeping and payroll records, Class Counsel was able to evaluate the probability of class certification, success on the merits, and Defendant’s maximum monetary exposure for all claims. Class Counsel also investigated the applicable law regarding the claims and defenses asserted in the litigation. Class Counsel reviewed these records and prepared a damage analysis prior to mediation. (Declaration of Tyler J. Woods (“Woods Decl.”), ¶¶4-5.)

3.   Is Class Counsel experienced in similar litigation?  Class Counsel is experienced in class action litigation, including wage and hour class actions. (Hyun Decl., ¶¶30-46; Woods Decl., ¶¶19-29.)

4.   What percentage of the class has objected?  This cannot be determined until the fairness hearing.  See Weil & Brown, Cal. Practice Guide: Civil Procedure Before Trial (The Rutter Group 2014) ¶ 14:139.18, (“Should the court receive objections to the proposed settlement, it will consider and either sustain or overrule them at the fairness hearing.”).

           

            CONCLUSION:  The settlement is entitled to a presumption of fairness.

4.     May conditional class certification be granted?

1.     Standards

A detailed analysis of the elements required for class certification is not required, but it is advisable to review each element when a class is being conditionally certified (Amchem Products, Inc. v. Winsor (1997) 521 U.S. 620, 622-627.)  The trial court can appropriately utilize a different standard to determine the propriety of a settlement class as opposed to a litigation class certification.  Specifically, a lesser standard of scrutiny is used for settlement cases.  (Dunk at 1807, fn 19.)  Finally, the Court is under no “ironclad requirement” to conduct an evidentiary hearing to consider whether the prerequisites for class certification have been satisfied. (Wershba at 240.)

2.     Analysis

a.     Numerosity.  There are approximately 595 Class Members. (Hyun Decl., ¶10.) This element is met. 

b.     Ascertainability.  The proposed class is defined above.  The class definition is “precise, objective and presently ascertainable.”  (Sevidal v. Target Corp. (2010) 189 Cal.App.4th 905, 919.) The class members are identifiable from Defendant’s records. (Hyun Decl., ¶11.)

c.      Community of interest.  “The community of interest requirement involves three factors: ‘(1) predominant common questions of law or fact; (2) class representatives with claims or defenses typical of the class; and (3) class representatives who can adequately represent the class.’”  (Linder v. Thrifty Oil Co. (2000) 23 Cal.4th 429, 435.)

As to predominant questions of law or fact, counsel contends that the common legal and factual questions in this case include whether Defendants applied the same compensation, reimbursement, and meal and rest break policies to all Class Members; whether those policies or their implementation resulted in Labor Code violations; whether Defendants’ conduct was intentional; and whether Class Members are entitled to penalties, among other things. These questions could be resolved using Defendants’ time and payroll records, corporate representative testimony, and written policies and practices. (Hyun Decl., ¶14.)

As to typicality, Plaintiffs allege that they and the other Class Members were employed by the same company and injured by the company’s common policies and practices related to compensation including, wages, meal periods and rest breaks, and derivative violations. Plaintiffs seek relief for these claims and derivative claims on behalf of all Class Members. (Id. at ¶15.)

As to adequacy, Plaintiffs represent that they have participated in the litigation and are aware of the duties of serving as class representative. (Declarations of Marvin Pereira and Bryan Bernabel.)  

d.     Adequacy of class counsel.  As indicated above, Class Counsel has shown experience in class action litigation, including wage and hour class actions.

e.     Superiority.  Given the relatively small size of the individual claims, a class action appears to be superior to separate actions by the class members.

 

      CONCLUSION:  The class may be conditionally certified since the prerequisites of class certification have been satisfied.

6.     Attorney fees and costs

CRC rule 3.769(b) states: “Any agreement, express or implied, that has been entered into with respect to the payment of attorney fees or the submission of an application for the approval of attorney fees must be set forth in full in any application for approval of the dismissal or settlement of an action that has been certified as a class action.”

            Ultimately, the award of attorney fees is made by the court at the fairness hearing, using the lodestar method with a multiplier, if appropriate.  (PLCM Group, Inc. v. Drexler (2000) 22 Cal.4th 1084, 1095-1096; Ramos v. Countrywide Home Loans, Inc. (2000) 82 Cal.App.4th 615, 625-626; Ketchum III v. Moses (2000) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1132-1136.)  Despite any agreement by the parties to the contrary, “the court ha[s] an independent right and responsibility to review the attorney fee provision of the settlement agreement and award only so much as it determined reasonable.” (Garabedian v. Los Angeles Cellular Telephone Company (2004) 118 Cal.App.4th 123, 128.)

            The question of whether Class Counsel is entitled to $250,000 (1/3) in attorney fees will be addressed at the fairness hearing when class counsel brings a noticed motion for attorney fees. Class counsel must provide the court with billing information so that it can properly apply the lodestar method and must indicate what multiplier (if applicable) is being sought as to each counsel.    

Class Counsel should also be prepared to justify the costs sought (capped at $35,000) by detailing how they were incurred.

 

7.     Incentive Award

The Settlement Agreement provides for an enhancement award of up to $20,000 [$10,000 each] to the named Plaintiffs. In connection with the final fairness hearing, named Plaintiffs must submit a declaration attesting to why he or she should be entitled to an enhancement award in the proposed amount.  The named Plaintiff must explain why he or she “should be compensated for the expense or risk she has incurred in conferring a benefit on other members of the class.”  (Clark v. American Residential Services LLC (2009) 175 Cal.App.4th 785, 806.)  Trial courts should not sanction enhancement awards of thousands of dollars with “nothing more than pro forma claims as to ‘countless’ hours expended, ‘potential stigma’ and ‘potential risk.’ Significantly more specificity, in the form of quantification of time and effort expended on the litigation, and in the form of reasoned explanation of financial or other risks incurred by the named plaintiff, is required in order for the trial court to conclude that an enhancement was ‘necessary to induce [the named plaintiff] to participate in the suit . . . .’”  (Id. at 806-807, italics and ellipsis in original.)  The Court will decide the issue of the enhancement awards at the time of final approval.

TENTATIVE RULING

 

Defendant’s motion to compel arbitration is denied because the record fails to show consent to arbitrate.

 

 

BACKGROUND

 

Plaintiff used to work for Defendant as a server.  He alleges that Defendant subjected him and other current and former non-exempt employees to numerous wage-and-hour violations. 

 

Here, Defendant moves to compel Plaintiff’s individual claims to arbitration.

 

DISCUSSION

 

Existence and Assent

 

“[W]hen a petition to compel arbitration is filed and accompanied by prima facie evidence of a written agreement to arbitrate the controversy, the court itself must determine whether the agreement exists and . . . whether it is enforceable.”  (Rosenthal v. Great Western Financial Securities Corp. (1996) 14 Cal.4th 394, 413.)  “[T]he threshold question . . . is whether there is an agreement to arbitrate.’”  (Cruise v. Kroger Co. (2015) 233 Cal.App.4th 390, 396, emphasis in original.)

 

The determination of whether an arbitration agreement exists is a three-step process.  “First, the moving party bears the burden of producing ‘prima facie evidence of a written agreement to arbitrate the controversy.’”  (Gamboa v. Northeast Community Clinic (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 158, 165.)  “The moving party ‘can meet its initial burden by attaching to the [motion or] petition a copy of the arbitration agreement purporting to bear the [opposing party's] signature.’”  (Ibid.)  In the alternative, “the moving party can meet its burden by setting forth the agreement's provisions in the motion.”  (Ibid.) 

 

“If the moving party meets its initial prima facie burden and the opposing party disputes the agreement, then in the second step, the opposing party bears the burden of producing evidence to challenge the authenticity of the agreement.”  (Ibid.)  “The opposing party can do this in several ways.”  (Ibid.)  “For example, the opposing party may testify under oath or declare under penalty of perjury that the party never saw or does not remember seeing the agreement, or that the party never signed or does not remember signing the agreement.”  (Ibid.)

 

“If the opposing party meets its burden of producing evidence, then in the third step, the moving party must establish with admissible evidence a valid arbitration agreement between the parties.”  (Ibid.)  “The burden of proving the agreement by a preponderance of the evidence remains with the moving party.”  (Id. at 165-166.)

 

Defendant passes the first step.  A copy of the arbitration agreement is physically included with the moving papers.  (See Thakare Decl., Ex. A; see also Gamboa, supra, 72 Cal.App.5th at 165.)[1]

 

For the second and third steps, Plaintiff contends the Court should deny the motion to compel because (1) his e-signature is not written on the agreement’s signature line, (2) Defendant fails to authenticate the agreement, and (3) Defendant fails to show that he agreed to arbitration by clicking the “submit” button during onboarding.  (See Opposition, pp. 6-11; see also Miranda Decl., ¶¶ 5-6.)

 

Point (1) is persuasive. Defendant fails to show assent to the agreement. The agreement is three pages in length.  Each page is numbered and has a header identifying it as the GEN employment-at-will arbitration agreement.  The signature line, on page three, is blank.  Strikingly, the e-signature appears on an unnumbered fourth page, with no other text, and no header indicating it is connected in any way to the arbitration terms.  (See Thakare Decl., Ex. A.)  Defendant offers no evidence that this fourth page is part of the agreement, or reflects assent to arbitration. Plaintiff may have electronically signed something, but Defendant has not shown that Plaintiff signed the arbitration agreement. Defendant does not address this issue in its Reply memorandum. This discrepancy is fatal and is grounds to deny the motion on the basis for failure to establish formation of the arbitration agreement. 

 

Accordingly, the court need not address the other two arguments relating to authentication and the electronic signature.[2]

 

Even if the court had found an arbitration agreement exists, it is unconscionable.

 

Although unnecessary, for the benefit of a full record, even if the court found contract formation, it would find that unconscionability renders the agreement to arbitrate unenforceable.

 

As a preliminary matter, if an arbitration agreement were established, it would be governed by the Federal Arbitration Act (FAA). Section 2 of Defendant’s agreement states: “The FAA applies to this Agreement because the Company’s business involves interstate commerce.”  (Id. at Ex. A, p. 1, § 2.)  The wording renders the FAA controlling.

 

Unconscionability and Enforcement

 

Unconscionability is a contract defense that can be utilized to “invalidate [an] arbitration agreement[.]”  (Torrecillas v. Fitness International, LLC (2020) 52 Cal.App.5th 485, 492.)  Courts apply state law to test whether the agreement is unconscionable.  (See, e.g., Lagatree v. Luce, Forward, Hamilton & Scripps (1999) 74 Cal.App.4th 1105, 1119.)

 

“[U]nconscionability has both a procedural and a substantive element, the former focusing on oppression or surprise due to unequal bargaining power, the latter on overly harsh or one-sided results.”  (Armendariz v. Foundation Health Psychcare Services, Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 83, 114, internal quotation marks omitted.)  “The prevailing view is that [procedural and substantive unconscionability] must both be present in order for a court to exercise its discretion to refuse to enforce a contract or clause under the doctrine of unconscionability.”  (Ibid.)  “But they need not be present in the same degree.”  (Ibid.)  “Essentially a sliding scale is invoked which disregards the regularity of the procedural process of the contract formation, that creates the terms, in proportion to the greater harshness or unreasonableness of the substantive terms themselves.”  (Ibid.)  “In other words, the more substantively oppressive the contract term, the less evidence of procedural unconscionability is required to come to the conclusion that the term is unenforceable, and vice versa.”  (Ibid.)

 

The burden belongs to the party opposing arbitration.  (See Ajamian v. CantorCO2e, L.P. (2012) 203 Cal.App.4th 771, 795.)

 

With respect to procedural unconscionability, Plaintiff claims the agreement constitutes a contract of adhesion.  He further states that he was not given an opportunity to negotiate, that Defendant did not explain the terms to him, and that he does not remember e-signing it.  (See Opposition, pp. 12-14; see also Miranda Decl., ¶¶ 4-10.)

 

The Court finds that Plaintiff’s lack of ability to negotiate and lack of memory are insufficient grounds for showing a high degree of procedural unconscionability. First, “a predispute arbitration agreement is not invalid merely because it is imposed as a condition of employment.”  (Lagatree, supra, 74 Cal.App.4th at 1122.)  Second, the plain language undercuts Plaintiff’s position.  In addition to allowing employees to “opt out of certain portions[,]” the agreement states that “execution of this document is not a condition of employment.”  (Thakare Decl., Ex. A, pp. 1, 2, §§ 2, 5, emphasis added.)  Third, failure to read and understand is not a defense.  (See Knight, supra, at ¶ 5:90.)  Fourth, there is no statement under penalty of perjury stating that Plaintiff actually requested extra time to read the onboarding documents or assistance from an attorney or other consultant to explain the meaning of the arbitration terms or that Defendant denied such requests, assuming they were made.  Given these factors, the level of procedural unconscionability is low.

 

On substantive unconscionability, Plaintiff argues that (1) the arbitration agreement must be interpreted together with a confidentiality agreement that was included among the onboarding documents (see Opposition, p. 14), (2) the confidentiality agreement requires claims related to the confidentiality agreement to be litigated in court (see id. at pp. 14-16), and (3) the arbitration agreement is one-sided in that it obliges him to arbitrate against third-party entities that do not similarly agree to arbitrate in return.  (See id. at pp. 16-17 [citing Phan v. Knight Sacramento SU Inc. (2026) 121 Cal.App.5th 641 and Cook v. USC (2024) 102 Cal.App.5th 312].)

 

The first two points, relating to the effect of the confidentiality agreement, have merit.  In Cluck v. Geo Secure Services (2026) 122 Cal.App.5th 249, 347 Cal.Rptr.3d 44, the Court of Appeal held that Civil Code section 1642 mandates that, where an employee signs an arbitration agreement and a confidentiality agreement in the same onboarding transaction, the agreements must be construed as a single contract.

 

In Cluck, the plaintiff signed an arbitration agreement and a confidentiality agreement on the same day.  The arbitration agreement obligated the parties to arbitrate employment-related disputes. The confidentiality agreement “create[d] an exception for [the defendant’s] confidentiality-or-competition-type claims[,]” calling for the claims to be litigated in court in Florida.  (Cluck, supra, 347 Cal.Rptr.3d at p. 51)  Analyzing Alberto v. Cambrian Homecare (2023) 91 Cal.App.5th 482 and Silva v. Cross Country Healthcare, Inc. (2025) 111 Cal.App.5th 1311, the Fourth District determined that the agreements should be read together because they both “pertain[ed] to the resolution of disputes arising from [the plaintiff’s] employment.”  (Cluck, supra, 347 Cal.Rptr.3d at p. 53.)

 

The Cluck opinion also rejected the defendant’s focus on the integration clauses in the agreements:

 

GEO suggests that we should not construe the agreements together under section 1642 because each contains an integration clause. Indeed, the Arbitration Agreement provides that it “replaces all prior agreements regarding the arbitration of disputes and is the full and complete agreement relating to the formal resolution of disputes covered by this Agreement.” And the Confidentiality Agreement states:

 

“Except as otherwise provided herein, the terms contained in this document are the entire agreement between the parties concerning the matters covered in it. The parties are not relying upon any representations, understandings or agreements outside of this Agreement in making the decision to enter into it. This Agreement supersedes prior agreements between me and the Company regarding the subject matter addressed in this Agreement .... And, this Agreement supplements and does not replace or modify confidentiality, invention assignment, and restrictive covenant provisions that may be contained in ... other agreements between the parties.”

 

The mere existence of an integration clause, however, does not necessarily preclude us from considering whether two agreements should be construed together under section 1642. (R.W.L. Enterprises v. Oldcastle, Inc. (2017) 17 Cal.App.5th 1019, 1031, 226 Cal.Rptr.3d 677.) The integration clauses in this case do not clearly indicate the agreements should be read independently. Neither the Arbitration Agreement nor the Confidentiality Agreement specifically references the other. (Cf. Subaru of America, Inc. v. Putnam Automotive, Inc. (2021) 60 Cal.App.5th 829, 840–841, 275 Cal.Rptr.3d 120 [applying § 1642 not warranted where one agreement expressly states it does not modify a second agreement and “ ‘is a separate, negotiated contract apart from’ ” the second (italics omitted)].) And where two agreements are signed at the same time, both covering dispute resolution and both containing an integration clause, it is not clear which is controlling.

 

(Id. at p. 54.)

 

Cluck is analogous.  The Court finds that Cluck requires Defendant’s arbitration agreement and confidentiality agreement to be read together.

 

More importantly, Cluck compels the conclusion that there is a high degree of substantive unconscionability.  The Fourth District found that the overall agreement – the arbitration agreement and the confidentiality agreement combined – provided the defendant an “unfairly one-sided” benefit of “pursu[ing] its most likely claims in court.”  (Cluck, supra, 347 Cal.Rptr.3d at p. 57.)  Combining Defendant’s arbitration agreement and confidentiality agreement generates an equivalent “unfairly one-sided” benefit.  (Ibid.; see also Shirinian Decl., Ex. 1, p. DEF00022, § 6 [“In the event of a breach or threatened breach by the Employee of any of the provisions of this Agreement, the Employee hereby consents and agrees that the Employer shall be entitled to seek, in addition to other available remedies, a temporary or permanent injunction or other equitable relief against such breach or threatened breach from any court of competent jurisdiction . . . .”]; id. at Ex. 1, p. DEF00023 [“Any action or proceeding by either Party to enforce this Agreement shall be brought only in any state or federal court located in the state of California, County of Los Angeles. The Parties hereby irrevocably submit to the exclusive jurisdiction of such courts . . . .”].)  

 

Defendant’s Reply lacks candor concerning the circumstances of the signing of the two agreements. It argues that Plaintiff cannot verify they were signed at the same time.  (Reply, p. 6.) But a list of when the documents were purportedly signed, produced by Defendant on Bates page DEF000149, purports to reflect that they were signed within seconds of each other.  (Shirinian Decl., Exh. 3.)

 

Accordingly, even if Defendant had established formation of the arbitration agreement, the court would find it unconscionable.

 

Defendant Gen Cerritos’s motion to compel arbitration is denied.

Tentative Ruling Re: Motion to Compel Arbitration

 

Date:                                  9/22/26

Time:                                 11:00 am

Moving Party:               Alpha Structural, Inc. (“Defendant”)

Opposing Party:           Rodolfo Rodriguez (“Plaintiff”)

Department:                  11

Judge:                               Bruce G. Iwasaki

________________________________________________________________________

 

TENTATIVE RULING

 

Defendants’ motion to compel arbitration is granted.

 

The case shall be stayed until the arbitration is finished.

 

BACKGROUND

 

Plaintiff used to work for Defendant.  He alleges that Defendant subjected him and other current and former non-exempt employees to numerous wage-and-hour violations.

 

Here, Defendant moves to compel Plaintiff’s individual claims to arbitration.

 

DEFENDANT’S EVIDENTIARY OBJECTIONS

 

Objections 2, 3, and 8 are sustained.  Objections 1, 4, 5, 6, and 7 are overruled.

 

DISCUSSION

 

It is undisputed that an agreement to arbitrate exists and that the Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”) applies.

 

Nevertheless, Plaintiff contends the motion to compel should be denied because the delegation clause and the arbitration agreement are unconscionable.

 

Plaintiff also cites Civil Code section 1642, arguing that the arbitration agreement, the confidentiality agreement, and the employment agreement should be treated as one contract.

 

Delegation Clause

 

The delegation clause states:

 

The Arbitrator shall have exclusive authority to resolve any dispute relating to the interpretation, applicability, enforceability, or formation of this Agreement, including but not limited to any claim that all or any part of this Agreement is void or voidable. The Arbitrator also shall have the authority to hear and rule on pre-hearing disputes, as well as motions to dismiss and/or motions for summary judgment by any Party. The Arbitrator further shall have the authority to award any form of remedy or damages available in a court. The Arbitrator shall have no authority to hear or decide any matter that was not processed in accordance with this Agreement.

 

(Duenas Decl., Ex. A, § 9.a.)

 

Defendant claims the delegation clause requires the arbitrator to decide gateway questions, primarily, arbitrability and unconscionability.  (See Motion to Compel, p. 10.)   

 

Plaintiff insists that the delegation clause is unconscionable.  (See Opposition, pp. 11-12.) Defendant argues that the delegation clause is clear and unmistakable, and not unconscionable. (Reply at pp. 5-7.)

 

The Court will analyze Plaintiffs’ argument first.  (See Malone v. Superior Court (2014) 226 Cal.App.4th 1551, 1560 [instructing that, “if the party is making a specific challenge to the delegation clause, the court must determine whether the delegation clause itself may be enforced (and can only delegate the general issue of enforceability to the arbitrator if it first determines the delegation clause is enforceable)”].)

 

Unconscionability is a contract defense that can be utilized to “invalidate [an] arbitration agreement[].”  (Torrecillas v. Fitness International, LLC (2020) 52 Cal.App.5th 485, 492.)  Courts apply state law to test whether the agreement is unconscionable.  (See, e.g., Lagatree v. Luce, Forward, Hamilton & Scripps (1999) 74 Cal.App.4th 1105, 1119.)

 

“[U]nconscionability has both a procedural and a substantive element, the former focusing on oppression or surprise due to unequal bargaining power, the latter on overly harsh or one-sided results.”  (Armendariz v. Foundation Health Psychcare Services, Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 83, 114, internal quotation marks omitted.)  “The prevailing view is that [procedural and substantive unconscionability] must both be present in order for a court to exercise its discretion to refuse to enforce a contract or clause under the doctrine of unconscionability.”  (Ibid.)  “But they need not be present in the same degree.”  (Ibid.)  “Essentially a sliding scale is invoked which disregards the regularity of the procedural process of the contract formation, that creates the terms, in proportion to the greater harshness or unreasonableness of the substantive terms themselves.”  (Ibid.)  “In other words, the more substantively oppressive the contract term, the less evidence of procedural unconscionability is required to come to the conclusion that the term is unenforceable, and vice versa.”  (Ibid.)

 

The burden belongs to “[t]he party resisting arbitration[.]”  (Malone, supra, 226 Cal.App.4th at 1561.) 

 

Procedural element: Plaintiff asserts that he “was not given a meaningful opportunity to review . . . the delegation clause.”  (Opposition, p. 6.)  He further states that he felt pressure to sign, that he did not get a chance to read the onboarding documents or to “consult with any outside sources[,]” and that the delegation clause is hidden in the ninth paragraph of the arbitration agreement.  (Ibid.)

 

The Court disagrees.  First, failure to read and understand is not a defense.  (See Knight, et al., Cal. Practice Guide: Alternative Dispute Resolution (The Rutter Group 2026) ¶ 5:90.)  Second, the plain language of the arbitration agreement undercuts Plaintiff’s position:

 

12. Employee's Consent to Agreement. Employee acknowledges and agrees that Employee enters into this Agreement freely and voluntarily, although understanding it is a condition of employment or continued employment. Employee further acknowledges that Employee has had the opportunity to consult with an attorney or other advisor before signing this Agreement.

 

* * *

 

Each of Employer and Employee acknowledges the following:

 

• Such Party has carefully read this Agreement which consists of five (5) pages including this acknowledgment and sixteen (16) numbered paragraphs;

 

• That all understandings between Employer and Employee relating to the subject matter of arbitration are contained in this Agreement;

 

• That such Party's signature on this Agreement means that such Party is giving up such Party's rights to a jury trial and to a trial in a court of law for any Claim covered by this Agreement;

 

• That such Party's signature on this Agreement means that such Party is giving up such Party's rights to pursue collective or class procedures in the arbitration process as well as in court; and

 

• Such Party has entered into this Agreement voluntarily and not in reliance on any promises or representations other than those contained in this Agreement.

 

Each of Employer and Employee further acknowledges that such Party has had an opportunity to discuss this Agreement with attorneys of such Party's choice prior to signing it and that such Party has used that opportunity to the extent such Party wishes to do so.

 

(Duenas Decl., Ex. A, § 12 and p. 5, underlining and bolding in original.)  Third, there is no statement under penalty of perjury stating that Plaintiff actually requested extra time to read the onboarding documents or assistance from an attorney or other consultant to explain the meaning of the arbitration terms, including the delegation clause, or that Defendant denied such requests, assuming they were made.  Fourth, the delegation clause is conspicuous.  It appears in its own paragraph under the underlined heading “Authority of Arbitrator” and is written in standard font size.  (See id. at Ex. A, § 9.a.)  Given these factors, the level of procedural unconscionability is low.

 

Substantive element: Plaintiff claims (1) “[t]he delegation clause delegates the issue of formation . . . to the arbitrator” and (2) the arbitration agreement is one-sided in that in that it obliges him to arbitrate against third-party entities that do not similarly agree to arbitrate in return.  (Opposition, pp. 7-8.)

 

Point (1) is moot.  While it is true that “parties cannot delegate issues of formation to the arbitrator” (Ahlstrom v. DHI Mortgage Company, Ltd., L.P. (9th Cir. 2021) 21 F.4th 631, 635), Plaintiff does not contest formation.  As noted above, the existence of an agreement to arbitrate is undisputed.[1]

 

Point (2) fails.  The argument attacks the arbitration agreement, not the delegation clause, and, regardless, it is based on distinguishable law.  Plaintiff cites Cook v. USC (2024) 102 Cal.App.5th 312.  The overarching problem with the Cook agreement was the unlimited scope.  (See Cook, supra, 102 Cal.App.5th at 321-325.)  It covered “all claims, whether or not arising out of Employee’s University employment[.]”  (Ibid.)  Nothing was excluded.  (See id. at 317, 322-324.)  By contrast, Plaintiff concedes that Defendant’s agreement covers “claims claims and controversies arising out of or relating to Employee’s employment relationship with Employer (or the termination of that employment relationship)[.]”  (Opposition, p. 7; see also Duenas Decl., Ex. A, § 1 [defining Defendant as “Employer” and Plaintiff as “Employee”]; id. at Ex. A, § 7 [calling for application of the JAMS Employment Arbitration Rules and Mediation Procedures].)  This difference makes Cook distinguishable.[2]

 

Consequently, like the level of procedural unconscionability, the level of substantive unconscionability is low.

 

Which brings the Court to Defendant’s argument.  Under the FAA, “[u]nless the parties clearly and unmistakably provide otherwise,” arbitrability and unconscionability of the arbitration agreement “to be determined by the court.”  (Ajamian v. CantorCO2e, L.P. (2012) 203 Cal.App.4th 771, 781.)  To prove the exception, “it must be shown by ‘clear and unmistakable’ evidence that the parties intended to delegate the issue to the arbitrator.”  (Ibid.)  “The ‘clear and unmistakable’ test reflects a ‘heightened standard’ of proof.”  (Id. at 782.)  “That is because the question of who would decide the unconscionability of an arbitration provision is not one that the parties would likely focus upon in contracting, and the default expectancy is that the court would decide the matter.”  (Ibid. [remarking that “a contract’s silence or ambiguity about the arbitrator’s power . . . cannot satisfy the clear and unmistakable evidence standard”].)

 

However, in Dream Theater, Inc. v. Dream Theater (2004) 124 Cal.App.4th 547 (“Dream Theater”), the Second District Court of Appeal held that parties “state a clear and unmistakable agreement that the arbitrator will decide whether the dispute is subject to arbitration when they incorporate into their agreement the [American Arbitration Association (‘AAA’)] Commercial Arbitration Rules which specify the arbitrator will decide arbitrability, and nothing in the parties’ agreement excludes from the jurisdiction of the arbitrator the decision whether the dispute must be submitted to arbitration.”  (Dream Theater, supra, 124 Cal.App.4th at 549; see also id. at 556 [“It is difficult to imagine how parties could state any more comprehensively than they did in the Contract the intent to avoid litigation at every step of the dispute resolution process. The Contract provides that if a contested claim is not settled within the contractual deadline, then it must be submitted to binding arbitration in accordance with the AAA Commercial Arbitration Rules. These rules specify that the arbitrator will decide disputes over the scope of the arbitration agreement. We conclude that the parties' agreement to arbitrate according to this rule is clear and unmistakable evidence of the intent that the arbitrator will decide whether a Contested Claim is arbitrable.”]; Oracle America, Inc. v. Myriad Group A.G. (9th Cir. 2013) 724 F.3d 1069, 1074 [recognizing that “[v]irtually every circuit to have considered the issue has determined that incorporation of the” AAA “arbitration rules constitutes clear and unmistakable evidence that the parties agreed to arbitrate arbitrability”]; Knight, supra, at ¶ 5:214.4 [citing cases].)[3]

 

The Ninth Circuit reached the same conclusion vis-à-vis incorporating JAMS rules.  (See Patrick v. Running Warehouse, LLC (9th Cir. 2024) 93 F.4th 468, 480-481.)

 

Based on these authorities, delegation is warranted.  Defendant’s arbitration agreement incorporates the JAMS rules (see Duenas Decl., Ex. A, §§ 7, 8, 9.b-c), meaning the arbitrator must decide all arbitrability, unconscionability, and enforceability challenges to the overall agreement.

 

The motion to compel is granted for this purpose.

 

Arbitration Agreement and Civil Code Section 1642

 

The Court declines to reach Plaintiff’s arguments concerning the unconscionability of the arbitration agreement and whether Civil Code section 1642 applies.

 

Stay

 

Defendant’s request to stay the case pending completion of the arbitration is granted.

 

The representative PAGA claims are stayed. The class claims are stricken.  When the FAA applies a class waiver as appears in the agreement here is enforceable.

 

The Court will set the date for a post-arbitration status conference at the hearing.

Trans Valley Transport (2022) 75 Cal.App.5th 748, 776 [“[W]e conclude that although the delegation clause provides that the arbitrator ‘shall have exclusive authority to resolve any dispute relating to . . . formation of the arbitration policy,’ as a matter of law, the question whether the parties entered into an agreement to arbitrate anything at all is for a court to decide.”], emphasis in original.)

Tentative Ruling Re: Motion to Compel Arbitration

 

Date:                           9/22/26

 

Time:                           11:00 am

 

Moving Party:            CG Hospitality Group, Inc. (“CG Hospitality”) and Perian Management                          Co. (“Perian”) (collectively “Defendants”)

 

Opposing Party:         Matthew Scott (“Plaintiff”)

 

Department:              11

 

Judge:                         Bruce G. Iwasaki

________________________________________________________________________

 

TENTATIVE RULING

 

Defendants’ motion to compel arbitration is granted, the class claims are stricken, and the case is stayed as to the representative claim under the Private Attorneys General Act (“PAGA”).

 

BACKGROUND

 

CG Hospitality operates Round Table Pizza restaurants in California. 

 

Perian “is a management company engaged by CG Hospitality to assist with the operation, management, and human resources functions of its restaurant locations.”  (Abergel Decl., ¶ 2.)

 

Plaintiff used to work at a Round Table Pizza in Tulare, California.  He alleges that CG Hospitality and Perian jointly employed him and subjected him and other current and former employees to numerous wage-and-hour violations.

 

Here, CG Hospitality and Perian move to compel Plaintiff’s individual claims to arbitration.

 

DEFENDANT’S EVIDENTIARY OBJECTIONS

 

Defendant’s objections 1, 3 and 6 are sustained. Objections 2, 4, 5, 7, and 8 are overruled.

 

 

DISCUSSION

 

Existence and Assent

 

“[W]hen a petition to compel arbitration is filed and accompanied by prima facie evidence of a written agreement to arbitrate the controversy, the court itself must determine whether the agreement exists and . . . whether it is enforceable.”  (Rosenthal v. Great Western Financial Securities Corp. (1996) 14 Cal.4th 394, 413.)  “[T]he threshold question . . . is whether there is an agreement to arbitrate.’”  (Cruise v. Kroger Co. (2015) 233 Cal.App.4th 390, 396, emphasis in original.)

 

Fundamentally, determination of the existence of an arbitration agreement is a three-step process.  “First, the moving party bears the burden of producing ‘prima facie evidence of a written agreement to arbitrate the controversy.’”  (Gamboa v. Northeast Community Clinic (2021) 72 Cal.App.5th 158, 165.)  “The moving party ‘can meet its initial burden by attaching to the [motion or] petition a copy of the arbitration agreement purporting to bear the [opposing party's] signature.’”  (Ibid.)  In the alternative, “the moving party can meet its burden by setting forth the agreement's provisions in the motion.”  (Ibid.) 

 

“If the moving party meets its initial prima facie burden and the opposing party disputes the agreement, then in the second step, the opposing party bears the burden of producing evidence to challenge the authenticity of the agreement.”  (Ibid.)  “The opposing party can do this in several ways.”  (Ibid.)  “For example, the opposing party may testify under oath or declare under penalty of perjury that the party never saw or does not remember seeing the agreement, or that the party never signed or does not remember signing the agreement.”  (Ibid.)

 

“If the opposing party meets its burden of producing evidence, then in the third step, the moving party must establish with admissible evidence a valid arbitration agreement between the parties.”  (Ibid.)  “The burden of proving the agreement by a preponderance of the evidence remains with the moving party.”  (Id. at 165-166.)

 

Defendants pass the first step.  A copy of the arbitration agreement is physically included with the moving papers.  (See Regan Decl., Ex. A; see also Gamboa, supra, 72 Cal.App.5th at 165.)

 

For the second and third steps, Plaintiff challenges the assent element.  Plaintiff admits that, during onboarding, he clicked the e-signature button to e-sign the arbitration agreement and other onboarding documents.  (See Scott Decl., ¶¶ 3-5; see also Opposition, pp. 2-4.). He appears to claim assent is lacking because “I did not download, open, or read the onboarding documents because I did not have the data to do so.”  (Scott Decl., ¶5.) Scott does not explain what he means by “data” he lacked. He does not say that he attempted to download or read onboarding documents, only that he did not do so.  He also testified that the “only text on each page said: ‘By signing this document electronically, you agree that your electronic signature is the legal equivalent of you signing this document in writing.’” (Ibid.) But Exhibit B to Scott’s declaration states that one of the forms he signed was entitled (following an exclamation point and appearing to be a hypertext link) “Perian_CG_Hospitality_Arbitration_Agreement.”  Scott’s declaration omits this.

 

Plaintiff’s contention regarding lack of assent to the arbitration agreement lacks evidentiary support.  Failure to read and understand is not a defense.  (See Knight, et al., Cal. Practice Guide: Alternative Dispute Resolution (The Rutter Group 2026) ¶ 5:90.)  In addition, there is no statement under penalty of perjury stating that Plaintiff requested extra time to read the onboarding documents or assistance from an attorney or other consultant to explain the meaning of the arbitration terms or that Defendants denied such requests, assuming they were made.

 

Defendants’ burden is satisfied.  An agreement to arbitrate exists.

 

Federal Arbitration Act (“FAA”)

 

Plaintiff contends the FAA is inapplicable because Defendants fail to show that “the employment relationship between Plaintiff and Defendants had ‘a specific effect or bear[ing] on interstate commerce in a substantial way.’”  (Opposition, p. 4, emphasis deleted.)

 

The FAA applies if the plain language says it applies or if “the underlying contract facilitates interstate commercial transactions or directly or indirectly affects” interstate commerce.  (Knight, supra, at ¶ 5:50.2, emphasis deleted.) Section 5 of Defendants’ agreement states:

 

This Agreement and its validity, construction, and performance is governed by the Federal Arbitration Act (the “FAA”) and cases decided thereunder and, to the extent relevant, the laws of the State of California. Further, the terms and procedures governing the enforcement of this Agreement must be governed by and construed and enforced in accordance with the FAA, and not individual state laws regarding enforcement of arbitration agreements.

 

(Regan Decl., Ex. A, p. 3, § 5.)  The wording renders the FAA controlling.[1]

 

Unconscionability and Enforcement

 

Unconscionability is a contract defense that can be utilized to “invalidate [an] arbitration agreement[.]”  (Torrecillas v. Fitness International, LLC (2020) 52 Cal.App.5th 485, 492.)  Courts apply state law to test whether the agreement is unconscionable.  (See, e.g., Lagatree v. Luce, Forward, Hamilton & Scripps (1999) 74 Cal.App.4th 1105, 1119.)

 

“[U]nconscionability has both a procedural and a substantive element, the former focusing on oppression or surprise due to unequal bargaining power, the latter on overly harsh or one-sided results.”  (Armendariz v. Foundation Health Psychcare Services, Inc. (2000) 24 Cal.4th 83, 114, internal quotation marks omitted.)  “The prevailing view is that [procedural and substantive unconscionability] must both be present in order for a court to exercise its discretion to refuse to enforce a contract or clause under the doctrine of unconscionability.”  (Ibid.)  “But they need not be present in the same degree.”  (Ibid.)  “Essentially a sliding scale is invoked which disregards the regularity of the procedural process of the contract formation, that creates the terms, in proportion to the greater harshness or unreasonableness of the substantive terms themselves.”  (Ibid.)  “In other words, the more substantively oppressive the contract term, the less evidence of procedural unconscionability is required to come to the conclusion that the term is unenforceable, and vice versa.”  (Ibid.)

 

The burden belongs to the party opposing arbitration.  (See Ajamian v. CantorCO2e, L.P. (2012) 203 Cal.App.4th 771, 795.)

 

Procedural Element: Plaintiff claims the arbitration agreement constitutes a contract of adhesion.  He further states that he felt pressured to sign, that he did not get a chance to read the agreement, that he did not receive attorney assistance, and that Defendants failed to give him hard copies of the onboarding documents.  (See Opposition, pp. 9-10; see also Scott Decl., ¶¶ 3-8.)

 

The Court disagrees.  “[A] predispute arbitration agreement is not invalid merely because it is imposed as a condition of employment.”  (Lagatree, supra, 74 Cal.App.4th at 1122.)  Moreover, to repeat, failure to read and understand is not a defense (see Knight, supra, at ¶ 5:90), and there is no statement under penalty of perjury stating that Plaintiff requested extra time to read the onboarding documents or assistance from an attorney or other consultant to explain the meaning of the arbitration terms or that Defendant denied such requests, assuming they were made.

 

Substantive element: Plaintiff contends the agreement (1) is one-sided in that it requires him to arbitrate against third-party entities that do not similarly agree to arbitrate in return (see Opposition, pp. 10-11 [citing Phan v. Knight Sacramento SU Inc. (2026) 121 Cal.App.5th 641 and Cook v. USC (2024) 102 Cal.App.5th 312]), (2) contains a class-action waiver that runs afoul of the Gentry rule (see id. at pp. 12-14), (3) waives the right to a jury trial (see id. at p. 14), and (4) waives the right to bring a representative PAGA claim.  (See id. at pp. 11-12.)

 

Point (1) fails.  Phan relies on Cook.  The overarching problem with the Cook agreement was the unlimited scope.  (See Cook, supra, 102 Cal.App.5th at 321-325.)  It covered “all claims, whether or not arising out of Employee’s University employment[.]”  (Ibid.)  Nothing was excluded.  (See id. at 317, 322-324.)  By contrast, Defendants’ agreement only covers “dispute[s] that arise[] out of or relate[] to Employee’s employment with Employer[.]”  (Regan Decl., Ex. A, p. 1, § 1.)  Given this material difference, Cook is distinguishable.[2]

 

Points (2) and (3) also fail.  Plaintiff cites California rules to support these points.  As he acknowledges, though, the FAA preempts the California rules.   (See Opposition, pp. 11-14.)

 

Which brings the Court to point (4).  Multiple opinions address the interplay among the FAA, representative PAGA claims, and representative-action waivers. The primary ones are Iskanian v. CLS Transportation Los Angeles, LLC (2014) 59 Cal.4th 348, Viking River Cruises, Inc. v. Moriana (2022) 142 S.Ct. 1906 (“Viking River”), and Adolph v. Uber Technologies, Inc. (2023) 14 Cal.5th 1104. 

 

Considering Iskanian, Viking River, and Adolph together, the standard approach in Department 11 has been to compel individual PAGA claims to arbitration and to stay representative PAGA claims until the arbitrations finish.  The Court intends to do the same in Plaintiff’s case.

 

Consequently, the level of procedural unconscionability is low, the level of substantive unconscionability is low, the agreement is enforceable, the motion to compel is granted, the class claims must be stricken, and the representative PAGA claim must be stayed.

 

This ruling applies with equal force to Perian.  The right of nonsignatories to enforce is well settled.  (See Knight, supra, at ¶ 5:262.)  The rule applies in several instances.  (See id. at ¶¶ 5:263, 5:265.7, 5:266.5, 5:266.7, 5:266.15 [either when the nonsignatory is a third-party beneficiary, employee, associate, agent, or assign or when equitable estoppel applies]; see also Marenco v. DirecTV LLC (2015) 233 Cal.App.4th 1409, 1419-1420 [holding that “a nonsignatory defendant may invoke an arbitration clause to compel a signatory plaintiff to arbitrate its claims when the causes of action against the nonsignatory are ‘intimately founded in and intertwined’ with the underlying contract obligations”].)  At least two (agency and equitable estoppel) appear applicable and support Perian’s position.  (See First Amended Complaint, ¶¶ 1, 10-13 [alleging that all Defendants were agents, alter egos, and joint venturers and jointly employed Plaintiff]; see also Garcia v. Pexco, LLC (2017) 11 Cal.App.5th 782, 787-788 [applying the agency and equitable-estoppel examples in a joint-employer action]; Knight, supra, at ¶ 5:266.18a [discussing Garcia].)

 

CONCLUSION

 

Defendants’ motion to compel arbitration of Plaintiff’s individual wage and hour claims is granted. The class claims are stricken, and the case is stayed as to the representative PAGA claims.

 

At the hearing the court will set the date for a post-arbitration status conference.
