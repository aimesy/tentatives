DEPARTMENT 509 LAW AND MOTION RULINGS


Case Number: 22STCV11245    Hearing Date: July 17, 2026    Dept: 509

Enrique Curioso, et al., v. County of Los Angeles, et al.

(1) IRVING’S MOTION TO QUASH

(2) IRVING, PALMA, AND TOPORKOV’S MOTION TO QUASH
 

MOVING PARTY: (1): Plaintiff Steven Irving and (2): Plaintiffs Steven Irving, Konstantin Toporkov, and Rodrigo Palma

RESPONDING PARTY(S): (1) & (2): Defendant County of Los Angeles

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

This is an employment action. Plaintiffs Enrique Curioso, Steven Irving, Hatuey Jalisco, Konstantin Toporkov, and Rodrigo Palma (Plaintiffs) bring this action against defendants County of Los Angeles and the Los Angeles County Department of Public Works. Plaintiffs allege they each worked for the County and its third-party vendor, American Airports Corporation. In mid-2021, the County informed American Airports Corporation that it would take over the management of its own airports, and invited Plaintiffs to apply to retain their employment positions. Plaintiffs, all of whom are aged 40 or older, allege they applied to retain their positions, but that Defendants instead hired or retained younger employees. Plaintiffs bring causes of action for: (1) age discrimination, (2) failure to hire, (3) national origin discrimination, (4) failure to take steps to prevent discrimination, and (5) wrongful termination.

Plaintiff Steve Irving (Irving) now moves to quash the deposition subpoenas for production of business records issued by the County of Los Angeles (Defendant) to his subsequent employer, Atlantic Aviation FBO, Inc., seeking Irving’s personnel records. Defendant opposed. 

Plaintiffs Irving, Konstantin Toporkov (Toporkov), and Rodrigo Palma (Palma, collectively Moving Plaintiffs) move to quash the deposition subpoenas for production of business records issued by Defendant to their subsequent employers: United States Air Force for Irving; Ontario International Airport for Palma; and CPF BDA, LLC dba Bermuda Dunes Airport for Toporkov. Defendant opposed.

On June 29, 2026, Irving’s motion was heard. The Court continued the matter until July 17 and ordered the parties to meet and confer in efforts to resolve the issue. Considering this order, it appears that Defendant agrees to withdraw the subpoenas requests for tax records and performance evaluations.

The Court now addresses both motions to quash.

TENTATIVE RULING:

Irving’s Motion to Quash is GRANTED IN PART AND DENIED IN PART, as discussed infra.

Moving Plaintiffs’ Motion to Quash is GRANTED IN PART AND DENIED IN PART, as discussed infra.

The third parties are ordered to turn over all requested documents consistent with their respective subpoenas excluding tax records and performance evaluations.

Defendant is ordered to give notice, unless waived.

DISCUSSION:

Motion to Quash

A. Legal Standard

A court may quash a subpoena entirely or partially, and issue an order to protect parties, witnesses or consumers from unreasonable or oppressive demands including violations of privacy. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.1.) A motion to quash the production of documents or tangible things requires a separate statement. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.13459(a)(5).) “[U]pon motion reasonably made by the party, judges may rule upon motions for quashing, modifying or compelling compliance with, subpoenas.” (Lee v. Swansboro Country Property Owners Ass'n (2007) 151 Cal.App.4th 575, 582-583.)

B. Analysis

Moving Plaintiffs bring two different motions to quash business records issued to their subsequent employers. Both motions are made on the same grounds.

1. Background

a. Irving’s Motions to Quash

Irving moves to quash the deposition subpoena served on his subsequent employers Atlantic Aviation FBO, Inc. (the Atlantic Subpoena) and the United States Airforce (the US Subpoena).

The Atlantic Subpoena states:

THE RECORDS REQUESTED ARE regardless of date FOR THE FOLLOWING TYPES OF RECORDS: ANY AND ALL EMPLOYMENT RECORDS, EMPLOYMENT APPLICATIONS, WAGE RECORDS, PERSONNEL RECORDS, ATTENDANCE RECORDS, W-2’S, W-4’S, CONTRACT, 1099’S, RESUMES, PAYROLL RECORDS, VACATION SCHEDULE, SICK LEAVE, LEAVE OF ABSENCE RECORDS, INSURANCE RECORDS, BENEFITS, EVALUATIONS AND ANY OTHER RECORDS, INCLUDING BUT NOT LIMITED TO ANY RECORDS/DOCUMENTS THAT MAY BE STORED DIGITALLY AND/OR ELECTRONICALLY PERTAINING TO THE EMPLOYMENT OF STEVEN IRVING, DOB: 6/12/1969.

(Spurley Decl. iso Irving’s Motion to Quash, Exhibit 1.) 

The US Subpoena requests identical documents. (Spurley Decl., Exhibit 1.) 

b. Toporkov’s Motion to Quash

Toporkov moves to quash the deposition subpoena served on his subsequent employer CPF BDA, LLC dba Bermuda Dunes Airport (the Dunes Subpoena).

The Dunes Subpoena requests:

THE RECORDS REQUESTED ARE regardless of date FOR THE FOLLOWING TYPES OF RECORDS: ANY AND ALL EMPLOYMENT RECORDS, WAGE RECORDS, PERSONNEL RECORDS, ATTENDANCE RECORDS, EMPLOYMENT APPLICATIONS, W-2’S, W-4’S, CONTRACTS, 1099’S, RESUMES, PAYROLL RECORDS, VACATION SCHEDULE, SICK LEAVE, LEAVE OF ABSENCE RECORDS, INSURANCE RECORDS, BENEFITS, EVALUATIONS AND ANY OTHER RECORDS, INCLUDING BUT NOT LIMITED TO ANY RECORDS/DOCUMENTS THAT MAY BE STORED DIGITALLY AND/OR ELECTRONICALLY PERTAINING TO THE EMPLOYMENT OF KONSTANTIN TOPORKOV, DOB: 4/111975, SS#: .

(Spurley Decl., Exhibit 3.)

c. Palma’s Motion to Quash

Palma moves to quash the deposition subpoena served on his subsequent employer, the Ontario International Airport (the Ontario Subpoena).

The Ontario Subpoena requests:

THE RECORDS REQUESTED ARE regardless of date FOR THE FOLLOWING TYPES OF RECORDS: ANY AND ALL EMPLOYMENT RECORDS, WAGE RECORDS, PERSONNEL RECORDS, ATTENDANCE RECORDS, EMPLOYMENT APPLICATIONS, W-2’S, W-4’S, CONTRACTS, 1099’S, RESUMES, PAYROLL RECORDS, VACATION SCHEDULE, SICK LEAVE, LEAVE OF ABSENCE RECORDS, INSURANCE RECORDS, BENEFITS, EVALUATIONS AND ANY OTHER RECORDS, INCLUDING BUT NOT LIMITED TO ANY RECORDS/DOCUMENTS THAT MAY BE STORED DIGITALLY AND/OR ELECTRONICALLY PERTAINING TO THE EMPLOYMENT OF RODRIGO PALMA, DOB: 5/8/1964, SS#: .

(Spurley Decl., Exhibit 2.)

2. Analysis

Here, both motions and all subpoenas are nearly identical. The subpoenas request the same information, and both motions request the same relief. 

Moving Plaintiffs’ position is that the subpoenas are so overbroad as drafted and seeks records that are protected from production by Moving Plaintiffs’ Constitutional Right to Privacy. Moving Plaintiffs argue a heightened standard applies because the records sought include constitutionally protected material, and thus, Defendant must demonstrate a compelling interest that outweighs the privacy interest.  
 
In opposition, Defendant contend that Moving Plaintiffs have placed their subsequent employment history at issue by bringing this lawsuit. Defendant argues that the discovery is relevant to mitigation of damages, among other things. Further, in their July 6, 2026, opposition Defendant agreed to limit the scope of the subpoenas and withdraw its requests for tax records and performance evaluations. (See 7/6/26 Opp. at p. 3:16-17.) The Court addresses the subpoenas with these specific requests withdrawn.

“The party asserting a privacy right must establish a legally protected privacy interest, an objectively reasonable expectation of privacy in the given circumstances, and a threatened intrusion that is serious. [Citation.] The party seeking information may raise in response whatever legitimate and important countervailing interests disclosure serves, while the party seeking protection may identify feasible alternatives that serve the same interests or protective measures that would diminish the loss of privacy. A court must then balance these competing considerations.” (Williams v. Superior Ct. (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 552.) Because Moving Plaintiffs’ constitutional privacy rights are implicated, the burden is on Defendant to demonstrate that the information sought are both (1) directly relevant to claims or defenses in this action (Britt v. Superior Court (1978) 20 Cal.3d 844, 859), and (2) not available through a less intrusive means of discovery (Allen v. Superior Court (1984) 151 Cal.App.3d 447, 453). 

Here, Moving Plaintiffs have a recognized expectation of privacy in his employment records, although to an extent, they have reduced that privacy interest by bringing this case. When balancing Moving Plaintiffs’ privacy interests against Defendant’s interest in discovering documents that are particularly relevant to Defendant’s defense of the action, this Court finds the balance shifts in favor of disclosure. The Court agrees with Defendant that the categories are relevant and not overbroad. In addition to addressing Moving Plaintiffs’ income and work conditions at the subsequent employer, the documents sought are relevant to his performance as an employee—questions Moving Plaintiffs’ have put in issue by bringing this action. It also appears there are no feasible alternatives to obtaining the documents sought. The Court further notes there is a confidentiality order in place the parties may utilize in the handling of such documents. (See 6/1/26 Protective Order.)

Moreover, admissibility at trial is not to be mistaken with discoverability. “[A]ny party may obtain discovery regarding any matter, not privileged, that is relevant to the subject matter involved in the pending action or to the determination of any motion made in that action, if the matter either is itself admissible in evidence or appears reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence.” (Code Civ. Pro. § 2017.010; Davies v. Superior Court (1984) 36 Cal.3d 291, 301 [“discovery is not limited to admissible evidence”].) Evidence is relevant if it “ha[s] any tendency in reason to prove or disprove any disputed fact that is of consequence to the determination of the action.” (Evid. Code § 210.) The discovery here sought is relevant, even if it is ultimately not admissible at trial, a question this Court expresses no opinion on now. 

This conclusion is consistent with the legislature’s “very liberal and flexible standard of relevancy,” such that any “doubts as to relevance should generally be resolved in favor of permitting discovery.” (Williams, supra, 3 Cal.5th at p. 542.) 

Accordingly, Moving Plaintiffs’ Motion to Quash is GRANTED AND DENIED IN PART. 

Each third party is ordered to turn over all documents consistent with their respective subpoenas excluding tax records and performance evaluations.

C. Sanctions

In making an order pursuant to California Code of Civil Procedure section 1987.1, “the court may in its discretion award the amount of the reasonable expenses incurred in making or opposing the motion, including reasonable attorney’s fees, if the court finds the motion was made or opposed in bad faith or without substantial justification or that one or more of the requirements of the subpoena was oppressive.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.2, subd. (a).)

Accordingly, the Court declines to award any expenses or attorney’s fees since neither party has formally or properly requested sanctions. (See Code Civ. Pro. § 2023.040, [any request for sanctions must be contained in the notice of motion or opposition.])

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Case Number: 25STCV11911    Hearing Date: July 17, 2026    Dept: 509

The Friendship Foundation v. Linda Toibb

DEFENDANT’S MOTION TO QUASH
 

MOVING PARTY: Defendant Linda Toibb, individually and as trustee of the HLTT Trust

RESPONDING PARTY(S): Plaintiffs The Friendship Foundation and The Friendship Educational Foundation

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:

Plaintiffs The Friendship Foundation and The Friendship Educational Foundation (collectively, Plaintiffs) filed this action against defendant Linda Toibb, individually and as trustee of the HLTT Trust (Defendant), asserting causes of action for: (1) breach of contract; and (2) promissory estoppel. Plaintiffs allege Defendant and her husband decedent Harris Toibb were large donors to many projects involving Plaintiffs. Plaintiffs allege that before decedent passed, decedent pledged over $8 million to Plaintiffs but Defendant has refused to honor the pledge. 

Defendant, filed a cross-complaint against Rabbi Joseph Mintz, The Friendship Foundation and The Friendship Educational Foundation, asserting causes of action for: (1) fraud by concealment; (2) intentional misrepresentation; (3) negligent misrepresentation; (4) breach of confidential relationship; (5) elder abuse; (6) reformation; (7) rescission; (8) breach of contract; (9) breach of contract; (10) declaratory relief; and (11) accounting.

Defendant now moves to quash the deposition subpoenas for production of business records issued by Plaintiffs to Defendant’s bank, Wells Fargo Advisors, seeking various banking information and financial records. Plaintiffs opposed. 

TENTATIVE RULING:

Defendant’s Motion to Quash is GRANTED.

Defendant’s Request for Attorney’s Fees to be ARGUED at the hearing.

Defendant is ordered to give notice, unless waived.

DISCUSSION:

Motion to Quash

A. Legal Standard

A court may quash a subpoena entirely or partially, and issue an order to protect parties, witnesses or consumers from unreasonable or oppressive demands including violations of privacy. (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.1.) A motion to quash the production of documents or tangible things requires a separate statement. (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 3.13459(a)(5).) “[U]pon motion reasonably made by the party, judges may rule upon motions for quashing, modifying or compelling compliance with, subpoenas.” (Lee v. Swansboro Country Property Owners Ass’n (2007) 151 Cal.App.4th 575, 582-583.)

B. Analysis

Defendant moves to quash the subpoena served Wells Fargo Advisors, seeking various banking information and financial records (the Subpoena). (See Wisnia Decl., Exh. 7.) There are twenty-two requests for documents. 

Defendant’s position is that the Subpoena seeks documents that: “(1) violate Linda’s right to privacy, (2) are not relevant, nor “directly relevant” to the subject matter of the dispute, (3) are overbroad as to time and scope. It is also brought on the grounds that Friendship has issued the subpoena for improper purposes (including to oppress and harass Linda), and has failed to serve the required notices to consumer pursuant to CCP Section 1985.3 to the various Toibb affiliated entities (i.e., Toibb Delaware Trust).” (Not. of Mot. at p. 5:18-22.)
 
In opposition, Plaintiffs argues the Subpoena “seeks third-party evidence directly tied to issues Linda herself placed at the center of this case.” (Opp. at p. 2:2-3.)

“The party asserting a privacy right must establish a legally protected privacy interest, an objectively reasonable expectation of privacy in the given circumstances, and a threatened intrusion that is serious. [Citation.] The party seeking information may raise in response whatever legitimate and important countervailing interests disclosure serves, while the party seeking protection may identify feasible alternatives that serve the same interests or protective measures that would diminish the loss of privacy. A court must then balance these competing considerations.” (Williams v. Superior Ct. (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531, 552.) Because Defendant’s constitutional privacy rights are implicated, the burden is on Plaintiffs to demonstrate that the information sought are both (1) directly relevant to claims or defenses in this action (Britt v. Superior Court (1978) 20 Cal.3d 844, 859), and (2) not available through a less intrusive means of discovery (Allen v. Superior Court (1984) 151 Cal.App.3d 447, 453). The Court applies a “very liberal and flexible standard of relevancy,” such that any “doubts as to relevance should generally be resolved in favor of permitting discovery.” (Williams, supra, 3 Cal.5th at p. 542.)

However, here, Plaintiffs have not met that relaxed standard of relevancy as Plaintiffs have failed to demonstrate any tangible connection between the claims or defenses in this action and the requested discovery. 

Evidence is relevant if it “ha[s] any tendency in reason to prove or disprove any disputed fact that is of consequence to the determination of the action.” (Evid. Code § 210.) “[A]ny party may obtain discovery regarding any matter, not privileged, that is relevant to the subject matter involved in the pending action or to the determination of any motion made in that action, if the matter either is itself admissible in evidence or appears reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence.” (Code of Civ. Pro. § 2017.010; Davies v. Superior Court (1984) 36 Cal.3d 291, 301 [“discovery is not limited to admissible evidence”].)

Plaintiffs make three arguments as to why the Subpoena seeks relevant evidence. 

First, “Linda alleges Harris was vulnerable, elderly, in declining health, and susceptible to undue influence. (FACC ¶¶ 75-76, 91.) She alleges Mintz obtained the pledge agreements by undue influence and fraud. (FACC ¶¶ 85-91, 105-107.) Evidence from Wells Fargo about Harris’s account authority, financial instructions, advisor communications, meetings, transactions, investment activity, and ability to manage financial affairs directly bears on those allegations.” (Opp. at p. 5:10-15.) “The subpoena seeks records showing whether Harris continued to give instructions, communicate with financial professionals, control accounts, make investment decisions, and engage in trust-related activity. (Tippens Decl., Ex. 1, Attachment 3, Requests Nos. 1-8, 16-22.) It also seeks records showing whether Wells Fargo observed or recorded any concern about capacity, cognitive status, vulnerability, undue influence, elder abuse, suspicious activity, fraud, financial exploitation, compliance, legal, or risk review. (Id., Requests Nos. 9-10.)” (Opp. at p. 5:16-21.)

However, none of these requests are relevant to the allegation that Harris Toibb was vulnerable, elderly, or in declining health. Further, all requests that Plaintiffs cite to go well beyond Harris Toibb’s ability to manage his financial affairs. (See e.g. Request No. 4 [“All transaction histories trade confirmations, order tickets wire transfer records ACH records, check images, deposit records, withdrawal records, journal entries, internal transfer records, and cash movement records for each account identified in response to Request No. l during the RELEVANT TIME PERIOD.”]) The most relevant request out of all seventeen requests Plaintiffs cite is Request No. 10 which states: “All DOCUMENTS reflecting any elder abuse, vulnerable adult suspicious activity, fraud, undue influence, financial exploitation, unusual transaction, compliance, legal, or risk review concerning HARRIS, LINDA, the TRUST, TFF, the TOIBB DELAWARE TRUST, or HL TT REAL ESTA TE HOLDINGS during the RELEVANT TIME PERIOD.” However, by including “LINDA, the TRUST, TFF, the TOIBB DELAWARE TRUST, or HL TT REAL ESTA TE HOLDINGS during the RELEVANT TIME PERIOD” Plaintiffs have gone well beyond their own claimed purpose for discovering evidence “Harris was actively managing financial affairs” as Plaintiffs claim. (See Opp. at p. 5:22-23.) Indeed, it appears that Plaintiffs are seeking nearly every financial document within Wells Fargo’s control pertaining to Defendant, Harris Toibb, and numerous other non-parties associated with Defendant including after Harris Toibb’s passing. (See e.g Requests Nos. 5-16; Definition No. 9, [“’RELEVANT TIME PERIOD’ means January 1, 2018 through the date of production.”]) None of the requests seek only information relevant to Plaintiffs’ claims and defenses but appear to be designed to go beyond the stated purpose.  

Second, Plaintiffs argue the Subpoena “is directly relevant to trust authority, post-death account control, and financial tracing.” (Opp. at p. 5:26-27.) Plaintiffs argue:

Linda alleges that Mintz was protector of the Toibb Delaware Trust, director of TFF, and connected to FF, FEF, JCC, and Chabad. (FACC ¶¶ 10, 14.) She alleges he used trust-related authority after Harris’s death to pressure payment. (FACC ¶ 36.) She alleges commingling and movement of funds among FF, FEF, JCC, and Chabad, including Toibb money allegedly reflected as held at JCC. (FACC ¶¶ 31-34, 112-113, 117-119.)

Wells Fargo records concerning TFF, the HLTT Trust, the Toibb Delaware Trust, HLTT Real Estate Holdings, post-death account-control changes, successor trustee activity, changes in authorized signers, account transfers, retitling, and communications with trustees, estate representatives, attorneys, accountants, investment advisors, bankers, or other representatives directly bear on those issues. (Tippens Decl., Ex. 1, Attachment 3, Requests Nos. 11-17, 19-22.)

(Opp. at p. 5:28-6:9.)

Again, while these allegations surrounding Mintz may warrant investigation into his involvement in the various entities, Plaintiffs Subpoena goes well beyond such inquiry. (See e.g. Request No. 15. [“ All DOCUMENTS reflecting any accounts, transactions, transfers, signatures, authorizations, instructions, or COMMUNICATIONS RELATING to any business, corporation partnership limited liability company, trust, foundation, or other entity for which HARRIS was an owner, co-owner trustee co-trustee, officer, director manager, member, partner fiduciary signatory, authorized trader, authorized representative, power of-attorney holder, investment decision-maker or other PERSON authorized to direct transactions during the RELEVANT TIME PERIOD.”]) Plaintiffs include entities that Plaintiffs have not demonstrated, nor alleged, are relevant to the requests. Further, Plaintiffs argues that “[Defendant] alleges commingling and movement of funds among FF, FEF, JCC, and Chabad, including Toibb money allegedly reflected as held at JCC” (Opp at p. 6:2-4.) However, Plaintiffs expanded their Subpoena to include “the HLTT Trust, the Toibb Delaware Trust, HLTT Real Estate Holdings” which are not alleged to have had funds comingled. (Opp. at p. 6:5-6.) This pattern of including additional entities irrelevant to the action appears throughout the Subpoena. 

 Additionally, even if some relevant documents are within the scope of Plaintiffs’ Subpoena, Plaintiffs have not demonstrated this information is available through a less intrusive means of discovery. (Allen v. Superior Court, supra, 151 Cal.App.3d at p. 453. [“The court abused its discretion when it failed to require a less intrusive method of discovery.”) This Court entertains serious doubts that the scope of this Subpoena is the least intrusive means of discovery. In their opposition, Plaintiffs have not addressed, and therefore not satisfied, this requirement to overcome Defendant’s privacy interests.

And finally, Plaintiffs argue “Linda’s prior opposition confirms that the financial discovery is relevant.” (Opp at p. 6:15.) However, that prior motion has zero weight on this current motion to quash as they involve entirely different balancing interests.  [FN 1]  The parties’ allegations and needs for discovery are naturally different. Further, this Court is not denying access to financial discovery outright. Instead, it independently balances Defendant’s privacy interests with the Plaintiffs’ need for discovery here.

In the end, when balancing Defendant’s privacy interests against Plaintiffs’ interest in discovery, this Court finds the balance shifts in favor of quashing the Subpoena. The Court finds each of the twenty-two requests are either irrelevant, overbroad, or the information may be available through a less intrusive means of discovery. 

Accordingly, Defendant’s Motion to Quash is GRANTED IN ITS ENTIRETY. 

C. Sanctions

In making an order pursuant to California Code of Civil Procedure section 1987.1, “the court may in its discretion award the amount of the reasonable expenses incurred in making or opposing the motion, including reasonable attorney’s fees, if the court finds the motion was made or opposed in bad faith or without substantial justification or that one or more of the requirements of the subpoena was oppressive.” (Code Civ. Proc., § 1987.2, subd. (a).)

The Court will hear further argument on whether either of these exceptions apply in this motion, and if not, what would be the reasonable amount to be awarded, and against whom. 

IT IS SO ORDERED.

Foad Chavmeh v. Blue Star Insurance Company

CASE NO.:  26STCV03481 
 

MOTION TO COMPEL CONSOLIDATED ARBITRATION
 

MOVING PARTY: Petitioner Foad Chavmeh 

RESPONDING PARTY(S): Respondent United States Fire Insurance Company 

STATEMENT OF MATERIAL FACTS AND/OR PROCEEDINGS:
Petitioner Foad Chavmeh (Petitioner) seeks to compel arbitration against respondent United States Fire Insurance Company (Respondent) arising from a dispute of insurance coverage. Petitioner was involved in a motor vehicle accident on July 4, 2023, and seeks reimbursement of damages arising from this accident from Respondent)

On March 18, 2026, Petitioner filed a notice of related cases (“NORC”) in this case regarding, Mobilitas Insurance Company v. Foad Chavmeh, LASC Case. No. 25STCV31326 arising from the same July 4, 2023, motor vehicle accident. However, Petitioner did not file a NORC directly in 25STCV31326 despite his obligation to do so. (CRC Rule 3.300(d); See Rule 3.300 (h)(1)(A) [“Where all the cases listed in the notice are unlimited civil cases, or where all the cases listed in the notice are limited civil cases, the judge who has the earliest-filed case must determine whether the cases must be ordered related and assigned to his or her department.”].)  See also, Judge Randolph M. Hammock, To Relate or Not to Relate (Advocate, June 2022).

Now, Petitioner filed a motion to compel consolidated arbitration. Respondent opposed.

TENTATIVE RULING:

Petitioner’s Motion to Compel Consolidated Arbitration is CONTINUED to September 17, 2026. Petitioner is ordered to properly file a NORC in 26STCV03481.  That judge will rule on that NORC accordingly.
