MISSION PLAZA FAMILY APARTMENTS, LP v. RAMIREZ, et al.

(Hearing:
8/31/2026)

25STUD12687

 

Relief
Requested

Defendants’
Motion for Attorney’s Fees

 

Tentative
Ruling

Defendants’
Motion for Attorney’s Fees is GRANTED in the reduced
amount of $24,735.00.

 

Background

 

On
December 1, 2025, Plaintiff Mission Plaza Family Apartments, LP (“Plaintiff”)
filed this unlawful detainer action against Defendants Gladys Y. Ramirez and
Andrea Ramirez (“Defendants”) for the residential premises located 2234
Parkside Ave., #304, Los Angeles, CA 90031 (the “Property”). The complaint
alleges that on or about July 1, 2023, Defendants entered into a written
agreement to rent the Property for a monthly rent of $1,405.00—now $1,421.00—due
on the first of each month. (Compl. ¶ 6.) The complaint alleges that on or
about October 16, 2025, Plaintiff had Defendants served, via posting and
mailing, with a thirty-day notice to pay rent or quit (the “Notice”) demanding
$16,584.05 in unpaid rent for the months of November 2024 through October 2025.
(Id. ¶¶ 9–10, Exh. 2.)

 

The
court granted Defendants’ motion for summary judgment on July 23, 2026, and
entered judgment that same day. Defendants now move for an award of contractual
attorney’s fees in the amount of $30,925.50,[1]
contending that they are entitled to fees under the parties’ lease. In support
of the motion, Defendants’ counsel testify that this fee award represents 40.4
hours of work reasonably performed by attorney Alanna Holt at an hourly rate of
$600.00, 2.4 hours of work reasonably performed by attorney Jeanette
Alvarez-Webster at an hourly rate of $650, 7.5 hours of work reasonably
performed by attorney Kamilah Zadi, and 5.0 hours work reasonably performed by
attorney Arturo Gomez at an hourly rate of $500.00.

 

Plaintiff
opposes the motion, contending that the hours Defendants’ counsel claim and the
rates requested are unreasonable. In particular, Plaintiff contends that some
of the work performed by Defendants’ counsel may have been connected with a
separate civil action pending between the parties and that, as a result, this
time should be excluded from the present award.

 

Legal
Standard

 

Civil
Code § 1717(a) provides:

 

In
any action on a contract, where the contract specifically provides that
attorney’s fees and costs, which are incurred to enforce that contract, shall
be awarded either to one of the parties or to the prevailing party, then the
party who is determined to be the party prevailing on the contract . . . shall
be entitled to reasonable attorney’s fees in addition to other costs.”

 

One
of the purposes behind section 1717 is to make otherwise unilateral attorney’s
fees provisions reciprocal as a matter of law. (ABF Capital Corp. v. Grove
Properties Co. (2005) 126 Cal.App.4th 204, 217 [“Section 1717(a) is
no default provision or gapfiller, subject to override by the parties. Rather,
it represents a basic and fundamental policy choice by the state of California
that nonreciprocal attorney’s fees contractual provisions create reciprocal
rights to such fees.”].) The party seeking fees and costs “bear[s] the burden
of establishing entitlement to an award and documenting the appropriate hours
expended and hourly rates.” (ComputerXpress,
Inc. v. Jackson (2001) 93 Cal.App.4th 993, 1020.) In determining a
reasonable attorney fee award, a court decides the “reasonable hours spent” on
the case and multiplies the number by “the hourly prevailing rate for private
attorneys in the community conducting noncontingent litigation of the same
type.” (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th
1122, 1133.) The result is called the “lodestar” figure. (Id.)

 

Reasonable attorneys’ fees are determined by
examining the time spent and the reasonable hourly rate charged. (Serrano v. Priest (1977) 20 Cal.3d 25,
48-49.) Other considerations that may result in an upward or downward shift of
the fees include the novelty and difficulty of the case, the legal skill needed
to render services to the prevailing party, the customary fee for similar work,
whether the case was taken under contingency, the attorneys’ experience and
reputation, and other circumstances. (Id.)

 

Discussion

 

I.
Defendants Are the Prevailing Parties in this Contract Action

 

CCP § 1021 states that “[e]xcept as
attorney’s fees are specifically provided for by statute, the measure and mode
of compensation of attorneys and counselors at law is left to the agreement,
express or implied, of the parties.” CCP § 1032(b) states that “a prevailing
party is entitled as a matter of right to recover costs in any action or
proceeding.” A prevailing party under this section includes “a defendant in
whose favor a dismissal is entered.” (CCP § 1032(a)(4).) Further, CCP §
1033.5(a)(10) provides that costs can include attorney’s fees when authorized
by contract, statute, or law.

 

Civil Code § 1717(a) provides that
“[i]n any action on a contract, where the contract specifically provides that
attorney’s fees and costs, which are incurred to enforce that contract, shall
be awarded either to one of the parties or to the prevailing party, then the
party who is determined to be the party prevailing on the contract, whether he
or she is the party specified in the contract or not, shall be entitled to
reasonable attorney’s fees in addition to other costs.” However, “[w]here an
action has been voluntarily dismissed or dismissed pursuant to a settlement of
the case, there shall be no prevailing party for purposes of this section.” (Civ.
Code § 1717(b)(2).)

 

Moreover,
unlawful detainer actions based on breach of the lease during its term, such as
for nonpayment of rent, are actions on a contract subject to Civil Code § 1717.
(Mitchell Land and Imp. Co. v. Ristorante
Ferrantelli, Inc. (2007) 158 Cal.App.4th 479, 486.) Further, the main
objective of an unlawful detainer action is the right to possession. (Id.)

 

Here, it cannot be
disputed that this action sounded in contract and that Defendants are the
prevailing parties. This action plainly sounded in contract, as it was based on
Defendants’ alleged nonpayment of rent. (Mitchell, supra, 158
Cal.App.4th at 486.) Moreover, it is undisputed that Defendants prevailed on summary
judgment and had judgment entered in their favor. They are therefore the prevailing
parties under Civil Code § 1717 and are entitled to an award of attorney’s fees
so long as the parties’ lease so provides.

 

II.
The Attorney’s Fees Provision Applies to this Action

 

When not authorized by statute,
entitlement to attorney fees derives from the contractual terms chosen, as
parties may limit or expand the circumstances under which attorney fees may be
awarded. (Chacker v. JPMorgan Chase Bank,
N.A. (2018) 27 Cal.App.5th 351, 357; see Rideau v. Stewart Title of California, Inc. (2015) 235 Cal.App.4th
1286, 1301 (whether a party to an agreement is entitled to attorney fees under
the agreement depends on its language).) In the case of a contractual attorney
fees provision, any inquiry begins with the language of the subject attorney
fees provision, and thus, for attorney fees to be recovered, the claim on which
the fees are incurred must fall within the scope of the attorney fee provision.
(Exxess Electronixx v. Heger Realty Corp.
(1998) 64 Cal.App.4th 698, 708; see Mountain
Air Enterprises, LLC v. Sundowner Towers, LLC (2017) 3 Cal.5th 744, 752,
760 (“it is necessary to determine whether the parties entered an agreement for
the payment of attorney fees, and if so, the scope of the attorney fee
agreement.”).)

 

This determination requires the
court to apply traditional rules of contract interpretation. (Mountain Air Enterprises, supra,
3 Cal.5th at 752.) Courts decline to take an overly formalistic approach in
deciding whether a prevailing party is entitled to contractual attorney fees,
and instead “consider the pleaded theories of recovery, the theories asserted
and the evidence produced at trial, if any, and also any additional evidence
submitted on the motion in order to identify the legal basis of the prevailing
party’s recovery.” (Id. at 760-61.)

 

[W]e first consider the mutual
intention of the parties at the time the contract providing for attorney fees
was formed. (Civ. Code, § 1636.) Our initial inquiry is confined to the writing
alone. (Id., § 1639; [Citation
omitted].) “‘The “clear and explicit” meaning of these provisions, interpreted
in their “ordinary and popular sense,” unless “used by the parties in a
technical sense or a special meaning is given to them by usage” ([Civ. Code], §
1644), controls judicial interpretation. (Id.,
§ 1638.) Thus, if the meaning a layperson would ascribe to contract language is
not ambiguous, we apply that meaning.’ [Citations.]” ([Citations omitted].) At
the same time, we also recognize the “interpretational principle that a
contract must be understood with reference to the circumstances under which it
was made and the matter to which it relates. (Civ. Code, § 1647).”

 

(Id. at 752.) Further, “[t]he whole of a
contract is to be taken together, so as to give effect to every part, if
reasonably practicable, each clause helping to interpret the other.” (Civ. Code
§ 1641.)

 

Here, paragraph 19 of Defendants’ written
lease, which Plaintiff produced in discovery, provides that “[i]f Lessor, in
order to enforce any provision of this lease or in defense

of
any claim regarding this lease asserted by the Lessee, incurs attorney’s fees
or other costs or expenses, Lessee agrees to pay all such fees and costs
whether or not suit is filed by or against Lessee.” (Mot. Holt Decl. ¶ 2, Exh.
A.) This broad language is sufficient to encompass this contract claim. Accordingly,
the court finds that Defendants, as the prevailing parties in this action
brought as a result of their failure to pay rent, are entitled to an award of
reasonable attorney’s fees.

 

III.
Calculation of the Fee Award

 

The
party seeking fees and costs “bear[s] the burden of establishing entitlement to
an award and documenting the appropriate hours expended and hourly rates.” (ComputerXpress, Inc. v. Jackson (2001)
93 Cal.App.4th 993, 1020.) In determining a reasonable attorney fee award, a
court decides the “reasonable hours spent” on the case and multiplies the
number by “the hourly prevailing rate for private attorneys in the community
conducting noncontingent litigation of the same type.” (Ketchum v. Moses (2001) 24 Cal.4th 1122, 1133.) The result is
called the “lodestar” figure. (Id.)

 

Reasonable attorneys’ fees are determined by
examining the time spent and the reasonable hourly rate charged. (Serrano v. Priest (1977) 20 Cal.3d 25,
48-49.) Other considerations that may result in an upward or downward shift of
the fees include the novelty and difficulty of the case, the legal skill needed
to render services to the prevailing party, the customary fee for similar work,
whether the case was taken under contingency, the attorneys’ experience and
reputation, and other circumstances. (Id.)

 

                        i. Number of Expended Reasonable
Hours

 

A
court determining the number of hours reasonably expended on a case “must
carefully review attorney documentation of hours expended.” (Ketchum, supra, 24 Cal.4th at
1132.) In doing so, the court must exclude hours that were “duplicative or
excessive.” (Graciano v. Robinson Ford
Sales, Inc. (2006) 144 Cal.App.4th 140, 161.) “Reasonable compensation does
not include compensation for ‘padding’ in the form of inefficient or
duplicative efforts.” (Donahue v. Donahue
(2010) 182 Cal.App.4th 259, 271 [quoting Ketchum, supra, 24 Cal.4th at 1131–1132].) A court
determining the number of hours reasonably expended on a case “must carefully
review attorney documentation of hours expended.” (Ketchum, supra, 24 Cal.4th at 1132.) In
doing so, the court must exclude hours that were “duplicative or excessive.” (Graciano v. Robinson Ford Sales, Inc. (2006)
144 Cal.App.4th 140, 161.) “Reasonable compensation does not include
compensation for ‘padding’ in the form of inefficient or duplicative efforts.”
(Donahue v. Donahue (2010) 182
Cal.App.4th 259, 271 [quoting Ketchum, supra, 24 Cal.4th at 1131–1132].) Here,
Defendants seek to recover attorney’s fees for work performed by four attorneys
on this matter. The court will address each counsel’s hours in turn.

 

                                    a.
Attorney Alanna Holt

 

Defendants
claim that Ms. Holt spent a total of 40.4 hours working on this matter. Upon
review of counsel’s testimony and the record in this action, and given this
court’s experience and observations thereof, the requested amount is excessive.
Accordingly, the court reduces Ms. Holt’s requested 40.4 hours to 35.8 hours
for the reasons set forth below.

 

·        
Counsel requests a total of 3.5 hours for time
spent drafting Defendants’ motion for summary judgment. Considering that Ms.
Zadi also spent 3.0 hours on the same motion and the nature of the issues
involved, the Court finds the requested time excessive and will reduce it by
3.0 hours.

·        
Counsel requests a total of 2.0 hours for time
spent on July 7, 2026, for preparing “jury docs.” Considering the trial
documents counsel filed that day, the court finds this time excessive and will
reduce the request by 0.5 hours.

·        
Counsel requests a total of 3.6 hours for time
spent reviewing Plaintiff’s opposition to Defendants’ motion for summary
judgment and preparing a reply. Considering the nature of the issues raised in
the opposition and addressed in the reply, the court finds this time excessive
and will reduce the request by 1.1 hours.

 

                                    b.
Attorney Jeanette Alvarez-Webster

 

Defendants
request that Ms. Alvarez-Webster be compensated for a total of 2.4 hours spent working
on this matter. Upon review of counsel’s testimony and the record in this
action, and given this court’s experience and observations thereof, the
requested amount is reasonable.

 

                                    c.
Attorney Kamilah Zadi

 

Defendants
request that Ms. Zadi be compensated for a total of 7.5 hours spent working on
this matter. Upon review of counsel’s testimony and the record in this action,
and given this court’s experience and observations thereof, the requested
amount is unreasonable. In particular, the court notes that Ms. Zadi requests a
total of 3.0 hours for time spent on “Substituted into case, research defenses
and facts of the case,

review
all actions taken with case.” This entry is block billed, making it difficult
to ascertain what time was necessary and reasonably incurred and raising
credibility questions about the billing records submitted. (See Guillory v. Hill (2019) 36 Cal.App.5th
802, 815–816 [trial court made implicit determination that counsel’s billing
records were not credible, as “[c]ounsel may not submit a plethora of
noncompensable, vague, block-billed attorney time entries and expect
particularized, individual deletions as the only consequence”].) Accordingly,
the court will reduce Ms. Zadi’s requested time to 5.5 hours.

 

                                    d.
Attorney Arturo Gomez

 

Defendants
request that Mr. Gomez be compensated for a total of 5.0 hours spent working on
this matter. Upon review of counsel’s testimony and the record in this action,
and given this court’s experience and observations thereof, the requested
amount is excessive. In particular, Mr. Gomez requests a total of 2.0 hours for
time spent propounding discovery on January 30, 2026. Considering the nature of
this dispute and the fact that Ms. Holt is also being compensated for 0.8 hours
spent drafting the same discovery, the court finds this time to be excessive
and will reduce the request by 1.0 hour.

 

                        ii. Reasonable Hourly
Rate

 

It is well established that “[w]hen the trial court
is informed of the extent and nature of the legal services rendered, it may
rely on its own experience and knowledge in determining their reasonable
value.” (In re Marriage of Huntington
(1992) 10 Cal.App.4th 1513, 1524.) “‘In making its calculation [of a reasonable
hourly rate], the court may rely on its own knowledge and familiarity with the
legal market, as well as the experience, skill, and reputation of the attorney
requesting fees [citation], the difficulty or complexity of the litigation to
which that skill was applied [citations], and affidavits from other attorneys
regarding prevailing fees in the community and rate determinations in other
cases.’ [Citation.]” (Morris, supra, 41 Cal.App.5th at 41; see Mountjoy v. Bank of America, N.A. (2016)
245 Cal.App.4th 266, 272 (“[A] reasonable hourly rate is the product of a
multiplicity of factors .... the level of skill necessary, time limitations,
the amount to be obtained in the litigation, the attorney’s reputation, and the
undesirability of the case.”).) Here,
Defendants seek to recover different hourly rates for each of their two
attorneys. The court will address each counsel’s rate in turn.

 

                                    a.
Attorney Alanna Holt

 

Ms. Holt requests that the court set an hourly rate
of $600.00/hour for her time, which she testified is reasonable in light of her
thirteen years of legal experience. Given the nature of the issues involved in
this action, and the nature of the work Ms. Holt performed, the court finds
this rate to be unreasonable. (In
re Marriage of Huntington,
supra, 10 Cal.App.4th at 1524.)
Rather, the court finds that a reasonable hourly rate for the work Ms. Holt
performed in this action is $550.00. (Ketchum,
supra, 24 Cal.4th at 1132; see In
re Marriage of Huntington, supra, 10 Cal.App.4th at 1524.)

 

                                    b.
Attorney Jeanette Alvarez-Webster

 

Ms. Alvarez-Webster requests that the court set an
hourly rate of $650.00/hour for her time, which she testified is reasonable in
light of sixteen years—five before passing the bar exam and the remainder since—of
experience litigating landlord–tenant disputes. Given the nature of the issues
involved in this action, and the limited nature of the work Ms. Alvarez-Webster
performed, the court finds this rate to be unreasonable. (In re Marriage of Huntington, supra, 10 Cal.App.4th at
1524.) Rather, the court finds that a
reasonable hourly rate for the work Ms. Alvarez-Webster performed in this
action is $550.00. (Ketchum, supra, 24 Cal.4th at 1132; see In
re Marriage of Huntington, supra, 10 Cal.App.4th at 1524.)

 

                                    c.
Attorney Kamilah Zadi

 

Ms. Zadi requests that the court set an hourly rate
of $350.00/hour for her time. Upon review, the court finds this rate to be
reasonable.

 

                                    d.
Attorney Arturo Gomez

 

Mr. Gomez requests that the court set an hourly rate
of $500.00/hour for his time, which he testified is reasonable in light of his
five years of experience litigating landlord–tenant disputes. Given the nature
of the issues involved in this action, and the limited nature of the work Mr.
Gomez performed, the court finds this rate to be unreasonable. (In re Marriage of Huntington, supra, 10 Cal.App.4th at
1524.) Rather, the court finds that a
reasonable hourly rate for the work Mr. Gomez performed in this action is
$450.00. (Ketchum, supra, 24 Cal.4th at 1132; see In
re Marriage of Huntington, supra, 10 Cal.App.4th at 1524.)

 

Conclusion

 

Defendants’ Motion for
Attorney’s Fees is GRANTED in the reduced amount of $24,735.00, reflecting a
total of:

·        
35.8 hours reasonably worked by Defendants’ counsel Ms. Holt at
a reasonable hourly rate of $550.00;

·        
2.4 hours reasonably worked by Defendants’ counsel Ms.
Alvarez-Webster at a reasonable hourly rate of $550.00;

·        
5.5 hours reasonably worked by Defendants’ counsel Ms. Zadi at a
reasonable hourly rate of $350.00; and

·        
4.0 hours reasonably worked by Defendants’ counsel Mr. Gomez at
a reasonable hourly rate of $450.00.
